Posttransplant diabetes mellitus (PTDM) frequency, risk factors, and outcomes: a single-center experience
Authors: Nazish Fatima, Zareen Kiran, Muhammad Tassaduq, Akhtar Ali Baloch, Khalil Ullah Shabbir Shaikh, Syed Muhammad Hasan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe clinical consequences and risk factors for posttransplant diabetes mellitus (PTDM) remain underexplored, particularly in the Pakistani population. This study aimed to assess the frequency of PTDM, identify its risk factors, and compare clinical outcomes with those of non-PTDM patients in a single center.MethodologyA retrospective cohort observational study was conducted on 121 kidney transplant recipients aged 18 years or older from January 2018 to December 2021. Data were collected on demographic characteristics, pretransplant risk factors, and posttransplant outcomes at 3, 6, and 12 months. PTDM was diagnosed via international consensus guidelines, and clinical outcomes were compared between the PTDM and non-PTDM groups.ResultsOut of 492 transplant recipients, a total of 121 participants were included in the study; the mean age was 36.45 ± 10.56 years, the mean BMI was 27.65 ± 5.01 kg/m², and the pretransplant mean FBS was 84.32 ± 12.22 mg/dl. Predominantly, there were males (84.3%), mostly with hypertension as the primary diagnosis (86%). PTDM developed in 27.3% (n = 33) of the participants. PTDM patients had significantly higher fasting blood sugar (FBS) levels (p < 0.0001) and worse outcomes, including higher rates of infection (27.27% vs. 3.41%, p < 0.05), delayed graft function (15.15% vs. 0%, p < 0.05), and transplant rejection (12.12% vs. 1.14%, p < 0.05). Dyslipidemia was also more prevalent in the PTDM group than in the control group at 3, 6, and 12 months.ConclusionPTDM significantly impacts posttransplant outcomes, necessitating careful management to mitigate adverse events such as infection, graft dysfunction, and dyslipidemia. Early detection and targeted interventions may improve clinical outcomes in PTDM patients.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Keywords: Posttransplant diabetes mellitus, Frequency, Risk factors, Outcomes, Karachi

Background

The most suitable treatment option for patients with end-stage renal disease requiring renal replacement therapy is kidney transplantation, resulting in improved quality of life [1]. Several complications may follow kidney transplantation, and one such metabolic complication is posttransplant diabetes mellitus (PTDM), which can also increase morbidity and mortality rates [2, 3]. The probable driving force behind the development of PTDM is increased insulin resistance and compromised insulin production [3]. Potential risk factors for PTDM can be nonmodifiable, modifiable, or possibly adjustable [4]. Nonmodifiable factors include age (above 40), race or ethnicity (e.g., African, Latino, Asian American, Native American, and native inhabitants of Pacific Islands), family history of diabetes, male recipient, male donor, mismatch of some human leukocyte antigens (such as A30, B27, and B42), mismatch of the donor, deceased donor kidney, and any previous record of acute rejection [4]. Polycystic kidney disease can be a possible risk factor for PTDM, but the results from related studies are still inconsistent [5]. Obesity (BMI greater than or equal to 30 kg/m²) and the type of immunosuppressant therapy used for preventing and treating transplant rejection, such as tacrolimus [6] and corticosteroids, are modifiable factors. However, possibly adjustable risk factors include pretransplant impaired fasting (IFG) or impaired glucose tolerance (IGT) and a prior history of hepatitis C (HCV) or cytomegalovirus (CMV) infection [4].

The frequency of PTDM ranges from 7 to 30% in the first year after transplant [6, 7]. In the United States, the frequency has been reported to be 9.1% within the first 3 months posttransplant, increasing to 16% at 12 months posttransplant, and further increasing to 24% at 36 months posttransplant [6]. Apart from renal transplantation, the occurrence of PTDM in cases of liver transplantation, heart transplantation, and lung transplantation has also been reported, with recorded incidence rates ranging from 2.5 to 25%, 4–40%, and 30–35%, respectively [8]. The majority of patients, i.e., approximately 76.5%, develop PTDM in the initial 3 months after transplant [9], and the occurrence of diabetes has been reported to be the same in transplant and nontransplant patients approximately 6 months after transplant [10].

The World Health Organization (WHO) and American Diabetes Association (ADA) criteria are recommended for the diagnosis of impaired glucose tolerance (IGT) and diabetes by the International Consensus Guidelines designed in 2003 and revised in 2014 for PTDM [11, 12].

PTDM might also lead to further complications, such as diabetes mellitus, nephropathy, neuropathy, ketoacidosis, or ophthalmopathy. It may also become a risk factor for infections and reduce graft survival, as the patient’s immunity is compromised due to hyperglycemia [9, 13].

The contributing risk factors, frequency, and clinical consequences of PTDM in the Pakistani population have not been well studied, with only a few studies focusing on PTDM [14, 15]. Therefore, the present study primarily aimed to determine the frequency of posttransplant diabetes mellitus, along with the potential risk factors for its development, followed by a comparison of its clinical outcomes with those of non-PTDM patients in the Pakistani population in a tertiary center in Karachi.

Methodology

Study design

This was a retrospective cohort observational study of all adult kidney transplant recipients from the Renal Transplant Unit at Dow University Hospital, Dow University of Health Sciences, Ojha Campus, Karachi, conducted between January 2018 and December 2021 using a purposeful sampling technique. While this may introduce selection bias by excluding incomplete records, the approach ensured data accuracy for PTDM and clinical outcomes. The study was reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) Statement guidelines.

Sample size

The sample size was calculated through open epi, keeping the frequency of PTDM at approximately 27.2%, the CI at 95%, and the margin of error at 7%. The sample size was calculated to be 155 [16]. Despite a calculated sample size of 155, only 121 participants out of 492 transplant recipients met the inclusion criteria within the study timeframe, leading to a slight reduction in statistical power.

Primary and secondary outcome

The primary outcome was to determine the frequency of PTDM and to identify associated risk factors, such as male sex, age, smoking status, obesity, family history of diabetes, pretransplant impaired fasting (IFG), impaired glucose tolerance (IGT), Hepatitis C virus (HCV) status, and deceased transplant. The secondary outcome was to compare posttransplant complications, including infections, graft function, rejection, and mortality, between PTDM and non-PTDM groups in the Pakistani population.

Participants

All kidney transplant recipients of either sex, aged 18 years or above after transplantation, between January 2018 and December 2021, were included in this research. The process of patient inclusion and exclusion is summarized in Fig. 1 (Fig. 1).

Participant eligibility criteria

Patients who were previously diagnosed with diabetes before transplantation and/or on insulin or oral hypoglycemic agents to control their increasing blood sugars, patients who were pregnant or breastfeeding at the time of transplantation/enrollment, patients with graft failure or those who did not survive after a month of transplantation or who had a history of any malignancy were excluded.

Definitions

PTDM was defined based on the criteria suggested in the International Consensus Guidelines [11]. Patients were classified as PTDM if they fulfilled diagnostic criteria at any time point within the first-year post-transplant (3, 6, or 12 months). A fasting plasma glucose level of ≥ 7 mmol/L (≥ 126 mg/dL) was detected on two different occasions 3 months after transplantation. A random plasma glucose level of ≥ 11.1 mmol/L (≥ 200 mg/dL) was checked on two different occasions [11] 3 months after transplantation. A positive oral glucose tolerance test with blood sugars was performed at baseline, i.e., after 8 h of fasting and 2 h after a 75-g/m oral glucose load, and the hexokinase method was performed via a spectrophotometer for further confirmation, which is the gold standard test. HbA1c >6.5% was not used as a diagnostic criterion in the first 3 months post-transplant due to potential alterations in hemoglobin turnover. However, it was assessed at 6 and 12 months [17]. Posttransplant patients who were previously nondiabetic started insulin or oral hypoglycemic agents to control their increasing blood sugars and remained on these medications 3 months after transplant. However, impaired fasting glucose (IFG) was defined as a fasting plasma glucose between 5.6 and 6.9 mmol/L (100–125 mg/dL) and/or impaired glucose tolerance was considered between 7.8 and 11 mmol/L (140–199 mg/dL). All plasma glucose measurements were performed using the hexokinase method on a spectrophotometer [11].

Infections were defined as clinically suspected and microbiologically confirmed episodes requiring antimicrobial therapy. These included urinary tract infections (positive urine culture ≥ 10⁵ CFU/ml with symptoms), respiratory tract infections (clinical plus radiological features with or without positive culture), wound infections, bacteremia/sepsis (positive blood culture with systemic features), and opportunistic infections such as CMV, BK virus, or fungal infections confirmed by serology, PCR, or culture [18].

Delayed graft function (DGF) was defined as the requirement of dialysis within the first 7 days post-transplant. Although patients were followed at 3, 6, and 12 months, DGF was recorded only if documented during the immediate post-transplant period; subsequent persistent dysfunction beyond 3 months was considered as graft dysfunction or failure rather than DGF [19].

Clinical and laboratory data

After approval was obtained from the institute’s ethical committee, all patients who met the inclusion criteria were enrolled in the study. Data were collected for age, sex, height, weight, and BMI. Although BMI ≥ 30 kg/m² is conventionally used to define obesity in Western populations, we applied a lower cutoff (≥ 25 kg/m²) based on WHO recommendations for South Asians, who are known to develop metabolic complications, including diabetes, at comparatively lower BMI levels [20]. This ethnic-specific threshold provides a more accurate risk stratification in our population. Any history of diabetes already diagnosed before transplant, pregnancy, breastfeeding, or malignancy was noted as an exclusion criterion. Data on patient demographics, medical history, treatment information, and family history of diabetes were collected.

All the patients who were candidates for renal transplantation fasted, and random blood glucose levels and HbA1c levels were checked before transplantation. OGTTs were performed if blood glucose levels were higher than the normal range. Data were collected at baseline and at 3 months, 6 months, and 1-year posttransplant. At each visit, a brief history of any presenting complaint was obtained, and a routine clinical examination was performed.

All the patients who underwent blood investigations related to graft functions (including serum creatinine), urinary protein, fasting, and plasma glucose were checked posttransplant, and the results were documented in the patients’ hospital records. The patient with an FPG ≥ 126 mg/dL had a confirmatory test in which the FPG was measured on another day. Patients with two readings of FPG ≥ 126 mg/dL or two random blood sugars > 200 mg/dl were considered diabetic. A few patients also underwent OGTTs for further confirmation. After all, posttransplant patients were enrolled, they were further divided into two groups. (i) PTDM patients with fasting blood sugars > 126 mg/dl and/or random blood sugars > 200 mg/dl on two occasions that can be diagnosed at each follow-up visit after checking the FBS, RBS, and/or OGTT. (ii) Non-PTDM patients, who did not fulfill the diagnostic criteria for diabetes after transplantation and did not require any oral hypoglycemic medications or insulin for control of their blood sugars 3 months post transplantation. HbA1c was checked at 6 months and 1 year after transplantation, which also helped in the diagnosis of PTDM. Steroid and immunosuppressant doses were recorded at each visit. Induction therapy included a single intravenous dose of Solumedrol (500 mg), followed by either antithymocyte globulin (ATG) at 1.5 mg/kg per dose for 3–5 days or basiliximab administered as two doses of 20 mg each.

The standard maintenance immunosuppressive regimen consisted of tacrolimus, cyclosporin, mycophenolate mofetil (MMF), and corticosteroids. Patients were started on a low-dose steroid regimen (0.5 mg/kg/day), which was tapered at the end of the first month over the next two months and subsequently maintained at 5 mg of prednisolone daily.

Urinary protein and lipid profiles were monitored at each follow-up visit. Both groups were followed for 1 year and compared in terms of posttransplant infection, proteinuria, delayed graft function, rejection rate or graft failure, hospitalization, dyslipidemia, thromboembolic disease (such as pulmonary embolism and deep venous thrombosis), and death, etc.

Rejection management

The treatment approach depended on the type of rejection.

For T-cell mediated rejection, patients were treated with antithymocyte globulin (ATG) at a dose of 3–5 mg/kg, along with high-dose intravenous methylprednisolone (Solumedrol) at 7.5 mg/kg/day for 3 days.

For antibody-mediated rejection (ABMR), treatment included intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) at 2.5–5.0 g/day for 5–7 days, plasmapheresis sessions, and rituximab at a dose of 375 mg/m², administered as two doses one week apart.

In cases of mixed rejection (T-cell and antibody-mediated), management involved ATG (3–5 mg/kg), IVIG (5 g/day for 5–7 days), plasmapheresis (5–7 sessions), and rituximab (375 mg/m² in two doses one week apart).

The identity of each patient in this study was treated as confidential, and patients were identified by codes and MR numbers. The data were entered by their codes and were password protected.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was conducted using SPSS (version 24). Continuous variables were compared using Student’s t-test or Mann–Whitney U test as appropriate, and categorical variables using chi-square or Fisher’s exact test. Variables with clinical relevance or showing significance (p < 0.10) in univariate analysis were entered into a multivariate logistic regression model to identify independent predictors of PTDM. Collinearity among predictors was assessed using variance inflation factor (VIF), and highly collinear variables were excluded to avoid model instability. Adjusted odds ratios (aOR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI) were reported. Given the number of variables analyzed, results were interpreted with caution regarding multiple comparisons; however, no formal correction (e.g., Bonferroni) was applied due to the exploratory nature of the study.

Predominantly, there were males (84.3%), mostly with hypertension as the primary diagnosis (86%). Regarding risk factors, most of the participants did not smoke (97.5%), were not alcoholic (100%), or did not have coronary artery disease (CAD) (99.2%). The hepatitis C virus (HCV) was positive for only 16.5% of the patients, and all of them received treatment. Over half of the participants (53.7%) had 2 to 3 HLA-DR mismatches. Among the 33 patients with PTDM, 12 (36.3%) had more than four HLA mismatches, compared to 20 (22.7%) among the 88 non-PTDM patients. HLA-B27 mismatch was detected in 8% of the total participants, with a prevalence of 5% in the PTDM group and 3% in the non-PTDM group. Most donors were less than 50 years old (92.6%), and 96.7% were living. In terms of immunosuppressants, 75.2%, 24.8%, and 95% of the participants received tacrolimus, cyclosporin, and MMF, respectively (Table 1).

A total of 33 participants out of 121 developed PTDM. Compared with non-PTDM patients, PTDM patients were more likely to have deceased donors (6.1% vs. 2.3%) (OR 2.77, 95% CI 0.37–20.55; Fisher’s exact p = 0.299), indicating no statistically significant difference given the small number. Over half of the recipients (53.7%) had 2–3 HLA-DR mismatches. PTDM patients were more likely to have higher DR mismatches compared with no-PTDM patients (36.3% vs. 22.7%). Smoking was also more common in the PTDM group (9% vs. 0%) (Table 1).

At all follow-ups, PTDM participants had significantly higher FBS than non-PTDM (p < 0.001), rising from 133.97 ± 53.113 mg/dl at 3 months to 147.22 ± 75.549 mg/dl at 1 year. Creatinine levels showed no significant difference except at 6 months, where PTDM participants had slightly higher levels (p = 0.047). (Table 2).

Higher BMI was significantly associated with an increased risk of PTDM (aOR = 1.08, 95% CI: 1.01–1.16, p = 0.03). For every 1 kg/m² increase in BMI, the odds of developing PTDM increased by 8%. Smoking was a significant independent risk factor for PTDM (aOR = 4.50, 95% CI: 1.10–18.40, p = 0.04). Smokers had 4.5 times higher odds of developing PTDM compared to non-smokers. Receiving a kidney from a deceased donor was associated with a higher risk of PTDM (aOR = 2.80, 95% CI: 1.05–7.45, p = 0.04). Patients with deceased donors had 2.8 times higher odds of developing PTDM compared to those with living donors. In univariate analysis, > 4 HLA mismatches were observed more frequently among PTDM patients than non-PTDM patients (36.4% vs. 22.7%); however, this difference did not reach statistical significance (χ² p = 0.14, Fisher’s exact p = 0.19). In contrast, multivariate logistic regression revealed that having 2–3 HLA-DR mismatches was significantly associated with an increased risk of PTDM (aOR = 1.90, 95% CI: 1.10–3.30, p = 0.02). This indicates that DR mismatching, even at intermediate levels, may independently predispose to PTDM. Patients with 2–3 HLA mismatches had 1.9 times higher odds of developing PTDM compared to those with fewer mismatches. These factors were not significantly associated with the development of PTDM in the multivariate analysis (p > 0.05) (Table 3).

The results highlight that PTDM participants generally faced more adverse clinical outcomes than non-PTDM participants did, with higher rates of infection, delayed graft function, transplant rejection, and dyslipidemia across all follow-up periods. These findings were statistically significant for most conditions (P value < 0.05). The PTDM group exhibited higher infection rates than the non-PTDM group, with rates of 27.27%, 21.21%, and 12.12% compared to 3.41%, 5.68%, and 3.41% at the 3-month, 6-month, and 1-year follow-ups, respectively. Additionally, PTDM participants experienced delayed graft function at rates of 15.15%, 6.06%, and 9.09%, whereas the non-PTDM group had lower rates of 0%, 3.41%, and 2.27% over the same periods. Moreover, transplant rejection rates were higher among PTDM participants at 12.12% and 3.03%, while the non-PTDM group recorded a significantly lower rate of 1.14% at the 3-month, 6-month, and 1-year follow-ups. Although the rate of rejection in PTDM participants decreased over time, it was still greater than that in non-PTDM participants. Additionally, the PTDM group presented a greater prevalence of dyslipidemia (24.24%) at the 3-month follow-up than did the non-PTDM group (17.05%). At the 6- and 12-month follow-ups, PTDM participants still presented a higher percentage of dyslipidemia, with the gap narrowing over time. This consistent elevation of dyslipidemia in PTDM patients suggests a potential metabolic difference that may require closer management. Moreover, at all follow-up periods, proteinuria remained rare, with minimal differences between the two groups. There were no thrombotic cases in non-PTDM participants, but 3.03% in the PTDM group at the 3-month follow-up and 1.14% at the 1-year follow-up. Nevertheless, hospitalization was more common in PTDM participants (12.12%) than in non-PTDM participants (10.23%) at all follow-up periods (Table 4).

Discussion

In this study, out of 492 transplant recipients, 121 patients were included in the analysis, of which 33 (27.3%) developed post-transplant diabetes mellitus (PTDM). The FBS levels were significantly higher in the PTDM group than in the non-PTDM group (P value = 0.0001). The multivariate regression analysis identified BMI, smoking, deceased donor status, and HLA mismatches as independent risk factors for PTDM. These findings align with previous studies that have highlighted the role of obesity, smoking, and immunological factors in the development of PTDM [21, 22, 26].

In our study, we found that compared with non-PTDM patients, PTDM patients experienced significantly greater adverse outcomes across several clinical parameters at the 3-month, 6-month, and 1-year follow-up periods [22]. PTDM participants had significantly higher infection rates than non-PTDM participants across all follow-ups. At the 1-year follow-up, infection was still more common in the PTDM group (12.12%) than in the non-PTDM group (3.41%). This elevated infection risk among PTDM patients could be due to immunosuppressive therapy combined with hyperglycemia, which is known to impair immune function [23].

A study conducted in Pakistan reported increased infection rates in PTDM patients, like the findings in this study, further confirming that PTDM poses a significant risk to posttransplant outcomes in the Pakistani population [24]. Similarly, international studies have shown that PTDM significantly increases the risk of infections. A large study reported infection rates (P value < 0.01) in PTDM patients, which aligns with the current study results observed at 1 year. This suggests that the immunocompromised state, along with diabetes-related immune dysfunction, is a universal challenge for PTDM patients globally [25].

PTDM participants consistently presented higher rates of delayed graft function, with 9.09% at 1 year compared with only 2.27% in non-PTDM participants. Furthermore, the PTDM group had a notably higher rate of transplant rejection, particularly in the early follow-up stages (12.12% at 3 months compared with 1.14% in non-PTDM participants). Although the rejection rate in the PTDM group decreased by the 1-year mark, it was still higher than that in the non-PTDM group, indicating that PTDM might predispose patients to graft rejection. Studies have reported a 35% increased risk of graft failure in PTDM patients [26]. These findings suggest that PTDM might contribute to complications related to kidney graft recovery, potentially through metabolic dysregulation [27, 28].

Dyslipidemia was consistently greater in the PTDM group (18.18% at 1 year) than in the non-PTDM group (15.91%). These findings suggest a persistent metabolic challenge in PTDM patients, necessitating closer management of lipid levels to prevent cardiovascular complications [29]. Elevated rates of dyslipidemia in PTDM patients have also been reported in international studies [20–33]. Our study demonstrated that HLA mismatching plays a contributory role in the development of PTDM. While univariate analysis of >4 mismatches did not achieve statistical significance, multivariate regression highlighted that 2–3 HLA-DR mismatches were independently associated with a nearly two-fold higher risk of PTDM. This finding emphasizes the importance of immunological stress in transplant recipients: even moderate DR mismatching may necessitate more intensive immunosuppression, thereby increasing susceptibility to metabolic complications such as PTDM. These results align with previous studies linking HLA-DR mismatch to graft rejection and metabolic sequelae, underscoring its relevance in risk stratification [21, 22, 26, 27].

Thrombotic complications, such as deep vein thrombosis (DVT), pulmonary embolism (PE), and other thromboembolic events, are significant concerns in patients with PTDM. In our study, thrombotic events were observed in 3.03% of PTDM patients at the 3-month follow-up and 1.14% at the 1-year follow-up, while no thrombotic cases were reported in non-PTDM patients. This suggests that PTDM is associated with an increased risk of thrombotic complications, likely due to the prothrombotic effects of hyperglycemia, insulin resistance, and chronic inflammation. Thrombotic events were assessed as part of post-transplant outcomes given the pro-thrombotic milieu associated with kidney transplantation and the additional contribution of PTDM to endothelial dysfunction, platelet activation, and impaired fibrinolysis. The findings of our study are consistent with recent studies, such as Ünlütürk et al. [33] and Lin et al. [26]. , which have also reported a higher incidence of thrombotic events in PTDM patients. Tight glycemic control, prophylactic anticoagulation, and lifestyle modifications may help reduce the risk of thrombosis in this population [26].

The study results highlighted that PTDM is a significant risk factor for adverse clinical outcomes in transplant patients, including infection, delayed graft function, transplant rejection, and dyslipidemia. These findings align with both national and international studies, underscoring the need for careful monitoring and management of PTDM patients to improve long-term transplant outcomes [23–33]. The relatively small sample size, especially in the PTDM group (n = 33), might limit the generalizability of the findings. However, the results provide valuable insights into the management of PTDM in the Pakistani population. A larger cohort would provide more robust conclusions. Furthermore, purposeful sampling was used to ensure complete follow-up and accurate outcome ascertainment; however, this approach may introduce selection bias and reduce representativeness. Future studies should employ consecutive or multicenter recruitment with larger sample sizes to overcome these limitations and enhance external validity. As this study was conducted at a single institution, the results might not be representative of other transplant centers with different protocols, patient demographics, or healthcare resources. The study’s follow-up period of 1 year might not capture the long-term effects of PTDM, such as chronic graft dysfunction or cardiovascular events, which could emerge over time. Overall, addressing these limitations and expanding research in these areas could significantly improve the management and prognosis of PTDM in transplant recipients.

Future studies should extend the follow-up period beyond 1 year to assess the long-term impact of PTDM on graft survival, cardiovascular health, and patient quality of life. Conducting multicenter studies across different geographic regions would improve the generalizability of the findings and help identify variations in PTDM incidence and outcomes on the basis of healthcare practices and patient demographics. Future research should explore targeted interventions for preventing and managing PTDM, such as optimizing immunosuppressive regimens, lifestyle modifications, and early detection protocols, to reduce complications and improve outcomes.

Conclusion

In this single-center retrospective cohort, PTDM was associated with higher risks of early post-transplant complications, including infection, rejection, and dyslipidemia. Key risk factors, including hypertension, HLA mismatches, and immunosuppressive therapy, highlight the need for vigilant risk assessment and targeted interventions. While our study was not powered to assess survival outcomes, these findings underscore the importance of early detection and proactive management of PTDM to mitigate short-term morbidity. Larger, multicenter studies with extended follow-up are needed to evaluate the long-term impact on graft and patient survival.
