Electroacupuncture Attenuates Cerebral Ischemia–Reperfusion Injury via Vagus Nerve–α7 Nicotinic Acetylcholine Receptor–Dependent Microglial Polarization
Authors: Jing‐Yuan Niu, Xin Luo, Xian‐Dong Li, Jian Zhang, Qiong Wu, Yi‐Feng Pei, Jia‐Qi Wang, Qian‐Kun Zhao, Yue Wang, Gong Zhang, Hui‐Sheng Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundNeuroinflammation is a central pathological process in cerebral ischemia/reperfusion injury. Electroacupuncture has demonstrated neuroprotective potential in ischemic stroke; however, whether electroacupuncture engages a defined central cholinergic anti‐inflammatory reflex to regulate microglial phenotype remains unknown.MethodsA rat model of middle cerebral artery occlusion/reperfusion was employed. Low‐intensity electroacupuncture was applied to the acupoints Large Iintestine 10 and Stomach 36 immediately after reperfusion. Neurological deficits and structural damage were assessed using neurological behavioral scores, 2,3,5‐triphenyltetrazolium chloride staining, and hematoxylin and eosin staining. Western blot, immunofluorescence, quantitative real‐time polymerase chain reaction, and ELISA were used to evaluate α7nAChR (α7 nicotinic acetylcholine receptor) expression, microglial phenotypic markers, and TLR4/NF‐κB (toll‐like receptor 4/nuclear factor kappa B) pathway activity. Functional validation was performed using the α7nAChR antagonist α‐BGT (α‐bungarotoxin) and subdiaphragmatic vagotomy.ResultsElectroacupuncture intervention significantly improved neurological function scores, reduced infarct volumes in middle cerebral artery occlusion/reperfusion rats. Electroacupuncture upregulated α7nAChR expression in the ischemic penumbra, primarily localized to microglia, and this effect was abolished by vagotomy. Eelectroacupuncture suppressed the TLR4/MyD88 (myeloid differentiation primary response 88)/NF‐κB signaling pathway in an α7nAChR‐dependent manner and shifted microglial polarization from a proinflammatory phenotype toward an anti‐inflammatory phenotype. These effects were reversed by α‐BGT and subdiaphragmatic vagotomy.ConclusionsThis study elucidates that low‐intensity electroacupuncture alleviates neuroinflammation following cerebral ischemia/reperfusion injury by activating the vagus nerve–α7nAChR axis, which negatively regulates the TLR4/NF‐κB pathway and drives microglial polarization toward an anti‐inflammatory phenotype. These findings identify the vagus nerve–α7nAChR axis as a critical neuroimmune pathway underlying electroacupuncture‐mediated neuroprotection and suggest that targeted neuromodulation of this axis may represent a promising adjunctive strategy for ischemic stroke therapy.Keywords: cerebral ischemia–reperfusion injury, electroacupuncture, microglial phenotype, vagus nerve, α7nAChRSubject Categories: Cerebrovascular Disease/Stroke

Research Perspective

What Is New?

This study provides the first systematic evidence that low‐intensity electroacupuncture alleviates cerebral ischemia–reperfusion injury by activating the vagus nerve–α7nAChR (α7 nicotinic acetylcholine receptor) axis, which suppresses the TLR4/NF‐κB (toll‐like receptor 4/nuclear factor kappa B) signaling pathway in an α7nAChR‐dependent manner and drives microglial polarization from a proinflammatory toward an anti‐inflammatory phenotype.

What Question Should Be Addressed Next?

Future studies should use microglia‐specific α7nAChR knockout models to clarify the direct role of this axis in microglia and explore the optimal therapeutic time window and potential sex differences of electroacupuncture to facilitate its clinical translation.

Ischemic stroke remains a leading cause of mortality and long‐term neurological disability worldwide, contributing to an increasingly heavy disease burden. 1 , 2 Although recanalization therapies, represented by intravenous thrombolysis and mechanical thrombectomy, constitute the most effective clinical strategies, the pathological process of cerebral ischemia/reperfusion injury (CIRI) significantly limits improvements in patient outcomes. 3 , 4 , 5 Epidemiological evidence indicates that >40% of patients receiving reperfusion therapy experience severe sequelae and long‐term disability, 6 highlighting the urgent need to develop effective adjuvant therapeutic strategies.

CIRI involves complex pathophysiological mechanisms, among which neuroinflammation is recognized as a critical link connecting the initial ischemic insult to secondary neuronal cell death. 7 , 8 The severity of the inflammatory response within the ischemic penumbra, the region surrounding the core infarct, directly determines the ultimate fate of neural cells. In this context, microglia, the primary immune effector cells of the central nervous system, play a central role. 9

These cells are rapidly activated following ischemia and can acquire functionally distinct phenotypes: classically activated proinflammatory microglia exacerbate tissue damage by releasing cytokines such as TNFα (tumor necrosis factor‐α) and IL1β (interleukin‐1β); whereas alternatively activated anti‐inflammatory‐ microglia promote repair by secreting factors like TGF‐β (transforming growth factor‐β) and IL‐10 (interleukin‐10), as well as neurotrophic factors. 10 , 11 The disruption of this dynamic balance often leads to a vicious cycle of inflammation. Therefore, modulating the functional phenotype of microglia is considered a highly promising therapeutic strategy. Among the various signaling pathways regulating proinflammatory microglial activation, the TLR4/NF‐κB (toll‐like receptor 4/nuclear factor‐kappa B) pathway plays a central role by recognizing endogenous danger signals and initiating inflammatory cascades. 12 , 13 Following cerebral ischemia, damage‐associated molecular patterns, such as HMGB1 (high‐mobility group box 1), activate TLR4, triggering the MyD88 (myeloid differentiation primary response 88)‐dependent signaling pathway, ultimately leading to NF‐κB nuclear translocation and the release of proinflammatory cytokines. Clinical evidence shows a positive correlation between TLR4 expression levels and the severity of neurological deficits in patients with ischemic stroke, 14 further underscoring the importance of this pathway in disease progression.

Concurrently, the body's innate cholinergic anti‐inflammatory pathway offers a unique neuroimmune perspective on inflammation regulation. The vagus nerve (VN) and its core effector receptor, the α7nAChR (α7 nicotinic acetylcholine receptor), have been proven to effectively suppress excessive inflammatory responses in various pathological conditions. 15 , 16 α7nAChR, a member of the ligand‐gated ion channel family, is highly enriched in the central nervous system and participates in neuroprotection by modulating neuronal excitability, inhibiting neuroinflammation, and maintaining synaptic plasticity. Studies have shown that α7nAChR agonists and VN stimulation can effectively prevent CIRI, whereas α7nAChR antagonists attenuate the protective effects of VN stimulation. 17 However, within the specific pathological context of ischemic stroke, key scientific questions remain requiring systematic experimental evidence: how to effectively activate this pathway noninvasively, and whether the anti‐inflammatory effects mediated by α7nAChR specifically involve the suppression of the TLR4/NF‐κB pathway to coordinate the phenotypic shift of microglia.

Electroacupuncture, a therapeutic method integrating traditional acupuncture theory with modern electrical stimulation technology, has garnered widespread attention in neurological disease research due to its standardized parameters and controllable operation. Particularly, stimulation of specific acupoints such as Zusanli (Stomach 36 [ST36]) can reflexively activate the VN, providing a unique technical approach to investigate the aforementioned questions. 18 , 19 , 20 Finding from Ma et al. (Neuron) reveal that low‐intensity electroacupuncture (0.5 mA, 10 Hz) at Large Intestine 10 and ST36 suppresses systemic inflammation by activating the VN, an effect observed under various physiological conditions. 19 The finding was further confirmed by our recent study that electroacupuncture at ST36 produced neuroprotection through activating vagal efferent pathways. 21 Collectively, these findings provide a novel perspective for the specific study of electroacupuncture's anti‐inflammatory mechanisms. In this context, we hypothesize that electroacupuncture may alleviate CIRI by activating the VN, enhancing α7nAChR function in the brain, subsequently interfering with the activation of the TLR4/NF‐κB signaling pathway, and ultimately promoting the polarization of microglia toward an anti‐inflammatory phenotype. This hypothesis integrates 2 crucial aspects including neural conduction and immune regulation, which would potentially offer new insights into understanding electroacupuncture's mechanism of action.

METHODS

Data Availability

Data are available upon reasonable request to the corresponding author.

Experimental Animals and Grouping

A total of 318 specific pathogen‐free male Sprague–Dawley (SD) rats (weighing 280–320 g, aged 8–9 weeks) were purchased from Beijing Huafukang Bioscience Co., Ltd. To avoid potential confounding effects of estrous cycle variations on ischemic outcomes and electroacupuncture responses, only male rats were used in this study. During the experiment, 53 rats were excluded due to modeling failure or death. Animals were housed under controlled environmental conditions with a temperature range of 19 °C to 23 °C, humidity of 50% to 70%, and a 12‐hour/12‐hour light/dark cycle. They were provided with free access to sterilized standard laboratory chow and purified water. The experimental protocol was approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of the General Hospital of the Northern Theater Command (Approval No. 2025‐16), and all procedures were strictly performed in accordance with the Guidelines for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals. Our experiment was conducted in 3 parts. Part I: We investigated the neuroprotective effect of electroacupuncture (0.5 mA, 10 Hz) in the middle cerebral artery occlusion/reperfusion (MCAO/R) model. A total of 108 male SD rats were randomly assigned to 3 groups (n=36 per group): Sham group, MCAO/R group, and MCAO/R+electroacupuncture group. Part II: We examined the protective mechanism of α7nAChR in cerebral ischemia. A total of 90 male SD rats were randomly divided into 5 groups (n=18 per group): Sham group, MCAO/R group, MCAO/R+electroacupuncture group, MCAO/R+electroacupuncture+α‐BGT (α‐bungarotoxin) group, and MCAO/R+electroacupuncture+vehicle group. The specific α7nAChR antagonist (α‐BGT) was administered via intracerebroventricular injection 0.5 hours before each electroacupuncture session. The vehicle control group received an injection of the solution without α‐BGT. Part III: This part focused on the potential role of the VN in mediating the neuroprotection induced by electroacupuncture. A total of 60 male SD rats were randomly allocated to 5 groups (n=12 per group): Sham group, MCAO/R group, MCAO/R+electroacupuncture group, MCAO/R + subdiaphragmatic vagotomy (SDV) group, and MCAO/R+SDV+electroacupuncture group. In the MCAO/R+SDV group, SDV was performed 7 days before the induction of MCAO/R. The MCAO/R+SDV+electroacupuncture group received electroacupuncture stimulation in addition to SDV and MCAO/R procedures.

Animal Model

The MCAO/R model in SD rats was established as previously described to induce transient focal cerebral ischemia. 22 Briefly, rats were anesthetized using isoflurane delivered via an animal anesthesia machine, and the analgesic carprofen (5 mg/kg) was administered subcutaneously. A midline neck incision was made to expose the right common carotid artery. A silicone‐coated nylon monofilament (model 2438A5, Beijing, China) was inserted into the right common carotid artery and gently advanced ∼18 mm to the origin of the right middle cerebral artery, thereby occluding blood flow. Reperfusion was initiated by withdrawing the filament after 2 hours of MCAO. Core body temperature was maintained at approximately 37 °C using a thermostatically controlled heating pad throughout the procedure.

Electroacupuncture Intervention

Electroacupuncture intervention was initiated immediately after successful establishment of the MCAO/R model. Unipolar stainless steel needles (0.25×25 mm) were inserted perpendicularly into the unilateral acupoints: Large Intestine 10 at a depth of 3.0±0.5 mm and ST36 at a depth of 7.0±0.5 mm, with an interneedle distance of 2.0 mm. The stimulation parameters were set as follows: a base frequency of 10 Hz, an intensity of 0.5 mA, administered once daily for 20 minutes. During electroacupuncture sessions, rats were anesthetized with isoflurane as described previously. Rats in the Sham and MCAO/R groups did not receive electroacupuncture stimulation but were subjected to the same duration of anesthesia. It is noteworthy that this focused electroacupuncture paradigm, characterized by the close proximity (2.0 mm) between the anode and cathode needles, fundamentally differs from traditional diffuse stimulation methods (eg, where current enters through one limb and exits via the contralateral limb), thereby ensuring precise acupoint targeting and localized current confinement.

Intracerebroventricular Injection

Under isoflurane anesthesia, the head of the SD rat was routinely disinfected. The scalp was incised to fully expose the skull area, and the rat was securely fixed in a stereotaxic apparatus. A location 1.5 mm lateral and 1.0 mm posterior to the bregma was marked. A micro‐drill was used to create a burr hole at the marked site. A microsyringe was used to draw up the α‐BGT solution at a dosage of 0.5 μg/kg. 23 The needle was inserted to a depth of 3.7 mm from the brain surface. The solution was infused over a period of 10 minutes. Following the injection, the needle was left in place for an additional 5 minutes before being slowly withdrawn.

Subdiaphragmatic Vagotomy

Under isoflurane anesthesia, rats were secured on a heating pad to maintain body temperature. An upper midline laparotomy was then performed. The stomach was gently retracted caudally to expose the esophagus, allowing for identification of the anterior and posterior vagal trunks between the diaphragm and the cardia of the stomach, which were subsequently transected. 24 In the sham‐operated group, the vagal trunks were exposed but neither ligated nor transected. The muscle and skin layers were sutured closed. Rats were allowed to recover for 7 days post SDV before being used in subsequent experimental procedures.

Neurological Behavioral Assessment

Neurological function was assessed using a comprehensive set of behavioral tests. To minimize interference from environmental stress, all animals were acclimated in a quiet environment for 1 hour before evaluation. Subsequently, neurological function was evaluated using 3 well‐validated scoring systems: the Zea Longa scale, 25 the modified Garcia scale, 26 and the Petullo score. 27 All assessors received uniform training and were assigned exclusively to a single scoring system throughout the study. The entire assessment procedure was executed under a strict double‐blind design, where both the operators performing the tests and the data analysts were blinded to the group allocations.

2,3,5‐Triphenyltetrazolium Chloride Staining

Cerebral infarct volume was assessed using 2,3,5‐triphenyltetrazolium chloride (TTC) staining. Following rapid extraction, brains were frozen at −20 °C for 20 minutes. Subsequently, each brain was sectioned coronally into 5 sequential slices, each 2 mm thick. The sections were incubated in a 2% TTC solution at 37 °C for 10 to 20 minutes, protected from light. After staining, the slices were rinsed with phosphate‐buffered saline and photographed. The stained sections were then analyzed quantitatively using ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, USA). The relative infarct volume, corrected for edema, was calculated according to the following formula: [(volume of left hemisphere−noninfarcted volume of right hemisphere)/volume of left hemisphere]×100%. All analyses were performed by investigators blinded to the group assignments.

Hematoxylin and Eosin Staining

Brain tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned into 4 μm thick slices. All samples were collected from the ischemic penumbra of the ipsilateral cortex. The sections were then subjected to hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining following standard protocols. Histopathological alterations in the ischemic penumbra were examined under a light microscope.

Quantitative Real‐Time Polymerase Chain Reaction

Total RNA was extracted from rat ischemic penumbra tissues using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, Massachusetts, USA). RNA concentration was measured with a spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific NanoDrop 2000, USA). Subsequently, RNA was reverse transcribed into cDNA using the HiScript III RT SuperMix for quantitative polymerase chain reaction (+ gDNA wiper) (Vazyme Biotech, China). Quantitative real‐time polymerase chain reaction was performed using SYBR Green Master Mix (Solarbio, China). Gene expression was analyzed using the 2‐ΔΔCt method, with all samples normalized to the mRNA levels of β‐actin. The primer sequences used were as follows: α7nAChR: forward 5′‐GCT CCT GCT ACA TTG ACG‐3′, reverse 5′‐TGG GTA TGG CTC TTT GC‐3′; β‐actin: forward 5′‐GGA GAT TAC TGC CCT GGC TCC TAG C‐3′, reverse 5′‐GGC CGG ACT CAT CGT ACT CCT GCT T‐3′.

Western Blot Analysis

Proteins were extracted from rat ischemic penumbra tissues using a lysis buffer supplemented with phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride. Protein concentration was quantified using a bicinchoninic acid protein assay kit. The extracted proteins were separated by SDS‐PAGE and subsequently transferred onto polyvinylidene fluoride membranes via electroblotting. The membranes were then blocked with 5% skim milk in Tris‐buffered saline containing 0.1% Tween 20 for 1 hour at room temperature to prevent nonspecific binding. Following blocking, the membranes were incubated with specific primary antibodies at 4 °C overnight, followed by incubation with a horseradish peroxidase‐conjugated secondary antibody (1:5000; Proteintech, China) for 1 hour at room temperature. Protein bands were visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescence detection system. The following primary antibodies were used: Arg‐1 (arginase 1; 1:30 000; Proteintech, #16001‐1‐AP), TGF‐β1 (transforming growth factor beta 1; 1:1000; Abclonal, #A18692), iNOS (inducible nitric oxide synthase; 1:1500; Affinity, #AF0199), TNF‐α (tumor necrosis factor alpha; 1:1500; Wanleibio, #WL01581), TLR4 (1:1000; Cell Signaling Technology, #14358T), MyD88 (1:500; Abclonal, #A0980), α7nAChR (1:500; Abclonal, #A1588), β‐tubulin (1:100000; Proteintech, #80713‐1‐RR), p‐IκBα (phospho‐IkappaB alpha; 1:800; Abclonal, #AP0999), IκBα (1:1000; Abclonal, #A11168), NF‐κB p65 (1:1000; Cell Signaling Technology, #8242S), histone (Proteintech, #17168‐1‐AP), and β‐actin (1:1000; Wanleibio, #WL01372).

Immunofluorescence Staining

Brain sections were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde. All sections were obtained from the ischemic penumbra or the medulla oblongata. Immunofluorescence staining was performed according to the kit manufacturer's instructions. Briefly, after deparaffinization, the brain sections were washed with phosphate‐buffered saline and covered with BSA. The sections were then incubated in a blocking solution for 1.5 hours, followed by incubation with primary antibodies for 24 hours. After washing, the sections were incubated with fluorescent dye‐conjugated secondary antibodies for 1.5 hours at room temperature. This was followed by incubation with the appropriate fluorescent dyes. The same procedure was applied to subsequent specimens. Finally, nuclei were counterstained. The following primary antibodies were used: anti‐α7nAChR (1:50; Abclonal, #A1588); anti‐NeuN (neuronal nuclei; 1:100; Abcam, #ab104224); anti‐Iba‐1 (ionized calcium binding adaptor molecule 1; 1:200; WAKO, #011–27 991); anti‐Arg‐1 (1:400; Proteintech, #16001‐1‐AP); anti‐iNOS (1:200; Affinity, #AF0199); anti‐TLR4 (1:100; Proteintech, #19811‐1‐AP); anti‐NF‐κB p65 (1:400; Cell Signaling Technology, #8242S); anti‐c‐Fos (1:100; MCE, #HY‐P80616); and anti‐ChAT (choline acetyltransferase; 1:50; Proteintech, #20747‐1‐AP). Fluorescence signals were visualized and captured using a fluorescence microscope.

Enzyme‐Linked Immunosorbent Assay

The ischemic penumbra tissues were collected and pulverized in liquid nitrogen. The homogenates were then lysed and centrifuged at 12 000g for 15 minutes at 4 °C. The resulting supernatant was filtered through a 0.22 μm membrane. The levels of CD206, CD86, IL‐1β, and IL‐10 were determined using specific commercial ELISA kits (Andy HuaTai, BeiJing, China) according to the manufacturers' instructions. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader.

Statistical Analysis

Data were analyzed using R software (version 4.3.0) and GraphPad Prism 9.0.0. To avoid the potential biases associated with preliminary normality testing, permutation tests were used for group comparisons. For 2‐group comparisons, permutation‐based 2‐sample t tests were applied. For multi‐roup comparisons, permutation‐based 1‐way ANOVA was performed, followed by permutation‐based pairwise comparisons with Bonferroni correction. These analyses were implemented using the coin package in R. For experiments with 2 independent variables, 2‐way ANOVA with Bonferroni's multiple comparisons test was used. A P value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

RESULTS

Effect of Electroacupuncture Stimulation on Neurological Recovery in MCAO/R Rats

We first investigated the effects of electroacupuncture stimulation administered after reperfusion on both short‐ and long‐term neurological function in MCAO/R rats. Neurological scores, infarct volume, and cerebral histology (via H&E staining) were assessed at 1, 3, and 7 days post MCAO (Figure 1A). Neurological scoring revealed no deficits in sham‐operated rats. At 1 day post MCAO, no significant differences were observed in the Zea Longa score, modified Garcia score, or Petullo score between the MCAO/R and MCAO/R + electroacupuncture groups. However, at 3 and 7 days post‐MCAO, all 3 neurological scores were significantly improved in the MCAO/R + electroacupuncture group compared with the MCAO/R group, with the most pronounced improvement noted at 3 days (Figure 1B through 1D). TTC staining showed no infarct areas in the sham group at any time point. Infarct volume was significantly reduced in the MCAO/R+electroacupuncture group compared with the MCAO/R group at 1, 3, and 7 days after MCAO (Figure 1E and 1F). Furthermore, H&E staining was performed to evaluate the neuroprotective effects of electroacupuncture from a histopathological perspective. H&E staining revealed normal cortical histology in the sham group. In contrast, the MCAO/R group displayed disordered neuronal arrangement, significant neuronal loss, and vacuolar degeneration, which was obviously ameliorated in the MCAO/R+electroacupuncture group (Figure 1G).

Electroacupuncture Stimulation Alleviates Injury in MCAO/R Rats by Upregulating α7nAChR

The expression of α7nAChR was evaluated at 1, 3, and 7 days after MCAO in the ischemic penumbra. Our results showed that the mRNA and protein expression levels of α7nAChR were decreased in the MCAO/R group compared with the Sham group. In contrast, α7nAChR expression was gradually restored in the MCAO/R+electroacupuncture group compared with the MCAO/R group, with a significant recovery beginning at day 3 (Figure 2A through 2C). Based on these findings combined with our previous results, we selected day 3 post MCAO as the time point for subsequent investigations. To further explore the cellular localization of α7nAChR within the central nervous system, we performed immunofluorescence double‐labeling on penumbral sections. Compared with the Sham group, the MCAO/R group showed significantly decreased immunofluorescence intensity for α7nAChR colocalized with the microglial marker Iba‐1 and the neuronal marker NeuN. Notably, compared with the MCAO/R group, the MCAO/R + electroacupuncture group exhibited a significant increase in the colocalization fluorescence intensity of α7nAChR with Iba‐1, whereas the colocalization intensity with NeuN showed no significant change (Figure 2D through 2G). To further confirm the critical role of α7nAChR in electroacupuncture‐mediated neuroprotection, we administered the specific α7nAChR antagonist α‐BGT via intracerebroventricular injection. Neurological scoring and TTC staining revealed that, compared with the MCAO/R+electroacupuncture+vehicle group, the MCAO/R+electroacupuncture+α‐BGT group exhibited aggravated neurological deficits, as evidenced by worse modified Garcia and Petullo scores (Figure 2H, 2I), and increased infarct volume (Figure 2J, 2K). These results indicate that antagonism of α7nAChR interferes with the neuroprotective effects of electroacupuncture against MCAO/R injury.

Electroacupuncture Stimulation Modulates Microglial Phenotypic Polarization via α7nAChR

To investigate whether electroacupuncture regulates microglial phenotypic balance via α7nAChR, we assessed the expression of both anti‐inflammatory and proinflammatory markers in the ischemic penumbra at 3 days after MCAO using Western blot, immunofluorescence, and ELISA. As shown in Figure 3, Western blot analysis revealed that the protein levels of anti‐inflammatory markers (Arg‐1 and TGF‐β1) were significantly increased in the MCAO/R+electroacupuncture group compared with the MCAO/R group (Figure 3A through 3C), whereas proinflammatory markers (iNOS and TNF‐α) were significantly decreased (Figure 3D through 3F). Furthermore, the electroacupuncture‐induced upregulation of anti‐inflammatory markers and downregulation of proinflammatory markers were both reversed by intracerebroventricular administration of the α7nAChR antagonist α‐BGT (MCAO/R+electroacupuncture+α‐BGT versus MCAO/R+electroacupuncture+vehicle, Figure 3G through 3L). Immunofluorescence results were consistent with the Western blot findings, showing that the colocalization fluorescence intensity of Arg‐1 and iNOS with the microglial marker Iba‐1 followed similar patterns across the experimental groups (Figure 3M and 3N). Additionally, ELISA measurements demonstrated that electroacupuncture treatment elevated the levels of anti‐inflammatory markers CD206 and IL‐10 while reducing proinflammatory markers CD86 and IL‐1β, and these effects were attenuated by α7nAChR blockade (Figure 3O–3R).

Electroacupuncture Stimulation Modulates the TLR4/NF‐κB Pathway Following Cerebral Ischemia/Reperfusion Injury via α7nAChR

Given that α7nAChR is a pivotal component of the cholinergic anti‐inflammatory pathway and the TLR4/NF‐κB pathway plays a critical role in inflammatory regulation, we postulated that α7nAChR might mediate the regulatory effect of electroacupuncture on the TLR4/NF‐κB pathway. The expression of proteins associated with the TLR4/NF‐κB pathway was measured by Western blot in ischemic penumbra tissues. Western blot analysis revealed that the protein levels of total TLR4, MyD88, the p‐IκBα/IκBα ratio, and nuclear NF‐κB p65 were significantly increased in the MCAO/R group compared with the Sham group. Electroacupuncture intervention markedly reduced the levels of these proteins. However, the suppressive effects of electroacupuncture on the TLR4/NF‐κB pathway were significantly attenuated by administration of the specific α7nAChR antagonist α‐BGT (Figure 4A through 4E), indicating that α7nAChR contributes to the neuroprotective effects of electroacupuncture by modulating this pathway. Furthermore, immunofluorescence staining for TLR4 and NF‐κB p65 in penumbral sections yielded results consistent with the Western blot data (Figure 4F and 4G).

Electroacupuncture Activates the Vagus Nerve to Upregulate α7nAChR and Alleviate Cerebral Ischemia/Reperfusion Injury

To determine whether the protective effect of electroacupuncture against brain injury in MCAO/R rats is mediated by the VN, we examined the expression of c‐Fos in ChAT‐positive neurons within the dorsal motor nucleus of the VN (DMV) at day 3 after MCAO. Immunofluorescence results demonstrated that the expression of c‐Fos in ChAT+ neurons in the DMV was significantly increased in the MCAO/R+electroacupuncture group compared with the MCAO/R group (Figure 5A and 5B), indicating activation of vagal efferent signaling during electroacupuncture intervention. To further investigate how the VN mediates the protective effects of electroacupuncture, SDV was performed 7 days before MCAO modeling. Western blot analysis revealed that α7nAChR protein expression was decreased in the MCAO/R + SDV group compared with the MCAO/R group, indicating that SDV suppressed the postischemic expression of α7nAChR. Furthermore, α7nAChR protein levels were significantly lower in the MCAO/R+SDV+electroacupuncture group compared with the MCAO/R + electroacupuncture group (Figure 5C and 5D), demonstrating that the upregulation of α7nAChR by electroacupuncture is mediated by the subdiaphragmatic VN. Neurological scoring showed that compared with the MCAO/R group, the MCAO/R+SDV group exhibited increased Petullo scores and decreased modified Garcia scores. Conversely, compared with the MCAO/R + electroacupuncture group, the MCAO/R+SDV+electroacupuncture group showed increased Petullo scores and decreased modified Garcia scores (Figure 5E and 5F). TTC staining revealed that infarct volume was increased in the MCAO/R+SDV group compared with the MCAO/R group, and was also increased in the MCAO/R+SDV+electroacupuncture group compared with the MCAO/R + electroacupuncture group (Figure 5G and 5H). These findings indicate that subdiaphragmatic vagotomy aggravates cerebral injury in MCAO/R rats and abolishes the neuroprotective effects of electroacupuncture.

Electroacupuncture Stimulation Modulates Microglial Phenotypic Polarization and Suppresses Neuroinflammation via the Vagus Nerve

To investigate whether electroacupuncture modulates microglial phenotypic polarization through the subdiaphragmatic VN, we assessed the expression of microglial markers in the ischemic penumbra using Western blot and immunofluorescence. Western blot analysis demonstrated that compared with the MCAO/R+electroacupuncture group, the protein levels of the anti‐inflammatory markers Arg‐1 and TGF‐β1 were significantly decreased in the MCAO/R+SDV+electroacupuncture group (Figure 6A‐6C). Conversely, the expression levels of the proinflammatory markers iNOS and TNF‐α were significantly increased in the MCAO/R+SDV group compared with the MCAO/R group. Furthermore, the MCAO/R+SDV+electroacupuncture group exhibited elevated iNOS and TNF‐α levels compared with the MCAO/R+electroacupuncture group (Figure 6D through 6F). Immunofluorescence staining confirmed these findings, showing consistent patterns in the colocalization intensity of Arg‐1 and iNOS with the microglial marker Iba‐1 across the experimental groups (Figure 6G and 6H). Collectively, these results indicate that the subdiaphragmatic VN mediates the electroacupuncture‐induced shift in microglial polarization from the proinflammatory phenotype toward the anti‐inflammatory phenotype.

The Subdiaphragmatic Vagus Nerve Mediates the Regulatory Effect of Electroacupuncture on the TLR4/MyD88 Pathway

To assess whether the subdiaphragmatic VN influences the regulatory effect of electroacupuncture on the TLR4/NF‐κB pathway, we evaluated the expression of proteins associated with the TLR4/MyD88 pathway using Western blot and immunofluorescence on penumbral tissues. Western blot analysis revealed that compared with the MCAO/R group, the MCAO/R+SDV group exhibited increased protein levels of total TLR4, MyD88, the p‐IκBα/IκBα ratio, and nuclear NF‐κB p65. Furthermore, compared with the MCAO/R+electroacupuncture group, the MCAO/R+SDV+electroacupuncture group showed significantly higher levels of TLR4, MyD88, the p‐IκBα/IκBα ratio, and nuclear NF‐κB p65 (Figure 7A through 7E). Consistent with these findings, immunofluorescence staining for TLR4 and NF‐κB p65 yielded results that aligned with the Western blot data (Figure 7F and 7G). Collectively, these results demonstrate that the subdiaphragmatic VN mediates the suppressive effect of electroacupuncture on the TLR4/MyD88/NF‐κB signaling pathway.

DISCUSSION

Through a series of experiments, this study aimed to elucidate the neuroprotective mechanism of electroacupuncture in CIRI. The principal findings indicate that low‐intensity electroacupuncture activates the VN–α7nAChR axis, thereby suppressing the downstream TLR4/NF‐κB signaling pathway. This suppression promotes a shift in microglial polarization toward an anti‐inflammatory phenotype, ultimately mediating anti‐inflammatory and neuroprotective effects. This comprehensive signaling pathway elucidates a novel mechanism through which electroacupuncture improves stroke outcomes via neuroimmune regulation, providing valuable insights for the development of nonpharmacological interventions in stroke treatment.

Our findings align with previous studies, 28 , 29 , 30 which have demonstrated that electroacupuncture significantly enhances neurological function scores and reduces infarct volume. From a temporal dynamic perspective, the neuroprotective effect was most evident on day 3 after reperfusion—a time point that coincides with a marked rebound in α7nAChR expression following electroacupuncture intervention. This synchrony between functional recovery and key molecular changes carries important physiological implications. Day 3 post reperfusion generally represents the peak of the inflammatory response, characterized by maximal infiltration and activation of inflammatory cells, along with the most pronounced changes in blood–brain barrier permeability. 31 The fact that electroacupuncture exerts optimal efficacy at this critical phase underscores its potential to intervene in central pathological processes during the inflammatory peak.

In this study, α7nAChR, a key molecule in the cholinergic anti‐inflammatory pathway, was downregulated after MCAO/R, which was effectively reversed by electroacupuncture intervention. From a pathophysiological aspect, downregulation of α7nAChR may reflect compensatory suppression of cholinergic anti‐inflammatory function during CIRI. Under cerebral ischemic stress, disrupted neurotransmitter release and altered membrane stability might lead to this receptor downregulation. 32 Electroacupuncture potentially restores this endogenous protective mechanism, thereby reestablishing neuroimmune homeostasis. Crucially, pharmacological blockade of α7nAChR with the specific antagonist α‐BGT significantly attenuated the neuroprotective effects of electroacupuncture. This evidence further confirms that α7nAChR is necessary for electroacupuncture's protective effects. Furthermore, immunofluorescence double‐labeling indicated a more prominent increase in α7nAChR expression within microglia, suggesting microglia as a primary cellular target for electroacupuncture. This finding aligns with the established recognition of α7nAChR as an important therapeutic target in neurological diseases. 15 , 33

Microglial phenotypic switching plays a central role in poststroke neuroinflammation. 34 , 35 Our data indicate that electroacupuncture significantly elevated anti‐inflammatory marker levels (Arg‐1, TGF‐β1, IL‐10, CD206) while reducing proinflammatory marker levels (iNOS, TNF‐α, IL‐1β, CD86). This bidirectional regulation suggests that electroacupuncture not only suppresses detrimental inflammation but also promotes beneficial repair processes. Functionally, anti‐inflammatory microglia, by secreting neurotrophic factors and anti‐inflammatory cytokines, may facilitate synaptic remodeling and neurogenesis, potentially constituting a key mechanism for electroacupuncture‐induced functional improvement. This beneficial polarization shift was abolished upon α7nAChR antagonism, clearly indicating that electroacupuncture modulates microglial phenotypic balance through the α7nAChR signaling pathway. This finding resonates with the antineuroinflammatory mechanisms of electroacupuncture reported in previous studies 36 and precisely identifies α7nAChR as the specific molecular mediator. We acknowledge that microglial activation exists along a broad spectrum of functional states, and the binary classification into “proinflammatory” and “anti‐inflammatory” phenotypes represents an oversimplification of the complex in vivo reality. Our use of selected markers provides a functional snapshot, but comprehensive transcriptomic or proteomic profiling would be required to fully capture the diversity of microglial responses after stroke. It is noteworthy that this regulatory effect might not be confined to the local brain tissue but could also indirectly influence the central nervous system's inflammatory milieu by modulating systemic immune status.

An important consideration is that the markers we employed (iNOS, Arg‐1, CD86, CD206, etc.) are not exclusively expressed by microglia but are also found on infiltrating peripheral myeloid cells. the protective effects of electroacupuncture may involve both resident microglia and peripheral immune cells. Vagal nerve stimulation is known to suppress splenic release of proinflammatory monocytes via the cholinergic anti‐inflammatory pathway. It is plausible that electroacupuncture, by activating the VN, similarly limits the egress of peripheral myeloid cells from the spleen, thereby reducing their infiltration into the brain and skewing their polarization toward a reparative phenotype. This hypothesis is supported by recent reports in other neuroinflammatory models: a comprehensive review highlighted that VN stimulation can modulate microglial function and exert protective effects in neurodevelopmental disorders 37 ; moreover, a direct experimental study demonstrated that transcutaneous VN stimulation improves microglial morphology and phenotype and reduces neuroinflammation in a mouse model of delirium superimposed on dementia. 38 Together with our results, these observations suggest that VN‐mediated microglial regulation represents a conserved neuroprotective mechanism across diverse neurological conditions, reinforcing the critical role of the vagus‐α7nAChR axis in ischemic stroke. Future studies using lineage tracing or microglia‐specific depletion are needed to dissect the relative contributions of resident versus infiltrating myeloid cells to electroacupuncture‐induced neuroprotection.

The TLR4/NF‐κB pathway is a crucial signaling cascade driving proinflammatory microglial activation. 39 We found that the suppressive effects of electroacupuncture on TLR4, MyD88, and nuclear NF‐κB p65 levels were significantly weakened after α7nAChR blockade. Mechanistically, α7nAChR might negatively regulate this pathway by interfering with TLR4 signalosome formation or promoting the recruitment of inhibitory signals. Particularly, as a ligand‐gated ion channel, α7nAChR activation‐induced calcium influx might influence downstream pathways via calcium‐dependent signaling molecules. 40 , 41 This discovery links the cholinergic anti‐inflammatory signaling with the canonical immune pathway, providing a novel molecular basis for understanding the multitarget regulatory properties of electroacupuncture. Compared with previous studies on electroacupuncture inhibiting the TLR4 pathway, 42 , 43 our research further clarifies the causal relationship between this effect and α7nAChR activation, representing a significant advancement in mechanistic understanding.

A key finding of this study is the identification of the VN as the initiating component of the entire signaling axis. We confirmed electroacupuncture‐induced activation of DMV via c‐Fos expression, indicating that acupoint stimulation indeed elicits a functional response in central vagal nuclei. Neuroanatomically, stimulation signals from Large Intestine 10 and ST36 might travel via the spinal cord‐nucleus tractus solitarius‐DMV pathway, ultimately activating vagal efferent fibers. 18 More important, decisive evidence was obtained through SDV: vagal transection not only blocked the electroacupuncture‐induced upregulation of α7nAChR but also reversed the suppressive effects of electroacupuncture on the TLR4/NF‐κB pathway, the regulation of microglial polarization, and the ultimate neuroprotective outcomes. Collectively, our evidence delineates a sequential signaling cascade, initiated by VN activation and propagated through α7nAChR, which subsequently inhibits the TLR4/NF‐κB pathway to drive microglial polarization.

This finding is mechanistically consistent with observations in other systems, 20 suggesting that VN activation might be a universal anti‐inflammatory mechanism across organs. Notably, VN activation might influence cerebral inflammation not only through direct neural connections but also indirectly by modulating the function of peripheral immune organs as well as the brain–gut axis, 21 thereby altering the inflammatory environment of the central nervous system.

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, although we provide evidence that electroacupuncture activates DMV neurons and that subdiaphragmatic vagotomy abolishes the neuroprotective effects, direct electrophysiological recordings of vagal efferent activity during electroacupuncture would provide more definitive proof. Second, although we have included essential controls, certain procedures (eg, a sham SDV group with VN exposure only, without transection) did not receive complete sham controls due to animal ethics and experimental feasibility constraints. Future studies will incorporate full sham controls for all interventions. Third, the sample size for Western blot and immunofluorescence experiments was relatively small (n=3 per group). Although the results were consistent and statistically significant, larger cohorts would increase statistical power. Fourth, only male rats were used to avoid confounding effects of estrous cycle; however, sex is an important biological variable. Future studies should include both sexes to evaluate potential sex‐dependent differences in electroacupuncture efficacy. Fifth, our behavioral assessments focused primarily on sensorimotor function. Whether electroacupuncture also improves cognitive deficits after stroke remains to be determined. Sixth, we did not distinguish between resident microglia and infiltrating macrophages; therefore, the precise cellular target of the vagus–α7nAChR axis within the myeloid compartment requires further investigation using cell‐type‐specific manipulations (eg, conditional knockout of α7nAChR in microglia). Seventh, the electroacupuncture protocol was initiated immediately after reperfusion, which is clinically challenging. Although this study employed an immediate electroacupuncture protocol to establish a mechanistic proof of concept, future studies should systematically investigate the therapeutic time window of electroacupuncture. Finally, although immunofluorescence confirmed notable α7nAChR expression in microglia, the intervention targeting this receptor was not cell‐type‐specific. Definitive evidence for its microglia‐specific role requires models with conditional knockout of α7nAChR in microglia. Addressing these limitations will significantly enhance the clinical translatability and mechanistic depth of the findings.

CONCLUSIONS

This study demonstrates that low‐intensity electroacupuncture activates the VN–α7nAChR axis, suppresses TLR4/NF‐κB signaling, and shifts microglial polarization toward an anti‐inflammatory phenotype, thereby alleviating CIRI. These findings identify the VN–α7nAChR axis as a critical neuroimmune pathway underlying electroacupuncture‐mediated neuroprotection and suggest that targeted neuromodulation of this axis may represent a promising adjunctive strategy for ischemic stroke therapy.

Experimental Animals and Grouping

A total of 318 specific pathogen‐free male Sprague–Dawley (SD) rats (weighing 280–320 g, aged 8–9 weeks) were purchased from Beijing Huafukang Bioscience Co., Ltd. To avoid potential confounding effects of estrous cycle variations on ischemic outcomes and electroacupuncture responses, only male rats were used in this study. During the experiment, 53 rats were excluded due to modeling failure or death. Animals were housed under controlled environmental conditions with a temperature range of 19 °C to 23 °C, humidity of 50% to 70%, and a 12‐hour/12‐hour light/dark cycle. They were provided with free access to sterilized standard laboratory chow and purified water. The experimental protocol was approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of the General Hospital of the Northern Theater Command (Approval No. 2025‐16), and all procedures were strictly performed in accordance with the Guidelines for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals. Our experiment was conducted in 3 parts. Part I: We investigated the neuroprotective effect of electroacupuncture (0.5 mA, 10 Hz) in the middle cerebral artery occlusion/reperfusion (MCAO/R) model. A total of 108 male SD rats were randomly assigned to 3 groups (n=36 per group): Sham group, MCAO/R group, and MCAO/R+electroacupuncture group. Part II: We examined the protective mechanism of α7nAChR in cerebral ischemia. A total of 90 male SD rats were randomly divided into 5 groups (n=18 per group): Sham group, MCAO/R group, MCAO/R+electroacupuncture group, MCAO/R+electroacupuncture+α‐BGT (α‐bungarotoxin) group, and MCAO/R+electroacupuncture+vehicle group. The specific α7nAChR antagonist (α‐BGT) was administered via intracerebroventricular injection 0.5 hours before each electroacupuncture session. The vehicle control group received an injection of the solution without α‐BGT. Part III: This part focused on the potential role of the VN in mediating the neuroprotection induced by electroacupuncture. A total of 60 male SD rats were randomly allocated to 5 groups (n=12 per group): Sham group, MCAO/R group, MCAO/R+electroacupuncture group, MCAO/R + subdiaphragmatic vagotomy (SDV) group, and MCAO/R+SDV+electroacupuncture group. In the MCAO/R+SDV group, SDV was performed 7 days before the induction of MCAO/R. The MCAO/R+SDV+electroacupuncture group received electroacupuncture stimulation in addition to SDV and MCAO/R procedures.

Animal Model

The MCAO/R model in SD rats was established as previously described to induce transient focal cerebral ischemia. 22 Briefly, rats were anesthetized using isoflurane delivered via an animal anesthesia machine, and the analgesic carprofen (5 mg/kg) was administered subcutaneously. A midline neck incision was made to expose the right common carotid artery. A silicone‐coated nylon monofilament (model 2438A5, Beijing, China) was inserted into the right common carotid artery and gently advanced ∼18 mm to the origin of the right middle cerebral artery, thereby occluding blood flow. Reperfusion was initiated by withdrawing the filament after 2 hours of MCAO. Core body temperature was maintained at approximately 37 °C using a thermostatically controlled heating pad throughout the procedure.

Electroacupuncture Intervention

Electroacupuncture intervention was initiated immediately after successful establishment of the MCAO/R model. Unipolar stainless steel needles (0.25×25 mm) were inserted perpendicularly into the unilateral acupoints: Large Intestine 10 at a depth of 3.0±0.5 mm and ST36 at a depth of 7.0±0.5 mm, with an interneedle distance of 2.0 mm. The stimulation parameters were set as follows: a base frequency of 10 Hz, an intensity of 0.5 mA, administered once daily for 20 minutes. During electroacupuncture sessions, rats were anesthetized with isoflurane as described previously. Rats in the Sham and MCAO/R groups did not receive electroacupuncture stimulation but were subjected to the same duration of anesthesia. It is noteworthy that this focused electroacupuncture paradigm, characterized by the close proximity (2.0 mm) between the anode and cathode needles, fundamentally differs from traditional diffuse stimulation methods (eg, where current enters through one limb and exits via the contralateral limb), thereby ensuring precise acupoint targeting and localized current confinement.

Intracerebroventricular Injection

Under isoflurane anesthesia, the head of the SD rat was routinely disinfected. The scalp was incised to fully expose the skull area, and the rat was securely fixed in a stereotaxic apparatus. A location 1.5 mm lateral and 1.0 mm posterior to the bregma was marked. A micro‐drill was used to create a burr hole at the marked site. A microsyringe was used to draw up the α‐BGT solution at a dosage of 0.5 μg/kg. 23 The needle was inserted to a depth of 3.7 mm from the brain surface. The solution was infused over a period of 10 minutes. Following the injection, the needle was left in place for an additional 5 minutes before being slowly withdrawn.

Subdiaphragmatic Vagotomy

Under isoflurane anesthesia, rats were secured on a heating pad to maintain body temperature. An upper midline laparotomy was then performed. The stomach was gently retracted caudally to expose the esophagus, allowing for identification of the anterior and posterior vagal trunks between the diaphragm and the cardia of the stomach, which were subsequently transected. 24 In the sham‐operated group, the vagal trunks were exposed but neither ligated nor transected. The muscle and skin layers were sutured closed. Rats were allowed to recover for 7 days post SDV before being used in subsequent experimental procedures.

Neurological Behavioral Assessment

Neurological function was assessed using a comprehensive set of behavioral tests. To minimize interference from environmental stress, all animals were acclimated in a quiet environment for 1 hour before evaluation. Subsequently, neurological function was evaluated using 3 well‐validated scoring systems: the Zea Longa scale, 25 the modified Garcia scale, 26 and the Petullo score. 27 All assessors received uniform training and were assigned exclusively to a single scoring system throughout the study. The entire assessment procedure was executed under a strict double‐blind design, where both the operators performing the tests and the data analysts were blinded to the group allocations.

2,3,5‐Triphenyltetrazolium Chloride Staining

Cerebral infarct volume was assessed using 2,3,5‐triphenyltetrazolium chloride (TTC) staining. Following rapid extraction, brains were frozen at −20 °C for 20 minutes. Subsequently, each brain was sectioned coronally into 5 sequential slices, each 2 mm thick. The sections were incubated in a 2% TTC solution at 37 °C for 10 to 20 minutes, protected from light. After staining, the slices were rinsed with phosphate‐buffered saline and photographed. The stained sections were then analyzed quantitatively using ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, USA). The relative infarct volume, corrected for edema, was calculated according to the following formula: [(volume of left hemisphere−noninfarcted volume of right hemisphere)/volume of left hemisphere]×100%. All analyses were performed by investigators blinded to the group assignments.

Hematoxylin and Eosin Staining

Brain tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned into 4 μm thick slices. All samples were collected from the ischemic penumbra of the ipsilateral cortex. The sections were then subjected to hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining following standard protocols. Histopathological alterations in the ischemic penumbra were examined under a light microscope.

Quantitative Real‐Time Polymerase Chain Reaction

Total RNA was extracted from rat ischemic penumbra tissues using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, Massachusetts, USA). RNA concentration was measured with a spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific NanoDrop 2000, USA). Subsequently, RNA was reverse transcribed into cDNA using the HiScript III RT SuperMix for quantitative polymerase chain reaction (+ gDNA wiper) (Vazyme Biotech, China). Quantitative real‐time polymerase chain reaction was performed using SYBR Green Master Mix (Solarbio, China). Gene expression was analyzed using the 2‐ΔΔCt method, with all samples normalized to the mRNA levels of β‐actin. The primer sequences used were as follows: α7nAChR: forward 5′‐GCT CCT GCT ACA TTG ACG‐3′, reverse 5′‐TGG GTA TGG CTC TTT GC‐3′; β‐actin: forward 5′‐GGA GAT TAC TGC CCT GGC TCC TAG C‐3′, reverse 5′‐GGC CGG ACT CAT CGT ACT CCT GCT T‐3′.

Western Blot Analysis

Proteins were extracted from rat ischemic penumbra tissues using a lysis buffer supplemented with phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride. Protein concentration was quantified using a bicinchoninic acid protein assay kit. The extracted proteins were separated by SDS‐PAGE and subsequently transferred onto polyvinylidene fluoride membranes via electroblotting. The membranes were then blocked with 5% skim milk in Tris‐buffered saline containing 0.1% Tween 20 for 1 hour at room temperature to prevent nonspecific binding. Following blocking, the membranes were incubated with specific primary antibodies at 4 °C overnight, followed by incubation with a horseradish peroxidase‐conjugated secondary antibody (1:5000; Proteintech, China) for 1 hour at room temperature. Protein bands were visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescence detection system. The following primary antibodies were used: Arg‐1 (arginase 1; 1:30 000; Proteintech, #16001‐1‐AP), TGF‐β1 (transforming growth factor beta 1; 1:1000; Abclonal, #A18692), iNOS (inducible nitric oxide synthase; 1:1500; Affinity, #AF0199), TNF‐α (tumor necrosis factor alpha; 1:1500; Wanleibio, #WL01581), TLR4 (1:1000; Cell Signaling Technology, #14358T), MyD88 (1:500; Abclonal, #A0980), α7nAChR (1:500; Abclonal, #A1588), β‐tubulin (1:100000; Proteintech, #80713‐1‐RR), p‐IκBα (phospho‐IkappaB alpha; 1:800; Abclonal, #AP0999), IκBα (1:1000; Abclonal, #A11168), NF‐κB p65 (1:1000; Cell Signaling Technology, #8242S), histone (Proteintech, #17168‐1‐AP), and β‐actin (1:1000; Wanleibio, #WL01372).

Immunofluorescence Staining

Brain sections were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde. All sections were obtained from the ischemic penumbra or the medulla oblongata. Immunofluorescence staining was performed according to the kit manufacturer's instructions. Briefly, after deparaffinization, the brain sections were washed with phosphate‐buffered saline and covered with BSA. The sections were then incubated in a blocking solution for 1.5 hours, followed by incubation with primary antibodies for 24 hours. After washing, the sections were incubated with fluorescent dye‐conjugated secondary antibodies for 1.5 hours at room temperature. This was followed by incubation with the appropriate fluorescent dyes. The same procedure was applied to subsequent specimens. Finally, nuclei were counterstained. The following primary antibodies were used: anti‐α7nAChR (1:50; Abclonal, #A1588); anti‐NeuN (neuronal nuclei; 1:100; Abcam, #ab104224); anti‐Iba‐1 (ionized calcium binding adaptor molecule 1; 1:200; WAKO, #011–27 991); anti‐Arg‐1 (1:400; Proteintech, #16001‐1‐AP); anti‐iNOS (1:200; Affinity, #AF0199); anti‐TLR4 (1:100; Proteintech, #19811‐1‐AP); anti‐NF‐κB p65 (1:400; Cell Signaling Technology, #8242S); anti‐c‐Fos (1:100; MCE, #HY‐P80616); and anti‐ChAT (choline acetyltransferase; 1:50; Proteintech, #20747‐1‐AP). Fluorescence signals were visualized and captured using a fluorescence microscope.

Enzyme‐Linked Immunosorbent Assay

The ischemic penumbra tissues were collected and pulverized in liquid nitrogen. The homogenates were then lysed and centrifuged at 12 000g for 15 minutes at 4 °C. The resulting supernatant was filtered through a 0.22 μm membrane. The levels of CD206, CD86, IL‐1β, and IL‐10 were determined using specific commercial ELISA kits (Andy HuaTai, BeiJing, China) according to the manufacturers' instructions. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader.

Statistical Analysis

Data were analyzed using R software (version 4.3.0) and GraphPad Prism 9.0.0. To avoid the potential biases associated with preliminary normality testing, permutation tests were used for group comparisons. For 2‐group comparisons, permutation‐based 2‐sample t tests were applied. For multi‐roup comparisons, permutation‐based 1‐way ANOVA was performed, followed by permutation‐based pairwise comparisons with Bonferroni correction. These analyses were implemented using the coin package in R. For experiments with 2 independent variables, 2‐way ANOVA with Bonferroni's multiple comparisons test was used. A P value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.
