Positioning palliative oncology in health care in Caribbean and Sub-Saharan African countries: Leveraging social and economic opportunities
Authors: Lindonne Telesford, Karl Theodore, Christine Edan, Kester Nedd, Tamara Green, Karim Assani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Each year, millions of people around the world experience serious health-related suffering from life-threatening and life-limiting illnesses and require palliative care. Palliative care is a human right to adequate and timely interventions for the well-being of patients with serious illnesses and their families. The World Health Assembly incorporated palliative care as a component of universal health care, reinforcing the World Health Organization's mandate to support governments in integrating the service into primary health care. Yet, fewer than half of the countries across the globe have realized this integration. Low- and middle-income countries lag in the provision of palliative care and pain relief for children and adults and have disproportionately higher mortality rates among people who experience serious health-related suffering. Our objective is to create awareness of barriers to positioning palliative oncology in healthcare and to propose affirmative measures to improve the services for children and adults in Caribbean and Sub-Saharan African countries. Four critical barriers influencing the positioning of palliative care in healthcare systems are presented: the exclusion of palliative care in universal health care schemes, restrictions to oncological end-of-life care, insufficient deliberate distinctions in services for children and adults, and limited access to medications and the absence of medication policies and regulations. We present three measures, with examples, of how to address barriers to integrating palliative oncology in healthcare systems: an approach to capacity building for pediatric palliative care in African countries, ensuring more equity in delivering primary care services; repositioning culturally sensitive psychosocial oncology care in Sub-Saharan African countries and the Caribbean, focusing on the habilitative opportunities that exist and bridging cost-effective efforts within health systems; and a matrix of domains and dimensions supports the construction of financial structures for the sustainable resilience of health care systems. Finally, we present recommendations for advocacy and to generate support for implementing the proposed measures to transform palliative services for children and adults. This paper supports the development of policies and interventions to close gaps in delivering healthcare to people affected by cancer. Further research may stem from this work, focusing on palliative care for critical conditions from other illnesses.

Background

The global burden of serious health-related suffering is high but could be significantly reduced with the introduction, integration, and expansion of palliative care and pain relief interventions. 1 For decades, palliative care was initially offered with a view of imminent death; however, the practice of palliative medicine has progressively transformed care delivery to punctuate chronic illness trajectories with rapid functional decline, such as advanced-stage cancers; intermittent decline, such as multimorbidity; and gradual decline, such as neurodegenerative disorders and frailty.2,3 Under the International Covenant on Economic, Social, and Cultural Rights Article 12(d), palliative care is underscored as a human right, requiring State Parties to ensure conditions that guarantee appropriate medical services in the event of sickness. 2 According to the World Health Organization (WHO), each year, approximately 56.8 million people around the world require palliative care. 4 The Lancet Commission on Global Access to Palliative Care and Pain Relief stated that over 80% of serious health-related suffering was experienced by people in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs). 1

Despite the apparent high demand for palliative care and pain relief, the Worldwide Hospice Palliative Care Alliance Global Atlas of Palliative Care reports that in 2017, among 198 countries worldwide, 47 (24%) had no known palliative care activity; 13 (7%) had capacity-building activities ongoing; 65 (33%) had isolated provision of services; 22 (11%) had generalized provision of services; 21 (11%) had preliminary integration into mainstream provision; and 30 (15%) had advanced integration of palliative services. 5 World Bank income grouping, universal health coverage, and Human Development Index were found to be significantly associated with the development of services, with 32 countries progressing from having no services or in the process of developing services, and 17 joining countries with integrated and advanced service provision. 5 The highest quality of services, available to about 14% of the global population, was mostly centered in the European countries 5 while 53% of the global population lived in countries where care was provided in silos and at low-quality standards. 6 In Latin American and Caribbean countries, as early as 2001, it was estimated that approximately half a million people needed palliative care services. 7 However, published data on the unmet need for palliative care in the region have remained sparse, especially in the smaller Caribbean islands. In Trinidad and Tobago, approximately 24% of patients in an inpatient medical ward were palliative in 2014. 8 In Africa, approximately 10 million people were estimated to need palliative care each year. 9 In the majority of African countries, the ratio of caregivers and services to patients and the population represents a miserable situation, with typically fewer than two palliative care services available per million people. 9 This situation mimics understaffing in other areas of care as well, remaining below the minimum thresholds calculated for adequate health coverage.

In this paper focusing on the Caribbean and Sub-Saharan African countries, we discuss the exclusion of palliative care in universal health care schemes, restrictions to oncology end-of-life care, insufficient deliberate distinctions in services for children and adults, and limited access to medications and the absence of medication policies and regulations. We propose three measures for integrating palliative oncology in healthcare systems, including a matrix of domains and dimensions that supports the construction of financial mechanisms for the sustainable resilience of healthcare systems. Finally, we present advocacy measures to better support implementation and building partnerships for the advancement of palliative oncology in the Caribbean and Sub-Saharan Africa.

Method

We conducted a literature review to examine the complexity and variability of barriers to delivering palliative oncology care to children and adults in Caribbean and Sub-Saharan African countries. The review was conducted in Google and Google Scholar with search terms, “palliative care,” OR “end of life care” OR “palliative oncology” AND “universal health care” OR “medical care” or psychosocial care” or “advocacy” AND “Caribbean” OR “Sub-Saharan Africa”. Peer-reviewed articles and grey literature were extracted and examined for relevance to the topic. Documents were included if they addressed medical, social, or economic enablers or barriers related to the provision of palliative care in general or palliative care for oncology patients; palliative care specialized for pediatric and adult patients; and based on work in the Caribbean and Sub-Saharan Africa. Documents that were not produced by reputable organizations, such as the World Health Organization and government organizations, or that provided data updated in more recent publications were excluded. The authors drew on their experiences from working in health care systems in African and Caribbean countries to develop a matrix of domains and dimensions that supports the development of sustainable financial mechanisms for palliative care.

Barriers to implementing palliative care in healthcare

In 2014, the World Health Assembly (WHA) adopted palliative care as an ethical and essential responsibility of healthcare systems in Resolution WHA 67.19 and called on governments to incorporate the service as an integral component of primary healthcare. 10 The inclusion of palliative care has requalified the imperative of health services to prevent and control premature death and suffering throughout the lifespan and healthspan. The charge further emphasizes that people who are made vulnerable due to illness should not experience additional undue financial hardship.10,11 Nonetheless, evaluation of the early impacts of Resolution WHA 67.19 revealed that the lack of funding, limited technical capacity and support, absence of affirmative measures and proposals, and cultural taboos relating to end-of-life were key barriers to the integration and expansion of palliative care services in several countries. 12 A systematic review of forty-nine studies, published in 2018 on palliative care policy and practices in African countries during the period 2005—2016, shows there was a need for palliative care programs, enabling policies, and medications in most of the countries. 13 A similar review of nine studies on palliative care in the Caribbean region, published in 2016. Show similar needs to African countries but also a need for training and public education.14,15

We discuss four major challenges to implementing, strengthening, and expanding palliative care and pain relief in Sub-Saharan and Caribbean countries that have implications for children and adults.

Exclusion of palliative care programs in universal health care schemes

The healthcare systems in many African and Caribbean countries do not integrate palliative care in universal health care schemes. Specific to the Caribbean region, the categorization of palliative care was as follows indicative of low uptake of palliative services in the healthcare structure: no known activity = 10; capacity-building activity = 4; isolated provision = 8; generalized provision = 0; preliminary integration into mainstream care = 1; and integrated provision = 0. 16 Developing countries, including many small island states, are normally disadvantaged in providing capacity to provide an expansive range of health services and performance of the health sector depends on limiting mortality rate and, less so, on providing and maintaining high-quality care for people living with chronic conditions. In many context, palliative patients are deemed to be at the end of life, calling into question the financial and ethical responsibilities of the system to patients. Additionally, cultural and social perspectives of death and dying strongly influence healthcare practices and may lead to deep structural inequalities in access to care that surpass the provisions of universal health care. A systematic review of palliative care in African countries underscored paternalistic patient–provider relationships as a significant barrier to the expansion and integration of palliative care in local and regional health systems. 17 An additional complexity is the reluctance of private and public insurance to cover palliative services, including hospice care, 5 deepening the inequities in healthcare delivery, with people being made vulnerable because of illnesses left in a disadvantaged position.

Restricting palliative care to adult oncology end-of-life care

Another major challenge in upscaling palliative care is primarily directing services to oncology patients while excluding the needs of patients with other life-threatening conditions. In the clinical context, pain management is generally oriented to treating injuries and acute conditions and less so to managing chronic serious health-related suffering. 18 The prevalence of chronic diseases that are associated with life-threatening and life-limiting conditions is increasing in many countries 18 and was estimated to have doubled between 1990 and 2017, propelled in low-income countries and among older people with dementia. 19 Apart from oncology cases, the WHO identified other conditions for which palliative services may be required, including cardiovascular diseases (38.5%), cancer (34%), chronic respiratory diseases (10.3%), AIDS (5.7%), and diabetes (4.6%). 4 Kidney failure, chronic liver disease, multiple sclerosis, Parkinson’s disease, rheumatoid arthritis, neurological disease, dementia, congenital anomalies, and drug-resistant tuberculosis also lead to palliative conditions. 4

A study on palliative care for breast cancer patients in the Caribbean identified challenges that may pervade life-limiting and life-threatening services: insufficient health care infrastructure, significant gaps in meeting palliative care needs, opiophobia in the health care system, late presentation by patients for clinical care, and the common use of complementary and alternative medicines while rejecting conventional care. 20 During the COVID-19 pandemic, the gaps in palliative care services widened owing to additional issues related to race, discrimination, and cultural barriers. 21 Other studies corroborate drug regulations, cultural factors related to the etiology of diseases, and alternative treatments in place of conventional medicine as limitations for palliative service delivery among sub-populations in the Caribbean. 22

Absence of differentiation in palliative care services for children and adults

Nearly 2·5 million children worldwide die from a lack of palliative care and pain relief, although more than 90% of the cases of mortality are avoidable. 1 According to the WHO, 98% of children needing palliative care are in LMIC countries, with almost half of them living in Africa. 4 In the case of children, family members, including the extended family, are also recognized as integral in the care process, helping to keep focus on the child’s body, mind, and spirit. 23 The mortality rate of life-limiting and life-threatening illnesses among children is greater in African countries than in the Caribbean; however, palliative care is not often regarded as a stewardship responsibility, cost-effective, and efficacious option. Although pediatric care for serious health-related suffering may be in lower demand in the Caribbean, integrated palliative care, with a wide spectrum of services, is available in only a few countries in the region.

Limited access to medications and the absence of medication policies and regulations

Several LMICs have limited access to basic pain relief medications.1,5 While palliative care is more commonly provided in Kenya, South Africa, and Uganda, other countries are increasing the gamut of services with policies in place, such as in Malawi, Mozambique, Rwanda, Swaziland, Tanzania, and Zimbabwe. 13 The results of the review of palliative care in African countries from 2005--2016 revealed that restricted access to medications, such as opioids, low update of morphine, and laws restricting palliative care practices, were barriers to service on the continent. 13 A systematic review of palliative care in the Caribbean region revealed a need for staff training, policies for health care, public education on palliative care, access to medications and support services in the region, and information on care models used in the Caribbean. 15 In the First Ever Global Ranking of Palliative Care: 2025 World Map Under the New WHO Framework on the state of implementation pf palliative care, Sub-Saharan Africa was not assessed to have made much progress in the three years prior in the areas of policy, essential medicines, education, service delivery, research, and community empowerment. 24 The countries achieved low scores across all dimensions and, particularly, in access to medicine and research. Twenty-three African countries were in the emerging category and 19 in the progressing category, indicating few policies and programs may be instituted. Very few African countries were in the established category and none in the advanced category. Eleven Caribbean countries were ranked in the emerging category and six in the progressing category, indicating low implementation and policy development to support palliative care. Approximately $1 million could close the gap in the unmet need for opioid analgesics to relieve serious health-related suffering in children in low-income countries, 25 and millions of people across the world could benefit from an investment of $145 million in morphine in palliative care to treat preventable pain. 25

Strengthening policy and practice to address palliative oncology barriers

Building capacity for palliative care programs for children

An example of a major cross-cutting initiative for building capacity for pediatric palliative oncology is provided in the French-speaking African countries. Since the 2000s, the French African Pediatric Oncology Group (GFAOP) has fostered and supported the development of pediatric oncology units throughout French-speaking African countries. 26 Children have been successfully treated in these units, providing to families and society that cancer is not necessarily a death sentence. This represents significant progress in holistic cancer care in Sub-Saharan Africa. Even so, cure rates remain far below those reported in Western countries, and symptomatic treatment and family-centered care are often neglected. Therefore, for many children with cancer and other life-threatening illnesses in Africa, providing palliative care is the most humane option.

To address this situation, the GFAOP launched the first palliative care training program in March 2016 and continued to December 2019, 26 targeting healthcare professionals working in pediatric oncology units in French-speaking Africa. Three initial training sessions, held in Dakar, Abidjan, and Rabat, were attended by multidisciplinary professionals, representing 19 pediatric oncology units in fifteen African countries. The GFAOP training program has contributed to the emergence of leaders and healthcare practitioners capable of changing practices and attitudes regarding pain management and the organization of palliative care services. The lessons that emerged can be instructive for future training iterations in palliative care organization and delivery:• Shift from very large-scale training projects to more targeted initiatives to adapt as best as possible to the complexity of local systems;• The partnership with the health system of each country should be strengthened;• Redirect training efforts to peripheral centers and community health centers, as has already been done in English-speaking Africa, to promote the circulation of knowledge in systems that are still too compartmentalized;• Switching from a training model to a support model by twinning, while maintaining collective mentoring promotes the sharing of experiences and data and mutual trust.

In 2022, a survey among the 19 participating units revealed favorable responses for the resumption of a training and supervision program, prompting a new project.

Despite progress in delivering palliative care practices and policies, generally, there are still gaps in understanding the interplay between clinical and cultural practices and how to integrate these dimensions in community interventions rooted in health care. With a more diverse population, this challenge may be more inherent in the Caribbean. The misunderstandings may very well arise from the historic separation of clinical and social practice. However, there has been a growing call for a holistic approach to wellbeing. We now highlight how palliative care can be advanced through affirmative cultural transitions, potentially leading to a reduction in psychosocial morbidity, that implicate quality to life and disease outcome.

Repositioning culturally sensitive psychosocial care in palliative oncology

On World Cancer Day, February 2023, the World Health Organization launched a new priority focus called “high-level determinants of cancer.” This focus was derived from the overwhelming evidence that the dominant biomedical approach that belies cancer prevention and control knowledge has a pervasive theoretical and technical disregard for biobehavioral and social primacy. 27 The visibility of this relegation is seen in clinical practice and policy-level national cancer control plans in Caribbean and African nations, where there is a dearth of integrated onsite or offsite referrals to psychosocial services; supportive or vague language and absent content to describe evidenced effective psychosocial interventions; and the observation of least and last placed allotments on the agendas of largely held and attended cancer conferences with nongovernmental organization partnerships.28–30 Psychosocial determinants and their health sequelae are amplified along the cancer continuum in LMICs in Africa and the Caribbean. Cancers and their treatments result in psychosocial morbidity 2–3 times greater than that of the general population and impose substantial and persistent burdens on patients, families, and healthcare providers, especially in resource-constrained settings. 31 Psychosocial morbidity represents the most frequent unmet need of individuals with cancer.32,33 Psychosocial interventions lead to immediate and lasting improvements in quality of life.34,35 The genomic discoveries of the Conserved Transcriptional Response To Adversity (CTRA) in the past decade have played an elucidatory reorienting role in the alchemization of the cancer experience, clinical precision, and synergy of psychosocial interventions in both secondary and tertiary cancer prevention [of suffering and premature death] compared with standard care. 36 These findings support psychosocial care as an affordable priority for low-resource African and Caribbean nations.37,38

An increasing number of representations and collaborative works in the International Psychosocial Oncology Society (IPOS) from the Caribbean in recent years have borne the Caribbean Psychosocial Steering Committee (CPOS) work in improving the delivery of high-quality care for all people. These internal solutions aim to grow leadership and bridge culturally sensitive psychosocial solutions. African nations and the Caribbean psychosocial context have well-established strengths of religion, spirituality, and community. Such collectivistic cultures emphasize the needs of the related members of the group. We see examples of this culture in censorship outside families, where “one’s dirty linen should not be washed in public,” as well as the culture of a pedadogical or paternalistic stance in helping relationships. 39 Findings such as those of Elk and Gazaway’s descriptions of the fundamental differences in cultural values using community participatory approaches highlight the clinical dangers of nonadaptive tailoring of interventions 38 which widen inequities and are counterproductive to universal healthcare. Community-based participatory approaches have been the gold standard for developing culturally sensitive palliative care solutions as well as creatively disrupting established systems of care delivery in a manner that helps to achieve the Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs).37,40

Other initiatives have been taken to support culturally sensitive capacity building to bridge gaps and reduce disparities in the provision of palliative care in the Caribbean and Sub-Saharan Africa. Extension from Community Health Outcomes (ECHO) has been transformative in leveraging digital technology to facilitate knowledge transfer between global experts in pediatric and adult cancer care in low-resource African and Caribbean nations. 14 This is a cost-effective and expansive solution to capacity building. Its model of in-person training, which uses remote access with virtual audio‒video interactive learning, and case-based sharing has been substantively applied. 14 Caribbean initiatives included a theoretical and practical pilot project by the University of Alabama at Birmingham in Jamaica in 2022 41 ; ECHO virtual training from 2020 for palliative care specialists in collaboration with the Pan American Health Organization (PAHO), Massachusetts General Hospital, the Jamaica Cancer Care and Research Institute (JACCRI), and the Caribbean Palliative Care Association14,42; and training by PAHO for regional allied health and medical care professionals from 2022 through PAHO’s virtual campus for public health. 43 The outcomes of these programs have been anecdotally transformative to the clinical practices of participants in recharacterizing trauma-informed approaches to care, advocating for the routine clinical recognition of distress, applying distress screens, and identifying psychological tipping points experienced at key clinical turning points in the patient’s illness course. 14 In Africa, pediatric oncology teams from 15 French-speaking countries were trained in a 3-year program in the use of analgesics and palliative care. 26

To understand, rationalize, and participate in palliative cancer care, it must be viewed as a multiple-dimensional construct within several domains. Dimensions have to do with a construct that stands alone, whereas domains consist of multiple dimensions that are grouped together to establish relationships. Even within this construct of dimensions and domains, decisions about such a human experience are difficult from various viewpoints: the human condition, resource allocation and priorities, beliefs and concepts around death and dying through the eyes of religion, philosophy, and other experiences, and policy-makers and legal constructs.

Framing palliative care in health care in the context of certain domains and dimensions

Can we ever make sense of palliative care using scientific, philosophical, and practical rules of engagement to drive policy and funding? End of life, in the context of comfort care, as a construct, can be confusing because the complex issues of our existence often come with decisions about funding, policies, and philosophy or beliefs. For insurance executives and policy-makers to make decisions on benefit design and funding allocation in deciding whom, when, and what limits should be considered when extending resources to those in need of palliated care. This, by no means, is an easy process, let alone viewing this situation contextually.

Starting with the human condition, on a personal level, each human will someday experience end-of-life issues, irrespective of the context. Looking at the dimensions of time, severity, and a person’s wishes and the wishes of the family, society and medical community can be contextually and ethically challenging. Despite this challenge, looking at all sides of the equation can be a way out and an opportunity for resolution. Starting with time, the mere announcement that someone’s life is near ending conjures up a sense of finality that can be confusing for the patient, policy-makers, doctors, and the public. When these factors consider the severity and nature of the disease, the model, method, and extent of palliative care goals espous in the palliative medicine field often conjure a sense of what is right, wrong, and best. For example, in the context of comfort care, what is the understanding of the patient, care providers, and community when the severity and state of the condition are considered? Is there a decision matrix that can work for all people, irrespective of the domain or dimension being considered? At the most basic level, the human condition should dominate the direction in framing policy and allocating resources.

People’s beliefs, which are based on their religious experience, culture, and sense of rights against a backdrop of pain and suffering, can play a dominant role in how policies around palliative care are decided and framed. LMICs with limited resources can be forced to consider end of life as a way out without the need to pay attention to the manner in which it is considered standard for more developed nations with comprehensive cancer care. In the palliative care world, as with limited resources, consideration is preferentially allocated to those who continue to experience life without the classification of an end-of-life condition. Therefore, decisions on resource allocation are often guided by matters that are associated with continuing life, not ending life.

The mere fact that many end-of-life measures are considered in more developed nations as a form of euthanasia, allowing a person during this period to end their life with dignity but with comfort throughout their life, poses an ethical dilemma for many in the religious community. Certain palliative measures can relieve suffering but can hasten the death of a terminally ill person. Therefore, where is the balance between the length and quality of life and the need to terminate suffering? Efforts to study the methods utilized to include bioethics, cost, and people’s rights remain complex in any context.

From a resource allocation standpoint, the development and implementation of sustainable palliative care in developing nations must involve a level of education where all sides of the issues must be well understood, depending on the constituents. In building benefit plans for coverage in developing nations, palliate care is not a priority. Providing a decision matrix for policy-makers, health care providers, patients and their community is paramount to the development, implementation, and management of a palliative care program. Domain and dimension matrix for palliative care introduction into health care.

In designing and implementing benefit plans for coverage in the public and private sectors, governments of developing nations face significant challenges in deciding what they will financially support in health care. Having a matrix that clearly defines the factors that contribute to the decision-making process is among the best ways to establish value in the context of healthcare service and financing. While private sector insurance plans are often more flexible in covering services such as palliative care, it is difficult to utilize universal health care principles of access to make palliative care available on a “whole population” basis with government-invested tax-based revenues alone, as there is significant competition for such resources within health care and with other priorities. Hybrid models of health care where capital risk and care risk are spread across a broader population needing care, providers’ participation as a network, and payers consolidating their health expenditure allow for economies of scale to where more health care services could be offered from larger consolidated premiums and expenditure dollars. In the Caribbean, there is a significant movement toward the implementation of private‒public arrangements between the government and private sector, utilizing population health principles to ensure that there is a hand shake between universality and sustainability to allow for a broader set of benefits while preserving human dignity, irrespective of economic status, preexisting conditions, gender, race, and other factors that limit equitable access to sustainable quality health care. For example, the JIPA Network, which is based on the United States and works with Caribbean governments, has created a stratified pooled risk coverage (SPRC) where benefits and contributions from various payers, including government and insurance companies, are staked to ensure that each entity covers services at different financial limits, with the government covering the first tier and the private insurance company covering the second and potential third tiers of coverage. Integrating initiatives, however, will need to be supported by advocacy, bringing all stakeholders on board and with a clear understanding of the gaps and mandates that are necessary for advancing healthcare in the countries.

Approaches to advocacy for palliative care integration in healthcare

LMICs in Africa and the Caribbean are facing an increasing prevalence of noncommunicable diseases (NCDs). NCDs impose a significant burden on societies, communities, and households, creating substantial financial strain on these countries. 44 . Nonetheless, the World Health Report recommends that any universal health coverage system must be designed to provide all people with access to needed health services (including prevention, promotion, treatment, and rehabilitation) of sufficient quality to be effective; ensure that the use of these services does not expose users to financial hardship; and identify essential packages of health services (EPHSs) to achieve universal health services. 45 The International Monetary Fund (IMF) has recommended the establishment of country-specific schemes, and the World Bank has suggested 21 Packages of Interventions (POIs) to help achieve the goals outlined in the World Health Report. 46

Although the Advocacy Coalition Framework (ACF) was developed for a North American context, studies have explored its applicability in African and other countries and found that the ACF offers a sound foundation for policy change approaches, even when applied in low- or middle-income countries with different political contexts.47,48 Incorporating empathy and understanding the perspectives of opposing coalitions can improve the likelihood of dialog and build trust. Advocates should open dialogs built on empathetic principles, engage in research, and conduct continuous monitoring and evaluation of educational campaigns, awareness events, and media outreach.49,50 Increasing awareness of the work done by other advocates, highlighting the size of supporting coalitions, and showcasing progress toward achieving policy goals are strategies to help shift advocacy coalitions in favor of the policy. Policy brokers play a key role in this phase of the change management process. These actors facilitate cross-coalition learning, identify areas where collaboration is possible, act as gatekeepers, and influence the flow of information among advocacy coalitions.

Strategies

An inventory of resources needed for advocacy should include funding, office facilities, information libraries, media contacts, health authority technocrats, and volunteers (e.g., community members, patient relatives, and survivors). These resources are allocated according to the advocacy coalition’s needs. Creating a coalition matrix allows planners to determine each actor’s coalition membership—as an ally or opponent—and identify those who need to become supporters of the policy. The main targets are individuals likely to transition from opposition to ally, including government policymakers, healthcare workers, community groups, and social influencers. Resources and assets can be directed at these targets to influence their positions on the policy. Agents of change are those that can affect this influence. The following strategies are essential for policy advocacy to support the integration of palliative care in health care in low-resources countries.

Education of policy actors

Education and training programs for target policymakers and leaders are crucial. This education can alter the beliefs surrounding the policy and increase allyship. A lack of formal training in palliative care is a major barrier to universal access in LMICs. Training increases the confidence of healthcare workers and key actors in implementing and sustaining the policy. Programs can result from partnerships with established academic palliative care programs in high-income countries. Collaborating with local palliative care leaders and policymakers allows for capacity building, research, knowledge management, case consultations, and expertise sharing. It is essential to identify and respect cultural nuances that differ from those of foreign partners.

Public education

The public is a key policy actor. Information, education, and communication (IEC) campaigns are effective tools for increasing public awareness of the need for access to palliative care through sustainable health care models. Campaigns should be adapted to respect and leverage cultural contexts, such as views on suffering and illness, patient rights, and healthcare workers' roles. Public support increases pressure on politicians to act according to their constituents' desires. Framing the issue clearly and linking it to the population’s quality of life is vital.

Community-based projects

Community-based projects can include the following:• Conducting research to determine community quality-of-life metrics;• Establishing opioid registration and education programs;• Organizing non-medical palliative care volunteer groups for patient visits or household assistance;• Providing informal caregiver relief and addressing other identified needs.

This aligns with the objectives of providing all people with access to needed health services (including prevention, promotion, treatment, and rehabilitation) of sufficient quality to be effective and ensure that the use of these services does not expose users to financial hardship.

Integrating palliative care into universal health care schemes in low-resource countries is a complex but essential endeavor to improve healthcare outcomes and quality of life. By understanding coalition behaviors, addressing barriers through empathy and policy-oriented learning, and implementing well-structured advocacy plans, stakeholders can drive meaningful policy change. To realize any change, African and Caribbean countries must approach healthcare development as a stewardship responsibility for the common good of each person and the whole community across the human lifespan. Through collaborative efforts, education, and community engagement, the integration of palliative care into universal health care can become a reality, providing equitable and compassionate care for all.

Limitation

This work is limited in ways that are important for interpretation of the findings. The review is based on published work and does not substantively benefit from other interventions that remain unpublished. We did not find extensive publications on palliative care programs and integration in universal health care in low-income and developing countries. Limited information was available to support extensive discussions on the four barriers, particularly in the Caribbean context. This gap, however, presents the opportunity for this paper to be an eye-opener on challenges related to palliative care for children and adults in resource-limited settings.

Another limitation is the scarcity of information on programs in the smaller islands in the Caribbean. The highlighted programs were centered in the larger islands. This literature gap limits understanding of the unique challenges of the smaller islands, such as the Windward Islands, and the potential for adopting the recommendations across the region.

The proposed model of domains and dimensions is a theoretical concept which was not tested to determine its efficiency in framing effective palliative care integration in healthcare services. We hope that future publications will report on the effectiveness of the model and present other recommendations to build the model. As an eye-opener, we bring the experiences and expertise of the authors together to produce substantive content. We hope readers will reflect on palliative care in other sub-specializations and adopt the recommendations appropriately, across various contexts.

Conclusion

Although palliative care is recognized as a human right and an essential part of quality care and dignity for both adults and children experiencing serious and life-threatening conditions, the provision of such care is still a challenge in Sub-Saharan Africa and Caribbean countries. Many factors drive this issue, and this paper highlights the complexities and variability of barriers as well as presents options for positioning palliative care in health care systems in the countries. For palliative care to advance in developing nations, it must be offered as a benefit under some kind of consolidated arrangement in health care. The colonial system of health care that is purely dependent on taxpayer revenues is not sustainable. Legislative and financial resource allocation in the context of health insurance and national health insurance, when related to palliative care for children and adults, should be considered in a multidimensional manner, especially for LMICs. We present options for leveraging diverse institutional and economic structures, considering the cultural landscape, to enable access to quality and structured palliative care with financial protection for people in these resource-limited countries. Consideration should be given to how certain domains and dimensions interface with and implicate decisions regarding palliative care services. The matters of human conditions, resource allocation and priorities, beliefs and concepts around death and policy-makers and legal constructions significantly influence the health decisions and directions of both governments and affected people. At the same time, we cannot ignore the pitfalls that dot the global world in which we live. In Africa, particularly in the field of health, the triple burden is also a challenge: the persistence of life-threatening infectious diseases (malaria, HIV, multidrug-resistant tuberculosis, hepatitis and other emerging epidemic fevers) and the emergence and increasing prevalence of noncommunicable diseases (diabetes, cancer, hypertension, obesity, etc.). Another important consideration is the imperative of repositioning culturally sensitive psychosocial care for better cost-effectiveness and co-ownership between health systems and communities. Repositioning psychosocial care in Africa and the Caribbean, taking into account the uniqueness of cultural environments, is a prudent step toward improving receptivity, ownership, and communication in societies. Advocacy and heightened awareness of stakeholders at all levels are necessary to build partnerships from shared understanding and interest in healthcare system development. Finally, our goal is to create an agenda to begin and advance dialog and advocacy for the inclusion of palliative care as a necessary component of primary health care and health care schemes in Sub-Saharan African and Caribbean countries. We target policymakers, current and future advocates for palliative care, and bilateral and international agencies with an interest in supporting health care schemes in low-resource settings, health economists, researchers, and academicians. We have selected capacity-building approaches to provide a framework and options for strengthening advocacy for the integration of palliative care in health systems. Leveraging collaboration within and between regions will foster a global and multidisciplinary approach to healthcare policy and program development. Undoubtedly, these measures will also better position the countries to achieve the Sustainable Development Goals related to health and well-being and leave no child or adult behind in the provision of health care in the Sub-Saharan African and Caribbean countries.
