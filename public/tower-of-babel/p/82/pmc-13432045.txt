Comparative evaluation of effective curing depth and degree of polymerization of different flowable bulk fill composites: An in vitro study
Authors: Nitin Palakal, Parul Bansal, Nikita Sharma, Darshana Baruah, Priyal Raj, Adithi Sunil Patil
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Adequate polymerization of resin-based composites is essential for the clinical success of restorative procedures. Depth of cure (DOC) and degree of conversion (DC) directly influence the mechanical properties, marginal integrity, wear resistance, and biocompatibility of composite restorations. Bulk-fill flowable composites permit placement in increments of up to 4 mm, reducing clinical time. Recently introduced single-shade flowable bulk-fill composites claim improved optical blending and simplified shade selection; however, comparative evidence regarding their polymerization efficiency remains limited.Aim:To compare the DOC and DC of a single-shade flowable bulk-fill composite with two conventional flowable bulk-fill composites at a 4-mm increment.Methodology:Thirty-six cylindrical specimens (4 mm × 6 mm) were prepared and divided into three groups (n = 12): Charisma Bulk Flow ONE, Beautifil Bulk Flowable, and SDR Flow Plus. Specimens were light-cured using an LED curing unit and stored at 37°C for 24 h. DOC was assessed using Vickers microhardness testing at top and bottom surfaces, and bottom-to-top hardness ratios (HRs) were calculated. DC (%) was evaluated using Fourier Transform Infrared Spectroscopy with Attenuated Total Reflectance (FTIR-ATR). Statistical analysis was performed using one-way analysis of variance and Tukey’s post hoc test (P < 0.05).Results:Beautifil Bulk Flowable demonstrated the highest DOC and DC, followed by SDR Flow Plus and Charisma Bulk Flow ONE. Charisma Bulk Flow ONE failed to achieve the clinically acceptable HR of ≥ 0.80. Significant differences were observed among all groups (P < 0.05).Conclusion:Polymerization efficiency was material dependent. Conventional flowable bulk-fill composites showed superior curing behavior compared to the single-shade flowable bulk-fill composite.

INTRODUCTION

Resin-based composites have become an integral part of contemporary restorative dentistry due to their esthetics, adhesive properties, and ability to support minimally invasive treatment approaches.[1] Their long-term clinical success depends on the efficient conversion of monomers into polymers through adequate polymerization. Factors such as depth of cure (DOC) and degree of conversion (DC) significantly influence the mechanical performance and durability of composite restorations.[2]

DOC refers to the thickness of resin that undergoes sufficient polymerization upon light activation, whereas DC represents the percentage of carbon–carbon double bonds converted into polymer chains during curing.[2] Reduced light intensity with increasing depth compromises monomer conversion, negatively affecting strength, wear resistance, color stability, and biocompatibility of restorations.[3] Conventional resin composites, therefore, require incremental placement of 2 mm to ensure adequate polymerization. However, this approach is time-consuming and technique-sensitive, particularly in large posterior restorations.[4]

Advances in resin chemistry and curing technology have led to the development of bulk-fill composites that allow placement in increments of up to 4–5 mm while maintaining acceptable curing efficiency. Bulk-fill flowable composites achieve improved adaptation through reduced filler content and modified resin matrices, though polymerization efficiency remains material dependent.[5]

More recently, single-shade universal and bulk fill composites based on smart chromatic or structural color technology have been introduced to simplify shade selection and enhance translucency,[6] potentially improving light penetration and DOC, as shade characteristics themselves can influence the DOC.[7]

Charisma Bulk Flow ONE is a single-shade flowable bulk-fill composite designed for 4 mm incremental placement with low shrinkage stress. Beautifil Bulk Flowable incorporates surface prereacted glass-ionomer (S-PRG) fillers, providing bioactive properties such as fluoride release along with bulk-fill capability. SDR Flow + utilizes stress-relieving resin technology to reduce polymerization shrinkage while enabling efficient bulk placement.

Despite these advancements, variations in resin composition, filler content, and photoinitiator systems may influence curing behavior at greater depths. Polymerization efficiency can be reliably assessed using Fourier Transform Infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy for DC analysis and Vickers microhardness testing as an indirect indicator of DOC.[8] Therefore, using these parameters, evaluating and comparing the DOC and DC of single-shade and conventional flowable bulk-fill composites remains clinically relevant.

Cylindrical specimens (4 mm height × 6 mm diameter) were fabricated using a metallic mold placed on a glass slide covered with a Mylar strip. Each mold was filled in a single increment using the bulk-fill technique. A Mylar strip was placed over the mold to obtain a smooth surface.

Specimens were light-cured using a cordless LED curing unit (Woodpecker iLED) in continuous mode for 20 s at an intensity of 2500 mW/cm2. The curing tip was positioned in direct contact with the top glass slide. Polymerization was performed from the top side only. The irradiated surface was designated as the top surface and the opposite surface as the bottom surface.

After curing, specimens were removed from the mold and stored in distilled water at room temperature for 24 h prior to testing.

Vickers microhardness testing

Microhardness was measured using a Vickers microhardness tester with a load of 0.25 kgf applied for 5 s. Three indentations were made at random, equidistant points on both top and bottom surfaces. Mean Vickers Hardness Numbers (VHN) were calculated for the bottom and top surface.

Depth of cure evaluation

DOC was assessed indirectly using the bottom-to-top hardness ratio (HR):

HR = VHN_bottom/VHN_top

An HR ≥ 0.80 was considered indicative of adequate polymerization.

Fourier transform infrared analysis for the degree of conversion

After microhardness testing, the specimens were sectioned horizontally at the midpoint using a slow-speed diamond disc under continuous water cooling. From the top and bottom halves, approximately 1 mm-thick sections were obtained to represent the respective regions. Top and bottom halves were pooled separately and powdered using a mortar and pestle. Uncured composite samples served as controls.

Statistical analysis

The data were analyzed using Microsoft Excel 2007 and SPSS version 23.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Results were expressed as mean ± standard deviation. Normality and homogeneity were assessed using Shapiro–Wilk and Levene’s tests. As parametric assumptions were satisfied, intergroup comparisons were performed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s post hoc test. Categorical data were analyzed using the Chi-square test. P <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

RESULTS

The distribution of acceptable HRs varied among the groups. Group 1 showed the lowest acceptability (16.7%), whereas Group 2 demonstrated the highest (41.7%), followed by Group 3 (33.3%). The mean HR was lowest in Group 1 (0.753 ± 0.042) and higher in Group 2 (0.792 ± 0.019) and Group 3 (0.786 ± 0.018).

One-way ANOVA revealed a significant difference in mean HR among the groups (F = 6.677, P = 0.004). Tukey post hoc analysis showed significantly lower values in Group 1 compared with Group 2 (P = 0.002) and Group 3 (P = 0.007), whereas no significant difference was found between Group 2 and Group 3 (P = 0.617).

One-way ANOVA showed highly significant intergroup differences in DC at both the top and bottom surfaces (P < 0.001). Tukey post hoc test confirmed significant differences between all groups. Group 2 showed the highest DC at both surfaces, followed by Group 3, whereas Group 1 showed the lowest values.

DISCUSSION

Bulk-fill resin composites were developed to reduce clinical working time and to overcome the limitations of the conventional incremental placement technique.[9] Their clinical performance is primarily governed by DOC and DC, which reflect the ability of curing light to polymerize the material throughout its thickness and the extent of monomer conversion, respectively. Inadequate DOC and DC adversely affect mechanical properties, marginal adaptation, biocompatibility, and long-term stability, while increasing the release of unreacted monomers that may contribute to postoperative sensitivity and pulpal irritation.[10] Effective curing of resin composites is also essential to prevent the potential cytotoxic effects of inadequately polymerized material.[11] Composites often exhibit incomplete conversion, leaving residual unreacted monomers that act as plasticizers and reduce the mechanical strength of the restoration.[12]

In the present study, specimen fabrication using a metallic mold followed ISO 4049 guidelines, ensuring standardized curing conditions. Metallic molds restrict peripheral light transmission and allow a more accurate assessment of depth-wise polymerization, as reported by Novin Rooz and Yousefi Jordehi.[13] A 4-mm increment was selected based on manufacturer recommendations and existing literature, as bulk-fill composites are designed to polymerize effectively at this thickness. Price et al.[14] demonstrated that enhanced translucency, optimized resin matrices, and modified photoinitiator systems enable bulk-fill composites to achieve adequate curing at 4–5 mm increments. Czasch and Ilie further emphasized that 4 mm represents a clinically relevant increment for evaluating bulk-fill materials.[15]

FTIR-ATR spectroscopy was used to assess the DC at both the top and bottom surfaces, complementing DOC and microhardness analysis. The results demonstrated that Beautifil Bulk Flowable exhibited the highest DC, followed by SDR Flow Plus and Charisma Bulk Flow ONE, a trend that closely paralleled the microhardness findings and confirmed the relationship between depth-wise hardness and monomer conversion. This approach is particularly relevant, as attenuation of light at greater depths significantly reduces polymerization efficiency, especially at the bottom surface.[16]

Among the tested materials, Beautifil Bulk Flowable demonstrated the highest DOC and DC values, achieving the greatest HR and the highest top and bottom DC%. DC values exceeded the clinically acceptable range reported for bulk-fill composites, indicating effective polymer network formation.[17] The superior performance of Beautifil may be attributed to the presence of S-PRG fillers, which improve translucency and reduce internal light scattering, facilitating deeper photon penetration and more uniform polymerization.[18] These findings are consistent with the observations of Yap et al.[19] and Abdelwahed et al.,[20] who reported favorable DOC and DC for giomer-based bulk-fill materials. However, contradictory report by Jameel et al.[21] suggests that the polymerization efficiency of Beautifil may be sensitive to curing conditions and experimental protocols.

SDR Flow Plus demonstrated intermediate performance, with DOC and DC values significantly higher than Charisma and comparable to Beautifil. Its favorable curing behavior has been attributed to a modified UDMA-based resin matrix with polymerization-modulating photoactive groups, resulting in controlled polymerization kinetics and improved depth-wise curing.[22]

These findings align with the results of Alshali et al.,[23] who reported adequate polymerization of SDR at 4-mm increments. Nevertheless, reduced curing efficiency under suboptimal curing conditions has been reported by Flury et al.[24] highlighting its technique sensitivity.

Charisma Bulk Flow ONE exhibited the lowest DOC and DC values, with most specimens failing to achieve the clinically acceptable HR. Lower DC at the bottom surface indicates insufficient depth-wise polymerization at a 4-mm increment. Similar findings have been reported by Leprince et al.,[5] and Czasch and Ilie.[15] where unfavorable optical properties and resin matrix characteristics limited light transmission and curing efficiency.[15] Although acceptable curing has been reported under ideal laboratory conditions by Ilie and Stark,[25] the present findings suggest a higher risk of under-polymerization in clinically relevant scenarios.

Across all groups, top surfaces consistently showed higher DOC and DC than bottom surfaces, reflecting progressive light attenuation with increasing depth. This emphasizes the importance of adequate curing protocols even when using bulk-fill materials.

Although this study followed ISO 4049 guidelines, certain limitations must be acknowledged. As an in vitro investigation, it could not fully replicate clinical conditions such as cavity configuration, transdental light attenuation, and intraoral environmental factors. The use of metallic molds ensured standardization but did not simulate the optical behavior of enamel and dentin. In addition, only a single 4-mm increment and one curing protocol were evaluated, and only three flowable bulk-fill composites were tested. The DC was assessed immediately after curing, without long-term aging or postcure evaluation. Furthermore, recently introduced bulk-fill and core build-up composites with advanced photoinitiator systems and modified resin matrices were not included in this study. Therefore, extrapolation of the results to clinical practice should be done with caution. This study evaluated only three bulk-fill composites; hence, further research is required to compare newly introduced materials with improved optical properties, enhanced DOC, and advanced polymerization systems with those evaluated in the present study.

CONCLUSION

Within the limitations of this in vitro study, Beautifil Bulk Flowable demonstrated the most reliable polymerization at 4 mm, followed by SDR Flow Plus, whereas Charisma Bulk Flow ONE showed inferior depth-wise curing. Clinicians should therefore consider material composition, cavity depth, and curing conditions rather than relying solely on bulk-fill claims, as inadequate polymerization may compromise restoration longevity.
