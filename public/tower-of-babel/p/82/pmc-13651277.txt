Measles susceptibility in a large urban center in Northeast Brazil and its implications for viral reintroduction risk
Authors: Maria Eduarda Maia Vilar, Florisneide Rodrigues Barreto, Ramon da Costa Saavedra, Olindo Assis Martins-Filho, Maria da Glória Lima Cruz Teixeira
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundRecent studies indicate that declining levels of measles-specific IgG antibodies among vaccinated individuals without exposure to wild-type virus may reflect changes in population-level serological profiles, rather than direct measures of protective immunity. Such findings may have implications for understanding population susceptibility following viral introduction in measles-eliminated settings. This study aimed to analyze changes in the age distribution of protective measles antibodies in the population of Salvador, Brazil, between 2000 and 2024.MethodsA population-based seroprevalence study was conducted using a household-based cluster sampling design across 30 sentinel areas in Salvador, Brazil. A total of 1,508 individuals were included, and a comparable survey had been conducted in 2000. Data on sociodemographic characteristics, vaccination history, and prior measles infection were collected through household interviews. Venous blood samples were obtained for measurement of measles-specific IgG antibodies using ELISA. Seroprevalence estimates were calculated by age group, and logistic regression analysis was performed to identify factors associated with measles seropositivity. ELISA-based IgG detection does not directly measure functional neutralizing antibodies or protective immunity.ResultsOverall measles IgG seroprevalence was 82.4%, with antibody concentrations predominantly ranging from 275 to 1,000 IU/L. The highest seroprevalence was observed among individuals aged over 50 years (92.1%), whereas the highest proportion of seronegative individuals occurred in the 20–29-year age group (39.6%). This age group also showed the most pronounced decline in seropositivity between 2000 and 2024, from 95.7% to 60.4%. In multivariable analysis, only previous measles infection was positively associated with seropositivity (OR = 1.67; 95% CI: 1.04–2.66; p = 0.032).ConclusionsAlthough seroprevalence was higher than seronegativity in the study population, the observed levels remain below those generally considered sufficient to interrupt measles transmission (≈ 92–95%). These findings indicate important immunity gaps among young adults and children that may facilitate outbreak occurrence following virus introduction in large urban centers.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12889-026-28093-7.

Background

The re-emergence of vaccine-preventable infectious diseases, particularly measles, a highly contagious and potentially severe illness, has become a major concern for public health authorities and professionals worldwide [1]. The etiological agent, the measles virus (MeV), belongs to the family Paramyxoviridae and the genus Morbillivirus [2] and is recognized as one of the most transmissible human pathogens. Measles outbreaks may occur even in populations where fewer than 10% of individuals are susceptible, which underscores the need for very high levels of herd immunity to interrupt viral transmission, typically estimated at approximately 92–95%. For this reason, the World Health Organization (WHO) recommends that at least 95% of the population, particularly children under five years of age, be adequately immunized with two doses of a measles-containing vaccine [3–5].

In Brazil, coverage rates of the measles-mumps-rubella (MMR) vaccine remained consistently high until 2014. However, during the 2015–2016 biennium, nine federal states failed to achieve the 95% coverage target, indicating a decline in vaccination homogeneity. In the state of Bahia, this target had been met until 2014. Nevertheless, in 2017, coverage dropped to 84.9%, with 69.1% of municipalities falling below the recommended level, coinciding with the occurrence of measles cases between 2018 and 2020 [6]. Although vaccination indicators have shown partial recovery since 2023, the risk of measles virus reintroduction and sustained transmission remains, as a growing proportion of the population lacks adequate protection.

This situation is further aggravated by accumulating evidence showing a gradual decline in measles-specific immunoglobulin G (IgG) antibody levels over time among vaccinated individuals who have not been exposed to the wild-type virus, which may not fully reflect protective immunity. Such waning immunity may increase susceptibility to measles and facilitate viral circulation following reintroduction in previously measles-free areas [7–11]. Although measles cases in vaccinated individuals are generally milder, these individuals may still contribute to measles virus transmission [8, 9]. Together, these factors highlight the importance of assessing population-level measles serological profiles to identify susceptible groups and inform prevention and control strategies.

In this context, the present study aimed to analyze changes in the age-specific distribution of measles IgG antibodies in the population of Salvador, Bahia, by comparing the results of seroepidemiological surveys conducted in 2000 and 2024.

Methods

Study setting

The study was conducted in Salvador, the capital city of the state of Bahia, located in the Northeast region of Brazil. In 2023, Salvador had an estimated population of 2,417,678 inhabitants, distributed over an area of 693.442 km², resulting in a population density of 3,486.49 inhabitants per km². The city has a Municipal Human Development Index (MHDI) of 0.759, classified as medium, and is characterized by marked social and economic inequalities across its 170 neighborhoods [12].

Study design, population, and period

This was a population-based cross-sectional seroprevalence study of measles-specific immunoglobulin G (IgG) antibodies conducted among residents of Salvador aged two years or older. The study employed a methodology comparable to that used in a previous survey carried out in 2000 by Andrade (2005) [13], which was based on a similar sampling framework, as described below. Participants were selected between February and July 2024 using household-based cluster sampling within the same 30 sentinel areas (SAs) originally defined in the 2000 survey, using comparable sampling parameters to enable temporal comparisons between the two surveys.

Sampling strategy

Definition of sentinel areas

The sentinel areas were originally delineated in 1999 for the development of the Health Impact Assessment Project of the Environmental Sanitation Program of Salvador and surrounding municipalities of Todos os Santos Bay, conducted by the Institute of Collective Health of the Federal University of Bahia (ISC-UFBA). Access to basic sanitation and household income were considered relatively valid and objective proxies of living conditions. Accordingly, census tracts in Salvador were classified based on these two variables and grouped into seven strata: high household income and predominantly sanitized; middle household income and predominantly sanitized; middle household income and moderately sanitized; middle household income and unsanitized; low household income and predominantly sanitized; low household income and moderately sanitized; and low household income and unsanitized.

From these classified census tracts, 30 microareas were randomly selected as sampling units and designated as sentinel areas. These 30 sentinel areas comprised a total of 37 census tracts, as some areas included more than one tract. Each sentinel area consisted of one or more census tracts and included a minimum of 500 and a maximum of 900 households [14]. For the present study, a new sample of residents from these same sentinel areas was selected to determine current levels of measles IgG antibodies and to assess changes relative to the 2000 survey.

To update the delineation of the sentinel areas, population data from the study conducted by Andrade (2005) [13] were used. Based on the addresses composing each sentinel area, the current population of each census tract was estimated using population projections from the 2010 Brazilian Demographic Census conducted by the Brazilian Institute of Geography and Statistics (IBGE), as shown in Fig. 1.

Sample size calculation

The sample size of the original survey was estimated based on a household-based cluster sampling design, assuming an expected seroprevalence of 50%, a 95% confidence level, a 5% margin of error, and a design effect appropriate for cluster surveys, with proportional allocation across census tracts within the 30 sentinel areas. This conservative assumption was adopted to maximize statistical power in the absence of precise prior estimates.

For the present study, a comparable sampling framework was maintained to allow temporal comparisons between surveys. Using a household-based cluster sampling design, the number of participants was estimated and proportionally allocated across the 37 census tracts corresponding to the 30 sentinel areas. A uniform allocation of 42 individuals per census tract was adopted, resulting in an estimated sample size of 1,554 participants.

The sampling design consisted of stratification by census tract combined with single-stage cluster sampling. Households were defined as the sampling clusters, and individual residents constituted the elementary sampling units. Households within each census tract were selected using probabilistic sampling. After household selection and agreement to participate, all eligible residents of the selected households were invited to participate in the interview and blood sample collection.

Data collection and laboratory procedures

All residents of selected households who agreed to participate were informed about the objectives of the study and provided written informed consent. A structured questionnaire was administered to collect information on participant identification, sociodemographic characteristics, self-reported history of measles infection, and measles vaccination status. Vaccination status and number of doses were self-reported and could not be verified for most participants. The questionnaire was developed for this study and is available as Supplementary Material 1.

Venous blood samples of 5 mL were collected from participants for qualitative and quantitative serological assessment of measles-specific IgG antibodies. Laboratory testing was performed using a commercial enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kit (Euroimmun, EI 2610–9601 G) in 1,508 samples. Optical density readings were obtained using a Multiskan FC microplate reader (Thermo Scientific) at a wavelength of 450 nm. Results were converted to international units (mIU/mL) using the standard provided by the kit and were categorized as positive (> 275 mIU/mL), indeterminate (220–275 mIU/mL), or negative (< 220 mIU/mL). Indeterminate results were retested to confirm the final classification. However, these levels do not necessarily correspond to individual clinical protection.

Data analysis

Seroprevalence of measles-specific IgG antibodies was calculated as percentages for the total study population and stratified by age, sex, household income, educational level, self-reported history of measles infection, and vaccination status. An age-stratified graph was constructed to assess variations in seroprevalence across age groups.

Logistic regression analysis was performed to estimate associations between seropositivity and sociodemographic variables, vaccination status, and history of measles infection. The association between sex and serological status was assessed using Pearson’s chi-square test. To evaluate temporal changes and estimate the accumulation of susceptible individuals within the sentinel areas of Salvador, age-specific seroprevalence estimates from the 2000 and 2024 surveys were compared using Z-tests. A significance level of 5% (α = 0.05) was adopted, and all analyses were conducted using Stata software, version 16.

Ethical considerations

The study protocol was approved by the Research Ethics Committee of the Institute of Collective Health of the Federal University of Bahia (ISC-UFBA), under Certificate of Ethical Approval (CAAE) number 28435420.3.0000.5030, in accordance with Brazilian National Health Council Resolution No. 466 of 2012.

Results

Most participants (35%) had completed secondary education or had incomplete higher education, and 39% reported a monthly household income between one and two minimum wages (Table 1).

Among participants, 29.4% reported a previous history of measles infection. Of these, 56.7% indicated that infection occurred between 6 and 12 years of age, and 78.8% of cases were reported based on family information. Only 17.3% reported having received a medical diagnosis. Most participants (77.3%) reported having been vaccinated against measles. Among unvaccinated individuals, 10.4% stated that they chose not to be vaccinated, with lack of knowledge about the need for vaccination being the most frequently reported reason (50.7%). Only 85 individuals (7.3%) presented vaccination records in their immunization cards. Among these, 78.8% had documentation of the second vaccine dose, and 70.3% were younger than 14 years of age. Overall, 1,242 participants (82.4%) tested positive for measles specific IgG antibodies, defined as concentrations equal to or greater than 275 IU/L. Among seropositive individuals, 45.3% had antibody concentrations ranging from 275 to 1,000 IU/L (Table 2).

Among participants who reported being vaccinated (n = 951), seropositivity was 84.3%, whereas it was 93.2% among those who reported not being vaccinated (n = 68). Individuals who reported not being vaccinated had higher odds of seropositivity compared to those who reported being vaccinated (OR = 2.53; 95% CI: 1.01–6.36; p = 0.048). A total of 233 participants were uncertain about their vaccination status, among whom seropositivity was 91.0%, and this group also showed higher odds of seropositivity (OR = 1.88; 95% CI: 1.15–3.07; p = 0.011) (Table 3).

Logistic regression analysis showed that self-reported vaccination status and continuous use of medication were not significantly associated with measles IgG seropositivity (OR = 0.79; 95% CI: 0.26–2.42; p = 0.675 and OR = 1.21; 95% CI: 0.63–2.29; p = 0.567, respectively). Age showed a borderline association with seropositivity (OR = 0.90; 95% CI: 0.79–1.01; p = 0.081). In contrast, self-reported previous measles infection was significantly associated with higher odds of seropositivity (OR = 1.67; 95% CI: 1.04–2.66; p = 0.032). Socioeconomic variables were not statistically associated with seropositivity and were therefore not included in the final regression model (Table 4).

Discussion

The mean seroprevalence of measles-specific IgG antibodies observed in Salvador in 2024 was 82.4%, substantially lower than that reported in 2000, when seroprevalence exceeded 95%. This reduction was accompanied by marked heterogeneity across age groups, with the most pronounced declines observed among individuals aged 20–39 years and children aged 5–9 years. In contrast, higher seropositivity was observed among individuals who reported a previous history of measles infection (92.8%). No significant difference in seroprevalence was observed between sexes in either survey. While individuals older than 50 years consistently showed the highest seroprevalence across both time points, in 2024 several age groups—including young adults and school-aged children—presented seroprevalence below 80%.

The observed reduction in measles IgG seroprevalence over the last 25 years suggests important changes in population-level serological profiles in this urban setting. Although these findings indicate a lower proportion of individuals with detectable IgG antibodies, they should be interpreted as reflecting serological status rather than direct measures of protective immunity or individual-level susceptibility [15, 16]. The combination of reduced seroprevalence in younger cohorts and historically lower levels in certain adult groups may indicate potential gaps in population immunity that could become epidemiologically relevant in the context of viral importation. However, such interpretation must remain cautious, as serological data alone do not allow direct inference of transmission dynamics or individual protection against infection.

A particularly notable finding was the marked decline in seroprevalence among individuals aged 20–39 years. This pattern is likely multifactorial and may reflect cohorts primarily vaccinated during a period of reduced measles virus circulation, when natural boosting from wild-type exposure was limited. In addition, the two-dose measles vaccination schedule was only introduced in Brazil in 2004 [17], meaning that older cohorts may have received only a single dose during childhood immunization programs. Together, these factors may contribute to lower long-term IgG levels in young adults, as described in previous longitudinal studies showing waning antibody titers over time in the absence of natural exposure [7, 14, 18]. Similar immunity gaps have also been described in other Brazilian states, including São Paulo, where reductions in vaccination coverage and heterogeneous vaccine uptake have contributed to increased population susceptibility and sporadic measles outbreaks in recent years [19–21].

Measles-specific IgG measured by ELISA does not necessarily correspond to functional neutralizing antibody activity. Although ELISA is widely used in seroepidemiological studies, plaque reduction neutralization testing (PRNT) remains the reference method for assessing protective immunity against measles [15, 16].

Therefore, the seroprevalence estimates presented here should be interpreted cautiously, as they may not fully reflect functional population immunity. The observed seroprevalence in 2024 remains below levels generally considered sufficient to interrupt measles transmission, which are typically estimated at approximately 92–95% [3–5]. However, herd immunity thresholds are population-level constructs and may vary depending on contact patterns, vaccination heterogeneity, and spatial clustering of susceptible individuals. In this context, the presence of immunity gaps—particularly among young adults and certain pediatric groups—may indicate increased vulnerability of the population to sustained transmission following virus introduction, especially in densely populated urban environments with heterogeneous vaccination coverage. The broader epidemiological context further reinforces the importance of these findings. Measles circulation continues to be reported in multiple regions globally, including areas in Africa, Europe, and the Americas, and imported cases remain a persistent threat even in countries with previously eliminated status [22–25]. In this setting, the accumulation of susceptible individuals in specific age groups may represent an important vulnerability for reestablishment of transmission, although this risk cannot be quantified directly from serological data alone and should not be interpreted as evidence of imminent outbreak risk. From a programmatic perspective, the lower seroprevalence observed among children aged 5–9 years is particularly relevant, as it may reflect recent gaps in vaccination coverage and disruptions in routine immunization services [19–21]. Strengthening catch-up vaccination strategies and school-based immunization initiatives may therefore be important to reduce immunity gaps in younger cohorts and improve overall population immunity profiles. This study has several limitations. First, vaccination status and history of prior measles infection were self-reported for most participants, which introduces potential recall bias and misclassification. Second, ELISA-based IgG detection, although widely used in seroepidemiological studies, does not distinguish between binding and neutralizing antibodies, limiting direct interpretation of functional protection, for which neutralization assays such as PRNT remain the reference method. Third, the sample was drawn from sentinel areas rather than being fully representative of the entire city population, which may limit generalizability. Finally, the relatively small sample sizes in some age strata may have affected the precision of certain age-specific estimates.

Despite these limitations, the study provides valuable insights into long-term changes in measles serological profiles in a large urban center over a 24-year period. By comparing two population-based surveys conducted under similar methodological frameworks, the findings contribute to understanding temporal shifts in population immunity and may support planning of immunization strategies within the Brazilian Unified Health System.

Conclusions

Although the proportion of seropositive individuals was higher than that of seronegative individuals, the seroprevalence observed in 2024 remains insufficient to ensure herd immunity against measles in the event of viral circulation. However, herd immunity thresholds should be interpreted as population-level theoretical constructs rather than direct measures of individual protection. The substantial immunity gaps identified among young adults, together with reduced seroprevalence in children, suggest potential heterogeneity in population immunity profiles rather than definitive evidence of transmission risk, indicating a potential increase in population vulnerability following viral introduction in large urban centers.

These findings underscore the need for targeted vaccination strategies, including catch-up and school-based interventions, to improve population immunity levels and support measles elimination goals within Brazil’s Unified Health System.

Finally, preserving the historic achievements attained in Brazil in controlling measles and other vaccine-preventable diseases, achieved through coordinated and sustained efforts over more than five decades, requires strengthening and adequate financing of the Unified Health System, with particular emphasis on the National Immunization Program.
