Impact of Preclinical Verification in Physiologically Based Pharmacokinetic Modelling for First-in-Human Predictions: Application of a Published Model Building Strategy for Five Compounds
Authors: Claire Jackson, Rebecca H. Graves, Thuy T. Tran, Neil A. Miller
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background and ObjectivePhysiologically based pharmacokinetic modelling is routinely used in the pharmaceutical industry and has an impact on drug labels. New applications have emerged, one of which is first-in-human pharmacokinetic predictions. Scientists in this field often believe that verification of models in preclinical species is essential for accurate predictions, but consensus has not been reached and animal use across the industry deserves continual examination with the aim to reduce use.MethodsA published model-building strategy was used to assess the accuracy added by preclinical verification for human PK prediction. Three sequential approaches were explored using five compounds covering a range of physicochemical properties and chemical classes. Approach 1 (QSPR FIH prediction), uses parameter inputs predicted using in silico Quantitative Structure Property Relationship models; Approach 2 (In vitro FIH prediction), supplements predicted parameters with in vitro measurements where available data exist; Approach 3 (Verified FIH prediction), uses preclinical in vivo pharmacokinetic data to verify the in vitro measurements.ResultsPreclinical verification models were able to provide predictions of first-in-human pharmacokinetics within two-fold of the observed data for all five compounds studied, which was a significant improvement compared with predictions using in silico or in vitro inputs without verification in preclinical species. In most cases, the predictions generated purely from structure were superior to those supplemented with in vitro data without preclinical validation. Prediction of human clearance via in vitro in vivo extrapolation proved challenging and was identified as the most common cause of poor predictions of human PK from in vitro data.ConclusionsThe work here supports the continued considered use of preclinical verification in PBPK modelling.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s40262-026-01655-z.

Key Points

Preclinical verification models were able to provide adequate predictions of first-in-human pharmacokinetics as they were within two-fold of the observed data for all five compounds studied, which was a significant improvement compared with predictions using in silico or in vitro inputs without verification in preclinical species.Prediction of human clearance via IVIVE without any adjustment for observed data proved to have limited reliability and was identified as the most common cause of poor predictions of human pharmacokinetics from in vitro data.Preclinical verification may involve plausibly modifying numerous compound-specific input parameters to capture the observed preclinical PK, without the measured data being available to validate specific mechanisms. This may result in a complicated model that adequately predicts first-in-human pharmacokinetics, but that will need mechanistic verification if the compound progresses in the clinic.

Introduction

Physiologically based pharmacokinetic (PBPK) modelling combines physiology (tissue and organ composition, volumes and blood flows) with drug-specific data to predict the pharmacokinetics of drugs and enables the generation of plasma and tissue concentration versus time profiles. PBPK is now routinely used in the pharmaceutical industry, is accepted by Regulatory Agencies and impacts on drug labels are now routine [1–3]. Additionally, PBPK modelling can be used to combine the many pieces of data that are generated during the early stages of drug discovery on a new chemical entity into a cohesive, mechanistic model that can be used for hypothesis testing, critical thinking, and that evolves as additional data, and knowledge are incorporated. Early data can comprise the experimental determination of physicochemical properties, human and preclinical in vitro measurements and pharmacokinetics (PK) determined in preclinical species. PBPK models, which use quantitative structure–property relationship (QSPR) predictions for those properties that have not yet been measured, can be prospectively applied to predict clinical PK in advance of first-in-human (FIH) studies, thus allowing screening of potential new drug candidates with limited data or that have yet to be synthesised; high-throughput PBPK (HT-PBPK) modelling of large numbers of compounds in early discovery can be used for compound ranking and prioritisation and has demonstrated accuracy in predicting rat PK from a combination of structure predictions and in vitro measurements [4]. Papers published by Jones et al. [5] and later by Miller et al. [6] set out a model building strategy to integrate measured data to develop the essential components of a FIH prediction. Whilst these authors believe that preclinical verification is currently an essential element of the model building strategy, it is fair to say that a consensus has not yet been reached across the pharmaceutical industry. When considered in the context of animal use in the industry, and the goal of ultimately reducing, refining or replacing animal use entirely [7], the continued scrutiny of available methodologies is warranted.

Here, we have used the flow diagrams proposed by Miller et al. [6], prior to looking at the observed clinical data, to predict the human pharmacokinetics using a PBPK model that was verified with the preclinical data i.e. the PBPK model inputs predict the pharmacokinetics in preclinical species consistent with observed data (approach 3). In addition, a prediction was performed using only the predicted input parameters from QSPR models by ADMET Predictor (AP) (approach 1), alongside one that utilised any available human in vitro data and measured physicochemical properties (approach 2). Each prediction was compared with the mean clinical profile from the lowest dose in the FIH study and the most predictive approach highlighted on the basis of the accuracy of the prediction. As preclinical verification identifies critical parameters that can then form part of a sensitivity analysis, this has also been undertaken for each compound and the results fed back into the FIH prediction. We believe that this analysis of the modelling process and results provides additional real-world examples to the ongoing debate regarding the added value of preclinical verification in FIH PBPK modelling.

Methods

To enable the PBPK models to be used for robust decision-making, a consistent approach to model building was applied. Simulations were conducted using GastroPlus® v9.7 (Simulations Plus Inc., Lancaster, CA, USA). The default physiologies available in GastroPlus were utilised with some modifications for company-specific parameters for each species (e.g., liver blood flow (LBF)). The compounds modelled covered a range of physicochemical properties and chemical classes within the active chemical space at the time the analysis was carried out (Table 1); all the compounds were typical drug-like small molecules. The simulated dose was set at the lowest dose tested in the FIH study, and the appropriate formulation parameters were used. The first prediction, approach 1, utilised solely QSPR inputs from ADMET Predictor® v9.0 (Simulations Plus, Inc., Supplementary Table S1). The second prediction, approach 2, used human in vitro and measured physicochemical data in place of QSPR inputs where available (Supplementary Table S2). The third prediction, approach 3, built on the first two and used what was learnt from the preclinical in vivo pharmacokinetic data (Table 2). For the third prediction, the measured data inputs used reflect what was historically generated to support the internal decision point of Candidate Selection which identifies compounds suitable for progression through regulatory safety assessment studies towards a FIH study. In the absence of either measured data or QSPR predictions for a specific preclinical species or human, a value was estimated on the basis of knowledge of the compound’s properties in other species. Where preclinical pharmacokinetics were not adequately captured using measured inputs directly, the input parameters were iteratively and plausibly adjusted to obtain a preclinically verified model, i.e. a PBPK model that is consistent with the pharmacokinetics in each species. Results

Five compounds with human pharmacokinetic data were included in the analysis. The compounds included a range of biopharmaceutics and other properties (Table 1).

A variety of in silico, in vitro and in vivo data normally generated in preclinical development were used in model parameterization. The specific inputs and strategy for each compound are detailed in Table 2.

Clearance proved a challenging input parameter to define, and the elimination strategy in Table 2 defines what was used for each compound, after both methods had been assessed, with the single species scaling using LBF being used for most compounds. The distribution predictions consistently used the Lukacova Kp method with modification of some of the input parameters required for most of the compounds. The oral absorption strategy was specific to each compound and heavily reliant on the iterative use of pharmacokinetic data in the preclinical species to determine scientifically plausible inputs.

The AFE for both AUC(0–t) and Cmax were calculated for each compound compared with the mean plasma concentration profile (Table 3, and Fig. 2). Across all compounds, to compare each approach, the AAFE was calculated by averaging the Cmax and AUC AFE. Approach 3 simulations had the lowest AAFE for both AUC and Cmax (1.5 and 1.4, respectively) with all predictions within 2-fold of the observed values. On an individual compound basis, combining the AUC and Cmax errors to calculate a compound AAFE, also indicated that the approach 3 predictions were superior in all five compounds assessed. Discussion

Methodology

The published FIH PBPK model-building strategy [6], with preclinical verification, was retrospectively applied to human PK predictions for five compounds from historical drug discovery projects aimed at oral administration. The final human PK predictions were made before assessing the observed human PK data, and so the human PK predictions can be classified as prospective. Although a consistent set of input data were used, for some compounds, valuable input data were not available from these historical projects but would be generated on current projects. It is also worth noting that all the modelling was conducted with the same version of the PBPK software, unlike some other manuscripts [6, 18, 19].

As per Miller et al. [6], compound assessment using quantitative structure–property relationship plus PBPK modelling was carried out first. Secondly, we modelled distribution before metabolism and elimination, as a modified lipophilicity can impact the calculation of the unbound intrinsic clearance. In addition, gut wall metabolism was assessed before modelling oral absorption as gut first-pass extraction decreases the free concentrations in enterocytes which then impacts absorption. We determined that this was the most efficient order to use the previously defined flow diagrams, although this is not the order that the flow diagrams were presented by Miller et al [6]. Mechanistic absorption modelling was used by default rather than a simple first‐order absorption model which has been generally used by other groups at this stage [18]. The lowest dose in the FIH study was used in this analysis, so the accuracy of the absorption prediction was not fully tested in terms of whether the model was able to capture any dose-limited absorption.

Santos et al. [19] also modelled distribution before clearance, and similar to Mao et al [18], they used the Kp scalar approach to simultaneously scale all predicted tissue specific Kp values to an identical extent when there was a mismatch between the predicted and observed distribution. However, we followed Miller et al. [6] and adjusted the predicted Kp values via the input relevant to the compound in question, in this way the differences in tissue composition are utilised in a physiologically plausible manner. The majority of compounds in our data set were highly lipophilic and therefore the logP was the primary input parameter that was fitted, with other parameters adjusted on the basis of the availability or reliability of experimental data. This approach ensures that tissue composition is taken into account when predicting the overall volume of distribution, which is critical when the model is used to translate between species, and when the prediction of tissue concentrations is vital to either efficacy or safety predictions. The sensitive input parameter was shown to vary between compound classes, in line with the equation used for predicting tissue Kps. Where in vitro measured logP was available, the justification for adjustment lies in the difference between an octanol:water or on-column method and in vivo binding to neutral lipids and phospholipids in tissues.

Although all the compounds were identified as potential substrates of CYP3A4 from QSPR predictions (Table 1), only two of the compounds (Table 2) warranted the inclusion of intestinal metabolism based on the measured data that was available for approach 3.

As per Miller et al. [6] and Santos et al. [19], sensitivity analysis on key parameters was included to assess the inherent uncertainty at this stage of drug discovery. The results from the PSA were incorporated into the approach 3 predictions and, once the clinical data was compared, it was determined whether the accuracy could have been improved.

Accuracy

Predictions of FIH pharmacokinetics within twofold are considered adequate given the many unknowns at this early stage of drug development. For all five compounds the predictions using approach 2 which utilized human in vitro and measured physicochemical data without preclinical verification resulted in inadequate predictions, as was also the case for Mao et al [18].

In four of the five compounds modelled, apparent inaccuracies in predictions of clearance contributed to poorer overall predictions of human PK for the models without verification. None of the compounds studied demonstrated a robust IVIVE from in vitro to in vivo clearance for preclinical species; so, approach 3 relied heavily upon empirically scaled data from in vivo clearance. None of the compounds studied here had available in vitro data to indicate the involvement of transporters (other than P-gp); there is the possibility that both clearance and distribution processes are transporter-driven, which may explain the lack of IVIVE. The inclusion of transport processes in PBPK models is complex even when in vitro data are available [20, 21]. Cynomolgus monkey has been suggested as the most appropriate species for translation to human for liver blood flow scaling of clearance [12, 22], global predictions of Vss [23], and as specific models of drug transporters [24]. Analysis of the only compound for which monkey data was available in this work (compound 3) noted significant differences in the prediction of human clearance and distribution from approach 3 compared with approaches 1 and 2. However, for the other compounds, approach 3 produced apparently adequate estimations of clearance using the liver blood flow scaling method, even when the most predictive species (monkey) was unavailable. Different LBF values were used, based upon a more recent meta-analysis, from the previous work by Ward et al [12]. Human IV PK data would be required, as recommended in several publications [25], to validate the core components of human distribution and elimination, providing more confidence in the predictive capability of the model in later development. The methodology applied here uses HLM predictions of clearance from structure, which is the default setting in GastroPlus; however, it should be highlighted that QSPR enzyme kinetic models would incorporate additional mechanisms into the simulations, such as gut metabolism and saturable clearance, which was not explored in the predictions from structure.

The best overall prediction from structure was for compound 3; however, analysis suggests this is owing to an incorrect combination of mechanisms. Compounds 4 and 5 also were predicted by QSPR within an average of two-fold of the observed data. For compound 4, this appears to be owing to the very close match to Cmax, while the AUC prediction remains poor. For compound 5, this finding is predictable, considering at the dose tested it falls into a BCS/DCS 1 classification, and is consistent with outcomes from similar analyses [26]. Further exploration is needed to determine if the complex absorption processes captured in approach 3 for compound 5 would prove to be more predictive than the other approaches in accurately predicting higher human doses.

For all compounds, PSAs were performed on the human models produced from approach 3, focussing on the most sensitive parameters as determined from preclinical species modelling. Where parameters were interlinked, sensitivity analyses combined them in such a way as to analyse the extreme scenarios. In several cases, the predictions could have been improved by modifying the sensitive parameters within the predetermined sensitive windows; however, in others no improvement in prediction was seen. This work placed focus on comparison to mean FIH clinical data and does not consider population variability in the measure of prediction error; however, some of the parameters tested in PSAs could also inform likely risk from inter- and intra-subject variability on clinical success. The assessment of prediction error may change when considering multiple studies or larger population data sets [27]; the significance of population variability must be considered in the context of level of uncertainty in a baseline human PK prediction prior to obtaining human data. During a development program, these learnings would be invaluable in focussing resources on critical data generation or guiding compound development or clinical trial design to minimise risk, which could lead to resource waste, clinical inefficacy or undesirable side effects.

Caveats

The data set presented here is too small to draw firm conclusions on which categories of molecules are currently well-served by QSPR predictions alone; however, as more examples of prospective predictions are added to the literature, it is believed this will become more evident and advance the field. No empirical scaling of animal preclinical PK profiles to predict for human was carried out in this analysis as PBPK was routinely performed from the outset at the time of analysis.

Preclinical verification may involve pragmatically modifying numerous input parameters such as those affecting precipitation, without the measured in vivo data to validate the effect on individual ADME processes. This may result in a complicated model that adequately predicts FIH PK. A good example of this is the model for compound 2, where the inclusion of CYP3A4 mediated gut metabolism coupled with P-gp enabled the prediction of the observed non-linear oral PK in preclinical species. Whilst we had no mechanistic data, the preclinical verification coupled with knowledge of the compound supported the inclusion of both mechanisms and delivered an adequate human model. In these cases, additional experimental data may be required post the FIH study to provide justification for the mechanisms included in the model. The combination of gut metabolism and efflux mechanisms has been previously noted as a particularly complex scenario to capture [28], and preclinical verification PBPK modelling is a pragmatic approach to identify and incorporate potential mechanisms. Identification of knowledge gaps through the modelling process and discovering potential ‘known unknowns’ [29] can guide data generation and give a greater understanding of risk; the models presented here are based on historical data; however, current programs would likely benefit from this data generation during development. As science evolves and data on more compounds are shared, the belief is that preclinical verification will involve fewer pragmatic manipulations.

Conclusions

The suggested workflows from Miller et al. [6] provide step-by-step guidance and a practical linear process for each stage of PBPK model construction. However, some parameters that needed to be fitted (due to missing or uncertain in vitro/in vivo measurements) required a circular modelling approach of fitting and cross verification, and interdependencies of certain parameters necessitated revisiting earlier assumptions. The authors found the best way to minimise the revision of core parameters throughout the process was to complete the modelling workflows in the order of distribution, elimination, Fg and finally absorption, as is consistent with others in the field [19]. The separation of each aspect of ADME is only possible with the availability of IV PK.

For the five compounds studied, the predictions of FIH pharmacokinetics following approach 3 utilizing preclinical verification are generally adequate given the uncertainty at this stage of drug development. Predictions from QSPR are a useful guide and give a reasonable prediction of human oral exposure profiles, but in vitro data alone are currently not recommended for use without preclinical verification. The analysis determined that a key limitation of the in vitro predictions was the reliable estimation of drug clearance. For some large molecules (e.g. mAbs), there is sufficient human PK data and consistency of drug behaviour that preclinical testing is moving towards being replaced [7]; however, small molecules demonstrate wide variability in both the clearance mechanisms and the relative contributions of each mechanism. In order to reduce the requirement to use preclinical species to determine clearance, new (e.g. microphysiological systems [7]) or improved in vitro methodology is required, especially for low metabolic clearance, non-CYP metabolism and transporter-driven processes.

Although model verification based on preclinical data is not sufficient justification for the assumption of accurate human prediction [19], preclinical verification results in improved predictions of FIH pharmacokinetics for all five compounds compared with predictions using in silico or in vitro inputs without verification in preclinical species. To adequately model the in vivo preclinical data, plausible adjustments can be made to key input parameters, such as the unmeasured in vivo permeability and the uncertain fluid volumes in the GI tract, that are incorporated into the human predictions. Although monkeys have been often identified as the ideal species for translation to human, this presents practical and heightened ethical concerns. This work found adequate predictions could be obtained without monkey data; through hypothesis testing within the PBPK models, it is possible to identify which compounds may have PK driven by complex mechanisms (e.g. transporter-driven processes) which would benefit from obtaining PK data from monkeys, rather than this being routine practice. Thus, additional studies and a more considered approach to animal use, for all species, should be taken throughout the lifespan of a project.

The work here supports the continued considered use of preclinical verification in PBPK modelling.
