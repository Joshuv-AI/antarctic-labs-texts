The relationship between thoracic kyphosis and Q angle with balance and falls in patients with osteoporosis
Authors: Muhammet Şahin Elbasti, Muhammed Korkmaz, Nevzat Yeşilmen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundFalls drive fragility fractures in postmenopausal osteoporosis, but integrated evidence linking thoracic kyphosis, quadriceps (Q) angle, and ultrasound-derived quadriceps size indexed to body mass index to balance and self-reported fall history remains limited. We hypothesized that greater thoracic kyphosis and higher Q angle would be associated with poorer balance performance and self-reported fall history.MethodsIn this cross-sectional study between May and August 2024, 100 postmenopausal women with osteoporosis were assessed. Thoracic kyphosis was measured on lateral radiographs using the Cobb angle, and the Q angle by standing goniometry. Quadriceps muscle thickness was measured by ultrasound, and the previously published Sonographic Thigh Adjustment Ratio was calculated as thickness/body mass index. Balance was assessed using the Berg Balance Scale and one-leg standing test. Fall history was defined as at least one self-reported fall in the previous year. Spearman correlation, between-group comparisons, exploratory multivariable logistic regression, multiple linear regression, and receiver operating characteristic analyses were performed.ResultsOf 125 screened individuals, 100 were included (mean age 65.9 ± 7.9 years; 51 fallers). Thoracic kyphosis and mean Q angle were correlated (r = 0.700, p < 0.001), and both were associated with poorer balance and lower quadriceps measures (all p < 0.001). After adjustment for age, body mass index, 25-hydroxyvitamin D, mean Q angle, and Sonographic Thigh Adjustment Ratio, thoracic kyphosis remained associated with self-reported fall history in the exploratory model (odds ratio 1.444 per 1°; 95% confidence interval 1.143–1.824; p = 0.002), whereas the Sonographic Thigh Adjustment Ratio was inversely associated with falls (odds ratio 0.35 per 0.1-unit increase; 95% confidence interval 0.15–0.79; p = 0.011). In multiple linear regression, thoracic kyphosis, Q angle, and the Sonographic Thigh Adjustment Ratio were associated with Berg Balance Scale score (p ≤ 0.004).ConclusionsIn postmenopausal women with osteoporosis, greater thoracic kyphosis and lower ultrasound-derived quadriceps size indexed to body mass index were associated with self-reported fall history and poorer balance. Quadriceps angle was related to balance but not associated with self-reported fall history after adjustment. These findings are preliminary and hypothesis-generating; because of the cross-sectional design, self-reported fall history, and possible residual confounding, they do not establish causality, predictive utility, or clinical cut-off values.Keywords: Postmenopausal osteoporosis, Thoracic kyphosis, Q angle, Berg Balance Scale, Falls

Introduction

Osteoporosis is a skeletal disorder characterized by a decrease in bone strength, which is an integrated reflection of bone mineral density (BMD) and bone quality. It often progresses without symptoms and increases the risk of fractures [1]. Particularly in the geriatric population, most osteoporotic (fragility) fractures occur following low-energy trauma, such as a fall from standing height [1, 2]. Due to this etiological relationship, the clinical assessment of fracture risk should not be limited to BMD measurements alone; it should be addressed with a comprehensive approach that also integrates fall risk [1, 2]. This comprehensive approach requires the clinical assessment of underlying biomechanical and postural factors that can negatively affect an individual’s postural stability and gait dynamics, thereby increasing the risk of falls [2, 3]. In this context, clarifying the possible relationship between parameters such as thoracic kyphosis (TK) and the Quadriceps (Q) angle, which reflects lower extremity alignment, and postural control and tendency to fall may contribute to better defining the biomechanical determinants of fall risk.

It has been reported that the TK angle increases and the degree of kyphosis is higher in individuals with osteoporosis [4]. This deformity appears to be associated with reduced spinal extensor muscle strength and impaired joint position sense [4]. A study by Sinaki et al. (2005) showed that thoracic hyperkyphosis, which develops secondary to decreased muscle strength, plays an important role in increasing body sway, gait instability, and the risk of falls in individuals with osteoporosis [5]. Similarly, Koelé et al. (2022) reported an association between hyperkyphosis and increased fall frequency in the oldest quartile of a large prospective cohort of community-dwelling older adults [6]. These findings suggest that an increase in TK angle may be a potential indicator of fall risk and may be considered a postural target.

However, there is no consensus in the literature regarding the relationship between TK angle and fall risk. In contrast, a prospective observational study by Nikolaou et al. (2021) found that postmenopausal (PM) osteopenic and osteoporotic TK angle changes were not significantly related to fall risk and did not significantly predict falls [7]. These conflicting findings in the literature are thought to stem from the measurement methods used to assess kyphosis reflecting different clinical and biomechanical parameters. For example, a study by McDaniels-Davidson et al. (2020) showed that kyphosis measured by the Cobb angle (CA) did not predict falls, whereas kyphosis assessed by the block method was significantly associated with the risk of falls over a three-year period [8]. The authors explain this divergence by the fact that while CA is a measurement that quantitatively assesses TK radiographically, the block method can also reflect accompanying abnormalities, including fixed flexion in the cervical region in addition to thoracic curvature [8]. These findings suggest that focusing solely on the degree of thoracic curvature when interpreting fall risk may be insufficient; it may be useful to evaluate changes in sagittal alignment together with compensations aimed at maintaining balance (particularly pelvic and lower extremity alignment) [6, 9].

In this context, when examining postural control in individuals with osteoporosis, it may be appropriate to consider the Q angle, a clinical measure reflecting lower extremity alignment, as a potential related variable. The Q angle is defined as the angle between the spina iliaca anterior superior (SIAS)–mid-patella line and the mid-patella–tibial tubercle line [10]. The literature reports that increased Q angle is associated with lower extremity alignment and patellofemoral biomechanics; furthermore, some studies have reported its connection with dynamic balance impairments [10]. In this context, clinical studies on the relationship between Q angle and balance and falling are also important. For example, a study conducted by Taşçı Bozbaş and Gürer (2017) revealed that lower extremity alignment disorders may be associated with the risk of falling; in particular, increased Q angle was reported to have a moderate positive correlation with the Fall Index [11]. These findings suggest that increased Q angle may be associated with impaired postural control and a greater risk of falls.

In addition to all these specific postural and biomechanical factors, it has been reported that losses in muscle mass, strength, and function in the osteosarcopenia phenotype, defined as the coexistence of osteopenia/osteoporosis and sarcopenia, may be associated with impaired postural control and increased fall risk [12]. Therefore, the combined assessment of biomechanical alignment variables such as TK and Q angle in osteoporotic individuals within the context of osteosarcopenia may strengthen the multidimensional clinical interpretation of fall risk. Objectively quantifying these musculoskeletal changes in clinical practice is of great importance. Previous literature has shown that lower rectus femoris (RF) muscle thickness measured by ultrasound is associated with an increased risk of falls in older adults [13]. Ultrasound assessment of RF muscle thickness has also been shown to be reliable across raters and time points [14]. In addition to these anatomical findings obtained through imaging methods, it has been shown that postural sway parameters are higher in osteoporotic individuals with a history of falls compared to those without falls [15]. The clinical assessment of such biomechanical and postural risk indicators may support the planning of patient-specific targeted rehabilitation strategies. Indeed, current systematic review and meta-analysis findings suggest that balance-focused exercise interventions may have positive effects on overall balance performance in individuals with osteoporosis [16]. However, recent studies have generally examined kyphosis, lower-extremity alignment, quadriceps ultrasound measures, and balance in separate methodological frameworks, and integrated evidence in PM women with osteoporosis combining radiographic TK, Q angle, US-derived quadriceps measures, and clinical balance tests remains limited [3, 6–8, 10, 13–16]. Clarifying these relationships may help refine future multidimensional fall-risk research frameworks in PM osteoporosis, while clinical applicability requires prospective validation.

The primary objective of this cross-sectional study was to evaluate the associations of TK, measured radiographically using the CA, and Q angle with balance performance, assessed using the Berg Balance Scale (BBS) and one-leg standing test (OLST), in PM women with osteoporosis, and to examine their associations with self-reported fall history. Secondarily, we investigated whether quadriceps ultrasound parameters, namely quadriceps muscle thickness (QMT) and the Sonographic Thigh Adjustment Ratio (STAR), were associated with balance performance and fall history. We hypothesized that greater TK and a higher Q angle would be associated with poorer balance performance; associations with self-reported fall history and quadriceps ultrasound parameters were evaluated as related but distinct exploratory outcomes.

Materials and methods

Study design

This is a cross-sectional study planned to investigate the associations between TK and Q angle with balance performance and self-reported fall history in PM women with osteoporosis. The study was designed and reported in accordance with STROBE recommendations for cross-sectional observational studies. Because of the cross-sectional design, analyses were interpreted as associative rather than causal or predictive.

Study setting and period

The study was conducted at Elazığ Fethi Sekin City Hospital Physical Medicine and Rehabilitation Clinic between May and August 2024.

Fallers were defined as participants reporting ≥ 1 fall in the previous 12 months; non-fallers reported no falls. Falls were assessed by self-report and were not prospectively monitored; recurrent falls, injurious falls, and fall mechanisms were not separately classified. No separate comorbidity subgroup was predefined. Instead, comorbid conditions likely to directly influence balance, posture, or lower-extremity alignment were handled through the exclusion criteria, whereas stable chronic conditions not expected to materially affect these outcomes were not considered exclusionary. Accordingly, the “comorbidity population” was not analyzed as a separate subgroup; rather, it comprised clinically stable participants who remained eligible after exclusion of comorbidities judged likely to directly influence balance, posture, or lower-extremity alignment.

Sample size

Sample size was calculated using G*Power 3.1.9.4 (Heinrich Heine University, Düsseldorf, Germany) software. The primary outcome measure of the study was balance performance. It was aimed to test the relationship between TK and Q angle (°) and balance measures. In a study by Ishikawa et al., which previously examined the relationship between spinal curvatures and postural balance in patients with osteoporosis, it was reported that lumbar kyphosis showed weak to moderate correlations (r = 0.25–0.33) with postural imbalance in analyses performed with 93 patients [17]. Based on these findings, an a priori power analysis was performed for a two-tailed correlation test, assuming a medium effect size (r = 0.30), a significance level of α = 0.05, and a statistical power of 80% (1 − β = 0.80). Accordingly, the minimum required sample size was calculated as 85 participants. Considering an anticipated 10% loss due to missing or incomplete data, the target sample size was set at 100 participants. This calculation was based on the primary correlation objective; therefore, multivariable regression and ROC analyses should be considered exploratory within this sample size.

Participant flow and missing data

Participants were recruited consecutively between May and August 2024. Eligibility was assessed using predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria. Participant flow (assessed, excluded with reasons, declined, enrolled, and analyzed) is reported in the Results section and Fig. 1. As this was a cross-sectional study with a single assessment visit, there was no follow-up attrition. Data completeness was verified by cross-checking clinical records and study forms prior to analysis; therefore, no imputation methods were required.

Ethical approval and participant rights

The study protocol was approved by the Fırat University Non-Interventional Research Ethics Committee (date: 09.05.2024, no: 2024/07–31). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Data collection and measurements

All measurements were performed by the same physician experienced in musculoskeletal ultrasonography (US) to reduce interobserver variability and ensure measurement standardization.

Demographic and clinical data

Participants’ age, body mass index (BMI), age at menopause, history of previous fractures, and number of falls in the past year were recorded. Vertebral fractures were assessed using lateral radiographs obtained during routine clinical follow-up.

Biochemical measurements

Participants’ serum 25-hydroxyvitamin D [25(OH)D] levels were obtained from biochemistry laboratory results recorded in the hospital information system. Measurements were made using chemiluminescence immunoassay (CLIA) in accordance with the hospital’s routine laboratory protocols. All analyses were performed in the same laboratory in accordance with quality control standards.

The values obtained were evaluated according to the reference ranges of the Endocrine Society Clinical Practice Guideline (Holick et al., 2011) [18]:

Radiological measurements

Dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DEXA)

DEXA measurements were retrieved from routine clinical densitometry records. Dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DEXA) is the gold standard for the assessment of BMD, with well-established validity and precision in clinical and research settings. The densitometry unit performs routine quality assurance procedures, including daily phantom calibration according to the manufacturer’s recommendations and scheduled maintenance. Since only one DEXA measurement per participant was available, inter-session reliability was not assessed within the study. BMD measurements were performed using DEXA at the lumbar vertebrae (L1-L4), femoral neck, and total femur regions. A T score of ≤ -2.5 and/or a low-trauma vertebral fracture at any site was considered osteoporosis. All scans were performed using the same device [GE Lunar Prodigy dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DEXA) system (GE Healthcare, Madison, WI, USA)] and conducted by the same experienced technician.

Degree of thoracic kyphosis

The degree of TK in all patients was measured manually using the CA method on upright lateral spine radiographs. A CA ≥ 50° was considered thoracic hyperkyphosis.

The most reliable and valid method for radiographic evaluation of kyphosis is currently accepted as the CA measurement. While the CA was initially defined to determine the degree of curvature of scoliosis in the coronal plane, it has become widely used in the quantitative analysis of TK with the adaptation of the imaging plane to the sagittal projection. Technically, the segment between the T4 and T12 vertebrae is usually included in the evaluation because of the overlap of the upper thoracic vertebrae with other anatomical structures. During the measurement, the intersection angle of the lines drawn over the upper end-plate line of T4 and the lower end-plate line of T12 is calculated and this value constitutes the thoracic CA (Fig. 2) [19].

Quadriceps muscle thickness (QMT) and STAR score

Ultrasonographic evaluation was performed by a physician experienced in musculoskeletal US using the ESAOTE My Lab 70 model B-mode US device in our clinic and a 4–13 MHz linear probe. In the US evaluation of lower extremity QMT (the sum of RF and vastus intermedius muscle thicknesses), the reference measurement points in the SARCUS update (Sarcopenia Through Ultrasound) of Perkisas et al. were taken into account, and the midpoint of the line between the greater trochanter and the upper edge of the patella was marked, with the patient in the supine position and the knee in full extension [20]. QMT was not developed as a new measure in the present study; rather, QMT was assessed by ultrasonography in line with the standardized anatomical landmark recommendations reported in the 2020 SARCUS update [20]. The probe was placed transversely at a 90-degree angle using a sufficient amount of water-soluble ultrasound coupling gel, with minimal compression. The distance between the superficial fascia of the RF muscle and the femur was recorded, with the quadriceps muscle visible. Similarly, the STAR score was not developed for the present study; it was used as the previously published Sonographic Thigh Adjustment Ratio proposed by Kara et al. [21], calculated by dividing QMT expressed in millimeters by BMI. In the original STAR study, values 2 SD below the mean of healthy young adults were proposed as cutoffs for low muscle mass. In women, this reference value is set at 1.0 [21]. Since population-specific normative STAR values for the local population are limited, STAR was primarily analyzed as a continuous variable in all statistical models. A cutoff value of 1.0 was provided for clinical interpretation in women, and the distribution of STAR values in the study cohort was reported descriptively (Table 1). QMT was recorded in centimeters (cm), but STAR was calculated using the millimeter equivalent of QMT. In this manuscript, STAR is interpreted as an anthropometrically adjusted ultrasound-derived quadriceps-size measure rather than a direct measure of physiological reserve. Intrarater reliability for QMT and the STAR was assessed in a randomly selected subsample of 15 participants. All measurements were performed by a single experienced assessor. Each parameter was measured twice on the same day (intra-session reliability), with the US probe repositioned between measurements. The assessor was blinded to the initial results during the second assessment. Reliability was quantified using the intraclass correlation coefficient based on a two-way mixed-effects model with absolute agreement for single-measures (ICC(3,1)), and 95% confidence intervals were reported. The standard error of measurement (SEM) was calculated as SEM = SD × √(1 − ICC), and the minimal detectable change at the 95% confidence level was computed as MDC95 = 1.96 × √2 × SEM. Because all measurements were obtained by one assessor, interrater reliability was not evaluated.

Functional and postural measurements

During all functional tests, short rest intervals of approximately 10 s were given to ensure that participants’ fatigue and motivation differences did not affect the measurements.

The one-leg standing test (OLST)

Participants’ static balance was assessed by recording the time in seconds they could stand on one leg with their eyes open and closed. OLST was performed by asking participants to use their right and left legs alternately. Subjects were positioned in a barefoot position, standing upright on the supporting leg, with the opposite leg knee flexed approximately 90° and the thigh positioned parallel to the floor. During each trial, the duration of participants balancing on one leg was recorded with a stopwatch. The maximum duration was set at 30 s; if this time was reached, the test for that leg was considered complete. For participants who could not reach 30 s, they were allowed to make a maximum of three attempts, with 10-second rest intervals between these attempts. The longest time obtained from three trials was recorded as the valid value for the relevant leg. In the present study, both open- and closed-eye trials were recorded. Results were reported as right-leg (OLST-R) and left-leg (OLST-L) times, and the analysis used the mean of the 2 legs [mean OLST = (OLST-R + OLST-L)/2]. This approach prioritized overall static balance performance; side-to-side asymmetry was outside the scope of the present analysis. This represents a study-specific modification of the protocol described by Khanal et al., who used an eyes-open summed score from both legs [22]. Standardized rest intervals were provided to reduce fatigue and learning effects. The OLST is a widely used clinical measure of static balance with established reliability in older adults. Previous studies have demonstrated that OLST performance is associated with muscle mass, strength, and physical function, supporting its use as a screening tool for balance impairment [22].

Berg balance scale (BBS)

The BBS is a 14-item performance-based clinical scale developed to assess postural control and functional balance. It evaluates static and dynamic balance during tasks commonly performed in daily life. Each item is scored from 0 to 4, and the total score ranges from 0 to 56 [23]. In the present study, the validated Turkish version described by Sahin et al. was used [24].

Q angle measurement

The Q angle is a widely used clinical measure of lower-extremity alignment; however, its clinical value in representing dynamic knee biomechanics remains debated in the literature [10]. Static Q angle was assessed using a standardized standing clinical protocol. Participants stood barefoot in a neutral stance with feet shoulder-width apart, knees fully extended, the pelvis in a neutral position, and equal weight borne on both lower extremities. The Q angle was defined as the angle between the line connecting the anterior superior iliac spine and the midpoint of the patella and the line connecting the tibial tubercle and the midpoint of the patella [10]. Measurements were performed by the same examiner using a goniometer, repeated three times on each side, and averaged for the analyses. The bilateral mean was selected to represent overall static alignment and to limit multiplicity; asymmetry-specific analyses were not planned. Given that Q-angle measurements are known to vary according to subject positioning and measurement technique, and that no universally accepted standard measurement protocol exists, the values in the present study should be interpreted as protocol-specific standing static Q-angle measurements [10]. Static Q-angle measurements obtained by goniometry have demonstrated variable reliability depending on the measurement technique and protocol [10].

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 22.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Continuous variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median (25th–75th percentile) according to distributional properties. Categorical variables were expressed as frequencies and percentages. Normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test together with evaluation of skewness and kurtosis values. For between-group comparisons (fallers vs. non-fallers), the independent samples t-test was used for normally distributed variables (after checking homogeneity of variance with Levene’s test), whereas the Mann–Whitney U test was used for non-normally distributed variables.

Based on clinical relevance and prior literature, age, BMI, and serum 25(OH)D levels were considered potential confounders and were included in multivariable models. TK angle, mean Q angle, and STAR were included to address the prespecified study aims. Other potentially relevant factors, including physical activity, frailty status, pain severity, detailed medication burden beyond the exclusion criteria, and vertebral fracture severity, were not available for modeling and were therefore considered possible sources of residual confounding.

Potential confounding was reduced by restrictive eligibility criteria, standardized measurement procedures, use of the same assessor/device where applicable, and multivariable adjustment in regression analyses. Homogeneity of variance for parametric comparisons was evaluated with Levene’s test. Because this was an observational cross-sectional study, additional control of potential selection and information bias was attempted through consecutive recruitment, predefined eligibility criteria, and standardized single-visit assessments.

Potential sources of bias were considered a priori, including selection bias (minimized through consecutive recruitment), recall bias (related to self-reported fall history), and measurement bias (reduced through standardized protocols and single-assessor measurements).

Associations between TK angle, Q angle, and clinical, functional, and biomechanical parameters were examined using Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient. Spearman correlation was preferred because several study variables were bounded or ordinal in nature, showed ceiling effects (e.g., BBS score and OLST duration), or represented count-type data (e.g., number of falls), which may deviate from normality. Pearson correlation requires assumptions of approximate normality, linearity, and bivariate normal distribution; therefore, Spearman correlation was considered a more robust method for evaluating monotonic associations in this dataset.

To control for multiple testing in correlation analyses (Table 2) and univariable group comparisons (Tables 3 and 4), p-values were adjusted using the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) procedure. Both nominal p-values and FDR-adjusted q-values are reported. Statistical significance for these analyses was accepted at q < 0.05.

Multivariable logistic regression analysis (enter method) was performed to examine variables associated with fall history (dependent variable: fall in the last year; 0 = no, 1 = yes), rather than to develop a clinical prediction model. Regression coefficients (B), standard errors, Wald statistics, odds ratios (OR), and 95% confidence intervals (CI) were reported. Model fit was assessed using the Omnibus test of model coefficients and the Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test. Discriminative ability was further evaluated using exploratory Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curve analysis and area under the curve (AUC) with 95% confidence intervals; no clinical cut-off values were derived for practice. For STAR, lower values were expected to indicate less muscle-related size; therefore, inverse discrimination was reported with the complementary AUC (1 − AUC) for interpretive clarity. Multiple linear regression analysis was conducted to examine variables associated with balance performance (BBS score) in the adjusted model. Assumptions of linear regression were evaluated as follows: normality of residuals was examined using histograms and normal P–P plots of standardized residuals; homoscedasticity was assessed via scatter plots of standardized residuals versus predicted values; outliers were checked using standardized residuals (± 3 limits); independence of errors was confirmed using the Durbin–Watson statistic. Multicollinearity was assessed using variance inflation factor (VIF), tolerance values, and condition index. No problematic multicollinearity was observed (VIF range: 1.32–2.76; maximum condition index: 3.61). An adjusted prediction plot derived from the final multivariable linear regression model (Model 2) was generated to illustrate the association between TK angle and BBS score.

Intra-rater reliability of QMT and STAR measurements was evaluated using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC), calculated with a two-way mixed-effects model for absolute agreement (single measurement; ICC(3,1)), along with 95% confidence intervals and standard error of measurement (SEM).

All tests were two-tailed, and statistical significance was set at p < 0.05 for multivariable analyses.

Results

Participant flow and data completeness

A total of 125 individuals were assessed for eligibility; 18 were excluded prior to enrollment (10 did not meet inclusion criteria and 8 met exclusion criteria), and 7 declined participation. One hundred participants were enrolled, completed the assessments, and were included in the final analyses. No missing data were observed for the variables included in the primary analyses (Fig. 1).

Intrarater reliability of the ultrasonographic measures was excellent for QMT (ICC(3,1) = 0.94, 95% CI: 0.88–0.98; SEM = 0.42 mm [0.042 cm]). Intrarater reliability was also high for STAR (ICC(3,1) = 0.93, 95% CI: 0.86–0.97; SEM = 0.03). The corresponding MDC95 values were 1.16 mm [0.116 cm] for QMT and 0.08 for STAR. As all measurements were performed by a single assessor, interrater reliability was not assessed.

Baseline characteristics

The cohort consisted of 100 PM women with osteoporosis (Table 1). Overall balance performance was moderately reduced (median BBS 41), and the mean TK angle and mean Q angle were 40.07° and 20.15°, respectively (Table 1).

Bivariate associations (correlations)

Higher TK angle and higher Q angle were strongly associated with poorer balance performance (e.g., OLST open and BBS; all p < 0.001) and were moderately to strongly associated with reduced quadriceps US measures (QMT and STAR) (Table 2). TK and Q angle were also strongly correlated with each other (r = 0.700, p < 0.001) (Table 2).

Fallers vs. non-fallers comparisons

Compared with non-fallers (n = 49), participants with a fall history (n = 51) demonstrated higher TK and higher Q angle, along with worse performance on balance tests (OLST and BBS) (Table 3). Fallers also showed lower QMT and lower STAR values (Table 4).

Multivariable models

In multivariable logistic regression (dependent variable: self-reported fall in the previous year), TK angle remained associated with self-reported fall history in the adjusted exploratory model including age, BMI, 25(OH)D, mean Q angle, and STAR mean (OR = 1.444 per 1° increase, 95% CI: 1.143–1.824, p = 0.002). Higher mean STAR was associated with lower odds of reporting a self-reported fall in the previous year (OR = 0.35 per 0.1-unit increase; 95% CI: 0.15–0.79; p = 0.011). Mean Q angle did not remain associated with self-reported falls in the adjusted model (OR = 1.137, 95% CI: 0.842–1.535, p = 0.401) (Table 5). In the adjusted multiple linear regression model for balance performance (BBS score; Model 2), TK angle, mean Q angle, and STAR mean were associated with BBS score (Table 6). For interpretability, the regression coefficients indicate that a 10° higher TK angle corresponds to an approximately 3.35-point lower BBS score, a 5° higher mean Q angle corresponds to an approximately 3.82-point lower BBS score; conversely, a 0.1 higher STAR value corresponds to an approximately 1.96-point higher BBS score (Table 6). Collinearity diagnostics indicated no problematic multicollinearity across the multivariable models (VIF range: 1.32–2.76; maximum condition index: 3.61).

Discriminative performance (ROC)

Exploratory ROC analysis showed discrimination for TK angle and mean Q angle in distinguishing participants with and without self-reported fall history (AUC = 0.911, 95% CI: 0.855–0.967 and AUC = 0.883, 95% CI: 0.812–0.954, respectively) (Fig. 3). For STAR, the ROC analysis yielded an AUC below 0.50 (AUC = 0.117, 95% CI: 0.054–0.180), indicating discrimination in the inverse direction because lower STAR values were associated with falls. Accordingly, the complementary AUC (1 − AUC) is 0.883 (95% CI: 0.820–0.946) and was presented only for interpretive clarity (Fig. 3).

Effect sizes

The associations between posture/alignment measures and balance outcomes were of large magnitude. For example, TK angle showed a strong negative correlation with OLST (eyes open) (r = − 0.737) and BBS (r = − 0.657), and mean Q angle showed a strong negative correlation with BBS (r = − 0.706) (Table 2). Subgroup comparisons also demonstrated large effects: fallers had lower BBS scores than non-fallers (median 37 vs. 47; Mann–Whitney effect size r ≈ 0.66) and worse OLST (Table 3). In the multivariable linear regression model (Model 2), the coefficients indicate that a 10° higher TK angle corresponds to an approximately 3.35-point lower BBS score, a 5° higher mean Q angle corresponds to an approximately 3.82-point lower BBS score, and a 0.1 higher STAR value corresponds to an approximately 1.96-point higher BBS score (Table 6).

Discussion

The present study has four main findings. First, greater radiographic TK was associated with poorer balance performance and was associated with a history of falls in the adjusted exploratory model. Second, lower ultrasound-derived quadriceps-size index (STAR) was associated with a history of falls and lower BBS scores. Third, a larger Q angle was related to worse balance, but its association with falls did not remain significant after multivariable adjustment. Fourth, TK and Q angle were strongly correlated, suggesting possible shared variance between sagittal spinal alignment and lower-extremity alignment. Among these findings, TK and STAR remained significantly associated with a history of falls in multivariable analysis, whereas the association between Q angle and falls attenuated after adjustment.

The TK findings are broadly consistent with previous studies showing that hyperkyphosis is associated with impaired balance [3], as well as with an increased risk of falls in older adults [3, 25]. However, our results differ from those of Nikolaou et al. [7], who did not observe a significant association between annual change in kyphosis and falls in PM women with osteopenia or osteoporosis, and from McDaniels-Davidson et al. [8], who found that radiographic Cobb-angle kyphosis was not associated with falls in older men. These discrepancies may reflect differences in study population, fall ascertainment, kyphosis assessment, and confounder structure [7, 8]. More specifically, a single radiographic Cobb-angle measure, a longitudinal kyphosis-change metric, and the blocks method capture related but not fully equivalent aspects of kyphotic posture [7, 8]. Taken together, the literature remains heterogeneous; within this context, our findings suggest an association between TK and fall-related outcomes in PM osteoporosis that requires prospective validation.

Several mechanisms may underlie the association between greater TK, poorer balance, and falls. Forward curvature of the upper trunk may shift the center of mass anteriorly and bring it closer to the limits of stability, thereby requiring additional corrective responses [5, 25]. Hyperkyphosis has also been associated with greater body sway and gait unsteadiness [5], and, in osteoporotic women, greater kyphosis has been linked to lower trunk extensor strength [4, 5]. In osteoporotic individuals, particularly in the presence of vertebral deformity, these biomechanical disadvantages may be further compounded by chronic back pain and alterations in trunk joint position sense [4, 9]. Compensation for sagittal malalignment may also extend beyond the spine to the pelvis and lower limbs, including the knee and ankle [9]. Within this framework, the strong correlation between TK and Q angle observed in our cohort may be compatible with interconnected changes across axial and lower-extremity segments. However, this interpretation remains hypothesis-generating, and the present cross-sectional data cannot determine whether lower-extremity alignment represents adaptation, shared disease severity, or residual confounding.

The Q-angle findings should therefore be interpreted with caution. In our cohort, a larger Q angle was associated with poorer BBS and OLST performance; this observation is consistent with previous studies suggesting that abnormalities in lower-extremity alignment may adversely affect postural stability and fall risk [10, 11]. Nevertheless, the attenuation of the association in the adjusted fall model suggests that Q angle may be more closely related to balance performance than to fall history once TK and STAR are considered. This interpretation is clinically plausible, as falls are multifactorial events and cannot be explained by static alignment alone [2]. The non-significant adjusted association with falls may reflect insufficient power, shared variance with TK, limitations of protocol-specific static Q-angle measurement, residual confounding, or a true lack of independent association with self-reported falls [10]. By contrast, the persistent association of lower STAR with both fall history and lower BBS scores is consistent with previous US-based evidence linking reduced quadriceps muscle size to falls and STAR to mobility-related performance, and it aligns with the broader osteosarcopenia framework [12, 13, 21]. This finding is biologically plausible, as reduced quadriceps muscle size may contribute to impaired balance-related function and increased fall vulnerability in osteoporosis. Nevertheless, STAR should be interpreted here as an associated sonographic marker rather than a validated predictor, causal factor, or treatment target.

A strength of this study is the integrated evaluation of radiographic spinal alignment, lower-extremity alignment, US-derived quadriceps parameters, and validated clinical balance tests in the same cohort of PM women with osteoporosis. However, several limitations should be emphasized. The cross-sectional design precludes causal inference and raises the possibility of reverse causality. Fall history was self-reported, may be influenced by recall bias, and did not distinguish recurrent falls, injurious falls, near-falls, or fall mechanisms. The single-center sample of independently ambulatory PM women may limit generalizability and may have excluded higher-risk osteoporosis patients. Residual confounding cannot be excluded, particularly for factors such as physical activity, frailty, pain severity, vertebral fracture burden/severity, and medication exposure not captured beyond the exclusion criteria. Kyphosis may therefore partly represent overall frailty or disease severity rather than a purely biomechanical driver. The static/manual assessment of alignment may not fully capture dynamic biomechanics during gait or perturbation, and the use of bilateral mean values may obscure clinically relevant asymmetry. Furthermore, ultrasonographic measurements were performed by a single assessor, precluding evaluation of inter-rater reliability, and blinding to fall history was not formally implemented. Finally, the sample size was calculated for correlation analysis, so multivariable and ROC results should be interpreted as exploratory.

Despite these limitations, the findings should not be interpreted as evidence that the assessed variables predict future falls or that specific rehabilitation targets reduce falls. Rather, they identify preliminary associations among sagittal alignment, static lower-extremity alignment, ultrasound-derived quadriceps-size indices, and balance/fall history in PM osteoporosis. Prospective studies with fall monitoring, broader confounder assessment, external replication, and prespecified clinical thresholds are needed to confirm temporality, predictive value, and clinical applicability.

Conclusion

In this cross-sectional cohort of PM women with osteoporosis, greater radiographic TK angle and lower STAR were associated with poorer balance performance and self-reported fall history, whereas Q angle was associated with balance performance but not with fall history after multivariable adjustment. These findings should be considered preliminary and hypothesis-generating. No causal inference, predictive utility, clinical implementation threshold, or rehabilitation benefit can be inferred from the present data. Longitudinal studies with prospective fall monitoring, more comprehensive confounder control, and external validation are needed.
