A scoping review of autoethnography in nursing
Authors: Martin Salzmann-Erikson
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveAutoethnography combines personal experiences with cultural analysis, emerging as a response to the limitations of traditional ethnography. This review aimed to explore, describe, and delineate the utilization of autoethnography by nurses published in peer-reviewed journals.MethodsA scoping review was conducted according to the Arksey and O’Malley framework. On October 12, 2023, autoethnographic studies in nursing were identified through searches of CINAHL, PubMed, PsycINFO, and Scopus. Peer-reviewed articles published in English language were retrieved. We applied no date restriction. Data were extracted on nursing, epiphany, results, style of writing, implications for nursing, and ethical considerations.ResultsTwenty-six articles met the inclusion criteria. Mental health nursing, covered by nine articles, elucidated experiences of stigma, ethical dilemmas, and professional identity. Nursing education, represented by seven articles, highlighted identity struggles, systemic biases, and evolving pedagogies. Palliative care, addressed by three articles, provided insights into communication challenges and emotional complexities in end-of-life care. The remaining articles explored rehabilitation, cultural competence, and chronic pain management. A conceptual framework integrating ontological, epistemological, ethical, and practical dimensions was developed, emphasizing the interplay between personal and professional roles.ConclusionsThis review underscores autoethnography’s value in uncovering the cultural and ethical dimensions of nursing. This framework advocates for a reflective, culturally attuned approach to healthcare, fostering transformative changes in nursing. Further research should explore underrepresented nursing specialties to harness autoethnography’s potential fully.

Introduction

“We know so many things but don’t know ourselves!” (Meister Eckhart, n.d.)

Autoethnography is a research methodology that intertwines personal narrative with cultural analysis and reflection, as elucidated by Ellis et al. [1]. The ‘Auto’ component involves deep introspection, allowing researchers to explore their experiences and perspectives. The ‘Ethno’ aspect integrates these personal narratives with broader cultural and social phenomena, highlighting the interaction between individual and cultural dynamics. The ‘graphy’ part represents the systematic documentation and analysis of these experiences using rigorous research methods, as Snyder [2] describes.

Autoethnography positions the individual’s story within a broader social framework, blending narrative depth with analytical acuity to dissect the dynamic interplay of personal journeys and their broader cultural implications. This process of self-reflection is not just introspective; it serves as a conduit for understanding one’s place and values within the larger societal mosaic, enriching sociological discourse [3]. Echoing this sentiment, Denzin [4] underscores the pivotal role of significant personal ‘epiphanies’ in autoethnography, which deepen the comprehension of the intricate tapestry of personal narratives entwined with the cultural, social, and political landscape. Storytelling elements—character, scene, plot—are employed to create narratives that are both aesthetically rich and emotionally resonant [5].

Autoethnography reveals the intricacies of personal and professional experiences and encourages a deeper, more empathetic understanding of an experience or situation in the researcher’s life [6]. Le Roux [7] articulates a dichotomy within autoethnographic research, delineating evocative and analytic methodologies. The evocative approach is characterized by its aim to forge an emotional rapport between author and reader, employing vivid storytelling and rich descriptions to provoke emotional engagement. Conversely, Ellis et al. [6] further elaborates on the evocative approach, emphasizing its reliance on narrative techniques to compellingly present personal and shared experiences.

In contrast, analytic autoethnography employs empirical data to illuminate broader social phenomena, integrating fieldwork, interpretive qualitative data, and systematic data analysis. The goal is to develop theoretical insights, engaging readers in a deeper exploration of cultural, social, or personal issues [7]. Anderson [8] presents a framework for analytic autoethnography, emphasizing complete member researcher status for genuine immersion and an insider perspective. This approach integrates analytic reflexivity, with the researcher critically evaluating their role throughout the study and narrative visibility, highlighting personal experiences within academic discourse. Additionally, Anderson stresses the importance of dialogic engagement with others to broaden perspectives and enrich the research. This method also commits to theoretical analysis, blending personal and empirical insights to enhance and refine broader theoretical frameworks.

Autoethnography, characterized by integrating personal narrative with cultural analysis and reflection, represents a significant methodological shift within qualitative research, offering profound insights into the interplay between individual experiences and broader cultural dynamics [1,2]. Originating as a response to the limitations of traditional ethnographic approaches, autoethnography transcends the mere representation of the ‘other,’ advocating for a self-reflexive exploration that blurs the lines between researcher and subject [4,9,10]. This paradigmatic shift towards introspection and personal narrative has enriched various disciplines by providing a deeper understanding of complex social phenomena. Nurses have long recognized the value of ethnography in capturing the nuanced realities of patient care, healthcare systems, and the nursing profession itself, given its focus on understanding the cultural, social, and personal dimensions of health and illness [[11], [12], [13]]. The application of autoethnography within nursing has extended this ethnographic tradition, providing a platform for nurses to explore and articulate their own experiences within the healthcare landscape. Autoethnography thus serves as a conduit for producing accessible, insightful texts that foster dialogue and positive change in healthcare practices, emphasizing vulnerability, transparency, and emotional openness as key elements in nursing research [14]; however, little research has been conducted on how nurses have used autoethnography as a method to deepen insights in nursing. Nurses’ subjective experiences and personal epiphanies provide invaluable perspectives often overlooked in traditional research methods. Autoethnography allows for a nuanced exploration of these experiences, offering a rich, reflective understanding that quantitative studies cannot capture. Personal narratives can reveal nursing practice’s emotional and ethical complexities, contributing to a more holistic view of patient care. Emphasizing the importance of subjective experiences in research can lead to more empathetic and effective nursing practices, ultimately enhancing patient outcomes and professional satisfaction. A comprehensive review that examines the methodological approaches adopted in these studies is lacking; this gap underscores the need to review the existing autoethnographies written by nurses.

Addressing this gap, this review aimed to provide a methodological overview of autoethnography’s application in nursing, assessing its potential to offer deeper, more empathetic understandings of the nursing profession. By exploring the diverse ways nurses have employed autoethnography, this review seeks to highlight its value in enhancing the depth and breadth of nursing science, ultimately contributing to the development of more holistic and compassionate nursing practices. Thus, the aim was to explore, describe, and delineate the utilization of autoethnography by nurses published in peer-reviewed journals.

Methods

Design of the review

The scoping review methodology was employed [15]; this method has previously been successfully utilized to investigate and review other nursing-related queries [16]. Arksey and O’Malley describe the scoping review approach as adept at accommodating the broad and exploratory nature of emerging research fields, allowing for identifying key concepts and themes across a diverse body of literature. Unlike systematic reviews, which are narrowly focused and methodologically stringent, scoping reviews offer a broader, more flexible framework that is conducive to synthesizing heterogeneous studies. This methodology supports the comprehensive mapping of existing literature and facilitates the development of a conceptual framework.

Identifying the research problem

Existing literature indicates that autoethnography, which combines personal narrative with cultural analysis, has been used to explore complex social and cultural dynamics. It has particularly highlighted issues related to identity, systemic biases, and emotional impacts. However, there is a need for a review of how autoethnography is applied across various nursing contexts and the ethical considerations involved. This review seeks to fill this gap by providing a comprehensive overview of the characteristics, areas of application, and methodological approaches of autoethnographic studies in nursing. The research questions are ‘What are the characteristics of the publications?’, ‘In what areas of nursing are autoethnography used?’, ‘What ethical considerations are addressed?’, and ‘How do nurses incorporate evocative writing style?’

Literature search

To formulate criteria for inclusion and exclusion, the Population-Concept-Context (PPC) framework was used [17]. The inclusion criteria were formulated to be: a) original research articles; b) autoethnography as the method; c) at least one of the authors should be a nurse; d) address the private sphere of a nurse or the work-life; e) written in English; and f) qualitative study design. The exclusion criteria were: a) editorials/commentaries/perspectives, method articles, and theses; b) articles that were not considered relevant to nursing; c) quantitative study designs; d) articles that only use ‘autoethnography’ in the title, abstract or keywords but not in the article text. No limitations were set regarding publication year or geographic area. Four databases were chosen as they index a broad range of nursing journals and nursing research: The Cumulative Index to Nursing and Allied Health (CINAHL), PubMed, PsycINFO, and Scopus. The search string “autoethnography AND nurs∗” was used in all databases and executed on October 12, 2023.

Selection of sources

The search resulted in 287 records; after duplication removal, 157 records remained. These were screened on title and abstract, from which 98 records were excluded due to the criteria. During full-text reading and data extraction from the remaining 59 articles, 33 articles were excluded. Hence, the final sample consisted of 26 articles that all met the criteria for inclusion, see the PRISMA 2020 flowchart in Fig. 1. Two articles [18,19] authored by the same author were considered part of a single study and treated accordingly to ensure methodological rigor. This approach aligns with guidance provided by the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions [20], which recommends consolidating findings from related studies to prevent overrepresentation and potential bias. Quality assessment is not necessary for a scoping review [21]. Moreover, since this review does not intend only to include methodological strong autoethnographies but rather explore nurses’ use of the method, it was decided not to assess the quality of the articles and to ensure that articles that only used the term autoethnography in the title, abstract or keywords were not included, these were excluded as an exclusion criterion.Fig. 1Flowchart of the study selection process.Fig. 1

Data extraction and data analysis

The following data points were extracted from all 26 articles: author names and number of author(s), publication year, country, journal name, area of nursing, sample, epiphany, evocative writing, ethical considerations, and implication for nursing (Appendix A). The articles were read several times in full text to become familiarized; notes were taken. The formulation of the results was inspired by Braun and Clarke’s [22] thematic analysis to formulate the results.

Results

The analysis of the 26 included articles revealed that autoethnographies in nursing are mainly published by authors in Australia and New Zeeland (n = 10), U.K. (n = 10), USA (n = 2) and Canada (n = 3), and one article from Turkey. The two dominant fields were mental health nursing (n = 9) and education (n = 7). Nurses publish autoethnographies as single-author publications (n = 11, 42%). However, nine studies were published by two or three authors (35%). The results are presented in categories corresponding to different areas in nursing. An overview of the included articles is presented in Appendix B.

Implications of different aspects of care

Mental health nursing

Mental health nursing emerged as the predominant focus in 9 of 26 articles (35%). Within this domain, Peterson [18,19] reflects on his dual identity as nurse and patient, exploring self-identification and stigma among colleagues. Similarly, Grant [23] details his struggle with alcoholism, examining the social and cultural stigma associated with it. Kidd and Finlayson [24] combine personal and collective experiences of mental illness among nurses, highlighting the difficulties of balancing clinical duties with mental health challenges. Collectively, these studies address the internal and external stigma faced by mental health nurses experiencing illness themselves [18,19,23,24]. In a different context, Molloy [25] critiques the dominance of biomedical models in mental health care for Indigenous populations, arguing they reinforce white privilege by neglecting culturally specific care. Wyder et al. [26] contrast this by focusing on the limitations of biomedical models in acute psychiatric care, pointing out the disconnect between clinical recovery and patients’ personal recovery narratives. Hodge [27] reflects on the ethical challenge of validating dementia patients’ experiences versus reorienting them to objective reality, stressing the context-dependent nature of such decisions. Hayward [28] examines the role of mental health nurses in bushfire-affected communities in Australia, outlining strategies for prioritizing care. Similarly, Whybrow [29] shares his experiences establishing relationships with soldiers as a military mental health nurse, developing a communication template for liaising with Officer Command to streamline his work. These three articles [25,28,29] focus on the authors’ objective and task-related experiences, in contrast to the more private and emotional narratives seen in earlier studies [18,19,23,24]. Taken together, autoethnography encourages mental health nurses to engage in reflective practice, examining their roles, biases, and interactions with patients. This reflective process is key to professional growth and development in the nursing field and underscores the complex interplay between the personal experiences of mental health professionals and the broader cultural and institutional contexts in which they operate; thus, it is crucial for professional growth and development.

Nursing education

Seven articles focused on nursing education. Gardner and Lane [30] explored the tutor-student relationship, where the student has a mental illness. Although in mental health education, the article was categorized under nursing education because it focuses on the teacher’s identity and crossing professional boundaries. It examines the complexities of getting too close, setting boundaries, and balancing teaching with the mental health nurse role. The authors emphasize the reciprocal dynamic between teacher and student, analyzing the emotional and ethical challenges inherent in such relationships within the educational context. Their article is not just a recounting of a personal tutor-student relationship but a critical examination of the complexities, emotional labor, and ethical considerations inherent in such dynamics, especially in mental health nursing education. Similar to that article, an exploration of self-identity as nurse educators was given focus in two other articles [31,32]. Summers [31] reflects on his transformation as a learner, counselor, and educator, while Wright [32] examines how personal and cultural contexts shape her identity as a female nurse and lifelong learner. Both studies address challenges in identity formation, such as negotiating boundaries, adapting to roles, and integrating personal experiences into teaching. They highlight the complex interplay between personal experiences, professional relationships, and educational contexts, offering deeper insights into the evolving identity of nurse educators. Two studies [33,34] emphasize the need to address systemic racism in nursing education. Taylor et al. [34] explore racial autoethnography’s role in fostering critical inquiry and challenging ideologies, while Mayoum et al. [33] examine the practical challenges of implementing an antiracism action plan. Both studies stress the importance of integrating anti-racist praxis into nursing curricula, focusing on the need to challenge and transform systemic structures that perpetuate racism actively. Winnington and Cook [35] used collaborative autoethnography to examine the experiences of academic women during COVID-19. Their study highlights how the pandemic worsened gender inequities, particularly in undervalued roles like administrative teaching and pastoral care, which do not aid career advancement. The articles [[33], [34], [35]] challenge the systemic structures and norms that perpetuate marginalization in their respective fields. Garvey et al. [36] explored how the flipped classroom model shapes nursing educators’ professional identity. Themes include identity transformation, evolving teaching methods, student learning improvements, and resource alignment and skill development challenges. The study highlights educators’ uncertainties as they adapt to new roles, impacting self-concept and teaching. These studies emphasize autoethnography as a reflective tool for examining nursing education’s complexities, including personal and professional challenges, systemic biases, and evolving identities. They advocate for more inclusive and effective educational practices, revealing the importance of addressing these nuances in nursing education.

Palliative nursing

In palliative nursing, three key studies [[37], [38], [39]] offer insights into the complexities of end-of-life care. One autoethnography [37] focused on the communication strategies of clinical nurse specialists, particularly in handling Do Not Attempt Cardiopulmonary Resuscitation (DNACPR) conversations. It highlighted the balance between clarity and sensitivity, addressing misconceptions held by patients and families. The study emphasized the responsibility of initiating difficult conversations, the learning involved, and the challenges faced in DNACPR discussions. Harris et al. [38] explored the emotional experience of caring for a loved one with motor neuron disease, focusing on themes of loss—of person, relationships, home, and future. The study underscores the challenges of achieving a meaningful death and the importance of person-centered care, especially for those with limited mobility and communication. Serçekuş [39]. Provided an autoethnographic account of a nurse who also served as a caregiver for a relative with end-stage cancer. These studies navigate the emotional complexities of palliative care, from DNACPR conversations [37] to the intersection of personal and professional caregiving responsibilities [38,39]. Serçekuş’s [39] narrative vividly portrays the intersection of personal and professional roles as she cares for her dying father. As both a nurse and academic, she recounts the added pressures and ethical challenges, particularly when medical staff expected her to perform procedures like catheterization on her father. This highlights the psychological strain such dual roles impose. These studies emphasize the ethical dilemmas and professional challenges inherent in palliative nursing [[37], [38], [39]]. These studies underscore the importance of clear, sensitive communication, caregiver support, and person-centered care in palliative nursing. Autoethnography enriches understanding by fostering empathy and relatability among healthcare professionals. The narratives advocate viewing palliative care not merely as a clinical practice but as a deeply human, interpersonal field. They highlight the interconnected and emotionally impactful experiences of patients, families, and caregivers, revealing the complex dynamics that define palliative nursing.

Miscellaneous

Seven articles did not fit a specific care context but used autoethnography across various nursing fields. Three of these involved authors who were both nurses and patients. Lawson et al. [40] explored a nurse’s rehabilitation journey after a traumatic brain injury, while Priddis [41] examined severe perineal trauma, blending her personal experience with those of other women. Priddis’s study highlighted the power of reflective research to drive change in health services, emphasizing the importance of self-determination and client-centered care. Both studies exposed the gap between theoretical client-centered care and the often systemic, impersonal practices in healthcare. White and Seibold’s [42] study explored life with chronic non-malignant back pain, highlighting the physical, social, and emotional struggles of sufferers. It called for healthcare professionals to reconsider their approaches to pain management. Four other articles employed autoethnography alongside traditional qualitative methods, enhanced by personal reflections. Choi et al. [43] examined the implementation of a culturally tailored diabetes education program in Australia, emphasizing the challenges of applying evidence-based interventions in multicultural settings. The study stressed the need for flexibility, cultural competence, and adapting care to patient needs to bridge the gap between research and practice.

Clancy’s [45] paper explores poetry to analyze the emotional and professional challenges of children’s nursing, demonstrating poetry’s cathartic role in expressing complex emotions and enhancing empathy and self-awareness among nurses. Nilson’s [46] study reflected on a non-Aboriginal White researcher’s experience in an Aboriginal Australian community, emphasizing the ongoing nature of cultural competence and the importance of building respectful relationships. These studies show how autoethnography adds reflexivity and introspection while closely aligned with traditional qualitative methods. They illustrate that autoethnography can enrich research by incorporating personal insights without requiring firsthand experience of a specific condition, broadening the exploration of professional and cultural themes in nursing.

Ethics in autoethnography

Nine studies did not address any ethical considerations at all [26,29,32,34,35,39,44,45]. On the contrary, proportionally equal, another nine studies received ethical committee approval [24,25,33,36,37,[40], [41], [42], [43]]. The diverse approaches to ethics in these studies highlight the complexity and multifaceted nature of ethical considerations in autoethnographic research. The articles showcase diverse approaches to addressing ethical considerations, particularly in determining who is included in the data and the need for ethical approval. Some argue that autoethnography, a “one-person’s show,” does not require ethical approval [28,38]. Harris et al. [38] contended that no full ethics review was needed as no participants were recruited. In contrast, Peterson [19] held that “those included in the researcher’s stories are considered participants” (p. 315), emphasizing the paradox of seeking approval from those who may have perpetuated the stigma. When others are included in autoethnographies, they must be protected through anonymity and confidentiality, similar to conventional research methods [19,23,24,28,31,33,37]. Hayward [28] was the only author to mention relational ethics explicitly, referencing Andrew’s [47] exposure grid. Summers [31] explores the complexities of informed consent as a researcher and subject in autoethnography, raising questions about what constitutes public versus private. Both Grant [23] and Summers [31] acknowledged the emotional risks to autoethnographers, with Summers employing strategies such as seeking support from a counselor and supervisor. Grant [23] emphasizes that “the act of writing about oneself places an ethical duty on oneself” (p. 578). Ethical responsibilities are crucial, whether autoethnography focuses solely on the author or involves other subjects. Priddis [41], who interviewed women about perineal tears, refrained from sharing her own experience during interviews to avoid overshadowing the interviewees’ voices. Autoethnography presents ethical dilemmas along two axes: the responsibility to maintain the integrity of the self versus others who intersect the experience and whether the experience is solely owned by the autoethnography or shared with others. Writing style

Four studies [33,36,37,44] did not adopt an evocative writing style; instead, they reported findings in a typical qualitative manner, using third-person narration and participant quotes. Notably, three of these were multi-author articles (six to nine authors), but one, Hadley [37], was a single-author study that also did not employ evocative writing. However, having multiple authors in autoethnography does not preclude using an evocative style. For example, Lawson et al. [40], with three authors, each wrote in first-person from their individual perspectives: the first author described her recovery from multi-trauma, the third author—a rehabilitation therapist—shared her experiences with the first author, and a researcher on coping with illness contributed her firsthand account. Similarly, with three authors, Choi et al. [43] included the first author reflecting on her experience as an Australian-trained researcher of Chinese background implementing a culturally tailored diabetes program in Melbourne. The other authors contributed to the intervention and writing. Gardner and Lane [30] explored the tutor-student relationship in mental health nursing, written from the perspectives of both the student (the first author) and the tutor (the second author). Another multi-authored study [48] examined the racial experiences of an African American nursing faculty member and two white graduate students, using racial autoethnography to understand diverse health experiences in the U.S. Evocative writing is more common in mental health nursing and nursing education, areas where subjective, personal experiences are central. Methods like first-person narrative, poetry, and reflective diaries help create an evocative tone in these fields.

Four studies [33,36,37,44] did not use an evocative writing style. All these studies reported their findings as usually seen in general qualitative studies – in the third person and using participant quotes. What should be noted is that three of these studies were multi-author articles, with six to nine authors. However, one of these studies, Hadley [37], was a single-author article but did not use an evocative writing style. However, being several authors in an autoethnography does not necessarily mean that evocative writing styles are not applicable. Lawson et al. [40] were three authors, all of them writing in the first-person style from their perspectives. In that study, the first author described her situation and experience from recovery during full-time rehabilitation after multi-trauma, including brain injury, and then the third author, as a rehabilitation therapist, wrote her experiences from meeting with the first author. A third person, a researcher focused on coping with illness, was invited to the project and wrote her firsthand experiences. Similarly, there were three authors in the study of Choi et al. [43]. The first author described herself as the research instrument: “an Australian-trained researcher with a Chinese background […] reflected on her experience while translating an evidence-based culturally tailored Chinese diabetes education program into practice in Melbourne, Australia.” (p. 68). The other authors’ contribution was to implement the intervention and write with assistance from the third author. Gardner and Lane [30] explored the relationship between a tutor and a student. Their autoethnography was written from both the first author’s experience as a student in a mental health nursing program with mental issues herself and the second author as the I as being a tutor. Other multi-authored autoethnography accounted for three people’s experiences: one being an American woman and a nursing faculty member, and two white graduate nursing students used racial autoethnography to learn about women and families’ different health experiences in the USA [48]. The use of evocative writing styles varies across the studies. Evocative writing appears more appealing to those who write in the context of mental health nursing and nursing education, reflecting these areas’ subjective and deeply personal nature. The use of first-person narrative, poetry, and reflective diaries are some of the methods employed to create an evocative tone.

Implication in nursing

The implications of autoethnographic research in nursing can be categorized into four key areas, each representing significant contributions to the field. Enhancing nursing practice and patient-centered care involves integrating culturally tailored interventions, improving communication on sensitive topics, and addressing both physical and emotional recovery needs [25,26,37,39,[41], [42], [43], [44]]. These studies emphasize the importance of understanding patients’ diverse needs to improve healthcare delivery. Improving nursing education highlights the need for professional development, supportive learning environments, critical self-reflection, and addressing systemic issues such as gendered labor divisions and antiracism strategies [[31], [32], [33],35,36,48]. This research underscores the role of educational frameworks in shaping nursing students’ and educators’ competencies and perspectives. Mental health awareness and support advocate for destigmatizing mental health issues, reducing nurse distress, and fostering systemic changes within nursing culture [18,19,23,24,30]. These studies emphasize creating supportive environments for patients and healthcare providers alike, promoting empathy and understanding in clinical and educational settings. Reflective practice and professional identity highlight the importance of extending care to consider patients’ past experiences, encouraging continuous reflexivity for cultural competence, and promoting reflective practices to balance personal and professional caregiving roles [28,38,39,46]. This body of research demonstrates the value of introspection in enhancing empathy and effectiveness in nursing practice. These categories collectively underscore the transformative potential of autoethnography in advancing nursing theory, research, education, and practice, fostering a more holistic and culturally competent approach to healthcare. The broad range of implications for nursing from these studies illustrates autoethnographic research’s diverse and profound impact (Fig. 3). The Powerful Navigation of Autoethnography in Nursing Science, presents a conceptual framework delineating four axes—ontology, epistemology, ethics, and praxis—each spanning a continuum between two polarities. Ontologically, the spectrum ranges from ‘Personal’ to ‘Professional,’ focusing on individual subjective experiences versus professional responsibilities. Epistemologically, the axis shifts from ‘Subjective’ to ‘Critical,’ moving from personal, emotional understanding to an analytical, systematic knowledge approach. Ethically, the axis transitions from ‘Prescriptive’ to ‘Relational,’ moving from rigid norms to context-driven, empathetic ethics in professional relationships. Finally, praxis extends from ‘Generic’ to ‘Specific,’ implying a shift from broad actions to those tailored to particular contexts or circumstances.Fig. 3The powerful navigation of autoethnograhy in nursing scienceFig. 3

Discussion

This discussion highlights the broader implications of autoethnography as a research method and its contribution to nursing. Synthesizing autoethnographic research across mental health nursing, nursing education, palliative care, and miscellaneous areas reveals the significant role of the ‘ethnic’ component in linking personal narratives to wider cultural and social phenomena [2]. The analyzed studies demonstrate how cultural dynamics in healthcare shape individual experiences in nursing.

In mental health nursing, nurses’ personal experiences with mental illness expose the cultural stigma and professional challenges within the nursing community [18,19,23,24]. Autoethnography’s ability to reveal stigma mirrors earlier research, such as Javaid’s [49] exploration of stigma faced by queer, Muslim, and single individuals and O’Connell’s [50] account of coping with deafness stigma. These studies highlight autoethnography’s power to uncover the complex dynamics of stigma across diverse social contexts, offering deeply personal yet broadly applicable insights. Such narratives illustrate how societal attitudes and professional norms shape the experiences of individuals balancing the roles of caregiver and patient. Autoethnography has been used in nursing education to explore identity and systemic biases, with studies outside nursing demonstrating its broader applicability. Kim [51] presents the racialized experiences of a Korean female scholar in U.S. higher education, emphasizing academic imperialism and the need for inclusivity. Similarly, Hughes [52] examines Black scholar-activism in predominantly White institutions, using autoethnography to reflect on key incidents shaped by racial dynamics. These studies show autoethnography’s ability to provide personal yet widely relevant insights into identity and systemic racism. Nurse educators’ and students’ experiences [[30], [31], [32]] echo these findings, highlighting the role of cultural and institutional factors in shaping pedagogical approaches and professional identity. Kim [51] and Hughes [52] offer valuable perspectives on using autoethnography to address mental health and racism in nursing education.

In palliative care, autoethnography illustrates end-of-life care’s emotional and ethical complexities, revealing the cultural nuances of communication, caregiving, and ethical decision-making [[37], [38], [39]]. These studies emphasize the need for culturally sensitive communication, respecting patients’ and families’ diverse backgrounds and values. Warne and Hoppes highlight the emotional toll on healthcare providers, as seen through an occupational therapy student’s profound connection with a terminal patient, which, while enriching, also underscores the strain of witnessing the end-of-life journey. This aligns with palliative nursing studies that emphasize balancing clinical professionalism with personal emotional responses [[37], [38], [39]]. Sensitivity, empathy, and person-centered care are essential for addressing the emotional needs of both patients and caregivers. The parallels between Warne and Hoppes [53] and other palliative studies underscore the shared emotional and ethical challenges, advocating for a holistic approach to care that addresses physical, emotional, and psychological needs, fostering a compassionate environment for a dignified end-of-life experience. Nath [54] deepens this discussion by exploring the emotional intricacies of forming deep attachments during terminal illness and the impact on the researcher’s personal and academic self. These autoethnographic studies converge on universal themes of empathy, connection, and human growth in challenging circumstances. Autoethnography in palliative care nursing offers a unique lens through which nurses can reflect on their interactions with patients and families, illuminating the emotional and ethical dimensions of care and deepening the understanding of empathy, compassion, and person-centered care in nursing practice.

The miscellaneous category of nursing research, covering topics from rehabilitation to cultural competence, highlights the diverse experiences within the nursing profession. These studies show the value of personal insights in addressing the cultural and social challenges embedded in various nursing roles, from chronic pain management to culturally tailored healthcare interventions. The ‘ethno’ component in these narratives links individual experiences to broader cultural and social contexts, providing valuable perspectives on the complexities of healthcare from a deeply personal viewpoint. This synthesis underscores autoethnography’s significance in exploring the cultural dimensions of healthcare, fostering more effective, responsive, and holistic nursing practices. Autoethnography thus becomes a pivotal tool in nursing research, examining the cultural, social, and personal dimensions interwoven in healthcare.

As Peterson [14] discussed, Autoethnography departs from traditional objective approaches in qualitative research, encouraging nurses to explore their experiences within a cultural context. This method acknowledges the complexity of reality and emphasizes the importance of culture and context in understanding human experiences in healthcare. Using autoethnography allows nursing practitioners and researchers to gain deeper, more nuanced insights into their field. This approach engages readers emotionally, prompts reflection, and provides a unique opportunity to examine how personal and professional cultural identities influence nursing practices. Its significance lies in its ability to share stories that might otherwise go untold, offering nurses a platform to share their experiences and foster collective understanding and empathy within the profession.

Unified framework of autoethnography in nursing

The potential of autoethnography in nursing, as highlighted in Peterson’s [14] work, extends beyond mere narrative sharing. It fosters a critical examination of the cultural underpinnings in healthcare, encouraging a reflective and introspective approach that can lead to transformative changes in nursing practice. This method underscores the need for an empathetic, inclusive, and culturally sensitive approach in healthcare research and practice, recognizing nurses’ diverse experiences and perspectives. In essence, autoethnography contributes to the enrichment of nursing knowledge and advocates for a more holistic and responsive healthcare system, attuned to the cultural dynamics and personal narratives that shape the nursing landscape (Fig. 3).

Synthesizing these axes, the figure seems to suggest that in the practice of nursing—or potentially any professional field—there is a dynamic interplay between one’s personal identity and professional role, how knowledge is understood and applied, and the adaptability of one’s actions from generic standards to specific situations. The framework could serve as a heuristic for examining the nature of professional practice, particularly in fields such as nursing that require a deep integration of personal empathic engagement with professional expertise. It may also reflect the varied approaches to inquiry and research in nursing, where the nurse-researcher’s personal experiences can inform and be informed by their professional practice and the critical theories that guide it. This model encourages a reflective stance, inviting practitioners and researchers to consider where they position themselves along these continuums in their daily work and scholarly endeavors.

Limitations

While contributing significantly to the understanding of autoethnography within nursing research, this review acknowledges certain methodological limitations that merit consideration. Initially, the literature search was confined to four databases: CINAHL, PubMed, PsycINFO, and Scopus. This delineation, albeit strategic, may have overlooked pertinent studies housed in alternative databases, thus potentially limiting the scope of gathered evidence. Moreover, the review focused exclusively on peer-reviewed journal articles, omitting various materials, including books, gray literature, and dissertations, which might harbor additional insights into autoethnographic practices. Another limitation is that only articles written in English were included. It is also important to note that a sister undertook screening, process selection, and data exsingle. While facilitating a coherent and consistent narrative synthesis, this approach may limit the findings’ robustness. However, within the paradigm of narrative reviews, such methodological decisions are not merely limitations but also reflective of the review’s epistemological stance. Narrative reviews prioritize a comprehensive, interpretive synthesis of the literature, allowing for a more flexible, exploratory approach to unveil emergent themes and insights not readily apparent in more rigidly structured reviews. This interpretive process, though subjective, brings to light the nuanced dimensions of autoethnography in nursing research, providing a rich, contextualized understanding that complements the existing body of knowledge. In acknowledging these limitations, this review underscores the importance of situating its findings within a methodological framework that is both reflective and critical.

Conclusion

The methodological richness and introspective depth that autoethnography injects into nursing research have been reviewed, unveiling the profession’s cultural, educational, and ethical intricacies. The implications of autoethnography refer to enhancing nursing practice and patient-centered care, improving nursing education, mental health awareness and support, and reflective practice and professional identity. Beyond this, the article has introduced the Powerful Navigation of Autoethnography in Nursing Science (Fig. 3), a model that integrates ontological, epistemological, ethical, and praxis dimensions, emphasizing the dynamic interaction between personal identity and professional roles, the journey from subjective to critical understanding, and the spectrum from generic to specific applications in practice. This framework enriches the comprehension of nursing practice and advocates for a reflective, inclusive, and culturally attuned approach to healthcare, aligning with the need for empathy and diversity in perspectives. The article contributes significantly to the discourse by championing this model, advocating for autoethnography’s integral role in fostering transformative changes in nursing practice. This review augments the understanding of the nursing landscape and sets a precedent for the integration and valorization of autoethnography in nursing science and nursing research, urging practitioners and researchers to introspectively align their personal and professional narratives with the broader cultural dynamics that relate to the cornerstones in nursing science: health, environment, human, and caring. However, certain nursing specialties remain underexplored through the lens of autoethnography thus further research is needed.
