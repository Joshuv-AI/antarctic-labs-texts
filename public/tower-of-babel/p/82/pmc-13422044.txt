Evaluation of injury prevention interventions using the stepped wedge cluster randomised trial design: key considerations
Authors: Yongdong Ouyang, Christian Vaillancourt, Anna Heath, Monica Taljaard, Colin Macarthur
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundInjury prevention interventions are often implemented at the group level via communities, hospitals, schools, etc, making cluster randomisation a suitable approach to evaluation. The stepped-wedge cluster randomised trial (SW-CRT) design has become increasingly popular for evaluating interventions in real-world settings.MethodIn this commentary, we describe the methodological characteristics of the SW-CRT design and highlight key threats to validity, relevant design and analytical issues, and scenarios in which the SW-CRT design might be a reasonable design choice. We illustrate these key points using a recently completed SW-CRT: the prehospital Canadian C-Spine trial.ResultsSeven potential biases associated with SW-CRTs, including: (1) secular trends, (2) confounding by external factors, (3) identification and recruitment bias, (4) contamination, (5) late and early transitioning, (6) risks of baseline imbalances due to small numbers of clusters and (7) statistical issues are discussed, along with potential mitigation strategies.ConclusionThe SW-CRT design offers a pragmatic approach to evaluating injury prevention interventions that may involve a staggered rollout across services or regions. The design allows an intervention to be rolled out to all participating sites and provides an opportunity to efficiently evaluate effectiveness. It is important, however, for researchers to consider the unique design and analytic issues associated with the SW-CRT design. Mitigating potential threats to validity when using the SW-CRT design helps ensure robust evaluation of injury prevention interventions.

Background

Stepped-wedge cluster randomised trials (SW-CRTs) are increasingly used in the field of injury prevention to evaluate system-level interventions where real-world evidence is needed.1–4 As opposed to the standard parallel arm CRT design in which clusters remain in the arm to which they were allocated for the duration of the trial, in the SW-CRT design, clusters are randomly allocated to sequences (consisting of time periods) that dictate the timing of implementation of the intervention (see figure 1). Key characteristics of the SW-CRT are: (1) all clusters begin the trial in the control condition and end the trial in the intervention condition; (2) clusters are randomised to distinct ‘sequences’ which determine the calendar times (‘steps’) at which clusters cross over to the intervention condition (one or more clusters may be allocated to each sequence); (3) once crossed over, clusters remain in the intervention condition until the end of the trial and (4) exposure and outcomes are measured during each cluster-period.

Standard stepped-wedge trial design.

SW-CRT designs have been applied to evaluate system-wide changes such as prehospital triage protocols, fall prevention programmes in long-term care facilities, helmet laws and safety promotion campaigns. In these contexts, parallel arm CRTs may be less practical, particularly when stakeholders require that all clusters eventually receive the intervention.5 The SW-CRT design offers advantages over traditional parallel CRTs such as enabling a staggered implementation, providing a more robust design than simple before-and-after studies (eg, interrupted time series),6 potentially increasing statistical power and possibly improving recruitment, given that all participating clusters eventually receive the intervention. Importantly, the SW-CRT design is considered a pragmatic design, enabling evaluation of interventions under real-world conditions, thus potentially improving generalisability of results. However, the SW-CRT design brings methodological challenges which may increase the risk of bias, and these issues may be under-appreciated among researchers.5

In this commentary, using the prehospital Canadian C-Spine trial as a case study, we review the methodological characteristics of the SW-CRT design, identify key threats to validity and highlight scenarios in which the SW-CRT design might be a reasonable study design choice for the evaluation of group-level injury prevention interventions.

Case study: the prehospital Canadian C-Spine trial

Paramedic services in the province of Ontario, Canada transport around half a million fully immobilised patients with a potential cervical spine injury (resulting from a variety of mechanisms including motor vehicle collisions and falls) to the emergency department (ED) each year. Only a small fraction of these transported patients, however, is subsequently diagnosed with a vertebral fracture, spinal cord injury or nerve damage. Immobilisation requires paramedic time, is uncomfortable for the patient and is costly to the health system.

The prehospital Canadian C-Spine trial was a pragmatic SW-CRT conducted across 12 paramedic services in Ontario, Canada, from March 2017 to May 2018. The trial aimed to evaluate the safety and effectiveness of implementing the Canadian C-Spine rule in prehospital trauma care.7 Paramedics were given the authority to transport ‘low-risk’ patients to the ED without immobilisation. The rationale for the trial was that unnecessary immobilisation of low-risk patients was costly, inefficient and a waste of health system resources and that the results would inform Ontario Ministry of Health policy.7 The study design included a 12-month pre-implementation period focused on paramedic training and data system setup, followed by a 12-month stepped-wedge rollout during which clusters crossed over from control to intervention every 3 months in one of three randomised sequences (four services per sequence). This SW-CRT used a cross-sectional design (continuous recruitment), where individuals entered the study at the time of the event and were observed only once. In the field of injury prevention, the continuous recruitment design is common, as it aligns well with the acute, short-term nature of injury events, such as falls, motor vehicle crashes or sports-related injuries. In contrast, cohort designs, which involve repeated follow-up of a fixed (closed) or dynamic (open) group of participants over time, are relatively uncommon in injury prevention research.

For the prehospital Canadian C-Spine trial, a total of 6392 eligible alert and stable trauma patients were enrolled. The intervention involved paramedics applying the Canadian C-spine rule to determine whether immobilisation was necessary prior to hospital transport. As per protocol, data were collected on all patients over a 12-month study period. The co-primary outcomes were transportation with immobilisation (yes/no) and measures of patient pain and discomfort. It was estimated that more than 60% of eligible trauma patients could be safely transported without immobilisation, potentially leading to decreased paramedic response times, less patient pain, shorter ED lengths of stay and reduced healthcare costs. The primary analysis was prespecified to use mixed-effects regression models to account for secular time trends and clustering by paramedic service, with generalised estimating equations planned for sensitivity analyses. Outcomes were obtained via linkage of paramedic electronic records to hospital administrative databases.

Key threats to validity in the SW-CRT design

SW-CRTs are vulnerable to the same biases that affect all CRTs, as well as some additional risks of bias unique to the SW-CRT design. Potential biases include: (1) secular trends, (2) confounding by external factors, (3) identification and recruitment bias, (4) contamination, (5) late and early transitioning, (6) risks of baseline imbalances due to small numbers of clusters and (7) statistical issues.5 These potential threats to validity, however, can be addressed in SW-CRT design and analysis.

Confounding by time (secular trends)

A feature of the SW-CRT design is that the intervention effect is, by design, partially confounded with time. All clusters start the trial under the control condition; therefore, observations under the control condition occur systematically earlier than observations under the intervention condition. The challenge occurs if there are changes in the outcome over time (secular trends) that occur irrespective of the intervention. Since injury patterns and incidence can vary by season, ED and system operational pressures, in injury prevention trials with a SW design, secular time trends are a critical consideration. Unlike parallel arm CRTs, where intervention and control groups are observed concurrently and thus similarly affected by secular trends, SW-CRTs introduce systematic differences in calendar time between intervention and control conditions. As a result, it is essential to adjust for secular effects in the statistical model, regardless of whether these effects appear statistically significant. Failing to account for secular trends can lead to biased estimates of the intervention effect.8 The analysis plan for the prehospital Canadian C-Spine trial specified the addition of three categorical time effects across the four periods of the trial to mitigate confounding by time.

Confounding by external factors

External factors, including concurrent public health policies, training initiatives or media coverage, may threaten the internal validity of a SW-CRT if the factor differentially affects clusters during the control and intervention periods. Rennert et al9 proposed an extension to the commonly used linear mixed-effect model in SW-CRT to control confounding effects of external factors. When the number of time periods is sufficiently large, another option is to include a binary indicator for before and after the external factor occurs as well as an interaction with the intervention effect. This yields an estimated intervention effect separately for the pre-external and post-external factor phases.10

Three months before the scheduled end of the prehospital Canadian C-Spine trial, the Ontario Ministry of Health implemented a new spinal motion restriction (SMR) protocol, which significantly reduced the use of rigid backboards by paramedics. This change did not affect the use of cervical collars but had major implications for the co-primary outcome. Because changes to paramedic protocols in Ontario are reviewed only once every 5 years, the Ministry moved forward with the SMR implementation despite ongoing data collection. As a result, during the final 3 months of the trial, very few patients were transported on backboards, which limited our ability to assess the impact of the Canadian C-Spine rule on pain and discomfort. We were still able to evaluate the safety in identifying low-risk patients who could avoid cervical collars, but the change effectively compromised one of the key trial outcomes. For this reason, we chose to exclude data from the final period when analysing pain and discomfort. Recruitment and identification bias

Recruitment and identification biases are a main concern in CRTs and may be a particular concern in injury prevention trials if providers and/or participants are unblinded and trial protocols rely on judgement from providers to assess eligibility, initiate care protocols or record outcomes. Risks of bias may be higher in SW-CRTs if providers are aware that an intervention will soon be implemented. This may consciously or unconsciously change their behaviour about how patients are identified, assessed or documented during the control phase.11 This can reduce the contrast between control and intervention periods. Such behaviour can bias estimates of intervention effects, especially when eligibility or outcomes are sensitive to provider discretion. To mitigate these biases in SW-CRTs, several strategies can be applied, which include not disclosing the crossover timing until the time of crossover,12 standardising eligibility criteria to ensure consistency across sites and time periods and automating data collection methods (e.g., use routinely collected health administrative data).13 14

In the prehospital Canadian C-Spine trial, individual patient-informed consent was waived by the research ethics committee, and paramedics were required to complete data collection on all eligible patients. To mitigate the risk of identification bias, paramedics were instructed to gather data on all consecutive patients meeting the criteria for assessment using the Canadian C-Spine Rule. In addition, health administrative data were also collected, which allowed investigators to monitor the inclusion of all eligible patients in the trial.

Contamination

In injury prevention trials, contamination occurs when control and intervention are mixed during the same cluster period due to shared personnel, overlapping service areas or widespread public awareness. Contamination can dilute the intervention effect, making it harder to detect true differences between groups. SW-CRTs with continuous recruitment are particularly vulnerable to contamination bias (compared with parallel-arm CRTs and cohort SW-CRTs), given that each cluster transitions from the control to the intervention condition during the trial and individuals recruited during the process of cross-over may experience a blend of control and intervention conditions, especially when the implementation of the intervention takes time.15 To address this, trials should strictly follow the protocol and consider analytic methods that account for partial exposure, if necessary.16 The use of ‘transition periods’ (also called implementation periods), that is, periods of time where a cluster is considered neither under the control nor intervention conditions, can also help reduce the possibility of contamination. Transition periods are not intended for data collection or data analysis.17 18 Hooper and Copas15 suggest a transition period to facilitate the closure of the control condition and the introduction of the intervention condition. However, transition periods do not always guarantee that the control condition is fully completed before crossover, particularly when clusters do not adhere to the planned implementation schedule (discussed more in the Late and early transitioning section).

Late and early transitioning

Clusters in SW-CRTs may not always transition as planned. Common reasons include staff shortages, longer-than-expected training or limited resources. In community-level injury prevention trials, given that interventions may involve practice change (such as the adoption of the SMR protocol in the prehospital Canadian C-pine trial), it is not uncommon that administrative approval processes may take longer than expected. Conversely, some sites might start earlier than scheduled if they independently accelerate training or resources. These variations imply that clusters may not always follow the planned schedule to switch from control to intervention.

Dealing with delayed and early transitioning is not always straightforward. At the design stage, investigators may schedule the trial to avoid cross-over times around major holidays. If the implementation of the intervention requires time or training, one approach is to include transition periods in the design before each cluster is considered fully exposed to the intervention condition (figure 3). If there is late or early transitioning of clusters, the standard statistical approach is to follow the intention-to-treat (ITT) principle. How to define an ITT analysis in SW-CRT, however, is still under debate.19 If the delay is due to an unforeseen event and unrelated to the outcomes or performance at that site, conducting an analysis according to the actual implementation (while maintaining the order of implementation) might be acceptable.

Stepped-wedge trial design with transition periods.

The prehospital Canadian C-Spine trial did not have transition periods but instituted a ‘training period’ before the actual trial as paramedic services had a limited number of training opportunities per year. This training ensured a quick and smooth transition (no delay or early transition) between control and intervention conditions, allowing the intervention to be switched on instantly.

Risks of baseline imbalances due to small number of clusters

The number of available clusters for injury prevention trials at the group level may be inherently limited. A recent review of 160 SW-CRTs (with at least five clusters) reported a median of only 11 clusters,20 substantially fewer than the median of 23 clusters typically seen in parallel-arm CRTs.21 This limitation increases the risk of baseline imbalances on key characteristics such as population size, injury burden or existing practices.22 23 These risks are especially important in injury prevention trials, where outcomes can be affected by the regional differences in existing safety practices, staffing, resources or local injury rates, which may not be evenly spread across clusters at baseline.

The fact that each cluster in the SW-CRT design partially serves as its own control may mitigate the impact of baseline imbalances. However, the anticipated value of cluster randomisation is to protect against time-varying effects by ensuring cluster characteristics are evenly distributed across implementation times (sequences). Randomisation techniques for balance at baseline in cluster randomised trials,24 such as covariate constrained and stratification randomisation,25–27 are encouraged; however, the implementation of such strategies is more complicated with SW-CRT designs. With routines available in both R and SAS,26 covariate-constrained randomisation in SW-CRTs is becoming easier to apply. In the prehospital Canadian C-Spine trial, covariate-constrained randomisation was used, based on the following factors: hospital catchment area (km2), number of immobilisations per month, average response time and staff makeup (advanced care paramedics and primary care paramedics).

Statistical considerations

The standard statistical models for SW-CRTs assume that the intervention effect is immediate and constant over time, that is, that the intervention effect is fixed regardless of the duration of treatment.16 28 However, in SW-CRTs, particularly those involving injury prevention or complex system-level interventions, treatment effects may initially be modest as providers adapt to the new protocols, with increasing treatment effects over time as the implementation becomes smoother. Conversely, effects might diminish later due to fatigue, reduced adherence or changing contextual factors. Therefore, the immediate treatment effect model may be misleading.29 Accounting for these time-varying effects allows a more accurate assessment of intervention effectiveness. One approach to achieve this is to replace the binary intervention indicator with an indicator of the number of periods of exposure (eg, time since implementation).16 This is called the ‘exposure-time indicator’ (ETI) model. If used, however, this approach requires a more complex power and sample size calculation method.16 30

SW-CRTs also require more complex modelling of correlation structures than parallel-arm CRTs. While a single intracluster correlation coefficient (ICC) suffices to summarise outcome similarity in CRTs with one post-intervention measure, SW-CRTs involve repeated measurements across cluster-periods where correlations may decay over time.31 Thus, assuming a constant ICC is inadequate. Flexible correlation structures that capture within-period correlations and allow for decay over time better represent these data. Correctly specifying these correlations is essential for accurate sample size calculation and analysis,32 33 as underestimation can lead to too few clusters and inflated Type I error rates.34–36 Because the true correlation structure is often unknown, it is advisable to assume more flexible correlation structures during sample size estimation and use robust variance estimators in analysis.30 37 Empirical ICC databases can aid in selecting appropriate estimates for correlation parameters.38 39

In the prehospital Canadian C-Spine trial, a nested exchangeable correlation structure was used to account for within-cluster correlations, which allowed us to distinguish between correlations within and across cluster-periods. However, at the time the trial was designed, concerns about potential bias in average treatment effect models for stepped-wedge designs had not yet been widely recognised. As in many published injury prevention trials, the primary analytic focus was on estimating the overall impact of a system-level intervention, and the investigators specified a mixed-effects model with a fixed treatment effect to estimate the average effect across the full trial duration. A more appropriate approach, however, is to use an ETI model, which allows for a flexible assessment of whether the intervention effect changes over time while still estimating the average treatment effect. The ETI model will be included in the Canadian C-Spine trial as a sensitivity analysis.

Adopting the SW-CRT design for injury prevention trials

The Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials extension for SW-CRTs recommends that authors should explicitly justify the use of this design over a parallel-arm CRT.19 The SW-CRT is particularly useful for evaluating population-level and system-level interventions, especially for those intended for implementation across regions, institutions or entire systems, where policy-driven rollout is planned and rigorous evidence of effectiveness is needed. First, the SW-CRT provides a more robust alternative to an uncontrolled before-and-after study, allowing for stronger causal inference while still supporting implementation.6 Second, SW-CRTs can increase cluster recruitment by assuring that all clusters have access to the intervention. Third, depending on the ICCs and cluster-period sizes, SW-CRTs often require fewer clusters than parallel arm CRT designs for equivalent power, which may make it the only feasible option.40 These considerations are highly relevant for injury prevention trials that evaluate public health strategies or group-level interventions, such as safety training programmes, triage procedures or legislation affecting large populations. In these trials, generalisability and external validity are essential, as the interventions are designed to operate at a system level. The SW-CRT design supports these goals by allowing every cluster to receive the intervention, which facilitates wide engagement and inclusion in real-world settings. Because the intervention is rolled out sequentially across different clusters, the design captures how the intervention performs under varying conditions across different time periods, which enhances external validity as the evaluation reflects the complexity of real-world implementation.

The prehospital Canadian C-Spine trial exemplifies these advantages: the intervention was considered for province-wide adoption, the design incentivised participation from paramedic services by ensuring exposure to the intervention, and the SW-CRT enabled the trial to proceed with 12 clusters instead of the 32 that would have been required for a parallel-arm CRT. However, the corollary is that while SW-CRTs may be chosen for adequate power with fewer clusters, longer study durations or larger cluster sizes may be required. In the pre-hospital Canadian C-Spine trial, investigators were required to enrol over 6000 participants across multiple time periods. Investigators must consider the trade-offs between number of clusters, cluster-periods, and participants when designing SW-CRTs, as reducing the number of clusters may increase trial complexity, cost, or duration.

Conclusion

The SW-CRT is an attractive design for evaluating system-level injury prevention interventions. However, the design is complex both from a methodological and practical standpoint and is prone to certain biases. While several methodological challenges discussed in this paper can affect both parallel arm and SW-CRTs, some biases are unique and/or more significant in SW-CRTs given the staggered implementation structure. A key distinction between SW-CRTs and CRTs is the risk of confounding by time in SW-CRTs, as control observations occur earlier than intervention observations. This necessitates a model-based adjustment for time. Other specific concerns for SW-CRTs are the requirement for clusters to implement the intervention on a prespecified schedule, and the possible change of treatment effects over time. In such cases, estimating a single, meaningful summary effect may be difficult. Moreover, the SW-CRT design relies on scheduled rollout, which makes it especially vulnerable to external disruptions. In injury prevention trials, where interventions are often embedded in health systems, policy changes or operational shifts can prevent clusters from receiving the intervention, leading to unbalanced data and reduced internal validity. These considerations highlight the importance of careful planning, monitoring of rollout timing and the use of sensitivity analyses (eg, ETI models) to ensure valid and interpretable results in SW-CRTs.

In summary, the SW-CRT design may be an appealing option for evaluating injury prevention interventions at the group, health system or policy level, as it allows an intervention to be rolled out to all participating sites, as well as providing an opportunity to efficiently evaluate effectiveness. However, it is important for researchers to carefully consider the unique design and analytic issues associated with the SW-CRT design.
