Flexor tendon rupture following repeated corticosteroid injections for carpal tunnel syndrome: A case report

Introduction

Corticosteroid injection (CSI) is a well described and effective non-surgical treatment for patients with carpal tunnel syndrome (CTS) [1]. While generally considered a safe and low risk initial treatment for CTS, minor adverse events may include injection site pain, subcutaneous atrophy, and skin depigmentation [1]. Major adverse events are very rare with an estimated incidence of less than 0.1 %, and include intraneural injection, gangrene, and tendon rupture [1]. There are only three reported cases in the literature of spontaneous flexor tendon rupture following CSI for CTS spanning the past 5 decades. The spontaneous rupture of the flexor tendon in these cases occurred after as few as 1 CSI to as many as 29 injections over 7 years [[2], [3], [4]]. We report a case of multiple flexor tendon ruptures following numerous steroid injections for CTS. This case report has been reported in line with the SCARE Criteria [5].

Presentation of Case

Written informed consent was obtained from the patient for publication of this case report and accompanying images. A 67-year-old right hand dominant female presented with acute loss of left index finger flexion 6 days after suddenly experiencing pain and a popping sensation while attempting to grab a door. She was unable to flex her left index finger and reported an “achiness” to her left hand. She underwent bilateral carpal tunnel releases 16 years prior followed by bilateral revision carpal tunnel release surgery a year later. Under the care of her local physician at an outside facility, she received 21 ultrasound-guided carpal tunnel CSIs with 20 mg Kenalog approximately every 3 months over a 6-year span in the same facility for persistent carpal tunnel symptoms. Of note, the patient did not have any significant risk factors that would otherwise put her at an increased risk for tendon rupture, including rheumatoid arthritis, history of trauma, infection, or clinical tendonitis.

On examination of her left hand, she demonstrated no active flexion of the left index finger (Fig. 1). She was able to make a full fist with her other fingers. There was no tenderness along the index finger flexor sheath or over the carpal tunnel. An ultrasound of the left index finger flexor tendons demonstrated complete rupture of the index finger flexor digitorum profundus (FDP) and flexor digitorum superficialis (FDS) tendons with retraction of the distal tendon stumps to the level of the mid palm (Fig. 2). Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the hand confirmed complete ruptures of the index finger FDS and FDP tendons with distal stumps at the mid palm and proximal stumps at the distal forearm, just proximal to the carpal tunnel. MRI additionally showed a complete rupture of the long finger FDS tendon and a partial tear of the long finger FDP tendon (Fig. 3). Exploration with revision carpal tunnel release and tendon transfers to restore the left index finger flexion were recommended. At surgery, there was a complete rupture of the index finger FDS and FDP tendons along with rupture of the long finger FDS tendon. There was also a partial rupture of the long finger FDP tendon (Fig. 4). A tenosynovectomy of the flexor tendons with excision of the index and long finger FDS tendons stumps was performed. A side-to-side tendon transfer of the long finger FDP to the ring finger FDP was done along with a side-to-side tendon transfer of the index finger to the long finger FDP. The patient tolerated the surgery and was discharged as an outpatient. Initial immobilization involved a dorsal blocking splint that allowed passive finger motion. She then transitioned to a postoperative flexor tendon rehabilitation program. During her 3-month postoperative visit, the patient reported doing well with functional range of motion and reported no pain (Fig. 5). She also noted complete resolution of her carpal tunnel symptoms. Discussion

Steroid injections have been used for both diagnostic and therapeutic purposes in managing CTS. Various carpal tunnel injection techniques have been described, although there has been debate on the most effective and safe injection technique. Green et al. reported an accuracy of 75.7 % in 756 attempted placements of a 25-gauge needle into the carpal tunnel just ulnar to the palmaris longus in a simulated carpal tunnel injection prior to open carpal tunnel release [6]. Ozturk et al. demonstrated the safest and most accurate (96 %) technique was inserting the needle 1 cm proximal to the wrist crease and directing the needle distally by roughly 45 degrees in an ulnar direction through the FCR tendon [7]. This was compared to the approach of inserting the needle just ulnar to the palmaris longus tendon and distal to the distal skin crease of the wrist in line with the fourth ray. Recently, there has been increased interest in using ultrasound-guided carpal tunnel injections. In addition to guiding the injection, ultrasound can also measure the median nerve size in the carpal tunnel. Fowler et al. demonstrated that the cross-sectional area of the median nerve can be used effectively to aid in diagnosing CTS with better specificity and equal sensitivity as compared with electrodiagnostic testing [8].

The overall incidence of adverse effects in patients receiving CSIs for CTS is not entirely clear given the lack of large studies specifically examining the safety of CTS injections. In 2018, Kaile and Bland reported on the safety of cortisone injections for carpal tunnel syndrome [2].

They published their experience with 9515 injections for carpal tunnel syndrome. The authors prospectively maintained a data base of carpal tunnel patients between 2004 and 2016. A cohort of 689 injections with 40 mg of triamcinolone done over a 2-year program were evaluated 6 weeks post injection for adverse effects. 33 % of patients reported possible side effects. The most common was short-lived local pain, occurring in 13 % of affected hands with all cases resolving after 3 weeks. Minor persistent local effects of subcutaneous atrophy and depigmentation were estimated to be 2 % while approximately 15–20 % of patients had transient effects that included pain, bruising, and facial flushing. In the review of the entire database, they reported only 4 serious adverse events. These included one tendon rupture, one probable intraneural injection, and two cases of ischemia of a fingertip leading to gangrene and partial loss of a digit.

The pathogenesis of intralesional steroid injection induced tendon rupture is postulated to result from a decrease in tensile strength of the tendon from steroid injections. This has been supported by a study by Unverferth et al. showing that the gastrocnemius tendons in rabbits demonstrated a statistically lower moduli of elastic stiffness than tendons injected with saline [9]. Furthermore, Mackinnon et al. studied the possible neurotoxic effects of steroid agents for peripheral nerve injections and found intraneural injections to be neurotoxic with dexamethasone causing minimal nerve damage when compared to hydrocortisone and triamcinolone [10].

Flexor tendon ruptures and intraneural injections from CSI in the hand are extremely rare There are only a few reported cases of flexor tendon ruptures associated with carpal tunnel CSIs. Gotlieb and Riskin described bilateral flexor tendon ruptures following carpal tunnel injection for CTS [4]. Park and Roh reported a case of a left little finger FDP rupture resulting in complete loss of flexion two months following a carpal tunnel CSI [4]. While these cases only involved rupture of the FDP tendons, our patient ruptured both her FDS and FDP tendons in two of her digits which, to our knowledge, has never been reported.

The question of how many CSIs are necessary to achieve continued therapeutic benefit and whether the number of CSIs correlates with an increased risk for tendon rupture remains unanswered. The European HANDGUIDE study has recommended that patients be limited to 3 CSIs when establishing their clinical practice guidelines [11]. In the case that more than 3 injections are given as the recommended maximum, an interval of 2 to 3 months between subsequent injections should be considered. The HANDGUIDE study also recommended intermediate-acting corticosteroid injections, including methylprednisolone and triamcinolone, should be used in the treatment of CTS. Given our patient's multiple CSIs per year over the course of 6 years, she may have been at increased risk of spontaneous tendon ruptures as extra-articular CSIs have been shown to be a risk factor in tendon ruptures [12]. Our patient did not have any significant past medical history that would otherwise put her at an increased risk for tendon rupture, including rheumatoid arthritis. However, it should be noted that the case of the flexor tendon rupture as reported by Park and Roh received only 1 CSI for CTS prior to rupture [3]. It is not currently known if repeated CSI for CTS increases the risk for tendon rupture.

Conclusion

This case highlights the rare, but potential major complication of FDP and FDS tendon ruptures following repeated CSIs for CTS. We recommend that care should be taken to perform proper injection techniques and adhere to established guidelines for injection frequency to minimize adverse effects from CSI and to avoid damage to flexor tendons and the median nerve.
