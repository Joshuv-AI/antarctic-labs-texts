Impact of green carbon dot nanoparticles on seedling emergence, crop growth and seed yield in blackgram (Vigna mungo L. Hepper)
Authors: Kanthavel Abinaya, Karuppannan Raja, Kalimuthu Raja, Ponnuraj Sathya Moorthy, Alagarswamy Senthil, Kalichamy Chandrakumar
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Carbon Dots (CDs) were synthesized from peanut shells (PNS) through pyrolysis and characterized using FTIR, XRD, HRTEM and BET analysis revealing an average size of 2–5 nm with amorphous nature. Synthesized PNS-CDs was employed both as priming and foliar agent for enhancing seed quality and crop productivity in blackgram (Vigna mungo L. Hepper). Different concentrations ranging from 50, 100, 200, 300, 400, 500 and 1000 ppm was used for seed priming and 5, 10, 15, 20, 25, 50, 75 and 100 ppm were given as foliar spray on 30th and 45th days after sowing (DAS). On accounting the best seed priming and foliar spray concentrations, field trial was conducted to validate the optimistic effect of PNS-CDs on blackgram crop productivity. Results revealed that priming with 200 ppm for 3 h exhibited maximum seed imbibition (54%), germination (88%) and vigour index (3165). Whereas, foliar spray with 50 ppm expressed significant improvement in leaf area index (2.6), total chlorophyll (2.70 mg/g), total soluble protein (71 mg/g), Number of nodules/plant (138), seed yield/plant (8.7 g) and 100 seed weight (5 g). The impact of PNS-CDs treatments resulted in increased photosynthetic rate (12.45 µmol CO2 m−2s−1), transpiration rate (3.13 mmol H2O/m−2s−1), stomatal conductance (0.55 mol H2O/m−2s−1) and internal leaf CO2 concentration (652 µmol CO2 m−2s−1) which ultimately enhanced the photosynthetic efficiency of plants. It has also exhibited a promising effect on the resultant seed in which the combination seed priming (200 ppm) followed by foliar spray (50 ppm) recorded maximum 100 seed weight (4.19 g), germination (97%) and vigour index (3019). Thus, this study highlights the promising role of PNS-CDs as a sustainable and effective agricultural nanomaterial, offering a novel approach to utilize the agricultural waste and also to enhance the crop productivity through advanced non-chemical approach.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-024-75366-5.

Introduction

Nanotechnology is well-thought-out as an advanced and effective way for increasing the agricultural productivity in a smart and precision way in various forms like fertilizers, pesticides, sensors, nanoparticles, etc. It is considered as a science of tiny materials that have a great impact on food security. Carbon is a single most important element for plants, forming the backbone of their existence and constituting almost half of their dry weight1. Carbon can be externally supplied in various forms. One among them is CDs, a new fascinating class of nanomaterial made up of pure carbon, typically less than 10 nm in size, with a crystalline or amorphous nature. It is smaller than a speck of dust but has bursting potential and was first confirmed during the purification of single-walled carbon nanotubes2. These properties and their wide range of applications in various field captivates scientists and researchers to identify favourable and eco-friendly method to synthesize CDs and to apply the same in agriculture. The application of CDs in agriculture can be applied through different modes viz., seed priming, seed coating, foliar nutrition and now-a-days it has been incorporated along with hydrogels for soil application to increase stress resistance and crop productivity3.

Despite the growing interest in CDs, there remains a significant research gap in the exploration of their potential in the context of eco-friendly, cost-effective synthesis methods and its agricultural applications. Synthesis of CDs can be done through various methods and sources. Whereas, it will be reasonable if it is from biological and cheap precursor that contain more carbon. Earlier, it was synthesized mostly from chemical sources, but during the present scenario, it has been from wide range of agricultural products like shells, husks, kernels, peels, straws, vegetable waste, woods, bagasse, etc. due to their low cost, easy availability, biodegradability and inexhaustible nature4. Globally, in agriculture, the remaining inedible parts of agricultural products after usage seek more attention as a precursor for the synthesis of various nanomaterials due to their recycling property and rich carbon content5. Pyrolysis is the commonly employed method for synthesis of CDs, because of its easy and environment friendly procedure than other methods6. It is a straightforward and well-liked thermo-chemical technique in which a carbon source is thermally degraded under high temperature in an oxygen-free environment7, causing the breakdown of the chemical bonds in the feedstock to decompose those materials and to transform them into carbon nanoparticles with specific structures8. Usually, the treatment temperature ranges from 300˚C to 1200˚C9.

In India, among the rainfed crops, blackgram (Vigna mungo L. Hepper) is the fourth most important crop among the pulses10. This has been given more importance in diet, as it contains vegetable protein supplement to cereal based diet. It also supplies good nutritive fodder to animals, because of which India has also becomes the major producer of blackgram. However, achievement of food security is at menace where 60 percent cultivable land is under unirrigated condition. To sustain the food security, the unirrigated and waste lands should be brought into cultivation with rainfed crops such as pulses. Hence, a research gap exist for adopting new and novel technologies to enhance the productivity and to reduce the import of pulse. Nanopriming is one such technology in which the seeds were primed with nanomaterials or with different nanoformulations for enhancing the seed quality parameters11,12. Recent reports also clearly disclose the remarkable role of CDs in different sectors like agriculture, medicine, sensing, energy harvesting, environment remediation and various biotechnological applications. Application of CDs enhances seed germination by improving the imbibition capacity of the seeds due to the presence of hydrophilic groups on its surface13. In addition, it activates aquaporin genes that accelerate the water absorption capacity of seeds and results in increased speed of germination and seedling vigour14. Carbon is a fundamental element in photosynthesis and CDs serve as a major source of the same. It acts as an optical amplifier that absorbs and converts light energy into chemical energy facilitating the source sink relationship and enhances the nutrient uptake by plants15. Stress resistance offered by CDs is one of the key roles, which happens because of its reactive oxygen species (ROS) scavenging ability, stabilization of photosystem and enhanced antioxidant system16. The impact of CDs in environmental remediation is high. The photocatalyst behaviour of CDs aids in breakdown of organic contaminants, removal of heavy metals in water due to the generation of ROS, hydroxyl and superoxide radicals. It also acts as a fluorescence sensor for detecting the presence of pollutants, pesticides and heavy metals in the environment17. The unique properties like high photoluminescence, biocompatibility, high water solubility and low toxicity made CDs more suitable for gene delivery, bioimaging, biosensing, antimicrobial activities and drug deliveries in medical field18. Next to priming, foliar application helps in delivering the targeted materials directly to the leaves of the plants which can directly enter into the source sink mechanisms, results in balanced plant growth and can increase the yield up to 15 to 20 percent19.

Peanut is the most important oilseed crop in India and as a result, their shells are abundantly available as a biological material. After the processing of peanuts, the removed shell doesn’t possess any economic value and results in a large amount of waste generation with a percentage of around 20–3020. The presence of Carbon (40.00%), Oxygen (26.82%), Hydrogen (5.76%), Nitrogen (1.37%) and trace amounts of Sulphur was confirmed through CHNS analyzer. This indicates carbon as a dominant element present in PNS21. Since, high amount of carbon and non-usable biomass waste are prerequisite for CDs green synthesis, PNS was selected as a precursor to synthesize PNS-CDs for further application in the agricultural sector. Although we have various studies reporting the optimistic effect of CDs in different field, studies on the use of CDs as seed priming and foliar agent for improving the seed quality is meager. Furthermore, the influence of CDs on crop productivity under field condition remains unexplored and created a research gap to take up this study. The general hypothesis is the application of PNS-CDs as a seed priming and foliar spray agent will significantly enhance the growth, stress tolerance and yield of blackgram by improving seed germination, field emergence, nutrient uptake efficiency and photosynthetic activity. Moreover, the PNS-CDs will contribute to environmental sustainability by utilizing agricultural waste, thus offering an eco-friendly alternative to conventional fertilizers, chemical spray agents and growth enhancers. In addition, it also offers an economically viable approach to nanotechnology applications in agriculture. With this hypothesis, the present study was framed to use PNS-CDs on blackgram with the following objectives (i) Biological synthesis process of carbon dots from peanut shells (PNS-CDs); (ii) Standardization of duration and concentration for priming and foliar spray with PNS-CDs and (iii) Evaluating the efficiency of PNS-CDs priming and foliar spray on crop productivity and its resultant seed quality under field condition. By addressing these gaps, the study will contribute to the development of sustainable agricultural practices that leverage the importance of green nanotechnology for improved food security.

Materials and methods

Synthesis and characterization of PNS-CDs

Freshly decorticated peanut shells [PNS] were collected from farmers holding and dried under sunlight for a week. Then, it was ground into a fine powder using a domestic blender and sieved under British Standard Sieve (BSS) 200 mm (37-micron mesh) to obtain uniform small-sized particles and stored in vials. Peanut shell powder was used as source material for synthesizing CDs. Pyrolysis was carried out in the presence of a nitrogen (N2) environment at the temperature of 400˚C for 2 h using a biochar unit. A schematic representation of the PNS-CDs synthesis was illustrated in Fig. 1. Carbonized samples were collected and stored for further characterization.

The general properties of PNS-CDs were analyzed using different instruments viz., CHNS analyzer [Elementar Vario EL III – Germany], Inductively Coupled Plasma-Mass Spectrometry (ICPMS) [Thermo Scientific™ iCAP™ RQ], Fourier Transform Infrared Spectroscopy (FTIR) [Jasco R-3000-QE], X-ray Diffraction (XRD) [Empyrean, Malvern Panalytical], Transmission Electron Microscope (TEM) [FEI Technai Sprit], High-Resolution Transmission Electron Microscope (HRTEM) [JEOL: JEM 2100], Energy Dispersive X-ray Analysis (EDAX) and Brunauer-Emmett-Teller (BET) Analyzer [Quantachrome TouchWinTM].

Fourier Transform Infrared Spectroscopy (FTIR)

The functional groups and chemical bonding of the components present in PNS-CDs was analyzed with FTIR spectroscopy analysis. Once the sample was placed on the port, they were irradiated with infrared rays that ranges from 10,000 to 100 cm⁻¹. The sample interacts with the radiation and absorbs specific frequencies based on its molecular composition that again transformed into vibrational or rotational energies. The absorption pattern was then measured with the help of a detector between 4000 and 400 cm⁻¹, which serves as a unique fingerprint of the functional groups and bonding present in the PNS-CDs. The respective FTIR spectrum of PNS-CDs was recorded and graphed by using ORIGIN Ver 8.5 software23.

X- Ray Diffraction (XRD)

The PNS-CDs were analysed using a fast and non-damaging technique called powder X-ray diffraction. The instrument was equipped with Cu Kα monochromatic radiation (ʎ= 1.54 Å). The analysis employed a current of 40 kV with 10,000 degrees per minute scanning speed, covering a range of 10° to 90° (2θ) and were positioned on dedicated holders and subjected to XRD analysis. The resulting diffraction data was collected, plotted and analyzed using ORIGIN Ver.8.5 software.

High Resolution Transmission Electron Microscope (HRTEM)

The HRTEM was employed to determine the size and morphology of the synthesized PNS-CDs. One mg of the PNS-CDs was dispersed in 10 ml of distilled water (D.H2O), resulting final solution concentration of 1 percent (w/v). The solution was sonicated for 15 min to ensure uniform dispersion of CDs in the solution. A small volume of this solution was then dropped using a micropipette onto a fresh copper grid and permitted to air-dry completely. The copper grid with the dried PNS-CDs was subsequently placed in the sample holder and images were recorded. The particle size of PNS-CDs was analysed using Image J software24.

Elemental analysis of peanut shells using EDAX

The chemical composition of the samples was analyzed using EDAX with the energy of the electron ranging from 10 to 20 keV focused onto the conductive sample surface, inducing the emission of X-rays which reveals the unique fingerprints of the elements present in the sample. Together with that, an element mapping was also done to observe the spatial distribution of specific elements within the sample.

Brunauer-Emmett-Teller (BET) analyzer

Initially, the known volume PNS-CDs were taken in the sample holder for degassing. Degassing was carried out in vacuum at 200 °C for 3 h to remove residual vapours and gases infused in the CDs. Precise measurement was taken during the loss of infused residues. After degassing, the sample cell was moved to the analysis port and nitrogen gas was purged into the sample cell containing PNS-CDs. Purged nitrogen gas interact and adsorbed on the surface of the PNS-CDs while liquid nitrogen was used to cool the sample and maintain it at a constant temperature. By using the inbuilt software, the pore size, surface area, and pore volume were measured and recorded.

Optimization of seed priming [SP] with PNS-CDs in blackgram

Blackgram cv. VBN 10 was collected from National Pulses Research Centre, Vamban, Tamil Nadu, India and graded to obtain morphologically uniform sized seeds and were used in further experiments. Blackgram seeds were supplemented with ZnSO4 (100 ppm) and PNS-CDs dispersed in D.H2O at different concentrations (50, 100, 200, 300, 400, 500 and 1000 ppm) for different durations (2.00, 2.30, 3.00, 3.30 and 4.00 h). Besides the treatments, the blackgram seeds that was used as such without any treatments (control) and soaked in water (hydropriming) were taken as an absolute control. After the prescribed durations, the excess PNS-CDs solutions were drained and seeds were shade dried until it reaches the original moisture content. The recovered seeds were subjected to further germination studies for demonstrating the efficacy of the PNS-CDs using various seed germination parameters.

Seedling length

During germination test evaluation, 10 normal seedlings were randomly selected to measure the seedling length (root length + shoot length). The length from the primary root tip to primary leaf tip was measured and the mean value was expressed in cm.

Vigour index

In accordance with Abdul-Baki and Anderson30, the vigour index of the seedlings were calculated using the formula, Vigour index = Germination (%) x Seedling length (cm) and expressed in whole number.

Optimization of PNS-CDs foliar spray [FS] concentration in blackgram under pot culture

Pot culture was raised to optimize the best FS concentration in blackgram. Finally, three seedlings were maintained in each pot and were given with the following treatments viz., Control, Water spray, DAP spray at 2%, FS of PNS-CDs at 5, 10, 15, 20, 25, 50, 75 and 100 ppm. Foliar spray was given on 30th and 45th DAS. Completely randomized design (CRD) with three replications was adopted in the study. The prescribed amount of PNS-CDs powder was taken and dispersed in water for giving foliar spray in pot culture. All pots were maintained with equal moisture and the spraying was done in a way that all seedlings in three replications of each treatment received the same amount of PNS-CDs. Observations on leaf area31, leaf area index32, plant height, plant vigour, chlorophyll a, chlorophyll b, total chlorophyll33 and total soluble protein34 were recorded on 45th and 60th DAS. The number of nodules/plant was counted on 50th day and number of flowers were counted to the end of reproductive phase. Number of pods/plant, plant fresh and dry biomass, seed yield/plant and 100 seed weight were recorded at the time of harvest.

Evaluating the efficiency of PNS-CDs SP and FS under field condition

The field experiment was conducted to study the efficacy of PNS-CDs SP and FS on blackgram crop productivity. The best performed SP concentration from first experiment and the best two treatments of FS from pot culture studies were accounted and carried forward to field trial as individual (i) SP (ii) FS and (iii) combinational treatments [SP + FS]. The experiment was laid out in randomized block design (RBD) with three replications. The treatment details imposed in the field were as follows: Control, Water spray, DAP spray at 2%, SP with PNS-CDs at 200 ppm, SP with PNS-CDs at 200 ppm + FS at 25 ppm, SP with PNS-CDs at 200 ppm + FS at 50 ppm, FS with PNS-CDs at 25 ppm and FS with PNS-CDs at 50 ppm. Treatments with foliar application were given with FS on 30th and 45th DAS. Observations on plant growth and photosynthetic characteristics were recorded at 45th and 60th DAS, while yield parameters were assessed at the time of harvest. The resultant seeds harvested from each treatment plots were subjected to physiological quality assessment and the results were recorded. The photosynthetic parameters such as photosynthetic rate, transpiration rate, stomatal conductance and internal CO2 concentration were recorded using Portable Photosynthetic System (PPS) [Model LCi-SD of ADC Bio Scientific Ltd., Great Amwell, UK] and plant vigour was assessed using GreenSeeker instrument that uses Normalized Difference Vegetation Index (NDVI) principle.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis of the obtained experimental data was done with R version 4.3.2 software35. Data were first subjected to one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) in R software. The Least Significant Difference (LSD) test was initially used to broadly assess significance, followed by Duncan’s Multiple Range Test (DMRT)36, as a validating test was carried out to confirm and group the highly significant treatments. The data are represented in table as mean values. Within each treatment, different letters at each column indicate significant differences by DMRT at P < 0.05 and the non-significant treatments were not grouped with letters. The graphical representation was prepared with the help of Origin Ver.8.5 software and Microsoft excel.

Results and discussion

The synthesised PNS-CDs had more functional groups such as C = O, C - F, C - O, C = C and O-H on the surface (Fig. 2), which directly relates with water solubility and stability of PNS-CDs. The dominant peaks observed at 2320 and 1584 cm−1 in the FTIR spectrum attributed to the carbonyl (C = O) stretching vibration which strongly suggests the presence of carbonyl (C = O) groups in the sample37. This functional group is common in many organic molecules, including ketones, aldehydes, carboxylic acids and their derivatives. Similarly, the peak at 1053 cm−1 in the FTIR spectrum likely indicates the presence of C-O stretching vibrations which are the characteristic of alcohol and ethers groups. The presence of O-H stretching vibrations within molecules has also been confirmed with the peaks observed at 3662 cm−1and 2972 cm−1that indicates the presence of hydroxyl groups bound within the samples molecules38. The subsequent peak at 752 cm−1 suggests the presence of an alkene group (C = C) stretching vibrations which was also similar to the results of Zhang et al.39. The presence of carbon fluorine (C–F) functional groups was also confirmed due to the presence of a peak at 1407 cm−140. The XRD analysis depicted in Fig. 3 registered a broad diffraction peak at 2θ = 24.5° indicating the amorphous nature of the PNS-CDs. In general, the broad peaks between 20˚-30˚ were considered as a common feature of amorphous carbon-based organic materials41. In addition, the (002) plane was consistent with an amorphous region described in earlier reports by Zhang et al.39 and Annamalai et al.42. This suggests that synthesized PNS-CDs possess mainly graphitic-like features and these findings are in consistent with previous reports on similar amorphous CDs prepared43,44. The results of XRD are consistent with the SAED pattern of the synthesized PNS-CDs with diffused and thick rings (Fig. 4) revealing its amorphous nature which was also evidenced in various studies on green synthesized CDs from coconut shells45 and teak leaves46.Fig. 3 XRD spectrum of showing the amorphous nature of PNS-CDs.Fig. 4 Selected Area Diffraction Pattern (SAED) confirming the amorphous nature of PNS-CDs with thick and diffused rings.

The synthesized PNS-CDs through pyrolysis technique at 400˚C for 2 h were subjected to characterization using different analytical methods. The results of the TEM and HRTEM analysis presented in Figs. 5 and 6 respectively, revealed the morphological properties of PNS-CDs. The images demonstrate that the particles are spherical, evenly dispersed and lack any discernible aggregation with diameters ranging from 2 to 5 nm measured with Image J software24. This information is confirmed in Fig. 5 and further supported by the particle size distribution histogram shown in Fig. 7. Furthermore, the HRTEM image (Fig. 6) also revealed well-defined lattice fringes that correspond to the CDs lattice, suggesting the amorphous nature of the PNS-CDs. Analysis using the BET method, revealed a surface area of the PNS-CDs as 58.86 m²/g. The smaller size promotes the increase in surface area of PNS-CDs. However, the total pore volume was 9.1588e−01 cc/g and the average particle radius was estimated as 2.3167e+01 nm and pore radius was recorded as 3.1120e+01 nm (Fig S1). Based on the shape observed in isotherm, the PNS-CDs was found to be Type IV isotherm which is the characteristic of mesoporous materials. Although this surface area is not extremely high and it suggests that CDs have significant external surface available for interaction with other molecules. The total pore volume 9.1588e−01cc/g is relatively high indicating the presence of a substantial porous network within the synthesised PNS-CDs. The high pore volume may be beneficial for applications that require high adsorption capacities. The average pore radius of 3.1120e+01 nm places the PNS-CDs in the mesoporous range (2–50 nm) that can host tiny molecules of appropriate range. Additionally, EDAX analysis (depicted in Fig S2) detected the presence of higher amount of carbon (75.3%) and oxygen (24.7%). The corresponding elemental mapping micrographs (Fig S3) further indicate the distribution of the same carbon and oxygen elements, which is consistent with the EDAX results and confirmed the presence of desired elements. This dominance of carbon and oxygen suggests a carbonaceous structure of the synthesised PNS-CDs. Conclusion

The promising results of this study demonstrate that CDs synthesized from agro-waste peanut shells can effectively transform low-value waste into high-value materials. The PNS-CDs was synthesized via pyrolysis at 400 °C for 2 h in a nitrogen environment and then characterized using different methods. The spherical shape, particle size (2–5 nm) and high carbon content marks the simplicity and eco-friendliness of the synthesis method in obtaining PNS-CDs. Application of PNS-CDs through seed priming for 3 h at 200 ppm significantly improved water absorption and other germination-related quality parameters in blackgram. Similarly, a foliar spray concentration of 50 ppm was found to be optimal for enhancing photosynthetic activity and boosting crop productivity. Additionally, the resultant seeds also exhibited improved germination and physiological attributes, confirming the enhancement potential of PNS-CDs throughout the entire growth cycle from seed (sowing) to seed (resultant seeds), which is one of the key findings that confirm the enhancement efficiency of PNS-CDs. Overall, this research has identified the optimal concentrations and application methods for PNS-CDs, offering an eco-friendly and sustainable approach to enhance seed quality and crop productivity in blackgram. It also exemplifies the potential of converting agricultural waste into valuable nanomaterials. Several future perspectives can be considered to expand the impact and applicability of this research. These include exploring the use of different agro-wastes for CDs synthesis, understanding the physiological, biochemical and molecular mechanisms by which CDs influence plant growth, stress response pathways and conducting extensive field trials to evaluate their impact under real-world conditions. Additionally, investigating various application methods will help to assess the effects of CDs on soil health and the environment, further broadening their potential use in agriculture.
