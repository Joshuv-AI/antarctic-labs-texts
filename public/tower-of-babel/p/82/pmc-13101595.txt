The Fatty Pancreas: Current Evidence, Treatment Implications, and Future Directions
Authors: Kunzah A. Syed, Matthew A. Manoj, Christopher E. Forsmark, Steven J. Hughes, Joseph R. Grajo, Michael M. Feely, Aleksey A. Novikov
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Here, we use the term fatty pancreas (FP), but more recent terminology includes pancreatic steatosis, nonalcoholic FP disease, pancreatic lipomatosis, and fatty atrophy. The absence of a universally accepted definition and standardized diagnostic criteria continues to hinder progress in both clinical practice and research. This condition is marked by abnormal fat content in pancreatic tissue and is becoming more recognized as clinically significant. The diagnosis has implications for metabolic dysfunction and other pancreatic diseases, including pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma. This review highlights current evidence on the classification, imaging modalities, histopathologic correlates, and clinical relevance of FP as a disease state. We discuss the utility and limitations of imaging techniques, including magnetic resonance imaging, computed tomography, ultrasound, and endoscopic ultrasound, for quantifying pancreatic fat and examine histological findings and their correlation with imaging features. The emerging association between FP and oncogenesis is critically evaluated, particularly in the context of the influence of intrapancreatic fat on acinar cell atrophy, inflammation, and neoplastic transformation. We also examine the therapeutic potential of weight loss through pharmacologic and bariatric surgical interventions in reversing intrapancreatic fat deposition. The possible assistive capabilities of AI in diagnosing FP are discussed. Finally, we emphasize the need for a unified framework to define, measure, and subclassify FP, which could facilitate its use as a marker of disease risk and inform surveillance strategies.

Introduction

Fatty pancreas (FP) is a nonspecific name for a condition or perhaps a range of diseases that result from either the excessive deposition of lipids in a healthy pancreas or the substitution of acinar cells with fat cells. These occurrences, respectively termed fatty infiltration and fatty replacement,1 are not mutually exclusive. In the average-risk population, estimates of FP prevalence range from 10% to 35%.2,3 In 2025, the highest prevalence of FP among individual countries currently exists in South Korea, while the lowest is in Taiwan.4 Regional differences in geographic prevalence also exist, with rates significantly higher in America than in Europe.4 The term nonalcoholic FP disease was recently proposed to parallel metabolic dysfunction associated steatotic liver disease. This clinical descriptor links pancreatic fat to the metabolic risk factors of obesity, insulin resistance, and dyslipidemia.5 However, clinical criteria for nonalcoholic FP disease remain poorly defined and are not widely accepted. Evidence to support this classification largely derives from surrogate imaging findings without histological validation or longitudinal outcomes data. Such evidence is accordingly viewed as low to moderate quality.5 Therefore, the clinical classification of this condition is an emerging concept that still requires validation and standardization. For this review, we will collectively use the term “FP.”

FP has numerous clinical associations and experts view it as etiologically heterogeneous. Increased intrapancreatic fat deposition is associated with the development of diabetes mellitus (DM).1,6 FP increases the risk of diabetes and glycemic progression even after controlling for various confounders.4 Interestingly, genetic variants of impaired beta-cell function and glycogen production are associated with the development of type 2 DM and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) measured histologically quantified pancreatic fat, as per at least one genome-wide association study.7 Chronic alcohol abuse and obesity, for example, promote ongoing inflammation, fibrosis, and acinar damage to the pancreas. Genetic syndromes, such as cystic fibrosis, Johanson-Blizzard syndrome, and Schwachman-Diamond syndrome, involve inherited mutations characterized by pancreatic inflammation and replacement of pancreatic parenchyma by adipose tissue. Iron overload in hemochromatosis and lipotoxicity from ectopic fat in conditions like malnutrition and highly active antiretroviral therapy-induced fat redistribution may contribute to oxidative stress and DNA damage, which promote FP. Other associations include infections such as reovirus and hepatitis B, which have also been linked to oncogenic pathways through chronic inflammation and direct cytopathic effects (Table). Risk Factors for FP Development and Hypotheses for Possible Underlying Mechanisms

Worsening fatty infiltration in the pancreas is seen as a potential risk factor for PDAC.1,19, 20, 21 FP causes lipotoxicity and changes in adipocytokines, and this reflects a disturbed pancreatic microenvironment.22, 23, 24 Oncogenesis occurs with longstanding, low-grade inflammation in and around pancreatic beta and acinar cells. Fluctuations in adipocytokines, such as leptin, insulin-like growth factor 1, angiotensin II, and monocyte chemotactic protein-1, are associated with angiogenesis and eventual lymphatic spread.23 The presence of FP indicates a worse prognosis in patients with pancreatic cancer.25

Taken together, FP is presently underrecognized, and consensus guidelines or International Classification of Diseases-10 codes for clinicians for FP have yet to be proposed. Clinically, the diagnosis is made radiographically using MRI, abdominal ultrasound (US), or computed tomography (CT), as well as endoscopic US (EUS), and/or histologically. However, no radiologic standard exists, complicating both research and patient care. The risk profile of pancreatic biopsy prevents the routine implementation of histology as the gold standard for diagnosis. This review examines the current evidence on the characterization and classification of FP, theories of oncogenesis, and clinical outcomes data relevant to the practicing gastroenterologist or surgeon.

Imaging

There is considerable variation in diagnosing FP, and little high-quality data on the application of each imaging type. Standard imaging cutoffs are not yet available, and using a single cutoff for FP can be problematic because the distribution of fat might prove more indicative of disease than its total amount. Notably, there is known heterogeneity in pancreatic fat distribution, which can be intralobular, interlobular, or involve peripancreatic adipocyte infiltration with lipid droplets within acinar cells and the islets of Langerhans. The intralobular vs interlobular location of pancreatic fat deposits can be viewed as morphologically distinct entities because these cell populations can behave differently, both metabolically and neurologically.26 The implications of fat distribution in the pancreas are still underinvestigated. Older data have demonstrated that a disease-free pancreas has a fat content of as much as 10.4%.27 A recent systematic review, meta-analysis, and meta-regression of 1209 participants proposed a lower threshold of 6.2% to designate FP, as this was 2 standard deviations above the weighted mean of 4.48%.28 This analysis utilized multiple imaging modalities, including CT, MRI, US, and EUS. Magnetic Resonance Imaging

MRI, especially with proton density fat fraction (PDFF) sequences, is currently the most specific and sensitive imaging modality for FP.29,30 It enables the precise quantification of pancreatic fat content without exposure to ionizing radiation. Tirkes et al31 retrospectively investigated the association between FP and the conditions chronic pancreatitis and type 2 DM. Quantifying FP on MRI-based PDFF with a cutoff that maximized the Youden index led to the conclusion that patients with chronic pancreatitis have higher pancreatic fat fractions compared to patients without chronic pancreatitis. The study reported PDFFs of 24% in mild, 23% in moderate, and 21% in severely affected patients with chronic pancreatitis, compared to 15% in controls without chronic pancreatitis. Patients with type 2 DM also had significantly greater FP (P = .03), as well as increased visceral (P = .007) and subcutaneous fat (P = .004).31 Pancreatic fat fraction was therefore correlated with visceral adiposity. The interobserver reliability for estimating pancreatic fat with MRI was superb, highlighting the reproducibility of MRI as a detection tool. Such findings suggest that MRI is superior to CT scan and US for accurately measuring pancreatic fat content. Still, PDFF has certain limitations. The technical proficiency needed for applying PDFF is available at nearly all academic centers and tertiary care hospitals, but not at most community hospitals. Implementing PDFF requires an appropriate protocol, which becomes a matter of technical expertise and a possible error source. Nevertheless, this technique is probably the most accessible noninvasive test for FP.

Magnetic Resonance Spectroscopy

Magnetic resonance spectroscopy can be used to optimally target the pancreas for measurement of fat. This modality is often employed for research purposes to evaluate fat content in the pancreas due to its noninvasive and relatively low-risk characteristics. However, it is resource-intensive and not widely available. The technique requires a radiologist to place a small voxel where the maximum pancreatic tissue can be captured in a specific sequence.1,32 Vascular structures and the pancreatic ductal system must be avoided during measurement. Combining this information with derived spectra allows the calculation of a voxel fat fraction. A voxel is a discrete tissue volume defined on a three-dimensional grid during an MRI or CT scan.33 Even this meticulous approach faces technical limitations, including motion artifacts, noise-related errors, voxel contamination, sampling bias, and interobserver variability. Both T1- and T2-weighted images can be affected by pancreatic inflammation32 in which case compensatory techniques, such as PDFF mapping, can enhance fat quantification, as previously described.29 Among noninvasive fat quantification methods, magnetic resonance spectroscopy remains regarded as the gold standard.29 Although magnetic resonance techniques are accurate for diagnosing FP, cost, and availability hinder their routine use in clinical practice. Such techniques can instead be leveraged for semi- or fully automated analysis of pancreatic fat content by artificial intelligence (AI).34,35

Computed Tomography

Detection of FP with abdominal CT, incidental or not, is becoming routine practice. Using unenhanced CT represents an opportunity to employ an objective grading system based on attenuation values. Pancreatic fat involvement can be graded from 0 (ie, no fat) to 4 (ie, >75% involvement).11 CT is performed on the pancreas similarly to how it is done on the liver during the evaluation of metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease. To qualify for metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease, the liver lobes should measure less than 40–45 mean Hounsfield units,36 or the liver's attenuation compared to the spleen's should be <1.0.37 In this context, the measured attenuation values of the pancreas are compared to those of the spleen in Hounsfield units to estimate relative fat content. A lower pancreatic attenuation or reduced pancreas-to-spleen ratio is considered fatty infiltration. This method involves a reproducible, noninvasive assessment of FP, which is beneficial in both clinical and research settings.38 Some evidence suggests that a P/S ratio of less than 0.70 signifies abnormal fat accumulation within the pancreas.39 This threshold is supported by studies of unenhanced CT, which have shown strong correlations between decreased pancreas-to-spleen attenuation ratios and presumed fatty infiltration, although histologic confirmation was not performed.40 Another widely used measurement is the pancreas-minus-spleen attenuation difference, where the average attenuation of the spleen is subtracted from that of the pancreas.41 A pancreas-minus-spleen difference under −5 Hounsfield units is consistent with FP.42 This measurement is especially useful in settings where both the pancreas and spleen are visible for intrapatient direct comparison.

However, there are no agreed-upon or validated absolute cutoffs for FP, although studies have proposed values based on modest evidence. For example, one possible cutoff derives from a cross-sectional, retrospective study of 110 patients with excessive fat deposition in the pancreas, where FP was defined as 21 Hounsfield units or less, but all participants had prediabetes or type 2 DM.43 Attenuation-based measurements in several other prospective and retrospective investigations have yielded moderate to high-grade evidence, particularly when calibrated against histological standards. High pancreatic surface lobularity has also emerged as an important macroscopic feature for identifying fatty infiltration on CT, as demonstrated by Previtali et al44 In this study, pancreatic surface lobularity was defined on CT by a threshold of 7.6 in the portal venous phase and FP as an attenuation <83.5 Hounsfield units, which resulted in an independent association with histopathologic fatty infiltration of the pancreas and 100% sensitivity. Despite the accessibility and relative objectivity of CT, it causes radiation exposure and is suboptimal for detecting mild or early steatosis.11

Ultrasound

US is conventionally used to ascertain fattiness within the pancreas by comparing its echogenicity to that of the liver. It is a cost-effective and efficient method of visceral evaluation. It grades pancreatic echogenicity relative to surrounding structures, such as the liver or renal cortex. For instance, grade 0 is for a normal echogenic pancreas, while grade 3 is for echogenicity equal to or exceeding retroperitoneal fat.11 Usually, the liver and pancreas are iso-echogenic on US, but when the pancreatic parenchyma is of greater echogenicity, it is fatty.27 Likewise, physicians can also diagnose FP by comparing the pancreatic echotexture to that of the neighboring kidney or the spleen. A pancreas that is hyperechoic to these organs is labeled as having FP.6,45 Overall, the US is unable to show the entire pancreas, is operator-dependent, and is affected by body habitus.11 It is not a procedure of choice to estimate pancreatic adiposity.

Endoscopic US

Some clinicians and researchers consider EUS to be the imaging modality of highest reliability for FP.46 A recent single-institution retrospective study of 16 years of FP EUS data from an endosonographer with over 25 years’ experience found that only 21.4% of patients with a concomitant CT or MRI within 3 months had FP listed as a cross sectional imaging finding.47 Nevertheless, for pancreaticobiliary conditions, the fullest use of EUS is likely in tandem with cross sectional imaging. The advantages of EUS for FP include its ability to image the entire pancreas in great detail, potentially with the addition of elastography to measure pancreatic stiffness, as well as the possibility of taking real-time pancreatic biopsies.48 To mitigate the uncertainties associated with fat distribution heterogeneity, multiple regions of interest can be averaged when diagnosing FP endoscopically. This strategy may also be applied to the other imaging modalities discussed. If the pancreatic echotexture looks brighter than the renal or splenic echotexture, the pancreatic duct appears poorly delineated, and/or the characteristic salt and pepper parenchymal dots are lost, FP is suspected on EUS.45,49 Interestingly, the specificity and sensitivity of these features, whether singular or as a triad, remain undefined. EUS elastography remains widely used to assess pancreatic stiffness in neoplastic conditions in particular.50

Overall, despite its increasing use, EUS is underinvestigated, and its potential is underappreciated in the setting of FP. Prospective studies are needed to better define the correlation between US images, pancreatic stiffness, and the degree and distribution of fat in histological specimens.

Histology

Pancreatic tissue samples are the gold standard for diagnosing FP. Key histological features include interlobular and/or intralobular adipose tissue (Figure 2), intracellular lipid droplets in acinar or islet cells, so-called acinar-to-adipocyte transdifferentiation, and substitution of necrotic acinar cells by fat.12 Animal studies spanning from 2008 provide direct experimental and mechanistic data to suggest that acinar-to-adipocyte transdifferentiation occurs during FP development.51, 52, 53, 54, 55 For example, Bonal et al51 showed that pancreatic adipocytes derive directly from transdifferentiating pancreatic acini through inactivation of the c-Myc pathway in mice using a genetic cell lineage tracing analysis and Oil Red O staining. Nevertheless, we remain cognizant of the gap between animal models and human clinical findings. In humans, it was recently identified that pancreatic adipocytosis and intraacinar cell lipid content are inversely related, albeit the context was patients with type 2 DM.56 Similarly, another 2025 study found that an increased degree of FP can correlate with increased dedifferentiation of beta cells in patients with DM.57 Luchini et al58 recently characterized 3 separate compartments of pancreatic fat deposition through histopathological analysis of human postpancreatectomy tissues, namely inter/intralobular adipose tissue, intraislet fat deposits, and intracellular lipid droplets inside pancreatic acini.Figure 2Fatty replacement of pancreatic parenchyma with both interlobular (black arrows) and intralobular (white arrows) adipose accumulation. The biggest problem associated with pancreatic biopsies is that it is rare to obtain them from an otherwise healthy FP, given the risk of pancreatitis. This is why the data linking imaging to pancreatic histology are scant. As late as 2017, no studies were available to correlate imaging with the pathological findings of intrapancreatic fat.28 However, Schawkat et al examined patients who underwent MRI with magnetization transfer before pancreatoduodenectomy. This work demonstrated that PDFF significantly correlated with lipomatosis grade on histopathology.59 In 2023, the quantification of pancreatic surface lobularity using CT was found to be associated with histological changes corresponding to fatty infiltration of the pancreas.44 In this study, Previtali et al determined that a combination of pancreatic surface lobularity and pancreatic attenuation on CT accurately predicts FP, which is graded from 0 to 4. Total fat area includes visceral and subcutaneous fat areas, which were significantly greater in patients with FP, given areas under the curve of 0.708 ± 0.069 and 0.718 ± 0.060, respectively.44 A retrospective study of patients who underwent the Whipple procedure for various indications, comparing fatty infiltration on pathology to average attenuation on CT, revealed that pancreatic attenuation on CT inversely correlates with the grade of fatty infiltration.60 At its current state, histologic assessment of the degree and distribution of pancreatic adipose tissue is mainly confined to research or incidental findings in pancreatic surgery or at autopsy.61 A study analyzed patients who had a CT scan 1 month before their pancreatectomies and found a significant difference between the pancreatic and splenic attenuation and the pancreas-to-spleen attenuation ratio.62 Although imaging techniques measure total fat content in the pancreas, they are unable to discern between fat compartments and still cannot glean adipocyte cellular origins. Future correlational studies are crucial for linking CT and MRI images with histologic samples, thereby enabling more advanced noninvasive FP assessment.

Oncogenesis and FP

The fourth most common cause of global cancer mortality is PDAC.21 Evidence is accumulating that pancreatic cancer is associated with FP.63 More specifically, among patients with pancreatic cancer, the odds ratio of having FP is 6-fold greater.19

Original fat deposition in the pancreas results from excess visceral fat growth and transformation of the normal cell types present in the pancreas to adipocytes. Surplus lipid deposition into the pancreas influences the tumor micro- and macroenvironment. It is thought that alterations in metabolism, possibly related to DM, could transform FP to PDAC.1 Obesity, which can lead to FP, can cause a proinflammatory systemic state that facilitates a pancreatic parenchymal change in people with benign pancreatic tumors.21 Frendi et al also described that PDAC could develop via acinar cell dedifferentiation, possibly before the processes of acinar-to-ductal metaplasia and pancreatic intraepithelial neoplasia (PanIN). Furthermore, their analysis of intralobular fat (ILF) and extralobular fat (ELF) lipidomic profiles for fatty infiltration revealed differences in the early alterations within acinar cells, which are referred to as acinar nodules. However, acinar nodules were only tied to obesity and did not correlate with ELF or ILF.21 In the same study, PanINs occurred at a higher incidence and correlated positively with both ELF and ILF fat in individuals with obesity, consistent with prior findings by Rebours et al,20 who identified obesity and FP as independent risk factors for PanIN development.21

The relative risk of FP in patients with premalignant lesions or pancreatic cancer is significantly increased by 2.78 times, according to a systematic review of 13 studies. Meanwhile, the pooled prevalence of FP in this population is 52%,64 which exceeds that of the average-risk population.

Several emerging studies support that FP development precedes PDAC. A temporal progression was described in recent longitudinal imaging studies in humans, which lasted 9.565 and 20 years, respectively.66 Other studies have employed rodent models to convey similar concepts through experimental work.67, 68, 69, 70 There is some evidence that intraductal papillary mucinous neoplasms (IPMNs), cystic precursor lesions to pancreatic cancer, may develop in the setting of FP.71 Kawamura et al63 described that FP could therefore represent a risk factor for both pancreatic cancer and IPMNs. A 2023 systematic review and meta-analysis by Lipp et al studied 2956 patients. Their analysis showed a significant positive association between FP and pancreatic cancer risk, with an odds ratio suggesting that individuals with FP were more likely to develop pancreatic cancer than those without pancreatic fat infiltration.19 Most recently, a retrospective study linked increased intrapancreatic fat deposition with an increased risk of pancreatic cancer in a prospective cohort of patients with pancreatic cysts. These findings indicate that FP may act as an independent risk factor for pancreatic tumorigenesis, beyond its association with general obesity or metabolic syndrome.72 Further supporting this theory, a retrospective study of 101 newly diagnosed patients with pancreatic cancer demonstrated that nearly 30% had imaging evidence of FP, primarily in the pancreatic head and body regions.73 Although there was no direct correlation between steatosis and cancer staging or survival in this study, the frequency of FP among patients with pancreatic cancer was notable.

One of the most compelling recent contributions in the field comes from a large-scale prospective study of 42,599 participants from the UK Biobank, which employed a deep learning-based abdominal organ segmentation technique to quantify intrapancreatic fat deposition, a radiologic marker of FP.74,75 The UK study identified FP in 17.9% of the cohort, and a Cox proportional hazard analysis revealed that FP was independently associated with an increased risk of both DM and pancreatic adenocarcinoma.74 Similarly, a Mendelian randomization analysis was done on the genes associated with pancreatic fat to establish causality with PDAC.76 This entailed a prospective cohort design involving 29,463 patients, in which 8 genetic variants were significantly associated with intrapancreatic fat deposition, and 2 of these were also significantly associated with PDAC.77 The UK and Mendelian study findings align with the PANcreatic Diseases Originating from intRa-pancreatic fAt hypothesis, in which fatty change of the pancreas is thought to underlie all common pancreatic diseases.26

Mechanistically, FP appears to foster a protumorigenic microenvironment. Histological analyses have shown that fatty infiltration can lead to acinar cell atrophy, followed by acinar-to-ductal metaplasia, a critical initiating event in pancreatic carcinogenesis.78 Acinar-to-ductal metaplasia facilitates progression toward PanIN, which is a known precursor lesion to PDAC. Philip et al68 demonstrated how Kras activation occurs via COX2 from high-fat diet exposure in LSL-Kras/Ela-CreERT mice, culminating in pancreas inflammation and fibrosis, PanIN, and PDAC. Other translational studies have demonstrated that FP correlates with increased infiltration of CD68+ macrophages and the upregulation of autophagy and oxidative stress-related markers, such as p62 and NQO1, indicating a state of chronic inflammation and cellular stress conducive to malignant transformation.78 Macrophage infiltration suggests a degree of immunomodulation, which is a key feature of oncogenesis in PDAC.79 Macrophage signaling and its interactions with tumor-associated fibroblasts, adipocytes, acinar, ductal, and tumor cells, as well as numerous other cell types within the tumor microenvironment, are an area of active investigation.

In summary, the convergence of imaging, epidemiological, and mechanistic data suggest that a FP is more than an incidental finding and may be a premalignant condition. However, at this time, there are insufficient high-quality data on patients with FP to recommend monitoring and treatments for long-term cancer risk reduction.

Screening for Pancreatic Cancer in Patients With FP

Currently, guidelines for routine pancreatic cancer screening solely based on the presence of FP are missing for the general population. Nonetheless, emerging evidence signifies that FP may be an independent risk factor for pancreatic cancer, prompting discussions about its role in risk stratification and surveillance strategies. Given the association between FP and pancreatic cancer, interest is growing in investigating whether individuals with FP would benefit from screening, even in the absence of other established high-risk features. The American Gastroenterological Association and the International Cancer of the Pancreas Screening Consortium currently recommend pancreatic cancer screening primarily for individuals at high risk due to genetic predispositions or significant family history.80,81 In high-risk populations, surveillance programs using MRI and EUS have demonstrated the potential to detect pancreatic cancer at earlier stages and to improve survival outcomes. Patients with FP might benefit from targeted surveillance strategies. In this context, magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography may identify ductal irregularities or early parenchymal changes associated with high-grade PanIN, as highlighted by Posa and Genco.82 While FP is recognized as a potential risk factor for pancreatic cancer, routine screening based solely on its presence is not currently recommended. Recent evidence has begun to challenge the current state; however, for example, a prospective study of 2070 patients followed for 12 years, matched for age and sex, found that being in the top quartile of fat deposition significantly increases the odds for developing non-IPMN–derived pancreatic carcinomas.72 Nonetheless, more prospective studies are needed to inform the efficacy and cost-effectiveness of possible surveillance approaches for patients with FP. Clinicians should continue to assess individual risk profiles while considering factors such as family history, genetic predisposition, and other risk factors when determining the need for pancreatic cancer screening.

Therapeutic Approaches for Treating FP

Pharmacological Weight Loss in Patients With FP

Numerous compounds offer varied degrees of weight loss as options for patients who are classified as metabolically unhealthy and/or obese. Antiobesity medications include several classes of compounds such as adrenergic agonists (phentermine), gamma-aminobutyric acid agonists (topiramate), opiate antagonists (naltrexone), norepinephrine reuptake inhibitors (bupropion), lipase inhibitors (orlistat), glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP) receptor agonists (liraglutide and semaglutide), and a GLP-1/gastric inhibitory polypeptide (GIP) agonist (tirzepatide).83 These medicines can be taken in commercially available combinations or individually, resulting in clinically significant overall weight loss between 5% and 10% of total body weight. GLP-1 agonists and GLP-1/GIP agonist medicines stand out due to their substantially improved degree of weight loss,84, 85, 86 achieving a 24% reduction in total body weight in a monitored trial setting while using tirzepatide.

The use of GLP-1/GIP agonists has been associated with the risk of pancreatitis, making these medicines difficult to use in patients with pancreatitis despite their demonstrated benefits.87 The increased pancreatitis signal with GLP-1 drugs is explained by specific mechanisms. These mechanisms include rapid weight loss and gastrointestinal dysmotility, which foster gallstone formation and theoretically increase the probability of acute biliary pancreatitis.88 The Liraglutide Effect and Action in Diabetes: Evaluation of Cardiovascular Outcome Results (LEADER) trial supports this hypothesis,89 as does the Satiety and Clinical Adiposity-Liraglutide Evidence (SCALE) program.90 A recent matched study of 81,872 patients assessed the association between GLP-1 receptor agonist use and pancreatitis risk in comorbidity-free patients with type 2 DM through propensity score modeling.91 It concluded that there was no significant increase in pancreatitis risk at 0.5, 1, 3, or 5 years after the initiation of treatment. GLP-1 receptor agonist use correlated with a lower lifetime risk of pancreatitis (0.3% vs 0.4%, P < .001).91 Another meta-analysis looking specifically at the use of tirzepatide and the risk of pancreatitis found no significant association.92 A recent multicenter analysis by Nieto et al93 affirms that taking GLP-1 receptor agonists does not increase the risk of acute pancreatitis and instead correlates with lower complication rates in patients with type 2 DM. In such trials, well-documented outcomes regarding changes in pancreatic adiposity due to pharmacologic weight loss are lacking. We propose that the decision to use antiobesity medicines in patients with FP should be based on the risk/benefit ratio and individual risk profile. FP is an obesity-related comorbidity, and the decision to treat should be based on body mass index (BMI) cutoffs (>30 in patients without comorbid conditions and >27 in individuals with obesity-related comorbid conditions).94

Bariatric Surgery as a Targeted Strategy to Reverse Intrapancreatic Fat Accumulation

Bariatric surgery is a valuable strategy to reverse FP in obesity. Several large-scale systematic reviews and meta-analyses have demonstrated that bariatric surgery reduces the risk of pancreatic cancer.95, 96, 97 Bariatric operations, such as laparoscopic sleeve gastrectomy and Roux-en-Y gastric bypass, are similarly associated with reductions in intrapancreatic fat volume, as evidenced by several studies of modern imaging techniques. A 54-patient prospective study conducted with laparoscopic sleeve gastrectomy showed a mean decrease in pancreatic fat by 26.1% at 12-month follow-up.98 This change was accompanied by notable reductions in metabolic indices, such as hemoglobin A1c, fasting insulin, C-peptide, and homeostatic model assessment for insulin resistance levels, while markers of systemic inflammation, including high-sensitivity C-reactive protein, also decreased. Importantly, the study noted a mean loss of 9.0 cm3 of total pancreas volume, indicating morphologic changes concomitant with the fat reduction.98 Additionally, a systematic review and meta-analysis of 14 studies demonstrated a mean relative reduction in intrapancreatic fat deposition of 35.9% after metabolic bariatric surgery.99 The analysis showed that sleeve gastrectomy had a higher average relative reduction in intrapancreatic fat deposition (39.9%) compared to Roux-en-Y gastric bypass (20.6%), although this difference was not statistically significant. Furthermore, there was a significant absolute reduction in total pancreatic volume of 10.75 mL after surgery.99

FP should also be considered during perioperative risk stratification for pancreatic surgery. Patients with FP who have had a pancreatoduodenectomy have a significantly higher risk of postoperative pancreatic fistulae (POPF), as per the findings of 2 recent meta-analyses.100,101 The only independent predictor for POPF was a ratio of visceral fat area to skeletal muscle index exceeding 1.94 for POPF (OR 4.28, P = .0095), as Tanaka et al102 demonstrated. A predictive scoring system for POPF risk stratification has also emerged and entails checking for 3 risk factors, that is, FP, a lack of pancreatic fibrosis, and whether the patient’s BMI is ≥25.103

The effect of bariatric surgery on pancreatic fat in younger populations is an interesting consideration as well. A study evaluating adolescents and young adults with severe obesity, defined as a BMI ≥ 120% of the 95th percentile or ≥35 kg/m2, identified decreases in FP 24 months after sleeve gastrectomy, which correlated with reduced BMI, fat mass, and metabolic improvement.104

The literature collectively highlights the role of bariatric surgery in enhancing physical and metabolic outcomes. However, the mechanisms by which surgical interventions might benefit FP are still undefined. It appears that decreased visceral fat and systemic inflammation, secondary to weight loss, may be key factors in this process. Standard definitions and diagnostic criteria for FP are urgently needed to advance the field and assess the long-term advantages of surgical treatment on pancreatic disease.

Role of Endoscopic Bariatric Therapies in the Management of FP

Endoscopic bariatric therapies, including endoscopic sleeve gastroplasty and intragastric balloon placement, are minimally invasive procedures that promote weight loss and improve metabolic parameters, especially in patients with obesity who are unfit for surgery. These interventions have demonstrated efficacy in reducing BMI, improving insulin sensitivity, and reversing features of metabolic syndrome, with endoscopic sleeve gastroplasty showing significant reductions in hepatic steatosis and liver enzyme levels.105 Endoscopic sleeve gastroplasty has been proven to cause substantial reductions in radiologic and histologically measured hepatic steatosis, along with decreases in aspartate aminotransferase, alanine aminotransferase, and gamma-glutamyltransferase levels.105 Endoscopic bariatric therapies present a novel opportunity for metabolic disease treatment as a minimally invasive endoscopic alternative associated with lower complication rates and quicker recovery times than open or laparoscopic surgical alternatives.

The use of endoscopic bariatric therapies for decreasing FP is mostly unexplored. No studies have directly examined the effects of endoscopic sleeve gastroplasty or intragastric balloon placement on intrapancreatic fat deposition. Much of the FP benefit is inferred from studies of surgical bariatric procedures. It is reasonable to argue that endoscopic bariatric therapies may provide similar benefits for reducing pancreatic fat. Prospective analyses and randomized controlled trials are needed to determine whether endoscopic bariatric therapies can aid in FP reduction and, therefore, lessen the metabolic and oncologic hazards that accompany FP.

Applications of Artificial Intelligence to FP

AI, including machine learning and deep learning–based image analysis, is increasingly being applied to the evaluation of pancreaticobiliary diseases through algorithmic interpretation of CT and EUS imaging. Current applications have primarily focused on differentiating benign and malignant biliary strictures, detecting PDAC, and characterizing pancreatic cystic lesions.106 All of the approaches discussed below remain highly experimental and are not yet validated or approved for routine clinical use. AI is an attractive option for quantifying and stratifying pancreatic fat, with promise to overcome limitations of manual radiology assessments for FP. We already know that AI tools can be used to improve reproducibility and skirt issues deriving from routine region-of-interest sampling. Deep learning models have demonstrated high accuracy in the automated segmentation of the pancreas and fat quantification on MRI and CT scans.34,107 Adding AI assistance to imaging would permit estimation of fat in the whole organ and has supported a correlation between MRI-derived fat fraction and CT attenuation.34,74 Deep learning networks are also being paired with US images to increase diagnostic capabilities for FP, resulting in sensitivity and specificity significantly exceeding that of conventional US.108

Despite notable advances in AI techniques and applications, there are many limitations. AI is not yet ready to guide therapies or influence clinical management at the population level, as validation and standardization are both lacking.107, 108, 109 The use of AI in EUS for the quantification of FP is an exciting frontier, and the need for its research is supported by an expert consensus at the National Institute of Diabetes and Digestive and Kidney Diseases.110 In theory, AI should be able to provide real-time information about the degree and pattern of fat infiltration,110, 111, 112, 113, 114 especially considering the emerging role of FP in oncogenesis. In practice, AI FP assessment is complicated by a lack of pancreatic histology as a gold standard. One would need to find a standardized way to view and record pancreatic images to train neural networks, correlate this with available pathology, and then to finally enable real-time assessment of EUS images to allow endosonographers to make decisions.

Conclusion

FP is gaining acceptance as an epidemiologically and clinically significant disease state. Clinicians should familiarize themselves with the diagnosis through CT, MRI, EUS, and histological examination. Recognizing and, ideally, addressing any modifiable risk factors that contribute to the development of this disease offers potential value for preventive health. Arguably, FP occupies an early, inciting role on the pathway to pancreatic cancer, though the mechanisms underlying remain largely unknown. Practical and high-quality evidence for the relationship between imaging and histology is similarly lacking. More than ever, there is a pressing need for a uniform definition and reporting of this condition, followed by longitudinal, prospective therapeutic trials. It will be worthwhile for expert multidisciplinary groups to develop validated scales or scoring systems that include biomarkers of metabolic health and cancer progression, genetic information, plus imaging findings for accurate FP diagnosis and prognostication.

Future Directions for Defining and Evaluating FP

Future work on FP should begin by establishing an evidence-based, universal definition. The absence of standardized nomenclature and diagnostic criteria currently hinders the comparison between studies, validation of imaging methods, and assessment of clinical significance. Uniform FP criteria will guide the selection of optimal imaging modalities, such as MRI-based PDFF, CT attenuation measurements, and/or EUS, for accurate and reproducible quantification of pancreatic fat. With standardized imaging protocols, FP can be better evaluated as an indicator of metabolic dysfunction and pancreatic disease. For further optimization, AI tools can foster efficiency, reliability, and diagnostic accuracy. Through improved imaging, the implications of the anatomical location and severity of pancreatic fat deposition can be surmised. For example, it is unclear whether FP of the head, body, or tail vs diffuse infiltration is associated with differing levels of risk. Other aspects to explore include the relationship between age and FP development, as well as the association of FP with other ectopic fat depots, or the lack thereof. Though it is suggested that patients with FP have an increased risk of fatty liver, neither an independent association nor causality has been identified.4 After adjusting for BMI, age, sex, and ethnicity, it was demonstrated that only 25% of the association between fat in the liver and fat in the pancreas is mediated by triacylglycerol, high-density lipoprotein cholesterol, and glucose.115

Accrued evidence supports the relationship of FP to conditions including acute and chronic pancreatitis, diabetes, and PDAC. More studies on the causal mechanisms of the progression of FP to clinically significant pancreatic disease should be pursued. It would be worthwhile to pair such evidence with analyses that uncover new genetic risk variants. These types of evidence would inform possible surveillance efforts surrounding FP, especially in high-risk populations. The reversibility of FP is unclear. The question is whether GLP-1/GIP agonists, as well as bariatric surgery and endoscopy, can serve as therapeutic options for reversing FP, thereby reducing PDAC risk. New MRI sequences are also being developed to enhance the diagnostic yield of FP in chronic pancreatitis and type 2 DM, with potential advantages over conventional imaging, plus increased objectivity in the interpretation of parenchymal and ductal changes.31
