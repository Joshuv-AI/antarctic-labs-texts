Multidimensional determinants of disaster response in prehospital emergency dispatch centers: content analysis of expert perspectives
Authors: Mohammadreza Shafiei, Asghar Tavan, Seyed Mobin Moradi, Vahid Saadatmand, Mahmood Nekoei-Moghadam
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and objectivesAmid the escalating frequency and severity of disasters, the role of prehospital emergency dispatch centers in delivering a swift and effective response has become increasingly critical. Despite their pivotal role, there is a paucity of qualitative evidence, particularly from developing nations, concerning the multidimensional challenges these centers face during crises. This study aimed to identify the determinants affecting the performance of emergency dispatch centers during disaster response in Iran.MethodsThis qualitative study was conducted between October 2024 and May 2025, employing an inductive approach with conventional qualitative content analysis. Data were gathered through semi-structured interviews with 20 participants, selected from diverse geographical regions and professional roles. Data analysis occurred concurrently with data collection, and analytical rigor was established through member checking and independent coding by multiple researchers.ResultsA total of seven main categories and 19 subcategories influencing the performance of dispatch centers during disasters were identified. These categories include: (1) leadership and command (2), Resources and Logistics (3), Operations Management (4), Dispatch Assessment and Decision-Making (5), Call Management (6), Contextual and Environmental Factors, and (7) Enhancement of Functional Coordination. These factors, both independently and interactively, constitute the operational framework for an effective response in disaster situations.ConclusionAn effective dispatch response during disasters emerges from the dynamic interplay among a multi-level command structure, resilient resources, rapid decision-making, and organizational coordination. While the findings are rooted in the Iranian context, many of the identified concepts are transferable to Emergency Medical Services (EMS) systems in other countries. Enhancing dispatcher training, fortifying communication infrastructures, and reinforcing coordination protocols are crucial interventions for improving the preparedness and resilience of health systems in the face of disasters.Keywords: Dispatch centers, Response, Prehospital emergency, Disasters

Introduction

Disasters, both natural and human-induced, represent a significant and growing threat to public health and community safety, inflicting widespread human casualties and substantial economic losses [1].

The escalating frequency and severity of disasters place increasing demands on healthcare systems to deliver swift and effective responses. In such critical situations, the timeliness and efficacy of prehospital emergency services are paramount to reducing mortality and mitigating severe injuries [2].

Consequently, recent years have seen a growing focus from both the scientific community and policymakers on the necessity of strengthening prehospital emergency response systems [3]. This focus stems from the profound impact of disasters on infrastructure and the considerable strain they impose on healthcare systems and emergency responders [4, 5]. Within this context, prehospital emergency dispatch centers, as the initial point of contact for patients with the emergency response system, play a pivotal role in determining the quality and effectiveness of disaster response [5–8] These centers are responsible for the rapid acquisition and analysis of information, telephone triage, prioritization of emergencies, allocation of limited resources, coordination among responding agencies, and timely dispatch of field teams. Furthermore, dispatchers provide critical pre-arrival instructions, guiding callers in administering first aid until medical teams are on scene [9, 10]. The efficiency of dispatch processes, particularly during mass-casualty incidents with high call volumes, is a decisive factor in survival and recovery outcomes. Effective and timely operations significantly reduce response times, optimize resource allocation, and ultimately save lives while minimizing secondary consequences [11].

Research underscores the importance of trained dispatchers and advanced systems in improving response times and resource allocation accuracy [12, 13]. The precision of dispatch protocols directly impacts the optimal allocation of resources during disasters. Studies, such as that by Bohm et al. (2018), have demonstrated variability in the accuracy of medical dispatch systems in identifying urgency levels, highlighting the challenge of balancing over-triage against under-triage. This challenge becomes particularly critical during natural disasters, given resource constraints and surging call volumes, emphasizing the need to adapt dispatch protocols to disaster-specific conditions [14].

The COVID-19 pandemic provided new insights into the performance of emergency medical dispatch centers during prolonged public health emergencies. Zhang et al. (2023) showed that the first wave of the pandemic in New York City was characterized by a surge in demand, prolonged response times, and shifts in the spatial and demographic patterns of need, compelling dispatch centers to rapidly adapt their resource allocation strategies. This experience revealed that large-scale health crises generate demand patterns distinct from those of conventional disasters, necessitating the development of specialized protocols for pandemic response within dispatch centers [15].

Despite the vital role of dispatch centers in prehospital systems, the “science of dispatch,” particularly in Asian countries, remains in its nascent stages of development. A review of regional literature indicates that this emerging and understudied field faces a significant knowledge gap, highlighting the need for context-specific, locally generated evidence to design effective disaster response frameworks. Moreover, the considerable heterogeneity of emergency service systems across these nations—in terms of structure, operational maturity, and protocols—limits the effectiveness of one-size-fits-all, standardized solutions [16].

This knowledge gap stems largely from the predominant focus in the existing literature on quantitative approaches, such as mathematical modeling, optimization algorithms, and metrics like response time and triage accuracy [14, 17–20].

Several studies, including those by Sheng et al. (2015), Yang et al. (2020), Zayas-Cabán et al. (2013), and Memari et al. (2020), have addressed ambulance command, dispatch, and resource allocation in disasters. While these studies have provided valuable numerical insights and contributed to the development of technological tools, they have been unable to offer an in-depth analysis of qualitative factors such as human interventions, environmental stressors, and inter-organizational dynamics in complex disaster environments [21–24]. Specifically, metaheuristic models, despite their effectiveness in controlled settings, face limitations in real-world settings due to technological incompatibilities, personnel shortages, communication breakdowns, and the pressures of rapid decision-making [25] Furthermore, the performance of dispatch centers is often undermined by challenges such as difficult access to incident sites, volatile and unpredictable disaster conditions, and delays in the arrival of operational teams [26–28].

Similarly, research focused on simulation analyses, machine learning algorithms, and decision support systems, while successful in developing technology-driven tools, has often failed to comprehensively investigate the qualitative dimensions that influence the performance of these systems in complex disaster scenarios [18, 20, 29].

In this context, a clear gap exists in the scientific literature regarding a qualitative, in-depth, and contextualized understanding of the performance of these centers, which has been largely confined to technical components. This gap is particularly evident at the level of field-based decision-making and in relation to human, organizational, and cultural facilitators and barriers, as well as interactions with other response agencies [30–33].

Furthermore, existing evidence suggests that shortages of trained personnel, the absence of standardized protocols, the vulnerability of technological infrastructure, and the instability of emergency communications are structural challenges facing dispatch centers in Iran that have received limited qualitative analysis in previous research [20, 34, 35]. The literature review reveals that dispatch centers have often been studied implicitly as a subcomponent of the broader Emergency Medical Services (EMS) system, rather than as distinct entities. Independent qualitative analysis of the roles, functions, and challenges of these centers, especially during disasters, is scarce. This is despite the fact that decision-making in these centers during a disaster is shaped by the simultaneous pressures of information scarcity, time constraints, resource multiplicity, and scene complexity, and has a decisive impact on the efficiency of the entire response process.

This study was designed to address these identified gaps in the literature and the limitations of quantitative, model-driven approaches. Employing a qualitative approach with conventional content analysis, this study explores the lived experiences of dispatch personnel in responding to disasters. Its aim is to present a realistic and multidimensional portrait of the performance, challenges, and capacities of these centers within the actual context of disaster operations. By identifying the human, organizational, technological, and environmental factors that influence dispatch response, this research aims to inform the design of structural interventions, targeted training programs, protocol revisions, and ultimately, to enhance the resilience of the healthcare system in confronting disasters.

Methods

This qualitative study was conducted from October 2024 to May 2025 as part of broader research focused on designing a preparedness and response model for prehospital emergency dispatch centers in Iran during disasters. All data were collected through semi-structured individual interviews. To ensure reflexivity, the researcher maintained a reflective journal throughout the study to critically examine personal biases and assumptions and ensure they did not inappropriately influence the data collection and analysis processes.

The study employed Conventional Qualitative Content Analysis (CQCA) with an inductive approach. This approach is particularly suited for exploring areas with limited theoretical and empirical background, especially within local, complex, and context-specific settings. In CQCA, concepts and themes are derived directly from the raw data, without relying on predefined categorization frameworks, to generate novel insights within a particular domain [36–39]. This characteristic distinguishes CQCA from other content analysis approaches, such as Directed Content Analysis or Summative Analysis, as well as from methods like Grounded Theory. While Grounded Theory aims to discover relationships between themes and build an integrated, abstract, and overarching theory, CQCA primarily focuses on extracting and describing tangible, context-specific themes directly within the data [40] Given that the primary aim of this study was to identify and elucidate the context-specific factors influencing the performance of emergency dispatch centers in disaster settings, rather than to generate a comprehensive, abstract theory, the grounded theory methodology was not aligned with the research objective. Grounded theory is principally employed for explaining processes and building theory from the ground up. Consequently, conventional qualitative content analysis was selected as the analytical method for this study. This approach is particularly well-suited for inductively identifying a wide range of relevant factors from the practical experiences and insights of experts and practitioners. Moreover, its adaptability to complex and underexplored contexts made it the most appropriate methodology for addressing the research question.

Research paradigm

This study was underpinned by the interpretivist paradigm. Interpretivism posits that reality is subjective, multiple, and socially constructed, emerging from the interactions among individuals and their interpretations of their experiences [41]. The aim of this study was to develop an in-depth understanding of the lived experiences and perspectives of experts in prehospital emergency dispatch centers when confronting the complex, high-pressure conditions of disasters. In such settings, decision-making is shaped by a confluence of human, organizational, environmental, and technological factors. The selection of this paradigm was therefore critical, as it enabled a deep exploration of the participants’ subjective meanings, beliefs, and perceptions—grounded in both their personal experiences and their professional knowledge and expert analyses—within their authentic, high-stakes operational context.

Unlike the positivist paradigm, which emphasizes generalizability, causal relationships, and statistical regularities, the interpretivist framework centers on a qualitative understanding of meaning-making processes, interpretive frameworks, and individual experiences within real-world social settings. This approach provides the necessary lens to deeply examine complex issues such as risk perception, inter-organizational dynamics, communication barriers, and decision-making pressures during disasters, directly from the viewpoint of frontline practitioners and subject-matter experts [42].

Study population, participant selection, and data collection

To achieve a comprehensive and rich understanding of the concept under investigation and to encompass a broad spectrum of experiences and perspectives related to the performance of prehospital emergency dispatch centers, this qualitative study utilized purposeful sampling with a maximum variation approach. Participant selection was guided by specific criteria, including their professional roles (such as dispatchers, emergency managers, dispatch center officials and staff, emergency medical technicians, and operational control center experts), level of experience and work history (to gather data from individuals with varying levels of expertise and familiarity with the subject), and geographical diversity (encompassing different regions of the country, including provincial centers, large cities, medium-sized cities, and semi-urban areas, reflecting geographical variations across the North, South, East, West, Center, Northeast, and Southwest of the country, to ensure the influence of geographical context was reflected in the analyses). Additionally, gender was considered in participant selection. In specific cases, and to identify individuals with unique experiences or specialized expertise, snowball sampling was also employed. In total, 20 participants took part in this study, ensuring that the collected data represented various dimensions of the study population. Prior to commencing the interviews, participants were informed about the study’s objectives, the voluntary nature of their participation, the confidentiality of their data, and their right to withdraw at any time. They were also notified that the research was part of the principal investigator’s doctoral dissertation. Verbal informed consent was obtained from all participants for their involvement and for the audio recording of the interviews. Participants selected the time and location for the interviews at their convenience. Interviews were conducted either in person at the dispatch centers or via telephone, with durations ranging from 20 to 120 min.

Data were collected through individual, semi-structured interviews. This method allowed the researcher to guide the conversation using a general but flexible framework, enabling the exploration of emergent themes based on participants’ responses.

To facilitate the interviews, an interview guide was developed based on the research objectives, a review of the relevant literature, and consultation with the research team. The guide covered key domains, such as participants’ experiences with disaster response within dispatch centers.

Sample core questions included:

“Could you please describe an experience you had at the dispatch center during a disaster?”

“Could you please describe an experience you had at the dispatch center during a disaster?”

“In your view, what factors contributed to improving or hindering the center’s performance?”

“In your view, what factors contributed to improving or hindering the center’s performance?”

Probing questions, such as “Could you please elaborate on that?” or “Can you provide an example?“, were used to elicit more detailed information and clarify responses. The interview guide was piloted in the initial interviews and subsequently revised to ensure the questions were well-suited to the interview context and research objectives.

All interviews were audio-recorded and transcribed verbatim immediately following their conclusion. Key statements were highlighted, and participants were re-contacted when necessary for clarification. Participants were also given the opportunity to review their transcripts to verify the accuracy of the data (member checking). The researcher actively worked to bracket personal preconceptions and prior knowledge to prevent them from unduly influencing the data analysis.

Initial data analysis was conducted concurrently with data collection. This iterative approach allowed initial findings to inform and refine subsequent interviews. Some questions in the guide were rephrased or refined based on new concepts or ambiguities that emerged from early interviews. In a few cases, participants were re-contacted to clarify certain responses or explore emergent topics. This flexible process enabled the researcher to achieve a deeper and richer understanding of the participants’ lived experiences within the real-world context of dispatch operations during disasters.

The study protocol, including all interviews, recording, and data storage procedures, was approved by the Research Ethics Committee of Kerman University of Medical Sciences. Interview audio files were stored on a secure, encrypted drive accessible only to the research team and were deleted after transcription was completed. The transcribed texts were anonymized using confidential codes to ensure participant identities could not be traced. To maintain anonymity in the final report, non-identifying labels such as “Participant 1” and “Participant 2” or codes like “P1” and “P2” were used. This method preserved the continuity of quotations while protecting participants’ identities. Data will be deleted upon completion of the analysis, in accordance with ethical guidelines. Throughout the study, the researchers were committed to upholding the principles of confidentiality, anonymity, and respect for participants’ rights.

Interviews continued until data saturation was achieved. This point was reached after 18 interviews, when no new themes emerged from the data. To confirm saturation, two additional interviews were conducted, which did not yield any new findings.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria were: possessing sufficient experience and knowledge of prehospital emergency dispatch center operations and a willingness to participate in the study. Exclusion criteria included unwillingness to continue participation or a lack of relevant information on the topic.

Data analysis

First, the interviews were transcribed verbatim. The principal investigator and a co-researcher then independently read each transcript multiple times to achieve immersion. Open coding was performed, where meaningful phrases and sentences were assigned descriptive conceptual labels. Subsequently, similar codes were grouped into subcategories. These subcategories were then organized into main categories based on their conceptual relationships. Finally, overarching themes were identified by consolidating related categories [43].

Data analysis was conducted by members of the research team experienced in qualitative methodologies. Initially, two researchers independently performed the open coding of the interviews. Subsequently, the generated codes were reviewed and verified by two other researchers. Any discrepancies or ambiguities were then resolved through discussion in a consensus meeting attended by the entire research team and an experienced external researcher. The final coding framework was established based on this team consensus. This collaborative, consensus-driven process enhanced the rigor, consistency, and credibility of the analysis.

This consensus-based approach enhanced the rigor and consistency of the analysis. The analysis was performed concurrently with data collection, and the final analysis was facilitated using MAXQDA software (version 2020).

In this study, a total of 334 initial codes were generated from the analysis of interview transcripts and qualitative data. Through an iterative process of rereading, conceptual comparison, and merging of similar codes, these initial codes were subsequently organized into seven main categories and their associated subcategories.

Trustworthiness

To ensure the rigor of the study, the trustworthiness of the findings was established using the four criteria proposed by Lincoln and Guba (1985): credibility, transferability, confirmability, and dependability [44].

To enhance the credibility of the research, the researcher achieved an in-depth understanding of the phenomenon through prolonged engagement in the field and persistent interaction with the data. Furthermore, member checking was conducted by presenting the extracted themes to the participants for their review. Their feedback was subsequently incorporated to refine and finalize the analysis.

Regarding confirmability, several measures were implemented to ensure that the findings were grounded in the data rather than the researcher’s biases. Initially, two researchers coded the interviews independently. Following this, 20% of the coded transcripts were re-coded by two different researchers, and the results were compared. Any discrepancies were resolved through consensus in group meetings attended by the research team and an external researcher. Two faculty members, serving as independent auditors, reviewed the interview transcripts, initial codes, and the developed categorical structure, confirming their validity. Rather than using quantitative inter-rater reliability coefficients like Cohen’s kappa, a consensus-based approach and collective reflexivity were employed to strengthen analytical coherence. Additionally, through peer debriefing, the preliminary findings and categorical framework were reviewed by a group of experts external to the research team. Their feedback was integrated to improve the clarity, coherence, and applicability of the results.

To address transferability, purposeful sampling with maximum variation was utilized. This strategy ensured the inclusion of participants with diverse professional roles, work histories, and from various geographical locations.

Finally, the dependability of the study was secured by transparently documenting every stage of the research process, from design through data collection, coding, and analysis. A comprehensive audit trail was established, enabling other researchers to evaluate and potentially replicate the research process, thereby ensuring the stability of the findings.

Through the analysis of data from interviews and documentary review, a total of seven main categories and 19 subcategories were identified (see Table 2 for details on the categories, subcategories, and representative codes). These categories and subcategories provide a comprehensive framework for understanding the challenges and operational requirements that dispatch centers face in disaster situations. In the sections that follow, each category and its corresponding subcategories are described in detail, supported by illustrative quotations from the interviews.

Discussion

The findings of this qualitative study offer a multi-dimensional perspective on the complexities and interactions that shape the response of prehospital emergency dispatch centers during disasters. Conventional content analysis led to the identification of seven main categories and Nineteen subcategories: (1) leadership and command (2), Resources and Logistics (3), Operations Management (4), Dispatch Assessment and Decision-Making (5), Call Management (6), Contextual and Environmental Factors, and (7) Enhancement of Functional Coordination. These categories, both individually and interactively, constitute the foundational framework of disaster response within this setting.

In the context of disasters, the Internal Command and Control must be designed to facilitate rapid and effective decision-making across various echelons of command. This framework should incorporate clearly defined levels of authority, role flexibility, and a situational command approach tailored to the specific incident. Furthermore, centralized issuance of operational directives and a unified command structure are critical determinants of a successful response, particularly for preventing jurisdictional conflicts and operational overlap between different levels. This finding is consistent with the conclusions of Sorani et al. (2018), who highlighted the need for flexible and transparent command structures within Emergency Medical Services (EMS) agencies [3].

Regarding Resources and Logistics, challenges such as power outages, limited emergency equipment, and difficulties in mobilizing off-duty personnel were observed during disasters. However, leveraging community capacities and the involvement of trained first responders (known locally as Orjansyar) emerged as an innovative and context-specific solution to mitigate these shortages. This approach is consistent with the findings of Duan et al. (2020) [7].

Effective dispatch operations management in disasters necessitates the optimal allocation of response assets—including ground ambulances, motorcycle ambulances, and aerial resources—coupled with meticulous logistical planning, effective scheduling, and the dynamic, adaptive deployment of these resources. This process must address critical challenges such as delays in resource dispatch, ensuring adequate coverage of disaster-stricken areas, and distributing patients according to injury severity. Furthermore, it must align hospital capacity and specialty capabilities with patient acuity to optimize ambulance turnaround times. These findings are consistent with those of Friesen et al. (2024) [30].Key strategies within this domain include the rapid dispatch of the nearest available units, phased or tiered activation of teams based on urgency, the deployment of mobile dispatch units during communication disruptions, and route optimization using real-time data. Prioritizing patient triage and strategically managing ambulance circulation are also paramount.

Rapid assessment and decision-making under stress are crucial factors in resource allocation and operational prioritization, both on-scene by EMS personnel and within the dispatch center by dispatchers. This process involves determining the incident’s scope based on initial call information, conducting preliminary prioritization, initiating immediate dispatch, performing an accurate scene size-up with validated data, maintaining rapid bidirectional communication, and refining decisions based on incoming field reports. The integration of physician consultation, facilitated by their presence in the dispatch center, enhances the accuracy of both assessment and dispatch management. Similarly, a study by Wennlund et al. (2022) highlights the critical role of dispatchers and the need to enhance their decision-making skills under pressure [20].

In the context of call management, a surge in call volume, the dissemination of inaccurate information by callers, and the congestion of emergency lines significantly complicate dispatch functions. Practical strategies identified in this study to manage call influx effectively include the use of telephone triage systems, initial call screening by experienced operators, and the temporary deactivation of non-essential digital systems. Wennlund et al. (2022) also emphasize the utility of telephone triage systems and preliminary call filtering [20]. Moreover, meticulous documentation of incident information—whether performed manually or augmented by AI-driven systems for speech-to-text conversion and automated logging—is vital for improving the speed and accuracy of the response.

Environmental and contextual factors—such as the region’s geographical characteristics, prevailing weather conditions, the time of the incident (particularly at night or during concurrent disasters), and the specific nature of the scene—can substantially increase the complexity of decision-making and contribute to EMS response delays. These factors introduce operational challenges in remote or rugged terrain, impact access and operations due to climatic conditions, and require tailored responses for disasters occurring in specific seasons or at night. The scale and dispersion of an incident can also strain operational capacity, necessitating the implementation of specialized protocols, such as those for CBRNE (Chemical, Biological, Radiological, Nuclear, and high-yield Explosives) events. The findings of Liu et al. (2023) regarding the influence of time and incident type on emergency response patterns align with the results of this study [15].

Furthermore, Intra- and inter-organizational coordination is a cornerstone of successful disaster response. The structural integration of entities such as the Emergency Operations Center (EOC) and the Medical Care Monitoring Center (MCMC) with the dispatch center, alongside the establishment of resilient and alternative communication channels for use during outages, plays a key role in strengthening this coordination. Studies by Nazari et al. (2023) and Vinson et al. (2023) have underscored the implementation challenges and the necessity of systemic integration, which corroborates the findings of this research [2, 34]. From a functional perspective, it is critical to enhance coordination by utilizing integrated and backup communication channels, promoting synergy and task-sharing between EMS and other relief teams, and establishing common information-sharing platforms governed by strict protocols for sensitive data exchange. The challenge of inter-organizational data discrepancies must be addressed through joint periodic training and exercises, role clarification, and educational synergy among agencies to ensure a cohesive and effective performance in disaster situations.

These challenges are not unique to Iran and have global dimensions, a point underscored by a comparative analysis of the performance of emergency dispatch centers in Iran and several leading countries. This comparison reveals that despite differences in structures and technologies, many of the core challenges are similar. However, the approach to addressing these challenges is shaped by national managerial, infrastructural, and cultural factors. For instance, a study by Smith et al. (2014) on the New York City emergency system’s response to Hurricane Sandy demonstrated that the use of advanced triage systems, standardized protocols, and effective collaboration among agencies—including EMS, police, fire departments, and hospitals—significantly reduced the workload on dispatch centers during the disaster [45]. In Iran, while the dispatch system benefits from experienced personnel, persistent challenges such as a lack of inter-organizational coordination and the fragility of communication systems during disasters hinder an effective response.

In countries like China, research by Shen et al. (2024) emphasizes that during large-scale disasters, the establishment of communication networks and active collaboration between cities and various organizations have enabled dispatch systems to make faster and more effective decisions. In contrast, the absence of a robust data-driven infrastructure and cohesive inter-provincial communication in Iran impedes a coordinated national-level response [46].

In the domain of command and management, our results indicate that the direct intervention of senior managers in field-level decision-making during disasters undermines the autonomy and slows the operational performance of dispatchers. Conversely, countries with flexible command systems have sought to increase response efficiency by empowering mid-level managers and adhering to predefined protocols [47]. This experience suggests that reforming management structures may facilitate more rapid and autonomous decision-making at the operational level, a principle that could be applicable in other national contexts as well.

Regarding technology adoption, the study by Liu et al. (2023) shows that employing smart tools for resource allocation and call data analysis during the COVID-19 pandemic played an effective role in reducing dispatcher workload and improving dispatch performance [15]. In Iran, however, decisions are still predominantly based on personal experience and judgment, with limited use of information technology tools. Therefore, developing decision support systems (DSS) and location-based dashboards could significantly increase the precision and speed of resource allocation.

The classic study by Wong (2004) also emphasizes the importance of “situational awareness” among dispatchers. His work suggests that improved workplace design, smart alerts, and real-time feedback can enhance decision-making [48]. As Iranian dispatchers often contend with information scarcity and ambiguous conditions, designing tools to foster a better understanding of the overall disaster situation could markedly increase response effectiveness.

In summary, this comparative analysis reveals that many of the challenges facing dispatch centers in Iran are analogous to those experienced by countries with a history of major crises. Consequently, leveraging international experiences and adapting them to the local context can provide a viable pathway for enhancing performance, strengthening coordination, and promoting the resilience of disaster dispatch centers.

Conclusion

The findings of this study revealed that an effective response by dispatch centers during disasters results from the interplay of several key factors: multi-level command structures, resilient resources, rapid decision-making, efficient communication, and inter-organizational coordination. Operating amidst resource constraints, time pressure, and dynamic conditions, the performance of these centers hinges on the organization’s capacity for dynamic decision-making, optimal call management, and flexible resource utilization. The analysis of participants’ lived experiences illuminated previously overlooked yet influential factors contributing to the success of interventions. This underscores the importance of understanding the interplay of inter-organizational, human, and environmental factors in designing resilient response frameworks for the health system.

Although these findings are rooted in the Iranian context, the core challenges identified appear to have broader applicability to other prehospital emergency systems. Challenges such as communication instability, disruptions in resource allocation, scene complexity, and the imperative for rapid decision-making are likely pervasive concerns that confront dispatch systems worldwide, irrespective of structural, technological, or cultural differences. Factors like the need for flexible, multi-tiered command structures, resilient communication infrastructure, rapid and accurate assessment by dispatchers and technicians, the critical role of telephone triage in optimizing dispatch, and inter-organizational coordination are recognized as foundational requirements for an effective disaster response in most EMS models. Accordingly, although rooted in a specific local context, the findings of this study can serve as a foundation for evaluating, refining, and adapting response frameworks in dispatch centers within other healthcare systems. The transferability of these results is critically dependent on a comprehensive understanding of, and adaptation to, the unique cultural characteristics, available resources, and administrative structures of each setting.

This study is the disaster response process of prehospital emergency dispatch centers in Iran using a comprehensive, systematic, and context-specific approach, treating it as a complex and interconnected system. By employing a qualitative methodology and conventional content analysis, this research presents a multi-dimensional picture of these centers’ performance during disasters, articulating the key interactions within the response system through the identification of seven main categories and 20 sub-categories. The use of lived experiences from dispatch professionals provides a depth of understanding of decision-making processes under high-pressure situations that quantitative studies cannot offer. The diversity of participants and adherence to principles of credibility, such as theoretical saturation and independent re-coding, have strengthened the robustness of the findings. The study’s focus on the Iranian context, coupled with an analysis of universal challenges, makes its findings practical and applicable for domestic policy-making and for health systems with similar characteristics. By providing a comprehensive overview of the requirements for an effective response by dispatch centers in disasters, these results can offer a solid foundation for developing operational frameworks and redesigning structures to enhance the preparedness and resilience of the health system.

Health systems, particularly in disaster and resource-constrained settings, are advised to adopt a suite of context-specific and operational interventions to enhance the preparedness and resilience of emergency dispatch centers for disasters. A cornerstone of this strategy is the development of contextualized training programs for dispatchers, focusing on critical skills such as decision-making under pressure, structured telephone triage, and situational awareness. The integration of high-fidelity simulations and realistic disaster scenarios, complemented by simple decision-support tools like checklists and step-by-step guides, can significantly improve the accuracy and timeliness of the emergency response.

Concurrently, strengthening the communication infrastructure is of paramount importance. Establishing disruption-resilient communication protocols, including backup systems for digital technology failures, and implementing multilayered communication channels are essential to ensure operational continuity and inter-unit coordination. In this regard, conducting joint Exercise with other response agencies plays a crucial role in fostering effective inter-organizational collaboration.

Furthermore, the development of data recording and monitoring systems that can function under disaster conditions—such as paper-based forms and lightweight, offline applications—is vital for maintaining the continuity of critical operations. The deployment of simple, location-based dashboards can also significantly aid real-time decision-making on resource allocation and mission prioritization.

From an organizational standpoint, strengthening the operational command structure within dispatch centers is essential. This can be achieved through targeted management training and the delegation of authority to mid-level personnel during a disaster, which markedly enhances decision-making agility and response swiftness. Additionally, leveraging trained volunteers in support roles—such as call-taking, data entry, or logistical support—can substantially augment human resource capacity in emergencies, provided that their engagement is governed by standardized and secure protocols.

The findings of this study address several critical gaps in the existing literature. First, it responds to the paucity of qualitative evidence on disaster dispatch, particularly in developing countries, by systematically documenting the lived experiences of dispatchers and managers. Second, through the analysis of empirical data, a comprehensive framework was developed, comprising seven main categories and nineteen subcategories. This framework provides an integrated perspective on the human, organizational, and contextual dimensions of the dispatch process. Such a holistic view is often absent in prior studies, which have been predominantly quantitative and model-driven, typically focusing on only one or a few isolated components. Furthermore, the findings reflect the operational realities and challenges of dispatch centers within the Iranian context, making them potentially relevant for other countries facing similar cultural, organizational, and resource-constrained environments.

In the realm of research, it is recommended that future studies employ mixed-methods approaches to comprehensively examine the functional, technological, and human dimensions of the dispatch center response. Conducting comparative studies across different geographical regions and health systems can aid in identifying transferable and locally adaptable solutions. Moreover, the systematic assessment of emerging technologies—such as artificial intelligence (AI), machine learning (ML) algorithms, and decision support systems (DSS)—for optimizing triage and resource allocation must be established as a key research priority.

Adopting a systematic, evidence-based approach that is sensitive to contextual factors is fundamental to enhancing the operational effectiveness of dispatch centers during natural and man-made disasters. The findings of this research offer actionable recommendations that can inform the development of operational policies and protocols in various health systems, once they have been contextualized and adapted to meet local conditions.

Limitations of the study

While this study endeavored to identify the multifaceted dimensions of prehospital emergency dispatch center performance during disasters by drawing on the field experiences of experts, it is subject to certain limitations that should be acknowledged when interpreting the findings.

First, the research was conducted within the specific context of Iran, a setting characterized by a semi-centralized healthcare system, technological resource constraints, and a unique cultural background. These contextual factors may have influenced the findings; consequently, the direct generalizability of these results to other countries—particularly to EMS systems with more centralized structures or advanced technological infrastructures—should be approached with caution and requires rigorous comparative analysis. Nevertheless, many of the core themes identified in this study, notably challenges such as the instability of communication infrastructures, the necessity of rapid situational assessment, and the critical importance of inter-agency coordination, appear to resonate with common concerns in other healthcare systems globally.

Methodologically, this study employed purposive sampling with a maximum variation strategy to ensure a diverse representation of professional roles, levels of experience, and geographical regions. This approach was supplemented with snowball sampling, which was particularly instrumental in identifying individuals with specific or unique expertise. Unlike some qualitative studies that encounter significant challenges in participant identification and recruitment, this research did not face major obstacles in its sampling process, owing to robust access to experts in the field and a satisfactory diversity among participants. However, it is possible that some crucial perspectives, especially from high-level decision-makers or collaborating non-health agencies, may not have been fully captured.

The primary data collection instrument consisted of semi-structured interviews, which are inherently susceptible to potential respondent bias. Although strategies such as member checking and multiple-coder analysis were implemented to enhance the trustworthiness of the analysis, qualitative interpretation may still be influenced by researcher preconceptions, and the complete elimination of such influence is not feasible. Furthermore, logistical constraints, such as limited access to certain geographical areas or specific managerial echelons, may have impacted the diversity and depth of the data collected.
