Optimizing dielectric constant of noncommercial biobased fibres - Acacia Nilotica, Acacia Leucophloea and Prosopis juliflora - For composite applications with selected fillers
Authors: Cholapancheri Krishnaswamy Hemalatha, Gopalan Venkatachalam, Bhuvaneshwari Mohan
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn recent decades, most electrical/electronic products possess non – biodegradable components. The expanding consumption of these goods and their eventual disposal pose a serious environmental threat. To cope up with this issue, biodegradable electrical/electronic components using biobased fibres can be an alternative option. The novel exploration of this research work is the usage of underutilized biobased fibres, such as Acacia Nilotica, Acacia Leucophloea and Prosopis Juliflora, as sustainable alternatives to traditional synthetic fibres in natural fibre-reinforced epoxy composites. The novelty lies in the combination of these natural fibres with ceramic fillers like Silicon Carbide, Boron Nitride and Aluminium Oxide, aiming to enhance the composite properties. The objective of the study is to optimize the type of fibre, fibre content (2 wt%, 4 wt% and 6 wt%) and types of fillers (1 wt%) combinations using the Box-Behnken Design to improve the dielectric properties of these composites. Analysis of Variance (ANOVA) is used to evaluate the relevance of each parameter on dielectric constant of the biobased fibre reinforced epoxy composite. The significant outcome is identified that Prosopis Juliflora (6 wt%) combined with Boron Nitride (1 wt%) yields an optimal dielectric constant of 1.11, highlighting the potential of these biobased fibres to serve as effective substitutes for conventional glass and carbon fibres for electrical insulating materials used in switchboards and socket pins to prevent electrical conduction and ensure safety. This research not only contributes to the field of sustainable materials but also addresses critical environmental concerns associated with electric/electronic waste.Keywords: Natural fibres, Box-behnken design, Composite, Dielectric property, Electrical application

Introduction

Natural resources are key for sustainable manufacturing, emphasizing natural fibres (NF's) over synthetic ones to protect the planet [1]. Generally, NF's are derived from plant, mineral, or animal sources. The common plant fibres include bark, bast, leaf, seed, fruit, and stem fibres [2]. Researchers are now focusing on noncommercial fibres such as Arecanut, Sansevieria, Water hyacinth and Prosopis juliflora, rather than commercial fibres like cotton, silk, jute, flax, banana and coir. Composites made of glass, aramid and carbon fibres have wide applications in the automotive, sports, aerospace and biomedical sectors because of their special mechanical qualities and low production cost, but with limited end–of–life options [3]. However, the environment and public health may suffer from the use of synthetic fibres [4]. The market for natural fibre composite materials is increased noticeably in recent years, making it one of the fastest-growing segments among all types of composites [1].

The predominant class of polymer matrix composites known as bast fibre-reinforced biocomposites (BFBCs) [5]. In the composite sector, bast fibres can replace synthetic fibres because they are sustainable and degradable [6]. Bast fibres are advantageous due to their high specific strength, air permeability, reduced energy consumption and emissions although their moisture absorption characteristics require careful management to ensure suitability for electrical applications [7]. Natural fibers, being renewable and low-energy to produce, offer eco-friendly disposal options like composting, unlike carbon and glass fibers. With growing demand for sustainable materials, they are used in automotive components by companies like Mercedes-Benz and BMW, as well as in packaging, textiles and casings for household appliances like mobile phones and laptops [[8], [9], [10]].

This research is involved with three non-commercial bast fibres, namely Acacia Nilotica, Acacia Leucophloea and Prosopis Juliflora. Of these fibres Mesquite or Prosopis Juliflora, (PJ) is a softwood variety with several uses that are hard and adaptable. Global administrations including Kenyan, Ethiopian, Indian governments are trying to eradicate this invasive weed because of its rapid growth despite its potential use, as reported by Refs. [11,12] Prosopis causes a wide range of negative impacts on the environment, society and economy. These effects are alteration of ecosystems, such as through allelopathic suppression; physical barriers created by the plant; competition for essential resources like space, light, water and nutrients; health-related issues, including injuries from thorns and harm to livestock; and the depletion of groundwater reserves due to its deep-rooted water acquisition [[13], [14], [15], [16]]. Due to this reason, the researchers have been interested in assigning a utility value to Prosopis Juliflora weed, which might otherwise be disposed as a landfill or incinerated. Both Acacia Nilotica and Acacia Leucophloea have antimicrobial property which can protect the end product from fungus formation as all these fibres are from the natural source.

Acacia Nilotica (AN), also known as Egyptian mimosa or Indian babul, is a versatile plant with a variety of uses, including providing fruits, wood, fodder and gums, as well as possessing medicinal properties [17]. AN fibres are noted for their potential in green composites due to their chemical composition and thermal stability [18]. Studies indicated that combining AN with glass fibre, epoxy resin, or polyester resin could enhance the mechanical and dynamic properties of composites [[19], [20], [21]].Prosopis Juliflora (PJ) serves as fodder, timber and fuel wood, with parts also consumed as food [22]. Research showed that PJ fibres are suitable for use in composites, offering improved mechanical properties in hybrid composites and making them viable for structural applications [[23], [24], [25], [26]]. Acacia Leucophloea (AL), or white kabesak, is known for its analgesic, anti-inflammatory and antimicrobial properties [27]. AL fibres, when treated with alkali, demonstrated enhanced tensile strength, thermal stability, and crystallinity, making them suitable for reinforcing polymer composites [28]. However, research on AL's use in composites is limited. Collectively, these studies underscore the potential of AN, PJ and AL as reinforcing materials in composites.

Composite materials use various fillers, each with distinct qualities and applications. Recent studies highlighted the role of fillers in enhancing composite materials' mechanical, physical and thermal properties. Natural fillers from plant improve fibre-reinforced polymer composites [29], while both artificial and natural nanofillers enhance their qualities [30]. Lignocellulosic fillers like wood pulp and sawdust boost mechanical properties [31,32] and nutshells, despite potentially reducing mechanical quality, are biodegradable and cost-effective [33]. Synthetic fillers strengthen the fibre matrix interface, improving the performance of polymer composites [34]. Research also shows that proper filler loading is crucial for enhancing mechanical characteristics and fracture toughness [35].Aluminium oxide (Al2O3) also known as Alumina, is a widely utilized filler in composite materials, valued for its low cost, ease of processing and acquisition, excellent mechanical, electrical, thermal and chemical properties, making it a significant focus in engineered ceramics research [36,37] It possesses outstanding dielectric properties, hardness, wear resistant, stiffness and high strength [38]. It is used in medical implants [39] and are used as a bio safe material [39].The use of lignin - Al2O3 hybrid fillers in abrasive tools enhanced the mechanical and thermal properties of composites [40].

Silicon Carbide (SiC), another ceramic, is known for its low density, remarkable strength, hardness, thermal conductivity and resistance to thermal shock, with studies indicating that SiC derived from rice husk significantly improves the physical and mechanical properties of jute-epoxy composites [38,41]. The addition of SiC particles enhances fracture toughness, mechanical properties like tensile, flexural, impact resistance and hardness, as observed in jute/epoxy and cabon/banana peduncle composite materials [42,43]. Boron nitride (BN) is recognized for enhancing the thermal and mechanical properties of polymer composites. Boron Nitride (BN)/Banana Fiber (BF)/Epoxy based polymer composite exhibited good electrical resistance and low dielectric constant [44,45]. The use of boron nitride nanotubes (BNNTs), significantly improved thermal conductivity, reduced thermal expansion and maintained electrical insulation in polymeric composites [46]. Research on varying BN particle sizes highlights the positive effects on electrical, mechanical and thermal properties with dielectric constant values below 4.5, indicating optimal electrical insulating behaviour [47,48]. The dielectric constant measures a material's electrical energy storage capacity, with the dielectric properties of bast fibre composites, including resistivity and conductivity, being crucial for insulation or conducting applications. The dielectric property is crucial for circuit boards, since it has a direct impact on the integrity and speed of signal transmission in high-speed electronic systems [49]. Similar considerations apply to electronic insulating materials used in switchboards and socket pins, where the dependability and efficiency of electronic components depend on the material's capacity to efficiently insulate electrical circuits and minimize energy losses [50]. Optimizating the dielectric constant of coir fibre composites reduces the dependency on nonrenewable resources, thus benefitting the environment [51]. Adding boron nitride dispersed into silica sol enhances thermal conductivity and improves electrical insulation and dielectric property in advanced machineries [52].

Research on Caryota urens fibre and coconut husk biochar in epoxy biocomposites revealed that adding 7 % Caryota urens fibre results in an optimal dielectric constant [53] 5.6) while a composite of banana fibre, epoxy and BN, shows a low dielectric constant of 1.143 [48]. [54] reported that the dielectric properties of jute/bamboo fibres with unsaturated polyester and polypropylene decreased with increasing frequency and increased with increasing fibre content.

The research gaps identified from the literature review suggest that while natural fibres have been explored in various composite applications, their potential to improve the dielectric properties of composites, particularly when combined with ceramic fillers, remains underexplored. This study addresses these gaps by investigating the influence of underutilized biobased fibres such as Acacia Nilotica, Acacia Leucophloea and Prosopis Juliflora in enhancing the dielectric property of epoxy composites. By optimizing the fibre type, fibre content and types of fillers using the Box-Behnken Design, the study aims to fill the existing gap in knowledge regarding the role of these variables in achieving optimal dielectric performance. (RSM) Response surface methodology/(BBD) Box Behnken Design is utilized to ascertain the optimization, emphasizing the distinctive aspect of the investigation even more. This novel study is to investigate the influence of different variables on the dielectric property. The novel approach of combining these natural fibres with specific ceramic fillers, particularly the use of Prosopis Juliflora with Boron Nitride, highlights the study's contribution to the field by providing a sustainable alternative to traditional synthetic fibres in electrical/electronic applications, thereby addressing both material performance and environmental concerns.

Materials required

Natural fibres - Acacia Nilotica, Acacia Leucophloea and Prosopis Juliflora fibres are used as reinforcement materials in this study. These natural fibres are selected because they are cost-effective, abundant and eco-friendly [55]. The selected wood is sourced from Mambakkam, Chennai. The gathered wood is cleaned, chopped (10” - 12” length), air-dried at atmospheric temperature, ground into fibre powder (5 mm approximately), finely pulverized using pulverising machine and sieved to obtain fibres ranging from 150 to 300 μm in size, which are subsequently used as reinforcements in composites.

The matrix material used here is epoxy resin (LY556) along with hardener (HY951). Fillers such as silicon carbide, boron nitride and aluminium oxide are used to improve the strength of the end product. Initially, silicon rubber is used to prepare the mould of size 50 x 50 × 10 mm3 meant for a dielectric constant specimen. The materials for preparing the samples are procured from Herenba Instruments and Engineers, Chennai, Tamil Nadu, India.

Sample preparation

The fabrication of this composite utilizes a hand layup process. A consistent quantity of filler (Al2O3, SiC, BN), each with a weight of 1 %, is used with varying amounts (2 wt%, 4 wt% and 6 wt%) of fibre powder (Acacia Nilotica, Acacia Leucophloea and Prosopis Juliflora), and mixed with epoxy resin. The Matrix: hardener ratio is prepared in the ratio of (10:1) based on the supplier's recommendations. The fibre and the filler are completely incorporated into this matrix mixture. These are blended according to predetermined quantities based on the design of the experiment method and the dimensions of the test samples. The mixture is continually stirred using a magnetic stirrer until a uniform dispersion is formed. The resulting mixture is then poured into a clean silicon mould and cured. Cleaning of the moulds is mandatory for removing any dust particles such as dried composite laminate from the mould. The mould is kept constant for 3–4 h at room temperature to get cured. Dielectric constant (DC) test

The Dielectric constant test is performed using an analog dielectric constant apparatus (Fig. 4). The changing capacitance of test samples and standard specimen, at resonance conditions, is measured to calculate the dielectric constant. The capacitance of the test samples is measured by placing them between 2 metallic plates. With the sample removed between the electrodes, the second run is measured. The ratio between these two values is the dielectric constant. The testing is performed at room temperature. The same procedure is followed for the remaining samples. Response surface methodology

Response Surface Methodology (RSM), a statistical and mathematical approach, is involved to identify optimal experimental conditions with minimal experiments. It explores the relationship between measured responses and independent input variables to optimize outcome efficiently [56]. opined that the primary benefit of employing RSM is its efficiency. It means that in order to accurately estimate the optimal circumstances and forecast the impacts of the factor interactions, only small-scale tests are needed [57]. suggested that to produce the regression model and carry out the statistical optimization with RSM, Box behnken design (BBD) could be used. Due to its capacity to prevent undesirable outcomes by rejecting trials carried out under extreme conditions, this strategy has lately gained popularity among researchers. Hence, the experiments and optimization of the study are performed based on RSM/BBD. A three-level factorial design is created using Minitab software following BBD. Each parameter is allotted 3 levels namely, −1, 0, and 1, where −1 refers to the minimum value, 0 is the mid-level value and 1 is the maximum value as shown in Table 1. Minitab software is used to interpret the data and analyse the dielectric constant results. Each variable is given a separate nomenclature as A, B and C for the parameters like fibre type, fibre content (weight %) and type of filler respectively (Table – 1). The properties of fibre and filler properties are assessed in the study and shown in Table 2, Table 3. Fifteen samples are prepared in various combinations according to BBD and results are evaluated for the same (Table – 4).

Dielectric constant (DC) values are appropriate for different applications; high DC values are best for energy storage devices such as capacitors, while low DC values are better for high-frequency applications and insulators to reduce energy loss. DC values must be steady and consistent, particularly where variations could impair performance. In order to ascertain the DC's suitability for the desired application, it is crucial to compare the filler, fibre and matrix types with industry norms or comparable materials.

Confirmation test

Table 9 clearly shows that the dielectric constant value obtained by the experimentation method is 1.11 and that calculated through optimization is 1.07. The error value of 3.6 shows the authenticity of the optimization process. The minimum dielectric constant is achieved by Prosopis juliflora fibre (6 % wt.) with boron nitride fillers (1 % wt.). Dielectric materials with high and low values are frequently employed. Low dielectric materials are defined as those with a dielectric constant of less than 2. As electronic component dimensions continue to shrink, the necessity for low-k dielectric materials increases. This would facilitate the creation of a thin composite with superior dielectric qualities for use in electrical applications. From Table 2, Table 3 and it is found that the fibre PJ has less density of 580 (g/cm³) as compared to other fibres. Also, the density of filler i.e. boron nitride is relatively less compared to the remaining fillers namely silicon carbide and aluminium oxide. The dielectric constant of hexagonal boron nitride (DC value 4–5) [63], is also comparatively less than silicon carbide (6.5–10) [80] and aluminium oxide (9) [62], which depicts all these factors may be the reason for a less dielectric constant value. SiC and Al2O3 have comparatively high dielectric constants, which are just barely acceptable for electronic encapsulation. Because of its very high thermal conductivity and relatively low dielectric constant (about 4–5), h BN is seen as an excellent option [63]. From Tables 8 and it is confirmed that compared to other biobased fibres, Prosopis Juliflora (along with boron nitride) has obtained a very low dielectric constant value of 1.11 and can be opted for electronic applications where minimum electrical interference is required.

Conclusion

This paper investigates the dielectric constant of various natural fibres combined with different fillers, identifying the optimal combination with the lowest dielectric constant. The findings indicate that the type of fibre significantly influences the dielectric constant. The regression equation highlights the effects of these factors, offering insights for practical applications. The study concludes that Prosopis Juliflora (6 wt %) with boron nitride (1 wt %) filler achieves the lowest dielectric constant of 1.11, making it a promising material for developing environmentally beneficial products. The study acknowledges certain limitations as natural fibres tend to absorb moisture and are prone to biological degradation, which can limit their durability and performance in specific applications. The results suggest potential for further exploration of composite materials to enhance their electrical properties and applications, encouraging broader assessments of other characteristics as well.
