Transparent AI-driven personalized risk prediction system for acute kidney injury after total hip arthroplasty
Authors: Jiaojiao Tai, Linbang Wang, Yang Li, Mingxin Chen, Ziqiang Yan, Jingkun Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Acute kidney injury is a common and severe complication following total hip arthroplasty, particularly in elderly or high-risk patients with chronic conditions, significantly increasing morbidity and mortality rates. Traditional prediction methods often struggle with the complexity of multidimensional healthcare data. To address this, we developed a machine learning-based prediction model using multidimensional data from 4601 total hip arthroplasty patients, encompassing 16 general variables (e.g., demographic characteristics, surgical duration, and hospital stay) and 53 laboratory indicators (e.g., Cystatin C, D-dimer, and glucose). Feature selection was performed using Random Forest, Lasso regression, and mutual information analysis, with clinically relevant features such as Cystatin C, glucose, and N-terminal proBNP retained to enhance model interpretability and predictive power. To address class imbalance, we applied the Synthetic Minority Over-sampling Technique and Edited Nearest Neighbors. Among multiple models, CatBoost achieved the best performance, with an area under the receiver operating characteristic curve of 0.95 (95% CI 0.93–0.96), an accuracy of 0.88 (95% CI 0.85–0.90), and an F1-score of 0.79 (95% CI 0.75–0.84) in the internal validation set. External validation using an independent hospital dataset (n = 240) further confirmed the model’s robustness, with an AUC of 0.65 (95% CI 0.57–0.73). However, the substantial performance decline in external validation underscores the need for cautious interpretation of performance metrics and institution-specific validation prior to clinical deployment. Shapley Additive Explanations analysis identified Cystatin C, surgical duration, and creatinine as key predictors, demonstrating the model’s transparency and clinical relevance. A real-time prediction system, developed using the Flask framework, was validated externally, confirming its utility for acute kidney injury risk assessment and personalized postoperative management. These findings highlight the model’s potential to improve clinical decision-making and outcomes for high-risk patients undergoing total hip arthroplasty.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-21810-z.

Introduction

Acute kidney injury (AKI) is a common and severe complication following total hip arthroplasty (THA), particularly in elderly patients or high-risk individuals with chronic conditions, where morbidity and mortality rates are significantly elevated1. The incidence of AKI in THA patients varies widely, ranging from 0.5 to 29.5%, depending on patient comorbidities, surgical techniques, and perioperative management strategies. However, contemporary large-scale studies focusing specifically on primary THA procedures consistently report rates at the lower end of this spectrum. Most notably, Gharaibeh et al.’s comprehensive analysis of 8949 THA patients reported an overall AKI incidence of 1.1%, while Singh and Cleveland’s analysis of over 4.1 million primary THAs reported 1.5%2,3. This variability underscores the clinical relevance of AKI, as its occurrence is associated with prolonged hospital stays, increased healthcare costs, and a heightened risk of progression to chronic kidney disease (CKD) or mortality. Notably, severe AKI cases in THA patients can increase mortality risk by up to 5-fold and significantly elevate healthcare utilization, emphasizing the urgent need for effective risk stratification and preventive measures3,4. Therefore, early identification and effective intervention for AKI have become critical in improving patient outcomes.

However, the mechanisms of AKI are complex and influenced by multiple factors, such as oxidative stress, inflammation, hemodynamic instability, and nephrotoxic insults, making its prediction challenging5–7. Oxidative stress results from an imbalance between reactive oxygen species production and antioxidant defenses, leading to cellular damage in renal and distant organs. Inflammation, mediated by cytokines and immune cell activation, amplifies tissue injury and contributes to systemic organ crosstalk, further promoting distant organ dysfunction such as lung or heart injuries5,6. Hemodynamic instability, such as reduced renal perfusion, triggers ischemia–reperfusion injury and compromises renal recovery6,8. Nephrotoxic insults, including exposure to certain medications and contrast agents, directly impair tubular function and enhance AKI severity5,7. These interconnected mechanisms underscore the biological complexity of AKI and the need for predictive models capable of capturing nonlinear relationships and complex interactions to facilitate early identification and intervention6,9.

Traditional prediction methods primarily rely on statistical models and clinical experience, which, while offering some reference for risk assessment, exhibit notable limitations in handling multidimensional and complex medical data10. For example, logistic regression assumes linear relationships and independence among predictors, limiting its utility in scenarios where complex interactions exist11. Moreover, traditional models often struggle with class imbalances in datasets, resulting in reduced predictive accuracy for rare outcomes like AKI12. These challenges are particularly pronounced in the context of AKI, where the interplay of oxidative stress, inflammation, hemodynamic instability, and nephrotoxic insults creates nonlinear and multifactorial patterns that traditional approaches fail to capture effectively5,6.

Recent applications of machine learning (ML) to AKI prediction in orthopedic settings have shown promise but remain limited in scope. For instance, Chen et al. developed ML models to predict AKI in elderly orthopedic postoperative patients using nine algorithms and a nomogram, but relied on a single institutional cohort and did not fully address class imbalance13. Similarly, Tran et al. constructed a ML tool for perioperative kalaemia disturbance and AKI risk after total knee arthroplasty, achieving high AUC but without external validation or interpretable thresholds14. Nikkinen et al. used the RUSBoost algorithm to predict perioperative AKI in knee and hip arthroplasty patients, achieving AUROC of 0.91 (serum creatinine-based) and 0.98 (urine output-based), but lacked external validation15. Schilling et al. developed and validated perioperative risk-adjustment logistic models for hip fracture repair and THA using ACS-NSQIP data, demonstrating adequate discrimination but limited ML integration and no postoperative AKI focus16. A recent systematic review by Karlin et al. highlighted the growing use of supervised ML in total joint arthroplasty but found that fewer than 35% of studies achieved excellent prognostic accuracy and only 9 were externally validated17.

Despite these advances, no study to date has specifically addressed AKI prediction in THA patients using a multicenter cohort, robust resampling to handle extreme class imbalance, and explainable ML methods to derive actionable predictor thresholds.

In recent years, with the rapid accumulation of medical big data, ML techniques have demonstrated substantial potential in the medical field. ML can analyze vast and complex datasets, identify underlying patterns, and provide more accurate predictive outcomes. Notably, gradient boosting models such as CatBoost have shown excellent performance in addressing class imbalance and handling complex features, while also enhancing clinical interpretability through feature importance analysis, thus increasing their practical value in healthcare applications18.

This study aims to develop and validate a ML-based system to predict postoperative AKI in patients undergoing THA, utilizing advanced techniques to address challenges such as class imbalance and model interpretability, with the ultimate goal of improving early risk identification and supporting clinical decision-making to enhance patient outcomes. Specifically, by focusing on a multicenter retrospective THA cohort, we seek to fill the gap in AKI prediction research within orthopedic arthroplasty, apply robust resampling strategies to mitigate class imbalance, and employ explainable ML methods to derive clinically meaningful thresholds for key predictors. Additionally, an independent external validation cohort is incorporated to assess model generalizability across different institutions, addressing a common limitation in prior studies that relied solely on internal validation.

To achieve this, we utilized retrospective data from surgical patients to build and evaluated a ML-based AKI prediction system. The model integrates advanced models, including CatBoost, to enhance predictive performance. To overcome the challenge of class imbalance commonly found in clinical datasets, we applied the Synthetic Minority Over-sampling Technique and Edited Nearest Neighbors (SMOTEENN), which significantly improving the accuracy of identifying high-risk patients. Additionally, Shapley Additive Explanations (SHAP) were employed to provide transparent insights into feature contributions and to identify critical thresholds for key predictors, facilitating a better understanding of the factors influencing prediction outcomes and ensuring the model’s interpretability. Importantly, to evaluate the generalizability and robustness of the developed model, an independent external validation cohort was employed. The model demonstrated favorable performance in this external dataset, highlighting its potential for broader clinical application across different settings. These contributions establish a validated ML-based prediction model specifically designed for AKI prediction in THA patients, address key challenges in clinical datasets, and provide a reproducible framework for other clinical risk prediction tasks.

Results

This study utilized data from 4601 patients from Xi’an Honghui Hospital for model development and internal validation, and an independent external validation dataset of 240 patients from Ankang Central Hospital to assess model generalizability in predicting AKI following THA.

Study population and AKI incidence

Among the 4601 patients included in the final analytical cohort, 97 (2.1%) developed postoperative AKI according to Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO) criteria. This incidence rate is consistent with established literature on THA-associated AKI. Gharaibeh et al.’s comprehensive study of 8949 THA patients reported an overall AKI incidence of 1.1%, with rates varying dramatically based on patient risk profiles—from < 1% in low-risk patients to > 20% in those with multiple comorbidities2. Similarly, Singh and Cleveland’s analysis of over 4.1 million primary THAs reported an AKI incidence of 1.5%3, confirming that our observed rate falls within the established range for contemporary primary THA populations. The consistency across these large-scale studies spanning different institutions validates that AKI incidence in the 1–3% range represents realistic expectations for modern primary THA practice.

Baseline characteristics

Among the 4601 patients included in the analysis, the non-AKI group consisted of 4504 patients with a median age of 61.0 years (IQR: 52.0–68.2 years), while the AKI group included 97 patients with a median age of 67.0 years (IQR: 59.0–73.0 years). There was a significant difference in age between the two groups (P < 0.001, Mann–Whitney U test). In the non-AKI group, 72.6% of patients had no history of hypertension, compared to only 50.5% in the AKI group (P < 0.001, chi-square test), suggesting an association between hypertension and the occurrence of AKI. The median surgery time was 90.0 min (IQR: 70.0–110.0 min) in the non-AKI group, whereas it was significantly longer in the AKI group, at 100.0 min (IQR: 75.0–130.0 min) (P = 0.022, Mann–Whitney U test). There was also a significant difference in length of stay (LOS), with a median of 7.0 days (IQR: 6.0–9.0 days) in the non-AKI group, compared to 8.0 days (IQR: 7.0–11.0 days) in the AKI group (P < 0.001, Mann–Whitney U test).

Regarding laboratory indicators, the Cystatin C (Cys C) level was significantly higher in the AKI group than in the non-AKI group, at 0.9 mg/L (IQR: 0.8–1.1) versus 0.7 mg/L (IQR: 0.6–0.9) (P < 0.001, Mann–Whitney U test). Hemoglobin (Hgb) levels were significantly lower in the AKI group, at 123.0 g/L (IQR: 112.0–133.0) compared to 129.0 g/L (IQR: 118.0–141.0) in the non-AKI group (P < 0.001, Mann–Whitney U test). Additionally, the pro-B-type Natriuretic Peptide (proBNP) level was markedly elevated in the AKI group, at 133.5 pg/mL (IQR: 54.2-309.4) compared to 83.9 pg/mL (IQR: 40.5–165.4) in the non-AKI group (P < 0.001, Mann–Whitney U test). This suggests that elevated proBNP levels may be associated with the development of AKI, consistent with other studies showing similar trends19. External validation cohort

The external validation dataset from Ankang Central Hospital, comprising 240 patients discharged between January 1, 2022, and November 30, 2024, after excluding samples with more than 20% missing data for key features, was used to evaluate model generalizability. In contrast to the internal cohort, the external validation cohort demonstrated a substantially higher AKI incidence of 27.1% (65 of 240 patients), representing a 13-fold increase compared to the internal rate. This marked difference provides important validation of our predictive model’s ability to detect AKI across varying risk populations and supports the contention that our internal cohort’s low incidence reflects contemporary best practices in optimized primary THA patients rather than systematic underdetection. Input features were standardized to align with the internal cohort for robust validation.

Institutional differences and population characteristics

To ensure robust interpretation of our external validation results, we conducted comprehensive comparative analyses between Xi’an Honghui Hospital and Ankang Central Hospital to characterize potential population and institutional differences. Table S1 presents detailed demographic and clinical variable comparisons between the two institutions. Several significant institutional differences were identified. Patients at Ankang Central Hospital were significantly older (63.1 ± 9.6 vs. 60.2 ± 13.4 years, p = 0.001), shorter (161.5 ± 7.1 vs. 164.1 ± 8.1 cm, p < 0.001), and had lower body weight (60.3 ± 10.7 vs. 64.2 ± 11.9 kg, p < 0.001). Most notably, the median LOS was substantially longer at Ankang Central Hospital (15.5 ± 7.4 vs. 7.8 ± 2.8 days, p < 0.001), and surgical duration was significantly extended (122.0 ± 41.7 vs. 98.5 ± 40.7 min, p < 0.001).

Key predictive biomarkers also showed substantial distributional differences between institutions (Tables S2 and S3). D-Dimer levels were markedly elevated at Ankang Central Hospital (3.8 ± 8.5 vs. 0.9 ± 0.8 mg/L, p < 0.001), proBNP levels were significantly higher (248.2 ± 276.3 vs. 125.9 ± 113.0 pg/mL, p < 0.001), and red cell distribution width (RDW-SD) values were increased (46.3 ± 5.7 vs. 44.1 ± 3.6, p < 0.001). Most importantly, the actual AKI incidence rate was dramatically higher at the external validation site (27.1% vs. 2.1%, p < 0.001), representing a 13-fold increase in baseline risk. These substantial institutional differences explain the dramatic variance in AKI incidence rates and provide validation for our model’s capacity to maintain predictive performance across diverse clinical settings.

Data splitting and missing data

The dataset was divided into a training set (3220 patients) and and a test set (1381 patients) using stratified random sampling, maintaining the same proportion of AKI cases in both subsets (approximately 2:98). Among the training set, 68 patients (2.11%) developed AKI, while 3152 patients (97.89%) did not. Similarly, the test set included 29 patients (2.10%) with AKI and 1352 patients (97.90%) without AKI. This stratification ensured consistency in AKI distribution, minimizing bias during model development and evaluation.

Regarding missing data, analysis was conducted across the entire dataset of 4601 patients. BMI had the greatest missing rate at 23.41% (1077 missing values), followed by height (22.69%, 1044 missing values), aPTT (19.65%, 904 missing values), and glucose (13.24%, 609 missing values). Blood loss had a relatively lower missing rate of 9.19% (423 missing values). The overall pattern of missing data was consistent between the training and test sets. A detailed summary of missing values and missing rates for all features is provided in Table S4.

Comparative analysis of resampling techniques

Applied exclusively to the training set to address the severe class imbalance (AKI incidence: 2.1%), various resampling methods were systematically evaluated. Among the various resampling methods evaluated, SMOTEENN demonstrated the best overall performance for addressing class imbalance, achieving the highest F1-score (0.969) and a strong ROC-AUC (0.815) (Table S5). It outperformed oversampling methods like SMOTE, Borderline-SMOTE, and ADASYN, which had ROC-AUC values of 0.814, 0.802, and 0.810, respectively, and undersampling methods like ENN and TomekLinks, which showed lower ROC-AUC values (0.784 and 0.716). SMOTEENN also maintained high accuracy (0.979) while effectively reducing noise and outliers, resulting in a balanced trade-off between sensitivity and specificity. These results highlight SMOTEENN as the most suitable technique for developing robust predictive models for AKI, particularly in datasets with severe class imbalance.

Feature selection

A total of 13 core features were selected through this multi-step process: Blood loss, Age, Surgical Duration, Hgb, Weight, Glucose, Cys C, Creatinine, Alkaline Phosphatase (ALP), D-Dimer, Height, Fibrinogen (Fbg), and proBNP. These features were derived through rigorous statistical selection, as detailed in Tables S6, S7, and S8. Specifically, Table S6 presents the initial 41 features identified by the RF algorithm based on their importance scores. Table S7 highlights the 34 features retained after Lasso regression, while Table S8 shows the 12 features selected using mutual information analysis. Notably, proBNP was included in the final set due to its clinical significance, despite being excluded during the mutual information analysis step. To validate the effectiveness of this feature reduction pipeline (69 → 41 → 34 → 12 → 13) and address potential overfitting concerns given the low AKI event rate, we conducted an iterative proxy evaluation using Random Forest, reporting key performance metrics (e.g., AUC-ROC, F1-score) with 95% confidence intervals across varying feature numbers via tenfold cross-validation on the training set. This evaluation confirmed stable performance, minimal variance, and no evidence of overfitting, with the 13-feature set selected for its optimal balance of predictive power, parsimony, and clinical relevance (see Supplementary Results, Table S9, and Figure S1 for detailed metrics, including rationale for prioritizing 13 features over alternatives like the 34-feature set).

Multi-model comparison and generalization performance evaluation

In this study, we evaluated 11 ML models, including Support Vector Machine (SVM), LightGBM, CatBoost, AdaBoost, Random Forest (RF), Multilayer Perceptron (MLP), Linear Discriminant Analysis (LDA), k-Nearest Neighbors (KNN), Gaussian Naive Bayes (GaussianNB), Gradient Boosting, and a Stacking ensemble model, across internal validation, test, and external validation datasets. All classification metrics (Accuracy, Precision, Recall, F1, F0.5, F2, Kappa, and MCC) were calculated using a default classification threshold of 0.5, unless otherwise specified, to convert predicted probabilities into binary outcomes. On the internal validation set, the Stacking model achieved the highest AUC (0.97, 95% CI 0.96–0.98), followed by CatBoost (0.95, 95% CI 0.93–0.96) and RF (0.92, 95% CI 0.90–0.94) (Fig. 2A). Notably, on the test set, the Stacking model yielded the lowest AUC (0.59, 95% CI 0.48–0.71), indicating poor generalization capability (Fig. 2B). In contrast,CatBoost demonstrated superior generalization ability and simplicity, as evidenced by its high average precision (AP) scores of 0.89 (0.85–0.92) on internal validation set and 0.65 (0.51–0.75) on the test set (original data: 0.04 (0.02–0.09), weighted: 0.65 (0.51–0.75)) (Fig. 2C,D). High-capacity models such as MLP and Stacking were particularly scrutinized for overfitting risks due to the low event rate; however, the independent feature selection pipeline (detailed in Methods, with explicit reduction steps and decision criteria to achieve a 7.5:1 event-to-predictor ratio), combined with resampling (SMOTEENN), regularization, and bootstrap validation, resulted in stable performance metrics across datasets, with no evidence of overfitting as confirmed by learning curves (Fig. 3) illustrating narrow and often decreasing gaps between training and cross-validation scores as training examples increased (e.g., for key models like CatBoost and LightGBM, where curves converged without divergence, underscoring the pipeline’s effectiveness in mitigating low-event overfitting). Further analysis of calibration curves (Fig. 2E,F) highlighted that CatBoost exhibited a strong alignment with the “perfectly calibrated” line, underscoring its reliability. In comparison, RF showed modest deviations from ideal calibrated on the test set but maintained acceptable performance. Decision curve analysis (DCA, Fig. 2G,H) indicated that CatBoost consistently delivered higher net benefits across threshold probabilities of 0.1–0.4, confirming its strong clinical utility. Conversely, the Stacking model’s net benefit diminished at higher thresholds, indicating limited applicability, while RF remained inferior to CatBoost. On the external validation set, CatBoost maintained robust performance (AUC = 0.65, 95% CI 0.57–0.73, AP = 0.38, 95% CI 0.29–0.49), comparable to Gradient Boosting, while MLP achieved the highest AUC (0.75, 95% CI 0.68–0.81) and AP (0.47, 95% CI 0.37–0.60) (Figure S2A, S2B). The detailed performance metrics for all models are summarized in Table S10, further emphasizing CatBoost’s competitive performance across multiple key metrics, including accuracy (0.71, 95% CI 0.65–0.76), F1 score (0.31, 95% CI 0.18–0.41), and Brier score (0.19, 95% CI 0.17–0.22). Calibration curves (Figure S2C) confirmed CatBoost’s superior probability estimation, closely aligned with the “perfect calibration’line. DCA (Figure S2D) further highlighted CatBoost’s higher net benefits across clinically relevant thresholds (0.1–0.6). Although the Stacking model showed marginally better performance at higher thresholds (> 0.7), its complexity and limited generalizability may hinder its practical application in clinical settings. The performance decline observed during external validation (from internal AUC = 0.95 to external AUC = 0.65) can be attributed to the substantial domain shift characterized by the 13-fold higher AKI incidence rate and significant distributional differences in key predictive features between institutions, as detailed in the institutional comparison analysis above.

Collectively, these results underscore CatBoost’s exceptional performance on both internal and external datasets, demonstrating a unique combination of predictive accuracy, stability, and calibration. Its consistent generalization across datasets and competitive clinical utility position it as the most reliable model for practical implementation.

Learning curves and model performance

Figure 3 illustrates the learning curves for each model. CatBoost and LightGBM (Fig. 3C and G) exhibited stronger generalization ability as the number of training samples increased. Notably, the CatBoost model achieved a training score close to 1.0, with a small gap between the training and cross-validation scores, indicating a low risk of overfitting and stable performance across varying data scales20. In contrast, the SVM (Fig. 3E) and GaussianNB (Fig. 3B) models showed signs of overfitting much earlier in the process.

In summary, gradient-boosting models such as CatBoost and LightGBM demonstrated superior capacity to leverage large datasets, with higher prediction accuracy and stronger generalization ability. The outstanding performance of CatBoost on both the validation and test sets, along with its lower risk of overfitting, made it the final model of choice for this study21.

Identification of key thresholds and AKI risk prediction

By analyzing SHAP values, we identified specific physiological and biochemical thresholds associated with the occurrence of AKI. As shown in Fig. 6, AKI risk significantly increases when Cys C levels exceed 0.8 mg/L (Fig. 6A) Similarly, glucose levels higher than 6.0 mmol/L (Fig. 6B) and Fbg levels below 4.0 g/L (Fig. 6C) are associated with a marked increase in AKI risk. Additionally, AKI incidence significantly rises when D-dimer exceeds 0.5 mg/L, with the risk peaking around 2.8 mg/L (Fig. 6D), and when creatinine levels surpass 52 μmol/L (Fig. 6E), suggesting critical roles for these markers in AKI development. Further analysis revealed that surgical duration longer than 90 min (Fig. 6F), RDW-SD values over 43.5 (Fig. 6G), and body weight below 60 kg (Fig. 6H) are all linked to an elevated risk of AKI. Similarly, proBNP levels above 140 pg/mL (Fig. 6I), and ALP levels exceeding 80 U/L (Fig. 6J) were identified as key thresholds for increased AKI risk. Age greater than 70 years (Fig. 6K) and Hgb levels below 130 g/L (Fig. 6M) also exhibited a strong association with elevated AKI risk. In contrast, fluid infusion volume above 1600 mL (Fig. 6N) were associated with a protective effect. However, greater patient height (Fig. 6L) was linked to an increase risk of AKI, with risk contributions stabilizing for heights above 165 cm. Further details on the trends observed in SHAP analysis, including nonlinear relationships and threshold effects, are provided in the Supplementary Results. Discussion

In this retrospective study, we analyzed the Electronic Health Records (EHRs) of 4601 patients who underwent THA at a major orthopedic and trauma hospital in Western China. By leveraging a comprehensive suite of ML algorithms and integrating granular perioperative clinical variables, we identified and validated key risk factors associated with postoperative acute AKI. Unlike previous studies focusing on isolated predictors, our research provides a systematic approach to threshold determination, risk stratification, and the integration of real-time prediction tools. Key metrics such as AUC, F1 score, and calibration curves were comprehensively evaluated to ensure the robustness and reliability of the predictive model, demonstrating its clinical applicability in both internal and external validation datasets. Among the models tested, CatBoost demonstrated the most favorable trade-off between discrimination and generalization, achieving an AUC of 0.95 and an F1 score of 0.79 in the internal validation set, and retaining reasonable performance on both the test set (AUC = 0.66, AP = 0.65) and the external validation cohort (AUC = 0.65, AP = 0.38).These results underscore CatBoost’s consistency across datasets and support its potential deployment for perioperative risk prediction in clinical practice. The comparable performance between the test set and the external validation cohort from an independent center (Ankang Central Hospital), both using raw data without sampling, suggests that untreated class imbalance in these datasets may have influenced performance, particularly for metrics sensitive to imbalanced data such as AP.

Our observed AKI incidence of 2.1% requires interpretation within the evolving landscape of primary THA outcomes and contemporary surgical practices. This rate aligns well with the established literature on THA-associated AKI. Gharaibeh et al.’s landmark study of 8949 THA patients at Mayo Clinic reported an overall AKI incidence of 1.1%2, demonstrating that low AKI rates represent realistic expectations for contemporary THA practice. Importantly, their study revealed that AKI risk varies dramatically with patient characteristics, ranging from < 1% in optimally selected patients to > 20% in those with multiple comorbidities (CKD, heart failure, diabetes, and hypertension). This risk stratification pattern aligns with our findings and provides important context for our observed 2.1% rate, which likely reflects our patient population’s intermediate risk profile compared to highly optimized cohorts.

This consistency across multiple large-scale studies spanning different institutions and time periods validates that AKI incidence in the 1–3% range represents realistic expectations for contemporary primary THA practice. Singh and Cleveland’s complementary analysis of over 4.1 million primary THAs from the US National Inpatient Sample further supports this range with their reported 1.5% incidence.58 The convergence of evidence from single-institution studies (Gharaibeh: 1.1%, our study: 2.1%) and national databases (Singh: 1.5%) demonstrates that low AKI rates reflect genuine advances in perioperative care rather than systematic underdetection.

Several factors contribute to consistently low rates in modern primary THA practice: (1) improved preoperative patient optimization and risk stratification, (2) standardized perioperative management protocols emphasizing nephroprotection, (3) enhanced surgical techniques reducing perioperative stress, and (4) systematic avoidance of nephrotoxic medications during the perioperative period. Our institutional protocols align with these contemporary standards, including goal-directed fluid management, systematic preoperative optimization, and standardized monitoring protocols.

The substantial performance decline observed during external validation (AUC: 0.95 → 0.65, AP: 0.89 → 0.38, F1: 0.79 → 0.31) reflects significant domain shift between the two healthcare institutions, which our comparative analysis has systematically characterized. The 13-fold higher AKI incidence at Ankang Central Hospital (27.1% vs. 2.1%) represents one of the most substantial baseline risk differences reported in AKI prediction literature, indicating that the external validation cohort constituted a fundamentally different clinical population. This dramatic contrast provides important validation of our findings, demonstrating that when different patient populations or institutional practices are encountered, AKI rates can indeed approach the upper ranges reported in literature, supporting our contention that the low internal incidence reflects contemporary best practices in optimized primary THA patients rather than data collection limitations.

Several factors contribute to this domain shift. First, the patient demographics at Ankang Central Hospital suggest a higher-risk population with increased age, different body habitus, and significantly longer surgical procedures, all of which are established risk factors for perioperative complications. Second, the markedly elevated levels of key biomarkers (D-Dimer: 4.2-fold increase, proBNP: 2.0-fold increase) suggest either different laboratory standardization protocols or genuinely higher baseline inflammatory and cardiac stress in this population. Third, the dramatic difference in LOS (15.5 vs. 7.8 days) likely reflects different institutional practices, case complexity, or discharge criteria, indicating potential variations in perioperative care protocols.

These institutional differences likely reflect variations in laboratory standardization protocols, perioperative care pathways, patient referral patterns, and case complexity between a major regional trauma center and a district-level hospital, representing common challenges encountered when deploying AI models across different healthcare tiers in real-world settings. This domain shift phenomenon is consistent with recent findings in healthcare AI literature, where models trained at one institution often show degraded performance when applied to different healthcare settings due to variations in patient populations, clinical practices, and institutional protocols. The fact that our model maintained reasonable discriminative ability (AUC = 0.65) despite such substantial population differences actually demonstrates encouraging robustness, as this performance level remains clinically meaningful for risk stratification purposes.

These findings highlight the critical importance of institutional adaptation when deploying predictive models across different healthcare settings. Future implementations should consider local calibration strategies, population-specific threshold adjustments, or federated learning approaches to address such domain shifts while maintaining model utility.

Notably, the incidence of postoperative AKI observed in our cohort was lower than that reported in some surgical populations but consistent with contemporary primary THA literature. This finding may be attributed to several factors. First, our study employed relatively strict inclusion criteria, selecting patients with complete perioperative records and excluding those with pre-existing renal impairment or missing data, which may have led to a healthier study population. Second, institution-specific perioperative management strategies—including meticulous fluid management, hemodynamic optimization, and standardized anesthetic protocols—may have contributed to effective renal protection. Third, the urine output criterion was not applied due to incomplete electronic health record documentation, which likely resulted in the underestimation of oliguria-based AKI events. However, this limitation is shared by most retrospective AKI studies in surgical populations, including Gharaibeh et al.’s study which also used serum creatinine-based definitions, and is unlikely to fully account for our observed incidence rate given its consistency with multiple large-scale studies using similar methodology2.

This study provides several novel insights into the prediction and management of postoperative AKI in patients undergoing THA. Key contributions include: (1) Identification of clinically meaningful thresholds for critical predictors (e.g., Cys C > 0.8 mg/L, glucose > 6.0 mmol/L, and surgical duration > 90 min), aligning with prior biomarker-focused research while offering specific clinical guidance. For example, studies in patients with decompensated cirrhosis reported elevated serum Cys C levels in AKI patients (~ 2.4 mg/L) compared to non-AKI patients (0.8 mg/L), demonstrating its sensitivity as an early marker of renal injury25. Similarly, our SHAP analysis identified 0.8 mg/L as a critical threshold, where values below 0.8 indicated a protective effect against AKI, while higher levels increased AKI risk. This consistency reinforces the robustness of our model in reflecting real-world clinical patterns. The higher Cys C levels reported for AKI patients in critical illness (~ 2.4 mg/L) compared to our threshold (0.8 mg/L) likely reflect differences in patient populations and disease severity. For THA patients with less severe preoperative renal impairment, 0.8 mg/L may signify early renal stress, underscoring the importance of population-specific thresholds in clinical decision-making. (2) Application of SHAP clustering to stratify patients into distinct subgroups with unique risk profiles, offering a novel approach to personalized management strategies. (3) Development of an interactive online prediction platform, enhancing the utility and accessibility of AKI risk prediction in perioperative care.

The risk factors identified in our study align well with established literature. Gharaibeh et al. identified similar key predictors including older age (OR 1.4 per decade), CKD (OR 4.6), heart failure (OR 4.5), diabetes (OR 2.1), and hypertension (OR 2.1)2. Our SHAP analysis similarly identified age > 70 years, elevated Cys C levels, and surgical duration as primary contributors, providing complementary insights into the multifactorial nature of THA-associated AKI risk. The consistency of risk factor identification across different methodological approaches (traditional logistic regression vs. ML) strengthens the validity of these predictors and supports their clinical utility.

The study identified multiple significant risk factors influencing the incidence of postoperative AKI, including Cys C, surgical duration, and proBNP. Among these, Cys C emerged as the most sensitive predictor, with elevated levels strongly associated with an increased risk of postoperative renal injury. Cys C has also been validated in other clinical contexts, such as neonatal AKI, where Xu et al. proposed a neonatal AKI threshold of ≥ 2.2 mg/L and reported that Cys C was 6.5 times more sensitive than traditional creatinine-based criteria for identifying at-risk patients26. While neonatal AKI and THA-associated AKI differ in pathophysiology, this evidence underscores the broad applicability of Cys C as a robust biomarker for early renal injury detection. Our study advances this knowledge by demonstrating that even in patients with normal preoperative renal function, elevated Cys C levels (> 0.8 mg/L) signal underlying renal stress, warranting close monitoring and timely intervention.

Through SHAP analysis, we established critical thresholds for several key predictors, including Cys C > 0.8 mg/L, glucose > 6.0 mmol/L, and surgical duration > 90 min. These findings align with prior evidence highlighting the role of biomarkers and clinical parameters in AKI prediction. For example, previous studies have established the high diagnostic accuracy of serum Cys C, demonstrating its sensitivity and specificity in detecting early AKI27,28. Our study advances this knowledge by quantitatively defining a clinically actionable threshold (> 0.8 mg/L) for perioperative risk management. Additionally, our identification of a surgical duration threshold (> 90 min) is consistent with previous findings, which identified prolonged operative time (e.g., > 120 min) as a significant risk factor for AKI in neonates undergoing non-cardiac surgeries29. These findings not only enhance intraoperative decision-making but also contribute to a broader framework for refining AKI risk stratification and perioperative management strategies.Similarly, while prior research has demonstrated an association between elevated pre-procedural glucose levels and an increased risk of contrast-induced AKI, particularly in non-diabetic patients undergoing coronary angiography30, this study extends these findings by proposing a specific glucose threshold (> 6.0 mmol/L) with actionable implications for perioperative glycemic control.

To further understand the contribution of individual features to AKI risk, we applied SHAP clustering analysis, a novel method not previously utilized in AKI research. This approach allowed us to identify distinct patient subgroups characterized by unique risk profiles, integrating feature importance with clustering techniques. This innovative use of SHAP-based clustering represents a major advancement in AKI research, enabling a more precise stratification of patients and facilitating tailored perioperative management strategies. By combining ML interpretability with clinical relevance, this study bridges the gap between predictive analytics and real-world applicability, which is a novel contribution to the field. This stratification facilitates personalized management strategies, such as increasing surveillance intensity and customizing postoperative care for high-risk patients. By identifying patterns of risk within the population, clinicians can tailor their approaches to the specific needs of each subgroup, enhancing the precision and effectiveness of interventions31.

This study also developed an integrated AKI risk prediction platform that combines a nomogram with an online prediction system. The nomogram visualizes key risk factors, providing a quantitative risk scoring tool that enables clinicians to intuitively assess a patient’s AKI risk. The online prediction system allows for real-time input of patient data to generate personalized risk predictions and confidence intervals, as well as feature importance analysis. The integration of these tools is novel, as it translates advanced ML techniques into a clinically accessible format, ensuring that both experts and non-experts can benefit from the model’s predictive capabilities. To ensure the clinical reliability and usability of this online platform, we plan to initially deploy it within our hospital for internal use. This pilot phase will allow clinicians to test the platform, provide feedback on its performance, and evaluate its integration into routine perioperative workflows. Once the platform’s stability, accuracy, and ease of use are confirmed through this internal evaluation, we will consider broader public release to make it accessible to other healthcare institutions. This phased approach aims to optimize the platform’s functionality and clinical applicability before widespread dissemination. This platform has significant clinical utility in preoperative assessment, postoperative follow-up, and personalized management, offering a dynamic and interactive tool for improving decision-making and patient outcomes32.

The biological mechanisms underlying our findings are of considerable clinical relevance. Cys C, a sensitive biomarker for renal function, reflects subtle changes in renal tubular function and can detect early renal injury even when traditional markers, such as creatinine, remain within normal ranges. Elevated Cys C levels observed in our study were significantly associated with postoperative AKI, suggesting that it could serve as an early warning sign for patients at risk of renal complications, even in the absence of preoperative renal impairment. This finding underscores the need for closer monitoring and proactive management in patients with elevated Cys C, potentially reducing the incidence of postoperative AKI27.

Similarly, prolonged surgical duration was identified as another critical factor contributing to AKI risk. Prolonged operations can exacerbate physiological stress and inflammation, leading to adverse hemodynamic changes and renal hypoperfusion33. This is consistent with findings in related cardiac surgery populations, where elevated stress and hemodynamic instability during prolonged surgeries were linked to renal complications and higher AKI incidence rates34. This highlights the importance of minimizing operative time and employing intraoperative measures to maintain hemodynamic stability and prevent renal injury. Effective perioperative management strategies should include enhanced fluid administration and careful monitoring of blood pressure and renal function, particularly in surgeries exceeding 90 min.

Elevated preoperative glucose levels were also associated with an increased risk of AKI. Hyperglycemia can lead to endothelial dysfunction, oxidative stress, and inflammatory responses, ultimately resulting in microvascular damage and tubular injury. For patients with elevated glucose levels, strict glycemic control should be implemented both preoperatively and postoperatively to reduce AKI risk. The threshold identified in this study (glucose > 6.0 mmol/L) emphasizes the importance of glycemic control as an actionable perioperative strategy. Additionally, elevated proBNP levels, indicative of potential cardiac insufficiency, were linked to higher AKI risk, suggesting that comprehensive cardiovascular assessment and management are crucial in these patients to maintain hemodynamic stability and protect renal function28.

Our findings are consistent with previous studies that have highlighted the role of Cys C as a key predictor of AKI. However, by leveraging SHAP analysis, we have quantitatively evaluated its impact on postoperative AKI risk, providing a more detailed understanding of its predictive value. Moreover, while previous research has noted the association between surgical duration and AKI, our multifactorial analysis offers a more nuanced perspective on the interplay of various risk factors35. The integration of ML models enabled a more comprehensive assessment of these complex relationships, offering deeper insights into the predictors of AKI beyond traditional statistical methods.

The clinical implications of this study are profound, particularly in the context of developing personalized risk prediction models for AKI. By identifying critical thresholds for key biomarkers and integrating these into an online prediction system, we provide a practical tool for clinicians to assess and manage AKI risk more effectively32. Although our retrospective design precludes direct measurement of clinical impact, early AKI prediction through our model has the potential to substantially improve patient outcomes by enabling targeted interventions, such as enhanced fluid management, glycemic control, and avoidance of nephrotoxic agents in high-risk patients. Evidence from large-scale THA cohorts indicates that postoperative AKI is associated with prolonged hospitalization (mean increase of 2–4 days), elevated 30-day mortality (odds ratio 2.5–5.0), and increased progression to CKD (hazard ratio 3.8–8.0)2,3. By facilitating early identification of at-risk patients—even in low-incidence settings—our model could mitigate these adverse outcomes through proposed workflow integration, for example, potential automated alerts in EHRs that might trigger multidisciplinary consultations or protocolized care bundles. This integration would likely reduce healthcare utilization and improve long-term renal outcomes, though prospective trials are needed to quantify these benefits. Furthermore, regarding cost-effectiveness, implementing such predictive models has been shown to be economically viable in surgical settings by reducing AKI-related complications and associated costs, such as extended hospital stays and resource-intensive interventions36,37. For instance, studies evaluating AKI prediction tools in cardiac and general surgery populations report potential cost savings through early intervention, with one analysis estimating a net reduction in healthcare expenditures by preventing severe AKI episodes38. However, we recognize the risks associated with high false-positive rates in ML models, which could lead to unnecessary diagnostic tests, increased clinician workload, and alert fatigue—potentially burdening healthcare systems39. Our model’s strong specificity and calibration may help mitigate these issues by minimizing false alerts, but real-world deployment requires careful threshold tuning to balance sensitivity and specificity. These considerations highlight the importance of integrating user feedback and adaptive algorithms to prevent overuse and ensure sustainable implementation. Future research should focus on validating these thresholds across diverse populations and healthcare settings to enhance the generalizability of the findings. Additionally, incorporating variables such as medication use and intraoperative hemodynamic parameters could further refine the model and improve its predictive accuracy.

Limitations of the study

Despite the robust findings, this study has several limitations. While the model has been externally validated using an independent dataset from Ankang Central Hospital, further validation with large, multi-center datasets is needed to fully capture the heterogeneity of patient populations and assess the generalizability of the model.

The substantial domain shift observed between institutions, characterized by a 13-fold difference in AKI incidence rates and significant variations in key biomarkers, represents a major limitation that affects model generalizability. While this reflects real-world challenges in deploying AI models across different healthcare settings, it underscores the need for institution-specific validation and potential model recalibration before clinical implementation.

Most importantly, our AKI definition was limited to serum creatinine criteria due to the retrospective nature of the study and lack of standardized urine output monitoring in our EHRs. This represents a significant limitation, as AKI diagnosis based solely on serum creatinine criteria may underestimate true AKI incidence compared to studies incorporating both creatinine and urine output criteria. However, this limitation is shared by most retrospective AKI studies in surgical populations, including Gharaibeh et al.’s study which also used serum creatinine-based definitions, and is unlikely to fully acount for our observed incidence rate, given its consistency with multiple large-scale studies using similar methodology2,3. The substantial difference between our internal (2.1%) and external validation (27.1%) AKI rates demonstrates that our model can effectively detect AKI when it occurs at higher frequencies, suggesting that the low internal rate reflects genuine population characteristics rather than systematic under detection.

Although key intraoperative variables, such as fluid management, were included in the model, other potential predictors, such as medication use and specific hemodynamic parameters, were not considered. Including these additional variables in future studies may further enhance the model’s predictive performance. The absence of longitudinal follow-up data also limits the evaluation of AKI’s long-term impacts, such as its progression to CKD. Future studies should incorporate longitudinal data to better understand the chronic implications of AKI and enhance the model’s clinical utility in linking perioperative risk factors to long-term outcomes. Furthermore, while our study demonstrates promising predictive performance, it does not directly evaluate the model’s impact on clinical outcomes or workflow integration, which warrants prospective studies to assess real-world benefits such as reduced hospitalization duration and mortality.

A key limitation is the retrospective nature of our study, which prevents direct evaluation of cost-effectiveness, clinical workflow integration, or potential drawbacks such as alert fatigue from false positives. While our model demonstrates promising predictive performance, it has not yet been embedded in electronic health record systems and does not include realized features like automated alerts or protocolized care bundles; high false-positive rates in AKI prediction tools could result in unnecessary interventions and increased healthcare burdens, as noted in prior literature39. We propose that future prospective validation studies, including randomized controlled trials, incorporate economic analyses (e.g., cost–benefit assessments) and human factors evaluations (e.g., clinician surveys on alert fatigue) to quantify these aspects and optimize model deployment.

Finally, this study was retrospective and focused on preoperative and perioperative data. Real-time application during surgery, integrating dynamic intraoperative parameters like blood pressure and oxygen saturation, was beyond the scope of this research. Future studies should explore the integration of such real-time data to enable dynamic predictions, optimize perioperative decision-making, and improve patient outcomes.

Conclusions

This study identified several key risk factors and established critical thresholds for postoperative AKI, providing clinicians with a practical tool for risk assessment and management. By integrating these findings into an online prediction system, we have created a dynamic platform for real-time AKI risk prediction, offering valuable support for personalized clinical decision-making. The ability of the platform to generate individualized predictions and provide insights into feature importance can significantly improve perioperative care and patient outcomes. Given the low AKI incidence in optimized THA cohorts, our model’s early detection capabilities hold particular promise for preventing progression to severe complications, potentially reducing hospitalization duration, mortality, and CKD risk through timely interventions. This approach may also prove cost-effective by averting high-cost outcomes, for example through proposed workflow integrations like potential automated alerts in EHRs, though future prospective studies are essential to evaluate implementation costs, false-positive impacts, and overall economic viability. While this study demonstrates the potential utility of machine learning approaches for AKI prediction in the THA setting, the substantial performance decline observed during external validation (AUC from 0.95 to 0.65, AP from 0.89 to 0.38) underscores that reported performance metrics should be interpreted with caution. These findings confirm that machine learning can capture clinically meaningful patterns in this domain, but institution-specific validation and potential model recalibration will likely be necessary prior to clinical deployment. Future implementations should prioritize robust external validation across diverse healthcare settings over optimized performance metrics from single-center studies to ensure model reliability and generalizability. Future studies should focus on validating these findings in diverse cohorts and exploring additional risk factors to further refine the model and enhance its clinical utility.

Methods

This study received approval from the Ethics Committee of Xi’an Honghui Hospital [No. 202408020] and adhered to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Furthermore, the Ethics Committee of Xi’an Honghui Hospital has waived the requirement for patient informed consent due to the retrospective nature of the study and the use of anonymized medical records. Additionally, it followed the Transparent Reporting of a Multivariable Prediction Model for Individual Prognosis or Diagnosis (TRIPOD) reporting guideline40.

Study design and data collection

This study is a retrospective analysis based on electronic EHRs from the largest orthopedic and trauma hospital in Western China, covering 6271 patients who underwent THA between January 1, 2020, and March 31, 2024 (ICD-9 codes are listed in Table S11). Candidate predictors were systematically extracted from three perioperative phases: preoperative, intraoperative, and the entire hospitalization period. Preoperative variables were defined as the most recent available clinical or laboratory measurements recorded prior to the index surgery. Intraoperative variables included blood loss, infusion volume, and operative duration. Variables reflecting the entire hospitalization course encompassed comorbid conditions (e.g., hypertension, diabetes mellitus), perioperative interventions (e.g., continuous oxygen inhalation, drain tube drainage), medication exposure (Defined Daily Dose System), and healthcare utilization metrics (e.g., LOS, Charlson weight). Exclusion criteria included patients younger than 18 years, cases with more than 10% missing variables, patients with illogical medical records, and those with pre-existing kidney injury before surgery. After applying these criteria, a total of 4601 patients were included in the final analysis.

In this study, 69 variables were collected and analyzed, consisting of 16 general variables and 53 laboratory test variables. These variables were selected from an initial comprehensive set of potential predictors in the EHRs, retaining only those with non-missing values in the vast majority of patients to ensure data quality and reliability for model training. General variables included patient demographics, surgery-related information, and clinical data recorded during treatment, such as blood transfusion status, infusion volume, blood loss, gender, age, and LOS. Laboratory test variables covered a wide range of biochemical and hematological markers, including creatinine, D-dimer, Fbg, Hgb, albumin/globulin ratio (AGR), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), total bilirubin (TBIL), and glucose, among others (the full names and abbreviations of these variables are listed in Table S12).

External validation was conducted using an independent dataset from Ankang Central Hospital, comprising 326 patients discharged between January 1, 2022, and November 30, 2024. Patients were excluded if they were younger than 18 years, presented illogical medical records, had pre-existing kidney injury before surgery, or had more than 20% missing data for key features identified during feature selection. After applying these criteria, the final external validation cohort included 240 patients. Key features were collected and standardized in units and format to ensure compatibility with the internal cohort. The external dataset was used exclusively as an independent test set to assess model generalizability and was not involved in model training or internal validation.

Patient selection and study population

In this retrospective study, we initially identified 6271 patients who underwent THA at Xi’an Honghui Hospital between January 2020 and March 2024. The inclusion criteria were as follows: patients aged ≥ 18 years undergoing primary unilateral THA, elective procedures, with available preoperative serum creatinine (within 30 days), postoperative serum creatinine (within 72 h), and complete demographic data. Exclusion criteria included patients with missing creatinine measurements, revision THA, bilateral procedures, emergency surgery, incomplete demographic data, or pre-existing AKI. Of the initial 6271 patients, 1660 were excluded due to missing preoperative or postoperative serum creatinine data, and an additional 10 were excluded due to incomplete demographic information, resulting in a final analytical cohort of 4601 patients. In this cohort, 97 patients (2.1%) developed postoperative AKI, as defined by the KDIGO criteria based on serum creatinine changes. To address the impact of exclusions on AKI incidence, we evaluated the cohort with complete preoperative and postoperative creatinine measurements (n = 4611) before applying other exclusion criteria. In this cohort, 98 patients (2.1%) developed AKI, and 4513 patients did not, consistent with the AKI incidence in the final analytical cohort. This similarity suggests that the exclusion process, primarily driven by data completeness, did not systematically bias the reported AKI incidence. To further characterize the excluded patients, we compared demographic and clinical variables between the excluded (n = 1670) and included (n = 4601) cohorts (Table S13). The excluded cohort had a slightly higher mean age, greater intraoperative infusion volume, and higher blood loss, but similar BMI and length of stay, indicating minimal impact of exclusions on the study population’s overall characteristics.

Outcome definition

Postoperative AKI was defined in accordance with the KDIGO serum creatinine criteria: an increase in serum creatinine of ≥ 0.3 mg/dL (≥ 26.5 μmol/L) within 48 h or an increase to ≥ 1.5 times baseline within 7 days after surgery. Thus, our model predicts the risk of AKI occurring within the first 7 days post‐THA. Baseline serum creatinine was defined as the most recent preoperative measurement obtained within 7 days prior to THA. Urine output criteria were not applied due to inconsistent documentation in the electronic health record system. AKI staging followed the consensus definitions outlined by Ostermann et al.41.

Data preprocessing and sampling methods

This study initially employed stratified sampling to divide the dataset into training (70%) and testing (30%) sets, ensuring consistent proportions of AKI cases across both. The split preserved the class distribution, preventing bias in model training and evaluation. To avoid data leakage, the split was performed before preprocessing, ensuring the test set remained unseen during model development. This split facilitated internal validation, allowing performance assessment on a held-out set subset of the primary dataset. After the split, missing values across all datasets (training, test, and external validation) were imputed using appropriate methods: categorical variables and integer columns were imputed using the mode with the SimpleImputer, while the remaining missing values were filled using multiple imputation and linear interpolation, ensuring that the interpolated values remained within the original data range, preserving its inherent characteristics42. Laboratory units in the external validation datasets from Ankang Central Hospital were standardized to align with the internal dataset format, ensuring compatibility across all datasets.

Subsequently, normality testing was conducted on numerical variables using the Shapiro–Wilk tests, distinguishing between normally and non-normally distributed variables43. Statistical tests were selected accordingly: normally distributed variables were compared using the t-test, non-normally distributed variables using the Mann–Whitney U test, and categorical variables using the chi-square test44. Baseline characteristics were summarized using the TableOne package, providing descriptive statistics and p-values for comparisons across study groups. Statistical significance was defined as P < 0.05.

Class imbalance addressing through resampling

Given the severe class imbalance in our dataset (AKI incidence: 2.1%, 97 AKI cases vs 4504 non-AKI cases), we implemented a comprehensive resampling strategy applied exclusively to the training set to prevent data leakage and maintain realistic evaluation conditions. First, the complete dataset (n = 4601) was divided using stratified random sampling into training (70%, N = 3220) and test (30%, N = 1381) sets, preserving the original AKI distribution in both subsets. Second, multiple resampling techniques were evaluated exclusively on the training set, including oversampling methods (SMOTE, Borderline-SMOTE, ADASYN), undersampling methods (Edited Nearest Neighbors, TomekLinks), and hybrid methods (SMOTEENN, SMOTETomek). Third, after systematic evaluation (Table S5), SMOTEENN demonstrated superior performance with the highest F1-score (0.969) and ROC-AUC (0.815). Finally, SMOTEENN was applied to the training set with a sampling ratio of 0.4, whereby the minority class (AKI) was oversampled to achieve 40% of the majority class size.

No resampling was applied to the test set (N = 1381) or external validation dataset (N = 240), ensuring that model evaluation reflected real-world performance under natural class distribution conditions. This methodology prevents overoptimistic performance estimates while maintaining the integrity of validation processes.

Feature selection and model construction

A multi-step strategy was employed for feature selection to ensure a robust and interpretable set of predictors while mitigating the risk of overfitting, particularly given the low event rate of AKI (2.1%). The feature selection pipeline was designed to reduce dimensionality, prioritize clinically relevant predictors, and prevent model overcomplexity, which is critical in datasets with low event rates. First, the RF was used to select features based on their importance, with the top variables being retained such that their cumulative importance accounted for 80% of the total variance45. To further refine the feature set and address potential overfitting, Lasso logistic regression was applied with a regularization parameter (C = 0.05) to penalize less informative predictors46. Mutual information analysis was then conducted with a threshold of 0.2 to quantify the dependency between features and the target variable47. To balance statistical rigor with clinical relevance, three biomarkers proBNP was forcibly included in the final feature set due to its well-documented association with AKI risk48, as it was not selected through mutual information analysis. In contrast, Cys C and Glucose were retained through the mutual information step, confirming their statistical and clinical relevance. The multi-step approach was designed to ensure that only the most predictive and clinically meaningful features were retained, reducing the risk of overfitting by limiting the number of predictors relative to the number of events (97 AKI cases). The target event-to-feature ratio (approximately 7.5:1) was aligned with established guidelines for avoiding overfitting in predictive modeling. This stepwise approach combines statistical rigor with domain knowledge to derive a feature set that is both robust and clinically interpretable.

Designing and optimizing multi-model integration to enhance predictive performance

This study utilized multiple ML models, including SVM, LightGBM, CatBoost, RF, AdaBoost, MLP, KNN, Gaussian Naive Bayes, LDA, and Gradient Boosting. To enhance model performance and stability, hyperparameters for each model were optimized. For example, the SVM model’s C value was set to 0.5 with a linear kernel; CatBoost was configured with 50 iterations, a depth of 3, and a learning rate of 0.05; LightGBM’s n_estimators were set to 50 with a max depth of 3 and the same learning rate of 0.0549,50. Hyperparameter optimization was performed exclusively on the training data, with no modifications applied to the test or external validation datasets, ensuring that model performance evaluation was unbiased. Additionally, a Stacking ensemble model was constructed using Logistic Regression as the meta-learner51.

In this step, the training dataset (70% of the full dataset) was further divided into training (80%) and internal validation (20%) subsets. The training subset was used to train the models, while the internal validation subset was used to fine-tune hyperparameters and assess intermediate performance during model development. It is important to note that sampling techniques, such as SMOTEENN, were applied only to the training dataset and not to the external validation or test datasets, in line with best practices to avoid data leakage and ensure unbiased evaluation. Once the models were optimized, their performance was evaluated comprehensively using the internal validation subset, the testing set, and the external validation dataset to ensure generalizability and robustness.

The dataset was processed as follows: the target variable was binarized, categorical variables were converted using one-hot encoding, and numerical variables were standardized to ensure consistency across features.

Evaluating model performance via bootstrapping to select the optimal ML algorithm

Model performance was evaluated by plotting Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curves, PR curves, calibration curves, and decision curves, and analyzing model behavior across different training set sizes using learning curves52. To derive robust confidence intervals for all performance metrics, each trained model was evaluated on 1000 bootstrap resamples of each assessment dataset (internal validation subset, testing set, and external validation set). In each iteration, N observations were drawn with replacement (where N equals the size of the respective dataset), the model was applied to this resampled set, and metrics—accuracy, AUC, recall, precision, F1 score, Cohen’s Kappa, MCC, and Brier score—were computed. After 1000 iterations, empirical distributions were constructed for each metric, and we reported the median values along with 95% confidence intervals defined by the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles.

Performance evaluation was conducted on the internal validation subset, testing set, and external validation dataset to ensure the generalizability and robustness of the models. Calibration curves were used to assess the alignment between predicted probabilities and observed outcomes, and DCA evaluated net clinical benefits across clinically relevant thresholds.

Among all models, CatBoost performed the best in terms of AUC and F1 score, making it one of the top candidates for the optimal model. Considering classification performance, computational efficiency, and training time, CatBoost was ultimately selected as the best model, particularly suitable for large-scale datasets.

Analyzing feature interpretability and model decision basis using SHAP

To enhance the interpretability of the CatBoost model, SHAP was applied to analyze the model’s predictions. Using SHAP’s TreeExplainer, the contribution of each feature to the prediction outcomes was quantified, providing insights at both global and local levels. Globally, SHAP values were computed for all samples to assess the overall importance of each feature in the decision-making process. Locally, SHAP values were used to evaluate the impact of individual features on specific predictions, elucidating how each feature influenced outcomes for particular samples53.

Python scripts were used to compute SHAP values for each sample, and SHAP scatter plots were generated for each numerical feature to visualize their contributions to predictions. Visual guide lines were added to the scatter plots to enhance readability54.

Additionally, clustering of SHAP values was performed to reveal feature patterns. Consensus clustering, performed using the ConsensusClusterPlus package in R, employed a k-value range of 2 to 9, and bootstrap methods were used to assess clustering stability. The final clustering results illustrated the similarity of features in terms of model prediction. Heatmaps generated using the pheatmap package displayed the distribution patterns of features across different clusters and ranked features accordingly55.

This approach significantly enhanced the model’s interpretability, providing both global and local analysis, thus offering strong support in understanding the decision-making process of the CatBoost model56.

Designing nomograms and decision curves to quantify the clinical value of AKI risk prediction

A logistic regression model was constructed based on selected feature variables, and nomograms were generated using the rms package to quantify the impact of these features on the risk of acute AKI23. The model was calibrated using 1000 bootstrap resamples, and calibration curves were plotted to assess prediction accuracy57. DCA was employed to evaluate the net benefit at different risk thresholds, providing a reference for clinical decision-making58.

Developing a real-time AKI risk prediction system using the flask framework

An Application Programming Interface (API)-based backend system was developed using the Flask framework to predict the risk of acute AKI. The system trained a CatBoost model, stored the model and feature names, and provided a prediction API that accepted input feature data and generated probabilistic predictions. Additionally, it provided confidence intervals based on bootstrap sampling59. A feature importance analysis API was also developed to help users understand the contribution of each feature to the prediction. The system also made model parameters and performance summaries available through the API.

The user interface allowed input of feature data via a frontend form, and the system generated graphical displays of the prediction results, providing detailed numerical summaries and feature importance analyses, helping users understand the logic and outcomes of the model predictions60.
