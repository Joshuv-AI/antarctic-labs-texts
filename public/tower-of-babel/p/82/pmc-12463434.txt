Successful Percutaneous Transluminal Renal Angioplasty for Bilateral Renal Artery Stenosis due to Fibromuscular Dysplasia in a Young Woman with Renovascular Hypertension
Authors: Ryohei Akashi, Hiroaki Kawano, Takuya Hidaka, Tomohiro Honda, Tsuyoshi Yonekura, Satoshi Ikeda, Koji Maemura
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractA 21-year-old woman with renovascular hypertension secondary to bilateral renal artery stenosis secondary to fibromuscular dysplasia was admitted to our hospital. Successful percutaneous renal angioplasty was performed for the bilateral stenotic renal arteries using intravascular ultrasound guidance to determine the balloon size and pressure wire to confirm the improvement in pressure differences and avoid stent implantation. Her blood pressure improved from 175/113 to 100/70 mmHg without medication. Keywords: angioplasty, intervention, secondary hypertension

Introduction

Fibromuscular dysplasia (FMD), a non-atherosclerotic, non-inflammatory disease of medium-sized arteries, may lead to stenosis, occlusion, dissection, and/or aneurysms (1). FMD lesions are most frequently found in the renal arteries (87.5%), followed by the cerebrovascular arteries (24.6%) (2). FMD occurs primarily in young or middle-aged women (1,3), and approximately 70% of these patients with FMD have hypertension (1). Renovascular hypertension (RVH) is a common cause of secondary hypertension, and over 95% of RVH cases are caused by atherosclerosis or FMD (3). Approximately 25% of all patients with renal artery stenosis have bilateral disease (4).

Percutaneous transluminal renal angioplasty (PTRA) is an effective treatment for RVH due to FMD (5). Juvenile hypertension and bilateral renal artery stenosis are recognized as poor prognostic factors for PTRA in patients with FMD (6). Approximately 20% of patients undergoing PTRA for FMD require a second procedure later on (4). Therefore, initial treatment strategies are crucial for ensuring a good long-term prognosis of FMD. Furthermore, renal artery stent implantation is generally not indicated in patients with FMD; it is reserved for the treatment of procedural complications such as flow-limiting dissection or arterial rupture (5). Therefore, treatment should rely solely on balloon dilation (5,7). Safe and effective balloon dilatation is particularly important in patients with multifocal or bilateral renal artery stenosis undergoing PTRA for FMD.

Case Report

A 21-year-old woman presented with hypertension was referred to our department. Her medical and family histories were unremarkable, and she had no history of smoking or alcohol consumption. Hypertension was first noted during a routine health checkup, with a home blood pressure (BP) reading of 160/110 mmHg. Upon admission, her body temperature was 36.6°C, pulse rate 92/min, BP was 175/113 mmHg without left-right difference, and oxygen saturation was 97% on room air.

A physical examination revealed no heart murmurs or abdominal vascular bruits, and peripheral pulses were palpable. Renal artery ultrasonography revealed significant stenotic lesions in the intermediate portions of both renal arteries [right: peak systolic velocity (PSV) 5.5 m/s, renal-aortic ratio (RAR) 4.2; left: PSV 6.7 m/s, RAR 5.1)]. Laboratory tests were within normal limits, including an estimated glomerular filtration rate of 85 mL/min/1.73 m-2, potassium 3.7 mmol/L, and C-reactive protein ＜0.01 mg/dL (Table 1). Hormone assays revealed a plasma renin activity (PRA) of 5.2 ng/mL/h and aldosterone concentration (PAC) of 223 pg/mL (Table 2). Electrocardiography and chest radiography findings were normal, and transthoracic echocardiography demonstrated a normal cardiac function (ejection fraction, 57%) without left ventricular hypertrophy. Contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CECT) confirmed severe stenosis in the mid-segments of both renal arteries (Fig. 1), although there was no significant stenosis from the thoracic aorta to the bilateral femoral arteries. Furthermore, magnetic resonance imaging showed no significant stenosis in the subclavian, cervical, or cerebral arteries, although there was an old, small microbleed in the left frontal lobe (Fig. 2).

The patient was diagnosed with renovascular hypertension associated with bilateral renal artery stenosis secondary to FMD. Percutaneous transluminal renal angioplasty (PTRA) was planned after obtaining informed consent (one-stage PTRA for both renal arteries).

Arteriography revealed focal 90% stenosis in the mid-right renal artery and mid-to-distal left renal artery, with mild bead-like changes in the middle part (Fig. 3). PTRA was first performed on the right renal artery after aspirin (100 mg) and clopidogrel (75 mg) were used as a double antiplatelet therapy in addition to a proton pump inhibitor (vonoprazan 10 mg). The international consensus states that the translesional pressure gradient should be measured (5). A pressure wire (PressureWire™ X Guidewire; Abbott Vascular, Santa Clara, USA) was applicable for blood vessels including renal arteries, although it was mostly used for coronary interventions. We explained and obtained consent for its use for PTRA from the patient and her family.

Physiological indicators with a pressure wire showed a systolic pressure gradient (sPG) of 97 mmHg, mean pressure gradient (mPG) of 64 mmHg, and mean distal/proximal pressure (Pd/Pa) of 0.57. Quantitative vessel angiography (QVA) revealed reference vessel diameters of 6.5 mm before and after stenosis. As the stenotic vessel diameter was not discernible on QVA, intravascular ultrasonography (IVUS; OptiCross Imaging Catheter, Boston Scientific, Marlborough, USA) was performed, revealing characteristic FMD-induced hyperplasia of the medial smooth muscle and a reduced vessel diameter (Fig. 4). Angiography and CECT were able to evaluate the lumen diameter (white arrows), but the vessel diameter was difficult to assess (Fig. 4). In contrast, IVUS was able to measure the external elastic membrane diameter because the media was observed (yellow arrows), allowing for an accurate evaluation of the vessel diameter (Fig. 4).

The vessel diameter was measured using an external elastic membrane (EEM) as an index. The proximal and distal reference vessel diameters were 7.3 and 7.4 mm, respectively, whereas the stenotic site measured 3.5 mm (Fig. 4). To mitigate the risk of vascular rupture, the lesion was initially dilated using a 3.0-mm-diameter cutting balloon (CB; Wolverine peripheral cutting balloon, Boston Scientific) (Fig. 5). Angiography revealed 50% stenosis. The physiological measurements improved to an sPG of 19 mmHg, mPG of 9 mmHg, and Pd/Pa of 0.94. IVUS showed thinning of the thickened media and dilation of the stenotic vessel diameter from 3.5 to 4.6 mm (Fig. 5). The lesion was then dilated a second time using a 5.0-mm-diameter CB (Fig. 5), which was selected based on the EEM diameter measurement (4.6 mm) at the stenotic lesion using IVUS to ensure adequate and safe dilation. Post-dilation with a 5.0-mm CB, angiography revealed improved stenosis from 90% to 25%, IVUS revealed a diameter of 5.4 mm (Fig. 5), and physiological measurements further improved with an sPG of 6 mmHg, mPG of 0 mmHg, and Pd/Pa of 1.0, indicating no significant residual stenosis (Fig. 3).

Subsequently, PTRA was performed on the left renal artery using the same technique (Fig. 3). Pre-treatment physiological measurements with a pressure wire were as follows: systolic blood pressure 130 mmHg, mPG 78 mmHg, and Pd/Pa 0.4. Angiography demonstrated an improvement in the mid-left renal artery from 90% to 0% stenosis and in the distal left renal artery from 90% to 50% stenosis. The physiological indicators were sPG 7 mmHg, mPG 3 mmHg, and Pd/Pa 0.98, indicating the resolution of the pressure difference.

A rapid drop in blood pressure was observed postoperatively, with a decrease to 100/70 mmHg. The patient was not prescribed antihypertensive medication. Aspirin 100 mg, clopidogrel 75 mg, and vonoprazan 10 mg were administered for 1 month after PTRA. One month after the intervention, renal artery ultrasonography showed a right renal artery PSV of 1.8 m/s, RAR of 1.6, and a left renal artery PSV of 1.8 m/s, RAR of 1.6, without restenosis. Furthermore, PRA and PAC decreased, respectively (PRA, 5.2 to 1.6 ng/mL/h; PAC, 223 to 32.5 pg/mL). Aspirin (100 mg) and vonoprazan (10 mg) were administered. These agents are planned to be discontinued after one year of treatment.

Discussion

Previous case reports have demonstrated that the use of a coronary pressure wire combined with IVUS or both IVUS and optical coherence tomography (OCT) can enable precise localization of significant stenosis and guide effective PTRA treatment in patients with FMD (Table 3) (8,9). However, there have been no reports of PTRA treatment for bilateral renal artery stenosis during the same session in patients with FMD.

Recently, a consensus protocol for PTRA in patients with FMD recommended several key practices, as follows: 1) measurement of the translesional pressure gradient to assess the hemodynamic significance of stenosis, particularly in multifocal FMD and post-angioplasty, to ensure that the pressure gradient has been obliterated because imaging alone does not allow for determining the severity and hemodynamic significance of stenosis; 2) the combination of PTRA with IVUS or OCT; 3) for angioplasty, basing the initial balloon diameter on the diameter of the distal normal renal artery, as assessed using a calibrated catheter, QVA software program, IVUS, or OCT; and 4) incremental increases in balloon diameter of 0.5 mm until the translesional gradient is resolved or until there is a mean translesional gradient of ＜10% (5). However, these protocols do not specify how to accurately measure the vascular size using different modalities to determine the appropriate balloon size.

QVA can evaluate the lumen of a blood vessel but cannot assess the diameter of the vessel at the stenosis site, making it challenging to select a safe balloon size (10). IVUS can measure the EEM, which is recommended for evaluating vessel diameter during endovascular treatment of lower limb arteries (11). Therefore, in the present case, we used the EEM diameter measured with IVUS as a guide to determine the appropriate balloon size for PTRA. FMD often causes the stenotic site to appear constricted on IVUS, and balloon sizing based on the diameter of the blood vessel before and after stenosis increases the risk of vascular damage. Using the EEM diameter of the stenotic area as an indicator and performing staged balloon expansion, treatment can be conducted with a reduced risk of rupture.

The usefulness of a pressure wire in determining the significance of hemodynamic stenosis is well established, with standard values for significant stenosis being sBP ＞20 mmHg, mPG ＞10 mmHg, and Pd/Pa ＜0.9 for atherosclerotic renal artery stenosis (12) and Pd/Pa ＜0.9 for FMD (5). These parameters can serve as endpoints for evaluating the success of the procedure.

A CB was used in the present case. Cotroneo et al. (13) demonstrated the efficacy of CB angioplasty for renal artery FMD because of its reduced elastic recoil and potential for long-term patency.

There have been few long-term follow-up studies on PTRA in renal artery FMD (4,14). According to a meta-analysis, 35.8% of cases were cured of hypertension by PTRA for FMD, and 86.4% of cases were cured or improved. In addition, 18.2% of patients received re-treatment after PTRA for FMD. The factors associated with curing hypertension were an older age (hazard ratio, 0.48) and longer duration of hypertension (hazard ratio, 0.39) (4).

A retrospective follow-up study for 7.0±4.7 years of 59 patients (age 44±13 years old) treated for hypertension due to FMD demonstrated that PTRA (balloon angioplasty) has beneficial long-term effects, negatively affected by hypertension duration and branch artery involvement (14). Controneo et al. (13) followed six consecutive women with renal artery FMD who underwent CB angioplasty for poorly controlled hypertension despite antihypertensive treatment for five years. They reported that recurrence of hypertension was found in 2 patients within 12 months, and reinterventions were performed using CB angioplasty. The long-term prognosis of PTRA for renal artery FMD has not been determined, especially PTRA using a CB. Therefore, detailed follow-up is required for the present patient.

In conclusion, step-by-step determination of the balloon size using IVUS guidance coupled with confirmation of pressure difference improvement using a pressure wire can enhance the safety and effectiveness of PTRA in treating FMD.
