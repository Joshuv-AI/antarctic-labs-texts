What interests children about sexuality?: exploring narratives and questions from primary school children in Spain
Authors: Estel Malgosa, María-José Rodríguez-Jaume
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIn recent decades, international organisations have advocated for comprehensive sexuality education (CSE) in school curricula, emphasising its role in equipping children with knowledge, skills, and values for informed, healthy, and respectful decisions about sexuality. Despite this, CSE programmes often focus on adolescence, adopting a preventive approach centred on risks like HIV and unintended pregnancies, while neglecting topics such as pleasure, gender diversity, and decision-making. This study explores the interests and questions of primary school children (aged 9–11) in Spain regarding sexuality, aiming to inform CSE programmes that address their real needs.MethodsA qualitative study was conducted with 331 children from eight primary schools in Catalonia, Spain, between 2021 and 2024. Data were co-produced through anonymous written questions (237 submissions), 32 focus groups and group drawings. Through integrating these three techniques, we sought to capture the plurality of children’s voices: Anonymous questions enabled individual expression, focus groups facilitated collective narratives, and drawings served as a bridge between the material and the discursive. We conducted a thematic content analysis (Braun and Clarke, Qual Res Psychol 3:77-101, 2006), followed by a narrative analysis (Riessman, Narratives methos for human sciences, 2008). Ethical considerations included informed consent from parents and assent from participants.ResultsParticipants’ questions clustered around four main thematic areas: adult sexual practices (27%), childhood bodily experiences related to sexuality (23%), reproduction (16%), and pleasure (8%). While adult sexual practices and reproduction dominated the anonymous questions, focus group narratives revealed how these topics were negotiated through gendered, heteronormative, and age-based norms. Reproduction functioned as a socially legitimate entry point from which children explored broader and more ambiguous dimensions of sexuality, including pleasure, diversity, and bodily change. Children’s questions about menstruation and semen revealed fragmented knowledge, uncertainty, and affective responses such as fear, discomfort, and shame, alongside limited opportunities for collective discussion. Topics related to pleasure and masturbation were present but strongly regulated by moral discourses that positioned sexuality as appropriate only for adolescents or adults. Differences emerged across methods and group compositions, highlighting how what children express varies according to the space and conditions of participation.ConclusionsThis study shows that participants actively engaged with questions about sexuality that go beyond reproduction and risk, including bodily experiences, pleasure, and moral norms. However, their interests are produced through intra-actions (Barad, Meeting the universe halfway: quantum physics and the entanglement of matter and meaning, 2007) within adult-centred, reproductive, and age-regulated assemblages, which define possibilities for knowing, feeling and speaking about sexuality. The findings highlight a persistent gap between children’s lived experiences and current approaches to CSE, which often overlook affective dimensions, embodied knowledge, and children’s own meaning-making processes. Advancing a rights-based CSE requires recognising children as sexual subjects, and creating diverse, safe spaces that allow them to explore sexuality in ways that are inclusive and grounded in their everyday realities.

Background

In recent decades, various international organisations [53, 54, 58] have recommended the introduction of comprehensive sexuality education (CSE) into school curricula at all educational stages—including early childhood. This inclusion should equip children and young people with the knowledge, skills, attitudes, and ethical values to make informed, healthy, and respectful decisions about their sexuality [54]. This information is considered a right of children and young people [29], enabling them to protect their health, well-being, and dignity [55] and to guarantee their sexual and reproductive rights [9, 23, 56].

Although the definition of sexuality provided by these organisations [57] includes emotional, psychological, and social dimensions, current programmes remain focused primarily on adolescence and adopt a predominantly preventive approach, centred on HIV, unintended pregnancies, and sexually transmitted infections [3, 25, 30]. As a result, topics such as pleasure [37], gender, and sexual diversity [2, 18], or decision-making [39] generate social controversy and are frequently absent from CSE programmes [2, 48], thus reinforcing and perpetuating existing inequalities [22].

Research that incorporates children’s and young people’s voices reveals a gap between CSE programmes and the lived experiences of children and youth [36, 41]. These studies highlight the importance of adopting a child- and youth-centred perspective and engaging with their sexual values to promote sexual health (5, 38]). Much of the research—and many CSE programmes—have focused on young adults and adolescents (e.g. [33, 40, 52]), coinciding with the onset of reproductive capacity, thereby reproducing the conception of childhood as innocent and asexual [43] and reinforcing myths that reduce sexuality to genitality, heterosexuality, and reproduction [27].

In Spain, CSE has largely been characterised by “preventive” programmes aimed mainly at adolescents and young people [27]. The rise in sexual violence [14] and concerns around children’s access to pornography [7] led in 2020 to the inclusion of CSE in the curriculum across all educational stages. Despite these advances, health authorities have reported an increase in STIs and risky sexual practices among young people [28].

This research aims to identify the topics of interest regarding sexuality among children aged 9 to 11, in order to contribute to the development of CSE plans and policies grounded in the real needs of children. To this end, we explore, from a child-centred perspective, their interests related to sexuality through the analysis of questions and narratives from 331 children.

Methods

Study context

This study is part of the research project [project name anonymised], which aims to qualitatively explore the perceptions and experiences of primary school children regarding sexuality in the region of Catalonia (Spain). Specifically, this article explores, from a child-centred perspective, children’s interests related to sexuality. To this end, we analyse 237 anonymous questions written by children aged 9 to 11 from eight schools in the province of Barcelona, together with their collective drawings and narratives generated in 32 focus groups conducted between 2021–2024. The questions were produced during a classroom-based research activity facilitated by the research team. As part of the study design, prior to conducting the focus groups in the selected schools, all participating children (n = 331) were invited to write a question about sexuality to which they would like to receive an answer. This activity was conceived as a complementary research tool to inform the subsequent focus group discussions. Study design

The question activity was designed to complement the data generated during focus groups conducted as part of the [anonymised] project. The main data production technique used in the project was the focus group—a planned discussion aimed at gathering perceptions on a topic in a comfortable setting [1], where interaction between participants allows for insights that would be less accessible in individual interviews [34], and where one child’s response may trigger responses in others. The “safety in numbers” effect [26]—the sense of security children feel by being in a group—encourages them to respond in more detail than they might in individual interviews and helps reduce power asymmetries between the adult researcher and the children [1]. However, focus groups also have limitations, as they may hinder the expression of minority perspectives. Furthermore, children who are shy or find it difficult to articulate themselves may not feel confident enough to contribute to the discussion [11], and a more dominant child may end up flattening the group’s talk [12]. To address these limitations and to recognise different forms of children’s participation, we incorporated a voluntary anonymous question activity, which offered children a confidential space from which to express interest or curiosity about topics that might be silenced in the focus group context.

Participant selection

We conducted the research in eight public primary schools located in diverse socio-territorial contexts across the Catalonia region (Spain), with the aim of paying attention to the intersectional composition of the groups by including children from different socioeconomic backgrounds, migration histories, and genders. Four schools were located in small peri-urban or rural towns, and four in neighbourhoods of varying socioeconomic characteristics in the city of Barcelona. Schools were selected based on these criteria and for convenience, meaning we invited schools with whom the research team had a relationship of trust from prior collaborations or that expressed interest in participating. Of the 331 participants, 67% were in year 6 (11 years old), 14% in year 5 (10 years old), and 20% in year 4 (9 years old). The decision to focus on the 9-to-11 age range responds not only to a gap in the literature on childhood sexuality prior to adolescence, but also to educational and bodily considerations. In the Catalan educational context, it is in the final years of primary school (years 4 through 6) that sexuality is most often addressed more explicitly in the classroom, frequently reduced to biological reproduction when CSE is not implemented [35]. At the same time, these ages coincide with the increasing relevance of bodily experiences related to sexuality, such as early pubertal changes, which tend to generate growing interest among children [21].

The question activity was designed to complement the focus groups by offering an alternative space for expression for children who might find it more difficult to communicate their interests within the group setting. Its voluntary nature meant that 80% of year 5 pupils (10 years old) and 83% of year 6 pupils (11 years old) submitted a question, while only 27% of year 4 pupils (9 years old) did so. This is not a representative or probabilistic sample, and its purpose is not statistical generalisation but rather “theoretical generalisation” ([49]: 24), through the identification of qualitative response patterns and the in-depth exploration of children’s narratives.

Ethical considerations

Ethical considerations have been central to this study due to the sensitive nature of the topic—sexuality [4]—and the involvement of children. Informative meetings were held with participants’ parents or guardians in order to obtain informed consent. In addition, at the beginning of each activity, the objectives of the research and the voluntary nature of participation were explained to the children, and they were asked to sign an “informed assent” form, indicating their agreement with the consent provided by their parents. This procedure was approved by the ethics committee of [name of university and approval reference number].

Co-production of data

From feminist new materialist perspectives, sexuality is understood as “an impersonal affective flow within assemblages of bodies, things, ideas and social institutions, which produces sexual (and other) capacities in bodies” ([20]: 769). However, the elements of an assemblage [13] do not pre-exist as individual entities; rather, they are produced through their relations with one another, or, in Barad’s [8] terms, through their ‘intra-actions’. Similarly, what children express as interest in sexuality is produced within discursive-material assemblages through intra-action with multiple elements and contexts [51]. Within this framework, reflexivity is essential, as we, the researchers, are part of these entanglements, recognising that our methods are performative [32]. That is, they do not describe a pre-existing reality but participate in its production, and our specific positions (as adults, cis women, researchers) actively contributed to the co-production of the data. Therefore, this study was designed not as a “collection” of data, but as a space for the co-production of narratives and questions, in which these positions inevitably influenced our intra-actions with the children.

The procedure, conducted during school hours, was structured into three interconnected phases to provide multiple avenues for expression and acknowledge diverse forms of participation. In the first phase, participating children were invited to voluntarily and anonymously write down individual questions (topics of interest) about sexuality. In the second phase, they were asked to produce group drawings—in self-selected groups—which served both to break the ice and to act as a trigger for narratives [15] during the subsequent focus group. The collective drawing responded to the prompt: “What is the relationship between our body and sexuality?” This framing was intentionally chosen to ground sexuality in relation to children’s own bodies and to avoid centring narratives exclusively on adult sexual practices and/or reproduction, as had occurred in previous research [36]. Finally, in the third phase, the children presented their drawings during the focus group, which was facilitated using both their own questions (from the first phase) and those posed by the researchers.

The integration of these three techniques reflects an ethical and methodological commitment to the plurality of children’s voices. Anonymous questions prioritise the expression of individual concerns and minimise group pressure; focus groups generate situated, collective narratives; and drawings act as a bridge between the material and the discursive. This methodological triangulation [17] does not seek to validate a single “truth”, but rather to enrich and complexify our understanding of the phenomenon from multiple angles and expressive modalities.

The transcripts from the 32 focus groups were analysed from a dual perspective. First, we conducted a thematic analysis [10] similar to the one applied to the questions, in order to identify the central topics negotiated in the group discussions. Second, and more centrally, we carried out a narrative analysis [42]. This approach allowed us to move beyond content to examine how meanings about sexuality were co-constructed in the group dialogue: the sequencing of turns, negotiations, contradictions, and the use of narrative resources (such as anecdotes or collective explanations). The excerpts presented in the findings section are examples of this narrative analysis, revealing the process of knowledge construction through the intra-actions between the children and the researchers within a specific space–time.

Data from participants aged 9 to 11 were analysed collectively, understanding this age range as a continuum in which children collectively produce meanings about sexuality during the final stage of primary education. The analysis reflected this continuity, showing a common repertoire of concerns and narrative frameworks that transcended individual age differences.

The findings from both analyses were integrated through interpretative triangulation [17]. We contrasted the themes identified in the individual space (questions) with the narratives produced in the collective space (focus groups). In this triangulation process, we were not seeking to validate the findings, but rather to deepen and complexify our understanding. It allowed us to explore, for example, how a question expressed anonymously could become a topic of shame and bodily negotiation in the group interaction.

Topics of interest in reproduction

Notably, most questions about reproduction came from children who were theoretically expected to already know the answers, as this topic had been covered at school. This pattern suggests two possible interpretations: Either these questions originated from children who genuinely lacked understanding, with their concerns remaining unexpressed during focus group discussions, or reproduction operated as a stabilised node within the sexual assemblage—a socially acceptable framework [6]—serving as a reliable anchor from which children could tentatively probe less charted affective and corporeal territories.

A key interpretive finding is that many of these questions were posed by Year 6 children (aged 11), who had already worked on human reproduction in class. This leads us to a critical analytical point: Rather than indicating simple knowledge gaps, these questions often performed agential work within the sexual assemblage. Reproduction, as a stabilised and culturally intelligible element, functioned as a strategic affordance from which to intra-act with broader, more ambiguous, or potentially taboo dimensions of sexuality. For instance, 21% of the questions about reproduction posed by Year 6 pupils consisted solely of very general formulations such as “How are babies made?” or “How do we reproduce?”. These questions may signal children’s interest in further engaging with the topic, while simultaneously mobilising a socially accepted way of speaking about sexuality.

The question “How can gay men have babies if girls are the ones who menstruate?” (age 11), for example, mobilises this reproductive scaffold both to seek biological clarification and to interrogate—and potentially reconfigure—the material-discursive links between biology, gender, and kinship that constitute dominant sexual formations. In this sense, reproduction emerges in our analysis not merely as a topic, but as a critical site where children’s inquiries simultaneously participate in reiterating and unsettling the boundaries of the sexually thinkable.

In the focus groups, the participants narrated this fragmented knowledge collectively. In one mixed-gender group of 10-year-olds, guesses about frequency ranged from “every other month” to “every 9 months” culminating in one boy’s creative explanation, derived from the media, about an expiring “ovum”. The association of menstruation with pain was not a neutral observation but a material-affective entanglement frequently assembled as a site of dread and resistance. This assemblage was vividly captured in a drawing by 9-year-old girls annotated with “I hate periods”. When probed, they linked periods directly to pain and inconvenience (“It hurts when it comes out”; “We have to stay in the toilet all day”). The girls’ contested negotiation over whether periods should hurt (“Researcher: Should periods hurt? Girl 1: No. Girl 2: Yes. Girl 3: But it does hurt”) reveals how pain is not merely experienced but incorporated as an inevitable, naturalised component of the becoming-woman assemblage, further complicating their intra-actions with their own materialising bodies.

While practical concerns about management (“How do you put in a tampon?”) and the timing of menarche were present, only one anonymous question directly broached the theme of shame (“Why are girls embarrassed about having periods?”). This scarcity is analytically significant. It suggests that while shame may be a potent affective force within the menstrual assemblage, it often operates as a silently circulating and embodied regulation rather than an openly articulated query—materialising more readily in bodily discomfort and secrecy than in open discourse.

The focus group narratives, however, revealed a stark affective divide. In mixed-gender settings, speculation about the age of spermarche ranged widely (from 13 to 19 years), performing the collective production of uncertainty within the sexual assemblage. When the discussion turned to how semen is released, a palpable moral anxiety surfaced. In stark contrast, all-girls groups demonstrated a near-total void regarding semen. It was either unknown, linguistically confused with “cement” (“cemento” in Spanish), or vaguely associated with a clinical, passive model of reproduction (“They inject it into the girl”). This gendered asymmetry in knowledge—boys grappling with a morally charged, impending bodily event; girls largely unaware of its existence beyond a reproductive function—reflects and reinforces a broader cultural silence that segregates sexual knowledge along gendered lines within the educational assemblage, and often disassociates male sexuality from pleasure within formal educational contexts.

Mapping (and mis-mapping) the body: fragmented knowledge of genitalia

Participants’ direct questions about their own genitalia revealed not only gaps in knowledge, but also the active production of a fragmentary and often conflated corporeal cartography. Inquiries were equally split between male and female anatomy—particularly regarding the number of openings in the vulva, the clitoris and the testicles—probing structure, function, and vulnerability (“Can testicles break?”, age 11). This cartography emerged through intra-actions with social norms, peer discussions, and institutional silences. However, the focus group discussions uncovered a more profound disorientation, particularly regarding the topography of female genitalia and the conceptualisation of pleasure.

The case of the vulva and clitoris is analytically revealing. In one mixed-gender group of 11-year-olds, debate arose over the number of openings in the vulva (“one” versus “two”). More significantly, in another mixed-gender group of 10-year-olds, knowledge about the clitoris—a term introduced by a participant—was immediately destabilised. While one girl accurately identified it as “the pleasure organ for women”, another conflated it with the birth canal (“we come out from the clitoris”). This conflation performs a telling conceptual collapse: The organ exclusively dedicated to female pleasure is mis-mapped onto the reproductive passage, subsuming pleasure under a reproductive logic. This mis-mapping is not a random error but a symptom of a cultural discourse that renders female pleasure anatomically obscure and functionally secondary.

This moment triggered a critical question from a boy of the same group: “And what’s the pleasure organ for men?”. A peer suggested the glans (“the tip of the penis”). This exchange highlights a pivotal asymmetry. While the question about a male pleasure organ arose spontaneously, the girls’ discussion of the clitoris was researcher-prompted. This asymmetry suggests that the very concept of a dedicated pleasure organ is more readily available and subject to inquiry within a masculine framework, whereas for female bodies, it remains an obscure or assimilated concept. Had we directly probed children’s knowledge of the glans and its association with pleasure, parallel gaps would likely have surfaced. The boy’s question, therefore, points less to a specific knowledge deficit and more to the absence of a shared, non-reproductive language for corporeal pleasure across genders—a silence that our study’s design itself inadvertently reproduced.

Pleasure and bodily sensations: negotiating moral geographies and embodied arousal

Our analysis identified pleasure and bodily sensations as a fourth key thematic area, one uniquely saturated with moral and age-based negotiations. The anonymous questions attempted to discursively conjure the bodily sensations of sexual experience (“What does fucking feel like?”, age 11) and to arousal, which was almost exclusively framed around the visible male response of penile erection (“Why does a boy’s dick get hard for no reason?”, age 11).

In the focus group narratives, this pattern intensified as pleasure became a site where social regulations were not merely internalised but co-produced through intra-active assemblages of bodies, speech, and moral affect. In a mixed group of 9-year-olds, a query about penile erections prompted a range of explanations, from biological (“blood rushes to it”) to behavioural (“when you see a naked woman”). A girl’s associative comment—“When I see a naked person, sometimes I feel like I need to pee”—suggests a nascent, non-verbalised awareness of female corporeal arousal (lubrication) for which a clear, legitimate vocabulary is lacking, echoing the earlier void around the clitoris.

The most telling narratives emerged around masturbation, which served as a focal point for moral self-regulation. In a boys-only group, an 11-year-old provided a detailed, almost clinical description of arousal and masturbation, yet prefaced it with the hedge, “I don’t know if it’s correct”. This disclaimer performs a critical self-consciousness: The boy possessed the knowledge but explicitly doubted its social acceptability within the formal school context, internalising the boundary between private act and public discourse. His concluding statement— “You know if you’re aroused. Your willy stands up, so you notice it”—further reifies the erect penis as the definitive, legible sign of (male) sexual subjectivity, marginalising other, less visible forms of arousal.

This moral regulation was explicitly age-coded by the children themselves. Another group asserted that masturbation was permissible only from “16 or 18 years old” and was an intimate secret not to be shared with friends. Here, the participants’ intra-actions constrained their own agentive capacities, reinforcing beliefs and normative frameworks that legitimised sexual subjectivity as the exclusive domain of adolescence and adulthood. This movement within the assemblage constrains the possibility of expressing pre-adolescent curiosity and experiences, both their own and those of others. This constraint resonates with the anonymous questions that can be related to normative regimes—whether moral, health-related, or biomedical (“Is it good to masturbate?”, 11 years old), showing how children do not merely internalise but actively (re)produce truth regimes of sexuality [19] through situated intra-actions within specific material-discursive arrangements.

Discussion

The findings demonstrate how intra-actions among the participants, the researchers, the school environment, the methodological tools (anonymous questions, drawings), and social norms configure discursive-material assemblages that produce a sexuality predominantly associated with adulthood and reproduction [31, 43]. The vast majority of the concerns expressed by the children revolve around these topics, evidencing not only their interest but also the (re)production of a reproductive, adult-centred, heterosexual, and genitally focused model of sexuality—widely criticised by scholars such as Fine [16] and Allen [2], who denounce the systematic omission of pleasure in CSE programmes. This model also marginalises experiences and questions related to sexual and gender diversity, which appear only minimally in both the anonymous questions and group narratives, thereby both revealing and reinforcing a heteronormative framework that renders other ways of experiencing and feeling sexuality invisible.

This framework limits the possibilities of CSE, which is often reduced to preventive and biomedical content [23]. However, our data show that children are also interested in their own bodily experiences related to puberty, bodily fluids, pleasure, and masturbation. Many of these questions reveal not only a fragmentation of knowledge but also forms of affect such as fear, shame, or discomfort, especially around topics like menstruation or semen. As Schmitt et al. [46] point out, these forms of affect lead children to seek answers outside formal educational settings, with consequences for their emotional well-being and autonomy.

Despite the formal inclusion of CSE in the Spanish curriculum since 2020, its implementation remains irregular and adult centred. Menstruation, for example, is usually approached from a biological or reproductive perspective, without addressing girls’ experiences, questions, or emotions [45]. Similarly, semen is almost invisible in school and family discourses, and there is a clear low level of sexual literacy among boys [44], which coincides with findings from other cultural contexts where lack of information generates anxiety and confusion [47].

Our three-part methodological design (anonymous questions, drawings, focus groups) made it possible to visualise how the expression of certain topics varies depending on the discursive space. While anonymous questions enabled the broaching of topics that are less “sayable” in educational contexts (such as masturbation or pleasure), focus groups brought to the fore moral and normative regimes that restricted what could be shared. For example, the group silence around shame associated with menstruation and self-censorship in discussions about masturbation, reflect how intra-actions among peers and with adults (re)produce a view of childhood as innocent and asexual [43], thereby silencing their experiences.

The gender composition of the groups also proved relevant. In mixed-gender groups, more moral anxieties and uncertainties arose, while in single-gender groups, discussion of one’s own pleasure was somewhat facilitated, even as intra-actions within the assemblage continued to (re)produce normative frameworks of sexuality. These findings suggest that, for both research and educational intervention, it is crucial to offer multiple safe spaces and methods that allow children to express themselves in different ways.

Furthermore, we identified a fragmentation of knowledge and unequal forms of affect across genders. Although reproduction functions as a stabilised node from which children explore less-mapped affective territories, female pleasure was often subsumed under a reproductive logic, while male pleasure, though more visible, was laden with anxiety and shame. This asymmetry reflects and reproduces inequalities in bodily and sexual literacy.

Finally, it is important to highlight that the participants themselves reproduced age-based restrictions, internalising and expressing moral and normative conceptions that position adolescence as the legitimate threshold for sexual recognition (see also [24]). This age-based self-regulation limits children’s capacity to build plural narratives about their bodily and emotional experiences, thereby reinforcing the hegemonic discourse that associates sexuality with adulthood.

Taken together, this study reveals the potential of listening to children and of creating dialogic spaces where they can reinterpret and produce knowledge about their own sexuality. It underscores the urgency of moving towards a rights-based, plural, and situated CSE that recognises children as sexual subjects [50], capable of knowing, feeling, and narrating their bodies and desires from an early age.

Conclusions

This study makes visible the interests, questions, and forms of knowledge regarding sexuality among 9- to 11-year-old children, revealing a significant gap between their concerns and the sexuality education content they receive. Despite regulatory advances in Spain, adult-centred, prevention-focused education persists, rendering invisible pleasure, children’s bodily experiences, and situated knowing. Including these voices not only enriches our understanding of childhood sexuality but also raises urgent ethical and pedagogical challenges. It is essential to rethink CSE from a rights-based perspective that recognises children as sexual subjects and promotes more inclusive, plural, and transformative forms of sexual learning.
