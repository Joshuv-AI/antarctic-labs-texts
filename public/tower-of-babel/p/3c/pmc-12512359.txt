Evaluation of corneal densitometry using Pentacam in patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus: a controlled cross-sectional study
Authors: Çiğdem Coşkun, Sebile Çomçalı, Celil Orman
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
PurposeThis study aims to analyze corneal densitometry (CD) values in individuals diagnosed with Type 2 Diabetes Mellitus (DM) using the Pentacam Scheimpflug imaging system and to compare the obtained data with those from a control group consisting of healthy individuals.MethodsThe study included 74 eyes of 37 patients diagnosed with Type 2 Diabetes Mellitus (DM) and 54 eyes of 27 healthy volunteers matched for age and gender. All participants underwent measurements of flat keratometry (K1), steep keratometry (K2), maximum keratometry (Kmax), central corneal thickness (CCT), thinnest corneal thickness (TCT), average anterior and posterior elevation at the corneal apex (AAE and APE), corneal volume (CV) and CD. All measurements were performed using the Pentacam HR device (Oculus, Germany), which operates based on the Scheimpflug imaging principle. CD was evaluated in detail according to radial zones (0–2 mm, 2–6 mm, 6–10 mm, 10–12 mm) and corneal depths (anterior, central, posterior, and total). Only newly diagnosed Type 2 DM patients were included in the study, while those with diabetic retinopathy or maculopathy were excluded. HbA1c levels of all patients were recorded. Statistical analysis of the data was conducted using SPSS software version 22.0, and a p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.ResultsThe mean age of the Type 2 Diabetes Mellitus (DM) group was 50.51 ± 8.93 years, while the mean age of the healthy control group was 50.89 ± 5.71 years. There were no statistically significant differences between the two groups in terms of age and gender distribution (p = 0.42 and p = 0.20, respectively). Similarly, no significant differences were observed between the groups in terms of K1, K2, Kmax, CCT, TCT, AAE,APE and CV (p = 0.51; p = 0.50; p = 0.48; p = 0.95; p = 0.99; p = 0.50; p = 0.29; p = 0,58 respectively).In contrast, CD values were significantly higher in the Type 2 DM group compared to the healthy control group across all zones (0–2 mm, 2–6 mm, 6–10 mm, 10–12 mm) and at all corneal depths (anterior, central, posterior, total) (p < 0.01). A low-level positive correlation was found between HbA1c levels and AAE (r = 0.281; p = 0.015). In addition, a very weak negative correlation was observed between HbA1c levels and CD values in the anterior 2–6 mm zone (r = –0.240; p = 0.035).ConclusionIn this study, CD values were found to be significantly higher in patients with Type 2 DM compared to healthy individuals. This finding suggests that chronic hyperglycemia may lead to both morphological and functional changes in the corneal tissue.

Introduction

Diabetes Mellitus (DM) is one of the most common metabolic disorders worldwide and represents a growing public health concern due to its increasing prevalence. While diabetic retinopathy is the most frequently observed ocular complication of DM, various pathological changes can also occur in the ocular surface and corneal structures [1].

Diabetic keratopathy, one of the most prevalent ocular surface complications of DM, can present with conditions such as punctate epithelial keratitis, recurrent corneal erosions, delayed epithelial healing, persistent epithelial defects, and corneal ulcers. These manifestations arise as a result of structural damage to the epithelial and endothelial layers caused by DM [2–7]. Additionally, at the stromal level, the accumulation of advanced glycation end-products (AGEs), activation of keratocytes, and increased cross-linking of corneal collagen and proteoglycans due to prolonged hyperglycemia may occur [8].

The monitoring of subclinical changes in corneal transparency in diabetic patients has become feasible with the development of non-invasive imaging technologies. The densitometry module of the Pentacam device (Oculus Inc., Wetzlar, Germany), which operates based on the Scheimpflug principle, enables quantitative evaluation of the optical density in the anterior, central, and posterior layers of the cornea. It allows for an objective, reproducible, and reliable analysis of corneal transparency. In healthy individuals, due to low levels of light reflection from the cornea, densitometry values are low. However, in the presence of conditions such as edema, inflammation, or scar tissue, light scattering increases, resulting in elevated optical density values [9].

Kaji et al. reported that most diabetic patients exhibit keratopathic changes, but detecting these alterations at a subclinical level using slit-lamp biomicroscopy is challenging [10]. In this context, corneal densitometry (CD) is a valuable diagnostic tool for the early detection of structural changes that are not yet apparent biomicroscopically [9].

The aim of this study is to comparatively evaluate CD, anterior segment parameters, and the relationship between HbA1c levels and CD in individuals diagnosed with Type 2 DM versus healthy Subjects. In doing so, the study seeks to identify potential corneal pathologies related to Type 2 DM before clinical signs become evident.

Methods

This prospective comparative study included 74 eyes from 37 patients diagnosed with Type 2 DM and 54 eyes from 27 healthy individuals matched for age and gender. The study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and received approval from the Ethics Committee of Ankara Bilkent City Hospital (Tabed 2–24-469). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

All subjects underwent a comprehensive ophthalmologic examination, including best corrected visual acuity (BCVA), anterior segment evaluation, and fundus examination. Patients with signs of diabetic retinopathy on fundus examination were excluded from the study. Exclusion criteria included: active or past corneal infections, corneal dystrophies, ectatic corneal diseases, corneal degenerations, corneal scars, history of ocular surgery, contact lens use, and the use of topical medications that could affect corneal structure.

Before pupil dilation, all participants were evaluated using Scheimpflug imaging with the Pentacam HR device (Oculus Inc., Wetzlar, Germany) to assess anterior segment parameters. The measured parameters included maximum keratometry (Kmax), mean keratometry (Km), central corneal thickness (CCT), thinnest corneal thickness (TCT), mean anterior and posterior elevation at the corneal apex (AAE, APE) and corneal volume (CV).

CD measurements were performed by the same experienced operator in a dark room, immediately after asking patients to blink twice. During the measurements, care was taken to ensure that the cornea was not covered by the eyelids. The Pentacam device captures 25 cross-sectional images of the cornea using a Scheimpflug camera and a monochromatic diode light source emitting blue light at 475 nm. CD is based on the analysis of light reflected from a 12-mm corneal area centered on the apex. CD was evaluated across four concentric zones (0–2 mm, 2–6 mm, 6–10 mm, 10–12 mm) and at three different corneal depths (anterior: the anterior 120 μm; central; posterior: the posterior 60 μm). The anterior 120 μm of the cornea encompasses the epithelium, Bowman's layer, and anterior stroma, while the posterior 60 μm includes the posterior stroma and Descemet's membrane, which extends to the endothelium. Optical density values are expressed in Gray Scale Units (GSU), ranging from 0 to 100. 0 GSU stands for maximum transparency, minimum light scattering, 100 GSU stands for full opacity, maximum light scattering. For each eye, three consecutive measurements were recorded, and the average value was used for statistical analysis. Measurement data were obtained from the device’s automated densitometry analysis. Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 13.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). The distribution of the data was assessed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used for comparisons between groups. Correlations between parameters were analyzed using the Pearson correlation test. A p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Discussion

Chronic hyperglycemia induces various morphological, metabolic, physiological, and clinical alterations in the corneal tissue [11]. Previous studies have demonstrated that the interaction between the corneal epithelium and the basement membrane differs in patients with Type 2 DM compared to healthy individuals. Reported structural changes in these patients include a decreased number of hemidesmosomes in the basal membrane, reduced corneal nerve fiber density and nerve branching, decreased endothelial cell density, loss of hexagonality, and irregularities in cell morphology [12–14].

The widespread increase in CD values observed in our study across all corneal zones (0–2 mm, 2–6 mm, 6–10 mm, 10–12 mm) and depths (anterior, central, posterior) in newly diagnosed Type 2 DM patients suggests that these structural changes may occur early in the disease process, potentially due to prolonged, undetected hyperglycemia prior to diagnosis. The insidious onset of Type 2 DM often results in a period of subclinical hyperglycemia, during which metabolic changes, such as the accumulation of AGEs and increased collagen cross-linking, can alter corneal structure and transparency [1, 8]. These changes may affect all corneal layers, leading to increased light scattering and elevated CD values, as observed in our study. For instance, McKay et al. demonstrated that AGE-mediated collagen cross-linking in diabetic corneas disrupts stromal matrix organization, potentially contributing to reduced corneal transparency [8]. Similarly, Del Buey et al. reported altered corneal biomechanical properties, including increased corneal hysteresis and resistance factor, in Type 2 DM patients without retinopathy, suggesting early biomechanical remodeling driven by hyperglycemia [1]. Şahin et al. further supported this by reporting reduced corneal hysteresis in diabetic patients, indicating compromised corneal structural integrity even in the early stages of the disease [15]. These biomechanical changes align with our findings of increased CD values, which may reflect subclinical structural alterations affecting corneal transparency across all zones and layers.

The key factors that determine corneal transparency include the regular arrangement of collagen fibrils, the integrity of connective tissue, the balanced distribution of keratocytes, and the level of stromal hydration. The corneal epithelium and endothelium are the primary structures influencing light scattering. Disruptions and disorganization in these structures can lead to increased light scattering, resulting in reduced visual quality [16]. CD is an objective parameter for assessing corneal transparency; as transparency decreases, densitometry values increase [17]. Therefore, an increase in CD may indicate edema, inflammation, scarring, or subclinical structural changes associated with DM.

In our study, CD values were found to be significantly higher in all corneal zones (0–2 mm, 2–6 mm, 6–10 mm, 10–12 mm) and at all depth levels (anterior, central, posterior) in patients with Type 2 DM compared to healthy individuals. This finding suggests that structural changes associated with DM affect corneal transparency and can be objectively detected through CD measurements. However, it is well known that the reliability of CD measurements in the 10–12 mm zone is lower. This is due to the possibility that, in some individuals, the corneal diameter is less than 12 mm, causing this zone to include parts of the limbus and sclera. Therefore, results from this zone should be interpreted with caution [18].

There are numerous studies in the literature investigating the impact of DM on CD. In line with our findings, Özyol et al. reported that CD values in diabetic individuals were significantly higher compared to healthy controls in the 0–10 mm zones of the anterior layer and 0–2 mm zone of the total cornea [19]. The authors attributed the increase in anterior densitometry to thickening of the epithelial basement membrane. Yang et al.examined densitometry values in diabetic individuals aged 60 years and older and divided the patients into three age groups. According to their study, in diabetic patients aged 60–69 years, CD values were higher in the 0–2 mm region in the anterior layer compared to the control group. In diabetic patients aged 70–79 years, CD values were significantly higher in the anterior, middle and posterior 2–6 mm regions compared to the control group. In diabetic patients aged 80–89 years, CD values were increased in most corneal regions compared to the control group [20]. Similarly, Gao et al. reported increased CD values and central corneal thickness in patients with Type 2 DM, along with a decrease in endothelial cell density. In their study, in the DM and control groups, corneal optical density increased away from the center and gradually decreased from the outher corneal epithelium to the corneal endothelium [21]. Elagamy and colleagues also found significantly higher CD values in diabetic individuals, particularly in the 6–10 mm anterior corneal zone, the total anterior cornea, and the total corneal area [22]. Calvo-Maroto et al. reported significantly higher CD values in the DM group compared to healthy individuals when evaluating the central 3 mm and 5 mm zones (p = 0.016 and p = 0.014, respectively) [23]. The findings of these studies are consistent with the data obtained in our study and support the notion that subclinical corneal changes induced by Type 2 DM can be reliably monitored using Pentacam densitometry measurements.

Despite the significantly elevated CD values in our study, other anterior segment parameters, including K1, K2, Kmax, CCT, TCT, AAE, APE and CV showed no significant differences between the Type 2 DM and control groups. This contrast may be attributed to the early-stage disease in our newly diagnosed cohort, where hyperglycemia-induced changes may primarily affect corneal transparency before manifesting as measurable alterations in corneal curvature or thickness. While some studies have reported changes in these parameters in diabetic patients, such as increased CCT in patients with longer-standing DM [21, 22], our findings align with studies like Lee et al. which found no significant differences in corneal thickness in early-stage DM [14]. The absence of significant changes in our cohort may also reflect population characteristics, Such as relatively good baseline corneal health in newly diagnosed patients without diabetic retinopathy. These findings underscore the value of CD as a potentially earlier and more sensitive biomarker of corneal involvement in Type 2 DM. Unlike other anterior segment parameters, which may require more pronounced or chronic pathological changes to become detectable, CD can capture subtle, subclinical alterations in corneal transparency, making it a critical tool for early detection and monitoring of diabetic keratopathy [9, 17].

There are also some conflicting findings in the literature regarding the relationship between DM and CD. These discrepancies may stem from variations in patient populations, DM types, disease duration, levels of glycemic control, and differences in study methodologies. For example, Ramm et al. reported that CD values across all corneal regions were significantly lower in DM patients compared to the control group [24]. This result may be explained by alternative pathophysiological mechanisms such as diabetes-induced corneal thinning, stromal matrix degeneration, and increased corneal transparency. Similarly, Tekin et al. found no significant difference in CD values between Type 1 DM patients and controls. In that study, most patients had a relatively short disease duration (mean 7.05 ± 2.81 years), good glycemic control (mean HbA1c: 6.36 ± 1.36%), and those with retinopathy or maculopathy were excluded, all of which could have influenced the findings [25].

The discrepancy between our findings of significantly elevated CD values in newly diagnosed Type 2 DM patients and studies reporting less pronounced or non-significant differences in patients with longer-standing or treated diabetes may be attributed to several factors. In newly diagnosed patients, prolonged, uncontrolled hyperglycemia prior to diagnosis likely drives early corneal changes, such as AGE accumulation and epithelial basement membrane thickening, leading to increased light scattering and higher CD values [1, 8]. In contrast, patients with longer-standing DM who receive medical therapy (e.g., insulin or oral hypoglycemic agents) may achieve better glycemic control, potentially stabilizing or partially reversing these changes. For instance, Gekka et al. demonstrated that improved glycemic control enhances corneal epithelial barrier function [26]. Additionally, chronic DM may induce corneal remodeling or adaptive responses, such as alterations in stromal collagen organization or compensatory mechanisms in the epithelium and endothelium, which could attenuate CD elevations over time [8]. These findings suggest that CD is a dynamic parameter influenced by disease phase and management, making it a valuable tool for monitoring early corneal changes and treatment response in Type 2 DM.

The association between HbA1c levels and impaired corneal barrier function has been emphasized in several studies [26]. However, there is no clear consensus in the literature regarding the relationship between CD and HbA1c levels. For instance, while Yang et al. found a significant positive correlation between DM duration and CD values, they did not observe a significant relationship with HbA1c levels [20]. Similarly, Calvo-Maroto et al. found no significant association between CD values in the central 3–5 mm zones and the duration or stage of DM [27]. On the other hand, Ramm et al. reported a significant negative correlation between HbA1c levels and CD values in the central corneal layer (r = –0.424; p = 0.044) [24]. Özyol et al. also found no significant relationship between CD and HbA1c levels, but they did report a positive correlation between CD and the duration of DM. The authors noted that HbA1c reflects only the average glycemic status over the preceding three months and, therefore, may not always correlate directly with parameters like CD that reflect cumulative tissue changes [19]. In the study by Ramm et al., significant negative correlations were reported between CD values and both the stage of diabetic retinopathy (r = –0.271; p = 0.019) and HbA1c levels (r = –0.424; p = 0.044). Furthermore, it was shown that DM patients with maculopathy had lower CD values compared to those without maculopathy. On the other hand, Elagamy et al. found no correlation between CD values and either HbA1c levels or the duration of DM [23].

These discrepancies can be attributed to factors such as heterogeneous patient populations, differences between DM types (Type 1 vs. Type 2), varying levels of glycemic control, the presence of concomitant ocular complications, and measurement variability between devices. Therefore, when considering CD as a clinical biomarker in DM patients, these parameters must be taken into account.

In our study, no significant correlation was observed between HbA1c levels and CD values in zones or depths other than the anterior 2–6 mm zone. In that specific zone, only a very weak negative correlation was found between HbA1c and CD. This finding may be explained by the structural homogeneity of the cornea in this region and its potential resistance to systemic alterations. Similarly, Yılmaz et al. did not find a significant association between the various layers of the 2–6 mm zone and DM duration in their study. They also reported no significant difference in CD values between diabetic and healthy individuals in this region [2]. In the literature, studies conducted in both healthy and diabetic populations have consistently identified the 2–6 mm corneal zone as having the lowest optical density values, indicating it is the most transparent region [28]. Based on these findings, Yılmaz and colleagues concluded that the 2–6 mm zone of the cornea may be the last region to be affected by potential pathological processes [2].

This study has several limitations. First, the sample size is relatively small. Additionally, the study only included newly diagnosed Type 2 DM patients; however, the time of diagnosis may not correspond to the actual onset of the disease. As a result, some individuals may already exhibit effects of long-standing diabetes, which could have amplified the impact of chronic hyperglycemia on corneal structure. Neurotrophic keratopathy is a significant corneal complication associated with DM, and previous studies have reported reduced corneal sensitivity and decreased nerve fiber density in diabetic patients [29, 30]. Although no corneal pathology was observed during biomicroscopic examination in our study, the absence of corneal sensitivity assessment is considered a potential limitation. Moreover, individuals diagnosed with diabetic retinopathy were excluded from the study. Since the presence of retinopathy reflects the severity of systemic and ocular complications, including such patients could have provided valuable insight into the association between CD changes and diabetic microangiopathy.

The diagnosis of diabetic keratopathy can be challenging, particularly in asymptomatic patients and in those without observable pathology on biomicroscopic examination. However, as demonstrated in our study, increases in CD values may serve as early indicators of corneal changes that cannot be detected by slit-lamp biomicroscopy. These alterations, which can significantly affect visual quality, may be identified earlier through objective measurement techniques such as optical densitometry.

In conclusion, the evaluation of CD using Pentacam in patients with Type 2 DM may serve as a valuable tool for the early detection and monitoring of subclinical structural and functional changes in the cornea. This approach could facilitate the earlier identification and management of ocular surface complications, particularly diabetic keratopathy, thus improving patient outcomes.
