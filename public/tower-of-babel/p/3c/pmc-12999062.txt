Single-cell sequencing reveals unexpected genetic diversity among Bodo spp. flagellates and their bacterial endosymbionts
Authors: Sally D. Warring, Jamie McGowan, Estelle S. Kilias, James Lipscombe, Elisabet Alacid, Tom Barker, Leah Catchpole, Karim Gharbi, Seanna McTaggart, Thomas A. Richards, David Swarbreck, Neil Hall
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBodo is a cosmopolitan genus of free-living bacterivorous single-celled flagellates in the class Kinetoplastea. Genus Bodo is considered the closest free-living lineage to the parasitic lineages Trypanosoma and Leishmania, the causative agents of the human diseases sleeping sickness, Chagas disease and leishmaniasis. Currently, a single genome exists for the one formally described species in the genus, Bodo saltans. Previous studies on B. saltans have shown that it is dependent on an endosymbiotic bacterium from the order Holosporales, ‘Candidatus Bodocaedibacter vickermanii’. Using single-cell sequencing, we isolated, sequenced and assembled genomes for seven uncultured Bodo spp. cells from a freshwater sample from Royal Leamington Spa, UK. Using comparative genomics, we show that these seven cells represent three potentially novel Bodo species exhibiting unexpected levels of diversity at the genome level. Our results indicate that small subunit ribosomal DNA sequencing, often used to classify Bodo flagellates, is insufficient for determining species delimitation in this genus. In addition, we recovered a Holosporales bacterium genome from all seven Bodo spp. cells. Surprisingly, these seven endosymbionts also represent three novel species, congruent with the phylogeny of the host and exhibiting lineage-specific adaptations. This diversity and host–symbiont association would be indistinguishable in routinely used metabarcoding or bulk sequencing pipelines, thus demonstrating the power of single-cell sequencing to reveal diversity within lineages of microbial eukaryotes.

Impact Statement

By applying a cutting-edge single-cell genomics pipeline to individual Bodo cells isolated from a freshwater environment, we reveal three previously undescribed Bodo species, significantly expanding the genomic resources available for free-living kinetoplastids. Our single-cell approach also recovers the genome of three novel Holosporales bacterial endosymbionts associated with each Bodo species, demonstrating that single-cell genomics is a powerful approach for uncovering hidden diversity in populations of uncultured microbial eukaryotes and their associations with bacterial symbionts.

Introduction

Bodo is a genus of heterotrophic free-living bi-flagellated protists common in fresh and brackish water and soil. They are members of the Kinetoplastea, a class of parasitic and free-living protists distinguished by the presence of a large mass of mitochondrial DNA known as kinetoplast DNA or kDNA [2]. Class Kinetoplastea is divided into two subclasses, the Prokinetoplastina and the Metakinetoplastina, the latter containing four orders, Eubodonida, Parabodonida, Neobodonida and the Trypanosmatida [34]. Order Trypanosomatida includes the parasites responsible for human diseases such as sleeping sickness, Chagas’ disease and leishmaniases. Phylogenies based on small subunit (SSU) ribosomal DNA (rDNA) and protein sequences place order Eubodonida and its one genus, Bodo, as the sister clade to the Trypanosmatida, and Bodo is considered the closest free-living lineage to the parasitic Trypanosomatida lineages [57].

Traditionally, Bodo species were identified and distinguished by morphology, e.g. cell size and shape, length of the flagella, position of the nucleus and kinetoplast and ultrastructural features visible by light and electron microscopy [89]. More recently, phylogenies based on molecular data showed the genus Bodo was paraphyletic and its members were distributed into three of the aforementioned four orders, with only one clade containing three species, Bodo saltans, Bodo edax and Bodo uncinatus remaining the genus Bodo [35 10]. Further, it is suggested that B. edax and B. uncinatus are not true species and are likely isolates of B. saltans [311]. Despite the important phylogenetic position of genus Bodo, B. saltans remains the only formally described species in the genus, and a single genome from bulk culture currently exists for the B. saltans strain Lake Konstanz [1213].

Holosporales are alphaproteobacteria that are widespread obligate endosymbionts of eukaryotes, particularly protists [1415]. Holosporales form complex associations with their eukaryotic hosts, including infectious parasitic species [16], some that confer competitive advantages to their hosts [17] and some that may increase host fitness under certain conditions [1819]. Recent work has shown that B. saltans strain Lake Konstanz harbours an endosymbiotic bacterium from the order Holosporales, ‘Candidatus Bodocaedibacter vickermanii’ [20]. B. saltans appears dependent on its endosymbiont as antibiotic treatment results in rapid cell death, and it is hypothesized that this dependence is due to three putative addictive toxin/antitoxin systems encoded in the endosymbiont’s genome [20]. These systems render the endosymbiont essential to its host as they encode a long-lived toxin molecule alongside an antitoxin with a shorter half-life, and loss of the antitoxin results in the activation of the toxin and host cell death [21].

Here, we use single-cell sequencing to assemble genomes from seven uncultured cells from one environmental sample that were identified as belonging to the genus Bodo. Genomic comparisons show that the seven genomes form three clades, potentially representing three novel Bodo species that diverge significantly from B. saltans and each other. In addition, we recover a Holosporales bacterial genome from each raw assembly and show that these putative endosymbionts also represent three potentially novel species, forming three clades that appear congruent with the phylogeny of the hosts. We identify a putative toxin/antitoxin system in only one of these clades. These results highlight the utility of using single-cell sequencing and comparative genomics to reveal the diversity within and between populations of microbial eukaryotes and their cobionts.

Methods

Sample collection

Surface water (~1 m) was collected from the River Leam (52.287295, –1.547563), Royal Leamington Spa (UK) in August 2022. Initially, the sample was prefiltered to remove larger debris and then concentrated using different polycarbonate filter pore sizes (Millipore) to obtain concentrated subsamples of different protist size ranges (10 to 40 µm and 0.8 to 5 µm). The subsamples were supplemented with 2–3 autoclaved barley grains to support heterotrophic/mixotrophic growth via bacteria increase and incubated for 1 week prior to cell sorting. Single-cell nuclei were stained with 1xSybrGreen for 10 min and sorted into 96-well microplates (pre-filled with 5 µl autoclaved/sterile filtered media), using fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS) (flow rate=1) and selecting against chlorophyll a-negative cells while selecting for SybrGreen-positive cells. After cell sorting, 10 µl of RLT-plus lysis buffer (QIAGEN) was added to the wells, and the plate was frozen at −80 °C until further processing.

Whole-genome amplification, library preparation and sequencing

A modified G and T-seq protocol [22] was carried out as follows. Using a magnetic separator, Dynabeads MyOne Streptavidin C1 (Invitrogen) beads were washed according to the manufacturer’s guidance and then incubated with 2× binding and wash buffer (10 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.5, 1 mM EDTA, 2 M NaCl) and Biotinylated Oligo-dT primer (IDT, 5′-/BiotinTEG/AAG CAG TGG TAT CAA CGC AGA GTA CTT TTT TTT TTT TTT TTT TTT TTT TTT TTT TVN-3′) at 100 µM for 30 min at room temperature on a rotator. The oligo-treated beads were washed four times in 1× binding and wash buffer (5 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.5, 0.5 mM EDTA, 1 M NaCl) and then suspended in 1× SuperScript II First Strand Buffer (Invitrogen) supplemented with SUPERase-In RNase Inhibitor (Invitrogen) to a final concentration of 1 U µl−1. The lysate was thawed on ice. Ten microlitres of prepared oligo-dT beads was added to each well containing 12 µl cell lysate using a Dragonfly Discovery liquid dispenser (SPT Labtech). The lysate plate was sealed and incubated on a ThermoMixer C (Eppendorf) with a heated lid at 21 °C for 20 min shaking at 1,000 r.p.m. Using a Fluent 480 liquid handling robot (Tecan) and a Magnum FLX magnetic separator (Alpaqua), the lysate supernatant was transferred to a new plate, and the beads were washed twice in a custom wash buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl pH 8.3, 75 mM KCl, 3 mM MgCl2, 10 mM DTT, 0.5% Tween-20). The supernatant from the washes was added to the leftover cell lysate containing the genomic DNA, which was stored at −20 °C overnight. The mRNA was not used for this study. The remaining cell lysate was thawed and subjected to a 0.6×vols Ampure XP clean-up with 80% ethanol. The bead-bound gDNA was isothermally amplified for 3 h at 30 °C then 10 min at 65 °C using a miniaturized (1/5 vols) Repli-g Single-Cell assay (QIAGEN). The amplified gDNA was cleaned up with 0.8×vols Ampure XP and 80% ethanol and then eluted in 10 mM Tris-HCl.

Sequencing libraries for this project were constructed by the Technical Genomics Group at the Earlham Institute, Norwich, UK. Initial libraries were constructed for shallow-depth sequencing as follows: Amplified gDNA was quantified by fluorescence (Quant-iT HS-DNA, Invitrogen) on an Infinite Pro 200 plate reader (Tecan) and then normalized to a final concentration of 0.2 ng µl−1 in 10 mM Tris-HCl. The Mosquito HV and Dragonfly Discovery liquid handling instruments (SPT Labtech) were used to prepare miniaturized (1/12.5 vols) Nextera XT (Illumina) dual-indexed sequencing libraries as follows: a tagmentation mastermix comprising two parts TD buffer (Illumina) and one part ATM (Illumina) was prepared. Using the Dragonfly Discovery, 1.2 µl tagmentation mastermix was dispensed per well to a 384-well skirted PCR plate (Eppendorf). Using the Mosquito HV, 0.4 µl of normalized DNA was transferred to each well containing tagmentation mastermix. The plate was sealed and spun down and then incubated at 55 °C for 10 min on a thermal cycler. Using the Dragonfly Discovery, 0.4 µl 0.2% SDS was dispensed to each reaction. The plate was sealed, spun down and then incubated at room temperature for 5 min. Using the Dragonfly Discovery, 1.2 µl NPM (Illumina) was added to each reaction. Using the Mosquito HV, 0.8 µl index primer pairs (i5+i7) at a concentration of 0.5 µM was added to each reaction, ensuring a unique combination in each well. The libraries were amplified under the following thermal cycler conditions: 72 °C for 3 min, 95 °C for 30 s, 12 cycles (95 °C for 10 s, 55 °C for 30 s and 72 °C for 60 s), 72 °C for 5 min, 4 °C hold. The libraries were pooled and cleaned up using 0.8×vols Ampure XP and 80% ethanol. The library pools were eluted in 20 µl 10 mM Tris-HCl and assessed using a Bioanalyzer HS DNA assay (Agilent), HS DNA Qubit assay (Invitrogen) and finally an Illumina Library Quantification Kit assay (KAPA). These reads from these libraries were used for taxonomic assessment of the cells.

After taxonomic assessment, deep sequencing libraries for the Bodo cells were constructed using the KAPA High-Throughput Library Prep Kit (Roche Part No: KK8234/07961901001). Where possible, 1 µg of genomic DNA was sheared to 450 bp using the Covaris ML230 Sonicator (Covaris), and the ends of the DNA were repaired; 3′ to 5′ exonuclease activity removed the 3′ overhangs and the polymerase activity filled in the 5′ overhangs, creating blunt ends. A single ‘A’ nucleotide was added to the 3′ ends of the blunt fragments to allow for the ligation of barcoded adapters [6 bp – Perkin Elmer NEXTFLEX DNA Barcodes 1–48 (NOVA-514101/2/3/4)] or [12 bp – Perkin Elmer NEXTFLEX-HT (NOVA-51474/5/6/7)] at a concentration of 6 µM prior to a 0.8× clean up using Beckman Coulter AMPure XP beads (A63882). The size of the libraries was estimated using an Agilent High Sensitivity DNA chip (5067–4626), and the concentrations were quantified by fluorescence with a High Sensitivity Qubit assay (Thermo Fisher Q32854).

Both shallow and deep sequencing libraries were sequenced on either an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 (cells A8, A10, B2, F10, G10 and H10) with SP Reagent Kit v1.5 kit (300 cycles), using Real-Time Analysis (RTA) (version 3.4.4) and Control Software (version 1.7.5) or an Illumina NovaSeq X Plus (cell B7) with 10B Reagent Kit (300 Cycle) using RTA (version 4.6.7) and Control Software (version 1.2.2) to produce 150 bp paired-end reads. The resulting BCL files were converted to fastq with bcl2fastq (version 2.0).

Genome assembly, curation and classification

Sequencing reads were trimmed using Trim Galore (version 0.6.6) (https://github.com/FelixKrueger/TrimGalore) with Cutadapt (version 3.4) [23]. Genome assemblies were generated using SPAdes (version 3.15.5) [24] with single-cell mode enabled (--sc) and k-mer sizes 21, 33, 55 and 77.

Scaffolds less than 1,000 bp long were discarded from the assembly. Each assembly was manually curated, and contaminant scaffolds/bins were removed using a combination of metagenomic binning with MetaBAT2 [25] based on tetra-nucleotide frequencies and taxonomic classification with CAT (v5.2) [26], Blobtools (v1.1.1) [27], Tiara (v1.0.1) [28] and EukRep (v0.6.6) [29]. Bins that were classified as majority eukaryotic by all four classifiers and majority ‘Euglenozoa’ by Blobtools were retained, as well as any unbinned scaffold classified as ‘Euglenozoa’ by Blobtools or CAT.

The Holosporales bins were first identified from the taxonomic classifications described above. However, all raw assembly bins were assessed for completeness using CheckM (v1.1.2) [30], and all bins >70% complete were further classified using the Genome Taxonomy Database Toolkit (GTDB-TK) (v2.3.2) on KBase. In all the cases, only one bin in each assembly was classified as Alphaproteobacteria by GTDB-TK and was the bin retained as the Holosporales endosymbiont metagenome-assembled genome (MAG).

The retained scaffold sets were manually checked, which resulted in the removal of one scaffold from the B2 Holosporales bin that was a contaminant from another well on the 96-well plate.

The general statistics, classification summaries, CheckM results for all genome bins and the GTDB-TK classifications are tabulated in File S1 (available in the online Supplementary Material).

The SSU rDNA blasts were done through Geneious Prime (v2025.2.2). In each case, the subject sequence with the highest bitscore was considered the top hit.

Gene prediction annotation

Eukaryotic gene prediction was done using Companion (v2.2.11) [31] at the WebServer, with default settings and using B. saltans as the reference. rDNAs were annotated with Barrnap (v0.9). For one sample (B7), the SSU rDNA sequence was annotated as two overlapping fragments, which were manually merged into one sequence by alignment in Geneious Prime. Four of the seven SSU rDNA sequences are truncated (see Table 1 and File S2). Three of these (B2, G10 and H10) were manually extended by alignment of the scaffolds and truncated SSU rDNA sequences annotated by Barrnap in Geneious Prime and extracting the longest region with shared homology from each scaffold. All three sequences were extended by ~70 bp. For G10, the SSU rDNA annotation was truncated due to reaching the end of the scaffold, while B2 and H10 were truncated due to loss of homology, possibly due to misassembly of those scaffolds. Holosporales gene predictions were done using PROKKA (v1.14.6) [32]. Completeness of the genomes and protein sets was assessed with BUSCO (v5.3.2) [33].

Phylogenetic analyses of SSU rDNA sequences

SSU rDNA sequences were collected from GenBank, filtering for sequences that were at least 1,000 nt long (File S3). The sequences were aligned with MAFFT (v7.520) using the FFT-NS-i model [34]. The alignment was trimmed manually in Geneious Prime to remove all positions with less than 50% coverage. The untrimmed alignment used to build the tree is in File S2, while the sequence accessions are in File S3. The maximum-likelihood (ML) tree was built with IQ-TREE (v2.3.2) [35] using the TIM3e+I+R3 model, which was the best-fit model determined by ModelFinder [36], with 1,000 non-parametric bootstrap replicates, and rooting at two outgroup species Dimastigella trypaniformis and Rhynchomonas nasuta. The tree was visualized and plotted with its associated distance matrix using Interactive Tree Of Life (iTOL) (v7.2) [37].

Phylogenomic analyses

For the Kinetoplastea, protein sets for related species were collected from GenBank (File S4). Orthologous gene sets were identified using Orthofinder (v2.5.4) [38]. Protein sequences for shared single-copy orthogroups (n=488) were aligned with MAFFT (v7.520) using the FFT-NS-2 model [34] and trimmed with TrimAI (v2.0) using the gappyout option [39]. The trimmed alignments were concatenated with AMAS [40] into a matrix with 234,727 amino acid sites. The ML tree was produced from a partitioned analysis undertaken using IQ-TREE (v2.3.2) with a partitioning scheme that merged the 488 proteins into 20 partitions, with rooting at the outgroup Perkinsela sp. Model selection was performed by ModelFinder, and 1,000 non-parametric bootstraps were run.

For the Holosporales, protein sets were collected from GenBank for related species and several closely related MAGs (File S4). For one MAG (GCA_002422845.1), a protein set was not available on GenBank, so an annotation was done using PROKKA (v1.14.6). Orthologous gene sets were identified using Orthofinder (v2.5.4). Protein sequences from shared single-copy orthogroups (n=24) were aligned with MAFFT (v7.520) using the FFT-NS-2 model and trimmed with TrimAI (v2.0) using the gappyout option. The trimmed alignments were concatenated with AMAS into a matrix with 8,004 amino acid sites. The ML tree was produced from a partitioned analysis undertaken using IQ-TREE (v2.3.2) with a partitioning scheme that merged the 24 proteins into 6 partitions, with rooting at the outgroup containing Temperatibacter marinus and Kordiimonas pumila. Model selection was performed by ModelFinder, and 1,000 non-parametric bootstraps were run.

The Kinetoplastea tree (Fig. 5) is the Species Tree from All Genes (STAG) inferred species tree produced by Orthofinder. It is inferred from 331 gene trees from the orthogroups where all species are present.

All trees were visualized and plotted either using the iTOL or in R using the package ggtree (v3.14.0).

Functional analyses

PFAMs were assigned to each protein set using InterProScan (v5.52–86.0) using option -aap PFAM and with the pre-calculated match lookup service disabled (----disable-precalc). PFAM counts were tabulated in R. A principal component analysis (PCA) was run on the count matrix for Bodo spp. after removal of PFAMs with no variation, which reduced the dataset from 2,938 variables to 2,545. The PCA was run in R using the function prcomp with scaling and centring.

Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) identifiers were assigned to each protein set using KofamKOALA or kofamscan (v1.3.0) [41]. The completeness of each KEGG module was calculated using kegg-pathway-completeness-tool (v1.3.0) (https://github.com/EBI-Metagenomics/kegg-pathways-completeness-tool).

Venn diagrams showing the proportion of shared orthogroups were generated in R using the package ggven (v0.1.10). For an orthogroup to be missing from a species, it must be missing from all genomes in that species. For an orthogroup to be present, it can be present in one or more genomes in that species.

The presence of bacterial secretion systems was assessed manually by visualizing the results of KofamKOALA on the KEGG website using the KEGG mapper tool Reconstruct. The number of unique components present in each protein set that mapped to each secretion system was counted and compared to the total number of unique components listed for each system. The one exception was for the type VI system, which was annotated using a combination of KEGG Reconstruct, blastp and psiblast, where the type VI proteins from Ca. B. vickermanii were used as queries to search a database of the protein sets from all seven Holosporales MAGs. The query sequence identifiers and the significant hit subject identifiers are listed in File S5.

The toxin/antitoxin systems were investigated manually using blastp and psiblast, where the proteins of Ca. B. vickermanii systems were used as queries to search a database of the protein sets from all seven Holosporales MAGs. N-terminal signal peptides were identified using SignalP v6.0 at the webserver.

Amino acid identity, average nucleotide identity and aligned fraction analyses

Pairwise amino acid identity (AAI) was calculated for the Bodo spp. using the aai.rb ruby script and blast+ (v2.16.0) that is part of the Enveomics Collection at https://github.com/lmrodriguezr/enveomics [42]. For the Holosporales, pairwise AAI was calculated using FastAAI [43]. Average nucleotide identity (ANI) and aligned fraction (AF) were calculated using the tool Skani (v0.2.2) [44].

All plots were generated in R and edited for publication using Illustrator.

Results

Single-cell sequencing and genome assembly

Seven Bodo spp. cells were identified on two 96-well plates containing single cells isolated by FACS from an environmental sample. After DNA amplification and short-read sequencing, between 20,738,413 and 48,141,972 paired-end reads were generated for each cell (Table 1). The cells were named after the well in which they were deposited and are hereafter referred to as A8, A10, B2, B7, F10, G10 and H10. Each raw assembly was curated by binning and taxonomic assignment to remove scaffolds of bacterial and non-target origin, and the curated eukaryotic assemblies ranged in size from 29,029,105 to 36,294,242 bp compared to the bulk-culture assembly for B. saltans Lake Konstanz at 39,862,120 bp (Table 1). All assemblies were fragmented, with each in more than 2,619 contigs and contig N50s ranging from 22,220 to 31,876 bp (Table 1). All assemblies ranged from 54.8 to 56 mol% G+C, higher than B. saltans Lake Konstanz at 51.8% (Table 1). Protein annotations of each genome resulted in 11,121–13,166 protein annotations per single-cell genome, 30–40% less than B. saltans Lake Konstanz at 18,190 proteins (Table 1). Completeness analysis of the protein sets using BUSCO v5 with the Euglenozoa odb10 database gave 72.3−80.8% complete BUSCOs per assembly, compared to B. saltans Lake Konstanz at 88.5% complete BUSCOs (Table 1).

The seven Bodo cells form three clades

blastn searches against the GenBank nt database using the SSU rDNA sequences from each assembly returned three Bodo spp. as top hits (Table 2). For A8 and A10, the top hit is to ATCC isolate B. edax (ATCC30903), originally isolated from the Czech Republic. For B7 and F10, the best hit is to B. saltans strain HFCC309 isolated from a eutrophic pond in Germany [45]. For B2, G10 and H10, the top hit is to a B. saltans isolate from soil in Malaysia [11]. In all cases, the query sequences are more than 99% identical to their top hits. Fig. 1a shows the pairwise identities of the SSU rDNA nucleotide sequences from the seven single-cell assemblies. A8 and A10 are 100% identical. B7 and F10 are 99.82% identical, with one mismatch between the two sequences (1 nt indel of a G residue in a string of G residues); however, due to the fragmented nature of the assemblies, the SSU rDNA sequence from F10 is truncated, limiting full comparison of these sequences (Table 2 and File S2). B2 and H10 are 100% identical, with G10 99.92% identical to B2 and H10 with a 1 nt polymorphism (C to T transition in G10). All three SSU rDNA sequences from B2, H10 and G10 are truncated, missing ~700 nt from their 5′ ends (Table 2 and File S2). A8/A10 differs from B2/G10/H10 by 1 nt (A/G transition), while B7/F10 differs more substantially from all the others, with pairwise identities ~98% (Fig. 1a).

An ML tree of SSU rDNA sequences of Bodo and related species available in GenBank places our seven cells onto two clades, all within the B. saltans lineage (Fig. 1b). A8/A10 are sisters to B2/G10/H10 and are part of a radiation that includes B. edax, Bodo uncinatus and three other B. saltans isolates. B7/F10 are sisters to this clade, along with two other B. saltans isolates. B. saltans Lake Konstanz branches with other B. saltans isolates on a separate clade that diverges closer to the root of the tree (Fig. 1b). The seven single-cell Bodo genomes have SSU rDNA pairwise identities ranging between 94.7 and 95.7% with B. saltans Lake Konstanz and appear closer to the SSU rDNA sequence from B. edax, with pairwise identities ranging between 98.0 and 99.6% identity (Fig. 1b).

However, when we extracted near full length rRNA operons from four out of the seven single-cell assemblies and compared these sequences to those available for B. saltans, B. edax and a Bodo sp. isolate available in GenBank, we found that the pairwise identity between B. edax and our novel Bodo lineages was not maintained, with the percent identity dropping to <94% (File S6).

Extensive genomic diversity among the Bodo spp.

We next built an ML tree from 488 single-copy orthogroups shared by B. saltans, Lake Konstanz and our seven single-cell Bodo assemblies, using Perkinsela sp. as an outgroup (Fig. 2a). Here, the seven single-cell genomes split into three clades, with A8/A10 forming a sister clade to B2/G10/H10 and B7/F10 forming another clade branching closer to the root of the tree. B. saltans Lake Konstanz forms its own clade closest to the root of the tree. Next, we calculated average pairwise AAI for all eight Bodo assemblies, with each pairwise comparison averaging AAI over a minimum of 7,419 and a maximum of 10,181 reciprocal best hits (File S7). The AAI within each clade is high (>97%) but varies substantially between the clades (Fig. 2b). B. saltans lake Konstanz is equally distant from all seven single-cell assemblies, with pairwise AAI between 51.3 and 51.7% in all comparisons. B7/F10 are equally distant from A8/A10 and B7/G10/H10, with pairwise AAI between 62.1 and 62.6%. A8/A10 and B7/G10/H10 appear closer, with pairwise AAI between 84.8 and 85.0%.

We also compared the ANI and the AF of each Bodo spp. genome using the tool skani [44] (Fig. 2c). Here, a result of 0.00 for ANI and/or AF suggests that sequence similarity at the nucleotide level is too low for pairwise comparison using this method [44], e.g. B. saltans Lake Konstanz is too distant to any of the single-cell Bodo genomes to compare using this metric, with values of 0.00 in every pairwise comparison. However, ANI and AF are relatively high within each of the three single-cell Bodo clades (>98% ANI and >80% AF). Between the three clades, B7/F10 is too distant from the others to compare, while A8/A10 and B2/G10/H10 show an ANI of ~84%, but an AF of ~14–16%, indicating that only a small portion of the genome is close enough for comparison using ANI. The AF values shown in Fig. 2c are the average of two AF values calculated for each pairwise comparison. A matrix with both AF values calculated for each pairwise comparison is in File S8.

Next, we compared how many orthogroups are shared among the single-cell assemblies and B. saltans Lake Konstanz (Fig. 2d). The majority of orthogroups (55.5%) are shared by all eight assemblies. The two clades that branch most closely in the phylogenetic trees, A8/A10 and B2/G10/H10, share the highest percentage of orthogroups, 83.4%. B7/F10 shares 66.1% with A8/A10 and 68% with B2/G10/H10. B. saltans Lake Konstanz shares 62.8% with B7/F10, 61.4% with A8/A10 and 63.7% with B7/G10/H10. Each clade has a small percentage of orthogroups that are unique, with B. saltans from lake Konstanz having the most unique orthogroups.

To compare the functional complement of each genome, we mapped each protein set to the Pfam database. In each case, fewer than 50% of proteins in each annotation contain one or more Pfam domains (Fig. 2e). We tabulated the Pfams found in each protein annotation and ran a PCA on that table. The PCA separates the genomes into three clusters congruent with the phylogeny. Principal component (PC) 1 separates B. saltans lake Konstanz from all others, while PC 2 separates B2 and F10 from A8/A10 and B7/G10/H10, which cluster together (Fig. 2f, File S9).

Taken together, the phylogeny, AAI, ANI, AF and Pfam clustering indicate that the seven single-cell Bodo assemblies form three clades: clade A8/A10, clade B7/F10 and clade B2/G10/H10. All three clades are potentially novel Bodo species, and all appear equally distant from B. saltans Lake Konstanz.

All Bodo spp. harbour Holosporales bacterial endosymbionts

Previous studies showed that B. saltans Lake Konstanz is dependent on an endosymbiotic alphaproteobacterium from the order Holosporales, ‘Ca. B. vickermanii’ [20]. We went back to our binned raw genome assemblies and were able to identify a single bin in five of the genomes, 2 bins in B2 and 4 bins in B7 that were >80% complete according to CheckM (File S1). CheckM analysis of these genomes showed that all were at least 89% complete, and all but one had contamination levels <5% (Table 3).

Finally, we compared the number of shared orthogroups between the Holosporales genomes and ‘Ca. B. vickermanii’ (Fig. 3c). 23.2% of orthogroups are shared by all genomes. The genomes that are closest in the phylogeny, A8/10 and B2/G10/H10, share the highest proportion of orthogroups at 52.6%. In summary, B. saltans and each novel Bodo clade presented in this paper harbour a unique species of Holosporales endosymbiont. These seven endosymbionts split into two genera (genus ‘Ca. Bodocaedibacter’ and a novel genus) that appear congruent with the phylogeny of the host and further support the conclusion that these are three novel Bodo species.

Functional differences between the Holosporales genomes

We compared the secretion systems and metabolic modules present in the bacterial genomes and compared them to ‘Ca. B. vickermanii’ (Fig. 4). Genus ‘Ca. Bodocaedibacter’ (B7/F10) and the novel genus described in this study (A8/A10 and B2/G10/H10) encode a SEC-SRP and a type VI secretion system [20]. In addition, the novel genus encodes two out of the four proteins of the TAT secretion system, while a partial type IV conjugal transfer pilus assembly protein system is sporadically distributed across the taxa.

All seven genomes have a partially complete (≥66% complete) pathway for lysine biosynthesis, which is absent in all seven hosts (≤22% complete) (Fig. 4). In addition, the novel genus encodes several metabolic pathways absent in the genus ‘Ca. Bodocaedibacter’, notably including haem biosynthesis (Fig. 4). Heme is an essential nutrient, and to date, no complete haem pathway has been described for a Kinetoplastea member, which either requires an exogenous source of haem or contains endosymbiotic bacteria that produce it [58]. Fig. 4 only shows KEGG modules that were at least 50% complete in one or more genomes. A full list of all the KEGG modules, including those at less than 50% completeness, is presented in File S12.

Midha et al. [20] reported that ‘Ca. B. vickermanii’ encodes three putative polymorphic toxin/antitoxin systems, which may be responsible for its host’s dependency [20]. Polymorphic toxin/antitoxin systems are typically composed of a large multi-domain protein containing an N-terminal secretion signal followed by a toxin domain, a protective immunity protein and multiple cassettes encoding alternative toxic domains and associated immunity proteins [21]. The toxin genes can contain a homologous repeat region that enables recombination between the full-length toxin gene and the alternative toxins. We identified three genes belonging to one putative toxin/antitoxin system in both B7 and F10, but none in the other five genomes from the novel genus. These genes show some of the characteristics of a polymorphic toxin/antitoxin system including a homologous region. However, the putative large multidomain protein in B7 does not contain an N-terminal signal peptide, and the one putative orphan module in both B7 and F10 contains a toxin but no cognate antitoxin (Fig. S1). Therefore, the locus’s identity and function as a polymorphic toxin/antitoxin system are tentative. In addition, the incompleteness of the single-cell genomes presented here raises the possibility that key proteins and/or pathways are missing from these assemblies due to undersampling.

Discussion

In this study, we show that single-cell sequencing reveals unexpected levels of diversity among uncultured candidate Bodo spp. cells from a single environmental sample. Metabarcoding, particularly of SSU rDNA, and metagenomics have become the norm for assessing the diversity of microbial eukaryotes in environmental samples. For protists, traditionally, these methods involve clustering reads and/or amplicons into operational taxonomic units (OTUs) based on similarity thresholds, usually at around 97–99% identity, or into amplicon sequence variants produced by the DADA2 pipeline [60]. The seven single cells presented here all have a pairwise SSU rDNA identity greater than 99% and would, therefore, have been clustered into one OTU in some metabarcoding pipelines. Instead, by generating single-cell genomes, we show that these seven cells form three distinct clades, with each clade potentially representing a novel species. ANI has been used to compare distances within and between closely-related species of eukaryotic microbes, including microsporidia, yeast and some protists, with an interspecies ANI cutoff of 95% considered appropriate [6163]. Here, we found that genomes within each of the three putative Bodo species have an ANI>98%, supporting the conclusion that each clade represents a Bodo species. However, ANI is not useful when comparing more dissimilar genomes, where AAI becomes a more useful metric [6465]. While more commonly used to compare prokaryotic genomes, AAI has also been applied to eukaryotes. A comparison of 1,196 human and mouse protein sequences showed an AAI of 85% [66], while recent studies in fungal lineages found AAI values for members of the same species were often >97% and demonstrated an estimated genus boundary threshold of ~70–75% AAI for the family Hypoxylaceae (Ascomycota) [6768]. While further work is needed to explore appropriate species and genus cutoffs for AAI and ANI within and between protist lineages, our results showing that AAI within each Bodo species was >97% suggest that the species thresholds of 95–98% used in bacteria and fungi may be appropriate here too.

All three novel Bodo species presented here are more similar by SSU rDNA identity to B. edax ATCC strain 30903 than they are to B. saltans Lake Konstanz. However, comparing sequences from the longer rRNA operon showed that the sequence similarity is not maintained, suggesting that the single cells represent a novel Bodo species. Callahan et al. [6] mention that B. edax contains endosymbiotic bacteria and differs morphologically from B. saltans by lacking mastigonemes on the anterior flagellum [6], yet several authors have proposed that B. edax be reclassified as an isolate of B. saltans [311]. Given the extensive genetic diversity observed among the Bodo spp. presented in this paper, more work is needed to determine if B. edax is a true species. Importantly, this study suggests that the SSU rDNA locus is insufficient for species delimitation within the genus Bodo, as it does not reflect the genomic diversity within the group.

Further supporting our conclusion that the three Bodo clades represent three species is the finding that each putative species harbours a unique species of Holosporales bacterium. The three novel bacterial species split into two genera, with one species belonging to the genus ‘Ca. Bodocaedibacter vickermanii’ and the other two species forming a novel genus. The phylogeny of the host Bodo spp. appears congruent with the phylogeny of the endosymbionts; however, more sampling covering greater taxonomic distribution is needed to test if this congruence indicates co-evolution or is the result of other processes [6970]. Infectivity has been demonstrated in the Holosporaceae family of Holosporales, endosymbionts of various Paramecium ciliates [1871], and some Holosporales have demonstrated the ability to invade novel hosts experimentally [7273]. However, the distribution and genetic differences observed between the Holosporales genomes presented here suggest that these endosymbionts are and have been associated with their respective hosts for a long period.

The nature of the symbioses between Holosporales bacteria and their hosts is complex and varied [15]. Midha et al. [20] hypothesized that B. saltans is dependent on its endosymbiont due to the presence of three putative addiction polymorphic toxin/antitoxin systems encoded in the ‘Ca. Bodocaedibacter vickermanii’ genome [20]. These systems typically encode a long-lived toxin molecule alongside an antitoxin with a shorter half-life, so that if the system is lost, the toxin is activated and becomes lethal. We found evidence for a putative toxin/antitoxin system in the two genomes belonging to the genus ‘Ca. Bodocaedibacter vickermanii’, but not in five genomes in the novel genus. However, the identity and/or functionality of the toxin/antitoxin systems described in this study is speculative, as the proteins and loci differ from those in ‘Ca. Bodocaedibacter vickermanii’ by encoding fewer orphan modules. The lack of a similar system in the genomes of the novel Holosporales genus presented in this study suggests that alternative processes may be driving this symbiosis. All kinetoplasts, including B. saltans, lack the biosynthetic pathway required for haem biosynthesis [5974]. The Kinetoplastids Angomonas and Strigomonas harbour endosymbiotic beta-proteobacteria which provide their hosts with nutrients including haem, essential amino acids and vitamins [5875 76]. We found a pathway for haem biosynthesis (≥70% complete) in the novel Holosporales genus and a pathway for lysine biosynthesis (≥66% complete) in all the Bodo Holosporales genomes. Therefore, the two Holosporales genera presented here could demonstrate two alternative symbiotic strategies, with the genus ‘Ca. Bodocaedibacter vickermanii’ becoming adapted to its host through the toxin/antitoxin system, while the novel genus is possibly a source of haem and/or other essential nutrients. All seven Holosporales genomes presented in this paper encode a type VI secretion system, conserved throughout Holosporales and hypothesized to play a role in host–endosymbiont interactions [15].

Previous studies have shown that B. saltans shares the majority of its metabolic pathways with the parasitic lineages Trypanosoma and Leishmania [1359]. Our results show that the metabolic capacity of B. saltans is shared with the novel Bodo spp. presented here. We also show that the Bodo genus contains an abundance of unexplored and uncategorized protein diversity. The lineage contains far more unique orthogroups and proteins than the parasitic lineages. However, as most of these protein sequences have no known homology to functional domains in databases such as UniProt and KEGG, the functional implications of this unique gene repertoire remain unknown.

Most of our knowledge of protist genomic diversity comes from studies of species that are culturable, with few examples of genomes from uncultured protists. However, we know that environmental samples contain a wealth of underexplored diversity. This study uses single-cell sequencing and comparative genomics to show that seven Bodo spp. cells from a single environmental sample represent three potentially novel Bodo species. Our work also recovers the genome for a novel species of bacterial endosymbiont associated with each Bodo species. Comparing these data, we demonstrate the varied nature of these symbioses. This work shows that single-cell sequencing is a powerful method for exploring the diversity of uncultured protists and their cobionts.
