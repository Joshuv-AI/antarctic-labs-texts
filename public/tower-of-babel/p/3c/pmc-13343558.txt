Sex and gender reporting in Australian cancer clinical trials: are we doing enough?
Authors: Vikneswary Batumalai, Gowri Shivasabesan, Cheryl Carcel, Carole A Harris, Sue Haupt, Eng-Siew Koh, Angela Liao, Shalini K Vinod, Mei Ling Yap
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSex and gender influence cancer incidence, treatment response, and outcomes, yet reporting of these variables remains inconsistent in clinical research. Australia represents a distinct research governance context, where sex and gender considerations are recommended but not systematically embedded in regulatory oversight, and where no national evaluations of reporting in cancer trials exist. By examining Australian cancer randomised controlled trials (RCTs) published from 2014-2024, this study addresses a critical evidence gap and extends prior international assessments by providing the first national-level analysis of reporting practices in relation to the Sex and Gender Equity in Research (SAGER) guidelines.MethodsA review of RCTs conducted in Australia or led by Australian investigators and published between January 2014 and October 2024 was undertaken. Studies were assessed for adherence to key SAGER criteria, including correct use of terminology, reporting of sex/gender in abstracts and tables, consideration in study design, inclusion of sex/gender-based analyses, and discussion of implications. For temporal comparisons, the introduction of the SAGER guidelines in 2016 was used as the cut‑off, with trials grouped as pre‑SAGER (2014–2016) and post‑SAGER (2017–2024).ResultsOf 128 eligible studies, none reported how sex or gender was defined. Overall, 50% used appropriate terminology, 14% reported sex/gender in abstracts, 16% considered these variables in design, 28% conducted sex/gender-based analyses, and 11% discussed implications. Post-SAGER, there was an increase in appropriate terminology use (from 39% to 54%), sex/gender breakdown in abstracts (from 9% to 16%), and demographic tables with separate rows for males and females (from 41% to 69%). However, these tables typically provided only overall sex counts; only 10% of studies presented a complete sex/gender breakdown across all baseline characteristics. References to sex/gender relevance in introductions declined (12% to 2%), consideration in study design decreased (21% to 14%), and sex/gender-based analyses dropped (44% to 22%).ConclusionSex and gender reporting in Australian cancer RCTs remains inconsistent and suboptimal. While modest gains were achieved post-SAGER, stronger editorial oversight, funding mandates, and researcher education are needed to ensure equitable, rigorous, and generalisable cancer research.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s41073-026-00218-x.Keywords: Sex and gender reporting, SAGER guidelines, Equity in research

Background

Sex and gender are fundamental variables in health and medical research, shaping a wide range of factors including cancer incidence, progression, treatment efficacy, and outcomes [1]. Sex refers to a set of biological attributes in humans and animals that are associated with physical and physiological features including chromosomes, gene expression, hormone function and reproductive/sexual anatomy [2]. Sex is usually categorised as female or male, although there is variation in the biological attributes that constitute sex and how those attributes are expressed. Gender refers to the socially constructed roles, behaviours and identities of female, male and gender-diverse people [2]. It influences how people perceive themselves and each other, how they behave and interact and the distribution of power and resources in society. Biological sex influences tumour biology, pharmacodynamics, response to treatments, and adverse event profiles, while gender-related factors such as lifestyle choices, health-seeking behaviours, caregiver roles, and access to care can significantly affect cancer, diagnoses, treatment, and survivorship experiences [3]. Conflating sex and gender, or failing to report gender at all, is methodologically problematic because it obscures whether observed differences arise from biological mechanisms or social and structural factors. This reduces the accuracy of subgroup analyses, limits reproducibility, and constrains the interpretability of findings. As sex and gender influence outcomes, there is both a scientific and ethical obligation to clearly report participant sex and gender and to present research findings accordingly [4]. Despite the recognised importance of sex and gender, reporting remains inconsistent in clinical research, limiting the generalisability and real-world relevance of study findings across diverse populations and contributing to inequities in care and outcomes [5].

Persistent barriers to systematic implementation of sex- and gender-based analyses have been documented across multiple disciplines. For example, a systematic review reported that numerous COVID-19 studies enrolled disproportionate numbers of male and female, yet sex and gender differences were seldom examined in the analysis of the data, lack of sex- or gender-based analyses was rarely justified, and gender-related implications were almost never discussed [6]. Similar patterns have been observed across several medical specialties including neurology and cardiology, where improvements have been largely limited to terminology rather than meaningful analytical integration [7, 8]. Within this global landscape, the Australian context warrants focused examination. Cancer is a highly relevant field for sex‑ and gender‑sensitive research due to well‑established differences in incidence, treatment toxicity, outcomes, and survivorship experiences, yet no national‑level evaluation has assessed sex and gender reporting in Australian cancer randomised controlled trials (RCTs).

In response to recognised shortcomings in the field, the Sex and Gender Equity in Research (SAGER) guidelines were introduced in 2016 to improve the reporting of sex and gender in research publications [2, 9]. These guidelines provide a structured framework for the appropriate use of sex and gender terminology and for incorporating sex and gender considerations in study design, data analysis, and interpretation. Endorsed by several academic journals and funding agencies, the SAGER guidelines aim to promote transparency and equity in biomedical research. However, their implementation across clinical trials remains inconsistent, and the extent to which these guidelines have influenced research reporting practices over time in the Australian context remains unknown.

Given the complexity of cancer biology and treatment, and the clear sex- and gender-specific variations in incidence, outcomes, and adverse effects, rigorous and transparent reporting is essential. The aim of this study was to assess reporting practices in Australian cancer clinical trials in relation to the SAGER guidelines for sex and gender. We also examined whether these practices have changed over time by comparing trials published before and after the introduction of the guidelines in 2016.

Methods

Search strategy

This study draws on a broader review of Australian RCTs spanning all stages of the cancer care continuum (prevention, screening, diagnosis, treatment, supportive care, survivorship, and palliative care). A systematic search of Embase was conducted to identify peer-reviewed articles reporting on RCTs in cancer control and cancer care, published between 1 January 2014 and 31 October 2024. Embase was selected as the sole database because of its broad coverage of biomedical and oncology literature and its strong indexing of randomized controlled trials, which was considered appropriate for identifying Australian-led cancer studies across the cancer care continuum. The search strategy included terms related to cancer, RCTs, and Australian affiliations, and was limited to studies involving adults aged 18 years or older (Supplementary Material 1).

Selection criteria

The initial screening process identified RCTs of any phase relating to adult cancer care across the cancer care continuum, conducted in Australia or led by an Australian investigator or coordinating institution in the case of international trials. For the purposes of this review, Australian leadership in international trials was defined as the first author or the corresponding author being affiliated with an Australian institution. Only articles published in English-language peer-reviewed journals were included. Protocols and conference abstracts were excluded. Secondary analyses (e.g., subgroup, exploratory, or follow-up analyses from RCTs) were included only if the original trial publication fell outside the 2014–2024 date range or was otherwise unavailable. Trials in which the intervention did not involve cancer participants (e.g., trials focused on clinicians or practice‑change behaviours without patient participation) were excluded. The screening process involved an initial review of titles, abstracts and full-text articles by two researchers [VB and GS] based on the inclusion and exclusion criteria, including verification of RCT status. Any discrepancies in selection were resolved through discussion between the researchers. From this larger pool, a second round of screening was undertaken specifically for the present analysis, which focused on evaluating sex and gender reporting. In this step, trials investigating cancers that predominantly or exclusively affect one sex such as breast, cervical, ovarian, uterine/endometrial, prostate, testicular, and penile cancers were excluded as an analytical decision to improve comparability across studies when assessing adherence to the SAGER guidelines. This exclusion was not based on the assumption that sex or gender considerations are irrelevant in these cancers, rather, some SAGER domains may not be directly comparable between trials of sex-specific cancers and those enrolling populations in whom sex composition would be expected to vary. Sex and gender remain important considerations in sex-specific cancers, including breast cancer in men and transgender and gender-diverse populations.

Data extraction

Key data extracted included author name, year of publication, journal, cancer type, stage of the cancer care continuum, study location, and sample size. Each included study was assessed by two reviewers using the SAGER checklist for studies involving human participants [9] (Supplementary Material 2). The assessment included items addressing sex and gender terminology, reporting of participant sex/gender, consideration of sex/gender in study design and analysis, disaggregation of results, and interpretation of findings from a sex/gender perspective. Terminology use was assessed based on consistency within each manuscript and alignment with the SAGER guidelines. A decision framework was applied to infer terminology classifications, recognising that “appropriate” use reflects an analytical judgement. Use was classified as appropriate when “sex” referred to biological categories (e.g. male/female) and “gender” referred to identity or social constructs (e.g. woman/man, gender identity, or gendered roles). Terminology use was considered interchangeable when the terms “sex” and “gender” were used synonymously to describe binary biological categories. For example, a study that reported participant numbers as “sex: male/female” was classified as using appropriate terminology, whereas a study that reported “gender: male/female” but appeared to refer only to biological categories was classified as using the terms interchangeably. In cases where terminology use was unclear or inconsistent, the classification was based on the predominant context of use across the manuscript, and ambiguous cases were resolved through reviewer discussion to reach consensus. In addition to binary sex/gender reporting, we recorded whether any studies reported participant categories beyond male/female, including non-binary or other gender-diverse categories, where these were collected and presented. For this review, consideration of sex/gender in study design referred to any explicit description that sex or gender was taken into account during study planning, such as through recruitment strategies, eligibility criteria, or stratified randomisation. Sex- or gender-based analyses referred to any analysis in which results were examined according to sex or gender, including sex/gender-stratified analyses, subgroup analyses, or multivariable models including sex/gender as a covariate. Items that were not applicable to this study were excluded from analysis. Specifically, item 2 (title specifies sex/gender of participants if only one included) and item 3a (abstract specifies sex/gender of participants if only one included) were excluded, as studies involving sex-specific cancers had already been removed during screening. Item 6f, which relates to the analysis of epidemiological exposures, was also excluded, as the current study focuses only on RCTs. To examine trends over time, studies were grouped into two time periods: pre-SAGER (2014–2016) and post-SAGER (2017–2024), corresponding to the period before and after the publication of the SAGER guidelines in 2016. Publication year was used as a proxy for potential exposure to the guidelines, recognising that this may not reflect when trials were designed, initiated, or analysed. Comparisons between the two periods were intended to be descriptive only, to explore temporal patterns in reporting practices. For the post-SAGER analysis, journal endorsement of the SAGER guidelines was determined by reviewing each journal’s publicly available author instructions or editorial policy. Journals were classified according to whether the SAGER guidelines were endorsed in these policies; no distinction was made between recommendation and requirement.

Results

Study profile and distribution

The initial Embase search identified 629 articles (Figure 1). Following title and abstract screening, 273 articles were selected for second round of screening. The second screening phase, which excluded trials of cancers that predominantly or exclusively affect one sex, resulted in 165 studies being eligible for in-depth review. Of these, 128 studies met all inclusion criteria and were included in the final analysis. A total of 34 studies (27%) were published between 2014 and 2016, 52 studies (41%) between 2017 and 2020, and 42 studies (33%) between 2021 and 2024. The studies were published across 68 different journals, with the most frequently used journals being Supportive Care in Cancer (n=15, 12%), BMC Cancer (n=7, 5%), and Journal of Clinical Oncology (n=6, 5%). A summary of the included studies by cancer type, stage of the cancer care continuum, and trial location is presented in Table 1. The most common cancer types investigated were colorectal (n=26, 20%), skin including melanoma (n=18, 14%), and lung (n=14, 11%). A total of 47 studies (37%) were not limited to a single tumour type and included participants with multiple cancer types. The trials spanned various stages of the cancer care continuum, with the highest number focused on supportive care (n=37, 29%) and treatment (n=35, 27%), followed by prevention (n=16, 13%). About one-quarter of studies (n=32, 25%) were conducted Australia-wide, followed by Queensland (n=22, 17%), Victoria (n=20, 16%), and New South Wales (n=19, 15%). A total of 22 trials (17%) were part of international studies led or co-led by Australian investigators. The cumulative sample size across the 128 trials was 37,622 participants. One palliative care study did not report any sex or gender breakdown of its study population, while another study investigating skin cancer screening enrolled only male participants without providing a rationale for the exclusion of females. Across all trials, the overall participant distribution was 54% male and 46% female.

Sex/gender reporting practices

Reporting practices in relation to the SAGER guidelines varied across the 128 included trials (Table 2). Of these, 126 trials reported sex and/or gender, with half (63/126, 50%) using the terms sex and/or gender appropriately (as interpreted by the researchers). Two studies did not use either term. Instead, one reported only the number of male participants, while the other reported only the number of female participants. Among the 126 studies that used the terms sex and/or gender, 50% (n=63) used the term sex exclusively, 29% (n=36) used gender only, and 11% (n=14) used the terms interchangeably. Only 18/128 studies (14%) included a sex/gender breakdown of the study populations in the abstract.

In the introduction sections, references to prior studies addressing sex/gender differences were uncommon (6/128, 5%), and only 5% (6/128) acknowledged sex/gender as a potential effect modifier. In the methods section, none of the studies provided information on how sex or gender was defined (e.g., self-report, medical records, biological definition, sex recorded at birth, or legal definition). Only 20 studies (16%) described how sex or gender was considered in the study design. For example, 13 studies reported ensuring adequate representation of male and female participants through stratified randomisation.

In the results sections, only 13 studies (10%) reported a complete sex/gender breakdown of the study population, meaning that baseline characteristics were comprehensively disaggregated by sex/gender, including key characteristics such as age or smoking history. Seventy-nine studies (62%) included separate rows for male and female participants in the patient demographic table (typically Table 1 in the article), but in most cases this reflected only the overall sex distribution of the sample rather than a full breakdown of other participant characteristics by sex/gender. Results were disaggregated by sex/gender in 18 studies (14%), and sex- or gender-based analyses regardless of outcome were reported in 36 studies (28%). No included studies reported participant categories beyond male/female, such as non-binary or other gender-diverse categories.

Twenty-five studies reported only male/men figures, with no corresponding female/women data, while 19 studies reported only female/women figures, omitting male/men data. A further 5 studies did not include sex/gender information in any table but mentioned it in the text. In the discussion sections, only 14 studies (11%) reflected on the potential implications of sex or gender in the interpretation or generalisability of the findings. No studies offered a rationale for the absence of sex/gender analysis or discussed the implications of this omission.

Changes in sex/gender reporting practices pre- and post-SAGER

Overall, sex and gender reporting practices showed mixed trends following the introduction of the SAGER guidelines (Figure 2). There was a modest improvement in the appropriate use of sex/gender terminology, increasing from 39% pre-SAGER to 54% post-SAGER. Reporting of sex/gender breakdowns in abstracts also improved slightly, from 9% to 16%, and a notable increase in the inclusion of separate rows for male and female participants in the patient demographic tables (41% to 69%). In contrast, several key reporting practices declined over time. Citations of prior sex/gender research and mention of sex/gender as a relevant variable in the introduction dropped from 12% to 2%. Consideration of sex/gender in study design declined slightly (21% to 14%), while the proportion of studies presenting data disaggregated by sex/gender fell from 21% to 12%. Similarly, the reporting of sex- or gender-based analyses regardless of outcome decreased from 44% to 22%. The remaining SAGER reporting domains showed no notable change across the two time periods.

Impact of SAGER guidelines

A total of 94 studies were published across 56 journals post-SAGER (2017–2024). Of these journals, 34/56 (61%) endorsed the SAGER guidelines for sex/gender reporting in their publication policies, accounting for 51/94 (54%) studies, while the remaining 22/56 (39%) journals did not, accounting for 43/94 (46%) studies. Discussion

This study assessed sex and gender reporting practices in Australian clinical trials related to cancer care and examined whether these practices changed following the introduction of the SAGER guidelines in 2016. Despite growing recognition of the importance of sex and gender in biomedical research, our findings reveal that adherence to sex and gender reporting remains inconsistent and, in many aspects, suboptimal. These findings echo prior analyses across various health research fields, which have similarly found that sex and gender are often overlooked or poorly reported [10–12].

Encouragingly, there was a modest improvement in the appropriate use of sex/gender terminology over time, as well as a marked increase in the inclusion of separate male and female categories in patient demographic tables. Reporting of sex/gender breakdowns in abstracts also improved slightly. Although inclusion of sex/gender information in the abstract may appear to be a minor reporting element, it is important because the abstract is often the first, and sometimes only, part of a paper accessed by clinicians, researchers, and evidence reviewers. Reporting this information in the abstract provides a concise summary of the study population to whom the findings apply and supports interpretation of the applicability and generalisability of the results.

However, several key domains of the SAGER checklist showed a decline post-guidelines, including consideration of sex/gender in study design, disaggregation of results, and sex- or gender-based analyses. These findings align with prior literature highlighting that while awareness of sex and gender issues may be increasing, practical implementation remains limited and inconsistently applied [13, 14]. This disconnect raises concerns about the depth of engagement with equity-focused guidelines like SAGER and suggests that their uptake may be more superficial than substantive. These gaps matter for both efficacy and safety. On the efficacy side, clinical studies and meta‑analyses across cancer types have identified sex‑related differences in treatment effectiveness and survival outcomes including for immunotherapy, chemotherapy, targeted therapy, and radiotherapy, underscoring the need for consistent sex‑disaggregated reporting to judge generalisability [15]. On the safety side, reviews of adverse events by sex indicate higher risks of severe treatment‑related adverse events for women across modalities, particularly with immunotherapy, reinforcing the need to present adverse events by sex and to justify dosing and monitoring approaches [16]. In this context, a proportionate and realistic approach to sex/gender reporting in RCTs, particularly where trials are not powered for subgroup analyses, includes at minimum: stating how sex and/or gender were ascertained; reporting the sex/gender composition in the abstract; presenting key baseline variables and primary outcomes descriptively disaggregated by sex/gender; and briefly justifying when inferential sex/gender analyses are not undertaken.

The observed post-SAGER decline in several reporting domains may reflect broader implementation challenges rather than reduced relevance of sex and gender considerations. These include uneven uptake of reporting guidelines beyond surface-level elements, limited statistical power for sex- or gender-stratified analyses in many trials, insufficient practical guidance for researchers, and inconsistent enforcement of reporting expectations during peer review. An additional consideration is the long lead time associated with clinical trial design and conduct. Many studies published in the early post-SAGER period (2017–2024) were likely conceived and designed before the release of the SAGER guidelines in 2016, limiting opportunities to incorporate sex and gender considerations into study design, data collection, and planned analyses. This temporal lag may partly explain the persistence of suboptimal adherence to several SAGER domains in the post-guideline era. However, the pre/post patterns we observed should be considered alongside the limitations of the SAGER guidelines. SAGER is primarily a reporting framework and provides limited operational guidance for design, data collection, or analysis. Accordingly, improvements in terminology may not translate into deeper methodological integration without complementary protocol‑level requirements.

None of the studies defined how sex or gender was determined (e.g. self-report, medical records). This lack of clarity is a critical omission, particularly in RCTs where data are collected prospectively and the method of ascertainment should be straightforward to document. In practice, RCT case report forms are almost always completed by clinicians, meaning that the recorded variable is likely to reflect clinician interpretation rather than participant self-identification. This raises important questions about accuracy and inclusivity in trial reporting. Consideration could be given to participant self-completion of selected demographic fields, particularly those relating to sex, gender, and other sensitive characteristics.

The lack of reflection on sex and gender in the interpretation of results was another notable gap. Only 11% of studies discussed the potential implications of sex or gender in the context of generalisability or differential treatment effects. While it may be assumed that such discussion would occur only when sex-specific differences are found, the SAGER guidelines state that “the implications of sex and gender for the interpretation of study results should be elaborated, including the extent to which the findings can be generalized to all sexes and genders in a population.” Furthermore, “if no sex and gender-based analyses have been performed, authors should indicate the reasons … and discuss whether such analyses could have affected the results”. On this basis, the failure to comment on sex/gender implications regardless of whether formal subgroup analyses were conducted reflects suboptimal adherence to SAGER standards and limits the interpretive value and inclusivity of trial findings. Structural and methodological challenges, including limited sample sizes and lack of training or institutional guidance, may partly contribute to this pattern.

Australia continues to lag behind international standards in its policy approach to sex and gender in health research. A 2017 analysis found that eight out of the top ten Australian national funding agencies lacked policies on sex- and gender-specific reporting, highlighting systemic gaps in research governance [17]. In response to international efforts to improve sex and gender inclusion, some journals have adopted or endorsed guidelines such as the SAGER recommendations or the International Committee of Medical Journal Editors (ICMJE) [18] guidelines. Our study found that the presence of journal policies recommending or requiring the SAGER guidelines did not strongly influence reporting practices. Previous studies have similarly shown that even when journals endorse high-level guidance such as the ICMJE recommendations, this does not necessarily result in improved reporting of sex and gender in published manuscripts [10, 11]. This suggests that stronger editorial compliance mechanisms and clearer expectations during manuscript submission and peer review may be required to effect change.

Improving adherence to reporting guidelines also requires that researchers are equipped with the necessary knowledge and skills to design studies that appropriately account for sex and gender, and to report and analyse results accordingly [10]. Research institutions including universities, medical research institutes, and peak bodies can play a pivotal role by embedding education and support structures into research training. Broader engagement across the research sector is essential to build understanding of how sex and gender influence health, and to ensure these considerations are integrated from study conception through to translation and implementation.

We acknowledge several limitations of this study. First, our assessment of sex and gender terminology relied on inferred usage, as none of the included studies explicitly defined how these terms were determined. Most studies used the term “sex” to describe binary categories of male and female, consistent with conventional medical usage. However, several studies used “gender” in contexts that appeared to refer to biological sex, suggesting inconsistent or incorrect terminology. The absence of explicit definitions limits the certainty of our assessments, introduces an element of subjectivity, and may have resulted in misclassification where author intent was unclear. This limitation reflects a broader issue within the literature, namely the persistent conflation or interchangeable use of sex and gender, which undermines scientific clarity and inclusivity. Second, the relatively small number of studies classified under certain cancer types, such as brain and haematological malignancies, reflects our study categorisation and exclusion criteria rather than a lack of trial activity in these disease areas. Many trials involving these cancers were conducted across multiple tumour types and were therefore grouped under the “multiple” category. In addition, some multi-tumour trials that included haematological malignancies alongside cancers predominantly affecting one sex (e.g. breast cancer) were excluded by design. Third, the search was limited to a single database (Embase), and it is possible that some eligible studies indexed exclusively in other databases were not captured. Although RCT status was assessed during screening, reliance on database indexing may have limited the identification of all eligible trials meeting the search criteria, as trial design is not always consistently or accurately indexed. Fourth, defining Australian‑led trials based on first or corresponding author affiliation may not fully capture trial leadership or governance in large multicentre RCTs, potentially resulting in misclassification of some internationally led or collaboratively governed trials. Finally, our analysis is limited to what was reported in published trial manuscripts and does not necessarily reflect whether sex and/or gender were collected, considered, or analysed at the trial level but not reported.

Future research in cancer should examine how sex and/or gender are collected at the trial level, beyond what appears in publications. This includes auditing cancer trial protocols and case‑report forms. Registry‑based analyses (e.g. clinicaltrials.gov) can also be used to evaluate protocol‑to‑publication concordance. Prior evidence from cross-disciplinary comparisons of protocols and corresponding articles shows that sex and gender terms are often undefined or used imprecisely, highlighting the need to evaluate data‑collection instruments and definitions [19].

Conclusion

While there have been modest improvements in the use of sex/gender terminology and demographic reporting, fundamental aspects of study design, analysis, and interpretation remain inconsistently addressed in Australian clinical trials related to cancer care. These findings reinforce the need for stronger implementation of sex and gender reporting guidelines and the systematic integration of sex and gender considerations across all stages of the research process. Such efforts are essential to improve research rigor and equity, scientific integrity, and the applicability of findings to diverse populations.
