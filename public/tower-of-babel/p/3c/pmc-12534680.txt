Retinal and Choroidal Alterations in Heart Failure: A Systematic Review and Meta-analysis of OCT and OCT-A Findings with Emphasis on HFrEF
Authors: Kia Bayat, Parisa Pooyan, Jay Chhablani, Mozhgan Rezaei Kanavi, Hamid Ahmadieh
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
IntroductionRetina and choroid, given their high metabolic demands, are vulnerable to ischemic injury during the hemodynamic instability associated with heart failure (HF). Despite this, posterior segment ocular changes in HF remain understudied. This systematic review and meta-analysis consolidates optical coherence tomography (OCT) and OCT angiography (OCT-A) findings in HF.MethodsWe conducted a comprehensive search of Web of Science, PubMed/MEDLINE, and EMBASE on March 15, 2025. A random-effects meta-analysis of six studies was performed using Hedges’ g to estimate effect sizes, with six additional studies included for qualitative synthesis (total n = 1139 eyes). The meta-analysis was limited to studies on heart failure with reduced ejection fraction (HFrEF), as this was the only heart failure subtype with sufficient data for quantitative analysis.ResultsKey findings revealed significant reductions in parafoveal superficial capillary plexus vessel density (SCP VD) (Hedges’ g = − 0.42, 95% CI − 0.78 to − 0.05, p = 0.03) and subfoveal choroidal thickness (Hedges’ g = − 0.75, 95% CI − 1.31 to − 0.18, p = 0.01) in patients with HFrEF. Furthermore, severity-dependent thinning of retinal nerve fiber layer and significant correlations between whole macular vessel density and both left ventricular EF and New York Heart Association functional class emphasize the potential of these parameters in stratifying disease severity. Additionally, one study revealed thinning of ganglion cell–inner plexiform layer in patients with HF with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF). Those with acute decompensated HF showed macular VD attenuation in superficial layers and decreased subfoveal choroidal thickness. Pediatric cases demonstrated central macular thinning, selective decreases in SCP VD, and choroidal thinning.ConclusionsCollectively, OCT and OCT-A metrics provide useful information regarding systemic microvascular dysfunction, suggesting potential for disease detection and improved risk stratification. However, it is important to note that these findings are based on observational studies, which limits the ability to draw definitive causal conclusions. This highlights the need for prospective, longitudinal research to establish temporal relationships and validate these observations.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s40123-025-01238-4.

Key Summary Points

Why carry out this study? Heart failure is a major global health concern with high prevalence, imposing significant morbidity and economic costs. Noninvasive biomarkers of systemic microvascular involvement could enhance risk stratification, guide treatment, and help alleviate this burden.Optical coherence tomography (OCT) and OCT-angiography (OCT-A) enable noninvasive evaluation of the retina and choroid. However, existing studies in heart failure are heterogeneous, and no comprehensive synthesis has yet established consistent ocular signatures or their clinical relevance. What was learned from the study? Patients with heart failure exhibit characteristic retinal and choroidal alterations, including parafoveal superficial capillary plexus vessel density reduction and subfoveal choroidal thinning in heart failure with reduced ejection fraction (HFrEF); ganglion cell–inner plexiform layer thinning in preserved ejection fraction; macular superficial vessel density reduction along with subfoveal choroidal thinning in acute decompensated heart failure; and central macular thinning with selective superficial capillary plexus vessel loss and choroidal thinning in pediatric cases.In HFrEF, retinal nerve fiber layer thickness and whole macular vessel density correlate with disease severity.OCT and OCT-A metrics provide clinically relevant information regarding systemic microvascular dysfunction in heart failure and have potential utility for disease detection and improved risk stratification.

Introduction

Heart failure (HF) is a progressive clinical syndrome characterized by systemic hypoperfusion and multiorgan dysfunction due to impaired cardiac output and/or elevated filling pressures. Among the organs affected by this microcirculatory disturbance, the eye, particularly the retina and choroid, serves as a unique window into systemic vascular health [1, 2]. The retinal circulation, supplied by both the central retinal artery and the choroidal vasculature, is exquisitely sensitive to perfusion deficits and endothelial dysfunction [3]. Given its exceptionally high metabolic demand, the retina is especially vulnerable to ischemic injury during hemodynamic instability in HF [4].

In HF, systemic fluctuations in blood flow, including to the retina and choroid, can lead to intermittent hypoxia and nutrient deprivation. Subsequent reperfusion, while necessary for oxygen delivery and tissue survival, paradoxically exacerbates damage through ischemia–reperfusion injury, ultimately triggering apoptotic cell death in retinal neurons and vasculature [5, 6]. These mechanisms may underlie the structural and microvascular alterations of retina and choroid observed in patients with HF.

Advances in optical coherence tomography (OCT) and OCT angiography (OCT-A) have revolutionized in-depth analysis of retinal and choroidal integrity, enabling detailed evaluation of layer thickness, microvascular density, and perfusion patterns [7–10].

Growing evidence suggests that these changes correlate with HF severity and may serve as prognostic biomarkers [6, 11]. Notably, both HF with reduced ejection fraction (HFrEF) and preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF) exhibit ocular microvascular remodeling, though their distinct hemodynamic profiles may differentially affect posterior segment morphology [12, 13]. While most studies focus on chronic HF, acute decompensated HF also induces transient but detectable alterations [14]. Pediatric HF, though less studied, demonstrates similar microvascular perturbations, highlighting the systemic nature of HF-related endothelial dysfunction [4, 5, 15].

Although posterior segment alterations have been well documented in conditions affecting systemic vascular health like coronary artery disease [16–18], diabetes [19], and hypertension [20], HF-specific involvement remains understudied. Retinal and choroidal imaging may offer a noninvasive, accessible approach to aid in early HF detection, disease stratification, and progression monitoring. This systematic review synthesizes current evidence on posterior segment structural and microvascular changes in HF, primarily focusing on adults with chronic HFrEF compared to healthy controls. Additionally, we examine findings in HFpEF, acute HF, and pediatric populations, and discuss the potential clinical utility of ocular imaging in HF management.

Methods

This systematic review was designed and executed following the established Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines [21]. Prior to commencement, the research protocol was registered with International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) (Registration ID CRD420251018334). This article is based on previously conducted studies and does not contain any new studies with human participants or animals performed by any of the authors.

Search Strategy

A comprehensive electronic search was conducted across three major databases (Web of Science, EMBASE, and PubMed/MEDLINE) on March 15, 2025. An additional search of gray literature was conducted using Google Scholar. The search strategy aimed to explore all relevant studies investigating structural and microvascular changes in the retina and choroid of patients with HF using OCT or OCT-A imaging modalities. The search terms combined “heart failure” with either “optical coherence tomography” or “optical coherence tomography angiography”. The complete search algorithms for each database are available in Supplementary Material.

To ensure no potentially relevant studies were missed, we implemented backward and forward citation tracking methods. No limitations were applied regarding publication date or country of origin. Eligible study designs included case–control and cross-sectional studies, while we excluded experimental studies (in vitro and in vivo), editorial correspondence, case reports, case series, conference abstracts, and review articles.

All identified records were compiled in EndNote 20, where duplicate removal was performed through both automated functions and manual verification by two independent researchers (KB and PP). Any disagreements were resolved through consensus discussion.

Study Selection and Eligibility Criteria

Two authors (KB and PP) independently screened the articles to determine their eligibility, with any disagreements resolved through discussion.

Studies were included if they (1) were original research published in English; (2) involved human participants; (3) examined patients with HF without coexisting ocular pathology; and (4) included a healthy control group without ocular or systemic disorders.

Notably, we did not restrict inclusion on the basis of HF duration or severity.

While six studies were excluded from quantitative synthesis, they were retained for qualitative analysis. These comprised investigations of special populations (pediatric HF [4, 5, 15], HFpEF [12], acute decompensated HF [14]) and one study reporting inconsistent OCT measurements [11]. The meta-analysis was limited to studies on HFrEF, as this was the only heart failure subtype with sufficient data for quantitative analysis.

Data Extraction

Two investigators (KB and PP) independently extracted data using a standardized form that captured (1) study characteristics (first author, publication year, geographic origin, study design); (2) participant demographics (e.g., age, gender), sample size, and eye selection method; (3) clinical parameters [medical history, body mass index, blood pressure, HF etiology, New York Heart Association (NYHA) classification, left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF)]; (4) ophthalmic examination results (visual acuity, axial length, intraocular pressure); (5) manufacturer and model of the OCT/OCT-A device; and (6) quantitative OCT/OCT-A parameters.

Any discrepancies in data extraction were settled through joint review and discussion.

Risk of Bias Assessment

Study quality was assessed using the Newcastle–Ottawa Scale [22], which evaluates three key domains: selection, comparability, and exposure/outcome. Two reviewers (KB, PP) independently scored each study, with any discrepancies resolved through consensus meetings. All studies received scores of at least 7 (Supplementary Material).

Statistical Analysis

We conducted a random-effects model meta-analysis using Hedges’ g as the effect size measure, with restricted maximum likelihood estimation for variance components. Heterogeneity was quantified using tau-squared (τ2) and I-squared (I2) statistics, with Cochran’s Q test (p < 0.05) indicating statistical significance. A Galbraith plot was generated to visualize individual study contributions to heterogeneity. Sensitivity analysis was conducted by sequentially excluding each study and observing the impact on pooled estimates. Publication bias was assessed using multiple approaches: Egger’s regression test, Begg’s rank correlation test, and visual inspection of funnel plot symmetry. All analyses were performed using Stata software (version 17), with statistical significance set at p < 0.05.

Results

Study Selection

A systematic Literature search identified 1320 potential studies (99 from PubMed, 1091 from EMBASE, and 130 from Web of Science). After removal of 181 duplicate records, 1139 articles underwent title and abstract screening, of which 1128 were excluded for irrelevance. Full-text assessment of the remaining 11 articles revealed that six studies could not be included in the meta-analysis because of (1) pediatric populations [4, 5, 15], (2) patients with HFpEF [12], (3) acutely decompensated HF [14], and (4) inconsistent measurements [11]. However, these six studies were retained for qualitative synthesis to ensure a comprehensive evaluation of disease manifestations across distinct patient populations. Additionally, backward citation tracking yielded one eligible study, which was included in the meta-analysis [23]. The study ultimately incorporated 574 eyes from patients with HF and 565 eyes from healthy controls, totaling 1139 eyes.

Characteristics of Studies

Among the 12 included studies in this systematic review, four used a case–control [11, 14, 15, 24] approach and eight employed a cross-sectional design [4–6, 12, 13, 23, 25, 26]. The research spanned multiple regions, with contributions from Turkey (five studies) [6, 11, 23–25], followed by Poland (three studies) [4, 5, 15], Germany (two studies) [14, 26], and single studies from the Netherlands [12] and Iran [13]. The primary etiology of HF was dilated cardiomyopathy in five studies [4, 5, 15, 23, 24], while three studies included patients with both ischemic and dilated cardiomyopathy [13, 14, 25]; the remaining studies did not specify the etiology. LVEF inclusion criteria varied: < 40% (two studies) [13, 23], < 50% (four studies) [6, 11, 24, 26], ≤ 55% (four studies) [4, 5, 15, 25]. One study did not specify an LVEF cutoff [14] and another specifically included patients with HFpEF (LVEF ≥ 50%) [12]. Seven studies assessed one eye per patient [6, 11, 14, 23–26], four included both eyes [4, 5, 13, 15], and one used a mixed approach [12]. Controls were age- and gender-matched in ten studies [4–6, 13–15, 23–26], age-matched only in one [11], and unmatched in one [12]. OCT devices varied across studies: six used RTVue XR Avanti Optovue [4, 5, 13, 15, 24, 26], while others employed DRI Triton Topcon [11], Heidelberg [23, 25], Nikon RS-3000 [6], Zeiss Cirrus [14], and Zeiss PLEX Elite 9000 [12]. OCT imaging assessed multiple structural and vascular parameters. Macular thickness assessments covered central [4, 12–14, 23], parafoveal [4], and whole macular thickness [4]. Vascular evaluations included foveal avascular zone (FAZ) area [4, 12, 24, 26], perimeter, and circularity index [12], as well as macular vessel density in the fovea [4, 11–14], parafovea [4, 11, 13, 14, 24, 26], perifovea [13, 14], and whole macula [4, 13, 14, 26]. Ganglion cell complex (GCC) parameters comprised average, superior, and inferior thickness, ganglion cell inner plexiform layer (GCIPL) thickness [12], and focal/global GCC volume loss [5]. Choroidal measurements included general thickness [11], subfoveal thickness (SFCT) [6, 13–15, 23, 25], and thickness at 1500-μm intervals from the fovea (superior, inferior, temporal, nasal) [15], along with choroidal vascularity index (CVI) and choriocapillaris flow [13]. Retinal nerve fiber layer (RNFL) measurements included average thickness and quadrant-specific (superior, inferior, temporal, nasal) assessments [6]. Radial peripapillary capillary (RPC) density was evaluated for mean [24, 26] and regional distributions (superior/inferior hemi, superotemporal/nasal, nasosuperior/inferior, inferonasal/temporal, temporoinferior/superior) [24]. Additional parameters included total retinal volume [12] and superotemporal retinal vein/artery diameters [14].

Data Synthesis

Adults with Chronic HFrEF

Pooled Effect Size Estimates

Figure 2 illustrates the comparison of OCT and OCT-A parameters between patients with HF and healthy controls where data pooling was feasible. The analysis demonstrated significantly lower SFCT values in patients with HF compared to controls (Hedges’ g = − 0.75, 95% CI − 1.31, − 0.18, p = 0.01), with high heterogeneity (I2 = 86.56%, p < 0.001). Parafoveal superficial capillary plexus (SCP) vessel density (VD) was also significantly reduced (Hedges’ g = − 0.42, 95% CI − 0.78 to − 0.05, p = 0.03), though without significant heterogeneity (I2 = 55.97%, p = 0.11). In contrast, parafoveal deep capillary plexus (DCP) VD did not differ significantly between groups (Hedges’ g = − 0.62, 95% CI − 1.68 to 0.43, p = 0.25), despite high heterogeneity (I2 = 94.17%, p < 0.001).Fig. 2Forest plots depicting retinal and choroidal structural and vascular changes in patients with heart failure with reduced ejection fraction compared to healthy controls. Sensitivity Analysis

Sensitivity analyses assessed the robustness of these findings. Exclusion of studies by Khalilipur et al. [13] and Alkan et al. [24] rendered parafoveal SCP VD differences nonsignificant. Furthermore, after the studies by Khalilipur et al. [13] and Kanar et al. [6] were excluded, the observed differences in SFCT were no longer statistically significant. However, for parafoveal DCP VD, no individual study exclusion altered the overall effect sizes. Publication Bias

To comprehensively evaluate potential publication bias, we employed a multimodal analytical approach. First, funnel plots were systematically examined for visual asymmetry. Second, we conducted formal statistical assessments using Egger’s regression test and Begg’s rank correlation test to quantitatively measure bias. Meta-regression

To explore potential confounders, we performed a meta-regression, limited by available data to the relationship between systemic blood pressure and SFCT. Systolic blood pressure (SBP) was a significant moderator (p = 0.022), explaining R2 = 67.93% of the between-study variance. By contrast, diastolic blood pressure was not significant (p = 0.197) and accounted for a smaller share of heterogeneity (R2 = 22.05%). These results suggest that differences in SBP across studies may contribute to observed variability in SFCT (Table 2). Notably, the population in the study by Alur et al. [23] had the highest SBP, which may explain why it was the only study that did not observe a significant reduction in SFCT among patients with HF. Qualitative Evidence Synthesis

Central macular thickness (CMT) was comparable between cases and controls, a result that persisted after age/sex adjustment [13, 23]. Whole en face SCP and DCP VD were comparable between groups [13, 26], as were foveal and perifoveal SCP/DCP VD [13]. However, parafoveal DCP VD for superior/inferior hemi and all quadrants (superior, temporal, inferior, and nasal) was lower in HF, while parafoveal SCP VD in these same regions showed no significant difference [24]. Confirming these trends, Topaloglu et al. reported similar findings: comparable SCP VD but significantly reduced DCP VD in patients with HF. Additionally, they observed lower VD in both SCP and DCP when comparing NYHA class III versus class II patients, suggesting the potential utility of OCTA metrics as objective prognostic indicators for assessing heart failure progression [11]. Concordantly, macular flow density correlated with NYHA class and LVEF, in another study [26]. FAZ area findings were inconsistent: one study reported larger FAZ area in HF [24], while another found no difference in the superficial layer [26]. FAZ area did not correlate with LVEF [26].

Topaloglu et al. measured choroidal thickness but did not specify whether their measurements included SFCT; consequently, we excluded their data from our meta-analysis. Nevertheless, their findings align with our meta-analysis results, showing significantly reduced choroidal thickness in NYHA class II and III patients with HF compared to controls. Additionally, they observed no significant difference between NYHA II and III patients [11]. Two studies demonstrated significant correlations between SFCT and both age and LVEF [6, 25], whereas two others observed no correlation between SFCT and LVEF [13, 23]. Additionally, these studies found no significant correlations between SFCT and axial length, intraocular pressure, or systolic and diastolic blood pressure [13, 25]. Choriocapillaris flow and CVI did not differ between groups, even after age/sex adjustment [13]. CVI was unrelated to systolic/diastolic blood pressure or LVEF [13].

RNFL thickness, including average, superior, inferior, nasal, and temporal quadrants, was significantly reduced in patients with HF with LVEF < 30% compared to both patients with HF with LVEF 30–50% and healthy controls. However, no significant difference was observed between patients with HF with LVEF 30–50% and controls. Notably, while RNFL thickness demonstrated a significant correlation with age, it showed no association with LVEF values in the HF cohort [6].

In the study by Alkan et al., RPC density was significantly lower in patients with HF across all regions except the superotemporal area [24]. Similarly, Alnawaiseh et al. reported reduced optic nerve head flow density in HF, unrelated to NYHA class [26].

Notably, Kanar et al. identified SFCT < 250 μm and RNFL < 97 μm as discriminative parameters for HF [6].

Adults with HFpEF

Patients with HFpEF showed reduced GCIPL thickness and total retinal volume, though left eye measurements alone showed no significant difference. Similarly, macular VD was reduced in patients with HFpEF, with this finding remaining unchanged when data were stratified by right and left eyes. In contrast, average central retinal thickness, central retinal perfusion density, and all FAZ metrics (perimeter, area, and circularity index) showed no significant differences between groups in either combined or eye-specific analyses [12].

Several significant correlations were identified: (1) GCIPL thickness with both central retinal thickness and total retinal volume; (2) macular VD with FAZ perimeter and circulatory index; (3) total retinal volume with markers of diastolic dysfunction; and (4) specifically in right eyes, FAZ perimeter and area with left atrial volume index [12].

Adults with Acutely Decompensated HF

Abdin et al. examined posterior segment structural and vascular changes during both the initial 1–2 days of acute decompensation and the subsequent recompensation phase after diuretic treatment. Compared to controls, patients with HF exhibited significantly reduced SFCT in both phases, while maintaining similar CMT. During acute decompensation, patients demonstrated larger vessel diameters in both the superior temporal retinal vein and artery relative to controls. The investigators also reported significantly reduced both length and area of perfused vasculature per unit area across all measured regions in superficial layers during acute decompensation: the full scan area, foveal center (1 mm), inner ring (3 mm), and outer ring (6 mm) compared to controls [14].

Children with Chronic HF

Rakusiewicz et al. conducted three studies examining pediatric patients with chronic HF secondary to dilated cardiomyopathy [4, 5, 15]. Their findings demonstrated that average GCC thickness and superior/inferior quadrant measurements showed no significant differences between cases and controls. Both focal and global GCC volume loss were also comparable between groups. These measurements were independent of sex, age, eye laterality refractive error, LVEF, and NT-proBNP levels [5].

In a subsequent study, they observed significant reductions in whole macular, foveal (1 mm), parafoveal (3 mm) thicknesses, in addition to SCP VD across all regions. By contrast, DCP VD, whether measured across the whole macula or specifically in foveal and parafoveal regions, along with FAZ area in SCP showed no case–control differences.

Notably, while foveal thickness and foveal SCP VD correlated with age, and FAZ area correlated with foveal thickness, none of these parameters correlated with LVEF or NT-proBNP levels. Furthermore, neither SCP nor DCP VD showed any correlation with sex or refractive error [4].

Their choroidal thickness evaluation revealed significantly reduced SFCT and choroidal thickness at all 1500-μm locations (superior, inferior, temporal, and nasal to the fovea) in patients with HF compared to controls. The nasal location showed the thinnest measurements while the subfoveal region remained thickest. Although girls exhibited thicker choroidal measurements than boys, choroidal thickness showed no correlation with gender, age, refractive error, NT-proBNP levels, or LVEF [15].

Discussion

Patients with HF show ocular microvascular and structural changes, including reduced choroidal thickness and altered retinal vessel density, which may be associated with systemic hemodynamic alterations. These manifestations might reflect interactions between systemic circulation and ocular health and could possibly serve as a tentative diagnostic indicator of cardiac dysfunction, pending further validation. Converging evidence from OCT and OCT-A studies across diverse populations substantiates these mechanistic associations, supporting the utility of these noninvasive imaging modalities as potential surrogate markers for early decompensation detection, disease monitoring, and risk stratification. However, it should be noted that these findings are derived from observational studies, precluding definitive causal or prognostic conclusions. Prospective, longitudinal studies are therefore required to establish temporal relationships.

Reduced choroidal thickness emerged as a consistent finding in adults with HFrEF, acutely decompensated HF, and pediatric HF. Supporting this finding, Schuster et al. demonstrated that reduced LVEF, as a cardiovascular risk factor, is significantly associated with choroidal thickness [27]. Given the choroid’s high metabolic demand [28], its compensatory capacity to counteract perfusion pressure fluctuations [29–33] appears insufficient to withstand the microvascular changes seen in HF. One proposed mechanism is that low cardiac output in HF reduces systemic blood flow, triggering compensatory vasoconstriction in peripheral vessels, including ocular arteries, to prioritize oxygen delivery to vital organs [13]. Over time, this may damage choroidal arteries, restrict oxygen and nutrient delivery [14, 15, 34], and induce ischemic changes that promote retinal pigment epithelial atrophy and reduced SFCT [25, 35–37]. The choroid’s vulnerability stems from its reliance on terminal vascular branches with limited collateral circulation [14]. Furthermore, elevated central venous pressure may impair venous drainage, causing systemic venous congestion [14]. This stagnant blood fails to meet oxygen demands and may also exert hydrostatic stress on the choriocapillaris, promoting stromal fluid extravasation and compression of the choroidal stroma. Notably, a single study reported preserved choriocapillaris flow density and CVI in patients with HFrEF, suggesting that both stromal and luminal compartments contribute to HF-related choroidal changes [13]. While requiring further validation, this observation may underscore HF-specific mechanisms which contrasts with conditions like hypertension or diabetes, where CVI reductions predominate as a result of capillary degeneration [38, 39].

One study demonstrated significantly reduced RNFL thickness in patients with severe HFrEF (LVEF < 30%) compared to both healthy controls and moderate HFrEF groups (LVEF 30–50%) [6]. This reduction in severe cases may result from orbital vessel vasoconstriction secondary to diminished cardiac output and consequent chronic ischemia [6]. The preserved RNFL observed in moderate HFrEF may reflect compensatory mechanisms which may fail in advanced disease. These findings may indicate potential utility of RNFL measurements as a prognostic marker for HF severity stratification.

Regarding VD in the RPC network, both available studies consistently reported decreased values [24, 26]. While the underlying pathophysiology remains unclear, some evidence suggests that inadequate blood supply to the optic nerve may contribute to this finding [40, 41]. Alternatively, Alkan et al. proposed that technical factors, particularly excessive overlapping of radial capillaries near the optic disc that could have introduced measurement errors, potentially account for this observation [24].

The observed GCIPL thinning in HFpEF aligns with the microvascular paradigm of its pathophysiology. Retinal hypoxic-ischemic injury can cause retinal ganglion cell (RGC) loss [42–46]. Local renin–angiotensin system (RAS) hyperactivity may impair neurovascular coupling, the process by which light-stimulated retinal neurons release vasoactive signals to dilate nearby vessels and match blood flow to metabolic demand, thereby further contributing to GCIPL thinning [47–50]. Choroidal hypoperfusion may also contribute to photoreceptor degeneration, as it supplies the outer retina [51]. Preserved GCC thickness in pediatric HF may reflect age-dependent ischemic resilience, as inner retinal layers, though vulnerable to oxidative stress, have stronger adaptive defenses against hypoxia [46, 52, 53]. This resilience may stem from nutrient reserves in the vitreous and retina, which support anaerobic glycolysis in retinal cells to sustain ATP production during hypoxia [5, 54].

While CMT remained relatively unchanged in adults with HF across different subtypes, pediatric patients exhibited significant reductions in macular thickness across various regions. This discrepancy may arise from developmental variations in retinal structure or age-dependent differences in vulnerability to hypoxic damage, resulting in an impaired capacity to compensate for hemodynamic fluctuations associated with reduced cardiac output.

Compared to healthy controls, the macular microvasculature shows distinct alterations across HF subtypes, potentially influenced by the unique pathophysiological mechanisms and hemodynamic characteristics of each subtype. In HFrEF, reduced cardiac output and tissue hypoperfusion lead to FAZ enlargement and parafoveal SCP VD reduction. These alterations reflect compromised retinal autoregulation, likely due to dysfunctional constriction of terminal retinal vessels [4, 13], and ultimately leads to chronic ischemic stress. Peripheral vasoconstriction may further reduce VD [55–57]. Additionally, the sympathetic nervous system and RAS may be implicated [58]. Supporting this, Almeida-Freitas et al. demonstrated through color Doppler imaging that patients with HF exhibit elevated vascular resistance indices and compromised blood flow in the ophthalmic artery circulation [42].

A study of adults with HFpEF showed diffuse macular VD reduction without FAZ changes, reinforcing microvascular dysfunction as a central player in HF pathophysiology independent of systolic impairment. These findings substantiate the paradigm of HFpEF as not merely a disorder of myocardial stiffness but rather a systemic vasculopathy.

A study of acute decompensation revealed reduced VD in superficial layers secondary to hemodynamic instability and sudden increases in central venous pressure, which disrupts retinal venous outflow and induces vascular congestion [14, 59]. Concurrent blood pressure spikes may provoke vessel dilation or structural remodeling [14]. Notably, Abdin et al. reported a reduction in intraocular pressure following recompensation, attributing this to abrupt variations in systemic venous pressure. These alterations disrupt ocular venous hemodynamics and hinder transscleral drainage of intraocular fluid in decompensated states [14]. The temporary widening of retinal vessel diameters during acute decompensation may serve as a compensatory mechanism to maintain blood flow amid systemic hypoperfusion; however, this often proves insufficient to prevent tissue hypoxia, as evidenced by persistent VD and perfusion deficits. Furthermore, following recompensation, certain anatomical parameters (e.g., vessel densities) improve in parallel with NT-proBNP normalization. However, functional outcomes (e.g., visual acuity) often fail to recover, suggesting persistent damage following short-term treatment [14, 60].

Pediatric HF demonstrates selective SCP involvement with whole, foveal, and parafoveal VD decreases.

The DCP remains relatively spared in both HFrEF and pediatric cases. The distinct ischemic patterns observed between the SCP and DCP may stem from their anatomical differences [4]. The SCP contains medium-to-large arterioles with smooth muscle layers, while the DCP comprises delicate capillaries lined only by endothelial cells, basement membrane, and pericytes [61]. Notably, the heightened hypoxia sensitivity of pericytes renders the DCP particularly vulnerable to hemodynamic alterations and ischemic damage [62–64].

Collectively, these distinct patterns observed in OCT-A findings highlight the complex, layer-specific responses of retinal vasculature to systemic hemodynamic stress and underscore the importance of segmental retinal and choroidal analysis in HF characterization.

The observed reductions in parafoveal SCP VD and choroidal thickness may hold significant clinical relevance. However, our sensitivity analyses attenuated the statistical significance of these parameters, indicating that their utility remains inconclusive and requires further validation through additional studies. If confirmed, these OCT/OCTA-derived metrics could potentially serve as noninvasive surrogate biomarkers of systemic microvascular impairment in HFrEF. Such ocular parameters may enable earlier detection of deteriorating cardiovascular status prior to the manifestation of overt clinical symptoms.

Notably, severity-dependent RNFL thinning emerges as a robust biomarker for patient stratification, supported by recent large cohort data linking RNFL attenuation to elevated cardiovascular risk [65]. Furthermore, the correlation between whole macular VD and both LVEF and NYHA functional class in patients with HFrEF underscores its stratification potential. Together, these OCT/OCTA metrics may provide complementary, clinically accessible markers that reflect systemic HF severity. Their integration could enhance risk stratification, facilitate disease progression monitoring, and ultimately inform personalized therapeutic approaches in HFrEF management.

Limitations and Future Directions

While our findings offer compelling insights, several limitations should be acknowledged. The small number of available studies limits the generalizability of results for these subgroups. For several key parameters, interpretation was restricted by single-study reporting, underscoring the critical need for more studies with larger and diverse populations, particularly among patients with HFpEF and acute HF. The cross-sectional nature of most included studies limits our ability to determine whether observed ocular microvascular changes precede, coincide with, or result from systemic cardiac dysfunction.

Another limitation is that the eligible studies employed different OCT instruments from various manufacturers, each with its own test–retest reliability and segmentation algorithms. While this may limit direct comparison of parameters across studies, the validity of comparisons within studies, where measurements were taken using the same instrument, remains intact. Moreover, the inclusion of multiple instruments enhances the generalizability of this systematic review.

Future studies should prioritize longitudinal assessments to determine whether retinal and choroidal parameter changes predict clinically relevant endpoints including disease progression, rehospitalization, and survival outcomes. Notably, retinal and choroidal imaging represents a promising avenue for elucidating the microvascular mechanisms of heart failure-mediated end-organ damage, particularly in highly perfused tissues such as the brain and kidneys, an area that warrants prioritized investigation in future studies.

Conclusion

Our systematic review suggests that ocular posterior segment changes, including choroidal thinning and altered retinal vascular metrics, are observed across HF subtypes and may reflect systemic hemodynamic status. The integration of OCT/OCTA into HF management could provide clinicians with actionable insights to support precision medicine in cardiovascular care. However, it is important to note that the current body of literature remains limited, preventing definitive conclusions. Additionally, we observed high heterogeneity in the SFCT meta-analysis, and sensitivity analyses revealed that the differences in SFCT and parafoveal SCP VD were no longer statistically significant after excluding individual studies. Therefore, while ocular biomarkers show potential as accessible tools for HF detection and management, further research, particularly longitudinal studies, is needed to confirm their clinical utility.
