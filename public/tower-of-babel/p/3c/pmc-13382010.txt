Quality-driven design of rice wine lees fermented Litopenaeus vannamei: Unraveling the role of microbial communities in physicochemical and flavor formation
Authors: Wenjia He, Xiaoyan Zhang, Min Wang, Yicheng Ding, Tao Zhou, Yuting Ding, Yanbo Wang, Xuxia Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Microbial starters determine the quality of fermented shrimp products. Three rice wine lees starters (HSF, CZ, and AQ) were employed to ferment Pacific white shrimp under controlled conditions. The microbial community analysis indicated that HSF and CZ starters were dominated by Firmicutes or Ascomycota, with Pichia and Limosilactobacillus as the characteristic genera, whereas AQ was dominated by Mucoromycota, primarily Rhizopus. Fermentation with different rice wine lees significantly increased color values (L*, a*, and b*) and introduced more desirable volatile compounds. Total volatile contents varied markedly across fermented shrimps, ranked as HSF shrimp (236.41 μg/g) > CZ shrimp (178.60 μg/g) > AQ shrimp (87.27 μg/g). Correlation network model analysis further revealed that Firmicutes and Ascomycota exhibited significantly positive correlations with higher alcohols and esters, like 2-phenylethyl acetate and 2-phenylethanol. This study clarifies the microbial mechanisms underlying flavor formation and provides a theoretical basis for value-added fermented shrimp products.

Introduction

Shrimp is one of the most commercially important crustacean species globally, prized for its high-quality protein and extremely low fat content. Over the past two decades, global shrimp production has experienced dramatic growth, with the global market size was valued at USD 71.87 billion in 2024 and projected to reach USD 105.35 billion by 2033 (Widiasa et al., 2026). Notably, Pacific white shrimp (Litopenaeus vannamei) is the most widely farmed shrimp species across the world, accounting for approximately 80% of the global farmed shrimp production (Juhasz-Dora et al., 2025). A large quantities of Pacific white shrimp are wasted annually owing to spoilage and biochemical deterioration during post-harvest processing and storage (Zhu et al., 2024). Until now, the shrimp processing industry lacks diversified deep-processed options beyond traditional fermented shrimp paste, which fails to satisfy the consumer expectations regarding innovative flavorful and nutritional seafood products. Therefore, it is economically important to process fresh shrimps into innovative and value-added shrimp products with high-protein and low-fat levels.

Fermented glutinous rice is a traditional Chinese fermented product with profound historical roots that can be traced back to the Zhou Dynasty and its production was recorded in the Shijing (Book of Songs). “If it is made of wine, it will be tillered.” Traditionally, fermented glutinous rice is fermented from water and rice (or other cereal grains), yielding a nutrient-rich beverage with rich proteins, minerals, and vitamins. Generally, the fermentation of rice wine generates substantial quantities of alcoholic compounds by establishing an inhibitory high-alcohol environment, which facilitates its utilization in fermented meat/fish products to prolong their shelf-life (Latoch et al., 2023). Meanwhile, microbial metabolic activities could secrete abundant enzymes to catalyze and hydrolyze the proteins and carbohydrates in the substrate, concurrently generating unique flavor during fermentation (Li et al., 2024). Therefore, the advantageous bacteria and their metabolites in the fermented glutinous rice are always utilized to ferment the aquatic products.

Flavor plays a pivotal role in shrimp fermentation, serving as the primary factor that directly determines the consumer behavior. Generally, the evolution of flavor compounds during shrimp fermentation constitutes a complex process that is shaped by the raw shrimp materials, endogenous enzyme activity, and microbial metabolism of additional starters. Notably, the bacterial and fungal compositions of different starters vary considerably depending on the rice substrate and fermentation agent. (Nie et al., 2025). Thus, it is hypothesized that these starters are effective in enhancing the fermentation process and final quality of fermented shrimp. Generally, Pichia, Rhizopus, Limosilactobacillus, and Saccharomyces are commonly found as the dominant microorganisms in the fermented glutinous rice (Koay et al., 2022). For example, Pichia yeast is reported to contain genes encoding protease, α-amylase, lipase, and phytase as well as the key fermentation enzymes like pyruvate decarboxylase and alcohol dehydrogenase, which helps in reducing the phosphate pathway flux and enhancing the ethyl acetate metabolic pathway (Baumann et al., 2010). Rhizopus has been reported to contain at least four secreted aspartic protease genes (sap1-sap4) that encode acid proteases, which could degrade protein into small peptides and amino acids in the traditional Asian fermented foods, such as tempeh and rice wine (Chin et al., 2025). Additionally, lactic acid bacteria were reported to enhance flavor components like isoamyl alcohol, phenethyl alcohol, ethyl acetate, and isoamyl acetate. Nevertheless, limited information is available on the specific microbial profiles of different rice wine lees starters, as well as the correlations between the distinct microbial communities and flavor formation in aquatic products. High-throughput sequencing (HTS) technology provides a comprehensive profile of microbial community composition and reveals the dynamic changes in species diversity (Nie et al., 2022). Therefore, applying HTS technology to analyze the microbial community composition and profile of the utilized rice wine lees starters is of significant importance.

This study employed three QUs, including Anqi Qu (AQ), Huashifu Qu (HSF), and Chuzhai Qu (CZ), to produce three different types of rice wine lees starters to explore the potential of different starters for shrimp processing. Pacific white shrimp (Litopenaeus vannamei) was utilized as the experimental material in the current study, which is one of the most widely cultivated shrimp species in the global aquaculture. The dominant flora and key microorganisms in the three rice wine lees starters were firstly detected by HTS technology. Moreover, the physicochemical indexes related to the overall quality of shrimp products, including total volatile base nitrogen (TVB-N), enzymatic activity, reducing sugar, and amino acid nitrogen, were evaluated in the current study. Texture profile analysis was also conducted to investigate the textural properties of fermented shrimp with three utilized rice wine lees starters. Moreover, the flavor substances were analyzed by using headspace solid-phase microextraction-gas chromatography–mass spectrometry (HS-SPME-GC–MS) combined with electronic nose (E-nose) to screen out the optimal rice wine lees starter. Correlations between the physicochemical properties, textural properties, and flavor substances of fermented shrimps as well as the microorganisms in starters were investigated with multivariate data analysis. The approach of the current study is to develop a standardized production method for fermented shrimp products to improve their fermentation quality and flavor characteristics. And the findings provide a certain theoretical foundation for optimizing and advancing fermented shrimp products with rice wine lees starters.

Materials and chemicals

The raw Pacific white shrimp (Litopenaeus vannamei, average weight: 15 ± 2 g) was purchased from Qiushan Agricultural and Livestock Products Market in Deqing County (Zhejiang, China), and killed immediately prior to the experiment. Round glutinous rice was obtained from a market located in Harbin (Heilongjiang, China). HSF Qu was purchased from Huashifu Food Trading Department (Nanning, Guangxi, China), CZ Qu was purchased from Chuzhai Fermentation Products Co., Ltd. (Yichang, Hubei, China), and AQ Qu was purchased from Angel Yeast Co., Ltd. (Yichang, Hubei, China).

All chemicals used in high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) analysis were of chromatographic grade. Among which, the amino acid standards (purity ≥97%) were obtained from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA), including aspartate (Asp), threonine (Thr), serine (Ser), glutamate (Glu), glycine (Gly), alanine (Ala), cysteine (Cys), valine (Val), methionine (Met), isoleucine (Ile), leucine (Leu), tyrosine (Tyr), phenylalanine (Phe), lysine (Lys), histidine (His), arginine (Arg), and proline (Pro). The internal standard (2,4,6-trimethylpyridine) used for HS-SPME-GC–MS was purchased from Adamas-bete (Titan Technology Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China). A series of n-alkane (C7-C40) standards were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA). All other chemicals employed in this work were of analytical grade.

Preparation of rice wine lees starters

High-quality commercial round glutinous rice with complete grains were selected as the raw material for the preparation of rice wine. The rice was first washed with deionized water and soaked for 8 h at ambient temperature. Following hydration, the glutinous rice was transferred to a perforated steam vessel and steamed for 20 min. Subsequently, the rice was rinsed with sterilized cold water to facilitate grain separation and the temperature was rapidly decreased below 30 °C. The starters were then uniformly distributed throughout the rice matrix strictly adhering to the manufacturers' optimal formulations: HSF Qu and CZ Qu were inoculated at 0.2% (w/w), while AQ Qu was inoculated at 0.4% (w/w). The mixtures were incubated at 29 ± 1 °C for 52 h to complete the fermentation. The resulted rice wine lees were collected as the starter for subsequent shrimp fermentation.

Total DNA extraction, amplicon sequencing, and bioinformatics analysis

DNA extraction and PCR amplification of rice wine lees starters were conducted following the protocol described by Han et al. (2023). A composite sampling strategy was employed for ensuring the representativeness of the microbial community profile to minimize the experimental variability. For each starter group (HSF starter, CZ starter, and AQ starter), subsamples were collected from five different spatial points within the fermentation vessel and pooled into a single composite sample for DNA extraction (Luo et al., 2023). Genomic DNA was extracted from the starter samples using the OMEGA Soil DNA Kit (M5635–02) (Omega Bio-Tek, Norcross, GA, USA), in accordance with the manufacturer's instructions. The extracted DNA was then stored at −20 °C until further analysis. Amplification of specific genetic loci was carried out via PCR. To target the bacterial community, the V3–V4 hypervariable regions of the 16S rRNA gene were amplified utilizing the 338F forward primer (5’-ACTCCTACGGGAGGCAGCA-3′) paired with the 806R reverse primer (5’-GGACTACHVGGGTWTCTAAT-3′). Simultaneously, fungal identification involved amplifying the internal transcribed spacer (ITS) domain employing the ITS5 primer (5’-GGAAGTAAAAGTCGTAACAAGG-3′) in conjunction with the ITS2 primer (5’-GCTGCGTTCTTCATCGATGC-3′). The thermal cycling profile established for the reaction initiated with a denaturation step at 98 °C lasting 5 min. This was followed by 25 amplification cycles consisting of denaturation at 98 °C for 30 s, annealing at 53 °C for 30 s, and elongation at 72 °C for 45 s, concluding with a final extension period at 72 °C for 5 min. Purification of the synthesized PCR amplicons was achieved using Vazyme VAHTSTM DNA Clean Beads (Vazyme, Nanjing, China). Subsequently, the concentration of the purified products was quantified using the Quant-iT PicoGreen dsDNA Assay Kit (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA).

Genomic libraries were constructed utilizing the TruSeq Nano DNA LT Library Prep Kit from Illumina. To ensure library quality, final fragment selection and purification were executed via electrophoresis on a 2% agarose gel. Following quality assurance, the validated libraries underwent paired-end sequencing (2 × 250 bp) on an Illumina NovaSeq platform, utilizing the NovaSeq 6000 SP Reagent Kit (500 cycles).

Bioinformatics analysis of the microbiome data was conducted employing the QIIME2 software (version 2019.4), incorporating specific modifications and enhancements derived from the official tutorial guidelines (https://docs.qiime2.org/2019.4/tutorials/). Initially, raw sequencing reads were demultiplexed using the demux plugin. Subsequently, primer sequences were excised via Cutadapt, and the data were further processed for quality control and denoising using the DADA2 plugin.

Preparation of fermented shrimp

The raw shrimp specimens were processed by removing the shrimp head, shell, and dorsal vein, and then rinsed with purified water for 5 min. A 10 ppm ozonated water was obtained using an ozone generator (MLS-W-10G, Guangdong Mai Lang Ozone Equipment Co., Ltd., China), and the shrimps were submerged in the ozonated water to reduce the initial indigenous microbial load according to the method reported by Lin et al. (2022). Then, the shrimp were immersed in a curing solution (12% sucrose, 6% salt, and 0.5% phosphate) at 4 ± 1 °C for 2 h. The cured shrimp were layered with rice wine lees (1:1, w/w) in the fermentation vessel, and fermented at 25 ± 1 °C for 72 h. The final shrimp samples fermented with HSF, CZ, and AQ starters (rice wine lees) were named as HSF shrimp, CZ shrimp, and AQ shrimp, respectively.

Textural profile analysis

Textural attributes were evaluated using a TA-XT Plus texture analyzer (Stable Micro Systems Ltd., Godalming, UK), following protocols established by Xiao et al. (2023) with minor adjustments. A P36R probe was employed for the double-compression test under the following operating conditions: pre-test speed of 2.0 mm/s, post-test speed of 1.0 mm/s, 40% strain ratio, a trigger force of 10 g, and a 5.0 s delay between compressions. Determinations were performed on the second abdominal segment of the shrimp samples, reporting the mean of five replicates per sample group.

Microstructural observation of muscle tissue

Ultrastructural changes in shrimp muscle were visualized by adapting the technique described by Zhang, Xiong, et al. (2023). Samples were sectioned into blocks measuring 2.0 cm × 1.0 cm × 0.5 cm and fixed in 2.5% glutaraldehyde at 4 °C for 12 h. Following fixation, the tissues underwent triple rinsing with 0.1 mol/L phosphate buffer (pH 7.3) and serial dehydration using a graded ethanol series (50%, 70%, 80%, 95%, and 100%). The dehydrated samples were subsequently lyophilized (LGJ-12D, Beijing Sihuan Qihang Technology Co., Ltd.), sputter-coated with gold, and examined using a HITACHI Regulus8100 scanning electron microscope (Tokyo, Japan).

Determination of physicochemical properties

Surface chromaticity (L*, a*, and b*) was recorded via a precision colorimeter (3nh NR20XE, Guangdong ThreeNH Technology Co., Ltd.), taking the average of five independent readings per group. For pH quantification, 3 g of minced tissue was homogenized in 27 mL of deionized water, equilibrated for 30 min, and centrifuged (8000 ×g, 5 min). The supernatant pH was then measured in triplicate (Five Easy Plus FP20, METTLER TOLEDO). Total acidity (TA) and amino acid nitrogen (AAN) were assessed by homogenizing 3 g of paste with 27 mL of deionized water, followed by boiling and centrifugation. The resulting supernatant was analyzed using the acidometer method outlined by Chen et al. (2023). For reducing sugar analysis, its content was analyzed by the 3, 5-dinitrosalicylic acid method, as previously reported by Huang et al. (2024). For TVB-N analysis, a mixture of methyl red and bromo cresol green was conducted as the indicator. A solution of 20 g/L boric acid was used to absorb the nitrogenous substances in the sample, and the titration was carried out with 0.01 mol/L standard hydrochloric acid solution. Finally, the content of TVB-N was determined by Kjeldahl method (Liu et al., 2021).

Determination of total microbial counts

For total microbial counts analysis, 2 g of fermented shrimp samples was transferred into a sterile sampling bag (12 × 18 cm), and mixed together with 18 mL of sterilized NaCl solution (0.85%). The mixture was subjected to a 2-min homogenization using a Huxi HX-4 homogenizer (Shanghai, China), followed by the preparation of serial ten-fold dilutions. Total colony count was enumerated on Plate Count Agar (PCA), while total yeast counts were assessed using Rose Bengal Chloramphenicol (RBC) agar. Additionally, the fermented shrimp sample was subjected to the thermal sterilization (core temperature 75 °C, sterilization for 10 min) to keep the microbiological safety of the final shrimp product.

Determination of enzymatic activity

Crude enzyme extracts were prepared by modifying the procedure of He et al. (2025). The shrimp sample (5 g) was added to a 25 mL PBS buffer (pH = 6.8), homogenized, centrifuged (8000 ×g, 5 min, 4 °C), and the supernatant was collected and used for further analysis. All assays were performed in triplicate.

To analyze α-amylase activity, 0.1 mL of crude enzyme solution was mixed with 0.4 mL of 2% soluble starch solution and 0.4 mL of citrate buffer (pH 5.2). The resulting mixture was incubated in a water bath at 40 °C for 30 min. After incubation, the reaction was stopped by adding 0.8 mL of sodium hydroxide. The supernatant (0.4 mL) was then combined with 0.6 mL of 2-hydroxy-3,5-dinitro-benzoic acid and heated in a water bath for 5 min. Following this, the solution was diluted with 9 mL of distilled water. The absorbance was read at 520 nm.

For acidic protease analysis, 0.2 mL of crude enzyme solution was combined with 1.8 mL of 1% (w/v) acidic casein, and the mixture was incubated in a water bath for 20 min at 40 °C, where the acidic casein was prepared from lactate buffer (lactic acid: sodium lactate = 4:1, pH 3.0). The obtained hydrolyzed supernatant (0.2 mL) was reacted with 0.4 mol/L sodium carbonate and dilute Folin & Ciocalteu's reagent (40 °C, 20 min). Absorbance of the resulting chromogenic solution was monitored at 680 nm.

Determination of free amino acids (FAAs)

The determination of free amino acids (FAAs) was conducted using a method adapted from Zhu et al. (2023). Crushed samples (2 g) were homogenized with 15 mL of 15% TCA (8000 ×g, 1 min) and held at 4 °C for 2 h. Following centrifugation at 10,000 ×g (15 min, 4 °C), a 10 mL aliquot of the supernatant was adjusted to pH 2.0 with 1 M NaOH and the volume was fixed with deionized water to 20 mL. Solutions were filtered through a 0.22 μm nylon membrane (Nylon, Jinteng, Tianjin, China) prior to analysis on an automated amino acid analyzer (LA8080; Hitachi Co., Ltd.), with three replicates per sample.

Electronic noses analysis

Volatile flavor profiles were evaluated utilizing a cNose E-nose system (Baosheng Industrial Development Co., Ltd.) fitted with 18 metal-oxide sensors, as described by Bai et al. (2025). Details on the performance of E-nose sensor can be found in Table S1. A 3 g sample was placed in a 40 mL headspace vial and incubated at 50 °C for 20 min, which was then injected into the E-nose. The system was purged for 120 s before each analysis. The total acquisition time was settled at 60 s with an airflow rate of 1 L/min.

Sensory evaluation

Sensory evaluation was performed by a trained panel to assess the consumer acceptance of the fermented shrimp products. Ten panelists (5 males and 5 females, aged 22–30 years) with prior experience in sensory profiling of fermented foods were recruited. The unfermented shrimp and the fermented shrimp samples (HSF, CZ, and AQ shrimps) were served in randomized order to demonstrate the baseline sensory improvements. Each panelist should evaluate the color, rice wine aroma, taste, and textural profiles of the samples. Detailed scoring criteria are listed in Table S2. All the evaluations were performed in triplicate for each sample group.

Determination of volatile compounds

The profiling of volatile organic compounds was conducted using an Agilent 7890 gas chromatography system paired with a 7000B triple-quadrupole mass spectrometer (Agilent Technologies, Palo Alto, CA, USA). This analysis followed the protocol described by Zhang et al. (2024), with specific experimental modifications. A solid-phase microextraction (SPME) fiber coated with Divinylbenzene/Carboxen/Polydimethylsiloxane (DVB/CAR/PDMS) was utilized to extract volatile constituents from the headspace. Chromatographic separation was achieved using a DB-17MS capillary column (30 m × 0.25 mm × 0.25 μm).

For sample preparation, shrimp tissues were initially lyophilized to remove moisture. Subsequently, 1 g of the freeze-dried powder was accurately weighed into a 15 mL headspace injection vial. For quantification, 100 μL of 2,4,6-trimethylpyridine (100 mg/L) was introduced as an internal standard, and the vial was immediately sealed with a cap containing a PTFE/silicone septum. The SPME fiber was exposed to the sample headspace at 60 °C for 40 min to facilitate extraction, followed by thermal desorption at the GC inlet at 250 °C for 3 min.

The column oven temperature program was set to strictly control separation: starting at an initial 35 °C, the temperature was ramped at 10 °C/min until reaching 250 °C, where it was held for 2 min. Helium functioned as the carrier gas at a constant flow rate of 1 mL/min. Mass spectrometry data were acquired in full-scan mode with an electron impact energy of 70 eV and an ion source temperature of 230 °C. A homologous series of n-alkanes (C7–C40) was analyzed under identical conditions to calculate retention indices (RI). Compounds were identified by matching mass spectra with the NIST database and verifying against reference RI values, with relative concentrations (μg/g) calculated based on the internal standard. The odor activity value (OAV) was calculated to evaluate the actual sensory contribution of the identified volatile compounds. The OAV was determined using the following formula:OAV=CiTi

Ci represents the mass concentration of the specific volatile compound in the fermented shrimp sample (ug/g), and Ti represents the published odor threshold of the corresponding compound (ug/g).

To ensure scientific comparability, all odor thresholds were systematically sourced from the authoritative database Compilations of Flavor Threshold Values in Water and Other Media (van Gemert, 2011), with thresholds determined in comparable fermentation substrates or similar food being utilized as the standardized reference matrix for compounds.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were performed in triplicate, and data are reported as mean ± SD. Statistical analysis was conducted using SPSS 25.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL), utilizing one-way ANOVA and Duncan's multiple range test (p < 0.05). Multivariate analyses (PCA and PLS-DA) were executed on the MetaboAnalyst Cloud platform to visualize volatile flavor characteristics. Prior to PLS-DA, data were normalized; model quality was evaluated via LOOCV (R2Y, Q2) and verified by permutation tests (n = 200). Variables with VIP scores >1.0 were considered significant discriminants. The MetWare Cloud platform (https://cloud.metware.cn/), Mantel tests were used to assess the correlations between the microbial community (Bray-Curtis distance) and physicochemical/flavor metrics (Pearson). Heatmaps were subsequently employed to display the distribution of significant features. The number of permutations is 9999. The p-value was calculated as P = (Nextreme + 1) / (Ntotal + 1), where Nextreme represents the number of permuted statistics greater than or equal to the observed statistic, and Ntotal is the total number of permutations.

Composition of bacterial and fungal communities

The top 20 phyla and genera with the highest average relative abundance in the bacterial community are clearly illustrated in Fig. 1a and b. The bacterial microbiota of three starters predominantly originated from four phyla: Firmicutes, Proteobacteria, Cyanobacteria, and Actinobacteria. At the genus level, the dominant genera of the three starters displayed significant differences. For example, HSF was dominated by Limosilactobacillus (65.37%) and Acinetobacter (1.60%). Notably, a substantial proportion of sequences in HSF were identified as Chloroplast (16.68%) and Mitochondria (12.56%). These sequences may derive from the glutinous rice substrate rather than active bacterial taxa. Similarly, CZ was dominated by Limosilactobacillus (23.23%) and Leuconostoc (12.59%), with Chloroplast detected as a major non-bacterial component in the corresponding rice wine starters. The dominant bacterial genus Limosilactobacillus in HSF and CZ has been previously reported as a functional microorganism in rice wine starters (Lei et al., 2024). In contrast, the dominant bacterial genera in the AQ starter were identified as Streptococcus (25.53%), Enterobacter (24.77%), Acinetobacter (9.97%), and Pseudomonas (2.70%). Additionally, the top 20 fungal phyla and genera with highest average relative abundance are shown in Fig. 1c and d. There existed significant differences in the fungal microbial abundance among these three starters. For example, the dominant fungal phyla existed in HSF and CZ starters were mainly Ascomycota, whereas that existed in AQ starter was predominantly Mucoromycota. At the genus level, Rhizopus and Aspergillus were identified as the core fungal species in the AQ starter, accounting for 97.87% of the relative abundance. Microstructure and texture profiles analysis

The changes of the main textural parameters and microstructure of fermented shrimp with different starters are shown in Fig. 2. The textural characteristics of fermented shrimp showed a significant softening trend when compared with the unfermented fresh shrimp. The result was consistent with the following enzymatic activity results, that the microbial proteases and lipases produced by the rice wine lees starters could break down the large molecules into smaller molecules and resulted in the decrease of hardness, chewiness, and shear force in the fermented shrimp (Zhang, Tu, et al., 2023). The magnitude of this softening effect was starter-dependent, with AQ shrimp showing the most pronounced reductions, followed by CZ shrimp and then HSF. There were two primary mechanisms existed behind this textural degradation. First, the enzymatic hydrolysis of myofibrillar proteins (e.g., myosin and actin) by both microbial and endogenous proteases broke down the muscle fiber network, which directly reduced hardness and chewiness (Wang et al., 2022). Second, the microbial production of organic acids decreased the pH values, altering protein hydrophilicity and likely contributing to the substantial drop in adhesiveness observed in all fermented shrimp groups (Lucey, 2004).Fig. 2Changes in the textural properties of unfermented and fermented shrimp with different starters. a: Texture profiles. The visualization of the structural basis for the textural shifts was conducted by scanning electron microscopy (SEM). The muscle fiber structures of the unfermented shrimp remained intact and neatly arranged and a smooth surface and compact structure was revealed by the SEM. Compared to the control group (unfermented), the microscopic structures of fermented shrimps (HSF shrimp, CZ shrimp, and AQ shrimp) exhibited significant changes, The muscle tissue in the three experimental groups appeared more “swollen” under microscopic observation, with enlarged intercellular spaces, which can be resulted from the improved water-holding capacity. The HSF shrimp showed a relatively dense structure, while AQ shrimp exhibited larger pores, with the structure becoming increasingly loose and the arrangement of fiber bundles losing regularity. Moreover, the specific microbial consortium of each starter dictated the extent of proteolytic and acid-induced changes to the muscle microstructure, which ultimately formed the final textural properties of the fermented shrimp product.

Physicochemical properties analysis

Fermentation significantly altered the physical and chemical properties of the final shrimp products. As shown in Fig. 3a, the color values (L*, a*, and b*) of fermented shrimp sample were significantly different. Among the studied shrimp samples, the L* value of HSF shrimp was the highest, indicating that fermented shrimps made from HSF rice wine lees contained the most lustrous surface and highest brightness. This increase was attributed to protein denaturation during fermentation and resulted in the migration of internal moisture to the surface. The a* and b* values of HSF shrimp were also the highest, suggesting this fermented shrimp product contained a more characteristic red color together with a greater degree of browning reactions. This result may be due to the existence of Maillard reactions during shrimp fermentation. The TVB-N and pH also showed notable differences among the samples (p < 0.05), as shown in Fig. 3b. The pH values of all fermented shrimp products decreased significantly when compared with the unfermented shrimp, from an initial 7.22 to a range of 5.20–5.80. Concurrently, the acidity was highest in HSF shrimp, followed by CZ shrimp, and then AQ shrimp. This result can be ascribed to the higher proportion of Saccharomycopsis existed in the rice wine lees prepared from CZ, which could generate substantial acetic acid. The Firmicutes could synergistically metabolize carbohydrates into lactic acid and other organic acids, leading to the lowest pH (Liu et al., 2022). As for the TVB-N value, the fermented shrimp exhibited a 1.80 to 2.65-fold increase of TVB-N when compared to the unfermented shrimp. Among three types of fermented shrimps, AQ shrimp showed the highest TVB-N value (13.24 mg/100 g), which can be attributed to the higher enzymatic activities produced from Aspergillus and Rhizopus in the rice wine lees starter AQ, especially protease. The enzyme facilitated the breakdown of shrimp proteins into free amino acids, which are subsequently deaminated to form TVB-N (Wan et al., 2025).

As for AAN, all fermented shrimp samples showed a significant increase in AAN content (Fig. 3c). Among which, HSF shrimp had the highest AAN content at 0.282 g/100 g (4.4 times that of unfermented group). This result was in accordance with the highest free amino acid contents observed in the HSF shrimp. The AAN content of CZ shrimp was the lowest at 0.215 g/100 g. Notably, AQ shrimp exhibited the highest protease activity, yet their amino acid nitrogen (AAN) did not reach the highest level among the fermented shrimps. This result can be attributed from the active amino acid deaminases secreted by Rhizopus with intense deaminated metabolic characteristics, such as L-amino acid oxidase and dehydrogenase (El-Sayed., 2012). Consequently, the generated amino acids were rapidly metabolized or converted into volatile nitrogen compounds instead of accumulating in the fermented shrimps. Meanwhile, the principal microbiota in HSF was dominated by Limosilactobacillus and Pichia. On the one hand, the proteinases and peptidases secreted by Limosilactobacillus efficiently hydrolyze the intermediate oligopeptides into free amino acids, thereby elevating the AAN value. On the one hand, the acidic environment created by the rapid acid production of Limosilactobacillus could inhibit the growth of amino acid-degrading bacteria. As a result, the microbial community tended to retain more amino acids or convert them into flavor precursors rather than engaging in the extensive oxidative deamination. Additionally, the high acidic environment also synergized with the ammonia assimilation by Pichia, which resulted in the higher retained AAN and lower TVB-N values. The TA contents of all the shrimp samples were significantly higher when compared to the unfermented group (p < 0.05).

As shown in Fig. 3d, the total colony count in all the fermented shrimp was 2.50–3.50 log CFU/g higher than that of the unfermented shrimp, with HSF shrimp being the highest at 7.91 log CFU/g. These elevated microbial enumerations reflected the active proliferation of beneficial starter microbiota, which drives proteolysis and flavor formation during fermentation. While the fermentation process fosters high functional microbial loads, subsequent thermal sterilization of a fermented shrimp successfully decreased total colony and yeast counts to 1.15 and 1.00 log CFU/g, respectively, effectively ensuring final product safety. This robust microbial growth was ascribed to the highest proportion of Pichia and Wickerhamomyces in the HSF rice wine lees starters. Interestingly, the total colony count in the CZ shrimp was the lowest, which was different from the highest reducing sugar consumption. This result can be related with the dominant fungal genus Saccharomycopsis existed in CZ rice wine lees starter, which was reported to prioritize the monopolization of glycogen in the shrimp, and thereby limited the bacteria growth. The total yeast count in the three studied fermented shrimp groups was significantly higher (p < 0.05) when compared with the unfermented shrimp. And the highest total yeast count was found in the CZ shrimp (5.95 log CFU/g). This result further indicated that yeast species became the dominant microorganisms during shrimp fermentation.

There were significant differences in the enzymatic activities among the studied fermented shrimp groups (p < 0.05), as shown in Fig. 3e. For the amylase activity, the highest amylase activity was found in CZ shrimp (45.20 U/g), followed by HSF shrimp (40.53 U/g) and AQ shrimp (38.18 U/g). This result can be resulted from the high levels of Saccharomycopsis existed in CZ starter, which secreted diverse amounts of enzymes, like amylase, β-glucosidase, and acid protease (Su et al., 2020). As for the protease activity, AQ shrimp displayed the highest protease activity at 30.37 U/g, followed by HSF shrimp (16.6 U/g) and CZ shrimp (13.97 U/g). Previous study has demonstrated that proteases could break down the meat proteins into small peptides and free amino acids, which acts as the flavor precursors (Chen et al., 2015). Additionally, the existence of proteases could soften and tenderize shrimp muscle, which resulted in a unique, juicy, and easily chewable texture. The changes of reducing sugar contents in different fermented shrimp samples were shown in Fig. 3f. The reducing sugar content in HSF and CZ fermented shrimp were lower when compared with the unfermented shrimp at values of 7.19% and 4.10%, respectively. This result indicated that the high proportion of Saccharomycopsis existed in HSF and CZ starters could consume large quantities of hydrolyzed polysaccharides (Chi et al., 2009). Although Saccharomycopsis in HSF and CZ starter exhibited strong amylolytic activity, the reducing sugar content of HSF and CZ fermented shrimp decreased significantly after fermentation. Saccharomycopsis exhibited both strong saccharification efficiency and fermentation ability, and this dual capability could result in rapid metabolic conversion of hydrolyzed sugars into ethanol (Choo et al., 2016). And the starch decomposition resulted from Saccharomycopsis also provided nutrients for Limosilactobacillus and Candida. These metabolic processes during shrimp fermentation may exceed the starch hydrolysis rate, which resulted in the relatively low reducing sugar contents in CZ shrimp. In contrast, AQ shrimp exhibited a relatively higher reducing sugar content (10.76%). The AQ starter was dominated by Rhizopus (92.13%) and Aspergillus (5.74%), secreting highly active α-amylase and other enzymes. These enzymes driven a rapid sugar hydrolysis that exceeded microbial consumption and resulted in a high reducing sugar accumulation in the final AQ shrimp.

Analysis of FAAs

As shown in Table 2, the free amino acid categories that existed in three different fermented shrimps were 16, 15, and 13, respectively. Notably, there was an increased production of bitter amino acids, like Cys, Met, Ile, and Leu. These bitter amino acids may be produced from the rice wine lees starters and absorbed by the fermented shrimp, similar results were also found in the results of Kuang et al. (2022). Meanwhile, umami amino acids could influence the flavor characteristics of fermented shrimp products directly, as reported in the previous study (Hu et al., 2024). As shown in Fig. S4a and Fig. S4b, the percentage of sweet amino acids were the highest in AQ shrimp (76%), whereas the percentage of umami amino acids were the most abundant in HSF shrimp (58%). Currently, the umami amino acids, like Asp, Tyr, and Lys, significantly increased during shrimp fermentation. This could significantly improve the flavor characteristics of the final fermented shrimp products. However, the amino acid contents (Gly, Pro, and Arg) significantly decreased during shrimp fermentation. These amino acids can be utilized by the microorganisms for microbial growth. Similar result was previously reported by Hazelwood et al. (2008). This result also demonstrated that specific transaminases existed in the lactic acid bacteria could degrade certain amino acids during fermentation process. Meanwhile, yeast could absorb specific free amino acids and metabolize them into highly desirable aroma-active compounds primarily via the Ehrlich pathway. Specifically, yeasts utilize branched-chain amino acids (e.g., valine and leucine) and aromatic amino acids (e.g., phenylalanine) as precursors. Furthermore, these intermediate higher alcohols can be intracellularly esterified with acetyl-CoA to generate ester compounds such as ethyl 2-phenylacetate, which collectively imparted the characteristic floral and fruity notes to the final fermented shrimp products. E-nose analysis

The E-nose was used to characterize the flavor profiles of fermented shrimp, and the detailed information were clearly shown in Fig. 4a. Additionally, the sensors and their corresponded substances were clearly shown in Table S1. As shown in the radar chart (Fig. 4a), all the fermented shrimp samples exhibited low response values, especially for those sensors that corresponded to substances like propane, hydrogen, and alkane (sensors 1, 3, 7, 8, and 10). However, all the three fermented shrimps showed high response values for the sensors that were sensitive to the nitrogen-containing substances, aldehyde/ketone compounds, methane ammonia/amines, and acetone or ethanol (sensors 5, 6, 14, 17, 18). Overall, the unfermented shrimp materials showed low response values for all the detected sensors, while the fermented shrimps showed an upward trend of sulfur-containing compounds, nitrogen-containing compounds, alcohols, aldehydes, ketones, and other short-chain alkanes. Among which, HSF shrimp contained the highest flavor response values, followed by CZ shrimp and AQ shrimp, indicating that the applied fermentation starters played important roles in the flavor formation during shrimp fermentation process. The sensory evaluation also aligned with the E-nose and physicochemical analyses, further demonstrating the sensory improvements driven by fermentation. Notably, the unfermented shrimp exhibited the lowest sensory scores across all dimensions, yielding a total score of only 20.42, reflecting a bland taste profile and lack of desirable aromatic attributes. Following fermentation, all the groups demonstrated a significant enhancement in sensory acceptability relative to the unfermented shrimp. For example, HSF shrimp showed remarkable higher overall acceptability (total score: 81.63) when compared with CZ shrimp (72.63) and AQ shrimp (60.00), as shown in Fig. 4b. Specifically, HSF shrimp received significantly higher scores for both rice wine aroma and taste, resulted in the prominent rice wine aroma and rich fragrance. This finding was consistent with the strongest aromatic sensor responses of the E-nose to HSF shrimp. In contrast, the scoring points for texture and color of AQ shrimp were relatively low when compared with the other shrimp products. The texture of AQ shrimp was described as loose and mushy, which was consistent with the texture profile analysis and the high proteolytic activity of Rhizopus-dominated AQ starter. Variations of volatile compounds

A total of 111 volatile compounds were identified in the unfermented and fermented shrimp samples, including 13 alcohols, 10 aldehydes, 16 ketones, 5 carboxylic acids, 37 esters, 17 alkanes, 8 pyrazines and 4 others (Table S3). Overall, fermentation by rice wine lees starter significantly increased both the concentration and diversity of these volatile compounds, with the specific aromatic profiles that were highly dependent on the utilized starter. It is clear that HSF shrimp contained the highest volatile concentrations, especially for esters and higher alcohols, and characterized by a rich, fruity aroma. Similar with HSF shrimp, CZ shrimp also yielded high levels of higher alcohols. In contrast, AQ shrimp contained the lowest volatile contents which were uniquely characterized as strong roasted and nutty aromas. This result can be ascribed to the abundant amounts of pyrazine compounds inside the AQ shrimp. Additionally, a PLS-DA model was used to differentiate the fermented shrimp samples. A total of 15 key volatile compounds were identified that were responsible for the distinct flavor profiles. Compared with the unfermented shrimp sample, all the studied fermented shrimp samples exhibited significantly higher (p < 0.05) volatile compounds, both in concentrations and numbers. Detailed information was clearly shown in Fig. 5a and b. Higher alcohols and esters were detected as the primary volatile compounds in the fermented shrimp. The highest alcohol content was detected in HSF shrimp (119.55 μg/g), followed by CZ shrimp (108.23 μg/g) and AQ shrimp (48.98 μg/g). Among the higher alcohols, there existed the most pronounced increase of butane-2,3-diol and 2-phenylethanol which attributed fruity and rose-like aromas to the final fermented shrimp. These results can be ascribed from the higher proportion of yeast strains in the corresponded rice wine lees starters, which could produce ethanol via glycolysis as well as higher alcohols (1-propanol, 2-methyl-1-propanol, and 2-phenylethanol) from amino acid degradation (Ehrlich pathway) (Hazelwood et al., 2008). The highest ester content was found in HSF shrimp (76.28 μg/g), followed by CZ shrimp (44.18 μg/g) and AQ shrimp (17.56 μg/g). There also existed a significant increase of ethyl esters, like ethyl palmitate and ethyl butyrate. Ethyl palmitate contributes fatty and fruity notes to the fermented shrimp products, while ethyl butyrate provides star anise, apple, and banana aromas to the final products. In general, the ester production during shrimp fermentation was mainly driven by Pichia, Saccharomycopsis, Wickerhamomyces, Candida, and Limosilactobacillus existed in HSF and CZ starters. The high proportions of Pichia existed in HSF starter could rapidly induce the synthesis of key fermentation enzymes such as Pyruvate decarboxylase (PDC) and Alcohol dehydrogenase (ADH) under oxygen-limited conditions. PDC could catalyze the decarboxylation of pyruvate and generate acetaldehyde, which was further catalyzed by ADH and resulted in high production of ethanol (Baumann et al., 2010). Previous studies have shown that the inoculation of W. anomalus could notably increase ester contents and further improve floral and fruity aroma to the fermented shrimp (Gao et al., 2024). However, the shrimps fermented by AQ starter was less efficient in producing esters when compared with the shrimps fermented by HSF and CZ starters. Aldehydes have been identified as one of the primary flavor compounds, and their sensory contribution depends largely on their specific chemical structure and concentration. Research has shown that aldehydes with high molecular weights (>150 Da) or branched-chains usually imparted a sweet and fruity smell. However, aldehydes derived from lipid oxidation (such as nonanal and decanal, etc.) are often associated with fishy or rancid off-flavors (He et al., 2025; Que et al., 2023). The concentrations of nonanal and decanal both decreased during the end fermentation process, indicating that the microbial metabolism was driven by the starters and effectively reduced these potentially undesirable aldehydes. Generally, the total aldehyde contents decreased in CZ shrimp, which was decreased by 28% of total aldehyde contents compared to the unfermented shrimp. Interestingly, the total aldehyde contents showed an increase tendency in HSF shrimp and AQ shrimp. The volatile acids were significantly higher in all the fermented shrimps compared with the unfermented shrimp, with the ranking HSF shrimp > AQ shrimp > CZ shrimp. The primary volatile acids existed in the fermented shrimps were acetic acid, hexanoic acid, and decanoic acid, which were formed through the microbial breakdown of the sugars.

The visualization of the alterations in detected volatile compounds among different fermented groups were represented in a heatmap, as shown in Fig. 6. Generally, the concentration and diversity of total volatile compounds significantly increased in all fermented groups when compared with the unfermented group. For the unfermented shrimp samples, the volatile compounds were primarily composed of aldehydes and minor quantities of ketones, alkanes, and other low-concentration volatile compounds that originated from the shrimp materials. Notably, HSF shrimp showed higher concentrations in the esters and higher alcohols (e.g., ethyl acetate, 2,3-butanediol, and acetic acid). CZ shrimp was specifically enriched in certain alcohols and acetates (e.g., 2-methyl-1-propanol and octyl acetate), while AQ shrimp had higher concentrations of 2-phenylethanol, 3,7-dimethyl-1,6-octadien-3-ol, and 2,6-dimethylpyrazine. Overall, HSF shrimp contained a dominant ester aroma, CZ shrimp showed a rich alcohol-dominant aroma, and AQ shrimp contained a roasted aroma. The Odor Activity Value (OAV) was calculated to further validate the actual contribution of these key marker volatile compounds (VIP > 1) to the overall sensory profile of the detected fermented shrimps. Generally, the odorants with an OAV greater than 1 are generally considered as key contributors to the overall aroma profile. The OAV calculation revealed that these VIP compounds served as both statistical markers and potent aroma-active components (Table S4). Notably, 2-phenylethanol, known for its characteristic rose-like and floral notes, exhibited the highest olfactory impact, with OAV reaching 301.57, 171.57, and 162.49 in HSF, CZ, and AQ shrimps, respectively. Pearson correlation heatmap was used to investigate the relationships between physicochemical properties, dominant microbial phyla, and the 15 key volatile compounds (VIP > 1) (Fig. 7a). Specifically, Firmicutes and Ascomycota were positively associated with desirable aroma compounds, including esters and alcohols. Conversely, Proteobacteria and Mucoromycota showed negative correlations with ideal aromatic compounds, such as 2,3-butanediol and 2-phenylethyl acetate. These volatile compounds were positively associated with color parameters (L*, a*, b*) and certain E-nose sensor responses (sensor 6, 9, 14, 18), and exhibited negative correlations with pH value and acid protease activity. In this study, a significant positive correlation was found between color values (a*and b*) and volatile compounds (2, 6, 10, 14-tetramethylpentadecane, 3-methylheptadecane, ethyl myristate, and 2, 3-butanediol). Generally, the decrease in L* value and the increase in a* and b* values were associated with the oxidative degradation of astaxanthin, the formation of Maillard reaction intermediates, as well as the lipid oxidation reactions. These reactions were also positively correlated with the production of large numbers of volatile compounds, such as 2, 3-butanedione, 2, 5-dimethylpyrazine, and 2, 5-dimethylfuran (Shang et al., 2025). A negative correlation between pH values and 2, 3-butanediol was also found in the current study. Similar results were also reported in the previous study, the lower pH environment could alter the yeast metabolism and led to the increase of higher alcohols and esters (Liu et al., 2015). Notably, the current data revealed that a negative correlation between acidic protease activity and total volatile concentrations. For example, the AQ shrimp exhibited the highest acidic protease activity (30.37 ± 4.00 U/g) and resulted in the lowest volatile compounds (87.27 ± 10.78 μg/g). This result may be ascribed to the excessive Mucoromycota existed in AQ starter and this suppressed the yeast metabolism, thereby constraining the ester formation and higher alcohol production. At the phylum level, the positive correlation between Firmicutes and Ascomycota together with the esters and higher alcohols (e.g., ethyl pentadecanoate, 2-phenylethyl acetate, ethyl (9E)-9-octadecenoat, 2,3-butanediol, ethyl (9Z,12Z)-9,12-octadecadienoate, and ethyl decanoate) was observed in the current study. Additionally, a negative correlation between Proteobacteria/Mucoromycota with esters and higher alcohols were investigated. A plausible explanation is that Proteobacteria/Mucoromycota communities rapidly deplete glycogen, amino acids, and fatty acids for biomass and enzyme production, which prevent the alcohol generation and esterification.

The Mantel test visualized the multi-layer relationships between dominant genera and physicochemical properties (Fig. 7b and c). This network helps to distinguish the synergistic or antagonistic taxa which drives the texture, color, and volatile compounds. For example, Limosilactobacillus correlated positively with E-nose sensors with high response values (alcohols, ketones and aldehydes) and color values, and showed negative correlation with pH value. This observation aligned with previous literature that lactic acid-driven acidification led to production of flavor compounds as well as the release of fatty acid compounds (Tian et al., 2021). Meanwhile, the occurrence of Enterobacter and Streptococcus were positively with the increase of enzymatic activities (p < 0.01), which suggested their potential roles in the metabolic regulation during shrimp fermentation. In contrast, Leuconostoc displayed significant positive correlations with both Pediococcus (p < 0.001), indicating cooperative symbiosis of these two genera existed in the shrimp fermentation system. Additionally, positive associations were observed in the Pichia and Candida, Rhizopus and Aspergillus, which aligned with the elevated enzymatic activities could accelerate the hydrolysis of starch and protein. Conversely, Saccharomyces was negatively correlated with Rhizopus and Aspergillus, which pointed that niche competition existed in the mixed consortia. From a carbon-flux perspective, reducing sugars were negatively correlated with Pichia and Wickerhamomyces and the rapid respiratory, as well as the AAN values was positively correlated with Aureobasidium, Cyberlindnera, and Saccharomycopsis. The amylase activity showed a weak positive relationship with the volatile compounds, reflecting its role in the releasement of fermentable sugars and production of higher alcohol and ester compounds. In aggregate, the correlation landscape highlights that starters enriched in Firmicutes/Ascomycota and cooperative yeast consortia best promote favorable color (higher a*, b*) and aroma (esters, alcohols), whereas Mucoromycota-dominant system drive proteolysis without proportional aroma gains, likely through substrate competition and ester hydrolysis.

Conclusion

In summary, we systematically investigated the effect of three rice wine lees starters (HSF, CZ, and AQ) on the physicochemical and volatile flavor compositions of fermented shrimp. The distinct microbial profiles and enzymatic activities of three starters were found to modulate the resulting physicochemical and flavor characteristics. The utilization of HSF and CZ starters significantly increased the color values (L*, a*, and b*) together with abundant generation of higher alcohols and esters, whereas AQ resulted in the lowest total volatile compounds in the final fermented shrimp products. The correlation analysis further revealed the synergistic effects between microbial composition and the quality of fermented shrimp products. Among which, Firmicutes and Ascomycota existed primarily in HSF and CZ starters were positively associated with the production of esters and higher alcohols, but negatively with pH values and acid protease activities. Conversely, Proteobacteria and Mucoromycota in AQ starter were negatively associated with the total volatile compositions in the fermented shrimp products, indicating excessive protein or sugar metabolism may prevent the generation of esters. Moreover, the key bacterial genera like Limosilactobacillus exhibited a negative relationship with pH values and a positive relationship with higher alcohols and esters. This result also confirmed that the acidification process driven by lactic acid bacteria played a positive role in the flavor retention and quality enhancement of the fermented shrimp products. Practically, the fermented shrimps made from HSF showed a better aroma complexity together with abundant amounts of esters and higher alcohols thereby offering the most promising sensory profile. This study provides a theoretical foundation for standardized and flavor-targeted fermentation process of value-added shrimp products.
