Adolescent emergency department visits for self-harm after COVID-19–related social distancing in the Republic of Korea: a 7-year retrospective study
Authors: Kwang Yul Jung
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAdolescent suicide is a major public health concern and self-harm–related emergency department (ED) visits are key predictors. Although social distancing during and after the pandemic likely affected the incidence of self-harm, few studies have explored these changes. We examined the variations in the incidence of adolescent self-harm–related ED visits before, during, and after social distancing.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective analysis of data from the ED‑Based Injury In‑Depth Surveillance registry in the Republic of Korea from January 2017 to December 2023. Injured patients aged 10 − 17 years were included in this study. The incidence rate of self-harm was recorded quarterly. Data on demographic, pre-hospital, and clinical characteristics were compared across three periods: pre-pandemic; pandemic; and post-pandemic, using data from Quarter 1 of 2020 (when social distancing was implemented) and Quarter 2 of 2022 (when it was fully lifted) as reference points. Multinomial logistic regression analyses were performed for each group to evaluate the association with changes in the incidence of self-harm.ResultsWe included 109,929 adolescent patients with injuries, 6,316 (5.75%) of whom presented with self-harm. The incidence of self-harm increased significantly during the pandemic (from 3.6% to 8.8%), although this immediate increase was attenuated to borderline significance in a sensitivity analysis incorporating seasonal variation, and decreased modestly after social distancing was lifted (to 7.8%). Self-harm occurring at home, among individuals with medical-aid insurance, and in those with psychiatric problems was significantly associated with these overall changes.ConclusionsAdolescent self-harm increased with the onset of the pandemic and social distancing, followed by a modest relative decline in incidence after the restrictions were lifted; however, the absolute number of self-harm presentations did not return to pre-pandemic levels. Background

Suicide is a major global social issue [1], and the coronavirus disease (COVID-19) pandemic, which began in 2020, had a profound impact on this public health crisis [2, 3]. Numerous studies which investigated the effects of the pandemic on suicide rates, demonstrated that social distancing and prolonged isolation exacerbated social disconnection [2, 4, 5]. These factors particularly affected individuals already at risk of suicide, with varying impact observed across age groups and genders [3]. Adolescents are especially vulnerable, as suicide is a major risk factor for morbidity and one of the leading causes of mortality in this age group [6, 7]. Social isolation enforced during the pandemic may have posed an even greater challenge for adolescents, because of their critical developmental stage and heightened vulnerability to psychosocial stressors [8–11], as well as owing to multiple individual- and family-level risk factors that worsened during this period [12].

Most studies analysing the impact of the COVID-19 pandemic on suicide rates and mental health have focused on data from the first year of the pandemic (2020) [2, 8, 13, 14]. However, social distancing regulations (although varying across countries) were generally maintained for more than 1 year [15]. In the Republic of Korea, these measures were implemented in a stepwise manner and were tightened or relaxed depending on fluctuations in the number of COVID-19 cases. They remained in effect until April 2022 [16, 17]. Nationwide, in-person school attendance did not resume until September 2021; despite this, comprehensive analyses covering this extended period are scarce. Examining the peak pandemic year 2020, as well as the entire period during which social distancing regulations were enforced is crucial. Such analyses can provide valuable insights for the development of targeted policies to protect vulnerable populations, including those at risk of suicide.

Self-harm is a major risk factor for suicide and an important predictor of subsequent suicidal behaviour [18]. Since patients who engage in self-harm often present to the ED as their first point of contact with healthcare services, analysing ED visits for self-harm can provide valuable insights into suicide prevention.

We aimed to examine the incidence and characteristics of adolescent patients presenting to the ED for self-harm during the period in which social distancing measures were implemented.

Methods

Study design and setting

In this retrospective study, we used data from the Emergency Department-based Injury In-depth Surveillance (EDIIS) system in the Republic of Korea’s nationwide prospective registry encompassing all injured patients admitted to 23 EDs across 13 of the country’s 17 provinces. Data collection for the EDIIS was conducted following institutional review board approval, with anonymized personal identifiers. The registry was established to support the formulation of national injury prevention policies and is overseen and funded by the Korea Disease Control and Prevention Agency. In the Republic of Korea, EDs are designated as Level 1, 2, or 3 by the Ministry of Health and Welfare, according to resources and capacity, including facilities, equipment, and staffing. As of 2020, there were 38 Level 1 EDs (highest resource level), 129 Level 2 EDs, and 236 Level 3 EDs nationwide. The EDs participating in the EDIIS were exclusively Levels 1 and 2 centres [19].

Data source and collection

The EDIIS registry contains a comprehensive dataset that includes patient demographics, injury characteristics, pre-hospital EMS records, clinical assessments, diagnostic findings, ED management, ED dispositions, and post-admission outcomes. The initial surveillance and data entry were performed by general physicians, with daily reviews and corrections performed by emergency medicine physicians and trained research coordinators. The data were entered into a centralized online platform maintained by the Korea Disease Control and Prevention Agency. All participating ED staff members underwent mandatory training before joining the project. To ensure data quality, the Central Quality Management Committee reviewed the data monthly and provided ongoing feedback. We analysed EDIIS records from January 1, 2017, to December 31, 2023.

Participant selection

There is no universally accepted definition of adolescence; chronological age is only one of several possible criteria, alongside physical maturation and cognitive development. However, given that previous studies have identified self-harm cases presenting to the ED from individuals as young as 10 years old [20], we included patients aged 10–17 years who presented to the ED.

The “intention of injury” variable in the EDIIS is classified into five categories: unintentional or accidental injury, self-harm or suicide injury, violent or homicidal injury, other uncategorized injury, and unknown injury. Here, uncategorized injury refers to cases that do not fit the other specified groups, while unknown injury denotes cases in which the mechanism could not be identified. As this variable was mandatorily recorded for all patients, we included all individuals who met the inclusion criteria.

Self-harm characteristics included the following variables: demographic (e.g., sex, age, insurance), pre-hospital (ED visit time, ED visit method, injury location, self-harm method, preceding event), and clinical characteristics (e.g., mental status at ED presentation; ED disposition, including discharge to home, admission to the general ward or intensive care unit, death, or others; post-admission results, such as normal discharge, discharge against medical advice, death, or others; and in-hospital mortality). Insurance categories were divided into three types: general insurance (the standard health insurance that all citizens are required to have); medical aid (which is for low-income individuals); and none (which includes injuries not covered by insurance) [21]. “Preceding event” was defined as the most direct and significant cause of self-harm in each case. The total number of in-hospital mortalities included the number of deaths that occurred in EDs and those that occurred after hospitalisation.

Data analysis

The primary outcome was the incidence of self-harm among injured patients. We subdivided the 7-year period into 3-month intervals to examine changes in the incidence of self-harm injuries among all patients who presented to the ED, allowing for a more concise visualization of long-term temporal trends.

In the Republic of Korea, comprehensive social distancing measures, including school attendance restrictions, were implemented in March 2020, and their intensity was subsequently adjusted in phases according to fluctuations in the number of cases. Full in-person schooling resumed in September 2021 and all social distancing measures, including obligatory mask use, were lifted in April 2022. These policies were implemented nationwide under centralized government guidance, and all accredited schools in the Republic of Korea were required to comply with them.

As a secondary outcome, we examined the changes in the demographic and pre-hospital characteristics of patients presenting with self-harm injuries using data from Quarter 1 of 2020, when nationwide social distancing measures were first implemented, and Quarter 2 of 2022, when such restrictions were fully lifted, as reference time points. For analytical purposes, Quarter 1 of 2020 was defined as the pre-pandemic period, the interval from Quarter 1 of 2020 to Quarter 2 of 2022 as the pandemic period, and the period thereafter as the post-pandemic period.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are expressed as means and standard deviations with 95% confidence intervals (CIs), and intergroup differences were evaluated using the Student’s t-test or Mann–Whitney U test after testing for normality. Categorical variables are expressed as frequencies and proportions; Pearson’s chi-square analysis and Fisher’s exact test were used for intergroup comparisons. Results with p < 0.05 were considered to be statistically significant.

The incidence (95% CI) of self-harm in the 7 years from 2017 to 2023 was calculated for each quarter of each year. An interrupted time series (ITS) analysis was conducted to evaluate the changes according to the reference point in Quarter 1 of 2020 and Quarter 2 of 2022.

Multinomial logistic regression was performed with pandemic phase (pre-pandemic, pandemic, and post-pandemic) considered as the dependent variable to examine changes in the characteristics of patients presenting with self-harm across these periods. Independent variables included demographic characteristics and pre-hospital patient characteristics. Categorical predictors were coded with the following reference levels: sex (female); insurance type (general); self-harm location (other than home); ED visit time (00:00–08:00); ED visit date (weekday); mode of ED visit (non-EMS); method of self-harm (contusion); and preceding event (conflict with family or friend). Age was modelled as a continuous variable. Relative risk ratios (RRRs) represent the risk of each non-reference level relative to its reference level for the indicated pandemic-phase comparison (pre-pandemic for the “pandemic” comparison, pandemic for the “post-pandemic” comparison). To evaluate the robustness of the ITS estimates to unmodeled seasonal variation, we conducted a sensitivity analysis by refitting the ITS model with two alternative seasonal specifications: (i) quarterly indicator variables (with the first quarter as the reference) and (ii) sin/cosine harmonic terms representing an annual cycle. The statistical contribution of each seasonal specification was evaluated using an incremental F-test against the baseline ITS model, and the stability of the key pandemic-phase coefficients was compared across the three specifications.

All statistical analyses were performed using the R version 4.2.0 (2022-04-20; R Foundation, Vienna, Austria).

Ethical approval

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Chung-Ang University Gwangmyeong Hospital (IRB number: CAUGH 2508-262-135), which waived the requirement for informed consent owing to the retrospective study design. The study protocol satisfied the requirements specified by the Ministerial Decree of Health and Welfare issued by the National Bioethics Committee.

Results

Incidence and trend of self-harm

A total of 109,929 patients with injury, aged 10–17 years, were registered in the EDIIS database during the 7-year study period, of whom 6,316 had experienced self-harm (overall incidence: 5.75%). When divided by that of Q1 of 2020, the incidence of self-harm significantly increased during the pandemic (from 3.57% [n = 1,998] in the pre-pandemic period to 8.76% [n = 2,094] during the pandemic period) from Q1 of 2020. After Q2 of 2022, the incidence rate of self-harm declined to 7.84% (n = 2,224), as shown in Fig. 1.

Quarterly incidence rate of self-harm adolescent emergency department (ED) visits

An ITS analysis was performed to identify the significance of changes in the incidence of self-harm presentations to the ED. In this model, level changes represent immediate shifts in incidence at the time of each intervention point, whereas slope changes represent changes in the temporal trend following the intervention.

Before the pandemic outbreak, a significant upward baseline trend (slope) was noted in self-harm presentations (β = 0.0029, p < 0.05). At the onset of the COVID-19 pandemic in Q1 of 2020, a significant immediate level increase was observed (β = 0.0129, p < 0.05); however, the post-pandemic slope change was not significant (β = 0.0019, p = 0.07).

In contrast, following the complete lifting of social distancing measures in Q2 of 2022, a significant immediate level decrease was noted in the incidence of self-harm presentations (β = −0.0288, p < 0.05), accompanied by a significant negative slope change, indicating a sustained downward trend thereafter (β = −0.0046, p < 0.05). Sensitivity analysis with seasonal variation

The contribution of seasonal terms did not reach significance in either specification (quarterly indicators: F [3, 19] = 1.41, p = 0.27; sin/cosine harmonics: F 2,20] = 0.33, p = 0.72). The key pandemic-phase coefficients remained substantively unchanged across the three specifications. The immediate level decrease at the lifting of social distancing in Q2 of 2022 was − 2.88 (95% CI − 4.29 to − 1.48, p < 0.001) in the baseline model and remained essentially unchanged with seasonal adjustment (− 2.81, 95% CI − 4.23 to − 1.39 with quarterly indicators; −2.86, 95% CI − 4.36 to − 1.36 with harmonics; both p ≤ 0.001). The negative post-pandemic slope change was likewise stable (− 0.46, − 0.45, and − 0.45% points per quarter, respectively, all p < 0.01). The immediate level increase at the onset of the pandemic in Q1 of 2020 was attenuated only slightly when seasonal terms were added (+ 1.29, p = 0.04 in the baseline model; +1.14, p = 0.07 with quarterly indicators; +1.23, p = 0.06 with harmonics), with a consistent positive direction and similar magnitude across specifications.

Comparative analysis of demographic and pre-hospital characteristics by period

Several significant changes were observed in the characteristics of patients presenting with self-harm during the transition from the pre-pandemic to the pandemic period. The proportion of male patients significantly decreased (relative risk ratio [RRR] = 0.78, 95% CI 0.63–0.95, p < 0.05), while that of patients with medical-aid insurance increased (RRR = 1.41, 95% CI 1.03–1.92, p < 0.05). The proportion of self-harm incidents occurring at home markedly increased (RRR = 1.87, 95% CI 1.50–2.32, p < 0.05). Moreover, self-harm attributed to psychological problems increased significantly during the pandemic period (RRR = 2.64, 95% CI 1.27–5.50, p < 0.05).

Following the lifting of social distancing measures, the likelihood of self-harm presentations decreased with increasing age (RRR = 0.88, 95% CI 0.84–0.92, p < 0.05). The proportion of patients covered by medical-aid insurance (RRR = 0.62, 95% CI 0.48–0.80, p < 0.05) and those without insurance (RRR = 0.68, 95% CI 0.49–0.94, p < 0.05) both significantly declined. Self-harm incidents occurring at home became less frequent (RRR = 0.67, 95% CI 0.55–0.81, p < 0.05).

ED visits during daytime (08:00–18:00) and evening hours (18:00–00:00) increased significantly (RRR = 2.46, 95% CI 1.15–5.28, p < 0.05; RRR = 2.23, 95% CI 1.04–4.79, p < 0.05, respectively). Regarding self-harm methods, cases involving poisoning (RRR = 2.19, 95% CI 1.42–3.40, p < 0.05), stabbing (RRR = 1.79, 95% CI 1.16–2.76, p < 0.05), and falls (RRR = 1.77, 95% CI 1.08–2.89, p < 0.05) increased significantly. Conversely, self-harm attributed to conflicts with family or friends (RRR = 0.38, 95% CI 0.20–0.71, p < 0.05), health problems (RRR = 0.25, 95% CI 0.11–0.60, p < 0.05), psychiatric problems (RRR = 0.42, 95% CI 0.23–0.77, p < 0.05), school-related problems (RRR = 0.48, 95% CI 0.25–0.91, p < 0.05), and unknown causes (RRR = 0.39, 95% CI 0.20–0.77, p < 0.05) all significantly decreased.

Discussion

Main findings

Numerous studies have examined changes in self-harm–related ED visits following the onset of the COVID-19 pandemic [14, 22, 23]; however, to the best of our knowledge, this study is the first to analyse the trends across the entire period of social distancing, from its implementation to its withdrawal.

The ED self-harm incidence rate, which showed a significant upward trend prior to the outbreak, markedly increased following the onset of the pandemic and remained elevated thereafter. The observed post-pandemic pattern aligns with the findings of previous studies conducted in several other countries [14, 22–24]. These findings suggest that the observed changes coincided temporally with the implementation of social distancing measures, which may have affected adolescents, whose daily lives largely revolve around home and school environments. Their heightened emotional vulnerability may have also contributed to the immediate changes observed during the pandemic [25–27].

In the present study, a significant decline was observed in Q2 of 2022, coinciding with the lifting of social distancing measures, including the national mask mandate. While no distinct change was observed in the ED visits self-harm incidence rate during Q3 of 2021, when in-person schooling resumed following a prolonged period of remote education [17], a significant downward trend was identified only after Q2 of 2022, when all social distancing regulations had been lifted. However, despite the marked decline observed after the lifting of social distancing regulations, the incidence rate of self-harm–related ED visits remained higher than in the pre-pandemic period.

According to official statistics from the Korean government [28], the suicide mortality rate among individuals aged 10–19 years increased steadily from 4.7 per 100,000 in 2017 to 5.8 in 2018, 5.9 in 2019, 6.5 in 2020, 7.1 in 2021, 7.2 in 2022, and 7.9 in 2023, showing a continuous upward trend over the previous several years. The number of adolescent trauma patients included in this study increased from 2,217 in Q1 of 2022 to 4,379 in Q2 of 2022 immediately after the lifting of social distancing measures. These findings suggest that the observed decline in self-harm–related ED visit incidence following the withdrawal of social distancing measures should be interpreted primarily as a relative decrease rather than a confirmed absolute reduction in self-harm events. The apparent decline may partly reflect an overall increase in total ED visits driven by greater social activity. Furthermore, increased social engagement may have contributed to changes in help-seeking patterns. However, given that the overall suicide mortality rate and the number of self-harm–related ED visits remained elevated compared with pre-pandemic levels, further investigation is warranted. Taken together, while the relative incidence of self-harm decreased modestly after the lifting of social distancing, this should not be interpreted as a true decrease in absolute case burden, and the absolute number of adolescent self-harm presentations did not return to pre-pandemic levels.

The significant changes identified in our ITS analysis were preserved in a sensitivity analysis that incorporated seasonal variation. However, the statistical strength of the immediate level increase observed at the onset of the pandemic was modestly attenuated when seasonal terms were included, suggesting that the magnitude of the early-pandemic shift may not be entirely independent of seasonal influences and warrants cautious interpretation.

Changes in characteristics across the study period

The increase in the incidence rate from the pre-pandemic period to the pandemic period was primarily driven by females. The sex paradox of suicide (whereby adolescent girls are more likely to attempt self-harm and seek mental healthcare, whereas boys are more likely to die by suicide) is well documented [29, 30]; our findings are consistent with prior international reports showing a disproportionate increase in self-harm among females during the pandemic [31, 32]. Although the overall incidence of self-harm declined in the post-pandemic period, the proportion of female patients remained elevated. This suggests that the increase cannot be entirely attributed to temporary pandemic-related or social distancing measures alone; it may reflect a broader underlying upward trend in female adolescent self-harm across the study period, potentially driven by psychosocial, developmental, or help-seeking factors that warrant further investigation beyond the context of COVID-19.

The proportion of self-harm among individuals with medical-aid insurance and those occurring at home increased substantially during the pandemic but showed a decreasing trend after the lifting of social distancing measures. These findings suggest that vulnerable populations may respond immediately to changes in social restrictions. Furthermore, the transient increase in self-harm incidents occurring at home during the pandemic, followed by only a partial decline after the lifting of lockdown measures, suggests that interventions focusing solely on restricting physical environments or public spaces may have limited effectiveness. Rather than reflecting a displacement of self-harm to the home setting owing to temporary mobility restrictions, this pattern may indicate persistent psychosocial stressors within the home environment or shifts in help-seeking and supervision contexts that continued beyond the pandemic period.

Beyond changes in incidence, several shifts in the characteristics of self-harm presentations warrant discussion, following the order of patient characteristics in Table 2. With respect to ED visit timing, daytime presentations decreased while evening and night-time presentations increased slightly during the pandemic period (08:00–18:00: 56.0 → 54.7%; 18:00–24:00: 42.9 → 44.0%; 00:00–08:00: 1.1 → 1.3%). Given that adolescents spent more time at home and less time in school or community settings under social distancing, this shift toward non-daytime presentations appears intuitively consistent with reduced structured daytime activity and changes in supervision and help-seeking pathways during the pandemic. After the lifting of social distancing, the multinomial logistic regression showed an increased relative likelihood of daytime (RRR 2.46) and evening (RRR 2.23) presentations compared with overnight presentations (both p < 0.05), suggesting a partial recovery of help-seeking pathways outside the home environment [33]. With respect to self-harm method, poisoning was the most common method throughout the entire study period and its relative proportion increased consistently across the three periods (48.6 → 51.1 → 57.9%), suggesting an upward trend in poisoning that is largely independent of the social distancing intervention [34]. By contrast, stabbing, a less lethal method, rose during the pandemic (30.4 → 32.4%) and then declined to below pre-pandemic levels after the lifting of restrictions (28.6%), consistent with reports of pandemic-related shifts in self-harm methods toward less lethal means among adolescents presenting to emergency departments [35]. The continued increase in poisoning raises the question of whether expanded household availability of over-the-counter medications during prolonged stay-at-home periods contributed to this trend; this hypothesis cannot be tested with the present data and warrants dedicated further investigation, particularly given evidence that paracetamol and other over-the-counter analgesics constitute a growing share of adolescent self-poisoning [34]. Finally, with respect to preceding events, psychiatric problems accounted for a markedly higher proportion of self-harm during the pandemic and post-pandemic periods compared with the pre-pandemic period (34.7 → 54.6 → 53.7%), and this pattern closely paralleled the changes in the overall self-harm incidence rate. This finding most likely reflects the heightened vulnerability of adolescents with pre-existing psychiatric conditions to the psychosocial stressors associated with the pandemic, and is consistent with previous reports indicating that prior psychiatric vulnerabilities amplified the impact of COVID-19–related stress on self-harm behaviour [36]. This consistency further supports the view that addressing underlying psychiatric issues should be a key target in developing effective self-harm prevention strategies for adolescents.

When considering the incidence rate alongside changes in demographic and pre-hospital characteristics, the self-harm incidence rate was significantly associated with increases in the number of female adolescents and those with underlying psychiatric problems. However, this pattern should not be interpreted as a direct consequence of the policy relaxation. This shift likely reflects the interplay between multiple psychosocial, environmental, and contextual factors. From a policy perspective, our findings suggest that future preventive strategies should extend beyond short-term crisis responses and focus on sustained, targeted interventions for high-risk groups. Examples include school-based mental health screening and referral programmes, family-centred interventions addressing home-related stressors, and structured post–ED follow-up for adolescents presenting with self-harm.

Limitations

This study had some limitations. First, as it relied on a retrospective analysis of patient surveillance data from participating hospitals, the possibility of sampling bias could not be ruled out. Moreover, most of these hospitals are located in urban rather than rural regions, which may limit the generalisability of our findings. Nonetheless, this effect is likely to be minimal, as the participating hospitals represent 13 of the 17 provinces in the Republic of Korea. Second, the EDIIS registry collects data exclusively from Level 1 and Level 2 EDs, which are typically the referral destinations for patients presenting with self-harm who require psychiatric evaluation, while Level 3 EDs (regional emergency centres that often manage minor injuries) were not included. Consequently, self-harm cases of lower acuity managed solely at Level 3 facilities may have been underrepresented, potentially introducing a degree of severity-related selection bias into our findings. Third, this study did not account for diverse sociocultural influences that may affect patterns of self-harm and suicide. These include high academic pressure and competitive school environments, strong societal emphasis on educational achievement, and relatively limited access to school-based mental health professionals compared with academic counselling resources [37]. Furthermore, cultural norms that may discourage open discussion of emotional distress within families could contribute to delayed help-seeking and increased psychological burden among adolescents [38, 39]. Such contextual factors should be considered when interpreting our findings, particularly in an international context. Additionally, the definition of adolescence varies considerably across disciplines and research contexts; accordingly, our findings should be interpreted considering the specific age range (10–17 years) adopted in this study and should not be extrapolated beyond this range. Finally, our multinomial logistic regression involves multiple simultaneous comparisons across covariates and pandemic-phase contrasts; we did not apply a formal correction for multiple testing, and p-values near the 0.05 threshold should therefore be interpreted with appropriate caution, with interpretive emphasis placed on effect sizes and patterns of association rather than on individual significance. Lastly, given the observational design of this study and the absence of a contemporaneous control population unaffected by social distancing, the temporal coincidence between pandemic-phase transitions and changes in self-harm presentation patterns should not be interpreted as evidence of a direct causal relationship.

Conclusions

Adolescent self-harm increased following the onset of the pandemic and the implementation of social distancing measures. After these restrictions were lifted, the relative incidence of self-harm decreased modestly; however, the absolute number of adolescent self-harm presentations to the ED did not return to pre-pandemic levels and remained markedly elevated. Self-harm occurring at home, among individuals with medical-aid insurance, and cases associated with psychiatric problems followed similar patterns. These findings highlight the need for sustained and targeted preventive strategies for high-risk adolescent groups.
