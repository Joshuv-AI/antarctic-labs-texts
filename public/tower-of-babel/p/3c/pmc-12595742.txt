Genetic polymorphism of MSP2 and its implications for Plasmodium falciparum resistance in N’Djamena, Chad
Authors: Djobo Abel, Noumedem Anangmo Christelle Nadia, Yamssi Cedric, Tchuenkam Kom Pacome, Nganji Bismarck, Tordibaye Ngaryedji, Toumko Towa Merveille, Masra Mislengar Hervé, Darkaou Kidedji Deborah, Haibo Hu, Noubom Michel
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMalaria control is a major challenge for countries in endemic areas, and Chad is one of them. The objective of this study was to determine the genetic diversity of the merozoite surface protein 2 (MSP-2) of Plasmodium falciparum in four health facilities in N’Djamena in order to better understand the variability of circulating strains and its epidemiological implications for malaria control.MethodsBlood samples were collected from 302 patients for both parasitological and molecular analyses. Positive samples were spotted on Whatman paper for molecular analyses. The parasite DNA was extracted by the chelex-100 method. The MSP2 gene was amplified by nested PCR and the revelation of this gene was done on a 1.3% agarose gel. The diversity parameters were calculated from the fragments and the number of alleles obtained. The data obtained were analyzed using SPSS 23 software. The phylogenetic tree of the Pf population was carried out using R software.ResultsThe prevalence of P. falciparum infection was 40.72% with a higher parasite intensity in women (317.81±109.93). Of the samples analyzed, 101 carried the MSP-2 gene (78.21%) on which 12 different alleles were obtained, thus a genetic diversity of 9.37%. A multiplicity of infections of 1.26 was determined with a percentage of 73.26; 25.74 and 1% respectively for monoclonal, double clonal and triple clonal infections. The phylogenetic tree showed 11 clades in four health structures of N’Djamena with parasites that are more or less genetically related.ConclusionA significantly high genetic diversity thus indicates a high intensity of malaria transmission of P. falciparum. in N’Djamena. The multiplicity of infections and the high allelic diversity obtained allow us to understand that the malaria transmission situation in this area is critical and should guide studies of resistance to other antimalarial.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40794-025-00272-1.

Introduction

Malaria remains a major parasitic disease, caused by various species of the genus Plasmodium, with Plasmodium falciparum being the most prevalent and deadly in sub-Saharan Africa [1]. According to WHO’s latest World malaria report 2024, there were an estimated 263 million cases and 597 000 malaria deaths worldwide in 2023. This represents about 11 million more cases in 2023 compared to 2022, and nearly the same number of deaths. [1]. Malaria in Chad is the leading cause of consultation (40.6%), hospitalization (36.26%) and hospital deaths (35.98%), particularly affecting children under 5 years of age and pregnant women [2].

In Chad, malaria constitutes a considerable public health challenge. As per the World Health Organization (WHO), malaria represents the principal impetus for outpatient consultations (40.6%), hospital admissions (36.26%), and in-hospital mortality (35.98%) within the nation [3]. The Ministry of Public Health indicates that in the year 2023, there were an estimated 3.9 million cases of malaria and 13,707 fatalities [4]. Children under the age of five comprise approximately 50% of all malaria cases documented in healthcare facilities.

Malaria control faces major obstacles, including the ability of P. falciparum to develop resistance to antimalarial drugs, the emergence of insecticide-resistant mosquitoes, and the lack of a fully effective vaccine [5]. Antimalarial drug resistance is a critical public health problem that threatens progress in disease control. P. falciparum uses several complex mechanisms to evade the action of antimalarial drugs, making monitoring its genetic diversity essential to understand these dynamics [6].

The genetic diversity of P. falciparum populations is a key factor in the parasite’s adaptability and ability to escape selective pressures, whether immune or drug-induced. This diversity is notably characterized by the polymorphism of genes encoding merozoite surface antigens, such as Merozoite Surface Protein 2 (MSP2). Genotyping of polymorphic regions of MSP2 is commonly used to study the genetic diversity of P. falciparum [7]. Understanding this variability is fundamental for malaria control strategies, as it influences the efficacy of potential vaccines and the spread of resistant strains.

The MSP2 gene, located on chromosome 2 of P. falciparum, is characterized by a highly variable central region (block 3) that contains the distinct allelic families 3D7 and FC27 [8]. This variability allows the distinction of different parasite clones, reflecting the multiplicity of infections and transmission pressure in a given area. High genetic diversity of MSP2 is often associated with high malaria transmission intensity [9]. This study aimed to determine the genetic diversity of MSP2 in Plasmodium falciparum isolates from four health facilities in N’Djamena, Chad.

Materials and methods

Type and site of study

This was a cross-sectional and analytical study over a six-month period (February 2025 to July 2025 corresponding to the peak malaria transmission season in Chad.) in four health facilities in N’Djamena, Chad: Union Hospital (HU), the University Hospital Center for Mother and Child (CHUME), the Military Teaching Hospital (HMI), and the Order of Malta Health Center (CSOM) N’Djamena (Fig. 1), located at the confluence of the Chari and Logone rivers (12°8 North latitude, 15°2 East longitude). The aforementioned health facilities were meticulously chosen to ensure extensive geographical representation throughout N’Djamena, encompassing locations characterized by diverse patient volumes and varying degrees of malaria case prevalence, thus guaranteeing a representative cohort of the population utilizing healthcare services during the peak transmission period.Fig. 1Map of the city of N’Djamena [10]

Map of the city of N’Djamena [10]

Because it enables the simultaneous evaluation of exposure factors and health outcomes within the research population, offering a snapshot of their connections at a specific moment in time, a cross-sectional analytical design was selected. This design works effectively in environments with limited resources because it is also economical and time-efficient.

The study locations were chosen because they are important N’Djamena referral hospitals that see a wide range of patients from peri-urban and urban areas. Their inclusion guarantees the study sample’s representativeness and makes it easier to extrapolate the results to the capital city’s larger populace.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

All patients presenting to the selected health facilities for malaria diagnosis who provided free and informed consent were included. There were no age restrictions; both children and adults were eligible. Pregnant women were included, as they represent a high-risk group for malaria. Informed consent was obtained in writing from all adult participants. For minors, written consent was obtained from a parent or legal guardian, and assent was sought from children aged 7 years and above, in accordance with ethical guidelines. Patients who had taken antimalarial drugs within the previous two weeks were excluded.

Molecular analysis

DNA extraction

Parasite DNA was extracted from samples spotted on Whatman paper using the Chelex-100 method [14]. Pieces of filter paper containing blood were sterilely cut, treated with Tween 80 and then PBS, before the addition of pre-warmed 20% Chelex-100 resin. Tubes were vortexed and heated in a water bath, followed by centrifugation to separate the DNA from the pellet. The DNA-containing supernatants were transferred to final Eppendorf tubes and stored at −20 °C.

Amplification of the MSP2 gene by nested PCR

Nested PCR was used for MSP2 gene amplification to ensure high sensitivity (Table 1). Electrophoresis and revelation of the MSP-2 gene

Nest 2 PCR products were visualized on 1.3% agarose gel prepared in 0.5× TBE buffer, with safe view as the intercalating dye for UV-based visualization of DNA bands. Eight microliters (8 µl) of each PCR product were loaded into the gel wells, along with a molecular weight marker. Migration was done at 150 volts for 60 minutes. Gels were visualized under a UV transilluminator to observe DNA bands stained with SafeView and photographed to determine fragment sizes and confirm the presence of the MSP2 gene.

Statistical and phylogenetic analysis

Statistical analysis of the data was performed using Microsoft Excel 2010 and SPSS version 26.0 software. The Chi-square (X2) test was used to assess the association between qualitative variables, while logistic regression analysis (Odds Ratio > 1) was used to identify risk factors. A significance level of p < 0,05 was used. Genetic distances were calculated using the JACCARD formula [16]:

Molecular prevalence MSP2 gene

Figure 4 slows the molecular prevalence of MSP-2. It follows from this figure that of the 123 RDT-positive samples, 101 (82% of those tested) were confirmed positive for the MSP2 gene by nested PCR. Electrophoresis revealed bands ranging in size from 485 to 745 base pairs (bp), resulting in a total of 128 allelic bands observed across all samples as seen in Fig. 3. These corresponded to 12 distinct MSP2 alleles, yielding a genetic diversity of 9.37%. The genetic diversity of the MSP2 locus was estimated using expected heterozygosity (He), which accounts for both the number and frequency of alleles, and was calculated as 0.0937 (9.37%), indicating low-to-moderate variability among the Plasmodium falciparum strains analyzed. Additionally, the multiplicity of infection (MOI), defined as the average number of distinct MSP2 alleles per infection, was calculated as 1.27, suggesting that most infections involved a single or a few parasite clones. Thus, as shown in Fig. 4, of the 123 samples diagnosed as positive by RDT, 101 were confirmed positive for the MSP2 gene following amplification. The distribution of alleles revealed that some variants were more common than others. For example, alleles of sizes 500 bp and 600 bp were the most frequently observed, together representing approximately 30% of all 3D7 fragments. The predominance of certain alleles suggests that many infections were clonal, containing a single parasite genotype, while others displayed multiple alleles, indicating polyclonal infections. This is consistent with a calculated mean multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 1.27, showing that most infections carried one or a few parasite clones. Such a pattern reflects moderate genetic complexity within the local Plasmodium falciparum population and may provide insight into transmission intensity in the study area.

Genetic diversity and multiplicity of infections

Figure 6 shows the gel picture of MSP2. It appears from this figure that multiple-clone infections were observed. Genetic diversity was assessed based on the number of alleles (12) and the total number of DNA fragments obtained after amplification (128), resulting in a diversity index of 9.37%. The multiplicity of infection (Fig. 7) was calculated by dividing the total number of DNA fragments (128) by the number of MSP2-positive samples (101), yielding a value of 1.26. Discussion

This study highlighted the genetic diversity of the Plasmodium falciparum MSP2 gene and the multiplicity of infections in the population of N’Djamena, Chad. Understanding these parameters is crucial for assessing malaria transmission dynamics and guiding control strategies, particularly in the face of the problem of antimalarial drug resistance.

The prevalence of P. falciparum infection of 40.72% is comparable to that of other studies conducted in Chad, such as that of Abdelsalam et al. [17]. in Abéché (36.57%), confirming the persistence of a high prevalence of malaria in the country. The absence of significant difference in malaria infection according to age, sex or occupation is in agreement with some studies [17], but contrasts with others that have found significant associations [18]. In the present investigation, the intensity of parasitism did not exhibit statistically significant variations with respect to age, a result that stands in stark contrast to the prevailing literature pertaining to malaria epidemiology, which consistently documents elevated levels of parasitemia in children below the age of five, attributable to their limited development of acquired immunity. Several variables may elucidate this incongruence. Firstly, the sample size may have been insufficiently large to identify nuanced differences among various age cohorts. Secondly, the study cohort was selected from an urban environment, characterized by generally lower and more heterogeneous transmission intensity relative to rural locales, which could potentially diminish age-related disparities in parasite density. Thirdly, prior interventions or the partial immunity conferred by recurrent infections may have played a role in modulating parasite loads across all age strata. Collectively, these factors may have mitigated the anticipated correlation between younger age and heightened parasite intensity. The significant difference observed with marital status (single) could be explained by different social behaviors or living conditions influencing exposure to mosquitoes.

Risk factor analysis highlighted the impact of the use of preventive measures. Non-use of long-lasting insecticide-treated nets (LLINs) and non-wearing of protective clothing emerged as significant risk factors, increasing the risk of infection threefold for non-LLIN users. These findings are consistent with numerous African studies that confirm the effectiveness of LLINs as a physical barrier against mosquito bites [19]. The fact that proximity to ponds was not a significant risk factor could be related to the nighttime use of LLINs or the presence of natural mosquito predators, although this differs from observations in other studies [20].

The higher parasite intensity among women and informal sector workers is a notable observation. In women, this could be related to behavioral (domestic activities exposing to bites) or physiological (pregnancy and relative immunosuppression) factors, as suggested by a study in Uganda [21]. Informal sector workers, often exposed to precarious working conditions or working outdoors during mosquito activity hours, are also at risk. The higher risk observed among formal sector workers is an unexpected result that contradicts some studies [22], and may require further investigation of the specificities of their professional environment in N’Djamena. Although elevated mean parasite loads were documented in relation to sex and housing type, these correlations necessitate cautious interpretation. The density of parasites is significantly affected by confounding variables such as age, acquired immunity, nutritional status, access to treatment, and differential exposure to mosquito vectors. Numerous epidemiological investigations have consistently indicated that parasite intensity is predominantly elevated among children under five years of age, attributed to their limited immunity, and tends to diminish with advancing age as partial immunity is acquired. In the absence of adjustments for these well-established determinants, attributing variations in parasite load solely to sex or housing conditions presents considerable challenges. Future investigations employing larger sample sizes, multivariable analyses, and longitudinal follow-up are imperative to elucidate the relative contributions of these variables within the Chadian context. The strong association between not taking antimalarial medication and malaria infection (OR = 2.47) highlights the importance of access to and adherence to treatment [23].

The molecular analysis of the MSP2 gene, detected in 78.21% of positive samples, highlights the genetic diversity of P. falciparum strains circulating in N’Djamena, although this prevalence is lower than reported in some previous studies [24]. This level of diversity is comparable to that found in Benin [25], and corroborates the idea that high genetic diversity is an indicator of intense malaria transmission [26].

The multiplicity of infection of 1.26 is relatively moderate, comparable to that observed by Ravaorisa Elisabeth et al. [27]. Higher MOIs have been reported in other highly endemic regions. The predominance of monoclonal infections (73.26%) over multiple infections (26.74%) suggests that, although transmission is intense, the selection pressure on the parasite could be influenced by factors such as self-medication or controlled control strategies. A high MOI is generally associated with high transmission pressure, which favors co-infection by different strains and increases the likelihood of genetic recombination. This recombination is a key mechanism by which P. falciparum generates new combinations of alleles, including those potentially involved in antimalarial drug resistance.

The phylogenetic tree, revealing 11 clades, demonstrates complexity in the genetic relationships of P. falciparum strains in N’Djamena. The close relatedness between parasites from the Military Teaching Hospital and Union Hospital suggests local circulation of similar strains, likely due to geographic proximity and population movements between these areas. However, the presence of numerous distinct clades indicates heterogeneous transmission dynamics and a variety of strains that could have different implications for pathogenesis, host immune response, and susceptibility to antimalarial drugs.

The genetic diversity of MSP2, while not a direct marker of antimalarial drug resistance, is an indicator of the adaptive capacity of P. falciparum. A high degree of polymorphism, such as that observed, means that the parasite population has a high genetic variability, giving it a high potential to develop or spread adaptive traits, including drug resistance. The link between MSP2 diversity and antimalarial drug resistance is indirect but crucial: a genetically diverse parasite population is more resilient to treatment and control measures. Indeed, the selection of resistant strains is facilitated in an environment where genetic diversity allows the rapid emergence of new resistant genotypes under drug pressure. The situation in N’Djamena, with high diversity and multiplicity of infections, suggests that the P. falciparum population is well equipped to adapt to therapeutic challenges.

A limitation of this study was the sample size, which, although sufficient for the analyses performed, could have been larger for even greater precision in diversity.

Conclusion

These results indicate that large-scale surveillance of drug resistance is essential and, moreover, in a short time frame for effective measures to prevent and eradicate malaria in endemic areas.
