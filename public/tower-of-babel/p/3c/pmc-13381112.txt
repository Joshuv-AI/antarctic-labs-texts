The Effect of Negative Perfectionism on Hollow Syndrome in Medical Students: Serial Mediation of Self-Esteem and Achievement Motivation and Moderating Role of Subjective Well-Being
Authors: Haoge Bai, Yan Jin, Yiju Wang, Hui Xie, Yang Meng, Yinan Xu, Hao Sun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHollow Syndrome is a prevalent psychological adjustment problem in medical students, while the mechanism underlying the association between negative perfectionism (a common personality trait in medical education) and this condition remains unclear.MethodsA total of 2977 medical students from a medical university in Shandong, China were recruited via cluster sampling. Data were collected using the Chinese Hollow Syndrome Questionnaire (CHQ), Chinese Frost Multidimensional Perfectionism Scale (CFMPS), Self-Esteem Scale (SES), Achievement Motivation Scale (AMS), and Subjective Well-being Scale for Chinese Citizens (SWBS-CC20). Serial mediation and moderated mediation analyses were conducted using SPSS 26.0 and the PROCESS macro.ResultsNegative perfectionism was significantly negatively associated with Hollow Syndrome symptom scores (lower scores indicate more severe symptoms). Self-esteem and achievement motivation mediated the association through three distinct pathways, with the total indirect effect accounting for 74.26% of the total effect; the direct effect of negative perfectionism was not statistically significant after including the mediators, indicating a full mediation pattern. Subjective well-being significantly moderated both the pathway from self-esteem to Hollow Syndrome and the pathway from achievement motivation to Hollow Syndrome, with the strength of all three indirect effects decreasing as subjective well-being increased.ConclusionNegative perfectionism is associated with more severe Hollow Syndrome among medical students, mainly through reduced self-esteem and achievement motivation, and subjective well-being acts as a protective buffer against this adverse association. These findings provide clear, actionable targets for mental health interventions among medical students, and offer an empirical basis for alleviating Hollow Syndrome by protecting self-esteem, enhancing achievement motivation, and fostering subjective well-being.Keywords: negative perfectionism, Hollow Syndrome, self-esteem, achievement motivation, subjective well-being

Introduction

Concept Definition and Terminology Standardization

The concept of “hollow heart disease” was first proposed by Xu in a 2016 public academic speech at Peking University,1 with core features of meaning deficit, blurred life purpose and disorganized value system, which severely impair mental health and cause social maladjustment.2 This psychological phenomenon shares partial overlaps with internationally discussed constructs such as existential vacuum and anomie, while maintaining distinct cultural and contextual characteristics specific to Chinese young populations.3,4 This construct has been operationalized and validated through a localized Chinese questionnaire for college student populations.5 This study adopts the standardized term “Hollow Syndrome” for three core reasons: first, “syndrome” is an academic psychological term for clustered homogeneous symptoms, ensuring research rigor over the colloquial “hollow heart disease”6 Second, it avoids somatization-related cognitive bias from the literal “heart disease”, aligning with the study’s psychological attribute positioning.6 Third, it accurately reflects the correlated nature of the phenomenon, rather than framing it as a discrete disorder, consistent with its essential nature as a non-pathological psychological phenomenon.2 This study thus focuses on Hollow Syndrome as the core research subject.7

Research Necessity in Medical Student Population

Mental health problems among medical students represent a growing global public health concern; clarifying the formation mechanism of Hollow Syndrome and its influencing factors provides an evidence base for targeted early intervention.8 Medical students face multi-dimensional challenges such as heavy academic burden and high occupational pressure.9 A national meta-analysis covering 31 provinces in China reported that the pooled prevalence of depression and anxiety among Chinese medical students reaches 33.0% and 29.2% respectively, significantly higher than that of the general college student population.8 A proportion of medical students experience problems including weakened learning motivation, confused professional identity, and interpersonal alienation,10,11 and even develop mood disorders such as depression and anxiety.12,13 The core features of Hollow Syndrome — meaning deficit and blurred life purpose — often manifest as reduced learning drive, wavering professional identity and weakened interpersonal connection,7 and may further exacerbate the risk of depression, anxiety and other mood disorders over time.8 Hollow Syndrome represents an integrated early marker of these psychological and behavioral problems. Therefore, exploring the influencing factors and underlying mechanism of Hollow Syndrome among medical students has important practical significance.

Negative Perfectionism and Existing Research Gap

Negative perfectionism is a personality trait characterized by the pursuit of extreme standards and excessive attention to defects in one’s own performance, grounded in the classic multidimensional perfectionism framework proposed by Frost et al internationally.14 Against the background of contemporary medical education that emphasizes precision and rigor, negative perfectionism is highly prevalent among medical students.15 Existing studies have found that negative perfectionism is closely associated with a variety of psychological problems, and is an important risk factor for mood disorders including depression and anxiety.16,17 Existing studies have consistently documented the association between negative perfectionism and the core features of Hollow Syndrome such as inner emptiness and meaning deficit. International empirical research has confirmed that individuals with negative perfectionism tend to use extreme standards as a defensive strategy to cope with existential vulnerability, which in turn leads to a state of self-alienation and lack of life meaning.18 Relevant domestic research in China has also noted that overly high self-expectations correlate with value confusion and psychological maladjustment among college students.19 For example, Binder P.-E. et al found that individuals with negative perfectionism adopt perfectionism as a defensive strategy to cope with existential vulnerability, formulating achievement-dependent and extreme pursuit standards, and then falling into a state of inner emptiness, self-alienation, and lack of meaning in life.18 However, the internal mechanism between negative perfectionism and Hollow Syndrome has not yet been clarified, and there is a lack of in-depth exploration of mediating and moderating variables, making it difficult to fully reveal the pathways through which negative perfectionism is associated with Hollow Syndrome.

Theoretical Basis of Mediating and Moderating Variables

Self-esteem, defined as an individual’s overall evaluation of self-worth, is theoretically supported by Rosenberg’s classic self-esteem theory and Leary’s sociometer theory, and serves as a critical bridge linking personality traits to psychological adjustment.20,21 Individuals with negative perfectionism tend to develop low self-esteem due to excessive focus on personal shortcomings, which correlates with the self-denial and meaning deficit that form the core features of Hollow Syndrome.22 Meanwhile, low self-esteem reduces individuals’ confidence in success pursuit and is correlated with a decreased level of achievement motivation,23 the intrinsic drive for people to pursue success and avoid failure,24 and insufficient achievement motivation further aggravates an individual’s sense of nihilism about life meaning, thus correlating with more severe symptoms of Hollow Syndrome.25 In addition, subjective well-being is a core concept in positive psychology, which refers to an individual’s overall evaluation of emotional experience and life quality based on self-set standards and covers three core components of life satisfaction, positive affect, and negative affect.26 This moderating mechanism can be explained by Fredrickson’s Broaden-and-Build Theory, which posits that positive emotions and well-being broaden individuals’ thought repertoires and build enduring psychological resources to resist psychological risks.27 As a positive cognitive and emotional evaluation of life quality,28 subjective well-being plays a protective role by moderating the association with negative psychological factors,29 and individuals with high subjective well-being are more likely to adopt positive cognitive styles to cope with negative emotions and stress.30

Research Hypotheses

In summary, this study proposes the following hypotheses: First, negative perfectionism predicts Hollow Syndrome. Second, self-esteem and achievement motivation may play a serial mediating role in the association between negative perfectionism and Hollow Syndrome. Third, subjective well-being may moderate the adverse associations of low self-esteem and low achievement motivation with Hollow Syndrome.

The conceptual framework of this study is constructed following the classic “risk factor – mediating mechanism – protective factor” research paradigm. Negative perfectionism is set as the distal risk factor, self-esteem and achievement motivation are set as serial mediating variables reflecting the internal psychological transmission process, and subjective well-being is set as the proximal protective factor. This framework integrates personality traits, internal psychological processes and positive psychological resources to systematically explain the occurrence and development of Hollow Syndrome.

Theoretical and Practical Value of the Study

By adopting a serial mediation model combined with moderating effect analysis, this study systematically explores the psychological mechanism underlying the association between negative perfectionism and Hollow Syndrome among medical students. Theoretically, this study constructs a serial mediation model tracing the pathway from negative perfectionism through self-esteem and achievement motivation to Hollow Syndrome, and introduces subjective well-being as the moderating variable. It enriches the research on the internal mechanism of how negative perfectionism influences Hollow Syndrome, and provides a new theoretical reference for relevant studies on Hollow Syndrome. Practically, the results of this study can offer a scientific basis for mental health educators in colleges and universities to formulate targeted intervention programs, and help improve the mental health level of medical students. Overall, this study has important theoretical and practical implications.

Methods

Study Participants and Data Collection

Study Population: The target population of this study was full-time undergraduate medical students enrolled in general medical universities in China. The study population was all full-time undergraduate medical students at Jining Medical University in Shandong Province, China.

Sampling Method: A stratified cluster sampling method was adopted. From September to December 2024, we first stratified all undergraduate medical students by major and grade, covering all medical majors across four academic years. We then randomly selected compulsory course classes within each stratum as sampling clusters, and distributed questionnaires on site.

Sample Size Calculation: Sample size was estimated based on both cross-sectional epidemiological criteria and methodological requirements for moderated serial mediation analysis. First, using the standard cross-sectional study formula , with an assumed 30% prevalence of clinically relevant Hollow Syndrome symptoms among medical students, a two-sided α of 0.05, and a permissible error d of 0.02, the minimum required sample size was calculated as 2017. Second, for bias-corrected bootstrap mediation analysis, a minimum sample size of 1000 is recommended to ensure stable estimation of indirect effects and 95% confidence intervals. Taking a 10% non-response rate into account, we planned to distribute approximately 3000 questionnaires.

Inclusion criteria: (1) Full-time undergraduate students majoring in medicine; (2) Having the cognitive ability to complete the questionnaire independently; (3) Voluntarily participating in this study and signing a written informed consent form.

Exclusion criteria: (1) Self-reported severe neurological or mental disorders; (2) Individuals who did not regularly participate in university teaching activities due to academic suspension, dropping out, or other reasons; (3) Questionnaires with an item missing rate > 20% or obvious perfunctory responses.

All research staff received standardized training in scale administration and psychological crisis identification and response. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants before questionnaire distribution. The research team provided all participants with the contact information of the university’s 24-hour Mental Health Counseling Center, including the crisis hotline and on-campus consultation channels, so that participants experiencing psychological distress could seek support at any time. On-site checks were conducted during questionnaire collection to supplement missing information as much as possible. Any participant who expressed concerns related to suicidal thoughts during the survey was immediately referred to the counseling center for professional support.

A total of 2977 questionnaires were distributed in this study, and 2850 valid questionnaires were recovered, yielding an effective response rate of 95.73%. The research protocol was approved by the Ethics Review Committee of Jining Medical University (approval number: JNMC-YX-2025-052).

Measures

Demographic Characteristics

A self-designed questionnaire was used to collect participants’ basic demographic information, including gender, age, only-child status, left-behind experience before age 18, self and parental health status, monthly living expenses, annual household income, parental education level, major fondness, major reselection willingness, and post-graduation career goals. Variable coding details are shown in Supplementary Table S1.

Chinese Hollow Syndrome Questionnaire

This study used the Chinese Hollow Syndrome Questionnaire (CHQ) to assess the level of Hollow Syndrome.5 This 26-item scale uses a 5-point Likert scale, covering six dimensions: meaning in life, depressive symptoms, interpersonal interaction, loneliness, suicidality, and self-evaluation. Nine items (items 5, 6, 7, 10, 12, 13, 14, 17, and 22) are reverse-scored, and the remaining 17 items are scored in the original direction. Total scores range from 26 to 130, with higher scores indicating milder symptoms of Hollow Syndrome. This reverse scoring direction is consistent with the original scale development and standard usage in Chinese academic literature.31 The Chinese version of the CHQ is available in Supplementary Table S2. The CHQ showed good internal consistency in this study (Cronbach’s α = 0.893), with detailed reliability and validity results reported in Supplementary Table S7 and S8.

Chinese Frost Multidimensional Perfectionism Scale

This study used the Chinese Frost Multidimensional Perfectionism Scale (CFMPS) by Zi Fei to measure participants’ negative perfectionism.32 This scale was developed with undergraduate students in Chinese mainland as the survey sample, and was further optimized and improved by Professor Zi Fei on the basis of repeated revisions and validation by previous scholars. The revised CFMPS contains 27 items across five dimensions: Concern over Mistakes, Doubts about Actions, Parental Expectations, Personal Standards, and Organization. It uses a 5-point scoring method ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree). In this study, the total scale score excluding the Organization dimension (items 2, 4, 5, 20, 22, 24), namely FMPS-Neg, was used to measure the level of negative perfectionism of participants. Higher scores indicate a higher level of negative perfectionism.14 The standardized Cronbach’s α coefficient of FMPS-Neg in this study was 0.892, showing good internal consistency; the Chinese version of the FMPS-Neg is shown in Supplementary Table S3, and detailed reliability and validity test results are presented in Supplementary Table S7 and S9.

Self-Esteem Scale

This study adopted the Self-Esteem Scale (SES) developed by Rosenberg et al in 1965 to assess participants’ self-esteem level.33 This 10-item scale uses a 4-point Likert scale, with items 1, 2, 4, 6, and 7 positively scored, and items 3, 5, 8, 9, and 10 reverse scored. Higher total scores indicate higher levels of self-esteem.34 The Chinese version of the SES used in this study is shown in Supplementary Table S4. The standardized Cronbach’s α coefficient of the SES in this study was 0.818, indicating good internal consistency. Detailed reliability and validity test results are shown in Supplementary Table S7 and S10.

Achievement Motivation Scale

This study used the Achievement Motivation Scale (AMS) originally developed by Gjesme and Nygard in 1970, with the Chinese version translated by Ye Renmin and Hagtvet in 1992, to evaluate participants’ achievement motivation.35,36 This scale consists of 30 items equally divided into two dimensions: Motive for Success (Ms) and Motive to Avoid Failure (Mf), with each item rated on a 4-point scale. The overall achievement motivation score was calculated by subtracting Mf from Ms, with higher values indicating stronger achievement motivation.37 The standardized Cronbach’s α coefficient of the AMS in this study was 0.767, showing good internal consistency; the Chinese version of the AMS is shown in Supplementary Table S5, and detailed reliability and validity test results are presented in Supplementary Table S7 and S11.

Subjective Well-Being Scale for Chinese Citizens

This study adopted the short form of the Subjective Well-being Scale For Chinese Citizens (SWBS-CC20) developed by Xing Zhanjun to adapt to the Chinese cultural context, to measure participants’ subjective well-being.38 This 20-item scale is scored on a 6-point Likert scale, including 11 reverse-scored items. In this study, higher total scores indicated stronger subjective well-being.39 The standardized Cronbach’s α coefficient of the SWBS-CC20 in this study was 0.897, showing good internal consistency; the Chinese version of the SWBS-CC20 is shown in Supplementary Table S6, and detailed reliability and validity test results are presented in Supplementary Table S7 and S12.

Data Analysis

Double data entry and logical consistency cleaning were performed with Epi Data 3.1 to ensure data accuracy.40 After independent double-check by two researchers with no discrepancies, statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS 26.0.41 Continuous variables were presented as mean ± SD, categorical variables as frequency (%). For the large sample, approximate univariate normality was defined as absolute skewness ≤ 3 and kurtosis ≤ 10.42 Levene’s test was used to assess variance homogeneity; independent samples t-test and one-way ANOVA were performed for between-group CHQ comparisons, with LSD or Tamhane’s T2 for post hoc tests.43

Pearson correlation analysis examined associations between CHQ scores and core variables. Multiple linear regression was conducted, with demographic variables showing statistical significance in univariate analysis included as covariates to control for potential confounders, along with all scale scores. Core regression assumptions were verified prior to model construction, and multicollinearity was diagnosed via variance inflation factor (VIF),44 with VIF < 10 indicating no severe multicollinearity per standard criteria.45

Serial mediation (PROCESS macro version 4.0, Model 6) and moderated serial mediation (Model 88) analyses were performed to explore the mechanism of negative perfectionism on hollow syndrome and the moderating effect of subjective well-being.46 Notably, the PROCESS macro adopts a regression-based analytical framework rather than structural equation modeling (SEM), so conventional SEM fit indices are not required or reported in this study. The model set FMPS-Neg as X, CHQ score as Y, SES and AMS as serial mediators (M1, M2), SWBS-CC20 as W (moderating M1→Y and M2→Y pathways), with demographic covariates controlled. All continuous variables were mean-centered; effect values and 95% CIs were estimated via 5000 resamples bias-corrected Bootstrap (significant if 95% CI excluded 0); HC3 heteroscedasticity-consistent standard errors were used. Significant interactions were further analyzed via simple slope analysis (W at mean ± 1 SD) with visualization plots. All analyses were two-tailed, with statistical significance set at p < 0.05.

All serial mediation and moderated mediation analyses were performed using the PROCESS macro. This analytical tool assumes a theoretical temporal order of variables but does not empirically test temporal precedence or causal relationships. All reported pathways are interpreted as statistical associations rather than causal effects, consistent with the cross-sectional study design.

Common Method Bias Test and Correlation Analysis

Common method bias was evaluated using two complementary approaches. First, Harman’s single-factor test was conducted.47 The results identified 20 factors with eigenvalues greater than 1, and the variance explained by the first factor was 21.64%, which was well below the 40% critical threshold. Second, a confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) single-factor model was performed to further validate the result, loading all items from the six scales onto a single latent factor. The model fit was extremely poor: χ2/df = 13.358, CFI = 0.461, RMSEA = 0.066, indicating that no single general factor could account for the majority of the covariation between variables. Collectively, these findings confirmed that there was no significant common method bias in the study data.

Pearson correlation analysis was performed for the five core study constructs: Hollow Syndrome, negative perfectionism, self-esteem, achievement motivation, and subjective well-being. As presented in Table 3, all constructs showed statistically significant pairwise correlations (p < 0.01). Specifically, Hollow Syndrome symptom scores were significantly positively associated with self-esteem, achievement motivation, and subjective well-being (r = 0.620, 0.563, 0.717, respectively), and significantly negatively associated with negative perfectionism (r = −0.396) (Note: Higher scores indicate milder Hollow Syndrome symptoms, so a negative correlation indicates that higher negative perfectionism is associated with more severe symptoms).

Serial Mediation Effect Analysis

To clarify the mechanism underlying the association between negative perfectionism and Hollow Syndrome in medical students, we performed serial mediation analysis using Model 6 of the PROCESS macro. Demographic variables with significant between-group differences in univariate analyses were included as covariates, including gender, left-behind experience before age 18, major fondness and reselection willingness, family annual income, parental education level, and post-graduation goals.

Mediation analysis results (Table 5) showed that the 95% CIs of the direct effect and all indirect effects did not contain 0, indicating statistically significant path relationships. Specifically, negative perfectionism had a significant total negative association with Hollow Syndrome symptom scores (β = −0.3822, P<0.001) (Note: Higher scores indicate milder Hollow Syndrome symptoms, so a negative coefficient indicates that higher negative perfectionism is associated with more severe symptoms), which was decomposed into a total direct effect (β = −0.0985, 25.77% of the total effect) and a total indirect effect (β = −0.2838, 74.26% of the total effect). The total indirect effect comprised three distinct paths: the separate mediating effect of self-esteem (negative perfectionism → self-esteem → Hollow Syndrome, β = −0.1875, 49.06%), the separate mediating effect of achievement motivation (negative perfectionism → achievement motivation → Hollow Syndrome, β = −0.0404, 10.57%), and the serial mediating effect of self-esteem and achievement motivation (negative perfectionism → self-esteem → achievement motivation → Hollow Syndrome, β = −0.0559, 14.61%). Test of Serial Mediation Effect and Analysis of the Moderating Role of Subjective Well-Being

To further examine the serial mediating role of self-esteem and achievement motivation in the association between negative perfectionism and Hollow Syndrome, as well as the moderating effect of subjective well-being on the self-esteem → Hollow Syndrome and achievement motivation → Hollow Syndrome pathways, we conducted a moderated serial mediation analysis using Model 88 of the PROCESS macro. Demographic variables with significant between-group differences in prior analyses were included as covariates.

The overall model demonstrated excellent fit, with a significant combined predictive effect of core constructs on Hollow Syndrome symptom scores (F = 129.36, p < 0.001) and an adjusted R2 of 0.595, explaining 59.5% of the variance in Hollow Syndrome. Detailed regression path coefficients for each stage are presented in Supplementary Table S15.

The specific path diagram is shown in Figure 2. First, negative perfectionism significantly negatively predicted both self-esteem (B = −0.1524) and achievement motivation (B = −0.1235), and self-esteem was a significant positive predictor of achievement motivation (B = 1.1206), confirming the significance of the first half of the serial mediation pathway. Second, both self-esteem (B = 0.7316) and achievement motivation (B = 0.2201) significantly positively predicted Hollow Syndrome, and subjective well-being also exerted a significant independent positive predictive effect on Hollow Syndrome (B = 8.7056), while the direct effect of negative perfectionism on Hollow Syndrome was not statistically significant (B = 0.0032, see Supplementary Table S15), suggesting that the association between negative perfectionism and Hollow Syndrome was fully mediated by indirect pathways. Finally, the interaction terms of subjective well-being with self-esteem (B = −0.1994) and achievement motivation (B = −0.0513) both significantly negatively predicted Hollow Syndrome, indicating that subjective well-being exerted a significant moderating effect on both the self-esteem → Hollow Syndrome and achievement motivation → Hollow Syndrome pathways.

Discussion

This study took medical students as the research subjects, preliminarily explored the effects of demographic variables on their hollow syndrome (CHQ), systematically discussed the psychological mechanism of negative perfectionism acting on hollow syndrome, and verified the serial mediating effect of self-esteem and achievement motivation, as well as the moderating effect of subjective well-being.

Influencing Factors of Hollow Syndrome in Medical Students

Effects of Demographic Variables on Hollow Syndrome in Medical Students

Regression analysis showed that gender, left-behind experience, and professional identity had statistically significant effects on Hollow Syndrome symptom scores. Male students presented milder Hollow Syndrome symptoms than female students. This may be explained by gender differences in parental expectations for thinking, expression and ability development,49 as well as male students’ greater access to high-quality educational resources.50 Both factors can boost achievement motivation and thus reduce Hollow Syndrome risk. Medical students with childhood left-behind experience had more severe Hollow Syndrome symptoms, as the lack of early parent-child accompaniment may contribute to traits including low self-worth and emotional alienation,51 increasing their vulnerability to Hollow Syndrome under the stress of medical education. Notably, age showed no significant effect in this study, likely because most participants were undergraduates aged 18–21 years, facing homogeneous academic tasks with no prominent age-related differences.

In this study, major fondness and willingness to reselect the same major were used to reflect medical students’ professional identity. Existing studies have confirmed that higher professional identity correlates with stronger self-efficacy and achievement motivation in medical students,52 and our results further verified that higher professional identity was associated with milder Hollow Syndrome symptoms. The core features of Hollow Syndrome (lack of meaning, vague life purpose, and self-worth confusion) can be effectively buffered by high professional identity across three dimensions: it helps students gain intrinsic meaning and well-being from patient care and health protection work,53 fosters clear and stable career goals to alleviate purpose ambiguity,54 and enhances professional belonging and self-worth by linking personal development to the social value of the medical profession.55

Direct Effect of Negative Perfectionism on Hollow Syndrome in Medical Students

Correlation analysis results showed that medical students with higher negative perfectionism had more obvious Hollow Syndrome symptoms, which verified Hypothesis 1. This result is consistent with existing studies confirming that negative perfectionism is closely related to individual psychological maladjustment.56,57 Medical education emphasizes precision, rigor and standardization, which may promote the formation of perfectionist traits in medical students. However, individuals with negative perfectionism pay excessive attention to their performance defects, and are more prone to self-denial and value confusion under heavy academic and professional pressure, thereby correlating with more severe core Hollow Syndrome symptoms such as lack of meaning and vague purpose.58 Notably, the direct association between negative perfectionism and Hollow Syndrome was non-significant with a near-zero effect value in the moderated serial mediation analysis, suggesting that its association with Hollow Syndrome is mainly realized through indirect mediating paths. This also confirms the consensus that personality traits are associated with mental health through intermediate psychological processes.59

Serial Mediating Effects of Self-Esteem and Achievement Motivation on Hollow Syndrome

The serial mediation effect analysis showed that negative perfectionism is associated with Hollow Syndrome through three distinct pathways, with the total indirect effect accounting for 74.26%, which verified Hypothesis 2. Among them, the separate pathway from negative perfectionism to Hollow Syndrome via self-esteem made the largest contribution (49.06%), the serial pathway from negative perfectionism to Hollow Syndrome through sequential mediators of self-esteem and achievement motivation accounted for 14.61%, and the separate pathway from negative perfectionism to Hollow Syndrome via achievement motivation accounted for 10.57%. This result clearly reveals the psychological transmission mechanism of the association between negative perfectionism and Hollow Syndrome, and indicates that negative perfectionism is mainly linked to a higher risk of Hollow Syndrome alongside lower self-esteem among medical students.

First, negative perfectionism has a negative predictive effect on self-esteem. Individuals with negative perfectionism tend to judge themselves by extreme standards and excessively amplify their own shortcomings. This continuous self-criticism correlates with lower sense of self-worth and is associated with a state of low self-esteem.60 For medical students, the difficult academic goals and clinical practice requirements with extremely low fault tolerance further aggravate the fear of failure in individuals with negative perfectionism, resulting in continuous impairment of their self-esteem level.

Second, self-esteem positively predicts achievement motivation. Individuals with high self-esteem are full of confidence in their own abilities and are more willing to actively pursue success goals; while medical students with low self-esteem tend to avoid challenges in academic and career development due to lack of self-identity, leading to a reduced level of achievement motivation.61 Finally, the higher the level of achievement motivation, the less likely individuals are to develop Hollow Syndrome. This essentially reveals that the characteristics of clear sense of purpose and sufficient motivation possessed by high achievement motivation are in sharp contrast to the internal manifestations of vague sense of purpose and weakened motivation in Hollow Syndrome. Achievement motivation is the intrinsic motivation of individuals to pursue success and avoid failure. Medical students with low achievement motivation are difficult to establish a clear goal system in professional learning and career planning, and then fall into the state of Hollow Syndrome with meaningless learning and directionless future.

The serial mediating chain of negative perfectionism, self-esteem, achievement motivation, and Hollow Syndrome fully delineates the underlying transmission mechanism linking perfectionist traits to the syndrome. Negative perfectionism correlates with lower achievement motivation alongside eroded psychological foundation of self-esteem,62 and ultimately increases the risk of Hollow Syndrome. This transmission process is more specific in the context of medical education. Each stage of medical students’ study is accompanied by strict assessment requirements. Among individuals with negative perfectionism, lower self-esteem is associated with poorer stress coping and lower achievement motivation,63 forming a vicious cycle of accumulated academic stress, persistent self-denial, diminished achievement motivation, and pervasive meaning deficit, which ultimately traps students in the dilemma of Hollow Syndrome.

Moderating Effect of Subjective Well-Being on Hollow Syndrome

The results of the moderated serial mediation analysis further showed that subjective well-being exerted a significant moderating effect on both the pathway from SES to Hollow Syndrome and the pathway from AMS to Hollow Syndrome. With the increase of subjective well-being level, the absolute values of the effect sizes of the three indirect paths of the association between negative perfectionism and Hollow Syndrome showed a gradual decreasing trend, which verified Hypothesis 3. Subjective well-being comprehensively reflects people’s living conditions and mental health.64 Its core protective feature is that individuals with high subjective well-being tend to adopt adaptive emotion regulation strategies, which moderate the adverse association between negative psychological factors and mental health.65 This study further confirmed that this effect has good applicability in the medical student group.

Specifically, for the mediating pathway from self-esteem to Hollow Syndrome, high subjective well-being can buffer the adverse association between low self-esteem (associated with negative perfectionism) and worsened Hollow Syndrome symptoms. Negative perfectionism continuously erodes the self-esteem level of medical students through extreme self-evaluation standards, and then leads them into a situation of lack of self-worth. While high subjective well-being can help medical students improve their self-evaluation system and weaken the self-denial thinking brought by negative perfectionism,62 thus correlating with less decline in Hollow Syndrome symptom scores and lower risk of Hollow Syndrome.

For the mediating pathway from achievement motivation to Hollow Syndrome, high subjective well-being can similarly buffer the adverse association tied to low achievement motivation, which itself is indirectly linked to negative perfectionism through reduced self-esteem. Even if medical students are in a state of low achievement motivation due to the influence of negative perfectionism, subjective well-being can help individuals gain pleasure from the phased progress of their studies,62 or link the professional value of medicine with the social contribution of protecting lives, thereby finding a sense of meaning and direction in theoretical learning and clinical practice, maintaining relatively stable Hollow Syndrome symptom scores, and avoiding falling into the state of Hollow Syndrome with no goals and no motivation due to insufficient motivation.

The visualization results of simple slope analysis further verified the above moderating effects: at the same level of self-esteem or achievement motivation, medical students in the high subjective well-being group had significantly higher Hollow Syndrome symptom scores (ie, milder symptoms of Hollow Syndrome). This result fully indicates that subjective well-being can provide key psychological resilience resources for medical students. Its moderating association does not directly change the personality trait of negative perfectionism, nor simply improve the level of self-esteem or achievement motivation, but effectively buffers the cascading adverse association from negative perfectionism to diminished self-esteem and then to more severe Hollow Syndrome symptoms, as well as the longer serial chain from negative perfectionism to low self-esteem, reduced achievement motivation, and ultimately worsened symptoms, by weakening the strength of both core mediating pathways.

This finding also complements and improves the overall model of the association between negative perfectionism and Hollow Syndrome in medical students, and confirms that subjective well-being is an important protective factor for guarding the mental health of medical students and resisting Hollow Syndrome.

From a global theoretical perspective, Hollow Syndrome overlaps partially with two well-established cross-disciplinary constructs. For one, it mirrors the existential vacuum outlined in Frankl’s logotherapy, with shared core traits of pervasive meaninglessness, inner emptiness and a lack of clear life purposes.3 For another, it bears similarities to Durkheim’s anomie, where blurred social norms and shifting societal structures lead to fragmented personal value systems and goal confusion.4 Even so, Hollow Syndrome remains a culturally specific indigenous construct molded by distinctive academic pressure, intense social competition and the unique cultural environment of contemporary Chinese youth, and cannot be fully equated with either of the two Western theories. This study supplies empirical evidence for this localized mental health phenomenon and deepens worldwide academic comprehension of psychological struggles among young people in transitional societies.

Limitations

Several limitations of this study should be noted. First, this is a cross-sectional design. Although we adopted a theoretical temporal order for the serial mediation and moderated mediation analyses via the PROCESS macro, this tool only assumes variable sequencing and cannot verify temporal precedence or establish causal relationships. Therefore, all findings in this study are interpreted as statistical associations rather than causal effects. Longitudinal studies are needed in future research to clarify potential causal pathways. Second, this study recruited participants exclusively from a single top-tier medical university in eastern China, which limits generalizability. Furthermore, our findings are embedded in the unique context of Chinese medical education, which restricts direct extrapolation to international medical student populations. Future multi-site and cross-cultural studies are needed to verify the generalizability of our model. Third, all data were collected via self-report questionnaires, which may introduce potential common method bias. Fourth, convergent validity for some scale dimensions fell below the conventional 0.50 threshold. While composite reliability for all dimensions was acceptable, lower AVE values may have slightly attenuated the observed associations between variables. Future research should further refine these measures for use in Chinese medical student populations to improve their psychometric properties.

Conclusion

This study established a well-fitted multiple linear regression model for Hollow Syndrome in Chinese medical students (R2 = 0.586), explaining 58.6% of its variance. We confirmed that negative perfectionism is associated with more severe Hollow Syndrome via the serial mediating pathways of self-esteem and achievement motivation. Notably, the direct effect of negative perfectionism on Hollow Syndrome became non-significant (B = 0.0032, p = 0.8391) after including the two mediators, indicating a full mediation pattern. This finding carries clear practical implications: interventions do not need to fundamentally alter perfectionist personality traits, which are highly prevalent and even partially adaptive in medical education. Instead, the priority should be to protect self-esteem and achievement motivation from the adverse impact of negative perfectionism. Subjective well-being acts as a key protective factor that moderates this adverse association.

These findings clarify the psychological mechanism underlying the association between negative perfectionism and Hollow Syndrome among medical students, and propose a dual “burden reduction and empowerment enhancement” intervention strategy. Specifically, “burden reduction” does not refer to lowering academic standards or reducing clinical training requirements, but to alleviating the psychological burden brought by negative perfectionism, such as excessive fear of failure, relentless self-criticism and over-concern about mistakes. This framework guides students to establish a rational attitude towards flaws and setbacks in medical learning, without compromising the rigorous training standards required for clinical competence. “Empowerment enhancement” builds a multi-dimensional protection system by improving self-esteem, achievement motivation and subjective well-being, providing a theoretical basis and actionable targets for precise mental health promotion and Hollow Syndrome prevention in medical students. It should be noted that all directional pathways proposed in this study are theoretically derived, and the cross-sectional design cannot confirm causal relationships; all findings should be interpreted as statistical associations.
