Helicobacter pylori in esophageal carcinogenesis: Potential culprit or forceful defender?
Authors: Dan Ren, Manli Cui, Duo Yang, Yu Zhang, Yuanzhu Hao, Yixing Yue, Bin Wei, QingLing Fan, Chao Sun, Qian Li, Mingxin Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTHelicobacter pylori (H. pylori), a recognized gastric pathogen, has been extensively documented pertaining to its association with a spectrum of gastrointestinal disorders. Given its predominant dissemination via fecal-oral and oral–oral pathways, H. pylori colonization experiences transient esophageal transit prior to its gastric mucosal establishment, while reflux-mediated recrudescence may result in repetitive exposure. The interplay and causality between H. pylori infection and the onset and development of esophageal carcinoma (EC) has emerged as an area of intense scrutiny. Substantial evidence indicates subtype-dependent associations. H. pylori infection (particularly cytotoxin-associated gene A (CagA)-positive strains) reduces the risk of esophageal adenocarcinoma. However, its relationship with esophageal squamous cell carcinoma remains controversial, with studies reporting positive impact, irrelevance, or even an inverse correlation. This review elucidates H. pylori interactions with EC, summarizes protective and pathogenic mechanisms, discerns extant controversies and clinical implications, to inform novel therapeutic strategies for EC prevention and management.KEYWORDS: H. pylori, esophageal carcinoma, esophageal adenocarcinoma, esophageal squamous cell carcinoma, pathogenesis, protective mechanisms

Introduction

Esophageal carcinomas (ECs) rank as the ninth most common malignancy globally alongside the sixth-leading cause of cancer-related mortality [1,2]. In terms of histopathological characteristics, ECs are principally divided into two main subtypes: Esophageal Adenocarcinoma (EAC) and Esophageal Squamous Cell Carcinoma (ESCC). These two subtypes exhibit marked differences concerning epidemiological distribution, risk factors, and underpinning pathogenic mechanisms. In the United States, EAC accounts for around 64% of all ECs cases, while ESCC constitutes the rest 31%. In contrast, ESCC is predominantly identified in Asia, representing approximately 90% of ECs in China, though the incidence of EAC has been gradually but evidently rising attributable to wide adoption of westernized dietary patterns [3]. In recent decades, EAC is estimated to comprise nearly half of all EC cases [4]. Although endoscopic therapy effectively improves clinical outcomes for patients with early-stage EC, the majorities are diagnosed at advanced stages, resulting in therapeutic difficulties, high costs, diminished quality of life, and poor survival conditions [5,6]. Therefore, it is imperative to elucidate the etiology and protective factors of EC to optimize the prevention and refine the treatment strategies.

H. pylori, the most prevalent chronic gastric pathogen, has been definitively linked to the presence of peptic ulcer disease, chronic gastritis, and gastric carcinoma since its discovery in 1982 [7], leading to its classification as a Group 1 carcinogen by the World Health Organization [8]. However, H. pylori may exhibit a remarkably paradoxical role in the context of EC. Current epidemiological studies implicate [9] that H. pylori infection is linked to with the pathogenesis of ECs. However, investigations delving into its underlying mechanisms remain limited without definite conclusions. Over the past three decades, global decline in H. pylori infection rates have coincided with a rising trend in EAC incidence contemporarily [10], suggesting that H. pylori infection may exert a potential microbial defense against EAC pathogenesis [11]. Multiple studies have shown that H. pylori infection, especially with CagA-positive strains, is inversely related to EAC risk. This protection may be mediated by reduced gastric acid secretion, decreased incidence of gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD), and induction of apoptosis in Barrett’s esophagus (BE) cells, known as a premalignant precursor of EAC. Conversely, the association between H. pylori and ESCC remains under debate: some studies uncover that infection may aggravate the ESCC progression through diverse pathways including chronic inflammation, enhanced nitrosamine activation, as well as miRNA dysregulation, but other reports have implied irrelevance or even an inverse correlation (i.e. H. pylori counteract ESCC) (Figure 1). This subtype-specific heterogeneity likely results from complex interactions among strain-specific virulence factors (e.g. CagA), host genetic polymorphisms, and environmental exposures (e.g. smoking, high-temperature food consumption).

Collectively, we sought to synthesizes the current epidemiological evidence and underpinning molecular and cellular mechanisms to comprehensively delineate the link between H. pylori infection and different ECs subtypes, address potential defensive and pathogenic pathways, and evaluate confounders impacting the extant findings, therefore providing a theoretical foundation pertinent to precision-based prevention and therapeutic strategies.

Methodology of literature review

Although this is a narrative review, a systematic approach was employed to identify and select relevant literature to minimize selection bias and enhance reproducibility. The literature search was performed using the electronic databases PubMed/MEDLINE, Embase, and Web of Science Core Collection. The search covered the period from January 2000 up to October 2025 to capture the most relevant modern evidence. Search strategies were built around key terms: (“Helicobacter pylori ” OR “H. pylori ”) AND (“esophageal cancer” OR “esophageal adenocarcinoma” OR “esophageal squamous cell carcinoma”) AND (“risk” OR “carcinogenesis” OR “pathogenesis” OR “protection” OR “mechanism”). The search was limited to studies published in English. Additionally, the reference lists of included articles and recent review papers were manually searched for additional publications that might have been missed in the initial electronic search. The final selection of studies for inclusion was based on their relevance to the central theme of discussing the dual role of H. pylori in esophageal carcinogenesis.

Variations across the epidemiological links between H. pylori and ECs subtypes

The epidemiological associations between H. pylori infection and ECs subtypes appear to be markedly heterogeneous. This variation is not only observed between EAC and ESCC but also influenced by geographical disparities, study design variations, and population-specific characteristics [12]. Recent advancements in clinical studies to discern the H. pylori-EC relationship have increasingly uncovered this multifactorial nature.

The inverse correlation between H. pylori infection and EAC

Multiple epidemiological studies have consistently verified a strong negative correlation between H. pylori infection and the risk of EAC. Notably, this phenomenon is particularly pronounced in the Western countries. Since the late 20th century, as H. pylori infection rates have dramatically declined, a concomitant rise in EAC incidence has been observed and recorded [10]. This ecological inverse suggests that H. pylori colonization may confer a potential protection against EAC pathogenesis. A large-scale study involving 4471 subjects [13] has unveiled a notable inverse relationship between H. pylori infection, particularly with CagA-positive strains, and the risk of BE. This association may be linked to reduced GERD incidence, a major risk factor for BE, thereby interrupting the pathological transition from GERD to BE, and in turn EAC.

Furthermore, large-scale meta-analyses have further validated the aforesaid findings. A pooled analysis conducted by Zhuo et al. [14] demonstrated that H. pylori-infected individuals exhibited a 42% reduced risk of EAC relative to uninfected subjects (95% CI: 0.48–0.70, p < 0.01). The findings from Islami et al. [12] showed that infected CagA-positive strains was indicative of a reduced risk of EAC (OR = 0.41; 95% CI: 0.28–0.62). Consistently, Xie et al. [15] found that H. pylori infection was inversely associated with the risk of EAC, conferring an overall 41% reduction where enhanced protection was more pronounced in terms of CagA-positive strains infection (44% reduction). Another study by S. Nie et al. [16] yielded analogous conclusions. A 2019 meta-analysis by Gao et al. [17] also confirmed this robustness between H. pylori infection and reduced EAC risk, further verified in a most recent publication [18].

Geographical disparities have also contributed to the link between H. pylori infection and EAC. Geographically, regions with lower H. pylori prevalence (e.g. North America, Australia, Europe) exhibited higher EAC incidence rates, which was in agreement with the epidemiological trends of GERD and BE [10]. However, this inverse correlation is less definitive in reports from the non-Western countries. For instance, in some developing nations like Malaysia with low H. pylori endemicity, EAC incidence rates were absent of a concomitant rise despite declining infection rates, suggesting a pivotal role of geographical factors [19]. The complicated relationship between H. pylori and ESCC

Current evidence indicates that, unlike EAC, the association between H. pylori infection and ESCC demonstrates dramatical heterogeneity. Specifically, studies have yielded divergent outcomes. This variation likely stems from the complicated interplay concerning strain-specific characteristics, geographical disparities, and confounders. Because ESCC development involves multiple-risk factors, including but not limited to smoking, alcohol consumption, hot food/beverage intake, and obesity, H. pylori infection may influence carcinogenesis through synergistic interactions with other risk factors, contributing substantially to the high heterogeneity observed across the existing studies.

Evidence supporting a positive association

An observational study [20] found that H. pylori, particularly CagA-positive strains, was significantly associated with ESCC. The infection rates were markedly higher in tumorous and adjacent tissues (62.5% in ESCC, 74.1% in para-cancerous tissues) compared to normal tissues (26.7%). Mechanistically, H. pylori may lead to esophageal epithelial atypical hyperplasia and consequent carcinogenesis by inducing DNA strand breaks, oxidative damage, and chronic inflammation [21]. A 2023 study in Thailand identified a link between H. pylori infection and increased EC risk, with CagA-positive strains demonstrating a heightened risk of ESCC, indicating strain-specific virulence factors to exert pro-carcinogenic impacts [22].

Evidence of non-significant association

A case-control study on ECs by Zhuo et al. [14] demonstrated no significant link between H. pylori infection and ESCC risk (95% CI: 0.45–1.43, p > 0.05) compared to controls. Furthermore, studies conducted by Kamangar et al. [12] and Xie et al. [15] consistently reported no significant correlation. A large-scale meta-analysis by Gao et al. [17] analyzed 35 studies with 345,886 patients, demonstrated that overall, H. pylori infection was neither a significant protective nor a risk factor for ESCC (OR = 0.84; 95% CI: 0.64–1.09). Notably, this analysis revealed notable regional variations (e.g. a negative association in the Middle East) and substantial heterogeneity (I2 = 78.5%), suggesting that their findings may be modified by factors such as population characteristics.

The inverse association hypothesis

Khoshbaten et al. [23] denoted statistically significant differences in serum H. pylori IgG antibody titers between ESCC patients and counterparts (p = 0.002), indicating a potential protective role of H. pylori infection against ESCC development, but the underlying molecular mechanisms were elusive. A meta-analysis by Xie et al. [15] revealed an inverse correlation between H. pylori infection and the risk of ESCC among Eastern populations (OR = 0.66,95%CI: 0.43–0.89) alongside significant heterogeneity (p < 0.01, I2 = 79.50%). Notably, infection with CagA-positive strains may leverage a reduced risk of ESCC on Eastern populations (OR = 0.77,95%CI: 0.65–0.92) rather than the western counterparts. Accumulating evidence has supported this hypothesis and clarified a notable association between leukocytosis (p = 0.023), anemia (p = 0.045), and H. pylori infection in ESCC patients [24].

Potential mechanisms

H. pylori and EAC

H. pylori infection is significantly associated with a protective microbial phenotype against EAC. Epidemiological studies have consistently established an inverse relationship between H. pylori infection, especially CagA-positive strains, and EAC risk, with potential mechanisms involving multiple biological pathways: suppression of gastric acid secretion, reduction in GERD incidence, modulation of metabolic hormones, and specific pro-apoptotic effects (Table 1, Figure 3 and 5).

Potential mechanisms of H. pylori in EAC and ESCC.

Reduced gastric acid secretion linked to decreased risk of GERD

H. pylori infection induces gastric corpus atrophy, reducing parietal cell mass and acid secretion, which culminates in a hypo-acidic state that diminishes gastroesophageal reflux frequency and acidity, thereby lowering GERD incidence and subsequent EAC risk [25,26]. This pathway is corroborated by a large-scale meta-analysis [27], confirming that the GERD reduction interrupts the neoplastic sequence from GERD through BE to EAC [28].

Regulation of serum ghrelin (growth hormone-releasing peptide) levels

H. pylori infection may embrace indirect protective influences through modulating metabolism-associated hormones on esophageal epithelia, particularly ghrelin, a pivotal hormone regulating appetite and lipid metabolism, whose elevation may promote obesity development [29]. Obesity is a critical risk factor for GERD and EAC. Central obesity can elevate intra-abdominal and intragastric pressure, thereby exacerbating gastroesophageal reflux and facilitating EAC progression [26]. It has been hypothesized that post-H. pylori eradication therapy, the significant elevation in serum ghrelin may promote obesity by stimulating lipogenesis and suppressing lipolysis, which could subsequently impair lower esophageal sphincter (LES) function to increase susceptibility to GERD, and potential advance to EAC in obese subjects [30]. Accordingly, H. pylori infection is linked to decreased ghrelin levels, which may inhibit obesity-associated GERD risk, and in consequence lowering the incidence of EAC.

Mechanisms counteracting chronic inflammation, carcinogenesis, or directly inducing apoptosis

H. pylori may attenuate chronic inflammation and carcinogenesis by downregulating virulence factors (e.g. CagA, VacA) [31]. Additionally, it upregulates Fas receptor expression in BE-derived cells, activating caspase-mediated apoptosis to confer host protection [32].

In contrast, evidence also supports the notion that H. pylori infection may elevate the risk of EAC. Kountouras et al. demonstrated that H. pylori infection increases insulin resistance, induces metabolic syndrome and obesity, thereby further elevating EAC susceptibility [33]. The differences between the aforesaid findings and current meta-analyses can ascribe to critically methodological limitations, including failure to adjust for potential confounders, incomplete exclusion of competing risk factors, and inadequate evaluation of heterogeneity across included studies. Collectively, the “true” correlation regarding the H. pylori-EAC sequence necessitates further investigations with rigorous confounding control and stratified analyses.

H. pylori and ESCC

The relationship between H. pylori infection and ESCC exhibits a complex “bidirectional interaction” profile: while H. pylori may directly drive ESCC progression through chronic inflammation or epigenetic dysregulation, a minority of studies have proposed a potential protective hypothesis, representing limited supportive evidence and substantially ongoing debate (Table 1, Figure 4 and 5).

Protective mechanisms of H. pylori infection against ESCC

The protective effects of H. pylori infection against ESCC are analogous to abovementioned mechanisms in the context of EAC due to reduced gastric acid secretion to decrease gastroesophageal reflux and chronic inflammation, ultimately lowering ESCC risk [34]. Furthermore, research have indicated that H. pylori infection may modify the tumor immune microenvironment in patients with ESCC, specifically manifested by a reduction in CD103+ CD4+T lymphocyte populations and an expansion of CD45RA-CD25hi regulatory T cells (Tregs), orchestrating an immunosuppressive milieu to attenuate neoplastic progression [35].

Pathogenic mechanisms of H. pylori infection in ESCC

Indirect pathogenic mechanisms

H. pylori-induced gastric mucosal atrophy and hypo-acidic state promote the overgrowth of non-H. pylori acid-resistant bacteria, thereby increasing the synthesis of carcinogenic N-nitroso compounds (NOCs). These carcinogens adhere to the esophageal epithelia during gastroesophageal reflux or hematogenous dissemination, and by causing DNA damage and chronic inflammation, they become significant pathogenic factors in ESCC [36]. Notably, high red meat consumption further exacerbates NOC exposure, synergizing with H. pylori infection to collectively increase the carcinogenic risk of ESCC [37]. Additionally, H. pylori infection upregulates the expression of miR-196a, which suppresses the tumor-suppressive long non-coding RNA GAS5 (lncRNA GAS5), ultimately promoting ESCC progression [38,39].

Direct pathogenic mechanisms

Activation of the CagA/SHP2 pathway: The CagA protein binds to host SHP2 phosphatase, thereby triggering the RAS-mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) signaling cascade and driving aberrant cellular proliferation [40]. Furthermore, lipopolysaccharide (LPS) originated from H. pylori binds to Toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4) on ESCC cells, activating the ERK/p38 MAPK pathway to accelerate neoplastic proliferation [41].

Potential mechanisms

The gut microbiota constitutes a major component belonging to the human microbiome, forming diverse communities that colonize the gastrointestinal tract [42]. It catabolizes nutrients to enhance absorption, suppresses pathogens, reinforces intestinal barrier integrity, and modulates immune responses [43]. Additionally, it may indirectly participate in carcinogenesis by influencing local and systemic immune activities [44]. H. pylori infection contributes to colorectal carcinogenesis by triggering intestinal immune dysregulation and fostering a mucus-degrading microbial profile [45]. Similarly, H. pylori infection induces intestinal dysbiosis [46], thereby giving rise to chronic inflammatory insults [47], immune microenvironment perturbations [48], and metabolomic profile alterations [49]. Current investigations concentrated on the microbiota derived from EC cases remain scarce. For example, Elliott et al. [50] and Snider et al. [51] argued that microbial diversity in EAC samples is reduced compared to control groups. It has also been proposed [52] that H. pylori may modulate the esophageal microbiota, which could subsequently trigger pro-tumorigenic immune phenotype; however, the mechanical basis underlying this interaction remains elusive. Deshpande et al. [53] addressed that proton pump inhibitor (PPI) usage negatively impacts the microbial composition in the esophagus, a phenomenon mechanistically linked to reduced gastric acidity levels.

Future research directions

Taken together, these investigations currently represent an unexplored nexus in the field of esophageal microbiome research, as there is a lack of studies systematically assessing how host H. pylori status contributes to esophageal microbial community heterogeneity. Thus, future investigations must prioritize comprehensive profiling of the esophageal microbiota to establish novel treatment options for more effective management of patients with ECs. Building on these identified gaps, future studies should address the preliminary nature of current evidence on H. pylori-esophageal microbiome interactions, specifically focusing on: (1) longitudinal cohorts to track temporal dynamics between infection and microbial shifts; (2) mechanistic models (e.g. organoids) to validate causal pathways such as NOC-mediated DNA damage; and (3) interventional trials evaluating post-eradication microbial changes. This framework underscores that present knowledge is hypothesis-generating, aiming to elucidate these interactions through rigorous validation rather than asserting premature conclusions.

Implications for clinical practice and public health policy

The complex, dual role of H. pylori in esophageal carcinogenesis necessitates a refined and population-specific approach to eradication policies [54]. In Western populations with a high incidence of EAC, caution is warranted regarding mass eradication efforts due to the potential loss of its protective effect against EAC [55]; instead, targeted eradication in high-risk individuals – such as those with a family history of gastric cancer or active peptic ulcer disease – is recommended [56]. In Eastern Asian regions where ESCC predominates, eradication decisions should continue to prioritize the well-established benefits for gastric cancer prevention, given the unclear association with ESCC risk. In resource-limited settings, programmatic efforts should integrate cost-effective dietary and lifestyle interventions alongside selective eradication, balancing potential risks and benefits for both gastric and esophageal diseases. Long-term monitoring of esophageal outcomes following eradication remains essential to inform and adapt future policy recommendations.

Extant controversies and clinical implications

Indefinite causality

Some scholars have posited that the inverse correlation between H. pylori infection and EAC may stem from the gastric mucosal atrophy-induced “pseudo-protective” effects. In patients with atrophic gastritis, diminished gastric acid secretion impedes H. pylori colonization, leading to false-negative H. pylori detection results – rather than reflecting the intrinsic protective properties of H. pylori [7,23]. For instance, a prospective cohort study conducted by Wiklund et al. clarified that EAC incidence dose not significantly increase in response to H. pylori eradication, implying observed inverse relationship between H. pylori infection and EAC risk to be masked by gastric mucosal atrophy. It is imperative to carry out prospective studies with long-term monitoring of baseline H. pylori colonization to validate this causality.

Geographical and strain heterogeneity

The pathogenicity of H. pylori is strongly associated with strain-specific virulence factor distributions, and geographical disparities given that the prevalence of key virulence factor CagA may critically contribute to conflicting findings between the Eastern and Western regions. The EPIYA motif of CagA exhibits significant polymorphism, serving as a conserved tyrosine phosphorylation site. Upon phosphorylation, it binds the host SHP-2 phosphatase, thereby activating MAPK signaling pathways and inducing morphological changes and dysregulated proliferation in host cells. The East Asian (e.g. China, Japan, South Korea) EPIYA motifs predominantly comprise the ABD type, characterized by a stronger SHP2-binding capacity that activates more robust pro-inflammatory signaling pathways and exhibits more pronounced oncogenic potential. In contrast, the Western (e.g. Europe, North America, Australia) EPIYA motifs are the ABC type predominance, connected to severe gastritis and precancerous lesions as indicated by weaker carcinogenic capability [57] (Figure 6). This discrepancy may account for the protection of CagA-positive strains against ESCC observed in the East Asian studies, while studies from the Southeast Asian (e.g. Thailand) have reported positive associations between CagA positivity and elevated ESCC risk, owing to endemic strain subtypes or environmental synergistic interactions.

Beyond strain virulence and environmental exposures, the geographical variation in the H. pylori-ESCC association may be further elucidated by differing emphasis on infection status versus its long-term pathological sequelae. A Japanese nationwide cohort study demonstrated that gastric atrophy extent – not infection status itself – was strongly associated with ESCC risk in high-incidence regions, highlighting the importance of mucosal changes (e.g. atrophy, hypochlorhydria) over mere bacterial presence [58]. This finding is supported by a large-scale meta-analysis (Li et al., 2024) showing chronic atrophic gastritis significantly increases ESCC risk (pooled OR = 2.29) [59], especially when diagnosed histologically, and by a population-based study in China (Gao et al., 2021) where atrophy-defined serologically was more strongly linked to esophagogastric junction cancer (OR = 2.36) than H. pylori seropositivity alone (OR = 1.95) [60]. Further evidence from the Golestan Cohort Study indicates metabolic consequences of atrophy (e.g. vitamin B12 deficiency) contribute to ESCC risk [61], while the Linxian Trial underscores the multifactorial nature of risk where atrophy amplifies effects of other factors (HR = 3.06 for high-risk score) [62]. Collectively, these studies confirm that regional differences in the H. pylori-ESCC relationship are driven primarily by atrophy-related pathways rather than infection status, necessitating a shift in prevention strategies toward monitoring mucosal changes and implementing integrated interventions.

Clinical decision-making trade-offs

Although H. pylori eradication reduces gastric cancer risk, its impact on esophageal cancer remains undetermined. Doorakkers et al. [63] found that H. pylori eradication therapy reduces the risk of developmental ECs, whereas other study [34] stated no significant impact on EAC incidence following H. pylori eradication. It should be kept in mind that antibiotic therapy achieves a 93% of eradication efficacy against H. pylori, which is prone to diminish esophageal microbiota diversity, potentially increasing the risk of dysbiosis-associated esophageal pathologies; however, this conclusion is primarily based on murine models, and data from human cohorts warrant further validation [64]. The existing literature has unveiled that H. pylori infection and the esophageal microbiota share an intricate and clinically consequential mechanistic link. In this regard, ECs colonized by H. pylori CagA-positive strains exhibit a negative correlation with subsequent EAC risk. This implies that H. pylori absence within the gastroesophageal mucosal niche may instigate dysbiotic compositional shifts in the esophageal microbiota, thereby potentiating oncogenic progression [65].

When assessing the association between PPI use and the risk of EC, it is essential to balance the benefits of acid suppression against potential risks. PPIs are highly effective in controlling symptoms of GERD, which is a well-established risk factor for EAC. Thus, PPIs may confer a protective effect through this mechanism [66]. On the other hand, evidence suggests that long-term PPI use may alter the microbiota [67], potentially creating an environment conducive to carcinogenesis. Large-scale studies indicate that prolonged PPI therapy might be associated with an increased risk of EAC, though no significant association has been observed with ESCC [68,69]. It should be noted, however, that the role of PPIs in esophageal adenocarcinogenesis remains controversial. For instance, the gastrin antagonist netazepide has not been shown to improve BE, and current clinical guidelines continue to endorse PPI therapy for its management [70], highlighting that the underlying mechanisms remain incompletely understood.

Current evidence remains limited, and no definitive causal relationship between PPI use and EAC has been established. Clinical decision-making should therefore involve comprehensive consideration of the individual patient’s risk-benefit profile, including the severity of GERD symptoms, the presence of Barrett’s esophagus, and other risk factors.

This principle of balancing risks and benefits equally applies to the management of H. pylori infection, another modulator of the gastroesophageal environment. Current guidelines require optimization that incorporates the dual nature of H. pylori infection [54]. In regions with a high gastric cancer burden, population-based eradication strategies remain necessary. However, in Western populations with a high incidence of esophageal adenocarcinoma, guidelines should caution against mass eradication due to potential risks associated with diminishing its protective effect, advocating instead for a targeted eradication strategy focused on high-risk individuals [71]. Future guidelines should integrate evidence on this gastroesophageal dual effect to provide differentiated recommendations tailored to distinct epidemiological contexts.

Environmental and behavioral confounding factors

The association between H. pylori infection and EC is significantly confounded by diverse environmental and behavioral factors via multiple patterns including direct carcinogenic effects, host immunomodulation, or their synergistic interactions.

Smoking and alcohol consumption

Prior epidemiological evidence has corroborated that tobacco smoking (including various forms of tobacco use) and alcohol consumption (particularly heavy intake) are well-built risk factors for EC [72], in particular, ESCC. Their carcinogenic mechanisms – such as polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbon (PAH) exposure and acetaldehyde metabolites – may represent a “double-edged sword” with H. pylori infection’s pathogenicity, requiring rigorous adjustment in analyses evaluating the H. pylori-ESCC relationship [73].

Dietary factors

Dietary factors significantly influence EC, and their interplay with H. pylori infection can greatly impact the development of EC subtypes [74]. Current evidence indicates that nitrosamine exposure represents a significant precipitating issue for the development of ESCC, and H. pylori infection may potentiate the biological impacts of dietary carcinogens through gastric microenvironmental modifications. Latent class analysis revealed that a Western dietary pattern characterized by high red meat and low fruit/vegetable intake significantly elevates ESCC risk, whereas a health-conscious dietary pattern (high-fiber, antioxidant-rich intake) exhibits oppositely protective effects, by harnessing H. pylori infection-dictated pro-inflammatory activities [37]. Moreover, oral antioxidant supplementation and consumption of fresh fruits and vegetables are also capable of reducing the incidence of BE to finally lower the risk of developing EAC [75]. Therefore, future research endeavors are supposed to rigorously eliminate the interference of confounders to enhance the accuracy of research outcomes.

Public health implications

Under normal conditions, the gut microbiota supports systemic balance but exhibits marked susceptibility to exogenous perturbations such as dietary pattern modifications and broad-spectrum antibiotic interventions. An imbalance in the composition and function of the gut microbiota, known as dysbiosis, can contribute to the development of various diseases, including malignancies [76]. The association between H. pylori infection and ECs underscores the necessity of prompt preventive strategies. Emerging evidence has demonstrated that a high-nitrosamine diet may synergize with H. pylori infection to exacerbate ESCC risk, however, diets rich in dietary fiber embrace medicinal effects against EAC. This evidence resonates with El-Omar’s assertion that “a healthy diet should serve as a cornerstone of national strategy” [77]. From political perspective, public health campaigns should be implemented to promote dietary pattern modifications (e.g. advocating for reduced consumption of processed meat products) in combination with employing economic regulatory measures such as tax incentive policies to facilitate healthier dietary choices.

Future perspective

The association between H. pylori infection and ECs showed pronounced subtype heterogeneity. In EAC, robust epidemiological evidence indicates that H. pylori infection, especially CagA-positive strains, provides a protective effect. Its potential protective mechanisms may involve multiple pathways, such as suppression of gastric acid secretion, attenuating GERD symptoms, inducing apoptosis in BE epithelia, and remodeling of gastric microbiota. However, this association may be subject to reverse causality (e.g. gastric mucosal atrophy causing false-negative detection of H. pylori colonization) and affected by strain heterogeneity, and requires verification through large-scale prospective cohorts with well design [78]. Notably, a recent high-quality meta-analysis shows that H. pylori infection is not only conferring protection against EAC but also serves as a “defender” on eosinophilic esophagitis, another immune-mediated esophageal disease, and the underpinning mechanisms may be related to the regulation of Th1/Th2 immune homeostasis [79]. This finding further supports the pleiotropic effect of H. pylori in esophageal diseases.

However, the association between H. pylori infection and ESCC is still debated. Some studies suggest that H. pylori may promote ESCC through chronic inflammation, elevated endogenous nitrosamine generation, or dysregulation of miRNA expression, whereas other evidence indicates no statistically significant association or even an inverse correlation between these two entities. These conflicting conclusions can be partly attributed to various confounding factors, such as strain heterogeneity, host genetic polymorphisms, environmental interactions as well as drawbacks in study design (selection bias and differences in diagnostic methods). In clinical practice, H. pylori eradication therapy requires weighing its “double-edged sword” effects. In regions with a high incidence of EAC, eradicating H. pylori might indirectly increase the risk of gastroesophageal reflux by restoring gastric acid secretion. In areas where ESCC is prevalent, eradication strategies need to be combined with comprehensive measures such as smoking cessation and nutritional therapy. In addition, prolonged use of PPI may further complicate evaluating the long-term effects of H. pylori eradication. Future researches should focus on the following aspects: strain-specific pathogenic mechanisms, multi-omics integrative analysis, statistically powered prospective cohort designs, multicenter geographically diverse cohorts, personalized prevention strategies, and the dynamic interactions between the esophageal microbiome and H. pylori colonization. Leveraging integrated interdisciplinary collaborations and applying innovative technologies, it is expected to clarify the complex role of H. pylori in the context of ECs, launching a scientific foundation for optimizing clinical decision-making and reducing the disease burden.
