Experiences of women from ethnic minorities and underserved, marginalised and disadvantaged groups in communicating with health professionals during antenatal care: An overview of qualitative systematic reviews
Authors: Kusum Singal, Moira Cruickshank, Aniebiet Ekong, Clare Robertson, Pauline McDonagh Hull, Denitza Williams, Tara Fairley, Louise Locock, Mary Kilonzo, Mo Ade, Lilla Braithwaite, Debra Bick, Declan Devane, Magdalena Rzewuska Diaz, Gillian Taylor, Siladitya Bhattacharya, Mairead Black, Miriam Brazzelli, Frances Conti-Ramsden, Caroline Mitchell, Caroline Mitchell, Frances Conti-Ramsden, Samuel Oluwatobi Atiku
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMaternal mortality rates show disproportional disparities among disadvantaged groups. Objective To conduct an overview of qualitative systematic reviews to summarise the antenatal care experience of ethnic minority and underserved, marginalised and disadvantaged women in high-income countries. Search strategy Seven electronic databases were searched to identify reviews published between 2011–2022. Selection criteria Two reviewers independently screened search results and full texts of potentially eligible articles. Data collection and analysis Data were extracted by two independent reviewers, critically appraised using the JBI tool and assessed for overlap. A thematic analysis was conducted. Main results Nineteen qualitative reviews were included. Most were conducted in the UK (n = 12) and provided a thematic synthesis of findings. Studied populations included women from minority ethnic groups and those who were migrants, homeless, refugees, asylum seekers, disabled, obese, or had experienced genital mutilation or human trafficking. Common challenges included language and cultural differences, and lack of effective interactions with healthcare professionals. Many women experienced discrimination, isolation, limited awareness of available services and negative attitudes from maternity care staff. Limited access to maternity services was influenced by various factors, including costs and communication barriers. Positive experiences included interactions with culturally responsive healthcare professionals, support from social groups, and access to interpreters.ConclusionsOur findings highlight the complex challenges some women face during maternity care. Future research should focus on more personalised care solutions, long-term evaluations of maternity services, training of healthcare professionals, and ways to improve the quality of information provided and the interaction with healthcare professionals.

Introduction

Antenatal care plays a key role in the health of pregnant women and their unborn babies. 1 Effective communication between healthcare professionals and pregnant women during antenatal care is not critical only for shaping personalised care, and addressing specific needs, but also for fostering and achieving positive outcomes. 2 Communication in antenatal care shapes not only information exchange but also the extent to which women experience respect, autonomy, and inclusion. Failures in communication can lead to disengagement, discourage women from voicing concerns, and potentially increase the risk of adverse maternal and neonatal outcomes. Given the disproportionate adverse pregnancy outcomes in women from underserved and disadvantaged groups, a deep understanding of communication barriers and facilitators is essential for designing inclusive and patient-centred antenatal care services. 3

Various studies and systematic reviews have shown how underserved and disadvantaged women may experience a range of communication issues during antenatal care, which can result in misunderstandings, mistrust, and dissatisfaction with care, each of which could negatively impact engagement with maternity services. 4 , 5 While these reviews provide valuable insights into specific populations, including migrant women, women with disabilities, survivors of female genital mutilation, or trafficked women, the evidence base remains fragmented across groups and contexts. Few attempts have been made to synthesise this body of qualitative evidence at a higher level to identify cross-cutting, systemic patterns in communication practices that extend across individual populations. Healthcare professionals should be aware of the unique needs of underserved and disadvantaged women and take steps to improve their communication and interaction with them, such as providing culturally competent care, using interpreters, and involving family members in decision-making processes. To develop training in these areas, the full range of antenatal care experiences reported by underserved and disadvantaged women in high-income countries should be considered.

This overview of systematic reviews considers the experiences of communication during antenatal care among women from ethnic minority and underserved, marginalised, and disadvantaged groups in high-income countries. Thus, it provides the opportunity to build more culturally sensitive and inclusive antenatal care services. The plan for this overview was developed in partnership with patient and public involvement (PPI) contributors with lived experience of maternity care, whose perspectives informed the research focus and interpretation of findings. This co-produced approach strengthens the credibility of the synthesis and helps ensure that the analysis reflects priorities identified by women themselves. Although this overview will also inform an ongoing study, which aims to develop a birth plan decision aid for UK antenatal care, its implications extend beyond this specific aim. By synthesising cross-cutting evidence on communication barriers and facilitators, this overview seeks to contribute more broadly to the design of culturally responsive, equitable, and person-centred maternity services..

Methods

This overview of systematic reviews was conducted in line with current methodological standards and the PRISMA 2020 statement. 6 , 7 The research methods were predefined and registered in PROSPERO (registration number CRD42022372831, PROSPERO). This overview forms part of a larger mixed methods programme of research aimed at developing a decision aid for discussing planned modes of birth during routine antenatal care in the UK NHS and comparable healthcare settings (the Plan-A study; Research Registry ID: researchregistry8238). The requirement that included reviews contain at least one UK-based primary study was intentional, to maximise applicability to the UK NHS context and support the development of the Plan-A decision aid. Although this geographic focus may have excluded some evidence from other high-income countries, many of the themes identified are likely transferable to broadly comparable healthcare systems. While a previous publication from this programme ( https://doi.org/10.1016/j.xagr.2025.100556) employed similar methodological approaches and a comparable structure, it addressed a different research focus and reported distinct content and findings.

PPI involvement

Four Public and Patient Involvement (PPI) partners with lived experience of maternity care, including women from underserved backgrounds, contributed to this study to ensure inclusivity and meaningful engagement. They were members of the PPI panel for the wider Plan-A project, which comprised eight patient partners in total. PPI partners were involved throughout the entire research process, beginning as co-applicants at the grant proposal stage. They attended regular study meetings, contributed to key discussions, and played a central role in shaping the research question and defining the scope of the study. Their contributions were informed by their lived experience and experiential knowledge of maternity care.

Although one partner withdrew in the second year of the project, the remaining three PPI partners continued to make substantial contributions. They were closely involved in interpreting the study findings and reviewed and commented on draft versions of this manuscript. Their input helped ensure that the final outputs were clear, relevant, and accessible to a wide audience.

Inclusivity

The Plan-A decision aid will support all who become pregnant. See the project’s language statement for more information ( https://www.abdn.ac.uk/acwhr/research/plan-a-193.php#panel201).

Eligibility criteria and search strategies

Table 1 outlines the study eligibility criteria. Existing systematic reviews were eligible if they focused on women with clinically uncomplicated pregnancies and births after 37 weeks’ gestation. At the overview level, this criterion was applied on the basis of the inclusion criteria and study descriptions reported within each included systematic review. We recognise that many systematic reviews include mixed-risk populations and that it was not feasible to independently verify the clinical characteristics of all 381 primary studies. Therefore, while the overview aimed to focus on clinically uncomplicated pregnancies, some degree of clinical heterogeneity is likely and should be acknowledged as an inherent limitation of overviews of systematic reviews. Search strategies to identify relevant systematic reviews in the current literature were developed by an information scientist using appropriate MeSH and text terms. Major general and specialised databases (MEDLINE, EMBASE, CINAHL, CENTRAL, MIDIRS, ASSIA, and the Social Sciences Citation Index) were searched from January 2011 to December 2022. Details of the MEDLINE search are available in the data repository (see Data Availability statement); this strategy was adapted for searching other databases.

Two review authors (MC and CR) independently screened the search results and assessed the full texts of potentially eligible citations. Quality assessment

The methodological quality of the identified reviews was assessed using the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) critical appraisal checklist for systematic reviews. 8 One review author (AE) conducted the assessments, and a second review author (KS) cross-checked them. The original 11 JBI checklist questions were adapted for this overview. No reviews were excluded based on the quality assessment results. However, findings from reviews rated as low quality were interpreted cautiously during the synthesis and were not given greater prominence when the same themes were supported by higher-quality evidence. Additionally, one review author assessed the overlap of primary studies across reviews using the Graphical Representation of Overlap for Overviews (GROOVE) tool. 9 Although overall overlap was minimal, some review pairs showed higher overlap. Where duplication of primary studies was identified, findings were carefully examined to avoid over-representation of identical primary data in the thematic synthesis.

To enhance data integrity, 10% of the extracted data were independently cross-checked. Any disagreements arising during study selection, data extraction and quality assessment were resolved by discussion between review authors or, where necessary, consultation with the wider research team. This process strengthened the consistency of the thematic synthesis.

Data synthesis

We organised the findings of the included reviews into two main descriptive themes based on our research question: i) barriers and ii) facilitators to positive antenatal care and birth experiences. One review author (KS) conducted a thematic analysis, examining the similarities and differences across the reviews to identify specific barriers and facilitators (subthemes). Population heterogeneity was preserved by initially coding group-specific experiences (e.g., women with disabilities, obesity, survivors of trafficking or FGM) before identifying cross-cutting themes. This approach helped ensure that forms of exclusion unique to specific groups were not prematurely absorbed into broader thematic categories. To enhance reliability, a second review author (MB) independently reviewed the analysis and subtheme structure. Both review authors then discussed their interpretations, reviewed participants’ quotes, and considered the authors’ conclusions of the included reviews.

Results

A total of 3,559 citations were identified. After deduplication and removing irrelevant records, 2,540 citations were screened for eligibility. Of these, 2,494 were excluded for not meeting the inclusion criteria, and 46 were retrieved for full-text assessment. Two additional reviews, Balaam 2013 11 and Frank 2021, 12 were found by hand-searching the reference lists of the full-text reviews. After assessment, 19 systematic reviews published in 20 papers were deemed suitable for inclusion (see Figure 1).

Description of the included reviews

All included reviews were published between 2013 and 2022. Two reviews by Higginbottom et al., from 2019 and 2020, reported the same data, with the 2020 review selected as the primary source. 13 , 14 Most reviews (11/19) were conducted in the United Kingdom, 11 , 14 – 22 four in Australia 12 , 23 – 25 two in Ireland 26 , 27 one in Canada, 28 and one in several European countries (Denmark, Finland, Germany, Ireland, Italy, Norway, Portugal, Sweden, Switzerland, The Netherlands, UK) 29 . Across reviews, the number of primary studies, published between 1990 and 2020, ranged from six to 69. Most reviews used qualitative and thematic synthesis to analyse women’s communication experiences during pregnancy. One review used integrative systematic methods 28 and another used a realist synthesis technique. 19 The methodological quality of primary studies was assessed using various tools: Walsh & Downe’s criteria (n = 2), 11 , 16 SCHEMA (n = 1), 30 CASP tool (n = 9), 12 , 15 , 17 – 22 , 26 JBI tool (n = 2), 23 , 25 MMAT (n = 1) 28 and CEBMa (n = 1). 14 Overall, the primary studies were reported to be of good quality, though three reviews did not provide information on the methodological quality of the included studies. 24 , 27 , 29

The quality of the included reviews, assessed using the JBI appraisal checklist, was generally moderate to high. Two studies were rated as low quality. Most reviews clearly described the research question, used appropriate inclusion criteria, identified relevant evidence, developed effective search strategies and summarised results effectively. In most cases, primary studies were independently appraised by two or more reviewers. However, four reviews exhibited unclear practices or did not follow current standards. 21 , 23 , 25 , 27 Some reviews also lacked clear recommendations for practice and policy. 15 , 18 , 22 , 27 Details of the quality assessment are available in the data repository as extended data (see Data Availability statement).

We evaluated the extent of overlap among primary studies included in the identified systematic reviews using the GROOVE tool and found only a minimal overall overlap (1.08%). A total of 381 primary studies were included in the assessment, with a visual representation showing the extent of overlap across reviews. Out of the 171 possible review pairs, 152 shared less than 5% of the primary studies, indicating a minor degree of overlap and a diverse focus. However, 11 pairs had moderate overlap (5% to <10%), 5 pairs had higher overlap (10% to <15%), and three pairs showed very high overlap (sharing 15% or more of primary studies), suggesting some reviews had a more significant degree of similarity or duplication in their content (see Figure 2 and Figure 3).

Summary of findings from the included systematic reviews.

Theme 1 – Barriers to positive maternity care and birth experience

Within this overarching primary theme, four subthemes were identified. These highlight the challenges faced by ethnic minority and underserved groups during antenatal care, emphasising the need for person-centred, culturally sensitive, and equitable care. Some barriers, like language barriers, are common across subthemes. 1. Communication challenges Across reviews, women described various communication issues with healthcare professionals. These include language and cultural barriers leading to misunderstandings that threatened the quality and value of the interaction with maternity care staff and affected their experiences of antenatal care. Language barriers, in terms of English competence and fluency, were particularly problematic for ethnic and marginalised groups, with 14 of 19 reviews highlighting this issue. 11 , 12 , 14 – 17 , 19 – 22 , 24 , 28 – 30 The lack of interpreters and the use of complex medical terms led to misunderstandings. Women faced significant challenges in navigating the maternity care system and understanding the available services. Many reported feeling alone, isolated, and hesitant to ask questions or disclose their symptoms. They often expressed uncertainty about how the healthcare system operated, further complicating their ability to access appropriate care. Women with disability

•Desire for a normal pregnancy experience Women with physical disabilities expressed the desire to be treated as any other pregnant woman and not be labelled ‘high-risk’ because of their disability. Discussion

This is the most comprehensive and up-to-date summary of evidence from qualitative systematic reviews on the barriers and facilitators experienced by women from ethnic minorities, underserved, and marginalised groups during antenatal care. We identified 19 qualitative systematic reviews on women’s maternity care experiences, with the overall methodological quality rated as moderate to high.

Language barriers, cultural differences, and unfamiliarity with the healthcare system significantly impacted how women accessed and engaged with maternity care. Migrant, refugee, and ethnic minority women, in particular, faced challenges in establishing effective communication and trusting relationships with healthcare professionals, which became a major concern during pregnancy. Communication is not merely a functional exchange of information but a relational and structural process through which respect, autonomy, and inclusion are expressed. Failures in communication may therefore reinforce paternalistic and disrespectful experiences, contributing to disengagement from maternity services, reduced disclosure of concerns, and a potential increase in the risk of adverse maternal and neonatal outcomes. Currently, interpretation services and multilingual information are often insufficient or unavailable in many UK centres. Policymakers should prioritise strategies to increase interpreter use and provide culturally appropriate social support, which is vital for migrant and ethnic minority women. These women face high levels of stress and vulnerability, struggling to adapt to the country’s social norms and services. This overview highlights that many women miss antenatal appointments due to difficulties in navigating the healthcare system. Policymakers should improve information on maternity services and create tailored communication strategies.

The attitudes of healthcare professionals towards women from ethnic minorities, underserved, and marginalised groups were considered a major barrier to accessing antenatal care. Women felt they were treated with less dignity, respect, and attention compared to others, due to their cultural or religious background, deprived social status, ethnicity, or physical appearance. Some women, such as those with FGM or obesity, described feeling shocked and humiliated by rude, insensitive, and intrusive comments from healthcare professionals about their physical characteristics. These behaviours should not be viewed solely as individual failings, but also as manifestations of broader institutional cultures and deeply rooted preconceptions that sustain the ‘othering’ of marginalised groups.

There is a clear need to improve the understanding and attitudes of maternity staff and policymakers toward women from ethnic minorities, as well as underserved, marginalised, and disadvantaged groups, including migrants and refugees. Future research should explore organisational models that consider these diverse needs and build partnerships with immigrant communities. 31 Adopting an inclusive, individualised care approach, including cross-cultural training for healthcare professionals, could improve engagement with vulnerable women and better address their complex needs. Training of health professionals is essential, as these women often face severe mental health issues due to personal stressful circumstances and uncertainty about their future. 32 Strengthening relational competence within maternity systems should therefore be viewed as a strategy that supports both improved outcomes and more efficient use of healthcare resources.

Effective communication, meaningful interaction with health professionals, and consistent continuity of care were key factors linked to a positive antenatal care experience. For women with past traumatic experiences or negative interactions with healthcare professionals, consistent, supportive care can help build a trusting and safe relationship during pregnancy, especially when prior experiences have diminished their confidence in the healthcare system. In 2021, NHS England introduced guidance for Local Maternity Systems and Integrated Care Systems to implement continuity of care as a standard model for all pregnant women. 33 The midwifery continuity of carer (MCoC) model prioritises those at higher risk of poor outcomes. Evidence from continuity models indicates associations with reduced preterm birth and improved maternal satisfaction, suggesting that relational continuity is not only important experientially, but may also have clinical benefits. With the increasing number of women from ethnic minorities and underserved groups, there is a clear scope to tailor the MCoC model to these populations, supporting and enhancing their antenatal care experience while potentially mitigating avoidable adverse outcomes and associated healthcare costs. Investment in continuity of care, therefore, aligns ethical commitments to equity with measurable improvements in maternal and neonatal health.

PPI partners acted as research collaborators and provided valuable insights that ensured the findings of this overview were interpreted from the perspectives of women making mode-of-birth decisions. We believe that integrating individuals with lived experiences into the data synthesis process even though requires time and support significantly enhances the research output. This approach grounds the findings in real-world experiences, enriching the analysis with greater cohesion, depth, and authenticity.

Strengths and limitations

This overview was conducted following current methodological standards by an interdisciplinary team of clinical and methodological experts, as well as independent PPI partners. By concentrating on disadvantaged, marginalised, and ethnic minority women in high-income countries, this overview addresses critical gaps in understanding the unique challenges faced by these populations, thereby highlighting opportunities for equitable antenatal care improvements. The clear identification of barriers and facilitators provides actionable insights for policymakers and healthcare professionals to design more inclusive maternity services. Although most included reviews were conducted in the UK, the findings may be relevant to other high-income countries with universal healthcare systems, while recognising that differences in healthcare financing (such as insurance-based systems), service organisation, and cultural context may affect transferability. Participant demographic characteristics were often underreported, limiting full characterisation of the study populations. Furthermore, reliance on the inclusion criteria of the 19 identified systematic reviews meant that clinical characteristics could not be independently verified across primary studies. Despite these constraints, the findings of this overview will inform the development of a decision aid designed to facilitate antenatal discussions within the UK NHS and promote informed decision-making among expectant mothers, including those from ethnic minorities and marginalised and underserved groups.

Conclusions

This overview of systematic reviews highlights the challenges faced by ethnic minorities and underserved, marginalised, and disadvantaged women during antenatal care. To address health inequalities, maternity care must be re-evaluated at multiple levels. Our findings highlight the critical role of empathetic, accessible, and equitable communication for these women and stress the need for high-quality, personalised care encompassing attentive listening, psychological support, clear and unbiased information, and respect for individual choices. Building compassionate relationships, honouring cultural values, and acknowledging the diversity of women’s experiences should be central to positive maternity care. These insights can guide healthcare professionals and policymakers in enhancing the quality and inclusivity of maternity care and promoting woman-centred approaches.

Section-Specific Feedback

Introduction

The Introduction is clearly written and effectively establishes the importance of communication in antenatal care for women from ethnic minority and underserved, marginalised and disadvantaged groups. It provides appropriate background on maternal health disparities and offers a sound rationale for focusing on communication. However, the originality, scope and analytical ambition of the study are not yet fully conveyed.

First, the critical evidence gap addressed by this overview is not articulated with sufficient clarity. While the Introduction notes the existence of qualitative evidence on the experiences of underserved women, it does not explain why an overview of qualitative systematic reviews is a necessary next step. Numerous reviews focus on specific populations, including migrant women, women with physical disabilities, survivors of female genital mutilation, and trafficked women. However, the fragmentation of this evidence base is not made explicit. Given the minimal overlap between primary studies in the included reviews, the case for a higher-level synthesis to identify cross-cutting, systemic patterns in communication failures could be made more forcefully. As currently framed, the rationale risks reading as procedural rather than as a clear statement of unique contribution.

Second, communication is conceptualised primarily in practical and functional terms, as a means of information exchange and care personalisation. The findings, however, demonstrate that communication operates as a relational and structural mechanism through which power, discrimination, surveillance and exclusion are enacted within maternity systems. This deeper conceptual dimension is not sufficiently anticipated in the Introduction, creating a disconnect between the framing and the analytical depth of the results. Strengthening the conceptual framing would better prepare readers for the ethical and clinical issues that emerge later in the paper.

Third, the Introduction places relatively strong emphasis on the contribution of the study to informing the Plan A birth plan decision aid. While this is an important application, its prominence may inadvertently narrow the perceived relevance of the work. Readers, particularly those outside the UK, may interpret the paper as a local programme report rather than a high-level overview with broader international relevance. The synthesis clearly has wider implications for maternity service organisation, professional education and policy responses to maternal health inequities. These should be more explicitly foregrounded.

Fourth, the methodological ambition of the study is not fully signalled at the outset. The depth of the PPI partnership and its influence on the research question and interpretation are described later but not highlighted in the Introduction. This is a major strength of the work and is currently underplayed. Earlier acknowledgement of this co-production would strengthen the ethical grounding of the synthesis and signal that the analysis is informed by lived experience as well as academic expertise.

Finally, while maternal health inequalities are noted, the clinical and ethical stakes of communication failures are not made explicit early on. The links between poor communication, disengagement from services, delayed care-seeking and adverse maternal outcomes are implied rather than stated. This reduces the sense of urgency that is evident in the Results and Discussion.

Recommendation

I recommend revising the Introduction to more clearly articulate the unique contribution of this overview, deepen the conceptual framing of communication, broaden the perceived relevance beyond a single applied output, and foreground both methodological rigour and the high stakes of communication failures. These comments should be read not as a critique of the quality of the Introduction, which is strong, but as an opportunity to elevate the framing so it matches the depth, originality and international relevance of the findings.

Results

The Results section is the most significant contribution of this manuscript, characterised by a high degree of methodological rigour and a rich evidentiary base. The use of the GROOVE tool to confirm minimal overall primary study overlap (1.08%) is a technical highlight that validates the breadth of the synthesis and ensures the findings represent a diverse spectrum of qualitative evidence. The thematic organisation is logical and effectively captures the complexity of antenatal care experiences. However, the presentation would benefit from minor analytical framing to ensure the nuanced findings are synthesised into clear structural patterns.

First, the section identifies critical barriers to maternity care, but the framing occasionally stops at descriptive collation. Theme 1 successfully reports widespread communication failures and negative professional attitudes across 14 of the 19 reviews. However, to move beyond a topical list, each subtheme should conclude with an analytical synthesis.

Recommendation: At the end of the ‘Communication’ subtheme, add a sentence identifying communication not just as a failure of clarity, but as a structural gatekeeper that prevents marginalised women from navigating the system with the same agency as the majority population.

Recommendation: Within the ‘Attitudes’ subtheme, conclude by noting that the ‘processed’ or ‘functional but not supportive’ care reported by women evidences a systemic technocratic priority, where institutional efficiency is frequently valued over the dignity and psychological safety of the patient.

Second, the group specific findings for underserved populations are remarkably detailed but require more nuanced framing of the ‘othering’ process. The results for women with obesity, disabilities, and FGM are particularly strong, documenting how clinical focus shifts from the pregnancy to the mother’s physical ‘condition’.

Recommendation: This section would be strengthened by a concluding synthesis sentence identifying the cross cutting pattern of medical ‘othering’, where the pathology of the mother repeatedly diminishes her identity and agency as an expectant parent.

Third, the findings regarding trust and security reveal profound structural disconnects. You report that fears of deportation or the intervention of Child Protection Services are primary deterrents to seeking care.

Recommendation: Synthesise these findings by noting that for these specific populations, the healthcare system is often perceived as a site of surveillance rather than a site of support, fundamentally altering the nature of the patient professional relationship.

Recommendation: Conclude this theme by explicitly linking these facilitators to the structural barriers identified earlier. For example, note that continuity of care and culturally responsive staff serve as the primary mechanisms for dismantling the systemic mistrust and surveillance fears identified in Theme 1.

Fifth, minor methodological outcomes in the Results require more explicit reporting to ensure data integrity. The manuscript identifies two ‘low quality’ reviews and a 10% data cross check process, yet the impact of these factors on the final results is not stated.

Recommendation: Briefly state whether findings from low quality reviews were weighted differently and report the level of agreement reached during the data cross check to reinforce the dependability of the thematic synthesis.

Overall judgement: These Results provide a robust and deeply human look into antenatal inequality. Adding focused, synthesising sentences at the end of each subtheme will ensure these nuanced findings are framed as the structural and systemic insights they are, rather than a collection of qualitative reports.

Discussion

The Discussion section provides a comprehensive interpretation of the evidence, successfully bridging the findings from the 19 systematic reviews to the practical requirements of the Plan A study. It effectively synthesises the core barriers such as language, cultural differences, and institutional mistrust into a call for a re evaluation of maternity care delivery. However, to match the analytical depth suggested for the Results, the Discussion should more explicitly address structural power dynamics and the interdependence of the proposed solutions.

First, the Discussion correctly identifies communication as the central pillar of antenatal care, but it could further explore communication as an equitable process. The authors highlight that interpretation services are currently insufficient in many UK centres and that policymakers must prioritise tailored communication strategies.

Recommendation: While you advocate for ‘empathetic, accessible, and equitable communication’, the Discussion would be strengthened by explicitly framing communication as a redistribution of power. Ensuring women are ‘attentively listened to’ is not just a clinical courtesy but a structural necessity to counter the ‘paternalistic’ and ‘dehumanising’ experiences reported in the Results.

Second, the analysis of healthcare professional attitudes provides a clear mandate for training, though it should emphasise systemic change over individual behaviour. The manuscript notes that women felt treated with less dignity due to their background or appearance and highlights the need for cross cultural training.

Recommendation: T he Discussion should emphasise that these ‘rude’ or ‘insensitive’ comments are symptoms of a wider institutional culture. Training should therefore focus not only on individual cross cultural skills but on dismantling the unconscious biases that lead to the medical ‘othering’ identified in the findings for women with obesity and FGM.

Third, the recommendation for the Midwifery Continuity of Carer (MCoC) model is a significant strength that directly addresses the findings. You correctly identify that consistent, supportive care is vital for women with past traumatic experiences and that the MCoC model should be tailored to these specific populations.

Recommendation: Explicitly conclude this section by noting that continuity of care is the most effective structural remedy for the surveillance fears identified in the Results. By building a ‘trusting and safe relationship’, the MCoC model functions as a protective shield against the systemic mistrust that currently causes vulnerable women to avoid care.

Fourth, the integration of PPI insights adds unique authenticity to the Discussion. The inclusion of PPI partners in the data synthesis process is noted to have enriched the analysis with ‘depth and authenticity’.

Recommendation: The Discussion would benefit from a sentence explaining how the PPI perspective specifically helped the team interpret the nuance of the findings, perhaps by identifying that what researchers call ‘non compliance’, women experience as ‘self protection’ in the face of perceived institutional threat.

Fifth, the acknowledgement of limitations is transparent but could be more critical regarding the UK centric evidence base. You note that most included reviews were conducted in the UK, which informs the development of the UK specific decision aid.

Recommendation: Briefly discuss how the UK NHS context, as a universal healthcare system, might uniquely shape these findings compared with other high income settings with insurance based models, ensuring the equitable care improvements you suggest are contextually grounded.
