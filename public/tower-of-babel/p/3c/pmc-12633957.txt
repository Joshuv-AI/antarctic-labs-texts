Perceived effect of COVID-19 infection on communication skills among adolescents in the Eastern province of Saudi Arabia
Authors: Malak A. Al Shammari, Abdullah H. Alnasser, Ahmed A. Alsalman, Ali I. Alkhalifah, Yousef W. Alawadh, Abdulrahman G. Aljabri, Ali T. Alwail, Zeyad K. Aljaili, Mohammed I. Alsaihati, Ali Hussain Alkufair, Ali H. AlNass, Reem D. AlQahtani
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground:The American Speech-Language-Hearing Association (ASHA) defines communication impairment as difficulties in receiving, processing, sending, and understanding verbal and nonverbal symbols. Given the vital role of communication skills in our daily life and the high prevalence of COVID-19 among adolescents in the Eastern Province of Saudi Arabia, this study aims to assess the perceived impact of COVID-19 on communication skills.Methods:This is a comparative cross-sectional study among middle and high school attendees, from public and private schools, in 4 major eastern province cities: (Alkhobar, Dammam, Qatif, and Alahsa).Results:Women had significantly higher odds of communication skills by 2.24 the odds compared with men (AOR = 2.24; CI = 1.44-3.52; P value < 0.001). Students who live with one parent had significantly higher odds of communication skills by 2.67 odds compared with those living with both parents (AOR = 2.67; CI = 1.15-6.63; P value = 0.027). Students whose father earn from 5000 to 10,000 Riyals monthly and those whose parents earn more than 10,000 Riyals monthly had significantly higher odds of communication skills compared with those whose parents earn less than 5000 Riyals by 3.85 the odds (AOR = 3.85; CI = 1.75-8.89; P value = 0.001 and AOR = 3.85; CI = 1.73-8.94; P value = 0.001).Conclusion:Our findings show that despite contracting COVID-19, adolescents’ self-perception of communication skills remains unaffected. Factors such as gender, family income, and single-parent households significantly differentiate adolescents with high communication skills from those with lower skills.

Introduction

On March 11, 2020, the World Health Organization (WHO) declared the novel coronavirus infection (COVID-19) outbreak to be a global pandemic.[1] Since then, it has been recognized that a growing number of COVID-19 patients experience long-term complications, including impairments in communication skills. Approximately more than 27 million people who survived COVID-19 report post-COVID neurological complications that affect their quality of life. Furthermore, the WHO has highlighted the increased demand for mental health services during the pandemic due to factors such as social isolation, work impairment, and financial loss. In addition, COVID-19 infection itself has been linked to mental health complications, including agitation, delirium, anxiety, and depression.[23]

Beyond its well-known respiratory manifestations, COVID-19 has been shown to impact other systems, including the auditory system, leading to hearing loss in some individuals. Studies suggest that COVID-19–related hearing loss can manifest as conductive hearing loss or sensorineural hearing loss, depending on the underlying mechanisms.[45] Conductive hearing loss often results from middle ear effusion; fluid builds up in the middle ear due to infection or inflammation. On the other hand, sensorineural hearing loss is believed to be caused by vascular injury, inflammation, or direct damage to the inner ear or auditory nerve.[45] These auditory complications have raised concerns about the long-term impact of COVID-19 on communication and quality of life.

Emerging research has provided evidence of varying prevalence and severity of hearing loss associated with COVID-19. A study examining 63 patients with post-COVID sensorineural hearing loss found that 4.8% experienced moderate to severe conditions, with bilateral hearing loss in 27 cases and unilateral loss in 36 cases. Despite treatment, which primarily involved corticosteroids, only 12.5% of patients achieved full recovery, while 35.9% showed partial recovery, and the remainder experienced no improvement.[6] Another study analyzing 37 research articles on hearing loss in children with COVID-19 reported that while 30 studies found no hearing impairment, 7 studies observed predominantly conductive hearing loss due to middle ear effusion. Only one case of sensorineural hearing loss was identified, and it was associated with a preexisting genetic condition.[4]

Furthermore, large-scale studies have provided insights into the prevalence of hearing loss linked to COVID-19. A nationwide cohort study in South Korea involving 6,716,879 young adults revealed that the incidence of hearing loss among COVID-19 patients was significantly higher than in noninfected individuals, with 11.9 cases per 10,000 person-months compared with 3.4 cases per 10,000 person-months in the general population. The incidence of sensorineural hearing loss specifically was also higher, at 1.8 cases per 10,000 person-months among COVID-19 patients, compared with 0.5 cases per 10,000 in noninfected individuals.[7]

In younger populations, such as children and adolescents, hearing loss associated with COVID-19 has been reported less frequently. Studies suggest that when hearing loss occurs in these groups, it is often transient and related to middle ear issues. However, cases of sensorineural hearing loss, while rare, have been observed, particularly in those with preexisting vulnerabilities or genetic predispositions.[45] This indicates that the pediatric population may be underrepresented in current research on auditory complications, leaving gaps in understanding the full impact of COVID-19 on hearing in this age group.

According to the American Speech-Language-Hearing Association (ASHA), communication impairment is defined as difficulty in receiving, processing, sending, or understanding ideas and verbal or nonverbal symbols.[8] Such impairments can affect hearing, language, and speech processes and may be either developmental or acquired. Individuals may experience one or multiple forms of communication impairment simultaneously.[8] The potential association between COVID-19 and hearing loss further underscores the importance of examining how the pandemic has influenced communication abilities in affected populations.

A systemic review that was published in 2021 on the effect of the COVID-19 pandemic on the mental health of adolescents showed that the most common mental consequences in this age category were anxiety (n = 57.4%) and depression (n = 39.3%).[9] Similarly, a cross-sectional study in China found that the most noted symptoms in adolescents postinfection were anxiety (24.9%), depression (19.7%), and stress (15.2%) despite being generally satisfied with their lives.[10] However, research on how COVID-19 affects communication skills, including auditory and speech-related challenges, remains limited, particularly in regions such as the Eastern Province of Saudi Arabia.

To the best of our knowledge, studies about the effect of COVID-19 infection on adolescents’ communication skills in our region are still lacking, with the unclear perception of the existence of such an effect on our adolescent population. Furthermore, because of the importance of communication skills and their fundamental role in a person’s daily life, and the high prevalence of COVID-19 infections in adolescents in the Eastern province of Saudi Arabia, we aim to assess the perceived effect of COVID-19 infections on communication skills.

In addition, family physicians are the first-line providers of adolescent healthcare services, including behavioral, mental, and social evaluations. Understanding factors that impact communication skills, especially during the pandemic era, can help physicians more effectively recognize youth at risk. This understanding emphasizes the importance of a more holistic approach during consultations. Moreover, physicians are encouraged to recognize challenges when early interventions are needed, such as referrals to mental health services or speech-language pathologists. This also allows physicians to provide individualized recommendations to families coping with postpandemic complications.

Methodology

Research design and setting

This is a comparative cross-sectional study done from March to July 2023, among middle and high school attendees in the 4 major eastern province cities (Alkhobar, Dammam, Qatif, and Alahsa) including both public and private schools.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The study included male and female students aged 12 to 18 years from both public and private middle and high schools in four major cities of the Eastern Province of Saudi Arabia: Al Khobar, Dammam, Qatif, and Al Ahsa. Participants were selected based on their exposure to COVID-19 to explore its perceived effect on communication skills. However, students with mental disorders or disabilities that could affect their communication abilities, such as hearing disorders, deafness, or learning disorders, were excluded to ensure the accuracy and relevance of the findings.

Sample and sampling procedure

The study used a stratified random sampling procedure to select participants for the COVID-19–infected group. Schools were selected by a proportional allocation from the four major cities. To identify infected students, school administrations provided contact information, with an assumption of finding an average of three cases per school, resulting in the selection of 40 schools. The comparative (control) group was selected using simple random sampling from the same grade and academic level as the infected group in the same schools, maintaining a 2:1 control-to-case ratio. The minimum required sample size, calculated using Epi Info Companion software (5.5.7), was 112 cases and 223 controls. This estimation was based on a 95% confidence level, 80% power, an assumption of 50% poor communication skills among students, and a detectable odds ratio of 2.

Data collection techniques and tools

The data of the study were collected using an electronic self-administered questionnaire consisting of three main sections. First, a sociodemographic section. Second, a health statement section that focuses on COVID-19. Third, a communication skills assessment section which uses a 15-item scale.[11]

The questionnaire was translated into Arabic language by two neurobehavioral field experts and was piloted on 30 subjects to address unclarities and assess the feasibility, accessibility, understandability, content validity, and the time required to answer it. The reliability (internal consistency) of the Arabic version of the questionnaire was measured by Cronbach’s alpha which was 0.8. The scale consists of 15 questions measured on a 5-point Likert scale starting from 0 (never doing this) to 4 (constantly doing this). The scoring ranges from 0 (minimum) to 60 (maximum). The scoring interpretation is as follows: (<9: poor communication), (10-19: have communication difficulties), (20-29: average communicator), (30-39: above average communicator with intermittent gaps), (40-49: effective communicator who only occasionally causes communication failure), and (50-60: exceptionally effective communicator).

Pilot study

The data collected during the pilot testing phase were not included in the final result analysis. The pilot test, conducted with 30 participants, aimed to evaluate the questionnaire’s clarity, feasibility, accessibility, understandability, content validity, and the time required for completion. The primary objective of the pilot was to refine the survey tool and ensure its reliability, which was confirmed with a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.8. Following the pilot, the necessary adjustments were made to enhance the instrument, and only the data collected after these revisions were used in the final analysis. This approach ensured that the study’s findings were based on a validated and standardized version of the questionnaire.

Ethical considerations

The approval was taken from the institutional review board (IRB) at Imam Abdulrahman bin Faisal University (IAU), (IRB Number: IRB-2022-01-259), the Ministry of Education, and the school administration before beginning with the research. Informed consent was taken from the participants and their guardians at the beginning of the electronic questionnaire after ensuring that their data was anonymous, and only accessed by the researchers. Participation and withdrawal were voluntary.

Informed consent

Informed consent was obtained from participants and their guardians at the onset of the electronic questionnaire. Participants were assured that their data would be used exclusively for research purposes and would remain anonymous, accessible only to the research team. Participation in the study was entirely voluntary, and students were informed of their right to withdraw at any time without providing a reason. In addition, it was emphasized that their academic standing or treatment within their educational environment would not be impacted by their decision to participate or decline, ensuring an ethical and non-coercive approach to data collection.

Data analysis and statistical tools

The statistical analysis was performed by using R version 4.1.1. Categorical data were described by showing counts and percentages. The Shapiro test of normality was used to test for the normality of continuous data. Only two variables (age of the participants and siblings’ number) are continuous, and did not follow the parametric assumptions; therefore, they were described by their median and interquartile range. For the bivariate analysis, Pearson Chi-square and Fischer exact tests were used to compare two independent categorical variables, furthermore, the Wilcoxon rank-sum test was used to compare between two independent numerical variables. The level of significance is set at 0.05.

Scores were summed, and the median value of the sum of scores was calculated and used as a cutoff point below which the participants had poor communication skills and vice versa.

Bivariate analysis was performed to explore the significant differences in demographic characteristics across communication status. Univariate logistic regression was performed to show the crude odds ratios, and a multivariate logistic regression was performed to adjust for the effect of potential confounders.

All the potential risk factors of communication were inserted in the multivariate regression model and the assumptions tested for the model included Hosmer Lemeshow which was not significant indicating the goodness of fit of the model as well as the c-statistic which was 0.70 showing acceptable predictive ability of the model. The multicollinearity between the variables in the model was tested by ensuring that the variance inflation factor (VIF) was lower than the value of 5. Two variables were removed from the model as they had a high multi-collinearity with the other variables, and they are the severity of the symptoms and the period of the symptoms.

Discussion

The primary result drawn from our study is that, despite contracting COVID-19, the self-perception of communication skills among adolescents does not appear to be affected, regardless of the use of personal protective equipment (PPE). Nonetheless, our study found that being female, having a high family income, and living in single-parent households were significant factors that distinguished participants with high communication skills from those with poor communication skills.

The study results show that 50% got COVID-19 before, and Both gender and family monthly income are significantly different between students with poor communication and those with high communication skills. Except for gender, these results were not mirrored in other studies conducted elsewhere.[12] These results can be explained by the skewed sample towards women which may have affected the results, but further exploration of such results is needed. In addition, Students who live with one parent had significantly higher odds of communication skills compared with those living with both parents. Moreover, Students whose fathers earn from 5000 to 10,000 Riyals monthly and those whose parents earn more than 10,000 Riyals monthly had significantly higher odds of communication skills compared with those whose parents earn less than 5000 Riyals monthly. Factors reported to affect adolescent communication skills in general and not necessarily during the pandemic with its special circumstances were always reported to be affected by the family’s income and the social circumstances associated with living with one parent rather than living in a household with two parents.[1213]

According to the results of the study, a minority of adolescents self-reported experiencing communication difficulties during the pandemic, while the majority did not report any changes in their communication abilities. However, the impact of the pandemic on language development was found to be differential,[1415] with certain demographic factors such as gender and family monthly income being associated with higher odds of communication skills. These findings highlight the importance of targeted interventions and support to promote language development, particularly for individuals from disadvantaged backgrounds, during and after the pandemic.[1617]

Our study focused primarily on the inherent association between contracting COVID-19, and the self-perceived effects on communication skills. When comparing our results to other published studies, we found that many studies focused on the self-perceived impact of safety measures implemented during the pandemic, such as the use of personal protective equipment (PPE) on the self-perceived effects of communication skills rather than the inherent association. This emphasis on preventive measures makes direct comparisons to our study more challenging due to the inherent difference in methodology. Despite this, some studies provide valuable insights regarding the effect of PPE in a healthcare setting in which communication is crucial. The studies found that the use of PPE negatively impacted self-perceived communication both verbal and nonverbal. Chu et al. in their study found that the use of masks significantly impaired the ability to acknowledge emotions and suggested the use of transparent masks. In addition, Schlögl et al. reported similar findings and noted that providing clear information using unambiguous language was a challenge faced during the pandemic among clinicians due to the use of masks. Both studies were done in a small setting; in contrast to our study which was done on a larger scale and compromised a relevant sample of the population.[181920] However, our sample size is small compared with both studies and as a result, the sample is poorly distributed.

Hessami et al. published a systematic review and meta-analysis, including prospective studies that aimed to assess the risk of neurodevelopmental impairment in infants raised during the pandemic and infants that were exposed to COVID-19 during gestation. Interestingly, the study found an association between communication delay among infants raised in the first year of life during the pandemic, regardless of gestational exposure. In addition, the study found the other domains were not affected (e.g. fine motor, gross motor, and problem-solving). Though this study only includes infants, it correlates with our findings, indicating that communication was affected not due to COVID-19 itself but rather the preventative measures implemented during the pandemic.[21] A notable study published by Shuffrey et al.[22] with a similar methodology contradicts Hessami et al.’s results. Fine and gross motor, as well as personal-social domains, were significantly affected in infants who were born during the pandemic regardless of maternal infection.[22]

Mental health is another aspect that needs to be addressed. Participants diagnosed with autism were not included in our study; however, mood and anxiety problems, which have the potential to hinder communication, were not excluded.[23] Self-isolation during the pandemic was found to be a substantial contributor to an increase in the prevalence of anxiety and depressive symptoms, according to research conducted globally.[222425] Perhaps a significant limitation of our study is that it does not take into account anxiety and depressive symptoms in the participants or if some of the participants were previously diagnosed with a mood or anxiety disorder.

A recent study published in 2024 investigated how the shift to distance learning during COVID-19 pandemic affected college students socialization. The results showed that online learning led to communication difficulties. Moreover, unemployed students who lived without their families had the highest rates of loneliness. Meanwhile, those who lived with their own families reported better experiences with online social interactions. In addition, students who were unemployed had better psychological wellbeing at the start but experienced greater social anxiety as the period of the distanced learning progressed.[26]

Strengths and Limitations

This study is notable for its focus on adolescents in the Eastern Province of Saudi Arabia, a population and context that is underrepresented in research on communication skills during the COVID-19 pandemic. The use of a sufficiently large sample size adds credibility and reliability to the findings. Furthermore, the study’s examination of sociodemographic factors, such as gender, family income, and living arrangements, provides a comprehensive perspective on their potential impact on communication skills. Nevertheless, this study acknowledges certain limitations and the measures taken to address them. The self-reported data may introduce recall bias; however, this was mitigated by using clear, structured questions and piloting the survey to ensure its comprehensibility and reliability. While the sample had a higher proportion of female participants, stratified random sampling was employed to ensure balanced representation across school types, cities, and genders as much as possible. The reliance on electronic self-administered questionnaires may have excluded participants with limited digital access, but efforts were made to facilitate accessibility by collaborating with schools and offering guidance on completing the survey. Despite these limitations, the study provides valuable insights and highlights areas for further research, including longitudinal designs and expanded sample diversity.

Conclusion

The study examined the perceived effects of COVID-19 infection on communication skills among adolescents in the Eastern Province of Saudi Arabia, revealing several significant findings. Although contracting COVID-19 did not appear to negatively impact adolescents’ self-perception of communication skills, certain demographic factors such as gender, family income, and household structure influenced communication outcomes. Women, adolescents from higher-income families, and those living in single-parent households demonstrated stronger communication skills, highlighting the interplay between socioeconomic and familial contexts and communication development.

The findings underscore the importance of targeted support to address potential disparities in communication skills, particularly among adolescents from lower-income or dual-parent households. These results have implications for educational and public health initiatives, suggesting the need for tailored interventions to enhance communication skills postpandemic.

Future research should adopt a longitudinal design to explore causal relationships and investigate the long-term impact of COVID-19 on communication. In addition, studies examining specific aspects of communication, such as hearing and auditory processing, would further elucidate the potential mechanisms linking COVID-19 to communication outcomes.
