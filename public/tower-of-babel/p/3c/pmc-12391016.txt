Lung Transplantation Following Paraquat Poisoning: Time to Think
Authors: Hari Kishan Gonuguntla, Somesh Tripathi, Vimi Varghese, Sricharan Goda, Chetan Rao Vaddepally, Balasubramoniam Kavumkal Rajagopalan, M. Sheetal Kumar, Belgundi Preeti Vidyasagar
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTParaquat, a commonly available herbicide, when consumed in high doses, affects organs with high blood flow (lungs, heart, kidney and liver), leading to pulmonary fibrosis, respiratory failure and death. Few reports of rescue lung transplantation exist. Complete depletion of paraquat from the body is necessary prior to transplant; however, timing for and concerns after lung transplantation remain unknown. We report 2 patients (median age 19 years) with severe respiratory failure requiring extracorporeal membrane oxygenation support, acute kidney injury requiring haemodialysis and acute liver injury in the pre‐transplant period. Volume of paraquat consumption was more than 30 mL and PF ratio less than 100 before transplant. Once their urine paraquat level was negative (median time 32 days), both underwent bilateral lung transplantation after receiving an induction agent with basiliximab and were continued on triple immunosuppressant following the transplant. With reported mortality rates reaching up to 90%, lung transplantation remains a reasonable option for patients with paraquat poisoning not responding to conventional treatment options. Once paraquat levels are negative and after optimising renal and liver function, these patients could be considered for bilateral lung transplantation and have successful outcomes as reported here.

Introduction

Paraquat, a pyridyl compound, is a chemical that is increasingly used in agriculture. It is a rapidly acting herbicide that is inexpensive. However, deliberate ingestion has an extremely high case‐fatality rate. Due to this reason, paraquat has been restricted in many parts of the world. In the rural areas of countries where it is easily available, it is a common method for intentional self‐poisoning, which carries high mortality [1].

Only 1%–5% of paraquat compound is metabolised after ingestion. Gastrointestinal absorption occurs mainly, and unabsorbed paraquat is excreted through faeces [2]. The plasma paraquat concentration reaches its peak within 30 min to 4 h after absorption, which is widely distributed in the body, spreading to various tissues and organs [3]. The plasma paraquat is excreted almost unchanged in urine. Paraquat renal clearance is greater than creatinine clearance under normal renal function; however, renal tubular necrosis caused by paraquat results in the kidneys losing their ability to eliminate paraquat [4].

Paraquat poisoning can cause multiorgan dysfunction, with a mortality rate of 40%–80% [5]. This causes systemic reactions, such as pulmonary tissue oedema and liver and kidney damage, owing to the production of oxygen free radicals [6]. There is no specific antidote. The most common pulmonary complications are emphysema and interstitial fibrosis [7] which can lead to respiratory failure and even death.

In this study, we report two cases of pulmonary fibrosis who underwent bilateral lung transplantation after accidental paraquat poisoning, after optimised medical management and prolonged days on ECMO. Both cases involved accidental poisoning. In the first, a toxic substance had been stored in a juice bottle, leading to its unintentional ingestion. In the second, the individual drank the substance directly, mistaking it for a flavoured beverage.

Both patients were considered for transplant after one month. During this period, they were managed on awake ECMO, maintaining good muscle strength and undergoing psychiatric evaluation. Informed consent was obtained from both, and the entire process was video recorded. The recordings were reviewed by the state organ registry, following which clearance was granted. At the time of consent, both patients were fully coherent and demonstrated adequate decision‐making capacity.

Following detailed conversations with the patient and their parents, the event was determined to be accidental. As part of the transplant eligibility assessment, we applied the principle of psychiatric competence, also known as mental capacity, which refers to an individual's ability to make informed and rational decisions about their care. In cases where mental incapacity is evident, transplant is typically not pursued. To assess this, we utilised validated psychological tools such as the Beck Anxiety Inventory, Beck Depression Inventory, and the Minnesota Multiphasic Personality Inventory (MMPI).

We also present the follow‐up data after 12 months of transplantation.

Case Reports

Case Presentations

Case 1

23‐year‐old boy was admitted to hospital with nausea, vomiting, and weakness after ingestion of approximately more than 30 mL of paraquat. Evaluation showed renal and hepatic dysfunction with progressive respiratory failure (Figure 1). Gastric lavage and haemoperfusion were performed. Despite repeated cycles of haemoperfusion over 14 days, his respiratory failure worsened, needing mechanical ventilation (MV). Patient was initiated on veno‐venous ECMO (V‐V ECMO) on 15th day after consumption in view of worsening hypoxemia and PF ratios despite MV.

Timeline and course of events for case 1.

CT Thorax showed progressive fibrosis involving all zones with lower lobe predominance (Figure 2 Left). After multidisciplinary discussion by a team of specialists, the option of bilateral lung transplantation (LT) was considered. Serial urine paraquat levels were found to be negative. On the 35th day after consumption, bilateral sequential LT was performed through bilateral anterolateral incisions with V‐V ECMO support.

Pre‐transplant CT thorax of case 1 (left) and case 2 (right).

Patient was induced with steroids, MMF and Basiliximab and continued triple immunosuppression (Steroids, Mycophenolate, tacrolimus) with careful monitoring of tacrolimus drug level and necessary prophylactic medication was continued. Patient was decannulated from ECMO on the 4th day and extubated on the 6th post‐operative day respectively. Patient was discharged on the 14th post‐op day with good pulmonary compliance and muscle power. Bronchoscopy done prior to discharge showed a healthy anastomotic site bilaterally with no features suggestive of endobronchial infection. FVC was 2.78 L and 3.32 L, and FEV1 was 2.42 L and 2.87 L at 6 months and 12 months of transplantation respectively, and there were no features of chronic lung allograft dysfunction.

Case 2

15‐year‐old boy was admitted to hospital with a similar clinical profile as the previous case after ingestion of approximately more than 100 mL of paraquat Evaluation showed renal and hepatic dysfunction with progressive respiratory failure (Figure 3). Gastric lavage was performed, and haemoperfusion was started from day 6 in view of progressive declining renal function.

Timeline and course of events for case 2.

Baseline CT scan showed significant fibrotic changes in bilateral lower lobes (Figure 2 Right). On the 8th day after consumption, he developed worsening breathlessness, and chest X‐ray was suggestive of right‐sided pneumothorax, and an emergency intercostal drain was inserted. In view of worsening respiratory failure and progressive pulmonary fibrosis, he was intubated on the 23rd day of consumption. Veno‐venous ECMO was placed on the 24th day as a bridge for double lung transplantation. Urine Paraquat levels were serially monitored. After they turned negative, he underwent sequential bilateral lung transplant with appropriately matched O positive donor on the 30th day of consumption. In view of size mismatch, non‐anatomical graft volume reduction was performed intraoperatively. Induction agents used for patients were steroids, MMF, and Basiliximab followed by triple immunosuppression, with careful monitoring of tacrolimus drug level and necessary prophylactic medication.

Patient was decannulated from ECMO on 5th and extubated on 7th postoperative days respectively. CT Thorax was repeated on 14th postoperative day in view of persistent fever, which showed bilateral upper lobe nodular opacities. Bronchoalveolar lavage grew Chryseobacterium indologenes and antibiotics were optimised. He suffered an episode of generalised tonic‐clonic seizures on 20th postoperative day. MRI brain showed no organic lesion and CSF analysis was within normal limits. He was started on antiepileptics and discharged on the 30th postoperative day. Serial follow‐up on 3rd month after transplant showed FVC of 2 L and FEV1 of 1.7 L. Surveillance bronchoscopy showed stenosis of left main bronchus, which was perianastomotic, leading to more than 50% reduction in the cross‐section with no evidence of dehiscence or malacia. He underwent balloon bronchoplasty and serial dilatation. Margins of the stenosis post dilatation were ablated with cryoprobe. FVC was 2.39 L and FEV1 was 0.99 L at 12 months of transplantation.

Both patients were males with no comorbidities and consumed more than 30 mL individually, and the mean age was 19 years. After initial stabilisation with gastric lavage, both of them required haemoperfusion within 10 days of admission and were noted to have deranged liver function tests. The median time from ingestion to requiring veno–venous extracorporeal support (V‐V ECMO) was 20 days. They were listed for lung transplantation once the urine paraquat was negative, after around 30 days of ingestion of paraquat in view of progressive lung damage and an inability to wean ECMO support. Liver and renal function improved to normal ranges just before transplant. In case 1 and case 2, the last sessions of haemodialysis were performed 15 and 12 days prior to transplantation, respectively. Following this, neither patient required further haemodialysis, and both maintained adequate urine output.

The mean cold ischemic time for both lungs together was 7 h and 40 min, and warm ischemia time was 3 h. As the patients were on ECMO, both lung transplant patients required non‐anatomical graft volume reduction. During the surgery, veno–venous ECMO was changed to V‐V‐A ECMO with an average number of blood products required during surgery being 5 units of leucocyte reduced red blood cells (LRBC). There were no intra operative complications.

Average duration of hospital stay was 21 days after transplant. In view of prolonged days on ECMO, repeated blood stream infections like Acinetobacter baumannii and Chryseobacterium indologenes were a concern for both patients respectively. CMV infection after transplant was noticed in one patient and generalised tonic clonic seizures in another. One of the patients developed bronchial stenosis at the anastomotic site requiring serial dilatation. The mean FVC for both patients was more than 2 L and there has been no evidence of chronic allograft lung dysfunction till the current follow up. Our case report showed the importance of new treatment options for paraquat associated lung fibrosis, the timelines and characteristics to consider before transplantation and the incidence of different complications.

Discussion

Numerous paraquat poisoning cases have been reported from around the world, mainly due to deliberate suicide and accidental ingestion. However, there is always a difficult decision about the timing of double lung transplant if there is no clinical improvement. We report the first set of cases from our country about paraquat‐induced lung fibrosis undergoing double lung transplant and their 12 months post‐transplant follow up.

Both patients in our case series are young; the mean age was 19 years, and previous case reports [8, 9] were also suggestive of similar findings and an age group range from 15 to 30 years, respectively.

Paraquat rapidly spreads to multiple organs, causing irreversible damage. Paraquat toxicity is most harmful to the lungs, kidneys, and liver, mainly destroying the alveolar epithelium and causing acute pulmonary oedema, which progresses to severe pulmonary fibrosis, respiratory failure, and death [10].

Seventy percent of paraquat is eliminated by the kidneys, and different degrees of renal injury occurred in all the cases reviewed. According to Yen et al., early clearance (less than 4–5 h after the ingestion of paraquat), especially through haemoperfusion and haemodialysis, is associated with decreased mortality [11]. Both patients in our case report have renal and hepatic dysfunction requiring haemoperfusion before transplant.

All previous case series after paraquat consumptions followed standard recommendations [8, 9] with gastric lavage, haemoperfusion, haemodialysis, and administration of immunosuppressive agents (methylprednisolone and cyclophosphamide), antioxidants, and considering ECMO support as a bridge to transplantation as there is no antidote for paraquat poisoning; treatment is only supportive before considering lung transplantation. Lung transplantation is the only method to improve oxygenation and increase the chances of survival of patients with lung fibrosis as other visceral organ functions recover with appropriate intervention.

Lung transplantation can only be successful if the body is completely depleted of paraquat. Otherwise, fibrosis would potentially recur in the allograft. Longer waiting time leads to more bacterial colonisation. There was no established recommendation about the appropriate timing to consider the lung transplantation. If the waiting time for lung transplantation (LT) is too short, there is a risk of recurrent fibrosis in the transplanted lung. Conversely, if the waiting period is too long, the likelihood of preoperative complications such as infections and bacterial colonisation increases. In previous case reports spanning from 1968 to 2022, paraquat was undetectable in urine by the 17th and 19th days post‐ingestion, respectively. Although accidental factors cannot be entirely ruled out, these findings may represent a potential milestone in understanding paraquat clearance. On average, paraquat becomes undetectable in urine by 18 days; however, in the absence of formal guidelines, we recommend waiting at least 30 days before proceeding with transplantation. To confirm paraquat clearance, it is prudent to perform two urine paraquat tests at least one week apart within this 30‐day window. Plasma paraquat concentrations cannot be used to assess the extent of toxicant clearance from the body [12]. In both patients, urine drug level was negative after 2 weeks of paraquat consumption. Once two serial levels were negative, they were considered for double lung transplantation.

Both cases were induced with methyl prednisolone, mycophenolate, and Basiliximab during the transplantation, and post‐transplant, they were started on triple immunosuppression with steroids, mycophenolate, and tacrolimus, as well as prophylaxis for pneumocystis pneumonia, cytomegalovirus, and aspergillus for at least 3 months.

Postoperative infections seem inevitable due to multiple reasons like surgical trauma, weak respiratory resistance due to immunosuppression, prolonged ECMO support, and toxic damage. Both patients had repeated bloodstream infections following lung transplant, as mentioned (Figures 1 and 3), such as Acinetobacter baumannii and Chryseobacterium indologenes, which were considered due to long‐term ECMO. Patient 2 had generalised tonic‐clonic seizures during the stay in the ICU, even though imaging was done and CSF analysis showed no features of infection; hence, it was considered secondary to CNS involvement of poison.

Bronchial stenosis is the most common airway complication following lung transplantation [13]. Balloon bronchoplasty and cryoablation of proliferative necrotic tissue are effective [14]. Advanced Bronchoscopic interventions for post‐transplant airway complications have improved the outcome. Serial dilatation was performed in of the cases with Anastomotic site stenosis at surveillance bronchoscopy.

Both the patients attained a normal pulmonary function after 2 months of lung transplantation with no evidence of chronic allograft dysfunction [15] during their 3‐, 6‐, and 12‐month follow up respectively (Figures 4 and 5).

Our case series signifies the clinical profile and timing of lung transplantation in patients developing respiratory failure due to paraquat‐induced lung fibrosis when weaning from ECMO support was considered difficult and presents the course of events after transplant.

In conclusion, this case series describes the challenges of the preoperative course before considering lung transplantation. Lung transplant after paraquat poisoning should be planned when the hepatorenal function starts to recover and once urine/serum paraquat level is negative in patients on extracorporeal support. Bloodstream infection and bacterial colonisation of the airways are the main challenges before and after lung transplant, which determine the success of the allograft.
