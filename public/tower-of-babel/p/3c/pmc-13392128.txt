Sphingosine kinase-2 inhibition promotes immunogenic differentiation of myeloid-derived suppressor cells through an Acetyl-CoA carboxylase-phosphatidylcholine axis
Authors: Paramita Chakraborty, Shilpak Chatterjee, Mohamed Faisal Kassir, Wyatt Wofford, Seungho Choi, Natalia Oleinik, Ozge Saatci, Odai Darawshi, Satyajit Das, Nathaniel Oberhoeltzer, Anupam Gautam, Stephanie Mills, Reid DeMass, Zacharia Hedley, Yueying Liu, Rasesh Y Parikh, Sandip Paul, Souvik Seal, Charles D Smith, Michael B Lilly, Vamsi K Gangaraju, Yuri Peterson, Meenal Mehrotra, Elizabeth Hill, Ozgur Sahin, Norbert Leitinger, Paulo C Rodriguez, Besim Ogretmen, Shikhar Mehrotra
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMyeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) in the tumor microenvironment (TME) limit the efficacy of adoptive T cell therapies, highlighting the need to overcome tumor-associated immunosuppression. Sphingosine-1-phosphate (S1P), is an abundant signaling lipid in the TME. Here, we show that inhibition of sphingosine kinase-2 (SphK2), the enzyme generating S1P in MDSCs, reduces the suppressive activity of monocytic MDSCs (M-MDSCs) while promoting their differentiation toward a mature, immunogenic phenotype characterized by enhanced antigen presentation. Pharmacological SphK2 inhibition enhances the response to anti–PD–1 therapy in preclinical models of checkpoint-resistant breast, bladder, and melanoma cancers by mitigating MDSC-mediated suppression and limiting tumor progression. Mechanistically, S1P directly binds acetyl-CoA carboxylase-1 (ACC1) to inhibit its activity, thereby rewiring fatty-acid metabolism. Lowering intracellular S1P restores ACC activity, promotes phosphatidylcholine synthesis, and reduces MDSC immunosuppression. These findings identify the SphK2–ACC–phospholipid axis as a metabolic checkpoint controlling the immunogenicity of MDSCs and a potential therapeutic target for enhancing cancer immunotherapy.Subject terms: Tumour immunology, Immunosuppression

Introduction

A primary mechanism of tumor-immune evasion is through suppressing anti-tumor immune responses1. Myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) are essential immunosuppressive entities within the tumor microenvironment (TME) that facilitate cancer progression. The heterogeneous immature myeloid population expands markedly with tumor growth and suppresses T cell activity, thereby promoting tumor development, metastasis, and resistance to treatment. MDSCs secrete suppressive cytokines and express additional enzymes that deplete essential nutrients from T cells, including nitric oxide synthase (iNOS) iNOS, arginase 1, PDL1/2, and SLC7A112,3. Importantly, these cells do not express MHC and costimulatory molecules necessary for antigen presentation and cytotoxic T-cell activation4. Growing evidence indicates that MDSCs are characteristic of malignant tumors, and their presence in the TME constitutes a substantial obstacle to cancer immunotherapies. Thus, targeting these cells may improve therapeutic efficacy3,5.

The activity and development of various subsets of myeloid cells are crucially regulated by metabolic reprogramming from glycolysis to lipid metabolism. ATP is primarily derived from fatty acid oxidation (FAO) in tumor invasive MDSCs6. FAO is also the primary metabolic fuel to produce inhibitory cytokines, and MDSCs depend on FAO to perform their immunosuppressive function7,8. MDSCs boost fatty acid intake and activate FAO following tumor invasion, which raises mitochondrial biosynthesis and improves oxygen consumption rate (OCR). Another important factor influencing the immunosuppressive activity of MDSCs is glycolysis; a decrease in glycolysis diminishes the signaling necessary for MDSC differentiation9. MDSCs also undergo activation-induced cell death and transform into inflammatory cells when glutamine metabolism is inhibited10. Therefore, targeting molecules in metabolic pathways to cause nutrient/energy shortage may alter MDSC function and improve the therapeutic effects against different types of cancers. However, little is known about the molecular and transcriptional networks that govern metabolic rewiring and its immunosuppressive function.

Sphingosine-1-phosphate (S1P) is a bioactive lipid that plays a crucial role in the immune system, particularly in the egress of T cells from the thymus11. A network of metabolic enzymes governs the production of ceramide and S1P, acting as transducers of sphingolipid-mediated reactions linked to diverse external or endogenous cellular cues12. The equilibrium between S1P synthesis via sphingosine kinase-1 (Sphk1) and sphingosine kinase-2 (SphK2) and its breakdown by S1P lyase governs cellular concentrations13. SphK1 and SphK2 exhibit differences in location, with SphK1 residing in the cytoplasm and SphK2 in the nucleus, and they fulfill unique functions in diverse cancer and autoimmune models14. S1P transmits signals through G protein-coupled receptors (GPCRs; S1P receptors 1-5, S1PR1-5) via paracrine or autocrine mechanisms to facilitate cancer cell growth, proliferation, and survival15–17. Besides receptor-dependent signaling, intracellular S1P modulates signal transduction and pro-survival pathways through direct binding to its targets via lipid-protein interaction12. For example, while SphK2-derived S1P interacts with HDAC1/218, prohibitin-219, or telomerase20, the SphK1-derived S1P associates with TRAF2 for NFκB activation21, or PPARγ for induction of PPARγ-dependent transcription in human endothelial cells and T cells22.

Analogous to T cells, S1P receptor signaling is essential for the myeloid population, affecting dendritic cell homeostasis, macrophage migration, and functionality in both healthy and pathological settings23–26. S1P/S1PR signaling facilitates M2 polarization by augmenting IL-4 secretion, diminishing pro-inflammatory cytokine secretion, inhibiting iNOS activity, and elevating arginase I activity27,28. Direct activation of the S1P/S1PR axis may confer pro-angiogenic characteristics to macrophages, hence facilitating tumor angiogenesis and metastasis29–34. S1PR1 signaling additionally enhances Angiotensin II (Ang II)-dependent generation of TAMs in the spleen35. The receptor-independent function of S1P in macrophages has been investigated in multiple inflammatory conditions. Macrophages lacking in SphK2 exhibit an elevated presence of lipid droplet-containing autophagosomes and autolysosomes, alongside impaired lysosomal clearance of lipid droplets by autophagy36. However, the degree to which receptor-independent intracellular S1P signaling governs MDSC phenotype, function, and metabolic state, subsequently affecting their pro-tumor activity, is inadequately comprehended.

Here, we demonstrate that intracellular S1P generated by SphK2 directly binds and inhibits acetyl-CoA carboxylase (ACC), suppressing de novo fatty acid synthesis and phosphatidylcholine (PC) production in monocytic MDSCs (M-MDSCs). Pharmacological or genetic inhibition of SphK2 restores ACC activity, increases PC biosynthesis, and reprograms M-MDSCs from an immunosuppressive toward an immunostimulatory, antigen-presenting phenotype that enhances tumor-specific T cell responses. Consequently, SphK2 inhibition synergizes with anti-PD-1 checkpoint blockade to improve tumor control in preclinical models of checkpoint-resistant breast, bladder, and melanoma cancers. These findings establish the SphK2–S1P–ACC–PC axis as a metabolic checkpoint governing the immunogenicity of MDSCs, and identify its pharmacological targeting as a broadly applicable strategy to overcome myeloid-mediated immunosuppression and enhance the efficacy of cancer immunotherapies.

Results

SphK2 Deficiency Promotes an Immunogenic Shift in MDSCs

To investigate the effect of intracellular S1P on the regulatory activity of tumor-associated MDSCs, the expression of SphK1/2 isoforms in Gr1+CD11b+ MDSCs isolated from the tumors and spleens of B16-F10 melanoma and EL4 thymoma-bearing mice was determined using flow cytometry. We observed that SphK1 (Fig. 1A, and Supplementary Fig. 1A) and SphK2 (Fig. 1B, and Supplementary 1A) levels are elevated in MDSCs isolated from tumor sites compared to non-tumor-bearing spleens in both B16 melanoma and EL4 thymoma models (Supplementary Figs 1B, C). However, analysis of publicly available scRNA-seq data from three genetically engineered mouse (GEM) models of invasive breast cancer37, driven by concurrent deletion of Pten and Trp53 (p53), revealed a predominant expression of SPHK2 over SPHK1 within the myeloid population (Fig. 1C, and Supplementary Fig. 1D).

To ascertain the specific roles of SphK1 and SphK2 in modulating MDSC suppressive function, EL-4 tumors were established in WT, SphK1-KO, and SphK2-KO mice. After two weeks, MDSCs (Gr1+CD11b+) were harvested from ascites and evaluated for their ability to inhibit T cell proliferation and effector cytokine secretion. Purified MDSCs from SphK2-KO mice exhibited decreased inhibition of proliferation (Fig. 1D, and Supplementary Fig. 1E) and IFN-γ secretion (Fig. 1E) of TCR-stimulated CD8+ T cells. Importantly, MDSCs from SphK1-KO mice demonstrated suppression comparable to WT-derived MDSCs (Fig. 1D, E). Accordingly, SphK2-KO MDSCs displayed significantly lower S1P levels than WT MDSCs but not SphK1-KO MDSCs (Fig. 1F). Therefore, the diminished suppression observed in MDSCs from SphK2-KO mice appears to be distinctive and may relate to the cellular localization of S1P.

Importantly, SphK2 expression was consistently higher in intratumoral MDSCs compared with splenic MDSCs isolated from the same tumor-bearing mice (Supplementary Fig. 1F), indicating that SphK2 upregulation is preferentially induced within the TME rather than being primarily driven by systemic tumor burden. Moreover, while splenic M-MDSCs exhibited only modest changes (p = 0.0032) in SphK2 expression, intratumoral M-MDSCs displayed markedly elevated levels (p = 0.0007) with progressively increased tumor size (Supplementary Fig. 1F), suggesting that SphK2-mediated metabolic reprogramming occurs after myeloid cell infiltration into the tumor and becomes amplified as the TME evolves. We then investigated whether SphK2-KO MDSCs have lower immunosuppressive activity against tumor antigen-specific T cells in vivo, potentially leading to better tumor control. To explore this, we injected B16-F10 murine melanoma tumors into wild-type (WT) and SphK2-KO mice. Nine days after establishing the tumors, gp100-specific TCR-bearing Pmel T cells were transferred into the tumor-bearing mice, and tumor growth was monitored. Tumor growth was significantly slower in Sphk2-KO mice than in WT controls (Supplementary Fig. 1G).

To investigate the role of myeloid-specific SphK2 deletion in MDSC phenotypes within an S1P-sufficient environment, we generated a SphK2 (CD45.2) bone marrow chimera by transplanting SphK2 KO bone marrow into a WT C57BL/6 host (CD45.1). After eight weeks, EL-4 tumors were injected into the chimeric mice, and ascites were used to purify CD45.1+ or CD45.2+ Gr1+CD11b+ MDSCs (Fig. 1G, H). T cells co-cultured with CD45.2+ SphK2-KO MDSCs exhibited reduced suppression as compared to those with WT CD45.1, leading to improved proliferation (Fig. 1I) and increased IFN-γ production (Fig. 1J). These findings suggest that intracellular S1P plays a critical role in maintaining the immunosuppressive phenotype of MDSCs. Given the reduced suppressive capacity observed in SphK2 KO MDSCs, we next assessed their immunogenic profile within the TME. To this end, MDSCs were isolated from EL4 tumor-bearing WT and SphK2 KO mice. SphK2 deficiency increased the expression of key costimulatory and antigen-presenting molecules, including CD40, CD80, CD86, and MHC-I (H-2Kb) (Fig. 1K) in Ly6ChighLy6Glow monocytic MDSCs (M-MDSCs; Supplementary Fig. 1H). Furthermore, SphK2 KO M-MDSCs displayed reduced levels of arginase (Fig. 1L) and secreted higher amounts of IL-12 and lower levels of TGF-β (Fig. 1M). In contrast, Ly6ClowLy6Ghigh polymorphonuclear MDSCs (PMN-MDSCs) did not exhibit significant changes in surface marker expression (Supplementary Fig. S1H, I). Consistent with this, SphK2 expression did not differ between splenic and tumor-derived PMN-MDSCs. In contrast, tumor-derived M-MDSCs displayed significantly elevated SphK2 expression compared to splenic counterparts (Supplementary Fig. 1J), indicating selective upregulation of Sphk2 within the monocytic subset in the TME.

Next, we evaluated whether pharmacologic inhibition of SphK2 recapitulates the effects observed with genetic deletion. Mouse thymoma EL-4 cells were injected into C57BL/6 and treated with escalating doses of ABC294640, a sphingolipid metabolism regulator that functions as a non-lipid competitive inhibitor of SphK2 (SphK2i; 25, 50, or 100 mg/kg) for two weeks. While 25 mg/kg had no significant effect, treatment with 50 mg/kg increased MHC class I, CD80, and CD86 expression on M-MDSCs (Supplementary Fig. 1K), consistent with the phenotype observed in SphK2-KO mice. Therefore, we selected 50 mg/kg for all subsequent experiments to maximize efficacy while minimizing potential toxicity. MDSCs were also obtained from the ascitic tumor sites of both SphK2i treated (50 mg/kg) and untreated EL4-bearing mice and co-cultured with T cells to assess the effect on T-cell proliferation (Supplementary Fig. 1L). We observed that MDSCs from mice treated with the SphK2i exhibited a reduced ability to suppress the proliferation of CD4+ (Fig. 1N) and CD8+ T lymphocytes (Fig. 1O), consistent with the lower suppressive capacity observed in MDSCs that are genetically deficient in SphK2.

To characterize the transcriptional changes associated with this phenotype, we performed RNA sequencing. RNA sequencing analysis revealed 484 differentially expressed genes between WT and SphK2-KO MDSCs (p < 0.05, |log₂ fold change| >1), many of which are associated with immune responses and cytokine secretion (Supplementary Fig. 1M). ATAC-seq data supported altered chromatin landscapes (Supplementary Fig. 1N), including increased accessibility at the H2-Q locus in SphK2-KO MDSCs versus WT (Fig. 1P). Importantly, Genomic Regions Enrichment of Annotations Tool (GREAT) annotation of differentially accessible regions demonstrated enrichment beyond the H2 locus (Supplementary Fig. 1O), identifying regulatory programs associated with myeloid leukocyte activation, positive regulation of immune effector process differentiation, and leukocyte migration pathways (Supplementary Fig. 1P). These findings indicate that SphK2 deletion induces broader chromatin remodeling linked to immune activation rather than selectively affecting MHC-associated loci. These data suggest that SphK2 deletion reprograms M-MDSCs toward a more immunostimulatory phenotype, thereby enhancing CD8⁺ T cell-mediated anti-tumor responses.

Pharmacological inhibition of SphK2 improves tumor control by altering myeloid responses

To determine whether targeting SphK2 could improve tumor control by modulating the myeloid compartment, we examined the effects of pharmacological inhibition in multiple murine tumor models. Thus, we engrafted 4T1 mammary carcinoma, a highly tumorigenic and metastatic animal model for human breast cancer, into BALB/c mice. Two weeks after the injection, half of the tumor-bearing mice were treated with Sphk2i (50 mg/kg) on alternate days. In the Sphk2i-treated group, tumor growth was significantly suppressed compared to the untreated control group (Fig. 2A). A comprehensive evaluation of MDSCs revealed that monocytic MDSCs (M-MDSCs) had increased expression of immunological stimulatory markers MHC-I (H-2Kd), CD80, and CD86 (Fig. 2B, Supplementary Fig. 2A), along with decreased levels of the immune inhibitory enzyme arginase-I compared to untreated mice (Fig. 2C). Sphk2i also drove the differentiation of Ly6Chigh monocytic MDSCs into F4/80high macrophages (Fig. 2D, and Supplementary Fig. 2B), suggesting a shift from an immature suppressive phenotype toward more mature macrophages. Consistent with this, F4/80high macrophages from treated mice exhibited reduced CD163 (Supplementary Fig. 2C) and increased MHC class II expression compared to untreated controls (Supplementary Fig. 2D). Furthermore, T cells from the tumor site of Sphk2i-treated animals exhibited enhanced expression of the activation marker CD44 (Fig. 2E, and Supplementary Fig. 2E), increased secretion of IFN-γ (Fig. 2F), and reduced expression of PD1 (Fig. 2G).

We then used a PD1-resistant MB-49 tumor model to evaluate whether suppressing SphK2 could influence the response to anti-PD1 therapy by regulating MDSCs. Mice treated with a combination of Sphk2i and anti-PD1 reduced tumor development (Fig. 2H) and prolonged survival (Supplementary Fig. 2F) compared to untreated mice or those receiving only anti-PD1 antibody. Tumor M-MDSCs from mice treated with Sphk2i and anti-PD1 antibody had higher levels of CD80, CD86, and MHC-I (H-2Kb) (Fig. 2I, and Supplementary Fig. 2G), and lower levels of arginase I (Fig. 2J), as well as enhanced differentiation into F4/80high macrophages compared to untreated controls (Fig. 2K, Supplementary Fig. 2H). Consistent with this, F4/80high macrophages from treated mice exhibited reduced CD163 (Supplementary Fig. 2I) and increased MHC class II expression compared to untreated controls (Supplementary Fig. 2J). T lymphocytes isolated from the tumor site demonstrated increased expression of the activation marker CD44 (Fig. 2L, and Supplementary Fig. 2K), elevated IFN-γ secretion (Fig. 2M), and decreased PD1 expression (Fig. 2N), suggesting that SphK2 suppression alters the immune environment, increasing immunogenic MDSCs and enhancing T cell function.

Next, we evaluated if administering SphK2i in combination with adoptive transfer of Pmel T cells (ACT) and anti-PD1 checkpoint blockade could enhance control of subcutaneous murine melanoma (Supplementary Fig. 2L). The combination therapy (ACT + Sphk2i) significantly improved murine survival and reduced tumor growth compared to ACT with anti-PD1. The triple combination therapy showed the most significant benefit in tumor control (Fig. 2O, and Supplementary Fig. 2L). Additionally, we observed elevated expression of immunogenic markers on MDSCs (Fig. 2P, and Supplementary Fig. 2M), M-MDSCs differentiated into F4/80high macrophages (Fig. 2Q, and Supplementary Fig. 2N), and decreased levels of exhaustion markers PD-1, Lag3, and CD38 on tumor-infiltrating CD8+ T cells (Fig. 2R). Importantly, depletion of either MDSCs (anti-Gr1) or macrophages (anti-CSF1R) abrogated the antitumor effects of Sphk2i (Fig. 2S), indicating that its benefit is myeloid-dependent rather than directly T cell–driven. These findings suggest that targeting SphK2 along with a checkpoint inhibitor effectively inhibits the growth of PD1-resistant B16-F10 melanoma in vivo by transforming the TME into a less immunosuppressive state.

Moreover, because M-MDSCs represent a major precursor population for tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) within the TME, we investigated whether SphK2 deletion affects the ability of M-MDSCs to differentiate into TAMs in vivo. To address this, we performed congenic adoptive transfer experiments. In vitro-generated WT or SphK2-KO CD45.2+ MDSCs were transferred into B16 tumor-bearing CD45.1+ hosts, and donor-derived CD45.2+ cells were analyzed within tumors. WT donor MDSCs exhibited limited maturation toward Ly6Clow F4/80+ macrophage-like cells, whereas SphK2-KO donor cells demonstrated enhanced differentiation toward this more mature macrophage phenotype (Supplementary Fig. 2O, P). Consistently, Ly6C+ WT donor cells expressed lower levels of CD80, CD86, and MHC-I compared to SphK2-KO counterparts (Supplementary Fig. 2Q). Together, these data indicate that SphK2 deletion facilitates M-MDSC differentiation toward an immunogenic macrophage-like state within the TME. To further assess differentiation dynamics in mature macrophages, WT or SphK2-KO bone marrow cells were differentiated to macrophages using M-CSF in the presence of tumor explant supernatant (TES). Following SphK2 deletion, we found increased expressions of CD40, CD80, CD86, MHC-I (H-2Kb), iNOS, and IL-12, alongside decreased expressions of Arginase-1 and IL-10 (Supplementary Fig. 2R, S) in TES-conditioned SphK2-KO macrophages (CD11b+ F4/80+). Collectively, these findings suggest that SphK2 inhibition drives the differentiation of suppressive monocytic MDSCs into an immunostimulatory, mature macrophage-like phenotype, thereby unleashing T-cell activity.

SphK2 abundance is linked to higher levels of suppressive M-MDSCs in human malignancies

To understand the relevance of SphK2 expression in a physiologically and clinically relevant context, we next analyzed MDSCs from tumor samples of cancer patients in comparison to healthy controls. We first examined publicly available single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) data from 26 breast tumor biopsies, including 10 triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) tumor biopsies38 and PBMCs from healthy donors. This identified a monocytic MDSC subpopulation (CD33⁺CD11b⁺HLA-DRlowCD14⁺) within the myeloid cells, of which the majority (66%) originated from TNBC patients that express higher levels of SPHK2 (Fig. 3A, and Supplementary Fig. 3A). On the contrary, the corresponding CD14+ monocytic subpopulation in PBMCs from healthy donors exhibits lower SPHK2 levels (Fig. 3A, and Supplementary Fig. 3A). To corroborate these transcriptomic findings at the protein level, we analyzed tissue microarrays (TMAs) comprising 122 triple-negative high-grade breast cancer (TNBC) samples representing various disease stages. Using automated multispectral imaging, we observed increased infiltration of monocytic MDSCs (M-MDSCs; (pan-cytokeratinnegCD11b+HLA-DRneg/lowCD14+CD15neg) in breast tumor tissues relative to normal tissues (Fig. 3B and Supplementary Fig. 3B). Importantly, these infiltrating M-MDSCs exhibited significantly elevated SphK2 protein expression compared to those in normal tissue controls (Fig. 3C). However, due to the limited infiltration of these cells, we were unable to reliably examine the expression of SphK2 in PMN-MDSCs from prostate tumors.

Similar to TNBC samples, we found an increased frequency of M-MDSCs in 150 prostate cancer samples and higher expression of SphK2 in tumor-associated M-MDSCs compared to healthy prostate tissue (Fig. 3D, E). Overall, our findings support the hypothesis that higher SphK2 abundance is linked to increased suppressive MDSCs in human prostates. Notably, we obtained the PBMC samples from a recently completed Phase II trial (NCT04207255) involving Metastatic castration-resistant prostate cancer (mCRPC) patients who had previously failed androgen axis blocker treatment (abiraterone or enzalutamide) to determine if there were any differences in MDSCs. Our analysis showed a reduction in monocytic MDSCs following treatment with the SphK2i (ABC294640 or Opaganib; 500 mg BID) in combination with androgen blockers (Fig. 3F). Among the thirty patients tested, fifteen exhibited a decrease in monocytic MDSCs (Supplementary Fig. 3C), which exhibited an increase in granzyme B and IFN-γ-producing CD8⁺ T cells (Fig. 3F, and Supplementary Fig. 3D). Additionally, these patients showed stable or, in some cases, decreased PSA levels in the blood (Fig. 3G), suggesting a previously unrecognized beneficial effect of SphK2i in human cancer.

Building on these findings, we next investigated the association between SphK2 expression in MDSCs and patient responsiveness to immune checkpoint blockade (ICB) therapy. We analyzed tumor biopsy samples from three patients with PD1-resistant and three with PD1-sensitive oral cavity squamous cell carcinoma (OCSCC), obtained from the MUSC biorepository39. Notably, monocytic MDSCs in PD1–resistant tumors displayed higher SphK2 expression compared to those in PD1–sensitive tumors, suggesting a potential link between elevated SphK2 levels and resistance to ICB therapy (Fig. 3H and Supplementary Fig. 3E).

Since tumor-infiltrating human MDSCs exhibited high SphK2 expression, we next tested whether pharmacological or genetic inhibition of SphK2 in human MDSCs could modulate their immunogenicity. To this end, we generated human MDSCs by differentiating CD33⁺ cells, isolated from the PBMCs of healthy donors, in the presence of GM-CSF and IL-6, supplemented with 30% tumor-conditioned media from the ovarian cancer cell line SK-OV3, for one week. Some groups were treated with SphK2i. We observed that SphK2i-treated monocytic MDSCs (CD33⁺CD11b⁺HLA-DR⁻/lowCD14⁺CD15⁻) exhibited significantly higher expression of the co-stimulatory molecules CD80 and CD86 (Fig. 3I& Supplementary Fig. 3F). Furthermore, in co-culture assays with T cells, SphK2i-treated MDSCs demonstrated a reduced capacity to suppress T-cell proliferation (Fig. 3J, and Supplementary Fig. 3G) and an increase in IFN-γ production (Fig. 3K, and Supplementary Fig. 3H).

To further validate the role of SphK2 in MDSC-mediated immunosuppression, we used SphK2-targeting shRNA to genetically knock down SphK2 expression in MDSCs. Similar to pharmacological inhibition, SphK2 knockdown led to MDSCs with significantly elevated CD80 and CD86 expression, indicating a shift toward a less immunosuppressive phenotype (Supplementary Fig. 3I and J). Moreover, SphK2-deficient MDSCs exhibited a markedly reduced ability to inhibit T-cell proliferation and showed a substantial increase in IFN-γ secretion in co-culture assays, further confirming that SphK2 plays a crucial role in maintaining MDSC-mediated immune suppression (Supplementary Fig. 3K, L).

Taken together, our findings demonstrate that both pharmacological inhibition and genetic silencing of SphK2 impair the suppressive function of MDSCs, promoting a more immunostimulatory phenotype that enhances T-cell activation. These results highlight SphK2 as a potential therapeutic target for modulating MDSC function and improving anti-tumor immunity.

S1P–acetyl-CoA carboxylase (ACC) interaction alters lipid signatures in MDSCs

To understand how intracellular S1P affects MDSC activity, we used an S1P pull-down assay followed by a proteomics study to identify proteins linked to S1P in WT MDSCs. To address the need for a high number of MDSCs in our proteomic experiments, we generated tumor explant supernatant (TES)-exposed bone marrow-derived MDSCs (BM-MDSCs). After confirming that pre-treatment of murine BM-MDSCs with B16-F10 TES causes upregulation of SphK2 (Supplementary Fig. 4A), comparable to the effects generated by the TME, wild-type MDSC lysates were incubated with biotin-S1P to detect the S1P-bound proteins. Proteins that bind specifically with biotin-S1P, rather than biotin alone, were identified by MS-based proteomics analysis (Fig. 4A). The study revealed that many metabolic pathway proteins and proteins related to ribosome biogenesis bind to S1P (Fig. 4A). In agreement with a prior report19, we also observed that mitochondrial prohibitin (PHB) is linked to S1P (Fig. 4A), supporting our sample processing and findings. Notably, we found S1P bound to both isoforms of Acetyl CoA carboxylase (ACC) (Fig. 4A), which catalyzes the conversion of acetyl CoA to malonyl CoA. These two isozymes, ACC1 and ACC2, have synergistic physiological roles within the cell to promote fatty acid synthesis40. ACC1 produces malonyl-CoA in the cytosol, which acts as a carbon donor for long-chain fatty acid synthesis via fatty acid synthase (FASN)41. ACC2-produced malonyl-CoA acts as an inhibitor of CPT1, reducing the transport of long-chain fatty acids into mitochondria for β-oxidation42. To confirm our proteomics findings, we used a Proximity Ligation Assay (PLA)43–45 to verify in situ interactions between S1P and ACC1 in MDSCs. PLA revealed that S1P binds to ACC1 in WT MDSCs (when S1P levels are high) but not in SphK2 KO MDSCs (when intracellular S1P levels are low) (Fig. 4B). As S1P is also known to bind and allosterically modulate enzymes20, we sought to determine if S1P binding regulates ACC1 activity. We found that tumor-derived SphK2-KO MDSCs showed elevated ACC activity relative to WT controls (Fig. 4C), implicating that S1P is a potential inhibitor of ACC1. As ACC1 can be regulated allosterically by other molecules, such as AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK)46,47, we repeated these studies with AMPK inhibition. Importantly, we observed that pharmacological inhibition of AMPK further increases ACC1 activity compared to SphK2-KO MDSCs alone, indicating different allosteric binding sites for S1P and AMPK on ACC1 (Supplementary Fig. 4B).

Given the role of ACC in lipid synthesis and our finding that the loss of S1P increases ACC activity, we investigated whether enhanced ACC activity in SphK2-KO MDSCs affects their lipid metabolism. Thus, we employed an untargeted global and LC-MS-based lipidomic method. The global analysis identified 1300 putatively named lipid structures from 33 lipid groups. A thorough principal component analysis reveals a clear separation of the two groups along the PC2 axis (Supplementary Fig. 4C). Unsupervised clustering revealed 860 lipid features that were significantly altered in the SphK2-KO group compared to the WT group (p = 0.01; Log2 fold change ≥0.5). Notably, phosphatidylcholine (PC) species were the most highly upregulated lipid class in SphK2-KO MDSCs (Fig. 4D). To improve the interpretability of the lipidomic changes, we reorganized the data at the lipid-class level and generated consensus heatmaps summarizing dominant lipid subclasses (Fig. 4E). The heatmap highlights global lipid class remodeling, demonstrating enrichment of structural phospholipids in SphK2-KO MDSCs, including diacyl-phosphatidylcholine (PC), ether-linked phosphatidylcholine species (plasmanyl-PC and plasmenyl-PC), phosphatidylinositol (PI), cardiolipin (CL), and selected ceramide subclasses. In contrast, lysophospholipids such as Lyso-PC, Lyso-PE, and Lyso-PG were relatively reduced. (Fig. 4E). Together, these findings suggest that SphK2 inhibition, through enhanced ACC activity, reshapes the lipid landscape of MDSCs, particularly by enhancing phospholipid species that contribute to reprogramming them toward a more immunogenic phenotype.

Inhibiting ACC activity reduces the immunostimulatory potential of SphK2 KO MDSCs

Given the function of ACC in lipid synthesis and our finding that loss of S1P increases ACC activity, we next investigated whether enhanced ACC activity in SphK2-KO MDSCs affects their immunogenicity. To assess the role of ACC in the immunostimulatory phenotype of SphK2-KO MDSCs, we inhibited ACC activity using ND64648. Dose titration in SphK2-KO monocytic MDSCs (0.03, 0.3, and 3 µM) identified 3 µM as the optimal concentration, significantly reducing ACC activity without inhibiting proliferation (Supplementary Fig. 5A). ACC inhibition reduced MHC class I, CD80, and CD86 and increased Arginase1 expression in SphK2-KO MDSCs to levels comparable to WT controls (Supplementary Fig. 5B), indicating that elevated ACC activity drives the enhanced immunogenic phenotype observed in SphK2-deficient MDSCs. To determine the individual involvement of ACC1 and ACC2 in regulating the increased immunogenicity observed in SphK2-KO MDSCs, siRNA was employed to silence either ACC1 or ACC2 (Supplementary Fig. 5C–E). We found that silencing any ACC isoforms in SphK2-KO MDSCs reduced MHC class I, CD80, and CD86 expression and increased Arginase1 expression. Notably, ACC1 silencing exerted a more pronounced effect than ACC2 silencing (Fig. 5A). However, inhibiting both isoforms in SphK2 MDSCs led to the lowest levels of immunostimulatory molecule expression (Fig. 5A, Supplementary Fig. F, G) and IL-12 release (Fig. 5B) in SphK2-KO MDSCs. Inhibiting both ACC isoforms in SphK2-KO MDSCs and co-culturing them overnight with CTV-labeled gp100-reactive Pmel T cells significantly reduced antigen-specific proliferation (Fig. 5C, and Supplementary Fig. 5H, I) and IFN-γ secretion (Fig. 5D), indicating that ACC inhibition reverses the immunogenic phenotype of SphK2-KO MDSCs.

Fatty acid production is linked to a heightened immunogenic phenotype in dendritic cells (DCs), as evidenced by increased expression of CD80, CD86, and MHC class II molecules49,50. Given the role of ACC enzymes in fatty acid synthesis and oxidation, and our observation that their loss dampens the immunostimulatory phenotype of SphK2-KO MDSCs, we next examined whether ACC1/2 mediates SphK2-driven lipid metabolic reprogramming in MDSCs. To this end, we used unbiased LC–MS–based lipidomics to assess the impact of ACC1/2 loss on lipid metabolism in SphK2-KO MDSCs. Silencing ACC1/2 altered the levels of over 400 lipid species compared to control MDSCs and resulted in a distinct clustering pattern in PCA analysis (Supplementary Fig. 5J). Notably, the SphK2-associated enrichment of several PC species was reduced in ACC1/2-deficient MDSCs (Fig. 5E). Lipidomic profiling further demonstrated that (Supplementary Fig. 5K) suppression of ACC1/2 in SphK2-KO MDSCs substantially altered the lipid landscape. Hierarchical clustering (Supplementary Fig. 5K) revealed that ACC1/2 knockdown reversed the accumulation of several lipid classes that were elevated in SphK2-KO cells, including phosphatidylcholine (PC), triglycerides (TG), and diacylglycerols (DG). Concomitantly, several lysophospholipid classes, including LysoPE and LysoPG, were relatively increased following ACC1/2 suppression. These findings indicate that ACC activity is a major driver of the lipid remodeling induced by SphK2 loss and suggest that enhanced fatty acid synthesis contributes to the accumulation of structural membrane phospholipids and neutral lipid species in SphK2-KO MDSCs. To exclude the possibility that this effect reflects a chronic consequence of genetic deletion of SphK2, we isolated MDSCs from tumors treated with the SphK2 inhibitor and compared them to untreated controls. Pharmacologic inhibition similarly increased total PC abundance in MDSCs, confirming that SphK2-dependent PC remodeling is dynamically regulated (Supplementary Fig. 5L).

Given the prominent reduction in PC species following ACC1/2 silencing, we next sought to determine whether ACC1 and ACC2 contribute differentially to this lipid remodeling. Isoform-selective knockdown experiments revealed that ACC1 silencing preferentially reduced PC enrichment associated with the SphK2-KO phenotype (Fig. 5F), supporting a dominant role for cytosolic ACC1 in driving de novo lipid synthesis required for PC generation. In contrast, ACC2 silencing resulted in increased fatty acid oxidation (FAO), evidenced by an elevated oxygen consumption rate (OCR) and spare respiratory capacity (SRC), consistent with its established role in regulating CPT1 activity (Fig. 5G), and a metabolic phenotype associated with suppressive and tolerogenic MDSCs6. This was also accompanied by decreased glycolytic activity, as indicated by a lower extracellular acidification rate (ECAR) (Fig. 5H). Notably, dual ACC1/2 knockdown produced the most pronounced metabolic shift, indicating complementary contributions of both isoforms (Fig. 5F–H). Glycolytic enzymes are tightly regulated by post-translational modifications such as acetylation, which relies on the availability of nucleocytosolic acetyl-CoA51–53. We hypothesized that in SphK2-KO MDSCs, elevated ACC activity diverts acetyl-CoA toward malonyl-CoA synthesis, thereby limiting the acetyl-CoA pool required for protein acetylation and reducing the acetylation of glycolytic enzymes. To assess this, we performed acetylated protein pull-down assays followed by mass spectrometry in WT MDSCs. Acetylome profiling revealed several differentially acetylated proteins, including PKM2 (Supplementary Fig. 5M), a key glycolytic enzyme that catalyzes the final step of glycolysis and is known to be regulated by acetylation51. Proximity ligation assays (PLA) confirmed higher PKM2 acetylation in WT MDSCs than SphK2-KO MDSCs (Supplementary Fig. 5N). This enhanced acetylation in WT cells was associated with reduced pyruvate kinase activity (Supplementary Fig. 5O), suggesting lower glycolytic flux relative to SphK2 KO MDSCs. Our findings suggest that ACC isoforms exert complementary metabolic functions in SphK2 KO MDSCs, with ACC1 driving PC synthesis and ACC2 regulating FAO and glycolytic balance, collectively sustaining their immunogenic phenotype.

To functionally evaluate the role of myeloid ACC in vivo, we performed tumor growth studies using LysMCreAcc1fl/fl mice in the setting of SphK2 inhibition (ABC294640), with or without dual ACC1/2 inhibition (ND646). Tumor-bearing mice were assigned to the following groups: Acc1fl/fl, Acc1fl/fl+ABC294640, LysMCreAcc1fl/fl+ABC294640, and ABC294640, LysMCreAcc1fl/fl+ABC294640+ND646. Myeloid-specific deletion of ACC1 significantly attenuated the tumor control achieved by SphK2 inhibition (Fig. 5I, Supplementary Fig. 5P: p = 0.0103). Moreover, dual ACC1/2 inhibition with ND646 resulted in an even greater loss of tumor control compared with the ACC1-intact group (p = 0.0001). Collectively, these findings reveal that SphK2 loss promotes ACC-dependent PC enrichment and metabolic rewiring in MDSCs, which is required to sustain their immunostimulatory phenotype.

Inhibiting S1P-ACC1 binding increases the immunogenicity of WT MDSCs

The interaction of S1P with signaling molecules can mimic the effects of protein phosphorylation on its activity20. Molecular docking simulations were conducted to investigate potential S1P binding modes to ACC using the existing X-ray crystal structure of ACC1 (PDB: 6G2D). The simulations predict that S1P interacts with ACC1 primarily via the Ser1996 residue (Fig. 6A). Additional potential binding sites include Glu1015, Lys2009, and Lys1018. Further analysis revealed that ACC2 shares 72% sequence similarity with ACC1. Docking predictions for S1P on ACC2, supported by pairwise alignment, indicate analogous binding residues at all sites except Ser1996. These molecular details led us to hypothesize that S1P serves as a phospho-mimetic, phosphorylating ACC in WT MDSCs. To test this idea, we generated a WT ACC1 plasmid and a mutant ACC1 plasmid with the Ser1996Ala change. In the presence of TES, BM-MDSCs were produced from mice with myeloid-specific knockdown of both ACC1 and ACC2 isoforms (LyMCreACC-DKO hereafter) and from ACC1/2fl/fl animals. LyMCreACC DKO MDSCs were then transfected with either WT ACC1 (ACCwt) or Ser1996Ala ACC1 mutant (ACC1mut) plasmids (Fig. 6B, and Supplementary Fig. 6A). LyMCreACC-DKO MDSCs were overexpressed with plasmids containing either WT-ACC1 (ACCwt) or the S1996A ACC1 mutant (ACC1mut) (Fig. 6B).

To directly quantify the interaction between S1P and ACC1, we performed saturation binding assays using partially purified FLAG-tagged WT or mutant ACC1 expressed in HEK 293T cells and isolated with anti-FLAG agarose beads. The immobilized proteins were incubated in the presence of increasing concentrations of biotinylated S1P (starting at 0.3 nM) with or without excess of unlabeled S1P (10 uM) to define nonspecific binding. Bound biotinylated S1P was measured by using a biotin-sensitive ELISA. Nonlinear regression analysis revealed an equilibrium dissociation constant (Kd) of ~61 nM for WT ACC1 and 60 nM for the mutant protein (Fig. 6C, and Supplementary Fig. 6B). Interestingly, while Kd values remain in a similar range, the binding stoichiometry derived from Bmax values demonstrates an ~2:1 ratio of S1P to WT ACC1 compared to 1:1 for the mutant protein (Fig. 6C). Collectively, these data show that S1P binds directly to ACC1 with modest affinity in vitro and suggest that this interaction may regulate ACC1 function by modulating its enzymatic activity. Moreover, these results indicate that the Ser1996 residue is critical for optimal S1P-ACC1 interaction.

To further validate S1P–ACC1 interaction in a cellular context, we overexpressed FLAG-tagged WT or mutant ACC1 in ACC1-deficient (LysMCreAcc1flox/flox) MDSCs and incubated cells with C17-Sphingosine for 4 h in serum-free media. FLAG-tagged ACC1 was immunoprecipitated, and associated lipids were analyzed by targeted lipidomics. WT ACC1 exhibited significantly higher association with C17-S1P compared to the mutant protein (Fig. 6D), confirming reduced S1P binding capacity in cells. These data independently validate our biochemical binding assays and demonstrate that the mutated residue contributes to optimal S1P-ACC1 interaction in a cellular environment. Furthermore, overexpression of the ACC1mut protein in LyMCreACC2fl/fl MDSCs resulted in enhanced ACC activity relative to ACCwt overexpression (Fig. 6E). This result suggests that changing the Ser1996 residue to Alanine reduces S1P-ACC1 interaction, preventing S1P from inhibiting ACC1 activity. Additionally, the flow cytometry study demonstrated that overexpressing ACC1mut with altered S1P binding in LyMCreACC2fl/fl MDSCs promotes an immunogenic phenotype in terms of immunogenic markers (CD80, CD86, MHC Class I, as shown in Fig. 6F, and Supplementary Fig. 6C) and cytokine secretion (IL12, Fig. 6G). ACC1mut overexpressed MDSCs presented more gp100 antigen to Pmel T cells, leading to higher proliferation (Fig. 6H, and Supplementary Fig. 6D–E) and IFN-γ production (Fig. 6I).

Phosphatidylcholine is required to increase the immunogenic potential of WT MDSCs

Our unbiased lipidomic analysis data revealed that SphK2-KO MDSCs have significantly higher levels of phospholipids, including PC, than WT KO MDSCs due to enhanced ACC1/2 dependent FAS. Phospholipids constitute the backbone of most cellular membranes, and many of them act as signaling molecules by binding to proteins. Because phospholipids are essential structural and signaling components of cellular membranes, reduced PC availability distorts membrane structures, including ER membranes (“lipid bilayer stress”)54, promotes lipolysis-resistant lipid droplet (LD) accumulation55. Both contribute to the activation of ER stress sensors that support MDSC survival in the TME56. Conversely, adequate PC prevents LD coalescence by acting as a surfactant55 and supports normal ER function. Impaired PC production is also associated with reduced cytokine release from murine macrophages57,58.

To directly determine whether PC functions as the critical downstream effector of the SphK2-ACC axis in regulating MDSC immunogenicity, we performed complementary loss-of-function and rescue experiments targeting choline cytidylyltransferase-α (CCTα), the rate-limiting enzyme in de novo PC biosynthesis59. We found that siRNA-mediated inhibition of CCTα (Supplementary Fig. 7A) significantly reduced the expression of immunogenic markers (MHC class I, CD80, CD86) (Fig. 7A, Supplementary Fig. 7B) and IL-12 secretion (Fig. 7B), while increasing Arginase-1 expression (Fig. 7A). Furthermore, inhibiting CCTα in SphK2-KO MDSCs reduced their ability to stimulate Pmel T cell proliferation (Fig. 7C& Supplementary Fig. 7C) and IFN-γ secretion (Fig. 7D); thus, restoring their immunosuppressive phenotype like WT MDSCs. Importantly, supplementation with exogenous BSA conjugated PC (derived from natural sources, such as soy) rescued antigen-presenting molecule expression and restored T-cell stimulatory capacity, indicating that PC availability directly governs the acquisition of the immunogenic phenotype following SphK2 inhibition (Fig. 7A–D). Next, we examined whether supplementation with exogenous PC could modulate the suppressive phenotype of tumor-derived MDSCs. Addition of BSA-conjugated PC to the culture media increased the expression of immunostimulatory molecules (Fig. 7E, Supplementary Fig. 7D, E) and enhanced IL-12 secretion (Fig. 7F) in otherwise suppressive tumor-derived WT MDSCs. Importantly, PC-treated tumor-derived WT MDSCs promoted greater antigen-specific T cell proliferation (Figs 7G, S7F) and IFN-γ secretion (Fig. 7H) than untreated controls.

Mechanistically, SphK2-KO MDSCs exhibited increased global membrane fluidity, quantified by the excimer/monomer fluorescence ratio of the pyrene probe PDA (470/400 nm) (Fig. 7I). Similarly, PC supplementation increased membrane fluidity in WT MDSCs. To determine whether PC-induced MDSC maturation was mediated by alterations in membrane fluidity, we directly manipulated membrane cholesterol using methyl-β-cyclodextrin (MβCD)60, a reagent that depletes cholesterol and thereby alters membrane order. Under our experimental conditions, MβCD treatment alone was insufficient to induce expression of MHC-I, CD80, or CD86. However, when combined with PC supplementation, MβCD significantly potentiated the upregulation of these immunostimulatory markers (Supplementary Fig. 7G). Because PC promotes membrane fluidity, whereas cholesterol stabilizes lipid packing, PC enrichment combined with cholesterol depletion likely shifts the PC-to-cholesterol ratio toward a highly fluid membrane state. The inability of cholesterol depletion alone to induce marker expression suggests that membrane reorganization requires increased phospholipid availability in addition to reduced cholesterol content. Moreover, SphK2 KO MDSCs also displayed reduced LD formation, and exogenous PC markedly suppressed LD accumulation in WT MDSCs exposed to BSA-conjugated oleic acid (Fig. 7J, Supplementary Fig. 7H), suggesting that PC availability prevents the buildup of large, lipolysis-resistant LDs.

Finally, we assessed whether inhibiting CCTα or ACC1/2 inhibits PC synthesis and thereby activates endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress pathways. Inhibition of CCTα or ACC1/2 in SphK2 KO MDSCs significantly increased phosphorylated IRE1α, with minimal effects on PERK phosphorylation (Fig. 7K, Supplementary Fig. 7I–K). Conversely, PC supplementation reduced phosphorylation of IRE1α (Fig. 7K, Supplementary Fig. 7J). Together, these findings demonstrate that SphK2 deletion enhances PC availability, which improves membrane fluidity, reduces LD formation and ER stress, and reprograms MDSCs from an immunosuppressive to an immunostimulatory phenotype.

Discussion

The evolving immunotherapy landscape has transitioned from single-agent immune treatment to the present trend of combining multiple immune treatments61. This multimodal approach, which combines drugs with distinct modes of action, showed improved therapy response. Despite remarkable success stories, clinical trial failures, such as recurrence and substantial tumor development, even after combination treatments61,62, highlights the need for novel approaches. As a result, developing a multipronged approach is critical. The TME, infiltrated with immunosuppressive myeloid populations such as MDSCs and TAMs, is a significant hurdle in immunotherapy63, and approaches to skew the suppressive phenotype of these myeloid subsets toward an immunogenic phenotype hold high translational significance and impact.

The development of MDSCs and TAMs is interconnected. Over time, monocytic MDSCs in the TME downregulate Ly6C expression and upregulate markers like CX3CR1, F4/80, and MHC II, resulting in differentiation to suppressive macrophages64. Lipid metabolism significantly influences the suppressive activity of both MDSCs and TAMs. Tumor-derived factors increase lipid transport receptor expression in tumor-infiltrating MDSCs, enhancing their fatty acid uptake and oxidation, thereby boosting their suppressive abilities. Elegant studies from several laboratories also indicated that lipid metabolism and lipid uptake drive TAM differentiation and polarization, enhancing their pro-tumor functions. However, the role of de novo fatty acid synthesis in MDSC and TAM differentiation and function is still unclear. Our findings show that genetic knockdown or pharmacological inhibition of SphK2/S1P signaling increases the activity of ACC1, the rate-limiting enzyme of de novo fatty acid synthesis. This promotes the differentiation of monocytic MDSCs into inflammatory macrophages rather than immunosuppressive TAMs. Therefore, blocking the SphK2/S1P pathway eliminates the pro-tumor function of MDSCs and enhances their capacity to trigger an immune response against the tumor. Overall, these modifications reduce the immunosuppressive milieu, promoting anti-tumor response.

Several studies have shown that, in the tumor environment, MDSCs rely not only on FAO, but that increased glycolytic activity aids MDSC differentiation and suppressive phenotype9,65. Our findings indicate that these recognized pathways are linked. Inhibition of SphK2/S1P increases glycolysis (as seen by increased ECAR) and overall ACC activity, resulting in increased fatty acid synthesis (FAS) and reduced fatty acid oxidation (FAO, as indicated by lower OCR). This metabolic reprogramming transforms the suppressive phenotype of MDSCs into an immunogenic one, boosting antigen presentation to T cells. This finding is consistent with the metabolic properties of immunogenic M1-type macrophages, which rely on glycolytic metabolism to meet high energy needs and provide precursors for biosynthetic processes66–68. In contrast, immunoregulatory M2 macrophages primarily use FAO. Additionally, de novo FAS promotes pro-inflammatory macrophage activation, recruitment, phagocytosis, and inflammatory cytokine production69,70. Corroborating this, ACC activity is critical for the early metabolic switch to glycolysis and lipidome remodeling in macrophages in response to lipopolysaccharide, with ACC deficiency affecting innate immune responses and bacterial clearance71. However, more research is needed to determine the underlying metabolic requirements that drive the reprogramming of suppressive MDSCs into an immunogenic phenotype.

Targeting metabolic intermediates in MDSCs reveals how their suppressive or immunogenic functions rely on distinct metabolic pathways in the TME. It is important to note that ACC influences both the biosynthesis and degradation of long-chain fatty acids, thereby playing an immunoregulatory role beyond myeloid cells. ACC1 deficiency promotes Treg subset generation while attenuating Th17 generation72. Furthermore, inhibiting ACC activity has been demonstrated to enhance anti-tumor CD8+ T cell memory by increasing FAO73. Therefore, in clinical scenarios where pharmacological inhibition of SphK2 may impact multiple cell populations (resulting in elevated ACC activity), carefully considering drug dosage and treatment windows is crucial to attain the optimum therapeutic outcomes.

Our results demonstrate that inhibiting de novo FAS and enhancing FAO through genetic deletion of ACC1/2 leads to increased activation of ERS sensors in MDSCs, promoting their immunosuppressive capacity. Additionally, inhibiting ACC1/2 alters the composition of various phospholipids, particularly affecting PC synthesis in MDSCs. Supplementing PC in the media partially mitigated ER stress activation, suggesting a critical role for de novo FAS and PC in regulating ER stress and the suppressive capacity of MDSCs. The ER membrane is primarily composed of glycerophospholipids, such as phosphatidic acid (PA), PC, phosphatidylethanolamine (PE), phosphatidylinositol (PI), and phosphatidylserine (PS). Disruption in the ratios of these glycerophospholipids within the ER membrane can compromise the bioactivity of the ER. Additionally, disruptions in PC biosynthesis pathways have been shown to cause ER stress74,75. Our data further suggests that membrane biophysical properties are also important determinants of MDSC function. Because PC promotes membrane fluidity, whereas cholesterol stabilizes lipid packing76, alterations in the PC-to-cholesterol ratio can influence membrane order and receptor organization. Consistent with this model, PC supplementation together with cholesterol depletion enhanced the expression of immunostimulatory markers, suggesting that increased membrane fluidity may facilitate clustering and promote the formation of functional immune synapses required for effective MDSC maturation and T-cell activation. Therefore, targeting SphK2/S1P signaling or enhancing fatty acid synthesis to increase PC biosynthesis presents an attractive strategy to reduce ER stress in MDSCs and decrease their suppressive function.

Diet has also been shown to influence phospholipid composition and biofunction77. The composition of short-chain fatty acids (SFAs), monounsaturated fatty acids (MUFAs), and polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs) in the ER membrane is tightly regulated to maintain membrane functionality78. Changes in fatty acid synthesis, particularly an abnormal ratio of saturated to unsaturated fatty acids, can trigger an ER stress response by altering the lipid content of the ER79. Supplementing cell cultures with palmitic acid induces membrane saturation and alters membrane fluidity, triggering the unfolded protein response (UPR). In contrast, PUFAs and MUFAs may provide cytoprotective effects and counteract saturation-induced ER stress. However, it remains to be investigated whether adjusting the lipid composition (monounsaturated/polyunsaturated) in the cellular environment or through diet can alleviate ER stress in MDSCs and enhance their immunogenicity. Additionally, it is essential to determine if abnormal lipid composition in the TME disrupts ER homeostasis in MDSCs, thereby influencing their immunogenic versus suppressive potential.

AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK) plays a central role in regulating energy metabolism and immune responses, influencing both innate and adaptive immunity. The catalytic activity of eukaryotic ACCs is tightly controlled through phosphorylation, ligand binding, and other mechanisms80,81. AMPK inhibits fatty acid synthesis and promotes FAO by phosphorylating ACC1 at Ser79 and ACC2 at Ser21282. Research from our collaborator’s (Rodriguez) indicated that AMPKα1 regulates the immunosuppressive activity and differentiation of tumor associated MDSCs, suggesting that inhibiting AMPK could be a therapeutic strategy to restore protective myelopoiesis in cancer83. Our findings show that S1P interacts with ACC1 at its Ser1996 residue to inhibit ACC1 activity. Additionally, we uncovered that pharmacological inhibition of AMPK and deletion of SphK2 synergistically increase ACC activity in SphK2 KO MDSCs, suggesting different allosteric binding sites for S1P and phosphorylation on ACC1. This opens the possibility of simultaneously targeting both SphK2 and AMPKα1 to enhance ACC1 activity, potentially controlling the expansion and activation of MDSCs to counteract the immunosuppressive TME and improve anti-tumor responses.

In summary, our study highlights the role of the SphK2-S1P-ACC axis in influencing the metabolic fate and immunosuppressive activity of MDSCs in tumor-bearing animals (Supplementary Fig. 8). We further emphasized the significance of PC synthesis in reducing ER stress and thereby increasing MDSC immunogenicity. SphK2i (Opaganib (ABC294640)), used in our study, is now being investigated in clinical studies to treat prostate cancer (NCT04207255) and cholangiocarcinoma (NCT03414489), and has been proven effective against various tumor lines, inhibiting proliferation and migration while disrupting microfilaments. Therefore, it is crucial to investigate further how SphK2 inhibition impacts the immunological population within the TME. Identifying metabolic and molecular targets shared by cancer cells and immune cells would improve the efficacy of anti-cancer immunotherapies and open the way for combinatorial approaches to treat cancer.

Methods

Mice

C57BL/6, BALB/c, B6-Rag-/-, and Pmel mice were obtained from Jackson Laboratory (Bar Harbor, ME). SphK1 KO and SphK2 KO mice were a kind gift from Besim Ogretmen (MUSC). LyMCreACC-DKO mice were a kind gift from Norbert Leitinger71 (University of Virginia). The myeloid-specific ACC1 knockout mice were generated in-house by crossing Acacatm1Jdh/J (Acc1 flox) mice with LysM-Cre mice (Jackson Laboratory). Mice were housed under specific pathogen-free conditions at 22 ± 2 °C and 50 ± 10% humidity on a 12-h light/dark cycle, with ad libitum access to food and water. All experimental procedures were conducted in accordance with protocols approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of the Medical University of South Carolina, Charleston (Protocol No. IACUC-2018-00628-2). For tumor experiments, an equal number of age- and gender-matched (both male and female) mice were randomly assigned for the experiments when they were between 8–10 weeks old to minimize the bias of sex. The strain and number of mice used in each experiment are indicated in the corresponding figure legends. Mice were monitored regularly for tumor growth, mobility, posture, and overall signs of distress. Tumor size was measured using digital calipers two times per week, and tumor volume was calculated using the formula: length × width² × 0.5. The maximum tumor burden permitted by the institutional ethical guidelines was 4000 mm³, and all experiments were conducted within these approved limits. Mice were humanely euthanized if they showed signs of impaired mobility, significant weight loss, severe distress, or if tumor burden reached the approved endpoint criteria. Euthanasia was performed using CO₂ inhalation followed by cervical dislocation as a secondary physical method to ensure death.

Cell lines

B16-F10 (CRL-6475), EL4 (TIB-39), and 4T1 (CRL-2539) were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC) and suggested to be of male origin. MB49 mouse bladder Carcinoma Cell line was a gift from Dr. Michael I. Lilly. All cell lines were validated to be mycoplasma-free and cultured in RPMI-1640 supplemented with GlutaMAX 100 U/ml penicillin and streptomycin, 20 μM β-mercaptoethanol, and 10% heat-inactivated Fetal bovine serum (FBS) and maintained at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2.

Generation of bone marrow derived MDSCs and macrophages

To obtain bone marrow, mice were euthanized by CO2 inhalation. Femoral bones were removed, and all remaining tissue was dissected off the bone. The ends of each bone were cut off, and the bone marrow was flushed from the center of the bone. Bone marrow-derived MDSCs were generated by culturing bone marrow-derived cells in complete media for 4 days with 20 ng/ml GM-CSF, 20 ng/ml G-CSF in the presence of 30% tumor explant supernatant (TES). To generate macrophages bone marrow cells were then cultured on non-tissue culture-treated 6-well plates in complete media for 7 days with 20 ng/ml M-CSF in the presence of 30% tumor explant supernatant (TES). The media was changed every two days. Floating cells were removed, and the loosely adherent cells were considered macrophages. Macrophages were detached using Accutase. FACS analysis was then performed to confirm the successful generation of MDSCs and macrophages.

Immune cell isolation

CD3+ T cells were enriched using mouse T cell negative selection kits (MagniSort, Invitrogen) from the spleen of C57BL/6 mice. Purity ranged between 95% and 99% as tested by flow cytometry. For antigen-specific priming studies, CD8+ T cells were enriched using negative selection kits (Cat# 8804-6825-74) from the spleens of Pmel mice. For ACT experiments, sorted CD8+ T cells were previously activated with gp100 for 72 h before transferring to B16-bearing mice. Tumor-MDSCs were isolated from EL4 ascites or cellular suspensions of digested B16 tumors, followed by positive selection (Myeloid-Derived Suppressor Cell Isolation Kit, mouse from Miltenyi Biotech). Also, M-MDSCs (CD11b+Ly6GlowLy6Chigh), PMN-MDSCs (CD11b+Ly6GhighLy6Clow) were sorted from tumors in FACS AriaII (BD Biosciences).

Isolation of tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes

To obtain tumor-infiltrating T cells (TILs) from subcutaneously established solid B16-F10, 4T1, or MB49 melanoma-bearing mice, tumors were excised, chopped finely using tweezers and scissors, and then digested using the Tumor Dissociation Kit, mouse (Miltenyi Biotech). Following digestion, tumors were filtered through 70 cell strainers (BD Biosciences, San Jose, CA). The cell suspension was washed in a culture medium twice by centrifugation at 1500 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C and finally re-suspended in FACS staining buffer for further staining procedures.

Human subjects

All studies involving human samples were conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of the Medical University of South Carolina (IRB Protocol No. Pro00138336). Human PBMCs were obtained from healthy donors; no covariate-relevant participant characteristics were available to the investigators. Patient-derived samples obtained from the MUSC Biorepository were provided in fully de-identified form, and no covariate-relevant participant information was available to the investigators. Collection and use of patient-derived samples from the MUSC Biorepository were approved by the IRB of Medical University of South Carolina (IRB Protocol No. Pro114891). Tissue microarray (TMA) slides were commercially purchased (TissueArray.Com).

Generation of human MDSCs from healthy donor PBMCs

Peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were isolated from healthy donors, and CD33⁺ myeloid cells were enriched using the EasySep™ Human CD33 Positive Selection Kit II (STEMCELL Technologies) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. CD33⁺ cells were seeded at a density of 1 × 10⁶ cells/mL in RPMI-1640 medium supplemented with 10 ng/mL recombinant human GM-CSF and 10 ng/mL IL-6. To enhance the acquisition of suppressive function, 30% of the culture medium was replaced with Skov3 ovarian carcinoma cell-conditioned supernatant. On day 2, half of the medium was replaced with fresh cytokine-supplemented medium. On day 5, the entire culture medium was replaced with fresh GM-CSF and IL-6-containing medium. Cells were harvested on day 7 for phenotypic and functional analysis.

Lentiviral transduction of human monocytic MDSCs with SPHK2 shRNA

Lentiviral particles encoding SPHK2-targeting shRNAs (pLV[shRNA]-EGFP-U6>hSPHK2[shRNA#1] or a non-targeting control were produced in HEK293T cells using the Lenti-X Single Shot Packaging Kit (Takara Bio) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The target sequence was 5′-GAGGGTAGTGCCTGATCAATG-3′. Human MDSCs were differentiated from CD33⁺ PBMCs as described previously. On day 3 of differentiation, cells were harvested and plated in a non-tissue culture-treated 24-well plate was coated with Retronectin (Takara, Cat# T100B) at 10 μg/cm² overnight at 4 °C, then blocked and washed according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Lentiviral supernatant was added to each well, and the plates were centrifuged at 2000 × g for 90 min at 32 °C. Cells were incubated overnight at 37 °C in a 5% CO₂ incubator. The following day, the medium was replaced with fresh MDSC differentiation medium, and cells were cultured for an additional 48–72 h. Transduction efficiency was determined by GFP expression via flow cytometry and GFP sorted cells were used in functional assays or other downstream applications.

Transfection of MDSCs

Bone marrow cells from WT and SphK2-KO mice were cultured in the presence of GM-CSF (20 ng/mL) and 30% B16-F10 tumor explant supernatant (TES). On day 2, MDSCs were electroporated with siRNAs targeting Acaca, Acacb, Pcyt1a (Cct1a), or a scrambled negative control using a Nucleofector Kit (Lonza) according to the manufacturer’s instructions and maintained in the same culture conditions for an additional 48 h. Bone marrow cells from ACC1/2fl/fl and ACC-DKO mice were similarly cultured with GM-CSF and B16-F10 TES. MDSCs were subsequently electroporated with plasmids encoding either wild-type or mutant ACC1, and ACC1 expression was assessed. Transfected cells were cultured for an additional 48 h prior to downstream analyses.

T cell suppression assays

Murine BM-MDSC or tumor-MDSC were co-cultured with negatively selected CD3+ T cells in a 96-well plate in the presence of soluble anti-CD3 and anti-CD28 (2 μg/ml each) for three days. T-cell proliferation was assessed with Cell-trace violet (Invitrogen) dye dilution detected by flow cytometry. Results are expressed as a percentage of proliferating T cells, determined by the dilution of fluorescence compared with non-stimulated T cells.

Antigen presentation assay

For antigen presentation experiments, MDSCs were generated from bone marrow or sorted from EL4 tumors and then pulsed with 1 μg/ml complete human gp100 antigen. After 16 h, cells were washed twice and 2 × 104 MDSCs co-cultured with 2 × 104 CTV-labeled naive CD8+ T cells obtained from spleens of Pmel mice. Positive controls included co-cultures of unsorted Pmel T cells with 1 μg/ml complete hgp100 antigen. T cell proliferation was measured after three days of co-culture. Culture supernatants were also collected to perform ELISA.

Flow cytometry

Staining for cell surface markers was performed by incubating cells with antibodies at 1:200 dilutions in FACS buffer (0.1% BSA in PBS) for 30 min at 4 °C. For intracellular cytokine (IL-12 and TGFβ) and Sphk1/2, ACC1 staining, surface markers were stained before fixation/permeabilization (BD Cytofix/Cytoperm Kit, BD Biosciences, San Jose, CA). For pIRE1α and pPERK staining, cells were fixed/permeabilized using BD perm III buffer set (BD Bioscience, San Jose, CA) before staining with cell surface markers and pIRE1α or pPERK primary antibody (Cell Signaling Technologies, Danvers, MA), followed by fluorochrome-conjugated secondary antibody (Jackson ImmunoResearch Laboratories, West Grove, PA). LIVE/DEAD™ Fixable Yellow Dead Cell Stain Kit (Invitrogen # L34959), to evaluate cell death, following the manufacturer’s protocol. Samples were acquired on LSR Fortessa and analysed with FlowJo software (Tree Star, OR).

Real-time quantitative-PCR

Total RNA was extracted from pellets of the indicated T cell subsets (2 × 106 cells) using Trizol reagent (Life technologies, Grand Island, NY). cDNA was generated from 1 µg total RNA using iScript cDNA Synthesis Kit (BioRad, Hercules, CA). SYBR Green incorporation quantitative real-time PCR was performed using a SYBR Green mix (Biorad, Hercules, CA) in the CFX96 Detection System (BioRad, Hercules, CA). Expression of different genes was quantified relative to Actb.

Total acetylome analysis

The acetylated protein pull-down assay was performed using the Signal-Seeker Acetyl-Lysine Detection Kit (Cat# BK163-S, Cytoskeleton, Inc.) following the manufacturer’s protocol. Cells were lysed in the kit’s proprietary buffer containing deacetylation and protease inhibitors. Equal amounts of protein (1 mg) were incubated with acetyl-lysine affinity beads for 2 h at 4 °C with gentle rotation. Beads were washed three times with the provided buffer, and acetylated proteins were eluted by heating at 95 °C for 5 min. Eluates were resolved by SDS-PAGE, stained with the Pierce™ Silver Stain Kit (Cat# 24612, Thermo Fisher Scientific), and excised for identification at the Harvard Taplin Mass Spectrometry Facility.

Multiplex immunohistochemistry

For multiplex immunohistochemistry, unstained slides with 4–5 μm sections of FFPE tissue were deparaffinized and stained using the Roche Ventana Discovery Ultra Automated Research Stainer (Roche Diagnostics, Indianapolis, IN). Heat-induced epitope retrieval was performed in EDTA buffer pH 9 (Cat# S2367 Agilent/Dako Santa Clara, CA) for 32 min at 100 °C, and endogenous peroxidase was blocked with a hydrogen peroxide solution after incubation of the first primary antibody. Optimized multiplex immunofluorescence was performed using the OPAL multiplexing method based on Tyramide Signal Amplification. Antibodies used included CD11b (Abcam, 1:100), CD14 (Proteintech, 1:100), CD15 (SCBT, 1:50), HLA-DR (Abcam, 1:500), SphK2 (Bioss, 1:100), and pan-cytokeratin (Roche, RTU), as detailed in the Key Resources table. Fluorescence signals were generated using the following Akoya OPAL TSA fluorophores: OPAL 480, OPAL 520, OPAL 570, OPAL 690, and OPAL 780 (Akoya Biosciences, Marlborough, MA), and nuclei were visualized with DAPI counterstaining. Slides were incubated in citrate buffer pH 6 (Cell Conditioning Solution (CC2) Cat. #980-223, Roche Diagnostics) between each sequential antibody staining step at 90 °C to remove the previous primary and secondary antibody complexes. Multiplex-stained slides were mounted with ProLong Gold Antifade Reagent (Cat. # P36934, ThermoFisher) and scanned using the Vectra® Polaris Automated Imaging System (Akoya Biosciences, Marlborough, MA). Whole slide scans were reviewed, and images were captured using Phenochart whole slide contextual viewer software in regions of interest (Akoya Biosciences, Marlborough, MA). Spectral unmixing and elimination of autofluorescence was performed using the inForm® Software (Akoya Biosciences, Marlborough, MA). Identification of MDSC populations (CD11b+, CD14+, CD15-, HLA-DR-, PanCK-) and determination of nuclear SphK2 expression was performed on regions of interest using the PhenoptrReports Open-Source R Package (https://akoyabio.github.io/phenoptrReports/index.html, Akoya Biosciences, Marlborough, MA). Regions of interest comprised of carcinoma and peri-carcinoma tissues as identified by an independent pathologist using paired H&E tissue sections. Data was generated and tabulated using PhenoptrReports (Akoya Biosciences, Marlborough, MA).

Lipidomic analysis

For lipidomic analysis, six million cells were resuspended in 50 µL of 50 mM ammonium bicarbonate (ABC) in water, followed by the addition of 5 µL of QC internal standards for quality control (QC). Quality control samples, consisting of equivalent HeLa cell extracts, were interspersed throughout the analysis to ensure data accuracy and consistency. The cell suspension was extracted with 200 µL of isopropanol, and the mixture was vortexed at room temperature for 1 h at 1400 rpm using a thermomixer. Post-extraction, the mixture was clarified by centrifugation at 14,000 RCF for 5 min, and 225 µL of the supernatant was transferred to a new microtube and cooled to −80 °C for 15 min. The samples were subsequently dried using a SpeedVac, and the dried extracts were redissolved in 15 µL of 80% isopropanol with 10 mM ABC, followed by vortexing at room temperature for 1 min. This concentrated extract was then clarified by centrifugation again, and 13.5 µL was transferred to injection vials for storage at 4 °C until analysis. Liquid chromatography (LC) separation was performed using a Waters CSH C18 UHPLC column (1 × 150 mm). The mobile phases consisted of water with 0.1% formic acid (Solvent A), acetonitrile with 0.1% formic acid (Solvent B), and isopropanol with 5 mM ammonium acetate and formic acid (Solvent C). The flow rate was set at 0.07 mL/min. The gradient program began with 2% Solvent B and 2% Solvent C, maintaining this composition for the first minute. From 1 to 2 min, Solvent B increased to 98% while Solvent C was kept constant at 2%. From 2 to 3 min, Solvent B decreased to 10% while Solvent C increased to 90%. This was followed by a hold at 90% Solvent C from 13 to 28 min. The composition was then rapidly returned stepwise to the initial conditions of 2% Solvent B and 2% Solvent C from 28 to 31 min and maintained until 40 min to re-equilibrate the column. Mass spectrometry (MS) analysis was conducted on a Thermo Scientific Q Exactive Plus instrument, with fast polarity switching between positive and negative modes and using data-dependent acquisition (DDA). The scan events included Full MS and ddMS². Full MS settings included a resolution of 70,000, AGC target of 3e6, maximum IT of 100 ms, and a scan range of 175 to 1575 m/z. For ddMS², the settings were a resolution of 17,500, AGC target of 1e6, maximum IT of 50 ms, TopN of 10, isolation window of 0.5 m/z, and normalized collision energy (NCE) of 20, 30, and 40. For data processing and analysis, we utilized proprietary software for data alignment and calibration at retention time (RT) and mass-to-charge ratio (m/z) levels, as well as correction for run order and global mode-based intensity sample-wise normalization. Label-free quantification with match between runs (LFQ-MBR) was performed using identifications from LipiDex software with greater than 500 and 700 forward and reverse dot product, and 2+ matching fragment ions, with 1-min and 5 ppm tolerances for intensity summation. Data analysis included quality control (QC), principal component analysis (PCA), clustering, and differential associations using limma eBayes. Weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) was used to identify lipid co-expression modules, and Kolmogorov-Smirnov (KS) based lipid class enrichment analysis was performed. Features with a p value < 0.05 and |log2 fold change| >1 were considered significant and visualized in the volcano plot. For the heatmap, lipid species were aggregated to the class level, and data were plotted pairwise using a heatmap with correlation-based hierarchical clustering.

Real-time flux assay

Oxygen consumption rate (OCR) and Extracellular acidification rate (ECAR) were determined using Seahorse Xfe96 analyser (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA). Briefly, MDSCSs (0.5 × 106/well) were plated on a Cell-Tak-coated Seahorse culture plate for 30 min. Both OCR, a measure of OXPHOS, and ECAR (a measure of glycolysis) were analyzed under basal conditions, or in response to 1.0 µM oligomycin, 1.0 µM fluoro-carbonyl cyanide phenylhydrazone (FCCP) and 2 µM rotenone, plus 100 nM antimycin A. All reagents were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO.

S1P-binding protein identification by biotin-S1P pull-down

Wild-type MDSC lysates were incubated with 5 µM biotin or 5 µM biotin-S1P at 30 °C for 30 min. Streptavidin magnetic beads were then added to the mixtures, and the bead-lysate mixture was passed through a magnetic column. The columns were washed at least 5× with 50 mM ammonium bicarbonate solution to remove nonspecifically bound proteins, and the beads were then collected in 100 μl of the same 50 mM ammonium bicarbonate solution. The beads were then sent for mass spectrometry proteomic analysis (Taplin Biological Mass Spectrometry Facility at Harvard University). Proteins specifically interacting with the biotin-S1P were then identified. Proteins that did not show consistent interaction with biotin-S1P across the different replicates, or those that showed inconsistencies between the sum intensity measures and the number of peptides identified, were eliminated from the analysis. After calculating the relative intensity of each protein in the submitted samples, we subtracted the average relative intensity of the protein bound to biotin from the average relative intensity of the protein bound to biotin-S1P to get a quantitative value for the amount of protein interacting with biotin-S1P specifically, and not just biotin. Proteins with positive values for the difference in relative intensity were considered to be specifically interacting with S1P, while those with a value of 0 or a negative value were considered to be non-specifically bound to biotin, irrespective of the S1P.

Anti-FLAG immunoprecipitation

Cells were lysed on ice using lysis buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl, 150 mM NaCl, 1% IGEPAL CA-650) supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors. Homogenates were centrifuged at 19,000 × g at 4 °C for 15 min. The resulting supernatants were quantified and incubated with pre-washed anti-FLAG magnetic beads (Sigma-Aldrich, M8823) at a ratio of 1 mg total protein to 20 μL beads in a total volume of 1 mL. Samples were incubated overnight at 4 °C with rotation. Beads were then washed five times with lysis buffer and five times with PBS and finally resuspended in sample buffer for SDS-PAGE or PBS for lipidomic analysis.

Quantitative detection of S1P-ACC1 binding

The experiments were essentially performed as described before20,84. In brief, HEK293T cells were transfected with FLAG-tagged WT and mutant of ACC1 using Lipofectamine 3000 Transfection Reagent. Cells were then lysed in buffer containing 50 mM tris (pH 7.4), 150 mM NaCl, 1 mM EDTA, 0.5% NP-40, and 1:500 protease inhibitor cocktail. Lysates (2 mg of protein/10 ug per sample) were incubated with 100 ul of agarose-conjugated antibodies specific to FLAG (Sigma-Aldrich) at 4 °C for 18 h with gentle agitation. The beads then were extensively washed and incubated in binding buffer (50 mM tris (pH 7.5), 137 mM NaCl, 1 mM MgCl2, 2.7 mM KCl, 15 mM NaF, and 0.5 mM Na3VO4) for 30 min at RT with increasing concentrations of biotinylated S1P (starting at 0.3 nM) either in presence or absence of unlabeled S1P (10 ug) to assess unspecific binding. Bound biotinylated S1P was quantified using a biotin-ELISA kit (Creative Biomart) according to the manufacturer’s instructions and normalized to protein input. Saturation binding curves were generated and fitted by nonlinear regression using GraphPad Prism 10 to determine the equilibrium dissociation constant (Kd) and maximum binding capacity (Bmax). The stoichiometry of binding was calculated using the following equation: n = Bmax (nm/mg protein) /molar amount of ACC1 per mg protein.

Description of mutated plasmids

To generate the site-directed mutant plasmid, pcDNA3.1 vectors with the wild-type or mutated Acaca1 gene insert were ordered from GenScript. The mutated sequence (Ser1995Ala) was generated from the wild-type Acaca1 sequence by replacing the codon coding for a serine at position 1995 (AGT) with a codon that codes for Alanine (GCT). This site-specific mutation generated the mutated plasmid used in the above studies.

Proximity ligation assay (PLA)

Cells were fixed and permeabilized using 4% paraformaldehyde (15 min) and 0.1% Triton X-100 in 1xPBS for 15 min each. Cells were blocked and washed following the Duolink in situ hybridization kit manufacturer’s instructions and reagents (Olink Biosciences). Anti-ACC1 (Thermo Scientific, PA5-17564), mouse anti-Sphingosine 1-Phosphate Antibody (Echelon Biosciences, Z-P300), Anti-PKM Rabbit Polyclonal Antibody (15822-1-AP), Acetylated-Lysine Antibody (Cell Signaling Technology #9441) were used as primary antibodies. MDSCs were fixed with 4% PFA for 15 min, then permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X for another 15 min at room temperature. PLA blocking agent was then used to block non-specific binding, and the cells were then incubated overnight in the presence of antibodies of interest. After washing, secondary PLA probes conjugated to oligonucleotides were added to the cells, then a ligase was added to the samples to ligate the oligonucleotides that are in proximity. DNA rolling-circle amplification was then performed using the PLA polymerase in the presence of fluorescence-bound oligonucleotide probes, which then yielded a fluorescent signal only where the two targets of interest are interacting in close proximity. Cells were imaged using the Olympus FV10i laser scanning confocal microscope. Signal quantification was performed using the Duolink in Situ Image Tool software.

RNA Sequencing

RNA was extracted using mirVana miRNA Isolation Kit following the manufacturer’s protocol. Samples were then sent to Novogene Corporation Inc for sequencing. RNA-Seq data were quality controlled using Trim Galore (https://www.bioinformatics.babraham.ac.uk/projects/trim_galore/). Cleaned reads were aligned to the mouse reference genome (GRCm39) using the STAR aligner85. A count table was generated with feature Counts86 from the aligned BAM files. The resulting count matrix, together with sample metadata, was imported into R (R package version 1.0.7) for differential gene expression analysis using DESeq287.

Assay for transposase-accessible chromatin using sequencing

We performed the assay for transposase-accessible chromatin using sequencing (ATAC-seq) as described previously88 with modification as described below. EL4 tumor cells were injected into WT and Sphk2 KO mice. After 14 days, tumor-derived MDSCs (FACS-sorted Gr1+CD11b+ cells from ascitic fluid) were collected from WT and Sphk2 KO mice and used for making ATAC-seq libraries. We used 2 × 104 MDSCs and modified the concentration of the Tn5 enzyme for the transposase reaction. ATAC-seq libraries with 40% of the recommended Tn5 per reaction were prepared as described before88. Data analysis was performed using Partek Flow as described before88. Differential expression analysis was performed using DESeq2 and gene-specific analysis (GSA), with a p value threshold of 0.05 to identify statistically significant changes. Differentially accessible and closed genomic regions were then analyzed using GREAT (v4.0.4) (http://bejerano.stanford.edu/papers/GREAT.pdf) with a ‘Basal plus extension’ association rule (5 kb upstream, 1 kb downstream, 1 Mb extension) to identify associated genes. Statistical significance was determined using the region-based Binomial test, which accounts for the total genomic fraction covered by the regulatory domains of each gene set.

Single-cell RNA sequencing analysis

scRNA-seq datasets from breast tumor samples, peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) of healthy donors, and syngeneic genetically engineered mouse (GEM) models of breast cancer were obtained from the Single Cell Portal (https://singlecell.broadinstitute.org/single_cell). Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) plots were used to visualize immune cell populations. Expression levels and percentages of cells expressing SPHK1, SPHK2, CD14, CD11B, CD33, and HLA-DR were extracted across immune subsets. Heatmaps displaying the mean expression and frequency of expressing cells were generated using Morpheus software (https://software.broadinstitute.org/morpheus/).

Statistical analysis

All data are presented as the mean ± SD from at least five independent experiments unless otherwise stated. Statistical significance between two groups was determined using an unpaired two tailed Student’s t test. For comparisons involving more than two groups, ordinary one-way ANOVA followed by multiple comparisons tests was used. A p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism software (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA). A longitudinal analysis of tumor size over time and a survival analysis were performed for tumor experiments. For the longitudinal analysis, a linear mixed effects (LME) model was fitted to the data with the natural logarithm of tumor size as the outcome variable. Covariates included the natural logarithm of baseline tumor size, time (treated as a categorical variable), experimental group, and the interaction between time and experimental group. Additionally, a random intercept was included for each individual mouse. The model only included data from days in which at least one mouse was alive in each experimental group. Linear contrasts were used to evaluate group differences each day, for which three mice were alive in the experimental groups of interest. All statistical tests were made on the natural logarithm scale of tumor size, and p values were adjusted for multiple comparisons using Holm’s method of correction. For the survival analysis, Kaplan-Meier curves were constructed for each experimental group, and comparisons were conducted using log-rank tests. All mice either reached the criteria for euthanize or were found dead; therefore, no event times were censored. Again, multiple group comparisons were made, and pvalues were adjusted using Holm’s method. All calculations were performed using R version 4.4.1 and packages “nlme” version 3.1-164, “emmeans” version 1.10.3, “survival” version 3.6-4, and “survminer” version 0.4.9.
