Macrophage Phospholipase D3 promotes atherosclerosis via exacerbating foam cell formation and inducing inflammatory responses
Authors: Teng Li, Xiaobao Gu, Xiangyang Yin, Pengbo Zhai, Hongxu Yan, Zixun Wang, Yang Li, Bing Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAtherosclerosis is a chronic inflammatory disease and a major cause of global morbidity and mortality. Phospholipase D3 (PLD3) has been reported to be elevated in atherosclerotic plaques, yet its functional role and molecular mechanisms remain unclear. This study investigated the role of PLD3 in atherosclerosis.MethodsSingle-cell RNA sequencing of human atherosclerotic tissues was analyzed to define PLD3 expression. Validation was performed in ApoE^-/- mice and THP-1-derived foam cells by qRT-PCR and western blotting. Lentiviral-mediated PLD3 knockdown was followed by oxidized LDL (ox-LDL) stimulation. Lipid accumulation and uptake were assessed by Oil Red O, BODIPY, and DiI-ox-LDL assays, while inflammatory cytokines were quantified by qRT-PCR. RNA sequencing was conducted to explore downstream mechanisms.ResultsPLD3 expression was markedly upregulated in atherosclerotic lesions and enriched in plaque macrophages, with diagnostic value confirmed by ROC analysis. In vitro, ox-LDL induced PLD3 upregulation in THP-1 macrophages. PLD3 silencing reduced lipid accumulation and uptake through downregulation of CD36, while concurrently decreasing IL-1β and TNF-α expression. Mechanistically, PLD3 deficiency suppressed NF-κB pathway activation.ConclusionPLD3 is highly expressed in plaque macrophages and promotes atherosclerosis by enhancing CD36-mediated lipid accumulation and activating NF-κB–driven inflammation. These findings identify PLD3 as a potential therapeutic target for atherosclerotic disease.Keywords: PLD3, Atherosclerosis, Macrophages, Foam cell, Inflammation, NF-κB pathway

Introduction

Atherosclerosis is a chronic inflammatory disease and remains a leading global cause of mortality and morbidity [1]. During the disease progression, macrophages engulf excessive oxidized low-density lipoprotein (ox-LDL) and transform into foam cells. The formation of foam cells and their mediated inflammatory response are considered pivotal in exacerbating vascular wall inflammation, promoting plaque formation, and driving lesion progression [2,3]. Therefore, identifying key molecules that regulate the inflammatory response during foam cell formation and elucidating their functions are of great significance for deepening our understanding of the pathogenesis of atherosclerosis and for providing potential targets for developing new therapeutic strategies.

Phospholipase D3 (PLD3), an endolysosomal hydrolase, is traditionally recognized for its role in neuronal lipid metabolism, where it synthesizes lysosomal phospholipid S,S-BMP and facilitates ganglioside degradation [4]. Previous studies have focused on the role of PLD3 in the nervous system [5,6], while its functions outside the nervous system remain less explored. Recent research has identified PLD3 as one of the “core macrophage gene signatures” [7]. Yao et al. further confirmed that PLD3 is highly expressed in foamy macrophages within atherosclerotic plaques [8]. Wang et al. identified PLD3 as one of the diagnostic markers for carotid artery stenosis through urinary proteomics [9]. Additionally, studies have suggested that PLD3 possesses DNA 5′-exonuclease activity and can regulate inflammatory cytokine responses by degrading endosomal nucleic acids [10]. However, whether PLD3 participates in lipid homeostasis regulation and inflammatory responses in atherosclerosis through specific molecular signaling pathways remains to be fully elucidated.

The mechanistic role of PLD3 in atherosclerosis has not yet been clarified. This study explores its potential functions in the disease. By analyzing gene expression datasets from human atherosclerotic lesions, we found that PLD3 is significantly upregulated in the disease and exhibits specific overexpression in lesional macrophages. Subsequently, through transcriptome sequencing and experimental validation, we uncovered the mechanism by which PLD3 promotes the progression of atherosclerosis.

Material and methods

Bioinformatics analysis

We performed data processing and analysis of the single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) data from the GSE159677 dataset (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/) [11] using the Seurat R package. Additionally, we conducted differential gene expression analysis on the GSE100927 microarray dataset (29 carotid atherosclerotic lesions and 12 controls) from the GEO database using GEO2R.

Animal experiments

All animal procedures in this study were approved by the Animal Experimentation Committee of the Fifth Affiliated Hospital of Zhengzhou University (approval ID: KY-Y-2025072). All animal experiments were conducted in accordance with the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals issued by the National Institutes of Health (NIH). ApoE-/- mice were obtained from Hangzhou Ziyuan Experimental Animal Technology Co., Ltd. (Hangzhou, China) and were housed, bred, and maintained at the Animal Experiment Center of the Fifth Affiliated Hospital of Zhengzhou University.Mice in the disease group were fed a high-fat diet (HFD; containing 40 % kcal fat, 43 % kcal carbohydrates, and 17 % kcal protein; Beijing Keao Xieli Feed Co., Ltd., Beijing, China), while control group mice received a normal diet (ND). Both HFD and ND groups were randomly assigned. At the end of the animal experiments, all mice were euthanized under pentobarbital anesthesia, and the aortas were fixed in 4 % paraformaldehyde. Adeno-associated virus (AAV) vectors were purchased from Suzhou GentleGen Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Suzhou, Jiangsu, China). Eight-week-old ApoE−/− mice were randomly divided into two groups according to experimental design and fed a high-fat diet. Mice were intravenously injected via the tail vein with AAV9 vectors carrying either shNC or shPLD3 (1 × 1011 viral genomes per injection) every 4 weeks for a total of 16 weeks.

Cell culture

The human immortalized monocyte cell line (THP-1) was purchased from ViCell Biotechnology (Zhengzhou, China) and cultured in RPMI-1640 medium (Gibco) supplemented with 10 % fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco) at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5 % CO2. Prior to experiments, THP-1 cells were pretreated with 50 μg/mL ox-LDL (Yiyuan Biotech, Guangzhou, China) for 24 h to simulate a hyperlipidemic environment.

Gene knockdown by lentiviral transfection

The lentivirus-mediated short hairpin RNA (shRNA) targeting PLD3 was provided by Suzhou GentleGen Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Jiangsu, Suzhou). Two lentiviruses, TPV498-v1 and LVC17, were used to infect THP-1 cells at an MOI = 10. After 48 h of lentiviral infection, the cells underwent puromycin resistance screening. The knockdown efficiency of PLD3 was assessed by RT-PCR. A non-targeting scrambled shRNA (shNC) was used as the control. The sequence of the PLD3-targeting shRNA (shPLD3) was as follows:5′-ctactcaacgtggtggacaat-3′.

H&E staining

The paraffin-embedded sections were deparaffinized to water. Then, the sections were stained with hematoxylin solution for 3–5 min, followed by differentiation with a differentiating solution. Finally, the sections were dehydrated in 95 % alcohol for 1 min, stained in eosin solution for 15 s, and mounted with neutral balsam.

Oil Red O Staining

The frozen serial cross-sections of mouse aortic tissue were stained using the Oil Red O Stain Kit (Solarbio, Beijing, China). The sections were immersed in Oil Red O working solution for 10 min, rinsed with wash buffer, and then counterstained with hematoxylin solution to visualize nuclei.For THP-1 cells, the same staining protocol was applied. Briefly, cells were cultured in 24-well plates, and after treatment, the medium was aspirated and washed with PBS. Following fixation with 4 % paraformaldehyde for 30 min, the cells were stained with Oil Red O working solution for 10 min, rinsed, and then counterstained with hematoxylin solution for nuclear staining.

Immunohistochemistry

The tissue sections were blocked with 3 % bovine serum albumin (BSA) to minimize nonspecific binding. Subsequently, they were incubated overnight at 4 °C with PLD3 primary antibody (Proteintech, Wuhan, China) diluted at 1:250, followed by incubation with a biotin-conjugated secondary antibody. Finally, protein expression was visualized using a 3,3′-diaminobenzidine (DAB) chromogen kit.

Immunofluorescence

Frozen sections were dried in a 37 °C oven for 10–20 min. After removing moisture, they were fixed in fixative solution for 30 min. Endogenous peroxidase was blocked with 3 % H2O2, followed by incubation with 3 % BSA for 1 h. The sections were then incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies including: PLD3 antibody (1:50, Proteintech, Wuhan, China), F4/80 (1:500, Servicebio, Wuhan, China), CD31 (1:200, Servicebio, Wuhan, China), and α-SMA (1:300, Servicebio, Wuhan, China). The next day, after washing with PBS, tyramide signal amplification kit (Servicebio, Wuhan, China) was used for double fluorescence staining. Nuclei were stained with DAPI for 1 min. Images were captured using a fluorescence microscope.

Ox-LDL uptake assay

To assess cellular uptake, THP-1 cells from different treatment groups were incubated with 50 μg/ml DiI-ox-LDL (Yiyuan Biotech, Guangzhou, China) at 37 °C for 4 h. After washing with PBS, the cells were imaged using a Leica TCS SP8 confocal laser scanning microscope.

BODIPY staining

Cells from different treatment groups were seeded in confocal dishes and pretreated with 50 μg/mL ox-LDL for 24 h. Subsequently, the cells were co-stained with BODIPY 493/503 (AbMole, Shanghai, China) and DAPI. After removing excess dye by washing, imaging was performed using a Leica TCS SP8 confocal laser scanning microscope.

Western blotting

Aortic tissues or cells were lysed in RIPA buffer containing protease and phosphatase inhibitors. The proteins were separated by gel electrophoresis and then transferred onto PVDF membranes. The membranes were blocked with 5 % skim milk or 5 % BSA at room temperature for 1 h, followed by overnight incubation with primary antibodies at 4 °C. After incubation with primary antibodies, the membranes were incubated with secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature, and the protein bands were detected using ECL detection reagents. Total RNA was extracted and purified from mouse aortic tissues or cells using the RNAiso Plus kit (Takara, Shiga, Japan). The RNA was then reverse transcribed into cDNA. RNA-seq

Total RNA was extracted from cells using TRIzol reagent and sent to Cosmos Wisdom Biotech (Hangzhou, China) for RNA sequencing analysis. Differential expression analysis between groups was performed using the DESeq2 R package, with genes satisfying |FoldChange| > 2 and padj < 0.05 being considered as differentially expressed genes (DEGs). Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analysis and Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) were conducted using the clusterProfiler R package.

Statistical analysis

The analyses were performed using R 4.3.0. All p-values were two-sided, with p < 0.05 considered statistically significant. Data were analyzed with GraphPad Prism 9.5 and presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Comparisons between two groups were conducted using Student's t-test, while comparisons among multiple groups were performed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey's post hoc test for pairwise comparisons.

Result

PLD3 is upregulated in atherosclerotic lesion tissues and holds diagnostic value

To determine the relationship between PLD3 and atherosclerosis, we first examined its expression in atherosclerotic lesions. Differential expression analysis of human carotid artery transcriptome data (GEO dataset GSE100927) revealed that PLD3 was significantly elevated in disease samples (Fig. 1A). Next, samples were divided into high- and low-expression groups based on median PLD3 expression levels, and differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between the two groups were identified using the limma package to generate a volcano plot (Fig. 1B). A total of 174 DEGs were identified, including 47 upregulated and 127 downregulated genes, suggesting that PLD3 may be a key regulatory node in the atherosclerotic disease network. Consistently, experimental validation in high-fat diet (HFD)-fed ApoE-/- mice showed that PLD3 was significantly upregulated at both mRNA and protein levels in the aorta (Fig. 1C-G).

We further evaluated the predictive ability of PLD3 for disease status using logistic regression models in two independent datasets (GSE100927 and GSE43292), generating ROC curves and calculating AUC values (Fig. 1H and I). The results demonstrated that PLD3 expression levels effectively predicted atherosclerosis occurrence in both the training and validation sets. Together, these findings highlight the potential of PLD3 as a diagnostic biomarker for atherosclerosis.

PLD3 knockdown attenuates atherosclerotic progression in ApoE-/- mice

To investigate the role of PLD3 in the development of atherosclerosis, we established an in vivo model by intravenously injecting ApoE-/- mice with AAV-shNC (control) or AAV-shPLD3 to knock down PLD3 expression. The experimental timeline is illustrated in Fig. 2A. Western blot analysis revealed that PLD3 protein levels in the aortas of AAV-shPLD3-treated mice were significantly reduced compared with the AAV-shNC group (Fig. 2, Fig. 2). We next assessed the impact of PLD3 knockdown on atherosclerotic plaque formation. Oil Red O staining of the thoracic and abdominal aortas showed a marked reduction in lipid accumulation in the AAV-shPLD3 group, with quantification as a percentage of total aortic area demonstrating statistically significant differences (Fig. 2, Fig. 2). Consistently, histological analysis of the aortic root revealed that PLD3 knockdown led to a significant decrease in plaque size. Both H&E staining (Fig. 2, Fig. 2) and Oil Red O staining (Fig. 2, Fig. 2) confirmed this observation. Collectively, these results indicate that PLD3 knockdown effectively slows the progression of atherosclerosis in ApoE-/- mice.

PLD3 exhibits macrophage-specific upregulation in atherosclerotic lesions

To investigate in which cell type PLD3 exerts its role in atherosclerosis, we analyzed a single-cell RNA-seq dataset (GSE159677) of human atherosclerotic carotid arteries and identified 8 distinct cell clusters (Fig. 3A). The results showed that PLD3 was enriched in macrophages (Fig. 3B). Further immunofluorescence staining of mouse aortas revealed that PLD3 co-localized with the macrophage marker F4/80 (Fig. 3D), but not with the smooth muscle cell marker α-SMA (Fig. 3E) or the endothelial cell marker CD31 (Fig. 3F). Additionally, PLD3 expression was significantly increased in macrophages within atherosclerotic plaques of high-fat diet (HFD)-fed ApoE−/− mice. In vitro cell model studies further confirmed that ox-LDL stimulation upregulated PLD3 expression in macrophages (Fig. 3G and H). These findings demonstrate that PLD3 is upregulated in macrophages of atherosclerotic lesions and may contribute to the pathogenesis of atherosclerosis.

Differences in the content of high- and low-expression cells and cell communication

Cell-cell communication is closely associated with maintaining tissue homeostasis and the occurrence and progression of diseases [12]. Based on the median expression level of the gene PLD3, cells were divided into high- and low-expression groups, and bar plots were generated to analyze their distribution differences across various cell types (Fig. 4A). Further, the CellChat algorithm was applied to perform cell communication analysis on the single-cell data of the PLD3 high- and low-expression groups, revealing the total communication quantity and weighted communication strength between the two groups (Fig. 4B). Additionally, differences in communication strength (Fig. 4C) and the differential communication patterns of specific pathways between cell types were presented through heat maps (Fig. 4D).The analysis identified significant potential interactions between macrophages and other cell types, particularly with Vascular Smooth Muscle Cells (VSMCs). In Fig. 4E, bubble plots respectively displayed the interaction ligands of macrophages in the high- and low-expression groups of PLD3 with other cells when they were signal target cells. Notably, in the PLD3 high-expression group, the interactions between macrophages and smooth muscle cells primarily involved the SPP1-(ITGAV + ITGB5), SPP1-(ITGAV + ITGB1), and SPP1-(ITGA8 + ITGB1) pathways. These receptor-ligand pairs are the top-ranked interactions based on communication probability weights within the SPP1 signaling pathway and are most relevant to atherosclerosis pathology. These pathways are closely related to biological processes such as cell adhesion, migration, and inflammatory responses [13].

PLD3′s immune regulatory network and involved signaling pathways

The formation of intravascular atherosclerotic plaques is not only associated with abnormal lipid metabolism but also involves the aberrant activation of the immune system and the interaction between inflammatory factors and vascular cells [14,15]. To investigate this, we analyzed the correlation between PLD3 and various immune factors, including immunosuppressive factors, immunostimulatory factors, chemokines, and receptors. The results demonstrated that PLD3 is closely associated with immune cell infiltration levels, suggesting its critical role in the immune microenvironment of atherosclerosis (Fig. 5). Subsequently, we further explored the specific signaling pathways involving PLD3 and its potential molecular mechanisms in disease progression. GSVA analysis revealed that PLD3 is significantly enriched in pathways such as INTERFERON_GAMMA_RESPONSE, INTERFERON_ALPHA_RESPONSE, and INFLAMMATORY_RESPONSE (Fig. 6A), indicating its potential regulation of disease progression through inflammation-related pathways. Additionally, we obtained disease-related regulatory genes from the GeneCards database (https://www.genecards.org/). Based on the Relevance score ranking and their expression in single-cell data, we selected CCL2, MSR1, and VCAM1 as the three disease genes. Through correlation analysis to explore the co-expression network between key genes and disease genes, we found that PLD3 exhibits significant co-expression relationships with the aforementioned genes (Fig. 6B-D). This suggests that PLD3 may cooperatively participate in regulating key pathological processes in atherosclerosis, such as macrophage infiltration, lipid accumulation/foam cell formation, and endothelial cell activation [16,17].

Knockdown of PLD3 alleviates foam cell formation and macrophage inflammation

To investigate the role of PLD3 in atherosclerosis, we infected THP-1 cells with lentivirus carrying PLD3 shRNA to stably knockdown PLD3 expression (Fig. 7A). Subsequently, RNA-seq analysis was performed on shNC- or shPLD3-treated THP-1-derived macrophages stimulated with ox-LDL. Compared with the shNC group, the shPLD3 group exhibited 395 upregulated genes and 481 downregulated genes (Fig. 7B). Functional enrichment analysis revealed that PLD3 knockdown may affect multiple pathways associated with atherosclerosis. KEGG enrichment analysis indicated that these pathways included the chemokine signaling pathway, cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction, lipid and atherosclerosis, NF-κB signaling pathway, and cell cycle (Fig. 7C-D), suggesting that PLD3 may regulate inflammatory responses and lipid metabolism in atherosclerosis. GSEA further supported the association of PLD3 with these pathways (Fig. 7E-F).

The transformation of macrophages into foam cells is a key driver of atherosclerosis progression and complications [18]. Therefore, we evaluated whether PLD3 is involved in macrophage foam cell formation. Oil Red O staining showed that THP-1 cells in the shPLD3 group had significantly reduced lipid accumulation compared with the shNC group (Fig. 8A). BODIPY staining yielded consistent results (Fig. 8B). In lipid uptake assays, PLD3 knockdown decreased ox-LDL uptake after 4 h of DiI-oxLDL stimulation (Fig. 8C). These findings suggest that PLD3 plays a role in regulating macrophage foam cell formation. Macrophages actively take up modified lipoproteins (e.g., ox-LDL) through scavenger receptors, including CD36, SR-A, and LOX-1, which is a central pathological mechanism of foam cell formation. Western blot results showed that under ox-LDL stimulation, CD36 protein expression was significantly downregulated in shPLD3-treated cells, whereas SR-A and LOX-1 levels remained unchanged (Fig. 8D and H-J). This indicates that PLD3 knockdown may reduce lipoprotein uptake and foam cell formation by downregulating CD36 expression.

Foam cell formation triggers a cascade of inflammatory cytokines and chemokines, promoting the infiltration and activation of pro-atherogenic immune cells, thereby exacerbating atherosclerotic inflammation [19,20]. Thus, we examined the regulatory role of PLD3 in pro-inflammatory cytokine expression. RT-qPCR analysis of ox-LDL-treated THP-1 cells revealed that IL-1β and TNF-α mRNA levels were significantly lower in the shPLD3 group than in the shNC group (Fig. 8F and G). Previous RNA-seq enrichment analysis suggested that the anti-inflammatory effect of PLD3 knockdown might involve suppression of the NF-κB signaling pathway. NF-κB is a key transcription factor regulating inflammatory responses and contributing to inflammatory atherosclerosis [21]. We assessed the phosphorylation level of the NF-κB p65 subunit and found that it was significantly reduced in shPLD3-treated cells compared with the shNC group (Fig. 8E and K). This suggests that PLD3 knockdown may attenuate inflammation by inhibiting NF-κB pathway activation in macrophages.

Discussion

In this study, we investigated the role of PLD3 in atherosclerosis, with three key findings: (i) PLD3 is significantly upregulated in macrophages within atherosclerotic lesions; (ii) PLD3 remodels the immune microenvironment and mediates intercellular communication; (iii) PLD3 drives macrophage foaming and inflammatory responses by upregulating CD36 to enhance lipid uptake and activating the NF-κB pathway to promote pro-inflammatory cytokine release.

The known functions of PLD3 primarily stem from its research in Alzheimer's disease (AD), but its actual expression levels and distribution in AD remain controversial [[22], [23], [24]]. Wen et al. were the first to detect PLD3 in the cardiac tissue of AD mice using nanoprobes [22], which prompted us to investigate whether PLD3 plays a role in cardiovascular diseases such as atherosclerosis. Previous studies have shown an increased number of PLD3-expressing foamy macrophages in atherosclerotic plaques in mice [8]. Additionally, elevated PLD3 expression has been reported in recurrent glioblastoma [25], HBV-infected liver tissue [26], patients with carotid artery stenosis [27], and diabetic macroangiopathy [28]. However, most of these studies were limited to sequencing analyses and lacked experimental validation. By combining bioinformatics analysis with experimental validation, we conclusively demonstrated that PLD3 is significantly upregulated at both the mRNA and protein levels in atherosclerotic lesion tissues. Logistic regression analysis of two independent human atherosclerotic plaque datasets (GSE100927 and GSE43292) showed that PLD3 is highly expressed in atherosclerotic plaques and can distinguish diseased from control tissues, suggesting it may serve as a novel adjunctive diagnostic biomarker. However, its diagnostic specificity, sensitivity, and independence from traditional biomarkers require further validation in prospective clinical cohorts. Importantly, our study found elevated PLD3 levels in macrophages within atherosclerotic lesions of HFD-fed ApoE−/− mice. In vivo experiments showed that PLD3 knockdown alleviates atherosclerosis in mice. In vitro experiments demonstrated that PLD3 knockout significantly reduced the expression of inflammatory factors as well as lipid uptake and accumulation in macrophages. Thus, this study is the first to uncover the novel role of PLD3 in inflammatory atherosclerosis and indicates that PLD3 mediates oxLDL-induced inflammatory responses and foam cell formation in macrophages.

Throughout the entire process of atherosclerosis development, which includes plaque formation, progression, and rupture, macrophage-mediated inflammatory responses persist and play a pivotal pathogenic role [18,29]. This process initiates when peripheral blood monocytes are recruited into the subendothelial space, where they differentiate into macrophages [30]. After local proliferation, these macrophages internalize modified lipoproteins, particularly ox-LDL, via scavenger receptors such as SR-A1, CD36, and LOX-1. Scavenger receptors serve as indispensable mediators for ox-LDL uptake by macrophages, exhibiting high affinity for ox-LDL, while their expression remains unaffected by negative feedback regulation from intracellular cholesterol levels [31]. Consequently, macrophages continuously and extensively engulf ox-LDL, leading to excessive intracellular lipid accumulation and the formation of lipid-laden “foam cells,” which represent a hallmark pathological feature of early atherosclerotic plaques [32]. Moreover, lipid stimuli such as ox-LDL act as key initiating factors that trigger inflammatory responses in macrophages. These stimuli induce significant metabolic reprogramming in macrophages, thereby driving the secretion of numerous pro-inflammatory cytokines and chemokines, which amplify the local inflammatory milieu [33]. The nuclear factor kappa-B (NF-κB), a canonical inflammation-associated transcription factor [34], occupies a central role in this process. Activated NF-κB directly regulates the transcriptional expression of multiple pro-inflammatory genes, serving as a core hub that sustains and amplifies the inflammatory cascade in atherosclerosis [21,35]. Through single-cell RNA sequencing combined with wet-lab validation, this study reveals that PLD3 promotes foam cell formation by upregulating CD36 expression and activates the NF-κB pathway, thereby enhancing the secretion of inflammatory mediators in oxLDL-stimulated macrophages. Additionally, we observe significant differences in intercellular communication between PLD3-high and PLD3-low groups, particularly in the interactions between macrophages and vascular smooth muscle cells. In summary, this study provides empirical evidence linking PLD3 to foam cell formation and inflammatory responses, while identifying a mechanism involving the scavenger receptor CD36 and the NF-κB pathway. These findings offer novel insights into targeting PLD3 or its mediated cellular interactions to intervene in atherosclerosis progression.

Limitations

However, it must be recognized that this study has its limitations. The limited number of samples in scRNA-seq may affect the reliability of the analysis results. Moreover, the molecular mechanism by which PLD3 regulates the scavenger receptor CD36 has not yet been fully elucidated and requires further research. In addition, the in vitro experiments were conducted only in THP-1 cells, and validation in other cell lines or primary macrophages would help to strengthen the robustness and translational relevance of the findings. Despite these limitations, our analysis results point to new directions for future research.

Conclusion

In summary, this study reveals the critical role of PLD3 in atherosclerosis: it significantly upregulated in macrophages within atherosclerotic lesions and may serve as a novel adjunctive biomarker; it promotes lipid uptake by upregulating CD36 and drives foam cell formation and inflammatory responses by activating the NF-κB inflammatory pathway. These findings indicate that PLD3 is a potential therapeutic target, providing a new direction for disease intervention.
