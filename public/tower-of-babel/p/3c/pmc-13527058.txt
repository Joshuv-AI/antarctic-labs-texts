Robust automated detection of small-scale rainfall-induced landslides in Italy Using SegFormer and high-resolution satellite imagery
Authors: Abdelkader Riche, Pierluigi Confuorto, Mawloud Guermoui, Silvia Bianchini
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Rainfall-induced landslides pose significant challenges in Italy due to their small scale, fragmented distribution, and the severe damage they cause to populations, structures, and infrastructures. These challenges are amplified by the increasing frequency of intense precipitation events and the complex geomorphological settings of the Italian landscape. Therefore, the identification of such phenomena remains of critical importance. Despite extensive research on semi- and automatic landslide detection, many regional landscapes remain underexplored with advanced deep learning models. This study employs high-resolution (3 m) RGB Planet imagery to evaluate the performance of UNet and SegFormer for small landslide segmentation in these scarcely investigated regions. Unlike UNet’s reliance on localized convolutional operations, SegFormer leverages a transformer-based architecture with attention mechanisms, enabling enhanced capture of both local and global features crucial for detecting small and spatially scattered landslides. Experimental results on Emilia-Romagna (Central Italy, affected by two major thunderstorms in May 2023) show that the optimal SegFormer configuration (patch size 64, batch size 16) achieves an F1-score of 73.42%, Recall of 71.72%, and mIoU of 63.87%, outperforming UNet in segmentation precision and reliability. Further cross-region testing on Tuscany (Central Italy, affected by abundant rainfall in March 2025) confirms SegFormer’s potential for robust generalization, achieving an F1-score of 50.01%, Recall of 51.77%, and mIoU of 66.35%. These findings underscore SegFormer’s potential for operational-scale mapping of rainfall-induced small landslides and the pressing need for automated mapping solutions to support disaster preparedness in precipitation-prone contexts.

Introduction

Landslides are natural hazard events that occur when rock, soil, or debris moves downhill under the influence of gravity. Various factors can trigger these movements, including intense rainfall, seismic activity, volcanic eruptions, deforestation, and human alterations to land use1,2. As a major geological threat, landslides result in significant loss of life, extensive damage to infrastructure, and environmental harm3. They contribute to a substantial share of natural disasters, with approximately 9% of such events linked to landslides, leading to thousands of fatalities annually4. Rainfall-induced landslides are particularly challenging to identify due to their sudden occurrence, small size, and the rapid post-event changes in terrain and vegetation. Their occurrence and impact have intensified in many areas due to climate change and rapid urban development, which disturb natural slopes and drainage patterns5.

Traditional field surveys such as geomorphological mapping, soil testing, and slope analysis remain accurate but are time-consuming, labor-intensive, and spatially limited6–8. Remote sensing and GIS techniques overcome some of these limitations by enabling large-scale coverage and cost-effective susceptibility mapping, but they can be hindered by factors such as cloud cover, vegetation regrowth, and coarse spatial resolution1,9,10. Machine learning (ML) approaches further automate landslide mapping and improve prediction accuracy,however, their reliance on hand-crafted features may limit performance in complex terrains.

Deep learning (DL) addresses several of these shortcomings by learning hierarchical spatial features directly from raw data, reducing manual feature engineering, and improving adaptability across varying conditions11. Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) have shown strong performance in landslide mapping12, but generalizing to diverse regions remains challenging due to differences in causal factors, morphology, and environmental settings13. Recent developments, including attention-based architectures such as SegFormer, aim to capture multi-scale spatial relationships and enhance generalization14.

While numerous studies have applied DL to earthquake-induced landslides, fewer have focused on rainfall-triggered cases14–16. Rainfall often produces smaller, more fragmented landslides, and post-event cloud cover frequently delays optical data acquisition. Vegetation regrowth can further obscure landslide scars if imagery is obtained weeks after the event17. These factors make post-disaster detection particularly challenging.

Detecting small-scale landslides presents additional challenges for DL-based methods, as these events often occupy only a few pixels in satellite imagery, making them prone to omission or misclassification. Their subtle spectral signatures, especially in vegetated or shadowed terrain, further complicate delineation. Previous studies have addressed these issues by using higher-resolution imagery16,18, multi-source data fusion19, and attention-based architectures such as SegFormer, which have shown promising performance for small-object segmentation by capturing fine-grained shapes and boundaries14. However, cross-regional generalization without fine-tuning remains a significant challenge. In this study, we test two SegFormer variants (MiT-B0 and MiT-B1), a prompt-based segmentation framework, to evaluate their ability to detect small, fragmented landslides in high-resolution PlanetScope imagery and to generalize across regions without additional training.

This study focuses on landslides triggered by extreme rainfall in Emilia-Romagna, Italy, in May 2023, where persistent cloud cover and rapid vegetation regrowth hindered timely post-event detection using optical imagery. To address these challenges, we employed high-resolution PlanetScope imagery (3 m of resolution) with two semantic segmentation architectures U-Net and the attention-based SegFormer to map small, fragmented landslides. In addition to evaluating performance in Emilia-Romagna, we applied the trained models directly to a newly curated benchmark dataset from the Tuscany region as a transfer learning inference test, assessing their ability to generalize without fine-tuning. This experiment demonstrates the potential for rapid deployment of pre-trained models to support early post-emergency mapping when local training data are unavailable. While several global studies have addressed similar events, no prior research in Italy has applied robust segmentation models for such post-disaster landslide mapping. The study area had not been central to segmentation research, except for32, who included it among six regions using 5,847 landslides from17, an inventory later updated by20 to 80,997 manually identified events. Our work fills this gap by combining state-of-the-art segmentation techniques with cross-regional inference tests, highlighting both methodological advances and practical strategies for early-response mapping in the Italian context.

Materials and methods

Study area

The Emilia-Romagna region in northern Italy extends from the Apennines across the Po Valley to the Adriatic Sea and is among Italy’s most economically advanced areas, particularly in industry. Geomorphologically, the region is highly diverse featuring mountainous terrains in the south, hilly zones in the northeast, and broad alluvial plains across the central and northern areas. This varied terrain contributes to significant landslide susceptibility, as identified in the study by21. The region topographic variability and landslide susceptibility distribution are illustrated in Fig. 1, which shows a digital terrain model alongside a susceptibility map highlighting areas most prone to slope failures. Geologically, the region is complex, comprising over 600 formations that span from the Jurassic to the Miocene. The northern Apennines are primarily composed of sedimentary rocks mainly flysch sequences, marls, clays, and chaotic mélanges with tectonic origins. In the western and central Apennines (from Piacenza to Bologna), the Ligurian and Sub-Ligurian Domains dominate, characterized by clay-rich formations with low mechanical strength and high deformability. These include tectonic mélanges known as “Argille Scagliose,” which consist of block-in-matrix structures, often highly susceptible to landslides due to their swelling clays and weak cohesion. In contrast, the eastern Apennines and the ridge areas are made up of more competent sandstone-marl sequences of the Tuscan-Umbrian Domain, which are generally more stable. Overlying some of these older units is the Epiligurian Sequence, consisting of flat-lying sandstones and minor clayey breccias and olistostromes, which are locally less resistant to degradation. These lithological and structural variations ranging from chaotic, clay-dominated mélanges to more consolidated turbidites significantly shape slope morphology, weathered soil properties, and vegetation cover, all of which influence landslide behavior20.Fig. 1Study area location: The top panel shows the Emilia-Romagna region overlaid on the DEM. In May 2023, two extreme rainfall events hit the region. The first (May 1–3) delivered ~200 mm of rain in 48 hours; the second (May 16–17) brought 200–250 mm over the same duration, affecting overlapping zones17,20. Each event corresponds to a 100–300-year return period,together, they represent a 500-year event. Using NDVI changes and geomorphic filters, PANDA rapidly mapped potential landslides22. In response20, manually identified 80,997 landslides using 0.2 m resolution aerial imagery (RGB and NIR), supported by 3D visualization tools. This dataset is publicly available (https://zenodo.org/records/13742643).

Based on the findings of23, there is still a lack of robust automated approaches for landslide detection in Italy, where most existing studies focus on susceptibility mapping or change detection using spectral indices. These methods often require significant manual interpretation and are not well suited for rapid post-event mapping of numerous small-scale landslides. This limitation is particularly evident in events such as the Emilia-Romagna 2023 disaster, where thousands of small, spatially distributed landslides occurred. To address this gap, this study proposes a deep learning-based semantic segmentation approach using SegFormer applied to high-resolution (3 m) satellite imagery. By leveraging its multi-scale representation capability, SegFormer improves the detection and delineation of small-scale rainfall-induced landslides, enabling more efficient and scalable post-event mapping.

For their analysis20, classified the region’s formations into eight structural-lithological units, grouped into fine- and coarse-grained rock types. Fine-grained units yield clay- and silt-rich soils, while coarse-grained rocks form sandy and gravelly soils each influencing landslide mechanisms differently.

In addition, we selected the Mugello area in northern Tuscany, impacted by heavy rainfall and landslides on March 14, 2025. This event, part of a broader pattern of extreme precipitation linked to elevated air and sea temperatures, delivered 100–250 mm of rain over 24–72 hours (avg. ~180 mm). The Mugello basin is located 30 km north of Florence, with a WNW–ESE structural trend, and extends for approximately 20 km2 to the north of the Sieve stream, a left tributary of the Arno River. Geologically, it is one of the intermontane basins of the western Northern Apennine belt, filled with middle Pliocene to Pleistocene alluvial and lacustrine deposits. The northeastern margin of the basin is composed of Paleocene–Eocene calcareous turbidites tectonically overlying early–middle Miocene turbiditic sandstones and siltstones, while the southwestern margin features a thrust fault carrying coarse-grained calcareous and terrigenous turbidite deposits of the Oligocene–early Miocene. We manually delineated 71 landslide polygons using Planet imagery and created a binary mask to evaluate segmentation model performance in a meteorologically distinct region.

Dataset description

Our dataset documents landslides associated with the extreme weather events of May 2023 in Italy Emilia-Romagna region. We utilized high-resolution PlanetScope imagery with 3 m of spatial resolution accessed through the Planet Explorer platform (https://www.planet.com/explorer) to support the spatial analysis. The imagery corresponds to surface reflectance products, which are radiometrically calibrated and atmospherically corrected by the provider, ensuring spectral consistency across the study area. For reference data, we adopted the landslide inventories provided by20, as they offer detailed and accurate mapping of all recorded landslides across the affected area.

Satellite images

The dataset was constructed by generating 45 image tiles, each with a resolution of 3000 × 3000 pixels. From these, 40 tiles were used to build the training and validation sets. The division performed randomly at the patch level, allocating 80% of the patches for training and 20% for validation. The remaining 5 tiles were selected from regions geographically distinct from the training and validation areas to support transfer-learning experiments. As illustrated in Fig. 1, all the tiles correspond to zones most severely impacted by the May 2023 rainfall events.

Tile selection was based on the availability and quality of post-event satellite imagery. Due to cloud cover in certain regions immediately following the event, we considered a 30-day window for image acquisition. When available, cloud-free imagery captured shortly after the event was prioritized; otherwise, the best available image within the specified period was selected.

Masks inventories

The landslide inventories provided by20 were mapped from aerial imagery with a spatial resolution of 0.2 m and delivered as high-resolution polygon shapefiles. These were converted into binary raster masks aligned with the 3 m PlanetScope RGB imagery, where “0” denotes non-landslide areas and “1” denotes landslide-affected regions. To address the resolution mismatch and ensure spatial consistency, rasterization was performed using bilinear interpolation combined with a maximum area overlap criterion, allowing each 3 m pixel to be assigned the most representative class label. This process minimized boundary mismatches and label noise, improving the quality of training data for semantic segmentation.

Finally, both the satellite images and the corresponding binary raster masks were partitioned into smaller patches of 256 × 256, 128 × 128, and 64 × 64 pixels, as illustrated in Fig. 2. This multi-scale patching strategy was employed to train models under diverse scenarios, enhancing generalization across spatial resolutions. In total, for the 256 × 256 patches, we generated 2,733 for training, 684 for validation, and 445 for testing. For the 128 × 128 patches, we obtained 8,044 for training, 2,011 for validation, and 1,152 for testing. For the 64 × 64 patches, we created 19,324 for training, 4,832 for validation, and 2,602 for testing. The dataset exhibits a class imbalance between landslide and non-landslide pixels. Landslide pixels account for approximately 7.75% of the training set and 7.60% of the validation set, while in the test set they represent about 5.37%, with non-landslide pixels exceeding 90% in all cases. This imbalance reflects the natural spatial distribution of landslides, which typically occupy a small fraction of the landscape.Fig. 2Sample images, masks, and overlays from the dataset across all sizes. the RGB image patches are derived from planetscope imagery provided by planet labs (https://www.planet.com/explorer). the figure was generated using Matplotlib 3.10.

Methods

In the aftermath of the severe landslide events that occurred in May 2023 in the Emilia-Romagna region of Italy, high-resolution satellite imagery offers an effective means to assess terrain changes. In this study, we utilize RGB imagery captured by PlanetScope satellites as the input for semantic segmentation models aimed at detecting landslide-affected areas.

PlanetScope provides daily imagery with a ground sampling distance of approximately 3 meters, making it suitable for detecting landscape changes caused by mass movements. The detection task is framed as a binary segmentation problem, where the objective is to assign each pixel in the image to either a “landslide” or "non-landslide" class. To achieve this, we use pre-labeled binary masks derived from post-event ground truth data as the target labels. The methodological pipeline, including data preprocessing, model training, and inference steps, is summarized in the flowchart shown in Fig. 3.Fig. 3Methodological flowchart of the pipeline used in this study.

Methodological flowchart of the pipeline used in this study.

Model training is initially performed on a labeled training area within Emilia-Romagna, as defined in Fig. 3. After training, the resulting models are directly applied without further fine-tuning to an independent test area in the same region to evaluate spatial generalization performance. Subsequently, the trained models are used to predict landslide occurrences in a different geographical setting: the Tuscany region, where a new landslide event occurred in March 2025. This cross-regional application enables the assessment of model transferability and robustness across different landscapes and event scenarios.

To identify the most effective configuration, experiments were conducted under multiple training scenarios. Specifically, we tested three patch sizes 256×256, 128×128, and 64×64 to explore the impact of spatial resolution on model performance. For each patch size, we also evaluated the models with different batch sizes (16, 32, and 64). This multi-scenario approach was critical to selecting the optimal model setup, balancing segmentation accuracy, generalization capability, and computational efficiency. All experiments were implemented in PyTorch and executed on an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 4090 GPU (24 GB VRAM) using the AdamW optimizer with a learning rate of 6×10⁻5, over 50 training epochs. Early stopping was applied when no performance improvement was observed, with Recall used as the primary criterion for saving the best model, while cross-validation considered balanced metrics such as F1-score and mIoU.

To address class imbalance, we applied a weighted binary cross-entropy loss function, assigning higher weight to the landslide class. U-Net architecture

U-Net, originally proposed by24, is a convolutional neural network architecture specifically designed for biomedical image segmentation. Its distinctive encoder–decoder structure has since been widely adopted across a range of segmentation tasks, including remote sensing and environmental hazard analysis.

U-Net architecture is characterized by its symmetric “U”-shaped layout: a contracting path (encoder) that captures context and a symmetric expanding path (decoder) that enables precise localization. As shown in Fig. 4, the network includes skip connections that link each encoder layer to its corresponding decoder layer, allowing for the preservation and refinement of spatial features across different scales an essential attribute for detecting landslides in high-resolution satellite imagery.Fig. 4U-Net architecture employed in this study.

U-Net architecture employed in this study.

The encoder consists of repeated applications of two 3×3 convolutional layers, each followed by a rectified linear unit (ReLU) activation and a 2×2 max-pooling operation for downsampling. At each downsampling step, the number of feature channels is doubled, allowing the model to extract increasingly abstract representations. Where W represents the convolutional filter, X is the input feature map, b is the bias term, * denotes convolution, and σ sigma is the ReLU activation function.

The decoder mirrors the encoder structure by using up-convolution (transposed convolution) layers to increase the spatial resolution of the feature maps, followed by concatenation with corresponding high-resolution features from the encoder through skip connections. This feature fusion enhances localization by reintroducing spatial information lost during downsampling. Due to its effectiveness in pixel-wise classification, U-Net has been widely applied to landslide detection tasks using remote sensing imagery. Recent studies have leveraged U-Net for detecting and mapping landslides in various geographic and climatic contexts, demonstrating its high accuracy and generalizability16,25–27.

SegFormer architecture

SegFormer, introduced by33, represents a new generation of semantic segmentation models that integrates transformer-based architectures into the domain of pixel-wise classification. A key innovation in SegFormer is its elimination of positional encoding, a common component in standard transformers.

This design choice enhances the model’s flexibility and prevents performance degradation when input image resolutions vary between training and inference stages. Instead of relying on complex decoder structures, SegFormer uses a lightweight multilayer perceptron (MLP) decoder that efficiently consolidates multi-scale features from the encoder, striking a balance between local detail and global context. In the context of landslide detection, these characteristics are particularly relevant for identifying small and irregular landslide boundaries. The transformer-based encoder in SegFormer relies on hierarchical feature extraction and self-attention mechanisms, allowing the model to capture long-range spatial dependencies and global context more effectively than traditional CNN-based architectures, which are limited by local receptive fields. This enables a deeper and more adaptive feature representation across multiple scales, improving the discrimination of landslides from spectrally similar features such as bare soil or sparsely vegetated areas. Such capabilities are especially important when working with high-resolution imagery, where landslides often appear as fragmented and heterogeneous patterns, requiring robust multi-scale feature integration for accurate delineation.

Discussion

The experimental results highlight the superior adaptability and robustness of the SegFormer model for landslide detection in remote sensing imagery, particularly within the Emilia-Romagna region. While the region is included in the study, the focus on small-scale, rainfall-triggered landslides is more reflective of recent climatic events across Italy, rather than being strictly characteristic of the study area itself. Unlike larger, earthquake-induced events typically examined in benchmark datasets, these small and shallow failures require models that are both precise and sensitive to fine-scale spatial features and subtle spectral variations, which is supported in our study by the use of higher-resolution imagery compared to Sentinel-2. To evaluate the effectiveness of our proposed approach, we included UNet as a baseline benchmark due to its widespread use and well-established performance in remote sensing segmentation tasks. While not the focus of this study, UNet provides a reference point to contextualize the improvements achieved by more advanced architectures. In this work, patch size refers to the dimensions (in pixels) of the image tiles into which the original satellite imagery is divided before being input into the model. Smaller patch sizes preserve localized detail and reduce spatial averaging, which can blur or obscure small landslide features when using larger patches. A key finding of the study is that smaller patch sizes (64×64 pixels) significantly improved segmentation performance for both UNet and SegFormer, with U-Net benefiting from enhanced delineation of fragmented landslide boundaries and SegFormer gaining additional advantage from its transformer-based architecture, which captures both fine-scale detail and broader spatial context even within smaller tiles. This aligns with the findings of16, who observed similar improvements when detecting rainfall-induced landslides in Brazil. In both cases, finer spatial resolution and smaller patches enhanced the recognition of localized landslide patterns, which are often blurred or lost with larger patch sizes due to spatial averaging.

The SegFormer model, equipped with a multi-scale attention mechanism, was consistently more effective than UNet at integrating both local textural cues and broader contextual patterns critical for distinguishing landslides from spectrally similar features in vegetated or complex terrain. This conclusion held true for both the MIT_b0 and MIT_b1 backbones, with MIT_b1 showing a slight improvement in F1-score and mIoU over MIT_b0 while maintaining comparable recall performance. This suggests that increasing backbone complexity can offer marginal segmentation benefits without compromising the recall-driven objective that guided our model selection, which is especially relevant for early post-emergency mapping. Even under varying patch and batch configurations, SegFormer maintained strong segmentation performance, while UNet results were significantly more sensitive to configuration changes. This robustness is essential for real-world deployment in dynamic geohazard environments, especially where data quality, resolution, and acquisition timing may vary. Our findings align with and expand on prior works. For instance14, applied both models to the GDCLD dataset, with SegFormer achieving a significantly higher recall of 87.09% compared to UNet 61.22%. Similarly28, worked with the CAS landslide dataset, reporting recall values of 95.15% for SegFormer versus 82.36% for UNet. In another study30, observed comparable recall (83.3%) for both models, but SegFormer outperformed UNet in terms of F1-score (84.1% vs. 76.9%) when applied to landslides in the Jiuzhaigou scenic area, which was severely impacted by the 2017 earthquake.

The study by32 also applied U-Net variants to landslides in Emilia-Romagna, but relied on a limited sample of the historical inventory (5,847 events) and coarser Sentinel-2 imagery (10 m). While their attention-based UNet achieved a recall of 80.01%, our work utilized the updated landslide inventory (80,997 mapped landslides) from20 and higher-resolution PlanetScope imagery (3 m). UNet was included in our study primarily as a baseline to contextualize the performance gains of SegFormer, rather than as a competing approach. These improvements in data granularity coupled with SegFormer architectural advantages enabled higher segmentation quality, especially in detecting and delineating the smaller and more fragmented landslides prevalent in our study area. SegFormer strength lies in its multi-scale attention mechanism, which captures both global and local features, allowing it to distinguish subtle morphological cues of landslides. This capability is particularly critical in heterogeneous terrain, where landslides exhibit complex spatial signatures that span multiple scales. Its consistent performance underlines its suitability for real-world applications where variability in input data such as resolution or scene complexity is common.

However, several factors complicated the modeling process. One major limitation was the temporal delay in obtaining cloud-free imagery after the May 17, 2023, event. Persistent cloud cover prevented the acquisition of immediate post-event imagery, and the data used for training and evaluation were obtained in late May and June 2023. This temporal gap was a direct result of data acquisition constraints imposed by cloud cover following the event, which affected multiple tiles in the study area. This delay introduced vegetation regrowth, which significantly reduced the spectral contrast between landslide scars and the surrounding terrain. As seen in the bimodal NDVI difference distribution (Fig. 9), a notable proportion of landslide-labeled pixels exhibited positive NDVI changes, indicating partial vegetation recovery. These challenges are further amplified by the inherent subjectivity of ground truth labeling in post-event conditions, where coalescent failures and vegetation overlay make boundaries difficult to define. Finally, our test on the Tuscany region provides a more rigorous evaluation of the model’s transferability under different geological and climatic conditions. Both Emilia-Romagna and the Mugello area (Tuscany) are located within the Northern Apennines and are characterized by sedimentary formations, including turbidites, marls, and clay-rich units that are prone to landslides. However, Emilia-Romagna is dominated by highly deformable clay-rich mélanges, while the Mugello basin includes alluvial and lacustrine deposits with different geomorphological characteristics. The triggering rainfall also differs between the two regions, with Emilia-Romagna experiencing extreme events of up to 200–250 mm in 48 hours, compared to approximately 100–250 mm over 24–72 hours in Tuscany. Despite these differences in lithology, geomorphology, and rainfall conditions, SegFormer maintained a balanced and robust performance with an F1-score of 50.01%, recall of 51.77%, and mIoU of 66.35%, while UNet exhibited a sharp drop in recall (14.60%) despite achieving high accuracy (99.45%). These results suggest that the model’s generalization capability is supported not only by its architecture but also by the presence of partially comparable geomorphological and triggering conditions across the two regions, including similar landslide types, sizes, and slope processes typical of the Northern Apennines.

Model performance in detecting small landslides is reasonably acceptable, particularly given the inherent challenges posed by post-event conditions. As shown in Fig. 10, the number of predicted small landslides closely matches the true count, with a detection rate of 95.20%, indicating a strong capability to identify these events despite their spatial and spectral complexity. In comparison, medium and large landslides were detected at slightly lower rates of 84.89% and 83.07%, respectively. This discrepancy may be attributed to vegetation regrowth and spectral mixing in larger landslide areas, which can obscure the landslide signal and reduce detection accuracy. In this study, landslides were categorized based on their mapped area using the distribution and standard deviation of the full landslide dataset. Specifically, landslides smaller than 18 pixels (equivalent to less than 162 m2, given the 3×3 m resolution of PlanetScope imagery) were classified as small, those between 18 and 61 pixels (162–549 m2) as medium, and those larger than 61 pixels (over 549 m2) as large. Limitations remain due to temporal gaps in imagery, spectral ambiguity from vegetation regrowth, and resolution mismatches between ground truth and input data. Working in a context like Italy where landslide detection is complicated by fragmented terrain, frequent rainfall-triggered failures, and rapid post-event vegetation recovery poses unique challenges rarely addressed in the remote sensing and deep learning literature. Unlike more extensively studied regions (e.g., earthquake-induced landslides in Asia or South America), Italy lacks a strong tradition of applying robust, data-driven methods for operational landslide mapping. In this context, our work is among the few studies demonstrating the effective use of transformer-based models and high-resolution PlanetScope imagery for rainfall-induced landslides in Italy. Crucially, we emphasize that the primary scope of this study is post-emergency rapid mapping, aiming to support early response and situational awareness after major events. Our results show that despite environmental and data-related challenges, SegFormer offers a practical and accurate tool for timely landslide delineation in one of Europe’s most landslide-prone regions.

However, the reliance on optical imagery introduces limitations for real-time deployment, particularly during prolonged rainfall periods when cloud cover restricts image availability. To address this limitation, future work will explore the integration of Synthetic Aperture Radar (SAR) data, which is not affected by atmospheric conditions, enabling more consistent monitoring during extreme weather events. In addition, the fusion of multi-source data, including DEM, slope, and geomorphological information, combined with optical RGB imagery, is expected to improve model robustness and enhance generalization across diverse environmental conditions. Such approaches could reduce false detections and improve the reliability of landslide mapping in operational scenarios.

Conclusion

In the last decade, Italy is facing frequent rainfall-triggered rapid and small-scale landslides but lacks robust, automated models adapted to its complex geomorphological and climatic conditions. Existing methods are often regionally limited or not suited for fine-scale detection needed in Italian terrain. This study compares two deep learning models for landslide detection: UNet, a convolutional network known for local feature extraction, and SegFormer, a transformer-based architecture that combines multi-scale attention to capture both local textures and global spatial context. We applied these models to the Emilia-Romagna May 2023 and Tuscany March 2025 rainfall-induced landslide events. In Emilia-Romagna, SegFormer successfully detected 6,383 out of 8,900 annotated landslides, while in Tuscany it identified 37 of 71. SegFormer consistently outperformed UNet, showing greater robustness and adaptability, especially in complex vegetated landscapes and different climatic settings. However, it is important to note that the Tuscany dataset represents a limited independent test case, and the number of mapped landslides (71) remains relatively small for a comprehensive assessment of model generalization. Therefore, these results should be interpreted as preliminary evidence of transferability rather than definitive validation.

Our work is among the first to successfully apply high-resolution satellite data and transformer models for landslide detection in Italy, offering a scalable, transferable framework for rainfall-induced landslides in data-constrained, topographically complex regions. The primary focus is on post-emergency rapid mapping an essential tool to support civil protection, emergency response, and damage assessment after extreme rainfall events. Rapid and accurate landslide detection helps mitigate risk and improve public safety in vulnerable areas. While SegFormer demonstrated promising potential for operational deployment, further validation on larger and more diverse datasets is required to confirm its robustness across regions. However, limitations include temporal delays in cloud-free imagery acquisition causing vegetation regrowth and spectral ambiguity, boundary uncertainties from debris beneath vegetation, and mismatches between high-resolution ground truth and lower-resolution imagery. These challenges impact precise landslide delineation and model performance. Future work should explore multi-temporal and multi-sensor data fusion, particularly the combination of Synthetic Aperture Radar (SAR) imagery with optical imagery, to improve detection in persistently cloudy conditions. Further directions include integrating topographic and rainfall information, refining annotations with semi-automated methods. Evaluating near-real-time satellite acquisition pipelines will also be important to reduce delays and maximize operational readiness for emergency response.
