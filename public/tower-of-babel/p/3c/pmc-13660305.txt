A versatile toolset for Staphylococcus aureus gene inactivation enables testing of anti-phage defense systems in native genetic backgrounds
Authors: Łukasz Kałuski, Stephen Amankwah, Laura Wicke, Ján Bíňovský, Emil Stefańczyk, Ivana Mašlaňová, Lucie Kuntová, Roman Pantůček, Małgorzata Łobocka
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundFunctional gene analysis of Staphylococcus aureus across different strains requires the development of genetic engineering tools. In studies on phage therapy, such analysis is especially important for understanding the genomic differences between strains, underlying the differences in sensitivity to phages. Meanwhile, difficulties in introducing DNA constructs for genetic manipulations into non-laboratory S. aureus strains hinder the elucidation of staphylococcal anti-phage defense strategies.ResultsWe used a transducing S. aureus bacteriophage and an E. coli -S. aureus shuttle plasmid with a DNA fragment of this phage to develop a toolset for recombinational gene replacement in difficult-to-transform S. aureus isolates. The plasmid carried an erythromycin-resistance cassette flanked by unique restriction sites for cloning the flanking regions of target DNA to be replaced. Plasmid loss from recombinants with the desired gene replacement was achieved by removing the plasmid par gene, which is essential for plasmid inheritance. Recombinants that acquired the cassette but lost the plasmid were resistant to erythromycin and susceptible to chloramphenicol selective for plasmid backbone. We applied our system to inactivate the predicted anti-phage defense gene avs2 in a clinical S. aureus isolate and inactivated the identical gene in a laboratory strain. While the mutation sensitized the laboratory strain to three phages, it did not affect the clinical strain’s sensitivity to phages, demonstrating the complexity of phage-bacterial interactions and limitations of bioinformatic phage susceptibility predictions without experimental validation. The large terminase subunits of Avs2-sensitive phages were predicted to form complexes with Avs2, suggesting their direct involvement in triggering Avs2.ConclusionsOur new, simple toolset for introducing plasmids carrying donor DNA for genetic manipulation into S. aureus strains that are difficult to transform proved suitable for generating chromosomal gene knockouts in clinical S. aureus isolates. Its use to knock out an exemplary anti-phage defense gene, avs2, in a clinical S. aureus strain and comparative phenotypic analysis of the obtained mutant with that of a laboratory strain revealed differences resulting presumably from different genomic backgrounds of target genes in each strain. This supports the validity of our approach and shows the importance of empirical phage sensitivity testing of strains in native backgrounds.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12934-026-03063-1.

Background

Phage therapy represents a promising alternative to traditional antibiotic treatment, especially for infections caused by antibiotic-resistant bacteria [1, 2]. A major problem with its wider use is the variation in the sensitivity of strains of a pathogenic species to phages [3, 4]. No phage has been described that infects all strains of a given bacterial species. While successful initiation of infection depends on the presence and accessibility of specific bacterial surface receptors that enable phage adsorption and genome delivery [5], general phage metabolic functions are typically compatible with metabolic functions of strains representing a given species [6, 7]. At the same time, it is being discovered that the number and diversity of defense systems protecting bacteria from phage infection in a species pan-genome exceed previous estimations [8–10]. Moreover, bacteria typically encode multiple phage-defense systems [9, 11]. Certain of these systems mutually interact or are inactivated by counteracting systems encoded by the mobilome elements of a given strain [12, 13]. This complex picture is further blurred by the varying abilities of different phages to overcome defense mechanisms [14, 15]. Thus, it is difficult with current knowledge to predict which phage from those qualified for therapy will be the right choice to pick against a given infection without experimental verification of the infecting strain’s sensitivity to the given phage. Typically, activity assays are performed by the introduction of predicted defense genes cloned in a plasmid into a strain that lacks them, and challenging the obtained strain with various phages [13, 16]. This approach allowed the functional verification of numerous bioinformatically predicted anti-phage defense systems of various bacteria. However, while there is an increasing amount of data concerning phage resistance determinants and phages’ ability to overcome them for laboratory bacterial strains, it is unclear how the resistance determinants identified in that way will act in other genetic backgrounds, including clinical bacterial isolates, where they are present in different variants and various combinations.

S. aureus is a pathogen responsible for the largest increase in the number of morbidity and mortality cases from among other bacteria of the ESKAPE group [17]. Certain phages have been proven to be effective in the fight against this bacterium in experimental phage therapies in humans and animals and in compassionate phage therapy (see, e.g. [18–21]. Cases of therapeutic efficacy were demonstrated not only for obligatorily lytic phages but also for certain temperate phages, of which some were used as therapeutics by themselves and some as delivery vectors for targeted bactericidal load [22–25]. Many S. aureus phages, both obligatorily lytic and temperate, are polyvalent and can infect over half of the isolates in S. aureus strain collections [26–28]. However, the infectivity of single phages rarely exceeds 40–80% of the tested strains, dependent on collection, and certain strains are refractory to infection even with cocktails of multiple phages. Thus, the identification of defense systems targeting certain phages in clinical S. aureus strains and testing their anti-phage activity in a native background, in isogenic strains with and without these systems, could help to speed up the right therapeutic phage selection by switching to simple PCR assays to determine the strain’s phage resistance phenotype. The primary challenge is genome engineering of clinical S. aureus isolates to acquire pairs of strains with and without the predicted phage resistance determinant. While laboratory S. aureus strains with their restriction-modification systems inactivated can be transformed with plasmids at a satisfactory efficiency, transformation of environmental or clinical S. aureus isolates is either highly inefficient or impossible [29–32]. Additionally, products of defense system genes are often toxic to bacterial cells when expressed (see e.g. [13, 33–36]) . Thus, cloning of these genes requires their tight repression in target cells prior to challenging these cells with a phage.

A standard method of bacterial gene inactivation is based on the recombinational replacement of a chromosomal gene with its insertionally inactivated version flanked by the regions of homology to the flanks of the target gene, and provided in a donor plasmid [37]. The temperature-sensitive replicon of a donor plasmid used in these methods enables simple screening of recombinants for the presence of an antibiotic resistance cassette in the target gene and the absence of plasmid in recombinant cells upon selection at elevated temperature. However, difficulties in the transformation of non-laboratory S. aureus isolates narrow the use of this method to laboratory strains with main restriction modification system genes inactivated. To overcome this problem, we developed a toolset that allows the simple introduction of a desired plasmid to practically any S. aureus strain [38], and using the introduced plasmid as a source of donor DNA to replace a target gene with an antibiotic resistance cassette. To verify the utility of our system in comparing the phage resistance phenotype of different S. aureus strains with the same resistance marker, we selected a laboratory strain RN4220, representing clonal complex 8 (CC8), and a clinical isolate representing a different CC, and sharing some phage resistance determinants with those of RN4220.

Strain RN4220 is expected to be more susceptible to infections with certain phages compared to its parental strain NCTC8325, and to clinical S. aureus isolates. First, it is depleted of type I restriction-modification system of its parent [39, 40]. Second, it does not contain any prophages and hence any superinfection exclusion genes [41]. Third, it does not produce a capsule, which forms a barrier from infection with certain phages [42]. Fourth, its wall teichoic acid (WTA) is preferentially modified by α-O-GlcNAcylation, which can serve as a receptor for adsorption for certain siphoviruses of serotype B, such as phi11, phi80, and phi52A [43]. However, despite the aforementioned properties, RN4220 appeared to be resistant to infection with six (3A, 3C, 71, 75, 77, and 94) and has the decreased sensitivity to infection with two (83 A and 84) of the 23 typing phages of the International Basic Set [44, 45]. While phages 3A, 3C, and 75, and phages 77 and 84 represent serogroup A and F, respectively [46], phages 71 and 83 A represent serogroup B [47, 48], suggesting the presence of effective anti-phage defense mechanisms unrelated to phage receptor modification, in cells of RN4220.

Plasmids, plasmid isolation and manipulation procedures

Plasmid pMLE5, an S. aureus - E. coli shuttle expression vector that carries a chloramphenicol resistance marker for selection in S. aureus cells and an ampicillin resistance marker for selection in E. coli cells [52], served as the ancestor of plasmids used in this study. Re-sequencing this plasmid revealed that it spontaneously acquired a deletion of repeat-rich 74-bp region preceding the par gene, which was inherited also by its derivatives. The deleted region overlaps two of the three par gene promoters and a part of the pSK1 plasmid-derived centromere-like site, but does not include any sequences involved in the plasmid replication in S. aureus cells (Fig. S1). Plasmid pLKA19 is a derivative of pMLE5 with the reconstructed rop gene of pBR322, incomplete in pMLE5. It was constructed with the use of Gibson assembly (GA) method by assembling the pBR322 plasmid rop gene with its regulatory region (amplified with the OMLO1173 and OMLO1174 primers) with the pMLE5 plasmid core (amplified with the OMLO1175 and OMLO1176 primers). It was enriched with the terS gene of phage ASZ22RN by assembling the phage ASZ22RN terS gene (coord. 1-495 in ON513432 sequence deposited in GenBank), (amplified with the OMLO1177 and OMLO1178 primers) with the pLKA19 plasmid core (amplified with the OMLO1179 and OMLO1180 primers) with the use of GA method to form the pLKA20 plasmid. The pLKA20 plasmid was depleted of its par gene by blunt-end ligation of the ends of the pLKA20 amplicon obtained with primers OMLO1183 and OMLO1198 to obtain the pLKA21 plasmid. To test the influence of par gene and its promoter deletion on the stability of pLKA21 and pLKA21-derived constructs we used for comparative purposes the pMLE5S plasmid, which is a derivative of pMLE5 with the wild-type par regulatory region. It was constructed by the replacement of the pMLE5 Bsp1407-BsaI/Eco31I fragment with the relevant fragment of pMLE5 ancestor plasmid pMLE4, described earlier [52], and was stable, as compared to pLKA21 (Table S1). To reduce the size of pLKA21, we removed from it the expression cassette, including the pro3 and proA promoters, the multicloning site (MCS) and the c1 and bof genes, to form pLKA22, by blunt-end ligation of amplicon ends of the pLKA21 plasmid obtained with primers OMLO1199 and OMLO1200. The pLKA23 plasmid was enriched with the erythromycin resistance gene (erm) and its regulatory region of pAT19 plasmid described previously [53] by the insertion of XbaI-AgeI-digested amplicon of pAT19 erm cassette obtained with primers OMLO1622 and OMLO1623 in place of XbaI-Age fragment of pLKA22. The erm cassette in pLKA23 is flanked by ClaI-AgeI and XbaI-SphI downstream and upstream cloning sites, respectively. The pLKA33 plasmid is a derivative of pLKA23 with cloned 1,009 bp (coord. 1,795,441-1,796,449) and 1,017 bp (coord. 1,800,563-1,801,579) upstream and downstream fragments, respectively, flanking the avs2 gene of RN4220. It was obtained by the insertion of ClaI-AgeI- and XbaI-SphI-digested amplicons of RN4220 DNA obtained by amplification with primers OMLO1675 and OMLO1676, and OMLO1679 and OMLO1680, respectively, and inserted in ClaI-AgeI and XbaI-SphI sites of pLKA23. Various steps of plasmid construction were performed either by the GA method with the use of Gibson Assembly Master Mix (New England Biolabs [NEB] cat. no. E2611) and Q5 High-Fidelity DNA Polymerase with 3’ -> 5’ exonuclease activity (NEB, cat. no. M0491) or with the use of restriction-modification enzymes (Thermo Fischer Scientific Baltics, Vilnius, Lithuania) according to the manufacturers’ protocol. Oligonucleotides used for PCR amplifications in these DNA manipulations and for the verification of plasmid sequence correctness are listed in Table S2. Plasmid isolation, restriction digestions, ligations, PCR reactions, and DNA purification were performed as described before in [38], if not indicated otherwise.

Bacterial growth conditions

Bacteria were grown in Tryptone Soya Broth (TSB; Oxoid) liquid medium or on Tryptone Soya Agar (TSA; Oxoid) as described previously [28]. TSB solidified with 0.7% bacteriological agar (Oxoid) was used for spot tests. The medium was supplemented with Ca2+ ions to a final concentration of 5 mM for experiments with phages. When indicated, antibiotics were added to the medium to the following concentrations: ampicillin 100 µg/mL, chloramphenicol 20 µg/mL, and erythromycin 5 µg/mL.

Plasmid stability determination

Plasmid stability was assayed as described previously, with some modifications [52]. S. aureus RN4220 cells, containing the tested plasmids, were freshly streaked out on TSA plates supplemented with chloramphenicol (20 µg/mL) and incubated at 37 °C overnight. Single colonies were picked and diluted in 500 µL fresh TSB medium. After 1.5 h incubation at 37 °C with shaking (200 rpm), the cultures were serially diluted in TSB, spread onto TSA agar plates without chloramphenicol, and incubated overnight at 37 °C. Colonies that grew were replica plated onto TSA plates with chloramphenicol. Plasmid retention was calculated as the ratio of the number of colonies that grew on an antibiotic-containing plate to the number of colonies that grew on a plate without antibiotic. Calculations were based on colony counts from the progeny of verified carriers of the plasmid. Verification was based on the presence of chloramphenicol-resistant colony-forming units in undiluted suspensions. Each experiment was conducted in five/six biological and three technical repetitions.

Preparation of high phage titer lysate

Cell lysate containing phages was serially diluted (to obtain confluent lysis zone on a plate), and 0.1 mL of such dilution was mixed with 0.1 mL of overnight culture of propagator strain, supplemented with Ca2+ ions, and incubated at room temperature for 15 min. Next, 1 mL of TSB and 5 mL of TSB with 0.7% agar (molten and cooled to ~ 50 °C) were added, and the mixture was poured onto TSA plates. Plates were incubated overnight at 37 °C. Next, 5 mL of TSB was poured onto plates and incubated for ~ 1.5 h at room temperature to allow for phage diffusion into TSB. Finally, the TSB with diffused phages was collected and filtered through syringe filters (Sarstedt AG & Co. KG) of 0.45 and 0.22 µM pore diameter.

Determination of phage host range with a spot test

Sensitivity of bacteria to phages was tested by a spot test. Overnight cultures of S. aureus strains in TSB medium (0.4 mL) were supplemented with Ca2+ ions, gently mixed with 1.5 mL of TSB and 7 mL of molten TSB with 0.7% Difco agar (cooled to ~ 50 °C), overlaid on TSA-containing square plates, and left to solidify. Each phage lysate was serially diluted in TSB to obtain a 109-102 PFU/mL range. Ten microliters of each phage dilution were spotted on the cell layer of each strain. Plates were incubated overnight at 37 °C, and inspected for lysis zones or single plaques.

Isolation of S. aureus mutants with the avs2 genes replaced with the ermR cassette

Plasmid pLKA33 was introduced to RN4220 cells by transformation and to NMI717/05 cells by transduction with phage ASZ22RN, as described previously [38]. Transformants/transductants were selected on TSA medium containing chloramphenicol and erythromycin, purified by re-streaking through single colonies on a similar medium, used to inoculate TSB medium with erythromycin, but without chloramphenicol, and incubated overnight with shaking at 42 °C. After that, the culture was diluted 1:50 in a fresh medium and incubated again overnight with shaking at 42 °C. The culture dilution and growth step were repeated several times. Next, 0.1 mL aliquots of each culture were diluted, plated on TSA medium with erythromycin to obtain single colonies and incubated overnight at 37 °C. The grown colonies were replica plated on TSA medium with chloramphenicol and erythromycin and with erythromycin alone, and incubated overnight at 37 °C. Colonies that grew only on the TSA medium with erythromycin were picked, further purified on erythromycin containing medium, tested again for their chloramphenicol sensitivity, and used as a source of template DNA for further testing by colony PCR, to verify the presence of ermR cassette in place of their avs2 gene.

Alignments, phylogenetic trees and bioinformatics predictions

S. aureus anti-phage defense arsenal was predicted with the use of DefenseFinder, v 2.0, with default parameters (https://defensefinder.mdmlab.fr/; [54–56]. Nucleotide and amino acid sequence alignments were performed with the use of Geneious Prime v. 2025.0.1 (https://www.geneious.com). Protein structure predictions were made with the use of AlphaFold Server (https://alphafoldserver.com/; [57]. The protein phylogenetic trees were calculated with the use of Geneious Prime software with the “Jukes-Cantor” genetic distance model, the “Neighbor-Joining” tree build method, the “Blosum 62” cost matrix, a “12” gap open penalty, and a “3” gap extension penalty. Structure predictions of protein complexes were performed by AlphaFold2 Multimer [58] using default settings. PISA web server [59] was used to calculate interface areas. Structures were visualized using ChimeraX [60–62].

Results

Prediction of anti-phage defense systems in the laboratory S. aureus RN4220 strain

To identify potential anti-phage defense systems of the RN4220 strain, we analyzed its genomic sequence using DefenseFinder. Our search revealed the presence of 8 potential anti-phage resistance determinants (Table 2). Two of them are the methyltransferases and associated specificity determining subunits of the restriction-modification systems of type I (type I R-M), but they lack endonucleolytic subunits, which is consistent with restriction deficiency. The remaining systems: Gcu233, Avs2 (Avast2), FS-Sma, Abi2, and Dodola, represent various kinds of defense mechanisms.

The Gcu233 systems, known also as Brc233, are proposed to function as putative dimeric NADP(H) oxidoreductases depleting cells of NADPH in response to stress [63]. Their genes were identified in mobile integrons of Gram-negative bacteria and shown to protect bacteria from infection by various phages. Avast proteins of various types are nucleoside triphosphatases (NTPases) of the STAND (signal transduction ATPase with numerous domains) superfamily, first found in eukaryotes, where they are part of innate immunity and programmed cell death traits [64, 65]. The Abi2 proteins represent a wide family of bacterial abortive infection systems designated as Abi, which were initially discovered in E. coli, Bacillus, and lactococci [66]. FS_Sma is a MazF-like antiphage system encoded by one gene. It inhibits phage infection and formation of phage progeny [12]. Dodola is a two-protein (DolA and DolB) defense system. Its molecular mechanism of action is unknown, but DolB is predicted to contain a ClpB domain, which is linked with ATPase activity. Dodola was shown to have antiphage activity against temperate Bacillus subtilis phage SPP1 [13].

Construction of a toolset to inactivate defense system genes in difficult to transform S. aureus strains

We previously developed an efficient method of shuttle E. coli -S. aureus plasmid introduction to practically any clinical S. aureus strain with the use of transducing phage ASZ22RN, provided that the phage can penetrate cell envelopes of the target strain and that the plasmid contains a fragment of ASZ22RN DNA [38]. To modify this system to be able to compare the effect of selected defense gene inactivation on phage sensitivity of any other S. aureus strain, we used one of our unstable E. coli - S. aureus shuttle vectors, pLKA22, originally derived from the pMLE5 (see the Materials and Methods section). Compared to pMLE5, pLKA22 has lower copy number in E. coli cells, which decreases the potential toxicity of products of cloned S. aureus DNA to E. coli. It is adapted for its efficient phage ASZ22RN transduction-mediated transfer to various S. aureus strains by the enrichment with the terS gene of ASZ22RN. Additionally, it is depleted of the pMLE5 plasmid expression cassette to decrease its unnecessary DNA load. To facilitate its spontaneous loss from cells, it is also depleted of the par gene with its promoter and a part of centromere-like site derived from the S. aureus pSK1 plasmid core [52, 67] and essential for effective plasmid segregation upon cell division [68]. As expected, the retention of the relevant pLKA22 ancestor with these deletions was significantly decreased compared to a plasmid with the intact par region (41% vs. 87%, Table S1). To enable cloning of PCR-amplified fragments flanking the S. aureus genes to be inactivated by the recombinational replacement, the pLKA22 plasmid was further modified to create pLKA23 by the insertion of the erythromycin resistance cassette (ermR) flanked by ClaI-AgeI and XbaI-SphI restriction sites, and derived from the streptococcal pAT19 plasmid.

Insertional inactivation of avs2 gene in RN4220 and in a clinical S. aureus strain

The avs2 gene was selected as a target for inactivation experiments. It was identified in the genomes of 14 out of 47 S. aureus strains characterized by us previously [38], among them the NMI717/05 strain of CC1 isolated from human sputum. Importantly, the sequences of RN4220 and NMI717/05 avs2 genes and their neighboring regions are identical (Fig. S2). To inactivate the avs2 gene in the RN4220 and NMI717/05 strains, we constructed a derivative of the pLKA23 plasmid, pLKA33, in which the ermR cassette is flanked by the 1017-bp upstream and 1009-bp downstream regions of the RN4220 avs2 gene. The obtained plasmid was introduced into RN4220 cells (by electroporation) and to NMI717/05 cells (by transduction with the ASZ22RN phage). After ~ 10 cycles of refreshing and regrowth of liquid overnight cultures of strains containing pLKA33, cells in which avs2 was replaced with the ermR cassette began to appear, as verified by the ability of colonies of these cells to grow on TSA with erythromycin and their inability to grow on TSA with chloramphenicol. Additionally, the comparison of the amplicon size of DNA from these colonies obtained with primers complementary to the region flanking the avs2 gene, with the amplicons of wild-type strains DNAs, revealed size differences corresponding to the expected differences between the ermR cassette-inactivated avs2 and wild-type avs2 (Fig. S3).

Sensitivity of RN4220 and NMI717/05 strains and their avs2Δ::ermR mutants to various phages

The RN4220 and NMI717/05 strains and their derivatives with the inactivated avs2 gene were tested for sensitivity to 10 S. aureus phages representing different genera and different serotypes, some of them being components of the S. aureus typing set (Table 1).

The wild-type RN4220 and NMI717/05 strains differed in sensitivity to these phages significantly (Fig. 1). Six phages (11, 53, 80, 80α, 85, and 96) infected the RN4220 strain productively, and their infectivity was not influenced by the avs2 deletion. Three phages, 3A, 71, and 77 (Fig. 1A and C), which did not infect the RN4220 strain, infected its avs2Δ::ermR mutant productively, indicating that the Avs2 system of RN4220 is activated by these phages. Moreover, turbid lysis zones could be observed when the concentrated lysates of phages 3A, 71, or 77 (Fig. 1A) were spotted on a layer of wild-type RN4220 cells, which is consistent with the expected activity of the abortive infection systems to which Avs2 has been classified.

The wild-type NMI717/05 strain was resistant to all tested phages, except for phage 80 provided that the titer of phage 80 lysate was high (Fig. 1B, and 1C). It did not become sensitive to phages 3A, 71, and 77 upon the inactivation of the avs2 gene, suggesting that these phages trigger other defense systems of this strain. Neither the wild-type RN4220 and NMI717/05 strain nor their avs2Δ::ermR mutants were sensitive to phage 187, which did not even lyse from without cells of any of these strains or caused their abortive infection (Fig. 1A and B, and 1C). This is consistent with the phage 187 requirement for the unique N-acetyl-D-galactosamine modification of poly-glycerol-phosphate wall teichoic acid for adsorption, absent from RN4220 and NMI717/05 [69]. Surprisingly, phage 96 infected productively the wild-type RN4220 strain, despite the presence of the FS_Sma system, similar to one that was shown previously to cause resistance to phage 96 in a different genomic background [12].

A search for the Avs2 triggering factors of phages 3A, 71 and 77

In enteric bacteria, terminase large subunits (TerL proteins) of certain phages were shown to activate Avs2 [70]. Identity between the large terminase subunit sequences of phages triggering Avs2 of RN4220 ranges from ~ 22% (between phages 3A and 77) to ~ 7% (rest of variants) (Table S3). Additionally, TerL proteins of phages that trigger Avs2 do not group on the same branch of proteomic tree (Fig. S5). Similar diversity was observed in the case of TerL proteins of phages triggering the Avast type 2 system in E. coli, suggesting that the structure rather than the sequence of TerL is a factor activating Avast type 2 defense [70]. Thus, we attempted to model the interaction of RN4220 Avs2 protein with the TerL of phages 71 and 77 (Fig. 2A and B), which trigger, and phages 53 and 80, which do not trigger Avs2 (Fig. 2C and D).

Based on the structural predictions, Avs2 protein potentially forms a heterodimer complex with TerL of phage 71, 77, and 80 with relatively moderate confidence (pTM = 0.69-0-0.72, ipTM = 0.70–0.76). The complex of Avs2 with TerL from phage 53 was predicted with a different mode of TerL binding and low confidence scores (pTM = 0.55, ipTM = 0.23) compared to other three complexes, suggesting an unlikely association of this TerL with Avs2. A noticeable difference in Avs2 binding can be observed in complexes with TerL of phage 71 and TerL of phage 77 compared to the complex with TerL from phage 80. One lobe of the Avs2 TPR domain interacts with both domains of phage 71 TerL and phage 77 TerL (Fig. 2A, B), while the same lobe does not form contacts with the N-terminal ATPase domain of phage 80 TerL (Fig. 2D). To quantify this interaction, we calculated buried surface area between Avs2 and ATPase domain of TerL proteins. In complexes with phage 71 TerL and phage 77 TerL, the buried surface area amounts to 2,614 A2 and 2,528 A2, respectively. In case of phage 80 TerL, its ATPase domain shares 2,083 A2 of the buried surface area with Avs2. Therefore, the ~ 500 A2 difference can be explained by missing contacts of Avs2 TPR lobe with phage 80 TerL ATPase domain. In the predicted Avs2 complex with phage 53 TerL, a shift of TerL ATPase domain away from the Avs2 results in the interaction of only 200 A2.

Discussion

The diversity of phage resistance determinants in bacteria, the limited knowledge of which phages activate each of them, and what is the influence of their genomic background on this activity are among the factors that slow down the introduction of phage therapy to widespread use [71, 72]. To enable the functional studies of phage-defense determinants in native backgrounds of clinical S. aureus strains, we developed in this work a transformation-independent toolset for the replacement of target genes to be tested for functionality with an antibiotic resistance cassette. As an example of our method implementation, we demonstrate its use to inactivate the predicted anti-phage defense gene, avs2, in a clinical S. aureus isolate. Comparative analysis of the obtained avs2 mutants of clinical and laboratory strains with respect to phage sensitivity revealed differences in the phenotype of mutants, supporting the importance of studying phenotypes of mutations in predicted anti-phage determinants in a given strain to determine whether, or to what extent, the identified determinants contribute to that strain’s resistance to a given phage.

As the basis of donor DNA introduction to S. aureus cells to be genetically modified, we used transducing phage ASZ22RN and a shuttle E. coli – S. aureus plasmid containing a fragment of ASZ22RN DNA. The enrichment of rolling-circle replicating plasmids with the DNA fragments of transducing phage was previously shown to increase their transduction efficiency [73–75]. We demonstrated recently that it also dramatically increases the transduction efficiency of plasmids replicating via the theta mode, and that shuttle vectors derived from such plasmids and enriched with transducing phage DNA can be efficiently introduced to clinical S. aureus strains of different clonal complexes [38]. The use of this system for delivering plasmids to S. aureus cells offers several advantages. (i) It allows one to overcome the barrier for DNA exchange between strains of different clonal complexes, which is provided by restriction-modification systems. (ii) The ability of phages (e.g., ASZ22RN) to penetrate cell envelopes of numerous S. aureus strains at DNA injection even if they cannot further propagate in cells of these strains and lyse it expands the range of strains that can serve as the recipients of transduced plasmid [38, 76]. (iii) The predominance of solely plasmid concatamers in plasmid transducing particles of phage, as exemplified in the case of phage ASZ22RN, limits the probability of concomitant phage DNA introduction to cells that received a plasmid [38].

We show here that the transduction-based plasmid delivery method may serve as a part of pipeline for targeted gene inactivation by recombinational gene replacement in S. aureus when used to deliver a donor DNA-carrying plasmid. In our example we focused on the construction of a donor DNA carrier that could be modified to target different S. aureus genes, and easily lost from the cells of recombinants. It is based on a shuttle S. aureus - E. coli vector enabling the preparation of any of its derivatives in a laboratory E. coli strain. Its erythromycin resistance cassette to replace a target gene is flanked by two pairs of recognition sites for the restriction enzymes, providing a simple way to clone any target gene flanks. A potential toxicity of the cloned flank products to S. aureus or E. coli is minimized by using as plasmid components, the low-copy-number S. aureus pSK1 plasmid replicon, and the medium-copy-number E. coli pMB1 plasmid replicon, respectively. Minimizing the vector copy number in alternative hosts additionally allows minimizing the metabolic burden in cells with final plasmid constructs, even if the plasmids contain inserts with large ermR cassette flanks, which may be required for homologous recombination to occur with satisfactory frequency. An additional advantage of our vector is its instability. Shuttle plasmids that were used previously for gene replacements in S. aureus were mostly based on the temperature-sensitive rolling-circle replicating pE194ts plasmid to facilitate plasmid loss from double recombinants by temperature shift [77–81]. They were originally developed for use in Bacillus subtilis [82], but their replication was incompletely shut down in S. aureus at non-permissive temperatures, which prolonged the acquisition of recombinants that lost the plasmid [79, 81]. Here, we show that alternatively, one can use a low-copy-number plasmid replicating via the theta-mode and deprived of the active partition gene, which makes it unstable in S. aureus to begin with. The recognition of cells that lost a donor plasmid but gained the antibiotic resistance marker in a target gene by homologous recombination can be easily achieved by comparing their resistance to antibiotics by replica plating. They will grow only on a medium with an antibiotic selective for donor DNA, but not with antibiotics selective for donor DNA and a donor DNA-carrying plasmid. An additional advantage of this method is the independence of plasmid loss on the incubation temperature of recombinants. This eliminates an undesired S. aureus cell response to temperature stress, which may cause the increased rate of mutation acquisition, as was noted previously [81].

A limiting factor of our method was the frequency of recombination between donor DNA in a plasmid and target DNA in a chromosome. The acquisition of avs2::ermR recombinants that lost the plasmid (CmR) required a culture of cells with a donor plasmid to be passaged to a fresh chloramphenicol-free medium a few times. Most likely the increase in the size of flanks of the target gene in donor DNA could increase the recombination frequency. Consistent with that, we could not replace any of a few tested chromosomal genes with the ermR cassette using ~ 200 bp flanks of the target gene in donor DNA but acquired recombinants using ~ 1 kb flanks as recombination platforms. Flanks within this range of sizes (0.5-1.0 kb) have also been previously shown to be sufficient for successful gene exchange between donor DNA in a plasmid and chromosomal DNA in the laboratory RN4220 strain [78, 83, 84].

Using the developed method, we show here that the resistance of RN4220 strain to the infection with three S. aureus siphoviruses, 3A, 71, and 77, is caused by the activity of the Avs2 system. The sensor domains of enterobacterial Avs2 proteins were shown to recognize phage large terminase subunits (TerLs) or certain other phage proteins of unknown function [70, 85]. Thus, by analogy, in our search for factors of phages 3A, 71, and 77, potentially triggering RN4220 Avs2, we focused on TerLs of these phages as likely candidates. Our models of predicted phage 71, 77, and 80 TerL complexes with Avs2, strongly support this possibility. Predicted complexes of Avs2 with potential triggers, TerL of phages 71 and 77, show a difference in binding interactions compared to Avs2 complex with TerL of phage 80, which did not trigger Avs2 in our experiments. One of the differences concerns the interaction of Avs2 TPR domain, with the TerL ATPase domain, which is in the N-terminal moiety of TerL (Fig. 2). This is consistent with previous suggestions that in the case of enterobacterial Avs2 activated by certain phage TerL proteins, the ATPase domains of these TerLs are responsible for the Avs2 activation [70]. Despite the phage 3A, 71, and 77 ability to trigger Avs2, overall these phages are not closely related, representing different genera of staphylococcal siphoviruses (Triavirus, Phietavirus, and Biseptimavirus) and different subfamilies (Table 2). Moreover, despite that two of these phages (3A and 71) belong to the same lytic group (lytic group II) they are from a different serological group (group A, and B, respectively) [44]. Most likely, the similarities between their TerL structures reflect the mosaic genome structure of staphylococcal siphoviruses, which is the result of horizontal gene transfer driven by the host-mediated selective pressure [86]. A mechanism of horizontal gene transfer leading to the formation of chimeric phages was revealed recently in the case of staphylococcal temperate phages that use pac-mediated packaging mechanism [87]. Triggering the action of certain anti-phage defense systems by large terminase subunits of certain phages could facilitate the establishment of chimeras with new variants of TerL by allowing them to overcome anti-phage defense systems in hosts resistant to their parental phages. Similar mechanism might be responsible also for the exchange of certain other phage genome modules determining host specificity.

We did not expect to find that the avs2Δ::ermR mutant of NMI717/05 strain remains resistant to phages that trigger the Avs2 system in the RN4220 strain, despite the identity of Avs2 proteins and the regions in the direct neighborhood of avs2 genes in both strains (Fig. S1). Our search of the NMI717/05 strain genome with DefenseFinder revealed, that, besides avs2, it can potentially encode three other anti-phage defense systems: Abi2 and two AbiDs (designated by us here as AbiD-1 and AbiD-2) (genome coordinates 380049–381023, 377374–378342, and 403353–404459 bps, respectively). They all represent the Abi_2/AbiD/F protein family of related abortive infection systems [66, 88–90]. The NMI717/05 abi2 gene is separated by only 265 bp from the right border of the staphylococcal pathogenicity island SaPI4/SaPI1028, where the abiD-1 gene is located. Our preliminary attempts to replace the NMI717/05 abi2 gene with the ermR cassette resulted in the replacement of the entire DNA region containing this island and abi2. The resulting double mutant was resistant to phages that trigger RN4220 Avs2 (data not shown). Presumably AbiD-2 of strain NMI717/05 is involved in the resistance of NMI717/05 avs2 mutant to these phages. It shares 28% identity over 77% of its length with the 351-aa AbiD-1 protein of Lactococcus lactis (Prot. ID AAA79209.2) and its homologs, which have been shown to trigger cell death in response to infection by certain siphoviruses infecting lactococci or streptococci [88]. However, whether AbiD-2 of NMI717/05 is active in anti-phage defense requires experimental verification.

An additional defense system of RN4220 predicted by DefenseFinder is FS-Sma. The RN4220 FS-Sma-encoding gene is located on genomic island vSaα (coord. 378,601 − 417,468). A similar system was first detected on the phage-inducible chromosomal island SaPIpT1028 and was originally designated as Sma [12]. The SaPIpT1028 sma gene cloned in a plasmid in the RN4220 strain was activated by phage 96 [12]. However, RN4220 by itself is sensitive to this phage, as shown previously [12] and confirmed in our studies (Fig. 1). It is unclear why the native FS-Sma predicted in RN4220 is inactive against phage 96. The FS-Sma of RN4220 and SaPIpT1028 Sma differ significantly in their amino acid sequence, but the amino acid residues essential for the function of Sma are conserved in FS-Sma (Fig. S6). The predicted structures of both proteins align in their N- and C-terminal regions, but differ in the length of central region, which is significantly shorter in RN4220 FS-Sma (Fig. S7). In that respect, FS-Sma resembles MazF proteins of MazEF systems [12]. Possibly, the regions that differentiate RN4220 Sma from SaPIpT1028 Sma determine the lack of activity of the latter. Alternatively, we cannot exclude that RN4220 Sma and SaPIpT1028 Sma are activated by different phages.

Conclusions

We developed here a simple, transformation-independent toolset for targeted S. aureus gene inactivation. It is based on introducing donor DNA-carrying plasmids to S. aureus by transduction, and using donor plasmids modified to increase their transduction efficiency and to decrease their stability in S. aureus. Our toolset extends the range of S. aureus strains for genomic manipulations to environmental isolates. It provides the possibility to test the phenotypes of mutants in anti-phage defense genes in diversified genomic backgrounds of clinical S. aureus strains. Here, we used it to inactivate the avs2 defense system gene in a clinical strain and compared the resulting mutant with that of a laboratory strain. Based on the properties of the laboratory strain mutant we showed that three out of ten tested staphylococcal siphoviruses of different genera activate the Avs2 defense. Our models of predicted Avs2 complexes with terminase large subunits of activating and non-activating phages suggest that the former may be involved in this activation. The phage sensitivity phenotype of the clinical avs2 strain mutant did not change compared to the wild-type strain, indicating an additional defense system as activating these phages. Taken together, our results show that bioinformatic predictions of phage resistance markers in a given strain—important for the choice of phages for therapy—need to be supported by empirical analysis of phage resistance in the tested strains and relevant mutants before they can be successfully implemented in practice. Such integrated data will be essential for building future databases that enable accurate prediction of phage-resistance profiles across various genomic contexts of predicted markers.
