Prevalence of cyp51A variants in a US collection of Aspergillus fumigatus clinical isolates and comparative analysis of their contribution to triazole antifungal resistance
Authors: Luisa Fernanda Gómez Londoño, Ana Camila Oliveira Souza, Brooks Burt, Sourav Das, Wenbo Ge, Vanessa Meza-Perez, Nathan P Wiederhold, Anang A Shelat, Christina A Cuomo, Jarrod R Fortwendel, P David Rogers
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesTo determine the frequency of cyp51A variants among a contemporary collection of triazole-resistant Aspergillus fumigatus clinical isolates from the USA and compare their specific contribution to reduced triazole susceptibility.MethodsThe genomes of 87 isolates with decreased susceptibility to at least one mould-active triazole antifungal collected between 2007 and 2020 were sequenced. The cyp51A variants were identified and genetically introduced into a cyp51A-null strain. Antifungal susceptibilities for the mould-active triazole antifungals were determined following CLSI guidelines, and variant positions were mapped after protein homology modelling.ResultsMost clinical isolates (60%) carried one of 15 variants that reduced susceptibilities to short-chain (G448S, Y121F, TR46/Y121F/T289A and TR46/Y121F/T289A/N512), long-chain (P216L, F219S and substitutions in the G54 and M220 residues) or multiple (TR34/L98H and TR34/L98H/S297T/F495I) triazoles. While TR34/L98H and TR46/Y121F/T289A were among the most common variants affecting susceptibility, occurring in 13 and 9 isolates, respectively, variants exclusively affecting the coding region and influencing susceptibility were most common, occurring in 31 isolates. A total of 14 isolates (16%) exhibited reduced susceptibility that could not be explained by variants in any known resistance determinants (cyp51A, hmg1 or hapE).ConclusionsOur findings demonstrate the range of mutations affecting triazole resistance among US isolates and provide a much-needed side-by-side comparison of the impact of these variants on the most currently used mould-active triazole antifungals. Moreover, they underscore the extent to which triazole resistance remains unexplained by known genetic determinants and the need for further discovery.

Introduction

Aspergillus fumigatus is a leading cause of invasive fungal infections among immunocompromised individuals. Invasive aspergillosis (IA) is associated with significant morbidity and mortality, with an estimated 2.1 million cases annually and an 85% rate of mortality.1 The triazole antifungals voriconazole and isavuconazole are frontline therapy for treatment of IA2; however, resistance to triazoles has become more prevalent, threatening their utility. Triazoles exert their antifungal activity by competitively inhibiting lanosterol 14α-demethylase, a key enzyme in the ergosterol biosynthetic pathway, resulting in fungicidal activity in this species.3 Although several genetic determinants of triazole resistance in A. fumigatus have been identified, including mutations in the genes encoding HMG-CoA reductase4 and the CCAAT-binding transcription factor complex (CBC) subunit HapE,5 the most prevalent resistance determinant involves mutations in the cyp51A gene, which encodes one of two sterol demethylase enzymes in A. fumigatus.6

Here, we examined a contemporary collection of 87 clinical A. fumigatus isolates from the US that exhibit resistance to at least one of the four mould-active triazole antifungals to better understand the involvement of cyp51A mutations in this process. We identified 24 unique cyp51A alleles among these isolates, assessed their prevalence, expressed each of them in a common genetic background to determine and compare their impact on susceptibility to the four mould-active triazoles and performed molecular dynamics simulations to examine the locations of their mutations and their interactions with triazole antifungals. Our findings provide insight into the frequency of cyp51A variants in US clinical isolates, clarify the contribution of specific cyp51A mutations to triazole resistance in A. fumigatus and underscore the extent to which resistance is unexplained by known genetic determinants of resistance.

Material and methods

Aspergillus fumigatus clinical isolates and strains

A total of 87 A. fumigatus triazole-resistant clinical isolates from the University of Texas Health Science Center Fungus Testing Laboratory and from the Center for Disease Control collected from patients in the USA between 2007 and 2020 were investigated (Table S1, available as Supplementary data at JAC Online). The laboratory strain PyrG+7 was used as a genetic background for the creation of a cyp51A gene deletion strain, which was further used to reintroduce each of the 24 cyp51A variants in the native locus.

A. fumigatus cultures and growth conditions

A. fumigatus conidia were seeded in 25-mm3 cell culture bottles containing agar glucose minimal media (Fisher Scientific) and incubated at 37°C. Conidia were harvested after 48 h. Culture concentration was determined, and a working stock with 2 × 106/mL conidia suspensions was used as a starter for broth microdilution and mycelia cultures.

Whole-genome sequencing of A. fumigatus clinical isolates

Genomic DNA was extracted from A. fumigatus mycelia by using the Quick-DNA Fungal/Bacterial Microprep Kit (Zymo Research). DNA samples were submitted to the Genome Sequencing Facility at the Hartwell Center of St. Jude Children’s Research Hospital for library preparation and whole-genome sequencing on the NovaSeq6000 Illumina platform, targeting 50 million reads. Raw data were deposited to NCBI under BioProject accession numbers PRJNA985736 and PRJNA1301956 (Table S1). Variant calling was performed on Terra.bio by utilizing fungal-variant-call-gatk4 (https://github.com/broadinstitute/fungal-wdl/tree/master/gatk4), which incorporates GATK HaplotypeCaller v4.1.8.1 and uses GCA_000150145.1_ASM15014v1 as the reference genome. Per-sample GVCF files were subsequently combined and genotyped by using CombineGVCFs and GenotypeGVCFs. Variants were selected and filtered via VariantFiltration by using ‘QD < 20.0 ∥ FS > 60.0 ∥ MQ < 40.0. ∥ SOR > 3.0 ∥ FS > 60.0’ and indels ‘QD < 20.0 ∥ FS > 200.0 ∥ QUAL < 30.0’. Genotypes were filtered with a script in this workflow requiring a minimum genotype quality of <50, a per cent alternate allele of <0.8 or a depth of <10. For phylogeny generation, the output VCF file from the variant-calling pipeline was run through a custom script (https://github.com/broadinstitute/broad-fungalgroup/blob/master/scripts/SNPs/vcfSnpsToFasta.py) with parameters –max_amb_samples set to 10 (10% of max number of samples). Maximum likelihood phylogenies were built by using RAxML v8.2.12, with parameters ‘-f a (rapid bootstrap analysis and search for best scoring ML tree) -N 1000 (bootstrap replicates) -m GTRCAT (Substitution Model)’.8 Phylogenies were then visualized and annotated by using the iTOL website.9

CRISPR-Cas9-mediated transformation of A. fumigatus

The list of oligonucleotides used in this study can be found in Table S2. A diagram describing the gene-editing approaches is also provided (Figure S1). To investigate the contribution of each cyp51A variant to triazole susceptibility, we first generated a cyp51A gene deletion mutant in the susceptible laboratory strain background PyrG+,7 which is a prototrophic ΔakuB strain. For that, CRISPR RNAs (crRNA) were designed to target upstream (cyp51A 5′ crRNA) and downstream (cyp51A 3′ crRNA) regions of the cyp51A ORF. A repair template containing the phleomycin-resistance cassette (PhleoR) was amplified from the pAGRP vector10 by using the primers P1 and P2, which also incorporated a 35-bp homology sequence in the cyp51A ORF flanking region. A. fumigatus protoplasts were obtained, and gene editing was achieved by using an in vitro-assembled CRISPR/Cas9-mediated system, as described elsewhere.11,12 After confirmation by PCR screen using three primer sets (Reactions A: P3/P4; B: P3/P6 and C: P4/P5), the Δcyp51A strain was further used as the recipient background strain for reintroduction of the clinically derived cyp51A variants.

Prior to reintroduction in the Δcyp51A strain, each cyp51A allele (including 497 bp of upstream and 330 bp of downstream sequences flanking the ORF) was amplified from gDNA of a representative clinical isolate (Table S1) by using primers P3 and P7, which incorporated the HindIII and BcuI restriction sites, and cloned into the vector pCR-HygR11 containing a hygromycin-resistance cassette (HygR). The resulting plasmid was used in the amplification of a microhomology repair template (using primers P8 and P9) containing the cyp51A sequence and HygR, which was used to reintroduce the various mutated alleles back into the Δcyp51A strain. For that, two crRNAs targeting both ends of PhleoR (PhleoR 5′ crRNA and PhleoR 3′ crRNA) were used. A. fumigatus transformation proceeded as described above, and confirmation of mutants was performed by PCR screen using three primer sets (Reactions A: P3/P4; B: P3/P11; and C: P4/P11), followed by nanopore sequencing of PCR Reaction A to confirm the presence of the variant.

Antifungal susceptibility testing

Antifungal susceptibility to mould-active triazoles (voriconazole, isavuconazole, itraconazole and posaconazole) was assessed by using broth microdilution assays following the CLSI methodology.13 A suspension of A. fumigatus conidia was added to achieve a final concentration of 2 × 104 conidia/mL in each well, and the final drug concentrations tested ranged from 0.0625 to 32 µg/mL.

Real-time qPCR for cyp51A gene expression

To evaluate the impact of cyp51A mutations on gene expression, real-time qPCR was performed. Mycelia from cyp51A mutants were obtained by incubating 2 × 105 conidia in 10 mL of RPMI at 37°C and 250 rpm for 24 h. RNA extraction was performed by using the RiboPure Yeast kit (Invitrogen).14 cDNA synthesis was performed with 500 ng of RNA using the RevertAid First Strand cDNA Synthesis kit (ThermoScientific). Then, qPCR was conducted with the SsoAdvanced™ Universal SYBR Green Supermix (Bio-Rad), using the primers P2/P13 for cyp51A amplification, and P14/P15 for tubA amplification, which was the housekeeping gene used for data normalization. Gene expression changes were calculated by using the 2−ΔΔCT method, with the cyp51A allele from CEA10 serving as the control. Experiments were performed in triplicate with technical duplicates, and statistical analysis was conducted by performing one-way ANOVA with Tukey’s multiple comparisons test in GraphPad Prism 10.

Protein homology modelling

The fasta sequence for the sterol 14-alpha demethylase cyp51A OS = Aspergillus fumigatus (strain ATCC MYA-4609/CBS 101355/FGSC A1100/Af293)15 was used to generate a homology model in MOE16 from the template structure of Cyp51B (PDB code: 4UYM17). The two sequences shared an identity of 65.3%. The position of haeme in the Cyp51A structure was derived by aligning the Cyp51B structure from 4UYM with the homology model. The positions of voriconazole, isavuconazole, posaconazole and itraconazole were obtained by aligning structures from 4UYM, 6UX0,18 6E8Q19 and 5V5Z,20 respectively, to the homology model, resulting in four complexes. The resulting complexes were then minimized in Maestro21 with the converge heavy atoms to RMSD set to 0.3 Å in the Protein Preparation Wizard.22 A TIP3 water box was set up around each complex and minimized before a molecular dynamics simulation of 500 ns was run at 300K to obtain the final structure as described in Nishiguchi et al.23

Results

Genetic relatedness, triazole antifungal susceptibilities and prevalence of known genetic determinants of triazole resistance among resistant clinical isolates

The genomic dataset from a collection of 87 clinical Aspergillus fumigatus isolates was queried to identify genetic variants in Afu4g06890 (cyp51A; Figure 1a, see web-only Table S1). These clinical isolates displayed reduced susceptibility to at least one of the four mould-active triazole antifungals, and were collected after voriconazole, itraconazole and posaconazole were approved to treat aspergillosis. A phylogeny of the isolates indicated two distinct clades with no correlation to triazole resistance phenotype (Figure 1b). Although alterations in intronic and untranslated regions and synonymous mutations were observed, only single-nucleotide variants resulting in amino acid substitutions were investigated here. Altogether, 24 unique Cyp51A sequences were found: 3 isolates carried the same Cyp51A sequence as the triazole-susceptible strain Af293, used as the reference genome for whole-genome sequence analysis; 2 isolates carried the T248N and E255D substitutions; the remaining 83 isolates harboured five amino acid substitutions (Y46F, V172M, T248N, E255D, K427E) observed in the triazole susceptible strain CEA10, with or without additional mutations (Table 1). Relative to CEA10, 68 isolates had mutations in cyp51A, 28 of which also carried a mutation in Afu2g03700 (hmg1). Eight isolates carried mutations in hmg1 but not in cyp51A, and one has a mutation in both hmg1 and Afu6g05300 (hapE). No Afu7g03740 (cyp51B) variants unique to resistant isolates were found. The mutations observed in cyp51A have been previously reported, although a direct contribution to triazole resistance has not been previously established for all of them.

Effects of cyp51A alleles on triazole antifungal susceptibilities and gene expression

To verify the impact of the cyp51A variants on triazole susceptibility, we introduced the clinically derived cyp51A alleles into a triazole susceptible cyp51A-null background strain and measured the resultant susceptibilities to the mould-active triazoles voriconazole, isavuconazole, itraconazole and posaconazole (Figure 2). CLSI MIC breakpoints for voriconazole (1 mg/L: intermediate resistance; ≥2 mg/L: resistance)24 and isavuconazole (2 mg/L: intermediate resistance; ≥4 mg/L: resistance)25 as well as approved (itraconazole, >1 mg/L)24 and suggested (posaconazole, >0.25 mg/L)26 epidemiologic cut-off values were used to categorize the cyp51A strains into the following interpretive categories: resistant to short-chain (voriconazole and isavuconazole) triazoles (which included strains with isavuconazole MIC at 2 mg/L and voriconazole MIC≥2 mg/L, as recommended by CLSI)25; resistant to long-chain (itraconazole and posaconazole) triazoles; or resistant to three or four triazoles (multi-resistant).

Among the most common cyp51A alleles was TR34/L98H, observed in 11 of the 87 resistant isolates, which resulted in increased resistance to all four triazoles tested (Table 1, Figure 2). Two additional isolates carried the TR34/L98H/S297T/F495I allele, which led to higher MICs for isavuconazole and itraconazole but a slightly less-pronounced effect on voriconazole susceptibility (Table 1, Figure 2).

The TR46/T289A/Y121F combination allele was observed alone in 9 isolates, one of which carried the N512I substitution. These alleles resulted in dramatic increases in voriconazole and isavuconazole resistance, with only a modest effect on posaconazole susceptibility (Table 1, Figure 2). No effect could be attributed to the N512I substitution. Importantly, the mutation leading to the Y121F substitution was found alone in one isolate and conferred resistance to voriconazole and isavuconazole, with a less pronounced effect on MICs than the TR46/T289A/Y121F allele, and no influence on posaconazole susceptibility.

Another common variant was the mutation leading to the G448S substitution found in 12 resistant isolates (Table 1, Figure 2). This mutation conferred resistance to voriconazole and isavuconazole but had only a modest effect on itraconazole and posaconazole susceptibility. The combination of G448S with A9T, found in one isolate, had no greater impact on triazole susceptibility than did G448S alone (Table 1, Figure 2).

Mutations at the M220 position were also common, with 5 isolates having the M220I variant and 5 having the M220K variant (Table 1). Both mutations resulted in resistance to itraconazole and posaconazole but have little to no effect on susceptibility to voriconazole and isavuconazole (Figure 2). One of the isolates that carried the M220I substitution also carried the I242V substitution, which appears to have a negligible effect on triazole susceptibility.

Four isolates had mutations at the G54 residue: one leading to the G54E substitution; one, to the G54W substitution; and two, to G54R substitution (Table 1). Although these lead to resistance to itraconazole and posaconazole, they also result in a slight hypersusceptibility to voriconazole and isavuconazole (Figure 2). A similar but less prominent phenotype was observed for a strain carrying the P216L substitution, which occurred in 3 isolates. One isolate had a mutation leading to the F219S substitution, resulting in resistance to itraconazole and posaconazole.

Some of the cyp51A variants analysed here had little to no effect on triazole susceptibility. Alleles carrying the G434S (in combination with A9T, present in one isolate), G138S (3 isolates), G138C (1 isolate) or M263I substitutions (3 isolates) had only a slight impact on triazole MICs, comparable to the phenotype of the strain carrying the reference allele. One isolate carried the I367F substitution, displaying a modest but reproducible increase in voriconazole and isavuconazole MICs without appreciable effect to itraconazole or posaconazole susceptibility (Table 1, Figure 2). Although A9T and I242V substitutions have been reported in triazole susceptible isolates, we investigated their effect on triazole MICs because of their co-occurrence with resistance-associated mutations. Neither A9T nor I242V substitutions, when present individually, affected triazole susceptibility. The MIC values for these mutants were highly reproducible and not different from those of the control allele.

Changes in cyp51A gene expression were evaluated for all mutants by using RT-qPCR. As expected, only strains carrying the tandem repeats in the cyp51A promoter region presented significantly higher (P < 0.0001) levels of gene expression (see web-only Figure S2). Conversely, none of the other mutations had an appreciable effect on cyp51A expression.

Molecular mapping of Cyp51 variant positions

To assess the structural basis of resistance, we generated a novel Cyp51A homology model and aligned the structures of voriconazole, isavuconazole, posaconazole and itraconazole to it (Figure 3). Modelling revealed the locations of residues affected by mutation. Broadly, four classes of mutations affected activity: (i) those near the haeme group; (ii) those lining the binding site close to the haeme; (iii) those lining the binding site close to its entrance; and (iv) those further away from both the binding site and the haeme (see Figure 3). Haeme is shown in green; voriconazole is shown in blue; isavuconazole is shown in aqua; posaconazole is shown in green; and itraconazole is shown in purple.

Among the amino acid substitutions that caused short-chain triazole resistance were Y121F, T289A and G448S (Figure 2). The Cyp51A residue Y121 interacts with the carboxylic group of haeme and with voriconazole and isavuconazole via water bridges (Figure 3). The Y121F substitution leads to loss of these interactions. The residue T289 is near voriconazole (3.7Å) and isavuconazole (3.1Å), indicating direct interaction with these ligands. Amino acid position G448 is 3.7Å from the haeme, and the G448S substitution is expected to directly affect the haeme orientation.

Multi-azole resistance was caused by Cyp51A variants containing the promoter TR34 and the amino acid substitutions L98H, S297T and F495I (Figure 2). The L98 residue does not interact directly with any of the four triazoles or the haeme (Figure 3). Meanwhile, the amino acid position S297 is at a closer distance of 3.7Å to itraconazole and is expected to interact with it directly. In addition, S297 is only 4.1Å from isavuconazole, indicating a possible interaction via neighbouring residues such as F495. Amino acid position F495 is near isavuconazole (3.5Å) and itraconazole (3.7Å), so mutations in this residue are expected to disrupt interactions with both ligands.

Susceptibility to long-chain inhibitors such as posaconazole and itraconazole was exclusively affected by mutations in class C because their long chains are near the mutations at the entrance of the binding site (Figures 2 and 3). The residue M220 is expected to directly interact with itraconazole (3.7Å) and interacts with posaconazole (3.4Å) via a water bridge. Therefore, mutations in this position are expected to alter the interaction of Cyp51A with both long-chain triazoles. Similarly, the position G54 forms a hydrogen-aryl interaction with both itraconazole and posaconazole and is at a distance of 3.6Å and 3.5Å, respectively, suggesting that any mutation in this residue would disrupt these interactions. Position F219 makes a pi edge-to-face interaction with posaconazole and is 3.7Å from itraconazole. Although it was not observed to make a pi edge-to-face interaction as with posaconazole, the residue still interacts directly with both ligands, and those interactions would be expected to be disrupted upon mutation. Finally, the residue P216 is near posaconazole (3.3Å), indicating direct interaction with the ligand. Mutation of this residue may affect the integrity of adjacent helices on which other residues that interact with posaconazole sit, such as F219 and M220.

Discussion

Alterations in the coding sequence of cyp51A, alone or in combination with tandem repeats in its promoter region, are the most reported cause of triazole resistance in A. fumigatus clinical and environmental isolates. Multiple studies have genetically characterized the contributions of cyp51A variants to triazole susceptibility, with few examples analysing the effect of more than one variant simultaneously. Here, we provide an unprecedented side-by-side comparison of over 20 cyp51A variants in a common genetic background, allowing for direct and quantitative evaluation of their specific contributions to susceptibility to mould-active triazoles without the interference of genomic variability.

We confirmed reported phenotypes for several of the Cyp51A variants analysed in this study and, for the first time, assessed their susceptibility to isavuconazole. Overall, the Cyp51A variants analysed here led to 3 potential clinically relevant triazole resistance phenotypes: short-chain (TR46/Y121F/T289A, TR46/Y121F/T289A/N512I, Y121F, G448S),27–31 long-chain (G54 and M220 mutations, P216L and F219S)32–36 and multi-triazole resistance (TR34/L98H and TR34/L98H/S297T/F495I).37–39 No impact on triazole susceptibility was observed by the substitutions A9T, I242V, and a slight increase in MICs displayed by strains carrying the cyp51A reference sequence (derived from Af293) and T248N/E255D substitutions over that of the control allele (CEA10-derived) containing the Y46F/V172M/T248N/E255D/K427E substitutions, as suggested in the literature.40

The contributions of the Cyp51A amino acid substitutions G434S, M263I and I367F for azole susceptibility were first assessed in this study. The G434S and M263I alterations generated a mild MIC increase, corroborating previous observations showing that heterologous expression of cyp51AG434C in a hypersusceptible Saccharomyces cerevisiae strain,41 as well as reversion of M263I in two pan-triazole resistant A. fumigatus clinical isolates had no effect in triazole susceptibility.42 The I367F substitution had a greater impact on short-chain azole MICs, although not enough to increase MICs to CLSI clinical resistance breakpoints.

Alterations at residue G138 have been widely reported and associated with multiazole resistance in both clinical and environmental A. fumigatus isolates.41,43–45 This residue is predicted to lie near the ligand access channel and to participate in triazole docking.36 However, in our analyses, four independently generated mutants carrying either the G138S or G138C substitutions exhibited only a modest (2-fold) increase in triazole MICs, remaining below established clinical resistance breakpoints and epidemiological cut-off values. Consistent with these findings, Umeyama et al.45 reported a similar increase in voriconazole (3-fold), itraconazole (3-fold) and posaconazole (1.5-fold) E-test MICs in G138S mutants, while Cao et al.44 observed no MIC changes in two of five G138S mutants relative to the parental strain. Collectively, these data challenge the long-held assumption that G138 substitutions alone are sufficient to drive clinically meaningful triazole resistance. Further illustrating this inconsistency, correction of the G138S mutation in a highly resistant isolate substantially reduced MICs in the study by Umeyama et al.,45 whereas Rybak et al.42 observed no decrease in voriconazole or itraconazole MICs following reversion of a G138C mutation. Such variability across studies may stem from differences in genetic manipulation strategies, strain backgrounds and antifungal susceptibility methods. It is also plausible that G138 substitutions require the presence of additional resistance determinants to exert a significant phenotypic effect. Taken together, our findings do not support the conclusion that Cyp51A G138S or G138C substitutions alone constitute relevant clinical triazole resistance mechanisms in Aspergillus fumigatus.

Voriconazole and isavuconazole are currently first-line therapy for the most typical cases of infections caused by A. fumigatus. In the collection of isolates described here, 77% displayed clinically relevant resistance to short-chain triazoles, and 48% showing resistance to the remaining azoles. Although patient outcomes are unknown, there is a high likelihood of therapy failure if the standard of care was applied in these cases. This exemplifies the potential utility of molecular diagnosis to predict therapy failure in cases of resistant infections, contributing to a more relevant therapeutic decision-making in such cases. The much-needed side-by-side comparison of different cyp51A genotypes in a common strain background provided by this study can potentially be used to guide triazole selection during resistant infections in the clinical setting.

It is noteworthy that fourteen clinical isolates studied here carried no variants in cyp51A, hmg1, or hapE that could explain their azole resistant phenotypes, including 6 isolates with decreased susceptibilities to all four mould-active triazoles. In parallel, as pan-triazole resistance was exclusively observed in Cyp51A laboratory strains carrying the TR34/L98H variant, this variant was only detected in 10 out of 42 pan-triazole-resistant isolates. From the remaining 32 pan-triazole-resistant isolates, 15 harboured mutations in cyp51A and/or hmg1 that alone would not account for this phenotype. These observations strongly indicate that additional determinants of resistance are in play in these isolates. Efflux pump upregulation, cyp51B overexpression, and mutations in mitochondrial and other CCAAT complex genes are plausible contributors to resistance and should be considered as potential mechanisms, as well as novel resistance determinants that remain to be elucidated. This study underscores the need for a more comprehensive understanding of the determinants of clinical triazole resistance in A. fumigatus.
