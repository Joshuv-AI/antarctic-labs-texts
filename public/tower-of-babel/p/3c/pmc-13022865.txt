Bioluminescent Probes for Multiplexed RNA Imaging
Authors: Erin B. Fuller, Kyle H. Cole, Lila P. Halbers, Christelle E. T. Chan, Kevin K. Ng, Lorenzo Scipioni, Christoph Gohlke, Michelle A. Digman, Oswald Steward, Jennifer A. Prescher, Andrej Lupták
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
A comprehensive understanding of RNA biology requires methods to visualize transcripts and their interactions within physiological environments. However, few technologies can report on multiple RNAs continuously and in living systems. We are addressing this void with bioluminescent probes. Bioluminescence does not require excitation light and can enable sensitive, noninvasive readouts in a variety of settings. Here, we report a panel of structured RNA tags and luciferase fragments (RNA lanterns) for multiplexed RNA detection. The lanterns emit few photons on their own but assemble and emit light when brought into proximity by the RNA tags. Three orthogonal tag–lantern combinations were designed based on known RNA-binding proteins that recognize distinct targets. The probes were optimized in vitro for selective transcript detection. We further applied the tags and lanterns in cultured cells, achieving multitarget imaging with good subcellular resolution. Collectively, the probes expand the toolkit for RNA imaging and will facilitate efforts to trace RNA targets in living systems.

Introduction

RNA dynamics are fundamental to many cellular processes, including organismal development, memory formation, and other events. − Distinct transcripts are often trafficked to specific cellular locations to carry out their function. Some mRNAs localize based on the proteins they encode. − For other mRNAs, differential localization may be related to stability. Several nonprotein-coding RNAs, such as certain long nonprotein-coding RNAs (lncRNAs) and microRNAs (miRNAs), are enriched in the nucleus where they regulate gene expression. ,

Capturing the location and movements of RNAs is a critical step toward a full understanding of organismal biology. Conventional methods (e.g., in situ hybridization) can provide snapshots of RNA localization, typically in fixed samples. However, such techniques cannot produce readouts on dynamics, resulting in an incomplete picture of RNA trafficking and interactions. Dynamic imaging can be achieved with optical probes, most of which rely on fluorescent tools. For example, CRISPR-Cas targeting, aptamer tagging, and hybridization-based approaches have been used to direct fluorophores to transcripts of interest. − Another well-known approach relies on bacteriophage MS2 and PP7 coat proteins (MCP and PCP). MCP/PCP fused to split fluorescent proteins can bind MS2/PP7 aptamer tags, producing a signal in response to a given RNA target. − While these reporters have been used extensively for RNA imaging, they have some notable limitations. Fluorescence readouts require excitation light, which can contribute to cellular toxicity and photobleaching over extended periods of time. , Several copies of the MS2/PP7 aptamers are also necessary to achieve adequate signal-to-noise ratios, resulting in large tags that could interfere with localization, dynamics, and downstream functions. ,, Capturing the dynamics of a broader set of RNAs thus requires new tools.

To fill this gap, we and others are developing bioluminescent platforms for RNA imaging. , Bioluminescence features light production via the oxidation of a luciferin substrate by a luciferase enzyme. No external illumination is required, so continuous imaging is possible in both live cells and animals. We recently showcased the potential for bioluminescent RNA tracking, harnessing a split version of NanoLuc luciferase (NanoBiT). , The strategy features MS2 and PP7 aptamers that recruit MCP and PCP-tethered NanoLuc fragments (termed RNA lanterns). The aptamers were engineered into a single tag that can be appended to transcripts of interest. The tag also includes a Ni-NTA aptamer to rigidify the structure, enhance NanoLuc complementation, and allow biochemical validation of the complexes. This first-generation RNA lantern enabled low-background imaging of transcripts in live cells and rodents.

Here, we report an expanded series of RNA lanterns and structured tags for multitranscript imaging based on distinct RNA-binding proteins (RBPs) and their respective orthogonal RNA targets. The RNA motifs were designed using secondary structure modeling and systematically optimized to achieve turn-on bioluminescence in the presence of corresponding split luciferases. The lanterns were validated using model transcripts outfitted with the engineered tags. The orthogonal probes were easily distinguished both in vitro and within live cells. Finally, two distinctly localized RNA transcripts, NEAT1 lncRNA and β-actin mRNA, were imaged simultaneously within the same cells. Collectively, this work expands the RNA lantern system and sets the stage for multiplexed dynamic imaging in a range of biological systems.

Results and Discussion

Design of Orthogonal RNA Lanterns

As a starting point for generating orthogonal binders, we focused on RBPs (beyond MCP and PCP) that recognize unique RNA structures. We envisioned that substituting a single aptamer within the RNA tag (in this case, PP7) could achieve multiplexed transcript detection. Corresponding RBPs would be tethered to the LgBiT portion of NanoBiT. Because the MS2 aptamer is part of each RNA tag, an MCP-SmBiT fusion would be common to each lantern. Multicolored readouts could be achieved via bioluminescence resonance energy transfer (BRET), by inserting a fluorescent protein between the LgBiT and RBPs. ,, The resulting fusions would form a functional BRET reporter upon recognition of the target aptamers.

We also desired rigidified RNA tags, because such sequences were found to promote long-lasting complementation of the RNA lanterns. Thus, individual aptamers were constructed into four-way junctions using an established Ni-NTA aptamer as one of the arms (Figure A). ,

The new RNA tags were designed using ViennaRNAfold and Forna, , with boxB or kink-turn (k-turn) motif sequences used in place of PP7. The boxB motif is recognized by the Nun protein of bacteriophage HK022 and has been used previously for live cell RNA imaging. ,− The aptamer is small (15 nucleotides) and thus minimally perturbing to tagged RNAs of interest. Additionally, the Nun peptide binds boxB with nanomolar affinity (K D = 46 nM), which is on par with the affinities of MCP and PCP for their corresponding aptamers.

The k-turn was similarly desirable for RNA tag development. We targeted the box C/D k-turn (HMKT) and the evolved CS2 aptamer for the RNA tag designs: both sequences are recognized by the archaeal ribosomal protein L7Ae with picomolar dissociation constants (K D = 21 pM and 5.7 pM, respectively), making them ideal for RNA imaging. −

In parallel, we designed orthogonal lanterns to recognize the selected RNA aptamers. To evaluate the feasibility of the design, the lanterns (RBPs fused to SmBiT and LgBiT) were crudely modeled in PyMOL by aligning published structures (Figure B). Simple replacement of the PCP with alternative RBPs appeared possible based on the modeled orientation of the boxB/k-turn, relative to the MS2 aptamer. In addition to comprising orthogonal RBPs, the final RNA lanterns were designed to exhibit three distinct colors of luminescence based on the assembled proteins: blue (NanoBiT alone), yellow (NanoBiT fused to VenusΔC12), or red (NanoBiT fused to mScarlet-I, Figure A). ,, Venus and mScarlet-I exhibit red-shifted emission spectra, which can be beneficial for imaging in a variety of biological environments. Energy transfer from NanoLuc to these fluorescent proteins has also been previously optimized.

We showed that the three reporters are readily distinguished using standard emission filters on imaging rigs (Figure S1). In the modeled structures, a generic fluorescent protein was inserted at the N-terminus of LgBiT to mimic the BRET fusion. The fluorescent protein was predicted to increase the necessary spacing between the aptamers in the RNA tags. Nonetheless, the models suggested that NanoBiT complementation was feasible, with the newly designed RNA tags bridging the gap between the RBPs in both lanterns (Figure B). We therefore settled on a blue MCP-PCP lantern, a yellow MCP-Nun lantern, and a red MCP-L7Ae lantern for initial assessments.

Initial Evaluation of the Orthogonal RNA Lanterns

The new designs were evaluated by expressing the lanterns in HEK293T cells and transcribing the RNA tags in vitro. To test for complementation, cells were lysed and bioluminescence was measured upon addition of various concentrations of the RNA tags (0–10 nM). The MCP-Nun lantern exhibited signal above the no-RNA control, with a fold change of ∼14 in the presence of 1 nM RNA (Figure B and Figure S2). The MCP-L7Ae lantern exhibited luminescence in the presence of just 50 pM of the RNA tag (Figure B and Figure S2). The variation in optimal RNA concentration was likely due to differences in binding affinity between the RBP-aptamer pairs and expression levels of individual protein components. ,, Comparing the two k-turn aptamer sequences, HMKT and CS2, brighter bioluminescence was achieved with the CS2 aptamer (Figure S3). Thus, the MS2-CS2 tag was used going forward. We also observed that the signal outputs from the new lanterns were reduced compared to the originally optimized MCP-PCP design. This result was likely due to suboptimal complementation, or to the oligomerization of MCP and PCP coat proteins, which typically form dimers and higher oligomers that could enable more efficient NanoBiT complementation. In either case, additional engineering of the RNA tags would be necessary to enhance signal output.

The alternative RNA lanterns and tags were also selective and readily multiplexed. In a mixture of HEK293T cell lysates transiently expressing the three probe constructs, addition of each structured RNA tag resulted in distinct bioluminescence emission spectra (Figure C and Figure S4). The relative intensity values varied (likely due to differences in lantern expression), but the emission spectra were readily resolved. Each lantern complemented the NanoBiT fragments only in the presence of its cognate RNA tag and no cross-reactivity was apparent. The observed orthogonality suggested that the lanterns would be able to discriminate tagged RNA transcripts in cells; however, the varied brightness levels indicated a need to optimize the new RNA lanterns.

Optimization of the RNA Tags

To optimize signal turn-on for the lanterns, we systematically tuned the structured RNAs. A series of RNA tags was generated with varying numbers of nucleotides (nts) between MS2 and boxB/CS2, modulating the spacing and orientation between the aptamers. Such modifications influence the complementation of SmBiT and LgBiT and thus impact signal production. A similar strategy was previously used to improve the blue MCP-PCP lantern signal production by several hundred-fold.

For the MCP-Nun lantern, the tags were designed with base-paired spacers between the MS2 and boxB aptamers (M-X-B, where X is the number of additional nucleotides). The panel sampled two complete helical turns between MS2 and boxB (M-1-B through M-43-B), with a single helical turn rotating the aptamers 360° with respect to one another. The tags were transcribed in vitro and added exogenously to HEK293T cell lysate expressing the MCP-Nun lantern. Light emission was measured and compared among tag designs to determine the optimal structure. Relative luminescence varied based on lantern expression across three biological replicates, but overall RNA tag preference remained consistent (Figure A and Figure S5). Changes in signal over background were similar between each helical turn, and as the phase angle between the aptamers increased, the signal increased, but only to a point. Within the first full turn of an RNA helix (M-1-B through M-21-B, corresponding to a change of 20 nts, or 10 base pairs), maximum signal was achieved with seven intervening nucleotides (M-7-B, which has a predicted phase angle of ∼108°). Within the second helical turn (M-23-B through M-43-B), maximum signal was observed with M-29-B at a similarly predicted angle, exactly 360° greater than M-7-B. M-29-B dramatically increased bioluminescence in vitro with a ∼220-fold signal enhancement compared to a no-RNA control (Figure A and Figure S5). Additionally, an RNA tag with a mutated boxB aptamer (M-3-B mut ) produced nearly undetectable bioluminescence (Figure A and Figure S5), indicating that binding to both aptamers within the RNA tag is essential.

In the case of the MCP-L7Ae lantern, varying light outputs were also observed with increasing spacer lengths (M-X-C, Figure B and Figure S6). Peak luminescence was achieved with M-35-C, resulting in >100-fold turn-on. M-7-C also produced a strong signal, providing a more compact design option. Additionally, luminescence was abrogated with a mutant tag, M-3-C mut (Figure B and Figure S6).

For both new lantern systems, many tags with more than a single helical turn between the aptamers resulted in higher signal compared to more compact structures, in contrast to the original MCP-PCP probe. Longer aptamer spacing is likely needed to accommodate the additional bulk of the fluorescent proteins present in the new lanterns. However, when the larger RNA tags (M-29-B and M-35-C) were tested in live cell cultures, little bioluminescence was produced (Figure S7). It is possible that these tags are unstable in cells or more quickly degraded due to their long double-stranded regions, although further experiments are needed to confirm this model. Since the shorter motifs (M-7-B and M-7-C) produced bright signal in mammalian cells (Figure S7), we moved forward with these tags.

We next characterized the in vitro response of the RNA lanterns to the optimized tags. We aimed to test the hypothesis that the orthogonal motifs could be used in tandem across a range of RNA concentrations (picomolar to nanomolar) without cross-reactivity. We evaluated the newly optimized M-7-B and M-7-C tags and included the M-7-P tag for comparison. The structured RNAs were added individually at varying concentrations (0–100 nM) to HEK293T cell lysate expressing the split luciferase fragments. Each RNA lantern exhibited an increase in bioluminescence over background only in the presence of its cognate tag, with the nontarget tags resulting in near-zero relative luminescence (Figure C and Figures S8 and S9).

Bioluminescent outputs also varied depending on RNA lantern expression and the ratio of RNA tag to RNA lantern (Figure C and Figure S10). At low concentrations of RNA, less lantern complementation was observed and the background signal scaled with overall protein expression. The signal increased as the RNA concentration reached an optimal ratio with the RNA lantern. Further increases in RNA concentration resulted in reduced signal, though, likely due to each half of the RNA lantern binding separate transcripts (known as a “hook effect”). Peak bioluminescence was therefore observed when the lantern components and structured RNAs were present at similar levels. A similar hook effect was observed in live cells when probe concentrations were varied (Figure S11). The decrease in bioluminescence observed at higher tag concentrations in this context could also be due to limited transfection efficiency with high quantities of plasmid DNA. Overall, though, the RNA lanterns and tags were optimized in vitro for subsequent application to live cell imaging.

Multiplexed RNA Imaging Using Orthogonal Lanterns in Live Mammalian Cells

We initially tested the lanterns in live cells using a model transcript encoding a fluorescent protein, TagRFP657 (RFP). RFP mRNA was outfitted with two of the orthogonal tags (M-7-B and M-3-P) in the 3′ untranslated region (UTR, Figure A). In these experiments, HEK293T cells expressing the RNA lanterns were transiently transfected with DNA encoding the tagged RFP mRNA and RFP fluorescence, which could be resolved from the fluorescent proteins comprising the RNA lanterns, served as a readout for tagged RNA presence based on mRNA translation. Corresponding lanterns (a yellow MCP-Nun and a red MCP-PCP lantern) were selected for tandem imaging, and cell lines stably expressing the constructs were generated. Since the lanterns included fully functional fluorescent proteins (VenusΔC12 and mScarlet-I), fluorescence emission could be used to gauge probe expression. The tagged RNAs (RFP-M-7-B and RFP-M-3-P) were introduced into lantern-expressing HEK293T cells (Figures S12–S15). Bioluminescence and fluorescence imaging were performed using an ultracooled EMCCD camera. The background signal in the absence of the RNA tags was minimal for both lanterns, though more apparent with MCP-Nun. The higher level could be due to multiple factors, including higher lantern expression and Venus-LgBiT aggregation resulting from the known weak dimerization of Venus fluorescent protein.

We further imaged the lanterns in tandem to test their ability to distinguish orthogonally tagged transcripts in live cells. Cocultures of HEK293T cells stably expressing either the yellow MCP-Nun or the red MCP-PCP probe were transiently transfected with DNA encoding RFP-M-7-B, RFP-M-3-P, both transcripts, or neither. Importantly, M-7-B expression resulted in luminescence corresponding to the yellow MCP-Nun lantern cells, while M-3-P expression resulted in luminescence corresponding to red MCP-PCP lantern-expressing cells. The presence of both RNA tags resulted in target-specific luminescence of both colors (Figure B and Figure S16). Small areas of background signal corresponding to the MCP-Nun RNA lantern were observed in all cases, including the no-RNA sample. The dim background was easily distinguished, though from the bright, diffuse signal indicating RNA lantern complementation. Bulk cell measurements further indicated that bioluminescence and RFP fluorescence intensities correlated with mRNA expression (Figure S17). On a single-cell basis, though, the bioluminescent outputs varied and did not always correlate with RFP fluorescence. We attribute these results, in part, to an RNA-dependent hook effect. Differences in transfection efficiency on a per-cell basis, along with subtle differences in the z-position of the cells, also influence the signals observed. Quantifying RNA levels in a given experiment thus requires optimizing the expression levels for all lantern components and benchmarking the bioluminescent signals with known standards. Overall, though, orthogonal RNA-tagged transcripts could be readily differentiated when expressed in a mixed population of cells.

We also applied the RNA lanterns to multiplexed imaging of subcellular transcript localization. Multiplexed bioluminescence imaging has been historically challenging, given the broad and overlapping emission spectra associated with luciferase-luciferin probes. Separating colors via conventional optical filtering is further complicated by the time involved. Bioluminescent signals decay over time, so any lag in photon capture (e.g., from switching between filters) can lead to erroneous results. To circumvent these issues, we recently developed a new method for continuous multiplexed imaging known as bioluminescent phasor analysis. , The approach relies on imaging through sine and cosine emission profile filters, referenced to an unfiltered channel. The filtered bioluminescence is then converted at each pixel into spectral phasors using an optical Fourier transform.

Importantly, all wavelengths are detected simultaneously, in a single frame, with no filter switching required. The platform thus enables time-lapse imaging with dramatically enhanced multiplexing capabilities and is compatible with conventional brightfield and fluorescence microscopy.

Bioluminescent phasor analysis was used to image multiple RNAs with orthogonal tags and lanterns. We chose NEAT1 and β-actin as our initial targets, as they are biologically relevant transcripts known to traffic to different locations within cells: NEAT1 is a long nonprotein-coding RNA that is known to localize within substructures of the nucleus due to its affinity for the P54nrb nuclear protein, whereas β-actin mRNA is primarily found throughout the cytoplasm where it is processed by ribosomes and translated into protein (though it may also be present in the nucleus, where it is generated). , Each RNA of interest was labeled in its 3′ region with an orthogonal RNA tag, yielding NEAT1-M-7-B and β-actin-M-3-P constructs (Figure A). In parallel, HEK293T cells were engineered to stably express the yellow MCP-Nun RNA lantern. These cells were then transfected with plasmids encoding both tagged RNAs and the blue MCP-PCP lantern.

Bioluminescence, fluorescence, and brightfield images were captured using a custom optical setup for bioluminescent phasor analysis. The signal acquired at each pixel was transformed into points on a two-dimensional plot and false colored based on the phasor coordinates (Figure S18). As a result, clear differences in localization were observed. The blue MCP-PCP lantern exhibited luminescence throughout the cell corresponding to β-actin mRNA distribution, whereas the MCP-Nun lantern showed nuclear signal corresponding to NEAT1 localization (Figure B and Figures S18 and S19). Additionally, while the β-actin signal was observed in all cells, NEAT1 was clearly visible only in the brightest cells. This outcome was likely the result of differences in RNA target and probe expression among the cells, further underscoring the need for careful optimization of experimental parameters. Nevertheless, distinct MCP-Nun lantern signal in the brightest cells indicated subnuclear NEAT1 localization that was resolved from β-actin mRNA. Similar patterns of transcript localization were observed in separate populations of cells (S20–S21) and when using the MCP-L7Ae RNA lantern (Figures S22–S24). These results demonstrate the utility of the RNA lanterns for multiplexed RNA imaging with subcellular resolution.

We also aimed to determine if the RNA tags would perturb the degradation of target transcripts. We compared the half-life of a tagged RNA target, mCherry−β-actin 3′ UTR–M-3-P, to its untagged counterpart. The tagged and untagged RNAs were transcribed in vitro and incubated in HEK293T cell lysate expressing the MCP-PCP RNA lantern for varying periods of time. The transcript concentrations were measured by quantitative RT-PCR and an appreciable difference in degradation rate was not observed, but bioluminescence measurements indicated that the RNA lantern complex was stabilized for ∼1 h (Figure S25). It should be noted that the tags may affect each RNA target differently. Introducing a non-native tag on the RNA sequence has the potential to alter RNA behavior, including localization and degradation rates. We anticipate, though, that the small size of the RNA tags (65–83 nts) will be minimally perturbing.

Conclusions

A complete understanding of RNA dynamics in living systems has been hindered by the limited availability of tools for multiplexed live cell imaging. Commonly used methods require external illumination, which poses challenges in live cells and tissues. Bioluminescence, which does not require excitation light, is well-suited for long-term imaging applications in both cells and whole organisms. With this in mind, we developed three orthogonal RNA lanterns and corresponding tags for multiplexed imaging using multicolored bioluminescence.

The RNA lanterns and structured tags were optimized for sensitive detection of target transcripts. The lanterns feature a split luciferase (NanoBiT) readout combined with fluorescent protein fusions for resolvable multicolored emission via BRET. Orthogonal RBPs were implemented within the lanterns for simultaneous recognition of distinct RNAs. We determined the RNA lantern design to be highly modular, therefore requiring minimal probe optimization to incorporate the alternative RBPs. At the same time, we systematically tuned the structure of the RNA tags to achieve optimal NanoBiT complementation and thus sensitivity. We also determined the tags to be completely orthogonal to one another, with no detectable cross-reactivity.

The lanterns were applied to imaging multiple RNAs at the same time in live mammalian cells. Biologically relevant transcripts were readily observed using bioluminescent phasor analysis, with RNAs in the nucleus clearly distinguished from transcripts in the cytoplasm. We anticipate that the RNA lantern platform could be further expanded to include any number of orthogonal RBP-aptamer pairs.

Future work will include efforts toward multiplexed imaging of endogenous RNA transcripts. Genetic manipulation of RNA targets would be necessary to introduce the RNA tags. We anticipate that endogenous RNA imaging would require careful sequence analysis to position the tags. Optimization of RNA lantern expression compared to the transcript of interest would likely also be necessary. Overall, enhanced multiplexing capacity of the RNA lanterns will enable detailed visualization of dynamic RNA processes in vitro and in living systems across scales.
