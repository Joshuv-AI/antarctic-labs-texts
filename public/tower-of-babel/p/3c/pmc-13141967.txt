Lipoprotein(a) lipidome and diet: responses to reducing saturated fat intake in African Americans in a randomized trial
Authors: Munkhtuya Myagmarsuren, Hayley G Law, Wei Zhang, Heejung Bang, Lauren M Bishop, Tong Shen, Oliver Fiehn, Kristina S Petersen, Lars Berglund, Byambaa Enkhmaa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractLipoprotein(a) [Lp(a)] is a genetically determined cardiovascular risk factor. Additionally, Lp(a) levels are affected by dietary saturated fat (SFA) reduction. We previously reported an Lp(a) increase in response to SFA reduction in both white and black cohorts. However, less is known whether diets impact Lp(a)’s oxidized phospholipids (OxPL) and lipid components. We assessed responses of Lp(a)-OxPL concentration, Lp(a)-OxPL subspecies abundance, and the Lp(a)-lipidome to SFA reduction [from 16% energy with the average American diet (AAD) to 6% energy with a DASH-type diet] in 166 African-Americans. Responses by variability in Lp(a) levels and apolipoprotein(a) [apo(a)] sizes were tested. Mean age was 35 years; 70% were women; mean BMI was 28 kg/m2. Median Lp(a)-OxPL total concentration did not differ although particle concentration of four OxPL subspecies, in particular ALDOPC, decreased from AAD to the DASH-type diet (P = 0.001). Of 440 Lp(a) lipids annotated, 87 species (20%) responded significantly to SFA reduction, with major changes in phosphatidylcholine, sphingomyelin, and alkylphosphatidylcholine classes. A marked Lp(a)-lipidome difference between diets was observed for participants with higher (≥50 mg/dl) but not with lower (<50 mg/dl) Lp(a) levels. The Lp(a)-lipidome response varied across apo(a) sizes; small apo(a) (≤22 Kringles) carriers exhibited a greater decrease (80%) than an increase (20%) in lipids, while the opposite was the case for carriers of apo(a) sizes >22 Kringles. In conclusion, dietary SFA reduction in African-Americans resulted in significant changes in the Lp(a)-lipidome with differences by Lp(a) levels and apo(a) sizes. The findings support a dynamic intraindividual nature of the Lp(a)-lipidome and an impact of metabolic regulation.Supplementary key words: Lp(a), dietary fat, lipidomics, oxidized lipids, phospholipids, saturated fatty acids, oxidized phospholipids, Lp(a) lipidomics, DASH-type diet

Elevated lipoprotein(a) [Lp(a)] is an independent, causal, and prevalent risk factor for atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (CVD), where plasma levels guide CVD risk estimation (1, 2). Molecular properties of Lp(a), including its oxidized phospholipids (OxPL) content, contribute to its atherogenicity. Beyond a strong genetic control (3), the Lp(a) level is influenced by some non-genetic environmental factors (4). In controlled metabolic feeding studies, replacement of dietary saturated fats (SFA) with other macronutrients is associated with increased Lp(a) levels (5, 6, 7, 8). Previously, we reported a significant 24% increase in Lp(a) level following a dietary SFA reduction in a large cohort of African Americans in the GET-READI (The Gene-Environment Trial on Response in African Americans to Dietary Intervention) trial (7). African Americans exhibit substantially elevated Lp(a) levels compared to other population groups (9, 10, 11), and therefore dietary modulation may increase the risk burden associated with elevated Lp(a) levels. Furthermore, these previous findings suggest that Lp(a) is subject to metabolic regulation across diverse population groups (5, 6). However, evidence regarding the effect of diet on Lp(a) risk properties, including OxPL and/or other Lp(a) lipidome properties, remains limited. Addressing this gap, in a recent study in predominantly white cohorts, we demonstrated that dietary SFA reduction modulated the Lp(a) lipidome with an enrichment in proinflammatory triacylglycerols (TGs) and diacylglycerols (DGs) (12). For the first time, it was shown that the Lp(a) lipidome is a diverse, dynamic entity subject to metabolic regulation and responsive to environmental change.

The goal of the present study was to extend our understanding of dietary modulation of Lp(a) risk properties by addressing several research gaps. First, we aimed to examine the effect of SFA reduction on Lp(a) lipidome components in African Americans. Second, we sought to clarify whether the variability in the Lp(a) lipidome response patterns to dietary SFA reduction would differ depending on Lp(a) levels using a common Lp(a) cut-off for risk estimation (50 mg/dl) (1, 2). Third, we aimed to test whether genetic variability in apolipoprotein(a) [apo(a)] size polymorphism [a key genetic determinant of Lp(a) plasma levels] (9) would modulate changes in Lp(a) properties during dietary SFA reduction. To achieve these goals, we conducted a comprehensive assessment of the response patterns to dietary SFA reduction in Lp(a)-OxPL concentration as well as in individual OxPL subspecies abundance and of the Lp(a) lipidome profile for participants in the GET-READI study. The present study contributes to advancing our understanding of the metabolic impact on Lp(a) of a common dietary intervention recommended for CVD risk reduction at the population level (13).

Materials and methods

Study participants

The detailed study design of the GET-READI trial has been reported previously (7, 14). Briefly, the trial recruited 166 African Americans at the Pennington Biomedical Research Center (PBRC). African American families with two or more adult siblings, with at least one sibling having an LDL-C above the 50th percentile (adjusted for age, race, and sex), were eligible to participate. Exclusion criteria included age <18 and >45 years, LDL-C ≥200 mg/dl, triglycerides ≥500 mg/dl, and blood pressure >160 mmHg systolic or >95 mmHg diastolic. Additional exclusion criteria included documented presence of chronic disease (atherosclerotic, renal, hepatic, endocrine, gastrointestinal, or other systemic diseases, diabetes mellitus), taking antihypertensive medication, BMI ≥ 40 kg/m2, for women pregnancy, breast feeding, or being <6 months postpartum, history of drug/alcohol abuse, depression or mental illness, and chronic use of over-the-counter medications, including nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, laxatives, and antacids. The study followed the Declaration of Helsinki Principles and was approved by the Institutional Review Boards of the Pennington Biomedical Research Center and the University of California, Davis. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants (7). The participant recruitment process is shown in Supplemental Figure S1 (7), and the present report is based on the data of 166 individuals who completed both diet periods.

Intervention diets

Details of the intervention diets have been reported previously (7) and the diet composition is shown in Supplemental Table S1. Briefly, each participant was fed two diets: an Average American Diet (AAD) containing 16% kcal from SFA, and a diet similar to the Dietary Approaches to Stop Hypertension diet (henceforth referred to as the ‘DASH-type’ diet) with 6% kcal from SFA (7). Intervention periods were five weeks each, with a 1-week break between the diet periods. The order of interventions was randomized and only study foods were permitted during the feeding phase of the trial. Calorie levels were held at a weight maintenance level and dietary cholesterol was held constant throughout the intervention periods. Compliance checks included meal tray inspections, diet diaries, and measurements of the urinary potassium, sodium, and magnesium levels, which were higher following the DASH-type diet compared with the AAD (7).

Determinations of Lp(a) levels, apo(a) isoform sizes, and total oxidized phospholipids bound to Lp(a) in plasma samples

Plasma samples collected during the final fifth week of each diet period, stored at −800C, were used for analyses. Due to the study design, baseline samples were not available. Lp(a) levels were measured with a rate nephelometry assay at the PBRC clinical chemistry laboratory (7) and apo(a) isoform sizes expressed as Kringle (K) repeats were determined by Western Blotting as in our previous studies (14). Concentrations of oxidized phospholipids bound to Lp(a) [Lp(a)-OxPL total concentration] were measured using an immunoassay protocol (15). Briefly, the E06 antibody, which binds to non-native OxPL via the phosphorylcholine (PC) head group (16, 17, 18), was incubated overnight with MagnaBind Goat Anti-Rabbit, IgG, magnetic beads in a 50:1 ratio, forming a complex with the E06 antibody (19). This complex was incubated with plasma samples, capturing non-native, PC-containing OxPLs carried on all lipoproteins. Using a magnetic device, the E06-bead-OxPL complex was separated and collected in a sample buffer. At this stage, the sample contained OxPLs present on all lipoproteins. This sample was transferred to a plate coated with an anti-human LPA antibody to selectively capture and detect OxPLs carried on Lp(a) and analyzed as in our previous studies (15).

Lp(a)-bound oxidized phospholipids subspecies and Lp(a) lipidome analyses

We isolated Lp(a) by immunoprecipitation in stored (−800C) plasma samples from the final week of each diet period (AAD and the DASH-type diet) as in our previous studies (20). Briefly, plasma samples were incubated overnight with goat anti-rabbit IgG MagnaBind beads conjugated with rabbit anti-human Lp(a) antibody (Thermo Fisher Scientific). After separation using a magnetic device, the isolated Lp(a) was collected in 100 μl cold methanol with an added pre-chilled antioxidant, 0.01% (w/v) butylated hydroxytoluene and stored at −80°C until further analyses. Lp(a) levels determined in the isolated samples showed a strong positive correlation with plasma Lp(a) concentrations (12). A predetermined set of four major OxPLs containing phosphocholine (OxPC), including 1-palmitoyl-2-(9′-oxo-nonanoyl)-sn-glycero-3-phosphocholine (ALDOPC), 1-palmitoyl-2-(5′-oxo-valeroyl)-sn-glycero-3-phosphocholine (POVPC), 1-palmitoyl-2-azelaoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphocholine (PAzPC), and 1-palmitoyl-2-glutaryl-sn-glycero-3-phosphocholine (PGPC) (21) was analyzed using a Q-Exactive HF (Thermo Fisher Scientific) high-resolution liquid chromatography tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS). These OxPL subspecies were selected due to their associations with Lp(a)/apo(a) (19, 22) and contributions to Lp(a) atherogenicity via enhancing arterial inflammation (21, 23).

Lp(a) lipidome analyses were conducted in isolated Lp(a) samples as in our previous study (12). Briefly, 15 μl were aliquoted from each sample to create a pool quality control (pool QC). The remaining 85 μl of sample and pool QC was dried in a centrivap. Prior to LC-MS/MS analysis, the lipid extract was resuspended in 50 μl of MeOH:Toluene (9:1, v/v) containing internal standard mix [Ultimate SPLASH™ ONE, Avanti Polar Lipids, 1:250 diluted]. Lipids were separated on a Waters ACQUITY Premier BEH C18 Column, 1.7 μm, 2.1 x 50 mm, by a gradient from 15% to 99% of mobile phase B over 5 min at 65°C at a flowrate of 0.8 ml/min. Mobile phase A was 60:40 (v/v) acetonitrile-water with 10 mM ammonium formate and 0.1% formic acid; mobile phase B was 90:10 (v/v) isopropanol-acetonitrile with the same modifier. We used positive electrospray ionization mass spectrometry on a Q-Exactive HF mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific), acquiring accurate mass in 120–1700 m/z range at 60,000 mass resolution, using capillary voltage +3.5 kV and nitrogen gas temperature 380°C. Tandem mass spectra were acquired by top2 data-dependent acquisition. For OxPC targets, we optimized all parallel-reaction monitoring conditions with commercial standards of POVPC, PGPC, PAzPC and ALDOPC. Raw data accurate mass MS/MS files were processed by AWS computing-based LC-BinBase pipeline for peak picking (24). Recursive data analysis was used to search and replace missing values from raw data files. Data processing by LC-BinBase enabled searching all public mass spectral libraries, including MS-DIAL, LipidBlast, NIST23, MassBank.us, Agilent and GNPS. We obtained annotated lipids by matching accurate mass, diagnostic fragments from the tandem mass spectrum, and LC retention time. Samples were injected in a randomized order. The pool QC was injected periodically to correct for technical variations. A five-point calibration curve of the four PCs, ranging from 1 to 100 ng/ml was injected and used to quantify the four OxPCs. We estimated the OxPC subspecies per unit Lp(a) by dividing each of the four OxPC mass in isolated Lp(a) sample by the corresponding concentration of isolated Lp(a), expressed as grams per unit of Lp(a) (g/U) (i.e., particle concentration) (12).

Statistics

Standard descriptive statistics were used to summarize data with mean and standard deviation (SD) or median and interquartile range (IQR) for continuous variables and frequency and proportion for categorical variables. The median and IQR were used to describe non-normally distributed data. For data comparisons, non-normally distributed data were square-root transformed prior to analysis. We used two-tailed, paired Student’s t test for differences between two diet periods. To test the effects by variability in Lp(a) levels, we dichotomized the cohort into two groups using the 50 mg/dl cutoff based on the AAD Lp(a) level. Thus, responses were analyzed by higher (>50 mg/dl) and lower (<50 mg/dl) Lp(a) groups. Additional analyses were conducted within each of the apo(a) size groups. We calculated the fold change (FC) in Lp(a) lipid species as: FC = (median DASH-type diet/median AAD). FCs were transformed using log2 transformation to simplify data interpretation (25) as positive normalized log2 transformed FC (log2FC) values indicate an increase, negative log2FC values indicate a decrease, and log2FC values equal to zero indicate no change in abundance between the two diet periods. Principal component analysis (PCA) was conducted using all annotated lipids from a cube root-transformed, auto-scaled dataset. PCA plots were generated using MetaboAnalyst 6.0. Pearsons correlation tests were used to assess the relationship between changes in Lp(a) levels and the 10 most significantly altered lipid species. All P-values from the lipidomic data analyses were adjusted using Benjamini and Hochberg false discovery rate correction (26). P-values of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. SAS version 9.4 was used for analyses (SAS Institute).

Results

Participant characteristics

Characteristics of the 166 GET-READI participants have been described previously (7). Briefly, the mean age (±SD) of the cohort was 35 ± 11 years, 70% were women, the average BMI was 28 ± 5 kg/m2 and average diastolic and systolic blood pressure were 75 ± 7 mmHg and 118 ± 10 mmHg, respectively (Supplemental Table S2). The median (IQR) Lp(a) level was 44 (22; 80) mg/dl and 58 (28; 93) mg/dl at the end of the AAD and the DASH-type diet periods, respectively (P < 0.0001) (Table 1). Accordingly, the Lp(a) level increased by 11 ± 11 mg/dl (mean ± SD), corresponding to a significant 24% increase (P < 0.0001) with the DASH-type diet (7).

Responses of total concentration and individual subspecies’ abundance of Lp(a)-bound oxidized phospholipids to dietary saturated fat reduction

The median (IQR) Lp(a)-OxPL total concentrations were 6.1 (4.7; 8.3) U/L and 6.3 (4.4; 8.6) U/L at the end of the AAD and the DASH-type diet period, respectively, with no significant difference between the two intervention diets (Table 1). In contrast, when normalizing Lp(a)-OxPC subspecies concentrations to Lp(a) levels, i.e. expressing particle concentrations, there were significant reductions for ALDOPC (P < 0.0001) and the sum of 4 OxPC subspecies (P = 0.001) concentrations from the AAD to the DASH-type diet (Table 1).

We further tested whether the responses to SFA reduction in Lp(a)-OxPL would differ by lower (<50 mg/dl) (n = 94) versus higher (≥50 mg/dl) (n = 72) Lp(a) levels [dichotomized based on the AAD Lp(a) level]. Consistent with the findings for all participants, concentrations of ALDOPC and the sum of four OxPCs decreased significantly from the AAD to the DASH-type diet for both the lower and higher Lp(a) level groups (Table 2). Notably, a marked ∼4-fold elevation in the sum of the four Lp(a)-OxPC subspecies concentrations was observed for the lower Lp(a) group compared to the higher Lp(a) group during both intervention diets (AAD median: 0.56 vs. 0.13 g/U; DASH-type diet median: 0.45 vs. 0.11 g/U, respectively) (Table 2). These findings suggested that Lp(a) particle enrichment with OxPLs was higher for participants with lower Lp(a) levels compared to those with higher Lp(a) levels. Notably, this pattern was unaffected by the diet intervention.

In addition, Lp(a) levels increased significantly by 25% (P = 0.002) and 18% (P = 0.003) in the lower and higher Lp(a) groups, respectively, following the DASH-type diet relative to the AAD (Table 2).

Lp(a) lipidome responses to dietary saturated fat reduction in all participants

Our Lp(a) lipidome analysis annotated 440 individual lipid species across 20 lipid classes. The major lipid classes were TG (n lipid species = 90), sphingomyelin (SM) (n = 83), phosphatidylcholine (PC) (n = 74), alkylphosphatidylcholine (PC O) (n = 73), lysophosphatidylcholine (LPC) (n = 25), ceramide (Cer) (n = 16), DG (n = 14), acylcarnitine (CAR) (n = 11), and alkylphosphatidylethanolamine (PE O) (n = 11). Of the 440 lipid species annotated, 87 species (20%) showed significant changes in their abundance in response to the DASH-type diet relative to the AAD (Table 3). In terms of the direction of change, 45 lipid species (52%) increased, while 42 lipid species (48%) decreased significantly. As seen in Table 3, a majority of the significantly changed lipid species belonged to four lipid classes: PC O (22 species), SM (22 species), PC (19 species), and Cer (6 species).

When assessing the 10 most significantly changed lipid species in abundance during the DASH-type diet relative to the AAD (based on the degree of fold change), we found that lipids in PC and PC O classes were the most common species regardless of their direction of change (Fig. 2) (Table 4). Thus, the 10 most increased lipid species included 4 PCs (PC 40:1, PC 40:2, PC 42:7, and PC 42:2), 2 PC Os (PC O-44:4 A and PC O-44:8), 1 SM, 1 TG, 1 LPC, and 1 CAR (Fig. 2A). An up to ∼2-fold enrichment was observed for PC 42:7, PC 42:2, and PC 40:1, while the two PC O’s (PC O-44:4 A and PC O-44:8) abundance increased by ∼1.5 fold in response to SFA reduction (Table 4). The 10 most significantly decreased lipid species were dominated by 4 PC Os and 2 PCs, followed by 2 SMs, 1 Cer and 1 alkenylphosphatidylethanolamine (PE P) (Fig. 2B). Interestingly, in contrast to the increased PC and PC O species, the species that decreased had a shorter chain length, e.g., PC O-30:0, PC O-30:1, PC O-33:1, PC O-35:4, PC 28:0, and PC 33:1B (Table 4, Fig. 2B).

Lp(a) lipidome responses to dietary saturated fat reduction by higher versus lower Lp(a) levels

We next assessed whether the Lp(a) lipidome response to dietary SFA reduction would differ by variability in Lp(a) levels, i.e., by lower (<50 mg/dl) (n = 94) and higher (>50 mg/dl) (n = 72) Lp(a) level groups. PCA score plots of the Lp(a) lipidome showed a distinct separation pattern between the higher versus lower Lp(a) groups during both intervention diets, i.e., the AAD (Fig. 3A) and the DASH-type diet (Fig. 3B). To further explore this finding, we also tested the effect of SFA reduction within each Lp(a) level group. A separation of the Lp(a) lipidome between the AAD and the DASH-type diet was observed in the higher Lp(a) group (Fig. 3D), but less so in the lower Lp(a) group (Fig. 3C). These findings suggested that participants with an elevated Lp(a) primarily drove the overall Lp(a) lipidome response to a reduced SFA intake.

Lp(a) lipidome responses to dietary saturated fat reduction across the apo(a) size spectrum

To explore the potential role of apo(a) sizes in modulating the Lp(a) lipidome response, we dichotomized all participants [n = 165, excluding 1 participant without any detectable apo(a) band] into three apo(a) size groups: small (≤22K4) (n = 26), medium (23−27K4) (n = 69), and large (≥28K4) (n = 70). For carriers of double apo(a) isoform sizes, we used the kringle repeats corresponding to the smaller apo(a) size.

The total number of significantly changed lipid species in the first 10 lipid classes, along with the proportion of increased versus decreased lipids within each of these lipid classes for the three apo(a) size groups are shown in Table 3. While a total of 87 lipid species consistently and significantly responded to SFA reduction in all three groups, the response patterns differed across the lipid classes. Overall, the response pattern for the medium and large apo(a) size groups was consistent and differed from the response pattern in the small apo(a) size group. Specifically, the latter group was characterized by a higher prevalence of decreased (80%) versus increased (20%) lipid species, with more lipids decreased in particular for the PC, PC O and SM classes (Table 3). An overall opposite direction of response pattern for these lipid classes was observed for the medium and large apo(a) size groups.

The 10 most significantly altered lipid species across the three apo(a) size groups are shown in Fig. 5 and Supplemental Table S5. Notably, 4 PCs and 1 LPC increased in all three apo(a) size groups, 2 PC Os increased in the medium and large apo(a) size groups, and 3 TGs increased only in the small apo(a) size group (Fig. 5A) (Supplemental Table S5). The only DG species (38:4) that was among the 10 most increased lipid species in the lower Lp(a) level group was also among the 10 most increased lipid species in the large apo(a) size group (Fig. 5A). The composition of the 10 most significantly decreased lipid species across the three apo(a) size groups was dominated by SMs (n = 4 species) and PC Os (n = 2) in the small apo(a) size group, by PC Os (n = 4) and TGs (n = 3) in the medium apo(a) size group, and by PC Os (n = 3) and PCs (n = 2) in the large apo(a) size group, respectively (Fig. 5B) (Supplemental Table S6). Of note, 2 PC Os, 1 PC and 1 SM species decreased commonly in all three apo(a) size groups (Fig. 5B). Interestingly, while several TG species decreased in the medium and large apo(a) size groups, no TG species was present among the 10 most decreased lipids in the small apo(a) size group (Fig. 5B).

Discussion

While insights into the Lp(a) risk properties during diet interventions are limited, the present study represents the first effort to comprehensively characterize intraindividual changes in the Lp(a) lipidome following a randomized controlled dietary intervention in African Americans. Notably, the large cohort size as well as the overall higher Lp(a) levels in this group allowed us to systematically map heterogeneity in the Lp(a) lipidome responses over a wide range of Lp(a) levels as well as over a spectrum of apo(a) sizes.

Specifically, our novel findings demonstrated that a reduced dietary SFA intake in African Americans resulted in significant changes in the Lp(a) lipidome as well as in the OxPL spectrum. While overall Lp(a) concentrations of OxPL did not differ between the two intervention diets, there was a significant decrease in the Lp(a) particle concentration of the most frequent OxPC species. This was largely due to a decrease in the Lp(a) concentration of ALDOPC, the most abundant subspecies (21). Interestingly, ALDOPC (or PONPC) was shown to be the most abundant OxPL species in vessel wall specimens obtained during clinical interventions (27). Notably, the increase in Lp(a) levels in response to the DASH-type diet may explain the apparent discrepancy of a lack of difference in the overall Lp(a)-OxPL concentrations, while the Lp(a)-OxPC particle concentration decreased. Thus, more circulating Lp(a) particles would serve to offset the impact of a lower Lp(a)-OxPC particle concentration. These findings suggest an overall consistent OxPL burden in spite of a reduced Lp(a) particle atherogenicity.

More pronounced changes were seen for the broader Lp(a) lipidome profile, where 20% of all annotated individual lipid species varied significantly across diets. These findings closely mirrored the findings in largely white cohorts of the two DELTA (Dietary Effects on Lipoproteins and Thrombogenic Activity) studies (12), indicating that these observations go beyond any racial groups. However, we also observed some notable differences between these studies in the degree of change in specific lipid classes. Specifically, while changes in the PC, PC O, and SM classes were most common in the present study, TG species showed the most pronounced changes in the DELTA studies (12). It is well established that plasma TG levels are lower in black compared to white populations, and the present study cohort reflected this pattern with a median TG concentration of 71 mg/dl. This could have contributed to the lower frequency of TG species among the most significantly altered lipids in GET-READI compared with the DELTA studies (12). It is of interest to note that TG changes might largely reflect modifications of the hydrophobic core of the Lp(a) lipid entity, while PC and PC O changes would likely reflect modifications of the lipid entity surface involving the hydrophobic-hydrophilic boundary. These findings suggest a potential remodeling of the Lp(a) lipid portion in response to diet, with possible differences across population groups.

Some similarities between the two studies were noted as well. In the DELTA studies, the response of TG species was consistent in both DELTA 1 and 2 trials, with an increase in the abundance of longer-chain polyunsaturated TG species, whereas the abundance of shorter-chain unsaturated TG species decreased (12). We noted some similarities in the present study as the most significantly increased PCs and PC Os had longer chain polyunsaturated fatty acids, while the most significantly decreased PCs and PC Os had shorter chain monounsaturated or saturated fatty acids. Taken together, these observations suggest a consistent change in the fatty acid pattern irrespective of whether the lipid class was TG or PC/PC O. In this context, it is noteworthy that recent metabolomic studies have demonstrated an association between short-chain—more unsaturated fatty acids in TG and aging and indicators of frailty (28) as well as with an increase in metabolic disorders (29). The pattern change in fatty acid chain length and number of double bonds found in the present study, as well as in the two DELTA studies, may suggest that a diet with a reduced SFA content would result in more favorable Lp(a) fatty acid properties. Collectively, these findings underscore the dynamic nature of the Lp(a) lipidome in response to metabolic environmental changes.

We further explored whether variability in Lp(a) levels or apo(a) size would impact the findings. When dividing the cohort based on an Lp(a) level associated with CVD risk in clinical guidelines (50 mg/dl) (1, 2), the Lp(a) lipidome of participants with higher Lp(a) (≥50 mg/dl) differed from those with lower Lp(a) (<50 mg/dl) when responding to the dietary interventions. Thus, among participants with elevated Lp(a), a pronounced difference of the Lp(a) lipidome between the AAD versus the DASH-type diet was observed using principal component analysis as seen in Fig. 3. However, the same was not the case for participants with lower Lp(a) levels. These findings suggested that the overall Lp(a) lipidome response to a reduced intake of dietary SFA was mainly due to participants with an elevated Lp(a).

As apo(a) sizes are the key genetic determinant of plasma Lp(a) levels across population groups (9), we further tested whether the Lp(a) lipidome response patterns would differ across apo(a) size spectrum. Notably, we observed an overall apo(a)-size specific Lp(a) lipidome response, where the small (≤22K4) apo(a) size group exhibited a different pattern compared to the medium and large apo(a) size groups. First, the small apo(a) size group response was characterized by a higher prevalence of decreased (80%) versus increased (20%) lipid species, primarily in the PC, PC O and SM classes. In contrast, for the medium and large apo(a) size groups, the distributions of increased versus decreased lipid species were either fairly balanced or showed an opposite direction of response pattern compared to the small apo(a) size group. Second, the composition of the 10 most significantly altered Lp(a) lipid species in the small apo(a) size group also differed, with more TG species (n = 3) among the increased lipids and more SM species (n = 4) among the decreased species. This particular pattern was not observed for the medium and large apo(a) size groups where not a single TG species was found among the most altered lipids. On the other hand, all three apo(a) size groups experienced a uniform enrichment in Lp(a) with the same 4 PCs and 1 LPC. Collectively, these findings suggest a difference across apo(a) size ranges with regard to the Lp(a) lipidome response to diet changes.

Overall, the exact mechanism(s) and clinical significance of the diet-induced modifications of the Lp(a) lipidome remain to be determined. As noted above, while the overall OxPL burden was not significantly affected, Lp(a) particle OxPC concentrations decreased. Furthermore, the fatty acid profile changed into a more favorable pattern. On the other hand, previous studies also provide some further insights. Notably, during the DASH-type diet, the Lp(a) lipidome was enriched in a DG species (38:4) by 1.47-fold and 1.42-fold, respectively, among participants with lower Lp(a) levels as well as in carriers of large apo(a) sizes. As this particular Lp(a)-DG species has been shown to mediate Lp(a)-associated monocyte-driven inflammation (30), its increased abundance following a DASH-type diet could thus promote Lp(a) atherogenicity. In particular, individuals with lower Lp(a) levels and/or carriers of large apo(a) sizes—considered to be at a lower risk for Lp(a)-attributable CVD—may be susceptible to reduced SFA intake. On the other hand, the GET-READI cohort experienced a substantial upward shift in their distribution of Lp(a)-attributable CVD risk categories. For example, among GET-READI participants with an Lp(a) level of 30–50 mg/dl (the grey zone) during the AAD, 50% shifted their risk-associated category to a higher risk (>50 mg/dl) level during a DASH-type diet. In this context, our findings demonstrate the presence of a pronounced difference in the Lp(a) lipidome between the AAD and the DASH-type diet in participants with elevated Lp(a) but not in participants with lower Lp(a) levels merit attention. These findings indicate that participants with an elevated Lp(a) level are subject to a greater susceptibility to Lp(a) lipid modifications due to dietary changes. Further, a TG species (56:4) increased significantly in all participants, most prominently in carriers of small apo(a) sizes. This species was previously identified in plasma as a lipid species that deleteriously mediated the effect of ApoE2 on cognitive health (31). Interestingly, we also demonstrated that African Americans, but not whites, with the ApoE2 genotype had lower Lp(a) levels compared with those with the ApoE4 genotype (32). It is of interest to note that the Lp(a) lipidome is part of the overall cargo subject to delivery to the vessel wall, potentially contributing to initiate atherosclerotic processes. Overall, our novel findings suggest that dietary SFA reduction affects Lp(a) molecular composition beyond Lp(a) levels—thus a better understanding of its holistic effects on Lp(a) is required to optimize lifestyle modifications to manage CVD risk.

Our study has some strengths and limitations. The present study extends the notion of the dynamic intraindividual nature of the Lp(a) lipidome to African Americans, a population group with a high burden of elevated Lp(a). The large cohort size enabled assessments of heterogeneity and similarities by Lp(a) levels and apo(a) size spectrum—key aspects of Lp(a) research. However, due to method optimization restrictions and attempts to include as many lipids as possible, cholesterol and cholesterol esters, which are prone to in-source fragmentation and have poor ionization efficiency (33, 34, 35), were not considered in the lipidomics analysis. Thus, future studies targeting this area, as well as the potential role of other LPA genetic variants are needed to better understand dietary modulation of Lp(a) risk properties. Furthermore, the averages of replicate measurements for the endpoints taken at the end of the AAD and the DASH-type diet were used for analyses. This allowed a new steady state for Lp(a) with six weeks between the compared timepoints, reducing confounding by the randomization order and the carryover effect. Although five weeks of controlled feeding is a substantial period of time, it is brief compared to lifelong dietary habits and limits any assessments of long-term clinical outcomes. However, the intervention diet represented diets recommended for the population at large, i.e., specifically reducing SFA intake (13). Lastly, we used Lp(a) concentration measured in mg/dl to normalize OxPC subspecies mass to obtain OxPC particle concentrations. We recognize that this is not an ideal approach, and future studies using Lp(a) measured in nmol/L (i.e. particle concentration) will be required to confirm the current findings.

In conclusion, replacement of dietary SFA with complex carbohydrates in African Americans resulted in significant changes in Lp(a) lipidome properties, with the most pronounced changes occurring in the PC and PC O lipid classes. Specifically, reducing SFA intake from 16% energy at the AAD to 6% energy in the DASH-type diet led to an increase in the relative abundance of longer-chain polyunsaturated PC and PC O subspecies, while shorter-chain saturated or monounsaturated PC and PC O subspecies decreased. A pronounced difference of the Lp(a) lipidome profile between the AAD and the DASH-type diet was observed in participants with elevated Lp(a) but not in those with lower Lp(a) levels, emphasizing the key role of an elevated Lp(a) level or the Lp(a) lipidome response to a reduced SFA intake. While broad lipidome changes were noted, differences in OxPL levels were less prominent. Finally, the Lp(a) lipidome changes occurred across the apo(a) size spectrum; however, apo(a)-size-specific patterns were also noted particularly for the small apo(a) size range. The present study provides a window into Lp(a) lipidome changes and their potential implications, opening avenues for future studies to better understand diet-induced changes in Lp(a) lipidome properties and assess how they may impact an individual’s Lp(a) level-based CVD risk estimation. In summary, the findings provide support for the concept of an intraindividual dynamic nature of Lp(a) particle properties in response to diet modifications and to the presence of a metabolic regulation of Lp(a).
