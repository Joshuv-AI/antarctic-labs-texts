To the Editor:

Inspiratory capacity (IC) measurement allows for the assessment of operating lung volumes during exercise and is the gold standard method for evaluating critical inspiratory constraints, particularly during cardiopulmonary exercise testing. Accurate measurement of IC depends on an individual's ability to give a maximal inspiratory effort to total lung capacity, which is assumed to remain constant throughout exercise [1]. Nonetheless, there is substantial evidence that IC is a reproducible measurement [2], and is responsive to both pharmacological (e.g., bronchodilators [3]) and non-pharmacological interventions (e.g., hyperoxia [4]). Importantly, improving IC during exercise is associated with improvements in dyspnoea ratings and exercise tolerance [5].

Fatigue of the diaphragm, which has been observed during high-intensity exercise in healthy individuals [6, 7], and/or fatigue of the extra-diaphragmatic inspiratory muscles, may compromise the ability to reach total lung capacity during an IC manoeuvre. Underestimation of IC due to respiratory muscle fatigue could influence our interpretation of operating lung volumes, identification of dynamic hyperinflation, and the assessment of expiratory flow limitation. Alternatively, IC could be maintained in the setting of diaphragm fatigue if other inspiratory muscles increase in activation to compensate for the reduced pressure-generation of the diaphragm. The specific influence of diaphragm fatigue on IC as well as inspiratory muscle pressure generation and activation patterns during exercise has, to our knowledge, not been explored. The purpose of this study was to 1) evaluate the effect of diaphragm fatigue on IC responses during high intensity exercise, and 2) identify any physiological changes that occur when performing IC manoeuvres with and without diaphragm fatigue in healthy young males. We hypothesised that 1) IC would be maintained in the presence of diaphragm fatigue and 2) diaphragmatic and extra-diaphragmatic muscle activation would increase to compensate for the fatigued diaphragm.

This investigation included a retrospective analysis of 15 participants from a previously published study [8]. One participant was excluded for not giving consistent or maximal effort during his exercise tests. Participants were young, recreationally active males with normal spirometry and without smoking history or contraindications to exercise (age 26.6±5.3 years, body mass index 26.4±3.4 kg·m−2, forced expiratory volume in 1 s (FEV1) 108±11% predicted [9], FEV1/forced vital capacity 0.80±0.07, maximal inspiratory pressure 115±25% predicted [10], peak oxygen uptake 45.6±9.6 mL·kg−1·min−1). All participants completed three laboratory visits separated by ≥48 h. Visit one included an incremental cycle test to determine gas exchange threshold. Visits two and three each included a constant work-rate cycling test to exhaustion at an intensity equal to 60% of the difference between gas exchange threshold and peak work-rate, performed with or without diaphragm fatigue, in random order. IC manoeuvres were performed at rest and every 2 min during exercise as previously described [5].

Participants were instrumented with a multi-pair dual-balloon oesophageal electrode catheter (Yinghui Medical Technology Co., Ltd; Guangzhou, China) during all assessments to allow for continuous measurement of oesophageal (Poes), gastric (Pga), and transdiaphragmatic pressures (Pdi; calculated as Pga minus Poes) (DP15–34, Validyne Engineering; Northridge, CA, USA), as well as electromyography of the crural diaphragm (EMGdi), whereas surface electrodes were used to measure EMG of the scalene (EMGsca), and sternocleidomastoid (EMGscm) muscles, as previously described [8]. Data were collected using LabChart v7.3.7 software (ADInstruments).

Diaphragm fatigue was induced via inspiratory pressure threshold loading (PTL) using a custom device. Briefly, participants were instructed to breathe on a two-way non-rebreathing valve at 60% of their maximum Pdi, determined from maximal inspiratory pressure manoeuvres, with a prolonged 0.7 duty cycle and breathing frequency of 15 breaths·min−1. CO2 was titrated into the breathing circuit in the event of hypocapnia. Maximal potentiated transdiaphragmatic twitch pressures (Pdi,tw) via cervical magnetic stimulation of the phrenic nerves [11] were recorded pre- and immediately post-PTL in the “fatigue” visit, as well as pre-exercise, immediately following exercise, and again after 15 min of recovery for both constant work-rate exercise tests. Diaphragm fatigue was defined as a reduction in Pdi,tw ≥15% from baseline, or >2 times the coefficient of variation between stimuli, whichever was larger [11, 12].

IC, Poes, and EMG were examined using linear mixed effects models to estimate the fixed effects of condition (fatigue and control) and relative exercise duration as a percentage of individual exercise duration in the control condition. Quadratic models with interactions for condition and the first polynomial coefficient across time were selected based on the limits of collinearity, confirmed with Akaike and Bayesian Information Criteria, and visually validated from Q-Q and other residual plots [13]. Estimated marginal means were contrasted between condition at successive 20% increments of exercise duration. Analysis was performed in R 4.4.2 (www.r-project.org) with the packages lme4 [14], lmerTest [15] and emmeans [16].

The main finding of this study was that IC, Poes, and muscle activation over relative exercise time were not different between the fatigue and control conditions. However, we did observe significant marginal contrasts in IC, ranging from 150–250 mL, between the fatigued and control conditions, particularly from baseline to approximately 60% exercise time, with the largest difference occurring at baseline. This suggests that IC manoeuvres performed earlier during exercise, and therefore closer to the fatiguing PTL stimulus, may be adversely affected by diaphragm fatigue. While diaphragm fatigue experienced naturally during exercise is likely milder than what we induced with PTL, its presence could lead to underestimated IC measurements. In some cases, this underestimation might erroneously indicate dynamic hyperinflation. Changes in IC of a similar magnitude (150–250 mL) following tiotropium administration have been associated with improvements in exercise tolerance and dyspnoea in people with COPD [17]. However, a 150–250 mL change in IC for someone with normal lung capacity versus a hyperinflated individual (e.g., COPD) represents a greater relative change in the latter, and will likely have a more significant impact on dyspnoea and exercise tolerance.

Global respiratory pressure generation during IC manoeuvres, as demonstrated by Poes, was similar across conditions from baseline until 80% exercise time. This is in the context of slightly lower IC, similar relative activation of the diaphragm during IC manoeuvres across conditions, and higher EMGsca activation during IC manoeuvres with fatigue compared to control during exercise. These data may suggest that participants tried to compensate for diaphragm fatigue without fully compensating the IC response. It is important to note that our study population had normal inspiratory muscle strength. Thus, compensatory activation of other respiratory muscles to support the diaphragm may not be possible in individuals with respiratory muscle weakness and/or dysfunction. Interestingly, the fatigue condition resulted in slightly lower Poes values near the end of exercise (80–100% exercise time). However, this reduction in pressure generation did not translate into lower IC. Although Poes is a good marker of inspiratory muscle effort, one can still achieve total lung capacity during an IC manoeuvre despite generating less pressure.

Initial differences in IC between conditions, and concomitant differences in EMGsca, diminished beyond 60% exercise time, which could be attributed to diaphragm fatigue development by seven participants during exercise in the control condition. Additionally, eight of our participants showed some recovery post-exercise compared to post-PTL based on the per cent change in twitch pressure (i.e., they were less fatigued post-exercise than immediately post-PTL), with three no longer demonstrating fatigue at all according to our pre-specified criteria [8]. Previous work suggests that PTL is a much higher stimulus on the respiratory muscles compared to exercise [18]. Thus, with fatigue occurring later during the control condition and slight recovery occurring over the course of the fatigued condition, earlier time points during the exercise test are likely more sensitive to the dynamic effect of diaphragm fatigue on serial IC manoeuvre performance. When a model included pre- to post-exercise difference in Pdi,tw as a fixed effect, it explained only about 10% more of the total variance (marginal R2=0.136) compared to condition alone (marginal R2=0.049) [19]. The explanatory value of either model was quite low because the differences between the young healthy males in this sample group were considerably larger than any detectable within-participant differences related to diaphragm fatigue.

Our results suggest that diaphragm fatigue does not meaningfully affect IC manoeuvre performance in healthy young males. Although IC decreased shortly after PTL, which is a highly fatiguing stimulus, the physiological significance of this change remains unclear, especially since IC did not differ as exercise progressed. Extrapolation of our results is limited by our sample population, and further investigation in people with respiratory muscle weakness and/or chronic diseases is needed. Additionally, future work with a larger sample size is recommended to allow for a sub-group analysis of those who develop fatigue in the control condition, as well as those who “recover” during exercise in the fatigue condition.
