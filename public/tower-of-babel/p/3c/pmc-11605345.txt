The impact of 45S5 bioglass vs. β-TCP nanoparticles ratio on rheological behavior of formulated printing inks and 3D printed polycaprolactone-based scaffolds final properties
Authors: Mansure Kazemi, Hasti Esmaeili, Mansoureh Khandaei Dastjerdi, Farshad Amiri, Mehdi Mehdikhani, Mohammad Rafienia
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Extrusion based 3-D printing has been extensively applied to create geometrically complex composite polymer-ceramic structures as bone tissue substitute. The rheological features of the formulated bioink that regulate the printability and resolution of the printed scaffolds, rely on physicochemical properties of ink components, mainly their composition and chemical structure. The aim of this study was to evaluate the effect of different content of 45S5 bioglass (BG) and β-tricalcium phosphate (β-TCP) nanoparticles on the rheological behavior of printing inks and final composite scaffolds based on polycaprolactone (PCL)/BG/β-TCP. Ceramic nano-powders were first characterized and the composite bioinks were prepared by mixing various ratios of BG and β-TCP powders into the 50 % w/v of PCL solution (β-TCP/BG: 70/30, 50/50, 30/70, and 10/90 w/w %). All formulated inks showed a thixotropic behavior and viscosity significantly increased by applying higher fraction of BG. Interestingly highly loaded β-TCP ink (β-TCP/BG: 70/30) revealed a rubbery nature with high surface tension which reduced final scaffolds resolution. All printed scaffolds possessed highly porous structure with interconnected pores. However, porosity percentage and shape stability improved by increasing BG content which was accompanied with lower mechanical strength and superior biodegradation and bioactivity of scaffolds. The biological performance of the printed scaffolds was evaluated using osteoblast cell line MG-63. MTT assay and cell attachment observation by SEM confirmed that printed scaffolds are well biocompatible and properly support cell colonization and proliferation. In overall, besides more appropriate materialistic properties, scaffolds with β-TCP/BG: 30/70 composition provided the most favorable microenvironment for cell colonization and growth.

Introduction

Despite advances in medical and surgical techniques for treatments of bone tissue injuries, restoration of large and non-union defects still stands as a prominent clinical challenge. This is mainly due to the absence of a suitable substrate for cells migration in order to develop into new bone tissue and blood vessels [1]. In this regard, tissue engineering approach attempts to simulate natural bone matrix through the fabrication of appropriate scaffolds using a variety of biomaterials, including metals, ceramics (e.g. calcium phosphates and bioactive glasses, etc.), and natural/synthetic polymers. Scaffold serves as a temporal template and in pre-designed cases, a sustained-release source of growth-induced factors for implanted or native cells [[2], [3], [4]].

In addition to compositional similarities with natural bone tissue, other major prerequisites include the interconnected porous structure, adequate mechanical strength, and a biodegradation rate balanced with the new tissue growth [5]. Many of traditional scaffold fabrication methods have always been associated with the lack of having accurate control on scaffold's structural features. However, with the emergence of additive manufacturing (AM) technologies, detailed monitoring over internal porous structure and aimed-specific external geometry of scaffold became possible [6,7]. Extrusion-based 3D printing (EBP), is one of the most common AM strategies which provide a precise control over the macro/microarchitecture of the scaffold through the consecutive deposition of biomaterial ink in a layer by layer manner according to a predesigned 3-D model [8,9]. Although this approach makes flexibility in scaffold structure design, the final printing quality including the morphology and structural stability of deposited filaments entirely depends on rheological properties of the printing ink. Such intrinsic properties of the ink are direct product of the physico-chemical properties of ink ingredients and their interactions. Viscosity and flow behavior of printing ink as components influenced by the aforementioned parameters strictly affect the morphology and structural stability of deposited filaments [10,11].

Polymer-ceramic inks are among the most frequently applied ink compositions for fabrication of tissue engineering scaffolds. Hence, studying the polymer-ceramic interactions and their impacts on rheological behavior of the printing ink can be extremely important [12]. PCL is one of the most interesting polymers for bone tissue engineering applications. This semi crystalline polymer offers great features including, remarkable mechanical stability, biocompatibility, flexibility, and supreme combination potential [13]. Although, PCL possess undeniable disadvantages including hydrophobic nature, slow biodegradation rate, as well as lack of bioactivity [14,15]. Conversely, although most common bio-ceramics applied as bone substitute (calcium phosphates and bioactive glasses, etc.) are bioactive, but brittleness and low fracture toughness are considered as their intrinsic drawbacks [16]. In this regard, formulation of nanocomposite materials based on polymers and ceramics put forward to address abovementioned concerns. While the incorporation of bioactive ceramic into a polymeric substrate reduces its brittleness, ceramic phase improves bioactivity and stiffness of polymeric phase in the final construct [17].

β-TCP and BG are some of the most suitable materials for bone tissue engineering application [18]. β-TCP possesses great biocompatibility, due to the chemical similarity with the natural bone mineral phase, desirable osteoconductivity, and high biodegradability. However, the lack of osteoinduction and intrinsic brittleness which is a common characteristic of ceramic materials remain the main concerns of its application [19].

On the other hand, high bioactivity is highlighted characteristic of bioactive glasses, in particular 45S5 bioglass which is both osteoinductive and osteoconductive. Scaffolds with 10 % bioglass by weight exhibited elevated metabolic activity, which was linked to the presence of a calcium phosphate layer [20]. This bioceramic is able to bond firmly to neighboring hard and soft tissues due to the formation of an interfacial hydroxycarbonate apatite (HCA) layer and stimulate osteogenesis and angiogenesis by sustained release of ionic dissociation products [21]. Studies have also indicated that PCL/bioglass composites possess advantageous degradation kinetics and enhanced mechanical properties compared to PCL composites with other ceramics like hydroxyapatite and tricalcium phosphate. Therefore, they are more suitable for bone tissue applications [22].

Numerous studies have utilized 3D printing to fabricate bone scaffolds from PCL/β-TCP, PCL/BG, and β-TCP/BG, emphasizing scaffold design and composition [23]. Customizable scaffolds were produced using DIW and DLP technologies. DIW achieved high-resolution PCL/BG scaffolds with enhanced cell proliferation for PCL-20%BG [24], while DLP created β-TCP/BG gyroid scaffolds with favorable mechanical properties and effective bone ingrowth. These approaches demonstrate significant potential for bone defect treatments [25].

The current investigation was undertaken to extend prior research, with the objective of examining the influence of various proportions of 45S5 bioglass (BG) and β-tricalcium phosphate (β-TCP) nanoparticles combined with polycaprolactone (PCL), which are considered optimal materials for bone replacement. This study aimed to explore how these combinations affect the rheological characteristics of the composite ink and the architecture of the final scaffold. Initially, the ceramic powders were thoroughly characterized, which facilitated the preparation of printing inks by integrating different ratios of the powders into a 50 % w/v PCL solution (β-TCP/BG: 70/30, 50/50, 30/70, and 10/90). The rheological behavior of the formulated inks was examined using a previously established method with a uniaxial compressive strength machine. Following this, the printed scaffolds were characterized in terms of their morphological, microstructural, and mechanical properties, along with their degradation and bioactivity. Finally, the biological performance of the printed scaffolds was evaluated through the use of mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs).

Materials and methods

Materials

PCL beads with average size of 3 mm and a molecular weight of 60 kDa was obtained from Sigma-Aldrich (USA) and chloroform with purity of 99.8%, sourced from Merck Company (Germany), was utilized as a solvent for PCL. BG nanopowders (<100 nm particle size) was provided by Tofigh Daru Company (Iran) and β-TCP nanopowders (<100 nm particle size) was provided by APATECH Company (Iran).

Crystalline phases, functional groups, morphology, and particle size of ceramic powders were evaluated by the X-ray diffraction method (XRD; Siemens-Brucker D5000 diffractometer, 40 kV/40 mA, Cu-K), Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR; ABB Bomem, Quebec, Canada), Scanning electron microscopy (SEM, Philips XL30, The Netherland), and transmission electron microscopy (TEM; JEOL 2000FX, Japan). Specific surface area (SSA)/pore size distribution (PSD) and surface charge were also measured by Brunauer–Emmett–Teller/Barrett-Joyner-Halenda (BET/BJH; BELSORP MINI II, Japan) and zeta potential (Zetasizer 3600, Malvern Instrument Ltd., Worchestershire, UK) methods.

Preparation of the ink

The applied printing ink in this study was ceramic paste material consists of a PCL solution and varied amount of dispersed ceramic powder. The polymeric solution was prepared by dissolving PCL granules (50 w/v %) in chloroform solution at 45 °C. The final printing ink was prepared by adding a mixture of BG and β-TCP nanopowders with different proportions (β-TCP/BG: 70/30, 50/50, 30/70, and 10/90) into PCL solution under continuous stirring. The total solid mass of ceramic mixture was equal with PCL solid mass to obtain a composite with 50 % w/w composition. Mixing of the ink components was done in an open environment to modulate the viscosity via solvent evaporation to an empirically determined optimal viscosity which would be ideal for the extrusion process according to the previously reported methods [26].

Rheological analysis

In order to simulate capillary rheometry, the printing cartridge which would have stored the printing ink inside the printer was fitted with a Mechanical Testing Machine (Walter + Bai AG, Loehningen. Schahausen, Switzerland) in the compression mode. The applied external pressure (ΔP; stress) was recorded against the flow which is equal to deformation and define as shear strain. The viscosity (η) of the prepared inks was calculated according to equation (1):η=τγ=πΔP(d2)48QL,τ=d2ΔP2Lγ=4Qπ(d2)3Q=VΔt=mρΔtHere, Q is defined as flow rate which is measured based on the mass of extruded material (m) over time (Δt). While γ and τ are referred to the apparent shear rate and shear stress, respectively. Also, the inner diameter and length of extruder needle were kept constant at 410 μm and 6.35 mm, respectively.

3-D printing of nanocomposite scaffolds

The 3-D geometry of porous scaffolds in a 2 × 2 cm cubic-shaped block with inter-line spacing of 500 μm, was designed using Solidworks software program (Version 2017, Dassault Systèmes, France). The STL file was exported into G-code format using slic3r slicing software and the printing command was forwarded through Repetier software program (Version 2.1.6, Hot-World GmbH, Germany). The porous structures were printed using a 3-axis EBP platform equipped with a single-screw micro-extruder (Nika EBP, Adli Tissue Regeneration, Iran) through the continuous flow and layer-by-layer extrusion of the printing ink to the maximum number of 20 layers. The printing ink was extruded through a 22 gauge (internal diameter ∼ 410 μm) stainless steel nozzle at the constant extrusion rate of 0.015 g s−1 and printing rate of 10 mm s−1 at room temperature.

Characterization of scaffolds

Morphology

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) was applied to investigate printed scaffolds' structural features, such as fibers morphology and pores geometry. The scaffolds' surfaces were coated with a thin layer of gold (Au) and scanned by SEM (Philips XL30, The Netherland) operated at an accelerating voltage of 19 kV.

The volume of scaffolds (VT) was measured using their nominal dimensions (cm3), and then total pore volume (VP) was calculated using the weight (MS) and theoretical density (ρs) of the composite scaffolds according to equation. (2):(2)VP=VT−MSρs

Once VT and VP were measured, the total porosity (%) can be calculated through following equation (3):(3)%Porosity=VPVT

Mechanical strength

The compressive strength of 3D porous scaffolds was evaluated using a Universal Testing Machine (Walter + Bai AG, Loehningen. Schahausen, Switzerland). Cylindrical samples (n = 5, D = 5 mm, H = 10 mm) were compressed to 40 % of their original height at a crosshead rate of 0.5 mm/min. Stress and strain parameters were calculated from the applied force vs. displacement data. The maximum stress value at 40 % displacement and the linear section of stress-strain diagram slope were considered as compressive strength and young's modulus, respectively. The values are reported as the means ± standard deviation.

Degradability evaluation

In order to investigate the degradation rate of the composite constructs, the scaffolds (n = 3) were first weighed (W0), then immersed in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), and incubated at 37 °C for 2, 3, 4, 8, and 12 weeks. At each time point, the specimens were extracted from PBS solution, washed with deionized water (DI), dried at oven for 24 h at 45 °C, and weighted (W1), again. Finally, the weight reduction percentage was calculated according to equation (4):(4)Weightlosspercentage=W1−W0W0×100

Contact angle measurement

The hydrophilicity of 3D-printed scaffolds composed of varying ratios of β-tricalcium phosphate and bioglass (β-TCP/BG: 70/30, 50/50, 30/70, and 10/90 w/w %) was assessed using a contact angle meter (XCA-50, PMC, Iran). Water droplets were placed on each sample, and the average contact angle was determined from measurements taken at a minimum of three different points on each scaffold.

In vitro bioactivity

In vitro bioactivity of nanocomposite scaffolds was evaluated using simulated body fluid (SBF) prepared following Kokubo's protocol [27]. Scaffolds (n = 3), with identical morphology and surface area were immersed in SBF solution and incubated at 37 °C for 7, 14, 21, and 28 days. During the incubation time, the SBF solution was replaced twice per week. At the end of each time point, the scaffolds were removed from the SBF, rinsed with DI water, frozen at −80 °C, and subsequently freeze-dried for 48 h. Finally, the formation of the calcium phosphate layer on the surface of scaffolds was investigated using SEM observation.

Cell culture studies

Cellular studies were done using MG-63 osteoblastic cell line (MG-63, National cell bank, Pasteur Institute, Iran). Osteoblast cells were cultured in DMEM media supplemented with 10 % FBS and 1 % penicillin/streptomycin in a humidified incubator at 37 °C with 5 % CO2. The culture media was refreshed every two days and when cultured cells reached 80 % confluency, MG-63 cells were sub-cultured to expand.

For cell seeding, scaffolds were sterilized using UV radiation for 40 min and preconditioned in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) for 48 h prior to cell culture. Osteoblast cells were seeded on the scaffolds surface at the concentrations of 4 × 104 cells per sample. The cell-loaded scaffolds were then incubated at 37 °C.

In vitro cytotoxicity assay

Cytocompatibility of the printed scaffolds was evaluated by MTT test. Following 1, 3 and 7 days of cell seeding, 150 μL of MTT solution (5 mg/mL MTT in PBS 0.1 M) was added to 750 mL of cell culture medium (DMEM) in each well and incubated at 37 °C for 4 h. We used at least 3 wells for each sample. Then, the medium was replaced by 600 μL of dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) to dissolve the precipitated formazan crystals. Finally, 100 μL of the resulting purple-colored solution was transferred to a 96-well microplate and the optical densities (OD) was measured by an ELISA reader at 570 nm.

Cell adhesion and morphology

SEM observation was carried out to evaluate cell attachment and morphology on the printed scaffolds. 48 h after cell seeding, osteoblasts cultured scaffolds were washed twice with PBS. Then, the cells were fixed with 4 % paraformaldehyde for 45min at room temperature and dehydrated in a graded series of aqueous alcohol solutions (70, 80, 96, and two changes of 100 % ethanol). In the following, specimens were freeze-dried, coated with a thin layer of Gold (Au), and observed using SEM.

Statistical analysis

All quantitative data were expressed as the mean ± standard deviation. Statistical differences were calculated by one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey's HSD test using IBM SPSS Statistic Version 22 and P < 0.05 was considered to be statistically significant.

Powders characterizations

X-ray diffraction patterns of ceramic powders are depicted in Fig. 1(a) The XRD spectra of BG reveal its amorphous nature while diffraction pattern of β-TCP confirms its crystalline structure with characteristic peaks at 2θ: 25.8, 27.85, 31.05, and 34.45° which are well-matched with JCPDS card no. 09–0169. The XRD pattern of β-TCP/BG: 30/70 and 50/50 composites exhibit a combination of broad and sharp peaks. The broad peak around 2θ: 20–35° points to the presence of non-crystalline structure of 45S5 BG, while the sharp peaks correspond to the distinctive peaks of β-TCP. Furthermore, the XRD spectra of the composites exhibit two notable peaks at around 2θ: 21.5 and 24°, which can be attributed to the semicrystalline nature of PCL [28,29].Fig. 1(a) XRD patterns and (b) FTIR spectra of ceramic nanopowders.Fig. 1

(a) XRD patterns and (b) FTIR spectra of ceramic nanopowders.

Fig. 1(b) illustrates the FTIR diagram of ceramic powders. The PCL spectrum displays prominent peaks at 2936 cm−1 and 2863 cm−1 which can be attributed to asymmetric and symmetric stretching of –CH2 groups. Furthermore, the peaks at 1724 cm−1 and 1160 cm−1 are associated with vibrational modes such as C=O asymmetric and symmetric vibration of C–O–C [30,31]. BG spectra displayed silicate network characteristic absorption bands at 496, 740, 930, and 1038 cm−1. The bands at 496 and 740 cm−1 can be assigned to the Si−O−Si symmetric bending mode of bridging oxygen and Si–O–Si symmetric stretch mode of non-bridging oxygen atoms between tetrahedral. The absorption bands at 930 and 1038 cm−1 are related to Si–O stretching mode of non-bridging oxygen, and Si–O–Si asymmetric stretching mode, respectively [32]. Additionally, the band located around 634 cm−1 can be attributed to amorphous phosphate and the weak band at 1455 cm−1 is associated with the asymmetric C–O vibration mode. Finally, the absorption bands at 1633 and 3440 cm−1 are ascribed to the hydroxyl group which may be due to the adsorbed humidity [33]. Considering FTIR diagram of β-TCP, one can associate the peaks at 3430 and 1629 cm−1 to the adsorbed water molecules, while absorption bands located between 900 and 1200 cm−1 (stretching modes), and 550 and 602 cm−1 (bending modes) correspond to PO4−3 groups [34]. The PCL/BG/β-TCP samples exhibited nearly identical spectra with major peaks, suggesting that TCP and BG particles were integrated into PCL during filament production and resulted in alterations to the surface chemistry.

Discussion

Extrusion based 3-D printing enables creation of geometrically complex structures via the extrusion and deposition of an extrudable biomaterial ink through a nozzle. A desirable EBP ink should contain adequate ceramic load to minimize shrinkage, possess good homogeneity, and proper rheological behaviors to be extruded. Also, shape-retention ability of deposited filament guarantees the required resolution of the final product [36]. Herein, the rheological characteristics of the formulated paste play a significant role and regulate the extrudability of the bioink and the self-supporting capacity of the printed filaments. PCL, one of the most interesting biopolymers for fabrication bone scaffolds, shows great viscoelastic behavior [37]. In this study, BG and β-TCP nano-powders were added to the PCL matrix to improve its bioactivity and biological properties. The ceramic: polymer phase ratio was kept constant at 1:1. The ceramic nanoparticles were added to the polymeric solution with different β-TCP/BG ratios (i.e. 70/30, 50/50, 30/70, and 10/90 w/w %) to illustrate the impact of ceramic ingredients chemical and physical properties on the ink characteristic and consequently the macrostructure and morphology of the final scaffolds.

First, ceramic nano-powders were well characterized. The FTIR spectrum revealed that the main peaks in the composite samples matched those of the PCL, TCP, and BG samples, confirming their presence in the composition. Notably, the 1040 cm⁻1 peak in the β-TCP/BG: 50/50 sample was slightly sharper compared to the β-TCP/BG: 30/70 sample, indicating a higher concentration of TCP in the mixture [38]. The XRD spectra of BG revealed the predominant amorphous nature as a result of the glassy and internal disorder of the material. It is well known that relative to crystallized structure, the amorphous 45S5 BG samples exhibit higher solubility and an enhanced ion release profile, which could potentially promote degradation and increase bioactivity [39]. In addition, the β-TCP/BG: 50/50 composite showed a higher intensity of TCP peaks compared to the β-TCP/BG: 30/70, indicating an increased degree of crystallinity within the scaffold. While this enhancement in crystallinity may improve the mechanical properties of the composite, it could also hinder its bioactivity by reducing the degradation rate and ion release profile [40].

SEM observation revealed BG nanoparticles formed large and bulk aggregated clusters while β-TCP particles showed Rod-like particles which are agglomerated in various-shaped structures. The structure and size of nanoparticles were further studied using TEM observation. Although BG nanoparticles presented slight agglomeration, β-TCP nanoparticles indicated more uniformity in shape.

The surface charge of nanoparticles is reflected in the zeta potential (ζ) value. The magnitude and polarity of this value considerably affect particle interaction, resulting in electrostatic repulsion force which alleviates the agglomeration probability of solid particles in polymeric solution and causes more colloidal stability. The density of electric charge is a function of pH and particle concentration, so an increase in pH leads to an increase in the overall value of zeta potential [41]. While in β-TCP particles, calcium and phosphate ions represent positive and negative surface reactive sites, respectively, the dissolution of BG particles is accompanied by an initial burst release of alkaline ions and sudden increase in pH [42,43]. Consequently, an increase in BG content is associated with an increase in negative charge between the glass and the solution which encourages inter-particle repulsion.

DLS analysis was carried out to determine the hydrodynamic diameter of ceramic nanoparticles. The variation in size distribution, in particular in the BG sample, can be justified by agglomeration tendency of nanoparticles. In addition, DLS measures the hydrodynamic diameter of suspended particles which is certainly greater than the actual particle's size, particularly in bulk agglomerated ones [44]. The SSA and total pore volume of β-TCP was lower than BG nanoparticles. Strong tendency of BG particles to self-agglomeration leads to the formation of inter particle porosity and consequently higher specific surface area.

In order to evaluate the effect of different content of BG and β-TCP nanoparticles on the rheological behavior of formulated printing inks and final composite scaffolds, the apparent viscosity and flow rate were measured against the shear rate and applied pressure, respectively. All biomaterial mixtures revealed shear-thinning feature which is accompanied with the reduction in apparent viscosity following increase in shear rate. Overall, the addition of BG and β-TCP increase the viscosity of PCL solution due to the enhancing the surface area in contact, interaction, as well as inherent friction among the polymeric matrix and bio-ceramic nanoparticles [45]. Increase in nanoparticles content remarkably increases the ink viscosity. However, at the same ceramic content, different ink compositions showed distinct rheological behavior. Various features of nanoparticles including morphology and size distribution, and surface chemistry of nanoparticles lead to the different behaviors in colloidal system [46]. The rheological behavior of solid particles is a consequence of the volume fraction of the incorporated ceramic phase within the polymeric matrix [47]. Although both ceramics types had nano-scaled particle dimensions, but BG NPs with higher SSA tend to agglomerate into clusters and displayed a tendency to increase the ink viscosity. In fact, at the same ceramic volume and concentration, particles with smaller size provide a higher number of suspended particles with Brownian motion opposed to the applied shear [48]. BG particle clusters present higher aspect ratio and consequently elevated effective volume fraction and reduced extreme packing density which results in decreased percolation threshold and increased solution viscosity [49]. In other words, formation of a continuous ceramic phase throughout the PCL matrix limits the movement of long polymer chains which leads to a significant shift in the relaxation dynamics of the PCL macromolecules. So the homogeneity of biomaterial ink is decreased and the layers in the ink indicate restricted motion cumulating in increased viscosity [50]. At the same time, flake-like β-TCP NPs homogeneously disperse into PCL matrix and retain the level of short-range order of polymeric structure. Interestingly highly loaded β-TCP ink (β-TCP/BG: 70/30) revealed a rubbery nature with high surface tension and low viscosity and flow resistance [51]. While BG clusters occupy the most of the volume fraction at a disordered tightly packing, suspensions of soft and flat surfaces of β-TCP particles can deform and store elastic energy and form soft paste [47]. The pH of the solution regulates particle electrostatic interaction which affects its surface tension and stability. While the isoelectric point (IEP) of BG and β-TCP solution is close to 1.5, both nanoparticles take on negative charge at higher pH value than IEP [52]. As mentioned earlier, an increase in the percentage of bioglass is associated with an increase in pH of colloidal solution. So, highly accumulation of surface charge affects the magnitude of particle interaction and gradually overcomes the surface tension of the composite ink. Therefore, surface tension and viscosity are considered as the main characteristics of a printing ink to perform an efficient printing process [53]. Optimal viscosity and surface tension guarantee stability of deposited fiber and resolution of final scaffold.

Although printing of all formulated inks led to the development of interconnected porous scaffolds, the morphology of constructs revealed a corrective trend with the increase in the BG content. Shape retention of extruded filament and structural stability of printed scaffolds depend mainly on viscosity and surface tension of printing ink [54]. As expected from the above discussed results, the shape fidelity of lattice structure was not preserved in samples containing higher percentage of β-TCP (β-TCP/BG: 70/30). Due to the low viscosity, radial diffusion of the filaments has occurred and at the lattice junctions of two intersecting filaments formed dumbbell shaped overlays which negatively influence printing resolution. However high surface tension prevented adjacent fibers from fusion although extensive overlap was observed in corners. Consequently, the width of extruded lines exceeded the theoretical value and pores geometry in printed structure appears to form irregular rectangles rather than pre-designed squares. By increasing BG content, high surface tension and rubbery nature of highly loaded β-TCP composition disappeared and the ink viscosity increased. While β-TCP/BG: 30/70 scaffolds represented more stable printed-out filament and geometry of pores was more uniform, in 90 %w BG formulation, fiber diameter was closest to the designed value. By improving the stability of extruded filaments, the spatial structure of the scaffolds was preserved which led to the formation of pores with larger diameters and finally higher porosity volume. Bone substitutes with at least 50 % porosity and mean pore size of 200–500 μm are favorable for cell migration and new bone and vessel growth throughout the scaffold [55].

Mechanical behavior of the fabricated scaffolds was examined in compressive mode and the calculated stress-strain curves contains a clear elastoplastic nature with a linear elastic region associated with the bending of pores edge, a stress plateau correlated to the progressive collapse of the pores and permanent scaffold's deformation, as well as a rise of stress assigned to the final densification [56]. Following an increase in the BG content, the stress-strain curve showed a shorter linear elastic zone and longer plateau part and delays the densification process to higher values of elongation. According to previously discussed results, higher percentage of β-TCP is associated with lower viscosity and higher shrinkage which leads to denser internal structure and finally superior mechanical properties. While increasing in BG content was accompanied by improved internal structure's stability, increase in the porosity volume, and decrease in mechanical properties [57].

Evaluation of weight reduction of the printed scaffolds provides insight into the dissolution kinetics and degradation rate of different formulated constructs. Biodegradability is one of key features of scaffold, which causes its gradual elimination and replacement with newly growing tissue [58]. The rate of biodegradation can be affected by different parameters including scaffold hydrophilicity, porosity, and microstructure of the scaffold. It is well-known that incorporation of inorganic materials into PCL matrix decreases its structural stability. This is due to the improved hydrophilicity of composite material which facilitate penetration of biological fluids within the polymeric network and chemical disintegration of polymer backbone [59]. According to the results of the biodegradation test, it appears that β-TCP particles with smaller size and more uniform morphology are embedded better in the PCL matrix. The formation of large clusters of BG results in de-bonding of the polymeric matrix and void formation. Structural micro-porosity can significantly improve the permeability and thus degradability of scaffolds [60]. In addition, the rate of pH increase is proportional to the BG content which is due to release of bioglass alkaline ions such as Ca2+ and Na+. Increasing BG quantity leads to a less chemical stability and an extensive reaction with the surrounding aqueous solution which results in a swift change of the pH [61]. Also, extensive fluctuation of pH value is regulated by acidic products of PCL degradation. These data suggest that the BG nanoparticles function as a degradation accelerator in PBS [62].

Water contact angle is an essential metric for evaluating surface hydrophilicity, which has a profound impact on the biological performance of tissue engineering scaffolds, especially concerning cell attachment [63]. Polycaprolactone's hydrophobic nature can hinder the adhesion and growth of cells. To improve the hydrophilicity and efficacy of PCL, integrating it with other hydrophilic biomaterials is a viable strategy [64]. The influence of adding β-TCP to β-TCP or BG to PCL has been the subject of extensive research, and the findings of this investigation support previous studies. As the content of β-TCP increases, there is a slight reduction in the contact angle of PCL/β-TCP scaffolds, and the addition of BG to PCL in PCL/BG scaffolds results in a more substantial decrease in the contact angle. This phenomenon is attributed to BG's role in raising the local pH, which accelerates the breakdown of ester bonds [65,66]. The results from the contact angle measurements are consistent with those obtained from the biodegradability tests conducted in this study.

Bioactivity is one of the most important characteristics of bone substitute constructs which is defined as the potential of scaffold to form a carbonate-hydroxyapatite surface layer following implantation in the body. This layer intermediates the chemical bond formation between the scaffold and host tissue which could enhance the integration of the implanted scaffold into the defect site [67]. As expected, by increasing BG content more nucleation sites have developed which have grown in the form of mineral colonies with cauliflower-like appearance. This is due to an increase in silanol groups which provides active sites for hydroxyapatite crystals nucleation and growth [34]. An increase in pH due to the release of alkaline ions such as Na+ and Ca2+ favors the growth of CaP nuclei on the Si-OH functional groups of bioglass. Meanwhile, the negatively charged β-TCP nanoparticles and carboxyl functional groups resulting from acidic degradation of PCL attract Ca2+ ions and develop a Ca-rich layer. This positively charged surface accelerates accumulation of phosphate anions and stimulates CaP mineralization [68].

Viability and proliferation of cultured MG-63 cells on the printed scaffolds were evaluated by quantitative MTT test. The obtained results indicate that printed scaffolds are well biocompatible and properly support cell growth and proliferation. Although increasing the BG percentage in the scaffold composition (β-TCP/BG: 10/90) is associated with an increase in surface roughness, which in turn is suitable for cell adhesion, however subsequent increase in pH value caused by the release of alkaline ions can have an inhibitory effect on the cell's growth in such scaffolds.

SEM analysis was also done to observe the adhesion and morphology of cultured MG-63 cells on the printed scaffolds surface following 72 h of incubation at 37 °C. Osteoblast-like cells with an elongated morphology are attached to the surface of all the scaffolds. Cell population has increased with increasing the BG content. Actively proliferating osteoblasts formed a dense and thick layer of cells on the surface of β-TCP/BG: 30/70 scaffolds. The cells actively produced ECM components that provided an unsmooth appearance to them. At the same time in β-TCP/BG: 10/90 samples, cells were sparsely attached and cells with round morphology were observed. These findings are consistent with MTT results and imply undesirable situations for osteoblasts to settle down and grow. In β-TCP/BG: 70/30 scaffolds, although cells are well attached to the surface of scaffolds and reveal the classic osteoblast cell morphology, cell density is not persuasive. It seems, the hydrophobic nature of the PCL is still dominant, at least to some extent.

According to the MTT assay results and SEM observation, it can be concluded that scaffolds with β-TCP/BG: 30/70 composition provide the most favorable microenvironment for cell colonization and growth.

Conclusion

In this study, different ratios of 45S5 bioglass and β-TCP nanoparticles were used to fabricate polymer-ceramic composite scaffolds with PCL matrix. While both bioceramics possessed nanosize dimensions, different morphology, surface chemistry, and specific surface area led to obvious differences in viscosity of different formulated bioink. It was shown that under constant extrusion pressure, the flow rate of composite inks was decreased with increasing BG content. The composite ink with the highest amount of BG showed the highest viscosity among all blends. Although all printed scaffolds possessed porous structure with interconnected pore system, porosity percentage and shape stability improved by increasing BG content which was accompanied with lower mechanical strength and superior biodegradation and bioactivity of scaffolds. Biological assessments by MTT assay and SEM observation confirmed that printed scaffolds are biocompatible and support cell colonization and proliferation. As an end result, PCL/β-TCP/BG: 30/70 composition showed more appropriate materialistic properties and provided the most favorable microenvironment for cell colonization and growth.
