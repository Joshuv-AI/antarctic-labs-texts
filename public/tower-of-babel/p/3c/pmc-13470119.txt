Glycaemic control during Ramadan in adults with type 1 diabetes using continuous glucose monitoring in Saudi Arabia
Authors: Amal Mohammed Q. Surrati, Eman Alfadhli, Abdulhallem Almuarshi, Alaa Aljohani, Mariam Alanzi, Soaad Aljohany, Nadeen farhan Alanize
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Ramadan fasting poses clinical challenges for adults with type 1 diabetes mellitus (T1DM), particularly with respect to glycemic control. Real-world data describing changes in continuous glucose monitoring (CGM)–derived metrics across Ramadan remain limited. This study assessed changes in CGM-derived glycemic parameters during Ramadan fasting in adults with T1DM and explored whether these changes differed according to baseline glycemic control. This prospective observational real-world study included 81 adults with T1DM who intended to fast during Ramadan 2025 and were routinely followed at two diabetes centers. CGM-derived parameters, including average glucose concentration, time in range (TIR), time above range (TAR), time below range (TBR), and glycemic variability, were evaluated across three predefined periods: pre-Ramadan, the first two weeks of Ramadan, and the last two weeks of Ramadan. Changes over time were assessed using non-parametric paired comparisons. Subgroup analyses were performed according to baseline HbA1c level (≤ 7% vs. >7%) among participants with available data, and findings were interpreted descriptively. The median age of participants was 30 years, and 35.8% were men. Median HbA1c was 8.0% (IQR: 7–9%). TIR declined from the pre-Ramadan period (51.5%) to the first two weeks of Ramadan (45.0%) and remained lower during the last two weeks (47.0%). Average glucose concentrations increased during Ramadan, while TBR and glycemic variability showed no consistent changes. Participants with HbA1c > 7% experienced more pronounced deterioration in glycemic parameters, whereas those with HbA1c ≤ 7% demonstrated smaller and largely transient changes. In this real-world cohort of adults with T1DM, Ramadan fasting was associated with deterioration in CGM-derived glycemic control, particularly among individuals with suboptimal pre-Ramadan glycemic control. These findings highlight the importance of pre-Ramadan assessment, patient education, and CGM use to support safer fasting in routine clinical practice.

Introduction

Ramadan is a holy month for Muslims worldwide, and the ninth month of the Hijri calendar. Fasting, a religious obligation for every mentally sound and physically capable adult Muslim, involves refraining from oral intake from sunrise to sunset and may last between 13 and 14 h in many regions. Despite the Islamic rules permitting individuals with certain health conditions, such as diabetes, to abstain from fasting, many diabetic Muslims still choose to fast due to spiritual motivations, personal beliefs, and cultural practices.

From a medical perspective, individuals with type 1 diabetes mellitus (T1DM) who plan to fast are considered a clinical challenge for the patient, doctor, and even relatives. These patients require careful follow-up and adjustments to their management plan as their daily routines, including meal timing, medication schedules, and physical activity, undergo major changes during Ramadan. These changes can adversely affect metabolic control and, for example, increase the risk of hypoglycaemia, hyperglycaemia, and glycemic variability. Therefore, it is essential to follow evidence-based guidelines to ensure that patients understand how fasting may influence their glycemic control. According to the latest recommendations from the International Diabetes Federation and Diabetes and Ramadan Alliance Group, patients with T1DM are stratified as high-risk to very high-risk for fasting and are generally advised not to fast1.

A multinational study conducted across several Muslim-majority countries reported that 42.8% of individuals with type 1 diabetes mellitus (T1DM) fasted for more than 15 days during Ramadan, with the highest prevalence observed in Saudi Arabia (71.6%)2. Consistent with this high prevalence of fasting, Ramadan has been associated with an increased risk of acute metabolic complications. The Epidemiology of Diabetes and Ramadan (EPIDIAR) study demonstrated that the incidence of severe hypoglycemia increased 4.7-fold among individuals with T1DM and 7.5-fold among those with type 2 diabetes mellitus during Ramadan compared with non-fasting periods3.

The pathophysiological mechanisms underlying these complications include disruptions to the circadian rhythm resulting from altered meal timing, hydration status, and sleep-wake cycles during fasting. These disruptions can affect the hormonal balance of glucagon, cortisol, and catecholamines, thereby exacerbating the risk of hyperglycaemia and diabetic ketoacidosis4.

Another study conducted among patients with T1DM in Saudi Arabia revealed that fasting hurt glycemic control, with the highest glucose spikes recorded after the iftar (at sunset) and suhoor (before sunrise) meals. These post-prandial hyperglycaemic readings persisted during Ramadan and for at least one month afterward2. Furthermore, an international study from Pakistan observed that symptomatic hypoglycaemia occurred in 35.29% of patients with T1DM, whereas hyperglycaemic symptoms were reported in 33.33% during Ramadan5.

Managing and monitoring T1DM during Ramadan therefore present additional challenges for diabetologists and patients alike. Regular self-monitoring of blood glucose or review of continuous glucose monitoring data is essential to detect glycaemic excursions and prevent acute complications. However, adherence to glucose monitoring remains suboptimal. Data from the EPIDIAR study showed that although physicians provided education regarding blood glucose monitoring to 89% of patients with T1DM, only 67% reported regularly monitoring their glucose levels during Ramadan3.

Glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c), while widely used to assess long-term glycemic control, does not capture short-term glucose fluctuations or glycemic variability and is limited in its ability to identify acute hypo- or hyperglycemic events6. In contrast, continuous glucose monitoring (CGM) provides real-time and retrospective information on glucose trends, enabling detailed assessment of time in range (TIR), time above range (TAR), time below range (TBR), and glycemic variability. These metrics offer a more comprehensive evaluation of glycemic control, particularly during periods of altered daily routines such as Ramadan fasting.

The use of CGM has been shown to improve glycemic control, reduce hypoglycemia, and enhance patient engagement in diabetes self-management. In the context of Ramadan, CGM may be especially valuable for identifying fasting- and postprandial-related glucose excursions, supporting timely treatment adjustments, and enhancing the safety of fasting among individuals with type 1 diabetes mellitus7,8.

Individuals with diabetes who choose to fast during Ramadan should undergo a structured pre-Ramadan assessment and receive education regarding physical activity, meal planning, medication adjustments, and the appropriate use of continuous glucose monitoring (CGM). Evidence from a prospective study demonstrated that such an approach was associated with a reduction in acute diabetes-related complications among fasting individuals with either type of diabetes9.

Moreover, the integration of diabetes technology into patient education has been shown to increase the proportion of individuals with type 1 diabetes mellitus (T1DM) who are able to complete fasting safely, without experiencing hypoglycemia or inadequate glycemic control, and to reduce diabetes-related emergency healthcare utilization10. This is particularly relevant given that unrecognized hypoglycemia has been reported to occur frequently during Ramadan, especially during fasting hours11.

Despite growing evidence on the challenges of fasting during Ramadan for individuals with type 1 diabetes mellitus, real-world data describing changes in continuous glucose monitoring (CGM)–derived glycemic parameters across different phases of Ramadan remain limited. In particular, there is a need to better characterize how glycemic patterns evolve from the pre-Ramadan period through early and late fasting, and whether baseline glycemic control influences these changes in routine clinical practice. Accordingly, this study aimed to assess changes in CGM-derived glycemic parameters during Ramadan fasting in adults with type 1 diabetes mellitus by comparing pre-Ramadan values with those observed during the first and last two weeks of Ramadan, and to explore whether these changes differed according to baseline glycemic control, stratified by HbA1c level (≤ 7% vs. >7%) among participants with available HbA1c data.

Methods

Study design and participants

This prospective observational study used convenience sampling to enroll adult patients with T1DM from two specialized diabetes centres in Madinah during Ramadan 2025.

The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the ethical committee of the General Directorate of Health Affairs in Madinah (NCBE-KACST registration number: H-03-M-84). All procedures were conducted in accordance with relevant Saudi regulations and the Declaration of Helsinki.

Recruitment was conducted during the three months preceding Ramadan during routine follow-up visits at the diabetes clinics. Eligible patients were invited to participate and provided access, via a QR code, to an online information page outlining the study objectives, procedures, participant rights, and the electronic consent form. Informed consent was obtained electronically before participants proceeded with the questionnaire.

Inclusion criteria were age ≥ 18 years, a confirmed diagnosis of T1DM, intention to fast during Ramadan, use of continuous glucose monitoring (CGM) for at least four weeks before Ramadan, treatment with multiple daily insulin injections for more than one year, and availability of at least 70% active CGM sensor time during the predefined assessment periods.

Exclusion criteria included pregnancy, cognitive impairment, recent diabetic ketoacidosis, use of insulin pump or automated insulin delivery systems, or insufficient CGM sensor activity (< 70%).

All participants were treated with multiple daily insulin injections (MDI) and received routine follow-up by diabetologists/endocrinologists at the participating centres, with care broadly aligned with Diabetes and Ramadan (DAR) recommendations12. Continuous glucose monitoring was performed using the FreeStyle Libre 2 system, which does not require calibration. No structured Ramadan education program was implemented; instead, treating physicians provided individualized counselling as part of routine clinical care, including advice on insulin dose adjustments, glucose monitoring, and safety instructions for breaking the fast when blood glucose levels were < 70 mg/dL or > 300 mg/dL, or when symptoms suggestive of hypoglycemia occurred.

Data collection

HbA1c levels were obtained from medical records. Data from Continuous glucose monitoring (CGM) data were extracted after Ramadan by a trained specialist nurse using the device report dashboard and recorded in a standardized Excel data collection sheet.

CGM data were analysed over three predefined time intervals: the 14 days preceding Ramadan (pre-Ramadan period), the first two weeks of Ramadan, and the last two weeks of Ramadan. Only participants with at least 70% active sensor time during each assessment period were included in the analysis.

The following CGM-derived parameters were collected for each period:

Average glucose concentration (mg/dL).

Time in range (TIR; 70–180 mg/dL, %).

Time above range (TAR; >180 mg/dL, %).

Time below range (TBR; <70 mg/dL, %).

Glycemic variability (GV, %).

Statistical analysis

Data normality was assessed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test, given the sample size exceeded 50 participants. As most continuous CGM-derived parameters were not normally distributed, results are presented as median and interquartile range (IQR). Paired comparisons were performed using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test.

Comparisons were predefined a priori and limited to two clinically relevant paired contrasts: pre-Ramadan versus the first two weeks of Ramadan, and pre-Ramadan versus the last two weeks of Ramadan. These comparisons were selected to evaluate early and late fasting phases, respectively; no direct comparisons were made between the two Ramadan periods. Effect sizes are presented as absolute median differences between paired time points.

Subgroup analyses were conducted according to baseline HbA1c level (≤ 7% vs. >7%) and were restricted to participants with available HbA1c data; no imputation was performed, and these analyses were considered exploratory. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. Given the hypothesis-driven nature of the predefined comparisons and the observational design of the study, formal adjustment for multiple comparisons was not applied; therefore, p-values were interpreted descriptively in conjunction with the magnitude and direction of observed changes. All analyses were performed using SPSS version 25.

Results

A total of 121 patients agreed to participate; however, 81 were included in the final analysis, as 40 participants were excluded due to insufficient CGM sensor activity (< 70%). The mean age of participants was 30 years; 29 (35.8%) were men and 52 (64.2%) were women. The median HbA1c level was 8.0% (IQR: 7–9). The median weight was 66 kg, and the median body mass index was 25 kg/m².

Whole-group analysis

Across the entire cohort, glycaemic control showed a consistent pattern of deterioration during Ramadan. Median time in range (TIR) decreased from 51.5% before Ramadan to 45.0% during the first two weeks (median reduction of 6.5% points; p = 0.004) and remained lower during the last two weeks at 47.0% (median reduction of 4.5% points; p = 0.002).

Median average glucose concentrations increased during Ramadan compared with pre-Ramadan values, rising from 177.5 mg/dL to 183.0 mg/dL in the first two weeks and to 185.0 mg/dL in the last two weeks (p = 0.011 and p = 0.026, respectively). Time above range (TAR) showed a modest increase during Ramadan, with a more pronounced rise observed in the last two weeks (median increase of 5% points; p = 0.020). In the controlled group, changes in CGM metrics during Ramadan were generally small and transient. Median average glucose increased slightly during the first two weeks (from 160 to 161 mg/dL) and decreased during the last two weeks (to 152 mg/dL). Median TIR decreased modestly from 57% to 54% during the first two weeks but returned to baseline levels during the last two weeks. No consistent changes were observed in TAR, TBR, or glycaemic variability.

In contrast, participants with HbA1c > 7% demonstrated more pronounced and sustained changes during Ramadan. Median average glucose increased from 181 mg/dL before Ramadan to 198 mg/dL during the first two weeks and remained elevated at 194 mg/dL during the last two weeks. Median TIR declined substantially from 46% before Ramadan to 35% in the first two weeks and remained reduced at 39% in the last two weeks. TAR increased during Ramadan, particularly in the last two weeks (from 48% to 55%). TBR and glycaemic variability showed minimal change across study periods.

Discussion

This study provides real-world insights into changes in CGM-derived glycemic parameters during Ramadan fasting in adults with type 1 diabetes mellitus. Overall, Ramadan fasting was associated with a reduction in time in range and an increase in average glucose concentrations, with more pronounced changes observed among individuals with suboptimal pre-Ramadan glycemic control. This pattern is consistent with previous reports indicating a greater burden of hyperglycemia than hypoglycemia during Ramadan among patients with poorly controlled T1DM13. Similarly, a three-year observational study demonstrated recurrent postprandial hyperglycemic excursions after iftar and suhoor, with abnormalities becoming more pronounced over time and persisting beyond the Ramadan period, suggesting that insulin dose adjustments during fasting may be underestimated in routine clinical practice2.

In contrast, several studies have reported stable or improved glycemic control during Ramadan among selected adults with type 1 diabetes who received structured education and intensive glucose monitoring. A comparative study using flash glucose monitoring found no significant differences in time in range, time above range, time below range, average glucose, or glycemic variability before, during, or after Ramadan among patients considered suitable for fasting based on Diabetes and Ramadan (DAR) risk stratification14. Similarly, studies evaluating optimized diabetes care—including Ramadan-focused education, dietary counseling, insulin dose adjustment, and flash glucose monitoring—have demonstrated that fasting can be achieved without deterioration in glycemic control and, in some cases, with modest improvement in HbA1c15. Moreover, trials incorporating structured education programs and proactive insulin adjustment protocols, such as Dose Adjustment For Normal Eating (DAFNE)–based interventions combined with advanced glucose monitoring, reported reductions in hypoglycemia rates during Ramadan and no increase in severe metabolic complications, despite a slight increase in time spent above range16.

The divergence between these findings and our results likely reflects differences in study design and clinical context. Unlike the aforementioned studies, which enrolled carefully selected patients with uncomplicated T1DM and implemented standardized education and insulin adjustment protocols, our study reflects routine clinical practice in which Ramadan education and insulin adjustments were individualized rather than delivered through a structured program. These observations highlight the critical role of structured education, frequent glucose monitoring, and proactive insulin dose adjustment in maintaining glycemic stability during Ramadan fasting.

Baseline glycemic control emerged as an important modifier of glycemic responses to Ramadan fasting. Participants with higher pre-Ramadan HbA1c levels appeared more vulnerable to adverse glycemic changes, whereas those with better baseline control experienced only mild and transient alterations in CGM-derived metrics. These findings are consistent with a study involving adolescents with T1DM, which demonstrated that poorer pre-Ramadan glycemic control was associated with higher mean glucose levels, greater glycemic variability, and increased exposure to both hypoglycemia and hyperglycemia during fasting17. Collectively, these data support the concept that achieving optimal glycemic control before Ramadan may reduce glucose excursions and help identify individuals in whom fasting may be better tolerated under appropriate clinical supervision.

Previous evidence regarding the risk of severe hyperglycemia and diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA) during Ramadan has been mixed. The landmark EPIDIAR study reported an increase in acute hyperglycemic complications, including DKA, among individuals with type 1 diabetes who fasted during Ramadan, suggesting a potential elevated risk in inadequately controlled patients3. In contrast, more recent regional studies have found no significant increase in DKA incidence during Ramadan compared with non-Ramadan periods18. In the present study, no episodes of diabetic ketoacidosis were observed during the Ramadan period. These discrepancies may be explained by differences in study era, patient selection, baseline glycemic control, and access to structured Ramadan-focused diabetes care.

The strengths of this study include the use of continuous glucose monitoring to capture detailed, longitudinal glycemic patterns during Ramadan and the exploration of differences according to baseline glycemic control in a real-world clinical setting. However, several limitations should be acknowledged. The observational design and absence of a non-fasting control group limit causal inference. Subgroup analyses were exploratory and based on relatively small sample sizes, particularly in the HbA1c ≤ 7% group. HbA1c data were unavailable for a subset of participants, potentially limiting subgroup comparisons. Baseline clinical characteristics beyond sex were not systematically collected, and lifestyle behaviours were not standardized, reflecting real-world clinical practice. Finally, predefined pairwise comparisons were interpreted descriptively, and findings should be considered alongside the magnitude and direction of observed changes.

In conclusion, Ramadan fasting was associated with measurable changes in glycemic control in adults with type 1 diabetes mellitus, with more pronounced deterioration observed among individuals with suboptimal pre-Ramadan glycemic control. These findings emphasise the importance of pre-Ramadan assessment, patient education, and the use of continuous glucose monitoring to support safer fasting and informed clinical decision-making in this population.
