QTc Prolongation in Stress-Induced Cardiomyopathy
Authors: Jordan Holthe, Bradley Ternus, Rekha Mankad, Fadi Adel
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundStress-induced cardiomyopathy (SCMP) is a transient cardiac syndrome often mimicking acute coronary syndrome. It is commonly associated with QT prolongation and can lead to serious dysrhythmias.Case SummaryA 73-year-old man with severe chronic obstructive pulmonary disorder developed SCMP during an acute chronic obstructive pulmonary disorder exacerbation, complicated by marked QTc prolongation (>650 ms) and recurrent pulseless polymorphic ventricular tachycardia. Invasive coronary angiography showed no obstructive coronary artery disease. Arrhythmias persisted despite electrolyte correction. Ultimately, pacing at 100 beats/min shortened QTc and prevented arrests.DiscussionAlthough often self-limiting, stress cardiomyopathy may cause QT prolongation and arrhythmias. An underutilized therapy is high-rate pacing, which shortens QT and reduces early afterdepolarizations.Take-Home MessagesQT prolongation is not uncommon in SCMP, and severe prolongation often occurs, predisposing to polymorphic ventricular tachycardia and cardiac arrest. High-rate pacing is a highly effective, underutilized tool for acute management of severe QTc prolongation with associated ventricular tachycardia.Key words: arrhythmia, pacing, QT prolongation, stress-induced cardiomyopathy

History of Presentation

A 73-year-old man with severe chronic obstructive pulmonary disorder (COPD) (Gold Stage 3, forced expiratory volume in 1 second 37%) presented to the emergency department with shortness of breath. He reported that prior to the past 24 hours, he had been in his usual state of health. He denied any chest pain, arm/jaw pain, cough, rhinorrhea, sore throat, fever, nausea, vomiting, or abdominal pain. He did not have any orthopnea or paroxysmal nocturnal dyspnea. He did not have any lower extremity swelling. He was not able to self-identify any precipitating causes for his increased shortness of breath. Vitals on presentation included a temperature of 36.6 °C, heart rate of 70 beats/min, respiratory rate of 44 breaths per minute, and blood pressure of 120/102 mm Hg. He was found to be in acute hypoxemic respiratory failure and was subsequently initiated on bilevel positive airway pressure ventilation therapy.

Past Medical History

His medical history includes permanent atrial fibrillation status post atrioventricular nodal ablation (AVNA) and Micra VR leadless pacemaker (MC1VR01) implantation. Notably, AV nodal conduction was subsequently reestablished following the initial AVNA, necessitating a repeat ablation procedure. He was therapeutically anticoagulated on apixaban. Additional comorbidities include pulmonary hypertension (likely groups 2 and 3, right ventricular systolic pressure 51 mm Hg in February 2022), essential hypertension, heart failure with preserved ejection fraction (left ventricular ejection fraction [LVEF] 51% in February 2022), chronic normocytic anemia, and alcohol use disorder.

Investigations

Laboratory tests showed a mildly elevated white blood cell count of 10.1 × 103/μL. Creatinine was elevated at 1.38 mg/dL (baseline ∼ 0.77 mg/dL), glucose 172 mg/dL, and magnesium of 1.6 mg/dL (low-normal). Electrolytes were otherwise normal, including potassium. Lactate was elevated at 4.81 mmol/L. Further laboratory tests revealed elevated but stable fifth-generation troponin T assays (baseline and 2-hour levels: 155 ng/L-127 ng/L) and elevated N terminal pro–B-type natriuretic peptide at 15,657 pg/mL. No drug or alcohol testing was performed given low clinical suspicion for intoxication. Chest x-ray revealed an irregular mass in the left lower lung measuring approximately 5 cm concerning for malignancy. Initial electrocardiogram (ECG) revealed ventricularly paced rhythm at 70 beats/min with underlying atrial fibrillation and prolonged QTc of 525 ms. Leadless pacemaker was interrogated and demonstrated ventricularly paced rhythm at 70 beats/min. There was no evidence of ventricular arrhythmia.

Management

He was given 1 L bolus of 0.9% normal saline, 2 g of ceftriaxone, 100 mg doxycycline, and 10 mg of dexamethasone. Attempts to wean bilevel positive airway pressure were unsuccessful and he was admitted to the medical intensive care unit (ICU). He requested full code on admission. He did not require any pressors. Very shortly after admission to the medical ICU, the patient developed pulseless polymorphic ventricular tachycardia (VT) (Figure 1) in the setting of a prolonged QTc interval of 587 ms (Figure 2, Bazett formula, lead II). Cardiopulmonary resuscitation was performed for less than 1 minute, with return of spontaneous circulation. He denied any form of chest pain, palpitations or worsening shortness of breath before this episode. The patient was then administered intravenous magnesium. A transthoracic echocardiogram revealed a depressed LVEF of 31% with regional wall motion abnormalities in the basal septal and anterior segments, mid-septal and anterior/anterolateral and apical regions (Figure 3, Videos 1 to 4). The right ventricle was moderately enlarged with moderately reduced systolic function (right ventricular systolic pressure 74 mm Hg) and severe tricuspid regurgitation due to annular dilatation. The findings were concerning for possible coronary ischemia. A computed tomography pulmonary angiogram showed no evidence of pulmonary embolism, although calcifications were noted in the left anterior descending artery.

A repeat electrocardiogram in the afternoon demonstrated a further prolonged QTc of 680 ms (Figure 4, Bazett formula, lead II). Later that evening, the patient experienced a second episode of pulseless polymorphic VT arrest. Cardiopulmonary resuscitation was initiated, and the patient was defibrillated without an immediate change in rhythm. He received 1 mg of epinephrine and 60 mg of lidocaine. Return of spontaneous circulation was achieved after 5 minutes, and the patient was placed on continuous epinephrine and lidocaine infusion. He had an additional 40 mg lidocaine bolus. He was also subsequently intubated for airway protection.

Repeat 12-Lead Electrocardiogram Showing Worsening QTc Prolongation

Repeat electrocardiogram later the same day showing further QTc prolongation to 680 ms. The marked repolarization abnormality was associated with recurrent ventricular arrhythmias.

Given the persistent QTc prolongation and risk of recurrent arrhythmia, the consulting cardiologist recommended increasing the pacemaker rate to 100 beats/min. This was achieved noninvasively at the bedside using the leadless pacemaker programmer, without the need for a transvenous temporary pacing catheter placement. A subsequent ECG demonstrated a significantly shortened QTc of approximately 508 ms (Figure 5, Bazett formula, lead II), effectively reducing the arrhythmogenic substrate. Due to concern for cardiac ischemia driving his episodes, the patient was loaded with aspirin and initiated on a heparin drip. His potassium levels were maintained above 4 mEq/L and magnesium levels above 2 mg/dL. A lidocaine drip was continued at 2 mg/min to further mitigate the risk of recurrent arrhythmias.

Repeat electrocardiogram following pacemaker rate increase to 100 beats/min, demonstrating QTc shortening to 508 ms. The intervention successfully reduced the arrhythmogenic substrate and prevented further cardiac arrest.

Outcome and Follow-Up

His electrolytes remained within normal limits during his time in the medical ICU, and he was not on any home medications known to prolong the QTc interval. He had an invasive coronary angiogram that showed only mild coronary artery disease (Figure 6, Videos 5 to 8). Stress-induced cardiomyopathy (SCMP) secondary to acute exacerbation of COPD was suspected as the etiology for the prolonged QTc and subsequent pulseless polymorphic VT arrests. After increasing his pacing heart rate, he had no further arrests during his hospital stay. He was maintained at 100 beats/min for 4 days, and then the rate was decreased to 90 beats/min. During his hospitalization, he also had an implantable cardioverter-defibrillator (ICD) placed. This decision was made in the setting of 2 resuscitated pulseless VT arrests, QTc exceeding 650 ms refractory to pharmacologic management, and ongoing uncertainty regarding reversibility at the time of implant. His COPD exacerbation resolved and he was initiated on low-dose metoprolol succinate for SCMP. Repeat transthoracic echocardiography 10 days later showed LVEF recovery to an estimated 55% to 60% without regional wall motion abnormalities, further supporting the diagnosis of SCMP. The patient was transitioned back to a general medical floor for ongoing management. Unfortunately, the patient had multiple difficult-to-manage pneumothoraces and other complications that prolonged his hospital stay. Ultimately, the patient was discharged home on hospice care and his ICD was deactivated after goals-of-care conference made with the family in the setting of his debilitated state, poor oral intake, severe COPD, and overall poor prognosis.

Discussion

SCMP is a reversible cardiac condition characterized by transient systolic dysfunction in the setting of physical or emotional stress.1 Often, the clinical presentation of SCMP mimics acute coronary syndrome, with patients typically presenting with acute chest pain or shortness of breath, electrocardiographic changes, elevated troponins, and left ventricular wall motion abnormalities. It can be differentiated by the absence of obstructive coronary disease on angiography.2 The exact pathophysiology remains incompletely understood but is believed to involve catecholamine surge, microvascular dysfunction, and myocardial stunning.1

Diagnosis is based on the 4 components of the revised Mayo Clinic criteria: transient hypokinesis, akinesis, or dyskinesis of the left ventricular mid-segments, with or without apical involvement (the regional wall motion abnormalities extend beyond a single epicardial vascular distribution, and a stressful trigger is often, but not always, present); absence of obstructive coronary disease or angiographic evidence of acute plaque rupture; new electrocardiographic abnormalities (either ST-segment elevation and/or T-wave inversion) or modest elevation in cardiac troponin; and absence of pheochromocytoma and myocarditis.3

SCMP most commonly presents in the apical form, which is characterized by apical ballooning and basal hyperkinesis and accounts for approximately 80% of cases.4 Less frequent variants of SCMP include the basal form, the mid-ventricular form, and the focal form.4 In this case, the distribution of the wall motion abnormalities (involving basal, mid, and apical segments simultaneously) does not conform to any of these classic variants. Rather, the pattern is more consistent with a diffuse phenotype, which is a less common presentation. However, the revised Mayo Clinic criteria do not require conformity to a specific morphological subtype, requiring only that the wall motion abnormalities extend beyond a single epicardial vascular distribution, a criterion met in this case. The atypical distribution may reflect the severity of the catecholamine-mediated insult in the context of acute hypoxemic respiratory failure. Recognition of these phenotypic differences is important, as they may mimic different acute coronary syndromes and have distinct wall motion abnormalities that can be identified on echocardiography.

An underrecognized complication of SCMP is significant prolongation of the QT interval, which can predispose patients to malignant ventricular arrhythmias, including polymorphic VT and Torsades de pointes.5,6 In this case, the patient exhibited a markedly prolonged QTc interval (>650 ms) following the onset of SCMP in the setting of an acute exacerbation of COPD. Importantly, he developed recurrent episodes of pulseless polymorphic VT requiring multiple resuscitation efforts.

QTc prolongation in SCMP is thought to result from a combination of repolarization abnormalities caused by myocardial stunning, autonomic dysregulation, and possible electrolyte shifts.7 Despite maintaining normal electrolyte levels and avoiding known QT-prolonging medications, this patient's QTc remained markedly prolonged, leading to life-threatening arrhythmias. Dexamethasone may contribute to QTc prolongation via electrolyte shifts (particularly with regard to hypokalemia and hypomagnesemia), and the contribution of this medication cannot entirely be excluded. However, electrolytes were monitored and repleted throughout the hospitalization, the patient had prolonged QTc on admission, and the QTc worsened progressively in a pattern more suggestive of myocardial stunning rather than isolated drug toxicity. Therefore, this contribution may be a potential cofactor but is unlikely the primary etiology.

Management of QTc prolongation in SCMP primarily involves correcting electrolytes, discontinuing QT-prolonging medications, and close monitoring.7 However, in cases of recurrent arrhythmias, additional therapeutic strategies may be needed. One effective, yet underutilized intervention is temporary pacing at an elevated rate. By increasing the heart rate, the QT interval is shortened (via rate adaptation), thereby reducing the window for early afterdepolarizations and subsequent ventricular arrhythmias.8 In our patient, increasing the pacemaker rate to 100 beats/min resulted in significant QTc shortening and stabilization of the arrhythmia burden. Importantly, this was achieved noninvasively using the existing leadless pacemaker programmer at the bedside, which allowed for avoidance of the need for emergent transvenous pacing catheter placement in a critically ill, anticoagulated patient in the ICU.

Although SCMP itself is usually a self-limiting condition treated with supportive management, the risk of sudden cardiac death due to ventricular arrhythmias may be high, especially in patients with significant QT prolongation.9 Temporary rate pacing serves as an important bridge therapy, particularly in patients with preexisting pacemakers or defibrillators. By mitigating arrhythmic risk, it allowed for further evaluation of underlying causes, including possible acute coronary syndrome, which was pursued for definitive assessment the following morning in this patient case.

ICD insertion during the acute phase of SCMP, before confirmed recovery, is not routinely recommended. In this case, the decision to implant was driven by the occurrence of 2 resuscitated pulseless polymorphic VT arrests within a single hospitalization, QTc prolongation exceeding 650 ms refractory to pharmacologic management and electrolyte optimization, and significant uncertainty at the time of implant. In addition, the patient's known pacing dependency in the context of prior AVNA further heightened clinical concern for ongoing arrhythmogenic risk. In retrospect, the LVEF recovered to 55% to 60% by day 10 and the ICD was subsequently deactivated following a goals-of-care discussion. This case illustrates the tension clinicians face when making device decisions under conditions of clinical urgency and diagnostic uncertainty in the acute phase of potentially reversible cardiomyopathy. Whenever feasible, current guidelines favor deferring ICD implantation in SCMP until left ventricular recovery is confirmed; however, refractory recurrent arrest may justify earlier implantation in high-risk patients.10

It is important to acknowledge distinct limitations of this report. All QTc measurements were calculated using the Bazett formula (QTcB = QT/√RR). The Bazett formula is known to overcorrect at higher heart rates, which introduces a methodological confound when comparing QTc values obtained at 70 vs 100 beats/min.11 The magnitude of QTc shortening reported using the Bazett formula may therefore partially reflect formula overcorrection rather than a true biological QT shortening alone. Furthermore, telemetry review did not reveal short-long-short sequences preceding polymorphic VT onset (Figure 1), likely reflecting the fixed pacing rate in the setting of prior AVNA, which constrains the RR variability required for classic pause-dependent Torsades de pointes initiation. The arrhythmia appears to arise directly in the setting of marked QTc prolongation, consistent with spontaneous early afterdepolarization–mediated triggered activity, further supporting overdrive pacing as the mechanistically appropriate intervention.

Conclusions

This case highlights the importance of recognizing QTc prolongation as a serious complication of SCMP and demonstrates the utility of higher-rate pacing in managing this arrhythmogenic risk. Clinicians should maintain a high index of suspicion for SCMP in patients presenting with stress-induced myocardial dysfunction and QTc prolongation, even in the absence of chest pain, especially in high-risk settings such as COPD exacerbations.

Take-Home Messages

•Temporary high-rate pacing can rapidly shorten QTc and suppress malignant ventricular arrhythmias in SCMP complicated by pronounced QT prolongation, especially in patients with existing pacemakers.
