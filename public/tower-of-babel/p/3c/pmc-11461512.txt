Research on an identification model for mine water inrush sources based on the HBA-CatBoost algorithm
Authors: Jin Xu, Lulin Zheng, Hong Lan, Yujun Zuo, Bo Li, Shiyu Tian, Youwen Tian
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Accurate and efficient identification of water inrush sources, as one of the three critical elements of mine water hazards, is crucial for mine water management. To identify the sources of mine water inrush effectively, a model named HBA-CatBoost is introduced. This model is established on the hybrid bat algorithm (HBA)-optimized category feature gradient boosting tree (CatBoost), and the shapley additive explanation (SHAP) method is employed to elucidate the model’s decision-making process. Given the prevalent occurrence of water hazards in coal seam roofs and floors in the northern Guizhou coalfield, coupled with the challenges in pinpointing water inrush sources in mines, the HBA-CatBoost model is tested at the Longfeng Coal Mine in northern Guizhou to validate its practicality. Comparative analysis with the HBA-RF, HBA-XGBoost, CatBoost, RF, and XGBoost models demonstrates that the hybrid bat algorithm significantly enhances the classification performance of the CatBoost model, resulting in improved convergence speed and classification accuracy. The HBA-CatBoost model outperforms the aforementioned models in terms of classification effectiveness, achieving accuracy, recall, precision, and F1 scores of 96.43%, 97.22%, 96.43%, and 96.61%, respectively. The SHAP method elucidates the decision mechanism of the optimal HBA-CatBoost model, highlighting the significance of sample features and bolstering the model’s credibility. These outcomes underscore the superior performance of the HBA-CatBoost model and its potential for effectively identifying water inrush sources in mines.

Introduction

Coal mine water hazards, recognized as one of the five primary disasters in coal mining operations, are responsible for substantial human casualties and property damage1,2. The period from 2008 to 2023 featured 164 water hazard incidents in Chinese coal mines, resulting in 776 fatalities3–5, underscoring the persistent severity of the coal mine water hazard situation in China. The management of mine water inrush involves both early prevention and subsequent treatment phases. Within the control realm of mine water hazards, water-filling sources are recognized as one of the three pivotal elements of mine water hazards6, and accurate and prompt identification of water inflow sources holds paramount importance. This not only facilitates the development of effective treatment strategies based on the nature of the water source in later stages but also enables the early anticipation of potential inrush water sources, thereby fostering a shift from process-oriented management to source-focused prevention7,8. Hence, the precise and swift identification of water inflow sources, coupled with the prediction of inrush water characteristics, is a critical aspect of mine water hazard prevention efforts and emergency response protocols.

The traditional techniques employed for pinpointing inflow water sources in mines include isotope analysis9,10, water temperature11, water level12, water chemistry analysis13,14, and GIS theory15. Among these methods, water chemistry analysis has emerged as a widely researched and commonly utilized approach. Distinct types of water sources or aquifers present varying ion concentrations. As water sources within aquifers undergo migration or alteration, their ion concentrations experience corresponding changes. Consequently, researchers have utilized different ions as the primary basis for identification, constructing models to discern inrush water sources. These models encompass mathematical frameworks such as multivariate statistics, fuzzy comprehensive discrimination, and Bayesian discrimination, alongside machine learning models such as neural networks and support vector machines16–20. Presently, machine learning models represent a focal point of extensive research efforts within the field. Numerous scholars have endeavoured to enhance these models through diverse optimization strategies, yielding notable outcomes. Noteworthy advancements include the refinement of convolutional neural network models and feedforward neural network models utilizing algorithms such as the frog-leaping algorithm and stochastic gradient descent algorithm, thereby bolstering the discrimination accuracy of these models21,22.

Furthermore, optimization techniques involving algorithms such as Fisher and Grey Wolf have been applied to refine support vector machines and extreme learning machines, resulting in accelerated model iteration optimization and heightened accuracy in water source identification18,23,24. While advancements have been made in enhancing model performance, challenges such as low efficiency, susceptibility to local optima, and unclear discrimination mechanisms persist. To mitigate these problems, ensemble algorithms, which amalgamate individual learning algorithms, have emerged as viable solutions. These algorithms can explore optimal solutions from multiple starting points, demonstrate robust generalization capabilities, and exhibit reduced susceptibility to local optima25. Ensemble classification algorithms such as adaptive boosting (AdaBoost), random forest (RF), extreme gradient boosting (XGBoost), and category feature gradient boosting (CatBoost) are frequently utilized. Notably, CatBoost, as one of the most sophisticated integrated classification algorithms, has undergone significant enhancements in category feature processing, resulting in superior classification performance. Currently, researchers have successfully utilized CatBoost in the classification and prediction of beach water quality, yielding promising outcomes26. However, the application of this technology in the domain of mine water inrush source identification remains rare.

In addition, a single algorithm model, when applied without optimization, often yields suboptimal results. Consequently, many researchers who utilize machine learning algorithms for outcome assessment frequently find it necessary to optimize their models to better align with the specific requirements of their studies. This optimization process is essential for achieving more reliable results. For instance, in the study aimed at identifying the geochemical characteristics of valuable deposits using regional geochemical data from stream sediments, the K-means clustering algorithm model optimized by the Grey Wolf Optimizer (GWO) demonstrates greater accuracy compared to the standard K-means clustering model27. In the study of predicting coalbed methane well production, a multi-head attention mechanism combined with a convolutional neural network (CNN) is employed to enhance the prediction model of bidirectional long short-term memory (Bi-LSTM). The prediction results for CBM well production are compared with those obtained from single models, such as long short-term memory (LSTM) and gated recurrent unit (GRU). The findings indicate that the CNN-BL-MHA model significantly improves the prediction accuracy of CBM well production28. Thus, addressing the aforementioned deficiencies and building upon previous research, this study employs the CatBoost model to investigate the identification of sources of mine water inrush. A hybrid bat algorithm is employed to optimize the hyperparameters of the CatBoost model, with the objective of improving the model’s global search capability and prediction accuracy. Additionally, the SHAP method is utilized to elucidate the model’s decision-making process, enhancing its interpretability and credibility. This research aims to present a novel technical approach for identifying water inrush sources in mines.

Materials

Study area

The northern Guizhou coalfield (Fig. 1), which is located in the northern region of Guizhou Province, China, is the third largest coalfield in the province and is renowned for its substantial coal reservoirs. However, coal extraction operations in this area face significant challenges due to the prevalence of karst caves within the roofs and floors of coal seams, leading to recurrent water inrush incidents. Notably, on August 21, 2019, the Longfeng Coal Mine, a representative mine in the region, experienced a roof water inrush incident, tragically claiming the lives of two individuals. Positioned on the Yunnan–Guizhou Plateau, the Longfeng Coal Mine is characterized by a predominantly karst landscape featuring a low to medium karst peak cluster terrain29. The prevailing structural orientation of the mining site follows a near-southeast to northwest monocline pattern, encompassing 57 concealed faults with a vertical displacement of less than 3 m within the vicinity30.

The hydrogeological characteristics of the Longfeng Coal Mine are delineated based on the classification of karst aquifers. The water-bearing strata above the mine are primarily the Upper Permian Changxing Formation and the Lower Triassic Yelang Formation limestone confined aquifers, whereas the underlying water-bearing strata are the Middle Permian Maokou Formation limestone confined aquifers (Fig. 2). The Longtan Formation represents the primary coal-bearing strata in the region, characterized by a significant number of coal seams that form a closely spaced coal seam group. Consequently, the aquiclude separating these coal seams is relatively thin. Moreover, the area has experienced extensive karst and fissure development, resulting in diverse forms of water supply in the overlying aquifer31. These factors contribute to the challenge of identifying the water-filling sources for underground water inrush incidents, making water prevention and control efforts complex. Given the comprehensive assessment of hydrogeological conditions, coal seam occurrence, mining intensity, and the ecological environment, the Longfeng Coal Mine stands out as a representative site in northern Guizhou. Therefore, the Longfeng Coal Mine was chosen as the focal research area for this study.

Sample collection and processing

Data collection

In light of the hydrogeological conditions within the study area, four distinct water sample types were chosen for investigation: surface water, limestone karst water sourced from the upper Permian Changxing Formation, limestone karst water sourced from the middle Permian Maokou Formation, and accumulated water from the Old Kiln and goaf. A total of 21 water samples were directly collected onsite within the research area. These samples were carefully collected in 1-litre pure water bottles, which were meticulously precleaned with the respective water samples to be analysed 2 to 3 times before sampling32. The pH levels of the water samples were subsequently measured promptly and recorded onsite prior to collection. The gathered water samples were then meticulously preserved in a 4 °C insulated bag to prevent exposure to light and were promptly transported to the water chemistry laboratory within 72 h for analysis in accordance with the Groundwater Quality Analysis Method (DZ/T0064-2021). Additionally, to comprehensively summarize the water quality attributes of the primary aquifers in the study area and improve the precision of identifying water inrush sources, an additional 59 water chemistry datasets were integrated into the test sample set based on existing research data from the mining region. To establish a robust nonlinear correlation between water chemical composition and mine water origin, seven key features, including total dissolved solids (TDS), N++K+, Ca2+, Mg2+, SO42−, HCO3−, and Cl−, were employed as input variables, with numerical values of 0, 1, 2, and 3 denoting surface water, Changxing limestone water, Maokou limestone water, and old goaf water types, respectively, as output classifications.

Data preprocessing

In response to the initial data obtained, the “info()” method in the Pandas tool was employed to identify and fill in missing values. The isolation forest (IF) algorithm was subsequently utilized, and the “decision_function” from the Python toolbox was used to evaluate outliers in the dataset33. Ultimately, a total of 10 outlier data points were eliminated, effectively reducing the interference of low-frequency noise on the data.

Groundwater undergoes intricate hydrogeochemical processes during enrichment and transport, establishing close relationships among the aforementioned factors. To avoid excessive information overlap among input feature variables and the occurrence of multicollinearity issues, Pearson correlation analysis was conducted using SPSS software. Pearson correlation analysis can reveal the similarity and consistency of changes in various chemical components in groundwater samples. For detailed results34, please refer to Table 1.

Methodology

Basics of HBA and CatBoost

HBA algorithm

The bat algorithm (BA) is a heuristic search algorithm grounded in swarm intelligence, offering an efficient approach for seeking global optimal solutions. This algorithm functions through iterative optimization, commences with a collection of random solutions, progressively seeks the optimal solution, and creates new local solutions via random flights around the optimal solution to bolster local search efficiency. Nonetheless, the algorithm is not without limitations, including slow convergence speed, vulnerability to local optima, and diminished solution accuracy35.

The hybrid bat algorithm (HBA) is a proficient algorithm designed for resolving unconstrained optimization problems by enhancing the bat algorithm through the incorporation of chaotic sequences, Powell local search, and mutation strategies. Compared with the fundamental bat algorithm and particle swarm optimization algorithm, HBA has greater optimization capabilities and performance36.

CatBoost algorithm

The CatBoost algorithm represents an enhanced implementation within the gradient boosting decision tree (GBDT) algorithm framework. In contrast to the XGBoost and LightGBM algorithms, which operate in the same framework, the CatBoost algorithm leverages symmetric binary trees as base models37and facilitates numerical encoding of categorical features38. This characteristic contributes to accelerated prediction speed and heightened prediction accuracy39.

A significant innovation of CatBoost lies in its utilization of a method that differs from one-hot encoding by transforming categorical features into label values based on statistical target values. This involves converting categorical variables into numerical encoding in the input feature space and using the mean value corresponding to the labels as the criterion for node splitting. This approach effectively achieves numerical encoding of categorical features. This methodology addresses conditional shifts induced by greedy algorithms, enhancing the training process for labels. Moreover, the utilization of symmetric binary trees as base models distinguishes CatBoost from XGBoost and LightGBM, leading to improved prediction speed and the ability to predict multiple samples simultaneously. Furthermore, CatBoost mitigates prediction bias stemming from gradient estimation bias by employing ordered boosting in place of gradient estimation40. Nonetheless, the CatBoost algorithm entails a multitude of hyperparameters necessitating adjustment, a task that can prove time-intensive and is subject to a notable degree of randomness during the tuning phase. To mitigate these challenges associated with optimizing multiple parameters, the robust HBA is a viable solution. To assess the predictive performance of the model for each classification, four fundamental metrics are utilized: true positive (TP), false positive (FP), true negative (TN), and false negative (FN). To provide a comprehensive evaluation of the model’s predictive efficacy, the accuracy, precision, recall, and F1 score are subsequently calculated41.

Results and discussion

Dataset analysis

The dataset comprises six primary features: N++K+, Ca2+, Mg2+, SO42−, HCO3−, and Cl−. However, due to groundwater‒rock interactions in the study area, there is an overlap of water quality parameters in the aquifer, as depicted in Fig. 4. This overlap presents a challenge in accurately and efficiently distinguishing between different water inrush sources. Moreover, factors such as human error or equipment malfunctions during sample collection and processing can introduce anomalies in the water sample data, causing significant deviations from the initial values43. Therefore, the utilization of the isolation forest algorithm and the “info()” method is essential for detecting and eliminating outliers in the dataset and addressing missing values.

Furthermore, as illustrated in Fig. 4, relying solely on the Piper trilinear diagram is inadequate for effectively identifying distinct water sources44. This highlights the necessity of incorporating machine learning models for the swift and precise identification of various water sources.

Various models require different hyperparameters to be chosen. Nonetheless, for the tree model, three key hyperparameters stand out: the number of trees, the learning rate and the search depth45. The number of trees denotes the total quantity of trees constructed collectively. Typically, a greater number of trees enhances model performance; however, once a specific threshold is reached, the accuracy no longer increases, and the computational load for training the model increases progressively. Consequently, it is essential to establish the optimal number of trees for the model. The learning rate signifies the magnitude of adjustment made to model weights in each iteration, determining the rate at which the model converges during training. An excessively high learning rate can lead to divergence, causing the model to miss the optimal solution; conversely, a shallow learning rate may result in slow convergence, resulting in failure to reach the optimal solution within a limited number of iterations. The search depth is governed primarily by the maximum tree depth, which directly impacts the model’s complexity and fitting capability. An overly large depth can lead to overfitting, resulting in poor performance on test data, whereas a depth that is too small may lead to underfitting, causing the model to overlook intricate data relationships. In the RF and XGBoost models, the maximum tree depth is regulated by two factors: the minimum number of samples (min_samples_leaf) and the split gain threshold (gamma). Therefore, the tuning process primarily targets these three key hyperparameters. While other parameters are recognized, they are not the current focal points. Model interpretation

Currently, notable progress has been made in water inrush source identification technology, particularly within the domain of machine learning, where researchers and experts have introduced a range of convenient and effective methodologies. While these machine learning techniques offer heightened accuracy compared with traditional linear models, they often compromise the interpretability inherent in linear models. This interpretability deficit hampers researchers’ ability to comprehend how specific sample features influence prediction outcomes46, resulting in predictive models being perceived as “black box” with diminished credibility. In this investigation, the SHAP method was employed to elucidate the workings of the HBA-CatBoost model47, furnishing direct visual representations to evaluate both the global and local rationales of the model. This methodological approach bolsters the interpretability of the model’s predictive mechanisms, thereby fostering greater confidence among users in the model’s reliability.

The summary plot depicting feature importance derived from SHAP interpretation (Fig. 9) illustrates that Na±+K± and SO42− ions are the most pivotal features in water inrush source identification. Additionally, the intricate feature importance analysis gleaned from the plot specifies that SO42− ions serve as the predominant feature for surface water inrush sources, whereas HCO3− ions play a central role in old goaf water inrush sources. These results imply that the most significant chemical signature varies between different classes.

Study significance and outlook

Compared with established water inrush source identification models, the HBA-CatBoost classification model proposed in this study achieves exceptional accuracy and precision in identifying water sources based solely on conventional hydrochemical ion components. When tested on a subset derived from existing samples, the model achieves impressive performance metrics, including accuracy, precision, recall, and F1 scores of 96.43%, 96.43%, 97.22%, and 96.61%, respectively. These outcomes unequivocally highlight the robust stability and generalizability of the HBA-CatBoost model. Implementing this model in the realm of mine water hazard prevention and control can significantly enhance water intrusion management practices, thereby reducing the occurrence of water hazard incidents and facilitating emergency response operations.

In an effort to bolster the credibility of the HBA-CatBoost model and mitigate the inherent “black box” limitation associated with machine learning models, this study employs the SHAP method to offer insights into the predictive mechanism of the model48. By elucidating the pivotal roles of each feature in every sample, Na±+K± ions are highlighted as the most influential feature in detecting water inrush sources, closely followed by SO42− and Ca2+ ions. Moreover, the analysis reveals that distinct ion features exert varying impacts on different categories of water sources. Additionally, the utilization of the SHAP method allows for a comprehensive examination of misclassified samples from both feature and outcome-based perspectives, thereby significantly enhancing the model’s predictive performance.

The utilization of traditional hydrochemical ions for identifying water inrush sources implies that the HBA-CatBoost model introduced in this research holds promise for practical onsite implementation and offers significant potential for widespread adoption. An increase in the real-time monitoring system for mine water quality could enable the integration of source monitoring with subsequent laboratory analyses. This integration would establish a continuously updated water quality database, facilitating the dynamic identification of mine water sources in real time. Such a system would enhance early warning capabilities and aid in preventing mine water disasters. Furthermore, the versatile nature of the HBA-CatBoost model allows for its potential application in various domains, including the determination of groundwater pollution or airborne ash sources, contingent upon the specific input data provided.
