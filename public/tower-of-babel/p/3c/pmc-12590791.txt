Nano-silica improves the emergence of pepper seeds and the growth of seedlings under waterlogging stress by reducing oxidative damage
Authors: Peihua Yang, Chi Zhang, Huiling Li, Xiaoxiao Wang, Yuye Zhou, Htet Wai Wai Kyaw, Junliang Yin, Yongxing Zhu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractWaterlogging stress is an important environmental factor that limits global agricultural productivity. Silica nanoparticles (SiNPs) have been proven to enhance the tolerance of plants to waterlogging stress. However, the exact SiNPs-regulated waterlogging stress response mechanism in pepper remains unknown. The present study examined the influence of 300 mg/L silicon nanoparticles (SiNP300) on germination, emergence, and subsequent growth of pepper seedlings under waterlogging stress. During the early developmental phase, SiNP300 accelerated germination (36.70%) and enhanced cotyledon expansion (150.00%), while maintaining membrane integrity through reduced lipid peroxidation in radicles. Expression levels of CaAP2/ERF86 (23.79%) and CaAP2/ERF113 (29.89%) were elevated, suggesting a role in strengthening tolerance to waterlogging. At the seedling stage, foliar application of SiNP300 promoted root system development, as evidenced by increases in total root length (15.71%), surface area (14.83%), and root tip number (101.24%). In parallel, total chlorophyll content rose by 87.06%, the net photosynthetic rate improved by 251.72%, and visible chlorosis and wilting were mitigated. SiNP300 also reduced oxidative injury by lowering H₂O₂ (hydrogen peroxide) and O₂˙⁻ (superoxide anion) levels in leaves through modulation of SOD (superoxide dismutase), POD (peroxidase), and CAT (catalase) activities, thereby elevating soluble protein accumulation. Collectively, these findings clarify the mechanism by which SiNP300 alleviates waterlogging stress in pepper and provide a theoretical basis for applying exogenous SiNPs to enhance plant resistance.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-025-07546-y.Keywords: Waterlogging, Pepper, Emergence, Oxidative damage, Photosynthesis

Introduction

Pepper (Capsicum annuum L.), an annual herbaceous species of the Solanaceae family, represents one of the principal vegetable crops in China. Its high nutritional content and considerable economic value support extensive applications in food processing, healthcare, and the daily chemical industry [1]. Intensification of the global water cycle has recently enhanced the frequency and complexity of extreme weather events, thereby jeopardizing the stability and long-term security of agricultural production. Each year, nearly one-third of the world’s arable land experiences waterlogging, which severely impairs crop growth and yield [2]. Excess water saturates the soil profile, drastically reducing oxygen availability [3]. During seed germination, oxygen deprivation caused by waterlogging prolongs germination time and lowers germination rates [4, 5]. At the seedling stage, restricted root gas exchange and ensuing hypoxia disrupt physiological processes, alter growth dynamics, and modify dry matter accumulation and partitioning, ultimately suppressing plant development [6, 7].

Exogenous supplementation with hormones and nutrients has long been regarded as a cost-effective and efficient approach to enhancing crop tolerance to stress [8]. Increasing attention has been directed toward nanotechnology, where nanoparticles have demonstrated the capacity to strengthen resistance against both biotic and abiotic challenges in agricultural systems [9, 10]. Although not essential for most plant species, silicon functions as a beneficial element by modulating key metabolic pathways [11]. Nano-silica (SiO2NPs, SiNPs), with particle diameters of 1–100 nm, can be rapidly absorbed and exert diverse physiological effects owing to their minute size, extensive surface area, and high reactivity. Such characteristics have positioned SiNPs as a significant focus in contemporary studies on plant stress physiology [12, 13]. Based on existing research, the “stress buffering effect” of SiNPs constitutes a sequential regulatory process extending from germination through seedling development to maturation [14]. During germination, treatment with 200 mg/L SiNPs (20–30 nm) activated reactive oxygen species (ROS)-scavenging mechanisms and stimulated the synthesis of osmotic adjustment compounds, thereby mitigating drought-induced inhibition of cell division and elongation and accelerating wheat (Triticum aestivum L.) seed germination [15]. At the seedling stage, 250 mg/L SiNPs (20–30 nm) reduced ROS accumulation by enhancing antioxidant enzyme activity, which diminished oxidative injury in blueberry (Vaccinium corymbosum L.) subjected to waterlogging [16]. In ginger (Zingiber officinale Roscoe), 100 mg/L SiNPs (20–30 nm) stimulated root development and increased photosynthetic performance, improving tolerance to salinity stress [17]. Furthermore, Li et al. [1] demonstrated that 300 mg/L SiNPs (20–30 nm) enhanced pepper seed germination under saline conditions, promoted vegetative growth in field environments, and ultimately increased yield. Collectively, these findings indicate that SiNPs confer broad-spectrum physiological resilience against diverse environmental constraints, providing a solid theoretical and experimental basis for their application in stress-resilient agricultural production.

Seed germination and early seedling development represent fundamental phases in the plant life cycle, yet they remain highly susceptible to environmental stress, particularly waterlogging, which imposes severe restrictions on plant growth and productivity [18]. During sowing, reduced soil oxygen caused by water accumulation frequently induces seed decay and root rot, suppresses germination and emergence, and ultimately diminishes yield potential [19]. As shallow-rooted crops, peppers exhibit heightened sensitivity to waterlogging stress, making the mitigation of its adverse effects on germination and seedling establishment a key focus of agricultural research [20]. Although SiNPs are recognized for enhancing crop resilience and promoting growth, the regulatory mechanisms underlying their action in waterlogging-stressed pepper have not been fully clarified. The present study aims to provide a theoretical framework for integrating SiNPs into pepper cultivation and to elucidate their potential contribution to improving plant growth under waterlogging conditions.

Materials and methods

Plant materials and treatment

Pepper seeds (Capsicum annuum L. cv. Jinluochuanqi) were obtained from Jingzhou Jinghua Seed Industry Co., Ltd., Hubei Province. SiNPs (E551 food additive, purity 99.5%, particle size 10–20 nm) were purchased from Sigma Reagent. Based on preliminary evaluation, 300 mg/L nano-silica (SiNP300) was selected as the treatment concentration. The study included two independent experiments (Experiment 1 and Experiment 2).

Effects of SiNP300 on the germination and emergence of pepper seeds under waterlogging stress (Experiment 1)

To assess the impact of SiNP300 on pepper seed germination and emergence under waterlogging conditions, four treatments were applied, each with three container replicates: (1) Control (CK), seeds soaked in distilled water with soil moisture maintained at 60%; (2) SiNP300, seeds soaked in 300 mg/L solution with soil moisture at 60%; (3) WL, seeds soaked in distilled water with soil moisture at 80%; (4) SiNP300 + WL, seeds soaked in 300 mg/L solution with soil moisture at 80%. Uniform seeds were sterilized in hot water at 55 ℃ for 15 min, followed by incubation at 28 ℃ for 6–8 h. Subsequently, 120 seeds were sown in each container. The growth substrate was a mixture of perlite and Danish Punxtop peat soil (1:3, v/v), adjusted to 60% water content for normal irrigation and 80% for waterlogging stress. Waterlogging treatment began at sowing and continued for 5 days, after which normal irrigation was resumed. Emergence was recorded when the radicle extended above the soil surface. Germination rate was determined from day 5 to day 15. On day 15, fresh weight and seedling length were measured, and physiological indices including nitric oxide (NO) content, lipid peroxidation, and plasma membrane integrity of the radicle were analyzed. Whole seedlings collected on day 15 were rinsed with distilled water and stored at − 80 ℃ for subsequent assays.

Measurement of seed germination and emergence indicators

Seedling emergence was recorded from day 5 to day 15, after which germination rate, germination index, and germination speed were calculated following the procedure of Shi et al. [21].

The emergence time and cotyledon expansion time of pepper were counted on the 5th day of the experiment, and the cotyledon expansion was counted on the 15th day of the experiment [22]. The specific calculation methods are as follows:

Measurement of biomass

On day 15 of treatment, ten seedlings per group were rinsed with distilled water and rapidly dried. Seedling length (from the apical root to the basal bud) was measured with a ruler, and fresh weight was determined using an electronic balance. Each treatment was assessed in triplicate.

Fluorescence detection of the NO level in pepper radicle

NO levels in pepper radicles were evaluated using the fluorescence probe DAF-FM DA (Biyuntian Biotechnology Research Institute) [23]. The probe was diluted to 10 µmol/L (1:1000), and root tips were immersed in the dye solution and incubated at 37 °C in darkness for 20 min. Subsequently, the tips were thoroughly rinsed with PBS (0.2 mol/L Na2HPO4 and 0.2 mol/L NaH2PO4, pH 7.4) to eliminate residual dye. Fluorescence intensity in root tip cells was then observed with a Nikon NI-U fluorescence microscope equipped with a C-HGFI light source and Fi3 camera, using an excitation wavelength of 450–490 nm.

Histochemical staining analysis of pepper radicle

The integrity of the plasma membrane of pepper radicle was observed by Evans blue staining. The washed pepper radicle was immersed in 0.025% Evans blue staining solution for 30 min, and then rinsed with CaCl2 solution until the radicle did not fall off blue. The stained radicle was observed under an optical microscope for plasma membrane integrity. The degree of lipid membrane peroxidation of pepper radicle was observed by Schiff staining. The washed pepper radicle was stained with Shciff reagent for 20 min, and then washed with 0.5% K2S2O5 solution until there was no pink shedding. The degree of lipid membrane peroxidation was observed under an optical microscope [24].

Quantitative real time PCR (qRT-PCR) assays

Total RNA was isolated from entire pepper plants using TRizol reagent (GenStar, Beijing, China). First-strand cDNA was synthesized with the HiScript Ⅱ 1 st Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (Novozymes Vazyme, Nanjing). Gene-specific primers were designed with Primer Premier 5, and the corresponding sequences are listed in Additional Table S1. CaUBI3 was employed as the internal reference gene for qRT-PCR [25]. Amplification was performed on an MA-6000 Real-Time Quantitative Thermal Cycler (Molarray, Suzhou, China) using SYBR Green Master Mix (Vazyme, Nanjing, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions, as described by Li et al. [26]. Relative transcript levels were determined using the 2−△△ct method [27].

Effects of SiNP300 on the growth of pepper seedlings under waterlogging stress (Experiment 2)

To examine the influence of SiNP300 on pepper seedling growth and tolerance to waterlogging, seedlings were subjected to four treatments: (1) control with standard soil moisture management (CK), (2) foliar application of 300 mg/L solution under standard conditions (SiNP300), (3) water level maintained 2 ± 1 cm above the substrate surface (WL), and (4) foliar application of 300 mg/L solution prior to waterlogging (SiNP300 + WL). Seeds were immersed in 55 ℃ water for 15 min, placed in a 15 cm petri dish with moist filter paper, and transferred to a 72-hole plug tray on the third day of germination. Once seedlings reached the four-leaf stage with one central leaf, they were transplanted into pots containing substrate in a greenhouse. Environmental conditions were maintained at 28 ℃/20 ℃ (day/night), 60%–80% relative humidity, and a 16 h/8 h light/dark cycle. After seedlings established in pots, foliar application of 300 mg/L SiNP solution was performed every two days for one week before waterlogging, ending one day prior to the treatment. Spraying was continued until leaf surfaces were covered with droplets. Preliminary observations indicated that after 7 days of waterlogging, most seedlings exhibited varying degrees of wilting or chlorosis but remained viable. Accordingly, chlorophyll content, photosynthetic parameters, and related traits were determined after 7 days of waterlogging. Leaf and root samples were collected on the 1 st, 3rd, 5th, and 7th day of waterlogging, immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at − 80 ℃ for subsequent analyses.

Growth phenotype and root architecture of pepper

After 7 days of waterlogging treatment, photos of the pepper leaves under different treatments were taken using a camera (Canon EOS 77D, Japan).

Roots were sampled and scanned to measure the total root length, and surface area using a root scanner (Model MRS-9600TFU2L, Microtek, Shanghai, China). Each treatment included three replications.

Content of photosynthetic pigments

After 7 days of waterlogging treatment, about 0.2 g of the third leaf from the top of the pepper was soaked in 10 mL of 96% anhydrous ethanol and let it soak in the dark for 24 h until the leaves were completely decolorized. The supernatant was taken to measure the absorbance at 665 nm, 649 nm and 450 nm. Finally, the content of chlorophyll a (Chl a), chlorophyll b (Chl b), and carotenoid (Car) in the leaves was calculated according to the method proposed by Li et al. [28].

Photosynthetic parameters and chlorophyll fluorescence parameters

After 7 days of waterlogging treatment, the upper third leaf was fixed on the reaction plate and placed in a dark box for dark reaction for 20 min. Modulated Chlorophyll Fluorometer (PAM 2500; Walz, Germany) was used to measure maximum photochemical efficiency of PSII (Fv/Fm), photochemical quenching (qP) and non-photochemical quenching (NPQ). A portable photosynthetic fluorimeter (Yaxin-1102, Beijing, China) was used to measure the net photosynthetic rate (Pn), transpiration rate (Tr), conductance (Gs) and intercellular CO2 concentration (Ci) between 9 and 11 am.

Determination of leaf water content

After 7 days of waterlogging treatment, the upper third leaf of treatments were used to determine the water content, bound water content, free water content and water saturation deficit. The relative water content and water saturation deficit were measured according to the method described by Li et al. [29]. The free water and bound water contents of leaves were measured according to Ming et al. [30].

Determination of enzyme activity

Fresh leaves (approximately 1 g) were collected after 7 days of waterlogging stress, homogenized in sodium phosphate buffer (50 mM, pH 7.8), and centrifuged at 10,000 g for 20 min. The resulting supernatant served as the enzyme extract for the determination of superoxide dismutase (SOD), peroxidase (POD) and catalase (CAT) activities following the method of Zhou et al. [31].

SOD activity was assayed in a reaction mixture containing 130 mmol/L methionine, 750 µmol/L NBT, 100 µmol/L EDTA-Na₂, 20 µmol/L riboflavin, and 0.75 mL supernatant. The control was kept in darkness, while the reaction tube was exposed to light for 20 min before absorbance was recorded at 560 nm.

POD activity was assayed using a reaction system composed of sodium phosphate buffer (50 mM, pH 5.5), 50 mM guaiacol, and 0.1 mL enzyme extract. Initiation of the reaction was achieved by adding 2% H₂O₂, and activity was defined as a change of 0.01 per min at 470 nm.

CAT activity was determined using a system containing 0.1 mol/L H₂O₂ and 0.1 mL enzyme extract, with absorbance monitored at 240 nm at 1-min intervals. One unit of CAT activity corresponded to a change of 0.01 per min.

Histochemical staining analysis of pepper leaves

The 3,3′-diaminobenzidine (DAB) and nitroblue tetrazolium (NBT) were used to visualize the H2O2 and O2·− contents in the leaves of pepper according to the method described by Yin et al. [32]. The third fresh leaf from the top of the pepper was separately placed in the 1.5 mg/mL DAB staining solution and the 0.15% NBT staining solution. After the leaves turned brown and blue, respectively, they were placed in 90% ethanol to remove chlorophyll and photographic records.

The content of soluble protein

Soluble protein was determined according to Bradford [33]. About 0.5 g fresh leaves were ground into a homogenate with phosphate buffer and centrifuged at 8000 g for 10 min. The reaction system (5 mL) contained 0.1 mL clear supernatant and 4.9 mL Coomassie brilliant blue solution, the absorbance was measured at 595 nm. Finally the soluble protein content of the pepper leaf was calculated according to a standard curve.

Statistical analysis

All measurements were performed in triplicate. Treatment effects were analyzed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), and significant differences were identified by Fisher’s LSD test at P < 0.05. Graphs were generated with GraphPad Prism 10.1.2, and the mechanistic diagram was prepared using Adobe Illustrator 2020.

Results

Effects of SiNP300 on the germination and emergence of pepper seeds under waterlogging stress (Experiment 1)

SiNP300 promoted the germination and emergence of pepper seeds under waterlogging stress

As shown in Fig. 1, SiNP300 treatment did not alter germination rate under non-stress conditions, with CK and SiNP300 reaching 44.38% and 44.67% respectively on day 6. In contrast, WL and SiNP300 + WL treatments exhibited rates of 0 and 1.46%. From day 9 to day 15, germination under WL remained markedly lower than CK, whereas SiNP300 application effectively mitigated this inhibition. Relative to WL, germination rates under SiNP300 + WL increased by 82.61%, 176.56%, and 30.49% at days 9, 12, and 15, respectively. As presented in Fig. 1B–F, WL significantly delayed seedling emergence and cotyledon expansion while reducing the percentage of cotyledon unfolding. Compared with WL, SiNP300 shortened seedling emergence and cotyledon unfolding delays by 0.5 d and 1 d, and further raised the percentages of cotyledon unfolding and germination speed by 150.00% and 36.70%, respectively (Fig. 1B–F). WL also reduced seedling length and fresh weight by 72.98% and 26.33%, whereas SiNP300 treatment reversed part of this suppression, producing increases of 32.47% in length and 34.31% in fresh weight relative to WL (Fig. 1G–H).

SiNP300 decreased membrane lipid peroxidation and increased integrity of pepper radicle

Visualization using Schiff’s reagent (Fig. 2A) and Evans blue staining (Fig. 2B) demonstrated minimal staining in radicles subjected to SiNP300, comparable to CK, whereas WL treatment markedly intensified both pink and blue signals. In contrast, SiNP300 + WL markedly diminished the stained areas relative to WL alone, indicating protection of membrane structure. NO, a key signaling molecule involved in root morphogenesis, seed germination, and stress responses, was further examined (Fig. 2C). Under SiNP300 treatment, NO levels did not differ significantly from CK. WL exposure induced a strong increase in NO fluorescence intensity, while SiNP300 + WL treatment slightly reduced this enhancement compared with WL.

SiNP300 induce expression of AP2/ERF genes in pepper seedlings

As shown in Fig. 3, relative to CK, WL markedly elevated the expression of most CaAP2/ERF genes except CaAP2/ERF127, which declined by 42.31%. Substantial increases were observed for CaAP2/ERF78, CaAP2/ERF86, CaAP2/ERF113, CaAP2/ERF129, and CaAP2/ERF171, with respective changes of 645.73%, 126.17%, 28.70%, 137.72%, and 70.28%. Effects of SiNP300 on the growth of pepper seedings under waterlogging stress (Experiment 2)

SiNP300 alleviated leaf chlorosis of pepper under waterlogging stress

On the seventh day of treatment, leaves in both CK and SiNP300 groups maintained normal morphology and a uniform green coloration, whereas WL treatment induced pronounced yellowing, wilting, and curling (Fig. 4A). Application of SiNP300 under WL conditions markedly reduced chlorosis and wilting in pepper seedlings. Compared with CK, SiNP300 alone produced no significant change in bound water or total water content but led to a significant elevation of relative water content (Fig. 4B–E). WL treatment reduced relative water content by 3.45% and increased water saturation deficit by 14.02%, whereas SiNP300 + WL treatment decreased the water saturation deficit by 3.19% and produced a slight recovery in relative water content.

SiNP300 improved the photosynthesis of the pepper leaf under waterlogging stress

As shown in Fig. 6A, relative to CK, SiNP300 increased carotenoid content by 6.41%, while Chl a and total chlorophyll rose by 13.31% and 4.43%, respectively. WL treatment markedly reduced Chl a, Chl b, and Car contents; however, supplementation with SiNP300 under WL conditions counteracted this decline. Compared with WL alone, SiNP300 + WL significantly elevated Chl a, Chl b, and Car levels by 71.23%, 123.55%, and 39.34%, respectively. Under non-stress conditions, SiNP300 application exerted no detectable influence on chlorophyll fluorescence. WL stress decreased Fv/Fm and qP by 19.73% and 6.63%, respectively, while NPQ rose by 49.41%; SiNP300 + WL restored Fv/Fm and qP, with increases of 21.24% and 4.95%, respectively (Fig. 6B-D). Similarly, SiNP300 had no measurable effect on photosynthetic parameters in the absence of stress. Under WL, leaf Pn, Tr, and Gs decreased by 91.12%, 93.01%, and 38.77%, respectively, whereas Ci increased by 21.92%. In contrast, SiNP300 + WL significantly improved Pn, Tr, and Gs by 251.72%, 393.60%, and 34.34%, respectively, while Ci declined by 9.98% compared with WL.

In contrast, SiNP300 + WL treatment reduced MDA accumulation relative to WL, lowering root and leaf contents by 26.90% and 34.77%, respectively, at day 7. Discussion

Waterlogging stress suppresses seed germination and emergence, prolongs germination duration, and diminishes seed vigor [34]. In soybean (Glycine max), significant reductions in germination rate, radicle length, fresh weight, and vigor index have been observed under waterlogging compared with non-stressed plants [5]. Exogenous application of SiNPs has demonstrated positive effects on seed emergence in various species; for instance, soaking seeds in 2 mmol/L SiNPs enhanced the emergence of dragonhead (Dracocephalum moldavica L.) and increased bud length and biomass [35]. Li et al. [20] further reported that treatment with 300 mg/L SiNPs (20–30 nm) improved germination rate, germination index, and radicle length of pepper seeds under salt stress, thereby mitigating the inhibitory effect of salinity. In the present study, waterlogging suppressed germination and emergence of pepper seeds and reduced seedling length and fresh weight, whereas application of SiNP300 enhanced germination and promoted biomass accumulation under stress. These results indicate that SiNP300 alleviates the inhibitory impact of waterlogging on pepper seed germination and emergence.

Hypoxia in plant roots disrupts metabolic homeostasis and antioxidant balance, leading to excessive accumulation of ROS. Elevated ROS induces lipid peroxidation of cellular membranes, which in turn impairs structural integrity and functional stability [36]. Iqbal et al. [37] demonstrated that waterlogging stress intensified membrane lipid peroxidation in citrus (Poncirus trifoliata L.) roots, whereas application of 250 mg/L SiNPs (20–30 nm) effectively attenuated oxidative injury. In the present study, SiNP300 markedly suppressed membrane lipid peroxidation and preserved membrane integrity in pepper radicles under waterlogging conditions (Fig. 2). Similarly, Li et al. [15] observed in wheat subjected to drought that 200 mg/L SiNPs (20–30 nm) enhanced SOD, CAT, and POD activities, thereby reducing ROS accumulation and mitigating oxidative stress. On this basis, SiNP300 is inferred to activate the ROS-scavenging system in pepper root tips, restraining ROS buildup and lipid peroxidation, ultimately strengthening pepper tolerance to waterlogging stress.

Nitric oxide (NO) has been identified as a signaling regulator that stabilizes ROS levels by activating antioxidant enzymes, thereby mitigating cellular oxidative damage and enhancing plant stress tolerance [38]. In rapeseed (Brassica napus L. ‘Tammi’), NO accumulation in roots was markedly elevated after 72 h of waterlogging, indicating its role in the adaptive response [39]. A comparable trend was detected in pepper radicles, where waterlogging for 15 days markedly increased NO content, while exogenous SiNP300 suppressed this elevation. Similar regulatory mechanisms have been reported in other species: in Chinese cabbage (Brassica campestris spp. chinensis L., Aijiaohuang), melatonin application restrained NO accumulation under cadmium stress and alleviated oxidative damage [40], whereas in Pisum sativum L., salicylic acid enhanced antioxidant enzyme activity, reduced ROS accumulation, and indirectly limited NO generation associated with ROS signaling [41]. Based on these observations, SiNP300 in this study is proposed to mitigate waterlogging-induced oxidative stress through enhancement of antioxidant capacity (Fig. 2A-B). As a consequence, plants appear to rely less on an intensified NO signal to initiate defense responses, resulting in reduced NO levels [42].

Precise modulation of gene expression constitutes a central mechanism by which plants adapt to adverse environments [43]. Within this context, the AP2/ERF family, one of the largest transcription factor groups in plants, comprises multiple subfamilies that contribute to waterlogging stress responses [44]. In Corchorus olitorius L., Kabir et al. [45] reported significant up-regulation of CoERF-21, CoERF-34, and CoERF-39 under waterlogging conditions. Similarly, in petunia (Petunia × hybrida), waterlogging markedly induced PhERF2 expression, and heterologous overexpression of PhERF2 enhanced waterlogging tolerance in transgenic Arabidopsis thaliana [46]. In this study, the regulatory influence of SiNP300 on pepper gene expression was classified into three distinct categories. First, waterlogging stress markedly induced CaAP2/ERF78, CaAP2/ERF129, and CaAP2/ERF171, suggesting their involvement in stress responses. SiNP300 treatment, however, suppressed their expression, likely reflecting mitigation of waterlogging-induced injury and a reversion of transcriptional activity toward control levels. Comparable trends were documented in cucumber, where both salt stress and exogenous silicon altered numerous transcripts, yet silicon supplementation under salt stress restored many of those transcripts to baseline [47]. Second, waterlogging stress substantially reduced CaAP2/ERF127 expression, whereas SiNP300 markedly up-regulated its transcription under stress, indicating a positive regulatory effect. Previous research on ginger demonstrated a similar pattern, in which waterlogging suppressed aquaporin gene expression, while SiNP200 enhanced water uptake and improved tolerance by stimulating their transcription [19]. Third, CaAP2/ERF86 and CaAP2/ERF113 were induced under waterlogging, and their expression was further elevated following SiNP300 application, implying that both genes contribute to waterlogging adaptation and that SiNP300 may strengthen tolerance through their active regulation. Zhu et al. [48] likewise observed that exogenous Na2SiO3 enhanced aquaporin gene expression in cucumber under salt stress, promoting water uptake and transport in roots. Collectively, the roles of CaAP2/ERF-related genes and their mechanisms in SiNP-mediated stress adaptation in pepper require further elucidation.

The seedling stage determines nutritional status and stress tolerance of pepper, thereby influencing subsequent yield and quality [49]. To assess the impact of SiNP300 on waterlogging-induced injury during this period, morphological and physiological responses were examined. Waterlogging is known to restrict root water and nutrient uptake, ultimately suppressing plant growth [50]. Leaf morphology serves as a direct indicator of stress-induced injury, with waterlogging typically leading to progressive chlorosis, wilting, and eventual abscission [51]. In the present study, SiNP300 application markedly alleviated leaf yellowing and curling under waterlogging conditions. Previous evidence indicated that waterlogging severely reduced root length, surface area, and tip number in ginger seedlings, whereas exogenous SiNPs substantially mitigated such reductions and enhanced tolerance [19]. A similar trend was observed in pepper, where waterlogging suppressed root development, while foliar SiNP300 application significantly increased total root length and root tip number, thereby counteracting waterlogging-induced inhibition of root growth. Enhanced photosynthetic performance in leaves (Fig. 6) likely promoted assimilate allocation to roots, reduced lipid peroxidation of membranes, and improved root system architecture. Whether SiNPs influence pepper root development through additional pathways, such as hormone signaling, energy metabolism, or ion regulation, remains unresolved and warrants further investigation.

Photosynthesis represents the fundamental process by which plants synthesize organic matter and acquire energy, thereby influencing the entire life cycle. Chlorophyll, an essential component of this process, serves as an indicator of leaf growth status [52]. Waterlogging stress typically suppresses chlorophyll biosynthesis, leading to reduced chlorophyll content, diminished photosynthetic efficiency, and subsequent growth [53]. Gao et al. [17] reported that foliar application of 100 mg/L SiNPs (20–30 nm) alleviated the suppression of photosynthesis in ginger under salt stress by enhancing chlorophyll concentration and net photosynthetic rate. Similarly, Raza et al. [54] demonstrated that applying SiNPs (40–80 nm) to wheat under drought stress significantly increased Pn and Gs, thereby improving photosynthesis and strengthening drought tolerance. In the present study, SiNP300 markedly elevated chlorophyll and carotenoid levels in pepper leaves subjected to waterlogging (Fig. 6), which improved light energy absorption and utilization, enhanced photosynthetic efficiency, restored Gs and Pn, and reduced Ci. These results suggest that waterlogging impairs pepper photosynthesis primarily by decreasing stomatal conductance and restricting CO2 entry, whereas exogenous SiNP300 promotes intercellular CO2 assimilation and sustains photosynthetic rate by maintaining leaf water content and lowering water saturation deficit (Fig. 4), ultimately supporting stomatal conductance. Previous research indicated that SiNPs can reduce transpiration and water loss by forming a cuticle–silicon double layer on the leaf surface, thereby enhancing plant resistance [55]. For instance, treatment with 2 mM SiNPs in purslane (Portulaca oleracea L.) resulted in the deposition of a compact silicon film on the epidermis, partially obstructing stomata, which lowered transpiration and mitigated water loss [56]. In contrast, other studies have demonstrated that silicon application may increase leaf transpiration. Desoky et al. [57] reported that foliar application of 1.5 mol/L SiNPs (20–30 nm) elevated the transpiration rate in soybean under drought conditions, and earlier work also revealed that SiNP200 markedly increased transpiration in ginger exposed to waterlogging stress [19]. In the present study, SiNP300 treatment significantly enhanced the Tr of pepper leaves under waterlogging, likely due to improved root development that promoted water uptake and elevated transpiration through increased stomatal conductance, as described by Zhu et al. [24]. Collectively, the impact of SiNPs on leaf transpiration appears to vary with environmental context and plant species, and further investigation is required to elucidate the underlying mechanisms.

ROS (O2·−, ·OH, and H2O2) accumulate markedly in plants under adverse environmental stress [58]. Excess levels initiate lipid peroxidation cascades, ultimately causing membrane rupture and generating malondialdehyde (MDA), a commonly used indicator of lipid peroxidation [59]. To counteract oxidative injury, plants employ enzymatic and non-enzymatic defense systems that neutralize free radicals [60]. The enzymatic network involving SOD, POD, and CAT serves as a major component in restricting ROS accumulation and mitigating cellular damage [61]. Numerous studies have demonstrated that silica nanoparticles modulate antioxidant defenses by altering the activities of SOD, CAT, and POD, thereby reducing ROS-induced injury [62]. For instance, application of 250 mg/L SiNPs (20–30 nm) enhanced SOD, POD, and CAT activities in blueberry (Vaccinium corymbosum L.) subjected to waterlogging, which strengthened ROS scavenging capacity and alleviated oxidative stress [16]. Li et al. [20] reported that exogenous application of 300 mg/L SiNPs (20–30 nm) alleviated salt-induced oxidative stress in pepper by reducing H₂O₂ and MDA accumulation, thereby lowering the activities of antioxidant enzymes such as SOD and CAT. In the present study, prolonged waterlogging significantly increased SOD, POD, and CAT activities in roots and leaves compared with CK. SiNP300 pretreatment, however, reduced SOD and CAT activities in leaves during the later stage of waterlogging, suggesting diminished demand for ROS scavenging, consistent with the observed decline in H₂O₂ and O₂·⁻ levels (Fig. 8C–D). In roots, SiNP300 treatment resulted in higher POD and CAT activities relative to waterlogging alone, while SOD activity exhibited an early increase followed by a decline, likely reflecting its rapid induction during initial stress and subsequent reduction as ROS levels decreased. Collectively, these observations indicate that antioxidant enzyme activities are influenced by stress duration, tissue-specific responses, and distinct regulatory mechanisms.

Soluble proteins are integral to osmotic adjustment, energy metabolism, and signal transduction [63]. Waterlogging typically induces excessive ROS accumulation, disrupting protein biosynthesis or accelerating protein degradation, thereby impairing cellular metabolic functions [64, 65]. Yu et al. [66] reported that waterlogging suppressed protein synthesis and markedly reduced soluble protein content in maize (Zea mays L.) leaves. In the present study, soluble protein levels in both leaves and roots progressively declined with increasing duration of waterlogging. Under stress conditions, SiNP300 treatment initially decreased soluble protein content in pepper leaves relative to WL from days 3 to 5, but by day 7 levels surpassed those of WL. In roots, SiNP300 significantly elevated soluble protein content after 5–7 days of treatment. This temporal pattern likely reflects rapid ROS accumulation in leaves during the early phase of waterlogging, when the antioxidant defense induced by SiNP300 had not yet reached sufficient activity. Oxidative stress directly degraded proteins and hindered their synthesis at the initial stage; however, as antioxidant enzyme activities increased, ROS levels declined, and photosynthetic recovery enhanced carbon and nitrogen assimilation (Fig. 6). Consequently, leaves resumed compensatory protein synthesis, ultimately exceeding protein levels observed under WL treatment. Roots, as the primary organs subjected to direct injury under waterlogging, rapidly shifted to fermentative metabolism, accompanied by simultaneous suppression of protein synthesis and degradation, resulting in relatively stable protein content at the early stage. During subsequent SiNP300 treatment, root growth improved (Fig. 5), antioxidant enzyme activity increased, and ROS levels declined, ultimately restoring soluble protein content. A comparable pattern was reported in ginger, where waterlogging reduced soluble protein levels in leaves, whereas SiNP200 pretreatment restored protein content by mitigating oxidative stress [19]. The present results suggest that SiNP300 reduces ROS accumulation, thereby contributing to higher soluble protein levels. The increase may result from enhanced synthesis or diminished degradation, which requires further investigation. Overall, SiNP300 treatment appears to mitigate oxidative damage induced by waterlogging in pepper seedlings and to support plant growth. Conclusion

This study showed that SiNP300 treatment markedly improved the emergence of pepper seeds under waterlogging stress by increasing germination rate, germination index, and seedling biomass. The treatment also mitigated oxidative injury to the radicle, suppressed lipid peroxidation, and preserved cell membrane integrity. In addition, SiNP300 induced upregulation of CaAP2/ERF86 and CaAP2/ERF113, thereby strengthening transcriptional responses to waterlogging stress. During seedling development, foliar application of SiNP300 enhanced waterlogging tolerance by stimulating root growth, which contributed to improved water status and alleviated leaf curling and wilting. SiNP300 further increased chlorophyll content, optimized photosynthetic parameters, and elevated photosynthetic rate. Regulation of antioxidant enzyme activity, reduction of ROS accumulation, mitigation of oxidative injury, and accumulation of soluble proteins were also observed. Collectively, the results support the potential of SiNPs to enhance waterlogging tolerance in pepper.
