Thermo‐Chemo‐Mechanically Robust, Multifunctional MXene/PVA/PAA‐Hanji Textile with Energy Harvesting, EMI Shielding, Flame‐Retardant, and Joule Heating Capabilities
Authors: Jiheon Kim, Yong Choi, Hoyoung Jang, Sohyung Jiong, Xinqi Chen, Byungseok Seo, Wonjoon Choi
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe rapid development of wearable electronics, personal mobile equipment, and Internet of Things systems demands smart textiles that integrate multiple functions with enhanced durability. Herein, the study reports robust and multifunctional textiles with energy harvesting, electromagnetic interference (EMI) shielding, flame resistance, and Joule heating capabilities, fabricated by a facile yet effective integration method using the deposition of cross‐linked MXene (Ti3C2Tx), poly(vinyl alcohol) (PVA), and poly(acrylic acid) (PAA) onto traditional Korean paper, Hanji via vacuum filtration. Comprehensive analyses confirm robust cross‐linking, structural integrity, and interface stability in the MXene/PVA/PAA‐Hanji (MPP‐H) textiles, which synergistically boost their multifunctional performance. The MPP‐H textiles exhibit remarkable power generation lasting over 60 min with a power density of 102.2 µW cm−3 and an energy density of 31.0 mWh cm−3 upon the application of 20 µL of NaCl solution. The EMI shielding effectiveness (SE) per unit thickness in the X‐band (8.2–12.4 GHz) is up to 437.6 dB mm−1, with the ratio of absorption to reflection reaching 4.5, outperforming existing EMI shielding materials. Superior thermo‐chemo‐mechanical properties (flame resistance, rapid Joule heating, durability, and washability) further demonstrate their versatile usability. The MPP‐H enables diverse functionalities within a single, robust textile through a scalable fabrication method, offering transformative potential for wearable and mobility platforms.Keywords: electromagnetic interference (EMI) shielding, energy harvesting, joule heating with flame resistance, MXene, smart textile

Introduction

With the rapid development of wearable electronics, personal technology, and IoT systems, smart textiles that can integrate multiple functions have attracted increasing attention.[ 1 ] Among various functions, energy harvesting,[ 2 ] electromagnetic interference (EMI) shielding,[ 3 ] and thermo‐chemo‐mechanical robustness[ 4 ] are particularly crucial to ensure sustainable and versatile functionalities of a single component based on their synergistic effects. Energy harvesting enables the generation of power from ambient sources for sustainable usage, eliminating the need for heavy and maintenance‐intensive batteries and improving the long‐term stability of wearable systems.[ 5 ] EMI shielding protects the human body from electromagnetic radiation and prevents interference and performance degradation of electronic components.[ 6 ] In addition, thermo‐chemo‐mechanical robustness enhances the safety, functionality, and durability of smart textiles for the practicality of smart textiles. Integrating these functionalities not only addresses the diverse demands of contemporary wearable technologies and IoT applications but also enhances the overall textile performance by ensuring that these features complement and enhance each other, rather than existing in isolation.[ 7 ] This integrated approach provides a cohesive solution that meets complex requirements and adapts to evolving needs in advanced applications, demonstrating the multifaceted benefits of smart textiles.

Specifically, energy harvesting offers a sustainable functionality for smart textiles, allowing them to power embedded electronic devices without relying on external power sources.[ 8 ] For instance, triboelectric, piezoelectric, thermoelectric, and electromagnetic methods,[ 9 ] have been developed to convert mechanical vibrations, thermal gradients, or electromagnetic fields into electrical power.[ 10 ] However, the low power density of these energy harvesting methods in a compact form factor cannot fully satisfy the requirements of advanced wearable devices.[ 11 ] Additionally, sustainable electricity generation is another concern, as the required energy sources, such as human activities or temperature differences, may not always be available.[ 12 ] Meanwhile, as the multiple characteristics of smart textiles are necessary, integrating energy harvesting with other functionalities such as sensing, energy storage, and wave controls poses substantial challenges to device design and material compatibility.[ 13 ] Thus, developing smart textile technology that combines high power density, seamless integration with other components, and reliable, sustainable power generation remains challenging.

EMI shielding is essential for smart textiles to protect sensitive electronic components from external electromagnetic interference, which causes performance degradation and malfunctions.[ 14 ] Conventional EMI shielding materials include metals, conductive polymers, carbon‐based substances, and hybrid composites.[ 15 ] Metals offer excellent conductivity but are heavy and inflexible.[ 16 ] Conductive polymers are flexible while they provide relatively low shielding effectiveness.[ 17 ] Carbon‐based materials are lightweight and conductive but may lack durability.[ 18 ] As alternative approaches, their rationally hybridized composites can combine the above benefits in a single material.[ 19 ] However, their hybridizing process still involves critical problems, such as uniformity, interfacial disturbances, and complex manufacturing processes.[ 20 ] Additionally, many existing materials display high EMI shielding effectiveness, whereas their low absorption rate potentially causes secondary damage to neighboring components due to the reflected EMI.[ 21 ] Integrating EMI shielding materials with other functional components, such as sensors or energy harvesters, adds further complexity, which can affect the overall performance and reliability of the smart textile.[ 22 ] Thus, developing materials and integration strategies that provide effective, broad‐spectrum EMI shielding while also preserving flexibility, durability, and multifunctionality presents a significant task in advancing smart textile technology.[ 23 ]

In addition to EMI shielding, thermo‐chemo‐mechanical robustness is essential for smart textiles, encompassing flame resistance, thermal regulation, and durability in various environments. This includes flame retardancy to protect users from potential fire hazards and the ability to implement thermoregulation through heating and cooling functions, enabling adaptability to varying temperatures.[ 24 ] Furthermore, chemo‐mechanical durability and washability against humid environments ensure that the textiles maintain their integrity under disturbances and preserve functionality through regular use and cleaning.[ 25 ] Effectively integrating these diverse features with other functionalities, such as energy harvesting and EMI shielding, through innovative material solutions will be key to unlocking the full potential of smart textiles in practical applications.

MXenes, a family of 2D materials with unique physicochemical properties, have emerged as promising candidates for developing multifunctional smart textiles due to their high electrical conductivity and versatile energy conversion capabilities.[ 26 ] However, the practical application of MXenes in smart textiles faces significant challenges. Their inherent poor mechanical durability and limited flexibility restrict their use under mechanical stresses.[ 27 ] Additionally, MXenes are susceptible to swelling and have low hydrophilicity, which are crucial concerns for wearable applications due to exposure to human sweat, humid environments, and washing.[ 28 ] To address these limitations, MXenes are often combined with other materials to leverage their strengths while mitigating their weaknesses. For instance, MXenes can enhance energy harvesting through mechanisms such as piezoelectricity and thermoelectricity when integrated with flexible polymers or conductive fabrics, thereby improving power generation efficiency.[ 29 ] Their high electrical conductivity also makes them effective for EMI shielding, especially when hybridized with materials like carbon‐based substances or conductive polymers to achieve broad‐spectrum shielding.[ 30 ] Despite these advancements, integrating MXenes with other materials still presents challenges. Ensuring uniform distribution, achieving effective interfacial bonding, and maintaining consistent performance across various applications are critical issues.[ 31 ] Additionally, the complex manufacturing processes required to fabricate MXene‐based textiles can hinder scalability and increase production costs.[ 32 ] Therefore, it is required to develop efficient integration strategies and cost‐effective methods to fully exploit the multifunctional potential and high performance of MXene composites in smart textiles. By leveraging the unique properties of MXenes and overcoming their limitations through cost‐effective methods, the enhanced flexibility, washability, and multifunctional performance of MXene‐incorporated textiles can be realized for various applications in wearable electronics, personal technologies, and IoT systems.

Herein, we report the development of MXene/PVA/PAA‐Hanji (MPP‐H) textiles through a simple and synergistic integration of cross‐linked MPP and Hanji, a traditional Korean paper known for its excellent thermo‐chemo‐mechanical properties.[ 33 ] These textiles are engineered for multifunctional and wearable applications, offering capabilities in energy harvesting, EMI shielding, flame resistance, and Joule heating (Figure 1 ). The fabrication of MPP‐H textiles involves the transformation of MXene (Ti3C2Tx) from an MAX(Ti3AlC2) phase, cross‐linking with poly(vinyl alcohol) (PVA) and poly(acrylic acid) (PAA), and subsequent deposition onto Hanji by vacuum filtration. This straightforward method results in asymmetric MPP coatings and is suitable for scale‐up to prepare MPP‐H textiles with different MPP loadings. Comprehensive chemical analyses confirm the robust cross‐linking between the hydroxyl and carboxyl groups in MPP, which strengthens the interface with Hanji, enhancing durability against swelling, increasing the interlayer spacing of MXene, improving hydrophilicity, and reducing impurities. These advancements collectively impart the MPP‐H textiles with superior mechanical, electrical, chemical, and thermal properties. MPP‐H textiles demonstrate high power density and energy output in the presence of human sweat and environmental humidity, which confirms their capability for sustainable power generation in various environments. Additionally, the EMI shielding of the MPP‐H textiles reveals a combination of complementary and absorption‐based mechanisms inherent to their configurational features. The textiles exhibit remarkable shielding effectiveness (SE) per unit thickness and a high ratio of absorption to reflection, A/R ratio, indicating exceptional shielding performance compared to existing technologies. Furthermore, the thermo‐chemo‐mechanical characteristics correlated with flame resistance, Joule heating capability, flexibility, and washability are validated. The resulting MPP‐H textiles demonstrate a wide range of capabilities, underlining their potential as multifunctional smart textiles for wearable electronics, personal technologies, and IoT systems. The straightforward and affordable integration strategy of multi‐materials in this study highlights scalability toward rationally hybridized materials, paving the way for advancing versatile functionalities in smart textiles.

Results and Discussion

Preparation of MPP‐H Textiles for Smart Applications

To synthesize MPP‐H textiles, the MAX(Ti3AlC2) phase was first transformed into an MXene solution by etching, intercalation, and delamination (Figure 2a(i); Figure S1 and Note S1, Supporting Information). PVA and PAA at concentrations of 0.75 g L−1 each were then added to the MXene solution (3 g L−1) (Figure 2a(ii)). The mixture was subjected to vortex mixing for 15 s, followed by ultrasonication for 1 h. A cross‐linking reaction occurred between the hydroxyl groups of MXene and PVA and the carboxyl groups of PAA to form an MPP composite solution (Figure 2a(iii); Figure S2 and Note S2, Supporting Information). The resulting MPP solution was poured onto the Hanji textile and subjected to vacuum filtration, depositing MPP particles on the Hanji (Figure 2a(iv)). After drying at 25 °C for 6 h, the free‐standing flexible MPP‐H textiles were obtained (Figure 2a(v)). The network structure of the randomly distributed fibers in the Hanji created micropores throughout its thickness, enabling progressive deposition of MPP with increasing thickness. This results in three distinct regions within the textile: the bare Hanji at the bottom, the MPP‐filled overlapping region in the middle, and the MPP layer on top (Figure 2a(v)). The asymmetric MPP deposition led to a color difference between the top (black) and bottom (gray tint) surfaces of the Hanji. Furthermore, as shown in Figure 2b, when the concentration of MPP solution was high, the pores at the bottom of the Hanji could be clogged, resulting in thicker MPP deposition layers. The MPP‐H textiles were designated as MPP‐H‐0 (pristine Hanji), MPP‐H‐1, MPP‐H‐2, MPP‐H‐3, and MPP‐H‐4 when the deposited MPP amounts were 0, 0.63, 1.56, 2.77, and 3.51 mg cm− 2, respectively. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) was employed to analyze the micro‐morphology and structure of the MPP‐H textiles (Figure 2c). For the MPP‐H‐0 textile, the Hanji fibers (composed of D‐glucose with β‐1, 4‐glycosidic bonds) were randomly distributed, forming numerous pores of various sizes (Figure 2c(i)). Upon initial MPP deposition, the MPP‐H‐1 textile showed cross‐linked MPP within the pores of Hanji (Figure 2c(ii)). As the concentration of the MPP solution increased, the micropores became fully covered by MPP particles, rendering the infilled fibrous structure without voids (Figure 2c(iii)–(v); Figure S3, Supporting Information). The MPP‐H textiles were frozen in liquid nitrogen and fractured, and their cross‐sectional SEM images are shown in Figure S4 (Supporting Information). The thickness of the MPP layers in MPP‐H‐1, MPP‐H‐2, MPP‐H‐3, and MPP‐H‐4 textiles were 0.294, 0.635, 5.17, and 6.85 µm, respectively. This variation in thickness is due to the progressive deposition of MPP particles. Smaller particles with shorter cross‐links penetrate deeper into the Hanji structure, while larger particles with longer cross‐links tend to accumulate on the surface (Figure S5, Supporting Information). Consequently, the thickness increases in a non‐linear manner with concentration. A large amount of MPP leads to more surface‐level deposition, especially for particles with longer cross‐links, whereas the low loading of MPP could be mostly infiltrated and deposited into the micropores of Hanji textiles, resulting in sparse surface deposition.

Chemical Characterization of MPP‐H Textiles

The chemical characteristics of MPP‐H textiles were comprehensively characterized (Figure 3 ). As shown in Figure 3a, X‐ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) survey scans revealed core elements (Ti, C, O, and F) in MXene and MPP, confirming the successful transformation of MXene from the MAX phase and the effective deposition of MPP onto the Hanji paper (C6H10O5)n). Compared with MXene, MPP displayed a significantly increased carboxylate (O—C═O) peak in the narrow‐scan XPS spectra at the C 1s core level with a 13.4% increase in the full width at half maximum (FWHM) calculated through Gaussian curve fitting and a minor binding energy shift of 0.1 eV. (Figure 3b; Figure S6, Supporting Information). This enhancement suggests the formation of ester bonds between the hydroxyl groups of MXene and PVA and the carboxyl groups of PAA, signifying successful cross‐linking within the MPP. The robust cross‐linking considerably increases the interlayer spacing of MXene, thereby enhancing the electromagnetic shielding properties of the MPP‐H textiles (Figure S2 and Note S2, Supporting Information). In addition, the durability of the textiles against swelling is also improved after cross‐linking, making them compatible with aqueous environments (Figure S7 and Note S3, Supporting Information). Meanwhile, in the O 1s spectra (Figure 3c,d), the H2O peak in the MPP increased compared to that in MXene, indicating enhanced hydrophilicity. Contact angle measurements further demonstrated this improvement, showing angles of 74.1° for M‐H (MXene‐Hanji) textiles and 49.7° for MPP‐H‐3 textiles (Figure S8, Supporting Information). Additionally, the TiO2 peak was significantly lower in MPP than that in MXene, and the decreased TiO2 impurity likely reduced the impedance and enhanced the performance in energy conversion. Furthermore, X‐ray diffraction (XRD) analysis also suggested the structural changes in MXene and MPP (Figure 3e). The dominant (002) peaks were observed at 2θ = 5.76° for MXene and 4.78° for MPP. The insertion of PAA and PVA chains between the MXene layers disrupted the regular periodicity of the MXene structure in specific regions, resulting in extensive diffraction scattering. Based on the calculation using Bragg's law, the d‐spacing significantly increased from 1.53 nm for MXene to 1.85 nm for MPP, indicating successful intercalation of PAA and PVA. To examine the cross‐linking structures of MPP‐H textiles, Fourier‐transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) spectra of MXene, PAA, PVA, and MPP were measured (Figure 3f). The spectrum of MXene revealed predominant peaks at 1632 cm−1 and 3436 cm−1, corresponding to the stretching vibrations of C═C and O─H bonds, respectively. Pristine PAA showed a peak at 1710 cm−1 related to C═O bonds, while the peak at 3261 cm−1 in the PVA spectrum was associated with stretching vibrations of O—H groups. Due to the abundance of carboxyl groups in PAA and hydroxyl groups in PVA molecules as well as on the surface of MXene, PAA, PVA, and MXene can be easily cross‐linked in the MPP composite. In its spectrum, the C═O stretching vibrational mode of carboxyl groups becomes wider after chelation with MXene, indicating strong hydrogen bonding in MPP composites. Additionally, a noticeable shift in the —OH peak was observed from 3436 cm−1 in MXene to 3421 cm−1 in MPP, confirming the formation of hydrogen bonds among MXene, PVA, and PAA.[ 34 ] A broad peak corresponding to TiO2 in the 500 cm⁻¹ to 600 cm⁻¹ range was also observed, which aligns with the XPS O 1s analysis shown in Figure 3c,d.[ 35 ]

XPS narrow scan spectra and deconvolutions of b) C 1s for MXene and the MPP and O 1s for c) MXene and d) MPP. e) X‐ray diffraction (XRD) patterns of MXene and the MPP composite. f) Fourier‐transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) analysis of MXene, PAA, PVA, and the MPP composite. g) Cross‐sectional view SEM image and energy dispersive spectrometry (EDS) mapping of the MMP‐H‐3 textile for elemental analysis.

Cross‐sectional elemental distribution in MPP‐H‐3 textile was analyzed using energy dispersive spectrometry (EDS) mapping (Figure 3g). The core elements in MPP (Ti, O, Al, F, and Cl) were predominantly on the top surface of Hanji, indicating the deposition of MPP. A top‐view analysis of EDS mapping of MPP‐H‐3 textiles and their atomic percentages also supports the successful fabrication of MPP‐H textiles (Figure S9, Supporting Information). These analyses demonstrated that robust cross‐linking was successfully formed in MPP through the interactions between hydroxyl and carboxyl groups. This cross‐linking establishes a strong interface between MPP and Hanji, enhances durability against swelling, increases the interlayer spacing of MXene, improves hydrophilicity, and reduces impurities. These advancements are expected to enhance the mechanical, electrical, and thermal properties of the MPP‐H, making them highly suitable for multifunctional smart textile applications.

Energy Harvesting Performance of MPP‐H Textiles

Benefiting from synergistic integrations, MPP‐H textiles can be utilized as multifunctional smart textiles for energy harvesting, particularly through flow‐to‐energy conversion mechanisms (Figure 4 ). Figure 4a illustrates a schematic of energy harvesting from ambient water sources around the human body and the environment, emphasizing the flow‐to‐energy conversion principle. Driven by the hydrophilicity of MPP‐H, water permeates the textiles via internal microfluidic channels and evaporates, creating a cyclic flow (Figure 4a(i)). This fluid movement generates electrical energy owing to ion distribution differences across the textiles. More specifically, the microchannels within MPP layers possess a negative surface charge and can establish an electrical double layer (EDL) upon contact with polar fluids (Figure 4a(ii); Figure S10, Supporting Information). Cations are captured by the Stern layer along MPP surfaces, and their movement due to internal pressure gradients generates a streaming current along the textiles. Typically, conventional energy harvesting technologies based on streaming current systems employ low‐conductive channels, and the potential difference is caused by the flow‐induced positive charge gradient within the channels.[ 36 ] In contrast, MPP‐H textiles utilize highly conductive MPP channels with abundant free electrons, which enhance the attraction of cations to the channel surfaces. This increased surface charge strengthens the EDL, leading to more efficient ionic movement during fluid flow. Consequently, the MPP‐H channels can maximize localized charge differences and minimize energy losses, thereby improving the overall energy conversion efficiency of the textiles (Figure S11, Supporting Information). Additionally, owing to the broadly adaptable operating principle, the MPP‐H textiles may generate power using various polar working fluids containing different ions (Figure S12, Supporting Information).

The corresponding d) peak power density and e) energy density for determining the optimal configuration of MPP‐H textiles. f) Repeatability test of OCV and SCC for the MPP‐H‐3 textile using 20 µL of 0.05 m NaCl solution drops. g) Long‐term power measurement for sustainable power generation when 2 µL of 0.5 m NaCl solution was applied repeatedly at a rate of 1.08 Hz on the MPP‐H‐3 textile.

To evaluate the flow‐to‐energy harvesting performance, MPP‐H textiles (5 mm × 60 mm) were tested for open‐circuit voltage (OCV) and short‐circuit current (SCC) using a 20 µL drop of 0.5 M NaCl as the working fluid (Figure 4b,c). The OCV peak value decreased from 0.17 V in MPP‐H‐1 to 0.06 V in MPP‐H‐4, whereas the SCC peak was gradually enhanced from 0.002 mA in MPP‐H‐1 to 0.13 mA in MPP‐H‐4, with sustained operation for over 400 s using only single drop of working fluid. The opposing trends between OCV and SCC are related to the internal resistance of MPP‐H textiles (Figure S11, Supporting Information). Based on the tendency, MPP‐H‐3 yielded an optimal power density of 102.2 µW cm−3, while those for MPP‐H‐1, MPP‐H‐2, MPP‐H‐4 were 3.2, 50.0, and 65.0 µW cm−3, respectively (Figure 4d). For energy generation, the highest energy density was obtained in MPP‐H‐2 (31.0 mWh cm−3) by applying 20 µL of working fluid (Figure 4e). The varying flow rates in the microchannels affect the sustained time of power generation and the energy density generated from a single drop of the working fluid. In this study, MPP‐H‐2 exhibited the highest energy output because its sustained time exceeded 800 s. In contrast, MPP‐H‐3 could achieve optimal performance using continuous drops of working fluids. Notably, MPP‐H textiles surpassed reported materials in both power generation and energy density through effective flow‐to‐energy conversion (Table S1, Supporting Information).

The repeatability of the optimal MPP‐H‐3 textile was assessed by measuring its OCV and SCC through cyclic applications of 0.05 M NaCl working fluid (Figure 4f). The OCV and SCC peak values increased from initial 8.14 mV and 5.46 µA with repeated cycles to 15.34 mV and 11.92 µA, respectively, suggesting that the accumulation of salts within the MPP‐H channels gradually increased the concentration of the working fluid (Figure S13, Supporting Information). This cumulative effect demonstrates that human sweat or environmental humidity can effectively serve as sustainable energy sources. Furthermore, long‐term power generation was investigated when 2 µL of 0.5 M NaCl solution was applied repeatedly at a rate of 1.08 Hz (Figure 4g). MPP‐H‐3 textile could generate stable OCV and SCC outputs over 60 min in response to these repeated drops. Given the demonstrated stability and sustainability of the power generation from MPP‐H textiles, integrating this energy with appropriate storage solutions, such as capacitors or batteries, could further enhance the practical applications. These exceptional flow‐to‐energy conversion performances of MPP‐H textiles are based on their synergistic integrations (Figure 3). The Robust cross‐linking within the textiles enhances the hydrophilicity and durability against swelling, thereby improving the energy efficiency in aqueous environments (Note S3, Supporting Information). Moreover, the extended spacing between MXene layers promotes the mobility of working fluid and ions within EDL systems, and the reduced oxide impurities in MPP improve the electrical properties for energy conversion. Thus, MPP‐H textiles hold promise for generating power from human sweat and environmental humidity for multifunctional smart textile technologies.

Electromagnetic Shielding Performance of MPP‐Hs

The asymmetric configuration of MPP‐H textiles, layered with MPP composites onto Hanji textile, facilitates effective electromagnetic wave absorption, providing excellent absorption‐based EMI shielding capabilities (Figure 5 ). In the MPP‐filled Hanji region, the micropores formed by the network of randomly distributed fibers in Hanji, combined with the conductivity of the MPP, absorb and gradually dissipate incident electromagnetic waves (EMWs) (Figure 5a; Note S4, Supporting Information). Residual waves penetrate the MPP layers through the interface between MPP and Hanji. The highly conductive MPP reflects the EMWs, which are then re‐absorbed by Hanji. This stepwise process (first absorption, reflection, and secondary absorption) extends the travel path, thereby increasing the overall absorption rate. Additionally, the enlarged layer spacing induced by cross‐linking enables numerous reflections within the multilayered MPP structure, further absorbing EMWs and converting them into thermal energy. Based on the complementary and absorption‐based EMI shielding mechanism of MPP and Hanji, the EMI shielding capabilities of various MPP‐H textiles were examined across the X‐band frequency range from 8.2 to 12.4 GHz (Figure 5b). The total EMI shielding effectiveness (SEtotal) of MPP‐H textiles was significantly improved with MPP loading, notably reaching nearly 50 dB at 12.4 GHz for MPP‐H‐4. The corresponding average values of absorptance (A), reflectance (R), and transmittance (T) were calculated (Figure 5c; Note S5, Supporting Information). All MPP‐H textiles exhibited almost zero transmission (T) and high fractions in absorptance (A) and reflectance (R). As the MPP loading increased, the electrical conductivity of the textiles improved, with a significant increase in the fraction of R from MPP‐H‐3 (Figure S11 and Note S4, Supporting Information). Specifically, the absolute SE values of absorption (SEA), reflection (SER), and SEtotal and their relative ratios in the different MPP‐H textiles were investigated (Figure 5d,e). In all cases, SEA accounted for >90.3% of SEtotal, highlighting that absorption is the predominant mechanism in EMI shielding for MPP‐H textiles.

Reference numbers are aligned with the reference list in the Supporting Information.

The EMI shielding performance of MPP‐H textiles was compared with previous studies using textile materials based on the SE per unit thickness and the A/R ratio (Figure 5f; Table S2, Supporting Information). When the A/R ratios of MPP‐H textiles reached up to 4.5, the SE per unit thickness reached 437.6 dB mm−1, indicating outstanding shielding performance through the absorbing mechanism compared to previous technologies. The specific configurational features of MPP‐H textiles, resulting from the integration of MPP with Hanji, can effectively enhance the EMI shielding capabilities. MPP‐H textiles offer excellent electromagnetic shielding performance relative to their thickness, making them both lightweight and comfortable, allowing for seamless integration into various devices, such as energy harvesters. Additionally, their high flexibility enables easy processing into different forms, while their compact design makes them ideal for mobile devices and wearable technologies. Moreover, the predominance of the absorption mechanism over reflection in EMI shielding offers several advantages, such as reducing secondary electromagnetic interference and minimizing signal reflections that could otherwise cause detection. This feature is particularly beneficial for military applications, where stealth capabilities are paramount. The ability of MPP‐H textiles to dominantly absorb EMWs can be extended to the development of advanced military smart textiles, offering effective stealth performance. This promising characteristic aligns with current advancements in 2D textile‐based stealth technologies, potentially providing multifunctional benefits in both EMI shielding and energy harvesting applications.

Thermo‐Chemo‐Mechanical Characteristics of MPP‐H Textiles

Figure 6 shows the thermo‐chemo‐mechanical robustness of MPP‐H textiles. First, the flame resistance of MPP‐H‐0 (pristine Hanji), M‐H, and MPP‐H‐3 textiles was assessed (Figure 6a; Figure S14 and Video S1, Supporting Information). Upon direct exposure to a flame, MPP‐H‐0 were ignited and burnt out within 15 s and 18 s, respectively, and the ignition and burnout times for M‐H were 18 s and 22 s, respectively (Figure 6a(i),(ii),b). In contrast, the MPP‐H‐3 textile resisted ignition for over 7 min, effectively inhibiting flame spread and protecting nearby objects (Figure 6a(iii),b). The improved flame resistance of MPP‐H‐3 is attributed to the cross‐linking of MPP, which enhances the thermal stability of textile structures. When the flame‐resistant MPP‐H‐3 is used as a wearable smart textile, it provides additional protection against thermal hazards.

Thermo‐chemo‐mechanical characteristics of MPP‐H textiles. a) Flame resistance test of i) MPP‐H‐0, ii) M‐H, and iii) MPP‐H‐3 textiles and b) their sustained time against ignition. c) Joule heating capability of MPP‐H textiles. d) Stepwise Joule heating test of the MPP‐H‐3 textile under various input voltage levels ranging from 0.5 to 5 V. e) Time constant (τ) to reach 63.2% of the saturation temperature at 4.5 V input voltage and f) corresponding time constants for various input voltages ranging from 0.5 to 5 V. g) Hysteresis test for repeatable Joule heating capability of MPP‐H textiles at 3 V input voltage. h) Differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) curves of the MPP‐H textiles at the 1st and 50th cycles of the thermal stability (measured from ‐20 °C to 80 °C at heat flows from ‐1.3 to 2 W g−1). i) Electrical resistance and Joule heating temperature at an input voltage of 3 V under bending angles of 0°, 60°, 90°, 120°, and 180°. j) Washability and durability test of MPP‐H‐0, M‐H, and MPP‐H‐3 textiles using a 1 g L−1 detergent solution at 40 °C with vortex mixing at 500 rpm for 5 min. k) Relative changes in weight and electrical resistance after washing. 100% indicates that the original weight and electrical resistance are maintained.

The MPP‐H textiles also demonstrate Joule heating capability. As shown in Figure 6c, thermal energy is generated through the Joule heating mechanism by applying a direct current (DC) power input. Specifically, the MPP‐H‐3 textile was cut into 60 × 10 mm2 pieces and tested at incremental voltage levels from 0.5 to 5 V over 1 min (Figure 6d). The surface temperature was increased from 23.4 °C at 0.5 V to 123.8 °C at 5 V, offering a wide temperature range from comfortable warmth to the human body (20–25 °C) to near the boiling point of water (≈100 °C) (Figures S15 and S16, Supporting Information). The saturation temperature is directly correlated with the input voltage, allowing precise temperature control by adjusting the DC voltage (Figure S17, Supporting Information). The low voltage levels required for Joule heating ensure the safety of human application. To assess the response time of the applied voltage, the time constant (τ) was determined when the temperature reached 63.2% of the thermal saturation value (Figure 6e). Across all voltage ranges, τ was consistently below 7.5 s, indicating rapid temperature modulation capabilities (Figure 6f). This swift response is attributed to the cross‐linking of MPP with robust interfaces and reduced impurities, which provide suitable electrical resistance and thermal properties essential for efficient Joule heating (Figure S11, Supporting Information). In addition, the durability and long‐term stability of MPP‐H textiles for Joule heating were evaluated through a hysteresis test using a 3 V input voltage (Figure 6g). The saturation temperature exhibited minimal oscillation (lower than 2.1%) during multiple heating and cooling cycles, highlighting the reliability and stability of the Joule heating function in MPP‐H textiles. Furthermore, the thermal stability of MPP‐H textiles was investigated using differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) in a temperature range from −20 to 80 °C with a heat flow range of −1.3 to 2 W g−1 (Figure 6h). DSC curves measured at the 1st and 50th cycles could demonstrate exceptional thermal stability.

Meanwhile, the thermo‐mechanical and chemical durability of MPP‐H textiles was evaluated by bending and washing tests (Figure 6i–k). The electrical resistance and Joule heating temperature variations of MPP‐H textiles were measured under different bending angles (Figure 6i). At a constant input voltage of 3 V, minimal oscillations were observed in both temperature and resistance. Specifically, under the bending angles of 0°, 60°, 90°, 120°, and 180°, the temperature oscillated by only 0.7 °C, while electrical resistance fluctuated by 0.01 Ω. Additionally, tensile loading, twisting, and folding tests were conducted, demonstrating the excellent mechanical properties of the MPP‐H textiles under these conditions (Figure S18, Supporting Information). Moreover, the washability of the MPP‐H‐0, M‐H, and MPP‐H textiles was evaluated in a 1 g L−1 detergent solution at 40 °C (Figure 6j; Video S2, Supporting Information). After vortex mixing at 500 rpm for 5 min, MPP‐H‐0 and M‐H textiles could not withstand the shear stress and aqueous environments and were torn and dispersed in the solution. In contrast, MPP‐H‐3 maintained its structure and morphology, successfully enduring the harsh chemo‐mechnical conditions. The washed MPP‐H‐3 only exhibited a 1.2% loss in weight and a minimal 0.4% change in electrical resistance (Figure 6k). The results were further validated by an ultrasonication test at 230 W and 40 °C, which showed that the electrical resistance of M‐H increased by 728%, while MPP‐H‐3 only changed by 2.0% (Figure S19, Supporting Information). These findings confirm the resistance of MPP‐H textiles to swelling and mechanical stress, which is due to their excellent cross‐linking and interfacial properties.

Through the above comprehensive evaluations, MPP‐H textiles demonstrated flame resistance, rapidly responsive Joule heating, and high flexibility. The synergistic integration of PAA, PVA, and MXene with Hanji promoted the formation of a robust interface and effective cross‐linking, providing high thermo‐chemo‐mechanical durability. The multifunctional capabilities, combined with energy harvesting and EMI shielding functions, warrant the feasibility of MPP‐H textiles as versatile smart textiles. This straightforward integration method not only ensures cost‐effectiveness but also delivers exceptional multifunctionality and high performance. The successful demonstration of multifunctionality and robustness of MPP‐H textiles highlights their potential to revolutionize the relevant fields. These textiles offer a practical solution for next‐generation smart textiles, paving the way for innovative advancements in wearable and smart technologies.

Conclusion

In summary, this study successfully developed multifunctional and wearable MPP‐H by integrating MPP with Hanji for advanced smart textile applications. MXene was first cross‐linked with PVA and PAA to form MPP composites, and MPP was deposited onto microporous Hanji via vacuum filtration. This method yielded flexible MPP‐H textiles characterized by asymmetric MPP deposition, gradient structures, and notable surface characteristics. The unique structure and effective incorporation of multi‐materials enable high‐performance functionalities, such as energy harvesting, EMI shielding, flame resistance, Joule heating capabilities, and chemo‐mechanical durability. Chemical characterization confirmed successful cross‐linking within MPP, resulting in the formation of ester bonds and increased interlayer spacing. Enhanced hydrophilicity and reduced impurities were observed, making MPP‐H textiles suitable for aqueous applications and energy conversion. For energy harvesting, the textiles demonstrated effective flow‐to‐energy conversion using human sweat and surrounding water. Notably, MPP‐H textiles achieved an optimal power density of 102.2 µW cm−3 and an energy density of 31.0 mWh cm−3 with a single drop of 0.5 m NaCl, supporting sustainable and repeatable power generation for over 60 min. In addition, MPP‐H textiles exhibited exceptional EMI shielding performance in the X‐band frequency range (8.2 to 12.4 GHz), leveraging a complementary absorption‐based mechanism in MPP and Hanji. With A/R ratios reaching 4.5, the SE per unit thickness can reach 437.6 dB mm−1, outperforming conventional textile materials. The MPP‐H textiles also exhibited superior thermo‐chemo‐mechanical properties, including effective flame resistance, precise temperature control with low‐voltage inputs, and strong durability. The cost‐effective integration of these diverse functionalities into a single textile is crucial for enhancing overall performance and utility. By combining energy harvesting, EMI shielding, heating, and mechanical robustness, MPP‐H textiles provide a comprehensive solution suitable for a wide range of applications, from advanced wearable technologies to integrated smart systems. This integrated approach demonstrates the potential of multifunctional textiles to address complex and varied requirements in next‐generation smart applications.

Experimental Section

Fabrication of MPP‐H Textiles

MXene (Ti3C2Tx) was synthesized by etching MAX phase precursors (Ti3AlC2, Sigma‐Aldrich, ≤40 µm particle size) in hydrofluoric acid (HF) following a modified HCl/LiF method (refer to Note S1, Supporting Information). The resulting MXene solution, at a concentration of 3 g L−1, was combined with polyvinyl alcohol (PVA; Sigma‐Aldrich, Mw = 89,000–98,000) and poly(acrylic acid) (PAA; Sigma‐Aldrich, Mv = 450,000) powders, each at a concentration of 0.75 g L−1 in the solution. The mixture was homogenized using a vortex generator for 15 s and cross‐linked using a bath sonication system for 60 min to obtain MXene/PVA/PAA (MPP) composites with a MXene:PVA:PAA mass ratio of 4:1:1. For vacuum filtration, the filtration apparatus was equipped with Hanji (Traditional Hanji, Dongwon Paper Co., Korea) as a filter membrane. The cross‐linked MPP mixture was poured onto the Hanji membrane and subjected to vacuum filtration to obtain MXene/PVA/PAA‐Hanji (MPP‐H) textiles. The textiles prepared by filtering 0, 20, 40, 60, and 80 mL MPP solutions were denoted as MPP‐H‐0 (pristine Hanji), MPP‐H‐1, MPP‐H‐2, MPP‐H‐3, and MPP‐H‐4, respectively. After drying at room temperature for 6 h, the final MPP‐H textiles had mass loadings of 0, 0.63, 1.56, 2.77, and 3.51 mg cm−2, respectively.

Characterization of MPP‐H Textiles

The morphological characteristics of the surfaces and cross‐sections of MPP‐H textiles were examined using a field emission scanning electron microscope (FE‐SEM; FEI, Quanta 250 FEG, USA). Elemental distributions of the textiles were investigated using energy dispersive spectrometry (EDS; AMETEK, APOLLO XPP, USA) mapping. Chemical compositions and characteristics of the MPP‐H textiles were analyzed using Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FT‐IR; Agilent, Cary 630 FTIR), X‐ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS; Thermo Fisher Scientific, K‐Alpha), and X‐ray diffraction (XRD; Bruker, D8 Advance). Contact angle was measured to evaluate the hydrophilicity of MXene MPP, using a 5 µL droplet of deionized (DI) water on M‐H and MPP‐H textiles. The contact angles were characterized after stabilization and analyzed using Image J software.

Evaluation of Energy Harvesting Performance

For energy harvesting tests, gold‐plated electric clips were used as electrodes and attached to both ends of the MPP‐H textiles (5 mm × 60 mm), and platinum electrode holders were connected to the clips for electrical measurements. Various solutions were used as working fluids, including DI water, ethyl alcohol (Daejung, anhydrous, ≥99.5% purity), lithium chloride (LiCl, Kanto Chemical Co., ≥98.2% purity), potassium chloride (KCl, Sigma‐Aldrich, ≥98.2% purity), and sodium chloride (NaCl, Sigma‐Aldrich, ≥99.5% purity) in concentrations ranging from 0.01 to 2.0 m. These solutions were dropped onto the MPP‐H textiles, and the energy harvesting performance was evaluated by measuring OCV and SCC using a digital multimeter (Keysight, 34401 A), an oscilloscope (Tektronix, DPO2004B), and a source meter (Keithley, 2636A), interfaced with MATLAB scripts.

EMI Shielding Performance

The EMI shielding performance of MPP‐H textiles was analyzed using a network analyzer (Agilent Technologies, E8364B) in the X‐band frequency range (8.2–12.4 GHz). Each MPP‐H textile was assessed for its SEA, SER, and SET. The electrical resistance of MPP‐H textiles was measured using a 4‐point probe sheet resistance meter (AiT Co., CMT‐SR1000N, Korea), and the intrinsic resistivity was calculated based on the thickness of the textile.

Evaluation of Thermo‐Chemo‐Mechanical Capabilities

The Joule heating capability of MPP‐H textiles was evaluated by applying various input powers ranging from 0.5 V to 5.0 V in a constant voltage mode using a direct current (DC) power supply (ITECH Electronics, IT6724H). The surface temperature of the MPP‐H textiles was monitored during Joule heating using an infrared (IR) thermal imaging camera (FLIR, T420). Differential scanning calorimetry (DSC; NETZSCH, 214 Polyma) curves from the 1st to 50th Joule heating cycles were measured in a temperature range of −20 °C to 80 °C with a heat flow range of −1.3 to 2 W g−1. The thermal stability of the MPP‐H textiles was analyzed by comparing the DSC curves of the 1st and 50th Joule heating cycles. The bendability was assessed by measuring the electrical resistance and Joule heating temperature at an applied voltage of 3 V under bending angles of 0°, 60°, 90°, 120°, and 180°. The washability was tested using a commercial liquid detergent containing sodium lauryl ether sulfate (SM Green Co., Korea) at a concentration of 1 g L−1. The testing was conducted at 40 °C by vortex mixing at 500 rpm using a magnetic stirrer for 5 min. An ultrasonication test was performed using a bath sonicator (POWER SONIC 420, Hwashin Technology, Korea) at 230 W and 40 °C for 1 h.
