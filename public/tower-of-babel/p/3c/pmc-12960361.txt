MAGL Inhibition Relieves Psoriasiform Inflammation and Pruritus Via Modulation of ALOX12-12-HETE Axis in Mice
Authors: Shiying Lin, Xin Liu, Jian Jiang, Wenqiang Ge, Qingxiao Tao, Suwen Liu, Ouyang Zhanmu, Yang Yang, Man Li, Hongxiang Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe endogenous cannabinoid system, comprising endocannabinoids, cannabinoid receptors, and enzymes responsible for synthesis and degradation of endocannabinoids, is integral to the regulation of skin inflammation. However, the precise contributions of cannabinoid-metabolizing enzymes including fatty acid amide hydrolase and monoacylglycerol lipase (MAGL) remain poorly defined in psoriatic pathogenesis. In this study, we demonstrated the significant MAGL upregulation in psoriatic lesions and established that pharmacological inhibition of MAGL markedly reduced psoriasiform inflammation and pruritus in imiquimod-challenged C57BL/6 mice. Mechanically, inhibition of MAGL decreased the expression of 12-lipoxygenase (ALOX12), subsequently reducing the synthesis of 12-hydroxyeicosatetraenoic acid (12-HETE), which is the principal metabolite of ALOX12. Importantly, these effects were reversed by cannabinoid receptor 2 (CB2R) antagonism or knockout, establishing a signaling axis in which MAGL inhibition activates CB2R to suppress ALOX12. Collectively, our findings identified MAGL as a potentially targetable nexus in psoriasiform inflammation and itch.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s10753-025-02383-5.Keywords: Psoriasis, Monoacylglycerol lipase, Arachidonic acid, Inflammation, Pruritus

Introduction

Psoriasis is a chronic, relapsing inflammatory dermatosis affecting an estimated 60 million individuals worldwide, characterized by scaly erythematous plaques with either localized or widespread distribution [1]. Clinically, pruritus is reported in approximately 60%-80% of individuals with psoriasis, substantially affecting their quality of life [2]. Although the pathogenesis of psoriasis is complex and remains incompletely elucidated, emerging evidence has suggested a potential role of the endocannabinoid system (ECS) in modulating both inflammatory and pruritic responses. The ECS is composed of endogenous ligands (such as 2-arachidonoylglycerol [2-AG] and N-arachidonoyl ethanolamine [AEA]), cannabinoid receptors (CB1R/CB2R), and metabolic enzymes [3]. Our previous experimental findings have established the significant involvement of CB1R and CB2R in the inflammatory and pruritic signaling of psoriasis [4, 5].

As the principal catabolic enzymes of the ECS, fatty acid amide hydrolase (FAAH) and monoacylglycerol lipase (MAGL) are responsible for the degradation of AEA and 2-AG, respectively [6]. Preclinical studies demonstrate that inhibition of FAAH or MAGL elevates endocannabinoid levels, exerting potent anti-inflammatory and antipruritic effects through the activation of CB1R or CB2R [7–11]. Notably, this enzymatic inhibition confers therapeutic effects comparable to those of direct CBRs agonists, while precluding the neuropsychiatric adverse effects associated with Δ9-tetrahydrocannabinol (THC) [12]. For example, Jiang et al.. showed that a peripherally restricted MAGL inhibitor reduced inflammation and pain without central nervous system effects [13]. Similarly, Lichtman et al.. demonstrated that FAAH and MAGL inhibition provided analgesia with fewer adverse effects than direct CB1R agonists [14]. These findings highlight the advantages of targeting ECS metabolic enzymes rather than directly activating cannabinoid receptors. However, the specific role of MAGL or FAAH in psoriasis pathogenesis remain unexplored.

Beyond their role in ECS regulation, cannabinoid-metabolizing enzymes are also critical in modulating arachidonic acid (AA) metabolism. Specifically, MAGL and FAAH hydrolyze 2-AG and AEA into AA, which is the principal substrate for cyclooxygenase (COX), lipoxygenase (LOX), and cytochrome P450 (CYP) pathways [15]. These AA-derived eicosanoids orchestrate cutaneous inflammation through facilitating immune cell recruitment and stimulating the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines including interleukin-1 (IL-1) and IL-23 [16–18]. Importantly, modulation of FAAH or MAGL activity differentially regulates the expression profiles of AA-metabolizing enzymes and the resulting eicosanoid production [19–22]. Within the skin, eicosanoids have been implicated in the pathogenesis of various skin disorders including psoriasis and chronic pruritus [23–25]. This bidirectional regulatory capacity positions cannabinoid-metabolizing enzymes as critical nodes interfacing ECS signaling with AA-mediated inflammatory cascades.

Given this background, we sought to investigate the therapeutic potential of inhibiting endocannabinoid-degrading enzymes in the context of psoriasis. Our experimental data indicated that inhibition of MAGL achieved significant efficacy in attenuating both inflammatory markers and pruritic severity in imiquimod (IMQ)-induced mouse model. Furthermore, we elucidated the mechanisms by which MAGL influences psoriasis, with a particular emphasis on its interactions with AA metabolism. These results suggested targeting MAGL represents a promising therapeutic strategy for the management of psoriasis.

Materials and Methods

Reagents

5% imiquimod cream was obtained from Mingxin Pharmaceuticals (Sichuan, China); the MAGL inhibitor JZL184 (HY-15249) and the ALOX12 inhibitor ML355 (HY-12341) was purchased from MedChenExpress (New Jersey, USA); the CB1R antagonist AM251 (abs813473) was sourced from Absin (Shanghai, China) and the CB2R antagonist AM630 (A124994) was acquired from Aladdin (Shanghai, China). ML355 was selected as ALOX12 inhibitor based on prior reports of in vivo efficacy in murine models [26], despite reported ortholog-specific potency differences in recombinant enzyme assays [27]. JZL184, AM251, AM630 and ML355 were dissolved in a solution consisting of 10% DMSO, 40% PEG300, 5% Tween-80 and 45% physiological saline solution (0.9% NaCl). 12-HETE (No. 34550) was purchased from Cayman Chemical (Michigan, USA) and diluted in physiological saline solution (0.9% NaCl). Recombinant cytokines IL-17 A (ENZ-PRT188-0025), IL-22 (ENZ-PRT250-0010), Oncostatin-M (RLT-100-088), IL-1α (ENZ-PRT186-0010), and TNF-α (ALX-520-002-C010) cytokines were purchased from PeproTech (RockyHill, USA).

Animals

Seven-week-old male C57BL/6J mice were purchased from Shulaibao Biotechnology Co., Ltd (Wuhan, China). CB2R knockout male mice (strain: B6.129P2-Cnr2tm1Dgen/J) of the same age and weight were generated by Jackson Laboratories using ES cell-targeted homologous recombination, resulting in a permanent, global deletion of the Cnr2 gene. All animals were housed and bred under specific pathogen-free (SPF) conditions at the Animal Center of Huazhong University of Science and Technology, with controlled temperature, humidity, and a 12-hour light/dark cycle. All the animal experimental procedures were conducted in strict accordance with the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals and the study involving animal experiments was reviewed and approved by The Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Huazhong University of Science and Technology (IACUC Number: 4509). During the experiment, all mice were assigned randomly to different groups, and the experimenters were unaware of the assignment. JZL184 (5 or 10 mg/kg), AM251 (3 mg/kg), AM630 (2.5 mg/kg), ML355 (5 or 10 mg/kg), and 12-HETE (5 or 10 ng/g) were administered via subcutaneous (s.c.) injection into shaved areas of the neck. For each administration, 50 µL of the drug solution was injected at 2 separate sites, once daily at the same time. AM251 and AM630 were injected 1 h prior to JZL184 administration to ensure adequate receptor blockade. The scratching behavior of the mice was tested in a blinded manner. We used n = 5 per group, which is commonly applied in comparable in vivo studies [4, 5], although subtle effects may require larger cohorts.

Human Subjects

The study involving human skin biopsies was reviewed and approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of Tongji Medical College of Huazhong University of Science and Technology (Wuhan, China). Informed consent was obtained from all donors, and all procedures were performed in accordance with the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. For immunohistochemistry analysis, skin samples from psoriasis patients and healthy controls were collected through surgical biopsies at Union Hospital, Tongji Medical College, Huazhong University of Science and Technology. The healthy group included a 43-year-old man (neck) and a 52-year-old woman (neck). The psoriasis group included a 34-year-old woman (back, PASI score 14.8) and a 28-year-old woman (back, PASI score 6.0). Psoriasis Area and Severity Index (PASI) scoring was performed as previously described [28]. Representative images per group are presented.

Genotyping of CB2R Knockout Mice

Genotyping was performed by PCR using genomic DNA extracted from mouse tail biopsies. Three primers were used to distinguish wild-type (WT) and knockout (KO) alleles: P1, 5’-GGGGATCGATCCGTCCTGTAAGTCT-3’; P2, 5’-GACTAGAGCTTTGTAGGTAGGCGGG-3’; and P3, 5’-GGAGTTCAACCCCATGAAGGAGTAC-3’. PCR products were resolved on a 2% agarose gel containing ethidium bromide and visualized under UV illumination. The expected band sizes were approximately 385 bp for the WT allele and 550 bp for the mutant allele. Mice displaying a single band at 385 bp were identified as WT, while those with a single band at 550 bp were identified as homozygous KO. Animals showing both bands were identified as heterozygotes and were excluded from subsequent experiments.

Mouse Model of Psoriasis Induced by IMQ

Seven-week-old WT male C57BL/6 mice and CB2R−/− male mice were acclimatized to the test environment for 30 min daily over 3 to 5 days. Psoriasiform dermatitis was induced by applying 5% IMQ cream topically to the shaved neck skin for 5 consecutive days, while the control group received Vaseline jelly treatment instead [29]. Disease severity was evaluated using the PASI, which independently scores erythema, scaling, and skin thickening on a scale from 0 to 4 (0 = none, 1 = slight, 2 = moderate, 3 = marked, 4 = very marked). The sum of these scores yields a total severity score ranging from 0 to 12 [30]. PASI assessments were recorded daily to monitor disease progression.

Cell Culture and Treatment

HaCaT keratinocytes (SCSP-5091) were obtained from Kunming Cell Bank of Type Culture Collection, Chinese Academy of Science (Kunming, China). The cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (Thermo Fisher, Waltham, USA; Cat. No. 11965092) supplemented with 10% Fetal Bovine Serum (Thermo Fisher, Waltham, USA; Cat. No. A5670701) in a humidified incubator at 37 °C with 5% CO2. Primary mouse keratinocytes were extracted as previously described [31]. Briefly, tail skin was collected from adult male mice (6–15 weeks) and incubated overnight in keratinocyte growth medium (Procell, Wuhan, China; Cat. No. CM-M094-100), containing basal medium supplemented with 2% FBS, 1% penicillin-streptomycin (P/S), and keratinocyte growth additives (0.06 mM calcium chloride, epidermal growth factor, bovine transferrin, insulin-like growth factor c1, prostaglandin E2, bovine serum albumin, and hydrocortisone), together with 4 mg/mL Dispase II® (Sigma, St.Louis, USA; Cat. No. 42613-33-2) on a rotator. After 12–18 h, the dermis and epidermis were peeled off and the epidermis was incubated in 0.25% (w/v) trypsin solution (Thermo Fisher, Waltham, USA; Cat. No. 15050065) for 30 min at room temperature. After digestion, epidermis was rubbed repeatedly, collecting the cell suspension, breaking the cell clumps and passing them through a 100 μm filter. The filtered cells were centrifuged, resuspended in 1 mL of keratinocyte growth medium, and seeded in a 12-well plate at a density of 105 cells per well. To establish a psoriatic inflammation model in vitro, these cells were treated with M5 cytokine cocktail (IL-17 A, TNF-α, IL-1α, IL-22, and Oncostatin-M, each at a final concentration of 2.5 or 5 ng/mL) or IL-17 A alone (20 or 50 ng/mL). Cell cultures were routinely tested for Mycoplasma contamination using the Mycoplasma Stain Assay Kit (Beyotime, Shanghai, China; Cat. No. C0296) according to the manufacturer’s instructions, and no contamination was detected.

Measurement of 2-AG in Mouse Skin

Cutaneous 2-AG levels were quantified using a CBA detection kit (Dakewe, Shenzhen, China; Cat. No. JOT-EK1699Mo) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, dorsal skin samples were collected, weighed, and homogenized in ice-cold PBS. Homogenates were centrifuged at 5000 × g for 5 min at 4 °C, and the supernatants were collected for analysis. 2-AG concentrations were calculated based on a standard curve and normalized to tissue weight (µg/g). All samples were processed on ice, and experiments were conducted in triplicate to ensure accuracy.

Itch Behavioral Assessment

On the designated days (Day 1, Day 3 and Day 5), the mice were individually placed in separate transparent box and their scratching behaviors were recorded for 30- or 60-minutes using video cameras. The scratching bouts were counted by two trained observers. A scratch bout was defined as a rapid lifting of the hind paw to scratch the shaved skin, concluding with either licking or placing the hind paw back on the floor [4].

Histology and Immunohistochemistry

Animals were euthanized using sodium pentobarbital anesthesia, after which the neck skin was fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde overnight at 4 °C, dehydrated through graded ethanol, cleared in xylene, and embedded in paraffin. Tissue Sect. (15 μm thick) were cut using a microtome and mounted on glass slides. For H&E, sections were deparaffinized in xylene (2 × 10 min), rehydrated through graded ethanol (100%, 95%, 80%, 70%, 5 min each), rinsed in distilled water, stained with hematoxylin (Beyotime, Shanghai, China; Cat. No. C0105S) for 5 min, washed in running tap water for 5 min, differentiated in 1% acid alcohol for 10 s, blued in 0.2% ammonia water for 30 s, counterstained with eosin (Beyotime, Shanghai, China; Cat. No. C0105S) for 1 min, dehydrated through graded ethanol, cleared in xylene, and mounted with neutral resin mounting medium. For immunohistochemical (IHC) staining, sections were deparaffinized and rehydrated as above, followed by antigen retrieval in citrate buffer (10 mM, pH 6.0; Beyotime, Shanghai, China; Cat. No. P0083) at 95 °C for 20 min and cooling to room temperature. Endogenous peroxidase activity was quenched with 3% hydrogen peroxide in methanol for 10 min. Sections were blocked with 5% bovine serum albumin in PBS containing 0.1% Triton X-100 for 1 h at room temperature. The sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with anti-MAGL (1:100; Abclonal, Wuhan, China; Cat. No. A6654) diluted in blocking buffer. After three washes with PBS (5 min each), sections were incubated for 1 h at room temperature with HRP-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG (1:200; Abclonal, Wuhan, China; Cat. No. AS014). Immunoreactivity was visualized using a DAB substrate kit (Boster Biological Technology, Wuhan, China; Cat. No. AR1027) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Following DAB staining, sections were counterstained with hematoxylin for 2 min, washed, dehydrated, cleared in xylene, and mounted with neutral resin mounting medium. To verify the specificity of MAGL immunolabeling, negative control was prepared by processing tissue sections in parallel but omitting the primary antibody. All other incubation and washing steps were performed identically to the experimental sections. No specific staining was observed in these controls. Images were acquired by microscopy (Olympus DO80, Japan) at 200× magnification. For each mouse, the epidermal thickness was measured in five randomly selected fields per section using Image J software (v1.54f), as previously described [4]. The mean value per mouse was used for statistical analysis.

Immunofluorescence

Skin samples were collected and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, followed by dehydration in 30% sucrose and embedding in OCT for sectioning into 15 μm slices. The sections were blocked with blocking buffer (5% donkey serum and 0.2% Triton X-100 in PBS) for 1 h at 37 °C. Primary antibodies were diluted in the same blocking buffer and incubated overnight at 4 °C: anti-CB2R (1:200; Abcam, Cambridge, UK; Cat. No. ab3561) and anti-ALOX12 (1:50; Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Dallas, TX, USA; Cat. No. sc-365194). After three rinses in PBS (5 min each), sections were incubated for 1 h at room temperature with secondary antibodies Alexa Fluor®488 (1:500; Invitrogen, Waltham, MA, USA; Cat. No. A-11008) or Alexa-Fluor®594 (1:500; Invitrogen, Waltham, MA, USA; Cat. No. A-11005) diluted in blocking buffer. Nuclear staining was performed using DAPI (1 µg/mL diluted in ultrapure water; Invitrogen, Waltham, MA, USA; Cat. No. D21490) at room temperature for 5 min, followed by PBS washes. For negative controls, primary antibodies were omitted during the incubation step. Sections were otherwise processed identically, including blocking, washing, and incubation with secondary antibodies. These negative control sections exhibited no specific fluorescence signal, confirming the specificity of the antibody staining. Imaging was performed on a confocal microscope (Nikon, AX) using a 20× objective lens. Z-stack images were acquired with an optical section thickness of approximately 2.5 μm.

UHPLC-MS/MS and Analysis

For skin samples, a 25 mg aliquot of each individual sample was accurately weighed, cut into small pieces, and extracted with extract solution (80% methanol/H2O, v/v). The extracts were purified using Solid-Phase Extraction and filtered to obtain a clear supernatant. Chromatographic separation was performed using an EXIONLC System (Sciex) equipped with a Waters ACQUITY UPLC BEH C18 column (150 × 2.1 mm, 1.7 μm, Waters). Isocratic elution was conducted with mobile phase A (0.01% formic acid in water), and mobile phase B (0.01% formic acid in acetonitrile). The injection volume was set to 10 µL. A SCIEX 6500 QTRAP + triple quadrupole mass spectrometer (Sciex), equipped with an IonDrive Turbo V electrospray ionization (ESI) interface, was used for assay development. The typical ion source parameters were: Curtain Gas = 40 psi, IonSpray Voltage = -4500 V, temperature = 500 °C, Ion Source Gas 1 = 30 psi, and Ion Source Gas 2 = 30 psi [33, 34]. The complete list of metabolites, along with their analytical parameters, is provided in the accompanying Excel file (Supplementary Table 1), following the reporting recommendations [35]. Data acquisition and processing were carried out using SCIEX Analyst Workstation Software (Version 1.6.3) and Multiquant 3.03 software. Euclidean distance matrices were calculated for quantitative metabolites values, and the metabolites were clustered using complete linkage, with results visualized in a heat map. Further, metabolite profiles obtained from UHPLC-MS/MS analysis were subjected to K-means clustering using the online platform Bioinformatics (https://www.bioinformatics.com.cn) to identify patterns of metabolite abundance across samples. Prior to clustering, data were normalized and mean-centered. The optimal number of clusters (k) was determined using the elbow method, and clustering was performed with Euclidean distance as the similarity metric.

ELISA

HaCaT keratinocytes were seeded in 12-well plates at a density of 105 cells per well, with 1 mL of culture medium per well. After the indicated treatments, supernatants were collected, and the concentration of 12-HETE were quantified using 12-HETE ELISA kit (Cayman, Michigan, USA; Cat. No. 34550). All procedures were performed according to the manufacturer’s protocol.

Real-time Quantitative PCR

Total RNA was isolated from homogenized skin samples using Trizol reagent (Vazyme Biotech Co. Ltd, Nanjing, China; Cat. No. R401-01) and reverse-transcribed into cDNA using a cDNA Reverse Transcription Kit (Vazyme Biotech Co. Ltd, Nanjing, China; Cat. No. R323). Real-time quantitative PCR (RT-PCR) was performed using SYBR Green Master Mix (Vazyme Biotech Co. Ltd, Nanjing, China; Cat. No. Q221). The relative expression levels of target genes were normalized to ACTB or Gapdh and calculated using the ratio of the quantity of the target gene (listed in Supplementary Table 2) to the reference gene.

Quantification and Statistical Analysis

Data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism software (version 9.5.0). The normality of data distribution was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. All data were presented as mean ± SEM and were analyzed using unpaired two-tailed Student’s t-test, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s multiple comparison test or two-way ANOVA followed by Holm-Šídák test, as appropriate. A P-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Pharmacologic Inhibition of MAGL Attenuates Psoriasiform Inflammation and Itch

To assess the role of FAAH (ortholog of human FAAH1) or MAGL in psoriasis, we established an IMQ-induced psoriasis model by daily topical application of 5% IMQ cream on shaved dorsal skin of C57BL/6 mice for five consecutive days. This intervention induced characteristic psoriasiform symptoms, including erythema, scaling, and epidermal thickening. Subsequently, by analyzing the expression levels of FAAH and MAGL proteins, we found a significant upregulation of MAGL in psoriasiform lesions compared to control skin, while FAAH expression remained unchanged (Fig. 1A-D). IHC revealed MAGL expression in murine hair follicles, with no significant differences between control and IMQ-treated mice. In contrast, MAGL expression was markedly elevated in the epidermis of both IMQ-challenged mice and human psoriatic biopsies. MAGL-positive cells were distributed throughout the basal, spinous, and granular layers of the epidermis (Fig. 1E).

To ascertain the cellular origin of MGLL, we employed single-cell RNA sequencing datasets derived from healthy and psoriatic human skin samples (GSE162183) [36]. For the presentation of the single-cell analysis outcomes, we applied Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) for dimensionality reduction, enabling the categorization of cells into ten distinct clusters (Supplementary Fig. 1A-C). Cluster annotation using canonical markers demonstrated predominant MGLL expression in undifferentiated keratinocytes from psoriatic specimens (Fig. 1F). These findings suggested undifferentiated keratinocytes might serve as the primary source of pathogenic MAGL in psoriasis.

Subsequently, we administered JZL184, a selective MAGL inhibitor [37], to evaluate its impact on psoriasis (Fig. 2A). We selected 5 mg/kg as the lower dose and 10 mg/kg as the higher dose for our experiments. Mice treated with IMQ received once-daily subcutaneous injections of JZL184 (5 or 10 mg/kg) or vehicle throughout the 5-day modeling period. We first quantified 2-AG levels in mouse skin. Compared with the control group, IMQ treatment led to a significant reduction in cutaneous 2-AG. Administration of 5 mg/kg JZL184 restored 2-AG levels, whereas 10 mg/kg JZL184 did not alter the IMQ-induced reduction (Fig. 2B). JZL184 treatment, particularly at a dose of 5 mg/kg, significantly ameliorated psoriasiform dermatitis, evidenced by reduced epidermal hyperplasia, and diminished inflammatory infiltrates, and improved PASI scores compared to the vehicle-treated controls (Fig. 2C-F). A subtle trend toward increased sebaceous gland size was also observed in some JZL184-treated mice; however, this effect was variable and not formally quantified. Additionally, mice treated with IMQ demonstrated a substantial increase in spontaneous scratching behavior, which served as a model for psoriasis-associated itch [29]. We counted the scratching bouts in 1 h of each group, and the results revealed that the heightened scratching behavior induced by IMQ was significantly attenuated following JZL184 administration, with the 5 mg/kg dosage exhibiting optimal efficacy (Fig. 2G).

We further stimulated HaCaT keratinocytes using IL-17 A or M5 (a cytokine cocktail comprising IL-17 A, TNF-α, IL-1α, IL-22, and Oncostatin-M) at varying concentrations and durations. qRT-PCR analysis showed significant MGLL upregulation after 20 ng/mL IL-17 A for 12 h or 2.5 ng/mL M5 for 24 h, whereas FAAH1 expression remained unchanged (Supplementary Fig. 2A). Western blotting confirmed increased MAGL protein levels under these treatments, with 2.5 ng/mL M5 for 24 h producing the stronger effect (Supplementary Fig. 2B and D). Using these optimal conditions, we tested the MAGL inhibitor JZL184 (5 µM and 10 µM). The 10 µM dose significantly reduced M5-induced MAGL expression compared with untreated cells, with greater inhibition than the 5 µM dose (Supplementary Fig. 2C and E). These parameters were subsequently applied to examine the effects of MAGL inhibition on inflammatory responses in HaCaT keratinocytes. qRT-PCR analysis revealed that JZL184 treatment significantly suppressed the expression of psoriasis-associated factors, including CXCL1, CXCL2, CXCL8 and IL23A, which are important in psoriasis (Supplementary Fig. 2F).

Anti-inflammatory and Antipruritic Effects of JZL184 in Psoriasis Are Partially Mediated by CB2R

We subsequently employed pharmacological and genetic approaches to determine the cannabinoid receptor dependency of MAGL inhibition’s therapeutic effects. Firstly, we pre-treated IMQ-challenged mice using the CB1R antagonist AM251 (3 mg/kg) and the CB2R antagonist AM630 (2.5 mg/kg) prior to administering the effective dose of JZL184 (5 mg/kg) [4, 5] (Fig. 3A). Co-administrated of JZL184 with either AM630 or AM251 in IMQ-treated mice resulted in exacerbated clinical outcomes compared to the JZL184 + IMQ group (Fig. 3B). Mice treated with AM251 or AM630 in combination with IMQ + JZL184 showed significantly higher PASI scores than the IMQ + JZL184 group, while no significant differences were observed compared to the IMQ group alone (Fig. 3E). Moreover, the histomorphometry analysis of the dorsal skin revealed AM630 coadministration reversed the suppression of epidermal hyperplasia and inflammatory infiltrates caused by JZL184, while AM251 showed no significant interference (Fig. 3C-D). Thus, we concentrated on the role of CB2R. An analysis of splenic indices across the four experimental groups further supported the role of CB2R in modulating the anti-inflammatory effects of JZL184 (Fig. 3F-G). These findings suggested that the protective effects of JZL184 in IMQ-induced psoriasis are primarily mediated through CB2R. Subsequently, we assessed whether inhibiting CB2R signaling could attenuate JZL184-mediated suppression of scratching episodes. The results showed that pre-treatment with AM630 completely abolished the effects of JZL184 (Fig. 3H).

JZL184 Mediates Psoriasiform Inflammation and Itch Via Modulating Arachidonic Acid Metabolism

Research has shown that the modulation of endogenous cannabinoid-metabolizing enzymes influences the expression levels of AA-metabolizing enzymes both in vitro and in vivo [19, 21]. To investigate the potential interaction between the ECS and AA metabolism in psoriasis, and to delineate whether the therapeutic efficacy of JZL184 is linked to AA metabolism, we performed UHPLC-MS/MS to quantify AA and its metabolites in the skin lesions of the four experimental groups. Our results indicated that AA levels were elevated in the epidermis of IMQ-treated mice. Treatment with JZL184 produced a downward trend relative to the IMQ group, although this change did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.1003), while co-administration of AM630 exerted no additional effect compared with JZL184 alone (Fig. 5A). The sequencing analysis demonstrated that multiple eicosanoids (such as 12-HETE, 5-HETE) generated by the LOXs were significantly up-regulated by IMQ, whereas JZL184 inhibited the expression of these products (Fig. 5B). This metabolic reprogramming was then abolished by CB2R antagonism, establishing a functional coupling between MAGL inhibition and LOX pathway regulation.

Then, we analyzed the expression levels of the LOX enzymes ALOX5, ALOX12, and ALOX15 in skin tissues to determine whether MAGL inhibitor influenced AA metabolism through the LOX arm. The results showed a significantly upregulation of ALOX12 expression in IMQ-induced psoriasiform lesions, which was attenuated by JZL184 treatment. However, the suppressive effect of JZL184 on ALOX12 expression was negated by co-treatment with AM630 or in CB2R−/− mice (Fig. 5C-F). Such changes were not observed in the expression of ALOX5 and AXLO15. Further, analysis via confocal microscopy showed that JZL184 reduced ALOX12 expression in the epidermis of IMQ-treated mice, whereas this effect was abolished by co-administration of AM630 (Fig. 5G). Fluorescence imaging also demonstrated colocalization of CB2R and ALOX12. Complementary in vitro studies confirmed this regulation. We treated different cell groups with 2.5 ng/mL M5, 10 µM JZL184, and 10 µM AM630 for 24 h. The results revealed that M5 stimulation induced ALOX12 expression in immortalized HaCaT keratinocytes and primary mouse keratinocytes, which was suppressed by JZL184 in a CB2R-dependent manner (Supplementary Fig. 4A-D). These results mechanistically linked MAGL inhibition to CB2R-driven suppression of ALOX12-mediated AA metabolism, proposing a novel therapeutic axis wherein MAGL regulated inflammatory eicosanoid biosynthesis in psoriasis pathogenesis.

MAGL-ALOX12-12-HETE Axis Drives Psoriasiform Inflammation and Itch Caused by IMQ

To confirm the role of ALOX12-mediated AA metabolism in psoriasis, we administered ML355, a selective ALOX12 inhibitor [38], subcutaneously into the dorsal skin of mice before applying IMQ (Fig. 6A). The administration of ML355 resulted in a dose-dependent attenuation of psoriasiform dermatitis, contrasting with the severe skin lesions and pronounced epidermal hyperplasia observed in mice treated with IMQ alone (Fig. 6B-E). Furthermore, ML355 treatment significantly diminished the spontaneous scratching behaviors caused by IMQ application on Day 5, with the 10 mg/kg dose showing the greatest effect (Fig. 6F).

Various metabolites derived from AA play a vital role in the processes of inflammation and pruritus associated with inflammatory skin diseases [25, 39]. Consequently, we sought to identify the specific metabolite serving as the most critical downstream molecule mediating the effects of ALOX12 on inflammation and itching in psoriasis. Analysis of the 50 metabolites in Fig. 5 classified them into eight distinct groups, with clusters 6, 7, and 8 displaying expression patterns closely aligned with that of ALOX12 (Fig. 7A). To elucidate the role of 12-HETE in psoriatic itch and inflammation, we conducted subcutaneous injections of 12-HETE into the skin, which resulted in an immediate escalation of acute scratching behavior (Fig. 7D-E). Subsequently, we investigated the contribution of 12-HETE to IMQ-induced psoriasiform inflammation and pruritus by administering 12-HETE to IMQ-treated mice. Photographic documentation and PASI scoring revealed that 12-HETE intensified IMQ-induced dermatitis (Fig. 7F and I). Histological analysis showed a significant increase in epidermal thickness following 12-HETE treatment compared to IMQ treatment alone (Fig. 7G-H). Additionally, scratching behaviors were significantly exacerbated in the IMQ + 12-HETE group (Fig. 7J).

Consistent with these in vivo findings, we next performed in vitro rescue experiments in HaCaT keratinocytes to further confirmed the involvement of the MAGL-ALOX12-12-HETE axis in psoriasis. qPCR analysis revealed that M5 stimulation markedly increased the expression of inflammatory cytokines including CXCL1, CXCL2, CXCL8 and IL23A, compared with the DMSO control. JZL184 treatment reduced this induction, and co-treatment with JZL184 and ML355 led to a further decrease, particularly in CXCL1, CXCL2, CXCL8 and CXCL9. In contrast, supplementation with 12-HETE abolished the inhibitory effect of JZL184, restoring cytokine expression to levels comparable to those of M5-stimulated cells (Fig. 8A-F). Totally, these results mechanistically positioned ALOX12-12-HETE as the key downstream effector of the MAGL signaling axis, orchestrating psoriatic pathogenesis.

Discussion

Emerging pharmacological strategies targeting the ECS demonstrate translational promise in dermatopathology [41]. However, receptor agonist-based approaches remain limited by substantial psychotropic side effects [12, 42]. As a result, the development of FAAH and MAGL inhibitors offers a viable therapeutic alternative to cannabinoid receptor agonists [13, 43]. In this context, our findings positioned MAGL inhibition as a potential therapeutic approach, leveraging enzymatic modulation to achieve cutaneous specificity.

In this study, we found that MAGL is significantly upregulated in skin lesions from both IMQ-induced psoriatic mice and patients with psoriasis, while the expression of FAAH remain unchanged. Despite the absence of paired lesional and non-lesional samples from the same patients, comparisons between independent healthy and psoriatic patients consistently confirmed MAGL upregulation, supporting our murine findings. In mice, MAGL was also detectable in hair follicles, consistent with previous reports [44], indicating that follicular structures contribute to local endocannabinoid metabolism. However, follicular expression remained largely unchanged in the IMQ model, whereas the epidermis exhibited pronounced MAGL upregulation, highlighting compartment-specific regulation. Limited human sampling prevented full assessment of follicular MAGL, and further work is required to clarify its functional role in follicular endocannabinoid signaling.

To access the role of MAGL, we employed JZL184, a well-characterized MAGL inhibitor extensively validated in animal models. Previous studies have demonstrated its efficacy in suppressing scratching behavior in rats (100 µg/50 µL, s.c.) [45] and alleviating pain in mice (10 µg/10 µL, s.c.) [46], providing a robust foundation for its use in our experimental design. In our model, treatment with 5 mg/kg JZL184 not only restored cutaneous 2-AG levels but also alleviated inflammatory changes, suggesting that the therapeutic effects of MAGL inhibition are mediated, at least in part, through normalization of endocannabinoid signaling. In contrast, 10 mg/kg JZL184 failed to restore 2-AG levels, indicating that its efficacy may depend on optimal dosing rather than a linear dose-response relationship. This phenomenon aligns with prior reports that modulators of the ECS often display bell-shaped dose-response curve [47, 48], potentially due to receptor desensitization, altered CB1R/CB2R signaling balance, or off-target effects at higher doses [49–51]. In our experiment, JZL184 at 10 mg/kg resulted in an insignificant reduction in inflammation but led to a marked improvement in itch behavior. This suggested that inflammation and pruritus in psoriasis were not always directly correlated. While JZL184 primarily reduced inflammatory cytokines (shown in our in vitro analyses of CXCL1, CXCL2, IL8 and IL23A levels), pruritus may involve additional mechanisms, such as sensory nerve activation, that are independent of the inflammatory response [52]. Therefore, the optimal dose for alleviating inflammation and pruritus may differ. Interestingly, some JZL184-treated mice exhibited a subtle trend toward enlarged sebaceous glands, consistent with previous findings of MAGL and CB2R expression in human sebaceous glands [53, 54]. Although this effect was not quantified and appeared variable among animals, it may represent an interesting secondary effect of MAGL inhibition worth exploring in future studies. Finally, we noted that Elcubragistat, a clinically advanced MAGL inhibitor [55], was not selected as a comparator due to limited availability and an undefined pharmacokinetic profile for subcutaneous administration in mice. Future studies should compare different MAGL inhibitors to identify the most effective candidate for translation.

Our findings indicated that MAGL functioned as a crucial regulator of psoriasiform inflammation and pruritus through CB2R-dependent mechanisms. Although MAGL inhibition elevates endogenous 2-AG, which can activate both CB1R and CB2R [41, 56], the therapeutic benefits in psoriatic models specifically required CB2R signaling, as evidenced by their complete reversal with AM630 antagonism and the loss of efficacy in CB2R−/− mice. In our previous studies, antagonism or knockout of CB2R exacerbated IMQ-induced inflammation and pruritus [4]. The present study demonstrated that co-administration of JZL184 with either AM630 or CB2R knockout abolished the protective effects of MAGL inhibition. PASI scores in these groups were not significantly different from those of the IMQ group alone, but were higher than those of IMQ + JZL184-treated mice, suggesting that CB2R may partially mediate the anti-inflammatory effects induced by MAGL blockage. Moreover, although some comparisons of scratching bouts (p = 0.06) did not reach statistical significance, the consistent trends across groups, together with the limited sample size (n = 5), suggested these findings were biologically meaningful. A limitation of this study is the absence of untreated CB2R−/− controls, such as CB2R−/− + IMQ or IMQ + CB2R−/− + AM630, which would have provided additional mechanistic clarity and helped assess potential multimodal regulatory pathways. However, these experiments were not feasible due to the limited availability of CB2R−/− mice. Future work incorporating these controls will be important for a more comprehensive characterization of CB2R’s role. Despite the limitation, our results indicated that CB2R signaling in the skin remains functionally preserved under chronic MAGL inhibition, in contrast to the CB1R desensitization reported in neural tissues [51], highlighting a tissue-specific maintenance of endocannabinoid responsiveness.

There have been investigations to establish a complex interplay between the ECS and AA metabolism [22, 57], a relationship further supported by our findings. Specifically, inhibition of MAGL by JZL184 altered the cutaneous eicosanoid profile, implicating interactions between endocannabinoid and eicosanoid signaling pathways in the protective effects of MAGL inactivation. Although direct regulation of the AA pool by MAGL may also contribute, JZL184 treatment only modestly attenuated AA accumulation, and the reduction did not reach statistical significance. This limited effect is likely attributable to compensatory pathways, as AA can also be generated from endocannabinoids via alternative enzymes such as FAAH [58]. Moreover, our metabolite profiling revealed that not all eicosanoids were reduced following JZL184 treatment, indicating that MAGL may play a role in regulating the expression of certain metabolic enzymes. Notably, the administration of JZL184 selectively reduced eicosanoids associated with the LOX pathway, and this effect was counteracted by the CB2R antagonist AM630. Given that AA metabolism in psoriasis is preferentially channeled through the LOX pathway [59], we next examined the expression of LOX enzymes (ALOX5, ALOX12, and ALOX15). While previous studies have demonstrated that MAGL inhibition modulates COX expression [60], our in vivo and in vitro data suggested that ALOX12 may represent a critical downstream effector of the MAGL-CB2R signaling axis. Spatial profiling further confirmed epidermal co-localization of MAGL/CB2R/ALOX12, forming a self-reinforcing signaling module. The functional significance of this axis was validated by ALOX12 inhibitor ML355. Although ML355 exhibits ortholog-dependent differences in potency, with reduced inhibition of recombinant murine ALOX12 under in vitro conditions [27], multiple independent studies have shown that ML355 lowers 12-HETE levels and produces phenotypic outcomes consistent with ALOX12 inhibition in mice [26, 61, 62]. Moreover, Zhang et al. [62] reported that ML355 did not confer additional benefit in Alox12-deficient mice, providing genetic evidence for on-target activity. Thus, while direct evaluation of ML355 against murine ALOX12 or the use of genetic loss-of-function models would further strengthen these findings, our results supported the utility of ML355 as a pharmacological probe for the ALOX12-12-HETE pathway in vivo.

Previous studies have identified elevated levels of 12-HETE, a primary product of ALOX12, in chronic psoriatic plaques [63]. Our analyses indicated that the secretion of 12-HETE was modulated by both JZL184 and AM630. Additionally, the topical application of 12-HETE in human volunteers has been shown to induce sclerosing erythematous reactions [64], and its injection in rats elicited pronounced scratching behavior [65]. Our findings extended these observations by demonstrating that 12-HETE exacerbated inflammatory processes and enhanced both acute and chronic pruritic responses in murine models of psoriasis. Beyond 12-HETE, MAGL inhibition altered several other AA metabolites implicated in skin inflammation. For example, LTB4 acts as a potent chemoattractant and enhancer of epidermal inflammation responses [66], while PGE2 promotes Th17 polarization and IL-17 production via EP2/EP4 signaling [67]. These findings agreed with previous reports of dysregulated eicosanoid profiles in psoriatic lesions [68, 69], reinforcing the translational relevance of our data. The cluster analysis in Fig. 7 further suggested that MAGL-CB2R signaling broadly regulates the eicosanoid network, with ALOX12-12-HETE as a major downstream effector but not the sole mediator of psoriatic pathogenesis. Moreover, recent studies have implicated additional eicosanoids, such as 20-HETE, in itch modulation through activation of TRPV1 channels on MrgprA3+ neurons [25].While 12-HETE also acts as a TRPV1 agonist [70], its contribution to TRPV1-mediated pruritus in psoriasis remains to be further elucidated.

Complementing these in vivo observations, our in vitro data further supported the notion that MAGL and the ALOX12-12-HETE axis act coordinately to drive psoriasiform inflammation. In M5-stimulated HaCaT keratinocytes, combined treatment of JZL184 and ML355 further suppressed inflammatory gene expression, suggesting an additive effect and indicating that MAGL and ALOX12 act along convergent pathways. Notably, the addition of exogenous 12-HETE reversed the anti-inflammatory effect of JZL184, confirming that 12-HETE functions downstream of MAGL to mediate pro-inflammatory signaling.

In summary, this study delineated a novel therapeutic mechanism wherein MAGL inhibition attenuates psoriatic pathogenesis through CB2R-dependent suppression of the ALOX12-12-HETE axis. Our integrated pharmacological and genetic approaches suggested that enzymatic modulation of endocannabinoid degradation not only enhanced protective signaling but also constrained pathogenic eicosanoid biosynthesis, thereby exerting dual control over inflammation and itching. These findings propelled MAGL inhibitors into translational consideration for psoriasis management. Although this work focused primarily on the mechanistic roles of MAGL and ALOX12 inhibition in psoriasiform inflammation, future investigations incorporating standard therapies, such as topical corticosteroids or vitamin D analogs, will be valuable to contextualize the therapeutic potential of these novel inhibitors in comparison to current treatment strategies.
