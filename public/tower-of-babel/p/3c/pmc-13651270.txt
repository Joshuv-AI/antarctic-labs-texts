Detection of periodontopathogens in umbilical cord blood and their correlation with the maternal bacterial profile
Authors: Fernanda Zanol Matos, Fernanda Silva de Assis, Isabella Rodrigues Porto, Francielle Nunes de Azevedo Romanowski, Priscilla Cardoso Lazari-Carvalho, Helder Fernandes de Oliveira, Luciana Prado Maia, Luís Vicente Franco de Oliveira, Orlando Aguirre Guedes, Alessandra Nogueira Porto
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMaternal periodontal disease is a prevalent condition that has been widely investigated for its association with adverse pregnancy outcomes, including preterm birth and low birth weight. The present study aimed to evaluate and compare the clinical and microbiological oral conditions of high-risk and low-risk pregnant women and to correlate these findings with the presence of periodontopathogenic microorganisms in the oral cavity and umbilical cord blood of their newborns.MethodsA total of 54 pregnant women and their respective newborns were evaluated during the perinatal period and allocated into two groups: a high-risk pregnancy group and a control group. High-risk pregnancy was defined according to criteria established by the Brazilian Ministry of Health, including systemic and obstetric risk conditions. Maternal clinical assessment included the following periodontal parameters: visible plaque index (VPI), gingival bleeding index (GBI), probing depth (PD), and clinical attachment level (CAL). Microbiological samples were collected from oral soft tissues (tongue and cheek swabs) and subgingivally (using size 30 sterile paper points). Umbilical cord blood was collected immediately after clamping, and oral microbiological samples from the newborns were obtained using swabs. The presence of Aggregatibacter actinomycetemcomitans (Aa), Porphyromonas gingivalis (Pg), and Tannerella forsythia (Tf) was investigated using real-time PCR. Statistical analysis was performed using Kruskal-Wallis, Mann-Whitney, and Spearman’s correlation tests to explore associations between clinical and microbiological data. The chi-square test was used for categorical data.ResultsEvidence of periodontal disease was observed in both groups, with a statistically significant increase in the detection of Aa in the maternal oral samples from women in the high-risk group (p = 0.042). A positive correlation was also found between CAL and the presence of Tf in subgingival samples, indicating that higher CAL values were associated with greater bacterial load (p < 0.05). However, no statistically significant associations were identified between maternal periodontal status and adverse obstetric outcomes (p > 0.05).ConclusionsThe detection of periodontopathogenic microorganisms in umbilical cord blood and neonatal oral samples suggests the possibility of maternal-fetal microbial transmission, although no association was observed with adverse obstetric outcomes.Clinical trial numbernot applicable.

Introduction

Pregnancy is a physiological process that typically progresses without major clinical complications [1]. However, when pre-existing medical conditions or gestational disorders are present, the pregnancy is classified as high-risk, increasing the likelihood of maternal and perinatal adverse outcomes [2]. These conditions represent a significant public health concern due to their association with increased morbidity, mortality, and healthcare costs [1–3]. Multiple factors, including systemic diseases, nutritional status, and socioeconomic determinants, may influence the course of pregnancy and contribute to unfavorable outcomes [4, 5].

In addition to systemic changes, pregnancy is also associated with physiological alterations that may impact oral health [6]. Hormonal fluctuations, particularly increased levels of estrogen and progesterone, can modulate the host immune response and influence the composition of the subgingival biofilm, leading to increased gingival inflammation [6–8]. Gingivitis is the most common periodontal condition during pregnancy, affecting a substantial proportion of women, while periodontitis is also frequently observed [9]. These findings highlight the importance of monitoring periodontal health during pregnancy, especially among individuals with additional systemic or gestational risk factors.

Periodontal disease has been extensively investigated as a potential contributor to adverse pregnancy outcomes [10–12], including preterm birth and low birth weight [13, 14]. One of the proposed mechanisms involves the systemic dissemination of oral microorganisms and inflammatory mediators, which may reach the fetal environment through hematogenous pathways and trigger inflammatory responses [15, 16]. Although several studies have reported the presence of oral bacteria in placental and amniotic tissues, suggesting a possible link between maternal oral infections and gestational complications [17–20], the available evidence remains inconclusive [21, 22].

Given that high-risk pregnancies are frequently associated with systemic and inflammatory alterations [4, 5], investigating potential differences in maternal oral microbiological profiles and neonatal microbial exposure may provide relevant clinical insights. In particular, the relationship between maternal periodontal conditions and the presence of periodontopathogenic microorganisms in neonatal biological compartments remains insufficiently explored [23, 24]. Therefore, the present study aimed to evaluate and compare the clinical and microbiological oral conditions of high-risk and low-risk pregnant women and to investigate the association between maternal oral microbiological profiles and the presence of periodontopathogenic microorganisms in the oral cavity and umbilical cord blood of their newborns.

Methods

Ethical approval

The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Research Ethics Committee (approval number 2.786.001). All participants provided written informed consent prior to enrollment, in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Study design

This observational cross-sectional study was reported in accordance with the STROBE (Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology) guidelines [25].

Study population

A cross-sectional study was conducted at the maternity unit of Santa Helena Hospital in Cuiabá, Mato Grosso, Brazil. A convenience sample of 60 pregnant women and their respective newborns was included, without prior formal sample size calculation, due to the observational and exploratory nature of the study. During screening, participants completed an anamnesis and a socioeconomic questionnaire, which categorizes households into classes ranging from A (highest purchasing power) to E (lowest purchasing power), based on household assets, education level of the head of household, and access to public services [26].

Pregnant women were divided into two groups:High-risk group: participants with one or more conditions defined by the Brazilian Ministry of Health, including diabetes mellitus, gestational or chronic hypertension, obesity (weight > 75 kg), underweight (weight < 45 kg), age < 15 or > 35 years, height < 1.45 m, premature rupture of membranes, history of recurrent miscarriage, or other recognized risk factor.Control group: participants without criteria for high-risk pregnancy.

Exclusion criteria included limited mouth opening, inability to obtain clinical or microbiological samples, current smoking, use of systemic antibiotics within the previous three months, and any medical contraindication to participation in the study.

A total of 60 pregnant women were initially assessed for eligibility. After applying the inclusion and exclusion criteria, 54 participants completed both clinical and microbiological evaluations and were included in the final analysis (Fig. 1). This sample size provided a 95% confidence level with a ± 7.7% margin of error, assuming an estimated 10% prevalence of high-risk pregnancies (National Statistical Service & Sample Size Calculator Definitions).

Clinical periodontal examination

Clinical periodontal assessments were performed in the labor and surgical delivery rooms by a periodontist with nine years of clinical experience. All examinations were conducted under stable conditions, without prior prophylaxis or supervised oral hygiene.

A clinical mirror (SS White Duflex; Juiz de Fora, MG, Brazil) and a Williams periodontal probe (Hu-Friedy® Mfg Co Inc., Chicago, IL, USA) were used. Six sites per tooth (mesiobuccal, buccal, distobuccal, mesiolingual, lingual, and distolingual) were examined, excluding third molars [4].

The following parameters were recorded:Visible plaque index (VPI): presence or absence of dental biofilm on dried tooth surfaces, assessed with gauze without mechanical disruption [10].Gingival bleeding index (GBI): bleeding after gentle probing at 0.5 mm within the sulcus, evaluated 30 s after insertion [4].Probing depth (PD): distance from the gingival margin to the bottom of the sulcus [4].Clinical attachment level (CAL): distance from the cementoenamel junction to the apical end of the junctional epithelium [4].

Periodontitis severity was categorized as: mild (1–2 mm CAL), moderate (3–4 mm loss), or severe (≥ 5 mm loss) [27]. Periodontal diagnosis was established according to the following criteria: (1) Periodontal health: < 30% of sites with gingival bleeding; (2) Gingivitis: ≥ 30% of sites with gingival bleeding; and (3) Periodontitis: ≥ 4 teeth with at least one site presenting PD ≥ 4 mm and CAL ≥ 3 mm at the same site [28].

Microbiological sample collection

Microbiological sampling was performed immediately after clinical evaluation. Subgingival microbial samples were collected with sterile size 30 paper points (Dentsply, Maillefer, Ballaigues, Switzerland) inserted into the mesiobuccal sulcus of teeth 16, 11, 26, 31, 36, and 46 for 60 s [27]. When one of the index teeth was absent, the adjacent tooth was used. The paper points were transferred to sterile microtubes (Bio-Rad®, Hercules, CA, USA) and stored at − 80 °C until processing. Oral soft tissues (supragingival) samples were obtained using sterile plastic loops to collect material from the dorsum of the tongue and cotton swabs (Absorve, Jiangsu Suyun Medical Materials CO Ltd., Jiangsu, China) from the buccal mucosa [29]. These samples were also stored at − 80 °C until analysis.

In newborns, two types of samples were collected. The first sample consisted of umbilical cord blood obtained from the placental side immediately after clamping, following routine neonatal procedures. An aliquot was separated and allocated for the study. The second sample consisted of a swab of the buccal mucosa and tongue using sterile swabs (Absorve, Jiangsu Suyun Medical Materials CO Ltd., Jiangsu, China). Samples were also stored in sterile microtubes (Bio-Rad®, Hercules, CA, USA) at − 80 °C until analysis.

DNA extraction and microbial quantification

Genomic DNA was extracted from oral and subgingival clinical samples using the PureLink™ Genomic DNA Purification Kit (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA), following the manufacturer’s instructions with protocol adaptations to improve bacterial lysis efficiency. Initially, 1,000 µL of ultrapure water were added to each Eppendorf tube (Hexasystems, São Paulo, Brazil) containing the biological sample. The suspension was homogenized in a vortex mixer (QL 901, Biomixer) for 30 s, and then 500 µL of the homogenate were centrifuged at 10,000 rpm for 3 min.

After discarding the supernatant, 180 µL Genomic Digestion Buffer/Lysozyme were added to the pellet. The suspension was vortexed for 30 s and incubated in a water bath at 37 °C for 30 min. Then, 20 µL of Proteinase K and 200 µL of Genomic Lysis/Binding Buffer were added, followed by vortexing for 30 s. The mixture was incubated at 55 °C for 120 min, with vortexing every 20 min to enhance cell lysis and protein digestion.

Subsequently, 200 µL of absolute ethanol were added, and the sample was vortexed for 5 s. The lysate was transferred to a PureLink™ Spin Column containing a silica membrane and centrifuged at 10,000 rpm for 1 min. The column was then washed sequentially with 500 µL of Wash Buffer 1 (centrifuged at 10,000 rpm for 1 min) and 500 µL of Wash Buffer 2 (centrifuged at 10,000 rpm for 3 min). Finally, DNA was eluted with 30 µL of PureLink™ Genomic Elution Buffer and stored at − 80 °C until analysis.

Reference strains of the target microorganisms were obtained from the Oswaldo Cruz Foundation - National Institute for Health Quality Control. Microbiological detection was performed using quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction. Quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR) with TaqMan® probes was used due to its high sensitivity and specificity for the detection and quantification of predefined bacterial targets.

Primers and probes were designed based on conserved regions of the 16 S rDNA gene for each microorganism, using Primer3 software (http://frodo.wi.mit.edu/) and synthesized by Invitrogen Tech-LineSM (Life Technologies do Brasil Ltda, São Paulo-SP) (Table 1). Real-time PCR was performed on the StepOne™ Real-Time PCR system (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA, USA) using TaqMan® probes (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA, USA) specific to Aggregatibacter actinomycetemcomitans, Porphyromonas gingivalis, and Tannerella forsythia.

Primer specificity was confirmed using the NCBI BLAST tool (http://blast.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/Blast.cgi). Optimal reagent concentrations and amplification parameters were established in preliminary validation assays. Data analysis was performed using the StepOne™ software (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA, USA). DNA concentrations were measured using a NanoDrop® ND-1000 UV-vis spectrophotometer (NanoDrop Technologies, Wilmington, USA) at 260 nm, and the 260/280 nm ratio was used to assess purity. Standard curves were generated from DNA extracted from pure cultures, allowing conversion of CT (cycle threshold) values into absolute bacterial concentrations. PCR reactions were considered valid when amplification efficiency ranged between 93% and 110% and the correlation coefficient (R²) was ≥ 0.98. All samples were analyzed in triplicate, alongside positive controls (standard curves) and negative controls (sterile water) to ensure accuracy and exclude contamination.

DNA extraction and quantification from umbilical cord blood

Genomic DNA was extracted from umbilical cord blood samples using the PureLink™ Genomic DNA Purification Kit (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA), following the manufacturer’s instructions.

After collection, 200 µL of blood were transferred to sterile Eppendorf microtubes, where the extraction procedure was carried out. Initially, 20 µL of Proteinase K and 20 µL of RNase A were added to each sample. The mixture was vortexed for 5 s and incubated at room temperature to initiate enzymatic digestion.

Subsequently, 200 µL of PureLink™ Genomic Lysis/Binding Buffer were added, followed by an additional vortexing step to ensure a homogeneous solution. The samples were then incubated at 55 °C for 10 min to promote efficient cell lysis and protein digestion.

For DNA precipitation, 200 µL of absolute ethanol (analytical grade) were added to each tube, followed by vortexing for 5 s. The entire lysate (approximately 640 µL) was transferred to a PureLink™ Spin Column containing a silica membrane, placed into a collection tube, and centrifuged at 10,000 × g for 1 min at room temperature.

Two sequential wash steps were performed: first with 500 µL of Wash Buffer 1 (centrifuged at 10,000 × g for 1 min), and then with 500 µL of Wash Buffer 2 (centrifuged at 10,000 × g for 3 min). DNA elution was performed by adding 100 µL of Genomic Elution Buffer, followed by centrifugation at maximum speed for 1 min. Following elution, the spin column was discarded, and the tubes containing the purified DNA were immediately stored at − 80 °C until further molecular analysis.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS software (version 20.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Descriptive statistics (mean, median, standard deviation, absolute and relative frequencies) were calculated for all clinical and microbiological variables. The Shapiro-Wilk test was applied to verify the normality of data distribution. For group comparisons, Mann-Whitney U test and Kruskal-Wallis tests were used for non-normally distributed variables, whereas normally distributed variables were analyzed using ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post hoc test. Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient was applied to explore associations between continuous variables. Chi-square test was used for categorical data. Paired comparisons between maternal and neonatal microbiological findings were performed using Wilcoxon signed-rank test. In addition, logistic regression analysis was conducted to evaluate the predictive value of maternal clinical and microbiological variables for prematurity and low birth weight (< 2.5 kg). A p-value ≤ 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Study population

A total of 54 pregnant women were enrolled, equally distributed between the high-risk group (n = 27; age range: 13–39 years), and control group (n = 27; age range: 17–32 years). The overall mean maternal age was 25.1 years, and the mean gestational age at delivery was 37.6 weeks.

Epidemiological variables, including race, marital status, education level, and socioeconomic classification, were homogeneous between groups. Most participants self-identified as mixed-race (68%), and 70% reported living in a stable union. Regarding socioeconomic status, the majority were classified as belonging to class C2 (a middle-to-lower socioeconomic stratum).

Clinical results

Distinct periodontal profiles were observed between the groups. In the high-risk group, periodontitis was the most prevalent condition, affecting 59.3% of participants, followed by gingivitis in 33.3%. This distribution of periodontal diagnoses was statistically significant (p < 0.05). In contrast, the control group showed no significant difference between the frequencies of gingivitis and periodontitis. However, when comparing periodontal health to the presence of any periodontal disease, a significant difference was observed (Fig. 2).

Obstetric outcomes

Vaginal delivery was the most common mode of birth in both groups. However, cesarean section was more frequent among women in the high-risk group (40.7%) compared to the control group (14.8%) (Table 3).

In both groups, most women were either primigravidas or in their second pregnancy. A high frequency of prenatal consultations was also observed; however, a greater proportion of high-risk women (81.5%) attended the recommended number of visits compared to the control group (59.3%) (Table 3).

BMI: Body Mass Index

Regarding gestational body mass index (BMI), the high-risk group showed a higher prevalence of overweight (48.1%) and obesity (18.5%). In contrast, the control group predominantly presented with a normal BMI, although 18.5% were classified as underweight.

Neonatal data

Figure 3 compares neonatal characteristics between the high-risk and control groups, showing no statistically significant differences (p > 0.05). A total of 5 preterm births (18.5%) occurred in the high-risk group and 8 (29.6%) in the control group. Neonatal length below the expected range was recorded in three newborns from each group. Regarding birth weight and head circumference, four cases in the high-risk group and three cases in the control group presented values below the normative parameters (Figure 3).

Microbiological analysis

When comparing clinical periodontal parameters and microbiological findings between groups, a statistically significant difference was observed only for Aggregatibacter actinomycetemcomitans (Aa) in the oral cavity of newborns. The concentration of Aa was significantly higher in the control group (median: 5,788) compared to the high-risk group (median: 728) (p = 0.042) (Table 4).

Discussion

This study evaluated the clinical, periodontal, and microbiological profiles of 54 pregnant women and their respective newborns. To our knowledge, this is the first investigation to comprehensively compare the oral and systemic microbiota of high-risk pregnant women with the microbiota profile identified in both the oral cavity and umbilical cord blood of their newborns. Although no statistically significant associations were demonstrated, the findings suggest potential interactions between the severity of maternal periodontal involvement and the presence of periodontopathogenic microorganisms across multiple maternal and neonatal biological compartments. These associations were particularly evident among participants classified as high-risk, highlighting the clinical relevance of monitoring and managing periodontal health during pregnancy.

The study population was predominantly composed of young women of reproductive age, ranging from 13 to 39 years, with a mean age of 25.1 years. This demographic profile is consistent with findings reported in previous studies [30–34]. Although the literature emphasizes the increased obstetric risks associated with pregnancies in early adolescence, particularly under the age of 15 [35], and in women over 35 years [33], maternal age was not identified as a significant determinant of gestational risk in the present study. These findings suggest that other factors, including comorbidities and socioeconomic determinants, may play a more prominent role in characterizing high-risk pregnancies within this population.

In this study, 68% of participants self-identified as mixed-race. The literature indicates that women of African descent are more likely to face adverse socioeconomic conditions, which may restrict access to healthcare services and contraceptive methods, thereby increasing the likelihood of unplanned pregnancies [36]. In addition, genetic factors linked to racial background may predispose these women to systemic conditions such as hypertension, further contributing to higher rates of maternal morbidity and mortality [36].

It was also observed that 44.44% of the pregnant women had completed only elementary education. Educational attainment is a well-established determinant of pregnancy planning, with lower education levels associated with a higher incidence of early and unplanned pregnancies. Early school dropout often restricts access to essential information on sexuality and family planning, thereby increasing vulnerability in reproductive health. Maternal education is consistently highlighted in the literature as a key risk marker for adverse obstetric outcomes, given its direct influence on both maternal and neonatal health. Limited education may hinder access to healthcare services and reduce comprehension of prenatal care instructions, self-care strategies, and neonatal health recommendations during pregnancy follow-up [37].

Regarding marital status, the presence of a partner emerged as a potentially protective factor, frequently associated with greater emotional support and improved financial stability. Conversely, the absence of such support may increase the risk of adverse neonatal outcomes, including low birth weight, particularly among single mothers [38]. In the present study, 70% of the women in both the high-risk and control groups reported living in a stable union. Within this subgroup, lower rates of low birth weight and prematurity were observed, suggesting a possible association between marital stability and favorable obstetric outcomes.

Concerning prenatal care, 70% of the participants reported attending more than six prenatal appointments. Such consultations are essential for promoting maternal and fetal health, providing guidance on breastfeeding, balanced nutrition, physical activity, immunization, and the scheduling of laboratory and imaging tests. Despite notable progress in prenatal coverage in Brazil over recent decades, ensuring both the quality and adequacy of prenatal visits remains an ongoing health challenge [39].

Overweight and obesity are well-established risk factors for perinatal morbidity and mortality, being associated with complications such as preeclampsia, gestational diabetes, miscarriage, macrosomia, and thromboembolic events [40–42]. In the present study, no statistically significant correlation was observed between maternal gestational BMI and prematurity. This finding suggests that, within the studied population, gestational BMI may function as an independent variable, without directly influencing this specific obstetric outcome.

Regarding obstetric outcomes, the present study did not identify a statistically significant correlation between periodontal disease and adverse pregnancy events. Although the literature suggests a potential association, particularly linking periodontal disease to low birth weight and preterm birth through systemic inflammatory pathways, this relationship remains inconclusive [43]. In our sample, no significant differences in clinical periodontal parameters were observed between pregnant women whose newborns presented low birth weight and/or prematurity and those with normal neonatal outcomes. The logistic regression analysis should be interpreted cautiously. Given the relatively small number of obstetric outcome events in the sample, the inclusion of multiple predictors in the model may increase the risk of model instability and overfitting, limiting the ability to detect significant associations.

The comparison of mean microbial levels between the high-risk and control groups showed no statistically significant differences for most of the microorganisms analyzed. Interestingly, higher concentrations of Aggregatibacter actinomycetemcomitans were detected in the oral cavity of newborns from the control group. Although this finding may initially appear counterintuitive, early colonization of the neonatal oral cavity is a complex and dynamic process influenced by multiple factors, including maternal microbial exposure, hormonal and microbiological changes during pregnancy, environmental contact, and early postnatal interactions. Previous studies have demonstrated that the maternal oral microbiota undergoes significant shifts during pregnancy and that the initial colonization of the neonatal oral cavity is shaped by microbial exposure from multiple maternal and environmental sources rather than a single maternal condition [29–31]. Therefore, differences in microbial loads between groups may not necessarily reflect maternal gestational risk status but rather the multifactorial nature of microbial acquisition during the neonatal period. However, in contrast to the findings of Mitchell-Lewis et al. [44], who reported elevated levels of Tannerella forsythia (Tf) in maternal oral biofilm, the present study identified significantly higher levels of Tf in both supragingival and subgingival samples from high-risk pregnant women. Although numerous epidemiological studies have suggested periodontitis as a potential risk factor for adverse pregnancy outcomes, including low birth weight, preterm birth, and preeclampsia [23, 24], intervention studies and meta-analyses have not confirmed a causal relationship. These findings are consistent with the results observed in this study.

The long-standing paradigm of the placenta as a sterile environment has been increasingly challenged by studies detecting bacterial DNA and viable microorganisms in placentas from uncomplicated pregnancies [45–48]. In contrast to the findings of Reyes et al. [49], who reported a significant presence of P. gingivalis in placental tissue and suggested its association with adverse obstetric outcomes, the present study identified various periodontopathogenic bacteria in both umbilical cord blood and the oral cavity of newborns. Notably, Porphyromonas gingivalis was the only species that did not exhibit a statistically significant difference in abundance when comparing maternal and neonatal microbiota. This observation is particularly relevant because it suggests a similar microbial burden between maternal and neonatal compartments, which may support the hypothesis of maternal–fetal microbial transmission. Previous studies have demonstrated that oral periodontopathogens are capable of disseminating through systemic circulation and reaching fetal or placental tissues, reinforcing the possibility of hematogenous transmission pathways during pregnancy [17, 19, 48]. Therefore, the detection of P. gingivalis in both maternal oral biofilm and umbilical cord blood may reflect the ability of this microorganism to translocate beyond the oral environment and colonize distant biological sites.

Although the presence of periodontopathogenic bacteria in umbilical cord blood may suggest a possible hematogenous route of maternal–fetal microbial transfer, other mechanisms should also be considered [17, 18]. These include potential contamination during the delivery process and the possibility of ascending infection from the birth canal, which may contribute to neonatal microbial exposure [45, 47]. Therefore, the interpretation of these findings should be approached with caution, and further studies using more controlled sampling protocols are needed to better clarify the mechanisms involved in maternal-fetal microbial transmission.

The microbiological analysis in the present study was based on a targeted approach using quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR) with TaqMan® probes, which allows highly sensitive and specific detection of predefined bacterial species. This method is widely used in periodontal research when the objective is to investigate clinically relevant periodontopathogens [27, 29, 32]. However, it is important to acknowledge that this approach does not provide a comprehensive characterization of the microbiome, as would be possible with high-throughput sequencing techniques such as 16 S rRNA gene sequencing. Therefore, the findings of this study should be interpreted within the context of a targeted microbial analysis, and future studies using broader microbiome profiling approaches are recommended.

This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, the relatively small sample size and the use of a convenience sample may limit the generalizability of the results and reduce the statistical power to detect significant associations. In addition, the cross-sectional design does not allow causal relationships to be established between maternal periodontal conditions, microbiological findings, and obstetric outcomes. Furthermore, some subgroup, correlation, and multivariate analyses were conducted in an exploratory manner, and no formal correction for multiple comparisons was applied, which may increase the risk of type I error. Finally, potential confounding factors related to maternal systemic conditions and socioeconomic determinants may also have influenced the observed outcomes. Therefore, the results should be interpreted with caution, and future studies with larger sample sizes and longitudinal designs are needed to better elucidate these relationships.

Conclusion

Maternal periodontal disease was not statistically associated with adverse obstetric outcomes. Clinical and microbiological periodontal findings in mothers showed no significant differences between groups, nor were notable differences observed in maternal and neonatal oral biofilms. Nevertheless, the detection of periodontopathogenic microorganisms in umbilical cord blood suggests the possibility of hematogenous vertical transmission, highlighting the importance of periodontal health monitoring during pregnancy.
