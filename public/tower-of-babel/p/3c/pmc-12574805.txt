Postoperative Gut Microbiota Changes after Colorectal Cancer Surgery: A Comparative Study Based on Resection Sites
Authors: Yutaka Hattori, Tadashi Fujii, Takumi Tochio, Tsutomu Kumamoto, Junichiro Hiro, Hiroshi Matsuoka, Koji Masumori, Kohei Funasaka, Eizaburo Ohno, Yoshiki Hirooka, Koichi Suda, Koki Otsuka
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectives: Colorectal cancer (CRC) is a major global health concern, and surgical resection remains its primary treatment. However, the impact of different surgical procedures on gut microbiota and their influence on postoperative outcomes remain unclear. This study investigated changes in the gut microbiota following three types of CRC resections: right-sided colectomy (RSC), left-sided colectomy (LSC), and low anterior resection (LAR). Methods: Fecal samples were collected from 34 patients with CRC who underwent curative resection at Fujita Health University Hospital between April 2022 and December 2023. Bacterial profiling was performed using 16S rRNA gene amplicon sequencing. The results were compared with data from 85 healthy controls. Results: Significant alterations in gut microbiota composition were observed in surgical groups compared to the healthy control (Ctrl) group. The RSC group exhibited the greatest reduction in alpha diversity, likely because of ileocecal valve loss. Beta-diversity analysis revealed distinct microbial profiles between the Ctrl group and the surgical groups, with notable alterations in key bacterial species. The RSC group exhibited significant reduction in beneficial bacteria, including Faecalibacterium prausnitzii and Bifidobacterium spp., alongside increase in Escherichia coli, suggesting a shift toward a pro-inflammatory environment. In contrast, the LSC and LAR groups exhibited enrichment of Akkermansia muciniphila, which may promote gut barrier integrity and immune modulation. Conclusions: CRC resection induces site-specific changes in gut microbiota composition. These microbial alterations may affect postoperative inflammation, metabolism, and cancer recurrence risk. Further studies are necessary to explore microbiome-targeted strategies for improving postoperative outcomes in cases of CRC. Keywords: colorectal cancer, gut microbiota, dysbiosis

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) is among the most prevalent malignancies worldwide, ranking third in incidence and second in cancer-related mortality. According to the World Health Organization, the global burden of CRC continues to increase, particularly in industrialized nations where dietary and lifestyle factors significantly influence disease risk[1].

Epidemiological studies suggest that excessive consumption of animal protein and dietary fats is associated with an increased risk of CRC development. Dietary patterns rich in red and processed meats, coupled with low fiber intake, have been implicated in the pathogenesis of CRC through mechanisms such as gut inflammation, bile acid metabolism alterations, and microbiota dysbiosis[2,3].

Recent studies have provided new insights into the role of the gut microbiota in CRC carcinogenesis. The gut microbiome influences multiple biological processes, including immune modulation, epithelial barrier integrity, and carcinogen metabolism. The presence of specific bacterial species, such as Fusobacterium nucleatum, Bacteroides fragilis, and Escherichia coli, with oncogenic potential, has been linked to CRC progression. Dysbiosis―an imbalance in gut microbial composition―has been proposed as a key factor in the multistep process of CRC carcinogenesis[4].

Surgical resection of the colon or rectum remains the primary treatment modality for most patients with CRC. However, despite extensive research on the role of the gut microbiota in CRC pathogenesis, the impact of surgical intervention on the gut microbial community remains unclear. Surgical resection may significantly alter the intestinal environment, including alterations in gut motility, microbiota diversity, and nutrient absorption, all of which can influence postoperative recovery and long-term health outcomes.

This study investigated the gut microbiota composition of patients with CRC who underwent colon resection. By analyzing stool samples, we sought to determine whether different surgical approaches result in distinct postoperative microbiota profiles. Understanding these microbiota changes could offer new perspectives for postoperative management and microbiome-targeted therapeutic strategies in patients with CRC.

Methods

Patients

Fecal samples were collected from 34 patients with CRC who underwent curative resection at least three months prior at Fujita Health University Hospital between April 2022 and December 2023. The consent has been obtained from all patients to publish the information in writing. Patients were classified into three groups based on the type of surgical resection:

RSC group: 9 patients undergoing right-sided colectomy (RSC)

LSC group: 15 patients undergoing left-sided colectomy (LSC)

LAR group: 10 patients undergoing low anterior resection (LAR)

The inclusion criteria were as follows: patients with histologically confirmed CRC, those with no antibiotic use within 1 week before surgery, and those with no history of inflammatory bowel disease. The exclusion criteria were as follows: patients who had received neoadjuvant chemotherapy and within 3 months of adjuvant chemotherapy, those who experienced severe postoperative complications, and those who had a history of gastrointestinal surgery.

The study protocol was approved by the Research and Ethics Committee of Fujita Health University (approval no. HM22-272), and the study was conducted according to the 1975 Declaration of Helsinki and its later amendments.

Healthy controls

This study included 85 healthy controls, as previously described[5]. These individuals had no history of chronic diseases, such as autoimmune disorders, cardiovascular diseases, gastrointestinal conditions, diabetes mellitus, or cancer, and had not taken antibiotics within 1 week before sample collection. The group comprised 43 males and 42 females, with a median age of 38.3 years (age range: 19-61 years).

DNA extraction and 16S rRNA gene sequencing

Fecal samples were self-collected by patients during natural defecation using the Fecal Collection Kit FS-0017 (Techno Suruga Laboratory, Shizuoka, Japan) in accordance with the manufacturer's instructions. Although this kit guarantees the stability of fecal DNA at room temperature for up to one month, the collected samples were stored at 4°C as promptly as possible. DNA extraction was subsequently performed within one month of sample collection. DNA extraction was performed using the QIAamp PowerFecal Pro DNA Kit (QIAGEN, Hilden, Germany) following the manufacturer's instructions. 16S rRNA amplicons were generated using a primer set of Pro341F and Pro805R, targeting the V3-V4 regions of the bacterial 16S ribosomal RNA gene[6]. Polymerase chain reaction was performed using the following conditions: initial denaturation at 94°C for 2 min, followed by 30 cycles of 94°C for 30 s, 55°C for 30 s, and 72°C for 30 s. The reaction was terminated with a 5-min extension at 72°C. Subsequently, dual indexes were attached, and sequencing was performed at Bioengineering Lab Co., Ltd. (Kanagawa, Japan), as previously reported[7].

The 16S rRNA gene amplicon sequencing datasets were deposited in the National Center for Biotechnology Information Sequence Read Archive under the accession number PRJNA1245636.

Microbiome data analysis

Sequencing data processing, statistical analysis, and graph generation were performed using the EzBioCloud 16S database and the 16S microbiome pipeline provided by ChunLab Inc. (EzBioCloud 16S-based MTP app, available at https://www.EZbiocloud.net). Alpha-diversity was evaluated using the Shannon index, with statistical comparisons performed using the Kruskal-Wallis test, followed by post hoc pairwise analysis. Beta-diversity at the species level, representing overall phylogenetic differences among groups, was evaluated based on the Bray-Curtis divergence and visualized using principal coordinate analysis (PCoA). To assess differences in beta-diversity between groups, permutational multivariate analysis of variance (PERMANOVA) with 9,999 permutations was performed.

To identify key bacterial species for distinguishing each patient group (RSC, LSC, and LAR) from the healthy control (Ctrl) group, the Linear Discriminant Analysis Effect Size (LEfSe) algorithm was employed. Species with a linear discriminant analysis (LDA) score of >3.9 were considered significant. P-values of <0.05 were used to denote statistical significance. In EzBioCloud's LEfSe analysis, the “P-value (FDR)” is effectively used as the Q-value and is thus referred to as the “Q-value.”

Statistical analysi

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism (version 9.5.1; GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). P-values for breed and sex were calculated using Fisher's exact test, whereas age was analyzed using the Mann-Whitney U test. P-values <0.05 were used to denote statistical significance.

Fecal microbiota analysis

The fecal microbiota was compared between each patient group (RSC, LSC, and LAR) and the Ctrl group. 16S rRNA gene sequencing yielded 44,892 ± 234, 44,537 ± 239, and 44,410 ± 270 valid reads from the DNA extracted from the RSC, LSC, and LAR groups, respectively, expressed as means ± standard errors of the mean. The 16S rRNA NGS results of the Ctrl group were previously reported[5]. Supplementary Figure 1 presents the fecal microbiota composition at the species levels. Alpha diversity, assessed using the Shannon index―which accounts for both species richness (the number of observed taxa) and evenness (the distribution of individuals among those taxa)―was significantly lower in the RSC group than in the other groups (Figure 1a). PCoA plots based on the Bray-Curtis divergence revealed distinct separations between each patient group and the Ctrl group (Figure 1b), highlighting differences in beta diversity. This observation was further supported by the results of PERMANOVA, which confirmed significant differences in beta diversity between the gut microbiomes of the RSC, LSC, and LAR groups and that of the Ctrl group (P ≤ 0.001 for each). Furthermore, the RSC group exhibited significant differences from the LSC and LAR groups (P = 0.015 and 0.080, respectively), whereas no significant difference was observed between the LSC and LAR groups (P = 0.348).

LEfSe analysis revealed alterations in specific bacterial groups within the fecal microbiota. In the RSC group, the relative abundances of Faecalibacterium prausnitzii, Fusicatenibacter saccharivorans, Bifidobacterium catenulatum, Bifidobacterium adolescentis, and Bacteroides stercoris were significantly reduced compared with those in the Ctrl group, whereas Escherichia coli was significantly increased (Figure 2a). In the LSC group, F. prausnitzii, B. catenulatum, and B. adolescentis were significantly decreased, whereas Akkermansia muciniphila and Parabacteroides distasonis were significantly increased (Figure 2b). Similarly, in the LAR group, F. prausnitzii exhibited a significant reduction, whereas A. muciniphila was significantly increased compared with those in the Ctrl group (Figure 2c).

Discussion

This study highlighted the influence of colorectal resection sites on the postoperative gut microbiota. PCoA revealed clear separations in fecal microbiota profiles between the surgical and Ctrl groups, with PERMANOVA confirming significant differences in beta diversity. The RSC group exhibited the most distinct microbial composition, significantly differing from both the LSC and LAR groups, which did not significantly differ from each other. Although briefly noted here, the particularly low alpha diversity in the RSC group and its potential association with ileocecal valve (ICV) loss are discussed in detail below.

LEfSe analysis revealed that the gut microbiota composition was significantly altered following colorectal surgery, with distinct microbial alterations observed depending on the resection site. Considering the relationship between the gut microbiota and CRC, these changes may have implications for postsurgical outcomes, including cancer recurrence risk and long-term gut health[8,9].

The RSC group exhibited a pronounced reduction in several beneficial bacterial taxa, including F. prausnitzii, F. saccharivorans, and various Bifidobacterium species, along with an increase in E. coli. This shift suggests a transition toward a proinflammatory microbial profile. F. prausnitzii and Bifidobacterium spp. are recognized for their roles in producing short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs), such as butyrate, which contribute to mucosal immunity, anti-inflammatory activity, and inhibition of CRC cell growth[10-13].

F. saccharivorans, another SCFA-producing bacterium involved in dietary fiber fermentation, was also decreased. Its decreased abundance has been observed in patients with ulcerative colitis[14], suggesting a potential association with gut inflammation.

The enrichment of E. coli in the RSC group is noteworthy because certain strains produce colibactin, a genotoxin that can induce DNA double-strand breaks[15]. Genomic studies have reported that colibactin-induced damage frequently overlaps with mutational hotspots in CRC, supporting its potential role in the carcinogenesis of CRC[16].

The LSC group also exhibited a decrease in F. prausnitzii and Bifidobacterium spp., which is consistent with the pattern observed in the RSC group, indicating a common depletion of SCFA-producing bacteria following colectomy. In contrast to the RSC group, the LSC group exhibited a significant increase in A. muciniphila and P. distasonis. A. muciniphila contributes to gut barrier integrity and is associated with enhanced responsiveness to immunotherapy in CRC[17], whereas P. distasonis may exert antitumorigenic effects through SCFA production and inflammation suppression[18]. These findings suggest that LSC promotes a more favorable microbial environment that could impact postoperative immunity and CRC outcomes. Given their functional relevance, the enrichment of A. muciniphila and P. distasonis may serve not only as potential biomarkers for postoperative recovery but also as promising targets for future microbiota-based therapeutic interventions.

In the LAR group, a similar reduction in F. prausnitzii was noted, along with an increased abundance of A. muciniphila. The microbiota profiles in both the LSC and LAR groups suggest site-specific adaptations that may influence postoperative inflammation, immune modulation, and the risk of CRC recurrence.

Collectively, these findings demonstrate that each colorectal resection type induces distinct changes in the gut microbiota, with potential implications for postoperative inflammation, metabolic function, and cancer recurrence. The consistent decline in F. prausnitzii across all surgical groups suggests that SCFA depletion is a shared outcome of colectomy, regardless of type, potentially compromising anti-inflammatory and tumor-suppressive mechanisms.

Among the surgical groups, the RSC group maintained a uniquely tumor-promoting, pro-inflammatory microbiota profile, characterized by increased E. coli abundance. In contrast, the LSC and LAR groups were enriched in A. muciniphila, suggesting beneficial adaptations that may enhance mucosal integrity and modulate the tumor microenvironment.

These microbial alterations may have important clinical implications. Reduced alpha diversity and loss of SCFA-producing taxa are associated with impaired mucosal healing, increased systemic inflammation, and higher susceptibility to infections, such as anastomotic leakage. The overrepresentation of E. coli―a tumor-promoting, pro-inflammatory species―can further compromise gut barrier function and immune regulation, potentially increasing the risk of metastasis[13,19].

Moreover, the pronounced microbial disruption observed in the RSC group supports the clinical hypothesis regarding the physiological role of the ICV, which remains underexplored in human studies. Recent studies have reinforced the physiological significance of the ICV in maintaining gastrointestinal homeostasis. The ICV acts as a critical barrier regulating the unidirectional flow of intestinal contents from the ileum to the cecum, effectively preventing the retrograde movement of colonic bacteria into the small intestine through neural and hormonal control mechanisms[20]. Dysfunction or surgical removal of the ICV has been associated with the onset of small intestinal bacterial overgrowth, primarily due to decreased valve pressure allowing bacteria-rich colonic contents to reflux into the small intestine, thus highlighting its role in protecting the upper gastrointestinal tract from microbial contamination[21]. Furthermore, the ICV contributes significantly to preserving intestinal barrier function and optimizing nutrient absorption; its loss can lead to disruptions in intestinal microbiota composition and reduced efficiency of nutrient assimilation[22,23]. Collectively, these findings underscore the clinical relevance of the ICV, emphasizing the potential consequences of its dysfunction or surgical removal on gut microbial balance, nutrient absorption efficiency, and overall gastrointestinal health.

To the best of our knowledge, this study is among the first to comprehensively compare the gut microbiota profiles across different colorectal resection sites, including RSC, LSC, and LAR. A similar study was reported by Suga et al.[24], although they did not investigate microbial changes following LAR. Their findings of reduced alpha diversity after RSC and decreased abundance of SCFA-producing bacteria after both RSC and LSC are consistent with our results. However, in contrast to our study, members of Verrucomicrobiae, including A. muciniphila, were abundant preoperatively but not detected postoperatively.

Taken together, these findings underscore the broader conclusion that colorectal cancer resections induce site-specific alterations in gut microbiota, which may in turn influence postoperative inflammation, metabolic function, and potentially even cancer recurrence. Importantly, these microbiological outcomes should be considered alongside traditional anatomical and oncological factors when choosing surgical strategies for CRC.

However, this study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. The relatively small sample size may limit statistical power, and the lack of long-term follow-up data precludes the evaluation of clinical endpoints, such as recurrence and response to chemotherapy. In addition, the retrospective nature of the study and the absence of preoperative microbiota data constrain causal interpretation. Furthermore, while comorbidities did not significantly differ among groups, as shown in Table 1, the potential influence of concomitant medications―including over-the-counter drugs and supplements―cannot be completely excluded, given the inherent limitations in retrospectively collecting complete medication and supplement histories. Moreover, the healthy control group had a substantially younger median age (38.3 years) compared to the patient groups (in their 60s to 70s), and since gut microbiota composition is known to change with age, this age difference may have influenced the observed differences between groups.

Future prospective studies with larger sample sizes and longitudinal sampling are necessary to clarify the prognostic relevance of these microbial changes and to explore microbiome-based interventions aimed at improving long-term outcomes in patients with CRC.
