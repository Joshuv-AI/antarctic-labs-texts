Elucidation of the intrinsic electronic mechanism governing interfacial chemical bonding in metal-polymer hybrids
Authors: Yifan Liu, Jianhui Su, Ri Han, Jiaheng Zhang, Fuyun Liu, Caiwang Tan, Swee Leong Sing, Xiaohui Han, Bo Chen, Xiaoguo Song
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntegrating metals and polymers is pivotal for advanced functional applications, yet their intrinsic electronic bonding origin remains elusive due to material mismatch. Combining density functional theory (DFT) calculations and experiments, this study establishes a unified interfacial electron donor-acceptor framework. We demonstrate that interfacial metal oxides and polymer carboxyl groups facilitate μ₂-bridging coordination, driven by p-p σ hybridization between metal vacant 3p and oxygen lone-pair 2p orbitals, which is verified by valence band spectra. Furthermore, introducing surface oxygen vacancies enhances the electron-accepting capability of metal sites, enabling the coordinated interface to resist thermodynamic fluctuations, thereby improving the tensile-shear strength by over 400%. Cross-system validations across various cation valence states (+1 to +4) and polymer heteroatoms successfully demonstrate the general applicability of this theory-guided acceptor mechanism among diverse structural alloys and thermoplastics. This work provides a predictive electronic-structure foundation for the rational design of high-performance multi-material hybrid interfaces beyond specific systems.Subject terms: Mechanical engineering, Composites, Physical chemistry, Chemical physics

Introduction

Metal-polymer hybrid structures attracted significant interest for their potential applications in advanced functional materials, including energy storage systems, flexible electronics, lightweight transportations, and biomedical implants1,2. Reliable joining of the hybrid structures required a precise understanding of interfacial bonding behavior at the atomic scale. Although direct thermal joining techniques emerged as a promising route for efficient fabrication, the inherent chemical disparity between metals and polymers often limited interfacial interactions, rendering joint performance difficult to predict3,4. As a result, the fundamental mechanism of chemical bond formation at the metal-polymer interface remained insufficiently understood.

A variety of thermal joining techniques had been developed, including laser joining, friction stir joining, and induction joining, which were applied to a wide range of material systems5–9. These methods generally relied on melting the polymer under sufficient interfacial pressure and appropriate heat input to achieve bonding with metal10. Early studies, such as the first laser joining of metal (stainless steel) and polymer (polyethylene terephthalate, PET), reported limited joint strength, with the bonding primarily attributed to micro-mechanical interlocking11. Subsequent study on magnesium alloy and PE (polyethylene) system further showed that the reliable joints could not be formed under their native surface states, promoting increasing interest in interfacial modification strategies12. Surface-roughening approaches, including sandblasting, laser texturing, and additive manufacturing (AM), were employed to enhance the mechanical interlocking effect13–15. Meanwhile, treatments such as silane coupling agent (SCA) and hot water treatment (HWT) mainly improved interfacial wetting and physical interaction (hydrogen bonding)16–18. Although these approaches enhanced physical contact or chemical states, they did not elucidate the underlying chemical bonding behavior. Consequently, the reason why stable joints can be formed by certain metal-polymer systems, whereas others failed even under optimized heat conditions, requires further investigation.

Recent advances have increasingly focused on interfacial enhancement strategies driven by chemical bonding, which could be broadly categorized into metal modification and polymer modification. Metal modification approaches, such as oxide, anodizing (AO), and chemical etching, produced a variety of metal-derived species, including oxides, hydroxides, and salts, which substantially improved chemical bonding beyond purely mechanical interlocking19–22. Experimental evidence of metal interactions was observed at interfaces involving pre-oxidized alloys joined with polymers, suggesting that these metal-derived species could participate in chemical bonding23–25. Polymer modification also demonstrated pronounced strengthening effects. Recent studies further suggested that polymer chemistry and surface functionalities governed adhesion strength at polymer-oxide interfaces, highlighting the critical role of interfacial chemistry beyond mechanical interlocking26,27. Introducing oxygen-containing functional groups could significantly promote chemical interactions via ultraviolet (UV) treatment, plasma treatment, and introducing functionalized polymer interlayers at the interface28–30. These modifications enabled reliable joining even in metal-polymer systems that were originally unjoined12. For instance, modified cyclic olefin polymer (COP) achieved successful joining with SUS304 stainless steel, highlighting the potential role of oxygen-containing functional groups in facilitating chemical bonding between metal and polymer31. However, most of these modification strategies were largely developed empirically, and the underlying strengthening mechanisms have not yet been systematically elucidated at the atomic scale. Key questions remained unanswered, such as how the metal-derived species interacted with specific oxygen-containing functional groups and what surface conditions could drive the interface from weak interactions to stable chemical bonds. Related first-principles studies on model interfaces showed that interfacial strength could be strongly influenced by orbital hybridization and charge transfer, underscoring the importance of understanding interface bonding at the electronic-structure level32–34. While recent computational studies attempted to explore the mechanism of interfacial bonding35, a complete mechanistic understanding applicable across different systems had not yet been established. Considering the diversity of metal-polymer combinations, existing studies remained fragmented, lacking a unified electronic-structure framework for predicting chemical bonding behavior, which was essential for the rational design of interfaces.

To address the gap, this study combines first-principles calculations with experimental analyses to investigate the interfacial chemical bonding in typical metal-polymer hybrid structures. By resolving electronic interactions at the initial interface between metal atoms and molecules, this work clarifies the characteristics of electron redistribution and the associated orbital contributions. Based on these insights, we design modification strategies in which the electronic structure is adjusted to promote stronger chemical bonding. Through systematic comparisons of interfacial electronic behavior across different material systems, the regularities and variations of metal-polymer bonding are revealed, and the mechanism of chemical bonding-driven joining at the interface is elucidated. These results establish a unified mechanistic framework, providing a clear electronic structural basis for understanding bonding behavior in cross-system metal-polymer hybrid structures and optimizing interfacial engineering strategies.

Results

From intrinsic interactions to theoretical design of metal-polymer interfacial bonding

Laser-assisted joining was employed to fabricate AZ31B/CFRTP (carbon fiber reinforced thermoplastics) hybrid structure, as shown in Fig. 1a. Under the pre-optimized joining condition, a joint free of macroscopic defects was successfully obtained. However, owing to the significant differences in thermophysical and chemical properties between metal and polymer, the tensile-shear strength remained low, reaching only 5.5 MPa, which was consistent with most previously reported studies. To investigate the origin of the low strength in direct metal-polymer joining, the complex interface was reasonably simplified to a pure Mg (0001)/PA66 system, considering that the surface resin layer minimized direct carbon-fiber contact, and the chemical influence of potential sizing agents aligned compatibly with the polymer matrix36,37. The Density Functional Theory (DFT) calculation results indicated that the surface Mg atom of Mg slab interacted with the O atom in the amide group (R-(CO)-(NH)-R’) of PA66, as shown in Fig. 1b. Figure 1c presents the projected density of states (PDOS) for these two atoms, both before and after adsorption. Upon adsorption, significant changes emerged, characterized by hybridization between O 2p orbital and Mg 3s and 3p orbitals (hereafter denoted simply as O 2p, Mg 3s, Mg 3p) in the energy range of −3 to −1 eV and around −4 eV, respectively, relative to the Fermi level (Ef). The overall downward shift of the O 2p states indicated the energetic stabilization of the adsorbed structure. Nevertheless, considering that the Mg surface was dominated by occupied states (derived from the 3s2 configuration), the observed overlap led to significant Pauli repulsion rather than typical covalent bonding, owing to the lack of accessible empty states to accept electrons. Furthermore, the hybridization involving Mg 3p at deeper energy levels was relatively weak, contributing less to bonding. This explained the low interfacial strength observed in the directly joined metal-polymer system, which could also provide insights for subsequent joint reinforcement.

Based on the above analysis, the hybrid interfacial interaction could be strengthened by introducing additional reactive functional groups on the polymer side, or by introducing more accessible vacant orbitals on the metal side. Given the intrinsic oxidation tendency of magnesium alloys, a controlled oxidation process could partially convert surface Mg atoms into Mg2+ cations. This transformation generated localized empty states (acting as Lewis acid sites) with strong electron-accepting capabilities, which was highly favorable for interfacial bonding. As shown in Fig. 2, the two adsorption structures exhibited distinct electronic interactions. As expected, the Mg2+ in MgO displayed stronger electronic coupling with the amide O in the MgO (001)/PA66 interfacial structure. Concurrently, the alternating arrangement of amide groups in the PA66 chain sterically forced the adjacent amide N-H group to approach the MgO surface, facilitating the formation of a secondary hydrogen bond with the surface oxygen (Fig. 2a) without altering the fundamental nature of this primary electronic coupling. The calculations revealed that the interaction between Mg2+ and O primarily originated from hybridization between Mg 3p and O 2p states, with the Mg 3p contribution being dominant over the Mg 3s vacant orbital. This was attributed to the superior energetic alignment (energy matching) between Mg 3p of Mg2+ and O 2p of amide groups, which enabled stronger hybridization. After introducing carboxyl functional groups (O = C-OH) containing carbonyl (C = O) onto the PA66 molecule, the results indicated that both amide and carboxyl O atoms interacted with Mg atoms. This effectively increased the number of interfacial adsorption sites, potentially enhancing the macroscopic bonding strength. Furthermore, PDOS results showed that the states of the carboxyl O shifted to lower binding energies (deeper levels) upon adsorption, suggesting that the modified Mg/PA66-g interfacial structure was more energetically favorable than the initial Mg/PA66 structure. The above analysis demonstrated that the electronic structure and orbital hybridization characteristics significantly influenced the metal-polymer interaction. Introducing additional reactive functional groups or modifying the metal surface states could enhance orbital overlap and electronic interaction, thus improving interfacial stability and potential joining performance. Based on this, oxidation and activation treatments were proposed for the metal and polymer surfaces, with the combined application of both strategies aimed at optimizing the performance of the hybrid interface.

Experimental validation of theory-guided interfacial modifications

To enhance the AZ31B/CFRTP joining strength, targeted surface modifications were performed on both metal and polymer components. On the metal side, native and oxygen-vacancy-rich oxide films were prepared to modulate the surface electronic states. Oxygen vacancies were a common structural defect in rapidly grown magnesium oxide film. In this study, an oxygen-vacancy-rich oxide film was fabricated via high-voltage plasma process. It drove rapid surface oxidation and removed O atoms from the lattice, creating vacancies with two electrons localized at the sites (F-center), as presented in Fig. 3a. On the polymer side, studies indicated that ultraviolet (UV) irradiation, and UV-activated chlorine dioxide/UV ozone could increase the density of oxygen-containing functional groups, thereby activating the surface38–40. A more efficient and controllable UV grafting process was selected to introduce carboxyl groups in this study. In this process, benzophenone (BP) was photo-activated under UV irradiation to abstract hydrogen atoms from PA66, which in turn generated surface radicals. These radicals subsequently reacted with the C = C bonds of acrylic acid (AA), forming PA66-g, as presented in Fig. 3b. To systematically evaluate the individual and combined effects of metal oxidation and polymer grafting on interfacial bonding, six types of AZ31B/CFRTP hybrid structures were fabricated by different combinations of surface conditions. As detailed in Table 1, each condition was denoted by an abbreviated code for clarity: “C” (clean), “O” (oxidized), “OV” (oxygen-vacancy-rich), and “G” (grafted).

The electronic structures of key atoms were analyzed to explore the impact of surface modifications on material surface states, as shown in Fig. 3c and d. The Mg PDOS of native MgO structure revealed characteristics of a typical wide-bandgap oxide, with the HOMO (highest occupied molecular orbital) and LUMO (lowest unoccupied molecular orbital) located at approximately 0 eV and 3 eV, respectively. The introduction of oxygen vacancies modified the local electronic environment of neighboring Mg atoms. Notably, defect states emerged within the original bandgap, raising the HOMO to about 2.5 eV while the LUMO remained near 3 eV. Consequently, the bandgap narrowed to 0.5 eV. This indicated that oxygen vacancy increased the surface state density and enhanced electron localization, because of the local symmetry breaking in the crystal field. This also caused the splitting, broadening, and intensity reduction of the sharp peak near −3 eV. This electronic transition from “wide-bandgap insulator” to “narrow-gap structure with defect states” might provide more active sites for interaction with functional groups. Compared to the amide O in PA66, the grafted carboxyl O exhibited reduced peaks near the Fermi level but developed new states at −2 to −1 eV, with its overall negative and positive energy states shifting closer to the Fermi level. These electronic features indicated a high degree of localization and increased electronic activity for the carboxyl O, suggesting that the grafted carboxyl group was more readily able to interact with metal atoms. Furthermore, the carbonyl O possessed higher reactivity than the hydroxyl O from the carboxyl group, which was attributed to the more localized electron density of carbonyl O (sp2 hybridization) than hydroxyl O (sp3 hybridization).

A series of experimental characterizations was conducted to investigate the structural and chemical changes after surface modifications. As shown in Fig. 3e, the OV-AZ31B exhibited a distinct paramagnetic resonance signal at a g-factor of ≈ 2.002 in the Electron Paramagnetic Resonance (EPR) spectrum, which provided direct experimental evidence for the existence of unpaired electrons trapped in MgO oxygen vacancies. Furthermore, X-ray Photoelectron Spectroscopy (XPS) analysis (Supplementary Fig. 1e–h) revealed a systematic broadening in the full width at half maximum (FWHM) of the O 1 s peak for the OV-AZ31B compared to the bare and naturally oxidized substrates, indicating an increased density of surface lattice disorder and defects. Cross-sectional Electron Energy Loss Spectroscopy (EELS) mapping (Supplementary Fig. 2) further confirmed an OV concentration gradient, revealing that a high concentration of OVs was localized within the surface pores of the OV-AZ31B layer. As shown in Fig. 3f, the Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) spectrum of the clean CFRTP showed characteristic peaks of PA66. Two new peaks at 1723 cm−1 and near 3400 cm−1 confirmed the successful introduction of carboxyl groups via grafting. Surface energy results clearly demonstrated a significant increase for the oxidized metal surface. The rise in the dispersive component (γd) was attributed to the introduction of oxygen, which localized the electronic distribution. The enhancement of the polar component (γp) mainly resulted from the formation of strong polar bonds and dipole interactions involving O 2p. Similarly, on the grafted CFRTP surface, the introduction of carboxyl groups led to a substantial enhancement of γp. Although the grafting process disrupted the original regular structure of the polymer, thereby reducing γd derived from van der Waals forces, the surface energy still increased. These results demonstrated that both oxidation and grafting treatments effectively enhanced the physical and chemical activities of AZ31B and CFRTP surfaces.

To verify whether surface modifications effectively strengthened the hybrid AZ31B/CFRTP structure, interfacial and fracture morphologies were examined, and mechanical tests were conducted, as shown in Fig. 4. Despite the joints with various modifications exhibiting tight interfacial bonding with no significant differences, the strength varied markedly with the applied modifications. The C + C joint exhibited a strength of 5.5 ± 1.2 MPa. For the joints involving single-surface modifications, the O + C, OV + C, and C + G joints exhibited tensile-shear strengths of 8.8 ± 1.0 MPa, 11.5 ± 0.9 MPa, and 12.1 ± 1.1 MPa, respectively. When bi-modification treatments were applied, the strengths of the O + G and OV + G joints further increased to 22.7 ± 0.8 MPa and 29.1 ± 1.3 MPa, respectively, demonstrating the significant enhancement achieved by bi-modification treatments and the additional contribution from oxygen vacancies. To precisely evaluate the distinct role of these oxygen vacancies, a heat-treated OV substrate (denoted as H-OV) was prepared. Characterizations including atomic force microscopy (AFM) and X-ray diffraction (XRD) (Supplementary Fig. 1) confirmed that the thermal treatment preserved the identical morphology and crystalline phase of the OV substrate, while EPR results (Supplementary Fig. 3a) revealed a reduced oxygen vacancy concentration due to surface vacancy healing. Consequently, the tensile-shear strength of the H-OV + G joint decreased to 25.1 ± 1.3 MPa, verifying that oxygen vacancies contributed to the interfacial bonding. To further assess the stability of the surface treatment, the aging effect of the grafted CFRTP was tracked over a 30-day period (Supplementary Fig. 4). The surface energy remained stable for the first 6 days but decreased thereafter, accompanied by a decline in the polar component. Consistently, the joint strength exhibited a matching degradation trend and retained 67.9% of its initial value on day 30, which aligned with the time-dependent degradation behavior reported in recent literature41. Compared with recent metal-polymer joining studies incorporating metal or molecular modifications, both the achieved interfacial strength and performance efficiency (strength increment over initial strength) ranked in the upper tier of reported values.

Macroscopic fracture surfaces exhibited apparent cohesive failure characteristics in all joints except O + C condition, with the resin-carbon fiber mixture adhering to the AZ31B surface. However, different failure mechanisms were observed in microscopic morphologies. For the joints without metal oxidation, only a thin layer of resin-carbon fiber mixture remained on the metal surface, characterized by prominent bubble-like defects. This observation suggested that the adhesive failure in these cases was driven by the expansion of interfacial gas, with cracks subsequently propagating along the paths defined by these bubble defects. In contrast, the joints with metal oxidation no longer presented such bubble-induced fracture features, which was attributed to the low thermal conductivity of the oxide layer, reducing the decomposition-induced bubble defects as previously reported7. Following the grafting of carboxyl groups onto the CFRTP surface, adhesion of the resin-fiber mixture increased. This effect was further amplified by introducing oxygen vacancies on the metal side, which progressively shifted the fracture mode from interfacial to cohesive failure. These results provided direct evidence that the oxide films and grafted carboxyl groups collectively strengthened the interfacial interactions, resulting in tighter bonding and significantly improved mechanical performance. Nevertheless, the underlying interaction mechanisms required further exploration.

Synergistic bonding behavior at the metal-polymer interface

To clarify the origin of the bi-modificated joint reinforcement phenomenon and the role of oxygen vacancies, the interfacial electronic structures of MgO/PA66(G) and MgO(OV)/PA66(G) adsorption systems were calculated. Figure 5 summarizes the features of interfacial interaction. Notably, the adsorption sites in these two calculated structures were not identical to those in the Mg/PA66(G) analyzed in Fig. 2b. The carboxyl group exhibited stronger polarity, allowing its two O atoms (each carrying a relatively high partial negative charge) to simultaneously attach to two neighboring Mg atoms on the slab surface, thereby forming a bidentate adsorption configuration. In contrast, the limited conformational flexibility of PA66 backbone restricted the amide O atom effective approach to the MgO surface. This difference indicated that the carboxyl group reacted more readily with metal oxides than the amide group, consistent with previously proposed electronic structure analysis results. In the defect-free MgO/PA66(G) system, the two Mg-O bond lengths were 2.28 Å (Mg-O = C) and 2.34 Å (Mg-OH), respectively, which were consistent with typical Mg-O bonding distances, suggesting the possible formation of stable chemical bonding. Further analysis revealed electron accumulation regions on the oxygen-adjacent side of Mg-O bonding path, indicating directional partial covalent character in this bond, as depicted by charge density difference (CDD) distribution in Fig. 5a. Electron localization function (ELF) maps similarly showed the highly localized electron regions near the oxygen atom within the Mg-O bonding zone (ELF ≈ 0.8). This confirmed that the interfacial chemical bond followed “lone pair to vacant orbital” coordination mechanism, in which the lone pairs from the carboxyl O atoms were donated into vacant Mg orbitals to form the μ₂-bridging coordination. Moreover, the introduction of oxygen vacancy further enhanced the coordination. As an electronic defect site, oxygen vacancy captured the H atom from the hydroxyl group, causing O-H bond cleavage and redistributing electrons toward the defect. This increased the electron deficiency of the neighboring Mg atoms and enhanced their ability to accept lone pair donation from O. As a result, the Mg-O bond lengths shortened to 2.03 Å (Mg-O = C) and 2.02 Å (Mg-OH), reflecting a more symmetric and stronger μ₂-bridging coordination. This trend, together with the enhanced electron transfer and higher ELF intensity along the Mg-O bonding region, demonstrated that oxygen vacancy significantly increased both the stability and reactivity of the interfacial coordination structure. To further verify this reinforced coordination stability from a dynamic perspective, ab initio molecular dynamics (AIMD) simulations were performed at 400 K, as presented in Fig. 5f, g. Under the combined effects of thermal fluctuations and polymer-chain conformational rearrangements, the OV + G interface exhibited a more stable coordination environment with smaller fluctuations in the Mg-O coordination distances compared to the highly fluctuating O + G interface (Fig. 5h). This dynamic evidence further confirmed that OV-mediated interfacial coordination effectively stabilized the Mg-O bonding configuration.

The specific bonding orbitals of the μ₂-bridging between MgO and the carboxyl group at the metal-polymer interfaces required additional investigation. In both systems, the O1 and O2 2p of carbonyl (C = O1) and hydroxyl (-O2H) hybridized with the Mg 3p within the -2 to 0 eV, confirming the p-p σ nature of the bonding. In the defect-free system, the peak intensity of the O1 2p was slightly higher than that of the O2 2p in the hybridization region, revealing a difference in their contributions to the μ₂-bridging coordination. Upon the introduction of an oxygen vacancy, the local potential energy was modified, leading to the redistribution of Mg orbitals. The peak intensity of O 2p in the hybridization region was enhanced, reflecting the increased electron-donating capability. Notably, the high-energy defect state induced by the oxygen vacancy shifted downward upon adsorption, and hybridization with O 2p was observed. This suggested that lone pairs formed new chemical bonds with the defect-associated Mg orbitals, compensating for the initially unstable high-energy defect state and stabilizing it energetically. Quantitative analysis of the electron transfer energy (ΔE) revealed that ΔE in the MgO(OV)/PA66(G) system was lower. These demonstrated that the defect state contracted the initial energy separation between the interacting orbitals, which enhanced the covalent resonance of the μ₂-bridging coordination. Consequently, the oxygen vacancy reinforced the interfacial bonding stability against thermal and configurational perturbations.

After the simulations, FTIR and XPS characterizations were conducted to verify the formation of interfacial bonding, as shown in Fig. 6a and b. The FTIR spectra showed that the grafted carboxyl groups exhibited varying degrees of red shift after joining, resulting from coordination interactions that weakened the π-bonding character of the C = O group and consequently reduced the stretching vibration frequency. Among the hybrid structures, the OV + G interface displayed the most red shift, reaching 1709 cm⁻¹ from 1722 cm⁻¹, which was consistent with the calculated results, indicating that this structure showed the stronger coordination interaction and the higher degree of bond formation. The high-resolution Mg 2p XPS spectra for the distinct joint configurations were compared in Fig. 6b, with their corresponding deconvoluted spectra provided in Supplementary Fig. 5. The non-oxidized C + C and C + G joints exhibited negligible binding energy shifts, serving as the baseline references. Conversely, for the oxidation-treated joints, the MgO characteristic peaks systematically shifted toward higher binding energy. Specifically, upon the implementation of bi-modifications, the O + G and OV + G joints exhibited prominent displacements of approximately +0.3 eV and +0.4 eV, respectively, whereas the heat-treated H-OV + G joint showed a slightly smaller shift than the OV + G group, as shown in Supplementary Fig. 5g. Based on the hybrid model, this increase in binding energy was attributed to the reduced local electron density on Mg upon coordination with the carboxyl O, which strengthened the binding of the Mg 2p electrons and shifted the peak toward higher binding energy. This provided conclusive evidence for the formation of a chemical bond between oxidized AZ31B and grafted CFRTP.

The comparison between the experimental valence band (VB) spectra and the calculated PDOS provided direct evidence of experiment-theory consistency. The VB structure obtained from theoretical calculations, after orbital weighting, broadening, and shifting, exhibited good agreement with experimental VB spectra, verifying the reliability of the DFT calculation results. The VB spectra across the six configurations were systematically analyzed (Fig. 6e). Within the shallow valence region (2-12 eV), the valence band maximum was dominated by substrate O 2p states. For the pristine substrate and the low-strength C + C joint, the characteristic doublet peaks remained sharp and localized. Conversely, as grafting (G) was introduced, these two characteristic peaks became systematically broadened. This change demonstrated interfacial orbital hybridization and electronic delocalization. After joining, the VB spectra exhibited pronounced new features in the 12-20 eV region, which were absent before joining. This evolution closely matched the PDOS results, where both Mg and O atoms developed new states in the deeper energy range after adsorption. Furthermore, within the deep valence region (20-30 eV) governed by the localized O 2s semi-core states, the characteristic high-intensity bands exhibited damping specifically after grafting (C + G, O + G, OV + G). It reflected the cross-interface charge transfer and core-level electrostatic perturbations induced by the carboxyl groups. Overall, the emergence of deep-level features and the flattening of shallow electronic states confirmed enhanced interfacial orbital hybridization, further corroborating the formation of coordination bonds.

Chemical bonding mechanism in metal-polymer hybrid systems

Through the previous analysis, it was discovered that the chemical bonding-driven joining of the AZ31B/CFRTP hybrid joint was primarily facilitated under the following conditions: the metal side provided sufficiently low-energy vacant orbitals to accept electrons, while the polymer side supplied high-energy occupied orbitals to donate electrons. These complementary electron states enabled effective electron redistribution and orbital hybridization across the interface. In addition, the degree of orbital energy alignment between the metal and polymer was found to significantly influence the strength of the chemical bonding.

To further verify the general applicability of the bonding mechanism and to systematically assess the effect of interfacial electron states on bonding capability, metal oxides with different cation valence states (Li₂O(111), MgO(001), Al₂O₃(001), and TiO₂(110)) were selected, and representative PA6 adsorption configurations containing amide group were constructed, as shown in Fig. 7a. Here, PA6 was utilized instead of PA66 to ensure strict variable control. Since orbital hybridization was highly localized, PA6 and PA66 shared identical fundamental capabilities as electron donors. Utilizing the simplified PA6 model effectively eliminated the secondary hydrogen-bonding noise that is structurally inherent in PA66, thereby enabling a pure and consistent comparison of metal-oxygen interactions across different metal oxides. Prior to the comparative analysis, the adsorption site selectivity of the PA6 monomer was evaluated on the MgO surface (Supplementary Fig. 6). Energy calculations revealed that O-adsorption configurations via the carbonyl group were thermodynamically more stable than the N-H binding configuration. The vertical C = O adsorption configuration was selected as the standard model for the subsequent analyses to specifically investigate the polar interactions. The PDOS and CDD results showed that the metal orbitals exhibited hybridization with the O 2p near the Ef, except for TiO2/PA6 system. In the Li₂O/PA6 system, both Li 2s and 2p hybridized with the O 2p, yet the interfacial electronic interaction remained weak, indicating a regime dominated by electronic redistribution rather than typical coordination bonding. Conversely, in the MgO/PA6 and Al₂O₃/PA6 systems, the hybridization mainly involved the M 3p and the O 2p. This orbital interaction shifted the interfacial bonding from a weak electrostatic regime to a dominant covalent-polar matching regime. With the increment of the cation valence, the electronic rearrangement became more pronounced, reflecting the enhanced electron-accepting capability of the high-valent metal sites. The TiO2/PA6 system presented a case where the surface chemisorption was geometrically constrained. This constraint stemmed from the fact that all metal oxide slabs were cleaved along their thermodynamically most stable facets. TiO2 cleaved along the oxygen-terminated (110) plane, where the surface bridging-oxygen atoms partially screened the incoming amide adsorption, thereby reducing the interfacial interaction within the simulation. Crucially, the Bader charge analysis captured a polymer-end dependent electron-donation behavior that aligned with the aforementioned hybridization trends: the lone pairs of the amide oxygen were drawn into the vacant orbitals of higher-valent main-group cations. This localized charge depletion induced an orbital splitting, which accounted for the progressive increase in the electron-transfer energy observed across the main-group oxides. In contrast, although the cross-interface electron exchange in the standard TiO2 system was suppressed by the geometric shielding effect, its ΔE remained lower, implying the high potential bonding capability. To explicitly evaluate this potential, a Ti-terminated TiO₂ surface was also calculated (Supplementary Fig. 7). On this oxygen-deficient model, the amide oxygen bonded with two 4-coordinated Ti atoms, yielding a stronger adsorption. This verified that introducing oxygen vacancies exposed inner metal sites and enhanced interfacial bonding. Overall, higher metal valence corresponded to stronger bonding trends. In hybrid systems, the interfacial bonding strength could be systematically modified by adjusting the electron-donating and electron-accepting capabilities of interfacial components. Macroscopic experiments on three representative structural metals (AZ31B, 6061 Al, and TC4) were also conducted to verify the broad applicability on the metal side (Supplementary Fig. 8). The results proved that the O + G modification consistently achieved a significant enhancement in strength. Furthermore, previous studies demonstrated that the chemical modification of higher-valent metal ions typically induced more pronounced strengthening effects, as summarized in Fig. 4g. Consistent with this observation, study on metallo-polyelectrolyte systems reported that higher-valency metal ions enhanced material stiffness and toughness by strengthening metal-polymer interactions42. This valence-dependent strengthening behavior provided additional support for the interfacial electronic-state modulation strategy adopted in this work, in which metal oxidation or defect engineering was employed to enhance the electron-accepting capability of metal sites, thereby promoting interfacial bonding.

Concurrently, to validate the polymer’s electron-donating requirement, interfacial simulations of various thermoplastics revealed that polar polymers (acrylonitrile butadiene styrene (ABS), polyamide (PA), and polyphenylene sulfide (PPS)) successfully bonded with the metal substrate, whereas non-polar polyethylene (PE) lacked electron-donating sites and exhibited no chemical interaction (Supplementary Fig. 9). These results demonstrated that the proposed bonding mechanism was effective for thermoplastics containing polar heteroatoms but was limited for non-polar hydrocarbon polymers. Therefore, incorporating such bond-forming heteroatoms provided a viable strategy to achieve interfacial bonding. Collectively, these cross-system validations established that synergistically matching electron-donating and electron-accepting capabilities across the interface was the fundamental design principle for fabricating robust hybrid structures.

Figure 8a illustrates the interfacial bonding mechanisms under different metal surface states and oxygen-containing functional groups. For clean metal surfaces, the interaction with oxygen-containing groups was relatively weak. Taking Mg adsorption as an example, the electronegativity of the oxygen atom attracted electrons from the metal s orbital, forming a weak s-p σ-like interaction. Due to the lack of high-energy unoccupied orbitals capable of effectively accepting electrons on the metal side, the overall orbital hybridization remained minimal. In contrast, in the metal oxide adsorption systems, the metal cations typically possessed high-energy unoccupied orbitals with strong electron-accepting ability. Meanwhile, the oxygen atoms in the functional groups, after sp² or sp³ hybridization, generated lone pairs corresponding to high-energy occupied orbitals capable of donating electrons to the metal. Consequently, the interface more readily formed a coordination bond via donation of lone pairs from the oxygen atoms to the metal unoccupied orbitals. In the MgO adsorption system, for instance, the energy levels of Mg 3p and O 2p were highly aligned, enabling the formation of typical p-p σ-type bonding. Furthermore, for carboxyl groups containing two oxygen atoms capable of donating lone pairs, a more stable μ₂-type bridging coordination could form when the spatial configuration of the metal adsorption sites matched the distance between the two oxygen atoms. Moreover, the introduction of defects such as oxygen vacancies on the metal oxide surface, which modified the energy levels of unoccupied orbitals closer to the O 2p occupied orbitals of the functional groups, further increased the orbital energy alignment between occupied and unoccupied states. This promoted stronger p-p σ hybridization, allowing more lone pairs to contribute to interfacial bonding and enhancing the coordination interactions. To provide a more intuitive representation of the electronic trends across different metal-polymer systems, energy-level schematic was employed as shown in Fig. 8b. Different metal oxides exhibited distinct metal unoccupied orbital energy positions. As the metal cation valence increased, the unoccupied orbitals progressively shifted closer to the O 2p occupied orbitals in their initial states, reducing the initial energy gap and facilitating the orbital hybridization drive from the polymer to the metal, and then enhancing the interfacial bonding capability.

Collectively, these results established a unified electron donor-acceptor mechanism governing chemical bonding at metal-polymer interfaces. Interfacial bonding arose from lone-pair donation from the polymer to vacant metal orbitals and was strengthened by improved orbital energy alignment, which promoted interfacial charge transfer and led to a marked increase in joint strength. This mechanism provided a general framework for designing high-performance hybrid interfaces beyond the specific systems examined here. On the polymer side, polar polymers inherently satisfied the electron-donating requirement, whereas nonpolar hydrocarbon polymers required the targeted introduction of heteroatoms (e.g., O, N, or S) through surface activation to enable chemical bonding. On the metal side, systems capable of providing sufficiently low-lying vacant orbitals, typically through a higher cation valence state or defect engineering, acted as effective electron acceptors. By defining these physicochemical criteria, this work offered a rational basis for the design of diverse metal-polymer hybrid interfaces.

Methods

Materials and surface modification

The metallic substrates used in this study included AZ31B magnesium alloy, TC4 titanium alloy, and 6061-Al aluminum alloy, with identical dimensions of 50 mm × 30 mm × 1.5 mm (Dongguan Huatai Metal Materials Co., Ltd., China). The CFRTP sheets (50 mm × 25 mm × 3 mm) were composed of a PA66 matrix reinforced with 30 wt.% carbon fibers, supplied by Hefei Genius Advanced Materials Co., Ltd., China. The melting point and decomposition temperature of the CF-PA66 composite were 265 °C and 390 °C, respectively. Prior to the surface modification and joining processes, the metallic substrates were mechanically abraded with 250# SiC sandpaper to remove surface contaminants and standard scale. The CFRTP sheets were ultrasonically degreased in an ethanol bath for 15 min and subsequently dried in a convective oven at 65 °C for 2 h.

To systematically evaluate the interfacial bonding mechanisms, three distinct metallic surface states were engineered. Clean Metal (C): The pre-treated metallic substrates were subjected to an immediate secondary mechanical abrasion prior to the subsequent joining process to minimize transient atmospheric contamination. Oxidized Metal (O): The pre-treated substrates were subjected to a thermal oxidation treatment by heating in convective furnace at 200 °C for 10 min, followed by continuous cooling to room temperature in an ambient atmosphere to yield passive oxide film. Oxidized Metal with Oxygen Vacancies (OV): The pre-treated substrates underwent a high-voltage plasma electrolytic discharge process. The optimized parameters were established at a peak voltage of 300 V, a frequency of 600 Hz, and a positive/negative duty cycle of 10% for a duration of 5 min under continuous closed-circuit water cooling. The aqueous electrolyte was prepared using deionized water and comprised five solutes: 15 g/L sodium silicate (Na2SiO3, ≥ 98% purity), 8 g/L potassium fluoride (KF, ≥ 99% purity), 4 g/L ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid disodium salt (EDTA-2Na, ≥ 97% purity), 8 g/L potassium hydroxide (KOH, ≥ 99.99% purity), and 3 g/L sodium tungstate (Na2WO4, ≥ 99% purity), all sourced from Shanghai Macklin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd., China.

Two distinct CFRTP surface states were applied: Clean CFRTP (C) and Grafted CFRTP (G). For the grafted CFRTP, acrylic acid (AA, ≥ 99.5% purity, 5 mL/L, Macklin) was employed as the functional monomer, benzophenone (BP, ≥ 99% purity, 0.4 g/L, Macklin) acted as the photoinitiator, and toluene (TOL, Yantai Far Eastern Fine Chemical Co., Ltd., China) served as the solvent. For each CFRTP specimen, a 2 mL aliquot of the grafting solution was uniformly applied to the surface. The ultraviolet irradiation was performed in a custom UV reaction chamber (SK-102, China) with continuously variable power output. The process was performed utilizing an operating power of 800 W with a peak emission wavelength centered at 365 nm. The target distance between the UV lamp and the CFRTP substrate was 18 cm. The reaction proceeded for 1 min under a continuous nitrogen (N2) atmosphere (10 L/min). Post-grafting, the specimens were ultrasonically rinsed in an acetone bath for 15 min and then desiccated in a convective oven at 65 °C for 2 h before joining, to eliminate unreacted monomers and residual photoinitiators.

Laser joining process

A laser-assisted joining process was employed to fabricate AZ31B/CFRTP hybrid structure before and after surface modification. Lap joints were assembled with the metal sheet on top of the polymer sheet, and laser irradiation was applied to the metal side to induce heat-conduction joining. The equipment employed for the joining process was a YLS-6000 fiber laser (IPG Photonics), laser head (Precitec), KRC-30 robotic arm (KUKA), and SC80×150 standard tie-rod cylinder (AirTAC). Optimized joining parameters were determined prior to joint fabrication: 700 W laser power, 15 mm/s joining speed, +60 mm defocus distance, 0.3 MPa interfacial pressure, and 15 ° beam incidence to reduce reflection from the AZ31B metal sheets.

Characterization

The tensile-shear mechanical performance of the joint configurations was evaluated at room temperature using a universal testing machine (UTM, Instron 5967, Instron, USA) operating at a constant crosshead displacement rate of 2 mm/min, following the ASTM D1002 and ASTM D3163-05 standards43,44. The fracture surface area was quantified using image processing software (ImageJ, v1.54, National Institutes of Health (NIH), USA). The tensile-shear strength was subsequently calculated by dividing the peak tensile-shear force by the actual measured fracture area. For the mechanical property evaluations, at least three distinct fabricated samples were tested for each cohort to ensure statistical reproducibility. Cross-sectional microstructures and fracture surface morphologies of the hybrid joints were observed via an optical microscope (OM, DSX510, Olympus, Japan) and a field-emission scanning electron microscope (FE-SEM, MERLIN Compact, Carl Zeiss, Germany). The nanoscale three-dimensional surface topographies and corresponding arithmetic surface roughness were characterized via an atomic force microscope (AFM, MultiMode 8 SPM, Bruker, Germany), conducted in the standard tapping mode. To evaluate the interfacial wetting behavior, the contact angles of the treated surfaces were measured via a contact angle measurement system (CAMS, SZ-CAMC33, Shanghai Sunzern Instrument Co., Ltd., China). Deionized water and diiodomethane were employed as the polar and nonpolar probe liquids, respectively, and the surface free energy was determined based on the classic Owens-Wendt-Rabel-Kaelble (OWRK) method45,46. All contact angle determinations were performed on three distinct replicates per condition to guarantee data reliability. The evolution of surface functional groups was monitored via an attenuated total reflection Fourier transform infrared spectrometer (ATR-FTIR, Nicolet 380, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) at a spectral resolution of 4 cm-1 with an accumulation of 32 scans per spectrum. The crystal structures and phase compositions of the substrates were verified via an X-ray diffractometer (XRD, DX-2700BH, Dandong Haoyuan Instrument Co., Ltd., China). Interfacial chemical state alterations and core-level valence band configurations were monitored via an X-ray photoelectron spectrometer (XPS, ESCALAB 250XI, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) equipped with a monochromatic Al Kα X-ray radiation source. Furthermore, electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR, EMXplus-6/1, Bruker, Germany) was employed to confirm the presence of oxygen vacancies. To ensure accurate comparative quantification, identical masses of sample powders were carefully weighed and loaded for each measurement. The relative concentration of oxygen vacancies was evaluated via the mass-normalized spin density. To analyze the localized spatial distribution of these oxygen vacancies, electron energy loss spectroscopy (EELS) was conducted using a spherical aberration-corrected scanning transmission electron microscope (Cs-corrected STEM, ARM 200 F, JEOL, Japan) equipped with a post-column energy filter system (GIF, Gatan 1065, Gatan, USA), focusing on the spatial evolution of the oxygen K-edge (O K-edge) and its pre-peak intensity profiles.

Density functional theory (DFT) calculation

First-principles calculations were performed within the framework of DFT using the Vienna Ab initio Simulation Package (VASP). Electron interactions were treated with the projector augmented-wave (PAW) method47, and the exchange-correlation functional was described via the generalized gradient approximation (GGA) in the Perdew-Burke-Ernzerhof (PBE) formulation48. To ensure numerical precision, a plane-wave cutoff energy of 520 eV was uniformly employed, and the electronic self-consistent field (SCF) iterations were monitored with an expanded maximum of 200 electronic steps to guarantee rigorous convergence. Van der Waals interactions were included via the DFT-D3 scheme to account for long-range dispersion forces. A vacuum layer of 18 Å was applied along the surface normal to avoid spurious interactions between periodic images. To eliminate the artificial electric field generated by periodic boundary conditions across the vacuum layer, dipole corrections were explicitly applied along the surface-normal z-direction for all asymmetric slab and adsorption models. Furthermore, non-spin-polarized calculations were employed across all configurations. This was guided by previous studies indicating that the neutral oxygen vacancy (VO0, F-center) on the MgO surface typically formed a non-magnetic singlet ground state featuring two paired electrons49,50.

Task-specific computational run-types and workflows were executed based on these consistent baseline criteria as follows: Geometric optimization: Structural relaxation was driven by the conjugate gradient algorithm with fixed cell parameters until the residual forces on each unconstrained atom were strictly below 0.02 eV/Å. To prevent unphysical structural distortion during the early alignment of long-chain polymers on the substrate, a two-stage relaxation scheme was implemented: the polymers were first fully relaxed while temporarily freezing the substrate, followed by a refined, unrestricted relaxation of the upper layers with only the bottom-most substrate layer constrained. PDOS: The PDOS was evaluated based on the optimized coordinates via a standard two-step scheme (a self-consistent ground-state charge density generation, followed by a non-self-consistent calculation). The tetrahedron method with Blöchl corrections (ISMEAR = -5) was explicitly enabled during the property step. To eliminate statistical noise and discrete spikes resulting from finite k-point sampling, the PDOS plots were systematically smoothed using the standard Savitzky-Golay filter equipped with a 20-point window. The PDOS data were subsequently broadened and convoluted to simulate experimental resolution, and the orbital contributions were weighted according to the tabulated photoionization cross-sections of Yeh and Lindau51, enabling direct comparison with VB spectra. Charge & bonding quantifications (CDD, ELF, and Bader): Interfacial charge redistribution, bonding localization, and quantitative net charge transfers were systematically investigated via single-step static electronic runs based on the fully relaxed configurations. AIMD: To capture the thermodynamic stability and finite-temperature kinetic behaviors of the coordinated boundaries, AIMD simulations were conducted within the canonical (NVT ensemble) at a simulated temperature of 400 K. The representative bond lengths were extracted every 10 simulation steps (corresponding to a time resolution of 2 fs) to evaluate the real-time interfacial bonding stability. The primary task-specific baseline configurations, input parameters, and representative structure coordinates for the main system models are provided in the zipped Supplementary Data folder.
