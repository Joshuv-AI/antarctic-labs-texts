Left Ventricular Assist Device Implantation and Aneurysmectomy for a Giant Left Ventricular Aneurysm
Authors: Wenjun Yu, Zhitao Chen, Jinping Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT

History of Presentation

A 68-year-old woman with chronic ischemic heart failure was urgently referred to our center. Initial electrocardiography (Figure 1A) revealed ST-segment elevation despite normal myocardial injury markers, prompting expedited surgical assessment.

Preoperative Electrocardiography and Coronary Angiography

(A) A 12-lead electrocardiogram obtained at presentation demonstrates ST-segment elevation in leads I, aVL, and V2 to V6, prompting urgent evaluation. (B to D) Subsequent coronary angiography reveals severe multivessel stenoses contributing to ischemic heart failure. Specifically, significant lesions are identified in the (B) right coronary artery, (C) left anterior descending and left circumflex arteries, and (D) left main coronary artery, which necessitated surgical revascularization.

Past Medical History

Comorbidities included diabetes mellitus and ischemic heart failure. The patient had previously undergone percutaneous coronary intervention for severe stenoses in the left anterior descending and left circumflex arteries (Figures 1B to 1D).

Differential Diagnosis

Differential diagnoses included acute myocardial ischemia and acute-on-chronic heart failure.

Investigations

After severe myocardial infarctions, the patient developed a giant anterior left ventricular (LV) aneurysm. Cardiac magnetic resonance imaging revealed severe LV dilation (end-diastolic diameter: 65 mm, end-systolic diameter: 58 mm) with markedly elevated volumes (end-diastolic volume: 280.3 mL; end-systolic volume: 214.9 mL). A massive LV aneurysm (7.0 × 3.5 × 8.0 cm) and a severely depressed LV ejection fraction (23%) were identified, corroborating echocardiography (LV end-diastolic diameter: 64 mm) and computed tomography findings (Figure 2). Notably, positron emission tomography demonstrated substantial viable myocardium (56%) and minimal fibrosis (7%), indicating a potential benefit from revascularization (Figure 3).

Multimodality Imaging of the Giant Left Ventricular Aneurysm

Preoperative assessment delineates the massive extent of the left ventricular aneurysm and severe cavity dilation. (A) Cardiac magnetic resonance imaging and (B) computed tomography transverse sections define the anatomical boundaries and wall thinning. These imaging modalities collectively indicated the unsuitability of standard ventricular reconstruction.

Positron Emission Tomography for Myocardial Viability

Preoperative metabolic imaging was used to evaluate the rationale for concurrent coronary revascularization. The positron emission tomography scan reveals a substantial proportion of viable myocardium (56%) alongside minimal fibrotic tissue (7%) in the affected territories. This crucial finding justified the decision to perform coronary artery bypass grafting alongside left ventricular assist device implantation to optimize residual myocardial function.

Management

Given clinical deterioration and donor unavailability, a continuous-flow left ventricular assist device (LVAD) (CH-VAD, CH Biomedical Inc) was implanted as a bridge to transplantation. Extracorporeal support was precluded by her comorbidities. The procedure was performed via median sternotomy on standard cardiopulmonary bypass.1 After aortic cross-clamping, the adherent LV was mobilized, and an intraventricular mural thrombus was evacuated. An LV vent was placed via the right superior pulmonary vein. Concomitant coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG) to the left anterior descending and left circumflex arteries used saphenous vein grafts.

As the giant aneurysm precluded complete excision, a partial anterolateral circular resection (sized to the LVAD sewing ring) was performed. The border zone was then everted and plicated to create a turtleneck, reducing residual LV volume and providing a robust anchoring site. The height of the turtleneck reached approximately 1 cm. This structure was secured using Teflon-pledgeted 3-0 Prolene sutures and circumferentially reinforced externally with a bovine pericardial patch (Figures 4A and 4B). The sewing ring of the LVAD was sutured to this turtleneck, which flattened laterally as the sutures were secured. Transesophageal echocardiography confirmed the inflow cannula was directed toward the mitral valve, avoiding the septum. After meticulous de-airing, the 8-mm outflow graft was anastomosed to the ascending aorta. Final LVAD parameters demonstrated an optimal flow of 5.0 L/min at 2,860 rpm.

Outcome and Follow-Up

The large residual LV compressed the left chest cavity, causing atelectasis and pneumonia. Consequently, the patient required a 15-day intensive care stay and was extubated on postoperative day 6. On postoperative day 2, abnormal neurological signs in the right leg prompted head computed tomography (CT), which revealed a right temporal ischemic infarct. Anticoagulation was subsequently titrated to strictly maintain an international normalized ratio of 2.0 to 3.0. Imaging confirmed optimal LVAD positioning (Figures 4C to 4D), and the patient was discharged on postoperative day 21.

At 1 and 3 months, the patient developed hematochezia. Capsule endoscopy identified chronic erosive gastritis, managed successfully with octreotide, acid suppression, and transfusions. Otherwise, 4-month follow-up demonstrated stable LVAD parameters without suction events (Table 1).

At 9 months, the patient was readmitted with a late cerebral infarction. Device interrogation showed decreased flow (2.61 L/min) and low power (5.50 W). This profile indicated inflow malalignment from pump migration rather than primary thrombosis. Indeed, CT confirmed downward pump migration with leftward mediastinal displacement (Figure 5). Corresponding 9-month echocardiography demonstrated significant LV reverse remodeling (LV end-diastolic diameter: 46 mm, LV end-systolic diameter: 38 mm) and a leftward septal shift, corroborating the displacement. The patient remains listed for transplantation.

Discussion

Our report describes the successful management of end-stage ischemic heart failure complicated by a giant LV aneurysm, using a continuous-flow LVAD combined with CABG and aneurysmectomy. Historically, LVAD was implanted intraperitoneally owing to the large size of the device,2 but modern devices permit routine intrapericardial implantation.3 However, noncontractile myocardium and a giant aneurysm present surgical challenges—namely, a thinned ventricular wall and unstable LVAD positioning. The modified turtleneck technique provided secure LVAD fixation without requiring full ventricular reconstruction, offering a superior alternative to extracorporeal support.

Although positron emission tomography revealed substantial viability (56%), the patient presented with acute-on-chronic decompensation and a severely depressed ejection fraction (23%). Revascularization and reconstruction alone carried a prohibitively high risk of failure to wean from cardiopulmonary bypass; thus, LVAD support was prioritized.

The Dor procedure, reconstructing by endoventricular circular patch plasty repair, can geometrically change the expanded ventricle into a more elliptical shape and achieve a stably hemostatic fixation of the inflow cannula for LVAD placement, orientation, and unhampered flow performance, which may also be a suitable alternative for transplant candidates.4,5 The Dor procedure is compatible with LVAD implantation and applicable for long-term circulatory support with good quality of life as improved ejection fraction and functional status. In our case, the massive baseline dilation (end-diastolic volume >280 mL) and giant aneurysm lacked a healthy muscular border, which made it unsuitable for Dor procedure application.

LV aneurysmectomy is typically indicated when the scar wall is weakened or intra-aneurysmal thrombi are present. A thin, highly mobile aneurysm wall during the cardiac cycle can lead to unpredictable LVAD hemodynamics and an increased risk of postoperative suction events. Furthermore, thrombus formation within the aneurysm predisposes patients to pump thrombosis and cardioembolic complications. In our patient, the aneurysm was partially resected to optimize the residual LV cavity size, a parameter that is often challenging to accurately determine preoperatively. The residual cavity must be sufficiently large to prevent inflow cannula suction, as a small LV size is an independent risk factor for device thrombosis.6 Conversely, an excessively large cavity secondary to ischemic heart failure can compromise the stable orientation of the device toward the mitral valve and impair left inferior lung expansion. When the LV exceeds the diameter the LVAD sewing ring, modified Fontan-type techniques can be employed to address the size mismatch.7,8

Additionally, we observed gradual downward migration of the LVAD pump postoperatively, accompanied by a leftward mediastinal shift. This phenomenon is likely attributable to dynamic pericardial adhesions combined with profound LV reverse remodeling. After chronic unloading, the rapid reduction in LV volume created structural “slack” or redundancy in the outflow graft. Concurrently, the substantial physical weight of the LVAD pump exerted a continuous downward pull on the structurally compromised ventricle. These factors synergistically alter the thoracic position of the pump and the alignment of the inflow cannula. Consequently, abnormal angulation of the inflow or outflow grafts increases the risk of pump thrombosis.9 To prevent similar occurrences in extreme ventricular dilation, we propose that the outflow graft or the pump itself should be strategically anchored to the chest wall, providing external mechanical support to stabilize the position of the device despite subsequent reverse remodeling.

The timeline of adverse events in our patient clearly illustrates these geometric and mechanical challenges. The early ischemic infarct on postoperative day 2 was likely a consequence of the giant aneurysm's geometry, which harbored a substantial mural thrombus preoperatively. Conversely, the late cerebral infarction at 9 months was associated with the mechanical migration of the pump. The downward displacement altered the alignment of the inflow cannula, creating areas of stasis and increasing the risk of thrombosis.

For postoperative monitoring, we recommend serial CT imaging to provide precise 3-dimensional localization of the pump and conduits, and reconstructed images can quantify inflow cannula angulation, assisting in thrombosis risk stratification. At the same time, continuous monitoring of LVAD parameters, including flow rate, rotational speed, and power consumption, can help in the early detection of device dysfunction. As the patient is currently awaiting a heart transplant, the transplantation is ultimately expected to resolve the increased thrombosis risk due to pump displacement.

Conclusions

This case demonstrates the feasibility of concurrent LVAD implantation, CABG, and aneurysmectomy for end-stage ischemic heart failure complicated by a giant aneurysm and substantial myocardial viability. Furthermore, it underscores the necessity of serial postoperative imaging to monitor for pump migration, thereby mitigating severe complications such as device thrombosis and hemodynamic compromise.
