Machine Learning-Based prediction models for postoperative delirium: a systematic review and Meta-Analysis
Authors: Yingying Tu, Haoyuan Zhu, Xiaozhen Zhang, Shaoyi Huang, Wenyi Tu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe number of risk prediction models for postoperative delirium(POD) has increased yearly, but their quality and applicability in clinical practice and future research remain unclear.AimsThis systematic review aimed to evaluate published studies on POD risk prediction models, evaluate the diagnostic accuracy of these models, and provide guidance for model establishment and improvement.MethodsWe conducted a systematic search in PubMed, Embase, the Cochrane Library, and other scientific databases to identify eligible studies published up to February 15, 2025. The included studies provided data on the sensitivity and specificity of predictive models.ResultsWe included 17 articles with 69 machine learning (ML) prediction models, encompassing 205,202 individuals who underwent surgerg, among whom 50,899 developed POD (24.8%). The average number of cases per study was 12,070, with the largest study including 163,436 cases. The combined area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC) for predicting POD, calculated by averaging the AUROC values reported in the studies, was 0.83 [95% CI, 0.79–0.86], with a sensitivity of 0.73 [95% CI, 0.68–0.74] and a specificity of 0.79 [95% CI, 0.74–0.82]. Subgroup analysis revealed that the random forest model exhibited the highest pooled AUROC, reaching 0.89 [95% CI, 0.86–0.92]. Notably, models predicting POD for orthopedic surgeries (AUROC: 0.88 [95% CI, 0.85–0.90]) and patients younger than 60 years (AUROC: 0.84 [95% CI, 0.81–0.87]) demonstrated superior predictive performance. Furthermore, models that underwent only internal validation had lower predictive performance compared to those validated both internally and externally (AUROC: 0.84 [95% CI, 0.81–0.87]). Compared to models developed for European and American populations, those applied to Asian populations performed better (AUROC: 0.85 [95% CI, 0.82–0.88]). Across the included studies, advanced age, preoperative cognitive impairment, comorbidities, anemia, and hypoalbuminemia were the most consistently reported predictors of POD .ConclusionsML models perform well in predicting the occurrence of POD, with stable performance across various surgical and demographic subgroups.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12888-025-07401-2.Keywords: Postoperative delirium, Prediction model, Machine learning, Systematic review

Introduction

Postoperative delirium (POD), a common surgical complication, refers to acute and nonspecific disorders of consciousness, attention, cognition, and sleep cycle that occur from several hours to days after surgery[1, 2]. POD is prevalent, especially among elderly surgical patients, and is associated with increased incidence of perioperative complications, costs, and long-term death [3–6]. The primary treatment for POD involves evaluating and managing underlying causes, such as infections, dehydration, pain, and metabolic disturbances [7, 8].Studies have shown that primary prevention of delirium is the most effective treatment strategy, with 30% of cases being preventable through perioperative non-pharmacological interventions, hospital programs for the elderly, or perioperative geriatric consultation[9–14]. Therefore, early identification and intervention for high-risk patients are crucial.

In recent years, ML methods[15], a branch of artificial intelligence, have emerged as a new approach driven by the development of complex algorithms and the analysis of large datasets. The accuracy and stability of these models have led many medical researchers to construct POD risk prediction models [16–24, 26–41]using techniques such as logistic regression (LR), gradient boosting (BOOST), random forest (RF), support vector machine classifier (SVM) and transformer architecture (TRAPOD). Lee et al.[34]developed and validated a ML-based model using XGBoost to predict POD in non-cardiac surgery patients with high accuracy (AUROC = 0.87), leveraging five key preoperative variables to facilitate early risk identification and clinical intervention. Rössler et al.[36] developed and validated static and dynamic models to forecast POD in patients undergoing noncardiac surgery, finding that models incorporating preoperative and intraoperative variables achieved high predictive accuracy (c-statistic up to 0.86), with predisposing factors contributing more significantly than intraoperative or postoperative variables.

ML has gained considerable traction in the medical field, demonstrating the potential to enhance patient treatment outcomes while reducing costs[42, 43]. Healthcare professionals can improve the quality and safety by utilizing predictive analytics tools. The development of POD risk prediction models through ML not only reveals underlying risk factors but also offers essential decision support for clinicians. This facilitates the identification of high-risk patients and enables early interventions, ultimately decreasing the incidence of POD. Such advancements not only reduce the workload of perioperative nursing staff but also effectively mitigate adverse events, thereby improving the overall safety and quality of perioperative care. Despite the growing number of predictive models for POD, there remains a lack of methodological consistency, and the quality and applicability of these models have yet to be systematically reviewed. Given the rapid progress in this research area, we have undertaken a comprehensive summary and critical evaluation of existing POD prediction models developed using machine learning techniques, with the aim of providing valuable insights for clinical practice and guiding future research endeavors.

Methods

This systematic review was registered with PROSPERO(CRD42024536703) and conducted as per Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Review and Meta-analysis (PRISMA) guidelines.

Search strategy and study selection

PubMed, Embase, the Cochrane Library, and Web of Science were searched from inception to February 2025. The search strategy included both Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) and natural language terms to ensure a comprehensive search. The keywords used were “Machine Learning” and “Delirium”, with the following search string for PubMed: ((Machine Learning) OR (((Learning, Machine) OR (Transfer Learning)) OR (Learning, Transfer))) AND ((Emergence Delirium) OR ((((((((Delirium, Emergence) OR (Post-Operative Delirium)) OR (Delirium, Post-Operative)) OR (Post Operative Delirium)) OR (Anesthesia Emergence Delirium)) OR (Delirium, Anesthesia Emergence)) OR (Postoperative Delirium)) OR (Delirium, Postoperative))). The included studies focused on adult POD prediction models based on machine learning, provided sufficient data, and used the gold standard for delirium diagnosis. The studies provided access to raw research data, including TP (true positive), FP (false positive), FN (false negative), and TN (true negative) outcomes. The results were published in English. Other relevant studies were identified by reviewing the reference lists of retrieved articles and reviews. Non-English papers, unpublished studies, case reports, conference abstracts, studies involving minors or animals were excluded. Two authors performed the initial literature screening using Zotero software, eliminating duplicates and assessing full texts when titles and abstracts did not clearly identify potentially eligible studies. Disagreements regarding study selection were discussed by three authors to reach a consensus.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

We included studies that (1) developed or validated machine learning (ML) models for predictingPOD; (2) involved surgical patients; (3) reported POD outcomes; and (4) provided sufficient data to extract performance metrics. Studies were excluded if they (1) did not use ML methods, (2) included non-surgical populations, (3) did not report POD outcomes, (4) were non-English publications, or (5) lacked extractable performance data. Eligible studies defined POD using established criteria such as the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (DSM), the Confusion Assessment Method (CAM), or structured chart review.

Study quality and risk of bias assessment

Data from the included studies were extracted using the CHARMS checklist, while the Prediction Model Risk of Bias Assessment Tool (PROBAST) was employed to assess both the quality and applicability of the predictive models. PROBAST evaluates bias risk across four key domains: participants, predictors, outcomes, and analytical methods. Each domain includes 2 to 9 signaling questions (totaling 20 questions), which guide the evaluation of bias risk [44].

Data analysis

The sensitivity, specificity, and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) of the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve for each of the included POD prediction models were extracted from the studies. The true positive (TP), false positive (FP), false negative (FN), and true negative (TN) values for each study were also collected. To quantify the heterogeneity across studies, we calculated the I² statistic and performed Cochran’s Q test. We used a random-effects model for the meta-analysis when P < 0.05 and I² >50%, indicating substantial heterogeneity. In contrast, a fixed-effects model was applied when P ≥ 0.05 and I² ≤ 50%, suggesting low heterogeneity. This approach ensures an appropriate model selection based on the level of variation between studies. The extracted data, including area under the ROC curve (AUC), sensitivity, specificity, and diagnostic odds ratio (DOR), were meta-analyzed using Stata 15.1 software. Due to the unavailability of patient-level data in the included studies, descriptive statistics such as means and interquartile ranges of POD incidence could not be calculated. Instead, we reported the incidence as provided in each study.

Subgroup analyses were conducted according to various study characteristics, such as type of surgery, ML methods, region of the study population, and age. These subgroup analyses aimed to identify possible sources of heterogeneity and evaluate the robustness of the findings. For the evaluation of likelihood ratios, we calculated both the positive likelihood ratio (PLR) and negative likelihood ratio (NLR), which indicate how much more frequently POD was detected in patients with the condition compared to those without. The formula for combining these ratios was based on the weighted averages of individual study estimates, adjusting for variance. To assess potential publication bias, we performed Deeks’ funnel plot asymmetry test using Stata version 15.1 with the midas command and the pubbias option. In this test, the natural logarithm of the diagnostic odds (InDOR) was regressed against the inverse square root of the effective samle size. A P value < 0.05 was considered indicative of significant asymmetry, suggesting the presence of publication bias.

Results

Basic characteristics of studies

Four databases were searched, yielding a total of 136 records. Forty-five papers were selected for full-text evaluation after analyzing the titles and abstracts. Following the full-text evaluation, 17 articles were included for analysis [16–24, 26–32], involving a total of 205,202 study subjects, among whom 50,899 experienced POD, resulting in an overall POD incidence of 24.8%. Across the included studies, the reported incidence of POD ranged from 5.33 to 36.87% based on study-level data. Additionally, the average number of cases per study was 12,070, with the largest single study including 163,436 cases. Figure1 illustrates the literature screening process and results.

Article selection flow diagram

Table 1summarizes the design and participant characteristics of the 17 included studies published between 2022 and 2025. Seven studies were conducted in China [17, 18, 21, 27, 30–32], three in Japan [22–24], three in Germany [28, 29], two in the United States [16, 26]and two in South Korea [19, 20]. Seven studies were prospective [17, 20, 24, 26–28]and ten were retrospective [16, 18, 19, 21–23, 29–32]. All studies’ ML prediction models underwent internal validation, with eight studies conducting external validation of their established prediction models[17, 19–23, 27, 29]. The Confusion Assessment Method (CAM), a validated tool with high sensitivity (94%−100%) and specificity (90%−95%) [45], was used in eight studies to assess POD[16–18, 23, 25–26,28−29]. Additionally, the Nursing Delirium Screening Scale (NU-DESC), another widely used screening tool, was discussed in several studies as an alternative or complementary method for delirium assessment 16. The Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fourth Edition/Fifth Edition (DSM-IV/V), a set of diagnostic criteria that aids in assessing the severity of delirium symptoms, was utilized in six studies[20, 21, 24, 30–32]. However, as the DSM-IV/V is not a diagnostic test per se, its criteria were applied manually by clinicians through patient observation and clinical judgment [46], often in conjunction with other assessment tools like the CAM or NU-DESC. Two studies[19, 27]by Han and Ren employed the Intensive Care Delirium Screening Checklist (ICDSC) [47], which is particularly suitable for patients with speech limitations. This scale has shown high sensitivity and specificity, proving useful for clinicians in accurately identifying delirium in postoperative patients.

Quality assessment and risk of bias in the included studies

We assessed the risk of bias in studies that developed or externally validated prognostic models using PROBAST, a tool specifically designed for the systematic assessment of diagnostic and prognostic prediction models [48]. Figure3 provides an overview of the bias risk assessments for the developed models by domain. across different domains for the constructed models. The risk of bias for participant selection was low across all models, with 14 models demonstrating a low risk of bias in predictor selection and 15 models showing a low risk of bias in outcome assessment. However, only 7 models exhibited a low risk of bias in statistical analysis. Overall, 5 models were evaluated as having a low risk of bias. Furthermore, for studies constructing models, despite receiving low-risk ratings in all four domains, the absence of external validation resulted in an upgrade to high risk.

Meta-analysis of studies

Regarding the various machine learning algorithms employed, the 17 studies included in this meta-analysis collectively developed 69 distinct computational models. This diversity underscores the breadth of approaches utilized in the assessment of POD prediction. Logistic regression (LR) was the most frequently used (n = 21, 30.4%), followed by gradient boosting (GBoost) (n = 13, 18.8%), random forest (RF) (n = 6, 8.7%), and support vector machine (SVM) (n = 5, 7.2%). The analysis of all data from the 69 included ML prediction models revealed a combined area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC) of 0.83 [95%CI, 0.79–0.86], a sensitivity of 0.73 [95%CI, 0.68–0.77], a specificity of 0.79 [95%CI, 0.74–0.82], and a diagnostic odds ratio (DOR) of 10 [95%CI, 8–13] (see Fig. 3 for more details). The positive likelihood ratio was 3.4 [2.9, 4.1], while the negative likelihood ratio was 0.34 [0.30, 0.40], providing further evidence for the robustness of the model. Moreover, all predictive models exhibited strong applicability across different domains, with detailed sensitivity and specificity values presented in Fig. 4. Deeks’funnel plot asymmetry test revealed no significant evidence of publication bias (slope coefficient = 5.81, 95% CI: − 0.88 to 12.50, P = 0.088), as outlined in Fig. 5..

Subgroup analysis of studies

The prospective studies included 34 machine learning (ML) models, with a combined area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC) of 0.77 (95% CI: 0.73–0.81). In contrast, the retrospective studies, which included 35 ML models, produced a higher combined AUROC of 0.87 (95% CI: 0.83–0.89). Additionally, ML models that underwent both internal and external validation exhibited a slightly higher combined AUROC of 0.84 (95% CI: 0.81–0.87) compared to those with only internal validation. Among these, prediction models established for orthopedic surgery patients showed the best predictive effect, with a combined AUROC of 0.88 [95%CI, 0.85–0.90]. Studies conducted in Europe and America included 18 predictive models, with a combined AUROC of 0.72 [95%CI, 0.68–0.76]. In contrast, 51 ML models have been developed in Asia, yielding a pooled AUROC of 0.85 [95% CI, 0.82–0.88]. Notably, ML prediction models specifically developed for the Chinese population demonstrated a higher AUROC of 0.86 [95% CI, 0.82–0.89], suggesting improved predictive performance in this cohort. In our analysis of predictive models employing ML techniques, the logistic regression (LR) model emerged as the most widely utilized, achieving a combined area under the ROC curve of 0.80 [95%CI, 0.76–0.83]. Despite the limited number of random forest models (n = 6), they exhibited superior predictive performance, attaining a combined area under the ROC curve of 0.89 [95%CI, 0.86–0.92]. Moreover, the model tailored for patients under 60 years of age demonstrated a combined area under the ROC curve of 0.84 [95%CI, 0.81–0.87]. This model outperformed those developed for patients aged 60 years and older. Further subgroup analyses are detailed in Table 2.

Discussion

We acknowledge that traditional models (such as clinical scoring systems) have been widely used to predict POD. However, Non-ML models often rely on simpler, linear relationships between variables, whereas ML models can capture more complex, nonlinear patterns in the data, potentially leading to better predictive accuracy. the ML models reviewed in our study generally show superior performance in terms of sensitivity and specificity, as reflected in the higher combined AUROC. Our analysis incorporated 17 studies encompassing 69 predictive models, resulting in a combined area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) of 0.83 [95% CI, 0.79–0.86] for predicting POD, alongside a sensitivity of 0.73 [95% CI, 0.68–0.77] and a specificity of 0.79 [95% CI, 0.74–0.82]. This meta-analysis assesses the effectiveness and reliability of machine learning-based predictive models for POD, highlighting their advantages over traditional statistical methods, particularly in managing large datasets with multiple features.

A consistent group of predictors was repeatedly highlighted across the included studies, most notably advanced age, preoperative cognitive impairment, major comorbidities, anemia, and hypoalbuminemia, which together reflect patient frailty and systemic vulnerability. Among these, age and cognitive status were the most influential and were consistently retained in high-performing models[17, 18, 20, 24, 29]. In addition, hemoglobin and albumin levels contributed significantly to model calibration and discrimination, as demonstrated in several recent machine learning studies[17, 20]. These findings indicate that future POD prediction models should prioritize the systematic collection of these core clinical and laboratory variables, as they are routinely available in perioperative practice and can substantially enhance predictive accuracy and clinical feasibility. For instance, Yoshimura et al. (2024) reported robust performance when incorporating demographic and laboratory data in a nationwide cohort, while Benovic et al. (2024) showed that models integrating age, cognitive function, and laboratory markers achieved an AUROC of 0.82, outperforming models restricted to demographic variables.

ML models each tackle classification challenges in distinct manners, revealing specific strengths and weaknesses in their predictive capabilities[16]. Leveraging these advanced predictive models can significantly improve the accuracy and reliability of POD predictions, allowing for more effective and timely clinical interventions. Logistic regression was the most frequently used method, but random forest models demonstrated superior predictive performance due to their ability to manage complex data structures and high-dimensional features[48]. Notably, a recent study by Giesa et al. [41] introduced a transformer-based architecture that leverages intraoperative temporal dynamics to enhance POD prediction. By capturing intricate temporal patterns, this approach represents a significant advancement in predictive modeling, improving both the robustness and accuracy of ML models in clinical settings.

The risk of bias assessment (Fig. 2) indicates a high or unclear risk of bias across included studies, primarily due to retrospective designs, lack of external validation, and inconsistencies in outcome definitions and predictor selection. Subgroup analysis revealed that ML models developed from retrospective studies demonstrated higher predictive performance (AUC = 0.87, 95% CI [0.83, 0.89]) than those from prospective studies (AUC = 0.77, 95% CI [0.73, 0.81]), though retrospective studies were more numerous (200,471 vs. 4,731 patients). However, retrospective designs are prone to confounding and may overestimate model performance[49, 50]. These findings highlight the need for rigorous prospective studies with comprehensive external validation to enhance model robustness and generalizability.

Subgroup analysis by surgical type revealed that POD prediction models exhibited superior accuracy in orthopedic surgery patients had higher predictive performance (AUC = 0.88, 95% CI [0.85, 0.90]), likely due to the distinct characteristics of this population, which includes a high proportion of elderly individuals with increased frailty and comorbidities such as preoperative anemia. Low hemoglobin levels, along with intraoperative blood loss, hemodilution, and cerebral hypoxia, may contribute to POD onset[51, 52]. Furthermore, this meta-analysis found that research from China reported superior predictive values relative to those from Western populations, potentially due to demographic, healthcare, and cohort-specific factors influencing POD incidence and model accuracy. These results highlight the necessity for further research to investigate differences across regions and surgical types. Additionally, validating predictive models in varied populations is crucial to improve their applicability and usefulness in clinical settings.

Models developed for patients under 60 years old exhibited higher predictive values than those for patients aged 60 and above. Given that POD is generally less common in younger patients, this finding is particularly unexpected and intriguing. A possible explanation is that younger individuals have fewer comorbidities, better baseline cognitive function, and fewer age-related physiological changes, which may enhance the predictive accuracy of these models. In contrast, older adults, especially those with cognitive impairments or dementia, are at significantly higher risk of POD due to neurodegenerative changes, increased neuroinflammation, and reduced cognitive reserve. Previous studies[53, 54] have shown that dementia is a major risk factor for POD, often complicating delirium prediction in elderly populations. Therefore, future research should incorporate dementia-specific analyses and draw on larger, more diverse cohorts to clarify the interaction between age, cognitive impairment, and POD risk. Such efforts would refine predictive models and enhance their generalizability across patient populations. Consistently, age and cognitive status emerged as the most influential predictors in high-performing models, while hemoglobin and albumin further improved accuracy, underscoring the importance of systematically including these routinely available clinical and laboratory variables in future model development.

This research confirms that ML models are effective in forecasting POD, with stable performance across various surgical and demographic subgroups. However, the significant risk of bias and restricted external validation highlight the necessity for more stringent studies. Future studies should prioritize well-designed prospective designs to strengthen predictive validity, standardize predictor selection and outcome definitions for improved reproducibility, and expand research to underrepresented regions to enhance cross-population generalizability. Additionally, increasing model transparency through open-access ML codes and data repositories, and developing multi-stage, real-time predictive models incorporating both preoperative and postoperative factors, will further optimize clinical decision-making. Refining these models will enable healthcare providers to better identify high-risk patients, implement targeted interventions, and ultimately improve surgical outcomes.

Despite its strengths, this meta-analysis has several limitations. A significant number of the reviewed studies demonstrated elevated bias risks, primarily stemming from their retrospective nature, absence of external validation, and variability in defining outcomes and selecting predictors.Additionally, the AUPRC data limited the comprehensiveness of model performance evaluation. Significant heterogeneity was observed in predictive variables and model construction methods, affecting result consistency. Furthermore, the geographic distribution of studies was imbalanced, with most originating from Asia, Europe, and North America, while Africa and Oceania were underrepresented, potentially limiting the global applicability of the findings.
