LYPLAL1 rare loss-of-function variants in humans and deletion in human hepatoma cells protect against MASLD
Authors: Rawdat Hussain, Chinmay Raut, Ponnandy Prabhu, Akram Irshaid, Yash Hegde, Yue Chen, Asmita Pant, Antonino Oliveri, Xiaochao Wei, Lishi Yin, Tiffany L. Russell, Brian Halligan, Clay F. Semenkovich, Elizabeth K. Speliotes
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Genetic variants near LYPLAL1 are associated with Metabolic dysfunction-Associated Steatotic Liver Disease (MASLD) in humans, but their impact on LYPLAL1 function is unknown. We identified LYPLAL1 loss-of-function variants from UK BioBank (UKBB) whole-exome sequencing data that had AlphaMissense or GPN-MSA scores in the top 20% of LYPLAL1 variants for being disruptive. We aggregated these variants and carried out burden analysis for effects on MRI Proton Density-Fat Fraction (MRI-PDFF) and ICD-based MASLD in UKBB. Rare loss-of-function LYPLAL1 variants were associated with reduced MRI-PDFF and ICD diagnosed MASLD across sexes. We used CRISPR to knockout and overexpress LYPLAL1 in human hepatoma cells (HuH-7), measuring lipid content, lipid uptake/export, and changes in de novo lipogenesis and mitochondrial β-oxidation. LYPLAL1 subcellular localization was determined by overexpressing LYPLAL1-HA tagged protein. We purified GST tagged human LYPLAL1 protein and conducted in vitro tests for esterase and depalmitoylase activity. Knocking out LYPLAL1 reduced triglycerides biochemically as well as lipid intensity after oleic (18:1, n-9) acid treatment. LYPLAL1 KO cells had increased expression of PPARα and MLXIPL, increased mitochondrial β-oxidation, and reduced capacity to both import fatty acids (FAs) and export lipoproteins. Overexpression of LYPLAL1 increased lipid droplet accumulation and decreased PPARα and MLXIPL. LYPLAL1-HA is partly localized to mitochondria when treated with oleic acid. Biochemical analyses showed that LYPLAL1 has strong esterase activity but lacks depalmitoylase activity. Reduction of LYPLAL1 esterase function likely increases β-oxidation of FAs in mitochondria through PPARα and MLXIPL and decreases FA import to protect against lipid accumulation in human liver cancer cells. Together, our results indicate that LYPLAL1 loss-of-function protects against MASLD in Europeans and in vitro, LYPLAL1 is an esterase for short-chain substrates which is involved in the regulation of mitochondrial β-oxidation and uptake of fatty acids, influencing lipid accumulation in the liver.

Metabolic dysfunction-Associated Steatotic Liver Disease (MASLD) is the most common chronic liver disease in the world (1, 2, 3), affecting approximately a third of the global population (1, 2). MASLD can progress from lipid accumulation in the liver to metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis (MASH) (3, 4). In severe cases, MASH can lead to cirrhosis and hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) (3). Current treatment options for MASLD are limited (5) and given its high prevalence, identifying the mechanisms causing MASLD can help develop better treatments to mitigate the effects of this disease.

Genetics is a strong risk factor for MASLD, with studies suggesting a heritability between 25% and 50% for hepatic steatosis (6). Human genome-wide association studies (GWAS) have identified single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) near LYPLAL1 (Lysophospholipase Like 1) that are associated with visceral to subcutaneous adipose tissue (VAT/SAT) ratio (7, 8, 9), waist to hip ratio (WHR) (10, 11, 12), and MASLD (13, 14). Previous studies have also found variants near LYPLAL1 affecting insulin resistance (15, 16, 17) and fasting serum triglyceride (TG) levels (15). However, whether these non-coding SNPs are affecting these metabolic traits through LYPLAL1 is still unclear. We previously demonstrated that female mice lacking Lyplal1 had altered distribution of body fat and lower levels of liver triglycerides (18), suggesting that LYPLAL1 is likely to be the gene regulating lipid metabolism.

To this end, we wanted to explore the cellular mechanisms through which LYPLAL1 disruption contributes to MASLD. Based on sequence homology and structural overlap with acyl protein thioesterase LYPLA1 (Lysophospholipase 1, also known as Acyl-Protein Thioesterase 1 or APT1) (19), LYPLAL1 is thought to play a role in protein acylation (20). Some studies also suggest that LYPLAL1 may be responsible for the depalmitoylation of other proteins such as cyclic GMP-AMP synthase (cGAS) to regulate immune responses (21).

To better understand the role of LYPLAL1 in the development of MASLD, we identified rare coding loss-of-function variants in the gene and determined their effects on MASLD in UK BioBank (UKBB). We employed AlphaMissense (22) and GPN-MSA (23) to identify potential loss-of-function LYPLAL1 variants in European individuals. The effect of these variants on MASLD was determined by burden testing with International Classification of Diseases (ICD) based MASLD and Magnetic Resonance Imaging derived Proton Density Fat Fraction (MRI-PDFF), which recent studies have used for an accurate and reliable measure of liver steatosis (24, 25, 26, 27, 28, 29). Our approach aims to establish a causal relationship between LYPLAL1 loss-of-function and MASLD in humans.

To functionally characterize the impact of LYPLAL1 loss-of-function, we eliminated the protein in human hepatoma (HuH-7) cells using the Clustered Regularly Interspaced Short Palindromic Repeats (CRISPR) system and quantified alterations in intracellular lipid content, triglyceride and cholesterol levels, gene expression changes, fatty acid uptake, lipid export, and mitochondrial β-oxidation. We also overexpressed LYPLAL1 using CRISPR in HuH-7 cells and determined its effects on gene expression and lipid levels. To determine cellular localization, we used immunocytochemistry after overexpression of HA tagged LYPLAL1. Lastly, we purified LYPLAL1 protein and conducted in vitro biochemical analyses to evaluate its esterase and depalmitoylase activity. Together, our results indicate that LYPLAL1 loss-of-function protects against MASLD in Europeans and in vitro, LYPLAL1 is an esterase for short-chain substrates which is involved in the regulation of mitochondrial β-oxidation and uptake of fatty acids, influencing lipid accumulation in the liver.

Materials and methods

Ethics

The Institutional Review Board of the University of Michigan (Ann Arbor, MI) approved all research conducted in this study; use of the UK BioBank (UKBB) data is considered exempt as it is secondary use of data. UKBB protocols were approved by the National Research Ethics Service Committee and all participants provided written informed consent. UKBB analyses in this project were conducted under Resource Project 18120 (to EKS). All study procedures complied with the Declarations of Helsinki and Istanbul.

Analysis of UK BioBank (UKBB) whole-exome sequencing (WES) data

We selected coding LYPLAL1 missense or stop-gain variants as defined by ANNOVAR (30) with a minor allele frequency of less than 5e-5 that had an AlphaMissense (22) or GPN-MSA (23) score for disrupting protein activity in the top 20% of variants from the LYPLAL1 gene. European individuals were genetically determined as previously described by Oliveri et al. (31). Briefly, individuals self-identifying as ‘White British’ with similar genetic ancestry based on principal components and a further subgroup determined by TRACE (32) were included as a part of the study (n = 441,811). We aggregated the LYPLAL1 variants and carried out burden association analysis in European males and females separately for effects on inverse normally transformed UKBB MRI Proton Density Fat Fraction (PDFF) (N = 29,895 males and 31,992 females) and separately in UKBB ICD-based MASLD (ICD-9 571.8 or ICD-10 K76.0) (N = 171,971 males and 207,953 females) that did not overlap with the MRI-PDFF individuals using REGNIE (33) controlling for age, age-squared, the first 20 genetic principal components, obesity status as described in Chen et al. (34), diabetes status, waist-to-hip ratio, alcohol intake in grams per week, and PNPLA3 I148M genotype. We meta-analyzed the results across sexes and MRI-PDFF and MASLD using a fixed effect sample size and direction of effect meta-analysis in METAL (35).

Generation of LYPLAL1 KO cell line

Single guide RNAs were synthesized by Synthego (guide 1: AUCAGCGAUGGCGGCUGCGU; guide 2: GCCAUGCAGGAAGAUCAGAG; guide 3: AUACUGGGGUAACCGAGGGG). HuH-7 cells were transfected with Cas9 protein (Synthego) and 200 ng guide RNA using Lipofectamine CRISPRMAX Cas9 Transfection Reagent kit (Thermo Fisher, #CMAX00003). After growing for a week, single cells were sorted into 96 well plates with a ThermoFisher Bigfoot Spectral Cell Sorter. Knockout of LYPLAL1 was confirmed via sequencing along with RT-qPCR and Western blot analysis.

Overexpression of HA-LYPLAL1 in HuH-7 cells

The cDNA for human LYPLAL1 coding sequence was PCR amplified (FP: 5′-CGCACGCGTGCCACCATGGCGGCTGCGTCG-3′; RP: 5′-CGCGAATTCCTAAGCGTAGTCTGACGTCGTATGGGTACATTTCTCCTGGCAGCTTTGT-3′) and subcloned into the pAT96 (pSMPUW-full EF1alpha-IRES-Bsd) lentivector (gift from the Andrew Tai lab at the University of Michigan). A HA-epitope tag (AYDPVDYPY) was added at the C terminal of the LYPLAL1 cDNA. HEK-293 T cells were used to generate the lentivirus which was incubated with polybrene (8 μg/ml) in HuH-7 cells. Cells stably expressing LYPLAL1-HA were selected with blasticidin. Expression of LYPLAL1-HA was verified by immunoblotting.

Preparation of cell-line with CRISPRa induced overexpression of LYPLAL1

The pXPR_050 vector (Addgene, #96925) was used to clone the guide RNA as described previously (36). Briefly, the vector was digested with BsmBI-V2 (New England Biolabs, #R0739S). The single guide oligo pair used for LYPLAL1 was: 5′-CACCGATTGAAAGCTCCTCAGAGAG-3′ and 5′-AAACCTCTCTGAGGAGCTTTCAATC-3′. Oligos were ligated into the empty vector with T4 DNA ligase (New England Biolabs, #M0202S). The ligated product was transformed into DH5α competent cells (Thermo Fisher, #EC0112). Plasmid DNA was sequenced and packaged into lentivirus. CRISPR Synergistic Activation Mediator (SAM) system integrated HuH-7 cells were generated as described in Sanson et al. (36). Briefly, HuH-7 cells were transduced with lentiviral pXPR_109 (Addgene, catalog no. 61425) to express dCas9. SAM integrated HuH-7 cells were transduced with guide RNA containing lentivirus in the presence of polybrene (8 μg/ml). Cells were selected using blasticidin and puromycin. Overexpression of LYPLAL1 was verified by RT-qPCR.

Lipid droplet staining and image quantification

5,000 cells per well were plated in 96-well plates. 24 h post seeding, different doses (0–1000 μM) of oleic (18:1, n-9) acid (OA) (Sigma, #O1383) were added. After incubating another 24 h, cells were stained with CellMask Orange (Thermo Fisher, #C10045) for cell membrane and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde. After fixing, cells were stained with Hoechst (Thermo Fisher, #62249) for nuclei and LipidTOX™ Green (Thermo Fisher, #H34475) for neutral lipid droplets. Images were acquired with a Nikon Ti2 widefield microscope at 40x magnification. 20 non-overlapping images were taken randomly in each well. Images were then processed through CellPose2 (37) for cell and nuclei segmentation and CellProfilerv4.2.1 (38) for image quality control, lipid droplet segmentation, and extraction of lipid morphology features. Integrated intensity of each cell (minimum of 1,000 cells) was quantified and plotted across oleic acid doses.

Triglyceride assay

Cells were seeded in 6-well plates at a density of 400,000 cells per well. 24 h post seeding, 200 μM OA was added. For etomoxir treatment, control cells received DMSO as vehicle control and treated cells received 100 μM etomoxir (Cayman, #11969). After incubating for 24 h, intracellular triglycerides were extracted using a 3:2 hexane:isopropyl alcohol solution. Samples were dried overnight and the total triglyceride quantity was measured using a Triglyceride Colorimetric Assay Kit (Cayman, #10010303) as per protocol. Results were normalized to total cellular protein determined using the Pierce™ BCA Protein Assay Kit according to the manufacturer’s protocol (Thermo Fisher, #23225).

Cholesterol assay

2 x 106 cells were plated in 10 cm tissue culture plates. 24 h after plating, cells were treated with 200 μM oleic acid (OA) or maintained under control conditions. After 24 more hours, intracellular cholesterol was extracted by homogenizing cell pellets in 200 μl of chloroform:isoporpanol:IGEPAL® CA-630 (Sigma, #I8896) (7:11:0.1). After centrifugation, the organic phase was transferred to new tubes and air dried overnight. Samples were resuspended in 100 μl of Assay Buffer from the Cholesterol Quantification Assay Kit (Sigma, #CS0005) and quantified according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Results were normalized to total cellular protein.

RT-qPCR

RNA was prepared using TRIzol™ (Thermo Fisher, #15596026) and purified by ZYMO RNA Clean and Concentrator Kit (Zymo, #R1017). The iScript™ Reverse Transcription kit (Bio-Rad, #1708891) was used to synthesize cDNA. RT-qPCR was performed using TaqMan® Gene Expression Assay FAM probes with TaqMan® Gene Expression Master Mix (Thermo Fisher, #4369016) following the manufacturer’s protocol. Probes used: GAPDH (Hs02786624_g1), ELF1 (Hs01111177_m1), MLXIPL (Hs00975714_m1), PPARα (Hs00947536_m1), ACACA (Hs01046047_m1), ACLY (Hs00982738_m1), FASN (Hs01005622_m1), SCD (Hs01682761_m1), CPT1A (Hs00912671_m1), ACADL (Hs00155630_m1), ACADM (Hs00936584_m1), ACADVL (Hs00825606_g1), EHHADH (Hs00157347_m1), HADHA (Hs00426191_m1), CD36 (Hs00354519_m1), and FABP1 (Hs00155026_m1).

Protein extraction and immunoblotting

Cellular protein was extracted with radioimmunoprecipitation assay buffer (RIPA) (Thermo Fisher, #PI89900) and Laemmli sample buffer (Bio-Rad, #1610747) was used to prepare lysates, which were size-fractionated by Mini-PROTEAN TGX gels (Bio-Rad, #4561093) and transferred to a PVDF membrane (Sigma, #IPVH85R). Membranes were blocked using 5% skim milk (Bio-Rad, #1706404) and immunoblotted with primary antibodies at 4°C overnight. For secondary antibody, membranes were incubated in horseradish peroxidase-conjugated anti-rabbit IgG (Sigma, #12–348). Membranes were visualized using SuperSignal™ West Pico PLUS Chemiluminescent Substrate (Thermo Fisher, #34580) on a Bio-Rad ChemiDoc MP Imaging System. For stripping and re-probing, Restore™ PLUS stripping buffer was used (Thermo Fisher, #46430). Antibodies used: anti-LYPLAL1 (Sigma, #HPA045806), anti-HA (Cell Signaling, #C29F4), and anti-GAPDH (Cell Signaling, #14C10).

Quantification of expression levels of β-oxidation enzymes

1.5 x 106 cells were plated in 10 cm tissue culture plates. 48 h post seeding, media was changed to add 200 μM OA for oleic acid treated plates. After incubating for another 24 h, the expression of major β-oxidation enzymes ACADVL, ACADM, and HADHA was quantified using the Fatty Acid Oxidation Assay Kit (flow cytometry) (Abcam, #ab118183) as per the manufacturer’s protocol. Fluorescence intensity was measured via a ZE5 Cell Analyzer. Data obtained were analyzed with FlowJo software version 10.10.0. Mean Fluorescence Intensity (MFI) of each cell (minimum 10,000 cells) was quantified by FlowJo and plotted with GraphPad Prism.

Seahorse XF mitochondrial respiration

The Seahorse XF Long Chain Fatty Acid Oxidation Stress Test Kit (Agilent, #103672-100) was used to measure oxygen consumption rate (OCR) in cells. 10,000 cells per well were plated on tissue culture microplates with DMEM and incubated overnight. 24 h later, 200 μM OA was added for oleic acid treated cells. After incubating for another 24 h, OCR was measured according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The following compounds were injected sequentially: etomoxir (4 μM), oligomycin (1.5 μM), FCCP (0.5 μM), and rotenone/antimycin A (0.5 μM). After calibration, OCR measurements were taken every 3 mins for 108 mins. Results were normalized to cell counts via the BioTek Cytation 5 Imager (Agilent).

Immunocytochemistry

Cells were plated on coverslips in a 24-well plate (40,000 cells/well). 24 h later, 200 μM OA was added for oleic acid treated cells. Cells were stained with MitoTracker™ Deep Red (Thermo Fisher, #M22426) and then fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and permeabilized with 0.2% Triton X-100. 5% goat serum was used for blocking. Fixed cells were incubated overnight at 4 °C with anti-HA (Cell Signaling, #C29F4). Goat anti-Rabbit IgG Secondary Antibody, Alexa Fluor™ 488 (Thermo Fisher, #A-11006) was used for secondary antibody incubation. After washing, cells were stained with Hoechst dye. Coverslips were mounted on slides with ProLong™ Gold Antifade Mountant (Thermo Fisher, #P36934). Images were taken at 60x magnification with immersion oil on a Nikon A1 inverted confocal microscope. Images were converted to 8 bit with ImageJ (39) and analyzed with the JACoP plugin for colocalization analysis (40). Manders coefficients for colocalized signal between HA-LYPLAL1 (green) and MitoTracker (purple) channels were calculated based on the same threshold values within the same experiment and the data was plotted as fraction of HA-LYPLAL1 signal that overlapped with MitoTracker signal.

Fatty acid uptake assay

300,000 cells were plated per well in 6-well plates. 48 h post seeding, cells were incubated in serum free DMEM medium for 4 h. After serum starvation, different doses (0–100 μM) of fluorescent C18 fatty acid analogue 12-N-methyl-(7-nitrobenz-2-oxa-1,3-diazo) amino stearic acid (NBD-stearate) (Cayman, #10011299) was added with 1% bovine serum albumin (BSA) (Sigma, #A9647) in DMEM. Cells were incubated with BSA conjugated NBD-stearate for 30 min, trypsinized, collected in microcentrifuge tubes, and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde. Fluorescence intensity was measured using a ZE5 Cell Analyzer. Data obtained was analyzed with FlowJo software version 10.10.0. Mean Fluorescence Intensity (MFI) of each cell (minimum 10,000 cells) was quantified by FlowJo and plotted with GraphPad Prism.

Apolipoprotein B (ApoB) enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

150,000 cells were plated in 12-well plates. 24 h later, media was changed, and 200 μM oleic acid was added to the applicable wells. After another 24 h, cell culture medium was collected, and the ApoB ELISA was performed according to the manufacturer’s protocol (Thermo Fisher, #EH34RB). Results were normalized to total cellular protein.

Purification of LYPLAL1

LYPLAL1 cDNA was cloned into pGEX-4T-1 expression vector (Addgene, #27458001) and transformed into E. coli BL21 competent cells (New England Biolabs, #C2530H). Transformants were grown overnight in 5 ml LB medium containing 50 μg/ml ampicillin and 34 μg/ml chloramphenicol. The overnight culture was diluted in fresh complex medium containing 50 μg/ml ampicillin and grown until mid-log phase (OD600 = 0.4). We induced the culture by adding IPTG (Sigma, #I6758) to a final concentration of 0.5 mM. Cell extracts were prepared using B-PER™ Bacterial Protein Extraction Reagent (Thermo Fisher, #78243). GST-LYPLAL1 fusion protein was purified by using Pierce™ GST Spin Purification Kit (Thermo Fisher, #16107). PreScission Protease thrombin (Cytiva, #27084601) was used to cleave the GST-tag. Expression of the LYPLAL1 protein was confirmed using SDS-PAGE gel electrophoresis and western blotting.

LYPLAL1 esterase activity assay

The release of yellow 4-nitrophenol (PNP) from 4-nitrophenyl acetate (pNPA) and 4-nitrophenyl butyrate (pNPB) was monitored by measuring the increase of absorbance at 405 nm at 37 0C. The reaction was performed with 500 nM of LYPLAL1 enzyme and 500 μM of pNPA and pNPB in assay buffer (20 mM HEPES, 150 mM NaCl, pH 7.5, 0.01% Triton-X 100). The resulting absorbance was measured with a BioTek Synergy HT microplate reader (Agilent).

Expression and purification of recombinant APT1 protein

The recombinant APT1 (LYPLA1) protein was generated as described (41). In brief, an expression vector pGEX-4T-1 containing mouse Lypla1 cDNA was used. GST fusion proteins were produced from E. coli BL21 (DE3) cells induced by IPTG (0.2 mM) and purified by glutathione agarose beads. The GST-tag was cleaved by thrombin.

Depalmitoylase activity assay with DPP3

In vitro depalmitoylation activity was evaluated by a fluorescence reporter DPP3 (42) as described (41). In brief, indicated amounts of recombinant proteins were incubated with DPP3 (5 μM in 20 mM HEPES, pH = 7.4, 150 mM NaCl, 0.1% Triton-X-100 buffer). Fluorescence signals were then measured every 30 s for 40 min.

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as mean ± standard error of the mean (S.E.M). Statistical analysis was carried out with GraphPad Prism version 10.0.0, with statistical significance set at P < 0.05. Statistical analyses included unpaired Student t tests, Mann–Whitney U test, and two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA).

Results

Rare loss-of-function LYPLAL1 variants protected against MASLD in Europeans

To investigate the effects of LYPLAL1 loss-of-function mutations in humans, we used whole-exome sequencing data from UK BioBank (UKBB) on 441,811 European participants (43). The MRI-PDFF cohort consisted of 29,895 males and 31,992 females and with a median age of 66 years (Q1: 60 years, Q3: 72 years) and the ICD-MASLD group consisted of 171,971 males and 207,953 females with a median age of 74 years (Q1: 66 years, Q3: 79 years) (Supplemental Table S1). 35.7% of males had MRI-PDFF of > 5% and 1.71% had ICD-MASLD and 22.2% of females had MRI-PDFF of > 5% and 1.5% had ICD-MASLD. We identified missense or stop-gain variants that had AlphaMissense (22) and GPN-MSA (23) protein disruptive scores in the top 20% of these two predictors, leaving us with a total of 100 LYPLAL1 loss-of-function variants (Fig. 1A and Supplemental Table S2). We aggregated these variants and tested them for association with MRI-PDFF or ICD-based MASLD in UKBB in European men and women separately controlling for age, age-squared, obesity, diabetes status, waist-to-hip ratio, alcohol intake in grams per week, PNPLA3 I148M genotype, and the first 20 principal components. We then meta-analyzed the results of the burden test using sample size and direction of effect fixed effect meta-analysis in METAL (35) (Fig. 1A). We confirmed that there was no heterogeneity of effect across the sexes of the samples. In this sample of individuals, LYPLAL1 loss-of-function reduced PDFF in females with a Z-score of −1.86 (P = 0.050) and in males with a Z-score of −0.95 (P = 0.320) (Fig. 1B). LYPLAL1 loss-of-function reduced ICD-MASLD in females with a Z-score of −0.54 (P = 0.625) and in males with a Z-score of −1.30 (P = 0.184). LYPLAL1 deletion decreased lipid accumulation after treatment with oleic acid (18:1, n-9) in human hepatoma cells

We used CRISPR-Cas9 to induce a deletion in exon 1 of the LYPLAL1 open reading frame that leads to an early stop during protein translation and results in nonsense mediated decay of the LYPLAL1 mRNA to create a single cell-derived knockout (KO) of the gene confirmed by RT-qPCR (Supplemental Fig. S1A) and immunoblotting (Supplemental Fig. S1B). Low levels of RNA can still be detected as this method relies on the introduction of premature stop codon(s) and nonsense mediated decay to reduce protein expression (44). We verified complete loss of LYPLAL1 protein through immunoblotting (Supplemental Fig. S1B). We loaded cells with different doses of oleic (18:1, n-9) acid, hereafter referred to as oleic acid or OA, for 24 h and stained for intracellular lipid droplets with the neutral lipid stain LipidTOX™. We found that LYPLAL1 KO cells had decreased lipid intensity with the difference in intensity between wild-type (WT) and LYPLAL1 KO cells increasing with oleic acid load (Fig. 2A and B). We biochemically measured cellular triglyceride (TG) and cholesterol levels. Treatment with 200 μM oleic acid highlighted the difference, where LYPLAL1 KO cells had significantly decreased TG compared to control cells (Fig. 2C). There were no observable differences in the amount of intracellular cholesterol regardless of oleic acid treatment between LYPLAL1 KO and WT cells (Supplemental Fig. S2). Reduced uptake of long-chain fatty acids (LCFAs) and lipoprotein export in LYPLAL1 knockout cells

Since treatment with oleic acid resulted in greater differences in lipid content between HuH-7 and LYPLAL1 KO cells, we examined whether LYPLAL1 KO cells have a differential ability to take up fatty acids, which would possibly contribute to the observed phenotype. We performed a fatty acid uptake assay using a non-hydrolyzable fluorescent C18 fatty acid (FA) analogue 12-N-methyl-(7-nitrobenz-2-oxa-1,3-diazo) amino stearate (NBD-stearate) as a substrate (45, 46). Cells were treated with different doses of NBD-stearate for 30 min, after which the fluorescence intensity was measured. Cells imported NBD-stearate in a dose-dependent manner (Supplemental Fig. S3). We found that treatment with 100 μM of NBD-stearate showed the highest difference of fluorescence signal in LYPLAL1 KO cells (Fig. 2D), suggesting that LYPLAL1 KO cells have a lower rate of FA uptake. Furthermore, to determine whether the lower levels of FA uptake was mediated by transporter expression, we assayed mRNA and found decreased expression of CD36, a membrane transporter responsible for import of long chain fatty acids (47), and fatty acid-binding protein 1 (FABP1) (Fig. 2E), a fatty acid binding protein that facilitates the intracellular transport of fatty acids (48). Together, our data suggest that LYPLAL1 KO cells exhibit decreased uptake of fatty acids, possibly by affecting the expression of CD36 and FABP1.

Next, we wanted to investigate the effects of LYPLAL1 KO on lipid export. We used Apolipoprotein B (ApoB) as a proxy of cellular lipoprotein secretion (49, 50). We found that after treatment with 200 μM oleic acid, LYPLAL1 KO cells had decreased ApoB secretion compared to controls (Fig. 2F). This indicates lipoprotein export activity is reduced in these knockout cells, possibly because of the low intracellular lipid accumulation in these cells.

LYPLAL1 knockout increases mitochondrial β-oxidation

Since LYPLAL1 KO cells have decreased fatty acid uptake, we wanted to explore if there are other mechanisms contributing to decreased lipid accumulation. We tested the expression of major regulators involved in fatty acids and cholesterol metabolism, such as PPARα, MLXIPL, PPARγ, SREBF1, and SREBF2 (data not shown for every gene). Peroxisome proliferator activated receptor (PPARα) is a major regulator of β-oxidation in the liver (51, 52, 53, 54). MLX-interacting protein-like (MLXIPL), also known as carbohydrate-responsive element-binding protein (ChREBP), is traditionally perceived as a regulator of glucose metabolism and de novo lipogenesis (55, 56, 57, 58, 59), although some studies suggest it may also play a role in controlling β-oxidation (60, 61, 62). Using RT-qPCR, we found that both these metabolic regulators, PPARα and MLXIPL, had increased expression in the LYPLAL1 KO cells (Fig. 3A). Based on this, we examined the expression of target genes of PPARα and MLXIPL (54, 63). We assayed mRNA levels for major de novo lipogenesis genes acetyl-CoA carboxylase alpha (ACACA), ATP citrate lyase (ACLY), fatty acid synthase (FASN), and stearoyl-CoA desaturase (SCD). We only found significant decreases in expression of ACLY and FASN in LYPLAL1 KO cells, but these effects disappeared when treated with 200 μM oleic acid (Fig. 3B). These results suggest there are likely no major changes in de novo lipogenesis in LYPLAL1 KO cells compared to WT cells to explain the difference in lipid content. We then examined the expression of mitochondrial β-oxidation genes. We found that the mRNA levels for carnitine palmitoyltransferase 1A (CPT1A) and long-chain acyl-CoA dehydrogenase (ACADL) were significantly increased in LYPLAL1 KO cells (Fig. 3C). It is important to note that CPT1A catalyzes the rate-limiting step of mitochondrial fatty acid oxidation (64). We also used fluorescent antibodies to quantify protein expression of very long-chain acyl-CoA dehydrogenase (ACADVL), medium-chain acyl-CoA dehydrogenase (ACADM), and hydroxyacyl-CoA dehydrogenase (HADHA) and found that the expression of these proteins was increased in LYPLAL1 KO cells (Fig. 3D). Furthermore, treatment with oleic acid resulted in a relatively modest increase of these mitochondrial β-oxidation genes in HuH-7 cells while significantly increasing expression of CPT1A, ACADL, and ACADM in LYPLAL1 KO cells (Supplemental Fig. S4), further corroborating the role of β-oxidation in LYPLAL1 KO cells. To confirm the hypothesis that increased fatty acid oxidation is protecting against steatosis in LYPLAL1 KO cells, we performed Seahorse analysis to measure mitochondrial respiration in the presence and absence of etomoxir, an inhibitor of CPT1A (64, 65). Adding etomoxir significantly decreased the oxygen consumption rate (OCR) in LYPLAL1 KO cells (Fig. 3E). Oleic acid treatment exacerbated the difference in OCR under maximal respiration conditions (Fig. 3F). At baseline, LYPLAL1 KO cells had a higher OCR than HuH-7 cells at both 0 and 200 μM oleic acid but this difference disappears with the addition of etomoxir (Fig. 3F). This suggests that LYPLAL1 KO cells are more dependent on fatty acid oxidation than HuH-7 cells. Additionally, treatment with 100 μM etomoxir restored intracellular triglyceride accumulation in LYPLAL1 KO cells (Fig. 3G). Taken together, our results suggest that LYPLAL1-deficient cells have increased rates of fatty acid oxidation, which may contribute to lower lipid content in these cells.

LYPLAL1 shows esterase but not depalmitoylase activity

Previous research into the enzymatic function of LYPLAL1 revealed a preference for short chain substrates (19) while other reports suggest that LYPLAL1 can catalyze the depalmitoylation of other proteins (20, 21). To define LYPLAL1 activities, we purified LYPLAL1 protein (Supplemental Fig. S6A and B) and assayed its enzymatic activity in vitro. Using depalmitoylation probe 3 (DPP3), a fluorescent molecule previously characterized as a tool to measure activity of thioesterases (42), we found that APT1, the homolog of LYPLAL1, has strong depalmitoylase activity while LYPLAL1 does not (Fig. 4C). In contrast, APT1 and LYPLAL1 had similar esterase activity using 4-nitrophenyl acetate (pNPA) and 4-nitrophenyl butyrate (pNPB) as substrates (Fig. 4D). We also found that LYPLAL1 displays a preference for pNPA over pNPB where pNPA has a shorter carbon chain (Supplemental Fig. S6C), confirming previous reports (19). Altogether, our results suggest that LYPLAL1 has esterase activity, and it prefers short-chain substrates.

Overexpressing LYPLAL1 increased lipid accumulation in HuH-7 cells

To determine whether overexpression of LYPLAL1 would have the opposite result of knocking out LYPLAL1, we used the CRISPR-mediated transcriptional activation (CRISPRa) system (67). HuH-7 cells expressing dCas9-SAM were used to overexpress LYPLAL1 (LYPLAL1a cell line) (Fig. 5A). We treated LYPLAL1a and control cells with different doses of oleic acid for 24 h and stained intracellular lipid droplets with LipidTOX™. LYPLAL1a cells had elevated lipid levels compared to control cells with increasing oleic acid concentrations (Fig. 5B and C), the opposite phenotype of LYPLAL1 KO cells. As expected, LYPLAL1 overexpression led to significantly decreased mRNA levels for MLXIPL and PPARα with a trending decrease in CPT1A mRNA levels after oleic acid treatment (Fig. 5D), the opposite of LYPLAL1 KO cells. Discussion

Human genetic studies have linked variants near LYPLAL1 to obesity and fat distribution (8, 9, 10, 11, 12). A sex specific effect is observed in humans, with females carrying these variants demonstrating a more significant impact on body fat distribution (8, 9). A limitation of these studies is that they do not establish a causal relationship between LYPLAL1 and the observed phenotypes (68). Multiple independent studies have found conflicting results regarding the functional role of LYPLAL1 in disease. One study found that Lyplal1 was not required for adipocyte differentiation in mice (69) and another had inconclusive evidence to suggest that Lyplal1 was needed for metabolic regulation in mice (70). However, our previous work showed that Lyplal1 KO mice had decreased liver triglycerides and altered body fat distribution when fed a high-fat, high-sucrose (HFHS) diet (18). A recent investigation suggested LYPLAL1 may be involved in hepatic glucose metabolism (71), which could be supported by the increased expression of glucose metabolism regulator MLXIPL in LYPLAL1 KO cells observed in our study. Furthermore, an independent group found that LYPLAL1 mRNA levels were elevated in subcutaneous adipose tissue of obese individuals (72). In a study focusing on sex-specific effects on cardiometabolic traits, a Lyplal1 missense variant (I58T, rs31134248) was associated with body fat percentage in female mice, with bulk RNA sequencing results from Lyplal1 knockout mice showing enrichment for oxidation and mitochondrial function pathways (73). Altogether, these results support that LYPLAL1 may affect fat distribution and possibly liver steatosis and that differences in phenotypes may occur under specific conditions, such as high fat and/or high sugar diets.

Here, we extended our previous work by analyzing the role of LYPLAL1 in human liver cancer cells and in individuals of European descent in the UK BioBank (UKBB) to determine whether it has a causal role in affecting liver steatosis in humans and, if so, to elucidate underlying mechanisms. We used the CRISPR system to knock out and overexpress LYPLAL1 in HuH-7 cells. LYPLAL1 knockout in HuH-7 cells reduced steatosis whereas overexpression increased it. These effects are consistent with what we see in UKBB where loss-of-function variants in LYPLAL1 associate with decreased steatosis as measured using MRI-PDFF and ICD MASLD. Furthermore, these results are consistent with previously published data where we showed that knocking out Lyplal1 in mice reduced hepatic steatosis (18). Taken together, these data highlight the causal relationship between LYPLAL1 and hepatic steatosis.

Several studies have found that different common variants near LYPLAL1 are associated with MASLD (13, 14) and biomarkers of liver damage, such as Alanine Transaminase (ALT) levels (74). To the best of our knowledge, one study looked at functional LYPLAL1 variants between controls and cases of MASLD (75). They found no evidence to suggest LYPLAL1 loss-of-function mutants affected MASLD, likely related to the lack of power due to the small number of variants in the study. We utilized large scale whole exome sequencing data, and we are the first group to analyze the effects of LYPLAL1 loss-of-function variants in MRI-PDFF, which provides a robust and quantitative measure of liver fat (24). Our results showed that loss-of-function LYPLAL1 variants had a protective effect in European individuals against MASLD, suggesting that LYPLAL1 is causally related to promoting MASLD in humans. Additionally, we showed that using predictors such as AlphaMissense and GPN-MSA may help identify novel rare damaging genetic variants across multiple phenotypes.

We used CRISPR systems to both knock out and overexpress LYPLAL1 in human liver cancer cells. We found that deletion of LYPLAL1 reduced lipid accumulation while overexpression increased lipid accumulation when treated with oleic acid. The expression of major liver metabolic regulators MLXIPL and PPARα and rate-limiting regulator of mitochondrial β-oxidation CPT1A was increased in LYPLAL1 KO cells and decreased in LYPLAL1 overexpressing cells. MLXIPL, also known as ChREBP, is a transcription factor that is activated in a glucose dependent manner and is a regulator of glucose flux and de novo lipogenesis (55, 57). Chronic MLXIPL activation in the liver can promote excessive lipid accumulation (59). Interestingly, a recent study suggested that MLXIPL may act together with PPARα to regulate β-oxidation of fatty acids by promoting the expression of genes such as CPT1A, ACADL, ACADM, ACOX1, and EHHADH (62). The transcription factor PPARα is a major modulator of β-oxidation amongst other metabolic pathways by targeting mitochondrial and peroxisomal β-oxidation genes (51, 52, 53, 54). We found that loss of LYPLAL1 results in the upregulation of MLXIPL and PPARα which likely promotes β-oxidation of fatty acids through CPT1A and other mediators of mitochondrial β-oxidation, providing new insights into how LYPLAL1 affects steatosis.

Since our previous study demonstrated that mice lacking LYPLAL1 had decreased steatosis compared to control mice on a high fat diet (18), we challenged cells with oleic acid to mimic a lipid rich diet. We found that oleic acid treatment exacerbated the difference in lipid content between control and LYPLAL1 KO cells. The observed phenotype may be partially explained by the fact that LYPLAL1 KO cells imported less exogenous fatty acids over time compared to HuH-7 cells. We demonstrated that the long-chain fatty acid (LCFA) transporter CD36 had lower expression in LYPLAL1 KO cells, which would also suggest lower uptake of long-chain FAs in LYPLAL1 KO cells. Taken together, our data suggest that one mechanism by which LYPLAL1 KO cells protect against steatosis in human liver cells is by blocking the intake of exogenous fatty acids.

To determine whether reduced lipid accumulation in LYPLAL1 KO cells is due to the promotion of CPT1A mediated mitochondrial β-oxidation of fatty acids, we measured mitochondrial respiration with Seahorse and intracellular triglycerides after treating cells with the chemical compound etomoxir, a well-known inhibitor of CPT1A (64, 65). We found that LYPLAL1 KO cells had an increase in triglycerides when treated with etomoxir compared to vehicle treated cells, whereas HuH-7 cells did not show significant differences when treated with etomoxir. In Seahorse experiments, we found that mitochondrial respiration was affected more in etomoxir-treated LYPLAL1 KO cells than control-treated LYPLAL1 KO cells. These data suggest that increased fatty acid oxidation protects against lipid accumulation in LYPLAL1 KO liver cells in part through CPT1A activity. We additionally observed decreased lipoprotein secretion in LYPLAL1 KO cells after oleic acid treatment. This is possibly due to the lower intracellular lipid accumulation in these cells. Altogether, our data suggests LYPLAL1 KO cells have reduced capability to import exogenous fatty acids while promoting the breakdown of endogenous stores of fatty acids through mitochondrial β-oxidation. Our in vitro experiments are congruent with our human population data, both showing that LYPLAL1 loss-of-function is protective against MASLD.

Our results provide novel insights into some of the downstream mechanisms of LYPLAL1 that regulate metabolic pathways, and we extended our search to decipher the biochemical function of LYPLAL1. Previous studies have claimed LYPLAL1 is responsible for the depalmitoylation of other proteins such as cGAS (21) or regulation of the localization of membrane big potassium channels (BK) (20). The dimensions of the active site of LYPLAL1 in its crystal structure suggest that LYPLAL1 is unable to bind long chain substrates such as long chain fatty acids (19, 71). In our study, we found that LYPLAL1 has esterase activity and prefers shorter substrates, consistent with what others have shown (19). We also found that LYPLAL1 does not have depalmitoylase activity whereas its family member, APT1, does. Structural studies showed that APT1 has a large active site capable of fitting longer substrates such as palmitate (76) whereas LYPLAL1 does not (19). Therefore, our results suggest that LYPLAL1 acts as an esterase to cause its effects.

The subcellular localization of LYPLAL1 was poorly understood. Several studies have investigated the localization of APT1 (66, 76, 77, 78, 79). Initial reports suggested that APT1 is in the cytosol and regulates Ras localization (79). Kathayat et al. recently showed that APT1 is mainly located in mitochondria (66), although the precise localization may vary between cell types and conditions (76). Similar to our findings, a recent study investigating the mitochondrial proteome identified LYPLAL1 protein in human mitochondrial extracts (80). We found that LYPLAL1-HA is abundantly present in mitochondria with oleic acid treatment. Whether the localization of LYPLAL1 affects the protein’s catalytic function is not known, but it is possible that LYPLAL1 acts as an esterase for its mitochondrial targets.

Our study had some limitations. Our work was done in human liver cancer cells, which could have altered metabolic pathways and are more reliant on glycolysis (81, 82) than normal hepatocytes. HuH-7 cells are homozygous for the PNPLA3 I148M mutant allele, which causes excessive lipid accumulation (83, 84), but whether this affected our results is not known. Experiments in this study were done in oleic acid-loaded conditions, and other treatments may induce different mechanisms of action of LYPLAL1. For instance, a recent study in HepaRG cells stimulated with insulin showed that LYPLAL1 knockdown increased lipid accumulation in cells (85). The biological mechanisms behind the conflicting results are still unclear, although they are likely due to differences in cell lines and culture conditions. Localization of LYPLAL1 was determined by overexpressing HA-tagged LYPLAL1 instead of staining for endogenous protein due to nonspecific binding observed when using antibodies probing for LYPLAL1. Lastly, our analyses in human populations were done mostly in individuals of European ancestry, while HuH-7 cells are derived from a patient of Japanese descent (86), and thus the results need to be tested for generalizability in other ancestries.

Our study had multiple strengths. We combined human population-based analyses with in vitro studies in human liver cancer cells, which increases the generalizability of our findings. We validated the finding that LYPLAL1 may have stronger effects in females than males. We created knockout cells to cleanly assess the effects of this gene on cellular processes. We found that LYPLAL1 deficiency decreases triglycerides and lipids in a cell-autonomous manner. We also found that fatty acid uptake was reduced, and β-oxidation of fatty acids increased to reduce cellular lipids with LYPLAL1 deficiency, thus identifying new mechanisms by which this protein may impact physiology. We also demonstrated that the protein is an esterase but not a depalmitoylase, clarifying its biochemical function, and localized it to mitochondria, which may help identify mitochondrial targets. Overall, our work advances our understanding of how loss of LYPLAL1 can safely protect against diet-induced MASLD, moving us closer to possibly targeting this gene for therapeutic intervention of MASLD.
