Influence of light levels and predator characteristics on the functional response of the European harvestman (Opiliones: Phalangiidae)
Authors: Hannah E Stowe, Stella Uiterwaal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSubject Editor: Rachid HannaPredatory interactions form food webs and shape ecosystems. Such predator–prey relationships underlie the survival, fitness, and landscape utilization of predatory arthropods. Synanthropic arthropod species thrive in agroecosystems and in other landscapes transformed by human activity. Understanding the characteristics of synanthropic arthropod predator–prey relations is crucial to understanding and mitigating their impacts in nonnative ranges. One way to quantify predation is via the functional responses, which describe the strength and nature of predator–prey interactions. Predator traits and environmental conditions can influence functional responses, the resulting energy and nutrient flows, and ultimately population dynamics. Here, we present the first functional response quantification for the European harvestman (Phalangium opilio L.), an introduced opilionid species in the United States. We explore how predator sex, body size, leg characteristics, and environmental light levels influence foraging rates of wild-caught P. opilio on midge prey. We find that P. opilio exhibits a type II functional response on midges, and that predator sex, environmental light conditions, and many continuous predator characteristics such as body size and leg length do not influence functional response parameters. The number of legs missing also did not influence functional response parameters. Our results show that foraging rates in this species are primarily driven by prey density, while many classic determinants of interaction strengths do not appear to alter foraging. Our results improve our understanding of the role of nonnative predators in food webs and highlight the need for species-specific functional response experiments to quantify interaction strengths between novel predators and native prey.Keywords: predation ecology, predator-prey relations, feeding behavior, Chironomidae, Arthropod predation

Introduction

Predation is a crucial ecosystem service in many human-altered landscapes (Tschumi et al. 2018) and the relationship between predator traits, their environmental context, and the efficacy of predation services is increasingly important as we face ecological uncertainty with the changing climate. Predator–prey interactions construct and shape food webs (Forrester et al. 1999, Ayal 2007), drive population dynamics (Gese and Knowlton 2001, Pettorelli et al. 2011), and influence physical and resource landscapes (Ripple and Beschta 2012, Monk and Schmitz 2022). The strength of predator-prey interaction helps to determine resource flow within food webs (Ruiter 1995, Wootton and Emmerson 2005). Predatory behavior then can be understood as a foundation of ecosystems and the energy and nutrient flow within them (Rigler 1975, Hairston and Hairston 1993). Studying predation in nonnative populations can therefore increase our understanding of their success in anthropocentric landscapes and potentially illustrate how human influences on the landscape alter communities and ecosystems.

One common method to describe the strength of predator-prey interactions is the functional response (Holling 1959), which quantifies how predation rates change with prey density. Although the functional response can take several characteristic shapes, the most common is the type II functional response, or the saturating functional response (Holling 1959, DeLong 2021),

where the per capita foraging rate (fpc) is captured by the space clearance rate (a), resource density (R), and handling time (h). The space clearance rate represents how quickly predators can find prey, while handling time describes processing time per prey individual. The type II functional response is asymptotic because the handling time becomes limiting at higher prey density. In other words, the maximum foraging rate is set by the (inverse of) handling time. In cases where handling time is essentially zero, the type II functional response collapses into a linear, type I response.

Because environmental conditions and predator and prey characteristics can alter the shape of the functional response (Gilioli et al. 2005, Kalyebi et al. 2005, Hammill et al. 2010), these variables are crucially important to understanding the nature and function of ecosystems. These variables can be both inherent to the organisms (ie body size, sensory capability) (Claessen et al. 2002, Uiterwaal et al. 2017), or environmental (ie ambient light levels) (Wells 1978, Tablado et al. 2014). Greater body size often increases space clearance rate due to higher mobility or greater sensory range (McGill and Mittelbach 2006) and decreases handling time due to a greater size differential between a predator and its prey. Similarly, greater leg length can increase predator movement, increasing the prey encounter rate (Willemart et al. 2009, Costa et al. 2016). The number of legs missing may conversely decrease space clearance rate or increase handling time. Body condition, which is reflective of energetic state, can also affect foraging (Uiterwaal and DeLong 2019). Environmental light conditions may intersect with the sensory biology of both the predator and prey, their diel activity norms, phototaxis (McCann et al. 1965, Willemart et al. 2009, Pallottini et al. 2024), movement speed, and predation (South et al. 2017) or other behavior (Townsend and Risebrow 1982).

Because the functional response is mediated by the morphology, behavior, and environmental context of the predator and prey, evaluating the functional response of diverse predator-prey pairs can provide insight into the process and function of predation. However, many studies use commonly reared and studied model systems (Hunter 2008, Uiterwaal et al. 2022) leading to a dearth of studies on some predators (Mammola et al. 2021, Allen et al. 2022), while the functional responses of others remain completely unquantified. Daddy-long-legs or harvestmen (Arachnida: Opiliones) are one such predator that lacks this foundational predation research. Unlike other arachnids, prey detection in harvestmen is thought to occur primarily through physical contact with the leg tips (Willemart et al. 2009), though some species can detect food via olfaction (Costa and Willemart 2013, Costa et al. 2016). Harvestmen body plans favor very long legs relative to their body size, extreme subdivision in their legs allowing whiplike movements of the foot tip (Wolff et al. 2019), and they can shed multiple legs with little impact on locomotion (Escalante et al. 2020). Further, the first and second pair of legs (legs I and II on both sides) are thought to serve a primarily sensory function (Pinto-da-Rocha et al. 2007, Willemart et al. 2009), leg I is held forward and leg II held up and forward as the harvestman navigates its environment. Harvestmen are ubiquitous throughout many regions (Dennis et al. 2001, Vink et al. 2004, Merino-Sáinz and Anadón 2018) around the world and persist both in remnant natural landscapes (Merino-Sáinz and Anadón 2018, Stašiov et al. 2021) and in increasingly interspersed human-altered fields, notably in many agricultural land use types (Vink et al. 2004, Merino-Sáinz and Anadón 2018, Stašiov et al. 2011, 2020). The introduced European harvestman (Phalangium opilio L.; Opiliones: Phalangiidae) is one such species that thrives in more intensively managed landscapes (Van de Poel 2016). Their persistence across diverse undisturbed and disturbed ecosystems indicates a high degree of adaptation to the Anthropocene.

As predators of various insects, arachnids, and other invertebrates, P. opilio holds potential for biological control services (Nyffeler and Symondson 2001, Allard and Yeargan 2005a) including in agricultural systems (Sivasubramaniam et al. 1997, Halaj and Cady 2000, Pfannenstiel and Yeargan 2002). Nonbiting midges (Diptera, Chironomidae) are a ubiquitous and a common nuisance pest to humans, especially around water systems where their populations can reach enormous numbers (Ali 1980, Roberts 2003). Because swarming nonbiting midges are phototactic (Pallottini et al. 2024), when reproductive swarms emerge they can converge on human lights and cause significant disruption and discomfort. Insect population pulses are important food sources for birds (Haney 1999, Hogstad 2005), and can increase soil microbial biomass and other nutrient availability (Carlton and Goldman 1984, Yang 2004). Such a glut of resources likely benefits the populations of local consumers, creating an ecological boom in these food webs. The movement of these midges from aquatic systems to the surrounding terrestrial environment can provide an important trophic resource for predators such as harvestmen, and these predator-prey interactions underlie critical aquatic food web linkages (Nakano and Murakami 2001, Sullivan and Manning 2019). Understanding the functional response of the widespread P. opilio can thus not only provide insight into the predator behavior of an understudied species but also provide valuable insights into the nutrient and energy flows produced by predation on midges.

Here, we explored how predator characteristics such as body size, leg length, and leg number, as well as ambient light levels altered the functional response parameters of wild-caught P. opilio harvestmen on commonly encountered midge prey. We selected this prey taxon because harvestmen readily ate midges in the area, and it is likely that midges form a significant proportion of the predator’s diet during this time of the year. To our knowledge, this trophic interaction is not documented elsewhere but has the potential to support the wide-spread success of harvestman populations and contribute to midge population control. We tested the effects of body length, inherent leg length (length of leg II irrespective of missing legs), and remaining leg length (average of leg II lengths on both sides, this latter is more affected by missing legs in the leg II position), size ratio (body length/inherent leg length), and light condition (dark vs light) on functional response parameters for harvestmen. We expected that larger harvestmen, harvestmen with longer legs, and harvestmen with higher body size ratio would have higher space clearance rate and lower handling times, and that harvestmen foraging under high light levels would display higher handling time due to the higher activity of the midges in light conditions.

Methods

We hand-collected P. opilio harvestman (n = 96, 18 males, 78 females) (Dindal 1990) from foliage approximately 0.5 km south of Lake Ogallala, Nebraska, United States. We isolated the predators in individual 120 ml plastic vials at approximately 23 °C and with a field approximate photoperiod of 15:9 h (L:D) during the experimental runtime (23 to 25 June 2024). We provided water using a dampened cotton ball and kept predators for at least 12 h before experimental trials.

We collected nonbiting midges from mating aggregations on the southern shore of Lake Ogallala (41.2098, −101.6481), gently immobilizing a subset and measured their length to the nearest millimeter from head to abdomen tip (n = 20, mean = 0.54 cm, SD = 0.05 cm). We held them at the same conditions as the predators for no longer than 24 h prior to feeding trials to ensure their behavior and nutritional quality did not decline prior to feeding.

We performed nonreplacement predation trials in circular plastic foraging arenas (9 cm high×12.5 cm radius) at 23 ± 2 °C. To assess the impact of light on foraging, we used dark (whole arena covered) or light (arena uncovered) treatments to test how ambient levels of natural daylight influence functional response parameters. All trials were conducted between 10:00 and 15:00 to coincide with plentiful indirect daylight in the trial space. We varied initial prey density (2, 4, 8, 16, or 32 midges per arena) assigned to each predation trial and randomized timing of trials during the day to mitigate effects of time of day and environmental variables on predator behavior. We added midge prey to arenas first to allow for dispersal, then added on predator to each trial arena. Predators were allowed to forage undisturbed for 1 h. After 1 h, we removed harvestmen from their trials and counted all remaining living midges. We randomly assigned individual predators to treatments.

Twenty-four hours after the completion of these trials, we gently pressed each harvestman flat between clear plastic and photographed them. We then measured body length and leg length and recorded missing leg number and position from the photo using ImageJ 1.54g (Schneider et al. 2012). We measured the body length of each predator from the tip of the abdomen to the base of the chelicera, and leg length from coxa to tarsal tip for the second pair of walking legs due to the important biological and sensory role of these appendages. We then used these measurements to calculate the body size ratio (body length/length of leg II) for each predator as a measure of body condition (Uiterwaal and DeLong 2019).

We fit the data on prey density, light vs dark conditions, and predator characteristics in Matlab (r2024a) using nonlinear least squares model fitting with 1,000 bootstrap resampling. For analyses of the effects of light vs dark conditions and predator characteristics, we focused on females only. We did not replenish consumed prey during feeding trials to minimize disturbance and prevent midge escape. For categorical variables, we fit functional response curves separately to each dataset and looked for significant differences between parameter estimates. For continuous variables, we modified the Roger’s Random Predator equation with power functions in place of a (aoMθ) or h (hoMθ), where M is the continuous variable (ie body size, leg length, sense leg [leg II] length, or body size-leg length ratio) and θ is the scaling exponent, and ao and ho are the value of a or h, respectively, when θ is equal to 1. This evaluates the nonlinear power law influence of the continuous variables tested on space clearance rate and handling time (Thompson 1975, Uiterwaal and DeLong 2020). Here, we looked for an estimate for θ for which confidence intervals did not overlap with 0 to indicate a power law effect of the trait (Rall et al. 2012, DeLong 2014) influencing the functional response parameters. To determine which size metric best describes foraging variation, we used AIC (Akaike Information Criterion), taking the model with the lowest AIC as the best supported model (Burnham and Anderson 2004).

Results

Of the collected harvestmen, 57 individuals had all 8 walking legs, 27 were missing 1 leg, and 12 were missing 2 walking legs. We found no individuals with fewer than 6 walking legs. Mean harvestmen body length was 0.71 ± 0.11 cm and mean = length of leg II was 3.64 ± 0.62 cm. Females had larger body lengths than males (female = 0.74 ± 0.1 cm, male = 0.58 ± 0.06 cm, t(93) = −6.21, P < 0.001) and had shorter legs (female = 3.56 ± 0.44 cm, male = 4.23 ± 0.32 cm, t(93) = 6.07, P < 0.001).

With all trials combined, harvestmen showed a type II functional response when foraging on midge prey (Fig. 1, a = 0.013 arenas/min, 95% CI, 0.008 to 0.017, h = 2.41 min/prey, 95% CI, 0.749 to 4.895, R2 = 0.91). Female space clearance rate (a = 0.012 arenas/min, 95% CI, 0.011 to 0.020, R2 = 0.93) and handling time (h = 1.61 min/prey, 95% CI, 0.377 to 3.83, R2 = 0.93) did not significantly differ from males (a = 0.005 arenas/min, 95% CI, 0.0009 to 0.031, h = 7.92 min/prey, 95% CI, 2.22E-14 to 35.233, R2 = 0.55) as the CIs overlapped (Fig. 2). Dark (a = 0.013 arenas/min, 95% CI, 0.012 to 0.029, h = 1.38 min/prey, 95% CI, 2.62E-14 to 4.58, R2 = 0.89) and light (a = 0.012 arenas/min, 95% CI, 0.008 to 0.017, h = 1.91 min/prey, 95% CI, 3.09E-14 to 6.14, R2 = 0.85) ­conditions did not significantly alter the functional response parameters (Fig. 3). Finally, the number of missing legs also did not significantly alter functional response parameters (Fig. 4) [1 (a = 0.013 arenas/min, 95% CI, 0.011 to 0.026, h = 0.7 min/prey, 95% CI, 2.44E-14 to 4.8, R2 = 0.8); 2 (a = 0.013 arenas/min, 95% CI, 0.006 to 0.08, h = 1.14 min/prey, 95% CI, 2.23E-14 to 5.5, R2 = 0.86); or zero missing legs (a = 0.013 arenas/min, 95% CI, 0.008 to 0.029, h = 1.9 min/prey, 95% CI, 2.79E-14 to 6.36, R2 = 0.9)]. Continuous variables (body length, inherent leg length-length of leg II, remaining leg length, and body size ratio) similarly had nonsignificant effects on space clearance rate or handling time (Fig. 5, Table 1). Of the 8 functional response models which included some metric of body size, the model which included an effect of remaining leg (leg II) on space clearance rate was best supported by AIC (Table 1).

Discussion

Handling time of harvestmen predators on midge prey was greater than zero, indicating a type II functional response. Thus, foraging rates will be saturated at high prey densities, such as those that occur during mass emergences. Midges (Diptera: Chironomidae) emerge seasonally surrounding bodies of water and form large aggregates throughout widespread regions, representing significant energy and nutrient shifting from aquatic to terrestrial ecosystems. Although the aggregations formed by midge are primarily reproductive congregations (Kon 1987, Tokeshi and Reinhardt 1996), the sheer volume of emergence also likely provides protection from predation by diluting the threat of predatory community members (Parrish and Edelstein-Keshet 1999, Armitage et al. 2012). Indeed, in type II functional responses, predation risk for individual prey decreases as prey density increases and predator foraging rates asymptote.

Arthropod dietary resource needs differ by sex, with reproductive demands in females outpacing that of males (Lease and Wolf 2011) due to energetic provisioning of eggs (Zhou et al. 1995) exemplified by high lipid content in female reproductive structures (Grapes et al. 1989). These resource differences manifest in foraging and predatory behavioral differences in arachnids (Givens 1978, Walker and Rypstra 2002). Although sex differences in arachnid functional responses are documented in other taxa (Coblentz et al. 2022), we did not detect any significant differences between the functional response parameters of females and males in this system. However, our sample size for males was relatively small, since 81% of collected harvestmen were female. As this is the first functional response estimation for this species, we recommend future work explore foraging in males in more detail. We did observe a significant sex difference in overall body size and leg length, likely a result of egg production optimization in female arthropods (Honěk 1993, Bauerfeind and Fischer 2008, Berger et al. 2012) via body size increases and potentially mobility or dispersal optimization in adult males for mate seeking (Lawrence 1987, Wickman and Rutowski 1999, McLean et al. 2018) via longer leg lengths. It is also important to note that the abstracted foraging arena environment and predator isolation could reduce the performance of nonpredatory behavior such as mate searching or courtship, which often differs between the sexes (Cassettari and Machado 2022). The removal of these behaviors could obfuscate sexual differences in field behavior that may influence foraging and predation rates.

Light and dark foraging conditions did not alter the functional response parameters for female harvestmen. Because swarming nonbiting midges are phototactic (Pallottini et al. 2024), we had expected faster or more frequent prey movements under light conditions to increase space clearance rate for the harvestman predators. However, it is possible that the behavior of the predators under different light conditions counteracted any increase in prey speed. Harvestman predators’ own predation defenses could have reduced their movement frequency and speed in response to perceived higher exposure under light conditions (Mougeot and Bretagnolle 2000, Viherluoto and Viitasalo 2001), as can occur with organisms that experience predation from visual predators (Jermacz and Kobak 2022). Indeed, some harvestmen display negative phototaxis (Willemart et al. 2009), though P. opilio is active diurnally and all predators included in the study were captured during daylight hours. However, in soybeans, the species’ activity peaks occur between 21:00-01:00 and 21:00-04:00 for adult females and adult males, respectively (Allard and Yeargan 2005b). It may be then that diel activity peaks are more influential on predator behavior than light levels alone. Predation increases with light in some systems (Mcmunn et al. 2019), but since harvestman visual systems are not well adapted for image processing, brighter light conditions are not likely to improve their searching ability or consumption rate directly. Prey recognition in these taxa depends on contact chemoreception (Willemart et al. 2009) rather than visual recognition, so direct prey contact is likely necessary for successful predation. Because harvestmen also exist under predation threat, their functional responses under these lighting conditions may result more from their role as a trophic resource rather than differences driven by consumer behavior.

We expected larger predators to forage at a higher rate than smaller individuals (Ball et al. 2015, Costa et al. 2016, Schröder et al. 2016). However, our data show no effect of body size or leg length on foraging rate. Body size and the differential between predator and prey body size is often hugely important for predation and functional response parameters (Ball et al. 2015, Uiterwaal et al. 2017), as the size differential between predator and prey will greatly influence both detectability of the prey and the time to overpower and consume prey. Size likely is also an important consideration for P. opilio, as they lack large envenomation structures and offensive toxins of many arachnid predators (Pinto-da-Rocha et al 2007, Enciso et al. 2025), suggesting that smaller, easier-to- overpower prey may be more suitable. In addition to inherent body size, body size ration evaluates both size and condition for P. opilio but this too lacked effects on foraging rate. It may be that the predator-prey size differential was already so large here that predator body size variation and condition did not have any appreciable impact on functional response parameters. In other words, all P. opilio tested were already so much larger than the midge prey items that space clearance rate and handling time was only negligibly affected by the predator’s body size.

We also expected longer legs to increase the foraging rate relative to shorter legged individuals. Leg length has previously been implicated in capture success for harvestmen (Costa et al. 2016) mostly attributed to detection difference with some reservations (Willemart et al. 2009) and indications that short-ranged olfaction also plays a role (Merfield et al. 2004, Willemart and Chelini 2007), though the scale of this detection varies by species (Costa et al. 2016). Since these harvestmen were collected from leaf litter and plants, the emptiness of the trial arena may help explain the lack of observed benefit of longer legs. Longer legs may be of limited value in crowded microhabitats or up against obstacles as has been suggested by Costa et al. (2016). However, the whiplike tarsi of Opiliones are mechanistically and behaviorally similar to those used by other arthropods to entangle and secure prey items, for example, Chilopoda or centipedes (Lewis 1981) so it is possible that they are important to harvestman capture success. Hyperflexion or curling of the tarsal tip improves traction and climbing ability (Wolff et al. 2019) in harvestmen, and this gripping function could similarly improve prey capture. Although the effect was nonsignificant, among all the variables tested, remaining leg length’s influence on space clearance rate explained variation better than any other continuous variable. This could be a fruitful direction for future work with this species. The lack of significant effect of leg length here contrasts with our current understanding of the role of Opiliones’ leg II and whiplike tarsi and indicates that research is critically needed to elucidate the role of this tarsal adaptation of P. opilio and harvestmen in general.

The number of missing legs also did not alter the foraging rate even when missing leg II which are thought to be important to sensory behavior in this species (Willemart et al. 2009, Wijnhoven 2013, Gainett et al. 2017). Autotomy, or voluntary appendage loss, is well documented in harvestmen and indeed appears to carry relatively low fitness cost in terms of survival or reproduction (Houghton et al. 2011, Escalante and O’Brien 2024), though some mobility affects have been documented (Guffey 1998, 1999). A little over half of all harvestmen collected for this work were missing zero legs (59%), and none were missing more than 2 legs. Although the proportion of harvestman missing zero legs appears about comparable (Guffey 1999) or slightly lower (Houghton et al. 2011) than other reports, no harvestmen were collected with fewer than 6 legs. It would seem then that this species either loses legs less often than other member of its suborder, Eupnoi (Escalante and O’Brien 2024), or faces greater mortality risk after legs have been lost and this risk increases greatly past the loss of the second leg. Taking this into account with the lack of impact on foraging ability documented here, we suggest that survival is not greatly altered by autotomy if 2 or fewer legs are lost, but we cannot rule out that negative impacts occur if more than two legs are lost.

The ubiquity of harvestmen across diverse natural and human-altered landscapes and the relative dearth of information on their predatory contribution within the ecosystem makes studies of their predator–prey interaction strengths crucial to our understanding of human-altered ecological systems. The predatory relationship between harvestman and midges represents just one of the food web connections supported by midge emergence. Trophic transfers from aquatic environments to the surrounding terrestrial ecosystems are common and characteristic of certain organisms, especially insects. Understanding the predatory behavior of terrestrial predators in response to this prey resource influx can reveal important trophic dynamics as well as exploring avenues to help mitigate nuisance effects of such massive emergences to humans. Little is known about the diet breadth of this harvestman species, however other prey types would yield unique functional response results. Larger or more dangerous prey could alter the willingness to attack; faster prey may be encountered more often but be better equipped for escape. Investigating these individual predator-prey relationships can help reveal more about the predation behavior of this species. Further work is necessary to characterize these predation relationships and better understand the role and importance of this predator in the ecosystem. Finally, evaluating diverse predator-prey systems provides necessary insights into the fundamentals of trophic interactions. Harvestmen are alarmingly understudied given their population sizes and their widespread existence across many ecosystems. The Opiliones taxon broadly, and the species under study, in particular, is widely present across the globe, likely due to its association with human-altered landscapes. Such widespread presence in anthropogenic landscapes speaks to their importance in ecological communities, with their predatory behavior contributing to food web structures around the world. Describing these relatively recent novel connections in food webs is crucial to our understanding of contemporary communities.
