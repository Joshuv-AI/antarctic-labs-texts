Extracellular vesicles derived from hADSCs rescue acute pancreatitis by carrying p-STK3 to regulate Treg differentiation
Authors: Rongli Xie, Guohui Xiao, Kaige Yang, Xiaofeng Wang, Cong Chen, Tong Zhou, Ying Chen, Erzhen Chen, Zhitao Yang, Jian Fei, Dan Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummarySevere acute pancreatitis (SAP) is a complex inflammatory disorder with severe immune imbalance. This study investigates the therapeutic potential of extracellular vesicles derived from human adipose mesenchymal stem cells (hADSC-EVs) in modulating Treg differentiation and alleviating SAP. We conducted a phosphoproteomics analysis to evaluate phosphorylation levels, and administered hADSC-EVs in a mouse model of SAP and assessed their impact on Treg differentiation. Phosphoproteomics revealed a significant increase in p-STK3 following hADSC-EVs treatment, restoring Foxp3 level diminished by STK3 knockdown. HADSC-EVs promoted Treg differentiation in a concentration-dependent manner by targeting Foxp3 transcription. In the SAP mouse model, hADSC-EVs improved survival rates and mitigated histopathological alterations. In conclusion, our study revealed that STK3 effectively promotes Treg differentiation and enhances their immunosuppressive capabilities, thereby ameliorating inflammation and attenuating the pathological phenotypes associated with SAP. These findings provide valuable insights into the potential role of hADSC-EVs in regulating immune responses and promoting tissue repair.

Introduction

Acute pancreatitis (AP) is a multifaceted inflammatory disorder primarily confined to the pancreas in its early stages. It sets off a cascade of events characterized by the activation of pancreatic enzymes, infiltration of immune cells, and the release of pro-inflammatory mediators.1 As the condition advances to a more severe form, known as severe AP (SAP), it can lead to systemic complications that affect multiple organ systems, significantly increasing morbidity and mortality rates due to the absence of effective preventive and therapeutic interventions.2,3 Pathologically, SAP is characterized by the destruction of pancreatic acinar cells and commonly leads to pancreatic necrosis and systemic inflammation.4,5 Current treatment options for SAP, such as immune therapy and hematopoietic stem cell transplantation, exhibit limitations in terms of efficacy and side effects.6,7,8 Therefore, there is an urgent need for better therapeutic strategies that can modulate the immune response in patients with SAP, target the inflammatory responses, and prevent the progression of this devastating condition.

T cells, particularly the imbalance in Treg/Th2 and Th1/Th17 subsets, are a key factor in exacerbating organ injury in AP.9 Tregs, a subset of CD4+ T cells, play a crucial role in maintaining immune homeostasis and reducing inflammation in AP.10,11 Increasing the differentiation of naive CD4+ T cells into Tregs can improve the treatment outcomes in AP by restoring immune balance and attenuating inflammatory responses.10,12,13 However, the role of Tregs in pancreatitis remains uncertain since recent data suggest that Tregs may have a detrimental effect in pancreatitis,12 reflecting the complexity of the role of the immune system in SAP and the need for additional studies.

Extracellular vesicles (EVs) are nanoscale secretory vesicles released by cells capable of transporting a variety of bioactive molecules,14 including proteins (such as phosphoproteins), enzymes, lipids, and genetic materials.15 These vesicles facilitate intercellular communication and modulate various biological processes, including immune responses and inflammation.16 EVs derived from human adipose mesenchymal stem cells (hADSC-EVs) possess anti-inflammatory properties and can modulate immune cell functions, cytokine release, and inflammatory signaling pathways, particularly by driving the differentiation of Tregs, which play a crucial role in maintaining immune homeostasis and reducing inflammation in AP.17 In the context of acute pancreatitis (AP), hADSC-EVs are closely associated with the occurrence and development of the disease, highlighting their therapeutic potential in immune regulation and inflammatory conditions.4,18 However, the specific impacts of hADSC-EVs in treating severe acute pancreatitis (SAP), particularly the roles of their bioactive components, have not yet been fully elucidated.16,19

Protein phosphorylation and dephosphorylation by kinases and phosphatases, respectively, are at the core of many metabolic processes.20,21,22 Phosphorylation and phosphoproteins are involved in inflammation, immune reaction, and Treg function. Indeed, the core Hippo pathway components, including the kinases MST1/2(also known as STK3/STK4), act to phosphorylate and inhibit the transcriptional co-activators YAP and TAZ23 and are involved in Treg differentiation.24,25 Specifically, STK3 (MST1) plays a critical role in the activation of the Hippo pathway by phosphorylating downstream targets, thereby modulating Treg function and immune homeostasis.26 Nevertheless, the precise mechanisms through which the Hippo pathway, particularly STK3, governs Treg development are not yet fully elucidated. Phosphoproteomics, the study of the patterns of protein phosphorylation at the cellular level,27,28 could help determine the mechanisms of the Hippo pathway on Tregs in SAP.

Using a murine model of SAP, we investigated the role of STK3-mediated phosphorylation in the regulation of Treg differentiation and the therapeutic potential of hADSC-EVs, highlighting the involvement of phosphoproteins and the Hippo pathway in immune regulation.

Results

Furthermore, nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA) provided a quantitative assessment, demonstrating a highly homogenous population of vesicles. To comprehensively delve into the functional roles of hADSC-EVs in intercellular communication and signaling pathways, we employed a 4D label-free phosphoproteomics strategy utilizing dia-PASEF acquisition to analyze the phosphorylation status of exosomal proteins. Statistical analysis showed that 46.52% of proteins exhibited two or more phosphorylation sites (Figure 1D), averaging 0.4 phosphorylation sites per 100 amino acids (Figure 1E). The distribution of phosphorylation sites on serine (S), threonine (T), and tyrosine (Y) residues was identified (Figure 1F). A Venn diagram analysis showed 284 consistently identified proteins in biological replicates of hADSC-EVs (Figure 1G). Notably, phosphorylation of STK3 (MST2), a key kinase in the Hippo pathway, was significantly enriched in hADSC-EVs (Figures 1H and S2). KEGG pathway enrichment analysis indicated significant alterations in the Hippo signaling pathway among differentially expressed phosphorylated peptides (Figure S3).

HADSC-EVs-induced Treg differentiation via transcriptional upregulation of Foxp3

Next, we investigated the immunomodulatory impact of hADSC-EVs on Treg differentiation. DiO-labeled hADSC-EVs were taken up by naive CD4+ T cells, confirming cellular uptake (Figure 2A). Stimulation of naive CD4+ T cells with the Treg differentiation inducer, a combination of cytokines and antibodies (5 ng/mL TGF-β, 10 ng/mL IL-2, 5 μg/mL anti-IFN-γ, and 5 μg/mL anti-IL-4), significantly increased the p-YAP/YAP ratio, Foxp3, TGF-β, IL-10 levels, and the Treg population, emphasizing the potent regulatory effects of the inducer on CD4+ T cell differentiation toward Treg phenotype. Similarly, exposure to hADSC-EVs also increased the p-YAP/YAP ratio and Foxp3 protein level (Figures 2B–2E). Furthermore, the levels of Foxp3, TGF-β, and IL-10 mRNAs increased with hADSC-EVs treatment in a significant concentration-dependent manner (Figures 2F–2H). Consistent with the mRNA data, ELISA confirmed a dose-dependent elevation of both TGF-β and IL-10 in the cell culture supernatant (Figures 2I and 2J). The increase in TGF-β is particularly noteworthy, as this cytokine plays a complex, dual role in pancreatic pathophysiology, being involved in both the initial tissue damage and the subsequent repair processes.29 Similarly, the elevation of IL-10 is a key finding, as it is a potent anti-inflammatory cytokine known to reduce the severity of AP by inhibiting macrophage activation and the release of pro-inflammatory mediators.30 Additionally, luciferase experiments confirmed the ability of hADSC-EVs to induce Foxp3 upregulation in Jurkat T cells (Figure 2K) and 293T cells (Figure 2L). Interestingly, the proportion of Treg distinguished by CD4+ Foxp3+ expression remarkably increased following hADSC-EVs treatment (Figures 2M and 2N). HADSC-EVs enhance the differentiation of Tregs and exert anti-inflammatory effects

Our results revealed a significant upregulation of Foxp3 and the Treg-associated cytokines TGF-β and IL-10 in the presence of Treg differentiation inducer (Figures 3A–3E), indicating the differentiation of Tregs. Notably, hADSC-EVs resulted in a concentration-dependent increase in the mRNA levels of Foxp3, TGF-β, and IL-10 in TGF-β and IL-2-induced Tregs (Figures 3A–3C). ELISA results further demonstrated a significant elevation in the levels of secreted TGF-β and IL-10 in response to hADSC-EVs (Figures 3D and 3E). We knocked down STK3 in hADSCs (si-STK3) and subsequently isolated EVs from these cells to further explore the functional implications of STK3 phosphorylation. STK3 knockdown led to a marked decrease in p-YAP levels and the p-YAP/YAP ratio (Figures 3H and 3I), possibly reducing cytoplasmic retention of YAP and inducing nuclear accumulation. Naive CD4+ T cells can differentiate into Tregs in response to specific signaling pathways.8 Additionally, the translocation of YAP into the nucleus activates the Hippo pathway, regulating the differentiation of Tregs.25 When EVs from si-STK3-transfected cells were applied to naive CD4+ T cells, the mRNA level of Foxp3, a critical player in the Hippo pathway regulation, was decreased (Figure 3J). Furthermore, transfecting si-STK3 into naive CD4+ T cells led to decreased levels of Foxp3, IL-10, and TGF-β. Importantly, hADSC-EVs significantly restored the decreased Foxp3, IL-10, and TGF-β levels induced by STK3 knockdown (Figures 3K–3M). To provide a direct mechanistic link, we further verified whether STK3 expression in the parent hADSCs directly altered the phosphoprotein cargo of the secreted EVs. Indeed, western blot analysis revealed that EVs derived from si-STK3-transfected hADSCs contained significantly lower levels of phosphorylated YAP (p-YAP), a key downstream effector of STK3. In addition, EVs derived from OE-STK3-transfected hADSCs contained significantly higher levels of p-YAP (Figure S4). This finding confirms that hADSC-EVs mediate their effect by transferring STK3-regulated p-YAP. Collectively, these results underscore the immunomodulatory potential of hADSC-EVs in fostering Treg differentiation via the Hippo pathway.

HADSC-EVs alleviated multiple organ damage and reduced mortality in SAP mice

Next, we investigated the therapeutic potential of hADSC-EVs in improving multi-organ damage and decreasing mortality in a mouse model of SAP. After intravenous injection of DiR-labeled hADSC-EVs, the distribution of hADSC-EVs was observed in various organs, including the liver, lungs, kidneys, and pancreas, at 12 h post-injection (Figure 4A). Significantly lower survival rates were evident in the SAP group compared to the Sham group, but significantly improved with hADSC-EV treatment, indicating the potent protective effects of hADSC-EV (Figure 4B). Moreover, lipase and enzymatic levels were significantly elevated in the SAP group relative to the Sham group. However, administration of hADSC-EVs led to a substantial reduction in lipase and amylase levels (Figure 4C). Histological examinations further revealed pronounced histopathological alterations in SAP mice, characterized by interstitial edema, congestion, inflammatory cell infiltration, and tissue destruction, which was ameliorated in the presence of hADSC-EVs (Figures 4D and S5). Furthermore, the levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines, such as IFN-γ, IL-6, TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-18 in serum were significantly elevated in the SAP model, whereas their levels were effectively attenuated with hADSC-EVs treatment (Figure 4E). HADSC-EVs regulate the pancreatic immune microenvironment in SAP mice

We conducted an analysis focusing on the quantities of pro-inflammatory M1-like (F4/80+CD206-) macrophages, anti-inflammatory M2-like (F4/80+CD206+) macrophages, and DCs (CD11c+ MHC-II+) to unravel the mechanisms by which hADSC-EVs influence the pancreatic immune response. Compared to the Sham group, the SAP group exhibited a significant increase in pro-inflammatory M1-like macrophages and DC, while the numbers of anti-inflammatory M2-like macrophages were markedly decreased (Figures 5A–5E), indicating an accumulation of pro-inflammatory macrophages induced by SAP. Intriguingly, treatment with hADSC-EVs resulted in a notable decrease in DC and M1-like macrophage accumulation and a significant increase in M2-like macrophages (Figures 5A–5E). Furthermore, the M1/M2 macrophage ratios were significantly elevated in the SAP group compared to the Sham group; however, the SAP+EVs group exhibited a noteworthy reduction compared to the SAP group (Figure 5F). Therefore, we found that EVs from hADSCs contain a large amount of phosphorylated proteins, which may influence the immune phenotype changes in SAP. Specifically, phosphorylated STK3 (p-STK3) is one of the most abundant proteins and can induce naive T cells to differentiate into Tregs through the Hippo pathway. This process significantly reduces the innate immune response in SAP, thereby mitigating injury to multiple organs, including the pancreas, liver, lungs, and kidneys, and prolonging the survival time in SAP (graphical abstract).

Discussion

In this study, the hADSC-EVs influenced macrophage polarization, decreased pro-inflammatory cells, and increased anti-inflammatory cells. In addition, administration of hADSC-EVs alleviated multi-organ damage, reduced mortality, and suppressed pro-inflammatory responses in SAP mice. Furthermore, the activation of STK3, a key component of the Hippo pathway, following hADSC-EVs treatment may have facilitated the enhancement of Treg differentiation. Overall, these findings underscore the potential of hADSC-EVs as an effective therapeutic strategy in managing SAP by harnessing the mechanistic pathways that govern immune responses and inflammation.

Recent studies have shown the impact of hADSC-EVs in resolving inflammation and maintaining tissue homeostasis.17 For instance, hADSC-EVs have been shown to regulate cytokine secretion and the balance between Th1 and Th2 cell responses,31 leading to a notable alleviation of nasal symptoms and a decrease in inflammatory infiltration.32 Building upon this foundation, we explored the therapeutic potential of hADSC-EVs in modulating the immune response and restoring immune homeostasis in the context of SAP. Our findings demonstrated that treatment with hADSC-EVs significantly improved survival rates and reduced tissue damage in SAP mice, providing compelling evidence for the therapeutic efficacy of these vesicles in this condition. In addition, we observed that SAP patients had elevated DC levels, which are pivotal in orchestrating immune responses. Following hADSC-EV treatment, these elevated DC levels were significantly reduced, indicating their potential therapeutic effects in inflammation resolution.

Macrophages are key regulators of inflammation and tissue repair in SAP,33 with distinct M1 pro-inflammatory and M2 anti-inflammatory phenotypes. Furthermore, hADSC-EVs influence macrophage polarization toward the anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype.34 Notably, our study demonstrated that hADSC-EVs influence macrophage polarization toward the anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype in SAP, thereby promoting tissue repair and reducing inflammation. Moreover, MSC-EV preparations differentially regulate the expression of surface markers on CD4 and CD8 T cells, resulting in altered frequencies of effector and memory T cells.35 The present study showed that SAP is characterized by an imbalance in T cell subsets, suggesting its immunomodulatory and regenerative properties.

Upon stimulation by antigens and inflammatory factors, naive CD4+ T cells can differentiate into subsets such as Th1, Th2, Th17, and Treg.36 Th1 and Th17 are associated with inflammation induction, while Th2 and Treg can suppress inflammation. Notably, a previous study suggested that hADSC-EVs have the potential to ameliorate allergic rhinitis symptoms by modulating cytokine secretion and restoring the balance of Th1/Th2 cell ratios.32 In this study, hADSC-EVs were also shown to rebalance the Treg/Th17 and Th2/Th1 cell ratios in the SAP mouse model, effectively regulating immune cell subsets, promoting immune homeostasis, and inhibiting inflammatory responses, suggesting promising implications for treating inflammatory conditions.

Tregs are a specialized subset of T cells that play a crucial role in maintaining immune tolerance and preventing autoimmunity. A recent study has highlighted the immunomodulatory properties of hADSC-EVs in regulating T cell activities, attracting attention for their therapeutic potential.17 Indeed, hADSC-EVs carry a functionally relevant and complex cargo. For instance, they contain activated (phosphorylated) STAT3 (p-STAT3), which can directly influence inflammatory processes.36,37 Previous studies have demonstrated that hADSC-EVs carrying p-STAT3 can alleviate experimental autoimmune and inflammatory conditions by modulating immune cell profiles and cytokine secretion patterns.36,38 Building on this, our phosphoproteomic analysis of hADSC-EVs revealed that p-STK3 was one of the most abundant phosphoproteins. In the present study, we focused on the Hippo signaling pathway, which plays a crucial role in orchestrating Treg differentiation39,40 because STK3 was identified as being among the phosphorylated proteins found in the hADSC-EVs, and STK3 is a major player in the Hippo pathway.41 Specifically, we examined the protein phosphorylation modifications in hADSC-EVs and identified significant differences in STK3 (MST2), a core component of the Hippo pathway. The results indicated that knocking down STK3 resulted in a decrease in p-YAP level, a key effector molecule downstream of the Hippo pathway, leading to decreased Foxp3 expression. YAP phosphorylation prevents its translocation into the nucleus.42 The translocation of YAP into the nucleus activates the Hippo pathway and regulates Treg differentiation.25 YAP deficiency is associated with dysfunctional Tregs.25 Deficiencies in Foxp3 can impair Treg function and contribute to the development of autoimmunity.43 Interestingly, YAP has been reported to be highly expressed in Tregs, enhancing Foxp3 expression and Treg functionality in vitro and in vivo.25,44 In cancer, unphosphorylated YAP is required for the immunosuppressive effects of Tregs in the tumor microenvironment.45 A study suggested that constitutive KRAS expression activates YAP1 and TAZ, which are essential for the development of acinar-to-ductal metaplasia in pancreatic cancer.46 In this study, we found that hADSC-EVs can be internalized by naive CD4+ T cells, promoting Treg differentiation through the upregulation of key factors such as Foxp3, TGF-β, and IL-10, which was aligned with previous research.17,47 Conversely, downregulating STK3 led to reduced levels of Foxp3, IL-10, and TGF-β, hindering the differentiation of naive CD4+ T cells into Tregs. Notably, the ability of hADSC-EVs to restore Foxp3 expression in STK3 knockdown cells highlights their potential to counteract Treg dysfunction. The p-STK3 content of hADSC-EVs is probably responsible for counteracting the STK3 knockdown. By leveraging the immunomodulatory properties of hADSC-EVs and their influence on the Hippo pathway, we may develop innovative strategies to enhance Treg functionality and restore immune tolerance, offering hope for the treatment of various autoimmune diseases. Protein phosphorylation is central to signal transduction and the regulation of many enzymes and pathways. Phosphoproteomics represents a promising avenue in the study of the patterns of protein phosphorylation at the cellular level and their impact on disease development.27,28 Of note, STK3 was not the only phosphorylated protein in the hADSC-EVs, but it was the most abundant. In addition, STK3 phosphorylation is mediated by the STRIPAK complex,42 which was not investigated here. In addition, the role of YAP phosphorylation has been extensively studied in the immune tolerance to cancer,48,49 but the available data regarding SAP and other inflammatory diseases are scarce.

In conclusion, the present study highlights the therapeutic potential of hADSC-EVs in ameliorating multi-organ damage, modulating inflammatory responses, and restoring immune cell balance in the SAP mouse model and elucidates the immunomodulatory properties of hADSC-EVs in promoting Treg differentiation through Hippo pathway regulation. These findings contribute to a better understanding of the mechanisms underlying the beneficial effects of hADSC-EVs in immune regulation and tissue protection, offering insights into potential clinical applications in immune-mediated diseases.

Limitations of the study

Our study has several limitations. The primary limitation is its focus on the STK3 pathway, as EVs contain a wide range of other bioactive molecules (such as proteins, lipids, and RNAs) whose potential contributions were not assessed. Indeed, while p-STK3 was the most abundant phosphoprotein identified in our hADSC-EVs, it was not the only one. Moreover, STK3 phosphorylation is mediated by the STRIPAK complex,42 which was not explored in this study. While the role of YAP phosphorylation in immune tolerance to cancer has been extensively studied, data regarding its involvement in SAP and other inflammatory diseases remain limited.48,49 Also, the source of the sample was not sex-balanced, which may lead to statistical differences and research bias due to sex. Further research is needed to clarify how these pathways intersect and interrelate. Future studies should therefore expand upon our findings, particularly by examining the effects of hADSC-EVs on the pancreas-resident Treg population through tissue-specific analyses, such as spatial transcriptomics. Ultimately, a more comprehensive characterization of the EV cargo beyond STK3, along with the validation of these mechanisms in human-based models, will be essential to fully understand their therapeutic potential.

Experimental model and study participant details

Cells

HADSCs were obtained from the National Stem Cell Translational Resource Center (ZB10DGAC) and grown in complete DMEM supplemented with 10% exosome-depleted fetal bovine serum (FBS).

Naive CD4+ T cells were extracted from spleens utilizing a mouse naive CD4+ T-cell isolation kit II (Miltenyi Biotec, 130-104-453) and subsequently underwent purification to yield enriched subsets, which were assessed via flow cytometry. For Treg differentiation, the cells were treated with TGF-β (5 ng/ml) and IL-2 (10 ng/ml) in the presence of anti-IFN-γ (5 μg/ml) and anti-IL-4 (5 μg/ml) for three days. Alternatively, the cells were treated with hADSC-EVs at concentrations of 10, 20, and 30 μg/ml for three days.

Specific siRNA sequences (provided in Table S3) targeting STK3 and a control siRNA (si-Con) were synthesized to construct and transfect si-Con and si-STK3 into hADSCs and naive CD4+ T cells. Briefly, cells were seeded in 6-well plates and grown to 70-80% confluency. The STK3 shRNA lentiviral vectors were then used to infect the hADSCs and naive CD4+ T cells, and the medium was replaced with fresh growth medium after 6 h. Transfection efficiency was assessed 24 h post-transfection by quantitative PCR and western blotting to confirm the knockdown of STK3.

Animals

Eight-week-old male C57BL/6 mice were purchased from Shanghai SLAC Laboratory Animal Co. (Shanghai, China). The mice were housed under specific pathogen-free (SPF) conditions, maintained on a standard diet ad libitum, and kept on a 12-h light-dark cycle. All animal experiments were conducted in strict accordance with the International Guiding Principles for Animal Research and were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of the Ruijin Hospital, Shanghai Jiao Tong University School of Medicine (SYXK 2023-0007).

The mice were randomly divided into three groups: Sham, SAP, and SAP+EVs, with six mice in each group. Briefly, mice received hourly intraperitoneal injections of cerulein (50 μg/kg; Sigma-Aldrich) in saline for two consecutive days, with eight injections per day. During the final cerulein injection, lipopolysaccharide (LPS) (30 mg/kg; Sigma-Aldrich) was administered to potentiate the inflammatory response and mimic multiple organ failure.52 Two hours after SAP induction, mice received a tail vein injection of hADSC-EVs (1 μg/μL, 200 μL) in the SAP-EVs group. For in vivo distribution studies, DiR-labeled hADSC-EVs were injected into mice via the tail vein. At 24 hours after injection, the mice were sacrificed by cervical dislocation following anesthesia with pentobarbital sodium at 50 mg/kg body weight via intraperitoneal injection.53 Following this, the liver, lungs, kidneys, pancreas, and spleen were harvested. The distribution of DiR-labeled hADSC-EVs within these tissues was observed and quantified using an in vivo fluorescence imaging technique (PerkinElmer IVIS Spectrum) to assess the biodistribution of the vesicles. Portions of pancreatic tissue were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for histopathological examination.

Method details

Isolation and purification of hADSC-derived EVs and protein quantification

After two days of HADSC culture, the conditioned media were collected and subjected to filtration using 0.22-μm filters. The filtrate was concentrated using 100 kDa molecular weight cut-off (MWCO) Amicon Ultra-15 centrifugal filter devices (Merck Millipore, Burlington, MA). Subsequently, hADSC-EVs were extracted from the concentrated medium using the Exoquick-TC precipitation reagent (System Biosciences, CA), following the manufacturer’s protocol. To enhance the purity and remove potential co-precipitated proteins, the resulting EV pellet was resuspended in a large volume of ice-cold PBS and subjected to a washing step via ultracentrifugation at 110,000×g for 90 minutes at 4°C. The final, purified EV pellet was resuspended in sterile PBS. The concentration was adjusted to 1 μg/μL, as determined by the BCA assay described below, and then characterized through a nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA) system (Particle Metrix, PMX120) and transmission electron microscopy (TEM) (Hitachi, HT-7700). The identity and purity of the isolated EVs were further confirmed by western blot analysis, targeting the positive exosomal markers Alix, TSG101, and CD63, and the negative marker Calnexin.

HADSC-EVs were mixed with 6× RIPA lysis buffer and incubated on ice for 30 min. Protein concentration was measured using the BCA assay. Absorbance was subsequently measured at 562 nm using a microplate reader.

NTA analysis

Initially, the performance of the nanoparticle tracking analyzer was validated using calibration standards. HADSC-EVs were then diluted and introduced into the instrument’s sample wells. The NTA system (Particle Metrix, PMX120) subsequently delivered comprehensive data on the size distribution and concentration of the hADSC-EVs. Throughout the analysis, the temperature was consistently maintained between 23°C and 30°C.

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) analysis

For TEM analysis, 10 μL of the exosome sample was initially deposited onto a copper grid and allowed to adhere for 1 min. After this incubation, excess liquid was carefully removed using filter paper. Subsequently, the grid was stained with 10 μL of uranyl acetate for 1 min, followed by blotting off the excess stain with filter paper. The grid was then left to dry at room temperature, and imaging was conducted using a Hitachi HT-7700 transmission electron microscope at an accelerating voltage of 100 kV.

Western blot assays

Proteins were isolated from cells and tissues using a whole-cell lysis buffer (KeyGen Biotech, Jiangsu, China), and their concentrations were determined according to the instructions provided by the BCA kit in our previous study.52,54 The extracted proteins were separated via polyacrylamide-sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS) gel electrophoresis and then transferred onto a polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membrane. After blocking the membrane with 5% non-fat dry milk, it was incubated overnight at 4°C with the primary antibodies. The following day, the membranes were exposed to the appropriate secondary antibodies for 2 h. Protein bands were visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescence reagent and quantified using ImageJ software. Antibody information is provided in Table S1.

Immunohistochemistry

Briefly, tissue sections were deparaffinized using xylene, rehydrated through graded ethanol, and subjected to antigen retrieval in citrate buffer (pH 6.0) at 95°C for 20 min. Sections were treated with 3% hydrogen peroxide to block endogenous peroxidase activity. Non-specific binding was minimized by incubating the sections in 5% normal serum in PBS for 1 hour at room temperature. Primary antibodies were then applied and left to incubate overnight at 4°C. The next day, sections were washed and incubated with biotinylated secondary antibodies for 30 min at room temperature. Visualization of antigen-antibody complexes was achieved using diaminobenzidine (DAB) as the chromogen, and the sections were counterstained with hematoxylin. Finally, the sections were dehydrated, cleared, and mounted for microscopic examination.

Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E)

Tissue samples were fixed in 10% formalin, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned at 4 μm for histological analysis. For H&E staining, sections were deparaffinized in xylene, rehydrated through graded ethanol, and stained with hematoxylin for 5 min. After rinsing, sections were differentiated in acid alcohol, counterstained with eosin for 2 min, dehydrated, cleared in xylene, and mounted.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

Briefly, high-binding 96-well microplates were coated overnight at 4°C with 100 μL of capture antibody in carbonate-bicarbonate buffer (pH 9.6). After three washes with PBST, wells were blocked with 5% non-fat dry milk in PBST for 1 h at room temperature. Then, 100 μL of standards or samples diluted in PBST were added and incubated for 2 h. After another wash, 100 μL of biotinylated detection antibody was added and incubated for 1 h. Following additional washing, 100 μL of streptavidin-HRP conjugate was added and incubated for 30 min. After a final wash, 100 μL of TMB substrate was added, and the reaction was stopped after 15 min with 50 μL of 2M sulfuric acid. Optical density was measured at 450 nm.

Flow cytometry

In order to assess T cell distribution, cell suspensions were incubated with fluorochrome-conjugated antibodies specific for CD11c, MHC-II, CD4, CD8, F4/80, CD206, CD25, Foxp3, IL17A, IL-4, and IFN-γ at 4°C for 30 min in the dark. Cells were permeabilized to allow protein localization across organelles. Following incubation, cells were washed with 2 mL of PBS to remove excess antibodies. The stained cells were then analyzed using a FACScan flow cytometer (BD Biosciences).

Real-time reverse transcriptase-PCR (RT-PCR)

Total RNA was extracted from pancreatic tissue and cells using the Trizol reagent according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The extracted RNA was then purified and reverse-transcribed into complementary DNA (cDNA) using the Prime Script RT Master Mix. Subsequently, gene expression analysis was carried out using quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) on a Step One Plus Real-Time PCR system with the Fast Start Universal SYBR Green Master. The relative gene expression levels were calculated using the 2ˆ-ΔΔCt method, and GAPDH was employed as an internal control to normalize the data. The specific primer sequences used for the qRT-PCR are listed in Table S2.

Uptake of DiO-labeled hADSC-EVs by naive CD4+ T cells

hADSC-EVs were labeled with the fluorescent dye DiO to investigate the uptake of hADSC-EVs by naive CD4+ T cells. Naive CD4+ T cells were isolated and cultured in optimal growth media. Subsequently, DiO-labeled hADSC-EVs were introduced to the cultured naive CD4+ T cells at a predetermined concentration. The co-cultures were incubated for a specific duration to facilitate vesicle uptake. Post-incubation, the cells were thoroughly washed to eliminate any unbound hADSC-EVs and then fixed for immunofluorescence analysis. Immunofluorescence microscopy was employed to observe the uptake of DiO-labeled hADSC-EVs by naive CD4+ T cells, and fluorescence intensity was measured to quantify the degree of uptake. The degree of uptake was determined by measuring the fluorescence intensity within the CD4+ T cells.

Luciferase assay

The pGL3-Foxp3 reporter plasmids were co-transfected into Jurkat T cells or 293T cells using a lipid-based transfection reagent. The cells were incubated for 24 h to allow for adequate gene expression. Subsequently, the medium was replaced with fresh medium containing 30 μg/mL of hADSC-EVs, which had been previously isolated and characterized. After the specified treatment duration, cells were lysed, and luciferase activity was quantified using a dual-luciferase reporter assay system according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Renilla luciferase activity was normalized to firefly luciferase activity. Data were collected from at least three independent experiments.

Phosphoproteomics analysis

Protein extraction and trypsin digestion

Samples were lysed in UA buffer (8 M Urea, 100 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8.5) containing phosphatase inhibitors. Protein concentration was quantified using the BCA Protein Assay Kit (Bio-Rad). For digestion, 20 μg of protein from each sample was reduced with dithiothreitol (DTT, 10 mM final concentration) at 37°C for 1 h and alkylated with iodoacetamide (IAA, 50 mM final concentration) in the dark for 30 min. The urea concentration was diluted to <1.5 M with 100 mM Tris-HCl. Proteins were digested with trypsin (Promega) at a 1:50 enzyme-to-protein ratio (w/w) overnight at 37°C. The digested peptides were desalted using C18 SPE Cartridges, lyophilized, and reconstituted in 0.1% formic acid.

Phosphopeptide enrichment

Phosphopeptides were enriched using the High-Select™ Fe-NTA Phosphopeptide Enrichment Kit (Thermo Scientific, Cat# A32992) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Eluted phosphopeptides were lyophilized and resuspended in 20 μL of loading buffer (0.1% formic acid) for LC-MS/MS analysis.

LC-MS/MS analysis

Liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) was performed on a timsTOF Pro mass spectrometer (Bruker Daltonics) coupled to a NanoElute system (Bruker Daltonics). Peptides were loaded onto a C18 reversed-phase analytical column (Thermo Scientific EASY-Spray, 25 cm × 75 μm, 1.9 μm C18) and separated using a linear gradient of buffer B (99% acetonitrile, 0.1% formic acid) at a flow rate of 300 nL/min. The mass spectrometer was operated in dia-PASEF mode with a mass range of 100–1700 m/z. The ion mobility range (1/K0) was set to 0.75–1.35 V·s/cm2. Dynamic exclusion was enabled with a release time of 24 s.

Data analysis and phosphosite localization

Raw MS data were processed using PEAKS software (Bioinformatics Solutions Inc.) for identification and label-free quantification. The search parameters were set as follows: precursor mass tolerance of 20 ppm; fragment mass tolerance of 0.05 Da; trypsin as the enzyme with up to 2 missed cleavages allowed. Carbamidomethylation (C) was set as a fixed modification, while Oxidation (M), Acetylation (Protein N-term), and Phosphorylation (S, T, Y) were set as variable modifications. The false discovery rate (FDR) was set to <1.0% at the PSM level. Phosphorylation site localization was evaluated, and only phosphosites with high localization probability were considered for downstream analysis.

Quantification and statistical analysis

GraphPad Prism 8 software (GraphPad Software, La Jolla, CA) was used for statistical analysis. The data are expressed as mean values accompanied by their standard errors (± SEMs). Differences deemed statistically significant were identified utilizing either two-tailed Student’s t-tests or one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). Prior to selecting the appropriate statistical tests, we thoroughly assessed the assumptions of normality and homogeneity of variances to ensure their suitability. A p-value of less than 0.05 was set as the criterion for statistical significance.
