Culture matters? Family norms, living arrangements, and loneliness in China
Authors: Shu Cai, Wei Li, Jia Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•We investigate how family cultural norms moderate the relationship between living arrangements and loneliness among older Chinese.•We utilize nationally representative survey data and novel historical records of family genealogies.•Older Chinese whose living arrangements diverge from local family norms report significantly higher levels of loneliness, a pattern more pronounced in regions with stronger traditional family culture.•Older adults whose living arrangements conform to local family norms experience consistently low levels of loneliness, regardless of cultural context.

Introduction

Loneliness has emerged as a pressing public health concern among older adults worldwide, with mounting evidence linking it to cognitive decline (Hong et al., 2023), depression (Banerjee et al., 2023; Cacioppo et al., 2006; Green et al., 2021), and higher risks of poor physical health and mortality (Cacioppo & Cacioppo, 2018; Luo et al., 2012; Surkalim et al., 2022). Studies on China have similarly highlighted the growing prevalence and impact of loneliness in later life (e.g., Li et al., 2024; Luo & Waite, 2014; Wang, 2025). Understanding the determinants of loneliness is therefore crucial. While existing studies have primarily focused on micro-level factors such as gender, marital status, education (Hawkley et al., 2008; Raymo & Wang, 2022), early-life experiences (Furuya & Wang, 2023), social participation (Teh & Tey, 2019), and living arrangements (De Jong Gierveld & Van Tilburg, 1999; Russell, 2009), the influence of broader cultural contexts remains underexplored. To address this gap, this study investigates how family culture moderates the association between living arrangements and loneliness among older Chinese adults.

Building on the cognitive discrepancy perspective (e.g., De Jong Gierveld & Tesch-Römer, 2012; Peplau & Perlman, 1982, pp. 1–18), we propose a cultural concordance-discordance framework that emphasizes the alignment between individuals’ living arrangements and external cultural norms. This approach highlights the role of cultural expectations as external reference points, shaping both the standards by which social relationships are evaluated and the consequences of deviating from these norms. By anchoring our analysis in observable cultural benchmarks—specifically, the strength of family norms regarding intergenerational coresidence—we systematically examine how the association between living arrangements and loneliness varies across different cultural contexts. Our framework predicts that older adults whose living situations diverge from strong local family norms are particularly vulnerable to loneliness, with this vulnerability increasing as the strength of family culture intensifies, while those whose arrangements conform to these expectations experience little variation in loneliness.

China presents a distinctive setting for examining these dynamics, as rapid socioeconomic and demographic transformations have dramatically altered family structures (Cai et al., 2023; Lei, Strauss, et al., 2015; Li et al., 2020), while traditional values like filial piety and intergenerational coresidence remain influential (Chen, 2019; Chen & Short, 2008; Lei, Strauss, et al., 2015). This tension between enduring family culture and shifting living arrangements may leave many older adults vulnerable to loneliness, particularly in regions where traditional norms are most deeply rooted. By analyzing regional variation in family culture and its interaction with living arrangements, this study deepens understanding of the cultural determinants of loneliness in later life.

Our findings contribute to both academic debates and policy interventions addressing loneliness in rapidly changing societies. Leveraging substantial geographic variation in family culture across China and employing a regionally nuanced, behavioral measure of family norms, this study provides new evidence on how cultural context shapes loneliness in later life. We highlight the importance of considering both individual circumstances and broader cultural environments when promoting the well-being of older adults.

Literature review

The cultural concordance-discordance framework

Loneliness is fundamentally a subjective evaluation of one's social relationships and interactions, arising when individuals perceive deficiencies in the quantity or quality of their social connections (Hawkley et al., 2008). The cognitive discrepancy perspective conceptualizes loneliness as the result of unmet social expectations—specifically, the perceived gap between one's actual and desired social relationships (De Jong Gierveld & Tesch-Römer, 2012; Peplau & Perlman, 1982, pp. 1–18). While this approach highlights the importance of internal, subjective evaluations, it tends to underemphasize the external cultural structures that shape individual perceptions and expectations. To address these limitations, we propose the cultural concordance-discordance framework, which extends the cognitive discrepancy model by explicitly considering how individual living situations align with societal-level cultural benchmarks. This framework underscores that individuals' standards for social relationships are not formed in isolation, but are deeply rooted in prevailing cultural contexts (Bachrach, 2014; Burholt, 2023).

Our framework introduces two key theoretical innovations. First, it moves beyond the traditional focus on internalized expectations by highlighting the role of external cultural norms as structural reference systems. While prior research has explored related concepts such as cultural non-conformity, transgression, and exclusion (Burholt, 2023), these constructs typically treat cultural influence as an internalized process (e.g., social identity negotiation) rather than as an external, structural force shaping individual expectations. In our framework, cultural concordance occurs when an individual's actual social situation (e.g. living arrangements) aligns with culturally prescribed standards, whereas cultural discordance arises when there is a deviation from these external norms. For example, in societies that value family solidarity and intergenerational coresidence, older adults living with adult children achieve cultural concordance and are likely to experience lower levels of loneliness compared to those in culturally discordant arrangements, such as living alone or without adult children.

Second, our framework explicitly incorporates the moderating role of cultural norms by treating their strength as a measurable contextual factor. By quantifying local cultural expectations, researchers can systematically examine how individual-level predictors (such as living arrangements) interact with broader societal contexts (such as family culture) to shape experiences of loneliness. For instance, the negative consequences of living arrangements that deviate from cultural norms are amplified in regions where those norms are particularly strong. This integrative approach offers a more nuanced understanding of loneliness, recognizing that individual loneliness is jointly influenced by personal circumstances and the cultural environment in which people live (De Jong Gierveld & Tesch-Römer, 2012).

To illustrate, Fig. 1 shows that the disparity in loneliness between older adults experiencing cultural discordance (upper solid line) and those experiencing cultural concordance (lower solid line) in terms of family living arrangements increases monotonically across regions with relatively weak, middle, and strong family cultures.1 We expect that older adults not living with adult children (e.g, those living alone, living with a spouse only; or living with other non-family members) will be increasingly susceptible to loneliness in areas where strong family norms emphasize intergenerational coresidence (positive slope). In contrast, loneliness among older adults living with adult children should remain relatively stable across regions with varying strengths of family culture, as their living arrangements already conform to cultural expectations (flat line). In the absence of the effect of family culture, the level of loneliness among those not living with adult children would not vary across regions (counterfactual, dash flat line).

We therefore propose the following hypotheses:

All else being equal, older adults living without adult children have higher levels of loneliness than those living with adult children.

The difference in loneliness between these groups is larger in regions with stronger family cultures.

Loneliness among those living with adult children remains stable across regions, while loneliness among those living without adult children increases with the strength of family culture.

Existing studies

Prior research has predominantly focused on how family living arrangements themselves affect loneliness. Far less is known about the psychological consequences when these arrangements deviate from prevailing cultural norms. Some studies have examined cultural differences in the relationship between living arrangements and loneliness (De Jong Gierveld & Van Tilburg, 1999; De Jong Gierveld et al., 2012; Jylhä & Jokela, 1990),2 but often lack consistent measures of culture and do not directly assess how family culture moderates the link between living arrangements and loneliness. For example, Jylhä and Jokela (1990) found that older adults living alone were lonelier in countries where family coresidence is the norm. De Jong Gierveld and Van Tilburg (1999) reported that living with adult children (without partners) increased loneliness among Dutch older adults but reduced it among Italians, reflecting the more traditional family orientation of Italians and the more individualized family orientation of the Dutch. De Jong Gierveld et al. (2012) expected greater loneliness among those living alone in Eastern Europe due to stronger family norms but found no clear regional differences, suggesting that family relationships might not be directly comparable across countries (Dykstra, 2009).

While these studies highlight important cultural variation, several methodological limitations persist. First, cross-national findings may be confounded by differences in welfare regimes, economic development, inequality, and policy environments, limiting the interpretability of cultural effects. Second, many studies rely on broad, static proxies for culture (e.g., national-level individualism/collectivism value scores) or regional classifications (e.g., Eastern vs. Western Europe), which lack granularity and fail to capture sub-national heterogeneity. Third, reliance on self-reported preferences for living arrangements or family values introduces measurement challenges and potential biases, especially among older populations, and may not accurately reflect actual cultural practices or structural constraints. Finally, the small number of countries included in prior research restricts statistical power and hinders rigorous testing of the moderating role of culture norms in shaping loneliness, thereby limiting the generalizability of findings.

To address these methodological limitations, we conduct our analysis within a single country, China, examining data from over 100 prefectures. This approach enables us to hold constant national-level confounders, thereby isolating the influence of regional cultural norms on loneliness. In particular, we utilize a regionally nuanced indicator, linear genealogy density, which captures sub-national variartion and directly reflects the strength of localized, kinship-based collectivist norms with greater precision than broad, static, or value-based cultural proxies. As a behavioral and structural measure derived from objective records of clan organization, genealogy density is unobtrusive and not subject to self-reporting biases, providing a more accurate representation of the actual prevalence of cultural structures that shape family norms and behaviors. Furthermore, our large-N design affords substantial statistical power to rigorously examine how regional cultural norms moderate the relationship between living arrangements and loneliness.

The Chinese context

China is characterized by a long-standing tradition of strong family culture that emphasizes close family ties and intergenerational solidarity (Raymo et al., 2015). Historically, the ideal family life has often been depicted as multiple generations living under one roof, exemplified by the concept of the “five-generation family” or “five generations co-residing” (wu shi tong tang), where older adults are believed to experience familial love and happiness (Baker, 1979). Although such extended family arrangements are rare in practice, traditional values continue to regard multigenerational households where older adults co-reside with their adult children as the preferred living arrangement (Logan & Bian, 1999; Sun et al., 2019).

Since the onset of market reforms in late 1970s, China has experienced profound social, economic, and demographic transformations. Major changes—such as the one-child policy, pension reforms, and large-scale rural-to-urban migration—have significantly altered family structures, resulting in a decline in multigenerational households and a rise in older adults living alone or only with a spouse (Cheung & Yeung, 2015; Giles et al., 2010; Lei, Strauss, et al., 2015). For instance, the proportion of traditional extended families with three or more generations living together has dropped dramatically, from about 40% in 1982 to less than 5% after the 2000s (Li et al., 2020). Meanwhile, older Chinese have become increasingly independent in their living arrangements, with living alone (or solo-living) or living only with a spouse becoming much more common over time (Cheung & Yeung, 2015; Lei, Strauss, et al., 2015).

The coexistence of rapid changes in family living arrangements and the persistence of traditional family culture has created conditions for cultural discordance, where individuals' actual living arrangements may not align with prevailing cultural expectations. Consequently, a growing number of older adults find themselves in situations that deviate from the traditional ideal, potentially heightening their vulnerability to loneliness. This issue is particularly pressing in China, which now has the worlds’ largest and fastest-growing ageing population, with the proportion of people aged 65 and above rising steadily since 1978 (Lu & Liu, 2019). Recent research has documented a growing prevalence of loneliness among older adults (Wang, 2025), wich might be partially attributed to the misalignment between actual living arrangements and cultural standards, as predicted by Hypothesis 1.

Importantly, there is substantial regional heterogeneity in the strength of familistic culture across China. In some regions, such as remote villages, expectations for intergenerational coresidence and strong family ties remain deeply rooted, while in others, such as large cities, more individualistic living arrangements have become increasingly normalized (Zhao et al., 2022). Geographic variation is also evident, with southern China often placing greater value on family lineage than northern China (Zhang, 2020). This regional diversity provides a unique opportunity to empirically assess how family-oriented cultural norms moderate the relationship between living arrangements and loneliness among older Chinese, as predicted in Hypothesis 2.

More specifically, we use prefecture-level lineage genealogy (jia pu) density to capture regional variation in the strength of traditional family values. Genealogy records reflect the importance of family history and relationships among lineage members within and across generations, and serve as carriers of traditional family culture for two main reasons. First, genealogy records indicate an enduring interest in family origins, lineage continuity, intra-lineage connections, and intergenerational solidarity (Shiue, 2016). Second, family genealogy is closely associated with experiences of living in extended families, corresponding to the culturally ideal family life that emphasizes adult children's (often sons') filial piety and care responsibilities for supporting older parents by living in the same household. In this sense, family genealogy highlights the instrumental functions of the extended family as a resource and support system (Alesina & Giuliano, 2011), and the symbolic values of multigenerational households in reinforcing strong family ties and culture in Chinese society.

As shown in Fig. 2, the geographic distribution of this measure aligns with known cultural patterns, with southeastern regions generally exhibiting higher genealogy density (and thus stronger inferred family norms) than the northeast and southwest. By leveraging this measurable variation in our key moderating variable, we directly test whether and how local familistic norms condition the association between living arrangements and loneliness among older adults.

Family genealogy has been empirically validated as an appropriate measure of family culture. Using data from the China Family Panel Studies (CFPS), earlier research demonstrates that residents in regions with higher genealogy density are more likely to value family, maintain close relationships, and interact frequently with family members (Xie & Yuan, 2025). To further establish the validity of genealogy density as an indicator of family culture, we analyzed multiple survey datasets to examine its associations with older adults' living arrangement preferences (see Appendix A1). First, analysis of the Chinese Longitudinal Healthy Longevity Survey (CLHLS) reveals a significant positive relationship between prefecture-level genealogy density (the logged number of genealogies per million people) and older adults' preference for living with adult children (Column 1, Table A1). Second, data from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS) show that higher genealogy density is associated with a stronger belief that living with adult children is the ideal arrangement for older adults, regardless of spousal presence (Columns 2 and 3, Table A1). Additionally, genealogy density is positively linked to older respondents’ willingness to rely on adult children for financial support in late-life (Column 4, Table A1). These findings support the use of regional genealogy density as a valid proxy for traditional family culture, particularly the ideal of coresidence with adult children among older Chinese.

Despite its validity, regional confounders remain, including prefecture-level economic development, geographic constraints, migration flows, and healthcare infrastructure. To isolate the specific effect of cultural norms, our main multivariate models systematically account for key regional confounders such as GDP per capita and county-level share of plains. In robustness checks (detailed below), we further control for additional factors, including migration rates and healthcare infrastructure. Our core conclusions regarding the moderating role of family norms remain robust.

Methods

Data and sample

Our primary data source is the Chinese Longitudinal Healthy Longevity Survey (CLHLS) conducted by the Center for Healthy Aging and Development Studies at Peking University. The CLHLS launched its baseline survey in 1998, with follow-up surveys in 2000, 2002, 2005, 2008, 2011, 2014, and 2018. The survey covers 22 of the 31 provinces in mainland China, representing 85% of the population. While the earliest waves (1998 and 2000) targeted individuals aged 80 and above, the sampling frame was expanded in 2002 to include those aged 65 and above (Zeng, 2008), providing a more representative sample of older adults.

For our main analyses, we focus on the 2008, 2011, and 2014 waves. This selection is based on both sample representativeness and the feasibility of accurately linking individual records to prefecture-level demographic and economic data. Accurate geographic linkage is essential for our study, as it enables us to merge individual-level information with prefecture-level measures of traditional family culture (described below). For the 2008–2014 waves, we are able to recover prefecture-level identifiers for over 99% of respondents, ensuring high linkage precision. In contrast, the match rates for the 2002 and 2005 waves are substantially lower (about 85%), raising concerns about potential selection bias in geographic linkage.3 The 2018 wave was excluded due to the unavailability of the necessary geographic identifiers at the time of our study. Thus, the 2008–2014 waves offer the optimal combination of a consistently representative sample (ages 65+) and high-quality geographic linkage. To ensure the robustness of our findings, we also conduct sensitivity analyses that extend the sample to include the 2002 and 2005 waves (Appendix Table A2).

We restricted our sample to Han Chinese aged 65 and above, as the tradition of compiling multi-generational written genealogies is historically and culturally most established within this group (Harrell, 2001). Excluding all ethnic minorities resulted in a total sample of 28704 observations. Of these, 3792 (13%) had missing values for loneliness, and 359 (1%) have missing values for explanatory variables. Our final analytical sample consists of 24558 observations from 14226 older adults.

To measure traditional family culture, we used historical records of family genealogies from the General Catalog of Chinese Genealogy (GCCG), compiled by the Shanghai Library between 2000 and 2008. The GCCG is the most comprehensive directory of family genealogies in mainland China, containing 52401 entries across 608 surnames. This catalog was assembled in collaboration with over a thousand public institutions and private collectors, both within China and overseas. Each genealogy was systematically cataloged with metadata, including title, edition, and ancestral origin. We matched these genealogy records to the CLHLS data at the prefecture level.

A potential concern with the genealogy data is that some records may have been lost or destroyed over time. However, we argue that the preserved genealogies themselves reflect the local intensity of family culture, as regions with stronger traditions of family heritage were more likely to maintain and safeguard these records. Nevertheless, to further empirically test whether the potential loss of records biases our results, we conducted sensitivity analyses in which prefectures without recorded genealogies were assigned the minimum value of genealogy density observed in the overall sample.4 This adjustment did not substantially alter our results.

For supplementary analyses, we also utilized data from additional sources, including the CHARLS survey, the China City Statistical Yearbooks, population census, and other historical datasets. Further details on these data sources are provided in Appendix A2.

Measures

Loneliness. The CLHLS asked respondents, “Do you feel lonely?” with answers recorded on a five-point Likert scale: “always,” “often,” “sometimes,” “seldom,” and “never.” We constructed two measures of loneliness. First, we created a continuous measure (ranging from 1 to 5), reverse-coding options so that higher values indicate greater loneliness. Second, we constructed a binary indicator: responses of “always,” “often,” and “sometimes” were coded as 1 (“feel lonely”), while “seldom” and “never” were coded as 0 (“not feeling lonely”).5 Previous research has demonstrated that this global question of loneliness possesses strong face and predictive validity (Luo & Waite, 2014), is highly correlated with multidimensional loneliness scales (Russell, 1996), and provides meaningful information across diverse contexts (Newmyer et al., 2022).

Living arrangements. The household roster in the CLHLS records the relationship and basic sociodemographic characteristics of each person living with the focal older respondent. Based on family relationships, we classified living arrangements into four mutually exclusive categories: (1) living with adult children (with or without a spouse), (2) living alone, (3) living with the spouse only, and (4) living with others (individuals other than a spouse or adult children).6 The first category is our reference group, representing the culturally preferred arrangement (“cultural concordance”). The other three categories indicate “cultural discordance,” as these older adults do not co-reside with adult children.7 For simplicity, we refer to the reference group as “living with adult children” throughout the manuscript.

Due to data limitations, our four-category variable may oversimplify the reasons behind older adults’ living arrangements and does not capture nuances such as geographic proximity or intergenerational interactions with non-coresident adult children. However, supplemental analyses that account for the frequency of visits and contacts with non-coresident adult children yield consistent findings and support our main conclusions (see Section 5.3).

Family culture. We measured local family culture using the density of family genealogies in each prefecture. To adjust for population size, we normalized the number of genealogies by the prefecture's total population (in millions) as of the year 2000.8 We then applied the logarithm to genealogy density per million people to minimize the influence of outliers, and centered the measure by subtracting the mean logged density across all sampled prefectures to facilitate interpretation of regression results.

To ensure the robustness of our findings with respect to the treatment of prefectures without genealogies and alternative normalization methods, we conducted several sensitivity checks: (1) normalizing by prefecture-level total population in 1990, (2) assigning the average genealogy density of neighboring prefectures to those with zero records, and (3) applying both adjustments simultaneously. Across all specifications, the magnitude, direction, and statistical significance of our key coefficients remain stable (see Section 5.3).

Covariates. At the individual level, we control for well-established predictors of living arrangements and loneliness identified in the literature, including gender (male vs. female), age group (young-old, middle-old, oldest-old), marital status (married vs. unmarried), self-reported health (scored 1-5), educational attainment (illiterate, primary school, middle school and above), main occupation before age 60 (farmer, worker, manager, others), residential type (rural vs. urban resident), pension coverage, and the logged household income per capita in the previous year.

Given the complex and nonlinear relationship between aging and loneliness (Shen et al., 2024), we distinguish among young-old (65-74, reference group), middle-old (75-84), and oldest-old (85 and above) (Quadagno, 2022). Marital status (married = 1) serves as a proxy for intimate support,9 a key resource for mitigating loneliness among older adults (Hsieh & Hawkley, 2018). Because family culture may shape marriage patterns and intimacy (Rokach, 2024), we also included an interaction term between marital status and family culture.

Educational attainment, main occupation, pension coverage, and household income per capita are considered as indicators of socioeconomic status (SES), which are fundamental determinants of loneliness (Raymo & Wang, 2022). Rural/urban residence is also controlled for, given the significant rural-urban divide and the influence of the hukou system on mobility and resource access in China (Meng, 2012).10 Self-reported health is included due to its close correlation with loneliness (Cacioppo & Cacioppo, 2018; Luo et al., 2012; Surkalim et al., 2022).

At the regional level, we control for prefecture-level GDP per capita (logged, lagged by one year) as a summary measure of economic development, and the county-level share of plains, which historical research links to the development of clan-based cooperation and social structure (Noblit, 2022). These controls help isolate the cultural channels of family norms from broader geographic and economic confounders.11

To ensure the representativeness of our estimates, we apply sampling weights to all models. We cluster robust standard errors at the individual level to account for heteroscedasticity and serial correlation. The robustness of our main findings is further confirmed using alternative model specifications (e.g., probit and ordered probit regressions; see Section 5.3).

Other covariates also reveal interesting patterns. For instance, the oldest-old are more likely to live with adult children, while those living alone tend to have poorer self-rated health and lower household income. Respondents living with a spouse only are generally more educated, have higher occupational status, and are more likely to receive a pension. Genealogy density is relatively consistent across groups, except for a slightly higher mean among those living alone. These systematic differences highlight the importance of controlling for these factors in our multivariate analyses to evaluate whether the observed differences in loneliness persist after adjusting for potential confounders.

After including the genealogy density and its interactions with living arrangements (Column 2), the results confirm the baseline pattern. In a prefecture with average family culture strength, the predicted probability of loneliness remains significantly higher for those living alone (14.3%) and those living with others (14.8%) compared to the reference group (living with adult children). The main effect of genealogy density (γ0) is near zero and insignificant, consistent with H2a, as this reference group is in cultural concordance with traditional norms.

Crucially, the interaction terms are all positive, supporting H2. Specifically, the loneliness gap associated with living alone widens in stronger family culture regions (interaction: 0.017, p < 0.10). The gap for those living with a spouse only also increases significantly (0.023, p < 0.01). The point estimate for the interaction term of “living with others” category is sizable (0.038) but statistically insignificant, likely due to limited precision from its small sample size. Collectively, these results suggest that older adults not living with adult children—those in the state of “cultural disconcordance”—are more likely to feel lonely in regions with stronger traditional family norms.

The magnitude of these effects is substantial. For example, a one standard deviation (SD) increase in genealogy density (1.47; see Table 1) is associated with a 3.4 percentage point (1.47 × 0.023) larger gap in loneliness between those living with a spouse only and those living with adult children. This difference is about half the size of the loneliness gap between older adults with middle school or higher education and those without formal education. Similarly, a one SD increase in genealogy density corresponds to a 2.5 percentage point (1.47 × 0.017) larger gap in loneliness between those living alone and those living with adult children—almost half the difference observed between older adults with primary school education and those without formal education.

Columns 3 and 4, which use the five-level loneliness measure, reveal similar patterns, though the estimates for the interaction terms are less precise. In terms of magnitude, Column 3 shows that, all else being equal, average loneliness scores are consistently higher among older adults not living with adult children than among those who are: 0.326 points higher for those living alone, 0.058 points higher for those living with a spouse only, and 0.234 points higher for those living with others. Column 4 indicates that a one SD increase in genealogy density is associated with 0.041-point (1.47 × 0.028) larger difference in loneliness between those living alone and those living with adult children (although this result is not statistically significant), and a 0.047-point (1.47 × 0.032) larger difference between those living with a spouse only and those living with adult children points larger (p < 0.05).

Formal tests for subgroup differences (reported in the bottom panel of Table 3) confirm that most observed variations are not statistically significant, with the notable exception of the age-specific moderating effect of family culture on living alone. This highlights the context-dependent nature of family norms and suggests that support policies should consider the heightened cultural sensitivity of younger-old adults.

Robustness checks

We conduct a range of supplemental analyses to further assess the robustness of our main findings. First, to probe whether our results are driven by the intended cultural mechanism rather than by broader regional confounders, we conducted placebo tests using subsamples of ethnic minorities. Our reasoning is that the practice of compiling written genealogies is far less prevalent among most non-Han groups (Harrell, 1995).13 Thus, genealogy density should have limited explanatory power for these populations. If a significant effect of similar magnitude were observed among ethnic minorities, it would suggest our measure might be capturing shared regional factors rather than the specific cultural practice. Table 4 reports results from several such tests: (a) Columns 1 and 2 present regressions for ethnic minorities (non-Han) respondents; (b) Columns 3 and 4 restrict the sample to individuals from prefectures or counties designated as ethnic autonomous areas; and (c) Columns 5 and 6 further limit the sample to ethnic minority respondents living in ethnic autonomous regions. In each case, the first column uses the binary loneliness indicator, and the second uses the five-level measure. As expected, across all placebo tests, most interaction terms between living arrangements and genealogy density are statistically insignificant. Crucially, the estimated coefficients for these interaction terms do not exhibit a direction or pattern of association that is consistent with our main theoretical predictions. These results reinforce the credibility of our main findings, supporting the interpretation that they are linked to family culture and helping to rule out potential regional confounders.

Additionally, we performed a permutation placebo analysis by randomly assigning genealogy density values across prefectures and re-estimating our main model 2000 times. The resulting distributions of the permuted interaction coefficients are centered around zero, while our original estimates for key interaction terms (e.g., for living alone and living with a spouse only) fall in the extreme tails of these distributions, with permutation-derived p-values indicating statistical significance (Appendix Figure A1). This provides strong quantitative evidence that our core findings are unlikely to be due to chance, further supporting their robustness.

Second, we conduct a set of robustness checks to address potential sample restriction and selection biases (Appendix Table A2). Including respondents from the 2002 and 2005 waves who were successfully followed over time yields results similar to the benchmark estimates (Column 2 vs. Column 1). Moreover, migration among older respondents could lead to a mismatch between the family culture of their current prefecture and that of their birthplace. Analyses restricted to respondents who remained within their birth provinces confirm our core findings (Column 3).14 To account for potential survival bias due to varying mortality rates across age groups that may correlate with loneliness, we controlled for the wave-by-age-group fixed effects (interactions between survey waves and age-group dummies), and again found results consistent with the benchmark (Column 4).

Third, supplemental analyses using alternative measures of genealogy density yield the same conclusions (Appendix Table A3). Specifically, the results remain consistent with the benchmark estimates when we either excluded respondents from prefectures lacking genealogy records in the GCCG dataset (Column 2) or assigned these prefectures the minimum observed value of genealogy density from the overall sample (Column 3). Similarly, using the prefecture-level total population from 1990 for normalization, as well as simultaneously applying imputed density values and the 1990 population for normalization, produces comparable findings (Columns 4 and 5).

Fourth, our main findings remain robust to potential omitted variables (Appendix Table A4). Adding a comprehensive set of additional prefecture-level controls, including emigration rates in 2000, the number of hospitals per thousand population, the percentages of workers in secondary and tertiary industries, urbanization rate, and the unemployment rate, does not alter our results (Column 2). Similarly, controling for historical variables, including the impact of the Cultural Revolution, distances to the nearest commercial, silk, and tea center in the Ming-Qing dynasties, the distance to the nearest large city in 1920, and their interactions with various living arrangements, yields patterns consistent with the benchmark estimates (Column 3). Replacing the original province and survey wave fixed effects with province-by-wave fixed effects and adding city-specific time trends also produce similar results (Column 4).

Fifth, we also examine whether our results hold after accounting for the number of adult children, proximity to non-coresident adult children, and alternative sources of support (Appendix Table A5). Including the number of adult children has minimal impact on our findings (Column 2). Accounting for frequent visits from or contact with non-coresident adult children, as well as their interactions with family culture, also doest not alter our key results (Column 3). Furthermore, when controling for support from extended family members during illness, having someone to talk to within the extended family, and receiving community support, along with their interactions with family culture, results also remain robust (Column 4).

Finally, our results are consistent across different model specifications. Estimates from probit and ordered probit models yield qualitatively similar findings (Appendix Table A6). We also examined whether respondents’ loneliness in the previous wave influenced their current living arrangements (Appendix Table A7). Multinomial logistic regression results indicate that prior loneliness was not associated with current living arrangements, suggesting minimal endogeneity bias. This pattern holds when using sampling weights (Columns 1 and 3) and when addressing attrition bias through inverse probability weighting (Columns 2 and 4).

To summarize, all sensitivity checks corroborate our benchmark results. The estimated coefficients remained remarkably stable, and their changes were small relative to benchmark estimations. This pattern implies that the potential bias from unobserved confounders would need to be substantially stronger than the collective bias from all our observed controls to nullify our results, which we consider unlikely (Altonji et al., 2005). Overall, results from the robustness checks underscore the importance of family norms in shaping the relationship between living arrangements and loneliness, particularly the heightened loneliness experienced by older adults whose living arrangements deviate from culturally prescribed norms.

Discussion

Drawing on nationally representative survey data of older Chinese adults and historical records of family genealogies, this study investigates how family culture—measured by genealogy density at the prefecture level—shapes disparities in loneliness among older adults based on their family living arrangements. We hypothesized that “cultural concordance” (alignment between actual living arrangements and culturally valued norms) would be associated with lower loneliness, while “cultural discordance” (misalignment) would heighten loneliness, with these disparities becoming more pronounced as family norms strengthen.

Our findings provide robust empirical support for the cultural concordance-discordance framework. Older Chinese adults whose living arrangements are culturally concordant—specifically, those co-residing with adult children—consistently report lower and more stable levels of loneliness, regardless of the strength of local family norms. In contrast, those experiencing cultural discordance, such as living alone or only with a spouse, are significantly more vulnerable to loneliness, and this vulnerability is amplified in regions where traditional family culture is strongest. These results underscore that the emotional impact of living arrangements is not universal, but is shaped by their alignment with prevailing cultural expectations.

These findings illuminate the processes underlying the cultural concordance-discordance framework. When individuals’ living situations deviate from deeply rooted cultural norms, the resulting discordance intensifies feelings of loneliness, reflecting a cognitive discrepancy between actual circumstances and culturally prescribed ideals. While our study focuses on the role of cultural alignment, future research could benefit from considering perspectives such as social identity theory (Haslam et al., 2018), which suggests that concordance with cultural expectations may foster a sense of belonging and social identity, whereas discordance may undermine these aspects and contribute to emotional distress. By highlighting these processes, our study underscores the importance of integrating both cultural structures and psychological mechanisms to better understand how living arrangements affect well-being in later life.

It is important to acknowledge that cultural norms are dynamic and adapt to social change. As diverse living arrangements become more common, they may reflect emerging cultural standards rather than mere deviations from tradition. Thus, what appears as cultural discordance may, over time, signal cultural adaptation or pluralism. While our framework posits that cultural discordance contributes to loneliness, we also recognize that this impact may diminish as cultural norms evolve and diversify, and findings should be interpreted with an awareness of the dynamic interplay between social change and cultural evolution.

We also observe heterogeneity in these patterns by gender, age group, and residence area. Both older men and women are sensitive to local family culture in how their loneliness relates to living arrangements. While there are indications that older women's loneliness may be more influenced by family culture when living alone or with a spouse only, and that older men's loneliness may be more sensitive when living with a spouse only or with others, these gender differences are not statistically significant. Nevertheless, unlike previous research in other East Asian societies (Lee & Yeung, 2021), we do not find that family circumstances primarily affect older women's well-being; rather, unmet expectations regarding living arrangements are important for both genders.

Furthermore, the moderating effect of family norms is mainly observed among respondents aged 80 or below, with little evidence of such effects among those older than 80, despite higher overall loneliness in this group.15 This aligns with previous findings that loneliness increases at advanced ages (Dykstra, 2009), but our results reveals a reversed age pattern in how loneliness is influenced by living arrangements and family culture.

Finally, we find that the moderating role of family culture is evident primarily among rural residents, but not among their urban counterparts. While this aligns with previous research suggesting that traditional family norms are more influential in rural areas (Zhao et al., 2022), other factors, such as disparities in economic resources and access to social services, as well as differing values (urban individualism vs. rural collectivism) may also shape these patterns. Future research could employ mediational analyses, such as path models, to test whether factors like social network quality mediate these rural-urban differences and further clarify the underlying mechanisms.

This study has several limitations that future studies could better address with appropriate data and measures. First, our single-item loneliness measure, though validated (Luo & Waite 2014; Russell, 1996), may not fully capture the multidimensional nature of loneliness and may be subject to recall bias, particularly among the oldest old (Abedini et al., 2020; Cai, 2022). Future studies should develop culturally validated, multidimensional loneliness scales for the Chinese population. Second, our four-category classification of living arrangements offers only a limited perspective; factors like proximity to adult children and the quality of intergenerational relationship warrant further consideration (De Jong Gierveld et al., 2012; Lei, Shen, et al., 2015). Third, while our supplementary analyses suggest minimal concerns about reverse causality, the cross-sectional design cannot fully establish causality. Unmeasured confounders, such as grandchild care and household labor division (Sun et al., 2019), may influence co-residence with adult children, though this is less likely given the older age of most CLHLS respondents. Future research should utilize longitudinal data and incorporate measures of grandchild care and household labor division to strengthen causal inference.

Despite these limitations, this study makes several novel contributions. First, our cultural concordance-discordance framework extends the cognitive discrepancy perspective by showing how external cultural norms moderate the impact of living arrangements on loneliness, a nuance less explored in earlier work. Second, unlike prior cross-country studies that use broad indices like individualism-collectivism (e.g., De Jong Gierveld et al., 2012), we employ genealogy density as a more contextually grounded and historically informed measure of family culture, enabling finer-grained analysis of cultural variation within a single country. Third, by focusing on contemporary China, a society experiencing rapid sociodemographic change yet retaining strong family traditions, our findings offer insights that may be relevant for other societies with similar cultural contexts. Finally, our findings suggest important policy implications: interventions to reduce loneliness among older adults may be most effective when culturally tailored, particularly in regions where traditional family norms remain strong.

In sum, our findings provide a cultural perspective on the growing prevalence of loneliness among older adults in China, highlighting the importance of considering both living arrangements and local family culture. While similar concerns about family structure and loneliness have been noted in other societies with strong intergenerational ties, such as Japan and India (e.g., Dewari et al., 2025; Takagi & Saito, 2020), direct comparative evidence remains limited. Future research should conduct cross-cultural replications to assess the generalizability of our findings. We also encourage interdisciplinary approaches that combine sociological and psychological perspectives to further elucidate how cultural contexts shape loneliness in later life.

By tracing the respondents, we can impute respondents' residential prefectures and extend the sample back to the wave 2002. However, such an exercise can only be conducted among respondents who were successfully followed over time in the survey, resulting in potential selection bias.

Of the 186 prefectures included in the CLHLS sample, 20 (11%) have no genealogy records according to the GCCG data, which accounts for only 5.7% of CLHLS respondents.

Findings remain qualitatively unchanged when using an alternative coding scheme in which responses of “always,” “often,” “sometimes,” and “seldom” are coded as 1, and “never” is coded as 0 (results available upon request).

Respondents categorized as “living with a spouse only” or “living with adult children (with or without spouse)” may also reside with other individuals. Older adults living in nursing homes or institutions were classified as “living with others.”

For childless respondents, who comprise only 3.7% of the CLHLS sample, we also considered them as experiencing “cultural discordance.”

Population statistics for all prefectures were obtained from the 2000 Population Census. Following prior practice (Chen, Fan, et al., 2020; Zhang, 2020), we included only family genealogies compiled in or before 1950 to avoid confounding effects from more recent social and political changes.

Unmarried respondents included those who were widowed, divorced, and never married. The vast majority (98%) of them were widowed in our analysis sample.

Since the CLHLS does not record hukou status, rural/urban residence is a reasonable proxy.

Our core findings are robust to a range of additional prefecture-level controls (see Section 5.3). However, due to concerns over multicollinearity among these covariates and to maintain a parsimonious specification, our baseline model includes only GDP per capita and the share of plains as regional-level controls.

We do not plot the relationship between living with others and loneliness, as only 1% of the respondents lived with others in our sample (see Table 1).

Less prevalent practice of compiling written genealogies does not imply that family culture is weaker among ethnic minorities. In China, ethnic minorities may possess equally strong, but distinct forms of family culture that do not necessarily emphasize the same patterns of multigenerational co-residence or genealogy compilation.

85% of respondents in our sample still reside within their birth county, and an even higher proportion (93%) live within their birth province.

In our analytical sample, the average level of loneliness among people older than 80 is 0.36, 0.12 points larger than that of older adults aged 80 and below (significant at 1% level).
