Development and evaluation of multi-strategy fusion machine learning models for predicting postoperative recurrence in non-small cell lung cancer
Authors: Mingxuan Lu, Qi Dai, Hai Chen, Han Zhang, Jingfeng Zhang, Jianjun Zheng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundWe developed multiple machine learning models using various fusion strategies to predict the risk of recurrence within three years post-surgery in non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) patients. Background

Lung cancer remains a leading cause of cancer-related morbidity and mortality worldwide [1], with non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) constituting nearly 85% of all cases [2]. For patients with operable disease, surgical resection is generally regarded as the cornerstone of curative treatment [3]. However, between 13% and 62% of patients experience local recurrence or distant metastasis within the first three years after surgery, which significantly impacts postoperative survival and quality of life [4].

Current postoperative surveillance strategies primarily rely on imaging studies and serum tumor markers [5]. While these tools can detect recurrence or metastasis, their effectiveness is limited by suboptimal sensitivity and potential delays in detection, especially in asymptomatic patients. Conventional risk stratification, typically based on TNM staging and tumor differentiation, also lacks sufficient predictive accuracy for early identification of high-risk individuals [6].

Radiomics, which extracts a large number of quantitative descriptors from routine imaging data, provides an opportunity to noninvasively capture intratumoral heterogeneity and complement clinical and pathological information [7]. In parallel, advances in machine learning and artificial intelligence have enabled the integration of multimodal data, facilitating the development of fusion-based predictive models with enhanced performance and robustness [8].

Recent studies have explored various multimodal fusion strategies, including early, intermediate, and late fusion, to integrate clinical, radiomic, and other data sources for oncologic prognostication. These strategies aim to balance information retention, model interpretability, and predictive accuracy [8, 9]. However, systematic comparisons of different fusion paradigms in the context of postoperative recurrence prediction for NSCLC remain scarce. Therefore, the objective of this research was to design multiple fusion-based machine learning models to estimate the risk of postoperative recurrence in NSCLC over a three-year horizon, and to systematically evaluate and compare these strategies in terms of predictive performance and interpretability.

Methods

Patients

This retrospective study included patients who received curative surgical resection for lung cancer at our hospital between January 2019 and December 2021. The study was approved by the ethics committee of our institution, and the requirement for written informed consent was waived. The research was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and relevant guidelines and regulations.

The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) histopathological confirmation of NSCLC; (2) preoperative chest CT performed within one month before surgery; and (3) regular postoperative follow-up for at least three years. Exclusion criteria included: (1) receipt of radiotherapy, chemotherapy, or other tumor-related treatments prior to surgery; (2) a history of other malignancies or concurrent diagnosis of another malignancy; and (3) poor image quality or significant missing clinical data.

Based on these criteria, 102 patients with postoperative recurrence were identified. In addition, 204 patients without recurrence were randomly selected from the eligible population using a 1:2 case–control ratio. A total of 306 patients were finally included in the analysis. All patients were incorporated into a nested five-fold cross-validation framework for model development and evaluation (Fig. 1).

Flowchart of patient selection

The primary endpoint was recurrence within three years after surgery, including regional recurrence and distant metastasis. Diagnosis was based on radiologic findings, clinical assessment by experienced physicians, and, when available, pathological confirmation. For patients where pathological diagnosis was not feasible due to clinical constraints or ethical considerations, recurrence or metastasis was determined based on consistent imaging progression and longitudinal clinical follow-up. Clinical and pathological data

Baseline demographic and preoperative laboratory variables were collected for all enrolled patients, including sex, age, and body mass index (BMI), as well as hematologic indicators such as white blood cell count (WBC), red blood cell count (RBC), platelet count (PLT), neutrophil count, lymphocyte count, monocyte count, hemoglobin (Hb), and albumin (ALB). Anemia was defined as Hb < 130 g/L in males and < 120 g/L in females. Additional tumor markers including alpha-fetoprotein (AFP), carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA), carbohydrate antigen 125 (CA125), and carbohydrate antigen 19 − 9 (CA19-9). Postoperative pathological data were also collected, including histological subtype, T stage, and N stage, classified according to the 8th edition of the AJCC TNM staging system.

Although some variables contained missing values, the overall proportion of missing data was low. Missing values in continuous variables were imputed using the median to minimize potential bias. Sensitivity analyses were performed by developing clinical models using datasets before and after imputation. The results showed that the model performance was highly consistent between the two analyses. Using the imputed dataset, the clinical model achieved an AUC of 0.775 (95% CI: 0.721–0.829), while the model trained on the original non-imputed dataset achieved an AUC of 0.774 (95% CI: 0.715–0.830), indicating that median imputation did not substantially influence model performance. Detailed information regarding missing data and imputation procedures is provided in Supplementary Table S1.

Equipment and scanning methods

Chest CT examinations were acquired using several multi-detector spiral scanners, including the Optima 620 (GE Healthcare), Brilliance 16 (Philips), SOMATOM Definition AS + and SOMATOM Force (Siemens), uCT 530 and uCT 710 (United Imaging), and ScintCare CT 16P (MinFound Medical Systems).

Patients were positioned supine with both arms raised above the head to reduce beam-hardening artifacts. To reduce motion artifacts, all scans were acquired during a single breath-hold at full inspiration. The acquisition parameters included a tube voltage of 120 kV and automatic tube current modulation ranging from 110 to 153 mA. Images were initially reconstructed with 5 mm slice thickness and spacing, and additional thin-section datasets were generated with slice thickness and intervals of 0.7–1.26 mm using a standard lung reconstruction kernel and a 512 × 512 matrix. All images were saved in DICOM format for subsequent analysis.

Radiomics analysis

Image preprocessing and segmentation

To ensure consistent spatial resolution, all CT scans were resampled to an isotropic voxel size of 1 mm³ using cubic spline interpolation. To minimize inter-scan intensity variability resulting from differences in imaging equipment and acquisition settings, Z-score normalization was applied to the gray-level intensity values.

Following preprocessing, pulmonary nodules were automatically segmented using an AI-based segmentation tool integrated into the United Imaging research platform. The initial segmentation results were reviewed by a thoracic radiologist, who confirmed the accuracy of the tumor region of interest (ROI) and excluded unrelated pulmonary nodules. The segmentation was then reviewed and finalized by a senior radiologist, and the confirmed tumor ROI was used for subsequent radiomic feature extraction and analysis.

Radiomics feature extraction

Feature extraction was conducted with the PyRadiomics package. Extracted features covered several categories, including first-order statistics, shape descriptors, and multiple texture-based features derived from gray-level co-occurrence (GLCM), run-length (GLRLM), size-zone (GLSZM), dependence (GLDM), and neighborhood gray-tone difference matrices (NGTDM). In total, 2,264 radiomic features were obtained for each ROI.

Radiomics feature harmonization

Radiomic features were harmonized using the ComBat method to reduce scanner-related variability. Harmonization was performed at the feature level to adjust for batch effects associated with different CT scanners. To avoid potential data leakage, ComBat harmonization was implemented within the cross-validation framework, with parameters estimated separately in each training fold. A sensitivity analysis evaluating feature variability before and after ComBat harmonization using the entire dataset is provided in the Supplementary Materials (Supplementary Table S5 and Supplementary Figure S2).

Feature selection and model construction

Feature selection

To enhance the robustness of feature selection and reduce potential selection bias, a nested five-fold cross-validation strategy was employed. Feature selection was performed independently within each inner fold, and features consistently selected in at least three inner folds were considered robust and retained for subsequent model construction. If no features met this criterion, up to five features with the highest selection frequency across inner folds were retained. The feature selection procedures for different feature sets are described as follows.

Clinical Features:All clinical variables were first evaluated using univariate logistic regression. Variables showing statistical significance (p < 0.05) were subsequently entered into a multivariate logistic regression model, and those remaining significant (p < 0.05) were identified as independent predictors.

Model construction

Six predictive models were developed: a Clinical model, a Radiomics model, an Early Fusion model, two Intermediate Fusion models, and a Late Fusion model. The Clinical model was implemented using logistic regression, while the Radiomics, Early Fusion, and Intermediate Fusion models were constructed using a random forest classifier. The Late Fusion model was implemented using logistic regression. Model training and performance evaluation were conducted within a nested five-fold cross-validation framework. The detailed parameter settings are provided in Supplementary Tables S2.

The Clinical, Radiomics, and Early Fusion models were constructed based on their respective robust feature sets. Two intermediate fusion strategies were explored: (1) Feature-level Fusion, in which robust clinical and radiomic features were directly concatenated as model inputs; (2) Radscore-level Fusion, in which the computed radiomics score (Radscore) was combined with robust clinical features. The Late Fusion model was constructed by integrating the predicted probabilities from the Clinical and Radiomics models into a logistic regression classifier.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using R software (version 4.3.0) and Python (version 3.9). Descriptive statistics were used to summarize baseline clinical and pathological characteristics. Continuous variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation or median with interquartile range, as appropriate, and categorical variables were expressed as frequencies and percentages.

Model performance was evaluated using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC), accuracy, balanced accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, precision, and F1-score. The optimal probability threshold for each model was determined using the Youden index derived from the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve. The Youden index is defined as sensitivity + specificity − 1, and the threshold corresponding to the maximum Youden index was selected as the optimal cutoff. Performance metrics were subsequently calculated based on this threshold. ROC curves, calibration plots, and decision curve analyses (DCA) were generated to assess discrimination, calibration, and potential clinical utility, respectively.

All predictive models were developed and evaluated using a nested five-fold cross-validation framework. For each outer fold, out-of-fold (OOF) predicted probabilities were generated for the held-out patients. The OOF predictions from all folds were then concatenated to form a pooled prediction set, from which overall performance metrics (including AUC) were calculated. Pairwise comparisons of model discrimination were performed using DeLong’s test based on these aggregated cross-validated predictions.

Discussion

Although previous studies have demonstrated that combining clinical and radiomic features can enhance prognostic modeling in NSCLC [10, 11], most have relied on a single fusion strategy, leaving the relative advantages of different approaches largely unexplored. Our study expands upon this body of research by systematically evaluating distinct fusion strategies—early fusion, intermediate fusion, and late fusion—within a unified framework. Notably, despite variations in the included variables and their performance metrics, all fusion-based models demonstrated robust predictive ability for recurrence within three years after surgery. Our findings suggest that both intermediate fusion strategies (feature-level and Radscore-level) significantly outperform individual Clinical or Radiomics models, achieving higher AUCs and more balanced performance metrics, and outperform early and late fusion models as well.

Intermediate fusion is commonly implemented as Radscore-level fusion, integrating meaningful clinical variables with a composite radiomic score to construct nomogram-based models, which improve predictive performance while maintaining interpretability. For example, Wang et al. [10] combined N stage with a radiomics signature to stratify postoperative recurrence risk in stage IA-IIIA NSCLC patients, and D’Antonoli et al. [11] incorporated pathological TNM stage with intratumoral and peritumoral radiomics signatures to identify high-risk patients for 3-year recurrence.

In our study, Radscore-level fusion slightly outperformed feature-level fusion, likely due to differences in input feature representation. Radscore condenses multiple radiomic features into a weighted composite index, providing a compact and informative representation while reducing model dimensionality and computational burden [12]. Feature-level fusion directly incorporates three radiomic features and two clinical variables, requiring the model to learn complex relationships among heterogeneous features, which increases computational demand. Despite the very small difference in AUC (0.004), feature-level fusion offers greater interpretability, which may be advantageous when understanding individual feature contributions is clinically important. The feasibility and effectiveness of feature-level fusion have been demonstrated in prior studies. Yan et al. [13] showed that combining radiomic features with tumor location, hydrocephalus status, and clinical factors enabled accurate prediction of molecular subgroups in medulloblastoma. Similarly, another study reported that integrating multiregional MRI-based radiomic features with patient age significantly improved the prediction of IDH1 mutation status in glioblastoma [14]. Similar benefits of feature-level fusion have been reported such as predicting EGFR mutation status in lung adenocarcinoma [15, 16].

Early fusion, which integrates multimodal features at the initial stage, theoretically captures cross-modal interactions more directly. However, in our study, the early fusion model demonstrated no significant improvement over the single-source Radiomics model, and its discriminative performance was lower than both feature-level and Radscore-level fusion models. After feature selection, only radiomic features were retained, suggesting that concatenating clinical and radiomic variables early may introduce noise rather than improve prediction. This may result from the algorithm simultaneously learning relationships across heterogeneous features with differing dimensionalities, scales, and distributions; low-dimensional clinical variables may fail to establish stable associations with high-dimensional radiomic features [9].

Late fusion, or decision-level fusion, combines predicted probabilities from unimodal models using a subsequent classifier [17]. In our study, late fusion achieved higher performance than the Clinical model; however, it did not show a clear improvement over the single-source Radiomics model. This may be due to redundancy or biases from individual models and reduced interpretability, as contributions of individual variables cannot be directly assessed. In contrast, feature-level fusion allows transparent interpretation, for example via SHAP plots.

It should be noted that no single fusion strategy is universally optimal; the selection of an appropriate approach depends on the characteristics of the dataset and the specific predictive task [18]. Intermediate fusion appears particularly effective in leveraging complementary information while minimizing noise amplification or redundancy inherent in early or late fusion, and may offer better performance benefits and interpretability. In this study, feature-level fusion achieved favorable predictive performance while simultaneously providing enhanced interpretability, making it a suitable strategy for postoperative recurrence risk modeling.

To enhance feature selection robustness, we employed nested cross-validation-based stability selection, a strategy widely used in previous studies [19–21]. Our cross-fold analyses revealed that many radiomic features behaved as “chance features,” appearing in only a single fold. By retaining features consistently selected across multiple folds, we minimized selection bias and improved reproducibility and interpretability [22, 23].

Among the stable features, clinical variables such as age and CEA were repeatedly retained, while radiomic features including additivegaussiannoise_firstorder_Skewness and wavelet_gldm_wavelet-LLL-DependenceVariance were the most robust. CEA, a well-established biomarker in lung and colorectal cancers, is consistently associated with recurrence and metastatic risk [24, 25]. Older age has been linked to higher recurrence risk and poorer prognosis in NSCLC, potentially due to reduced immune surveillance, increased comorbidities, or differences in tumor biology. Skewness captures the asymmetry of the gray-level intensity distribution within the tumor, which may reflect intra-tumoral heterogeneity and the presence of regions with distinct cellular density or necrosis. The wavelet-transformed GLDM Dependence Variance quantifies variability in the size of homogeneous gray-level clusters, providing a measure of spatial heterogeneity at multiple scales. High intra-tumoral heterogeneity has been associated with aggressive tumor biology, therapy resistance, and increased likelihood of recurrence. Therefore, the repeated selection of these features suggests that imaging-derived heterogeneity metrics can serve as reliable indicators of postoperative recurrence risk in NSCLC. In line with this interpretation, a recent radiogenomics study in localized clear cell renal cell carcinoma demonstrated that CT-derived radiomic features were not only predictive of postoperative distant metastasis but were also significantly associated with key biological pathways involved in tumor progression and metastasis, providing biological support for the prognostic relevance of imaging-derived heterogeneity features [26].

This study has several limitations. First, as a retrospective analysis, it is inevitably prone to selection bias, and the clinical variables included were not comprehensive, as certain potentially relevant factors such as smoking status and comorbidities were not considered. Second, we constructed binary classification models primarily to compare the performance of different fusion strategies. As such, these models are better suited for preliminary risk stratification, and large-scale external validation will be necessary in the future. Third, the 1:2 recurrence-to-nonrecurrence ratio used in our study was artificially balanced to facilitate model training. This sampling approach may slightly overestimate recurrence probabilities when applied to populations with different prevalence, potentially affecting model calibration and generalizability. Fourth, the imaging data were acquired using six different CT scanners, and variations in scanning parameters and reconstruction algorithms could not be fully controlled. Finally, the models employed a random forest classifier, and the performance of alternative machine learning algorithms was not explored, which may limit the generalizability of our findings.

Conclusions

In this study, we developed and compared multiple unimodal and multimodal models to predict three-year postoperative recurrence in patients with non–small cell lung cancer using preoperative clinical and CT-based radiomic features. All fusion strategies demonstrated improved predictive performance compared with unimodal models. Among them, the feature-level fusion approach maintained favorable discriminative performance while providing improved model interpretability, indicating its potential utility for postoperative recurrence risk stratification.
