Molecular dynamics insights into non-covalent functionalization of boron nitride nanotubes for doxorubicin delivery
Authors: Zahra Ghavi, Abdolhalim Torrik, Mozafar Rezaee, Mahdi Zarif
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractDeveloping effective drug delivery systems is an ongoing challenge for the treatment of cancer, in particular, enhancing the efficacy of chemotherapeutic agents like doxorubicin. This study explores the potential of pristine and non-covalently functionalized boron nitride nanotubes (BNNTs) as carriers for doxorubicin, leveraging their chemical stability, biocompatibility, and mechanical strength. Molecular dynamics simulations were conducted to analyze the effect of adding functional groups such as chitosan, ethylene glycol, poly-l-lysine, and folic acid onto the surface of BNNTs. The impact of polymers, their chain length, and the number of functional groups on optimizing drug loading profiles was investigated. Functionalization of boron nitride has increased hydrogen bonding with water and drugs. As a result, boron nitride functionalization increases the solubility of BNNTs and enhances drug loading. Analysis of the radial distribution function shows that the non-covalent functionalization of boron nitride causes the drug to be more adsorbed on the nanotube wall. In contrast, the drug in the pristine nanotube is more trapped inside it. From van der Waals (vdW) interaction energies and free energy calculations, it was revealed that functionalization using poly-l-lysine enhanced the interactions between the carrier and doxorubicin. These findings underscore the potential of functionalized BNNTs as effective carriers for targeted cancer therapy.Subject terms: Drug delivery, Biophysical chemistry, Computational chemistry

Introduction

Drug delivery is one of the most fascinating and challenging research topics in the twentyfirst century. Submicron nanoparticles (3 to 200 nm) that are used as carriers in drug delivery systems include polymer nanoparticles, metal nanoparticles, protein nanoparticles, lipid nanoparticles, viruses, and organometallic compounds1. Various characteristics of the carrier, including size, shape, surface chemistry, hardness and softness, chemical composition, solubility, pH sensitivity, and surface potential, have a significant impact on the efficiency of the drug delivery process and drug delivery to the target tissue2–6. Although nanoparticles have many advantages, such as increasing drug delivery efficiency, drug protection, time-dependent drug release, and drug delivery to a specific tissue, they still have several challenges, including poor bioavailability, instability in blood circulation, toxicity, and high cost of clinical experiments. For this reason, the synthesis and introduction of a new carrier for a specific drug seem very necessary7–9. Cancer treatment with traditional methods, such as chemotherapy, has caused special attention to nanocarriers and nanomedicines due to undesirable side effects, low transfer efficiency of NPs to cancer tumors, and drug resistance. However, only a limited number of nanomedicines have been approved by the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) for clinical use. One of the drugs of interest for the treatment of cancer cells is doxorubicin (DOX)10–13.

Functionalizing nanocarriers to deliver therapeutic agents to specific sites within the body is one of the promising approaches in targeted drug delivery14,15. This can be achieved through the attachment of targeting ligands, antibodies, or peptides onto the surface of the nanoparticles and other carriers16,17. This enhances the binding to desired receptors within the diseased cells, resulting in an improvement in the treatment of cancers, infections, and autoimmune diseases18. Moreover, optimizing the release profiles of the therapeutic agents is also of importance, aiming at controlling the release under varying physiological conditions such as pH and temperature19–21.

Functionalization can be performed either covalently or non-covalently18. In covalent functionalization strong chemical bonds between the carrier and functional groups are formed, this method allows the incorporation of small functional groups22–26. In the non-covalent functionalization approach, physical interactions such as hydrogen bonding, electrostatic interactions, and van der Waals forces are utilized to attach targeting moieties to the surface of carriers27–29. Apart from allowing the incorporation of multiple types of ligands, this method has the advantages of being reversible, preserving the properties of the carrier, offering flexibility in design, and reducing the risk of toxicity30,31.

Chemical stability, chemical inertness, low cytotoxicity, biocompatibility, thermal conductivity, good mechanical strength, and high thermal stability32–34 of BNNTs attract special attention for various applications such as sensors35, catalysts36, hydrogen storage37, and drug delivery systems. These properties seem excellent for a drug carrier in a drug delivery system38–41. For this reason, the use of boron nitride nanomaterials as carriers in drug delivery systems is increasing dramatically42–44. These nanomaterials bind to the drugs with appropriate adsorption energy or trap the drugs inside them and deliver the drugs to the desired cancer tissue where the drugs should be released45. Boron nitride nanotubes can interact with various biomolecules, including gene and proteins, as well as with chemotherapy drugs, via both physical and chemical interactions. Among the nano medicinal materials, hexagonal boron nitrides (h-BNs) have low toxic effect, and they are suitable to achieve successful drug carrying system. On the other hand, they may form hydrogen bonds due to their typical sp2 hybridized B–N bonds which result in biocompatibility problems. To alleviate this problem, it is needed to have some modifications in the dimension and surface of the nanomaterials. Therefore, nano material can be kept inside the living cell without any side effects35,46–54.

Molecular simulation has been done to study various nanostructures such as carbon materials, polymers, metal nanoparticles, and proteins as carriers in drug delivery systems55–58. Unfortunately, computational chemistry studies on drug delivery with boron nitride nanotube carriers are limited. For this reason, it is necessary to use molecular simulation studies on boron nitride nanomaterials in order to develop novel drug delivery systems.

There are numerous studies on the functionalization of carbon nanotubes (CNTs) and BNNTs, exploring their potential applications in various fields such as biomedicine and materials science. In their study, Shim et al. modified the surface of single-walled carbon nanotubes by means of functionalization with surfactants and polyethylene glycol (PEG) to enhance their biocompatibility and also reduce nonspecific protein adsorption59. Hilder et al. utilized molecular dynamics simulations to demonstrate that BNNTs can mimic and act like biological ion channels. They showed that BNNTs embedded in a silicon nitride membrane can be modified such that they can be catione- or anition-selective, driven by the nanotube diameter, suggesting that functionalization methods, such as polymer wrapping, could enhance the performance of the BNNTs in biosensors, antibiotics, or filtration devices60. Further, Dehaghani et al. investigated the self-insertion of the protein SmtA, also known as metallothionein, into BNNTs utilizing explicit solvent molecular dynamics simulations. They identified the van der Waals interactions –caused by the hydrogen bonds between the SmtA and BNNT– as the main driving force for protein insertion, making the complex pass the energy barrier of hydrogen bonds between the protein and network of water molecules present at the entrance of the BNNT, showing that the encapsulation process occurred automatically61. Zhi et al. presented a route for synthesizing multiwalled boron nitride nanotubes (MW-BNNTs) via carbon-free chemical vapor deposition; they also developed an original approach to functionalize BNNTs covalently. Their process involved reacting COCl group of stearoyl chloride and amino groups on the BNNTs, leading to an enhancement in solubility in organic solvents and also paving a way for tuning the band gap of the BNNTs, showcasing the relevance of covalent functionalization in BNNT applications62. Additionally, Ahadi et al.63 investigated the hydrogen storage capacity of MW-BNNTs using the grand canonical Monte Carlo method under varying temperatures and pressures. They focused on the structural parameters of the nanotube –such as wall number and nanotube diameter– on hydrogen adsorption capacity –fitting the Langmuir and Langmuir–Freundlich equations to the hydrogen adsorption data– and revealing that adsorption increases with the distance between the tubes and tube diameters, attributed to the van der Waals interactions, suggesting the potential of MW-BNNTs in hydrogen storage applications.

All of these studies highlight the diverse applications of CNTs and BNNTs and the importance of their functionalization in fields such as biomedicine and materials science; however, they have generally focused on covalent modifications or a limited set of functional groups. Moreover, prior MD studies have not systematically examined how the number of such functional groups affects drug loading.

In our study, we evaluate the effectiveness of non-covalent functionalization of BNNTs for the delivery of DOX in cancer treatment applications. We have selected several biologically compatible non-covalent functionalization groups, including chitosan (CS), polyethylene glycol (PEG), poly-l-lysine (PLL), and Folic Acid (FA). To achieve this, we employed molecular dynamics simulations to analyze the interactions between DOX and the functionalized BNNTs, examining parameters such as number of contacts, number of hydrogen bonds, vdW interactions, RDF, and binding free energies to evaluate loading efficiency in both surface-bound and encapsulated modes..

The remainder of this paper is structured as follows: In section “Conclusion”, we outline our model and the numerical simulation techniques employed in this research. Section “Results and discussions” presents a discussion of the results obtained from various analyses. Finally, we conclude in section “Conclusion”.

Results and discussions

Number of contacts

In drug delivery systems within the body, both the carrier and the drug are exposed to an aqueous environment. Consequently, understanding the behavior of these systems during their interaction with water molecules is essential. The behavior of drug delivery systems in aqueous environments significantly influences the performance of the drug-carrier complex. The analysis of the number of contacts between the carrier molecules and the carrier-drug system with solvents plays an important role in this context, as it determines the solubility of the carrier and the interaction of the drug with the carrier. The number of contacts between pristine boron nitride nanotube (BNNT) molecules and non-covalently functionalized nanotubes (FBNNT) with chitosan, polyethylene glycol, polylysine, and folic acid (BNNT-1Cs, BNNT-2Cs, BNNT-1PEG, BNNT-2PEG, BNNT-3FA, BNNT-7FA, BNNT-1PLL, BNNT-2PLL) with water molecules at the first and last time steps of the simulation, as well as the percentage change in the number of contacts, are presented in Table 1. The analysis reveals that the interaction of carrier molecules with water molecules is prominent. Furthermore, functionalization leads to reduced interactions between the carrier-drug system and water molecules, as evidenced in Table 1. The number of contacts between functionalized boron nitride and water molecules decreases with an increase in the number of functional groups. This indicates a competitive interaction in which functional groups displace water molecules, a common phenomenon in solid dispersion formulations64,65. More importantly, the percentage change in the number of contacts between FBNNT and water molecules indicates the amount of drug loaded onto the carrier, which results in a decrease in the number of contacts with water molecules. For the systems BNNT-1Cs, BNNT-2PLL, and BNNT-1PEG, this change is 35.22%, 34.21%, and 30.45%, respectively, which correlates with the higher drug adsorption observed on these compounds (Table 5). The relationship between the number of contacts and drug loading is further supported by studies demonstrating that drug loading is greater on modified carriers66,67.

Number of hydrogen bonds

In the context of drug delivery systems, hydrogen bonding plays a crucial role in determining the solubility and interaction dynamics among carriers, drugs, and solvents. The Hbond GROMACS program was utilized to analyze hydrogen bonding between the drug delivery systems and water as a solvent, as well as between the drug and the carrier. Hydrogen bonds were evaluated using radial and angular cutoffs of 0.35 nm and 30 degrees, respectively, for atoms capable of forming hydrogen bonds. To assess the number of hydrogen bonds, we employed two approaches: (1) Hydrogen bonding between carrier molecules and functionalized components on the carrier in a water solvent indicates the solubility of both pristine and functionalized boron nitride carriers. The initial assessment of hydrogen bonding at the beginning of the simulation is crucial, as it establishes a baseline for understanding how functionalization alters the solubility profile of the carrier. (2) Second approach examines hydrogen bonding between the drug (DOX) and both pristine and functionalized carriers, highlighting the significance of these interactions in the overall efficiency of the drug delivery process.

The number of hydrogen bonds between the carrier and solvent in all studied systems is presented in Table 2. The functionalization of boron nitride has enhanced hydrogen bonding with water compared to pristine boron nitride. The increase in functional groups has resulted in a greater number of hydrogen bonds. Among the functional groups, chitosan showcased the most significant impact on increasing hydrogen bonding, thereby enhancing solubility. This finding aligns with previous studies that have demonstrated that modifying carriers can substantially improve the solubility of nanocarriers in aqueous media68,69. Additionally, Naqvi et al. showed that incorporating hydrophilic functional groups onto carbon nanotubes led to a significant increase in their solubility in water, which parallels the results observed in the current study regarding boron nitride70.

Hydrogen bonding interactions between drugs and drug delivery systems (including carriers and their functional groups) are summarized in Table 3. Stronger hydrogen bonding interactions between drugs and carriers can enhance the stability of the drug-carrier complex, thereby improving the drug’s release profile. In all cases, the functionalization of boron nitride has increased the number of hydrogen bonds between the drug and the carrier compared to pristine boron nitride. When a functional group is present on the nanotube, chitosan has the most significant effect on increasing hydrogen bonding. This is supported by research indicating that chitosan, due to its hydrophilic nature and ability to form multiple hydrogen bonds, can significantly enhance the solubility and stability of drug formulations71. Boron nitride-polylysine (BNNT-2PLL) exhibits the highest number of hydrogen bonds with the drug. The presence of seven folic acid molecules on boron nitride has increased the average number of hydrogen bonds to 1.890. This is particularly relevant in targeted drug delivery applications, where folic acid is often employed to enhance the specificity of drug carriers for cancer cells. For instance, Chen et al. demonstrated that folic acid-conjugated carriers exhibit improved cellular uptake and therapeutic efficacy, primarily due to enhanced interactions with the drug72. Similarly, the efficacy of folic acid in the development of targeted nanoparticles that significantly improve antitumor drug delivery was reported in 201573. Further studies indicate that folic acid-modified carriers can achieve higher loading capacities and better release profiles for anticancer drugs, thus enhancing therapeutic outcomes74. In contrast, the functional group PEG has the least effect on increasing hydrogen bonding between the carrier and the drug. While PEG is widely recognized for its ability to improve solubility and biocompatibility, its role in enhancing hydrogen bonding may be limited compared to other functional groups such as CS or FA. These findings suggest that the selection of functional groups in drug delivery systems should be carefully considered to optimize both solubility and drug-carrier interactions.

The van der Waals (vdW) interactions

The data presented in Table 4 indicates that the vdW interaction energy between BNNT and DOX molecules in the pristine BNNT system is − 191 kJ/mol, which suggests a favorable interaction between the carrier and the drug. Additionally, this baseline value serves as a reference point for evaluating the effect of functionalization on the interaction energies. The favorable interactions between the pristine BNNT and the DOX molecules can be attributed to – interactions and covalent bonding. Furthermore, in the rdf section (Table 5), it can be observed that six DOX molecules are encapsulated within the BNNT, contributing to the high value of the vdW interaction energies.

For the BNNT-1CS system, the vdW interaction energy is − 175 kJ/mol. This indicates that the introduction of CS has weakened the interactions between the carrier and the drug. However, the hydroxyl and amino groups present in CS can promote non-covalent interactions, such as hydrogen bonding and electrostatic interactions75. The reduced interaction in this case is attributed to the encapsulation of only one DOX molecule inside the BNNT. In the BNNT-2CS system, the vdW interaction energy between the carrier and DOX further decreases to − 138 kJ/mol. Thus, the addition of another CS results in lower interaction energies, which fall below those of the BNNT-1CS system. This reduction can be ascribed to the steric effects and relative bulkiness of the CS groups, which influence the loading of DOX76 and result in the absence of drug molecules inside the BNNT.

In the BNNT-1PEG configuration, the vdW interaction energy is − 170 kJ/mol, which is comparable to that of the BNNT-1CS system. While PEG can positively contribute to the interaction between the carrier and the drug, the absence of DOX encapsulated within the BNNT results in vdW energies that fall below the baseline. Furthermore, similar to the BNNT-2CS system, the addition of another PEG in the BNNT-2PEG configuration leads to a reduction in the vdW interaction energies to − 122 kJ/mol. This decrease is attributed to the steric effects of the two PEG groups, which can limit the interaction between the carrier and the drug, particularly in the absence of any drugs encapsulated within the BNNT.

The vdW interaction energies for systems functionalized with folic acid, specifically BNNT-3FA and BNNT-7FA, are − 122 kJ/mol and − 107 kJ/mol, respectively. Modifying the carriers with FA can enhance their targeting capabilities due to its affinity for folate receptors, which are overexpressed in certain cancer cells77,78. However, the interaction energies indicate that the addition of FA to BNNT negatively impacts the loading of DOX. This phenomenon can be attributed to FA-FA intermolecular interactions, which can be detrimental and hinder effective79. Furthermore, addition of more FA resulted in reduced interactions between the carrier and the drug.

The analysis of systems containing PLL reveals more favorable vdW interaction energies between the carrier and the drug compared to other functional groups. It was observed that the interaction energy is − 146 kJ/mol for the BNNT-PLL configuration and − 183 kJ/mol for the BNNT-2PLL configuration. This indicates that the presence of two PLL groups enhances the stability of the carrier-drug system. This improvement can be attributed to the increase in the number of hydrogen bonds, which are quantified as 0.837 for BNNT-PLL and 3.518 for BNNT-2PLL. The increase in hydrogen bonds contributes to the enhanced binding of the drug-carrier system, as evidenced by the vdW interaction energies.

Radial distribution function

The radial distribution function (RDF) analysis is a crucial tool for understanding the interactions between drug molecules and nanocarriers, as well as for determining the positioning of drug molecules within or outside the carrier. The RDF plots illustrate the spatial distribution of DOX molecules around and within BNNTs, providing critical insights into drug adsorption and interaction dynamics. Figure 1 presents the radial distribution function plot for the interaction between the DOX drug and the boron nitride carrier in its pristine form, as well as when non-covalently functionalized with chitosan, polyethylene glycol, polylysine, and folic acid groups. These plots are generated from the last 3 ns of the simulation, where the peaks represent the adsorption of the doxorubicin molecule. The RDF analysis is depicted as a function of the distance from the center of mass of the BNNT axes for all systems. Peaks below 1 nm are associated with the adsorption and entrapment of DOX drug molecules within the BNNT nanotube. Furthermore, the RDF peaks around 1.5 nm indicate drug adsorption on the outer surfaces of the nanotubes, a phenomenon also reported by Mirsalari et al.80. The most pronounced peak below 1 nm belongs to the BNNT-DOX system, which contains the highest number of drug molecules within the nanotube. The quantity of DOX trapped in this system has resulted in the highest (absolute) van der Waals interaction energy of − 2482.565 kJ/mol (Table 5). The systems BNNT-1Cs, BNNT-3FA, BNNT-1PLL, and BNNT-2PLL exhibit a peak below 1 nm, each containing one drug molecule within their structure. This has led to an increase (in absolute value) in the van der Waals interaction energy for these compounds. Additionally, all nine structures investigated in this study display a peak in the 1.5 nm region. The peak for the BNNT-DOX system in this region is weaker than those of the other systems, indicating that it has the fewest drug molecules (six) adsorbed on the wall and above. Overall, all structures examined in this research have successfully encapsulated drug molecules, reinforcing the notion that while encapsulation dynamics may vary, the overall interaction between DOX and BNNTs remains consistent across different configurations. The findings of this article align with the study by Permyakova et al., which noted that the physicochemical interactions between DOX and boron nitride carriers significantly influence drug loading capacities and stability44.

From the table, it is evident that electrostatic interactions are negligible. The SASA energies are predominantly in the range of approximately − 50 kJ/mol to 75 kJ/mol. In contrast, polar solvation energy and the vdW interaction energies are the primary contributors to the overall binding energies.

Starting with the pristine BNNT system, the binding energy between BNNT and DOX molecules is − 2130.862 kJ/mol. This value indicates highly favorable interactions between the carrier and the drug. The strong binding primarily results from vdW interaction energy, making pristine BNNT a favorable candidate for drug delivery. In the BNNT-1CS system, the binding energy of the carrier-drug complex is − 1950.156 kJ/mol. Although this value is significantly negative and indicates a favorable interaction, it is lower than that of the pristine system. In the BNNT-2CS system, the binding energy further decreases to − 1528.690 kJ/mol. As previously mentioned in the vdW section, the increased steric interactions resulting from the addition of more CS groups hinder effective binding. This suggests that incorporating additional CS groups does not optimize drug loading.

The configuration BNNT-1PEG has a binding energy of − 1891.565 kJ/mol, indicating a relatively high interaction, although it remains lower than that of the pristine BNNT system. PEG can enhance binding by improving solubility; however, it does not surpass the stability of the pristine BNNT. With the addition of more PEG, the BNNT-2PEG system exhibited a further reduction in binding energy to − 1376.523 kJ/mol. Given that most of the binding arises fromthe vdW interactions, this reduction is attributed to increased steric hindrance from the two PEG groups. This suggests that while PEG enhances solubility, excessive functionalization, as seen in the case of BNNT-2CS, can diminish binding affinity.

Binding energies for the FA functionalized systems, BNNT-3FA and BNNT-7FA, are − 1385.034 kJ/mol and − 1205.124 kJ/mol, respectively. These values suggest that the incorporation of FA does not improve binding energy. As mentioned in the vdW section, unfavorable intermolecular interactions, particularly FA-FA interactions, may impede effective drug loading. In the BNNT-1PLL configuration, the binding energy is − 1648.440 kJ/mol. In contrast, the BNNT-2PLL system exhibits a more favorable binding energy of − 2031.692 kJ/mol, indicating that the addition of a second PLL group significantly enhances binding affinity. This improvement is attributed to the increased vdW interactions, which contribute to a more stable drug-carrier complex.

Overall, the analysis of binding free energies reveals that while functionalization can affect the binding affinity of the drug-carrier system, it can also introduce steric hindrance at higher degrees of functionalization, potentially negatively impacting interactions. Therefore, careful design of functionalized systems is essential to maximize the benefits of binding interactions while improving biocompatibility, reducing potential toxicity, minimizing adverse effects, enhancing targeted delivery, and controlling release.

In contrast, the DOX-1CS system, with a binding energy of − 37.129 kJ/mol, demonstrates a less favorable interaction with DOX. The data reveal that vdW interactions significantly influence the overall binding energy, as evidenced by the DOX-2PEG system, which has a binding energy of − 324.489 kJ/mol, underscoring the effectiveness of PEG.

Conclusion

One of the important aspects of cancer treatment is the design of an effective drug delivery system. A suitable carrier should have strong interactions with drug molecules and sufficient water solubility to ensure their delivery to target cells. This research focuses on the use of molecular dynamics simulations to analyze the effects of functionalization of pristine and non-covalently functionalized BNNTs with chitosan, ethylene glycol, poly-l-lysine, and folic acid as carriers for DOX. The functional groups used in this study increased the hydrogen bonding capacity of the drug-carrier complex with water molecules, which increased the solubility of the complex. Among these, the chitosan functional group had the greatest effect on increasing hydrogen bonding and, consequently, solubility. In all cases, functionalization of boron nitride led to an increase in the number of hydrogen bonds between the drug and the carrier compared to pristine boron nitride. Unlike hydrogen bonding, the van der Waals energy decreases with functionalization of boron nitride. This decrease is due to the trapping of more drug molecules inside the pure boron nitride and the steric repulsion effects in the functionalized boron nitrides. In functionalized boron nitride, most drug molecules stick to the surface and wall of the boron nitride, and the hydrogen bonding effects in binding energy overcome the van der Waals energy. The hydrogen bonding and van der Waals energy results are in complete agreement with the RDF analysis. The RDF analysis shows a prominent peak below 1 nm for the BNNT-DOX system, which is related to the trapping of 6 DOX molecules inside the pure boron nitride and consequently the highest absolute van der Waals interaction energy associated with it. In the region of about 1.5 nm, all the structures show a peak, indicating that all the structures investigated in this study have adsorbed drug molecules on their walls and on their surfaces. It is noteworthy that the BNNT molecule shows the smallest peak in this region, indicating that this structure has adsorbed the least drug molecules on its walls and consequently has the least hydrogen bonding with drug molecules. The binding free energy was higher for the poly-l-lysine functionalized systems, indicating effective encapsulation and strong adsorption of drug molecules on this carrier, consistent with the van der Waals, electrostatic, SASA, and hydrogen bonding energies appropriate for this structure. In contrast, systems with other functional groups—such as chitosan, polyethylene glycol, and folic acid—showed lower binding energies. In summary, functionalization of BNNTs in this study significantly improved the solubility of this carrier and affected the loading rate and the drug content of DOX. Therefore, the selection of functional groups and the degree of functionalization are crucial to enhance the effectiveness of BNNTs as carriers for DOX.

Model and methods

Initial structures and topologies

First, we constructed a single-walled BNNT in an armchair (14,14) configuration, with a diameter of 2 nm and a length of 3 nm using the Nanotube Modeler package82. The structure of the BNNT is illustrated in Fig. 4. The molecular structure of the DOX was retrieved from the DrugBank database83 and is shown in Fig. 5. PEG was generated from its monomeric unit, while CS and FA were obtained from the PubChem database84; all are depicted in Fig. 6. Furthermore, hydrogen atoms were added to the BNNT structure at terminally unsaturated parts to achieve a stable configuration. Additionally, the number of monomeric units for CS, PEG, PLL, and the number of FA molecules were carefully selected to ensure comparable molecular weights. Finally, geometry optimization of all molecular structures was performed using the GAMESS software, employing the B3LYP hybrid functional in conjunction with the 6-31G(d,p) basis set85.

Using molecular dynamics, we separately added PEG, PLL, and CS as one-chain and two-chains, whereas FA was added as three and seven chains. All of these numbers were chosen to make their respective molecular weights comparable. Fig. 7 depicts two example systems.

Topology files for DOX, FA, PEG, and CS were generated using the Automated Topology Builder (ATB) server86, whereas for BNNT, we used the x2top program in GROMACS with the combination of parameters from Ref.41,87. Additionally, corrections to the bonds, angles, torsion, and dihedral angles were applied in the topology created by x2top.

Partial charges

To obtain the partial atomic charges, population analysis was executed using GAMESS software. Because of the limitations, we created a smaller BNNT (around 100 atoms), generated the partial atomic charges for this structure, and then transferred the results to the main BNNT structure, as in previous studies88,89.

Simulation box

Nine boxes, each having dimensions nm3 and containing different BNNT configurations, were created with periodic boundary conditions. Additionally, we added 12 DOX molecules positioned in fixed positions in each box. We then proceeded to solvate each box and add ions to the charged systems for electro-neutrality. Molecular dynamics simulations

We conducted the simulations using GROMACS 5.1.15 software90,91 utilizing the GROMOS 54A792 force field. Additionally, we used the SPC model93 for water. Moreover, for non-bonded interactions, the cut-off radius was set to 1.2 nm. Furthermore, long-range electrostatic forces were computed using the Particle Mesh Ewald (PME)94 method.

After the solvation process, we used the steepest descent method to relax the system, minimize energy, and eliminating unnatural stresses. Further, to reach the intended temperature and pressure, it is necessary to equilibrate the system. This is achieved by fixing the temperature at 310 K and constraining the positions of all species using the LINCS method95. We utilized canonical ensemble and velocity rescaling thermostat96 for equilibration. Additionally, the Berendsen pressure coupling algorithm97 was applied to equilibrate the system at constant temperature and pressure (1 atm). It is important to note that we used the leap-frog integrator for equilibration steps.

We used the same sets of parameters with a total simulation time of 40 ns and a 2 fs time step for production MD simulations, with the exception of removing the position restraints for all species except BNNT. We used 40 ns as the total simulation time due to the limitations in computational resources and also the fact the all of our systems reached equilibrium in less than 20 ns. This is evident from the root mean square deviation plots inside the supplementary information, i.e. Figures S1-S9. After the simulations were completed, the last 3 ns of each trajectory were extracted for further analysis. Visual Molecular Dynamics (VMD)98 software (version 1.9.3) (available via http://www.ks.uiuc.edu/Research/vmd/) was used to represent and visualize the molecular structures shown in Figs. 2, 3, 4, and 7.
