Identification of pharmaceuticals and environmental contaminants as obesogens inducing a locomotion-independent thrifty phenotype
Authors: Sara Al Kassir, Théo Mercé, Laure M. Bourcier, Sandra Pedemay, Magalie Soares, Anja Knoll-Gellida, Patrick J. Babin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Obesity is a global pandemic that affects people of all ages. While behavioral, nutritional, and socioeconomic factors all contribute to weight gain, other factors in our immediate environment play an insidious role in the prevalence of obesity. Exposure to environmental and pharmaceutical compounds can be a contributing factor to increased weight gain. Using the zebrafish obesogenic test, our study demonstrates that amiodarone, dibutyl phthalate, rosiglitazone, tributyltin, and triclosan induce a thrifty phenotype under short-term fasting conditions. Diazepam significantly reduces locomotion without exhibiting any obesogenic effect, whereas tributyltin, which has the highest obesogenic potential among the tested compounds, has no effect on locomotion. The obesogen-induced resistance to fat loss is not correlated to inhibition of physical activity and a corresponding reduction in energy expenditure, nor to food consumption. Primary prevention measures to fight against the obesity pandemic may include reducing exposure to obesogens that can induce a thrifty phenotype.

Introduction

Obesity is defined as a condition in which abnormal excessive body fat accumulation causes adverse effects on health. High energy intake and a sedentary lifestyle have always been considered the key factors contributing to obesity. However, the obesity epidemic may only be partially due to human behavior. Exposure to compounds called obesogens, most of which are chemicals that disrupt metabolism, including endocrine disruptors, also contributes to the rising prevalence of obesity1–3. Obesogenic substances can have an impact on the basic homeostasis function of the metabolic system by acting on factors that regulate the balance between energy intake and expenditure. In a living organism, obesogenic substances can increase adiposity through various functional or anatomical changes, defined as their mode of action (MoA). However, the specific mechanism of action at the molecular level often differs from one compound to another. Increases in body mass index (BMI) may be induced by adipocyte hypertrophia and/or by hyperplasia, i.e., the production of new fat cells. Obesogens can modulate the commitment of mesenchymal stem cells and/or their differentiation into adipocytes1,2. They can also alter the energy balance by disrupting the control of satiety and/or affecting the way food is digested in the gastrointestinal tract and the nutrients absorbed and metabolized1,2.

The thrifty phenotype hypothesis of Hales and Barker4 proposed that limited fetal nourishment produces lasting glucose-insulin metabolism changes that prepare the resulting infants to better endure low levels of nutritional intake by more efficient use and conservation of energy. This phenotype can prove harmful later on when such an individual’s diet is no longer limited5,6. Notably, epidemiological associations between low weight at birth and the development of metabolic syndrome later in life led to the thrifty phenotype hypothesis, which in turn spawned the field of developmental origins of health and disease7.

Similar to reduced fetal nutrition, exposure to obesogens during early stages of human development can reprogram metabolic pathways, leading to a thrifty phenotype that predisposes individuals to obesity and associated disorders, but how this occurs remains poorly understood. It has been demonstrated in zebrafish and mice that some obesogenic compounds can prevent fat loss and even promote fat accumulation during starvation. The chemical compound tributyltin (TBT) and the drug rosiglitazone (RGZ) have been shown to induce a thrifty phenotype in fasting zebrafish larvae compared to untreated animals8–11. In this species, TBT at low nanomolar concentrations increased adiposity and induced adipocyte hypertrophy within hours of exposure using nuclear receptor pathways9. TBT exposure also induced a thrifty phenotype in male mice in which calories were more readily stored than expended, even during fasting conditions12–14. This obesogenic effect of TBT is epigenetically transmissible over at least three mouse generations by altering gene expression patterns related to adipogenesis and metabolic regulation12–14. It has been suggested that the molecular mechanisms underlying TBT-induced transgenerational obesity are related to altered higher-order chromatin structure13,15,16.

Zebrafish is an extensively studied model organism used to study vertebrate development, physiology, and disease. This model is also suitable for predicting the adverse effects of compounds on environmental and human health. The evolutionary conservation of lipid metabolism regulation in vertebrates makes zebrafish an attractive alternative to rodents17–19. In zebrafish, the metabolic organs/tissues are anatomically and physiologically relatively similar to those of humans, and this model is relevant for screening metabolic disturbances, including obesity20,21. Fully differentiated white adipocytes in vertebrates contain one large lipid droplet and are specialized in storing energy mainly as triacylglycerols. An optimized zebrafish obesogenic test (ZOT) protocol has recently been developed for high-throughput screening of obesogens and anti-obesogens, as well as dietary components that influence the size of lipid droplets in white adipocytes and, in turn, adiposity11.

In the present study, the obesogenic potential of selected compounds that might affect zebrafish white adipose tissue (WAT) was investigated in a physiologically relevant context. Thirteen selected pharmaceuticals or environmental contaminants were evaluated for their potential to induce a thrifty phenotype in this vertebrate model. The hypothesis of a relationship between the induction of the thrifty phenotype and reduced energy expenditure associated with decreased locomotor behavior was also tested.

Results

Systemic toxicity of selected compounds

Compounds selected for screening in the ZOT and the spontaneous locomotion test (SLT) are listed in Supplementary Table S1. Lethality and no observed effect concentration (NOEC), i.e., the highest concentration associated neither with mortality nor with systemic toxicity signs, induced by 24-h exposure to increasing doses (0.001–10 µM) of the selected compounds were determined using the systemic toxicity test (STOXT). Based on concentration-response analyses, TBT below the micromolar level induced larval mortality while the other tested compounds had much higher maximum tolerated concentrations (see Supplementary Table S2 and Supplementary Data 8 for details). The NOEC was used for ZOT and SLT screening, except for TBT, for which the concentration chosen was 50 nM to align with previously published data for this chemical11.

After normalization of adipose tissue area variation compared to the control DMSO group, a 100% increase in the adipose tissue surface area would signify that the quantity of fat remained stable during fasting under the effect of an obesogenic compound (Fig. 2, Supplementary Data 1). Resistance to adipose area loss during a 24-h fasting under exposure to 50 nM TBT or 10 µM DBP was demonstrated, in terms of normalized AFRSAT area variations of 71% ± 7% and 78% ± 13% (mean ± SEM), respectively (Fig. 2a, Supplementary Data 1). RGZ at 10 µM caused almost no change in adiposity between before and after 24 h of exposure, with a normalized AFRSAT area variation of 95% ± 11%. Greater than 100% area variations despite fasting indicate a larval adiposity increase, which was demonstrated with both AM at 2 µM and TCS at 3 µM that led to normalized AFRSAT area change of 117% ± 23% and 118% ± 25%, respectively (Fig. 2a, Supplementary Data 1). Supporting these quantitative findings, microscopic images revealed an increase in the size of the adipocyte lipid droplets, observed both with and without vital fluorescence labeling before and after a 24-h fasting under exposure to TCS at 3 µM (Fig. 1e–h).

Obesogenic potency of thrifty phenotype inducers

A dose-response study was conducted to calculate the values of the zebrafish obesogenic test index (ZOTI) and compare the obesogenic potencies of the compounds that induced a thrifty phenotype (Fig. 3, Supplementary Data 2). Kruskal–Wallis tests followed by post-hoc Dunn’s tests showed significant differences versus control (p < 0.05) from 0.05 µM TBT, 1 µM AM and TCS, 6 µM DBP and 10 µM RGZ for AFRSAT and from 0.05 µM TBT, 0.5 µM AM, 1 µM TCS, 3 µM DBP and 10 µM RGZ for VSAT (Fig. 3a, b, Supplementary Data 2). ZOTI is the concentration at which a compound induces a 50% increase in normalized AFRSAT or VSAT area, calculated along with 95% confidence interval (95% CI) values from fitted dose-response curves (Fig. 3, Supplementary Data 2) (see the “Methods” section for details). In terms of both AFRSAT and VSAT variations, TBT had the highest obesogenic potency, i.e., the lowest ZOTI values (Fig. 3c, Supplementary Data 2). AM and RGZ exhibited intermediate ZOTI values, followed by TCS and DBP. Whereas the respective ZOTI values of RGZ, TCS, and DBP determined in AFRSAT were similar to those determined in VSAT, there were significant differences between the ZOTI observed at these two adipocyte locations for both TBT and AM (Fig. 3c, Supplementary Data 2). Principal component analysis (PCA) and correlation analysis of ZOT variables

The first principal component (PC1) was primarily associated with larva standard length (variable 1), initial total WAT area (variable 2), initial VSAT area (variable 3), initial AFRSAT area (variable 4), and number of AFRSAT adipocytes (variable 5), all five of which showed high positive loadings on the PC1 axis (see loadings in Supplementary Data 3). Variables mainly influencing the second principal component (PC2) were total WAT area variation (variable 6), VSAT area variation (variable 7), and AFRSAT area variation (variable 8). PC1 and PC2 accounted for 41.6% and 20.9% of the variance of ZOT parameters, respectively (Fig. 4, Supplementary Data 3). In the PC1–PC2 score plot, treatment groups are represented as ellipses centered on group means (+), with dimensions corresponding to the standard deviations along both axes. The ellipses of the identified thrifty phenotype inducers were separated from the control ellipse, confirming their obesogenic character (Fig. 4a, Supplementary Data 3). This was not the case for the ellipses of non-obesogenic compounds, which overlapped with those of the control group (Fig. 4b, Supplementary Data 3). It should be noted that the separation of positive obesogenic compounds from controls occurred primarily along the PC2 axis, suggesting that treatment-related effects were predominantly determined by variations in AFRSAT, VSAT, and total WAT areas. Pearson correlation analysis of ZOT variables among individual fish data corroborated the structure identified by PCA (Supplementary Fig. S1, Supplementary Data 5). Modification of physical activity under the effect of selected compounds

SLTs were performed to evaluate locomotor activity during exposure to compounds identified as obesogenic and non-obesogenic by ZOT (Supplementary Figs. S2−S4). A similar locomotor profile was observed for fish water and 0.1% DMSO vehicle control groups (Supplementary Fig. S4a, Supplementary Data 7). For the control groups treated with 0.1% DMSO and shown in the panels of Supplementary Fig. S3 and Supplementary Data 6, the total distance traveled in darkness over 24 h averaged 324 ± 10 m. The larvae initially exhibited high locomotor activity, traveling an average distance of approximately 53.7 ± 1.2 m during the first hour, then reduced their activity over time to reach minimal locomotion between the beginning of the seventh hour and the end of the eighteenth hour of the exposure period, which corresponded to the larvae’s nocturnal period of the circadian rhythm. After 20 h of experimental exposure, the larvae resumed their activity the following “morning” and began to slightly increase the distance traveled per hour until the end of the 24-h period.

Diazepam (DZP) at 10 µM significantly reduced locomotor activity compared to that of control larvae during the first 5 h of exposure, an effect that was no longer significantly different thereafter, given the low level of activity in both groups (Supplementary Fig. S3a, Supplementary Data 6). Overall, a 49% ± 4% reduction in total distance traveled compared to the control group was observed over the 24-h period of exposure to DZP (Supplementary Fig. S3b, Supplementary Data 6). Although TBT has the highest obesogenic potential (Fig. 3c, Supplementary Data 2) corresponding to the lowest ZOTI, it did not induce any significant change in the physical activity profile and total distance traveled during the exposure period at the tested concentrations (Supplementary Fig. S3c, d, Supplementary Data 6). By comparison to its control group, AM, which also had a relatively low ZOTI value, increased by 43% ± 14% the total distance traveled at 1 µM but not at 2 µM, largely due to 4 h of increased locomotor activity that began 2 h after exposure (Supplementary Fig. S3e, f, Supplementary Data 6). RGZ affected larvae's locomotion within the first hour of exposure (Supplementary Fig. S3g, Supplementary Data 6). A decrease in the total distance traveled of 24% ± 6% and 33% ± 3% for RGZ at 1 and 10 µM, respectively, was observed compared to the control group (Supplementary Fig. S3h, Supplementary Data 6). Since the exposure period began at noon and the control larvae showed a sharp decrease in locomotor activity during the imposed nocturnal phase of the circadian rhythm, this RGZ-induced inhibition occurred mainly during the diurnal phases of the exposure window (Supplementary Fig. S3g, Supplementary Data 6). A similar sharp decrease in locomotor behavior was observed during the first hours of exposure to TCS and DBP (Supplementary Fig. S3i,k, Supplementary Data 6). This was followed by a rebound in locomotor activity for 2 h starting at the 5th and 7th hour for 3 µM TCS and 10 µM DBP, respectively. As a consequence, the total distance moved over 24 h demonstrated a non-monotonic dose-response, with a 33% ± 5% and 28% ± 9% overall decrease under the effect of 1 µM TCS and 3 µM DBP, respectively (Supplementary Fig. S3j, l, Supplementary Data 6).

Aside from DZP, the other compounds found to be non-obesogenic by the ZOT had no significant effect on the total distance traveled during the SLT (Supplementary Fig. S4, Supplementary Data 7). The locomotor profiles they induced were similar to those of the controls, with the exception of exposure to PFOA or BP, during the first third of which a slight increase in distance moved was observed (Supplementary Fig. S4m, q, Supplementary Data 7).

Spontaneous physical activity and adiposity variation

The identified obesogenic compounds induced a thrifty phenotype with monotonic dose-response curves (Fig. 3a, b, Supplementary Data 2). Two of the five compounds identified as obesogenic by ZOT (TBT and RGZ) induced monotonic locomotor dose-response curves (Supplementary Fig. S3d, h, Supplementary Data 6), whereas the other three (AM, TCS, and DBP) induced non-monotonic dose-response activity (Supplementary Fig. S3f, j, l, Supplementary Data 6). A correlation study was conducted to assess whether there was a statistical link between physical activity and the ability of all of the tested compounds to modify larval adiposity (Fig. 5, Supplementary Data 4). The slopes of the linear regression were not significantly different from zero, indicating that there was no significant correlation between the two variables for either AFRSAT or VSAT data. Discussion

The ratio between energy acquired and energy expended by an organism characterizes energy balance and associated metabolic health. Many interacting factors are involved in regulating this balance, including genetic factors, adaptive thermogenesis, physical activity, lifestyle, basal metabolism, psychosocial, and environmental factors. Among these environmental factors, obesogenic compounds are ubiquitous and can contaminate human or animal organisms primarily by ingestion, inhalation, and through the tegument. Existing data on the characterization of obesogenic compounds may be consistent or contradictory between cellular or animal models and humans, depending on the evolutionary conservation of the involved biological processes and limitations of the employed methodology1,20,22,23. While in vitro mechanistic studies using preadipocyte models or mesenchymal stem cells are important in environmental chemical toxicity assessment, they cannot fully substitute in vivo integrative models. Chemicals that induce adipogenesis in vitro but have not yet been shown to increase WAT storage in vivo are designated as adipogens or potential obesogens. A new class of obesogens, which can only be characterized by in vivo approaches, has recently emerged as being capable of inducing a thrifty phenotype in zebrafish and mice8–14. In these models, the balance between mobilization and storage of lipid reserves in adipocytes tilts towards storage because of the hindrance of loss of adiposity during fasting of limited duration, as demonstrated in zebrafish larvae exposed to TBT or RGZ8–11. It has also been demonstrated that TBT exposure confers a thrifty phenotype and predisposes male mice offspring to increased fat accumulation, an effect that can be exacerbated by high lipid content of the diet13,14.

The ZOT protocol is an integrative in vivo bioassay that uses zebrafish larvae as a vertebrate model to identify molecules and nutritional factors that can modulate the size of lipid droplets in adipocytes. As in humans, zebrafish WAT develops at different anatomical locations throughout the body, including subcutaneous and visceral regions8,11,24. Fully differentiated adipocytes are spherical in shape, unilocular, and store excess neutral triacylglycerols8,11,25,26. ZOT is therefore relevant for identifying compounds that induce a thrifty phenotype8,11, and was used in the current study to screen selected compounds for this adverse effect.

The present study confirmed that both TBT and RGZ induce a thrifty phenotype in zebrafish larvae as previously described8,9,11. They were therefore used as positive controls for the screening. The present work focused on subcutaneous adipose tissue locations to demonstrate, as with TBT and RGZ, that AM, DBP, and TCS, among the compounds tested, could induce a thrifty phenotype with monotonic dose-response curves. It has previously been shown that exposure to TBT or RGZ induced an increase in adipocyte lipid droplet size in fasted larvae in an initial nutritional context of a high-fat diet8,9. Even though the larvae had been initially raised on a standard diet (SD), an increase in larval adiposity despite fasting was induced by exposure to either AM or TCS.

It is noteworthy that no difference in obesogenic effects was found in four of the five obesogens when variations in total body adiposity were tested instead of variations in AFRSAT or VSAT. The assessment of obesogenic effects of both TBT and RGZ was previously shown to have greater sensitivity and reproducibility when using variations in AFRSAT or VSAT rather than using variations in total WAT area11. These differences were attributed to more consistent larvae positioning during epifluorescence imaging, which allows AFRSAT and VSAT to align within a single optical focal plane more effectively compared to whole larvae imaging11. Moreover, image analysis and adipocyte area quantification of AFRSAT and VSAT were performed in a fully automated and unbiased manner using the artificial intelligence-based software ZOTAS11. On the other hand, total WAT was quantified on whole-body fish images, wherein acquiring pictures consistently focused on the same focal plane in each fish was not technically possible. Moreover, fluorescence threshold determination for quantifying whole-body area using a single threshold (using ImageJ macros) was not always straightforward. Consequently, the resulting area quantification can be less than objective, resulting in false negatives. This could potentially explain why, in the present study, DBP was obesogenic in terms of AFRSAT and VSAT variation, but not according to the total WAT measurements. Differential sensitivity of adipocyte lineages to the tested compounds is another potential explanation for DBP not qualifying as obesogenic in terms of total WAT variation. Supporting this possibility were the significant differences observed in the obesogenic potency of both TBT and AM in AFRSAT and VSAT.

Among the obesogens identified, TBT presented the lowest ZOTI and therefore the highest obesogenic potency depending on its concentration in the fish incubation medium. Here, one may note that no relationship between the obesogenic potency of a compound and its log Kow partition coefficient value was found. For example, TBT and TCS both have a log Kow value of 4.76, but AFRSAT ZOTI values of 0.02 µM and 0.52 µM, respectively. This does not mean that the differential bioavailability of compounds based on other toxicokinetic considerations should be ignored, as the concentrations used are not necessarily representative of those in the body.

To the best of our knowledge, the present study is the first to associate AM exposure with obesity. AM is a widely prescribed antiarrhythmic drug27,28. One well-known side effect of AM is, however, hepatic steatosis29 in addition to an effect on thyroid gland function30–32. AM also has the capacity to accumulate in adipose tissue after chronic use33. AM can induce an endoplasmic-reticulum stress-dependent lipolysis and inhibit lipogenesis after repetitive administration in mice34. Similarly, decreases in rodent body weight35–38 and food consumption36 have been reported after chronic AM administration. The reported effects could be related to the induction of systemic toxicity. It should be noted that, in the present study, larvae were fasting and therefore the impact of AM on adiposity was not a consequence of modulation of food intake.

TCS is an antimicrobial widely used in personal care products and several commercial preparations as a preservative. It is considered to be an endocrine-disrupting chemical39. The available data associating TCS with obesity are sparse and often contradictory, but TCS has been classified as a potential obesogen1,40. Studies have revealed no41,42 or even a negative43 association between TCS exposure and obesity. However, an induced weight gain has been reported in rodents after exposure to TCS44,45. Epidemiological human studies have highlighted an association between TCS exposure, elevated BMI46, and an increased risk of childhood obesity47. The current study demonstrated that TCS is a powerful obesogenic compound able to induce a thrifty phenotype in zebrafish. In agreement with the present findings, an increase in BMI levels of adult zebrafish after a chronic exposure to TCS has been reported48.

DBP, a commonly used plasticizer, has exhibited an adipogenic capacity by enhancing substantial triacylglycerols accumulation in vitro, despite its limited capacity to increase 3T3-L1 cell proliferation49. Likewise, in vivo studies have demonstrated an increase in body weight and the risk of obesity in rats after prenatal50, perinatal51, and chronic adult exposure to DBP52. Furthermore, elevated childhood BMI has been associated with early-life exposure to DBP53. DBP is an obesogenic compound whose MoA may be, at least in part, to induce a thrifty phenotype as demonstrated by ZOT with the zebrafish model.

In the current study, the other compounds, which tested negative by ZOT in their ability to induce a thrifty phenotype under the conditions utilized, were selected because they are known as obesogens, potential obesogens, and/or inducers of hepatic steatosis1,40. This suggests a different obesogenic MoA, e.g., a factor inducing adipocyte hyperplasia or acting on food intake and nutrient absorption, processes that cannot be assessed by the present ZOT protocol. Furthermore, the same substance may be obesogenic by acting simultaneously on different MoAs, as has been demonstrated for TBT54. A non-monotonic dose response to these chemicals can also be considered. For example, obesogenic effects only after exposure to low doses of DDE55,56, DEHP57, bisphenols including BPA58,59, and perfluoroalkyl substances60–62 have been demonstrated. It has also been shown that the nutritional history of the zebrafish larvae, i.e., a standard or high-fat diet, can modify the obesogenic response during fasting8,9,11. An evaluation of the ability of these compounds to induce a thrifty phenotype in animals initially fed under high-fat diet conditions is indicated. Other potential limitations of the present study include the waterborne route of exposure and the unknown internal tissue concentrations, the window of exposure during the juvenile stage, the short-term exposure duration of 24 h, and the genetic background of the wild-type zebrafish strain used. Additionally, it would also be informative to test combinations of compounds, as humans are widely exposed to harmful mixtures of endocrine-disrupting chemicals54,63.

Locomotor behavior was assessed to investigate a possible causal link between energy expenditure related to physical activity and the obesogenic substances identified using ZOT. SLT demonstrated that DZP, a benzodiazepine known to inhibit locomotor behavior at a high concentration64, significantly reduced spontaneous physical activity by approximately fifty percent during the 24-h exposure period, without showing any obesogenic effect in ZOT. As there was no difference in adiposity compared to larvae not exposed to DZP, the energetic cost of locomotion appears minimal compared to the energy demand for basal metabolism, including body growth over the period. RGZ was obesogenic at the same time as it reduced locomotion at the maximum tested concentration by about one-third, which was less than the fifty percent drop in animals exposed to DZP. In contrast, TBT, which had the highest obesogenic potential among the tested compounds by ZOT, had no effect on locomotor behavior under the used experimental conditions. Furthermore, none of the other obesogens confirmed by ZOT (AM, DBP, and TCS) had any effect on 24-h locomotor activity at their maximum ZOT screening concentrations. Furthermore, normalized locomotor activity was not correlated with normalized variation in adiposity in the target WAT sublocations when all group data from the ZOT and SLT experiments at the different tested concentrations were used. Thus, a decrease in physical activity and therefore in associated energy expenditure was not sufficient to explain the MoA of the thrifty phenotype inducers. Because adverse effect pathways are not chemical-specific, causal relationships among key events leading to the thrifty phenotype remain to be determined.

Basal metabolic rate is generally recognized as the factor that contributes most to total daily energy expenditure in sedentary individuals65. The metabolic response to short-term fasting is one of the quantitative factors that characterize metabolic thriftiness66. This adaptive response has been shaped during the evolution of animals by optimizing lipid metabolism and resting energy expenditure17. Theoretically, environmental exposure to obesogens inducing a thrifty phenotype would lead to a decrease in basal metabolism and thus stimulate energy conservation mechanisms throughout the body. This phenotype can prove harmful by promoting weight gain in a context of high nutritional energy intake. The resting metabolic rate and whole-body energy expenditure under the influence of these obesogenic compounds remain to be measured.

In summary, modern-day obesity is generally believed to be caused by an imbalance in diets, with many people taking in and storing more calories through eating than they expend through metabolism and exercise. Our study demonstrated that AM, DBP, and TCS are obesogenic compounds able to induce a thrifty phenotype under short-term fasting condition as previously demonstrated with TBT and RGZ. The MoA of these obesogenic compounds was related neither to the modification of locomotor activity and the corresponding modulation of energy expenditure, nor to food intake and nutrient absorption, as experiments were performed with fasting animals. It remains to be determined how these obesogenic compounds disrupt metabolic homeostasis and could lead to obesity by inducing a thrifty phenotype. It also remains to be determined whether pharmaceuticals and environmental contaminants identified as obesogens in zebrafish, which induce a thrifty phenotype independent of locomotion, could have the same effect in humans. Primary prevention measures to fight against the obesity pandemic may include reducing exposure, in particular during early stages of human development, to obesogens that can induce a thrifty phenotype.

Methods

Zebrafish husbandry and ethics

All procedures, including the zootechnical strategy and compliance with ethical rules for animal experimentation, were carried out as previously described11. Zebrafish experiments were approved and performed according to French Directives (Ministry of Agriculture and Food) under permit number B33-522-6. All the procedures were conducted in compliance with the European Communities Council Directive (2010/63/EU) on the protection of animals used for scientific purposes and the local French legislation for the care and use of laboratory animals. The experiments were performed with commitment to refinement, reduction, and replacement to minimize the number of zebrafish used, and were approved by Ethics Committee no. 73 from the Ministry of National Education, Higher Education and Research under APAFIS approval number 21733 − 2019081217168685 v3.

The zebrafish (Danio rerio) wild-type UB strain was used. Embryos/larvae and adult zebrafish were reared in glass aquariums/containers to avoid contamination from plastic containers, which might contain endocrine disruptors that interfere with the obesogenic effects observed. The animals were reared in water with a conductivity between 300 and 500 µS, a dissolved oxygen content between 6 and 7 mg/l, a temperature of 28 ± 1 °C, and a pH between 6.8 and 7.2. The animals were illuminated by neon lights located above each row of aquariums and on each floor of the nursery, with an 11L:13D (light:dark) photoperiod. The illumination measured at the water surface was approximately 100 µW/cm² for embryo/larvae rearing and 300 µW/cm² for adult rearing, respectively. From 5 to 15 days post fertilization (dpf), the larvae were fed twice daily ad libitum with Zebrafeed (SPAROS Lda), a synthetic encapsulated feed with a grain size of less than 100 μm, and hard-boiled chicken egg yolk diluted in rearing water. Approximately 0.5 g of dried egg yolk was mixed with 10 ml of water, then ground, and only 50 μL of supernatant was extracted to feed an aquarium containing 30 larvae. From the 16th day after fertilization until they were enrolled in experiments, larvae were fed an SD composed of encapsulated Zebrafeed with a particle size of 100–200 μm, ad libitum twice daily until their use in the experiments. For more details on rearing and feeding, see ref. 11.

Chemical and pharmaceutical compounds

The compounds used for STOXT, ZOT screening, and SLT, including their Chemical Abstract Service (CAS) numbers, are listed in Supplementary Table S1. They were selected because they are considered to be obesogenic, potentially obesogenic, and/or steatogenic compounds1,40.

Systemic toxicity test (STOXT)

Systemic toxicity assessment was performed using STOXT as previously described11. A maximum concentration of 10 µM for each chemical was chosen, because 10 µM considerably exceeded the maximum concentration typically found in the environment. For all experiments, chemical stock solutions in 100% DMSO were diluted in fish water to reach the desired working concentration in 0.1% DMSO. In the case of CdCl2, the stock solution was prepared in fish water, and 0.1% DMSO was added separately upon exposure. NOEC was determined as the highest concentration that induced neither mortality, defined by the cessation of heartbeat, nor additional signs of systemic toxicity, including edema, especially pericardial edema, difficulty in controlling flotation and/or maintaining balance, or lethargic activity11. For each compound, the NOEC, corresponding to the maximum possible working concentration, was chosen for the ZOT and SLT screenings. These concentrations are shown in dark green in Supplementary Table S2.

Zebrafish obesogenic test (ZOT)

An optimized ZOT protocol with an artificial intelligence-based analysis software named ZOTAS, i.e., zebrafish obesogenic test analysis software, was used for compound screening as previously described11. On day 1 of the ZOT, larvae with a standard length between 8.5 and 9.5 mm were selected, as standard length provides a more accurate developmental reference than age for adipogenesis in zebrafish8,24. The length of each larva was determined using an automated system for zebrafish larvae standard length measurement11. All experimental and control groups consisted of larvae whose distribution and average standard length were equivalent. Animals measuring close to 8.5 mm may not yet have an AFRSAT. When this was the case, the animal was excluded from the analysis and replaced by another suitable animal. Selected larvae were fed the SD for 24 h then fasted for 24 h during day 211. On day 3, larvae were exposed individually to DMSO at 0.1%, used as a vehicle negative control, without or with a tested compound at a specific concentration, for one day in the dark and under fasting, at 28 °C. After anesthetizing the larvae in 2-phenoxyethanol solution (1:2000 dilution) for 1–2 min, larva total WAT, AFRSAT, and VSAT adiposity images were acquired before and after exposure to the tested compounds. Following the last image acquisition, the anesthetized larvae were euthanized by immersion in a 2-phenoxyethanol solution (1:200 dilution) for 15 min before being frozen at -20 °C. The ZOT procedure ended with the software-based processing of fluorescence images for adiposity quantification using ImageJ macros for total WAT or ZOTAS for AFRSAT and VSAT11. An adiposity measurement was obtained for each animal before and after exposure, and the variation in adiposity, thus individually matched, was used as the basis for calculating the difference between control and treated groups. Finally, individual fish adiposity variation was normalized to the average adiposity variation of the experiment control group, using the following formula:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$ {{{\rm{Normalized}}}}\; {{{\rm{Area}}}}\; {{{\rm{Variation}}}}\left( \% \right) \\ =\left(\frac{{{{\rm{Individual}}}}\; {{{\rm{Adiposity}}}}\; {{{\rm{Variation}}}}}{\left|{{{\rm{Average}}}}\; {{{\rm{Control}}}}\; {{{\rm{Variation}}}}\right|}\times 100\right)+100$$\end{document}NormalizedAreaVariation%=IndividualAdiposityVariationAverageControlVariation×100+100

After normalization, the control average is 0%. Positive normalized values, therefore, correspond to higher fat retention than controls, meaning less loss after 24 h between 0 and 100%, or a gain of adiposity despite fasting for values above 100%; while negative values indicate greater fat loss than controls.

Spontaneous locomotion test (SLT)

While different individuals were used for the SLT and ZOT, both groups of larvae were selected concomitantly based on an identical standard length interval criterion. After larva selection, followed by a 24-h feeding phase and a 24-h fasting phase as depicted in Supplementary Fig. S2, each larva was placed in a separate glass beaker filled with 10 mL of fish water for an acclimatization phase of 30 min in the dark. In both ZOT and SLT methods, larvae are exposed to the compounds in the fasted state after one day of fasting to avoid possible confounding effects on satiety and/or intestinal transit time with any direct obesogenic effect induced by the test compound. While fasting, larvae were then individually exposed to a specific concentration of the tested compound in 0.1% DMSO, in a total volume of 10 mL. The exposure took place in the DanioVision incubation chamber equipped with a temperature control unit (Noldus, Wageningen, The Netherlands) and maintained at 28 °C. The animals had previously been raised under a photoperiod cycle of 11 h of light and 13 h of darkness, with the lights turned on at 8 a.m. and turned off at 7 p.m. in the animal facility. The exposure period to the compounds was 24 h and always began at noon. The larvae were exposed to the test compound in the dark to avoid any photolysis of the compounds. Spontaneous locomotion of each individual larva was recorded in real time by video acquisition for 24 h, using an infrared digital HD Noldus video camera and EthoVision XT 16 video tracking software. A dynamic subtraction method was applied, using a sampling rate of 30 image/s, a current frame weight of 3, and an erosion and dilation of 1. The image contrast was adjusted to a dark scale of 38 to 255. A minimum distance input with a filter representing approximately 10% of the total larval length, equivalent to 0.8 mm, was used to remove the background noise. All samples were analyzed with the same detection and acquisition settings. The mean distance traveled in meters each hour of the 24-h period, and the mean total distance traveled during the 24 h of chemical exposure were calculated and compared between the control group (0.1% DMSO) and the group exposed to the chemical at each concentration. At the end of the SLT, larvae were anesthetized in a 1:2000 2-phenoxyethanol solution for 5 min and subsequently euthanized in a 1:200 solution for 15 min prior to freezing at −20 °C.

Statistics and reproducibility

The data were analyzed and represented using the GraphPad Prism 8 software (San Diego, CA, USA) or custom Python scripts. All assays involved multiple independent biological replicates (distinct spawns) and independent molecule preparations to ensure experimental robustness and reproducibility. Detailed information regarding sample sizes and the number of independent experiments for each treatment is provided in the Supplementary Data File associated with each figure and table. No experimental data were excluded from the analysis of the results. ZOTI values were calculated using the SciPy 1.15.3 Python module, by fitting a three-parameter agonist dose-response model to the dose-response ZOT data for each chemical (Fig. 3a, b, Supplementary Data 2) with the following equation:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{{\rm{Y}}}}={{{\rm{bottom}}}}+{{{\rm{X}}}}\times \frac{{{{\rm{top}}}}-{{{\rm{bottom}}}}}{{{{{\rm{EC}}}}}_{50}+{{{\rm{X}}}}}$$\end{document}Y=bottom+X×top−bottomEC50+Xwhere bottom and top represent the lower and upper asymptotes of the fitted curve, and EC50 the half-saturation constant.

ZOTI for each compound was defined as the concentration at which the fitted response reached a 50% increase over the control baseline:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{{\rm{X}}}}=\left(50-{{{\rm{bottom}}}}\right)\times \frac{{{{{\rm{EC}}}}}_{50}}{{{{\rm{top}}}}-50}$$\end{document}X=50−bottom×EC50top−50

Values of 95% CI were obtained by Monte Carlo sampling of the fitted parameters using the covariance matrix.

Differences among the treated and the control groups were examined for statistical significance using Brown-Forsythe and Welch ANOVA tests, followed by Dunnett’s T3 multiple comparison test or Kruskal–Wallis test, followed by Dunn’s multiple comparison test as appropriate. When possible, distribution is shown with raincloud plots containing half violin plots, boxplots with Q1, medians, Q3, and min-max whiskers excluding outliers, and individual data points. For dose-response studies, the data are presented as mean ± SEM. For all statistical results, the significance was *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001, and ****p < 0.0001, and the number of independent observations is provided in the figure legends and Supplementary File.

PCA and correlation analyses between ZOT variables were conducted using the authors’ in-house Python script. PCA results are presented as 2D plots of the first and second principal component (PC1 and PC2) scores, in order to provide insights into how eight different ZOT variables drove variance in the present results, and how these variables interrelated in terms of the direction and magnitude of each variable’s contribution to the PCs, designated as ‘loadings’. The eight variables chosen for PCA were larva standard length (variable 1), initial total WAT, VSAT, and AFRSAT areas (variables 2–4), number of AFRSAT adipocytes (variable 5), area variation of total WAT, VSAT, and AFRSAT areas (variables 6–8) (Fig. 4, Supplementary Data 3). Although PCA plots of the five chemicals that were identified as obesogenic (and of their controls) in the ZOTs are shown separately from those of the other ten chemicals and their controls in Fig. 4, the PCA loadings were applied to the ZOT results of all fifteen chemicals and their corresponding control groups. The correlation analyses between ZOT variables are shown in Supplementary Fig. S1 and Supplementary Data 5. Spreadsheets with values of PC1 and PC2 loadings are provided in Supplementary Data 3.

Due to practical constraints, specifically the differing throughput and methodological requirements of the ZOT and SLT, these assays were conducted on distinct individuals. However, both groups were selected concomitantly based on identical standard-length criteria to ensure biological comparability. Consequently, the correlation between larval adiposity variation, determined by the ZOT, and physical activity disruption, evaluated by the SLT, was assessed using the mean values per treatment, employing Pearson’s correlation coefficient for normally distributed AFRSAT data and Spearman’s rank correlation for non-normally distributed VSAT data.
