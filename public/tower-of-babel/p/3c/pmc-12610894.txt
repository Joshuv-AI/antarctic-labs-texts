Summary of the Best Evidence on Skin-Mucosal Care in Stevens-Johnson Syndrome/Toxic Epidermal Necrolysis
Authors: Yanping Lei, Zonghuang Ding, Chunrong Yuan, Ming Liu, Qiuting Dai, Jun Wei
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
OBJECTIVE:The aim of this study was to retrieve, evaluate, and summarize the best available evidence on skin-mucosal care for patients with Stevens-Johnson syndrome (SJS)/toxic epidermal necrolysis (TEN).DATA SOURCES:The data were gathered from national and international databases and reputable websites. A top-down approach was applied following the 6S Evidence Search Model, with a search time frame from the time of database construction to December 25, 2023. Multiple sources, including clinical decisions, guidelines, and expert consensus, were reviewed.STUDY SELECTION:Studies that addressed skin-mucosal care in patients with SJS/TEN were eligible for inclusion. The literature was rigorously screened to ensure its relevance and methodological quality. A total of 11 documents were included in this review, which consisted of 1 clinical decision, 5 clinical practice guidelines, and 5 expert consensus documents.DATA EXTRACTION:Two researchers independently assessed the quality of the included studies, extracted evidence, and summarized the findings. A total of 34 pieces of evidence spanning 6 dimensions of skin-mucosal care were identified and categorized.DATA SYNTHESIS:The extracted evidence was synthesized and summarized to provide actionable insights for clinical practice. The evidence focused on wound care, infection prevention, pain management, hydration, and specific recommendations for skin-mucosal care tailored to the unique needs of patients with SJS/TEN.CONCLUSIONS:This article offers a comprehensive evidence-based basis for effective skin-mucosal care to SJS/TEN patients. These best practices can improve patient outcomes and contribute to better clinical management.

INTRODUCTION

Stevens-Johnson syndrome (SJS)/toxic epidermal necrolysis (TEN) is a severe dermal-mucous reaction, usually drug-induced, characterized by blisters and generalized skin lesions that can be accompanied by multisystem damage. It is currently believed that SJS (skin lesion <10% of body surface area), toxic epidermal necrolysis (skin lesion >30% of body surface area), and overlapping SJS/TEN (skin lesion 10% to 30% of body surface area) are part of the same epidermal necrolysis disease spectrum.1 The mortality of SJS is <10%, whereas the mortality of TEN is as high as 30%, and the overall mortality rate of SJS/TEN is ∼22%.2 In the acute phase of SJS/TEN, various systemic complications may accompany the disease, among which skin infections are the most common, with a prevalence ranging from 31.8% to 78%. Meanwhile, epithelial damage can extend to the gastrointestinal tract, respiratory tract, and genitourinary system, which seriously affects patients’ safety and quality of life.3,4

Therefore, scientific and standardized skin-mucosal care is extremely important for improving the quality of survival and prognosis of patients with SJS/TEN. At present, although there are domestic and international consensus guidelines on the disease and care of SJS/TEN, the evidence on skin-mucosal care is scattered and not comprehensive, which is of little significance in guiding clinical practice. Consequently, this study systematically searched for evidence on skin-mucosal care for SJS/TEN patients in China and internationally, and applied an evidence-based approach to comprehensively summarize the evidence on skin-mucosal care for SJS/TEN patients, with the aim of providing more comprehensive evidence on skin-mucosal care for SJS/TEN patients and providing an evidence-based basis for better implementation of evidence-based clinical practice to improve the quality of patients’ survival and prognosis. The aim is to provide more comprehensive data for skin-mucosal care of SJS/TEN patients and better implementation of evidence-based clinical practice to improve patients’ survival quality and prognosis.

METHODS

Question Identification

Evidence-based questions were established according to the PIPOST format5—P (target population): patients with SJS/TEN; I (intervention method): interventions for skin-mucosal care of patients with SJS/TEN; P (professionals applying evidence): nurse-led; O (outcome): incidence of secondary skin injuries, incidence of skin infections, health care professionals’ knowledge of patients with SJS/TEN, knowledge of skin-mucosal care; S (place of evidence application): dermatology; and T (type of evidence): clinical decision-making, recommended practice, guidelines, evidence summaries, expert consensus, systematic evaluations, randomized controlled trials (RCTs).

Retrieval Strategy

Evidence-based databases and websites were searched in top-down order according to the “6S” evidence search model,6 including UpToDate, BMJ Best Practice, JBI Library, National Guidelines Network (NGC), Guidelines International Network (GIN), NICE Guidelines Network, Scottish Inter-Hospital Guidelines Collaboration Network (SIGN), Registered Nurses Association of Ontario (RNAO), Cochrane Library, Web of Science, PubMed, CINAHL, China Biomedical Literature Database (CBM), China Knowledge Network (CNKI), Wanfang Database, Medical Pulse, and the types of literature covered clinical decision-making, guideline, systematic evaluation, RCTs, expert consensus, and other evidence related to skin-mucosal care for SJS/TEN patients. A combination of subject terms and free terms was used, and the Chinese search terms were “Stevens-Johnson syndrome/toxic epidermal necrolysis” “adult Stevens-Johnson syndrome” “Nursing” “Management”; English search terms were “Stevens-Johnson syndrome/ Toxic Epidermal Necrolysis /Lyell’s Syndrome/ Necrolysis, Toxic Epidermal/“ AND “ nursing/ management.” The time frame for the search is from the inception of each database until December 25, 2023. Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

Inclusion Criteria

The study was conducted on (i) patients aged 18 years or older with SJS/TEN; (ii) the study involved content related to skin-mucosal care; (iii) the type of study was clinical decision, guideline, recommended practice, expert consensus, evidence summary, systematic evaluation, RCT; (iv) the language was Chinese or English.

Exclusion Criteria

The type of literature was (i) study proposal, report, study protocol, or abstract; (ii) literature that was not available in full text or had incomplete information; (iii) studies that did not pass the evaluation of the quality of the literature; (iv) duplicate publications or literature that had been updated.

Literature Quality Evaluation Process

Literature quality was independently evaluated by 2 researchers with a background in evidence-based nursing according to the evaluation criteria, and when there were conflicting evaluations, the evidence-based nursing team adjudicated and made the final decision on inclusion. When there were conflicting conclusions from different sources of evidence, evidence-based evidence, high-quality evidence, and recently published authoritative literature were prioritized.

Literature Screening and Data Extraction

Two nursing staff trained in systematic evidence-based nursing (one accounted for professional background and the other performed literature screening and information extraction) were responsible for literature search, literature evaluation, and proofreading of English translations; when disagreements arose, they were discussed and agreed upon by the department’s evidence-based research team. Information on the topic, nature of evidence, date of publication, authors, sources of literature, and studies related to skin-mucosal care for patients with SJS/TEN were extracted.

Level of Evidence Evaluation

This study graded the included evidence. To grade the quality of the evidence, the JBI Evidence Pregrading and Evidence Recommendation Level System 2014 was uniformly used to classify the evidence from 1 to 5, with 1 being the highest level and 5 being the lowest, and to evaluate the feasibility, applicability, clinical significance, and validity of the evidence using the FAME evaluation principle, and to make a recommendation (A being a strong recommendation and B being a weak recommendation).9

DISCUSSION

Regular Assessment of the Skin-Mucous Membranes and Individualized Care According to the Area of Involvement

Patients with SJS/TEN usually have different degrees of mucosal involvement at different sites during the acute phase. Of these, ocular mucosal involvement accounts for ∼80%.21 The management of the acute phase of SJS/TEN relies on early assessment by ophthalmologists, the use of lubricants to protect the ocular surface, anti-infective treatment, and amniotic membrane transplantation. Nursing staff need to focus on observing eye mobility and eyelid closure in addition to the presence of conjunctival congestion, and ocular surface and lid margin involvement in patients. While providing personalized care measures for the patient, attention should also be paid to the patient’s pain tolerance, and an anesthetic can be used to relieve pain and promote patient comfort if necessary.

Health care professionals should assess the oral cavity and its surrounding areas, as well as the esophagus, on a daily basis, observing the extent of oral mucosal detachment, the degree of restriction of mouth opening, tongue movement, and esophageal involvement. When SJS/TEN involves a large area of the oral cavity, it can cause pain and nutritional problems for the patient, who can be encouraged to choose light and easily digestible soft foods. When pain is intolerable, a pain relief mouthwash or spray can be applied before eating to reduce pain. Patients are advised to increase the frequency of mouth rinsing to keep the mouth clean. For esophageal involvement, choose the appropriate nutritional support for the patient.

Patients need to be admitted to the hospital for examination and assessment of the genitourinary tract by a specialist. Females need to be observed for vaginal wall involvement, and males need to be aware of circumcision mobility. In addition to regular examination and assessment, emollients, nonadhesive dressings, and topical antimicrobial therapy should be used regularly to prevent complications such as vaginal adhesions and urethral strictures.

Skin infections leading to sepsis are the most common cause of death for patients with SJS/TEN. Dynamically assess and record the site and degree of skin-mucosal involvement, the extent and number of areas involved, exudates, and the percentage of involved area to surface area in order to keep track of the patient’s disease progression. Aseptic principles were strictly followed, and the patient’s prognostic indicators were closely monitored to enable early detection of potential complications. In SJS/TEN, the necrotic epidermis tends to separate from the underlying dermis, so caring for the skin at the site of the patient’s lesion is critical. Caregivers need to take measures to maintain skin barrier function, reduce percutaneous water loss, use nonadhesive dressings on the exposed dermis to minimize fluid and protein loss, control pain, and reduce skin friction and shear forces. The skin condition of the site needs to be assessed before all operations on the patient, and operations should be performed with as much skin integrity as possible to avoid secondary skin damage.

An appropriate treatment setting was chosen based on the size and severity of the patient’s epidermal peeling area. The principle of skin management in SJS/TEN is similar to that of superficial partial-thickness burns, which involves varying degrees of detachment of the epidermis.22 When the area of skin lesion exceeds 30% of the epidermis, the patient needs to be transferred to the burn ward for treatment and protective isolation.

Large epidermal detachment is often accompanied by dysfunction of thermoregulation, large fluid loss, and electrolyte imbalance, so the ward needs to maintain a certain temperature to prevent overconsumption of heat due to epidermal detachment. The presence of hypothermia requires health care professionals to closely observe changes in body temperature and inlet and outlet volume to avoid disturbances of the internal environment.

Limitations

The limitation of this study is that the majority of the included literature was derived from secondary studies such as guidelines and expert consensus, which lacked the support of high-quality primary research data. Therefore, it is recommended that clinical health care professionals take into account the actual situation of local health care institutions when conducting clinical practice. At the same time, it is necessary to conduct high-quality clinical studies such as RCTs in the future.

CONCLUSIONS

This article summarizes 34 pieces of best evidence on skin-mucosal care for patients with SJS/TEN, including 6 specific practice areas: ocular assessment and care, oral assessment and care, genitourinary assessment and care, trunk and limb skin care, infusion site skin management, and environmental management. The aim is to provide an evidence-based basis for personalized skin-mucosal care in patients with SJS/TEN. Implementing these practices can help improve the quality of survival and prognosis for SJS/TEN patients.
