Hydrogel-forming microneedles for the treatment of skin diseases
Authors: Yao Zhang, Haiyang Li, Guangwei Li, Yu Chen, Yibin Zeng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe painless, non-invasive and highly efficient microneedle drug delivery method has received extensive attention from the medical community in recent years. Among all the microneedle technologies, the hydrogel-forming microneedle (HFM) technology demonstrates extremely promising application prospects. HFMs are composed of hydrogel-based micron-sized needle arrays that can penetrate the stratum corneum barrier of skin in a minimally invasive method and efficiently deliver drugs to the dermis layer. Compared to traditional microneedling technology with rapid drug release capability, HFMs can not only retain the drug in a biodegradable hydrogel matrix (Gelatin Methacryloyl (GelMA), chitosan(CS), hyaluronic acid(HA), polyvinyl alcohol (PVA), polyvinyl pyrrolidone (PVP),etc.) for slower release, thus achieving the goal of long-term treatment of disease, while reducing the frequency of dosing and improve patient compliance. The main factors affecting the performance of HFMs include size/surface morphology, bioadhesive/mechanical strength, in vitro drug release kinetics and transdermal permeation behavior, biosafety, and short-and/or long-term stability. These HFMs can be designed to have additional functions, including both photothermal therapy and drug sustained-release functions. This review systematically summarizes recent achievements on HFMs for the treatment of skin diseases and discusses the challenges posed on the development of novel and highly efficient HFMs, aiming to clarifying the key issues that hinders their practical applications.Keywords: Hydrogels, Hydrogel-forming microneedles, Skin diseases, Drug sustained-release, Photothermal therapy

Introduction

The skin is the largest organ of the human body and serves as a complete physiological barrier. The main function of the skin is a primary defense system, preventing any external substances from entering the body. Owing to its unique anatomical features, such as tightly packed corneocytes, strong intercellular junctions, and rapid inflammatory responses, the skin acts as a barrier to transdermal drug delivery systems(TDDS) [1]. Compared with conventional administration routes, transdermal delivery offers significant clinical advantages, including reduced plasma concentration fluctuations and improved patient adherence. Despite the widespread use of topical formulations, the full potential of transdermal drug delivery has yet to be realized [2]. For example, transdermal patches cannot penetrate the skin barrier, which restricts the diffusion of water and electrolytes internally and chemicals, allergens, and microorganisms externally. [3]. The barrier function of the skin is further enhanced when the skin thickens because of psoriasis or scarring or when biofilms are formed over infectious wounds, while reducing the effectiveness of conventional topical treatments. Therefore, various strategies have been developed to reversibly reduce skin barrier resistance for drug administration. Among them, active enhancement methods, particularly those employing physical or mechanical disruption, have demonstrated superior performance [4]. As a highly promising technology, microneedle (MN) technology has evolved into a highly valuable technique in the field of biomedicine. In general, MNs refer to micro-sized needles used for transdermal drug delivery, tissue fluid sampling, signal detection, biosensing and other purposes. They have the advantages of being painless, minimally invasive and easy to operate [5]. Traditional MNs can be categorized into solid, dissolving, hollow, and coated types [6,7] (Fig. 1A,B), each tailored for specific biomedical applications [8]. Exactly, solid microneedles (SMNs) are manufactured on a flat surface, resembling a board with sharp points. When the traditional subcutaneous injection needles are reduced in length and diameter to the micron scale, they become hollow microneedles (HMNs). They can be a single needle or multiple needles, connected to a board/base, which can be connected to a device that provides external driving force and injects liquid or drugs through internal holes or cavities. Coated microneedles (CMNs), prepared using various biodegradable and biocompatible materials, can rapidly deliver high-molecular-weight drugs into the skin. Storing the drugs in a solid phase as a coating of the microneedles can enhance their long-term stability. Dissolving microneedles (DMNs) are made from some biocompatible water-soluble matrix materials and do not need to consider whether they will break on the skin or biocompatibility issues. When the microneedles are inserted into the skin, the tissue fluid dissolves the needle tip, and thereby the encapsulated drugs are naturally released to achieve the purpose of drug delivery. However, the applicability of conventional MNs are limited by the possibility of skin irritation or mechanical damage due to material rigidity [9]. Additionally, conventional designs often rely on passive diffusion or surface coating techniques, which may result in suboptimal drug release and treatment outcomes. In contrast to these MNs, hydrogel-forming microneedles (HFMs) are prepared using the polymer matrix that forms the hydrogel. This micro-needle array rapidly absorbs liquid when inserted into the skin, causing the hydrogel to expand. This results in a continuous and unobstructed hydrogel conduit, allowing the drug to penetrate into the skin. These HFMs have many advantages, such as a large drug loading capacity and controllable drug controlled-release effect. The needle body materials have good biocompatibility and avoid potential damage to the skin caused by the fracture of the needle tip. Microneedles prepared using cross-linked high molecular biological materials can achieve subcutaneous swelling of the microneedles without dissolving, thereby effectively controlling the release rate of the loaded drugs (see Fig. 2).

Over the past few years, a large number of hydrogels with outstanding histocompatibility, hemocompatibility, good flexibility and biocompatibility have been synthesized, showing excellent application potential in the field of critical biomedicine(e.g., wound dressings, cell delivery vectors, tissue engineering and drug release). These hydrogels are cross-linked three-dimensional polymer networks swollen with water and can respond to various stimuli [10]. The physicochemical properties of hydrogel scaffolds are determined by material composition, concentration, crosslinking strategy, and fabrication method. Mechanical properties can be precisely modulated through physical or chemical crosslinking [11]. Physical approaches such as hydrogen bonding, hydrophobic interactions, polymer entanglement, and π–π stacking generally yield lower strength. By contrast, chemical crosslinking through free radical polymerization, enzymatic reactions, or covalent bonding offers enhanced mechanical stability [12,13]. Fabrication methods such as in situ gelation [14], electrospinning [15], micropatterning [16], 3D bioprinting [17], and microfluidic technology [18], further influence the structural and functional properties of hydrogels, determining their application potential.

Hydrogel-forming microneedles (HFMs) represent an innovative drug delivery system that integrates MN technology with hydrogel-based materials. HFMs offer several advantages over traditional MNs, including a relatively simple manufacturing process, wider availability of biocompatible materials, and avoidance of sharp waste disposal concerns [19]. These MNs swell upon insertion into the skin, forming a gel matrix that serves as a depot for sustained and controlled drug release [20]. Unlike conventional MNs, HFMs are not constrained by the amount of drug pre-loaded into the needles. Drug delivery is governed by the crosslinking density of the hydrogel instead of the permeability of the stratum corneum [21].

In pathological skin conditions, structural alterations limit the use of conventional MNs. Therefore, HFMs provide a more adaptable and effective solution by enabling the modification of material composition and structural parameters for the delivery of drugs or active ingredients. Although there are several excellent review papers already concerned the design, preparation and performance of HFMs [23,24] and their potential applications in food and agriculture [19], vitiligo [25] and melanoma [26], but much less work has been done for emphasis on their application in the treatment of skin diseases in these review articles, to which many readers have strong interests. For this reason, this review would be focused on the recent advances in HFMs for the treatment of various skin diseases and discusses several key challenges that hinder the practical application of the novel and highly efficient HFMs, including material limitations and formulation complexity, drug solubility and delivery efficiency, fabrication and scalability, and clinical translation barriers.

Fabrication of HFMs

The fabrication of HFMs involves several stages, including mold development, hydrogel formulation, casting, and post-processing treatments [9]. Multiple fabrication strategies have been developed to optimize MN structures. Six representative methods are illustrated in Fig. 1(C–H): hot embossing, micro-molding, thermal-drawing lithography, magneto-rheological lithography, laser drilling, and 3D printing [22].Among these, micro-molding is the most commonly employed technique for producing HFMs for dermatological applications [27,28]. This method involves injecting a hydrogel precursor into a mold using centrifugation and vacuum-assisted techniques, followed by a drying process. Initially, a master mold with the desired MN geometry is prepared. Subsequently, a biocompatible polymer is then thoroughly mixed with an appropriate crosslinker to produce the hydrogel solution. Following this, catalysts and active pharmaceutical ingredients are added to this mixture, which is subsequently introduced into the mold cavities. Vacuum or centrifugation techniques are applied to eliminate air bubbles. The hydrogel then undergoes chemical or physical crosslinking, typically initiated by ultraviolet irradiation, heat, or chemical agents, to ensure structural integrity and material homogeneity. Once solidified, the MNs are demolded [29]. Subsequent treatment steps such as dehydration, sterilization, and surface modification are conducted to enhance drug-loading efficiency, release kinetics, and mechanical strength [30,31]. Notably, the release behavior and permeation of active agents are primarily determined by the physicochemical properties of the hydrogel matrix rather than by the MN fabrication method [32]. Micro-molding is widely favored for its simplicity, cost-effectiveness, and scalability for biomedical applications. Nonetheless, certain limitations persist, including restricted control over MN geometry, the potential for bubble formation during processing, and difficulties in achieving uniform consistency.

HFMs in transdermal drug delivery

Materials

Selection of materials for HFMs must prioritize biocompatibility and non-immunogenicity. Adequate mechanical strength is also essential to ensure safe penetration and the effective delivery of complex macromolecular drugs [32]. The following materials are commonly used in HFMs designed for the treatment of skin disorders.

Gelatin methylacryloyl (GelMA)

GelMA, a gelatin derivative containing predominantly methacrylamide and a minor fraction of methacrylate groups, has been extensively utilized in biomedical applications due to its tunable physical properties and favorable biological compatibility [33]. As a photo-crosslinkable hydrogel, GelMA retains functional sequences such as arginine-glycine-aspartic acid (RGD) and matrix metalloproteinase-responsive sites, thereby preserving the bioactivity of native gelatin [34]. These properties contribute to its effectiveness in promoting wound healing, tissue morphogenesis, and regeneration [35]. A key advantage of GelMA lies in its ability to simulate the natural extracellular matrix environment and support the precise fabrication of complex microstructures via photopolymerization [36]. With advances in MN fabrication and crosslinking techniques, GelMA-based hydrogels have been increasingly applied in oncology, anesthesia, dentistry, and dermatology. In the context of skin disease treatment, GelMA MNs offer a combination of adjustable mechanical strength, controlled swelling, exceptional biocompatibility, and biological activity, making them a prominent research focus of current research in transdermal drug delivery [37].

CS

Chitosan (CS) is a linear cationic polysaccharide composed of repeating β-(1–4)-linked D-glucosamine and N-acetyl-D-glucosamine units, derived from the natural biopolymer chitin [38]. It exhibits a range of favorable properties, including biocompatibility, biodegradability, hydrophilicity, non-toxicity, high bioavailability, chemical stability, and ease of modification. CS also demonstrates selective water permeability and can be processed into various forms such as films, gels, nanoparticles, microparticles, and beads. In addition, it exhibits strong affinity for metals, proteins, and dyes [39]. Modified forms of CS have been shown to promote skin regeneration, supporting its use in clinical applications targeting tissue repair and regeneration [40]. Notably, CS exhibits inherent antibacterial activity, attributed to its unique status as the only naturally occurring polysaccharide polymer bearing a positive charge [41].

HA

Hyaluronic acid (HA), or hyaluronan, is a naturally occurring, non-sulfated glycosaminoglycan (GAG) widely distributed throughout human tissues [42]. It consists of repeating disaccharide units of D-glucuronic acid and N-acetyl-D-glucosamine, connected by alternating β-(1 → 4) and β-(1 → 3) glycosidic bonds [43]. Owing to its strong hydrophilicity, HA can absorb and retain large volumes of water through hydrogen bonding, forming a viscous and elastic gel [44]. Beyond its structural roles, HA participates in diverse biological processes, including immune regulation and maintenance of immune homeostasis. It promotes fibroblast proliferation, tissue regeneration, and skin repair processes [45]. HA also binds to various cell surface receptors and extracellular proteins, thereby facilitating its ability to deliver biologically active compounds [46]. Owing to its excellent biocompatibility and biodegradability, HA and its derivatives have been extensively investigated and applied in numerous biomedical applications [47].

PVA/PVP

Polyvinyl alcohol (PVA) is a water-soluble polymer derived from polyvinyl acetate that is non-toxic, biocompatible, and non-carcinogenic [48]. Upon crosslinking, PVA forms a porous, three-dimensional polymer network [49]. PVA hydrogels are easily prepared due to their high water absorption capacity; however, they generally exhibit inadequate mechanical strength and limited dimensional stability. To address these limitations, polyvinyl pyrrolidone (PVP) is incorporated into the PVA matrix to promote inter-chain hydrogen bonding, thereby enhancing network stability [50]. The addition of PVP modulates the morphology, swelling behavior, water retention capacity, and mechanical strength of the composite hydrogel [51]. Physical crosslinking methods are commonly employed to produce PVA hydrogels, as they offer advantages over chemical crosslinking, including the ability to fine-tune mechanical properties while preserving biomimetic characteristics. Considering these properties, PVA/PVP hydrogels are widely used in both therapeutic interventions and diagnostic monitoring of various skin conditions [52,53].

Main factors affecting the performance of HFMs

The material composition and preparation method of HFMs are critical determinants of MN performance. HFMs must exhibit favorable biocompatibility and mechanical stability to enable effective skin penetration without inducing excessive discomfort or tissue damage. In addition, the drug delivery efficiency of HFMs depends on their morphological characteristics and in vitro release behavior. Comprehensive consideration and optimization of these factors are essential to enhance overall drug delivery efficacy.

Size/surface morphology

Morphological characteristics of HFMs, including tip diameter, base width, height, length, and inter-needle spacing, can be accurately characterized using optical microscopy, scanning electron microscopy (SEM), optical coherence tomography (OCT), and reflectance confocal microscopy (RCM) [55]. Among these analytical tools, optical microscopy and SEM are widely used for assessing the geometry and surface morphology of HFM arrays, including needle height, width, and spacing. OCT [56] and RCM [57] provide high-resolution imaging to evaluate penetration depth and assess insertion effectiveness.

Bioadhesive/mechanical strength

Mechanical strength governs the structural integrity of MNs during insertion, ensuring resistance to fracture or deformation upon skin penetration. Needle length is a critical parameter; increased length is associated with higher insertion pain and reduced mechanical strength [24]. The likelihood of mechanical failure also increases with needle elongation [58]. Thinner needles are more prone to damage than wider ones due to reduced structural stability [59]. Inter-needle spacing affects the required insertion force according to the “bed of nails” effect, where closer spacing necessitates a more evenly distributed insertion force across the array [60]. Excessive proximity between needles may lead to contact and structural interference during swelling, potentially compromising array performance. Needle geometry also significantly influences mechanical strength; conical and pyramidal geometries are preferred for their superior structural reinforcement within the array [61].

In vitro drug release and transdermal behavior

In vitro drug release kinetics are typically assessed via diffusion by immersing MNs in phosphate-buffered saline and quantifying drug concentrations in the supernatant at designated time intervals [62].

Transdermal behavior describes the ability of MNs to deliver therapeutic agents into deeper layers of the skin, governed by needle geometry and material composition. Puncture depth can be assessed using polymeric films [63] or porcine skin models [64], the latter of which closely mimic the biomechanical properties of human skin. A 100 % puncture success rate, which is defined as complete epidermal penetration by all needles in an array, serves as a critical quality metric [65]. Needle geometry and mechanical strength play key roles in microchannel formation within the skin [66]. Fluorescence microscopy enables visualization of drug distribution and accumulation in the tissue. Additional advanced imaging techniques, such as laser scanning microscopy, in vivo imaging systems, OCT, Raman microspectroscopy, laser speckle contrast imaging, and photoacoustic microscopy, facilitate real-time drug distribution monitoring, non-invasive skin reaction assessment, and quantitative analysis of drug penetration.

Biosafety and short-/long-term stability

Biosafety and stability are critical factors limiting the clinical translation of HFMs. Biocompatibility forms the core of safety evaluations. Standard assays include cell viability tests [67], migration and proliferation assays, and tube formation analysis [68]. It is recommended to assess cytotoxicity, sensitization, irritation, intradermal reactivity, genotoxicity, and subacute/subchronic systemic toxicity at different stages of HFM development and application [32].

Stability testing ensures the protection and efficacy of active pharmaceutical ingredients during storage. These tests assess changes in quality over time under various environmental conditions, such as temperature and relative humidity [69].

Drug or active ingredient carriers

In addition to conventional MNs fabricated from polymers or hydrogels coated onto surfaces, three main drug-loading strategies are employed. Some MNs only have drugs confined to the needle tips, while some are contained within the patch, and others drugs distributed in both components [70]. Various polymeric materials have been engineered into nanoparticles to encapsulate active ingredients, including polyhydroxyalkanoates (PHA) [71], poly(lactic-co-glycolic acid) (PLGA), and poly(ethylene glycol) (PEG) [72]. Lipid nanovesicles have also been explored as drug carriers in HFMs [73].

Multifunctional HFMs

HFMs can be engineered to possess multiple therapeutic functions, including sustained drug release and photothermal therapy (PTT).

PTT

PTT was initially applied in oncology due to its precise tumor-targeting capability, low invasiveness, and minimal systemic toxicity [74]. PTT involves a photosensitizer that converts near-infrared (NIR) light into localized heat, enabling the selective destruction of tumor cells. Among various photosensitizers, polydopamine (PDA) demonstrates high photothermal conversion efficiency and favorable biocompatibility, making it a widely adopted material in hydrogel formulation [75]. Incorporating photothermal agents into HFMs enables multifunctionality, particularly enabling combined therapeutic approaches for skin cancer treatment.

Photothermal agents are also employed in antimicrobial applications. The photothermal effect facilitates deep tissue penetration (up to 1 cm), circumvents bacterial resistance, and effectively eliminates multidrug-resistant microorganisms and biofilms [76]. Various nanomaterials, including graphene, carbon nanostructures, black phosphorus, boron nitride, and MXenes, have been developed as photothermal antimicrobial agents [77]. Given the skin's constant exposure to pathogens and heightened susceptibility to infection, particularly in conditions involving barrier disruption (e.g., diabetic wounds), the integration of antibacterial nanomaterials enhances the therapeutic potential of HFMs.

Drug sustained-release

Drug sustained release (DSR) refers to the controlled, prolonged release of therapeutic agents to maintain effective concentrations at the target site while reducing dosing frequency. Compared with immediate-release systems, DSR improves patient adherence and enhances therapeutic outcomes. This approach is especially valuable in managing chronic conditions that require long-term or frequent drug administration.

Achieving and maintaining effective drug concentrations in the full thickness of the skin remains a key challenge in transdermal delivery [78]. Continuous release enables uniform drug distribution and sustained therapeutic levels. Hydrogels are widely used as carriers for controlled release due to their ability to encapsulate bioactive molecules and release them gradually [79]. Drug release from hydrogels relies on the diffusion of hydrophilic compounds through a porous polymer network [80], driven by solvent exchange with the surrounding environment [81]. Furthermore, these materials can be engineered to degrade over time, enabling long-term controlled release.

In addition to direct drug encapsulation, hydrogels can be combined with nanoparticles to enhance therapeutic efficacy. The hydrogel matrix maintains the structural integrity and functional activity of the embedded nanoparticles while offering design flexibility [82]. Common nanoparticle systems include liposomes, metal-based nanoparticles, and polymeric nanoparticles. As topical treatments for skin diseases often require repeated administration, sustained-release systems based on nanomaterial encapsulation provide an effective strategy for improving compliance and therapeutic efficiency in dermatological applications.

Remarks, challenges and perspectives

Skin, as an external protective barrier for human beings, plays a significant role in protecting our body from diseases, trauma and injuries. Due to the complex wound repair process and microenvironment changes of the skin, wound repair and regeneration are the important research focuses of tissue engineering and remain difficult problems in medicine. Therefore, developing advanced materials to create a better microenvironment for cell proliferation and accelerate wound healing is one of the key topics in the field of tissue engineering. At present, although artificial skin materials such as pigskin, “soft and moist” hydrogels, and nanofiber scaffold materials have been developed, there are still challenges in the regulation of the skin microenvironment.

HFMs have demonstrated broad applicability in the treatment of skin diseases owing to their excellent biocompatibility, safety, photothermal performance, and transdermal delivery. This review mainly focuses on the application of HFMs in the treatment of various skin diseases, including wound repair, inflammatory skin diseases, and discosmetic dermatosis. The application of HFMs in skin diseases requires attention for the following special aspects. Firstly, the accumulation of degradation products of polymer materials should be safe, mild and non-inflammatory. For instance, the hydrolysates of gelatin microneedles include oligopeptide fragments [127]. Excessive accumulation of oligopeptide fragments may induce local immune responses. The various chemical components degraded from the materials (such as additives, impurities, monomers, oligomers and other degradation products, etc.) can cause different types of tissue reactions, such as inflammatory reactions. The severity of the reactions is closely related to the toxicity, concentration, total amount, degradation rate and duration of the degradation. When the degradation products have greater toxicity and larger volume, they will cause stronger inflammatory response in skin. For skin diseases where inflammatory responses have already occurred, materials with low toxicity should be used as much as possible. Materials that may produce degradation products of benzene rings and aromatic hydrocarbons, which may cause cancer, must be avoided from selection. Additionally, an increasing number of recent studies have used compounds with natural components. There is recent research on developing squid ink, as a marine resource, to produce nanoparticle-reinforced biopolymer hydrogel for the treatment of oral ulcers in diabetes [128]. We believe that this type of natural material also has application potential for skin diseases.

Secondly, due to the fact that HFMs puncture can cause damage to the skin barrier, its application in specific skin diseases is currently limited. For infectious wounds, microneedles can penetrate bacterial biofilms to realize deep drug delivery and achieve good clinical effects. For other skin diseases with complete or damaged skin barriers, long-term use of highly swelling micrystals may disrupt the lipid arrangement of the stratum corneum. The use of thermoreversible hydrogel can reduce skin barrier damage. When it comes to dissolving MNs, we think that although dissolving or degradable MNs can carry relatively more drugs, their main component matrix dissolves or degrades after penetration the skin. Similarly, the matrix composition of HFMs may exacerbate skin diseases, especially among patients with reduced skin barrier function, so research on the effectiveness of HFMs for atopic dermatitis, one of the most common inflammatory skin diseases, is relatively limited. Furthermore, the mechanical strength and physical strength of HFMs are not as good as traditional MNs, which may affect their performance and durability in practical applications.

Thirdly, most HFMs do not fit well with the skin and tend to fall off easily. They require the assistance of medical tapes and other equipment to achieve the desired penetration depth and fixation, which seriously affects the practical application of HFMs. Moreover, the good biocompatibility of HFMs also provides conditions for bacterial infection, not only causing inconvenience in the storage and use of HFMs, but also increasing the risk of wound infection. Loading a certain amount of antibacterial infection drugs (e.g., β-lactam class (such as penicillin, cephalosporin), macrolide class (such as azithromycin), quinolone class (such as levofloxacin), polymyxin, etc.) in the hydrogel is expected to be used to inhibit potential bacterial infection risks during storage and use. It is worth noting that the rational use of antibacterial drugs requires taking into account the type of bacteria, the patient's constitution, and the characteristics of the drugs. Of course, adding antibacterial drugs to the hydrogel must be carried out in a standardized manner under the guidance of a doctor. And more, despite the efficient drug delivery capacity of HFMs, they are not suitable for drugs that require daily or prompt administration because prolonged retention of matrix components in the skin may discourage adoption and trigger side effects. Drug loading and release efficiency are also key points that should be taken into account and optimized in skin diseases with different microenvironments.

Despite these limitations, HFMs offer a promising alternative to conventional MNs, particularly in applications where controlled or sustained drug release is advantageous. Unlike degradable MNs, non-soluble hydrogel-based systems do not disintegrate within the skin, minimizing residue accumulation and offering more precise drug release profiles. The future development of HFMs should focus on the following aspects. Firstly, the structure of existing HFMs can be optimized by incorporating auxiliary materials to improve their mechanical properties and reduce the pain during skin puncture. Secondly, portable HFMs should be developed to improve compliance among patients with skin diseases. Thirdly, HFMs can be combined with artificial intelligence, optical imaging, and biomarker detection technologies to predict changes in the skin structure and the possible efficacy of treatment. Secondly, the future research of HFMs should also focus on their application in the management of chronic skin diseases, which account for large proportions of skin diseases with an urgent clinical need. We think the precise regulation of the multi-functional Janus particles can be extended to the microneedle system to achieve targeted and precise drug delivery [129]. Referring to this technology is expected to achieve precise treatment of skin. Finally, most of the existing research focuses on the therapeutic efficacy of new materials for skin diseases, while there is a relatively lack of studies on the regulation and influence of HFMs on the key pathogenic signaling pathways of various skin diseases. Further in-depth researches are also needed on the influence of materials on microscopic mechanisms.

The development of novel and highly efficient HFMs faces several key challenges that hinder their practical applications, especially in the fields of e.g. material limitations and formulation complexity, drug solubility and delivery efficiency, fabrication and scalability, and clinical translation barriers. Exactly, the range of biocompatible materials suitable for HFM fabrication remains limited. Balancing mechanical strength, swelling capacity and drug-loading efficiency is critical. For example, polyvinyl alcohol-based HFMs require precise crosslinking with citric acid and augmentation with polyvinylpyrrolidone to achieve optimal performance. Hydrophobic drugs like risperidone pose significant challenges due to poor aqueous solubility, necessitating cyclodextrin complexes to enhance transdermal delivery. Dual-functionality systems require careful optimization to ensure simultaneous oxidative stress alleviation and angiogenesis promotion. Reproducible fabrication processes for HFM arrays with consistent microconduit formation are technically demanding. Scaling up production while maintaining precision in needle geometry and drug reservoir integration remains a hurdle. Long-term biocompatibility and safety profiles of HFMs in human skin require further validation. Regulatory challenges, such as standardization of in vivo performance metrics (e.g., sustained release over 3–10 days for risperidone), must be addressed. Advanced applications like melanoma therapy demand HFMs capable of multimodal delivery (e.g., chemotherapy + immunotherapy), which complicates formulation design. Passive interstitial fluid extraction for diagnostic monitoring introduces additional stability and sensitivity requirements. Future research should focus on expanding material libraries, optimizing drug-carrier interactions, and streamlining manufacturing processes to overcome these barriers. The optimization and standardization of materials, in-depth long-term toxicological and biocompatibility studies are needed to ensure the safety of its complete degradation products. At the same time, industry standards for components, mechanical properties and pharmacokinetics should be established to meet the requirements of drug regulatory authorities, such as the US Food and Drug Administration. There should be a breakthrough in large-scale production processes. At present, most preparation technologies are limited to the laboratory level. Developing large-scale microneedle patch production which are low-cost, high-efficiency and comply with Good Manufacturing Practice (GMP) standards is important for the transformation of research achievements into clinical applications. In addition, the design of clinical trials needs to first focus on skin diseases that have relatively more laboratory researches, such as infectious ulcers, psoriasis, vitiligo, skin tumors, etc. Large-scale and multi-center randomized controlled trials, including the evaluation dimensions such as actual clinical efficacy, patient compliance, and improvement in quality of patient's life, will provide conclusive evidence to prove superiority of HFMs over traditional therapies. Overall, although HFMs have demonstrated many advantages, there is still a long way to go before they can be put into practical clinical use.
