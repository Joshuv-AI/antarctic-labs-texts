Exploration of the GRAS family in Dimocarpus longan: identification, characterization, expression analysis and DlGRAS24 role in flowering time
Authors: Jiasui Cao, Xuelian Sang, Liqin Liu, Jinlin Gou, Yao Liu, Ci Ren, Zhixin Zhang, Dengwei Jue, Shengyou Shi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLongan is a perennial woody plant, and flowering is crucial for its development and the improvement of its economic value. The GRAS gene family has an important impact on how plants respond to both biotic and abiotic stressors as well as their growth and development. However, current research on the GRAS gene family in longan remains relatively limited.ResultsIn this study, we identified a total of 56 GRAS gene family members in longan. Phylogenetic analysis revealed that these GRAS members were classified into 14 subfamilies. There are eight tandem duplications and eight segmental duplications in DlGRAS genes, indicating that the expansion of the GRAS family in longan may be driven by tandem duplication and segmental duplication. GRAS family genes showed differential expression patterns of diversity during the flowering process of the “SX” (“Shixia”) and “SJ” (“Sijimi”) varieties, and DlGRAS24 displayed a significant downregulation in ‘SJ’ during the T1-T2 stage. According to a subcellular localization study, DlGRAS24 is located in the nucleus, The DlGRAS24 over-expression Arabidopsis lines exhibited early flowering, and the expression levels of CO and LFY increased, indicating that DlGRAS24 promoted flowering by interacting with CO and LFY.ConclusionThis study conducted a comprehensive analysis of the GRAS gene family members in longan. Our results fill the gap in the identification of the longan GRAS gene family and provide a theoretical basis and reference for the regulation of flowering by the DlGRAS gene family.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-025-07421-w.Keywords: Dimocarpus Longan lour., GRAS family, Transcription factors, Genome-wide identification, Functional analysis

Background

As a transcription factor (TF), GRAS strongly influences plant growth, development, and stress. The initials of three early members of the family—gibberellic acid insensitive (GAI) [1], repressor of GA1-3 mutant (RGA) [2], and scarecrow (SCR) [3]— were combined to form the GRAS name. GRAS proteins are 400–770 amino acids long and have a conserved GRAS domain at the carboxyl C-terminus and a non-conserved GRAS domain at the N-terminus, which contains an inherently disordered protein (IDD), and a conserved DELLA domain [4]. The GRAS domain contains 390 amino acids and the GRAS domain is composed of five distinct motifs [5]. Leucine Heptad Repeat I (LHRI), VHIID, Leucine Heptad Repeat II (LHRII), PFYRE, and SAW are members of the GRAS domain [5]. VHIID is the core of the structure, and its motifs represent V for valine, H for histidine, I for isoleucine, and D for aspartic acid [6]. The three sequence characteristics of the PFYRE motif are proline (P), phenylalanine, tyrosine (FY), arginine, and glutamic acid (RE) residues. The SAW motif contains sequence features WX7G and LXW [6].

Plant flowering is induced by multiple internal signaling pathways, including photoperiod, vernalization, ambient temperature, and gibberellin pathway [7]. The GRAS gene family, a unique gene family in plants, plays important roles in various key processes of plant growth and development. Among them, the DELLA subfamily has attracted particular attention for its function in regulating flowering. Numerous studies have confirmed that DELLA proteins are key components of gibberellin signal transduction, with their multiple conserved domains exert regulatory effects in the gibberellin signaling pathway. In Arabidopsis thaliana, five DELLA proteins have been identified: RGA (repressor of GA1-3 mutant), GAI (gibberellic acid insensitive), RGL1 (RGA-like 1), RGL2 (RGA-like 2), and RGL3 (RGA-like 3) [8]. Among these, RGA, RGL1, and RGL2 jointly inhibit normal growth and development of petals and stamens in Arabidopsis thaliana through a complex regulatory network [9]. Chhun et al. isolated a gibberellin-insensitive mutant Slr1-d3 in rice, in which the mutation of the SLR1 gene (a DELLA protein gene in the gibberellin signaling pathway) result in a reduction in the number of viable pollen grains and a decrease in male fertility [10]. In the DELLA subfamily of mango, overexpression of MiSLR1 and MiSLR2 promotes flowering in transgenic Arabidopsis thaliana [11]. However, except for the DELLA subfamily, studies on the functions of other subfamilies in the GRAS gene family in flower development are very limited. With the advancement of research technologies, however, some studies have begun to focus on the potential roles of other members of the GRAS family in regulating plant flowering. In Arabidopsis thaliana, the SCL13 antisense lines flower approximately 3 days earlier than the wild type under long-day conditions and about 25 days earlier under short-day conditions [12]. Additionally, expression pattern analysis of SCL13 that it has relatively high mRNA levels in petals and sepals, suggesting that it may play a role in flowering-related growth and development [12]. Zhang et al. found that the GRAS protein DHD1 interacts with OsHAP5C/D by regulating the expression of Ehd1, thereby delaying rice heading (flowering time) and increased rice yield [13]. In tomato, overexpression of SlGRAS24 disrupts the balance between gibberellin and auxin, thereby interfering with the normal development of anthers and leading to a 75% reduction in tomato fruit set [14]. In the context of hormonal induction of flowering, the expression profiles of members of the LS, LISCL, PAT1, SCL and SHR subfamilies demonstrate their participation in gibberellin-, 6-benzyladenine-, and sugar-mediated floral regulatory pathways in apple trees [15].

Longan, commonly known as Guiyuan, is an evergreen tree belonging to Sapindaceae. Its fruit has a unique flavor and rich nutritional value [16]. Longan, native to southern China, is popular because of its unique taste and nourishing properties [17]. Longan is an important tropical and subtropical fruit, with most of its cultivars, including “Shixia” (SX), flowering only once per year. The longan cultivar “Sijimi” (SJ) is a tropical ecotype that can flower multiple times per year. Investigating the flowering mechanism of longan will provide insight for promoting the development of the longan industry [18]. Early flowering longans can mature and reach the market earlier than common varieties, filling market gaps and meeting consumers’ demands for fresh longans [18]. This ability allows longans to command higher market prices and increases the economic benefits for growers [19]. Additionally, the maturity period of early flowering varieties is staggered with that of medium- and late-flowering varieties, which extends the longan sales cycle [18]. Growers can flexibly adjust their sales strategies according to market demand, thereby avoiding the price competition pressure caused by concentrated market entries [19]. Finally, the cultivation and promotion of superior early flowering varieties has increased variation in longan structures, enhanced the overall competitiveness of the longan industry, and promoted its diversified development [18]. Existing research indicates that GRAS family genes crucially influence the flowering by regulating important traits such as flowering time. However, the precise function of the longan GRAS family in the regulation of flowering has not yet been investigated. The main objective of this study was to identify genes belonging to the GRAS transcription factor family in the longan genome using bioinformatic tools. Additionally, we examined the location of chromosomes, patterns of intron/exon distribution, existence of conserved domains, and cis-regulatory elements. To ascertain their orthologous relationships and potential roles, a comparative phylogenetic study of GRAS in Arabidopsis and longan was conducted. The subcellular localization of DlGRAS24 was also determined. The function of DlGRAS24 in longan flowering was verified by quantitative real time polymerase chain reactions (qRT-PCR). This study will deepen our understanding of longan flowering mechanisms and provide important genetic resources, as well as a theoretical basis for breeding high-quality early- or late-flowering longan varieties.

Results

Identification of DlGRAS family genes

In this study, 56 GRAS genes were identified in longan and named DlGRAS1–DlGRAS56 according to their physical location on the chromosome (Fig. 3A). The fundamental properties of the 56 DlGRAS proteins (Supplementary Table 1), including the number of amino acids, molecular weight (MW), isoelectric point (pI), instability index, and subcellular localization, were examined in this study. The number of amino acids encoded by GRAS gene of longan was between 87 and 822. Their molecular weights were 9.70 to 93.32 kDa. The range of the isoelectric points was 4.56 to 9.42. DlGRAS15, DlGRAS23, DlGRAS26, DlGRAS28, DlGRAS29, DlGRAS31, and DlGRASA43 were the only stable proteins in this family (instability coefficient: <40). Based on their projected subcellular localization, proteins expressed by the DlGRAS family were primarily found in the cytoplasm, mitochondria, nucleus, and plasma membrane.

Phylogenetic analysis of DlGRAS family members

A phylogenetic tree was constructed using 33 previously identified Arabidopsis thaliana GRAS (AtGRAS) genes, 60 Oryza sativa GRAS (OsGRAS) genes, 55 litchi (Litchi chinensis Sonn.) GRAS (LcGRAS) genes, and 56 longan (Dimocarpus longan Lour.) GRAS (DlGRAS) genes (Supplementary Table 2).The GRAS genes in longan were classified into 14 subfamilies, with the distribution of members as follows: the SCR subfamily contained 4 members; the SCL3 subfamily included 3 members; the DLT, SCL4/7, LAS, Os19, and Os43 subfamilies each have 1 member; the DELLA subfamily comprised 4 members; the LISCL subfamily had 8 members; the HAM subfamily consisted of 13 members; the Os4 subfamily contained 3 members; the SHR subfamily included 5 members; and the PAT1 subfamily had 7 members. Additionally, four longan genes, namely DlGRAS3, DlGRAS12, DlGRAS13, and DlGRAS14, are assigned to the L-GRAS subfamily (Fig. 1). The L-GRAS subfamily contained 4 members of the longan DlGRAS family, suggesting that this litchi-specific subfamily may have unique functions in longan, litchi, or their close relative species.

Gene structures and composition of motifs and domains

Longan GRAS proteins were found to include ten comparatively conserved motifs (Fig. 2A, B), with a larger number of these motifs found at the C-end than at the N-end. We have identified 10 motifs, corresponding to LHR I (motifs 4 and 8), VHIID (motifs 1 and 5), LHR II (motifs 10 and 9), PFYRE (motifs 7 and 2), and SAW (motifs 3 and 6) (Supplementary Tables 3 and Supplementary Table 4). Motif1, Motif2, Motif4 and Motif8 existed in most DlGRAS, and only one conserved motif was present in DlGRAS17 (Motif1). Although the distribution of the conserved motifs within each subfamily was similar, the composition of conserved motifs in DlGRAS differed significantly among the subfamilies. Nonetheless, DlGRAS genes from different subfamilies have distinct conserved motifs. For example, DlGRAS8 in the SHR subfamily contained motif 6 and DlGRAS6 in the PAT1 subfamily contained mostly Motif4. Motif7 was present in the majority of DlGRAS44 genes in the LISCL subfamily. Additionally, this study also found that motif 5 was only present in the PAT1, HAM, and LISCL subfamilies.

Gene structures and composition of motifs and domains in DlGRAS genes. A A tree of the GRAS members in Longan. B Distribution of motifs in DlGRAS proteins. C Domain of DlGRAS genes. D Exon–intron structures of the DlGRAS genes

All DlGRAS members contained GRAS domains and members of the same subfamily contained the same conserved domains (Fig. 2C). For example, three of the four members of the DELLA family contained DELLA-conserved domains, whereas members of the PAT1 subfamily had GRAS conserved domains. The most GRAS conserved domains were located at the C-end. DlGRAS8 had a DUF4220 superfamily conserved domain, and DlGRAS3 had a TenA E At3g16990-like conserved domain.

In the structure of the DlGRAS gene, twenty-five genes contained introns. All PAT1 family members contain introns in their DlGRAS structures. DlGRAS6 and DlGRAS16 contain four introns, DlGRAS3 and DlGRAS55 contain three introns, and DlGRAS25 and DlGRAS30 contain two introns (Fig. 2D). DlGRAS4, DlGRAS5, DlGRAS8, DlGRAS10, DlGRAS13, DlGRAS18, DlGRAS24, DlGRAS26, DlGRAS34, DlGRAS35, DlGRAS37, DlGRAS39, DlGRAS41, DlGRAS42, DlGRAS43, DlGRAS46, DlGRAS49, DlGRAS52 and DlGRAS53 each contained one intron, and the remaining DlGRAS genes contained no introns.

These results indicate that different subfamilies have distinct structural compositions and exhibit great functional diversity.

Chromosomal distribution and collinearity analysis of DlGRAS

The distribution of GRAS genes on the longan chromosomes were also analyzed using the longan genome (Fig. 3A). They were densely distributed on chromosomes Chr4, Chr6, Chr13, and Chr14, with Chr4 and Chr13 each containing nine genes, representing the most abundant distribution. In contrast, only one gene was present on chromosomes Chr3 and Chr7, where the members were sparsely dispersed. To further explore the evolutionary relationships among GRAS family members, collinear analysis of the longan genome was conducted. Within the longan GRAS family, 13 genes located on Chr4, Chr9, Chr13, and Chr14 were found to have undergone tandem repeat events (Fig. 3A, Supplementary Table 5). Specifically, the genes on Chr4, Chr9, and Chr14 belong to the HAM subfamily, while those on Chr13 belong to the LISCL subfamily. Additionally, eight segmental duplication events were identified among 13 DlGRAS sequences (Fig. 3B, Supplementary Table 6). In summary, tandem duplication and segmental duplication may have played a major role in the evolution of the longan GRAS gene family.

Collinearity analysis was carried out for four plants (Longan, Arabidopsis, rice and litchi) in order to determine the evolutionary relationships of GRAS genes between various species (Fig. 3C, Supplementary Table 7). According to these findings, 31 gene pairs were found to be directly homologous in Arabidopsis and longan, 16 gene pairs were found to be directly homologous in rice and longan, and 54 gene pairs were found to be directly homologous in litchi and longan. Longan and litchi each have 18 non-homologous genes (Supplementary Table 8). Eighteen longan GRAS genes were located on five different longan chromosomes (Chr4, Chr9, Chr10, Chr13, Chr14), and eighteen litchi GRAS genes were located on ten different litchi chromosomes (Chr2, Chr3, Chr6, Chr8, Chr9, Chr10, Chr11, Chr13, Chr14, Chr15) (Supplementary Fig. 1). Furthermore, DlGRAS3, DlGRAS8, DlGRAS9, DlGRAS18, DlGRAS20, DlGRAS21, DlGRAS35, DlGRAS49, and DlGRAS55 have syntenic loci in Arabidopsis, rice, and litchi. These genes belong to the L-GRAS (DlGRAS3), SHR(DlGRAS8, DlGRAS21), HAM(DlGRAS9, DlGRAS55), SCL4/7(DlGRAS18), PAT1(DlGRAS35), and LISCL(DlGRAS49) subfamilies, respectively, suggesting that they may have conserved biological functions in plants.

Cis-acting element analysis of DlGRAS genes

The upstream 2 000 bp promoter region of 56 DlGRAS gene were analyzed, and 1436 elements were obtained, including light, growth development, hormone, and stress response elements (Fig. 4). All members of the DlGRAS gene family contain photoresponse-related elements, 17 of which also contain MYB-binding sites that influence photoresponse-related (MRE) related elements. Auxin, salicylic acid, abscisic acid, methyl jasmonate, and gibberellin are five other cis-acting elements linked to plant hormones. The presence of these cis-acting elements suggests that these hormones regulate DlGRAS.

Distribution of cis-acting elements of DlGRAS family members. The different colors and numbers of grids indicate the numbers of cis-regulatory elements. The histograms on the right represent the sum of the cis-regulatory elements in each category.

Cis-acting elements linked to abiotic stressors, including anaerobic induction, drought stress, hypoxia-specific induction, low-temperature responses, trauma responses, and defensive stress responses have also been identified in the DlGRAS gene family. Cis-acting elements associated with seed specificity, endosperm expression, zein-olysin metabolism, palisade mesophyll cell differentiation, circadian rhythm control, MYB-binding site involvement in flavonoid biosynthesis, cell cycle regulation, and meristem-specific activation have also been identified in this family.

In summary, the most important function of DlGRAS family is the photoresponse, which strongly influences hormone responses and abiotic stress. Meanwhile, members of the longan GRAS gene family play important roles in photoperiod, circadian rhythm, and stress pathways, demonstrating the diversity of the functions of longan GRAS genes.

Expression analysis of DlGRAS genes in different tissues and organs

The transcriptional expression of the 56 DlGRAS genes was analyzed using data from the roots, stems, leaves, flowers, fruitlets, and seeds of longan (Fig. 5, Supplementary Table 9). DlGRAS genes were differentially expressed in various longan tissues. DlGRAS members from the same subfamily exhibited similar expression patterns. While most SHR family genes exhibited low expression levels across all tissues, most PAT1 family genes displayed high expression levels across all tissues. The stems and pulp showed higher levels of DlGRAS4, DlGRAS5, DlGRAS24, and DlGRAS30, whereas the leaves and fruitlets showed higher levels of DlGRAS4, DlGRAS5, DlGRAS6, and DlGRAS24. While DlGRAS24 was highly expressed in leaf buds, DlGRAS4, DlGRAS5, and DlGRAS30 were highly expressed in flowers.

Analyses of GRAS family gene expression in different longan varieties during flowering

The expression pattern of DlGRAS genes at three flowering stages of ‘SX’ and ‘SJ’ were analyzed. The expression of the DlGRAS gene family in ‘SX’ longan was not significant during the T1-T2 stage, but some genes showed significant upregulation or downregulation during the T2-T3 stage (Fig. 6, Supplementary Table 10). The upregulated genes included DlGRAS4, DlGRAS5, DlGRAS6, DlGRAS24, DlGRAS36, and DlGRAS45, whereas the downregulated genes included DlGRAS10, DlGRAS34, DlGRAS35, DlGRAS43, DlGRAS46, and DlGRAS51. During the T1-T2 stage of ‘SJ’ longan, some genes showed significant upregulation or downregulation. The upregulated genes included DlGRAS1, DlGRAS10, DlGRAS11, DlGRAS14, DlGRAS21, DlGRAS25, DlGRAS27, DlGRAS51, and DlGRAS56, and the downregulated genes included DlGRAS22, DlGRAS24, DlGRAS40, and DlGRAS47. However, throughout the T2-T3 stage of ‘SJ’ longan, the expression levels of DlGRAS genes were not statistically significant. Additionally, among these 56 DlGRAS genes, DlGRAS24 was significantly downregulated by 4.3 times in ‘SJ’ during the T1-T2 stage, but no significant differential expression was observed during the T1-T2 stage in ‘SX’ longan. Based on the expression results obtained during longan flowering, we selected the DlGRAS24 gene for subsequent functional validation.

Subcellular localization analysis of DlGRAS24

To detect the location of DlGRAS24 protein in cells, we constructed a fusion protein expression vector (35 S: DlGRAS24-GFP) containing enhanced green fluorescent protein (GFP), introduced it into Arabidopsis mesophyll protoplasmic cells using a PEG-mediated method, and observed it using laser confocal microscopy (Fig. 7). Under excitation at 480 nm, the fluorescence signal of 35 S: DlGRAS24-GFP was observed only in the nucleus, and no GFP signal was observed in the cytoplasm or cell membrane (Fig. 7). In contrast, the GFP signal was observed in whole cells of the 35 S: GFP control group, with no specific localization. This suggests that DlGRAS24 is localized to the nucleus.

Phenotypic analysis of DlGRAS24 transgenic Arabidopsis

Two positive DlGRAS24-OE transgenic plant lines with high expression levels were chosen at random to examine phenotypic changes in the transgenic plants after DlGRAS24 was inserted into wild-type Arabidopsis for heterologous overexpression (Fig. 8A-B). Compared with wild-type plants, the flowering times of the transgenic strains were significantly different: the transgenic strains had bolted flowering at approximately thirteen leaves (approximately 23 d after germination), and the wild-type flowering time was 5 d later than that of the transgenic lines, showing more obvious early flowering (Fig. 8C-D, Supplementary Table 11).

A Flowering of Arabidopsis with overexpression of the DlGRAS24 gene. B, E-G Relative expression of endogenous genes in Arabidopsis DlGRAS24 overexpression lines. To clarify the reasons for the early flowering of transgenic strains compared to that of the wild type, the expression levels of AtCO, AtLFY, and AtAP2 in the wild type and DlGRAS24-OE strains were analyzed using qRT-PCR. The expression levels of AtCO and AtLFY in transgenic Arabidopsis were significantly increased (Fig. 8E-F). AtAP2 showed no significant change (Fig. 8G). These results suggest that DlGRAS24 may promote flowering through its interaction with AtCO and AtLFY.

Discussion

The GRAS gene family has been identified in many plants. In this study, there were more GRAS genes identified from the entire genome of longan were more than in Arabidopsis (Arabidopsis thaliana) (33) [20], cucumbers (Cucumis sativus) (37) [21], peach (Prunus persica) (48) [22], grapes (Vitis vinifera) (52) [23], tomatoes (Solanum lycopersicum) (54) [24], pitaya (Selenicereus undatus) (45) [25] and litchi (Litchi chinensis) (48) [26]. However, this gene count was lower than that in rice (Oryza sativa) (60) [27], soybeans (Glycine max) (117) [28], apples (Malus domestica) (78) [15], and kiwifruit (Actinidia chinensis) (86) [29]. In the present, 56 GRAS genes were identified in longans with a genome size of 483 Mb [30]. Zhu et al. found that the number of GRAS genes might be related to genome size [29]. Pitaya has 45 GRAS genes [25], and its genome (1.41Gb) [31] is larger than that of longan. However, there is no clear relationship between genome size and the number of GRAS family members. In the phylogenetic tree analysis, members of the longan GRAS gene family were divided into 14 subfamilies. Chen et al. [26] researched the GRAS genes of litchi and classified them into subfamilies. They identified three specific litchi genes, LITCHI014380, LITCHI026563, and LITCHI026564, that form a distinct branch. They named this unique branch the L-GRAS subfamily. In our study, we assigned four longan genes, DlGRAS3, DlGRAS12, DlGRAS13, and DlGRAS14, to the L-GRAS subfamily (Fig. 1). This indicates that this litchi-specific subfamily may have unique functions in longan, litchi, or their closely related species. Previous analysis of gene structures and conserved motifs demonstrated that most members of the same subfamily have similar gene structures and conserved motifs. This finding suggests that the functions of members of the same subfamily are similar [32]. Similar to Arabidopsis (67.6%) [27], rice (55%) [27], tomatoes (77.4%) [33], peppers (Capsicum annuum) (84%) [34], and kiwifruit (60.5%) [29], 55.4% of DlGRAS genes were intron-free in a prior exon-intron structural analysis. A tight evolutionary link between GRAS proteins has been suggested based on the comparatively large percentage of plant GRAS genes that lack introns. In addition, the longan GRAS gene family members had a higher proportion of introns in the PAT1 family (all members of this subfamily contained introns), which is similar to previous findings in kiwifruit [29]. Genes are retained in the plant genome via segments and tandem repeats [35]. The GRAS gene family was horizontally transferred from the bacterial genome through gene duplication, as described by Zhang et al. [36]. We have hypothesized that the expansion of the GRAS gene would have arisen by a mix of tandem repeat events (Supplementary Table 4) and segmental duplications events (Supplementary Table 5), as the longan GRAS gene family contained eight tandem repeat events and eight segmental duplications events. In addition, these gene pairs in the Os4, HAM, LISCL, and L-GRAS subfamilies are similar to those in other plant species [37]. Gene repeat events amplify DlGRAS, thereby contributing to its various roles in regard to controlling plant growth and development [38]. The expression profiles of DlGRAS genes exhibited significant varietal differences between ‘SX’ and ‘SJ’ longan. Notably, ‘SX’ displayed predominant differential expression during the T2-T3 transition, whereas ‘SJ’ showed earlier regulation at T1-T2. Several genes, including DlGRAS10, demonstrated opposite expression trends between the two varieties, suggesting distinct transcriptional regulation during flowering development.

Flowering is the key period of plant transition from the vegetative growth stage to the reproductive growth stage, and it strongly influences offspring reproduction and ecological adaptation [39]. Flowering time is important agronomic trait that affects crop yield and quality [40]. In addition, the GRAS gene family plays a crucial role in controlling plant growth and development [39]. For example, the SCL, PAT1, and DELLA subfamilies are significantly expressed during flower growth and development [39]. Previous studies have shown that the DELLA proteins [41] of the GRAS gene family inhibit flowering in plants, whereas relatively little research has been conducted on PAT1 proteins [42]. Tomato SlGRAS11 and SlGRAS18, which are members of the PAT1 subfamily, are highly expressed during flower development [33]. Differential expression of JrCIGRa-b and JrSCL13b-d was observed in walnuts during the different phases of flower bud development [43]. In the present study, combined with previous studies, we found that DlGRAS24 belongs to the PAT1 subfamily and is highly expressed in flowers. This indicates that DlGRAS24 may influence the formation of flowers in longans.

Ttranscription factors bind to cis-acting sites in promoter regions to control gene production [44]. Recently, researchers have identified transcription factors that regulate gene expression in plants, including WRKY [45, 46], bZIP [47, 48], MYB [49, 50], bHLH [51], and MADS-box [52, 53]. Similar to other transcription factors, GRAS binds to the cis-acting regions of certain functional genes to control their expression, which in turn controls plant growth, development, and stress reactions. For example, the DELLA protein in the GRAS transcription factor interacts with the photoperiod-regulated F-box protein FKF1 (FLAVIN-BINDING KELCH return-box1) to form an FKF1-DELLA linked feedback loop and a gibberellin signal, which cooperatively regulates plant flowering time [54]. The GRAS family transcription factor FaSCL8 in strawberries can directly bind to the promoter of FaVPT1 in the strawberry vacuolar phosphorus transporter and activate its transcription, thereby regulating the accumulation of phosphorus and sugar in fruits and promoting the formation of strawberry fruit maturation and quality [55]. Similar to previous studies, qRT-PCR analysis of endogenous genes in this study showed that the expression of CO and LFY in Arabidopsis strains overexpressing DlGRAS24 was significantly upregulated. Although the expression level of AP2 was not significant. Previous studies have shown that CO [56] and LFY [57, 58] are positive regulators of plant flowering, suggesting that DlGRAS24 promotes flowering by regulating their expression of CO and LFY. Even if DlGRAS24 regulates the expression of AP2 [59], other regulatory pathways may counteract or weaken this effect, resulting in an insignificant change [60]. The role of DlGRAS24 in longan flower creation was briefly examined in this study, offering a theoretical foundation for examining the molecular control mechanisms of longan flower production.

Conclusions

In this study, 56 DlGRAS genes were identified in longan, which divided into 14 subfamilies. Analyses of gene structures and motif compositions revealed the complex evolutionary history of the GRAS family in longan. Gene duplication has contributed to the expansion of the DlGRAS gene family. Furthermore, DlGRAS genes showed differential expression in regulating flowering among different longan varieties (“SX” and “SJ”). Specifically, the expression level of DlGRAS24 was significantly downregulated during the T1-T2 stages in “SJ”, while no significant change was observed in “SX”. Subcellular localization experiments demonstrated that DlGRAS24 protein was specifically localized in the nucleus. By constructing transgenic Arabidopsis lines and comparing them with wild-type Arabidopsis, combined with verification using quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR), it was found that the longan DlGRAS24 gene had a significant flowering-promoting function. This study provides a theoretical basis for the functional analysis of longan GRAS genes and offers important targets for longan molecular breeding.

Methods

Identification of longan GRAS family members and analysis of their protein physicochemical properties

Longan genome sequences (Genome, Gff3 and Coding sequence) were obtained from the Sapindaceae Genomic Database (http://www.sapindaceae.com/about.html) to locate the GRAS gene in longans [30]. The coding sequences (CDS) were translated into protein sequences using the “Batch Translate CDS to Protein” tool in TBtools. The TAIR database (https://www.Arabidopsis.org/) provided the protein sequences for A. thaliana. In TBtools [61], BLAST analysis was performed using the protein sequences of Arabidopsis thaliana and longan with a relatively sensitive threshold (E-value set to 1e-5) to identify potential GRAS genes in the longan genome. Subsequently, the identified sequences were verified using the Pfam (http://pfam.xfam.org/) database with default parameters and the SMART website (http://smart.embl.de/) [62].

ProtParam (https://web.expasy.org/protparam/), an online program developed by ExPASy [63], was used to predict the molecular mass, and isoelectric point of the GRAS protein sequences. The Molecular Bioinformatics Center web tool (http://cello.life.nctu.edu.tw/) was used to predict the subcellular localization.

Phylogenetic analysis of DlGRASs, AtGRASs, OsGRASs, and LcGRASs

The protein sequence of members of the Oryza sativa GRAS gene family was obtained from PlantTFDB (http://planttfdb.cbi.pku.edu.cn/), and the protein sequence of members of the litchi GRAS gene family was obtained from the Sapindaceae Genomic Database (http://www.sapindaceae.com/about.html). The phylogenetic tree of the GRAS family in Arabidopsis, rice, litchi and longan was constructed using MEGA7.0 software with the maximum likelihood method, and the bootstrap test was performed with 1000 iterations [64]. The Internet application Interactive Tree of Life (iTOL) v5 (https://itol.embl.de/) was used for visualization.

Gene structures and recognition of conserved motifs and domains

Using the longan GFF3 file, the gene structure of each DlGRAS gene was analyzed and visualized using TBtools software [61].

To predict and examine the conserved motifs of the DlGRAS gene family proteins, motif analysis was carried out using the MEME website (https://meme-suite.org/meme/tools/meme); the number of conserved motifs was set to 10, and other parameters were left at their default settings. The drawings were visualized using TBtools software [61].

The NCBI Batch CD-Search Tool (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/Structure) was used to evaluate the domains, and the drawings were again visualized using TBtools software [61].

Chromosomal location and homology analysis of GRAS genes in longan

According to the physical location of longan genome, GFF file of longan was used to draw the chromosomal position diagram of DlGRAS through Show Gene on Chromosome. At the same time, to explore the relationship between homologous proteins, collinearity analysis was performed on the GRAS genes of longan, Arabidopsis, rice and litchi. The analysis results were displayed using the multicollinearity scanning tool (One Step MCScanX Super Fast) in TBtools software [61].

Analysis of cis-acting elements of the DlGRAS family

TBtools software was used to extract the 2000 bp gene sequence of the GRASstart codon ATG upstream of longan as the promoter region [61]. To anticipat-e the cis-acting elements of the promoters, the retrieved promoter sequen-ces were uploaded to the Plant CARE website (https://bioinformatics.psb.ugent.be/webtools/plantcare/html/) [65]. A visual examination was then conducted using TBtools software [61].

Expression analysis of longan GRAS gene

RNA-seq data from the NCBI Sequence Reading Archive (accession number GSE84467) were retrieved to analyze the expression patterns of the DlGRAS gene in longan tissues, including roots, stems, leaves, flowers, fruitlet, seeds, pericarp, pulp, seed, flower buds, and leaf buds. The TBtools software was used to draw heat maps [61].

Expression analysis of flowering in different varieties of longan

The expression profiles of DlGRAS genes at three different flowering stages (dormant stage (T1), flower primordium stage (T2), and flower organ-forming stage (T3)) in two longan cultivars, ‘Sijimi’ (‘SJ’) and ‘Shixia’ (‘SX’), were analyzed using RNA-seq data (SRS2241241-SRS2241258). Visual analysis was conducted using the TBtools software [61].

Subcellular localization analysis

Using the cloned DlGRAS24 sequence, primers designed without a terminator for PCR amplification were used to obtain the full-length ORF of DlGRAS24. After being attached to the pMD18-T vector (TaKaRa Biotechnology, Dalian, China), the PCR result was transformed into DH5α Escherichia coli. Subsequently, restriction enzyme digestion was performed on both the pBWA(V)HS-osgfp (Biorun Biotechnology, Wuhan, China) and DlGRAS24 plasmids, and the digested products were recovered for ligation. To obtain the pBWA(V)HS-DlGRAS24-osgfp plasmid, the ligated plasmid was transformed into DH5α Escherichia coli, and following positive detection, the appropriate strains for sequencing were chosen. Finally, PEG-mediated transformation was used to introduce the plasmid into Arabidopsis thaliana protoplasts, and the cells were left in the dark for 24 and 48 h. A confocal laser-scanning microscope (TCS-SP8MP; Leica, Germany) was used to capture images. An empty pBWA (V)-HS-osgfp vector was used as control.

Overexpression vector construction and acquisition of transgenic plants

The full-length CDS of DlGRAS24, under the control of the CaMV35S promoter, was cloned and inserted into the BamHI and SacI sites of pBI121 to construct an overexpression vector. Transgenic plants were generated by transferring the freeze-thaw method-constructed overexpression vector pBI121-DlGRAS24 into Agrobacterium strain GV3101. Following the Agrobacterium floral dip approach to transfer the DlGRAS24 gene into wild-type Arabidopsis thaliana, the positive Arabidopsis thaliana transgenic lines were screened on an MS solid medium containing 30 µg/mL hygromycin under 16-hour light and 8-hour dark conditions at 24 ℃.

Phenotypic analysis of DlGRAS transgenic Arabidopsis thaliana and expression analysis of related genes

The transgenic Arabidopsis thaliana lines and the wild-type lines were respectively cultivated in a growth chamber at 22 ± 1 °C, a light intensity of 3000 lx and a photoperiod of 16-hour light and 8-hour dark. Six plants were taken from each line to observe traits such as the number of rosette leaves (approximately three weeks after germination). The mean values were calculated and plotted using GraphPad Prism 8 software.

The RNA of the T3 generation transgenic plants was extracted using a Biosharp polysaccharide plant total RNA extraction kit (Biosharp, Hefei, China). All-in-One First-Strand Synthesis MasterMix (with dsDNase) reverse transcription cDNA from Jiangsu Bristol Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (China) (Supplementary Table 12)was used for RT-qPCR amplification. Simultaneously, fluorescent quantitative primers were designed using the Primer Premier 6 software and synthesized by Shenggong Bioengineering Co., Ltd. (China) (Supplementary Table 8). Arabidopsis TUB was used as a reference gene. The total reaction system was Taq SYBR Green qRT-PCR Premix (Universal) (10 µL), upstream and downstream primers (0.5 µL), cDNA (1 µL), ddH2O (8 µL). Three biological replicates were set up, and the experimental data were analyzed via 2−ΔΔCt [66]. Quantitative real-time PCR was performed using a PCR instrument-qTOWER 2.2 (Jena Analytical Instruments AG, Germany).

Statistical analysis

Data are means ± SD of 3 biological replicates, and the significance of differences between treatments and controls was analyzed using One-Way ANOVA. Asterisks indicate statistically significant differences (*P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001 and **** < 0.0001).
