Sudden death with pseudomembranous fibrinonecrotic enteritis in a zoo-housed hippopotamus (Hippopotamus amphibius): predominant isolation and genomic characterization of Clostridium perfringens type A
Authors: Thach Phan Van, Byungkwan Oh, Hadeer Khaled, Hyun Jong Park, Jaime C Cabarles, Chae Woong Lim, Bumseok Kim, John Hwa Lee, Sang-Ik Oh
Published: 2026

INTRODUCTION

Clostridium perfringens is a Gram-positive, spore-forming anaerobic bacterium that causes a wide spectrum of diseases in humans and animals, ranging from gas gangrene to fatal enterotoxemias [1]. Based on six major typing toxin genes, C. perfringens is currently divided into seven toxinotypes (A–G), of which type A (CPA) strains are the most prevalent and clinically important [2,3,4]. Toxinotype A strains—defined by the presence of the alpha-toxin gene (plc) and the absence of the other major typing toxin genes—have been reported in association with necrotic or hemorrhagic enteritis and enterotoxemia in domestic ruminants, pigs, foals, and occasionally wildlife [3,5].

Although C. perfringens–associated enteric disease is well documented in humans and domestic animals [5], clinicopathologic descriptions in large megaherbivores remain limited, with only sporadic reports in hippopotamids, rhinoceroses, and elephants [6,7]. The hippopotamus is a large semi-aquatic herbivore and a non-ruminating foregut fermenter with a complex forestomach, which may influence gastrointestinal microbial ecology [8]. As the species is widely maintained in zoological collections, husbandry practices (e.g., diet management, enclosure changes, and routine procedures) are critical determinants of gastrointestinal stability and may predispose to opportunistic enteric disease under disruption of gut homeostasis.

In zoological collections, sudden death events in large captive herbivores pose substantial welfare and biosecurity challenges, and rapid, practical diagnostic workflows are essential. Because CPA can be detected in clinically normal mammals, etiologic attribution in single cases requires careful integration of lesion patterns and bacteriology and, when feasible, toxin detection and/or in situ confirmation. Fatal clostridial infections in megaherbivores are rarely documented, and only a single neonatal case of CPA-associated enterotoxemia has been reported in a captive hippopotamus from South Korea [7]. Here, we describe peracute fatal enteritis in an adult zoo-housed hippopotamus in which CPA was the predominant β-hemolytic anaerobic isolate, and we provide a genome-based reference to support future comparative and epidemiologic investigations.

CASE PRESENTATION

A 40-year-old female hippopotamus (Hippopotamus amphibius) housed at Jeonju Zoo (Republic of Korea) was found dead without preceding clinical signs. The animal had been maintained under standard zoo husbandry conditions and fed grass hay, pelleted herbivore feed, and seasonal vegetables. No recent dietary changes, medical treatments, or environmental stressors were recorded in the preceding weeks. The only notable environmental condition was hot summer weather around the time of death, with a recorded daily maximum ambient temperature of 34°C. The animal was last observed clinically normal at approximately midnight, and the carcass was discovered in the early morning. Necropsy commenced approximately 4 h after discovery.

Grossly, the skeletal muscles were diffusely pale pink. In the intestinal tract, the mucosal surface was diffusely covered by adherent fibrinous pseudomembranes and showed marked hyperemia (Fig. 1A and B). Minimal content was present in the small intestine, with no gas distension throughout the gastrointestinal tract. Microscopically, the small intestine showed severe villous necrosis and acute mucosal blunting with an overlying fibrinonecrotic pseudomembrane and mucous exudate (Fig. 1C). Marked mucosal hemorrhage and submucosal congestion were also present (Fig. 1D). Severe inflammatory cell infiltration, composed predominantly of neutrophils with admixed mononuclear cells, was observed mainly within the mucosa (Fig. 1E). Notably, numerous intralesional bacilli were present within the necrotic/exudative mucosa (Fig. 1F), which were confirmed as Gram-positive bacilli consistent with C. perfringens morphology by gram staining. No significant lesions were observed in other major organs.

Intestinal contents and small intestinal tissue were submitted for bacteriological examination. Aerobic culture yielded Escherichia coli without major virulence genes, whereas anaerobic culture on blood agar plates incubated at 37°C under anaerobic conditions yielded abundant growth of large β-hemolytic colonies. Genomic DNA was extracted from isolated β-hemolytic colonies using a commercial kit (QIAamp DNA Mini Kit; Qiagen, Germany). Multiplex polymerase chain reaction (PCR) targeting the major C. perfringens toxin genes was performed as previously described [2]. The isolate was positive only for the plc gene and negative for the other five major toxinotyping genes, consistent with CPA.

To further characterize the predominant anaerobic isolate, whole-genome sequencing (WGS) was performed using an Illumina paired-end platform. De novo assembly yielded a draft genome (38 contigs; total 3,249,720 bp; GC 28.1%), including chromosomal contigs totaling 3,201,590 bp and a 48,130 bp contig predicted to represent a plasmid (GC 27.3%). The genome was annotated using Prokka [9], identifying 2,871 predicted coding sequences, ribosomal RNA genes (5S, 16S, and 23S), 79 transfer RNAs, and a single transfer-messenger RNA (Fig. 2A). The draft genome assembly of OH-TH01 strain was deposited in GenBank (National Center for Biotechnology Information) under accession number JBTWEQ000000000 (BioProject PRJNA1404688). Comparative analysis with 28 reference C. perfringens genomes placed the isolate within lineage V, clustering most closely with strain EHE-NE18 in the phylogenetic reconstruction (Fig. 2B, Table 1), supporting lineage-level placement [10].

Genomic screening for putative virulence and antimicrobial resistance determinants was performed to support comparative interpretation of the isolate. The draft genome was screened against the Virulence Factor Database and Comprehensive Antibiotic Resistance Database databases [11,12]. Putative virulence-associated genes included plc (alpha-toxin), pfoA (perfringolysin O), colA (collagenase), cloSI (alpha-clostripain), and multiple predicted sialidase/hexosaminidase genes (nanH, nanI, nanJ, nagH, nagI, nagJ, and nagK) (Table 2). Predicted antimicrobial resistance determinants included mprF and tetracycline-associated genes tetA(P)/tetB(P).

DISCUSSION

CPA is ubiquitous in the environment and gastrointestinal tract of animals, yet it can cause peracute, often fatal enteric disease under predisposing conditions [5]. In the present case, an adult hippopotamus was found dead without premonitory signs and showed severe fibrinonecrotic and hemorrhagic enteritis with abundant intralesional bacilli within necrotic/exudative mucosa. The lesion pattern was compatible with clostridial-type enteritis described in domestic species.

Bacteriological examination supported a clostridial-type process in that anaerobic culture yielded abundant growth of large β-hemolytic colonies, whereas aerobic culture yielded E. coli only. Multiplex toxinotyping PCR identified the predominant anaerobic isolate as CPA. Because C. perfringens may be present as an enteric commensal, etiologic attribution in single cases is challenging [13]. Differential diagnoses for acute hemorrhagic enteritis in large herbivores include salmonellosis, other clostridial enteric diseases, ischemic or obstructive intestinal injury, and septicemic bacterial disease. In this case, Salmonella spp. was not detected, and other bacterial pathogens were not isolated from samples. Therefore, the combination of compatible gross and histopathologic lesions, abundant intralesional bacilli on hematoxylin and eosin (H&E), and predominant isolation of a β-hemolytic anaerobe with a toxinotype A profile supported a presumptive diagnosis of clostridial-type fibrinonecrotic/hemorrhagic enteritis with predominant isolation of CPA. Compared with the only previous report of CPA-associated disease in a neonatal hippopotamus [7], which described hemorrhagic enteritis with mucoid and hemorrhagic changes on the mucosal surface, the present adult hippopotamus showed a similar pathological lesion pattern, including fibrinonecrotic pseudomembrane formation and marked intestinal hyperemia. Although etiologic confirmation remains presumptive in the absence of toxin detection or in situ confirmation, these findings indicate that adult H. amphibius may develop a comparable clostridial-type enteritis associated with predominant CPA isolation.

A specific predisposing trigger could not be identified in this case because no recent dietary change, antimicrobial administration, medical treatment, or husbandry procedure was recorded. The only notable environmental condition was hot summer weather around the time of death, with a recorded daily maximum ambient temperature of 34°C. Although high ambient temperature may have acted as a nonspecific environmental stressor, there was no direct evidence of heat stress, dehydration, or heat-associated intestinal dysbiosis; therefore, this factor should be interpreted cautiously.

WGS of the predominant isolate provided additional insight into genomic features and a predicted virulence repertoire. The overall genome characteristics were consistent with published C. perfringens genomes [14]. The predicted virulence profiles included membrane-damaging toxins (plc and pfoA), tissue-degrading enzymes (colA and cloSI), and mucosal barrier-modifying sialidases/hexosaminidases (nag/nan family members), all of which have been implicated in severe clostridial enteric disease (Table 2) [15,16,17,18]. Phylogenomic analysis clustered OH-TH01 within lineage V and most closely with strain EHE-NE18 [19], supporting the broad host distribution of related C. perfringens lineages. The genome also harbored predicted antimicrobial resistance determinants, including mprF and tetA(P)/tetB(P) [20]. Because these findings were based on in silico screening without phenotypic antimicrobial susceptibility testing, they should be interpreted as predicted determinants rather than confirmed clinical resistance. Nonetheless, these results highlight the value of continued antimicrobial stewardship and surveillance in captive settings where resistant anaerobes may emerge or circulate.

Key limitations include the absence of direct toxin detection in intestinal contents, lack of in situ confirmation of the organism within lesions (e.g., IHC/FISH), and lack of phenotypic antimicrobial susceptibility testing. Nonetheless, this report expands the clinicopathologic spectrum of clostridial-type enteritis in captive megaherbivores. To the best of our knowledge, the present case report also provides the first genomic characterization of a CPA isolate from an adult hippopotamus, offering a reference for zoo-based surveillance and comparative outbreak investigations.

In conclusion, clostridial-type fibrinonecrotic and hemorrhagic enteritis should be considered in the differential diagnosis of sudden death in adult hippopotami housed in zoological collections. In practice, a presumptive diagnosis can be supported by prompt necropsy with histopathology and anaerobic culture, together with toxinotyping PCR to characterize predominant C. perfringens isolates. In this case, the concordance of lesion pattern, abundant intralesional bacilli on H&E, and predominant isolation of CPA supported a presumptive diagnosis of clostridial-type fibrinonecrotic-hemorrhagic enteritis with predominant isolation of CPA, while definitive etiologic confirmation would require toxin detection and/or in situ demonstration of the organism within lesions. WGS of OH-TH01 strain provided additional genomic context and a reference dataset for future comparative and epidemiologic investigations in captive megaherbivores.
