Background

Primary cardiac tumors are rare, with an incidence ranging from 0.17% to 0.19% in autopsy series. Among these, lipomas represent approximately 8–10% of benign cardiac neoplasms [1]. Cardiac lipomas are composed of mature adipose tissue and may arise in any cardiac chamber or layer, including subepicardial, subendocardial, or intramyocardial regions [2].

Most cardiac lipomas are small and asymptomatic, often discovered incidentally during imaging or autopsy. However, larger lesions may result in mass effect, leading to chamber compression, impaired filling, valvular dysfunction, or arrhythmias [3]. Symptomatic cases usually present with nonspecific signs such as exertional dyspnea, chest discomfort, or palpitations. Giant lipomas may occasionally be misdiagnosed as malignant tumors or considered inoperable due to their size or anatomical involvement.

Lipomas involving the atrioventricular groove are particularly rare and pose considerable surgical challenges due to their proximity to the conduction system and coronary vessels [2]. Although several case reports and series have documented cardiac lipomas, only a few have described massive tumors involving the right atrium. In the largest systematic review to date, Hu et al. identified just a handful of cases exceeding 10 cm, and even fewer required complex surgical intervention [4]. Furthermore, only isolated reports have addressed cardiac lipomas initially referred for heart transplantation [5]. In this case report we show a rare case of subtotal resection of a giant right atrial lipoma (910 g) with following atrial wall reconstruction.

Case presentation

A 37-year-old male patient (89 kg, 186 cm, body mass index 25,7 kg/m2) was referred to our center for evaluation and consideration for inclusion on the heart transplantation waiting list due to a progressively enlarging cardiac mass and worsening heart failure symptoms. His medical history was significant for history of episode of paroxysmal atrial fibrillation, chronic kidney disease G2 according to KDIGO (glomerular filtration rate 66.4 ml/min/1.73 m²), hypothyroidism, a previous episode of acute myocarditis and stage 2 arterial hypertension.

During admission the patient presented with progressive exertional dyspnea, marked exercise intolerance, and fatigue consistent with impaired systemic circulation. He also reported episodes of palpitations and mild peripheral edema, but denied chest pain or syncope.

Electrocardiography (ECG) revealed sinus rhythm with a heart rate of 65 bpm. Chest X-ray demonstrated fully expanded lungs. The pulmonary vasculature was well-differentiated throughout all zones, with a uniformly increased vascular component. The hila were not enlarged. The mediastinal contour was not displaced but was markedly widened transversely, measuring 23 cm.

Transthoracic echocardiography revealed a left ventricular end-diastolic volume (LV EDV) of 167 mL, with an indexed LV EDV of 75 mL/m². The left ventricular ejection fraction (LV EF) was 60%. The left ventricular mass index was 75 g/m². The right atrium (RA) transverse diameter 7,7 cm. The RA was markedly deformed, with eccentric ectasia of the inferior wall. Only minimal tricuspid regurgitation and RA dilatation were observed.

Cardiac magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) (2025) demonstrated a large, non-enhancing, well-circumscribed mass in the anterior mediastinum, encasing and compressing the RA, with close relation to the superior vena cava, inferior vena cava, and inferior aspect of the left atrium. The lesion had imaging characteristics consistent with adipose tissue. No enhancement was seen with contrast. Compared to prior MRI in 2021, the tumor had significantly increased in size (from 145 × 100 × 50 mm to 173 × 100 × 114 mm) (Fig. 1 preoperative MRI). Despite the mass effect, left ventricular function remained preserved, with no evidence of myocardial edema, fibrosis, or perfusion defects.

The patient was referred to our center in late 2021. Approximately three months later, a full-scale war began in Ukraine, which severely limited access to specialized cardiac surgical care. As a result, the patient was unable to receive appropriate follow-up and definitive treatment during this period. Access to comprehensive medical evaluation and surgical management became feasible again only in 2025, when the patient was re-evaluated due to progressive tumor growth and worsening clinical symptoms.

After a detailed assessment, including advanced imaging, the tumor’s well-defined encapsulation, absence of myocardial infiltration, and the patient’s relatively preserved ventricular function supported the decision to proceed with surgical resection rather than placing the patient on the heart transplantation waiting list.

Preoperative optimization was undertaken given the patient’s comorbidities, including chronic kidney disease and hypothyroidism. Renal function was carefully monitored, and perioperative fluid management was adjusted to avoid volume overload and further renal impairment. Nephrotoxic agents were avoided, and low-dose inotropes were prepared for potential perioperative hemodynamic instability.

A median sternotomy was performed. Upon opening the pericardium, the tumor immediately protruded into the surgical field and was found to be densely adherent to the right atrioventricular groove – an unusual location for intracardiac lipomas. The intraoperative findings are illustrated in Supplementary Video 1. After achieving an activated clotting time of 460 s, central aortic and bicaval cannulation for cardiopulmonary bypass was established. The procedure was performed without cardioplegic arrest. The main stage of lipoma resection was carried out during electrically induced ventricular fibrillation lasting 34 min under moderate hypothermia (34 °C). A subtotal resection of the lipoma was achieved; a small remnant infiltrating the right atrial wall near the coronary sinus was intentionally left in situ due to deep myocardial involvement. The resulting right atrial defect was reconstructed using an autologous pericardial patch measuring 10 × 5 cm. Total cardiopulmonary bypass time was 115 min. Histopathological examination of the resected tumor revealed a well-circumscribed lesion composed of mature adipocytes arranged in lobules, separated by delicate fibrous septa. The adipocytes were uniform in size and shape, with small, peripherally located nuclei and abundant clear cytoplasm. No cellular atypia, pleomorphism, lipoblasts, necrosis, or increased mitotic activity were identified. There was no evidence of infiltrative growth into adjacent myocardial tissue within the examined specimens. Overall, the histological features were consistent with a benign cardiac lipoma, and no findings suggestive of liposarcoma or malignant transformation were observed.

Postoperative recovery was uneventful. No postoperative arrhythmias were observed; sinus rhythm was preserved. The patient was extubated after 4 h, transferred from the intensive care unit on postoperative day 1, and had chest drains removed on day 2. TTE follow-up on postoperative day 2 showed stable cardiac function with a LV EDV of 160 mL and LV EF of 60%. The transverse diameter of the RA was measured at 5 cm.

The patient was discharged home on postoperative day 11 in stable condition on the following pharmacological regimen: carvedilol 12.5 mg twice daily, with regular monitoring of heart rate and blood pressure; empagliflozin 10 mg once daily for long-term metabolic and cardioprotective benefits; acetylsalicylic acid 75 mg once daily for antiplatelet therapy; eplerenone 25 mg once daily, prescribed for a three-month course; and levothyroxine 12.5 mcg once daily as chronic hormone replacement therapy.

At 6-month follow-up, repeat cardiac MRI showed small, stable residual tissue near the coronary sinus with no evidence of regrowth or new recurrence and preserved biventricular function; the patient remains asymptomatic and in sinus rhythm. Routine TTE monitoring is conducted every three months.

Discussion and conclusions

The pathogenesis of cardiac lipomas, especially those reaching giant sizes, remains poorly understood. Most cases describe slow-growing, often asymptomatic lesions, indicating a long indolent course [2]. Recent studies suggest a genetic component, with alterations in chromosomes 12q13-15, 13q deletions, and recombinations on 6q21-23 [6]. Another hypothesis is that lipomas may originate from connective tissue near the Sondergaard recess, gradually infiltrating the right atrium due to the heart’s contractile forces, the thin atrial wall, and the firm tumor consistency [7]. Unfortunately, no genetic analysis was performed in our case. We speculate that prior myocarditis may have induced microenvironmental changes such as fibrosis, ischemia, or cytokine-driven remodeling that favored lipomatous tissue growth.

Our case is remarkable due to the tumor’s exceptional size (17 × 10 × 14 cm; 910 g), making it one of the largest right atrial lipomas reported. Only a few cases exceeding 10 cm have been described (Table 1), with the largest documented in 1995 (25 × 15 cm, right atrium, 17-year-old female with dyspnea) [8].

Also, in our case, subtotal resection of the lipoma with subsequent right atrial wall reconstruction effectively relieved the patient’s symptoms and eliminated the need for heart transplantation. To our knowledge, only one similar case has been reported, in which complete excision of a cardiac lipoma (5 × 6 cm2) was combined with right atrial wall reconstruction in a 52-year-old female [13].

In our patient, complete resection of the lipoma was not feasible; a small residual fragment infiltrating the right atrial wall near the coronary sinus was intentionally left to avoid excessive surgical risk. However, this remnant poses a potential risk of regrowth. Zhu J et al. reported a similar case of giant symptomatic cardiac lipoma recurrence five years after initial resection [14], with possible contributing factors including incomplete excision, intraoperative tumor seeding, and multifocal tumor origin. Residual tumor tissue may also lead to atrial wall infiltration, arrhythmias, and thromboembolic events. Although cardiac lipomas are generally benign, rare cases of rapid regrowth or even malignant transformation have been documented. Given the intentional preservation of a small residual tumor fragment infiltrating the right atrial wall near the coronary sinus, close postoperative surveillance was considered essential. Residual tissue may predispose to arrhythmias or atrial remodeling. Therefore, a structured follow-up protocol was adopted, consisting of transthoracic echocardiography every three months to monitor chamber size and hemodynamics, and annual cardiac magnetic resonance imaging to assess the stability of residual tissue and exclude regrowth.

In the event of tumor progression, repeat surgical intervention should be considered, taking into account anatomical complexity and procedural risk. Reoperation may require subtotal or radical resection with atrial wall reconstruction and should be performed in a multidisciplinary setting to minimize complications and optimize outcomes.

Although heart transplantation is a life-saving option, it carries significant risks and long-term implications. When feasible, surgical excision of benign tumors offers the potential for full functional recovery with substantially lower morbidity. Surgical subtotal resection is most appropriate for benign tumors in patients with preserved ventricular function when complete resection or transplantation carries prohibitive risk. Multidisciplinary collaboration, advanced imaging, and experienced surgical teams are essential for optimal outcomes in such complex cases.
