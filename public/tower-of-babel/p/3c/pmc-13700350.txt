Ultrasound-guided peripheral regional anesthesia vs local infiltration anesthesia for cardiac device implantation: A propensity score–matched prospective observational cohort study
Authors: Riyong Zhou, Ziyi Ni, Yaoxue Zang, Hao Zhou, Lan Su, Zhengzheng Li, Zhiting Wang, Lingling Zheng, Shengjie Wu, Yinuo Lin, Xiao Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundConventional local infiltration anesthesia (LIA) may not consistently provide adequate analgesia during cardiac device implantation.ObjectiveThis study aimed to compare the analgesic efficacy of ultrasound-guided peripheral regional anesthesia (PRA) with LIA during cardiac device implantation.MethodsWe conducted a single-center prospective observational cohort study including patients undergoing first-time cardiac device implantation. Data were collected in real time during routine clinical care. Patients received PRA or LIA according to routine clinical practice, as determined by the treating clinicians. Propensity score matching was performed to reduce selection bias, resulting in 48 matched patients per group. Additional anesthetic supplementation, pain-related movement, and adverse events were recorded.ResultsAmong the matched patients (n = 96), pain scores at all 7 procedural steps were significantly lower in the PRA group than in the LIA group (all P < .01). Postoperative Numerical Rating Scale scores at 2–24 hours were significantly lower in the PRA group (all P < .05). Introduction

Cardiac device implantation is widely performed for the management of bradyarrhythmias, conduction disorders, and heart failure,1,2 with more than 1 million pacemaker implantations performed worldwide annually.3

Current practice guidelines recommend the use of conventional local infiltration anesthesia (LIA) for cardiac device implantation, typically administered after skin preparation and before incision.4 Lidocaine provides analgesia for approximately 0.5–2 hours and is commonly combined with a long-acting local anesthetic such as bupivacaine or ropivacaine to extend intraoperative analgesia. However, LIA primarily exerts its effect at the site of injection and may be insufficient to anesthetize deeper tissue layers involved in key procedural steps. Inadequate analgesia can lead to patient discomfort and involuntary movement, potentially compromising procedural precision. Therefore, optimization of periprocedural pain management remains an important clinical challenge in cardiac device implantation.5, 6, 7

Peripheral regional anesthesia (PRA) refers to the targeted administration of local anesthetics adjacent to peripheral nerves or within defined fascial planes to interrupt nociceptive transmission, encompassing both peripheral nerve blocks and interfascial plane blocks.8, 9, 10, 11, 12 Ultrasound guidance enhances anatomic visualization and enables precise delivery of local anesthetics, thereby improving the efficacy and safety of regional anesthetic techniques. Although PRA has been increasingly adopted in various surgical settings, comparative real-world data evaluating its effectiveness during cardiac device implantation remain limited. In the present study, PRA was implemented as a combined multilevel regional anesthesia strategy, including supraclavicular nerve block, transverse thoracic muscle plane block, and pectoral fascial plane block.

Optimized periprocedural analgesia may contribute to improved procedural stability and patient cooperation, particularly as device implantation techniques become more technically demanding. Therefore, we conducted a propensity score–matched (PSM) prospective observational cohort study to compare periprocedural analgesic outcomes between ultrasound-guided PRA and conventional LIA during cardiac device implantation.

Methods

Study design

This was a single-center prospective observational cohort study conducted at the First Affiliated Hospital of Wenzhou Medical University and approved by the institutional ethics committee [no. 274 (2023)]. Consecutive patients undergoing first-time cardiac device implantation between December 2023 and November 2024 were included. Data were collected in real time as part of routine clinical care. The requirement for an informed consent was waived by the ethics committee because the study used deidentified routinely recorded clinical data collected during routine clinical care. The research reported in this study adhered to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. The choice of anesthesia (ultrasound-guided PRA or LIA) was determined by the treating clinicians according to routine clinical practice.

Patient population

Adult patients aged 45–75 years undergoing implantation of their first permanent cardiac device during the study period were eligible for inclusion. Exclusion criteria included coagulation disorders, known allergy to local anesthetics, active infection at the surgical site, inability to cooperate with the procedure, significant peripheral neuropathy, uncontrolled hypertension (blood pressure >180/110 mm Hg), advanced renal insufficiency (creatinine clearance <30 mL/min), severe anemia (hemoglobin <60 g/L), severe obstructive or mixed ventilatory dysfunction, confirmed primary or secondary neuromuscular diseases, and the requirement for deep sedation or general anesthesia (eg, owing to agitation or procedural complexity).

Vital signs, including blood pressure, heart rate, and oxygen saturation, were continuously monitored throughout the procedure. Pain intensity was assessed using a 10-point Numerical Rating Scale (NRS), where 0 indicated no pain and 10 indicated the worst imaginable pain. Assessments were performed at 7 predefined intraoperative procedural steps (skin incision, tissue dissection, pocket creation, venipuncture, sheath insertion, pocket suturing, and skin suturing) as part of standardized periprocedural pain assessment in routine clinical care. If intraoperative pain reached an NRS score of ≥4, additional local anesthetic was administered as supplemental analgesia according to routine clinical practice.

Postoperative pain, including resting and movement-related pain, was evaluated at 2, 4, 6, 8, 10, 12, and 24 hours after surgery. For patients with NRS scores ≥4 or intolerable pain, a single oral dose of celecoxib was administered. Patient satisfaction score was assessed before hospital discharge using a 10-point NRS.

Cardiac device implantation

The study did not impose any changes to transvenous–subcutaneous pacemaker implantation procedures, which were conducted according to current guidelines and expert consensus.4,13,14

Ultrasound-guided PRA

Ultrasound-guided PRA was performed using a standardized multimodal regional anesthesia protocol consisting of the following 3 components, with detailed technical descriptions provided in the Supplemental Methods:

(1)Supraclavicular nerve block (Figure 2A): a high-frequency ultrasound probe (SonoSite HF6–15, FUJIFILM SonoSite, Bothell, WA) was placed at the midpoint of the sternocleidomastoid muscle and oriented between the C4 and C5 transverse processes to identify the superficial cervical plexus deep to the sternocleidomastoid muscle. After identification of the supraclavicular nerve, 5 mL of the anesthetic mixture was injected.

After identification of the supraclavicular nerve, 5 mL of the anesthetic mixture was injected.

(2)Transverse thoracic muscle plane block (Figure 2B): under ultrasound guidance, relevant anatomic structures—including the pectoralis major muscle, external and internal intercostal muscles, transverse thoracic muscle, and internal thoracic vessels—were identified. 5 mL of the anesthetic mixture was injected superficial to the transverse thoracic muscle and lateral to the internal thoracic artery.

(3)Pectoral fascial plane block (Figure 2C): in the infraclavicular region, the subclavian artery, thoracoacromial artery, and cephalic vein were identified. Using an in-plane technique, muscle contractions of the clavicular and sternal portions of the pectoralis major muscle were elicited with a nerve stimulator, and 5 mL of the anesthetic mixture was injected to achieve lateral pectoral nerve block. An additional 5 mL of the anesthetic mixture was administered between the pectoralis minor muscle and the second rib to achieve medial pectoral nerve block.

Data collection and outcomes

Clinical data were recorded in real time as part of routine clinical care and subsequently obtained from the institutional medical record system and periprocedural documentation. Collected variables included demographic characteristics (age, sex, and body mass index), cardiovascular risk factors and comorbidities (smoking, obesity, hypertension, diabetes mellitus, hyperlipidemia, coronary artery disease, previous percutaneous coronary intervention, heart failure, stroke, and chronic kidney disease), indication for device implantation, type of implanted device, anesthesia method, and procedure duration.

The primary outcomes were intraoperative and postoperative NRS pain scores. Secondary outcomes included pain-related patient movement, additional local anesthetic supplementation, postoperative analgesic consumption, anesthesia satisfaction, venipuncture attempts, procedure duration, and procedure-related adverse events. All outcomes were assessed and recorded as part of routine clinical care.

Adverse events included nausea, vomiting, dizziness, hypotension, procedure-related bleeding, hematoma, hemothorax, pneumothorax, infection, and death and were recorded during both intraoperative and postoperative periods as part of routine clinical care.

Statistical analysis

No formal sample size calculation was performed, given that this was a prospective observational study based on consecutively enrolled patients. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 25.0 (IBM Corp, Armonk, NY). To reduce selection bias, propensity scores were estimated using a multivariable logistic regression model including age, sex, body mass index, hypertension, diabetes mellitus, chronic kidney disease, indication for device implantation, and device type. 1-to-1 nearest-neighbor matching without replacement was performed using a caliper width of 0.2 standard deviations of the logit of the propensity score. Covariate balance after matching was assessed using standardized mean differences (SMDs), with an SMD of <0.10 indicating adequate balance.

In the matched cohort, continuous variables were compared using paired t tests or Wilcoxon signed-rank tests, and categorical variables using McNemar’s test. Fisher’s exact test was used when expected counts were small. A 2-sided P < .05 was considered statistically significant. No missing data were present for the variables included in the analysis.

Adverse events

Adverse events were infrequent. 2 patients in the LIA group experienced intraoperative nausea, whereas no cases occurred in the PRA group. No cases of procedure-related bleeding, pneumothorax, hemothorax, infection, or death were observed. No regional anesthesia–related complications, including local anesthetic systemic toxicity, diaphragmatic involvement, or unintended brachial plexus spread, were observed. Discussion

In contrast to previous studies limited to case reports, uncontrolled pilot studies, or small cohorts,15, 16, 17 this single-center PSM prospective observational cohort study demonstrated 3 main findings: (1) PRA was associated with more effective intraoperative analgesia than LIA during cardiac device implantation, (2) PRA was associated with more stable and longer-lasting postoperative analgesia, and (3) PRA was not associated with an increased risk of perioperative adverse events, although the sample size may be insufficient to detect rare complications.

During cardiac device implantation, pain scores were consistently higher in the LIA group than in the PRA group, with the highest intensity observed during pocket creation. Pain-related movement and the need for supplemental anesthesia were also more frequent in the LIA group,18,19 predominantly during venipuncture and pocket creation. These findings suggest that conventional LIA may not provide sufficient anesthetic coverage for all procedural steps of cardiac device implantation, particularly during pocket creation. In contrast, ultrasound-guided PRA seems to be associated with more effective and comprehensive procedural analgesia throughout the procedure, without procedure-related complications. Although potential complications related to regional anesthesia techniques may occur, no such events were observed in the present study. These findings are consistent with previous reports.20,21

Postoperatively, pain scores remained consistently higher in the LIA group than in the PRA group at all assessed time points, both at rest and during movement, and with a higher rate of postoperative celecoxib use. These findings suggest that the duration of analgesic with LIA may be insufficient to provide sustained postoperative pain control, thereby increasing the need for additional analgesic medication. In contrast, PRA was associated with prolonged postoperative pain relief, potentially enhance patient comfort and facilitate early upper-limb mobility after device implantation.

The differing pain patterns likely reflect the distinct mechanisms of LIA and PRA. Because cardiac device implantation involves multiple tissue layers from the skin to deeper musculature, LIA may not adequately anesthetize deeper tissues involved in pocket creation and venipuncture.22, 23, 24 In contrast, PRA targets relevant sensory nerve pathways, providing broader and more sustained analgesic coverage across the incision and device pocket regions. Previous studies have attempted to apply ultrasound-guided regional anesthesia to cardiac device implantation procedures.25, 26, 27 However, these studies were limited by smaller sample sizes, single anesthesia techniques, or a lack of matched comparative analyses. Recently, Akelma et al28 reported improved analgesia and patient satisfaction using interscalene brachial plexus block combined with superficial cervical plexus block during pacemaker implantation. Similar to their findings, our study also demonstrated favorable periprocedural pain control with ultrasound-guided regional anesthesia. However, unlike their interscalene block–based approach, our strategy used selective chest wall and pectoral fascial plane blocks to achieve more targeted and comprehensive analgesic coverage during device implantation. Selective blockade of the supraclavicular nerve branches provides cutaneous anesthesia for the incision area,29 whereas the transverse thoracic muscle plane block anesthetizes the anterior cutaneous branches of the intercostal nerves in the pocket region.30 In addition, the pectoral fascial plane block provides analgesia to the pectoral musculature involved in pocket creation (Supplemental Methods). This layered and selective approach may partly explain the more stable analgesic profile observed in the PRA group. The prolonged duration of analgesia with PRA may be related to perineural drug deposition and slower systemic absorption than tissue infiltration.

Beyond pain intensity alone, adequate periprocedural analgesia has broader clinical implications. Pain-induced physiological stress responses are undesirable during cardiac device implantation31 and may impair patient cooperation, potentially affecting procedural safety and efficiency. In procedures requiring prolonged lead manipulation and precise positioning, such as conduction system pacing, stable analgesia and minimal patient movement are particularly important. Postoperatively, inadequate analgesia may result in significant ipsilateral pain, increasing the need for additional analgesics and potentially discouraging early upper-limb movement after device implantation. By providing broader and longer-lasting analgesic coverage without increasing adverse events, PRA may be associated with improved procedural conditions. Although patient satisfaction scores were higher in the PRA group than in the LIA group, overall satisfaction remained relatively high in both groups. Given the consistent improvements observed with PRA across multiple clinically relevant outcomes, including procedural pain scores, pain-related movement, rescue anesthetic requirements, and postoperative analgesic use, differences in overall satisfaction scores alone may not fully capture the potential clinical relevance of PRA. However, these findings are exploratory and require confirmation in larger studies.

This study has several limitations. First, its single-center design and relatively modest sample size may limit the generalizability of the findings. Second, although PSM was applied, residual confounding inherent to the nonrandomized design cannot be fully excluded. Third, more complex device implantations were underrepresented, and procedural outcomes may be influenced by operator-related factors. Finally, pain assessment relied on subjective NRS measurements, and the lack of blinding may have introduced performance and observer bias, potentially influencing the observed effect size. Larger multicenter studies are warranted to further validate these findings.

Conclusion

In this PSM prospective observational cohort study, ultrasound-guided PRA was associated with improved perioperative analgesia without an observed increase in adverse events. These findings suggest that PRA may represent a feasible option for periprocedural analgesia in selected patients; however, further studies are needed to confirm these observations.
