Efficient synthesis of complex supramolecular topologies via coordination angle modulation
Authors: Li-Long Dang, Xiao-Qian Wan, Jian-Xin Yang, Mei-Jia Chang, Francisco Aznarez, Ying Zhao, Feng Bai, Lu-Fang Ma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTDespite the appeal of mechanically interlocked supramolecular systems, precise control over their assembly into complicated topologies is still a central challenge. To address this, we employed a method centered on regulating the coordination angle. By designing two ligands (L1 and L2) with differing carbazole-pyridine connection angles and combining them with tailored building units E1(OTf)2 and E2(OTf)2, we successfully directed the synthesis of four unique architectures: a molecular-tweezer-like compound, a snowflake-shaped folded assembly, an 818 knot, and a closed three-link chain. Single-crystal X-ray diffraction analysis provided unambiguous structural elucidation, not only confirming the three-dimensional geometries but also revealing that the ligand angle dictates the outcome of assembly, steering it toward interlocked or folded structures. The formation and purity of these constructs in solution were verified by nuclear magnetic resonance spectroscopy and electrospray ionization time-of-flight mass spectrometry. Furthermore, photothermal conversion experiments demonstrated that compound 4(OTf)12 exhibits favorable photothermal performance, with a relatively high photothermal conversion efficiency as well as excellent cycling stability. This observation is also supported by electron paramagnetic resonance measurement data.

INTRODUCTION

In nature, the diverse functions of proteins and DNA are often dictated by their distinct folding and interlocking patterns [1–5]. Inspired by this, we have synthesized supramolecular complexes with programmable architectures to explore the structure–function relationship. Even minor structural variations can have a profound functional impact, a central question that drives research in this field. Given its significant potential in life sciences, this area merits in-depth exploration [6–10].

In recent years, diverse construction strategies have enabled the synthesis of a wide array of intricate supramolecular complexes, including molecular knots [11–14], ravels [15–18], catenanes [19–23], and cages [24–28]. The successful preparation of these compounds has not only accelerated the development of topological chemistry but also facilitated detailed investigations into structural interconversions among complex architectures. Notable examples include a silver-ion-promoted conversion between an 818 knot and a Borromean ring [29], as well as the transformation between a 41 knot and a double trefoil knot, driven by the coordination of metal ions to amide oxygen atoms [30,31]. Intriguingly, solvent and concentration effects have also been employed to convert complicated topologies into their corresponding macrocyclic compounds [32,33].

Despite these advances, the fundamental mechanisms governing the synthesis of high-order topological compounds remain poorly understood. Consequently, developing reliable methods to construct even more complex topologies, such as 818 knots or closed [3]catenanes, remains a significant challenge. Furthermore, there are very few reports on directing the mechanical interlocking of multinuclear compounds or forming multiply folded macrocycles through ligand configuration control. Advancing this area could hold substantial research significance, particularly for the field of life sciences.

Based on our systematic investigations and the collective findings accumulated in our group over the years, several empirical trends have emerged. In general, when employing a two-component assembly strategy to synthesize mechanical interlocking structures based on half-sandwich rhodium/iridium units, bidentate ligands with small coordination angles tend to afford metallamacrocycles [34] or molecular-tweezer-like compounds [35,36], owing to the steric hindrance imposed by the ligand framework. In contrast, linear rigid bidentate ligands frequently lead to the formation of linear [3]/[4]catenanes [37–39] and borromean rings [40–42]. Consequently, the realization of both cyclic and closed topologies—such as the 818 knot and the closed three-link chain—demands precise ligand angle control. Specifically, the ligands need to adopt a subtly curved conformation with a gentle angle to facilitate the formation of initial boat-shaped metallamacrocycles, which can meet the requirements for finally forming cyclic and closed topologies.

For this study, we synthesized two highly symmetrical carbazole-based bidentate pyridyl ligands, L1 and L2, both featuring extensive conjugate centers but differing in their coordination angles. We hypothesized that combining these with suitable half-sandwich rhodium building units—which possess specific conjugate planes—would steer the assembly toward distinct topological outcomes: high-order interlocked structures or folded, non-interlocked assemblies. To test this, we also prepared two such rhodium-based units, E1(OTf)₂ and E2(OTf)₂, which differ in their conjugate planes and longitudinal dimensions. Building on our previous work, where the combination of L1 and E1(OTf)2 yielded a molecular-tweezer-like folded assembly, 1(OTf)8, we now report that reacting ligand L1 with the larger E2(OTf)2 unit directly produces an unprecedented dodecanuclear folded cage, 2(OTf)12. Furthermore, when ligand L2 was employed, its combination with E1(OTf)2 and E2(OTf)2 resulted in the formation of an 818 knot, 3(OTf)16, and a closed three-link chain, 4(OTf)12, respectively. All the new complexes—2(OTf)12, 3(OTf)16, and 4(OTf)12—were isolated in high yields and thoroughly characterized by single-crystal X-ray diffraction (SC-XRD), nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectroscopy, and electrospray ionization time-of-flight mass spectrometry (ESI-TOF-MS). We attribute the divergent formation of these topologies primarily to the distinct coordination angles of ligands L1 and L2, coupled with the specific stacking interactions between their conjugate centers and those of the building units.

RESULTS AND DISCUSSION

The combination of building blocks E1(OTf)2 or E2(OTf)2 with ligands L1 or L2 led to the formation of four distinct supramolecular complexes in excellent yields: a molecular-tweezer-like compound 1(OTf)8 (86.9%), a snowflake-shaped folded assembly 2(OTf)12 (88.7%), an 818 knot 3(OTf)16 (85.2%), and a closed three-link chain 4(OTf)12 (89.4%). Single crystals of these compounds, suitable for X-ray diffraction analysis, were subsequently obtained by slowly diffusing isopropyl ether into their concentrated methanolic solutions (Scheme 1).

Synthesis of molecular-tweezer-like compound 1(OTf)8, snowflake-shaped folded assembly 2(OTf)12, 818 knot 3(OTf)16, and closed three-link chain 4(OTf)12.

The structure of compound 1(OTf)8 has been reported by our research team [43]. It was synthesized by connecting the half-sandwich building unit E1(OTf)2 with the pyridine-based ligand precursor L1. Notably, its architecture incorporates two bent tetranuclear metallamacrocycles embedded within an interdigitated macrocyclic topology via π···π stacking interactions between carbazolyl groups on adjacent ligands. The significant Rh···Rh separation within these units (∼8.44 Å; Fig. S3) facilitated this interpenetration by providing ample spatial accommodation. Consequently, two identical tetranuclear macrocycles adopt a clamped conformation, forming a stable octanuclear assembly (Fig. 1a and b). Distinct colors and simplified diagrams were employed to visualize the spatial relationships between components in 1(OTf)8 (Fig. 1c and d). Slightly twisted π···π stacking was observed, characterized by interlayer distances of 3.70, 3.78, and 3.67 Å. To probe how building block length affects the resultant topology, we utilized a longer NDI-based dinuclear unit E2(OTf)2 (featuring an extended conjugated plane) combined with L1. This yielded the unprecedented snowflake-shaped folded assembly 2(OTf)12, whose structure was confirmed by SC-XRD. Crystallizing in the triclinic P1 space group, 2(OTf)12 exhibits alternating connectivity between organic ligands and metallobuilding units (Fig. 1e). We propose that its formation stems from π···π interactions between ligand carbazolyl groups and NDI moieties of the building unit. Furthermore, the angular geometry of L1 inhibits interlocking in 2(OTf)12, instead directing self-assembly into this unique supramolecular framework—highlighting ligand design’s crucial role in controlling structural topology. The central cavity of 2(OTf)12 principle enables guest encapsulation within its stable architecture housing twelve Rh atoms (Fig. 1f and g). Following reduction via Reidemeister moves [44,45], 2(OTf)12 was identified as a large fold-type metallamacrocycle comprising over 400 non-hydrogen atoms (Fig. 1h).

The isolation of this rare contracted supramolecular metallamacrocycle represented a significant advancement in the exploration of supramolecular chemistry, offering new insights into the structural complexity and functional versatility of supramolecular compounds. This breakthrough not only expands the type of synthetic metallamacrocycle architectures but also develops our understanding of non-covalent interactions and molecular self-assembly. A structural comparison between compounds 1(OTf)8 and 2(OTf)12 revealed that both the appropriate size and conjugation effect of half-sandwich building units induced the assembly of multinuclear metallamacrocycle compounds, thereby suggesting that the key factor most likely resides in the geometry of the ligand. This insight provides a valuable foundation for advancing the construction of increasingly intricate entangled architectures.

We next acquired 1H NMR, 1H-1H correlation spectroscopy (COSY), and 1H diffusion-ordered spectroscopy (DOSY) spectra of 2(OTf)12 in solution (Fig. 2a–c). Splitting patterns were analyzed and integrals determined for all signals. Assignment of all resonances was unambiguously achieved by using the 1H-1H COSY spectrum (Fig. S11). The signals at 8.28 and 7.82 ppm were confirmed, which belong to the pyridine protons of the ligand precursor L1. Furthermore, the signals corresponding to the carbazole protons of L1 appeared at 7.88, 7.67, and 7.32 ppm, and the signal at 5.71 ppm was assigned to the protons of building unit E2(OTf)2. In addition, the resonances belonging to the protons of the Cp* groups appeared as a singlet at 1.72 ppm in the 1H NMR spectrum. Note that the presence of a single, sharp set of signals in the 1H NMR spectrum indicated a high degree of molecular symmetry. Compound 2(OTf)12 exhibited a single diffusion coefficient (D = 2.54 × 10−6 cm2 s−1), thereby confirming the presence of only one species in solution. The hydrodynamic radius of 2(OTf)12 (14.82 Å), derived from the 1H DOSY NMR diffusion constant, was in excellent agreement with its solid-state measurement (14.99 Å), which demonstrated structural consistency between the solution and solid phases (Fig. S18).

It was observed that the relatively small bite angle and remarkable conjugation in the ligand precursor L1 facilitated the formation of the molecular tweezer-like compound 1(OTf)8 and the snowflake-shaped folded assembly 2(OTf)12. In an attempt to increase the complexity of the resulting interlocking supramolecular structures, a new ligand precursor L2 with an expanded angle was synthesized using a Suzuki–Miyaura cross-coupling reaction (Figs S1, S2, and S7–S10). Then, ligand precursor L2 was reacted with building units E1(OTf)2 or E2(OTf)2 at ambient temperature for 12 h to produce compounds 3(OTf)16 or 4(OTf)12, respectively (Fig. 4a). Single crystals of the resulting self-assemblies suitable for X-ray diffraction analysis were obtained by slow diffusion of isopropyl ether into the corresponding methanolic solutions. SC-XRD analysis confirmed the intricately tangled architecture of compound 3(OTf)16, belonging to the P2 space group. As shown in Fig. 3a and b, each building unit E1(OTf)2 and ligand L2 were coordinatively bridged by a Rh metal center in the supramolecular assembly, thereby forming an interlocked supramolecular framework. Theoretical arithmetic revealed the presence of π···π stacking interactions between the tetraphenyl groups of E1(OTf)2 and the carbazolyl group of ligand L2, which enhanced the structural stability (Fig. S39). Additionally, the measured distances between ligand L2 and the building unit E1(OTf)2 were 3.56 and 3.64 Å. Notably, the hydrogen bonds between the carbazolyl group and the pyridyl group of E1(OTf)2 were separated by distances of 2.50 and 2.83 Å, respectively (Fig. S5). The synergistic interaction of coordination-driven self-assembly and π···π stacking interactions led to the formation of a highly stable hexadecanuclear supramolecular knot. Moreover, the compound 3(OTf)16 exhibited an outer diameter of 28.37 Å and a vertical height of 22.90 Å. The molecular structure was represented in five distinct colors (Fig. 3c) and simplified diagram (Fig. 3d) to provide an intuitive understanding of the spatial arrangement and interactions between moieties in this unique 818 knot.

Previous studies indicated that half-sandwich-based supramolecular compounds exhibited promising photothermal conversion properties, and multiple π···π stacking interactions might enhance their photothermal conversion efficiency [46,47]. To the best of our knowledge, neither 818 knots nor closed three-link chains have yet been subjected to photothermal testing. Note that compounds 3(OTf)16 and 4(OTf)12 contained eight and six sets of intramolecular π···π stacking interactions, respectively. Therefore, we hypothesized that compounds 3(OTf)16 and 4(OTf)12 would demonstrate remarkable photothermal conversion performance, thereby offering great potential for future applications in fields such as photothermal therapy, photothermal-driven actuation, or solar-powered seawater desalination. To ensure an equivalent number of π···π stacking interactions in both samples, compounds 3(OTf)16 and 4(OTf)12 were tested in a 3:4 molar ratio in the subsequent experiments. Ultraviolet–visible (UV–Vis) spectroscopy revealed remarkable absorption for both compounds 3(OTf)16 and 4(OTf)12 (Fig. 5a). Based on these results, a 730 nm laser was selected for the next photothermal conversion experiments. Moreover, the heating of compounds in methanol was recorded in real time using an infrared imager (Fig. 5b). As illustrated in Fig. 5c, both samples exhibited a significant photothermal response, with temperatures rising from 25.0 to 62.3°C and from 25.0 to 67.0°C, respectively (Figs S27 and S28). Thermogravimetric analysis also confirmed that these compounds exhibit high thermal stability (Figs S37 and S38).

In addition, over ten consecutive testing cycles, the samples exhibited no significant performance degradation, thereby demonstrating the robust stability of the compounds under photothermal conversion conditions and highlighting their potential for application across multiple fields (Fig. 5d). Linear fitting analysis revealed that the photothermal conversion efficiencies of compound 3(OTf)16 and compound 4(OTf)12 were from 53.82% to 57.12% and from 63.86% to 68.81% (Figs S29–S36), respectively. To further illustrate the photothermal performance level of compound 3(OTf)16 and 4(OTf)12, we summarize the performance data of representative photothermal materials for a comparative analysis (Tables S1 and S2). DOS calculations with and without π···π stacking interactions reveal that the narrowed bandgap promotes vibrational relaxation of excited electrons to the ground state and provides more nonradiative relaxation channels for excited state energy (Figs S40 and S41). The enhanced photothermal conversion efficiencies could be attributed to the synergistic effect of multiple stacking interactions within the supramolecular framework, which facilitated more efficient energy transfer and conversion processes. Electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR) experiments demonstrated a marked increase in signal intensity under light irradiation, confirming the generation of photoinduced organic radicals. The multiple π···π stacking interactions present in the supramolecular framework effectively suppress radiative transitions and favor non-radiative decay pathways. By promoting these non-radiative processes, the system enhances the yield of organic radicals, resulting in the observed desirable photothermal performance (Fig. 5e and f). Measurements of steady-state fluorescence spectra and fluorescence lifetimes also reveal that both compounds exhibit weak fluorescence emission intensity and short lifetimes, corresponding to a high non-radiative decay rate. A high non-radiative transition rate can remarkably boost the photothermal performance (Figs S42–S45). These findings underscore the importance of molecular design in optimizing photothermal conversion materials for specific applications.

CONCLUSION

In summary, we utilized a strategy revolving around tuning the coordination angle, which enables precise construction of diverse and topologically distinct supramolecular architectures. Four novel interlocked and non-interlocked structures have been successfully synthesized: a molecular-tweezer-like compound 1(OTf)8, a snowflake-shaped folded assembly 2(OTf)12, an 818 knot 3(OTf)16, and a closed three-link chain 4(OTf)12. This approach leverages two carbazole-based ligands with carefully tailored angular geometries, achieved through coordination-driven self-assembly of finely designed building blocks. Steric constraints prevented the smaller-angled ligand precursor L1 from forming interlocked structures, whereas the larger-angled precursor L2 offered greater conformational flexibility, thereby facilitating the formation of these intricate interlocked topologies. The structures of all compounds were unambiguously confirmed in the solid state by SC-XRD analysis, supported by NMR spectroscopy and ESI-TOF-MS data. We propose that multiple π···π stacking interactions within these architectures contribute to their enhanced photothermal conversion performance. Notably, compound 4(OTf)12 exhibited exceptional photothermal conversion efficiency, with EPR spectroscopy providing further mechanistic insight. Additionally, our findings highlight the broad potential of the coordination-angle modulation strategy. This approach not only offers a powerful method for constructing complex supramolecular topologies but also establishes a tunable platform for designing smart materials. By simply adjusting the ligand angle, it becomes possible to systematically control the size, shape, and interlocking behavior of molecular architectures. This capability enables new applications in areas, including molecular machines, stimuli-responsive materials, and photothermal devices.

METHODS

Synthesis of ligand L1

Under argon, 3, 6-dibromo-9H-carbazole (100 mg, 0.24 mmol), 4-(4,4,5,5-tetramethyl-1,3,2-dioxaborolan-2-yl)pyridine (301 mg, 1.47 mmol), Pd(PPh3)4 (28 mg, 0.02 mmol), and K3PO4 (156 mg, 0.73 mmol) were added to degassed DMF/H2O (3 : 1, 30 mL). The mixture was heated at 100°C in a pressure reactor for 3 days. After cooling, the mixture was washed with water, brine, and saturated NH4Cl, dried over MgSO4, and concentrated. The product was purified by column chromatography (pentane/acetone, 1:1) and GPC (chloroform, CHCl3) to afford yellow solid L1.

Synthesis of ligand L2

The synthesis of L2 was carried out similarly to that of L1 with the use of 2,7-dibromo-9H-carbazole (100 mg, 0.24 mmol) instead of 3,6-dibromo-9H-carbazole (100 mg, 0.24 mmol).

Synthesis of 2(OTf)12

AgOTf (123 mg, 0.48 mmol) and [Cp*RhCl2]2 (74 mg, 0.12 mmol) were added to methanol (18 mL) at room temperature, protected from light and stirred in the dark for 12 h. The AgCl precipitate was filtered off to get a yellow solution. 2,7-Dihydroxybenzo[lmn][3,8]phenanthroline-1,3,6,8(2H, 7H)-tetraone (36 mg, 0.12 mmol) and NaOH (10 mg, 0.24 mmol) were added to the filtrate, followed by stirring for 12 h, and the mixture was treated with ether to obtain E2(OTf)2. L1 (38.56 mg, 0.12 mmol) was added to E2(OTf)2 in methanol, and the mixture was stirred for 12 h to obtain a dark yellow solution. Single crystals were grown by diffusing ether into a methanol solution, and recrystallized from diethyl ether to afford brown block crystals 2(OTf)12. 1H NMR (500 MHz, CD3OD, ppm, with respect to Cp*Rh): δ = 8.28 (d, 2H J = 5.5 Hz, pyridyl-Ha), δ = 7.82 (d, 2H, J = 5.5 Hz, pyridyl-Hb), δ = 7.32 (d, 1H, J = 8.00 Hz, carbazole-Hc), δ = 7.88 (d, 1H, J = 8.00 Hz, carbazole-Hd), δ = 7.67 (s, 1H, carbazole-He), δ = 5.71 (s, 2H, naphthyl-Hf), and δ = 1.72 (s, 15H, Cp*-Hg).

Synthesis of 3(OTf)16

AgOTf (123 mg, 0.48 mmol) and [Cp*RhCl2]2 (74 mg, 0.12 mmol) were added to methanol (18 mL), stirred in the dark for 12 h, and then filtered. 6,11-Dihydroxytetracene-5,12-dione (35 mg, 0.12 mmol) and NaOH (10 mg, 0.24 mmol) were added to the filtrate, stirred for 12 h (purple solution), and treated with ether to get E1(OTf)2. L2 (38.56 mg, 0.12 mmol) was added to E1(OTf)2 in methanol, followed by stirring for 12 h. Single crystals were grown by diffusing ether into a methanol solution, and recrystallized from diethyl ether to afford dark green block crystals 3(OTf)16. 1H NMR (500 MHz, CD3OD, ppm, with respect to Cp*Rh): δ = 8.86, 8.78 (d, 2H J = 6.00 Hz, J = 5.50 Hz pyridyl-Ha1, Ha2), δ = 8.50, 8.25 (d, 2H, J = 6.0 Hz, pyridyl-Hb1, Hb2), δ = 7.87, 7.79 (d, 1H, J = 9.0 Hz, J = 8.0 Hz carbazole-Hc1, Hc2), δ = 8.42, 8.38 (d, 1H, J = 8.0 Hz, J = 8.0 Hz carbazole-Hd1, Hd2), δ = 8.18, 8.09 (s, 1H, carbazole-He1, He2), δ = 8.67, (d, 2H, E1(OTf)2-Hf), δ = 8.26, (d, 2H, E1(OTf)2-Hg), and δ = 1.704, 1.697 (s, 15H, Cp*-Hh1, Hh2).

Synthesis of 4(OTf)12

The synthesis of 4(OTf)12 was carried out similarly to that of 3(OTf)16 with the use of 2,7-dihydroxybenzo[lmn][3,8]phenanthroline-1,3,6,8(2H,7H)-tetraone (36 mg, 0.12 mmol) instead of 6,11-dihydroxytetracene-5,12-dione (35 mg, 0.12 mmol). 4(OTf)12 was obtained as a black solid. 1H NMR (500 MHz, CD3OD, ppm, with respect to Cp*Rh): δ = 9.22, 9.16 (d, 2H J = 4.00 Hz, J = 4.50 Hz pyridyl-Ha1, Ha2), δ = 7.49, 8.29 (d, 2H, J = 5.0 Hz, J = 8.0 Hz, pyridyl-Hb1, Hb2), δ = 7.90, 7.75 (d, 1H, J = 8.5 Hz, J = 6.5 Hz carbazole-Hc1, Hc2), δ = 8.72, 8.48 (d, 1H, J = 10.0 Hz, J = 7.0 Hz carbazole-Hd1, Hd2), δ = 8.36, 7.64 (s, 1H, carbazole-He1, He2), δ = 7.00, (d, 2H, E2(OTf)2-Hf), and δ = 1.94, 1.90 (s, 15H, Cp*-Hg1, Hg2).

Investigation of photothermal properties

To ensure equal conjugated π-area, compounds 3(OTf)16 and 4(OTf)12 were mixed in a 3:4 molar ratio with 3 mL CH3OH. After complete dissolution, 2.0 mL of the solution was transferred to a quartz cuvette for absorbance measurement and irradiated with a 730-nm laser at varying current intensities, with temperature changes monitored via an infrared camera. Additionally, 0.5 mL aliquots of the methanol solutions were placed in sample vessels, irradiated similarly, and temperature variations were detected using the same infrared camera.
