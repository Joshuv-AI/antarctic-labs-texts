The impact of environmental knowledge on green purchasing intention: a higher-order model
Authors: Mulin Liu, Bei Lyu, Niyom Suwandej
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCurrently, environmental problems are becoming increasingly prominent, and global warming is intensifying. The promotion of green product purchases is of great significance to social sustainability. Based on this, this study constructs a higher-order model to analyze in depth how environmental knowledge influences the green purchasing intention of undergraduate students. A total of 343 data samples were collected for quantitative research. Smart partial least squares (Smart PLS) was used for data analysis to measure the relationship between variables. The results of this study show that students’ green purchase intention is positively and significantly correlated with their environmental knowledge. Additionally, the relationship between environmental knowledge and students’ green purchase intention is significantly mediated by green product attitude, subjective norms, and perceived behavioral control. This study provides a theoretical foundation for enterprises to accurately define their product positioning and develop targeted marketing strategies, thereby enhancing the sales of their green products in the Chinese market. Moreover, it offers empirical evidence for governments and educational institutions to attach importance to the popularization of environmental knowledge and the deepening of green education.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-46964-2.Keywords: Environmental knowledge, Green purchase intention, Undergraduate students, Smart PLSSubject terms: Environmental sciences, Environmental social sciences

Introduction

A series of global issues, including climate change, resource scarcity, environmental pollution, and biodiversity loss, is becoming increasingly prominent in today’s world. To achieve sustainable development goals, it is essential to shift from production-side governance to a comprehensive governance framework that encompasses the entire process from production to consumption. The deterioration of the environment is largely due to consumers’ irrational consumption behaviors. Reports indicate that household consumption accounts for 40% of environmental degradation1. Thus, it is crucial to reshape consumers’ perceptions and behaviors to effectively address environmental issues2. Consumers can play a critical role in mitigating environmental degradation by choosing green products3. Green consumption has been driving growing demand for environmentally friendly products. These products neither harm the environment nor threaten ecosystem diversity, prompting consumers to consider various green attributes when making purchasing decisions4.

Although consumers generally hold positive attitudes toward sustainability and green products and express concern about environmental issues, green products still occupy a relatively small market share3. As the main force of future consumption, college students have strong abilities to accept new things and their consumption concepts have not yet been fully fixed. Their willingness to purchase green products is easily influenced by environmental cognition, and their consumption choices can serve as an example, encouraging other groups to practice green consumption. At the same time, as a group receiving higher education, college students have more extensive channels to acquire environmental knowledge. Examining how environmental knowledge influences college students’ willingness to purchase green products can help governments formulate sound policies and enterprises adjust their marketing strategies, thereby promoting the overall sustainable development of society.

This study developed a higher-order model to explore the relationship between university students’ environmental knowledge and their green purchasing intentions, as well as to test the mediating roles of green product attitudes, subjective norms, and perceived behavioral control. A total of 343 valid samples were collected via a multi-stage sampling method, and Smart Partial Least Squares (Smart PLS) was employed to analyze the data. The remainder of this paper is organized as follows: the literature review, research hypotheses, research methodology, results, discussion, conclusions, limitations, and future prospects.

Literature review

The theory of planned behavior

The Theory of Planned Behavior (TPB), a classic theory proposed by Ajzen5, has been validated by numerous scholars for its high reliability and validity and is widely applied in fields including management, social psychology, and sociology. The theory posits that an individual’s behavioral intention is determined by three core factors: attitude, subjective norms, and perceived behavioral control, with behavioral intentions further influencing actual behavior. The TPB model is used to explain pro-environmental behaviors, which include purchasing green or environmentally friendly products, using environmentally friendly services, waste disposal, and recycling. However, the theoretical framework is not a closed loop. An increasing number of scholars have expanded the model, continuously enhancing its explanatory power for pro-environmental behaviors. Chanda et al.6 incorporated environmental knowledge, environmental sensitivity, and green product quality into the TPB model to observe the relationship between these three antecedent variables and green purchase intention. Environmental knowledge refers to an individual’s awareness and comprehension of environmental issues. It influences consumers’ tendency to purchase environmentally friendly products by shaping their concerns about environmental issues, thereby triggering their environmentally friendly behaviors7.

Green purchasing intention

Ajzen and Fishbein, experts in consumer behavior, define purchase intention as the likelihood that a customer will choose to purchase a particular product or service. Green purchasing intention (GPI) refers to customers’ willingness to acquire eco-friendly products1. According to the China Consumers’ Association, the first level of green consumption is encouraging consumers to select products that improve public health; the second is considering waste disposal during consumption to preserve the environment; and the third is developing an environmentally conscious and health-seeking consumption mindset. Regarding the dimensions of green purchase intention, most scholars measure it by consumers’ propensity to buy and willingness to recommend others to use such products8. Some other scholars argue that green purchase intention should also include willingness to learn green knowledge, willingness to put effort into purchasing green products9, and willingness to pay additional costs7. Considering the principle of comprehensiveness in dimensional classification, this study divides green purchase intention into three categories: propensity to purchase, willingness to recommend purchases, and willingness to make efforts.

Scholars contend that factors influencing consumers’ green purchase intention can be primarily categorized into internal and external factors. Internal factors mainly involve the impact of socio-demographic characteristics and psychological factors on green purchase intention. Psychological factors typically encompass attitudes toward green products, environmental values, and environmental awareness. Consumers’ green purchase behavior is further influenced by external factors such as environmental knowledge, advertising appeals, product attributes, and social norms. Sun et al.10 found that environmental knowledge exerts the most significant impact on consumers’ green purchase intention among these external factors. According to cognitive learning theory, consumers with higher levels of environmental knowledge are more inclined to consider products’ green attributes when making purchase decisions11.

Environmental knowledge

Consumers’ ability to comprehend different environmental concepts, actions, and facts is known as environmental knowledge (EK)2. It also refers to the capacity to understand environmental challenges, such as the dangers posed by soil contamination, air pollution, and climate change, as well as how to better protect and improve the environment12. Environmental knowledge can be divided into two dimensions: subjective knowledge and objective knowledge, which has become a consensus in the academic community. Subjective knowledge pertains to an individual’s self-perceived understanding of environmental phenomena and their effects on humans and wildlife, and is often described as an abstract awareness of environmental issues. Objective knowledge, on the other hand, comprises factual information derived from observation or empirical evidence, representing a foundational comprehension of nature, the environment, and their core ecosystems6. Consequently, subjective knowledge focuses on self-assessment, whereas objective knowledge is grounded in factual or specific behavioral insights2. However, most scholars focus on subjective knowledge while neglecting the role of objective knowledge.

The concepts of environmental knowledge and green purchasing intention have been widely studied in the field of consumer behavior, but the findings remain inconsistent. Some studies have shown that environmental knowledge has a positive impact on consumers’ green purchase intention13. However, other studies indicate that the relationship between environmental knowledge and green purchase intention is insignificant14. A thorough analysis of the core reasons for these differences mainly involves four aspects: first, there is a discrepancy in variable measurement, as some studies do not fully consider the measurement dimensions of environmental knowledge and green purchase intention; second, there are group differences, as different educational levels and age groups have varying environmental knowledge conversion abilities, with college students showing significantly higher conversion ability than other groups; third, there are differences in cultural and economic development levels, as the distinct cultural traditions and economic development stages of different countries or regions directly affect consumers’ environmental cognition and consumption decision-making logic; fourth, there is a discrepancy in the selection of mediating variables15. Some studies do not introduce any mediating variables and directly explore the direct correlation between environmental knowledge and green purchase intention, overlooking the impact of intermediate transmission paths. Therefore, this study constructed a higher-order model to explore the influence of environmental knowledge on green purchasing intentions, thereby expanding the theoretical research on green consumption in specific groups and contexts.

Hypothesis development

Environmental knowledge and green purchasing intention

Consumers’ awareness of environmental issues influences their propensity to buy eco-friendly products16. Increasing environmental knowledge positively impacts consumers’ propensity to adopt environmentally friendly practices2. Individuals with greater environmental knowledge are more likely to recognize potential crises associated with environmental issues and to adjust their daily consumption habits. Among college students, who have received higher education, environmental knowledge level exhibits a significantly stronger positive correlation with green purchase intention compared to other groups17. This is because college students have access to a wider range of environmental knowledge resources, and their consumption patterns are not yet fully established, facilitating the transformation of environmental knowledge into green purchase intention. In light of this, we propose the following hypothesis:

H1

EK positively influences undergraduate students’ GPI.

Environmental knowledge and mediating variables

A person’s favorable or negative assessment of an action is referred to as an attitude18. Green product attitude (GPA) is reflected in consumers’ tendencies, feelings, and evaluations of green product purchase intention and behavior15. Environmental knowledge has a positive impact on consumers’ pro-environmental attitudes and willingness to engage in environmental protection. Consumers with greater environmental knowledge are more likely to recognize the harm caused by environmental issues to human society, care about environmental protection, and exhibit more positive environmental attitudes19. This, in turn, increases their willingness to purchase environmentally friendly products2. Thus, we propose the following hypothesis:

H2

EK positively influences GPA.

Subjective norms (SN) refer to the social pressure an individual feels when deciding whether to engage in or refrain from a specific behavior20. Subjective norms are also defined as external pressures from peers, family, friends, and society to engage in or refrain from particular behaviors, which can lead to either positive or negative behaviors. To better understand their nature, some scholars have further divided subjective norms into two categories: descriptive norms and injunctive norms. Descriptive norms provide information about what others typically do, while injunctive norms convey what others approve of or disapprove of20,21. Consumers with more environmental knowledge are more sensitive to social pressures associated with choosing non-eco-friendly products, which motivates them to engage in pro-environmental behaviors. Qureshi et al.22 employed the Theory of Reasoned Action as a research framework to pinpoint the factors impacting green purchase intention in Pakistan. The study revealed that environmental knowledge exhibits a positive and significant influence on subjective norms. Thus, we propose the following hypothesis:

H3

EK positively influences SN.

Perceived behavioral control, or PBC, refers to an individual’s perceived capacity, resources, and opportunities to perform a specific behavior. Individuals exhibit high levels of perceived behavioral control when they believe they possess more resources and opportunities to perform a specific behavior23. According to Ajzen18, perceived behavioral control comprises two components: self-efficacy and perceived accountability. Self-efficacy refers to the degree of confidence an individual has and the perceived difficulty in performing a particular behavior, while perceived accountability is the degree of control an individual has over the resources needed to carry out a specific conduct. Environmental knowledge and perceived behavioral control exhibit a strong positive correlation. Environmental knowledge enhances personal environmental beliefs, which in turn improves consumers’ perceived control and perceived behavioral control16,24. Thus, we propose the following hypothesis:

H4

EK positively influences PBC.

Mediating variables and green purchasing intention

Generally, green product attitude (GPA) is a positive predictor of behavioral intention in green product consumption25. Studies have shown that consumers with more favorable attitudes toward green products are more likely to make a green purchase. This is because positive green product attitudes indicate that consumers perceive these products as more valuable in terms of environmental benefits, social responsibility, and other aspects. Chanda et al.6 demonstrated a significant positive correlation between green product attitude and green purchase intention. Thus, we propose the following hypothesis:

H5

GPA positively influences GPI.

Subjective norms (SN) refer to the social pressures and norms that influence a consumer’s decision on whether to make a green purchase. When individuals perceive that significant others desire them to purchase green products, and that non-purchase may lead to social disapproval, their willingness to buy increases significantly. Numerous studies have shown that subjective norms have a significant positive effect on green purchase intention22,26,27. For instance, a study by Pang et al.28 demonstrated that subjective norms positively influence the green purchase intention of green skin care products. Thus, we propose the following hypothesis:

H6

SN significantly and positively affects GPI.

When purchasing green products, consumers with high perceived behavioral control will exhibit high motivation to take action; conversely, if PBC is low, a consumer’s motivation to act may be weak even when surrounded by favorable subjective norms and positive attitudes29,30. Consumers with higher perceived behavioral control and concern for environmental sustainability issues are more likely to develop green purchase intention31. A study by Ruslim et al.23 found that consumers’ perceived behavioral control exerts a significant positive influence on their green purchase intention of green skin care products. As a result, we propose the following hypothesis:

H7

PBC significantly and positively affects GPI.

Mediating effect

According to Moslehpour et al.1, environmental knowledge plays a crucial role in shaping consumers’ attitudes and behaviors, and the relationship between EK and green purchase intention can be mediated by green product attitude. Specifically, organizing environmental knowledge according to consumer attitudes is conducive to enhancing sustainable development. L.Wang et al. examined the positive relationship between educational level and consumers’ attitudes and green purchase intentions15. Consumers’ attitudes change as environmental knowledge increases, which in turn influences green purchase intention and behavior. When surveying 466 college students in northern India, the researchers found that environmental knowledge influences green purchase intention through pro-environmental attitudes32. Therefore, we propose the following hypothesis:

H8

The relationship between EK and GPI is mediated by GPA.

Environmental knowledge motivates consumers to engage in socially accepted or encouraged pro-environmental behaviors33, and millennials exhibit high sensitivity to peer opinions, which influences their green purchase intentions. When consumers observe social groups purchasing green products or perceive that society expects them to do so, their propensity to purchase green products increases significantly, thus contributing to the formation of green purchase intentions22. Therefore, we propose the following hypothesis:

H9

SN mediates the relationship between EK and GPI.

Perceived behavioral control can function not only as a determinant of behavior but also as a mediating variable. Specifically, the absence of control may hinder behavioral performance34. Perceived behavioral control can act as a mediating variable between environmental knowledge and green purchase intention, as behaviors are more likely to be performed if an individual possesses sufficient EK and is able to control available resources35. Therefore, we propose the following hypothesis:

H10

The relationship between EK and GPI is mediated by PBC.

Research methodology

Sample and data collection

The overall population of this study comprises all undergraduate students from all regular universities in Liuzhou City, Guangxi Province, China in 2024. Data collection was conducted through questionnaire surveys. As a major industrial hub in South China, Liuzhou City is a key industrial city in Guangxi and is currently promoting green industrial transformation and ecological protection. In recent years, relevant green consumption policies have been introduced sequentially, making the green purchase intentions of local university students more representative of the current status of green purchase intentions among Chinese university students. Moreover, as a city in southwestern China, Liuzhou has a different market penetration rate of green consumption from that of first-tier cities in eastern China, thereby enriching the research on the green purchase intentions of university students in small and medium-sized cities. This study employed a stratified sampling method, with one undergraduate university and two junior colleges selected. Subsequently, within each selected institution, simple random sampling was adopted to select the required number of samples.

Data collection was conducted in two phases: the first phase involved the distribution of 200 questionnaires to respondents from March to June 2024; the second phase, aimed at increasing the sample size and enhancing the representativeness, involved the additional distribution of 174 questionnaires from September to December 2024. To ensure the continuity and reliability of the data from both phases, a difference test was conducted on the key demographic characteristics of the sample, and the results revealed no significant differences in key demographic characteristics such as gender, grade, and major between the two phases (p > 0.05), indicating good consistency between the two phases of data. To verify the structural stability of the proposed model over time, a multi-group analysis (MGA) was performed in SmartPLS to compare the two sub-samples. The results revealed no significant differences in the key path coefficients between the two sub-groups. Thus, the data could be merged for subsequent statistical analysis. After the completion of data collection, the researchers systematically screened out questionnaires with missing data, outlier responses, speedy responses, and obvious logical conflicts. Through this screening process, 18 invalid questionnaires and 13 non-responsive questionnaires were excluded. Ultimately, a total of 343 valid and usable questionnaires were collected, with an overall effective response rate of 91.17%. This met the sample size requirements for formal quantitative research.

Demographic data are presented in Table 1. The sample consists of 186 male and 157 female participants. The questionnaire sample was relatively evenly distributed across different grade levels, thus being representative of the overall profile of students in each grade at the selected institutions. Senior-year students accounted for a relatively small proportion of the sample (22.16%), which was attributed to the fact that many senior-year students were absent due to internships or job searches. Engineering and technical majors accounted for 31.49%, and humanities and social sciences majors accounted for 24.20% of the total sample. These two fields had a relatively high representation, which is associated with the industrial layout orientation and higher education discipline development in Liuzhou. Additionally, Table 1 includes information regarding students’ living expenses. Most students (63.85%) reported monthly living expenses between 1000 and 1500 yuan, indicating a typical budget for students in the surveyed area.

Measures

The measurement scales used in this study were all derived from previously validated scales. All questionnaire items were scored using a five-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree, 5 = strongly agree), and the measurement scales are presented in Appendix 1. Green purchase intention comprised three dimensions and a total of nine items, adapted from Zahan et al.8 and Sultana et al.7. Environmental knowledge was measured in two dimensions and included ten items, adapted from Jaiswal & Kant36 and Chan12. Green product attitude was measured as a single dimension and included five items, adapted from Moslehpour et al.1 and Qureshi et al.22. Subjective norms were measured in two dimensions and included seven items, adapted from Zahan et al.8 and Rhodes & Courneya37. Perceived behavioral control (PBC) was measured in two dimensions and included six items, adapted from Nam et al.38 and Al-Swidi et al.39.

Ethical considerations

This study adopted a questionnaire-based approach for quantitative empirical investigation, with no involvement of human experiments or collection of human tissue samples. The research strictly adhered to ethical guidelines and complied with the Declaration of Helsinki (2000). All respondents were fully informed of the research purpose, data usage methods, and confidentiality agreement, and voluntarily completed the questionnaire with informed consent. Prior to the commencement of data collection, ethical approval had been obtained from the Institutional Review Board (IRB).

Results

This study employed the partial least squares structural equation modeling (PLS-SEM) approach to construct the research model and SmartPLS 4 statistical software for data analysis40.

Common method bias

Common Method Bias (CMB) is widely present in questionnaire-based studies. Given that this study’s data were collected from a single source, the presence of CMB was assessed through the full collinearity test41. The full collinearity test was evaluated using SPSS 26.0, and a variance inflation factor (VIF) of ≤ 5 indicates that there is no significant bias in single-source data in this study42. The results in Table 2 showed that all items had VIF values lower than 5, thus confirming that there was no CMB in the data. In addition, several procedural safeguards were implemented during data collection to minimize common method bias. These included strict anonymity and confidentiality as well as the use of clear, non-technical item wording without leading expressions. Furthermore, reverse-coded items were included to reduce response bias.

Higher-order constructs

The higher-order construct allowed researchers to define sub-dimensions of the core construct, thereby gaining a deeper understanding of complex relationships. This study constructed a reflective-reflective type second-order construct comprising four higher-order components and ten lower-order components. According to Sarstedt et al.43, we constructed the second-order construct using the two-stage embedded approach. In the first stage, all indicators of the lower-order components were associated with the higher-order components based on the standard repeated indicator approach, and the scores of the lower-order components were then saved. In the second stage, the measurement model of the higher-order construct used the lower-order component scores obtained in the first stage as indicators.

Measurement model

Indicator reliability, internal consistency reliability, convergent validity, and discriminant validity were used as the key criteria to evaluate the reflective measurement model44. The results were depicted in Table 3; Fig. 2. To be deemed acceptable, the measurement model should meet the following criteria: factor loadings ≥ 0.7, average variance extracted (AVE) ≥ 0.5 45, Cronbach’s alpha ≥ 0.7, and composite reliability (CR) ≥ 0.744. As outlined in Table 3, all AVE values of the constructs exceeded 0.5, Cronbach’s alpha values exceeded 0.7, and CR values consistently exceeded 0.7. Discriminant validity is used to test the degree of differentiation between different constructs and is typically evaluated using the Heterotrait-Monotrait (HTMT) ratio. As stated by Hair et al.44, acceptable HTMT levels are ≤ 0.85 for stricter criteria and ≤ 0.90 for more relaxed criteria. As indicated in Table 5, only one HTMT value (PBC <-> SN = 0.874) is less than 0.90, and all other HTMT values are below 0.85, meeting the more permissive requirements. Subjective norms reflected the influence of perceived social pressure on individuals’ green purchasing behavior, while perceived behavioral control represented individuals’ perceptions of their ability to engage in green purchasing behavior and their level of resource control. Among college students, social norms often indirectly affected their judgment of the feasibility of purchasing green products, which led to a slightly higher HTMT value. The results in Table 4 showed that the CR values of PBC and SN were both > 0.7, their AVE values were both > 0.5, and their factor loadings were > 0.7. This indicated that both constructs nevertheless had satisfactory internal consistency and discriminant validity, which would not affect the reliability of the subsequent empirical analysis results.

Structural Model

According to Hair et al.44, the statistical significance and relevance of the path coefficients, the coefficient of determination (R2), and the blindfolding-based cross-validated redundancy measure (Q2) are important evaluation criteria. Researchers should also use the PLS prediction process to evaluate the model’s predictive capabilities47.

Following the recommendations of Cain et al.48, we examined multivariate kurtosis and skewness. Criteria such as multivariate skewness ≤ 3 and kurtosis values ≤ 20 are used to evaluate multivariate normalcy. Given that Mardia’s multivariate skewness (β = 4.930, p < 0.01) and kurtosis (β = 49.492, p < 0.01) were both significant, the results showed that our data did not meet these requirements. Thus, in accordance with Becker et al.49, we used a bootstrapping approach with 10,000 samples to present the path coefficients, standard errors, t-values, and p-values for the structural model.

To investigate the relationships between the variables, we employed a 10,000-sample resampling bootstrapping approach with a 5% significance level for one-tailed testing. Figure 2 presents the impact of the four predictors on green purchasing intention, with an R2 of 0.647, indicating that these predictors accounted for 64.7% of the variance in green purchasing intention. As demonstrated in Table 6, environmental knowledge (β = 0.115, p = 0.01), green product attitude (β = 0.285, p < 0.01), subjective norms (β = 0.181, p < 0.01), and perceived behavioral control (β = 0.379, p < 0.01) were all positively associated with green purchasing intention, thus supporting H1, H5, H6 and H7. Additionally, we tested the effect of environmental knowledge on green product attitude, subjective norms, and perceived behavioral control, yielding R2 values of 0.341, 0.296, and 0.260, respectively (as shown in Fig. 2). Accordingly, 34.1% of the variance in green product attitude, 29.6% of the variance in subjective norms, and 26% of the variance in perceived behavioral control were explained by environmental knowledge. Table 6 further supports H2, H3, and H4, showing that environmental knowledge has a significant and favorable direct link with green product attitude (β = 0.584, p < 0.01), subjective norms (β = 0.544, p < 0.01), and perceived behavioral control (β = 0.510, p < 0.01). Our findings are confirmed by the 95% bootstrap confidence interval that does not include zero.

A third variable or construct that stands between two related constructions is said to have mediating effects. For particular indirect effects, we employed a two-tailed test, a resampling bootstrap technique with 10,000 samples, and a 5% significance threshold. If the confidence interval did not include zero, we determined that there was a significant mediating impact50. According to Table 7, there were significant relationships for EK -> GPA -> GPI (β = 0.166, p < 0.01), EK -> SN -> GPI (β = 0.099, p < 0.01), and EK -> PBC -> GPI (β = 0.193, p < 0.01). These results were further supported by the bias-corrected 95% confidence interval not including zero. Consequently, hypotheses H8, H9, and H10 were supported. Tables 6 and 7 further show that the direct effect of EK on GPI was β = 0.115 (p = 0.01), and the total mediating effect was 0.458. The mediating effect played an important role between environmental knowledge and green purchase intention. Further calculation showed that the proportions of green product attitude, subjective norms, and perceived behavioral control in the total mediating effect were 36.24%, 21.62%, and 42.14%, respectively. Since both the direct and indirect effects were positive and completely consistent in direction, green product attitudes, subjective norms, and perceived behavioral control played complementary partial mediating roles in the relationship between environmental knowledge and green purchasing intentions.

Finally, to further verify the practical value of the proposed model, its out-of-sample predictive performance was assessed for managerial decision-making. The assessment was conducted using the PLSpredict procedure developed by Shmueli et al.47. Importantly, Shmueli et al.51 suggested that the key endogenous construct of the model should be prioritized when interpreting the PLSpredict results. Following the recommendation of Shmueli et al.51, for indicators with a Q2 predict greater than 0, researchers should then compare the RMSE (or MAE) values with the naive LM benchmark. When compared to the LM, the PLS-SEM analysis can yield lower prediction errors in terms of RMSE (or MAE) for all indicators (indicating high predictive power), the majority of indicators (indicating medium predictive power), the minority of indicators (indicating low predictive power), or none of the indicators (indicating a lack of predictive power). The higher-order component “green purchase intention” was the primary target variable in this study and was selected as a proxy for evaluating the model’s overall predictive ability. Table 8 indicated that the proposed model had moderate predictive power, as all Q2 predictions were greater than zero and most indicators in the PLS-SEM analysis had smaller prediction errors compared to the linear model (LM). In addition, the global model fit indicator SRMR (Standardized Root Mean Square Residual) is reported as 0.079, which is less than 0.08, indicating that the model has a good overall fit with the data.

Discussion

Quantitative analysis results show that environmental knowledge has a significant positive impact on green purchase intention (β = 0.115, p = 0.01), thus supporting H1. This finding is consistent with previous studies by Rusyani et al.16, Cong Doanh et al.2, and Biswas and Roy52. This suggests that consumers with higher levels of environmental knowledge have a stronger awareness of environmental crises and are more likely to purchase green products. However, research by Elbarky et al.14 revealed that there is no significant relationship between environmental knowledge and green purchase intention. The main reason for the differences in findings is that the sample of Elbarky et al.14 consisted of a mixed group of consumers with different age groups and educational levels. The group without higher educational attainment had a relatively weak ability to transform environmental knowledge, and their consumption perceptions were relatively fixed, which made it difficult to effectively convert environmental knowledge into green purchase intentions. The sample of this study comprised college students, who had a better foundation in environmental awareness and higher efficiency in converting environmental knowledge into green purchase intentions. The differences in conclusions further illustrate the importance of environmental knowledge education for college students provided by schools and society.

Further analysis shows that environmental knowledge has a significant positive effect on green product attitude (β = 0.584, p < 0.01), thus supporting H2; on subjective norms (β = 0.544, p < 0.01), thus supporting H3; and on perceived behavioral control (β = 0.510, p < 0.01), thus supporting H4. This aligns with previous studies16,19,53. These results confirm the important role of environmental knowledge in shaping individuals’ green purchasing intentions. When consumers gain environmental knowledge, their attitudes toward green products are significantly strengthened, and they are more inclined to align their behaviors with social expectations. Additionally, environmental knowledge helps strengthen personal self-efficacy and enhance perceived behavioral control, thereby increasing individuals’ willingness to purchase green products.

According to the results of structural equation modeling (SEM) analysis, green product attitude (β = 0.285, p < 0.01), subjective norms (β = 0.181, p < 0.01), and perceived behavioral control (β = 0.379, p < 0.01) all have a significant positive effect on green purchase intention, thus supporting H5–H7. This aligns with the results of Qureshi et al.22 and Chanda et al.6. Green product attitude reflects consumers’ recognition and approval of green products; subjective norms capture the influence of social group expectations and pressures on green purchase intention; and perceived behavioral control indicates consumers’ confidence in overcoming difficulties in green product purchases. These findings further confirm the core tenets of the Theory of Planned Behavior (TPB) and reveal the multidimensional motivations that drive green purchasing intentions.

The results of the mediation effect analysis via the bootstrap method indicate that green product attitude (β = 0.166, p < 0.01), subjective norms (β = 0.099, p < 0.01), and perceived behavioral control (β = 0.193, p < 0.01) each play a significant mediating role in the relationship between environmental knowledge and green purchase intention, thus supporting H8–H10. These results are consistent with previous studies1,22,33. The proportions of the total mediating effect contributed by green product attitude, subjective norms, and perceived behavioral control are 36.24%, 21.62%, and 42.14%, respectively. This indicates that perceived behavioral control is the core mediating variable, suggesting that college students’ judgment on whether they can successfully implement green purchase behavior is the key bridge for transforming environmental knowledge into green purchase intention. As a group without stable income, college students are more sensitive to green product prices and have limited access to available purchase channels. Together, these factors make students’ perceived control over obtaining green products a key constraint in the process of translating environmental knowledge into green purchasing intention. Environmental knowledge exerts an important mediating effect by shaping college students’ positive evaluation of green products. The more environmentally knowledgeable a college student is, the clearer their recognition of the environmental value of green products, thereby forming a positive green product attitude and promoting green purchase intention. Environmental knowledge enhances college students’ perception of social norms regarding green consumption, which in turn exerts an indirect effect on green purchase intention, with subjective norms and the other two mediating variables functioning together.

Conclusion

This study is based on the framework of the Theory of Planned Behavior (TPB) and takes college students as research participants. It explores the mechanism by which environmental knowledge affects green purchasing intentions. The results show that environmental knowledge has a significant direct impact on green purchasing intentions (β = 0.115) and exerts significant indirect effects through green product attitudes (β = 0.166), subjective norms (β = 0.099), and perceived behavioral control (β = 0.193), with the latter exhibiting the strongest mediating role. These findings further deepen the theoretical understanding of the relationship between environmental knowledge and green purchasing intentions, and provide empirical evidence and practical implications for universities, governments, and enterprises to enhance college students’ green purchasing intentions. Specifically, universities should, on the basis of environmental knowledge education, strengthen the cultivation of green consumption attitudes and the improvement of perceived behavioral control, thereby facilitating the effective translation of environmental knowledge into green purchasing intentions. Governments should improve the green consumption policy system, innovate public communication strategies, and create a supportive environment for sustainable consumption. Enterprises should, based on the characteristics of young consumers, enhance the cost-effectiveness, accessibility, and transparency of green products.

Limitations and future directions

This study empirically examined the impact of environmental knowledge on undergraduate students’ green purchase intentions by investigating the mediating roles of green product attitude, subjective norms, and perceived behavioral control. This research still has certain limitations. Firstly, the sample selection is geographically restricted, and students from different geographical regions and university types were not included, which may not fully reflect the potential impact of regional differences on green purchase intentions. Secondly, the questionnaire adopts a closed-ended question format, which limits the exploration of the underlying reasons for college students’ green purchase intentions. Finally, this research exclusively examined the mediating roles of the three variables: green product attitude, subjective norms, and perceived behavioral control.

Future research can be further extended in the following aspects. First, expand the research scope to cover different types of cities in the eastern, central, and western regions, as well as other educational levels and occupational groups. Cross-regional comparisons can enhance the universality of the research conclusions. Second, improve the research methods and questionnaire design. Open-ended questions can be added, and quantitative and qualitative mixed research methods can be adopted to reveal the formation mechanism of college students’ green purchase intentions in a more in-depth and comprehensive manner. Third, optimize the variable system. Variables such as social cultural factors, personal values, and social media usage can be introduced to construct a more comprehensive theoretical model, in order to deepen the understanding of the relationship mechanism between environmental knowledge and green purchasing intentions.
