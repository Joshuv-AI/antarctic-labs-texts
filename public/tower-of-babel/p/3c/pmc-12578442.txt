Ulcerated tophaceous gout
Authors: Shyla Gupta, Anique Le Roux, Lorenzo Madrazo
Published: 2025

An 88-year-old man with peripheral vascular disease, chronic kidney disease, a 60-year smoking history, and gout (for which he was taking colchicine) presented to the emergency department with a wound on his left inner ankle that had been nonhealing for 18 months. It had worsened over the 3 months preceding presentation, raising concern for soft-tissue infection. Despite treatment with cephalexin and clindamycin, his pain intensified, and he became nonambulatory, prompting admission for presumptive osteomyelitis.

On examination, the patient had a raised, rounded ulcerating lesion with a chalky centre and surrounding erythema (Figure 1A). Bloodwork revealed a leukocyte count of 6.0 (normal range 3.2 to 9.4) × 109/L, erythrocyte sediment rate of 33 (normal ≤ 20) mm/h, and a C-reactive protein level of 28.8 (normal < 10) mg/L. We started a 5-day course of piperacillin–tazobactam. A computed tomography scan with contrast showed a hyperdense mineralized tophus on the medial aspect of the ankle, evidence of crystal deposition in the ankle, midfoot, and big toe, with no signs of osteomyelitis (Figure 1B). Synovial fluid analysis identified negatively birefringent uric acid crystals, confirming ulcerative tophaceous gout. Wound cultures grew Serratia marcescens and Staphylococcus aureus, so we added trimethoprim–sulfamethoxazole for 9 days, overlapping piperacillin–tazobactam for 3 days. Synovial fluid and blood cultures were negative.

(A) Large, ulcerated tophus seen on the medial side of the left ankle of an 88-year-old man. (B) A computed tomography (CT) scan showed a large hyperdense mineralized tophus on the medial aspect of the ankle, as well as other tophi at the first metatarsophalangeal and interphalangeal joints.

We initiated allopurinol to lower the patient’s urate level and continued colchicine. We did not prescribe nonsteroidal anti-inflammatories or increase colchicine to avoid worsening renal dysfunction. We avoided prescribing steroids, given the concern for infection.1 The ulcer improved on reassessment after 3 months.

Although tophaceous gout is common, ulcerative presentations are uncommon, and no treatment guidelines exist.2,3 Alongside medical treatment such as urate-lowering therapy, corticosteroids, and colchicine, management includes débridement and topical 3% citric acid in petroleum jelly.4 Interpretation of cultures from wound samples depends on multiple factors, including clinical signs of active infection, specimen quality, colonization, contaminants, and previous antibiotic exposure. In our patient’s case, imaging and fluid analysis were more useful than microbiology in identifying the cause of the ulcer.
