The art of conducting a good pilot study: a discussion of the BETA-ARREST pilot trial
Authors: Hanna L Hägglund, Sergio Cazorla-Calderón, Eline L Braekman, Amani Alenazi, Kasper Glerup Lauridsen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Graphical abstractKeywords: Pilot studies, Methodology, Refractory ventricular fibrillation, Landiolol

Introduction

This journal club article was conducted as part of the 2024/25 Young ERC Resuscitation Science Masterclass.1 In this paper, we provide a critical appraisal of the BETA-ARREST trial, titled “Landiolol for Refractory Ventricular Fibrillation in Out-of-hospital Cardiac Arrest: a Randomized, Double-blind, Placebo-controlled, Pilot Trial” by Gelbenegger et al. 2024,2 and discuss what constitutes a good pilot study. Pilot trials are commonly conducted within resuscitation science and provide many opportunities3, 4 but the term is sometimes misused and there are potential pitfalls to consider.5

What knowledge gap does the study address?

Out-of-hospital cardiac arrest (OHCA) complicated by refractory ventricular fibrillation (VF) involves significant challenges in emergency medical care and is associated with poor outcomes.6, 7, 8, 9 The authors aimed to address the knowledge gap in managing OHCA patients with refractory or recurrent VF, and how to terminate these.2 The study evaluates the efficacy and safety of prehospital intravenous administration of landiolol, a beta-1-receptor selective blocker, as an add-on treatment in this critical setting.2

What was the design of the study?

The study was designed as a randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled phase II pilot trial, conducted in Vienna, Austria.2 A total of 36 patients with OHCA and recurrent or refractory VF, pretreated with epinephrine and amiodarone according to international guidelines,10 were randomly allocated in a 1:1 ratio to receive landiolol or placebo.2 The study aimed to assess the efficacy and safety of prehospital landiolol in OHCA and refractory VF. Patients were included from March 2021 through June 2023.

What were the key findings of the study?

The primary outcome of the study was time from trial drug infusion to sustained return of spontaneous circulation (ROSC), showing no significant difference between landiolol and placebo (39 min [IQR 4–57] vs. 41 min [IQR 24–77], p = 0.387, respectively).2 Sustained ROSC and survival at 24 h were numerically lower in the landiolol group compared to placebo (7 patients [37 %] vs 11 patients [66 %], and 5 patients [26 %] vs 10 patients [59 %], respectively), however without statistically significant differences.2 Additionally, the study addressed potential safety concerns. Landiolol administration had a significantly higher risk of asystole within 15 min of trial drug infusion (7 patients in landiolol group compared to 0 patients in placebo group; p = 0.008).2

Methodological considerations

The BETA-ARREST trial was labeled both as a pilot study and a phase 2 trial. A pilot study is a specific type of feasibility study defined as a small-scale version of a future study, aiming to assess whether the main trial is feasible and should be conducted, and if alterations are needed.11 It is considered a “trial run”, conducted to identify potential issues, such as feasibility, duration, and cost, to enable needed adjustments for the main study, rather than to test hypotheses.12 Accordingly, pilot studies often include pre-specified definitions of what it considered a successful pilot, such as inclusion rate or adherence to the study protocol, to assess feasibility.13, 14 Importantly, feasibility studies may be distinguished from pilot studies. Feasibility studies investigate if it is, or how it could be, feasible to execute a study, whereas a pilot study also replicate the design of a possible larger, future study such as the BETA-ARREST trial.12 As such, all pilot studies are feasibility studies, but not all feasibility studies are pilot studies.15

Little is discussed about feasibility aspects in the BETA-ARREST trial. One important feasibility aspect in the BETA-ARREST trial was the recruitment rate; 478 patients were assessed for eligibility with 441 (93 %) being excluded due to “no capacity for trial inclusion”. This is likely due to the local set-up where an emergency physician or a study fellow had to be on site for inclusion, due to legal restrictions. Thus, the trial provides important feasibility information, suggesting that such a trial design is likely not feasible to use for a larger study given the low inclusion rate.

Recommendations for sample size in pilot studies range from 12 to 30 participants per group16, 17 with no consensus for the optimal number given that this will likely depend on the context. As pilot studies are not powered to detect statistically significant differences, they will often focus on descriptive statistics.18, 19 As such, the inferential statistics used to assess study outcomes by Gelbenegger et al should be interpreted with caution.2

What are the most important strengths and limitations of the study?

A key strength of the study is its randomized controlled trial design. Observational study designs are prone to confounding by indication as provision of a drug in a non-randomized setting may be due to unknown, underlying reasons and personal preferences. Therefore, the authors should be commented for successful execution of randomization which provides much more robust evidence although more demanding to execute compared to an observational study. However, the study had some limitations. First, the primary outcome of time to ROSC is prone to collider bias.20 Time to ROSC is implicitly conditioned on ROSC having occurred which appeared to be affected by both patient factors and landiolol. Thus, creating a spurious association for the time to ROSC. Second, the high exclusion rate raises concerns about the generalizability. Third, the study included both patients with initial shockable and non-shockable rhythm, as well as refractory and recurrent VF, and heterogeneity in treatment effects remain unknown. Finally, the study was retrospectively registered on Clinicaltrials.gov about 18 months after inclusion of the first patient which is against recommendations from The Declaration of Helsinki21 and the International Committee of Medical Journal Editors (ICMJE).22

How will the results affect your clinical practice?

Currently, beta-blockade is not recommended for routine use for cardiac arrest patients. Although the study is underpowered to conclude on harm or benefits of landiolol use, the substantial numerical difference in ROSC (37 % vs. 66 %) and higher risk of asystole in the landiolol raise ethical concerns about whether it would be appropriate to proceed with a larger-scale trial. Neither do the findings support routine use of landiolol for cardiac arrest patients with shockable rhythms in clinical practice. Notably, negative findings represent important clinical information to reduce the use of ineffective or harmful medications in clinical practice.

What do you see as the next steps in research?

The study by Gelbenegger et al. addresses an important knowledge gap. With a high mortality rate, and a lack of treatment options with effect on OHCA survival, further research on new treatment strategies is needed. Although the findings of this study do not suggest landiolol being beneficial for cardiac arrest patients, studies may look into alternative dosing strategies or whether certain patient subgroups may benefit from this treatment. Importantly, future trials should be prospectively registered in relevant international registries to reduce the risk of selective reporting.
