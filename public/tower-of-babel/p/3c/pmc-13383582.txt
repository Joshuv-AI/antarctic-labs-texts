Traumatic pseudoaneurysm of a distal middle cerebral artery branch with findings suggestive of an interdural location: illustrative case
Authors: Toshiaki Inomo, Eiji Ito, Yusuke Sakamoto, Akiko Ohashi, Mao Yokota, Tadashi Watanabe
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBACKGROUNDTraumatic intracranial pseudoaneurysms are rare, and those located between the dural layers (interdural space) are exceptionally uncommon. The authors describe a delayed pseudoaneurysm arising from a distal middle cerebral artery branch, with multimodal findings most consistent with an interdural location.OBSERVATIONSA 56-year-old man sustained a fall-related head injury with right frontal contusion, frontal bone fracture, and superior orbital wall fractures. Initial 3D CT angiography (3D-CTA) showed no vascular abnormality; however, follow-up 3D-CTA on day 20 revealed a de novo 4-mm pseudoaneurysm of the lateral orbitofrontal artery (LOFA) adjacent to the fracture. During surgical trapping, the parent LOFA was observed to enter and exit the dural layer proximally and distally, respectively, and the lesion was resected en bloc with the adherent dura. Histopathological analysis confirmed the lesion as a pseudoaneurysm, and the interdural location was inferred from the integration of intraoperative anatomical findings and gross specimen continuity with the dura.LESSONSThis case suggests that traumatic pseudoaneurysms from intracranial arteries may rarely develop in an interdural location and may present with a delayed onset. Intracranial pseudoaneurysms account for approximately 1% of all intracranial aneurysms and most commonly arise from infection, trauma, or iatrogenic injury.1 Pathologically, they are characterized by a full-thickness arterial wall defect contained within a surrounding encapsulated hematoma rather than within the true layers of the vessel.2 Because of their structural fragility, these lesions carry a high risk of rupture and rebleeding, with reported mortality rates reaching up to 50%,3 and prompt definitive treatment is therefore imperative.4

Two aspects of traumatic intracranial pseudoaneurysms remain poorly understood. First, the full anatomical spectrum of posttraumatic pseudoaneurysm locations, particularly those involving the dura mater, has not been systematically described.1,5,6 The middle meningeal artery (MMA) is a recognized source of dural surface pseudoaneurysms; however, lesions arising from intracranial arteries and located within the dural layers are rare. Second, traumatic pseudoaneurysms may form in a delayed fashion; therefore, an initially negative angiographic study does not exclude the subsequent development of a clinically important lesion.1,7 We report on a traumatic pseudoaneurysm of a distal middle cerebral artery (MCA) branch with multimodal findings that are most consistent with an interdural location, a case that informs both of these unresolved questions.

Illustrative Case

A 56-year-old man was transported to our institution after a fall that resulted in multiple traumatic injuries. CT performed on admission revealed a right frontal lobe contusion (Fig. 1A) associated with frontal bone and superior orbital wall fractures (Fig. 1B). Pneumocephalus was also noted. Initial 3D CT angiography (3D-CTA) revealed no significant arterial abnormalities, and the patient’s condition improved with conservative management. However, 20 days after the injury, a follow-up 3D-CTA study performed to screen for delayed vascular pathology showed a de novo 4-mm pseudoaneurysm at the anterior cranial base adjacent to the fracture site (Fig. 1C). Digital subtraction angiography confirmed the lesion (Fig. 1D), and the parent vessel was identified as the distal lateral orbitofrontal artery (LOFA), a branch of the right MCA (Fig. 1E).

C: Follow-up CT angiogram obtained 20 days after the injury, revealing a 4-mm pseudoaneurysm (arrowhead) near the orbital fracture site. D: Frontal and lateral view digital subtraction angiograms confirming the aneurysm (arrowheads). E: Three-dimensional digital subtraction angiogram demonstrating the parent vessel as a distal LOFA.

Considering the anatomical location and risk of intraoperative rupture, surgical trapping was performed. Endovascular trapping was considered as a potential alternative treatment; however, it was not selected due to several factors, including the risk of distal embolic complications associated with possible migration of embolic materials, as well as concerns regarding the limited ability to achieve immediate control in the event of intraoperative rupture. In addition, because the lesion was located relatively superficially, a direct surgical approach was considered feasible and not technically demanding. The key intraoperative findings and surgical procedure are demonstrated in Video 1. A small craniotomy was performed through a lateral eyebrow incision (Fig. 2A). The LOFA was identified on opening the dura. Tracing the artery revealed an apparent interdural course: the proximal segment entered the dural layer, whereas the distal segment exited it, suggesting that the aneurysm was located within the dural layers (Fig. 2B and C). After trapping the parent artery (Fig. 2D), a dark red mass corresponding to the aneurysm became visible, bulging into the epidural space (Fig. 2E). The pseudoaneurysm was resected en bloc, along with the adherent dura (Fig. 2F). Macroscopic inspection of the specimen confirmed that the aneurysmal mass was embedded within and continuous with the dural layers (Fig. 2G). Histopathological examination revealed a fibrous stroma with an organized thrombus (Fig. 2H), and Elastica van Gieson staining demonstrated complete loss of the normal trilaminar vascular architecture, including the internal elastic lamina and tunica media (Fig. 2I), confirming the diagnosis of pseudoaneurysm.

Discussion

Observations

This case expands the recognized anatomical spectrum of traumatic intracranial pseudoaneurysms. Traumatic pseudoaneurysms arising from distal MCA branches are uncommon and have typically been reported in association with subarachnoid or subdural hemorrhage.8,9 In contrast, pseudoaneurysms arising from dural feeding arteries, such as the MMA, have been described on the dural surface.10,11 To our knowledge, however, a traumatic pseudoaneurysm arising from a distal MCA branch and located within the interdural space has not been previously described. In the present case, the parent vessel was the LOFA, a distal MCA branch that normally courses within the subarachnoid space.12 Nevertheless, intraoperative tracing and macroscopic inspection of the resected specimen demonstrated that the lesion was located within the clinical interdural space, between the dural layers (Fig. 2B, C, and G).

An alternative interpretation is that the lesion originally arose in the subarachnoid space and subsequently became secondarily adherent to the dura after trauma, thereby mimicking an interdural location. However, the intraoperative finding that the parent LOFA traversed the dural layers at two distinct points (Fig. 2B and C), both proximal and distal to the aneurysm, strongly supports a true interdural course rather than focal adhesion limited to the dome. Histopathological analysis independently confirmed the pseudoaneurysmal nature of the lesion by demonstrating disruption of the normal vascular architecture on Elastica van Gieson staining (Fig. 2H and I), although definitive anatomical localization relied on the integration of intraoperative, macroscopic, and microscopic findings.

The anatomical relationship between the lesion and the superior orbital wall fracture (Fig. 1B), together with the delayed appearance of the pseudoaneurysm on day 20, suggests traumatic arterial incarceration as the most plausible mechanism. A comparable mechanism has been established for basilar artery entrapment within clival fractures.13,14 Conceptually, traumatic arterial incarceration may be understood as a sequential process: first, traumatic force causes skull fracture with associated dural disruption; second, inertial displacement of the adjacent intracranial artery occurs at the time of impact; and third, early healing and closure of the fracture site lead to entrapment of the displaced artery, resulting in stenosis, dissection, occlusion, or pseudoaneurysm formation.

Supporting this hypothesis, the pseudoaneurysm was located in close proximity to the superior orbital wall fracture identified on the day of injury (Fig. 4A and B), and a portion of the aneurysm appeared to be incarcerated within the fracture site (Fig. 4C). Therefore, it is reasonable to speculate that a dural rent at the fracture site may have permitted displacement of the LOFA into the interdural space, where subsequent fibrosis, adhesion, and progressive wall failure led to pseudoaneurysm formation (Fig. 4D). The demonstrated dural traversal of the parent artery provides strong support for this interpretation (Fig. 2B and C).

The delayed presentation of this lesion, detected 20 days after trauma despite initially negative 3D-CTA findings, has important clinical implications beyond the anatomical novelty of the case. Traumatic vascular injury should be regarded as a dynamic pathological process rather than an immediate static defect,1,3,7 and delayed pseudoaneurysm formation after initially negative angiography results has been documented.1,3 Lefevre et al. reported that the median interval from injury to aneurysm rupture was 9 days and that 73% of cases were diagnosed more than 1 week after injury, with a median time to diagnosis of 15 days.7 The present case is consistent with this angiographic latency and underscores the limitation of negative results of a single initial vascular study in high-risk trauma. Although a single case is insufficient to justify universal repeat imaging, follow-up vascular imaging may be considered when skull base or craniofacial fractures are anatomically adjacent to intracranial arterial pathways, even if the initial CTA results are negative.

This study has the inherent limitations of a single case report. The anatomical diagnosis was based on convergent intraoperative, gross, and histopathological findings rather than on a single definitive test. The proposed mechanism remains inferential, and the optimal frequency of repeat imaging cannot be generalized based on a single patient. Accumulation of similar cases with detailed anatomical documentation will be necessary to determine whether interdural pseudoaneurysms of intracranial arteries represent a distinct traumatic entity rather than an exceptional anatomical variant or incidental finding.

Lessons

We report a rare traumatic pseudoaneurysm arising from a distal MCA branch, with findings most consistent with an interdural location. The diagnosis in this case relied on the convergence of intraoperative, macroscopic, and histopathological evidence. This case may expand the currently recognized anatomical spectrum of locations in which traumatic pseudoaneurysms can arise. It also suggests that traumatic arterial incarceration may underlie pseudoaneurysm formation in selected skull base fractures and that repeat vascular imaging may be considered when fractures are anatomically adjacent to intracranial arterial pathways despite an initially negative CTA.
