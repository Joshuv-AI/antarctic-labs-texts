Social capital, objective social integration, subjective social integration, and utilization of public health services among older migrant population: a structural equation modeling analysis of multi-group chain mediating effect
Authors: Ting Wang, Xiaolong Bu, Naijiao Xing, Qian Dong, Feng Zhong, Cuiping Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEnhancing accessibility and equity in basic public health services for older migrants constitutes a pivotal objective within global healthcare systems. Previous research has elucidated the impact of social capital, as well as objective and subjective social integration, on the utilization of public health services. However, there exists a dearth of studies investigating the specific pathways linking these factors.MethodsThe present study conducted an analysis of data obtained from the 2017 China Mobile Population Dynamic Monitoring Survey, which involved a sample of 5729 older migrants. Structural equation modelling was employed to examine our hypothesized model encompassing social capital, objective social integration, subjective social integration, and the utilization of public health services. Multi-group analysis was utilized to validate the moderating role of migrant reasons within the proposed model.ResultsThe findings indicated a positive association between social capital and the utilization of public health services (β = 0.037, p < 0.05). Social capital was positively correlated with two mediating variables, objective (β = 0.216, p < 0.001) and subjective social integration (β = 0.095, p < 0.001). Objective social integration was positively correlated with subjective social integration (β = 0.144, p < 0.001). Both objective (β = 0.386, p < 0.001) and subjective social integration (β = 0.144, p < 0.001) exhibited a positive relationship with the utilization of public health services. Finally, the impact of social capital on the utilization of public health services varied depending on the reason for migration.ConclusionsSocial capital was positively associated with the utilization of public health services. Objective and subjective social integration were crucial factors in this process. The impact of social capital on the utilization of public health services varied based on migration motivations. These insights are valuable for policies promoting equal access to public health services and overall well-being for older migrants in an aging society.Keywords: Older migrant population, Mediation analysis, Objective social integration, Social capital, Subjective social integration, Utilization of public health services

Introduction

The global population is experiencing an aging trend, which has significant implications for public health systems worldwide. Data from the seventh national census in China [1] showed that in 2020, there were 260 million people aged 60 and above, accounting for 18.70% of the total population. It indicates that China’s population age structure is rapidly aging, leading to an increased burden in caring for and supporting older adults [2]. In recent years, China’s migrant population has been growing, including a notable increase in the proportion and scale of older migrant population. According to data from the National Health Commission [3], there are approximately 18 million older migrants in China, constituting 7.2% of the total migrant population of 247 million. With urbanization and population aging on the rise, this number is expected to further increase in the future [4]. Due to language barriers [5], cultural differences [6, 7] and limited social support networks [8], older migrant population encounters unique difficulties in accessing and utilizing public health services.

The concept of basic public health services was first introduced by the World Bank in 1993 [9] and has been associated with improved healthcare access and satisfaction globally [10]. In China, a national program providing free basic public health services was launched in 2009, with older adults as a key target group [11]. However, migrants often face barriers in accessing these services due to household registration restrictions [12]. Although policies were introduced in 2013 to promote equal access for migrants [13], a gap remains in achieving this national objective [9, 14]. Previous studies have suggested that social capital [15, 16] and social integration [17, 18] may have an impact on migrants’ use of public health services. By enhancing social capital, migrants can effectively integrate into local communities, develop a stronger sense of social affiliation [19, 20], and subsequently improve their access to public health services. However, there is limited research exploring the relationship between social capital, objective social integration, subjective social integration and the utilization of public health services among older migrants. Specifically, there is a lack of empirical research that simultaneously incorporates social capital, objective social integration, and subjective social integration into a single model to explain public health service utilization among older migrants, and investigates the potential chain-mediating mechanism through which social capital influences utilization via objective and then subjective integration. Furthermore, whether this mechanism differs across sub-groups, such as migrants with different primary reasons for moving, is underexplored.

Social capital and utilization of public health services

Social capital as defined by Coleman, refers to the outcome of actors engaging in social activities to achieve their objectives and establish continuous social connections throughout this process [21]. In public health research, it is often viewed through two complementary lenses: the cohesion perspective, which emphasizes shared trust and norms, and the network perspective, which focuses on the structure of social ties and access to resources through them [22, 23]. A common classification further distinguishes between cognitive social capital (perceptions of trust and reciprocity) and structural social capital (observable participation in networks and organizations) [23]. A further refinement relevant to understanding resource access is the typology of bonding (ties within similar groups), bridging (ties across diverse groups), and linking social capital (vertical ties to individuals or institutions in positions of authority) [23, 24]. For migrant populations, the process of relocation often disrupts existing social networks, depleting their stock of social capital, particularly bonding capital from their place of origin [25]. Research has shown that various forms of social capital can affect the utilization of public health services by influencing individuals’ healthcare-seeking behavior [26]. In certain countries, migrant populations face significant challenges in effectively accessing local health services primarily due to a lack of social capital [15, 27]. In summary, we propose hypothesis 1 (H1).

Social capital, objective social integration and utilization of public health services

Social integration is a multidimensional and interactive concept [28], that involves the mutual interaction between migrant populations and the receiving society [29]. It has both objective and subjective aspects [30]. The objective social integration was defined as the observable and behavioral aspects of an individual’s incorporation into the host society. In this study, the social capital examined refers to the individual-level social capital, which is the structural social capital related to participation in social networks. Previous studies have indicated that social capital enhances the health access levels of migrant populations by positively influencing social integration, and that social integration also has a positive impact on the utilization of basic public health services by migrant older adults [31, 32]. Social capital embedded in networks can positively influence social integration, which in turn increases the utilization of public health services through mechanisms such as promoting information flow, exerting influence, demonstrating social credibility, and strengthening identity and sense of belonging [33]. In summary, we propose hypothesis 2 (H2).

H2 Objective social integration mediates the impact of social capital on the utilization of public health services.

H2 Objective social integration mediates the impact of social capital on the utilization of public health services.

Social capital, subjective social integration and utilization of public health services

The level of social integration experienced by migrant populations consists of both the objective level of integration and their subjective evaluation of their own level of integration [34]. Subjective social integration is defined as an individual’s psychological willingness, sense of belonging and sense of identity towards the host society. Research has indicated that true integration into local society occurs only when the migrant population is psychologically adapted and locally recognized [35]. Some scholars argue that a critical element of achieving citizenization is assisting migrants in overcoming psychological barriers, eliminating marginalization, and ultimately developing a sense of belonging to the city [36]. Previous research has found that local friends can help migrants accumulate social capital and rebuild social networks while gaining more emotional support [37]. A strong sense of social cohesion within the local community has been shown to improve the accessibility of public health services for older migrants, thereby promoting their integration into local community and cultivating a sense of social belonging [20, 38]. Furthermore, older migrants who desire to integrate themselves as members of the local community are more likely to utilize public health services in their area [39]. In summary, we propose hypothesis 3 (H3).

H3 Subjective social integration mediates the impact of social capital on the utilization of public health services.

H3 Subjective social integration mediates the impact of social capital on the utilization of public health services.

Social capital, objective social integration, subjective social integration and utilization of public health services

The sequential relationship from objective to subjective integration is grounded in the theoretical framework of social integration, which posits a progressive sequence from economic integration and behavioral adaptation to ultimate identity formation [40]. Following this logic, successful integration in external, observable domains (such as social, group, and political involvement) establishes the material and behavioral foundation for internal, psychological outcomes. Strengthening social capital broadens the scope of social activities available to migrants. Older migrants can integrate into their new environment by actively engaging in various social activities. Their level of participation significantly represents their level of objective social integration. Actively engaging in local social activities, particularly those related to public affairs, fosters a sense of ownership among migrants, facilitating their subjective social integration [41]. The subjective degree of social integration among older migrants positively correlates with their objective social integration, forming a holistic understanding of the integration process. Moreover, migrants with robust social support and high levels of social integration are better equipped to alleviate feelings of loneliness during migration [42], leading to increased utilization of public health services. In summary, we propose hypothesis 4 (H4).

H4 Objective social integration and subjective social integration play a chain mediating role in the effect of social capital on the utilization of public health services.

H4 Objective social integration and subjective social integration play a chain mediating role in the effect of social capital on the utilization of public health services.

Influence of the difference in migrant reasons among older migrant population

The main reasons for elderly migration in China include relocating to be with family members, seeking employment, caring for their own children, and retiring in a different place [34]. This study classifies these motivations into three groups: employment-related, family -related, and other reasons. The classification of migrants as employment-driven, family-driven, or moving for other reasons (e.g., retirement, health) is standard in migration research. It captures distinct life contexts: employment-driven migrants often prioritize economic integration; family-driven migrants typically have strong familial ties but may lack initial community networks; and those moving for other reasons face unique adaptive challenges. While migration motives are often complex, this high-level grouping allows for examining systematic differences in how psychosocial factors affect service use across migrant types, which is essential for developing targeted policies. Some scholars have observed that health management and service behaviors of older migrants are impacted by the reasons behind their migration, while social integration plays a beneficial role in their usage of public health services [39]. In China, older migrants, driven by family-related reasons, may rely more on the social support provided by their families and kinship, leading to increased utilization of public health services, such as the establishment of health records [43]. Therefore, undertaking a comprehensive examination of migrant factors is crucial for gaining insights into the patterns and requirements of healthcare service utilization among older migrants. In summary, we propose hypothesis 5 (H5).

H5 Social capital is linked to the utilization of public health services through variations in objective and subjective social integration, which may be influenced by migrant reasons.

H5 Social capital is linked to the utilization of public health services through variations in objective and subjective social integration, which may be influenced by migrant reasons.

To address these gaps, this study aims to construct and test an integrated model linking social capital, objective social integration, subjective social integration, and the utilization of public health services. Using structural equation modeling, we will empirically examine the hypothesized chain-mediation pathway (see Fig. 1). Additionally, a multi-group analysis will be conducted to test if the proposed pathways are moderated by migration reasons. This approach seeks to provide a more nuanced understanding of the complex psychosocial processes that influence healthcare access for this vulnerable and growing population.

Methods

Data source

The data used in this study was obtained from the 2017 China Migrants Dynamic Survey (CMDS). This extensive nationwide sampling survey, conducted by the National Health Commission of China, encompassed data collected from 31 provinces, autonomous regions, centrally-administered municipalities, and the Xinjiang Production and Construction Corps. The survey specifically targets individuals who have resided within the migrant community for at least one month, along with the non-district registered population aged 15 years and older. Various aspects of the migrant population are examined, including their family members, income, employment, migration intentions, health and public services, as well as social integration. For the purposes of this study, we focused on older migrants. After excluding individuals below the age of 60, those with less than six months of migrant living experience, and those with incomplete data, we obtained a final sample size of 5729 valid participants.

Measures

Dependent variable

The measurement of the utilization of public health services among older migrants in this study focused on their awareness of these services, their acceptance of health education, and the establishment of health records. These indicators are commonly used in research based on CMDS to capture engagement with basic public health services [44, 45]. To assess their awareness of public health services, participants were asked a specific question: “Are you familiar with the National Essential Public Health Services Program?” The availability of health education was evaluated through a set of five questionnaire items. These items inquired about the participants’ exposure to health education on various topics. Additionally, the presence of a health record was determined by asking participants whether they possessed a resident health record issued by the local government. All responses were recorded on a two-point scale, where 1 indicated “no” and 2 indicated “yes”. The reliability of the measurements was evaluated using Cronbach’s alpha coefficient, resulting in a score of 0.845 in this study.

Independent variable

Social capital refers to the resources and advantages obtained through connections with individuals or groups [46]. The social capital of older migrants was assessed using a specific question in the questionnaire provided by Li et al. [47]. The question was, “During your leisure time, who do you primarily spend time with (excluding customers and other relatives)?” The available response options ranged from “1 = a small number of individuals” to “4 = individuals from the local community.” The response choices consisted of “1 = a few people,” “2 = fellow residents of the same town,” “3 = people from other areas,” and “4 = individuals from the local community.”

Mediating variable

In our study, we evaluated the social integration of this population based on Li Yutong’s classification of social integration [34], which encompassed both objective and subjective dimensions. Objective social integration was assessed by examining the involvement of older migrants in social, group, and political activities. The objective dimension of social integration consists of nine specific inquiries, which are answered using 1 = no and 2 = yes. Subjective social integration was determined by assessing the integration intention, local acceptance, and self-identity of older migrants. The subjective dimension of social integration consists of three specific questions. The response choices range from 1, indicating strong agreement, to 4, indicating strong disagreement. The Cronbach’s alpha coefficient was 0.647 for objective social integration, and 0.777 for subjective social integration. Although 0.70 is a commonly used rule of thumb, in exploratory social science research, especially for constructs with a small number of items, values above 0.60 are considered acceptable [48].

Covariates

In our research, we included the following variables: sex (1 = male, 2 = female), age group (1 = = 60–65 years old, 2 = = 66–70 years old, 3 = = more than 70 years old), educational attainment (1 = primary school and below, 2 = junior high school, 3 = high school and above), residential status (1 = agricultural, 2 = non-agricultural), marital status (1 = no spouse, 2 = spouse), migration scope (1 = cross-province, 2 = intra-province cross-city, 3 = intra-city cross-county), duration of migration (1 = less than 5 years, 2 = 5–9 years, 3 = 10–14 years, 4 = 15 years or more), and reasons for migration (1 = work reasons, 2 = family reasons, 3 = other reasons).

Date analysis

Data screening for this study was achieved using SPSS 27.0. Socio-demographic and migrant characteristics of the participants were examined through descriptive statistics. Continuous variables were analyzed using mean and standard deviation, while categorical variables were assessed through frequencies and percentage. Additionally, Pearson bivariate correlation was conducted to assess the relationships between the variables. The establishment and analysis of measurement and structural models between the variables were carried out using Amos 24.0. Various fit indices were used to evaluate the model’s fit to the data, including the comparative fit index (CFI), Tuck-Lewis index (TLI), root-mean-square error of approximation (RMSEA), and standardized residual root-mean-square (SRMR). The model fit was considered when CFI and TLI values above 0.90, and RMSEA and SRMR values below 0.08 [49]. Furthermore, bootstrapping with 2,000 resamples was performed to test the mediation effect, with significance determined by a 95% confidence interval (CI) that did not include 0. Finally, we conducted multi-group analyses in structural equation modeling (SEM) to investigate potential variations in path coefficients resulting from migrant reasons. The test was conducted with a significance level of 0.05.

Results

Common method bias test

When using the self-report method to collect data, it is important to consider the issue of common method bias. The Harman single-factor test was used to perform the common method bias test. The results showed the existence of six main components, each with eigenvalues greater than 1. Furthermore, the first principal component accounted for only 17.13% of the variance, which fell below the critical threshold of 40%. Therefore, this study did not experience any significant common method bias.

A total of 5729 older migrants were included in the sample, with males constituting 58% of the participants. Most of older migrants fell within the age range of 60–65, accounting for 59% of the sample. The primary education level was completed by most participants, representing 48% of the total. Furthermore, 57% of the participants originated from rural areas. A large proportion of older migrants were married, making up 84% of the sample. In terms of migratory patterns, 44% engaged in inter-provincial migration, and a significant majority had been mobile for over 5 years, accounting for 63%. The main reason for migration was family-related matters, as reported by 40% of the participants (see Table 1). These findings indicate a noteworthy relationship among the key variables studied. This correlation fulfills the prerequisite for conducting a mediation effect test, thus requiring further analysis.

To evaluate the model’s fit, confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) was conducted. The measurement model demonstrated a strong fit, as indicated by the following fit indices: CFI = 0.948, TLI = 0.938, SRMR = 0.055, and RMSEA = 0.025. To enhance the model fit, factors with a load coefficient lower than 0.4 were removed [49]. After these adjustments, the fitting indicators based on the CFA results were as follows: CFI = 0.956, TLI = 0.945, SRMR = 0.041, and RMSEA = 0.028. Importantly, all factor loadings for the latent variables were highly significant at the p < 0.001 level, ranging from 0.502 to 0.874. The data indicated that the baseline structural model had an acceptable fit (see Table 3). Previous studies have highlighted that the chi-square value of SEM can be influenced by large sample data [50]. Therefore, this study does not explore chi-square fit statistic/degree of freedom (CMIN/DF), as it is often affected by large sample sizes. Apart from this index, the overall fit indices met the desired criteria, with TLI (0.920) and CFI (0.947) surpassing the threshold of 0.90, and RMSEA (0.039) and SRMR (0.037) falling below 0.05.

Another pathway, “Social capital → Subjective social integration → Utilization of public health services,” demonstrated a mediating effect of 0.014, contributing to 10.07% of the total effect. Lastly, the pathway “Social capital → Objective social integration → Subjective social integration → Utilization of public health services” exhibited a mediating effect size of 0.004, accounting for 2.88% of the total effect. All paths were statistically significant, as the model tests aligned with CIs that excluded 0, indicating a significant relationship between the variables.

Discussion

Effect of social capital on utilization of public health services

This finding was consistent with previous research which demonstrated that migrants with higher social capital were more likely to establish health records and receive health education information [51]. It has been suggested that access to health services serves as a mechanism through which social capital affects health [52]. Social capital could influence the utilization of public health services by facilitating the exchange of information between community members and the shaping of health behaviors and perceptions [12]. Additionally, social capital could provide adequate social support for older migrants to adopt positive health behaviors, such as establishing health records and undergoing physical examinations [53]. To achieve the goal of equal access to public health services for older migrants, the government needs to consistently optimize its healthcare policies and effectively leverage community platforms to enhance the social capital of migrant older individuals. For example, creating older-friendly jobs in society can encourage older adults to re-enter the workforce, constantly expose them to new experiences, and enhance their social adaptability. Establishing public service positions within communities can promote mutual support among elderly residents and achieve the goal of mutual aid in aging. Organizing cultural and interest-based training activities in communities and actively encouraging participation from both older residents and children can tap into the wisdom of older adults and increase their social capital [54].

Mediating effect of objective social integration

This result was consistent with previous research that has demonstrated a positive correlation between social capital scores and community involvement, engagement in health education, acceptance of public health emergencies, and adherence to lifestyle guidance [55]. Social capital had the potential to strengthen interpersonal relationships, encourage community engagement and ultimately facilitate objective social integration. Research has shown that migrants who actively participate in labor unions, organizational activities, and local community government affairs are more likely to use basic public health services [56]. Therefore, it is crucial for relevant departments to establish appropriate and consistent opportunities for elderly migrants to participate in activities, enhance the objective social integration of this population through initiatives such as developing social networks and sharing resources, and ultimately improving their access to public health services [4]. Policies should prioritize initiatives that enhance objective social integration among elderly migrants, encouraging their active engagement in public health services.

Mediating effect of subjective social integration

This conclusion was consistent with previous investigations, which has shown that social capital could provide and reinforce psychosocial support, thereby promoting beneficial health behaviors and encouraging the use of health services [57]. Elderly migrants with a higher subjective sense of social integration were more inclined to integrate into the local community and build trust with local residents and public health service providers. This psychological integration reduced the loneliness of elderly migrants, which in turn increases their utilization of public health services. A study on the utilization of public health services by the migrant population revealed that individuals who paid attention to changes in their city, held positive opinions of local residents, and identified themselves as part of the local community had a high utilization of basic public health services [56]. In addition, the social support network, health knowledge, and self-efficacy of older migrants also had an impact on their subjective social integration, which in turn encouraged them to access health services [58]. Therefore, policymakers and healthcare providers can enhance the subjective social integration of older migrants through a variety of measures, such as organizing health information sessions, community cultural festivals, etc.

Chain mediating effect of objective social integration and subjective social integration

Social capital played a vital role in improving the health of older migrants by utilizing social networks to address their health-related needs [59]. According to social capital theory, the community played a vital role in enabling older migrants to build and enhance their social capital [60]. The formation of social capital necessitated communities to translate the interactive needs of residents into actual interactive behaviors [61]. Therefore, utilizing the services and capabilities provided by the community could help enhance the objective social integration of older migrants, and reduced barriers to their integration into society [4]. On the other hand, subjective integration enhanced their inclination and motivation to use such services by fostering a sense of belonging and trust within the community and society [12, 42]. As the older migrant population engaged with the community and institutions providing health services, they began to develop a sense of identity and connection to the community, ultimately leading to an increased level of subjective social integration. The interaction between subjective and objective social integration resulted in the formation of a comprehensive social integration [28]. Therefore, when analyzing the impact of social capital on the use of public health services, it is vital to take into account the consecutive mediating influence of objective social integration and subjective social integration.

Multi-group analyses by migrant reasons

Valuable findings were obtained from the multi-group analyses on migration reasons, indicating that the influence of social capital on the utilization of public health services varies depending on the reasons for migration. For older adults who migrated due to employment reasons, improving public health services posed a challenge due to the limited influence of social capital. This may reflect that their social ties are often work-centered, requiring translation into broader community engagement and local belonging to facilitate health access. Policies should prioritize increasing the level of objective and subjective social integration of this group, which in turn indirectly increased their use of public health services. For older adults who migrated due to family reasons, the pathways to improving the utilization of public health services were more diverse. Embedded in family networks, they may gain immediate health information and support, while external social engagement further strengthens integration and service use. The government should focus on three aspects: social capital, objective social integration, and subjective social integration, as they directly impact the utilization rate of public health services. For older adults who migrated due to retirement and other reasons, the impact of objective social integration on the utilization of public health services became more apparent. Lacking ready-made work or family anchors, structured community involvement serves as a critical first step for building local networks and identity. Policies should prioritize the level of objective social integration within this group.

Limitations

Several study limitations should be noted. Firstly, due to the cross-sectional nature of the data, the identified associations should be interpreted as proposed theoretical relationships rather than established causal effects. Secondly, there might be other variables beyond social integration that mediated the relationship between social capital and health service utilization among older migrants. Thirdly, a considerable portion of our data relied on self-reporting, which introduced the possibility of biases in terms of social expectations and potential underestimation or overestimation of our reported outcomes. Fourthly, this study utilizes data from 2017, which was the latest available at the time of analysis. Future research with more recent data is needed to verify the robustness of the identified pathways and to update the estimates within the current policy context. Lastly, the study only considered inter-group disparities based on reasons for migration.

Conclusions

The study findings suggested that social capital was positively associated with the utilization of public health services. Objective and subjective social integration were crucial factors in this process. Additionally, the research highlighted that the impact of social capital on the utilization of public health services varied based on migration motivations. Despite the limitations of the study, the results provide valuable insights for policymakers to enhance equal access to basic public health services and overall well-being among older migrant population within the context of an aging society.
