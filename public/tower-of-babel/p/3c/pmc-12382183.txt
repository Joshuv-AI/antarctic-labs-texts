Conditional immortalization of mesenchymal stem cells and their extracellular vesicles therapy for interstitial cystitis/bladder pain syndrome
Authors: Guolong Liao, Canling Long, Rui Guo, Sushan Wang, Ruijuan Han, Jianhua Yang, Jia Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundInterstitial cystitis/bladder pain syndrome (IC/BPS) is a chronic condition characterized by debilitating pelvic pain. Mesenchymal stem cell-derived extracellular vesicles (MSC-EVs) are recognized as pivotal mediators of MSCs’ paracrine activity and represent a novel therapeutic approach for IC/BPS. However, their efficacy is hindered by the inherent variability of primary MSCs (pMSCs) from different donors and their susceptibility to senescence during culture expansion.MethodsTo overcome these challenges, we developed conditionally immortalized mesenchymal stem cells (iMSCs) using a doxycycline-regulated simian virus 40 Large T expression system in pMSCs. The conditioned proliferation capacity of iMSCs was evaluated using cell counting and immunocytochemistry. The expression of surface markers on iMSCs was assessed by flow cytometry. The osteogenic and adipogenic differentiation capabilities of iMSCs at different population doubling (PD) numbers were analyzed by qPCR, alizarin red staining, and oil red O staining. The EVs secreted by iMSCs were characterized using Western blot, scanning electron microscopy, and particle size analysis. In vitro and in vivo bladder inflammation models were used to evaluate the therapeutic effects of EVs on IC/BPS.ResultsThese iMSCs exhibited precisely controlled proliferation, maintained surface marker expression and differentiation capacities, comparable to pMSCs up to PD 40. The characteristics of iMSC-EVs are equivalent to those of pMSCs. Furthermore, in vitro cellular experiments demonstrate that iMSC-EVs provide protective effects against LPS/ATP-induced damage in SV-HUC-1 cells. Additionally, administration of iMSC-EVs significantly enhanced tissue healing and anti-inflammatory capabilities in an IC/BPS animal model.ConclusionsIn summary, this approach produced a reliable source of functional MSCs and EVs, with iMSC-EVs demonstrating robust immunomodulatory properties and promoting tissue healing in IC/BPS. This method represents a promising alternative to pMSCs for IC/BPS therapy.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13287-025-04615-9.Keywords: MSCs, Conditional immortalization, EVs, IC/BPS, Anti-inflammatory

Introduction

Interstitial cystitis/bladder pain syndrome (IC/BPS) is a chronic inflammatory disorder that predominantly affecting females, characterized by symptoms of urinary frequency, urgency, and suprapubic pain [1–5]. Despite its high prevalence, the etiology of IC/BPS remains elusive, and effective therapeutic options are limited [2, 6, 7]. The prominent role of bladder inflammation in IC/BPS underscores an urgent need for innovative therapeutic approaches [8–11].

Mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) possess strong anti-inflammatory and immunomodulatory properties [12, 13], making MSC-based therapies a promising avenue for treating inflammatory conditions [14, 15]. Extracellular vesicles (EVs) derived from MSCs (MSC-EVs) are essential mediators of MSCs’ paracrine effects, contributing significantly to their immunomodulatory and regenerative potential [16, 17]. These EVs are nanoscale, membrane-bound vesicles ranging from 30 to 150 nanometers, offering a cell-free therapeutic approach that minimizes risks associated with immune responses, tumorigenicity, and vascular occlusion posed by MSCs themselves [18–21]. Consequently, MSC-EVs are emerging as a potential therapeutic modality for IC/BPS [22].

However, clinical application of MSC-EVs faces several obstacles, including limited availability of primary mesenchymal stem cells (pMSCs), finite in vitro proliferation, donor heterogeneity, ethical concerns surrounding human tissue sourcing, and cost-effectiveness challenges [23–26]. These limitations restrict the yield and quality of EVs, impeding both research and therapeutic development of MSC-EVs.

The immortalization of MSCs offers a solution to these limitations, providing a robust tool for MSC biology and therapeutic exploration. The simian virus SV40 Large T antigen (LT) is widely used for immortalizing primary cells, as LT extends cellular lifespan by inhibiting the cell cycle regulators p53 and Rb [27, 28]. Leveraging this approach, we engineered a lentiviral vector (LV) system based on the Tet-on mechanism to enable controlled expression of LT, thereby achieving conditional immortalization of pMSCs [29, 30]. This system allows LT expression to be toggled with doxycycline (dox) [31, 32].

In this study, we utilized the aforementioned conditional immortalization technique to generate an immortalized MSC line, designated as iMSCs. In the presence of dox, iMSCs exhibited rapid proliferation, demonstrating a population doubling (PD) time of 32 h, which gradually ceased upon dox withdrawal. When cultured without dox, iMSCs displayed characteristics resembling those of pMSCs, including comparable cell marker expression and capabilities for osteogenic and lipogenic differentiation. We then derived EVs from iMSCs (iMSC-EVs) and compared their properties to those of EVs from pMSCs (pMSC-EVs). We further assessed the therapeutic effects of iMSC-EVs in both in vitro and in vivo models of IC/BPS. Our findings demonstrate that iMSC-EVs exhibit potent anti-inflammatory effects and confer protective benefits in IC/BPS.

Materials and methods

Plasmid construction and lentiviral production

The dox-inducible system, pLVET-tTR-KRAB [32], was procured from Addgene (Cambridge, MA, USA; plasmid number: 116444). The LT fragment was synthesized based on gene sequences retrieved from GenBank, and a recombinant lentiviral (LV) shuttle plasmid was designed accordingly [33]. Specifically, the EEF1A1 promoter from the human gene and the Aequorea victoria enhanced green fluorescent protein (eGFP) coding sequence within pLVET-tTR-KRAB were replaced with the cytomegalovirus (CMV) promoter and the LT gene, respectively. Both the plasmid construction and LV production were conducted by VectorBuilder (Guangzhou, China).

Cell culture and immortalizarion of MSCs

MSCs derived from human bone marrow (Homo sapiens) were generously supplied by Dr. Xiaohong Li of Guangdong Provincial People’s Hospital, who acquired them from Cyagen Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Product No. HUXMA-01001, Suzhou, China). The MSCs were cultured in alpha-modified Eagle’s medium (αMEM, Gibco, Grand Island, NY, USA), supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco, Grand Island, NY, USA) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (Gibco, Grand Island, NY, USA). The cultures were maintained at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO₂. The culture medium was refreshed every three days.

For MSC immortalization, pMSCs were transduced with LV encapsulating the dox-inducible LT expression system. The following day, the medium was replaced with proliferation medium supplemented with 100 ng/mL dox (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) to initiate LT expression. Cells were subsequently maintained in fresh proliferation medium containing dox, replenished every other day. Once cells reached approximately 90% confluence, they were split at a ratio of 1:8.

Growth curve analysis

The PD value quantifies the cumulative replicative potential of cell populations through successive mitotic cycles. The PD value was calculated using the following formula: PD = log2 (Nf/Ni), where Ni is the initial number of cells seeded, and Nf is the final number of cells at the time of passage. Cells were seeded in 100-mm dishes, each containing 10 mL of medium. Specifically, pMSCs were initially plated at a density of 5 × 105 cells per dish. Passaging of pMSCs was performed every 3–4 days, once the cells reached 90% confluence, which corresponded to approximately 2 × 106 cells per dish. iMSCs were seeded in 100-mm dishes at an initial density of 2.5 × 105 cells per dish. Similar to pMSCs, iMSCs were passaged every 4 days when they reached 90% confluency, with a cell count of approximately 2 × 106 cells per dish. In comparison, pMSCs and iMSCs were sub-cultured at splitting ratios of 1:4 and 1:8, respectively. These splitting ratios corresponded to 2 and 3 PDs per passage for pMSCs and iMSCs, respectively. The sub-culturing process for pMSCs continued until their proliferation halted, while iMSCs underwent continuous sub-culturing beyond a cumulative PD level of PD60. The proliferation rate of iMSCs and their dependency on LT expression were assessed by culturing cells at low densities in media with or without 100 ng/mL dox. Cells were harvested and counted at designated intervals using a cytometer (BodBoge Biotechnology, Guangzhou, China).

Immunofluorescence

Immunofluorescent staining of cultured cells was performed as previously described [33]. Cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, permeabilized with 0.25% Triton X-100 in PBS for 15 min, and blocked with 10% goat serum for 1 h at 37 °C. Cells were then incubated with a mouse anti-LT antibody at a 1:200 dilution in PBS. Alexa 488-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG (H + L) (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) was used as a secondary antibody. Nuclei were stained with Hoechst 33342 (10 µg/mL; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), and images were captured using a Leica STELLARIS 5 confocal laser-scanning microscope (Leica, Wetzlar, Germany).

Western blot analysis

Western blot was conducted following a previously described protocol [34]. Samples were harvested, and proteins were extracted using RIPA lysis buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) containing protease and phosphatase inhibitors (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA). Protein concentration was measured with the Pierce BCA Protein Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA).

Extracted proteins were loaded onto SDS-PAGE gels, separated by electrophoresis, and transferred to PVDF membranes (Millipore, Burlington, MA, USA). Membranes were blocked with TBST containing 5% skim milk and probed overnight at 4 °C with the following primary antibodies: anti-LT (1:2000, Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Dallas, TX, USA), anti-TSG101 (1:2000, Abcam, Cambridge, UK), anti-CD81 (1:2000, Abcam, Cambridge, UK), anti-Calnexin (1:2000, Abcam, Cambridge, UK), anti-IL-1β (1:1000, Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA, USA), anti-TNF-α (1:1000, Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA, USA), and anti-GAPDH (1:5000, Proteintech, Wuhan, China). The following day, membranes were incubated with an HRP-conjugated secondary antibody (1:5000, Proteintech, Wuhan, China) for 1 h at room temperature. Protein bands were visualized using the Thermo iBright CL1000 imaging system (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) with Rapid-Step™ ECL reagent (Millipore, Burlington, MA, USA). Quantification of band intensities was performed using ImageJ (version 1.46r, National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD), with data from at least three independent experiments.

Flow cytometry

Flow cytometric analysis was performed on pMSCs and iMSCs cultured with and without dox at PD20 (referred to as iMSC + dox and iMSC, respectively). Cells were harvested with trypsin, centrifuged at 1,000 rpm for 5 min, and washed with PBS containing 0.5% bovine serum albumin (BSA; Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) and 0.5 M EDTA (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA). Cell numbers were quantified using a cytometer. For each sample, 1 × 106 cells were incubated with fluorochrome-conjugated anti-human monoclonal antibodies for 30 min at room temperature. The following CD surface markers were analyzed: CD90/APC, CD14/PE, CD45/PE, CD73/FITC, CD105/FITC, and CD34/FITC (BD Biosciences, San Jose, CA, USA). A minimum of 10,000 events per marker were recorded using an FACSCanto II flow cytometer (BD Biosciences, San Jose, CA, USA), and data were analyzed with FlowJo software (BD Biosciences, Ashland, OR, USA).

Osteogenic and adipogenic differentiation of MSCs

To induce osteogenic differentiation, both pMSCs and iMSCs at different PDs (PD20, PD40, and PD60) were seeded into 6-well or 48-well plates. iMSCs were either treated with dox (iMSC + dox at PD20) or untreated. Following cell attachment in αMEM, the medium was replaced with an osteogenic induction medium containing 10% FBS, 50 µg/mL ascorbate, 10 µmol/L β-glycerophosphate, 0.1 µmol/L dexamethasone, and 10 µmol/L glutamine (Cyagen, Suzhou, China). The medium was refreshed every two days for three weeks.

To evaluate the osteogenic differentiation, alkaline phosphatase (ALP) staining and Alizarin red staining (ARS) were conducted on the 7th and 14th days after induction. Cells cultured under osteogenic conditions in 48-well plates were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 10 min. The BCIP/NBT ALP Color Development Kit (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) was used to visualize ALP activity. Meanwhile, the ARS staining solution (Cyagen, Suzhou, China) was applied to detect mineralized nodules. Subsequently, the stained cells were imaged using light microscopy (Zeiss, Oberkochen, Germany).

For adipogenic differentiation, pMSCs and iMSCs (PD20, PD40, and PD60), including iMSCs treated with dox (iMSC + dox at PD20), were seeded in 6-well or 48-well plates and cultured in αMEM until cell adhesion. The medium was then replaced with the Adipogenesis Differentiation Kit (Cyagen, Suzhou, China) and refreshed every 3–4 days for two weeks.

To evaluate adipogenic differentiation, cells post-lipid induction were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 10 min and stained with Oil Red O solution (Cyagen, Suzhou, China) for 30 min. After staining, plates were thoroughly washed with PBS, and stained cells were imaged using an light microscope (Zeiss, Oberkochen, Germany).

Quantitative analyses of ALP activity and Oil Red O staining were performed with ImageJ (version 1.46r, National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD).

RNA isolation and reverse transcription-quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR)

RT-qPCR was performed as per established protocols [33]. Total RNA was extracted from cells using the AG RNAex Pro Reagent (Accurate Biotechnology, Changsha, China) following the manufacturer’s instructions. The extracted RNA was reverse transcribed into complementary DNA (cDNA) using the Evo M-MLV RT Premix for qPCR (Accurate Biotechnology, Changsha, China). The purified EV pellets (1 × 10¹⁰ particles/sample) were lysed in TRIzol LS reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) at room temperature for 5 min to isolate RNA. Subsequently, chloroform (Aladdin, Shanghai, China) was added to the lysate. The mixture was then vigorously vortexed and incubated for 3 min. Phase separation was performed by centrifugation at 12,000 g for 15 min at 4 °C. After that, the RNA-containing aqueous layer was transferred to an RNase-free tube. To precipitate the RNA, the aqueous phase was mixed with an equal volume of ice-cold isopropanol (Aladdin, Shanghai, China), and the mixture was incubation at -20 °C for 1 h. The RNA pellet was then collected through centrifugation (12,000 g, 10 min, 4 °C). Next, the pellet was washed twice with 75% ethanol (Aladdin, Shanghai, China), air-dried, and resuspended in 30 µL RNase-free water (Accurate Biotechnology, Changsha, China). Finally, RNA concentration was measured using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). cDNA synthesis was performed with the miRNA 1st strand cDNA synthesis kit (Accurate Biotechnology, Changsha, China), according to the manufacturer’s guidelines. Expression of target genes and miRNAs was quantified by real-time qPCR with the SYBR® Green Premix Pro Taq HS qPCR Kit (Accurate Biotechnology, Changsha, China) using specific primers listed in Supplementary Table 1. GAPDH and U6 were used as internal controls for mRNA and miRNA, respectively. PCR reactions were run on a QuantStudio 3 Real-Time PCR System (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), and expression levels were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCT method.

Isolation and characterization of EVs

First, FBS was subjected to ultracentrifugation at 100,000 g for 18 h at 4 °C, and the supernatant was harvested to yield EV-depleted FBS. MSCs were subsequently cultured in αMEM supplemented with 10% EV-depleted FBS at 37 °C for 48 h.

EVs were isolated from batches consisting of 15 culture dishes, each having a diameter of 100 mm. Within each dish, 2 × 10⁶ cells were cultivated in 10 mL of medium, which culminated in a cumulative volume of 150 mL of cell-culture medium. Approximately 2 × 109 EVs were obtained from each isolation batch. When harvesting the medium for EV isolation, the PD value of pMSCs ranged from 6 to 10. For iMSCs, the EVs used in characterization and functional experiments were obtained from cells at a PD of approximately 20 (ranging from PD18 to PD22), and these EVs were designated as “iMSC-EV”. In experiments comparing the EV yield and molecular content consistency of iMSCs at different PD values, EVs were collected from cells at three distinct PD ranges: PD20 (PD18-PD22), PD40 (PD38-PD42), and PD60 (PD58-PD60).

After the 48-hour incubation period, the culture medium were collected, and cell supernatants were sequentially centrifuged at 300 g for 10 min and 2000 g for 15 min to remove residual cells. To eliminate cell debris, an additional centrifugation step at 12,000 g for 30 min was performed. Supernatants were subsequently filtered through a 0.22 μm filter (Millipore, Burlington, MA, USA) to exclude particles exceeding 200 nm in size. The filtrate was then subjected to ultracentrifugation at 100,000 g for 2 h using a Beckman Optima XPN-100 centrifuge (Beckman Coulter, Brea, CA, USA). After centrifugation, the supernatant was carefully aspirated to avoid disturbing the pelleted EVs. The pellet was gently resuspended in PBS to remove impurities potentially adsorbed on the EV surface. The resuspended solution was then subjected to a second ultracentrifugation at 100,000 g for an additional 2 h. The final pellets were resuspended in 100 µL of PBS buffer and stored at -80 °C for later analysis. All centrifugation steps were performed at 4 °C to maintain EV integrity.

For structural examination, transmission electron microscopy (TEM) was employed. Purified EVs were diluted 1:10 in PBS and adsorbed onto carbon-coated copper grids (300 mesh) for 10 min at room temperature. Excess liquid was removed using filter paper, and samples were negatively stained with 2% uranyl acetate for 1 min. After air-drying, grids were imaged under a transmission electron microscope (HT7800, Hitachi, Tokyo, Japan) operated at 80 kV.

The size distribution of EVs was determined using a Nanoparticle Characterization System (NanoSight, Malvern Panalytical, Malvern, UK). Additionally, the concentration of EVs was measured using the Pierce BCA Protein Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). EV marker expression, including TSG101 and CD81, was confirmed by Western blot, and miRNAs extracted from EVs were analyzed by RT-qPCR.

ELISA assay

ELISA kits (Elabscience, Wuhan, China) were used according to the manufacturer’s protocols to quantify levels of IL-1β, TNF-α, IL-10, PD-L1, TGF-β1, NAG, and PGE2. Briefly, cell culture supernatants or urine samples were added to pre-coated wells with specific antibodies and incubated at 37 °C for 1 h and 30 min. Subsequently, a biotinylated antibody (1×) and streptavidin-biotin-peroxidase conjugate (1×) were added sequentially. After incubation with a chromogenic substrate for 30 min, a stop solution was added to terminate the reaction. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a multifunctional microplate reader (BioTek, Santa Clara, CA, USA).

CCK-8 assay

The CCK-8 assay is a common method for assessing cell viability and proliferation. It relies on the reduction of a water-soluble tetrazolium salt by dehydrogenases in living cells to form a water-soluble formazan dye, and cell viability is quantitatively evaluated by measuring the absorbance of the formazan solution at 450 nm. According to the manufacturer’s instructions, cells were seeded at 8,000 cells per well in a 96-well plate with 100 µL of culture medium per well. After the designated treatment period, 10 µL of CCK-8 reagent (Sigma, St. Louis, MO, USA) was added to each well. Plates were incubated in the dark for 1.5 h, after which absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader (BioTek, Santa Clara, CA, USA).

Culture and treatment of SV-HUC-1 cells

SV-HUC-1 cells were obtained from Wuhan Pricella Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Pricella, Wuhan, China) and cultured in Ham’s F-12 K medium (Gibco, Grand Island, NY, USA) supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (Gibco, Grand Island, NY, USA). Cells were maintained at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO₂.

To establish an in vitro injury model, SV-HUC-1 cells were treated with 10 µg/mL lipopolysaccharide (LPS) and 2.5 mM adenosine triphosphate (ATP) for 12 h. EVs at a concentration of 1 × 10⁹ particles/mL were added to assess their protective effects on cell viability. EVs were added in combination with LPS and ATP, followed by incubation for 12 h.

Cellular uptake assessment of EVs

EVs were labeled with 2 µg/mL DiR, a lipophilic near-infrared indocarbocyanine iodide dye (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA), at room temperature for 1 h. To remove unbound dye, the labeled EVs underwent ultracentrifugation at 100,000 g for 18 h. After centrifugation, the concentration of EVs was measured using NanoSight (Malvern Panalytical, Malvern, UK). This measurement was essential for standardizing the number of EVs used in the subsequent cellular uptake experiments. Specifically, EVs were prepared at a concentration of 1 × 10⁹ particles/mL in serum-free OptiMEM (Gibco, Grand Island, NY, USA). One day before the experiment, approximately 1 × 105 SV-HUC-1 cells per well were seeded into a 6-well plate. Subsequently, 2 × 10⁹ DiR-labeled EVs per well were incubated with the SV-HUC-1 cells at 37 °C for 6 h. After incubation, cells were washed with PBS, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde at room temperature for 15 min, and stained with Hoechst 33342 (10 µg/mL; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) for nuclear visualization. Samples were mounted using VECTASHIELD® Antifade Mountant (Vector Laboratories, Burlingame, CA, USA), and fluorescence images were captured using the Leica STELLARIS 5 confocal laser-scanning microscope (Leica, Wetzlar, Germany).

Animal experiment

All experimental procedures were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Longgang District People’s Hospital of Shenzhen (Approval No.: IACUC: KU19164). Female C57BL/6 mice (Mus musculus, 10 weeks old, body weight 24.6 ± 0.4 g) were purchased from the Guangdong Medical Laboratory Animal Center. The mice were housed in a pathogen-free environment under controlled conditions (24 °C, 12-hour light/dark cycle, 50% humidity) with free access to food and water. The animals were anesthetized via inhalation of isoflurane (95% oxygen and 5% isoflurane) provided by RWD Life Science Co., Shenzhen, China. At the end of the experiment, animals were euthanized through deep anesthesia with 5% isoflurane, followed by cervical dislocation while anesthetized. All animals were euthanized simultaneously to avoid the effects of circadian rhythm variations.

Mice were randomly assigned to four groups using a computer-generated random sequence: (1) Sham (sham-operated control), (2) IC/BPS, (3) IC/BPS + pMSC-EV, and (4) IC/BPS + iMSC-EV, with 10 mice per group. To induce an IC/BPS model, mice received intraperitoneal injections of 80 mg/kg cyclophosphamide (CYP, MedChemExpress, Monmouth Junction, NJ, USA) every two days for a total of four doses as previously described [35]. The sham group received PBS injections as a control. One week after the final CYP injection, mice underwent surgery to expose the bladder under sterile conditions. A 26-gauge Hamilton syringe (Hamilton, Bonaduz, Switzerland) was used to locally administer 5 × 10⁸ particles of pMSC-EVs or iMSC-EVs in 10 µL of 1×PBS into the bladder wall [22]. The Sham and IC/BPS group received an equivalent volume of PBS as a control. One week post-EV injection, urine samples were collected for ELISA analysis. Subsequently, the animals were euthanized, and bladders were collected for RNA and protein expression analysis and histological examination.

If animals die prematurely, they are excluded from the study to prevent the collection of molecular biology and histological data. All experimental groups were analyzed without the exclusion of any animals or data points. A double-blind experimental design is implemented to ensure the objectivity and reliability of results, mitigating researcher bias. The primary researcher manages experimental grouping, disease modeling, and treatment, while the secondary researcher, unaware of the grouping, conducts molecular biology and histological experiment, data analysis, and outcome assessment. Upon receiving the secondary researcher’s analysis report, the primary researcher integrates the grouping information to interpret results and draw conclusions. The work has been reported in line with the ARRIVE guidelines 2.0 [36].

Histopathological analysis

For histopathological evaluation, freshly excised bladders were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde and subsequently embedded in paraffin following standard histological protocols. Tissue sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E, Beyotime, Shanghai, China) for general tissue morphology and Masson’s trichrome (MT, Beyotime, Shanghai, China) for collagen fiber visualization. Stained sections were then examined under a light microscope (Zeiss, Oberkochen, Germany) to assess histopathological changes. Quantitative analyses of the percentage of fibrotic area from MT slides were performed using ImageJ (version 1.46r, National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD).

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism 8 software (GraphPad Software, San Diego, USA). The Shapiro-Wilk test was used to assess the normality of data distribution. For parametric comparisons between groups, Levene’s test was used to verify the homogeneity of variances. In parametric analyses, one-way ANOVA was applied to multiple-group comparisons, and Student’s t-test was used for two-group comparisons. Each experiment was independently replicated at least three times, and a P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Generation of conditionally immortalized human MSCs

To generate a conditionally immortalized human MSC line, pMSCs were transduced with a LV containing a dox-inducible expression cassette for the LT at PD 4 (Fig. 1a). Immunostaining and Western blot analysis confirmed robust LT expression in the transduced MSCs cultured in the presence of dox, whereas LT expression was absent in the untransduced pMSCs (Fig. 1b, c, and Supplementary Fig. 1). Regarding proliferative capacity, pMSCs exhibited a marked decline in proliferative potential after approximately 10 PDs. In contrast, the transduced cells, now termed iMSCs, demonstrated sustained proliferation up to at least PD 60, with no apparent reduction in their proliferation rate (Fig. 1d), indicating successful immortalization.

To investigate the dynamics of dox-induced LT expression, iMSCs were cultured with or without dox for varying durations (2, 4, 6, and 8 days) (Fig. 1e). LT expression was analyzed by Western blot and immunostaining (Fig. 1f, g, and Supplementary Fig. 1). iMSCs cultured continuously with dox exhibited strong LT expression. However, upon dox withdrawal, LT levels decreased progressively, becoming undetectable by day 6 post-dox removal (Fig. 1f and g).

Additionally, the dox-regulated proliferation of iMSCs was assessed. iMSCs cultured with dox exhibited an average PD time of approximately 32 h. In contrast, upon dox removal, cell numbers showed an initial slight increase but then plateaued, indicating a loss of proliferative capacity following dox withdrawal (Fig. 1h). These results collectively confirm that the dox-inducible LT expression system facilitates controlled and sustained proliferation of iMSCs.

iMSC retains the main characteristics of pMSC

To evaluate whether iMSCs retain the hallmark characteristics of pMSCs, we examined the expression of MSC-specific markers in iMSCs cultured under proliferative conditions with dox (iMSC + dox) and compared them to iMSCs cultured without dox for 6 days (iMSC) and pMSCs (pMSC). Flow cytometric analysis revealed that iMSCs were positive for CD73, CD90, and CD105, while negative for CD14, CD34, and CD45 (Fig. 2a-c). The overall immunophenotype of iMSCs was similar to that of pMSCs, showing no significant differences (Fig. 2d). Notably, exposure to dox did not alter the expression of MSC markers in the iMSC + dox group compared to the iMSC and pMSC groups (Fig. 2a-d). These findings indicate that, in the absence of proliferative pressure, iMSCs exhibit a surface marker profile consistent with pMSCs, and the presence of dox does not negatively affect MSC marker expression in iMSC + dox.

To assess the differentiation potential of iMSCs, we carried out both osteogenic and adipogenic differentiation inductions. Upon osteogenic induction, iMSCs exhibited ALP activity comparable to that of pMSCs, but this was significantly lower in iMSC + dox cultures (Fig. 3a and b). Further analysis by RT-qPCR revealed that osteogenic marker expression in iMSCs was similar to that in pMSCs. In contrast, in the iMSC + dox cultures, the expression of these markers was notably diminished (Fig. 3c). After adipogenic induction, iMSCs were observed to accumulate lipid droplets in a manner similar to pMSCs, whereas iMSC + dox cultures showed a substantial decrease in lipid droplet formation (Fig. 3d and e). RT-qPCR analysis further revealed that expression of adipogenic markers was comparable between iMSCs and pMSCs, but it was significantly decreased in iMSC + dox cultures (Fig. 3f). These findings strongly suggest that once dox is removed, iMSCs display differentiation potential and gene expression patterns that closely mirror those of pMSCs. Conversely, the presence of dox exerts a significant inhibitory effect on the differentiation potential of iMSCs. It is particularly noteworthy that silencing the expression of the LT gene before initiating osteogenic and adipogenic differentiation is a crucial step for the successful differentiation of these iMSCs.

We conducted further investigations to determine whether the presence of dox in the culture medium has an impact on the differentiation of MSCs. Our analysis confirmed that dox exposure does not impair osteogenic or adipogenic differentiation capacity, eliminating concerns about its interference with MSC multipotency (Supplementary Fig. 2a and b).

To evaluate the differentiation potential of iMSCs during long-term proliferation, we induced osteogenic and adipogenic differentiation at PD levels of 20, 40, and 60. Remarkably, when cultured without dox, iMSCs at PD40 demonstrated osteogenic and adipogenic differentiation capacities that were comparable to those of pMSCs and iMSCs at PD20. This was clearly substantiated by ALP activity assays, Oil Red O staining, and the expression levels of relevant marker genes (Supplementary Fig. 3a-f). Nonetheless, upon reaching PD60, a notable decline in the differentiation capabilities of iMSCs was observed. Specifically, ALP activity and Oil Red O staining results dropped to approximately 30% and 50% of their initial levels, respectively. Moreover, the expression of marker genes in iMSCs at PD60 was significantly lower compared to that at PD40 (Supplementary Fig. 3a-f). In summary, these findings highlight that iMSCs can maintain their differentiation potential up to PD40, yet this potential gradually diminishes as the proliferation progresses further.

iMSC switches between immortalized and Non-immortalized state with persevered differentiation capacity

To explore whether iMSCs can switch between the immortalized and non-immortalized states by regulating LT expression while retaining their differentiation capabilities, the cells were cultured in media either containing or lacking dox for two cycles. When the cell cultures reached 90% confluency, the cells were sub-cultured and then exposed to osteogenic differentiation conditions (Fig. 4a). Six days after dox withdrawal, the cells became LT-negative, showing only weak Ki-67 expression (Fig. 4b, day 6). Re-adding dox for the subsequent 6 days triggered the re-emergence of LT expression in almost all cells. This was accompanied by intense Ki-67 expression and a notable increase in cell number (Fig. 4b, day 12). Due to overcrowding, the cells were passaged. The passaged cells retained their mesenchymal morphology and proliferated at a PD time comparable to that of iMSCs continuously cultured with dox. The cells were then deprived of dox for another 6 days (day 18) and subsequently exposed to osteogenic differentiation stimuli (Fig. 4c). This led to obvious osteogenesis, as demonstrated by elevated ALP activity and positive ARS staining (Fig. 4c). RT-qPCR analysis of osteogenic marker expression indicated that the re-immortalized MSCs still exhibited a highly efficient osteogenic differentiation capacity (Fig. 4d and e). In summary, these results imply that iMSCs can alternate between proliferative and non-proliferative states while maintaining their differentiation potential.

Identification and characterization of EVs secreted by iMSCs

Subsequently, we isolated and comprehensively compared the characteristics of EVs derived from iMSCs and pMSCs.

Initially, Western blot assays were employed. The signal of Calnexin, a marker for non-EV contaminants, was faint in the EV samples. This result clearly indicated that the EV preparations had minimal contamination from non-EV sources. Subsequently, we successfully confirmed the presence of EV-specific markers, such as TSG101 and CD81, in the EVs derived from iMSCs (Fig. 5a). Notably, the expression levels of these EV markers in iMSC-EVs were highly comparable to those in pMSC-EVs. Finally, NanoSight analysis was conducted, revealing that the majority of iMSC-EVs had a size range of 100–150 nm (Fig. 5b). This result was further corroborated by electron microscopy images, which clearly showed circular, bilayered membrane structures with a diameter of approximately 100 nm (Fig. 5c).

We then delved deeper into comparing the molecular content of EVs from iMSCs and pMSCs. RT-qPCR analysis indicated that the expression levels of miRNA-9, miRNA-146a-5p, and miRNA-223-5p were similar in both iMSC-EVs and pMSC-EVs (Fig. 5d). Additionally, ELISA data showed no significant differences in the levels of inflammatory regulators, including IL-10, TGF-β1, and PD-L1, between iMSC-EVs and pMSC-EVs (Fig. 5e).

We further investigated whether the increase in PD of iMSCs would have an impact on the yield of EVs and the quantity of their internal contents. To address this question, EVs were isolated from iMSCs at different PD stages (PD20, PD40, and PD60). Subsequently, the BCA protein assay and RT-qPCR analysis were employed to quantify the EV concentration and the content of key molecules within the EVs. The results revealed no significant differences in EV yields (Supplementary Fig. 4a) and the quantity of internal contents (Supplementary Fig. 4b) among these groups. This finding implies that an increase in the PD of iMSCs does not exert a significant influence on the secretion level of EVs and the quantity of their internal contents.

In conclusion, our findings firmly confirm that iMSCs, similar to their primary counterparts, secrete EVs that exhibit morphological and biochemical characteristics consistent with canonical EVs. This implies that iMSCs hold great potential as a reliable source for the large-scale production of EVs for therapeutic applications.

iMSC-EVs ameliorated LPS/ATP-induced damage in SV-HUC-1 cells

We first assessed the internalization capacity of iMSC-EVs and pMSC-EVs in SV-HUC-1 cells. EVs were labeled with DiR and incubated with SV-HUC-1 cells for 6 h. Intracellular red fluorescence was observed, confirming the successful uptake of EVs into the cytoplasm (Fig. 6a). Additionally, we evaluated the effects of iMSC-EVs and pMSC-EVs on the proliferation of SV-HUC-1 cells. Both iMSC-EVs and pMSC-EVs promoted an increase in SV-HUC-1 cell proliferation (Fig. 6b).

To evaluate the protective effects of iMSC-EVs against IC/BPS in vitro, the SV-HUC-1 cells were treated with ATP (2.5 mM) and LPS (10 µg/mL) to establish a IC/BPS cell model. As expected, SV-HUC-1 cell viability significantly decreased following LPS/ATP treatment compared to the control group (Fig. 6c). Treatment with iMSC-EVs and pMSC-EVs attenuated the inhibitory effects of LPS/ATP on cell viability, with both EVs demonstrating comparable efficacy (Fig. 6c).

Further investigations were conducted to evaluate the effects of iMSC-EVs on LPS/ATP-induced inflammation in SV-HUC-1 cells. ELISA and Western blot assays were used to measure the protein levels of IL-1β and TNF-α in SV-HUC-1 cells. As shown in Fig. 6d-f, LPS/ATP treatment resulted in a significant increase in the levels of IL-1β and TNF-α. However, iMSC-EVs treatment reduced the LPS/ATP-induced increase in these pro-inflammatory cytokines, suggesting that iMSC-EVs mitigate LPS/ATP-induced inflammation in SV-HUC-1 cells.

RT-qPCR was employed to analyze the expression of epithelial cell markers (E-cadherin and ZO-1) and urothelial-specific markers (uroplakin-1 A, UPK1A, and uroplakin-2, UPK2) in SV-HUC-1 cells. LPS/ATP treatment notably downregulated the expression of both epithelial cell markers and urothelial-specific markers. However, the addition of either iMSC-EVs or pMSC-EVs restored the expression levels of these markers (Fig. 6g and h). These results suggest that iMSC-EVs exert protective effects against LPS/ATP-induced damage in SV-HUC-1 cells.

Finally, we compared the protective effects of EVs from iMSCs at different PDs on LPS/ATP-induced damage in SV-HUC-1 cells. EVs from iMSCs at PD20, PD40, and PD60 all significantly alleviated LPS/ATP-induced inflammation in SV-HUC-1 cells. Specifically, EVs from PD20 and PD40 showed comparable efficacy to pMSC-EVs in suppressing pro-inflammatory cytokines and restoring cell structure. In contrast, EVs from iMSCs at PD60 exhibited slightly reduced activity in some indicators (Supplementary Fig. 5).

iMSC-EVs alleviate bladder injury in CYP-induced IC/BPS mice

We next investigated the therapeutic potential of iMSC-EVs in a CYP-induced IC/BPS mouse model. To establish the IC/BPS model, mice were administered 80 mg/kg of CYP via intraperitoneal injection every two days for a total of four doses. One week after the final CYP administration, either pMSC-EVs or iMSC-EVs were injected into the bladder wall, while IC/BPS mice (CYP-induced) received an equal volume of PBS as a control. After one week of treatment, urine samples were collected, and the mice were euthanized to isolate bladder tissues for analysis (Fig. 7a).

The wet bladder weight in IC/BPS mice was significantly higher than in the sham group. In comparison to the IC/BPS group, both pMSC-EVs and iMSC-EVs treatment groups exhibited a significant reduction in wet bladder weight, with both treatments demonstrating comparable efficacy (Fig. 7b). H&E and MT staining revealed marked pathological alterations in the IC/BPS mice compared to the sham group, including fibrous tissue hyperplasia, clustered vascular ectasia with congestion, infiltration of inflammatory cells, and epithelial cell damage (Fig. 7c and d). Importantly, the administration of either pMSC-EVs or iMSC-EVs resulted in significant amelioration of these histological abnormalities, evidenced by reduced inflammatory cell infiltration, diminished angiogenesis, and improved fibrosis (Fig. 7c and d).

RT-qPCR and Western blot assays was employed to assess the expression levels of inflammatory cytokines IL-1β and TNF-α in the bladder tissues of IC/BPS mice. The results revealed a significant increase in the expression of these cytokines in the IC/BPS group. However, the injection of either pMSC-EVs or iMSC-EVs led to a reduction of IL-1β and TNF-α expression at both the mRNA and protein levels (Fig. 7e-g). Furthermore, we evaluated the mRNA expression levels of uroplakin-related genes in bladder tissues. A significant downregulation of Upk1a and Upk2 expression was observed in the IC/BPS group, whereas their expression levels were partially restored following the injection of pMSC-EVs or iMSC-EVs (Fig. 7h).

Additionally, we observed an increase in the expression levels of IC/BPS-related biomarkers, ITGAL and PSMB9, in the bladder tissues of IC/BPS mice. Following the injection of pMSC-EVs or iMSC-EVs, the expression of these biomarkers was significantly inhibited (Fig. 7i). ELISA analysis of urine samples revealed that the levels of IC/BPS-related biomarkers PGE2 and NAG were significantly elevated in the urine of IC/BPS mice compared to the sham group (Fig. 7j). In contrast, the levels of PGE2 and NAG in the urine of mice treated with either pMSC-EVs or iMSC-EVs were significantly reduced (Fig. 7j).

In conclusion, both pMSC-EVs and iMSC-EVs demonstrate effective and comparable therapeutic capabilities in the CYP-induced IC/BPS mouse model. They alleviate histological abnormalities, downregulate inflammatory factors expression, upregulate uroplakin expression, and inhibit the levels of IC/BPS-associated biomarkers, highlighting their potential for therapeutic application in IC/BPS treatment.

Discussion

IC/BPS is a chronic bladder disorder, and currently, effective treatment options for it are still limited. Previous research has indicated that MSCs hold substantial therapeutic potential in the treatment of IC/BPS, offering new prospects for the management of these intractable bladder diseases [37, 38]. MSCs exert potent anti-inflammatory and immunomodulatory effects, mainly through a paracrine mechanism [39]. In this regard, MSC-EVs play a crucial role in mediating these effects and represent a core characteristic of MSC-based therapies. Numerous studies have confirmed the anti-inflammatory properties of MSC-EVs in the treatment of various diseases [40–45]. As a cell-free therapy, MSC-EVs demonstrate superior capabilities in penetrating biological barriers compared to MSCs, while avoiding the risks associated with cell transplantation [42, 46–49].

However, large-scale production of EVs remains one of the main challenges for their clinical application [50]. Several obstacles, including the limited in vitro proliferative lifespan of their parent MSCs, inter-donor cell heterogeneity, and ethical considerations, have restricted their broader application in clinical therapies and basic research. To address these challenges, our study aimed to establish a conditionally immortalized MSC cell line, termed iMSCs. This innovation is based on the Tet-on system, which enables the regulation of immortalizing gene expression through dox. By adjusting the medium components, we achieved reversible control over gene expression, allowing for precise regulation of the cell proliferation dynamics.

Our research demonstrated that the immortalized phenotype of iMSCs could be reliably switched by manipulating dox-induced LT expression. Moreover, these immortalized cells retained the characteristic markers of MSCs and were negative for hematopoietic markers. Notably, iMSCs showed a strong ability to differentiate into osteogenic and adipogenic lineages, and in differentiation assays, they were functionally indistinguishable from pMSCs. Additionally, the secretion of EVs by iMSCs was maintained. The EVs secreted by iMSCs are highly similar to those derived from pMSCs in terms of marker expression, morphology, size, and content, further highlighting their therapeutic potential. Overall, our approach provides a reliable and cost-effective method for generating a large number of functionally consistent cells that are similar to pMSCs. This development shows great promise for expanding the therapeutic applications of MSCs and offers a reliable and efficient alternative for various biomedical purposes.

In this study, we delved into two crucial aspects: the impact of the increase in PD of iMSCs on the yield and internal content quantity of EVs, and the protective effects of these EVs from iMSCs at different PDs on LPS/ATP-induced damage in SV-HUC-1 cells. Regarding the influence of PD on EV characteristics, we isolated EVs from iMSCs at PD20, PD40, and PD60. By employing the BCA protein assay and RT-qPCR analysis, we were able to precisely measure the EV concentration and pivotal molecular content. The results, as presented in Supplementary Fig. 4a and 4b, indicated no significant differences in EV yields and the quantity of internal contents among the groups. This implies that the increase in PD of iMSCs does not substantially affect the secretion level of EVs and their pivotal internal contents. This finding is of great significance as it suggests that, in terms of basic secretion capacity, iMSCs maintain a relatively stable state regardless of the PD within the tested range. When it comes to the protective effects of EVs, we found that EVs from iMSCs at all tested PDs (PD20, PD40, and PD60) could significantly alleviate LPS/ATP-induced inflammation in SV-HUC-1 cells. EVs from PD20 and PD40 were as effective as pMSC-EVs in suppressing pro-inflammatory cytokines and restoring cell structure. However, EVs from iMSCs at PD60, despite having a consistent quantity of key internal contents, exhibited slightly reduced protective activity in some indicators (Supplementary Fig. 5). This observation aligns the phenomenon of a decline in differentiation potential at high PDs (Supplementary Fig. 3). The reduced efficacy of PD60-derived EVs may be attributed to culture pressure or age-related changes in iMSCs at this advanced PD stage. These factors could potentially alter the composition and function of the secreted EVs, even though the overall quantity of EVs and their internal contents remain unchanged.

A critical consideration in this study pertains to the safety profile of iMSC-EVs relative to their pMSC-EVs. While our functional analyses demonstrated comparable therapeutic potential between iMSCs and pMSCs, the biosafety of EVs derived from these cell sources requires careful scrutiny. A central safety concern arises from the genetic modification of iMSCs, which were engineered to express the virus-derived LT. To address this, we emphasize that LT expression in iMSCs is strictly regulated by a dox-inducible system. Under standard culture conditions without dox, LT remains transcriptionally silenced, ensuring that iMSC-EVs harvested from these cultures are devoid of LT. This design inherently mitigates potential oncogenic risks associated with LT. Furthermore, no adverse effects were observed in functional assays involving iMSC-EVs, supporting their preliminary safety profile. However, we recognize that conducting comprehensive safety validation, which involves proteomic, transcriptomic, and epigenetic profiling of iMSC-EVs, is still of utmost importance for a rigorous assessment of their biosafety equivalence to pMSC-EVs. Such omics-driven analyses would elucidate cargo composition differences, including the presence of immunomodulatory or pro-inflammatory molecules, which could influence clinical applicability. While these advanced characterizations exceed the scope of the current study, we explicitly advocate for their integration into future investigations to confirm functional parity and safety equivalence between iMSC-EVs and pMSC-EVs.

The Tet-on system, utilizing its LV vectors to regulate the expression of the LT, has successfully enabled the conditional immortalization of diverse cell types. These include brown preadipocytes from mice and humans, human epicardium-derived cells, and rat atrial myocytes, as referenced in previous studies [29, 30, 33]. This system presents a remarkable advantage: it allows for the precise and reversible modulation of LT expression. By simply adding or removing dox from the culture medium, potential secondary damage can be effectively avoided. In our experiments, the iMSCs maintained a continuous proliferative ability. When cultured in the presence of dox, this proliferative capacity endured for at least up to PD 60.

Although iMSCs cultured with dox retained certain characteristics of pMSCs, they showed a diminished efficiency in lipogenic and osteogenic differentiation compared to their primary counterparts. However, once dox was withdrawn, even iMSCs at PD40 closely mimicked the differentiation capacity of pMSCs. Intriguingly, when LT expression was suppressed and dox was re-introduced, the iMSCs regained their proliferative potential. Moreover, in the absence of dox, these cells could differentiate into bone tissue when provided with appropriate stimuli. One potential drawback of permanently immortalized MSCs is the impact of the immortalizing gene on their differentiation capacity. In contrast, our conditionally immortalized MSCs retained the characteristics of pMSCs for at least 40 PDs, thus providing an abundant and reliable cell source for high-throughput studies.

In in vivo settings, intravenous administration of MSC-EVs is a common practice. Nevertheless, systemic injection often leads to the accumulation of EVs in the liver and spleen, which in turn reduces their therapeutic efficacy [49]. To ensure that EVs can effectively accumulate in the bladder and directly act on damaged cells, we adopted a local injection method in this study. We directly injected EVs into the bladder wall of a CYP-induced IC/BPS mouse model. As expected, iMSC-EVs significantly decreased the expression levels of inflammatory factors and damage markers in the bladder tissue of IC/BPS mice. This led to a substantial improvement in the pathological changes associated with the disorder. Our findings clearly indicate that the therapeutic effects of iMSC-EVs on interstitial cystitis are comparable to those of pMSC-EVs.

The disparity in the processing times of EVs between in vitro and in vivo experiments holds substantial significance. In in vitro settings, SV-HUC-1 cells were subjected to LPS and ATP to mimic acute inflammatory injury, emulating the cellular pathological characteristics of IC/BPS. When EVs were applied to the cells following a 12-hour LPS/ATP-induced injury, no therapeutic efficacy was observed (data not shown). This lack of effect can plausibly be attributed to the severe damage inflicted upon the cells by the 12-hour LPS/ATP exposure, which might have rendered the cells unresponsive to EV treatment or pushed the damage beyond the reparative capacity of EVs. Consequently, we adopted an alternative approach of adding EVs concurrently with LPS/ATP. Under this experimental condition, EVs demonstrated the ability to alleviate cell damage. The treatment duration in this study was comparable to that reported in previous investigations. In these studies, SV-HUC-1 cells were initially pre-treated with pharmacological agents for 2–4 h and subsequently stimulated with LPS/ATP [51–53]. In the in vivo model of CYP-induced IC/BPS, which is characterized by chronic inflammation, the injection of EVs is targeted at established pathological conditions, including inflammation, fibrosis, and urothelial damage. Prior studies on IC/BPS models treated with MSC-EVs reveals a variety of EV administration timings, such as on the second day after induction [54], at the first week [22], and at the second week [55]. Given that our experiment involved an intra-bladder wall injection, similar to the approach in the study by Song et al. [22], we opted to administer EVs one week after the establishment of the model. In conclusion, EVs exert their therapeutic effects through the direct delivery of their cargo. In in vitro cell models, they can effectively modulate acute injury when introduced simultaneously with inflammatory stimuli. In in vivo animal models, at an appropriate time after model establishment, they can target chronic inflammation, anti-fibrosis, and tissue repair. This difference in the effective time points between in vitro and in vivo experiments underscores the complexity of EV-mediated therapeutic effects and emphasizes the necessity of carefully considering the experimental context when devising EV-based treatment strategies.

In conclusion, our research introduces a novel strategy for generating EVs from conditionally immortalized MSCs for the treatment of IC/BPS. This approach offers a powerful tool for future clinical applications in the therapy of human bladder diseases.

Limitations of the study

While our investigation demonstrates that iMSC-EVs exhibit morphological, biochemical, and partial content similarities to pMSC-EVs, as well as comparable efficacy in treating IC/BPS, several limitations remain. MSC-EVs are known to be enriched with a wide range of bioactive proteins and non-coding RNAs, yet the comprehensive internal composition of iMSC-EVs, including the specific bioactive molecules involved in their therapeutic effects, is not fully understood. Furthermore, the precise molecular mechanisms underlying the therapeutic actions of iMSC-EVs in the treatment of IC/BPS have not been elucidated. These knowledge gaps present important directions for future research to fully harness the potential of iMSC-EVs in clinical applications.

Conclusions

In this study, we successfully developed a reversible immortalized MSC line, termed iMSCs, using the Tet-on-based LT expression system. These cells exhibited stable immortalization in the presence of dox, with the ability to reverse this immortalization upon dox withdrawal. Characterization of iMSCs revealed consistent expression of MSC markers and maintained differentiation potential into osteogenic and adipogenic lineages when appropriately induced. Additionally, we confirmed that iMSCs retained the ability to secrete EVs, which demonstrated therapeutic potential in the treatment of IC/BPS. Our findings show that intravesical injection of iMSC-EVs effectively suppressed tissue inflammation and alleviated pathological changes associated with IC/BPS. Given these results, iMSCs offer a promising resource for advancing MSC biology and exploring novel therapeutic strategies in immunotherapy and regenerative medicine, especially for bladder diseases like IC/BPS.
