Synthesis of Biobased Aliphatic Poly(ester amide)s and Their Thermal, Tensile Properties, and Selective Depolymerization through Transesterification
Authors: Gaku Miyamoto, Mohamed Mehawed Abdellatif, Seiji Higashi, Kousaku Tao, Hiroki Takeshita, Hiroshi Hirano, Kotohiro Nomura
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Development of chemically recyclable biobased polymers has been an important subject in terms of circular economy. In this study, syntheses of high-molecular-weight poly­(ester amide)­s consisting of diesters of 10-undecenol with dicarboxylic acids containing succinamide linkage with two L-phenylalanine (PEA1, HPEA1), L-leucine (PEA2, HPEA2), L-valine (PEA3, HPEA3), L-alanine (PEA4, HPEA4), and β-alanine (PEA5) have been demonstrated by acyclic diene metathesis (ADMET) polymerization and subsequent tandem olefin hydrogenation. The ADMET polymerization afforded high-molar-mass amorphous unsaturated polymers (PEA1–4, M n = 39800–49300) or semicrystalline polymer containing β-alanine (PEA5, melting temperature at 116.6, 134.4 °C); the tandem hydrogenation gave the saturated poly­(ester amide)­s (HPEA1–4) without decreases in the M n values. Thermal properties in the resultant polymers were affected by M n values and amino acids in the monomer unit. These polymer films display promising tensile properties (strength, elongation at break) that were affected by the amino acid substituent; the properties in the saturated poly­(ester amide)­s (HPEA1–4) were also affected by the M n values and the method of preparation (solvent cast, hot press). The films containing L-alanine (PEA4, HPEA4) showed high tensile strengths (31.5, 40.2 MPa, respectively), whereas the L-leucine-based polymer (HPEA2) exhibited high elongation at break (706–1000%). Unsaturated L-phenylalanine-based polymer film (PEA1) displayed promising tensile (32.8 MPa, 684%), elastic, and self-healing properties; the elastic behavior through hysteresis experiments of 10 cycles using reused test specimen was precisely reproduced. These poly­(ester amide)­s were depolymerized by transesterification to afford diesters containing the succinamides, demonstrating a closed loop chemical recycling.

Introduction

The development of biobased polyesters, derived from nonedible renewable resourcessuch as plant oils of long chain fatty acid esters, terpenes, and sugarshas been recognized as an important subject in polymer chemistry and sustainable chemistry. − Owing to their relatively facile chemical recyclability compared to conventional polyolefins − and recent advances in upcycling technologies, , these biobased polyesters ,− are recognized as promising sustainable materials in circular economy. It has been known that, as reported in conventional polyesters, their physical, chemical properties, and their mechanical properties in film are highly influenced by monomer repeat units ,− such as alkyl chain length of diester or diol and formula of the (called) middle unit, exemplified in Chart which directly affect interpolymer communication (interaction) through the entanglement of polymer chains and crystallization behavior.

Selected Reported Biobased Aliphatic Polyesters with Tensile Properties

A recent study also revealed that the molecular weight (polymer chain length) also plays an essential role toward tensile strain and elongation at break in the resultant polymer film. , For example, tensile strength and elongation at break in the polyester films consisting of castor oil and isosorbide (C18IS) or isomannide (C18IM, Chart ) increased upon an increase in the M n values, due to the strengthening of interpolymer interaction through entanglement among polymer chains. Moreover, more recently, polyesters consisting of 10-undecenoic acid and two IM connected through trans-1,4-cyclohexane dicarboxylic acid [expressed C18(IM)2CD, Chart ] showed improved tensile strength (stress) at break compared to C18IM (with observation of the yield strength), whereas similar polyester films containing 2,5-furan dicarboxylic acid [expressed C18(IM)2FD] showed brittle fracture behavior without yielding. These results also suggest that the tensile properties of the biobased polyesters are influenced by the monomer repeat units.

We herein focused on the study of synthesis of biobased aliphatic poly­(ester amide)­s consisting of diesters of 10-undecenol and dicarboxylic acid, containing succinamide linkage with two amino acids (Chart ), since we believe that these poly­(ester amide)­s should display promising interpolymer interactions through hydrogen bonding of the amino group. As far as we know, comprehensive studies on the synthesis and analysis of tensile properties in the resultant polymer films have never been reported, whereas there have been studies on linear (biobased) aliphatic poly­(ester amide)­s, − polyolefin-containing amino acids and peptides, and the related diketopiperazine-based poly­(ester amide)­s. We thus herein report the synthesis of a series of biobased aliphatic poly­(ester amide)­s containing succinamide with various amino acids (expressed as HPEA1–5, Chart ) by acyclic diene metathesis (ADMET) polymerization and the subsequent olefin hydrogenation. The resultant polymers displayed better tensile properties than conventional polymers and unique elastic properties. Moreover, we also wish to demonstrate that the resultant polymers were depolymerized through selective transesterification with alcohol by a homogeneous titanium catalyst, affording starting monomers (succinamides) in exclusive yields.

Results and Discussion

Synthesis of α,ω-Diene Monomers and Poly­(ester Amide)­s by Acyclic Diene Metathesis (ADMET) Polymerization and Subsequent Olefin Hydrogenation

Five α,ω-diene monomers (expressed as MEA1–MEA5, Scheme ), diesters of 10-undecenol (derived from castor oil) ,, with dicarboxylic acids, consisting of two amino acids [ l -phenylalanine (Phe, MEA1), L-leucine (Leu, MEA2), L-valine (Val, MEA3), L-alanine (Ala, MEA4), and β-alanine (MEA5)] connected through succinic acid through amide linkage, have been chosen in this study to explore the effect of various amino acid substituents (with different steric bulk as well as electronic) toward the materials’ property. These monomers (MEA1–MEA5) could be synthesized in gram scales via a two-step synthesis (Scheme ) involving amidation of various l -amino acid methyl ester hydrochlorides with succinic acid dichloride in the presence of triethylamine (yield 41–83%), followed by transesterification with 10-undecenol in the presence of a CpTiCl3 catalyst (yield 50–90%): detailed procedures are described in Supporting Information. It was revealed that the transesterification established by us using CpTiCl3 afforded the diesters in a selective manner; reason for a rather low yield in MEA5 was due to a difficulty for isolation technically (due to the limited solubility) as a pure form. The prepared monomers (MEA1–MEA5) were characterized by 1H- and 13C­{1H}-NMR spectra (Figures S1–S20) and atmospheric pressure chemical ionization mass spectrometry (APCI-MS, Figures S39).

It was revealed that the polymerizations in chloroform (conducted under the reported initial monomer concentrations, 0.88–1.59 M for MEA1–4) , gave the polymers with moderate molecular weights [PEA1 (M n = 17900, 19000, runs 1,2), PEA2 (M n = 18800, 23300, runs 8,9), PEA3 (M n = 18300–25500, runs 13–15), PEA4 (M n = 14500, 16800, runs 17,18), and PEA5 (M n = 34400, run 21)]. The M n value in the resultant polymer (PEA2) decreased when the polymerization was conducted under low Ru loading (0.5 mol %, run 10). Moreover, the polymerization conducted under bulk conditions (without solvent) also gave PEA2 with a rather low M n value (run 11, M n = 15800), probably due to the difficulty of removal of ethylene by-produced from the reaction mixture (due to high viscosity). Further condition optimizations thus revealed that the polymerizations conducted under high initial monomer concentration conditions (2.80–3.50 M, for MEA1–4) gave the high molar mass poly­(ester amide)­s (PEA1, M n = 39800–55100, runs 3–7; PEA2, M n = 41200, run 12; PEA3, M n = 49300, run 16; PEA4, M n = 43100, 47700, run 19,20): observed differences in the M n values by PEA1 would be probably due to degree and efficiency of removal of ethylene by-produced from rather high viscous reaction mixture. High-molar-mass PEA5, possessing β-alanine as the amino acid unit, was also obtained under the optimized conditions (M n = 56600, run 22), although the concentration conducted was rather low compared to those for syntheses of other polymers (PEA1–4).

Olefinic double bonds in the resultant unsaturated polymers (PEA1–4) were hydrogenated (H2 2.0 MPa, 50 °C, 12 h) in a tandem manner upon the addition of a small amount of Al2O3 (ca. 2 wt %) into the reaction mixture (Scheme ), according to the reported procedure (after transferring the reaction mixture into a stainless steel reaction vessel using a minimum amount of chloroform). ,, The saturated poly­(ester amide)­s (expressed as HPEA1–4) could be isolated by pouring the reaction mixture into methanol (diethyl ether for HPEA2 and HPEA3). The complete hydrogenation was confirmed by 1H- and 13C­{1H}-NMR spectra, as observed by the disappearance of resonances ascribed to the olefinic protons/carbons with the other remaining resonances (as exemplified in Figure c, HPEA1, Figures S31–S38). Attempted tandem hydrogenation of PEA5 failed owing to inferior solubility in chloroform and THF. Table summarizes the results for the synthesis of saturated poly­(ester amide)­s (HPEA1–4) in a tandem manner. As reported previously, ,, it was revealed that the method enabled the synthesis of saturated poly­(ester amide)­s in high yields without significant changes in the M n values as well as PDI (M w/M n) values without affecting (hydrogenating, reduction) the other functionalities (such as esters and amide functional groups, Figures S40–S43).

It was revealed that the resultant unsaturated polymers (PEA1–4) were amorphous possessing sole T g values, and the value was affected by the substituent in the amino acid employed. The T g value in the high-molar-mass polymers (PEA1–4, M n = 39800–49300) increased in the order: PEA3 (−0.1 °C, isopropyl) < PEA2 (5.3 °C, isobutyl) < PEA4 (10.3 °C, methyl) < PEA1 (15.3 °C, benzyl). It seems likely that the T g values were affected by the rigidity of the polymer main chain (except PEA1 containing a benzyl group), which could affect the interpolymer interactions (degree of entanglement through hydrogen bonding and π–π stacking in PEA1 etc.). ,,,, Moreover, it might be interesting to note that the DSC thermogram in PEA5, possessing β-alanine as the monomer unit without branching, showed two T m values at 116 and 134.4 °C, suggesting that the resultant polymer is a linear semicrystalline (polyethylene-like) material. ,,,,− ,,

It was also revealed that the T g values in the polymers (PEA1–4) showed a molecular weight dependence. , For instance, as summarized partly in Table , the T g value increased upon increasing the M n value [T g = 12.7 °C (M n = 17900, run 2) vs 15.3 °C (M n = 39800, run 4), PEA1; −0.2 °C (M n = 14500, run 10) vs 5.3 °C (M n = 41200, run 12), PEA2; −3.9 °C (M n = 18300, run 14) vs −0.1 °C (M n = 49300, run 16), PEA3; 3.5 °C (M n = 16800, run 17) vs 10.3 °C (M n = 47700, run 20), PEA4]. As described below, this (polymer chain length) should also affect the interpolymer interaction (entanglement of polymer main chain, etc., probably through hydrogen bonding), leading to affect their tensile properties (tensile stress and strain at break).

It was also revealed that the prepared hydrogenated poly­(ester amide)­s, possessing a substituent except methyl group (HPEA1–3), are amorphous materials showing sole T g values, which not only suggest their uniform compositions but also strongly support the completion of the hydrogenation. The observed T g values (HPEA1–3) are higher than those in the unsaturated polymers (PEA1–3, shown above) and the values (in the high-molar-mass HPEAs) increased in the same order (as the PEAs): HPEA3 (14.6 °C) < HPEA2 (15.7 °C) < HPEA1 (22.2 °C). The trend thus also suggests the above assumption leading to the interpolymer interactions. ,,, Moreover, as observed in the unsaturated polymers, the T g values are affected by the M n values (Figure S45). For instance, as summarized in Table , the T g value in HPEA1 increased upon an increase in the M n values: T g = 16.9 °C (M n = 19300) < 18.1 °C (36200) < 22.2 °C (44500). It seems likely that this might be assumed as due to the degree of entanglement of the polymer main chain. As described below, the T g values of the prepared PEAs (−0.1–15.3 °C) below the room temperature seemed important for the motion of polymer chains to exhibit interesting properties such as self-healing. −

Moreover, HPEA4, possessing a methyl substituent, is a semicrystalline material possessing several melting temperatures (Table , Figure (right), and Figure S46), and, as observed in the other amorphous poly­(ester amide)­s (HPEA1–3), the T m values are affected by the M n value (Figure S46), probably due to the degree of interpolymer interaction through the chain entanglement including hydrogen bonding.

Effect of the Middle Segment (Amino Acid Substituents) in Biobased Poly­(ester amide)­s toward Tensile and Elastic Properties

Tensile properties of the prepared poly­(ester amide) films (PEA1–5, HPEA1–4) were analyzed through stress/strain experiments at an elongation rate of 50 mm/min using a universal testing instrument (23 °C, humidity 50 ± 10%). The sample polymer test specimens (dumbbell-shaped) were prepared by cutting the original films prepared by using the solvent cast or hot press method (detailed procedures are described in the Supporting Information): all measurements were performed at least three times to ensure reproducibility (Figures S50–S71). The detailed data (including reproducibility in the measurements) are summarized in Table S1.

Figure summarizes selected tensile analysis data for unsaturated poly­(ester amide)­s (PEA1, PEA4, PEA5) with high M n values; preparation of the polymer films of PEA2 and PEA3 failed probably due to T g values especially below room temperature [5.3 °C (run 12) and −0.1 °C (run 16), respectively]. It was revealed that the tensile strain at break of the hot press films increased in the order (Figure ): PEA5 (329%, run 22) < PEA4 (599%, run 20) < PEA1 (684%, run 5), whereas no significant differences were observed in the tensile strength (stress, 31.5–34.9 MPa) except that PEA4 and PEA5 showed higher initial moduli and yield strengths at 10.0 MPa and 12.0 MPa, respectively. As observed previously, , the sample films prepared by hot press showed higher elongation at breaks compared to those prepared by the solvent cast method (Figure , break lines for PEA1 and PEA4). As observed in polyester films containing isosorbide, both the tensile stress and strain in PEA5 increased upon increasing the M n value (data shown in SI, Figures S52 and S53): 25.3 MPa, 214% (M n = 34400, run 21) vs 34.9 MPa, 329% (M n = 56600, run 22). This could be probably explained by the increased degree of interpolymer interaction through the entanglement of the polymer main chain including hydrogen bonding.

It was revealed that tensile strength (stress) in the high-molar-mass polymers (M n = 36200–47900) increased in the order: HPEA2 (19.0 MPa) < HPEA1 (20.1) < HPEA3 (31.3) < HPEA4 (40.2). The tensile strain (elongation) at break displayed the opposite trend and the value increased in the order: HPEA4 (412%) < HPEA3 (497) < HPEA1 (537) < HPEA2 (706). It seems likely that the observed trends were probably influenced by the steric bulk of the substituents in the amino acid [−CH2CHMe2 (HPEA2) < −CHMe2 (HPEA3) < –CH3 (HPEA4)] employed except HPEA1 containing a phenyl group, which would affect the degree of the interpolymer interaction (degree of chain entanglement through hydrogen bonding). The order between HPEA1 and HPEA2 would probably be considered as due to the effect of π–π stacking rather than the steric bulk (−CH2CHMe2 vs –CH2Ph), as also discussed above as the effect of the substituent toward thermal property.

Note that, as shown in Figure b, the tensile property was also affected by the molecular weight in the polymers, and the trend was influenced by the substituents in the amino acid employed. For instance, in HPEA4 possessing the methyl substituent (semicrystalline polymers), both tensile stress and the strain at the break increased with an increase of the M n value [40.2 MPa, 412% (M n = 46600, run 32) vs 7.7 MPa, 223% (M n = 15600, run 31)], as observed in the (semicrystalline) polyester containing isosorbide (and isomannide) as the middle diol unit.

It should also be noted that molecular weight dependences toward their tensile properties in the low-molecular-weight amorphous polymer samples (HPEA1–3) were highly influenced by the amino acid substituent employed (Figure b). In HPEA3 possessing an isopropyl substituent, the strain (elongation) at break decreased upon an increase in the M n value with a slight decrease in the tensile stress (strength). HPEA2 possessing an isobutyl substituent showed the similar trend in the elongation at break with a decrease in the strength; the solvent cast film of the low-molecular-weight sample showed rather large tensile stress compared to that prepared by the hot press. Interestingly, for the polymer film of HPEA1 possessing a benzyl group, the tensile strength (stress) increased with an increase in the M n values [20.1 MPa, run 24 (M n = 36200) vs 7.7 MPa (or 16.8 MPa by solvent cast film), run 23], whereas the strain, especially in the low molecular weight samples, was highly affected by the method prepared. For instance, the HPEA1 film prepared by the hot press showed a large elongation at break (790%) compared to that prepared by the solvent cast (294%), whereas tensile stress of the solvent cast film (16.8 MPa) was apparently higher than that in the hot press film (7.7 MPa) with observation of rather high initial moduli and yield strength (Figure b). The observed facts would be probably considered as due to low T g values and/or inferior interpolymer entanglement of low-molar-mass polymers.

Wide-angle X-ray diffraction (WAXD) spectra of high molecular hydrogenated polymer (HPEA1–4, M n = 39000–58100) films prepared by the solvent cast or hot press were measured at 263 K and the spectra are summarized in Figures S72 and S73. On the basis of DSC thermograms, HPEA1–3 showed sole glass transition temperatures suggesting that the resultant polymers are amorphous, but the WAXD spectra showed a weak diffraction peak, suggesting the presence of crystalline domains. It was revealed that the semicrystalline HPEA4 films showed higher diffraction intensities and rather wide peaks (2θmax = 23.7° and 23.5° for hot press and solvent cast films, respectively) compared to those in the other films (HPEA1–3, 2θmax = 19.6°). It seems likely that the films prepared by the solvent cast showed higher intensities compared to those prepared by the hot press. This means that formation of the crystalline domains occurred in the long evaporation process; this (presence of crystalline domains) should affect the tensile properties (stress and strain). Indeed, interestingly, the polymer films in HPEA1 and HPEA3 prepared by the solvent cast method showed yield strengths with rather high initial moduli, whereas the tensile stress linearly increased upon increase in the tensile strain in the hot press film (Figure S48). It thus seems likely that the observed difference in the yielding behavior may be explained as due to differences in the degree of weakly developed crystalline domains that can be observed in the WAXD analysis (not by their DSC thermograms). Moreover, although the quality of the spectral data seemed insufficient for the quantitative analysis (and the detailed discussion, even simple assumption) likely because the degree of crystallinity was too low, the corresponding small-angle X-ray scattering (SAXS) spectra showed differences in the films (HPEA1 and HPEA4) prepared by solvent cast and hot press methods, whereas no apparent differences were seen in the spectra of the films in HPEA2 and HPEA3 (Figure S74).

It should be noted that the solvent cast film of unsaturated poly­(ester amide) possessing the benzyl group (PEA1, M n = 47200) displayed a unique self-healing behavior (see the submitted video in Supporting Information): once cut, the film could be connected within 1 min (30 s). − This might be related to the above discussion in the formation of weak crystalline domains and/or rather strong hydrogen bonding in addition to π–π stacking, although we do not have clear explanations for the observed fact at this moment.

Hysteresis experiments for the analysis of the elastic property of the PEA1 films (M n = 48000, run 6), prepared by hot press and solvent cast methods, were conducted by cyclically loaded and unloaded 10 times to a strain of 300% through a compact high-performance tensile tester (a video demonstrating the analysis with biobased elastomer is shown in our previous report), and the results are shown in Figure a,b and the analysis data are summarized in Table S2. It was revealed that the prepared PEA1 films showed a certain degree of unrecovered stress in the first cycle probably due to irreversible microstructural rearrangement during the first deformation, leading to stress softening (called Mullins-type behavior), and better elastic properties were observed after the second cycle, clearly demonstrating a certain level of good elastic properties, as previously observed in the biobased elastomer, poly­(ethylene-co-myrcene)­s. It seems that the solvent-cast film (Figure b) showed rather better tensile recovery (Figure a), whereas the hot-pressed film showed the better tensile stress (strength) at 300% elongation (Figure a vs b).

It should be noted that reused test specimens (recovered after 1 h) showed similar elastic behaviors, showing certain unrecovered stress in the first cycle and better recovery after the second cycle. It was revealed that the reused specimen of the film prepared by the hot press showed better recovery compared to the fresh one (Figure a vs 5c). Moreover, note that the elastic behavior was precisely reproduced in the PEA1 film prepared by the solvent cast film (Figure b vs 5d, Table S2), whereas, more interestingly, the reused hot press film displayed better tensile strength with good recovery (Figures a,c and S49). It may be speculated that observed improvement in the tensile stress in the reused hot press film would be probably due to the formation of a crystalline domain over the healing process, which should lead to high initial moduli with yield strengths (observed in the solvent cast film supported by WAXD analysis). We, however, do not have a clear explanation of the observed unique elastic properties including the reused ones (hot press and solvent cast film), but this might be assumed as due to rather strong interpolymer interactions with chain entanglements through hydrogen bonding and π–π stacking in the PEA1 film displaying self-healing behavior. −

Concluding Remarks

We herein demonstrated a successful synthesis of high-molecular-weight poly­(ester amide)­s consisting of diesters of 10-undecenol with dicarboxylic acids, containing succinamide linkage with two L-phenylalanine, L-leucine, L-alanine, L-valine, and β-alanine, by ADMET polymerization and subsequent tandem hydrogenation (Chart ). The ADMET polymerizations using a ruthenium–carbene catalyst afforded high-molar-mass amorphous unsaturated polymers (PEA1–4, M n = 39800–49300) possessing sole glass transition temperatures (T g), whereas the resultant polymers containing β-alanine (PEA5) were semicrystalline possessing rather high melting temperatures (116.6, 134.4 °C, M n = 34400). The subsequent (one pot) tandem olefin hydrogenation copresence of a small amount (2 wt %) of Al2O3 yielded the saturated poly­(ester amide)­s (HPEA1–4) without decreases in the M n values. Thermal properties in the resultant polymers were affected by M n values and amino acid in the monomer unit.

Summary of Prepared Poly­(ester amide)­s (PEA1–5, HPEA1–4) in This Study

Note that these polymer films display promising tensile properties that were influenced especially by the amino acid substituents in the monomer unit. As summarized in Figure , tensile properties in the resultant poly­(ester amide)­s, through this study, displayed higher both tensile stress and elongation at break than those in conventional polyolefins [polypropylene (PP), high-density polyethylene (HDPE), low-density polyethylene (LDPE)] and , commodity polyesters such as poly­(ethylene terephthalate) (PET), poly­(lactide) (PLA), and biobased linear polyester (PE18,18). The poly­(ester amide)­s conducted in this study showed higher tensile strain (elongation) at break compared to biobased polyamide (PA1010), whereas the tensile stresses (strengths) were low compared to those of PA1010. Moreover, some poly­(ester amide)­s, especially HPEA3 and PEA1, display high elongation (tensile strain) at break compared to those in biobased (high molecular weight) aliphatic polyester consisting of castor oil and isosorbide (C18IS, Chart ). The results clearly demonstrate that the biobased poly­(ester amide)­s presented in this study are not polyethylene-like materials but the functional materials displaying promising tensile properties beyond polyolefins.

Summary of tensile properties (plots of tensile stress and strains) of some poly­(ester amide)­s conducted in this study, conventional polyolefins [polypropylene (PP), high density polyethylene (HDPE), low density polyethylene (LDPE)], , poly­(ethylene terephthalate) (PET), and poly­(lactide) (PLA), PA1010. Data for biobased linear long chain aliphatic polyester (PE-18,18) and polyester consisting of castor oil and isosorbide (C18IS, Chart ) are also placed for comparison.

It was demonstrated that the properties especially in the saturated poly­(ester amide)­s (HPEA1–4) were also affected by the M n values and method of the film preparation (solvent cast, hot press); the properties especially in the low-molar-mass polymers were highly affected by the monomer repeat (amino acid) unit employed. The high-molar-mass L-alanine-based polymer films (PEA4, HPEA4) showed high tensile strengths (31.5, 40.2 MPa, respectively) with a relatively high elongation at break (599, 412%, respectively), whereas the polymer containing L-leucine (HPEA2) displayed a high elongation at break (706–1000%) with a rather low tensile strength (19.0 MPa, M n = 40700), which was strongly affected by the M n value and the preparation method. It should also be noted that the high-molecular-weight unsaturated L-phenylalanine-based polymer film (PEA1) not only exhibited promising tensile properties (32.8 MPa, 684%, M n = 49400) but also displayed self-healing property within 1 min (30 s, see video in the Supporting Information). Moreover, the elastic behavior of PEA1 through hysteresis experiments of 10 cycles using a reused test specimen was precisely reproduced from the fresh one; the reused hot press film displayed better tensile strength with good recovery.

These poly­(ester amide)­s were depolymerized by transesterification with ethanol in the presence of a CpTiCl3 catalyst to afford monomer units, diesters containing succinamides with two amino acids, that can be converted to monomers (α,ω-nonconjugated dienes) by transesterification with 10-undecenol, clearly demonstrating a possibility of the closed-loop (polymerization–depolymerization) chemical recycling.

We believe that the results should provide important information concerning the effect of monomer repeat units, especially the middle segment, toward materials’ (thermal and tensile) properties for the design of advanced biobased functional polymers to fulfill the desired properties. The efforts presented here should thus contribute to the development of advanced chemically recyclable polymers directed toward circular economy.
