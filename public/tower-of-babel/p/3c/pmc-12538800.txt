Gut microbiome alterations in IgG4-related ophthalmic disease
Authors: Tingting Ren, Jing Li, Rui Liu, Nan Wang, Qihan Guo, Liangyuan Xu, Haihan Yan, Yufei Zhang, Jianmin Ma
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis study aims to investigate the gut microbiome alterations in IgG4-related ophthalmic disease (IgG4-ROD) and explore the potential roles of serum IgE and IgG4 in this disease.MethodsIn this prospective study, we recruited a total of 34 participants, including 17 IgG4-ROD patients and 17 healthy controls (HC) from Beijing Tongren Hospital. Fecal and blood samples were collected and analyzed, including 16 S rRNA gene sequencing of fecal samples. We assessed alpha and beta diversity to identify microbial variations among IgG4-ROD and HC, IgG4+ and IgG4-, IgE + and IgE-, and hormone-treated and untreated groups. Correlation analyses between cytokines and microbial profiles were conducted to uncover novel insights into the pathogenesis of IgG4-ROD.ResultsSignificant differences in microbial composition were observed among subgroups. Patients with IgG4-ROD showed increases in Proteobacteria, Parvimonas, Novosphingobium, Shigella, and Allobaculum, along with decreased levels of Faecalibacterium, Barnesiella, and Weissella. The IgE + group exhibited an elevated abundance of Enterobacteriaceae, with several microbial changes overlapping between IgG4-ROD and IgE + individuals. Sphingomonas and Peptostreptococcaceae were more prevalent in the IgG4 + group, while Adlercreutzia and Acidimicrobiales were abundant in the IgG4- group. Furthermore, Ruminococcaceae were enriched in the HC and IgG4- groups. Pelomonas and Escherichia were increased in IgG4-ROD patients treated with glucocorticosteroids. Additionally, significant correlations were identified between several microbial taxa and serum cytokines such as IL-1β, IL-2, IFN-α, C3, and IgG4.ConclusionThe findings indicate gut dysbiosis in IgG4-ROD and suggest influential roles for serum IgE and IgG4, enhancing our understanding of microbial-clinical interactions in this disease.Supplementary informationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12866-025-04350-4.

Introduction

Immunoglobulin G4-related disease (IgG4-RD) is a systemic immune-mediated fibroinflammatory disorder that affects multiple organs, including the pancreas, bile ducts, lacrimal glands, and salivary glands [1–3]. IgG4-RD occurring at different sites differs in epidemiology, serologic findings, and prognosis [4]. When IgG4-RD involves the eye, it is called IgG4-related ophthalmic disease (IgG4-ROD), which mainly affects the lacrimal gland (87.3%), extraocular muscles (23.1%), other orbital soft tissues (23.1%) and the trigeminal nerve (4.6%) [5]. The most common manifestation of IgG4-ROD is eyelid swelling, and the treatment typically includes glucocorticoids and immunosuppressants [6]. Although the specific pathogenesis of IgG4-ROD remains unclear, patients often suffer from allergic conditions such as asthma and rhinitis, and some have elevated serum IgG4 or IgE [7].

The gut microbiome (GM) refers to the complex ecosystem of microorganisms and their genetic entities in the human gastrointestinal tract, including bacteria, viruses, fungi, and archaea [8, 9]. Recent studies have increasingly highlighted the GM’s critical role in the development of autoimmune diseases such as inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), rheumatoid arthritis, and Sjögren’s syndrome, providing new perspectives for studying the pathogenesis of IgG4-RD [10, 11]. Studies have shown differences in the composition and diversity of the GM in IgG4-RD. Nonetheless, research in this area remains limited [12–15]. Notably, patients with IgG4-ROD, a specific clinical subtype of IgG4-RD, have been found to exhibit distinct clinical features compared to those without ocular involvement, including earlier onset age, more prevalent allergic conditions, multi-organ manifestations, and higher serum IgG4 and IgE levels [6]. Moreover, further research has revealed the role of IgG4 in the GM’s composition in the general population [16]. Despite these findings, no studies to date have specifically examined the GM in patients with IgG4-ROD. This paper aims to explore and describe the GM alterations in IgG4-ROD and try to assess the roles of IgG4 and IgE, which may shed light on the pathogenesis of the disease.

Materials and methods

Subject recruitment and sample collection

A total of 34 participants were enrolled in this study, including 17 patients diagnosed with IgG4-ROD and 17 healthy controls (HC). Factors such as age, sex, and body mass index (BMI) were carefully matched between the disease group and the HC. The IgG4-ROD patients were recruited from Beijing Tongren Hospital, affiliated with Capital Medical University, from December 2022 to April 2023. All patients were confirmed by histopathology and exhibited clinical findings compatible with a diagnosis of “definite” or “probable” IgG4-ROD based on the diagnostic criteria for IgG4-ROD [17]. The group of HC consisted of 17 volunteers. Exclusion criteria for participation included: (1) positive for hepatitis B or C viral antigens; (2) any prior history of gastrointestinal or other autoimmune diseases; (3) administration of antibiotics or microbial agents within three months before admission. All participants were required to be at least 18 years of age.

Serum levels of IgE and IgG4 were measured in the patient group. Patients with IgE levels higher than the normal reference range (< 165IU/ml) were placed in the IgE-positive (IgE+) subgroup. Those with IgE levels within the normal range were categorized into the IgE-negative (IgE-) subgroup. Similarly, based on serum IgG4 levels (normal reference interval < 2 g/L), patients were classified into IgG4-positive (IgG4+) and IgG4-negative (IgG4-) subgroups.

Fecal samples were obtained from participants using sterile swabs. The samples were immediately transported to the laboratory on dry ice and stored at −80℃ until analysis. To minimize contamination from urine and surrounding bacteria, only the central portion of the stool was collected from each patient. Unique identifiers were assigned to each participant and their samples to ensure confidentiality. Additionally, blood serum samples were obtained from 17 patients with IgG4-ROD. These samples were analyzed for various cytokines, including interleukins (IL-1β, IL-2, IL-5, IL-6, IL-8, IL-10, IL-12p70, IL-17), immunoglobulins (IgA, IgM, IgE, IgG, IgG1, IgG2, IgG3, IgG4), tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α), interferon-alpha (IFN-α), and complement components (C3, C4 and C1q).

DNA extraction, 16 S rRNA gene sequencing, and data processing

DNA was extracted from intestinal content samples using the OMEGA Soil DNA Kit (M5635-02) (Omega Bio-Tek, Norcross, GA, USA), following the manufacturer’s instructions. Extracted DNA was stored at −20 °C until further analysis. The quantity and quality of DNA were measured using a NanoDrop NC2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) and agarose gel electrophoresis, respectively. PCR amplification of the bacterial 16 S rRNA genes V3-V4 region was performed using the forward primer 338F (5’-ACTCCTACGGGAGGCAGCA-3’) and the reverse primer 806R (5’-GGACTACHVGGGTWTCTAAT-3’), with 25 PCR cycles. PCR amplicons were purified with Vazyme VAHTSTM DNA Clean Beads (Vazyme, Nanjing, China) and quantified using the Quant-iT PicoGreen dsDNA Assay Kit (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA). After the individual quantification step, amplicons were pooled in equal amounts, and pair-end sequencing (2 × 250 bp) was performed on the Illumina NovaSeq platform with NovaSeq 6000 SP Reagent Kit (500 cycles) at Shanghai Personal Biotechnology Co., Ltd (Shanghai, China).

Sequence data were processed using Quantitative Insights Into Microbial Ecology (QIIME2, version 2019.4) (Bolyen et al., 2019) according to the official tutorials with slight modifications. Raw sequences were demultiplexed using the demux plugin, and primers were trimmed using the cutadapt plugin (Martin, M., 2011). Quality control, denoising, merging, and chimera removal were performed using the Deficiency of Adenosine Deaminase 2 (DADA2) (Callahan et al. 2016). Amplicon sequence variants (ASVs) were aligned using MAFFT (Katoh et al., 2002) and used to construct a phylogeny with fasttree2 (Price et al. 2010). ASVs that appeared only once across all samples were removed. Taxonomic classification was based on the Greengenes database (DeSantis et al., 2006). Finally, the raw counts of ASVs were normalized to relative abundances for further analysis.

Bioinformatic analysis

Alpha diversity was calculated by the Shannon, Simpson, Pielou’s evenness, and Chao 1 index to assess intra-sample diversity. Beta diversity was analyzed using Principal Coordinate Analysis (PCoA) with the Bray-Curtis distance. Based on their predominant relative abundances in the datasets, the bacterial community structure was depicted at the phylum and genus levels. To identify taxa with significant differential abundance among groups, we applied Linear Discriminant Analysis Effect Size (LEfSe) with default parameters: α = 0.05 for the Kruskal-Wallis test among classes, and a logarithmic LDA score cutoff of 2.0 to determine discriminative features. The Random Forest algorithm (Breiman, 2001) was implemented using the QIIME2 plugin with default settings. Nested stratified 5-fold cross-validation was used for automated hyperparameter optimization and sample prediction. Prediction of functional pathways within microbial communities was accomplished via the Phylogenetic Investigation of Communities by Reconstruction of Unobserved States (PIRCUSt2) (Gavin M. Douglas et al., preprint), targeting the Metabolic Pathways From all Domains of Life (MetaCyc) database. Significant differences in metabolic pathways between groups were identified using STAMP software (Version 2.1.3). Statistical significance was determined using Welch’s t-test, augmented by Storey’s FDR procedure for multiple testing correction, with a threshold for corrected p-values set at ≤ 0.05.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using R (Version 4.3.3) and SPSS (version 26). We used chi-squared and t-tests to compare clinical data differences between IgG4-ROD patients and HC. The comparisons of continuous variables across groups were analyzed through the two-tailed Wilcoxon rank-sum test. To ascertain the significance of bacterial communities, we employed the Adonis function from the vegan package in R, with 999 permutation tests. The associations between bacterial genera and clinical cytokines in patients were calculated using Spearman’s correlation coefficient analysis with the corrplot R package (v0.92). Only bacterial genera detected in more than four samples were retained in this analysis. A statistically significant p-value was considered to be < 0.05.

Alterations of microbial composition between IgG4-ROD and HC

The differences in microbial communities between IgG4-ROD patients and HC were evaluated. Compared to HC, IgG4-ROD patients showed a decrease in alpha diversity, as measured by the Shannon and Simpson indices (P < 0.05) (Fig. 1A). IgG4-ROD patients also showed a lower Pielou’s evenness index, indicating less species evenness. (P < 0.05) (Fig. 1A). The beta diversity was quantified using Principal Coordinate Analysis (PCoA) based on the Bray-Curtis distance matrix. The results indicated a significant divergence in the overall microbiome structure between IgG4-ROD patients and HC, as confirmed by a PERMANOVA test (F = 1.7448, P = 0.021, P < 0.05) (Fig. 1B). A taxonomic overview was performed to characterize the gut microbial composition in both groups. At the phylum level, Firmicutes, Proteobacteria, Actinobacteria, and Bacteroidetes were predominant in both groups. At the genus level, Faecalibacterium, Blautia, Bifidobacterium, and Gemmiger were the most prevalent (Fig. 1C). We conducted a detailed analysis to compare the abundance of gut bacteria in patients with IgG4-ROD and HC. LEfSe analysis revealed that IgG4-ROD patients had significantly higher concentrations of specific taxa, including Gammaproteobacteria, Proteobacteria, Parvimonas, Neisseria, Novosphingobium, Methylobacterium, Shigella, Bulleidia, and Allobaculum. Both IgE + and IgE- groups showed lower diversity in their GM compared to HC, based on the Simpson index (P < 0.05) (Fig. 2A). Microbial composition of IgG4-ROD patients with and without glucocorticoid therapy

Patients who were untreated had elevated levels of Methylobacterium, Allobaculum, Aeromonadales, and Sutterella. Functional prediction of the gut microbiota in IgG4-ROD patients and HC

To explore the influence of gut microbiota on the development of IgG4-ROD, we analyzed and identified 120 metabolic pathways with significant differential abundance between IgG4-ROD and HC (corrected p-value < 0.05) (Fig. 5A). Among these pathways, significant enrichments were observed in glucose metabolism and short-chain fatty acid synthesis pathways. Differences in pathway abundance were also noted within subgroups: IgE + versus IgE-, IgG4 + versus IgG4-, IgG4 + versus HC, and treated versus untreated IgG4-ROD patients. However, these differences did not retain significance after correction for multiple comparisons (corrected p-value > 0.05). Associations of gut microbiota and serum cytokines in IgG4-ROD patients

To investigate the potential role of immune cytokines in the pathogenesis of IgG4-ROD, we assessed the correlations between gut microbiota and serum cytokines in IgG4-ROD patients (Fig. 6). A positive correlation was observed between Bifidobacterium and IL-5 (p = 0.01). Conversely, Gemmiger was negatively associated with IL-1β and IL-2 (p = 0.002 and p = 0.037, respectively). Bacteroides showed an inverse association with several cytokines: IL-1β, IL-2, IL-6, IL-8, and IFN-α (p < 0.05). Dialister demonstrated negative correlations with IL-2, IL-6, IL-8, and IL-10 (p < 0.05). Additionally, Streptococcus and [Ruminococcus] were found to be positively related to IgA and IgM (p < 0.05). Clostridium was negatively associated with C4 (p = 0.04), while Methylobacterium showed a positive relationship with C3 (p = 0.023). Lower abundances of Parabacteroides and Lachnospiraceae-Clostridium were linked to higher concentrations of IL-1β (p < 0.05). Allobaculum, which increased in IgG4-ROD patients, was positively correlated with IgG4 levels (p = 0.035). In contrast, Lachnospiraceae_Clostridium demonstrated a decrease in the IgG4 + group and was negatively associated with IgG4 levels (p = 0.013). Our findings indicated that the gut microbiota may contribute to the development of IgG4-ROD by modulating cytokine levels. Discussion

In this study, we performed 16 S rRNA sequencing to explore gut dysbiosis in IgG4-ROD patients and evaluate the effects of serum IgE, IgG4, and hormone therapy. Previous studies have reported the correlation between IgG4-RD and GM [12, 13, 15, 16]. Yoshikawa reported that intestinal dysbiosis mediated experimental autoimmune pancreatitis (AIP) and IgG4-RD by activating plasmacytoid dendritic cells (pDCs), which can produce IFN-α and IL-33 [13]. Liu found that the depletion of Blautia and elevated succinate may contribute to IgG4-related sclerosing cholangitis [12]. Plichta compared the microbiome signatures of IgG4-RD and systemic sclerosis [15]. Koshida revealed the impact of the GM on serum IgG4 levels in the general population [16]. However, the bacteria they reported were not fully compatible with the bacterial genera described in this study.

Our research showed that Proteobacteria and Allobaculum were increased in the IgG4-ROD patients as well as in the IgE + group. Proteobacteria is a potent inducer of inflammation and is associated with the exacerbation of allergic rhinitis [18]. Such microbial shift may contribute to elevated serum IgE levels and be potentially connected to the allergic conditions observed in IgG4-ROD patients. Allobaculum, on the other hand, showed a positive correlation with IgG4 levels in our study, which is consistent with previous findings. It has been reported to trigger specific serum IgA and IgG responses and to exacerbate colitis in mice [19]. Our results suggest that Allobaculum may play a role in the immunomodulation of serum IgE and IgG4 in IgG4-ROD, thereby potentially promoting disease progression. Meanwhile, Parvimonas, Novosphingobium, and Shigella were found to be enriched in IgG4-ROD patients. Parvimonas is associated with promoting inflammatory responses and colorectal tumorigenesis [20, 21], while the lower abundance of Novosphingobium in tumor tissues is correlated with improved colorectal cancer outcomes [22]. In addition, Shigella has been reported to be significantly increased in pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma patients compared to HC [23]. Conversely, Barnesiella and Weissella were decreased in IgG4-ROD patients. Oral administration of live Barnesiella intestinihominis has been shown to considerably ameliorate liver metabolic disorders in type 2 diabetes models [24]. Barnesiella has been associated with a reduced risk of seropositive rheumatoid arthritis [25]. Our study suggests that Barnesiella may play a role in immune regulation and could serve as a potential therapeutic target in IgG4-ROD. Additionally, Weissella could facilitate intestinal mucosal barrier function by modulating gut bacteria and propionate levels [26]. Collectively, these findings highlight the complex relationship between the GM and immune regulation in IgG4-ROD, offering potential targets for future therapeutic interventions.

Firmicutes were found to be abundant in HC. Research shows that Firmicutes could induce systemic IL-34 and enhance immune defenses through this pathway [27]. Meanwhile, butyrate is a member of short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs), which could significantly inhibit neutrophils to produce proinflammatory cytokines [28]. Ruminococcaceae, as a major butyrate producer, was notably enriched in the HC and IgG4- groups. Our study also showed a positive correlation between [Ruminococcus] and serum IgA and IgM levels. Given that low serum IgA has been identified as a risk factor for airway hyper-responsiveness [29], the presence of [Ruminococcus] may confer protective benefits against developing IgG4-ROD. Additionally, Parabacteroides was negatively correlated with IL-1β, IL-2 and IL-6, and was positively correlated with C1q, suggesting its potential anti-inflammatory roles in this disease. These findings suggest a protective role of these microbes in IgG4-ROD and highlight the therapeutic potential of modulating Firmicutes, [Ruminococcus] or butyrate levels to restore immune balance.

In subjects with elevated serum IgE levels, an increased abundance of Enterobacteriaceae was observed. Previous studies have shown that IBD is often associated with dysbiosis and enrichment of Enterobacteriaceae, and persistence of Enterobacteriaceae helps to perpetuate intestinal inflammation and nosocomial infections with other microbes [30]. Faecalibacterium could alleviate experimental colitis by modulating Treg/Th17 cell balance and intestinal barrier integrity [31], and it was reduced in the IgG4-ROD and increased in the IgE- group. Our findings suggest that elevated serum IgE levels may contribute to the pathogenesis of IgG4-ROD, potentially leading to more severe inflammatory and allergic reactions in these patients than those with normal IgE levels.

Compared with the IgG4- group, Sphingomonas and Peptostreptococcaceae were abundant in the IgG4 + group. Sphingomonas levels decreased in patients with acute severe ulcerative colitis treated with exclusive enteral nutrition and conjugated corticosteroid therapy [32]. Peptostreptococcaceae has been reported to have negative associations with Crohn’s disease [33]. These species have not been reported in other IgG4-RDs, highlighting a potentially unique microbial signature in IgG4-ROD. Conversely, Blautia was decreased in the IgG4 + group, consistent with IgG4-related sclerosing cholangitis [12]. It was also associated with a high risk of allergic asthma, a condition commonly observed in IgG4-ROD patients, suggesting its role in allergic inflammatory pathways [34]. Besides, Adlercreutzia and Acidimicrobiales were enriched in the IgG4- group. Adlercreutzia is recognized as an anti-inflammatory commensal bacterium [34, 35], which might confer protection against disease progression. Interestingly, Acidimicrobiales has also not been reported in other IgG4-RDs, which further underscores a distinctive microbial profile in IgG4-ROD patients. In addition, Pelomonas and Escherichia were increased in IgG4-ROD patients treated with glucocorticosteroids. Such elevation suggests that these genera have the potential to serve as predictive markers of IgG4-ROD response to steroid therapy.

In our study, both Gemmiger and Bacteroides showed negative responses to IL-1β and IL-2 levels. Similarly, Lachnospiraceae-Clostridium was negatively associated with IL-1β, and its abundance was significantly reduced in the IgG4 + group. IL-1β is a potent pro-inflammatory cytokine that could induce type 1 allergic reaction [36]. Prior research indicated that IL-1β plays an important role in eosinophilia, IgE class switching, and Th2-mediated inflammation in allergic conditions [37]. IgG4-ROD appears to involve some of the same pathogenic mechanisms observed in allergic disease, such as Th2 and Treg activation, hypersecretion of IgG4 and IgE, and blood/tissue eosinophilia [7]. Furthermore, Bacteroides and Dialister were negatively correlated with IL-2, IL-6, and IL-8. Research shows that IL-2 is essential in inhibiting the secretion of IgE and IgG4 induced by IL-4 and is critical for maintaining FOXP3 + Tregs [38]. The absence of IL-2 results in severe Treg deficiency, which can lead to autoimmune diseases [39]. The negative correlation between these bacteria and IL-2 is not straightforward and warrants further investigation. This complexity suggests that the relationship between GM and immune regulation in IgG4-ROD is multifaceted, possibly involving indirect mechanisms. Future studies are needed to clarify these interactions and their clinical implications. Additionally, Bacteroides and Ruminococcus were negatively correlated with IFN-α. As mentioned before, activation of pDC mediates the chronic fibroinflammatory response in AIP [13]. Notably, Ruminococcus abounded in the HC and IgG4- groups, indicating a potential protective role in preventing the pathogenesis of IgG4-ROD. Finally, Methylobacterium correlated positively with C3 and was prevalent in IgG4-ROD and patients not treated with glucocorticoids. Thus, the levels of C3 may reflect treatment status and serve as a potential marker for monitoring therapeutic response in IgG4-ROD. Our findings suggest that the proliferation of pathogenic bacteria and reduction of probiotics in IgG4-ROD patients drive the onset and progression of the disease.

The functional analysis revealed elevated activity of the L-tryptophan, L-phenylalanine, and L-tyrosine biosynthesis superpathways in IgG4-ROD patients. These pathways are crucial for synthesizing essential amino acids in the human body. In addition, IgG4-ROD patients have reduced levels of L-lysine fermentation to the acetate and butyrate pathways. Which is consistent with our finding of a reduced abundance of butyrate-producing bacteria in IgG4-ROD patients. Both acetate and butanoate are SCFAs that play significant roles in maintaining intestinal health by enhancing mucosal integrity, increasing the number and function of Treg, and decreasing the expression of inflammatory cytokines [40]. Therefore, the gut microbial disorders in IgG4-ROD patients may cause decreased production of SCFAs, which may be involved in the occurrence of the disease. This suggests that restoring SCFA levels through dietary interventions, probiotics, or microbial modulation may offer novel therapeutic strategies.

Our study has several limitations. First, we did not exclude confounding factors such as the participants’ dietary habits. Second, many IgG4-ROD patients had received systemic glucocorticoid therapy before enrollment. Finally, given the limited prevalence of IgG4-ROD patients, our investigation had a relatively small sample size. The conclusions drawn require further validation. More extensive studies are needed to confirm these findings and to fully understand the relationship between IgG4-ROD and GM.

Conclusion

This study conducted a comparative analysis of gut microbiota between IgG4-ROD patients and HC, exploring the roles of serum IgE and IgG4 to identify potential disease-specific biomarkers. We observed significant gut dysbiosis in IgG4-ROD patients, characterized by increased pathogenic bacteria and decreased commensal bacteria, which varied across different patient types. These microbiome alterations correlated with disease activity, suggesting their critical role in the pathogenesis of IgG4-ROD. Our findings provide insights into the microbial and clinical interactions within IgG4-ROD.
