One-carbon metabolism and risk of PCOS: insights from Mendelian randomization
Authors: Yifan Zhao, Gaochen Zhang, Jiexue Pan, Hefeng Huang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAbnormal one-carbon metabolism has been reported by observational studies to contribute to an increased risk of polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS) and undesirable reproductive outcomes in women with PCOS. However, the underlying causal associations remain uninvestigated. This study aims to assess the independent causal links between essential substrates, enzymes and cofactors involved in one-carbon metabolism and PCOS, using bidirectional two-sample Mendelian randomization (MR) and Multivariable MR.ResultsGenetically predicted high levels of folate (OR = 2.92; 95% CI, 1.81 − 3.98; P = 8.32 × 10− 4), DHFR (OR = 1.25; 95% CI, 1.11–1.41; P = 1.68 × 10− 4) were causally related to an increased risk of PCOS. Increasing individuals’ long-term exposure to circulating vitamin B6 would mitigate their risk of developing PCOS (OR = 0.56; 95% CI, 0.34 − 0.91; P = 6.34 × 10− 3). Additionally, suffering from PCOS was revealed to possibly contribute to vitamin B12 deficiency (OR = 0.99; 95% CI, 0.98–1.00; P = 3.61 × 10–2).ConclusionsThis study integrates metabolomics and genomics to evaluate the causal effects of genetically determined one-carbon metabolism-related factors on PCOS risk, highlighting the critical role of one-carbon metabolism in maintaining reproductive health. Background

Polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS) is a lifelong and complex condition characterized by endocrine disruption and metabolic disorders. It affects 11–13% of women worldwide, possibly leading to infertility and poor health-related quality of life (HRQoL) [1–3]. The increasing prevalence of PCOS imposes a considerable health and financial burden worldwide. PCOS is diagnosed following the Rotterdam criteria [1] and manifests as a constellation of abnormalities including chronic anovulation, clinical or biochemical hyperandrogenism, and the presence of polycystic ovaries [2, 3]. Epidemiological evidence has suggested that women with PCOS often present various comorbidities such as obesity (20–80%), hyperandrogenism (40–80%), insulin resistance (50–80%), and increased risk of psychological disorders (four-fold) and endometrial cancer (five-fold) [3–11]. However, the management and treatment of PCOS remain expediential owing to its substantially understudied mechanisms, highlighting the urgent need for research to delineate the etiology and pathophysiology of this syndrome. Identifying modifiable risk factors for PCOS will pave the way for the development of effective interventions and therapeutic strategies.

One-carbon units are carried by tetrahydrofolate (THF) cofactors to participate in metabolic reactions, collectively known as one-carbon metabolism. One-carbon metabolism plays a crucial role in the vastly complex network of biochemical and cellular processes across tissues [12], encompassing de novo synthesis of nucleic acids, production of amino acids, and synthesis of methyl donors, such as S-adenosylmethionine (SAM) (Fig. 1). Folate, vitamin B12, and vitamin B6 are interrelated indispensable constituents involved in this process. Folate can reduce the number of preantral and antral follicles, thereby improving the quality of oocytes in patients with PCOS [13–16]. Oral supplementation with vitamins B6 and B12 has been proposed by several case–control and prospective epidemiological studies as a therapeutic intervention to ameliorate endocrine abnormalities and symptoms in individuals with PCOS [17–26].

Although the evidence is inconclusive, low levels of vitamin B6 and high levels of folate and total homocysteine (tHcy) have been hypothesized to be associated with a high risk of PCOS. Insufficient serum folate and vitamin B6 interfere with one-carbon metabolism and lead to DNA hypomethylation and epigenetic alterations, which are assumed to cooperate with aberrant histone modification, inducing the development of PCOS [27, 28]. On the other hand, women with PCOS tend to exhibit decreased levels of vitamins B6 and B12, and supraphysiological serum levels of folate and tHcy, which could negatively affect their metabolic and hormonal outcomes [29]. Furthermore, the levels of key enzymes involved in folate metabolism, such as 5-methyltetrahydrofolate-homocysteine methyltransferase reductase (MTRR), 5,10-methylenetetrahydrofolate reductase (MTHFR), and dihydrofolate reductase (DHFR), and polymorphisms, such as MTRR A66G, RFC-1 A80G, MTHFR C677T, and MTHFR A1298C are associated with PCOS [30–33]. However, whether an abnormal one-carbon metabolism profile influences PCOS etiology prior to the development of diagnostic hallmarks of this condition, such as clinical or biochemical hyperandrogenism and specific ovarian morphological characteristics, and the underlying mechanisms, are rarely studied and remain inconclusive.

Existing evidence from observational studies is inconsistent due to heterogeneity in the population and sample size [17, 22, 26, 28]. As they are cross-sectional, these studies are prone to substantial bias due to residual confounding and reverse causation (the comorbidities of PCOS such as obesity and insulin resistance, affect the levels of one-carbon metabolism-related factors). While randomized controlled trials (RCTs) could help determine the effects of one-carbon metabolism-related exposures and the risk or progression of PCOS, they are scarce or inconclusive at present [30–35]. The enormous consumption of human and material resources and ethical concerns limit their practicality. These challenges and key limitations should be overcome by other novel study designs before establishing causal effects and integrating these interventions and therapies into clinical practice.

Mendelian randomization (MR) is a nonexperimental approach used in epidemiology to explore the causal effect of an exposure on an outcome from a genetic point of view [36–38]. It involves the use of instrumental variables (IVs) that are robustly associated with a certain trait, minimizing the risk of potential confounding bias and reverse causality [38, 39]. Genome-wide association studies (GWASs) and GWAS meta-analyses have further strengthened the efficiency and feasibility of the MR method owing to the remarkable datasets [40, 41]. PCOS is a hereditary polygenic disorder, and genetically determined differences in circulating exposure between individuals are long-term and unaffected by self-reported or self-selected behaviors or environmental factors. Distinguishing significant genetic variants of exposure factors and their causal associations with PCOS may enhance the understanding of the development and management of this disease. To date, studies involving the use of MR have examined the causal effects of body mass index (BMI), type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), coronary heart disease (CHD), age at menopause/menarche, and the gut microbiota on PCOS [34, 42–44].

In the present study, we used MR to investigate the potential causal links between genetically determined one-carbon metabolism-related factors and PCOS on the basis of summary statistics from recent large-scale GWASs.

Methods

Study design

Bidirectional two-sample MR was performed to assess the causal effects between serum levels of B-group vitamins (folate, vitamin B6, and vitamin B12), key enzymes (DHFR and 5,10-methenyltetrahydrofolate synthetase [MTHFS]), and tHcy, which are involved in one-carbon metabolism, and the risk of PCOS from a genetic perspective. Multivariable MR (MVMR) was used to provide additional information on the direct causal effect of one-carbon metabolism-related biochemical indicators on PCOS. We adjusted for underlying confounding factors and aimed to identify independent risk factors for PCOS [45, 46]. An overview of the study design is shown in Fig. 2. The study protocol and details were not preregistered.

To achieve optimal interpretation, the following three assumptions for MR should be satisfied [47]: [1] relevance: IVs should be closely associated with the exposure; [2] exclusion: IVs should affect the outcome only through the exposure, without any direct relationship with the outcome; and [3] independence: IVs should not be associated with any confounding factors that strengthen or weaken the exposure–outcome relationship. We used single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) as IVs for both bidirectional two-sample MR and MVMR.

Data sources and study populations

As of March 28, 2024, the GWAS Catalog contained 4 961 publications, 251 401 top associations, and 72 655 full summary statistics. Owing to the remarkable datasets, GWASs can help explain the heritability of complex traits or diseases [40, 41]. To search for genetic instruments for one-carbon metabolism-related factors, we prioritized consistent, replicated findings from GWASs (categorized as continuous variables) for the identification of SNPs that determine differences in these serum biochemical markers. The GWAS datasets for levels of folate, vitamin B6, and vitamin B12 were obtained from the MRC Integrative Epidemiology Unit (IEU) OpenGWAS project, which includes 64 979 European participants and 9 851 867 SNPs [48]. SNPs associated with MTHFS and DHFR were obtained from another dataset containing 3 301 individuals [49]. Genetic instruments of branched-chain amino acids (BCAAs), adjusted for age, BMI, and obesity at assessment were accessed from 115 047 participants [50]. SNPs associated with circulating tHcy levels were obtained from a published meta-analysis of GWASs involving 44 147 participants, in which 18 SNPs reached genome-wide significance [51].

Two distinct GWAS datasets of PCOS were obtained from FinnGen biobank analysis round 10 [52] and a recent large-scale meta-analysis by Day et al. [53]. From the FinnGen project, GWAS results of 642 patients with a definitive diagnosis of PCOS on the basis of the Rotterdam criteria [54] and a control group of 118 228 individuals were retrieved, including 16 379 676 SNPs. The large-scale genome-wide meta-analysis conducted by Day et al. [53] involved 10 074 cases and 103 164 controls without PCOS from the following six cohorts: Rotterdam, London/Oxford, EGCUT, deCODE, Chicago, and Boston. According to its inclusion criteria, PCOS is diagnosed based on either National Institutes of Health (NIH) or non-NIH standards, which is inconsistent with the standards of diagnosis in the FinnGen project. Epidemiological evidence indicates that phenotypic variation between these definitions can influence associated metabolic and reproductive traits, potentially introducing heterogeneity into genetic association estimates [55, 56]. To improve phenotypic comparability, in this study, we included only four cohorts (Rotterdam, London/Oxford, EGCUT, and deCODE) from Day et al. [53], which reduced the number of cases (2 669 of 10 074).

All participants included in this study were of European descent, which minimized population stratification. Because the data used for analysis were obtained from published studies and public databases, the requirement of ethical approval from an institutional review board or written informed consent from participants was waived. Selection of genetic variants

Genetic variants are predetermined factors that are established prior to an individual’s birth, and exhibit stability throughout an individual’s lifespan, which renders them suitable as IVs and facilitates their replication and validation across diverse study populations. Changes in the genetic variants will lead to increased levels of biochemical indications and consequently to changes in the outcome for all individuals in the population. Independent SNPs with genome-wide significance (P < 5 × 10–8) were selected from the GWAS summary statistics as IVs. We performed a clumping procedure with an r2 value of < 0.001 and a window size of 10 000 kb to remove linkage disequilibrium (LD), which ensured the validity of the IVs and analytical results [37]. SNPs with a minor allele frequency (MAF) of < 0.01 were excluded, because they contribute minimal genetic information and potentially increase the risk of false-positive results. The proportion of variance explained (PVE) in the phenotype by each SNP was computed using the pertinent covariance components described in the results of GWASs to determine whether a single SNP primarily influenced the causality in each exposure–outcome group [57]. Candidate IVs associated with folate, DHFR, MTHFS, tHcy, vitamin B6, vitamin B12, and BCAAs were analyzed for violations of exclusion and independence assumptions using the GWAS Catalog [58] and the PhenoScanner database (version 2) [59, 60]. Palindromic SNPs with intermediate allele frequencies were removed through harmonization, and SNPs that were directly related to PCOS or its confounders (BMI, T2DM, waist-to-hip ratio, and obesity) were excluded by executing the pcf function in R software. We harmonized the SNP–PCOS associations from FinnGen cohort and the subset of the Day et al. [53] meta-analysis in the main MR analyses, using identical effect allele orientation, and combined them using inverse-variance weighted fixed-effect meta-analysis at the summary statistic level to derive a pooled genetic association estimate for each instrument.

The statistical power of IVs was assessed via the web version of mRnd [61]. The F statistic was calculated for each SNP individually and cumulatively using four parameters: (1) the sample size, (2) the percentage of cases among the subjects, (3) the true odds ratio (OR) of the outcome variable based on each standard deviation (SD) of the exposure variable, and (4) the PVE for the association between the SNPs and the exposure variable at a type 1 error rate of 5%. Statistical analyses

After appropriate IVs were selected, five classical MR approaches, namely, the Wald ratio [63], inverse variance weighted (IVW) [64, 65], MR-Egger [66], weighted median [67], simple mode, and weighted mode [68], were used to evaluate the causal effects of folate, DHFR, MTHFS, tHcy, vitamin B6, vitamin B12, and BCAAs on PCOS. The Wald ratio is an MR approach for deriving causal estimates when the exposure is associated with only one IV, calculating the coefficient of the SNP‒outcome association divided by the coefficient of the SNP‒exposure association [63]. The IVW was applied under a multiplicative random-effects model as the primary and preferred method for MR estimates with more than one SNP. It calculates a weighted average of Wald ratios and can yield the most reliable outcomes when there is no evidence of directional pleiotropy [69]. A more robust method in this situation is the combination of IVW and MR-Egger regression, which generates less pleiotropic bias but at the expense of statistical power.

The model assumptions of MR can be undesirably biased via combinations of potential confounders and alternate pathway(s), producing an invalid assessment of the causality of interest. Epidemiological studies have shown that each increase of 1 unit in BMI is associated with a 1% decrease in the serum folate concentration and a greater risk of PCOS [70–73]. A recent MR study reported a causal relationship between elevated BMI and the risk of PCOS [74]. Therefore, PhenoScanner [59, 60] was employed to identify IVs related to a range of traits and diseases, such as BMI, waist-to-hip ratio, T2DM, and obesity, that might serve as confounding variables and excluded them using the pcf function in R software from exposure and outcome samples [3].

Under a two-sample MR framework, we initially conducted the analysis on one-carbon metabolism-related factors and subsequently reversed the direction of the analysis to assess whether PCOS had any causal effects on these factors. The MR Steiger directionality test was used to rule out reverse causality. MVMR extends univariable MR by modeling multiple exposures simultaneously. In MVMR, IVs are combined through multivariable regression frameworks (two-stage least squares or multivariable IVW) to estimate direct causal effects of multiple biologically correlated exposures. This approach isolates exposure-specific effects by leveraging genetic variants as simultaneous instruments for multiple traits, while requiring stricter assumptions than univariable MR [39, 56, 75, 76]. In this study, we used MVMR to detect whether PCOS is causally related to any genetically predicted one-carbon metabolism-related factors independently.

Sensitivity analysis and heterogeneity test

Where IVW models were conducted, we repeated analyses using MR-Egger regression [66], weighted median [67], and MR pleiotropy residual sum and outlier (MR-PRESSO) to evaluate the reliability and precision of the MR results, which encompass the uncertainty in folate metabolism-related exposures more accurately than the simple weighting applied in IVW. In MR-Egger analysis, a P value of > 0.05 (indicating a zero intercept) suggested the absence of horizontal pleiotropy and the slope coefficient provided a reliable indication of causality. Additionally, funnel plots were constructed to illustrate the existence of horizontal pleiotropy. The MR-PRESSO method was used to detect and exclude potential outlier SNPs (P < 0.05) to adjust for both horizontal and vertical pleiotropies among the selected IVs [77].

If multiple variants were available, Cochran’s Q test was used to measure the heterogeneity of the estimations from each SNP [65]. A P value of > 0.05 suggested the absence of statistically significant heterogeneity, in which case a fixed effect model was used for data analysis. Furthermore, leave-one-out analysis was performed to assess the robustness and consistency of the estimates.

Software

All the statistical analyses mentioned above were performed using the “TwosampleMR” (version 0.5.7), “MendelianRandomization” (version 0.9.0), “phenoscanner (version 2.0)”, “MR-PRESSO,” “Fdrtool” (version 1.2.17), and “Forestplot” (version 3.1.3) packages in R software (version 4.2.3). The P values were corrected using a false discovery rate (FDR) to represent statistically significant causality. Traditional techniques, such as the Bonferroni correction, were avoided as they exhibit excessive conservatism and concurrently decrease the counts of true-positive and false-positive results. The results were reformatted by exponentiation to be expressed as odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for PCOS according to a 1-SD increase in genetically predicted circulating levels of one-carbon metabolism-related factors and BCAAs.

Results

Selection of instrumental variables

A total of 3, 12, 11, 4, 18, and 44 SNPs significantly associated (P < 5 × 10− 8) with folate, DHFR, MTHFS, vitamin B6, tHcy, and BCAAs, respectively, were obtained from public GWAS datasets and meta-analyses, whereas none qualified as an IV for vitamin B12. PhenoScanner (version 2) was applied to identify phenotypes associated with the filtered SNPs; of which, 11 SNPs (1 for DHFR, 1 for MTHFS, 3 for tHcy, and 6 for BCAAs) strongly associated with BMI, waist-to-hip ratio (adjusted for BMI), T2DM, and obesity were removed by executing the pcf function. After harmonization, 1 SNP for folate, 10 SNPs for DHFR, 10 SNPs for MTHFS, 1 SNP for vitamin B6, 15 SNPs for tHcy, and 35 SNPs for BCAAs were used for two-sample MR analyses. Supplementary Table 1 presents comprehensive details for each IV, including the effect allele (EA) and its frequency (EAF), β values, standard errors (SEs), and corresponding P values. The average PVE of each SNP was 2.18%, 15.3%, 15.7%, 2.25%, 3.85%, and 2.28% for folate, DHFR, MTHFS, vitamin B6, tHcy, and BCAAs, respectively. The calculated F-statistic values of all the selected IVs exceeded 10 (Supplementary Table 1), indicating the absence of weak instrument bias. There were no individuals overlapping between the exposure and outcome studies.

Main univariable Mendelian randomization

According to the primary results, each SD increase in the genetically predicted circulating levels of folate (IVW OR = 3.39; 95% CI, 1.50 − 5.50; P = 8.62 × 10− 3; FDR = 6.76 × 10− 3) and DHFR (IVW OR = 1.16; 95% CI, 1.01 − 1.33; P = 3.29 × 10− 2; FDR = 2.08 × 10− 2) was causally associated with an elevated risk of PCOS (Fig. 3). Notably, the only genome-wide significant SNP with a strong signal for the causal association between folate and PCOS (rs76630415, P = 2.40 × 10− 8) is at the DGKB (diacylglycerol kinase beta) locus. While DGKB has beenassociated with developmental and epileptic encephalopathy and the rs2191349 variant influences fasting glucose [78, 79], the effect of rs76630415 locus on folate metabolism or PCOS traits remains unstudied. Given our reliance on this single SNP for folate instrumentation, we cannot exclude potential pleiotropic effects on the risk of PCOS through non-folate pathways. Therefore, the effect sizes should be interpreted cautiously. In addition, the protective effects of MTHFS (IVW OR: 0.78; 95% CI: 0.68, 0.90; P = 5.56 × 10− 4; FDR = 6.54 × 10− 4) and BCAAs (IVW OR: 0.30; 95% CI: 0.16, 0.55; P = 1.07 × 10− 4; FDR = 2.52 × 10− 4) on PCOS were revealed (Fig. 3). There were no apparent differences in the risk of PCOS according to genetically predicted increases in exposure to circulating tHcy (IVW OR = 1.09; 95% CI, 0.89 − 1.34; P = 4.00 × 10− 1; FDR = 2.05 × 10− 1) or vitamin B6 (Wald ratio OR = 0.53; 95% CI, 0.01 − 1.33; P = 5.75 × 10− 1; FDR = 2.71 × 10− 1) (Fig. 3). The absence of genome-wide significant instruments for circulating vitamin B₁₂ levels constrained our ability to perform forward MR analyses for this metabolite.

These sizes and directions of effects were also strongly supported by MR-Egger regression, weighted median, and weighted mode methods. Because only one IV was used to instrument levels of folate or vitamin B6, these alternative MR methods were not applicable for these traits. MR-Egger regression (P > 0.05 for the MR-Egger intercept) and MR-PRESSO test (P > 0.05) provided no evidence of directional pleiotropy in any exposure–outcome pair of interest, except for BCAAs (Supplementary Tables 2 and 3). Although MR-PRESSO found no outlier concerning BCAAs, MR-Egger intercept tests were significant (P = 0.05), exhibiting some evidence of pleiotropy (Supplementary Table 3). For multi-SNP exposures, we saw no signs of heterogeneity (Cochran’s Q P > 0.05), which supports the consistency of SNP effects (Supplementary Table 4). Forest plots were also constructed to illustrate MR estimates of the effects of the SNPs representing DHFR, MTHFS, tHcy, and BCAAs on the risk of PCOS (Fig. 5). Funnel plots displayed symmetry, suggesting that the pleiotropic effects of IVs were evenly distributed around the overall impact (Supplementary Fig. 1). Additionally, when each SNP was excluded from the analysis, no specific SNPs were found to be responsible for the results, which indicated an overall combined pattern of independent relationships between each group of IVs and PCOS (Supplementary Fig. 2).

To further evaluate the trade-off between minimizing heterogeneity and preserving statistical power, we conducted additional MR analyses involved all cohorts from the Day et al. meta-analysis [53]. Results remained concordant with our original findings, supporting their robustness (Supplementary Fig. 3), which eases worries that our case definition restrictions skewed the results. Therefore, our restriction ensured phenotypic homogeneity and strong IVs (F-statistics > 10).

To enhance the statistical power of genetic instruments by including more SNPs, the genome-wide significance threshold was set to a P-value of < 5 × 10− 6, and we repeated two-sample MR analyses. With this change, the numbers of SNPs associated with folate, vitamin B6, and vitamin B12 increased to 23, 29, and 24, respectively (Supplementary Table 6). The average PVE by genetics was 0.48% for folate, 1.82% for vitamin B6, and 1.80% for vitamin B12. The IVs for folate exhibited weaker instrumental power with smaller R2 statistics, suggesting that they may not be robustly confirmed determinants of the variations. In this round of analyses, the causality between folate levels and the risk of PCOS yielded an IVW estimate with an OR of 1.00 (95% CI, 0.62 − 2.38; P = 0.58; Supplementary Table 7). Higher predicted exposure to circulating vitamin B6 (IVW OR = 0.83; 95% CI, 0.29 − 2.28; P = 0.72), or vitamin B12 (IVW OR = 0.74; 95% CI, 0.17 − 3.15; P = 0.68) was not significantly associated with an increased risk of PCOS (Supplementary Table 7). MR-PRESSO (folate: P = 0.59; vitamin B6: P = 0.86; vitamin B12: P = 0.36; Supplementary Table 2) and Cochran’s Q test (folate: IVW model Q test P value = 0.26; vitamin B6: IVW model Q test P value = 0.68; vitamin B12: IVW model Q test P value = 0.22; Supplementary Table 4) revealed that no outliers or heterogeneity influenced these estimations. However, MR-Egger regression (folate: P = 0.04; vitamin B6: P = 0.25; vitamin B12: P = 0.59; Supplementary Table 3) detected horizontal pleiotropy in the effects of folate-associated SNPs on PCOS. Therefore, to maintain methodological consistency, the results derived from the second round of two-sample MR analyses were rigorously considered invalid because of the bias introduced by a decreased P-value threshold (P < 5 × 10 − 6) for selecting folate IVs.

Reverse Mendelian randomization

Considering a genetic predisposition to PCOS as an exposure, we conducted reverse MR analysis to examine the causal effects of PCOS on factors involved in folate metabolism and BCAAs. One (average PVE = 2.07%) and seventeen (average PVE = 5.18%) qualified SNPs for PCOS were extracted from the Finnish biobank datasets for reverse MR analysis, respectively, with two different thresholds of P < 5 × 10− 8 and P < 5 × 10− 6 (Supplementary Table 5). No discernable correlations were detected between PCOS and genetically determined differences in the levels of folate, MTHFS, DHFR, vitamin B6, tHcy, or BCAAs (Table 2). Significant causality from PCOS to decreased levels of vitamin B12 (IVW OR = 0.99; 95% CI, 0.98 − 1.00; P = 3.61 × 10–2; Table 2) was found, without any evidence of heterogeneity or pleiotropy. According to the research by Day et al. [53], rs804279 in the GATA4/NEIL2 locus demonstrated significant heterogeneity among the groups and was consequently excluded from this study. When using 14 SNPs for PCOS selected from the latest meta-analysis [52], reverse MR analysis generated no significant causal effects of PCOS on one-carbon metabolism-related factors or BCAAs (Table 2) across the five MR methods. The MR Steiger test consistently supported the directional assumption of causality (Supplementary Table 4). The F-statistic values of all IVs for PCOS were consistently > 10, suggesting that there was no weak instrumental bias. The MR-PRESSO test did not reveal any potential outliers. Leave-one-out analysis did not detect any high-leverage SNPs that dominated the relationship. These results derived respectively from the two sources of PCOS GWAS data showed consistent direction and confirmed the unidirectional causal effects between the exposures and the outcome examined in the main MR analyses.

Multivariable Mendelian randomization analysis

MVMR was applied to disentangle direct effects of folate, DHFR, MTHFS, and vitamin B6 on PCOS, free from multiple intermediary factors, by partialling out correlated genetic impacts across these biologically interconnected exposures. Initial variant extraction identified 22 SNPs (P < 5 × 10− 8), among which 3 SNPs were removed due to LD with other variants or absence from LD reference panel (Supplementary Table 8). rs676457 was excluded for being palindromic with intermediate allele frequencies. After harmonization, 18 SNPs were used as IVs for the four exposures in MVMR analysis. IVs for PCOS were obtained from FinnGen R10 and the meta-analysis by Day et al. [53]. We queried the PhenoScanner database (version 2) [59, 60] to identify confounding factors, such as SNPs associated with BMI, waist-to-hip ratio, T2DM, and obesity, from inside R, and applied the pcf function to remove them from the analysis. In MVMR, evaluating instrument strength for multiple exposures is equivalent to collinearity test in conventional regression. Following the method proposed by Sanderson et al. [80], conditional F-statistics were calculated for each exposure and all were above 10 (folate: F = 12.73; DHFR: F = 29.55; MTHFS: F = 31.47; vitamin B6: F = 18.15). We removed each SNP suspected of strong pleiotropy from the IVs one by one and found that rs10018448, rs216311, and rs62143198 would cause drastic changes in the results (Δβ > 25%). Therefore, we deleted them and conducted MVMR analysis again. The results suggested a causal relationship between reduced folate levels and the genetic predisposition of PCOS (multivariable IVW OR = 2.92; 95% CI, 1.81 − 3.98; P = 8.32 × 10− 4; Table 3), which was consistent with the findings of the two-sample MR analysis. In addition, an increase of 1 SD in the serum DHFR was directly associated with a 25% higher lone-term risk of PCOS (multivariable IVW P = 1.68 × 10− 4; Table 3).

Contrary to the results of two-sample MR, MVMR substantiated significant causal effects of vitamin B6 on PCOS (multivariable IVW OR = 0.56; 95% CI, 0.34 − 0.91; P = 6.34 × 10− 3; Table 3), while the MTHFR–PCOS association was found insignificant (multivariable IVW OR = 0.50; 95% CI, 0.43 − 1.61; P = 0.30; Table 3). MVMR refuted putative causal effects identified in two-sample MR, suggesting that unmeasured mediators may underlie initial associations. Our findings indicate that the association of MTHFS with PCOS is not independent of vitamin B6 – i.e., MTHFS may influence PCOS risk via pathways that also involve B6 metabolism, rather than having a direct effect. On the other hand, the vitamin B6 instrument explained about 2.25% of the variance, which is a small amount of power. The univariable MR for B6 was indeed underpowered, but our MVMR analysis suggests that a true effect was hidden.

Sensitivity analyses using MVMR-Egger regression yielded an intercept term indistinguishable from null (β = 0.011; 95% CI, -0.15–0.11; P = 0.41), revealing no directional pleiotropy. Cochran’s Q test using IVW model detected no heterogeneity (P = 0.36). We mitigated the violation from weak instruments and pleiotropy by minimizing Q-statistic in our MVMR estimation. No change was observed in the results, robustly representing the independent causal effect of a specific exposure factor in folate metabolism mentioned above on PCOS, while accounting for potential mutual confounding.

Discussion

In this study, we investigated the causal relationship between genetically predicted exposure to one-carbon metabolism-related factors and BCAAs and the risk of PCOS using robust genetic variants as unconfounded proxies. The present work is the first and most comprehensive MR study to systematically explore the impact of folate metabolism-related factors on PCOS, using the largest-scale GWASs and meta-analyses to date.

Employing the bidirectional two-sample MR and MVMR methods, genetic evidence indicated that increasing individuals’ long-term high exposure to circulating folate and DHFR would increase their risk of developing PCOS, after FDR correction and adjustment for BMI, waist-to-hip ratio, and other confounders based on PhenoScanner database [59, 60]. Vitamin B6 insufficiency are hypothesized as an underlying contributor to PCOS etiology. Additionally, suffering from PCOS was revealed to possibly contribute to vitamin B12 deficiency. The MR-Egger regression, Cochran’s Q, MR Steiger, and MR-PRESSO tests observed no heterogeneity, horizontal pleiotropy, bidirectional causality, or outliers among IVs in the sensitivity analysis, validating the robustness of the results.

Although previous epidemiological studies have tentatively discussed the effects of several one-carbon metabolism-related factors and PCOS, the results have been inconsistent. The biochemical mechanism linking B-group vitamins to the development of PCOS remains incompletely understood. Excessive folate levels, insufficient serum vitamin B12, and elevated tHcy have been detected in the serum of both Chilean and Indian women with PCOS, which together increase methylmalonic acid (MMA) levels [12, 81, 82]. As an inhibitor of carnitine palmitoyl transferase 1 (CPT1), a key enzyme in the beta-oxidation pathway, MMA can downregulate the activity of CPT1, promoting lipogenesis and insulin resistance [83, 84].

Folate supports one-carbon metabolism and is critical for gametogenesis [13]. Inadequate folate intake is associated with increased anovulation [14]. Increasing the serum folate levels of women with PCOS has been shown to help reduce the numbers of preantral and antral follicles during in vitro fertilization [14]. In contrast, in vitro and animal studies have demonstrated that excess serum folate can increase Hcy levels and reduce the activity of MTHFR and 5-methyltetrahydrofolate-homocysteine methyltransferase (MTR) by interfering with DNA methylation [42]. Vitamin B12 is a co-factor that converts methylmalonyl-CoA to succinyl-CoA, a substrate for cellular respiration. Vitamin B12 deficiency can cause the accumulation of oxidative stress, epigenetic modifications, the modulation of gene expression or protein content, and alterations in lysosomal activity [24, 85]. Vitamin B6 functions as a crucial cofactor for the enzyme cystathionine beta-synthase (CBS) in the transsulfuration pathway, facilitating the enzymatic conversion of Hcy (Fig. 1). It also plays a pivotal role in the metabolism of glucose, lipids, and amino acids, by scavenging toxic carbonyl products and inhibiting reactive oxygen species. Szczuko et al. [19] reported that vitamin B6 can regulate hormonal balance and reduce inflammation by positively contributing to Hcy metabolism in individuals with PCOS. Here, we elucidated the causal effects of genetically predicted folate and vitamin B6 on PCOS using MR. Furthermore, our research demonstrated that the severity of PCOS may be one of the potential factors contributing to decreased vitamin B12 levels.

As an obligatory sulfur-containing amino acid in the methionine cycle, Hcy is obtained primarily from dietary proteins of animal origin, supporting protein synthesis and DNA repair in vivo. Therefore, it is required for general biological maintenance and cellular growth. Hcy is metabolized through two pathways. One is the infrequently used transsulfuration pathway, in which vitamin B6 acts as a facilitator, whereas the other is remethylation, which is relatively more crucial and involves folate and vitamin B12 (Fig. 1). Aberrant Hcy metabolism can damage endothelial cells and account for oxidative stress, increasing the risk of CHD, mainly through a reduction in PPAR gamma levels [86]. Compared with healthy individuals, patients with PCOS have a greater risk of hyperhomocysteinemia (HHcy) [13, 87–90]. Elevated tHcy levels could exacerbate autophagy in granulosa cells by manipulating the mammalian target of the rapamycin signaling pathway, potentially arresting follicular development and impairing ovulation [91–93]. In pigs, high serum levels of tHcy can impair oocyte quality via deregulation of one-carbon metabolism, leading to DNA hypermethylation and mitochondrial dysfunction in oocytes [94]. However, in this study, genetically predicted tHcy levels were not found to be causally associated with the risk of PCOS.

Moreover, both PCOS and HHcy have been identified as independent risk factors for arterial and venous thromboembolism and unfavorable pregnancy outcomes [95–97]. Specifically, excess Hcy aggravates hypercoagulability during gestation and increases the likelihood of developing thrombosis in the maternal–fetal circulation, leading to adverse pregnancy outcomes [97].

The causal association between DHFR and PCOS observed in both univariable and multivariate MR analyses suggests a potential mechanistic link between enhanced folate recycling and ovarian pathophysiology. DHFR is essential for regenerating tetrahydrofolate and increased DHFR activity could augment DNA synthesis and alter methylation patterns in ovarian tissue [98], potentially affecting follicular development and endocrine function. Our concordant findings for folate and DHFR support the notion that sustained high folate bioavailability may contribute to PCOS risk. These results underscore the need for further experimental and clinical studies to elucidate the role of DHFR variants in reproductive endocrinology.

The other pivotal regulatory enzyme within the folate metabolic pathway, MTHFS, catalyzes the irreversible conversion of 5-formyltetrahydrofolate to 5,10-methenyltetrahydrofolate, thereby regulating the distribution of folate derivatives within the one-carbon cycle [99]. While there is no direct biochemical requirement for vitamin B6 as a cofactor in the MTHFS-catalyzed reaction, perturbations in one-carbon metabolism are known to influence Hcy remethylation and transsulfuration pathways, the latter of which is B6-dependent [100, 101]. Upon re-examination of the IVs using the GWAS Catalog, no genome-wide significant associations were identified between MTHFS variants and serum vitamin B6 concentrations; whereas one SNP demonstrated certain association with Hcy levels (P = 4 × 10⁻⁴), probably suggesting an indirect metabolic link. Collectively, these findings support that MTHFS may modulate PCOS susceptibility through interconnected metabolic pathways involving B6 and Hcy metabolism, rather than exerting a direct causal influence.

Whether increased BCAA concentrations can be attributed to metabolic dysregulation in individuals with PCOS remains highly controversial. A prospective study has shown that the insulin-resistant PCOS group had higher levels of BCAAs than the noninsulin-resistant PCOS and control groups did (healthy individuals, n = 48) [70]. Targeted metabolomics and enrichment analysis have revealed that hyperandrogenemia influences the BCAA profile, highlighting a relationship between BCAAs and PCOS [102–104]. Mu et al. [105] reported that dysfunction of the endocrine and ovarian systems in PCOS patients was partially mediated by impaired BCAA catabolism and explored the underlying genetic associations using MR. In this study, we observed a causal relationship between low levels of BCAAs and an increased risk of PCOS, supporting the previous hypothesis that a BCAA-abundant diet may positively affect the metabolic health of women with PCOS.

Given that women with PCOS commonly have a low dietary intake of B-group vitamins, a corrective intervention to support normal one-carbon metabolism could ameliorate some hallmark features of PCOS, as reported by Schiuma et al. [88]. Oral administration of folate and vitamin B12 can decrease Hcy levels and improve metabolic and hormonal profiles in patients with PCOS, especially women with recurrent pregnancy loss ( [23, 24] [67, 106, 107]). Alesi et al. [25] provided an overview of nutritional supplements recommended for the treatment of PCOS by combining evidence from RCTs, systematic reviews, and meta-analyses. They emphasized that specific vitamins (B12, C, and folate) can improve the reproductive health of women with PCOS. These findings highlight the therapeutic value of vitamin B6 supplementation in intervention strategies of PCOS. Integrating prior evidence with our findings, it is biologically plausible that targeting folate metabolism may improve fertility outcomes and overall health in women with PCOS. This study may provide a reference for accessing whether supplementation with methyl donors and/or folate/Hcy metabolic enzymes can mitigate pathophysiological changes or improve outcomes in patients with PCOS in clinical settings. In future studies, we will establish a stronger theoretical basis for improving the management of PCOS.

Considering that PCOS is a hereditary polygenic disorder, the specific genes, gene risk variants, and biological pathways associated with the development and progression of PCOS have been investigated in many studies using summary datasets from GWASs and meta-analyses [53, 57, 108–111]. These sources have provided a sound foundation with a considerable sample size and diverse genetic variations.

Among the 18 tHcy-associated SNPs identified by van Meurs et al. [51], rs1801133 and rs12134663 were found in the MTHFR gene. As a metabolizing enzyme, MTHFR can promote the development and activity of blastocysts in bovine animals [20, 21]. A meta-analysis of case–control studies demonstrated that MTHFR A1298C could increase the risk of PCOS by increasing tHcy levels, whereas MTHFR C677T had a significant adverse effect on Hcy accumulation in Asian individuals [17, 22]. According to a recent study, hypermethylation of MTHFR at the promoter region, not the concentration of MTHFR, is an emerging risk factor for PCOS [30]. However, we failed to include MTHFR in MR analysis, due to the lack of publicly available GWAS summary statistics involving genome-wide MTHFR-associated SNPs.

Compared with existing observational studies, MR analysis yields more convincing and robust causal estimates and mitigates issues of reverse causality and potential confounding biases with the use of large-scale GWASs and meta-analyses of GWASs. However, to our knowledge, studies investigating the causal relationships between one-carbon metabolism-related factors and PCOS via MR analysis are lacking.

The main strengths of this work are as follows: First, we retrieved summary statistics from the most extensive GWASs and meta-analyses, which enabled us to include an adequate number of SNPs for an exposure or an outcome, ensured sufficient statistical power, and enhanced our ability to drive causal inferences from both population and genetic perspectives. Second, we selected the IVs at two different P thresholds, and meta-analyzed the SNP–PCOS associations from two sources to get a combined estimate for use in MR. Third, a range of MR approaches were carried out in the sensitivity analysis to corroborate the reliability and robustness of the results. Fourth, the datasets used in this study predominantly included individuals of European descent. Therefore, the findings were not influenced by differences in physiological characteristics across races. Last, considering that effect estimates from univariable MR may be biased in the presence of horizontal pleiotropy mediated by shared one-carbon metabolism pathways, MVMR was used to explicitly adjust for these correlated exposures. Conceptually, MVMR parallels mediation analysis.

Although MR provides methodologically robust causal inference, the conclusions should be framed with appropriate caution and its limitations should be acknowledged. First, MR requires strong genetic variants for specific exposure factors. Despite the use of the largest GWASs and meta-analyses, the limited number of IVs prevented us from performing MR analyses for vitamin B12. Regarding known B12 loci, we note FUT2 rs602662 exhibits BMI pleiotropy [112], while MTRR associations remain controversial [113–115], precluding their inclusion per MR assumptions. Larger, more diverse GWASs, especially those with more extensive sample sizes, are warranted to identify more genome-wide significant SNPs for future analyses of vitamin B12. Second, despite our efforts to mitigate pleiotropy and confounding factors, the potential for residual pleiotropic effects remains unremovable. For instance, pleiotropy may persist if an instrumental SNP influences an unidentified pathway pertinent to PCOS. Third, we focused on European-ancestry populations with consistent PCOS diagnostic criteria to reduce heterogeneity, but this meant that the total sample size was only moderate (about 3,300 cases). Although the results were robust even when using the full Day et al. dataset in sensitivity analyses, this may lead to a lack of generalizability to other populations, as LD patterns vary across ethnic groups [41, 77]. Future MR studies may benefit from incorporating multi-ethnic GWASs of PCOS to assess the validity and applicability of our findings. Fourth, PCOS is associated with endocrine and metabolic disorders (e.g., obesity, insulin resistance, and infertility), making subgroup analyses valuable. Limited data description hinders separate examination of exposure–PCOS phenotype correlations. Last, while this study highlights the genetic influence of one-carbon metabolism on PCOS and the potential of nutritional supplementation in the management of PCOS, environmental factors were not fully considered. Future studies should integrate epigenetics and metabolomics to explore gene–environment interactions in PCOS pathophysiology.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this study integrates metabolomics and genomics to evaluate the causal effects of genetically determined one-carbon metabolism-related factors on PCOS risk, offering critical insights into disease pathogenesis and providing novel avenues for developing preventive and therapeutic strategies targeting one-carbon metabolism. Our findings also challenge the preceding belief that decreased B-group vitamin levels and increased tHcy concentrations in blood are consequences of metabolic disorders in women with PCOS. Future MR studies should expand on these findings by utilizing larger replication samples and a larger number of IVs for each exposure of interest. Additionally, researchers should adopt innovative approaches to explore how metabolic disturbances impact reproductive and psychological functions in individuals with PCOS. Given the incurable nature of PCOS, developing novel symptomatic relief strategies is essential to enhance patients’ quality of life. Cohort studies may serve as valuable tools to assess the efficacy of interventions targeting one-carbon metabolism in the management of PCOS, providing robust evidence to guide clinical practice.
