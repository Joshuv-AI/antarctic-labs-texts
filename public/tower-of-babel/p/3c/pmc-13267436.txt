HAT‐PCR is non‐inferior to NGS when quantifying measurable residual disease for myeloma
Authors: Elizabeth Hughes, Piers Blombery, Sushmitha Kannan, Tiffany Khong, Andrew Spencer, Alexander Morley
Published: 2026

To the Editor,

Quantification of measurable residual disease (MRD) has emerged in clinical trials as a powerful predictor of response and survival in patients with myeloma (reviewed in reference 1 ). MRD has been accepted by the United States Federal Drug Administration as a valid end‐point for regulatory drug approval and data from recent trials suggest that MRD can be used for making decisions for individuals. 2 , 3 , 4

The most used methods for MRD in myeloma currently are multiparameter flow cytometry (MFC) and next‐generation sequencing (NGS) which, in typical clinical practice, provide a sensitivity of 10−5 and 10−6 respectively. 5 , 6 , 7 Historically, PCR for tumour‐specific immunoglobulin (IG) rearrangements has been used to quantify MRD in myeloma, but its use has declined owing to its lower sensitivity 8 , 9 from 10−4 to 10−5. However, we have recently described a modification of PCR, HAT‐PCR (High A/T or High Annealing Temperature‐PCR), which has demonstrated a sensitivity of 10−6 in studies of acute lymphoblastic leukaemia (ALL), chronic lymphocytic leukaemia (CLL) and myeloma. 10 , 11 The increase in sensitivity results from maximizing the specificity of the PCR by using an annealing temperature close to the maximum possible and by using primers with between one and three A or T bases at their 3' ends. These modifications reduce both false‐positive PCR results and false‐negative PCR results resulting from non‐specific amplification of genomic sequences. The latter phenomenon has been largely unrecognized. 12

In our previous study of MRD in myeloma, HAT‐PCR showed greater sensitivity than MFC. 8 In the current study, we directly compared HAT‐PCR to NGS in a total of 37 marrow samples from 26 patients who participated in the Australasian Leukaemia and Lymphoma Group MM17 trial. 13 Patient samples were obtained after written informed consent and the study was approved by the Flinders University Human Research Ethics Committee. Twelve samples were obtained during treatment and 25 were produced by mixing diagnosis DNA with pooled DNA obtained from five healthy individuals. NGS measurement was performed using the clonoSEQ assay (Adaptive Biotechnologies, Seattle, USA) which uses primers targeted against VDJ and DJ sequences from the IGH, IGK and IGL loci and which is FDA approved for MRD assessment in myeloma. The reverse primer for amplification of IGH sequences is directed to the conserved region of JH which is also the region to which the HAT‐PCR probe is directed.

For HAT‐PCR, the full details of library construction, sequencing of the baseline IGH locus by NGS, the design and testing of primers, and the protocol of HAT‐PCR are given in the Supplementary Information. In brief, to overcome the effect of immunoglobulin locus hypermutation, the preparation of libraries for sequencing involved facilitation of primer binding by using high concentrations of primers and prolonged annealing at a low temperature. Sequencing and bioinformatics analysis were performed by AGRF (Melbourne, Australia). The primers designed from the marker sequence to be used for subsequent MRD testing had one to three A or T bases at the 3' end; the forward primers had a Tm of 69°C–70.5°C; the reverse primers had a Tm of 71°C–73°C and were directed to sequences downstream of the probe sequence; and for each sample, 20 aliquots of 1 μg of DNA were amplified by the PCR which was performed at an annealing temperature of 72°C.

The marker sequences obtained by the clonoSEQ and the HAT‐PCR NGS protocols were similar. Of the VDJ sequences detected, 25 were detected by both methods, 1 only by clonoSEQ and 4 only by HAT‐PCR. Of the DJ sequences detected, 10 were detected by both methods, 1 only by clonoSEQ and 1 only by HAT‐PCR. For the seven samples in which a sequence was not detected by one or other methods, another marker sequence usable for MRD analysis was detected in six.

Immunoglobulin locus hypermutation in myeloma may theoretically interfere with the binding of the generic primers used for preparation of VDJ libraries and, if so, may interfere with detection of the marker sequence by the clonoSEQ or HAT‐PCR NGS protocols or the detection or quantification of MRD by NGS. Indirect evidence for this possibility comes from studies in which MRD was quantified by both MFC and NGS and in which MFC + ve samples were observed which were either NGS‐ve or in which the MRD level provided by NGS was much lower than that provided by MFC. 11 In this study, as marker sequences were detected by two different protocols, the potential effect of hypermutation was able to be studied directly. The germline immunoglobulin sequences were obtained from the International Immunogenetics Information System website (https://www.imgt.org/IMGT_vquest/input), and the number of mutations in each sequence was related to detection or to failure of detection by either the clonoSEQ or the HAT‐PCR protocol. The results are shown in Figure 1 and suggest an association between a high number of mutations and failure of marker sequence detection (Mann–Whitney U‐test, p = 0.016). Although most of the failures were seen with the clonoSEQ protocol, we caution against drawing any conclusion from this. The number of failures was small and, since the diagnosis samples studied had previously been associated with MRD detection by HAT‐PCR, the diagnosis samples studied were selected against failure of detection by the HAT‐PCR protocol.

Of the 37 samples assembled for comparative MRD analysis by clonoSEQ or HAT‐PCR, 2 samples, both from the same subject, were not studied owing to a diagnosis marker sequence not being detected by clonoSEQ. Figure 2 shows the results of MRD measured in the remaining 35 samples which were derived from 25 patients. MRD was not detected by clonoSEQ in two samples, both derived from the same patient. For the remaining 33 samples, there was a strong correlation between clonoSEQ and HAT‐PCR results, both for the total 33 samples (r = 0.96, n = 33, p < 0.001) and for the 11 patient samples (r = 0.97, n = 11, p < 0.001). Both methods measured down to 10−6, indicating equal sensitivity. For HAT‐PCR, there were nine MRD values that were less than 10−5.

Owing to its sensitivity, NGS is frequently used for quantification of MRD in myeloma. As our results show that the results of quantification by HAT‐PCR are the same as those obtained by NGS, we suggest that HAT‐PCR is an alternative to NGS for quantification of MRD in myeloma. PCR has advantages over NGS as it is simple, widely available, provides rapid results and is inexpensive. These advantages will be heightened if repeated MRD measurements are required or, potentially, if MRD is to be assayed in blood. Several features of myeloma facilitate the use of PCR—the marker sequence remains unchanged during treatment 14 and the mutations in the J region enable the design of a semispecific J primer which provides extra specificity. In our experience with HAT‐PCR, only one forward and one reverse primer need to be synthesized, testing of the primer pair is straightforward, the primers have uniformly high efficiency, 15 and in myeloma false‐positive MRD, results have not been seen.

The results also suggest that, owing to its sensitivity, HAT‐PCR could be considered for quantification of MRD in other lymphoproliferative disorders including ALL and CLL. HAT‐PCR could well replace conventional PCR in any situation where the latter is currently used as the modifications required are trivial. If HAT‐PCR is compared to NGS for conditions other than myeloma, HAT‐PCR does not detect clonal evolution, which can occur in ALL and CLL and can occasionally evidence a low level of false positivity. However, an acceptable solution to the problem of clonal evolution may be to follow several of the markers present at diagnosis by HAT‐PCR or to use both HAT‐PCR and NGS, HAT‐PCR for routine monitoring and NGS for specific time points, such as the end of induction in ALL, to detect clonal evolution.
