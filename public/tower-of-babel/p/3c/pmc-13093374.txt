Systemic Inflammation Markers in Migraine: A Cross‐Sectional Study
Authors: Marina Romozzi, Federico Tosto, Annalisa Gentile, Flavia Bianchi, Giada Giuliani, Caterina Ermio, Sonia Di Tella, Luigi Francesco Lannone, Paolo Calabresi, Catello Vollono
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundNeuroinflammatory and systemic immune mechanisms are increasingly recognized as contributors to migraine pathophysiology. However, peripheral markers of inflammation remain underexplored. This study aimed to evaluate systemic immune alterations in patients with migraine through complete blood count (CBC) parameters and derived inflammatory indices, comparing findings with healthy controls (HCs).MethodsWe conducted a cross‐sectional analysis of peripheral blood samples, assessing leukocyte subtypes including absolute and relative (percentage‐%) values, hemoglobin, and platelet levels. Inflammatory indices, including neutrophil‐to‐lymphocyte ratio (NLR), platelet‐to‐lymphocyte ratio (PLR), and systemic immune‐inflammation index (SII), were calculated. Migraine features were collected.ResultsA total of 409 subjects were included: 179 HCs, 194 with episodic migraine (EM), and 36 with chronic migraine (CM). Regarding inflammatory indices, SII significantly differed among EM, CM, and HCs (p = 0.024). In particular, in post hoc analyses, it was significantly higher in CM (514.20 ± 250.57) vs. HCs (423.14 ± 221.69) (p = 0.019) and in CM compared to EM (448.28 ± 266.53, p = 0.044). Similarly, neutrophil% significantly differed among the three groups (p = 0.022), and was higher in patients with CM (58.69 ± 9.57) vs. HCs (54.81 ± 8.86, p = 0.019) and in patients with CM vs. EM (55.39 ± 9.77, p = 0.016). Lymphocyte% differed among the three groups (p = 0.047), and it was lower in CM (31.51 ± 8.27) vs. HCs (34.55 ± 8.24, p = 0.047).ConclusionsSystemic immune dysregulation appears to be associated with migraine, particularly CM. Alterations in neutrophil and lymphocyte distributions, as well as increased SII, may represent peripheral correlates of central neuroinflammation. These findings suggest that simple, routinely available blood‐derived markers could help identify inflammatory endophenotypes in migraine, warranting validation in larger, prospective studies.Keywords: blood count, inflammation, migraine, pathogenesis, systemic immunoinflammatory index

Introduction

Migraine is a disabling neurological disorder currently affecting more than 1.16 billion individuals worldwide [1]. The precise mechanisms underlying migraine pathogenesis remain incompletely elucidated. However, accumulating evidence points to the activation of the trigeminovascular system and neuroinflammatory processes as key contributors [2].

Furthermore, several studies have shown that in the ictal phase of migraine, there is a release of neuropeptides, mast cell degranulation, neurogenic vasodilation, and platelet activation [3, 4, 5].

Neuroinflammation, characterized by the activation of glial cells and the release of pro‐inflammatory mediators such as interleukin‐1β (IL‐1β), tumor necrosis factor‐alpha (TNF‐α), and IL‐6, promotes sensitization of the trigeminovascular system, thereby amplifying nociceptive signaling pathways [3, 6].

In parallel, systemic inflammation has emerged as a critical component of migraine pathophysiology, complementing the trigeminovascular mechanisms [2]. Increasing evidence points to the involvement of immune activation and peripheral inflammatory responses that extend beyond the CNS. Elevated levels of pro‐inflammatory cytokines have been reported in the peripheral blood of migraine patients, particularly during attacks and in chronic migraine (CM). These circulating inflammatory mediators may contribute to the sensitization of trigeminal afferents and the amplification of nociceptive transmission. The immune cells, which include granulocytes, lymphocytes, and monocytes, can be easily measured peripherally in the complete blood count (CBC) [7]. Furthermore, some composite indices derived from the CBC have been largely evaluated in different pathologies, including neurological disorders, for assessing systemic inflammation, and have only been initially explored in patients with migraine [8, 9].

This observational, cross‐sectional study aimed to evaluate systemic inflammation based on the peripheral CBC in patients with migraine and compared them with healthy controls (HCs).

Methods

Study Design and Population

This is a cross‐sectional retrospective study conducted at the tertiary headache center of Fondazione Policlinico Universitario A. Gemelli IRCCS and Sapienza University of Rome, Italy. We included consecutive patients with a diagnosis of migraine, fulfilling the ICHD‐3 criteria, and HCs who performed a CBC within the 3 months prior to the evaluation. During the in‐person visit (when CBC data were collected), a comprehensive neurological examination was conducted, along with a review of the patient's medical history and an assessment of migraine characteristics.

The study's inclusion criteria for migraine patients are as follows: (i) age ≥ 18 years; (ii) signature of the informed consent; (iii) diagnosis of migraine according to ICHD‐3; (iv) a CBC performed within the 3 months preceding the evaluation; (v) no significant changes in treatments for other comorbidities or preventive migraine treatments between the time of the CBC and the in‐person visit.

Exclusion criteria include: (i) patients affected by autoimmune, inflammatory, or hematological diseases that may alter CBC parameters; (ii) treatment with steroids or other immunomodulator drugs.

HCs were recruited among hospital staff and individuals undergoing routine health check‐ups. Inclusion criteria for HCs were: (i) age ≥ 18 years; (ii) the absence of a previous or current history of primary or secondary headache disorders. The exclusion criteria for HCs were the same as those for the migraine group.

Variables Collected

Variables Related to Migraine

For patients with migraine, the following variables were collected: socio‐demographic data, general medical history, age at onset, monthly headache days (MHDs), severity of the attacks (Visual Analogue Scale [VAS]), presence of aura, acute and prior and/or concomitant preventive migraine, analgesic monthly number (AMNs), Headache Impact Test‐6 (HIT‐6), and the Migraine Disability Assessment (MIDAS) questionnaires.

Blood Count Variables

Peripheral blood samples were collected under standardized conditions and in the absence of acute illness. For patients with migraine, we obtained the samples during the interictal phase. The following parameters were measured: hemoglobin, total white blood cell (WBC) count, lymphocytes, monocytes, eosinophils, basophils, neutrophils, and platelets, including absolute and relative (percentage‐%) values. Derived inflammatory indices were calculated as follows:

Neutrophil‐to‐lymphocyte ratio (NLR): neutrophil count/lymphocyte count;

Neutrophil‐to‐lymphocyte ratio (NLR): neutrophil count/lymphocyte count;

Monocyte‐to‐lymphocyte ratio (MLR): monocyte count/lymphocyte count;

Monocyte‐to‐lymphocyte ratio (MLR): monocyte count/lymphocyte count;

Neutrophil‐to‐monocyte ratio (NMR): neutrophil count/monocyte count;

Neutrophil‐to‐monocyte ratio (NMR): neutrophil count/monocyte count;

Neutrophil‐to‐lymphocyte platelet ratio (NLPR): neutrophil count/lymphocyte count*platelet count

Neutrophil‐to‐lymphocyte platelet ratio (NLPR): neutrophil count/lymphocyte count*platelet count

Systemic immunoinflammatory index (SII): (neutrophil count*platelet count)/lymphocyte count;

Systemic immunoinflammatory index (SII): (neutrophil count*platelet count)/lymphocyte count;

Platelet‐to‐lymphocyte ratio (PLR): platelet count/lymphocyte count;

Platelet‐to‐lymphocyte ratio (PLR): platelet count/lymphocyte count;

Statistical Analysis

Considering the retrospective design, we did not perform any sample size calculation. We included all consecutive outpatients, and the analysis is based on a convenience sample.

Numerical variables were reported as mean and standard deviation (SD). Categorical variables were presented as absolute numbers (n) and percentages (%). The distribution of the variable was checked with the Shapiro–Wilk Test.

Categorical variables were compared with the chi‐squared (χ 2) test. The Mann–Whitney U test was used for comparisons between two independent groups, while the Kruskal–Wallis test, followed by Dunn's post hoc test with false discovery rate (FDR) correction for multiple comparisons. Spearman's rank correlation coefficient (ρ) was used to assess correlations between variables. The statistical significance was set at two‐tailed p < 0.05. All data were analyzed using SPSS software version 29.0 (IBM Corp. SPSS Statistics, Armonk, NY, USA). Graphs were generated using R software (version 4.4.0; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) and the ggplot2 package (version 3.5.1).

Results

Population Features

We included 409 subjects: 179 HCs, 194 patients with episodic migraine (EM), and 36 patients with CM. The flowchart of the study is reported in Figure S1.

Patients with CM reported higher MHDs (16.24 ± 7.37) compared to EM (4.58 ± 3.19; p < 0.01), as AMNs (17.30 ± 15.08 vs. 4.56 ± 3.33; p < 0.01). Similarly, disability scores were significantly higher in CM patients, both in HIT‐6 (68.42 ± 5.39 vs. 62.38 ± 1.55; p < 0.01) and MIDAS (89.15 ± 50.31 vs. 27.82 ± 28.92; p < 0.01). Blood Count and Inflammatory Indice

Hemoglobin was significantly lower in EM compared to HCs (p = 0.003). Regarding inflammatory indices, SII significantly differed among EM, CM, and HCs (p = 0.024). In particular, in post hoc analyses, it was significantly higher in CM (514.20 ± 250.57) vs. HCs (423.14 ± 221.69) (p = 0.019) and in CM compared to EM (448.28 ± 266.53, p = 0.044). Similarly, neutrophil% significantly differed among the three groups (p = 0.022), and was higher in patients with CM (58.69 ± 9.57) vs. HCs (54.81 ± 8.86, p = 0.019) and in patients with CM vs. EM (55.39 ± 9.77, p = 0.016). Lymphocyte% differed among the three groups (p = 0.047), and it was lower in CM (31.51 ± 8.27) vs. HCs (34.55 ± 8.24, p = 0.047).

No significant differences were found between groups for the other CBC variables and composite indexes (Table 1 and Figure 1).

Discussion

This cross‐sectional study showed that systemic inflammation, as reflected by CBC and derived indices, indicates peripheral immune activation, with distinct patterns emerging across clinically defined migraine subgroups and HCs.

Regarding leukocyte counts, we found significant alterations in subtype distribution. Neutrophil percentage was higher in CM patients compared to HCs and EM, while lymphocyte percentage was significantly lower in CM than in HCs. These alterations are in line with previous literature that an increased neutrophil‐to‐lymphocyte imbalance reflects a state of persistent, low‐grade systemic inflammation [10]. Neutrophils, which are key components of the innate immune system, are typically involved in early inflammatory responses and are known to release pro‐inflammatory mediators that can contribute to neuroinflammation [11]. The reduction in lymphocytes suggests a dysregulation of the adaptive immune system, which could reflect a chronic stress/inflammatory state that may alter lymphocyte proliferation or survival [11].

This neutrophil–lymphocyte imbalance may serve as a peripheral immune signature of the CM state, potentially reflecting repeated nociceptive activation, central sensitization, and neurogenic inflammation. Among the derived indices, the SII is a composite marker integrating neutrophil, lymphocyte, and platelet counts. In our cohort, this index was significantly elevated in CM patients compared to EM and HCs. This result supports the value of multicomponent inflammatory indices, rather than a single parameter, in capturing peripheral subclinical immune dysregulation.

Several previous studies have investigated composite inflammatory indices in migraine, with mixed results. Yazar and colleagues reported increased NLR, PLR, and MLR in ictal periods, while another study observed raised NMR and PLR levels in migraine with and without aura compared to HCs [8, 12].

An additional, unexpected finding was the significantly lower hemoglobin levels among EM compared to HCs, consistent with previous studies that have reported a link between iron deficiency anemia and migraine susceptibility. Indeed, this finding parallels the higher incidence of anemia due to iron deficiency in patients with migraine [13].

From a mechanistic perspective, the pathophysiology of the peripheral inflammatory alterations may mirror aspects of central neuroinflammation. Glial cell activation in the CNS releases pro‐inflammatory cytokines, which sensitize the trigeminovascular system. Furthermore, neuroinflammation is often associated with migraine attacks, with cytokines serving as crucial mediators in the process [14]. These cytokines have the capacity to sensitize pain pathways in the brain, thereby increasing sensitivity to pain stimuli. This phenomenon is believed to contribute to the intensity and persistence of migraine pain. These cytokines not only may modulate nociceptive signaling centrally but may also have a role in peripheral immune responses through bidirectional neuroimmune communication. This study has some strengths: (i) it is among the few investigations to apply complete blood count‐derived inflammatory indices in the context of migraine, offering insights on how peripheral immune markers may reflect central neuroinflammatory mechanisms, including a large sample size; (ii) rigorous selection criteria to exclude confounding conditions such as autoimmune disorders, infections, and anemia, thus enhancing the reliability of the observed associations; (iii) the inclusion of HCs, strengthening the interpretation of disease‐specific alterations; (iv) the focus on readily available, routine blood parameters like neutrophil and lymphocyte counts, and their derived ratios makes the findings clinically applicable and easily translatable to clinical practice if validated.

Limitations

Nevertheless, some limitations should be acknowledged. The cross‐sectional design precludes any causal inference regarding the relationship between systemic inflammatory alterations and migraine. Multivariable analyses were not feasible due to the relatively small sample size of the CM group. Additionally, the potential impact of medication exposure, unrecognized systemic conditions, ictal and interictal status, and the role of both preventive and acute treatments on hematological and inflammatory parameters was not assessed.

Conclusions

Our study supports the hypothesis that migraine, particularly CM, is associated with systemic immune dysregulation. These results confirm the complexity of the inflammatory process involved in migraine, a disease with bidirectional interactions between central neuroinflammatory and peripheral immune alterations.
