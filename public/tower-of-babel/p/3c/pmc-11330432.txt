Intentional Insulin Overdose and Depression in Subjects with and Without Diabetes Mellitus: A Commentary
Authors: Evanthia Gouveri, Aikaterini Gkouveri, Djordje S Popovic, Dimitrios Papazoglou, Nikolaos Papanas
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractInsulin is an essential medication for people with type 1 diabetes mellitus and for some people with type 2 diabetes. Interestingly, insulin abuse has been reported as a mode of suicide, not only among people with diabetes, but also among their relatives, and among medical and paramedical personnel who have access to insulin. The aim of the present commentary was to raise awareness of potential depression-related intentional insulin overdose and its complications, as well as of the diagnosis and treatment of this entity. Insulin overdose may lead to severe and prolonged hypoglycemia, hypoglycemic coma, and death. Moreover, hypokalemia, hypomagnesemia, hypophosphatemia, and elevated liver enzymes are common. Insulin overdose should be suspected among people with diabetes in case of unexplained prolonged hypoglycemia and among people without diabetes who exhibit hypoglycemia and may have access to diabetic medications. The ratio of insulin to C-peptide helps distinguish exogenous insulin administration from endogenous secretion. The cornerstone of therapy is prompt administration of concentrated glucose infusions for days with simultaneous oral intake, when possible, and intense glucose monitoring to prevent hypoglycemia. Moreover, monitoring of serum electrolyte levels is recommended. Finally, psychiatric evaluation aiming at early identification of depression and suicidality is of paramount importance.Keywords: Insulin overdose, Diabetes mellitus, Hypoglycemia, Depression, Attempted suicide

Introduction

People with type 1 diabetes (T1DM) and, sometimes, with type 2 diabetes (T2DM) need daily administration of insulin (human insulin or analogues, long or/and short acting insulin). However, beyond its essential therapeutic use, insulin has been reported to be abused in some cases as a mode of suicide, and very rarely, for homicidal purpose as well [1–3].

Diabetes requires life-long therapy (mainly for T1DM), as well as life-long self-control and diet, and it may lead to serious complications. For these reasons, it has been associated with an increased risk of depression and suicide, which varies among different types of diabetes mellitus (DM) and appears to be higher among individuals with a long DM history, people on insulin therapy and those with a poor glycemic control [4, 5]. Attempted suicide or suicide with insulin is not as rare as thought, but it often goes unrecognized [6, 7].

Of note, not only people with DM but also other people who have access to insulin, namely, relatives of people with DM and health professionals (doctors, nurses) have been reported to abuse insulin [1, 8].

In historical past, from the 1930s to the 1960s, “insulin coma”, namely hypoglycemic coma induced using large doses of insulin, was used by psychiatrists as a therapeutic option for schizophrenia. However, this method was not only dangerous and devastating for people with psychiatric disorders, but it was also proved to be ineffective, and so it has been abandoned [9].

The aim of this commentary was to raise awareness of potential depression-related intentional insulin overdose and its complications, as well as of the diagnosis and treatment of this entity.

This article is based on previously conducted studies and does not contain any new studies with human participants or animals performed by any of the authors.

Depression

Depression is more common among people with DM than in the general population. Prevalence between 8.5% and 27.3% has been reported [10]. Individuals with DM are 2–3 times more likely to develop depression, and it has been estimated that almost 30% of people with DM will sometime develop depression [11–13]. According to a systematic review, a threefold higher prevalence rate of depression has been reported among people with T1DM (12%, range 5.8–43.3% vs. 3.2%, range 2.7–11.4%) compared to people without DM [14]. Another study revealed a more than double incidence of mental disorders among new-onset T1DM compared to people without DM (66 per 1000 person-years vs. 29 per 1000 person-years) with an adjusted hazard ratio of 3.16 for depression (95% confidence interval: 2.99–3.35) [15].

A potential bidirectional relationship between DM and depression has been proposed [13, 16]. Individuals with DM, especially those with T1DM, experience more distress compared to people without DM, given their many daily challenges in glucose management and their fears of diabetes complications. On the other hand, depression seems to increase the risk of T2DM by 37–60% [13, 16]. Antidepressant agents have untoward effects like weight gain and hyperglycemia, leading to DM development [13, 16]. Of note, depression has been associated with a poorer glycemic control and more complications among people with DM [17].

Insulin Overdose Among People with DM

Among people with DM, insulin overdose is uncommon, but not as rare as it is thought to be. Indeed, it may remain undiagnosed. In the last decades, several cases have been reported: the majority of these have been encountered in adults between 30 and 60 years of age, while some were seen in adolescents or elderly people [6, 12, 17–40]. Most subjects typically had a psychiatric disorder or depressive symptoms. History of intravenous drug or other substance abuse and/or prior suicidal attempts has also been noted [23, 28]. One subject injected large insulin doses for euphoric experience [39].

Of note, physicians with DM have also been reported to abuse insulin [37, 38]. In addition to insulin, physicians also took overdoses of pills (sulfonylurea, digoxin, and propranolol) [37, 38]. One of these physicians died [38].

Some individuals misused long-acting (basal) insulins, some misused short-acting (prandial) insulins, while others injected overdoses of both long- and short-acting insulins [27, 28]. In addition, a fatal case of insulin overdose through insulin pump was reported in 2020 [40]. This was seen in a 51-year-old man with T1DM without diabetes complications but with depression taking high insulin doses (180 U) within 45–60 min through an insulin pump [40].

Subjects presented with severe, frequently prolonged, hypoglycemia, while epileptic seizures or coma were seen in the most severe cases [6, 12, 17–40]. Generally, hypoglycemia was successfully managed. However, some cases of intentional insulin overdose have been fatal [17, 38]. Permanent brain damage has been reported in one case [18].

A review of fatal cases of insulin overdose in South Australia among people with insulin-dependent DM and individuals without DM [5] identified 40 cases of insulin overdose between 2000 and 2019: of these, 72.5% were identified as suicides, while 27.5% were accidental or undetermined cases. The average age was 50.8 years with no sex difference. Among the 22 individuals with insulin-dependent DM, 13 had depression with ten of those having suicidal ideation [5].

Insulin Overdose Among People Without DM

There are occasional case reports of insulin overdose among people without DM who have easy access to it as health-care professionals (physicians, nurses, and pharmacists) or as family members of insulin-treated relatives [41–51]. Large doses of quick-acting and, secondarily, long-acting insulin, have been used [41–51]. Psychiatric history was present in some cases [41–44, 47, 51]. Several subjects were comatose [41, 42, 44–46, 49, 50]. Importantly, some cases were fatal [42, 44, 46].

Therefore, when a person without known DM presents to the emergency department with severe hypoglycemia or coma refractory to concentrated infusions of glucose, insulin overdose should be suspected. Suspicion should be raised especially when the person is medical or paramedical personnel, or he/she has a known depression [41–51]. Treatment with intravenous glucose over many hours, sometimes over days, may be needed [41–51].

Diagnosis of Suicidal Attempt with Insulin Overdose

Physicians in the emergency department often deal with hypoglycemia. Cases of intentional overdose are relatively uncommon. However, intentional insulin overdose should be suspected: (a) among people with DM in case of unexplained prolonged hypoglycemia which responds minimally to intravenous dextrose infusions; (b) among people without DM who may have access to diabetic medications and exhibit hypoglycemia.

If the etiology of hypoglycemia is unclear, evaluation of serum insulin and C-peptide levels may be needed to differentiate exogenous insulin injection from endogenous hyperinsulinemia [32]. C-peptide is elevated as a marker of endogenous insulin production in case of oral hypoglycemic medications and low in case of exogenous insulin administration. Combining data from medical history and clinical examination, intentional insulin overdose may be suspected when the person has a history of depression, when hypoglycemia does not respond to concentrated intravenous glucose and when plasma insulin levels are significantly elevated, while C-peptide is normal or low. Moreover, insulin antibodies could be detected and injection marks should be searched [32, 52].

The ratio of insulin to C-peptide (I:C) should not exceed 1 in healthy people, but it is elevated in case of exogenous insulin administration. Of note, this ratio changes significantly in post-mortem samples, even within 24 h. Hence, samples should be taken within 48 h of death and from a peripheral site [5, 53].

For more challenging cases, specific laboratory techniques have been proposed. These include immunocapture liquid chromatography tandem mass spectrometry assay of insulin [54], liquid chromatography tandem mass spectrometry analysis of the β-chain of insulin to identify each insulin analogue [55], liquid chromatography coupled with high-resolution mass spectrometry for insulin detection in post-mortem tissues (liver, kidney, muscle, injection site) [56, 57], as well as biochemical analysis by electro-chemiluminescence immunoassay (CLEIA) in the vitreous humor [17, 47].

Treatment of Hypoglycemia Following Insulin Overdose

In case of insulin overdose, delayed initiation of therapy with glucose infusions later than 6 h after insulin administration has been associated with unfavorable outcomes [18]. After insulin overdose, plasma insulin levels increase, and when they reach 50–60 μU/ml, hepatic glucose output is completely suppressed. Thus, glucose should be given exogenously [45]. The main goal is maintenance of normoglycemia. Infusion of 10% dextrose at a rate of 10 g/h (or 100 ml/h) with simultaneous oral intake, when possible, constitute the major therapeutic measures. The rate of dextrose infusion should be continuously adjusted, according to glucose measurements, in order to manage a glucose level of 100–150 mg/dl. Similarly, monitoring for any electrolyte changes is recommended, particularly low levels of potassium, phosphorus, and magnesium can occur and therefore, supplementation is mandatory [47].

It has been proposed that initial administration of 0.5–1 g/kg bolus glucose in case of unconscious patients should be followed by dextrose infusions. The administration of 10 g of exogenous glucose can raise plasma glucose levels by approximately 50 mg/dl for 1 h. If solutions with concentrations of glucose > 10% are needed, a central vein catheter is recommended [43]. Glucose levels should be initially monitored every 15–30 min. In case of restored consciousness, glucose measurements could be reduced to one every hour and glucose could be administered orally with high-carbohydrate foods. Intramuscular glucagon could also be used. However, in some cases, such as chronic liver disease or chronic alcohol consume, liver glycogen is low and cannot normalize blood glucose [32, 43].

Interestingly, surgical excision of the injected site has also been used in two case reports of comatose subjects [20, 58]. In these, increasingly large amounts of intravenous dextrose were necessary to restore consciousness. Accordingly, a 10 × 5 cm piece of skin and fat (where insulin had been injected) was excised under local anesthesia [20, 58]. This procedure was successful in reducing the amount of dextrose needed and in restoring consciousness [21, 58].

There is a significant relationship between the amount of insulin injected and the total amount of intravenous glucose needed. Similarly, there is a significant relationship between the amount of insulin injected and the total time of intravenous glucose administration required [59]. It has been estimated that glucose requirements until recovery could range from 160 to 1100 g and treatment duration from 12 to 62 h [24, 60].

Other successful therapeutic measures include intravenous glucagon infusion [22], octreotide (a somatostatin analogue) treatment [61] and artificial pancreas [62]. Each of these has been used in a case report only.

Of note, a significant proportion of people with DM who present to the emergency department with episodes of hypoglycemia of “unknown” etiology are likely to have intentionally injected insulin overdoses [12]. Untreated depression has been associated with misuse of medications. Hence, caregivers should be aware of this condition. After resolution of hypoglycemia, people with intentional insulin overdose must be evaluated by psychiatrists for depression and suicidality [12].

Complications of Severe Hypoglycemia

Physicians in the emergency department should be prepared to treat persistent or recurrent hypoglycemia in case of insulin overdose. Patients may need high rates of intravenous glucose for many hours to minimize complications [41]. In the analysis by von Mach et al., the etiology of insulin overdose was suicidal in 89.4% of cases [63]. The average age was 44.7 years. Rapidly acting insulins were used in 57.8% and other medications were frequently also consumed (benzodiazepines in 37.5% of the cases). Half of the cases presented to the emergency department within the first 6 h after injection and 21.3% of patients had serious symptoms. Most patients (94.7%) had a full recovery, but 2.7% had cerebral defects and 2.7% died [63].

Hypoglycemia may present with sympathomimetic (e.g., sweating, palpations, tachycardia, and nausea) and neuroglycopenic symptoms, which can vary from altered behavior and lethargy to coma and seizures [43]. Factors that affect hypoglycemic symptoms are: injected dose, type of insulin (long- or short-acting insulin), area of the body where insulin is injected, presence of lipodystrophies (variable absorption rate), use of beta blockers (they may hide symptoms), alcohol consumption (it inhibits gluconeogenesis), prior use of insulin, and presence of anti-insulin antibodies in people with DM. The latter may decrease absorption and may prolong effects [43, 45]. Basal, long-acting insulins and short-acting insulins in large amounts may have delayed effects, due to depot effect. Moreover, prolonged insulin action may be seen in case of renal and hepatic dysfunction [45].

Beyond persistent hypoglycemia, electrolyte disturbances like hyponatremia, hypokalemia and hypophosphatemia may occur. Water and salt retention due to hyperinsulinemia leads to dilutional hyponatremia. Similarly, potassium and phosphorous move from intravascular fluid into cells, leading to hypokalemia and hypophosphatemia. Hypokalemia, hypophosphatemia, and hypomagnesemia may lead to electrocardiographic (ECG) changes with serious untoward effects (e.g., prolonged QTc, ventricular arrhythmias) [43]. Therefore, administration of potassium should be given with caution and potassium levels should be closely monitored [24].

Additional significant complications (elevated liver enzymes, pulmonary edema due to sympathetic activation and hypoglycemic encephalopathy with permanent cognitive dysfunction) have been reported [35, 48, 64]. The brain primarily uses glucose as its energy source. In case of hypoglycemia, neurochemical changes may be characterized by increased cerebral flow, in contrast to cerebral ischemia. However, death of brain neurons after hypoglycemia happens more quickly than after cerebral ischemia [65].

Another rare complication is glycogenic hepatopathy [66]. This is characterized by hepatomegaly and reversible mild to moderate elevation of aminotransferases and alkaline phosphatase. Abdominal pain, nausea, early satiety, and vomiting may also occur [66].

Insulin overdose may result in electrolyte disturbances with electrocardiographic changes, encephalopathy, hepatopathy, lactic acidosis and pulmonary edema. In order to avoid serious complications, prompt glucose administration should be initiated. Delayed glucose administration for more than 6 h and use of mechanical ventilation in the intensive care unit (ICU) for more than 48 h are predictors of unfavorable outcomes in case of insulin overdose [60].

Limitations of Knowledge and Future Directions

The main limitation in our knowledge is that it is based solely on published case reports of people who intentionally abused insulin. By contrast, there are no larger studies. The true frequency of attempted suicide with insulin overdose depends on populations screened and is not entirely certain. Moreover, no data on long-term follow-up of these patients or on the effect of psychiatric treatment are available. Obviously, studies can only be observational. Indeed, it would be unethical to randomize participants to insulin overdose vs. normal dose.

Untreated depression has been associated with misuse of medications. Preventive strategies to reduce the emotional distress of chronic diseases could be implemented. In diabetes, continuous glucose monitoring (CGM) systems and sensor-augmented insulin pumps with low glucose suspend technology, which could prevent hypoglycemia, could be encouraged, as they enable an easier diabetes management [67, 68]. CGM devices can prevent extremely high or low glucose levels with the help of alarm functions, thus reducing the burden of acute diabetes complications, especially dangerous hypoglycemias [69]. Moreover, CGM devices are easy to apply and allow for remote monitoring of high-risk patients, including the use of camera surveillance. Fear of hypoglycemia is an important barrier in the management of diabetes. Of note, use of glucagon-like-peptide (GLP)-1 receptor agonists has been considered the therapy of choice in order to avoid unnecessary hypoglycemia. GLP-1 receptor agonists should be considered for the treatment of T2DM in addition to oral medications or as a monotherapy, before initiation of insulin when glucose targets are not met, thus minimizing the risk of hypoglycemia [70]. Moreover, optimal glycemic control could prevent the development of chronic diabetes complications, improving quality of life. In case of depressive symptoms, a mental health specialist should exclude suicidality. Early recognition of suicidality and treatment with pharmacological and psychological interventions could possibly save lives [71–73].

Conclusions

Insulin is not a recreational drug of abuse and should not be used as such. However, depression-related attempt at suicide with insulin overdose has been reported among people with and without DM. Cases of severe and prolonged hypoglycemia are often underestimated. Especially those of unknown etiology should give rise to suspicion of insulin overdose. The I:C ratio helps distinguish insulin administration from endogenous secretion. Complications of severe hypoglycemia include electrolyte disturbances (hypokalemia, hypomagnesemia, hypophosphatemia) coma, elevated liver enzymes, pulmonary edema, and hypoglycemic encephalopathy with permanent cognitive dysfunction. Prompt intravenous administration of concentrated glucose is required to correct persistent hypoglycemia. Finally, after resolution of hypoglycemia, people with intentional insulin overdose must be evaluated by psychiatrists for depression and suicidality.
