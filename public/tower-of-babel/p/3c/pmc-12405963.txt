Evaluation of Nurses' Knowledge of Incontinence‐Associated Dermatitis Among Acute Care Nurses: A Multi‐Center Cross‐Sectional Study
Authors: Nahla AbdulGadir Tayyib
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimThis study examined how well nurses across various acute care hospitals in Saudi Arabia understand and manage incontinence‐associated dermatitis (IAD), shedding light on existing knowledge gaps and clinical practices.MethodsA descriptive, cross‐sectional design was employed, with a total of 169 nurses recruited between January and July 2024. A psychometrically validated instrument, the Know‐IAD tool was used in a self‐administered online format to collect data and assess nurses' knowledge across three key dimensions: (i) causes and external contributing factors, (ii) classification and diagnosis, and (iii) prevention and management of IAD.ResultsOverall, participants demonstrated a low level of understanding, with total scores falling below 70% in all domains. A considerable proportion of participants answered incorrectly on key factors such as skin inspection identifiers and the appropriate use of all‐in‐one large pads. An image depicting IAD Category 1B was correctly identified by 89 participants (52.6%), while 119 participants (70.4%) correctly identified Category 2/Stage 2 pressure injury. Furthermore, only about 50% of nurses responded correctly to the item on conducting skin inspections every 48 h for incontinent patients. Knowledge domain scores were significantly associated with qualification and area of work, and moderately correlated with age.ConclusionThe study recommends an urgent need for enhanced education, standardized protocols, and improved clinical practices to support accurate diagnosis, prevention, and optimal management of IAD in Saudi healthcare settings.

Introduction

Incontinence‐associated dermatitis (IAD) is a common form of moisture‐associated skin damage (MASD), typically affecting patients with prolonged exposure to urine and/or feces. It is clinically characterized by skin erythema, edema, and, in some cases, serous exudates [1, 2, 3]. IAD often causes considerable discomfort, including pain, burning, and itching thereby negatively impacting patients' quality of life [4]. It is commonly linked to macerated, superficial skin damage caused by prolonged incontinence. Effective prevention and treatment must address not only skin maceration but also chemical and physical irritants contributing to skin breakdown [5, 6]. Key risk factors include frequent stools, poor skin condition, impaired oxygenation, pain, fever, and limited mobility [7, 8]. Additionally, comorbidities such as low serum albumin levels increase vulnerability to skin damage, while higher albumin levels may offer some protective effect [9, 10, 11]. IAD is also associated with complications including pressure ulcers and secondary skin infections caused by Candida species and Pseudomonas aeruginosa [1, 12, 13].

Owing to its impact on patient outcomes and healthcare resources, IAD has received increasing attention in both clinical practice and research. Its prevalence has been estimated to vary widely across healthcare settings, ranging from 5.6% to 50% [14]. A cross‐sectional study in Australian acute care patients reported a 10% prevalence, while another study conducted in two U.S. hospitals reported that 20% of incontinent patients in acute care had IAD [15, 16]. Additionally, a prevalence of 12% was observed among patients with urinary incontinence, rising to 26% among those using fecal management devices [17]. Notably, one study found that 73% of IAD cases developed after hospital admission, highlighting the importance of preventative strategies within healthcare facilities [18]. However, there are currently no published prevalence estimates of IAD in Saudi acute care settings, representing a significant data gap when compared to international reports.

Several shared risk factors such as prolonged hospital stay, use of multiple linen layers, and immobility link IAD and hospital‐acquired pressure injuries (PIs); however, IAD remains an independent risk factor for sacral region PIs even after adjusting for this limitation in mobility [17, 18]. Therefore, prompt and accurate identification of IAD is critical to preventing its progression and associated clinical complications. However, limited awareness among multidisciplinary teams, particularly nurses, about distinction between IAD and PIs presents a significant barrier to effective care. The absence of a diagnostically accurate, universally accepted, and reliably administered instrument further complicates prevention and treatment [19, 20, 21, 22]. Moreover, insufficient knowledge may lead to inappropriate product use or interventions, resulting in prolonged hospital stays and poorer patient outcomes [23, 24]. These challenges underscore the need to strengthen nurses' knowledge as a fundamental component of effective IAD prevention and management.

Numerous international studies have examined the status of nurses' knowledge related to IAD and the impact of training interventions. For example, a study among Turkish nurses reported that those working in intensive care and palliative care settings had insufficient knowledge, attitudes, and practices related to IAD [25]. Another study found that intensive care unit (ICU) nurses often relied on clinical experience and lacked formal training on the subject [26]. Similarly, a study conducted among nurses in a Brazilian teaching hospital revealed noteworthy knowledge gaps, particularly in identifying IAD, such as accurately locating and evaluating IAD in comparison to PIs [7]. Separately, another study found that ICU nurses generally had low knowledge scores regarding the prevention and management of IAD, with significant differences observed based on factors such as years of experience, job role, and prior training. These findings underscore the urgent need for structured, on‐the‐job education to enhance IAD‐related knowledge and promote standardized preventive care practices [27].

Educational interventions have been shown to enhance nurses' ability to correctly identify IAD, differentiate it from PIs, and implement appropriate skin care techniques [28]. Similar conclusions were drawn in a study conducted among Korean nurses, which emphasized the need for educational programs focused specifically on IAD. The study recommended incorporating case‐based and clinical skills training to strengthen nurses' ability to distinguish IAD from PIs [29].

Within the Saudi Arabian context, existing literature indicates that only one study has evaluated nurses' knowledge of IAD, reporting sufficient understanding of its etiology, identification, and associated risk factors, along with a strong intention to prevent it [30]. However, evidence remains limited regarding nurses' knowledge across other critical domains, including classification, diagnostic differentiation, and specific prevention and management strategies. Strengthening knowledge in these areas is essential to inform targeted clinical training, improve care delivery, and reduce the burden of IAD.

To address this gap, the present study employs the validated Know‐IAD instrument, which consists of 18 items across three key domains: etiology and extrinsic risk factors (7 items), classification and diagnosis (5 items), and prevention and management (6 items). In addition, two validated clinical images were included to assess nurses' ability to distinguish IAD from PIs, providing a more comprehensive evaluation of nurses' knowledge within acute care settings in Saudi Arabia.

Materials and Methods

Study Design

This study utilized a descriptive cross‐sectional approach to evaluate nurses' knowledge of IAD within acute care hospital settings in Saudi Arabia from January to July 2024.

Setting

The study was conducted in three acute care hospitals which provide services to a diverse patient population, including adult and older adult patients. These hospitals follow international standards for patient care and are equipped with modern facilities.

Study Participants

The study included 169 registered nurses (RNs) providing direct care to adult and older adult patients in inpatient and intensive care units. These participants were selected from a total of 240 nurses invited via email and internal hospital communication systems, yielding a response rate of 84.5%. Only RNs who provided direct patient care qualified for participation. Nursing care assistants (staff who provide basic patient care and support under the supervision of registered nurses), float nurses (reassigned across units as needed), and locum nurses (temporary or agency‐based) were excluded.

Sample Size Calculation

The sample size was determined using Epi Info software (version 6.04), relying on a previously reported IAD knowledge prevalence of 66% [31], with a 95% CI and 5% margin of error, with N = 240, p = 0.66, q = 0.34, Z (1 − α/2) = 1.96, and d = 5%, the equation becomes n = Npq/[(d2/Z21 − α/2*(N − 1) + pq]). As a result, the equation is [240*0.66*0.34/[(0.052/1.962 * 239) + 0.66 (0.34)]. The resulting minimum required sample size was approximately 142. To account for an anticipated nonresponse rate of 20%, the sample size was adjusted to 178 (i.e., 142/0.80 = 177.5). Ultimately, 169 nurses responded, exceeding the minimum requirement.

Data Collection Tool

Data collection was carried out through a self‐administered online questionnaire designed in English, the main language of instruction for nurses in Saudi Arabia. There were three main sections in the questionnaire. The first part provided participants with information about the study, including its purpose, ethical considerations, and the estimated time required to complete the survey. The second part collected demographic data such as age, gender, educational qualifications, area of work, years of clinical experience, and nationality.

The third part, the Know‐IAD instrument, is a psychometrically validated tool designed by Barakat‐Johnson et al. (2022) to evaluate clinicians' understanding of IAD. It consisted of 18 statements categorized into three domains (see Table 2) [31]. The Know‐IAD exhibited robust content validity, achieving scale‐level Content Validity Ratio (S‐CVR) scores of 0.75 for item relevance and 0.82 for item clarity. The Scale‐level Content Validity Index (S‐CVI) values indicated strong content validity, with scores of ~ 0.87 for relevance and ~ 0.90 for clarity [31].

Composite reliability was also satisfactory across domains, with values of 0.76, 0.75, and 0.64, respectively. Additionally, inter‐domain correlations ranged from 0.19 to 0.31, indicating that the three domains capture distinct aspects of clinician knowledge related to IAD [31]. Two validated images were included as well to help differentiate IAD from pressure injuries (PIs). Each correct answer was counted as addition of 1 point, while incorrect or “I don't know” responses were given 0. The maximum possible score was 18, with a cut‐off of 70% indicating satisfactory knowledge.

Data Collection Process

Data collection was coordinated through nursing directors in the participating hospitals. Nurses were invited to participate via email and memorandum which is the internal communication systems. The questionnaire was administered using Google Forms, and responses were collected anonymously. The survey was administered in English.

Ethical Considerations

Ethical approval to conduct this study was obtained from Research Ethics Committee in Umm Al‐Qura University with (Protocol approval no UQUPGR/2023/02116770) on 2nd November 2023. In addition, approval from referred hospitals has been received. The researchers coordinated with nursing directors to disseminate a memorandum to all hospital departments outlining the details of the study and emphasizing the voluntary nature of participation. The survey was conducted anonymously, ensuring that no personal identifiers were gathered. Consent to participate was deemed by completing the questionnaire and submitted.

Statistical Analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS version 25 and demographic variables and knowledge scores were summarized using descriptive statistics. To examine the relationships between knowledge levels and demographic factors, Spearman's correlation and Chi‐square tests were applied, considering p‐values of 0.05 or less as indicative of statistical significance.

RNs Knowledge Towards IAD

As shown in Table 2, 133 participants (84%) correctly identified the first item regarding the definition of IAD, which emphasized that IAD results from exposure to urine or feces and affects areas beyond just the perineal region. In contrast, 76.3% of nurses incorrectly agreed with the statement that IAD cannot be prevented in cases of sudden, severe incontinence, suggesting a gap in understanding of prevention strategies. Statements 3 and 4, which addressed extrinsic factors contributing to IAD, had the highest correct response rate at 133 (78.7%). However, knowledge regarding IAD prevalence was lower, as only 87 participants (51.4%) correctly answered item 6, which stated that the incidence of IAD is generally below 5% in older adult's care units.

Regarding prevention strategies, only 61.5% of respondents correctly identified that the use of a thick, absorbent zinc‐based incontinence pad can reduce the risk of IAD (statement 13). A similar level of knowledge was observed for statement 18, which emphasized the importance of using skin cleansers, moisturizers, barrier protectants, and microbiological testing in cases such as candidiasis. One of the lowest correct response rates was seen for statement 15, which recommended the use of all‐in‐one large pads (nappy style) for IAD prevention even in cases of infrequent incontinence, with only 68 participants (40.2%) answering correctly.

Overall, the domain‐specific scores for Etiology and Extrinsic Risk Factors, Classification and Diagnosis, and Prevention and Management were 68.46%, 60.36%, and 58.08%, respectively. The overall average score was 62.30%, falling below the 70% threshold and indicating a generally low level of knowledge among participants. Discussion

This study explored nursing professionals' understanding of IAD across critical clinical domains such as etiology and risk factors, classification and diagnosis, as well as prevention and management. While general awareness of IAD's definition and risk factors was adequate, significant gaps emerged in diagnostic accuracy and use of evidence‐based prevention strategies. Many participants struggled to differentiate IAD from pressure injuries (PIs), often misapplying PI‐specific criteria such as blanching erythema, an issue also documented in other international studies [7, 31]. The findings underscore the need for enhanced visual literacy in skin assessment, which has been identified as a critical competency requiring structured educational support [22, 31].

In the domain of prevention and management, nurses frequently endorsed outdated or ineffective practices, such as using large incontinence pads or relying on soap and water with a washcloth for skin cleansing, approaches that are not aligned with current evidence‐based guidelines for IAD prevention. Although some participants demonstrated familiarity with appropriate use of skin protectants and moisturizers, knowledge remained inconsistent, indicating a reliance on habitual practice rather than formal guidelines. These trends align with prior research reporting similar knowledge‐practice gaps among nurses in various settings [32, 33].

Significant links were identified between specific demographic variables and the knowledge domains related to IAD. As shown in Table 3, qualification and area of work were significantly associated with all three knowledge domains, whereas a moderate positive correlation was observed between age and knowledge scores across all domains. Nurses who were older, held bachelor's or master's degrees, and worked in specialized areas such as critical care or wound management scored higher. Area of work positively influenced knowledge in prevention and management (p < 0.001), suggesting that increased clinical exposure may enhance competency in IAD practices. Interestingly, professional experience alone did not correlate with higher knowledge, highlighting the potential limitations of experiential learning without structured continuing education.

Interestingly, the gender of nurses was found to be significantly associated with knowledge of the etiology and extrinsic risk factors of IAD, while no such association was observed for the other two domains. Limited evidence in the existing literature addresses gender‐based differences in knowledge across specific IAD‐related domains. A recent study by Asiri et al. (2024) reported gender‐related differences in nurses' intentions to prevent IAD, with female nurses expressing stronger preventive intentions than their male counterparts [30]. These differences were attributed to potential variations in empathy, caregiving attitudes, and professional exposure between genders [30]. However, given the current gap in the literature and the limitations of the data available in this study, the association identified remains unexplained and highlights the need for further investigation.

Overall, the findings underscore the need for structured educational interventions in Saudi Arabia, including certification programs and evidence‐based protocols tailored to the local healthcare context. Incorporating validated assessment tools and updated clinical guidelines into nursing curricula and in‐service training could significantly improve diagnostic accuracy and consistency in IAD management. Future research should adopt prospective and interventional designs to evaluate the effectiveness of such strategies in enhancing clinical performance and patient outcomes. Furthermore, as current data on the prevalence of IAD in Saudi Arabia remains limited, efforts should be directed toward generating robust epidemiological evidence to better understand its burden and to assess how improved training and standardized management approaches influence IAD incidence and overall care quality.

Study Limitations

While the study offers novel insights into nurses' understanding of IAD, limitations cannot be ignored. Firstly, the cross‐sectional design restricts the study's capacity to determine causal links between demographic variables and IAD knowledge levels. Longitudinal studies are needed to explore how knowledge evolves over time and with increased clinical experience. Second, the study is dependent on self‐reported data that may not be devoid of response bias, such as social desirability bias, where participants may answer what they perceive as favorable rather than accurate.

Third, the study involved only three acute care hospitals in Saudi Arabia, which may not reflect the broader national healthcare context. For instance, nurses in rural or private hospitals may have different training and resource availability, potentially influencing their knowledge levels. Additionally, the study overlooked organizational influences, including the availability of IAD management protocols, access to specialized training, or the presence of wound care experts, all of which could shape nurses' knowledge and clinical practices.

Furthermore, the high proportion of non‐Saudi nurses (57.4%) in the sample may introduce variability, as these nurses may have received their training in different healthcare systems with varying standards and practices. Finally, the use of convenience sampling may have introduced selection bias, restricting the applicability of the results to a broader population. Future studies should employ random sampling techniques and include a more diverse range of healthcare settings to enhance the representativeness of the results.

Despite these limitations, the study provides important baseline data on nurses' knowledge of IAD in Saudi Arabia and highlights critical areas for improvement. Addressing these gaps in subsequent studies will enhance the validity of findings and support the development of tailored interventions to improve IAD management in clinical practice.

Conclusion

Institutional support in the form of training for common injuries in hospitals, such as IAD, was determined to be necessary for nurses. This should allow for the critical and ongoing development of knowledge, which in turn will facilitate the promotion of high‐quality health care by completing “gaps“ in knowledge and improving understanding and application of scientific insights in clinical practice. The current study will also contribute to future publications by providing theoretical support for the design and implementation of targeted educational interventions in the training of nurses and other health care professionals.
