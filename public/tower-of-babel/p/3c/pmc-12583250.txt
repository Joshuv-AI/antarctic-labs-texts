Scientific Writing– an Editor’s Memo to Emerging Authors-1
Authors: Shahul H. Ebrahim
Published: 2025

Introduction

Scientific writing is a global and public good. The concept of scientific writing and scientific journals as we know them evolved over time. The 2,000-plus years of evolution of scientific writing may have started with Archimedes’s On Floating Bodies in 250 BCE [1]. The concept was institutionalized in 1805, when the Royal Medical Society of Edinburgh established The Edinburgh Medical and Surgical Journal, later known as The Edinburgh Medical Journal,² and followed by the founding in 1823 of The Lancet. [2]

Much has changed since 1823. Along with the increasing technological solutions to improve writing and increasing journal options there has been a steady increase in competition for the visibility of scientific work. The suggestions below are based on my three decades of writing, reviewing, and editing experience. Here, I outline general expectations for a scientific manuscript, common pitfalls, how the flow of thought is organized, some common missteps to avoid, and the use of writing support along with a summary Panel of pitfalls and best practices.

Due Diligence Pays off

It Matters – a Scientific Contribution is Eternal!

While the most difficult part of getting your work published is acceptance in a desired journal, it is important to remember that a published scientific paper is a permanent record (like a tombstone), and read by both experts and laypeople. A scientific paper remains your legacy; it can make you famous or infamous, and there is no time limit for its impact. The benefit of due diligence is everlasting.

Beware – Readers Include Editors, Journalists, and Interest Groups

In the 1800 s, scientific communication targeted a closed network of experts in the field. Now, information technology enables a broad spectrum of readers to access scientific journals.

Editors are the first readers of all science publications. Given their exposure to multiple papers of varying quality and rigor daily, they can identify issues with completeness, clarity, and authenticity from the first paragraph. For an editor, the most rewarding experience is reading a well-written paper—a prerequisite for a favorable decision, if all other journal criteria are met. Well-written does not mean the paper must be accepted by a particular journal; it may be referred to another journals, or alternate analysis suggested.

Journal Dynamics Reflect Human Dynamics… News-maker Themes Prevail!

Yes, it is true: there is competition for new or newsworthy themes in all journals—and for good reason. If it is a new disease or outcome, reports of the first few cases are critical for the entire global medical enterprise. Viewpoints and summary discussion on new issues [3] (cited the referenced scored 676 citations) can usefully inform policy dialogue. The human curiosity for knowledge also means that by making known themes newsworthy [4], they too will receive attention, as in reference #4 that scored 621 citations. Within a journal, as multiple editors serve the editorial roles, intra-editor variations on article evaluation can occur.

What is Meant by Well Written?

The attribution refers to both individual statements and the flow of thought. A well-written piece conveys the message in simple, non-verbose statements, devoid of self-praise, and with sufficient facts presented in a format where there is synchrony between the various elements of the paper (title, objectives, methods, findings, and recommendations). For example, among the two statements: (a) “Obesity is a major public health problem in the US.” (b) “In the US, more than 2 in 5 adults (42%) have obesity.” statement (b) is preferred.

Avoid Common Writing Pitfalls

Don’t Forget the Single Overriding Communication Objective (SOCO)

Also known as the “elevator pitch” or “sales pitch,” each paper is designed to convey one or more take-home messages. The title provides clues to the SOCO and is reinforced in the abstract or summary, the beginning of the Results section (if it’s a research study), and the first few statements and final lines of the Discussion.

What is Efficiency of a Paper?

Disorganized manuscripts make it hard to follow even great ideas. Science readers seek quick access to information without wading through superfluous details, so concise writing helps them grasp key findings. Remember, most readers don’t read beyond the abstract or summary. Efficiency applies to all parts of the paper and implies the avoidance of unnecessary elaboration or embellishment, and of repeating identical statements or themes. Reducing word count can improve efficiency, as in the citation #4 that has only seven Discussion statments [5].

What is Completeness in Statements?

Completeness refers to the provision of all information that answers common lines of thought—such as what, where, when, how, and why. For example, the statement “Smoking decreased in recent decades” may be adequate in a current discussion, but when read from a written document five decades later, the reader must search for the time and context, which interrupts the flow of reading. Therefore, a complete statement would be: “The overall percentage of women who had ever initiated smoking decreased significantly from 44.1% in 1987 to 38.2% in 1996.” [6, 7]

What is Clarity of Statements?

Clarity implies avoiding ambiguity, vagueness, and unnecessary complexity to ensure the reader can quickly grasp the intended meaning. In the previous example [6], the word ‘percentage’ specifies the metric of smoking. The word ‘women’ emphasizes demographic subgroup. The word ‘significantly’ implies statistical relevance.

What is Utility of Statements

Although all information is valuable, its utility depends on the reader’s purpose. For a health policy person, the current situation (e.g., “What is the prevalence of smoking today?”) is most relevant, while historical information is useful for evaluating interventions or judging a program. Data-rich statements related to your title and objectives, rather than subjective or content-free statements, have the highest utility.⁵.

What is Brevity?

Scientific writing conveys technical information in an unbiased, straightforward manner—unlike fiction, which includes stylistic elements and narrative depth to evoke emotions and create vivid experiences, often requiring more words. For example, the scientific statement: “Sunlight availability enhances photosynthetic efficiency of plants.” would be much longer in fiction: “With dawn, the little plant unfurled its leaves, reaching eagerly for the sun’s warm embrace; a surge of energy pulsing through its veins.” A paper does not have to be long to be impactful [5].

Avoid Speed-breaker Abbreviations and Jargons

Except for a few well-known abbreviations introduced at the start (e.g., WHO, ICD, DSM), minimize abbreviations, even if defined. They disrupt reading flow and are not universally recognized. Always consider readers from adjacent fields. Readers may forget and need to search back. Also abbreviations may change or become non-existent over time.

Avoid Standalone Analysis and Reviews on Arbitrary Sub-groups or Themes

Sub-analyses on children, adults, pregnant women, the elderly, marginalized populations, or socioeconomically relevant groups may have policy significance. However, arbitrary subgroups or subthemes rarely add scientific or policy value. For example, three separate papers on those aged over 70, 75, and 80 years—without a compelling rationale—are unlikely to be well received.

Avoid Passive Voice Overload?

Active voice is more direct and uses fewer words- most useful when reporting original research. Active voice indicates accountability, makes it clear who is performing the action, and sounds more confident and engaging. In active voice, the subject (the researcher/s) does the action (analyzed) to the object (the national mortality data). Some examples to begin statements in active voice: “I/We calculated…,” “I/We defined…,” “I/We estimated…,” Passive voice can be useful when the actor is unknown or irrelevant, when you want to soften the tone or be more diplomatic, or when you want to emphasize the action or result rather than the actor.

Don’t Let Perceived Novelty Kill Reader Interest

Researchers are passionate about their topics, and immersion in the work can reinforce their assumptions (most important, most comprehensive, or first of its kind). Public health implies equity, and all diseases are of concern. The researcher’s job is to convey the importance of their work with facts and let the reader be the judge. Opening statements such as “AIDS is a major/significant public health problem” or “This is the most comprehensive review…” are off-putting to informed readers. Example of an informative statement: “In (year), HIV was prevalent among one-third of the population in (country),”. Similarly, because all reviews are comprehensive, emphasizing “comprehensiveness” is an overkill. Describing a study as the “first of its kind [8]” may be appropriate for new or rare diseases.

Assure Representativeness of Data Across Title and Narrative

Lack of representativeness of a country or population group should be reflected in the title, rather than surprising the reader in the narrative or abstract. As mentioned earlier, if work is done in a specific city or among a specific group, state that in the title. [6]

Title is Your Key Marketing Piece

The decision to read a paper begins with the title. If it is too wordy, readers are likely to skip it. Overpromising titles may not make it past the editor. The title should clearly guide the reader on what to expect—no more, no less. For example, if a paper is titled “Smoking in the United States, 2025,” readers anticipate information on the overall burden and key determinants by demographics and geography. If you believe there are data gaps in some elements, you may add the word “select.” If focusing on a specific group, include that in the title—as in: “Trends in pregnancy-related smoking rates in the United States, 1987–1996” [6]

Abstract is Key to Sustaining Reader Interest

The abstract, structured (IMRAD format, see below) or unstructured, is the second opportunity after the title for you to showcase the most salient aspects of your work and often key in getting the work referenced. If the key answers to what is anticipated in the title are not included in the abstract, further interest in the paper is unlikely.

Author List

“Substantial contribution” is the key criterion for determining the number and order of co-authors and depends on the expertise involved in conception, execution (including lab work), analysis, facilitation, policy framing, and drafting. For policy-relevant public health themes, adequate country representation is important when reporting on few countries. Secondary analysis of single-country data on policy relevant themes by researchers from other countries would benefit from collaboration with relevant in-country experts, as in reference. [9]

The Lay Out

The Inverted Triangle Approach

The “inverted triangle” is an aphorism referring to the recommended sequence for framing information, where the most critical element is presented first, followed by details. This approach applies to all sections of the paper. If a reader is already familiar with the subject, they can skip the rest of the statements. For example, the methods section of a paper assessing national trends in influenza-related would start with: “We used the (country) national vital statistics data from 2000 through 2025 and obtained the number of deaths with influenza-related ICD codes (Panel 1) listed as the cause of death.” If the piece is a viewpoint focused on three themes, the opening sentence of each paragraph should clearly present those ideas: “First…,” “Second…,” and “Finally…”. [9]

The IMRAD Lay out of Journal Articles– Remember, it is not a Thesis

Each section has its own rules and audience expectations. Think of these sections as parts of a movie: the hook, the setup, the climax, and the resolution. Large studies with multiple variables or determinants may require subheadings under Methods and Results. Repetition of content across sections verbatim should be avoided.

The Methods section explains how the data were collected or what pre-existing data were used, and what was done with the data, including statistical methods—presented in a way that enables another researcher to replicate the work.

Results should begin with an overview (e.g., “Of the total sample of XX…”), followed by key stratified analytical findings and final statistical inferences. Do not dump all analytic outputs in the paper, pick and present what is relevant to the title.

The Discussion is a distilled summary of the Results as it relates to the broader knowledge on the theme. Many authors repeat the objective of the paper as the opening sentence of the Discussion—this should be avoided. Instead, Discussion should provide two to five key take-home messages that readers can recall and typically start with active voice statements such as, “We found… Our analysis suggests…., We showed…, This analysis reveals…. ” For instance, if you showed a 30.7% prevalence of adult obesity in the U.S. in the Results section, the Discussion could state, “Our analysis indicates that about one-third of the adult U.S. population is obese.” As in the above example, convert numbers to easy recall format to convey the magnitude. If the work is truly novel, such as reporting a first national survey, you may write, “This first national survey on (theme) in (country) found that…” Or, for rare conditions: “This largest sample of patients with (disease) reported worldwide reveals…” Succeeding paragraphs in the Discussion may compare findings to previous literature, propose new hypotheses, acknowledge limitations (see relevant section), and suggest follow-on research or policy actions. For example: “Our finding of no MERS outbreak among all camel-herding populations implies the role of other necessary co-factors in MERS expression. Studies X, Y, and Z could help further elucidate the transmission dynamics of MERS”. Avoid nonspecific statements such as “Further studies on MERS are needed.” or “More interventions are needed.” Be specific. Authors converting theses into journal articles often include too many subheadings—they should be subsumed in the IMRAD subheadings.

Tables and Figures

Data visualization through tables and figures enhances readability. However, excessive (generally no more than five combined) or overly lengthy visuals are discouraged. Most journals offer online supplementary materials for excess content. Common mistakes include Data cluttering, Incomplete titles, Repeating table/figure data in the narrative. Avoid data cluttering—tables with more than five columns are usually hard to read. For instance, when reporting demographic distributions: once the total number (N) is provided, percentages suffice for sub-strata: E.g., “Marital status (N): Single 30%, Married 50%, Other 20%.” Avoid repeating these exact data from tables in the narrative. Instead, summarize. Rather than stating, “The demographic characteristics of the study sample appear in Table 1,” for the above example, you could state, “The majority of the sampled population were married men with high school education or less (Table 1).” Align number values (especially decimals) to improve visual clarity of what values are single digit and multidigit. Avoid Portraying Limitations as a Key Subheading

If a study has “several” limitations, readers may question its credibility. Use “some limitations” instead or integrate them directly into the discussion. Limitations can often be balanced with strengths in the same statement. For example, “Though information recorded in patient charts may vary due to variations in clinician’s documentation styles, our findings demonstrate the utility of routine health system data to understand the epidemiology of (disease), offering a cost-effective alternative to prospective studies.”

What to Cite?

Generally, statistical statements or key concepts drawn from others work require citations, that also enable other readers to do their own review. Citation norms evolve—e.g., nobody today cites that HIV causes AIDS, but in the 1980 s, it was required. Ensure that recent data is cited.

What Section to Start Writing?

Start with the section you are most comfortable writing. Some authors begin with tables/figures, others with the abstract or presentation slides. Many follow the conventional IMRAD sequence.

Writing Support and Fine Tuning

Some journals offer dedicated in-house editing services for the articles they consider publishing, but the majority may not. Colleagues, supervisors, grammar check software, and even non-expert friends and relatives can offer comments on various aspects of the paper. The use of support technologies requires review by the authors because what is grammatically correct may not convey the intended meaning - an electronic tool may not detect the contextual difference between ‘STI related mortality’ and “STI-related morality”.

Given the recent concerns about the flooding of journals with artificial intelligence (AI)-generated commentaries and reviews, journal policies are emerging on the use of AI. AI-generated literature search is believed to be incomplete [10]. In addition to ‘AI slop’, AI outputs may create an illusion of completeness. AI-generated text can also be a marker of submissions that might have come from paper mills, and when undisclosed, Journals may flag authors associated with exclusively AI-written documents. For now, [10–12]just as computational statistical and writing tools evolved, AI should be viewed as a new tool to assist with tasks (for example, grammar corrections, data visualization, summarizing, data analysis), and not as a substitute for the critical thinking and originality required for scientific writing.

Conform to Norms First, then Innovate, and keep Writing

The mantra to good writing is: read – generate ideas – consult – conduct the work – write – revise – revise – revise. Large Language Models of (AI), underscores the role of “language” -implying that AI systems discern patterns and develop algorithms by skimming through online materials. Therefore, reading published work is the most important first step in perfecting the art of science concepts. Choose what to read. Reading reports on well-conducted studies—such as national surveys (e.g., NHANES, Demographic and Health Surveys), randomized controlled trials, and pharmaceutical research reports—all help in idea generation. Read comments and editorials related to such papers. Pause between revisions and drafts to allow time for your thoughts to crystallize. Pay attention to reviewer comments, even if they are negative. Generally, being competent in publishing original research work enables authors to expand their writing scope to commentaries, perspectives, and editorials. The goal in scientific writing s not just to get published—but to be read, cited, remembered and impactful.
