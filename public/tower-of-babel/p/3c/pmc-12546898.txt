Integrated physiological, biochemical and transcriptomic analyses revealed key genes regulating ascorbic acid biosynthesis during sweet potato development
Authors: Qian Zhou, Yajie Wang, Letan Luo, Yongxin Li, Wanying Ge, Huqing Yang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Sweet potato (Ipomoea batatas Lam.) is a globally important food crop with higher ascorbic acid (AsA) content than most staple crops. However, the regulatory mechanisms of AsA accumulation in sweet potato remain unclear. In this study, we systematically examined the regulatory network of AsA metabolism by integrating physiological and biochemical analyses with transcriptome sequencing in four representative varieties. The results revealed significant variation in AsA accumulation during root development, ranging from 10.96 to 36.45 mg/100 g FW. Metabolic pathway analysis showed that AsA biosynthesis is mainly regulated through the L-galactose pathway, involving the genes HK, PMI, PMM, GME, and GalLDH, and the myo-inositol pathway represented by MIOX. Weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) further identified MIOX as a highly connected hub gene within the AsA-associated network module. These findings provide important insights into the molecular basis of AsA metabolism in sweet potato and highlight promising candidate genes for future functional validation and molecular breeding efforts aimed at enhancing nutritional quality.

Introduction

Ascorbic acid (AsA), a key water-soluble antioxidant commonly known as vitamin C, is essential for plant metabolic regulation (Smirnoff & Wheeler, 2024). Beyond photosynthesis, AsA plays vital roles in processes such as cell proliferation and stress responses (Liao et al., 2023). In humans, AsA is a critical micronutrient that prevents and treats scurvy, supports metabolic homeostasis, and shows protective effects against chronic diseases and cancer (Miranda-Massari et al., 2020; Pawlowska et al., 2019). However, humans lack the gene for L-gulonolactone oxidase (GLO), the enzyme required for endogenous AsA biosynthesis, making dietary intake necessary (Smirnoff, 2018). Because of its crucial roles in plant biology and human health, AsA content is a major determinant of the nutritional quality and market value of agricultural products (Liao et al., 2023).

In plants, AsA homeostasis is maintained by the coordinated regulation of biosynthesis, oxidative catabolism, and recycling (Noctor, 1998). AsA biosynthesis in higher plants proceeds through four major pathways (Fig. 1): the L-galactose pathway, the D-galacturonic acid pathway, the L-gulose pathway, and the inositol pathway (Quiñones et al., 2024). The L-galactose pathway converts D-mannose to L-galactose sequentially, ultimately producing AsA. The key regulatory enzymes in these pathways vary among species. For example, in tomato, GDP-mannose-3′,5′-isomerase (GME) acts as the primary rate-limiting enzyme (Mounet-Gilbert et al., 2016), while in kiwifruit (Liu et al., 2021) and prickly pear (Huang et al., 2025), GDP-L-galactose phosphorylase (GGP) is predominant. Notably, in chili peppers, L-galactonate-1,4-lactone dehydrogenase (GalLDH) serves as the key rate-limiting enzyme, catalyzing the terminal oxidation step in AsA biosynthesis (Rodríguez-Ruiz et al., 2017).Fig. 1Metabolic pathways of ascorbic acid in plants. In the AsA metabolic regulatory system, the oxidation–regeneration cycle (Fig. 1) is a key mechanism for maintaining AsA homeostasis. In this cycle, ascorbate oxidase (AAO) and ascorbate peroxidase (APX) catalyze the oxidation of AsA to monodehydroascorbic acid (MDHA) and dehydroascorbic acid (DHA), respectively. Conversely, monodehydroascorbate reductase (MDHAR) and dehydroascorbate reductase (DHAR) catalyze the reduction of MDHA and DHA back to AsA (Yoshimura & Ishikawa, 2024). Evidence shows that AAO and APX are critical for AsA redox balance, while MDHAR and DHAR expression strongly correlates with AsA accumulation in plants such as lettuce (Zha et al., 2020) and prickly pear (Dong et al., 2025).

Sweet potato (Ipomoea batatas Lam.), a tuberous crop of the Convolvulaceae family, originated in South America (Liu et al., 2024). It is now cultivated in 116 countries, with Asia, Africa, and the Americas as the main production regions, owing to its broad adaptability and stress tolerance (FAOSTAT, 2022). Historically, sweet potato has been an important substitute for staple cereals during food shortages (Iese et al., 2018). Its storage roots are rich in carbohydrates, dietary fiber, proteins, vitamins (especially AsA), and essential minerals such as calcium, iron, and potassium. Compared with many staple crops, sweet potato roots contain higher AsA levels, with considerable variation among varieties (Alam et al., 2020; Kareem et al., 2020). However, most research on AsA metabolism has focused on fruit crops such as kiwifruit, strawberry, and prickly pear, while the accumulation pattern and regulatory mechanisms in sweet potato remain largely unknown.

In this study, we combined physiological and biochemical analyses, enzyme activity assays, and transcriptome profiling to investigate AsA accumulation in sweet potato varieties during development. This study aimed to define AsA accumulation patterns, explore its association with key metabolic enzymes, and identify the regulatory network governing AsA metabolism in sweet potato. Therefore, these findings clarify the molecular basis of AsA biosynthesis and provide a foundation for identifying candidate genes involved in its regulation.

Materials and methods

Plant materials and sampling

In this study, four sweet potato varieties were selected as experimental materials: ‘Yanshu 25’ (a high-AsA variety widely grown in northern China), ‘Pushu 32’ (a main variety in southern China), ‘Ziluolan’ (a characteristic purple-fleshed variety), and ‘Hangshu 2’ (a locally adapted variety). All were cultivated at Zhejiang Agriculture and Forestry University's Pingshan Experimental Base (30°26′ N, 119°72′ E). During growth, the ambient temperature was maintained at 25–30 °C with sufficient light. The plants were drip-irrigated weekly to keep soil moisture at 70–80 % of field capacity. Fertilizers applied included urea (46 % N), calcium superphosphate (12 % P2O3), and potassium sulfate (50 % K2O). Pest and weed management followed local standard cultivation practices to minimize biological stress. Samples were collected at 70 d (S1), 90 d (S2), 110 d (S3), 130 d (S4), and 150 d (S5) after planting. At each sampling, uniform roots were selected, washed, peeled, cut into pieces, snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at −80 °C for subsequent assays. For each physiological and enzymatic assay, three technical replicates were performed per biological sample to ensure precision. Reported values represent the mean ± standard deviation (SD) of these replicates.

Measurement of starch and soluble sugar contents

Starch and soluble sugar contents were measured following Yang et al. (2018). Starch content was determined by the acid hydrolysis method. Soluble sugars were removed with 85 % ethanol, and the precipitate was treated with 10 mL of 6 mol/L hydrochloric acid and 14 mL of water, then hydrolyzed in a boiling water bath for 60 min. The hydrolysate was filtered, neutralized to pH 7.0–7.5, and diluted to 50 mL for measurement.

The soluble sugar content was determined as follows. Sweet potato tissue (1.0 g) was homogenized in distilled water and extracted in hot water for 30 min. After centrifugation, the supernatant was collected, and the pellet was re-extracted under identical conditions. For quantification, 0.5 mL of the extract was mixed with anthrone reagent and concentrated sulfuric acid, incubated in boiling water, and the absorbance was measured at 630 nm.

Measurement of AsA content

AsA content was quantified using a modified protocol from Rautenbach et al. (2010). Frozen tissue (1.0 g) was homogenized in 0.1 % metaphosphoric acid (pH 3.5) under ice bath conditions, and the volume was adjusted to 20 mL. The supernatant was collected after ultrasonic centrifugation. An 800 μL aliquot of the clarified extract was mixed with 200 μL dithiothreitol in an HPLC vial before chromatographic analysis, incubated for 30 min at low temperature and protected from light to stabilize AsA, and then analyzed by HPLC.

Measurement of enzyme activities

GalLDH activity in sweet potato was determined using a commercial assay kit (Beijing Suolaibao Technology Co., Ltd., China) according to the manufacturer's instructions.

APX activity was measured using a commercial determination kit (Jiancheng, Nanjing, China).

AAO, DHAR, and MDHAR activities were assayed following Wang, Diao, et al. (2023) with modifications. Crude enzyme extracts were prepared from sweet potato tissue (1.0 g) homogenized in 10 mL extraction buffer (1 mM AsA, 1 mM EDTA, 20 g/L polyvinylpyrrolidone, 0.25 % Triton X-100). After centrifugation (16,000 ×g, 20 min, 4 °C), the clarified supernatant was used for enzymatic assays. AAO and DHAR activities were measured at 265 nm, while MDHAR activity was measured at 340 nm.

Transcriptome sequencing

Based on previous measurements of AsA content in four sweet potato varieties, we selected samples from each variety at two developmental stages (S3 and S5) for transcriptome analysis. Total RNA was extracted using the RNA kit (TIANGEN Biochemical Technology, Beijing, China) under standard room temperature conditions (25 °C). RNA-seq was performed by Meiji Biomedical Technology (Shanghai, China). The workflow included RNA fragmentation, cDNA synthesis, and end repair with the Illumina TruSeq™ RNA kit (Bioengineering Co., Ltd., Dalian, China), followed by paired-end 150 bp (PE150) high-throughput sequencing on the Illumina HiSeq 4000 SBS platform. For data analysis, the sweet potato reference genome (http://sweetpotato.plantbiology.msu.edu/gt4sp_download.shtml) was used as the benchmark. Read mapping was conducted with HISAT2, and transcript abundance was quantified by the FPKM method (Trapnell et al., 2010). Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified using DESeq2 (Love et al., 2014), applying a false discovery rate < 0.05 and |log2(fold change)| ≥ 1. Subsequently, functional annotation of DEGs was performed through Gene Ontology (GO) classification and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analysis.

Reverse transcription-quantitative polymerase chain reaction

For genomic DNA removal and subsequent cDNA synthesis, we used the PrimeScript™ RT Reagent Kit with gDNA Eraser (Takara, Dalian, China). qRT-PCR reactions were prepared following the TB Green Premix Ex Taq II kit instructions (Takara, Dalian, China) and run on a StepOnePlus™ Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems, USA). Primer sequences are listed in Table S3. Gene expression levels were quantified using the 2-ΔΔCT method (Wang, Li, et al., 2023), with α-tubulin as the endogenous control.

Co-expression network analysis and module identification

To identify key regulatory genes involved in AsA biosynthesis, we performed WGCNA using the R package and visualized the resulting gene regulatory network with Cytoscape (v3.10) (Shannon et al., 2003).

Statistical analysis

All experiments included three biological replicates. Statistical significance among groups was tested by one-way ANOVA in SPSS 22.0. Post-hoc analyses employed the LSD test, chosen for its sensitivity in detecting pairwise differences after significant ANOVA, and the Waller-Duncan procedure, applied as a Bayesian-adjusted means separation method to reduce error inflation. Data visualization and correlation analyses were conducted with OriginPro 2021.

Changes in starch and soluble sugar contents during roots development

As shown in Fig. 2D, ‘Hangshu 2’ accumulated significantly more starch than the other cultivars during development (P < 0.05), peaking at the early stage (S1) with 289.3 ± 1.6 g/kg FW. This indicates a strong starch synthesis capacity during early root development. Although starch content declined slightly at S5 (233.9 ± 12.9 g/kg FW), it remained higher than in the other cultivars, reflecting superior starch accumulation and compositional stability. In contrast, ‘Yanshu 25’ maintained consistently low starch levels, reaching its minimum at S5.

Soluble sugar accumulation patterns varied among varieties (Fig. 2E). At the early stage (S2), ‘Hangshu 2’ contained significantly higher soluble sugar levels, about 1–2 times greater than the other varieties (P < 0.05), indicating strong sugar accumulation capacity. At the middle stage of development (S3), it continued to maintain high levels, while ‘Yanshu 25’ declined sharply, reaching its lowest value at the late stage (S5).

Changes in AsA content during roots development

AsA content followed a typical “rise–decline” trend across all varieties (Fig. 2C), with significant differences among them (P < 0.05). Among them, ‘Hangshu 2’ demonstrated the greatest AsA accumulation, peaking at S3 with 36.4 ± 0.7 mg/100 g FW, approximately 1–3 times higher than the other varieties (P < 0.05). This advantage may result from greater AsA biosynthetic capacity or a more active metabolic regulatory network. At maturity (S3–S5), AsA declined in all varieties, though ‘Hangshu 2’ maintained the highest content at 150 d, while ‘Ziluolan’ dropped to the lowest level. Notably, ‘Yanshu 25’ consistently exhibited low AsA levels, reaching just 11.0 ± 0.4 mg/100 g FW at S4, lower than all other varieties.

All four varieties exhibited high APX activity in the early stages, followed by a gradual decline (Fig. 3B). ‘Pushu 32’ maintained significantly higher activity than the others across all stages (P < 0.05), peaking at S2 (1.48 ± 0.10 U/g FW). In contrast, ‘Yanshu 25’ showed relatively low APX activity throughout. Notably, APX activity in ‘Hangshu 2’ dropped sharply at S4, reaching the lowest level (0.72 ± 0.04 U/g FW), suggesting reduced antioxidant capacity at later stages, while ‘Ziluolan’ showed fluctuations but remained lower than ‘Pushu 32’.

As shown in Fig. 3C, AAO activity followed a rise–fall pattern during root development. In ‘Yanshu 25’, activity began at 1.34 ± 0.07 U/g FW (S1), peaked at S4 (1.99 ± 0.09 U/g FW), and declined to 1.14 ± 0.08 U/g FW at S5. A similar trend was observed in ‘Pushu 32’, though its peak at S4 (1.55 ± 0.04 U/g FW) was notably lower. In ‘Hangshu 2’, activity remained relatively high at S2 and S3 before gradually decreasing, while in ‘Ziluolan’ it peaked at S4 (1.68 ± 0.09 U/g FW).

The MDHAR activity of ‘Yanshu 25’ was 0.98 ± 0.08 U/g FW at S1, peaked at S3 (1.09 ± 0.08 U/g FW), and declined to 0.74 ± 0.08 U/g FW at S5 (Fig. 3D). In ‘Pushu 32’, activity peaked at S2 and then declined with fluctuations. ‘Hangshu 2’ displayed a distinct rise at S4 (1.31 ± 0.02 U/g FW), significantly higher than the other varieties (P < 0.05), indicating stronger AsA regeneration capacity at later stages. Similarly, MDHAR activity of ‘Ziluolan’ peaked at S3, followed by a gradual decline.

The DHAR activities of the four varieties followed a ‘W'-shaped pattern during root development (Fig. 3E), with significant inter-varietal differences (P < 0.05). In ‘Ziluolan’, DHAR activity was highest at S1 (5.61 ± 0.14 U/g FW), dropped to 3.82 ± 0.03 U/g FW at S2, and rebounded to 5.16 ± 0.28 U/g FW at S3. In ‘Pushu 32′, activity peaked at S3, indicating strong AsA regeneration capacity. ‘Hangshu 2′ showed a gradual increase toward the later stages, reaching 4.21 ± 0.38 U/g FW at S5, while ‘Yanshu 25′ consistently maintained the lowest activity, suggesting weaker AsA recycling capacity.

Correlation analysis

Heatmaps (Fig. 4A-D) and Pearson correlation analysis revealed variety-specific associations between AsA content and physiological or enzymatic parameters. In ‘Yanshu 25’, AsA levels correlated mainly with GalLDH (r = 0.74) and MDHAR (r = 0.65) activities. In ‘Pushu 32’, GalLDH (r = 1.00) and DHAR (r = 0.90) showed strong positive correlations with AsA accumulation, while AAO activity (r = −0.71) was significantly negatively correlated, consistent with its catabolic role. In ‘Hangshu 2’, AsA accumulation correlated positively with fresh weight (r = 0.93) and MDHAR (r = 0.62), but negatively with APX (r = −0.56) and starch content (r = −0.65). RNA-seq analysis during roots development

RNA-seq analysis generated 1,016,025,746 raw reads from 24 samples. After quality filtering, 1,009,833,382 clean reads were retained and successfully mapped to the reference genome, with >74.08 % alignment in each library. Additionally, base quality scores confirmed high sequencing accuracy, with Q20 and Q30 exceeding 98.58 % and 95.45 %, respectively, and GC content ranging from 44.59 % to 46.06 % (Table S1). These metrics confirm the high quality and reliability of the sequencing data, providing a robust foundation for transcriptomic analysis of sweet potato tuberous roots.

Principal component analysis (PCA) demonstrated tight clustering among triplicate biological replicates for all experimental groups, indicating high reproducibility and sample consistency (Fig. 5A). In contrast, samples from different developmental stages showed distinct spatial segregation in the PCA plots, reflecting major changes in gene expression during sweet potato development. Venn diagram analysis identified 10,589 and 11,569 DEGs at stages S3 and S5, respectively. Notably, the inter-variety comparison H5_vs_Y5 showed the largest transcriptomic differences, with 1744 upregulated and 2390 downregulated genes (Fig. 5B-D). WGCNA analysis of key gene modules related to AsA accumulation

To further investigate the key genes regulating AsA accumulation during sweet potato root development, we conducted a comprehensive analysis of RNA sequencing data. Of 32,301 expressed genes, 11,472 highly expressed genes (FPKM ≥1) were selected for downstream analysis. WGCNA was applied for dimensionality reduction and construction of a gene co-expression network. Based on Pearson correlation coefficients, these genes were clustered into 30 feature modules, each represented by a distinct color (Fig. 7A). Genes within the same module showed strong co-expression, suggesting functional relationships. To explore the biological roles of these modules, we performed correlation analyses between the 30 module-characterized genes (MEs) with key AsA metabolic indicators, including AsA content, GalLDH, APX, AAO, MDHAR, and DHAR activities. The darkturquoise module showed the strongest positive correlation with AsA metabolism (r = 0.817, P = 1.11 × 10−6), indicating that its constituent genes likely regulate AsA biosynthesis and metabolism (Fig. 7B). KEGG enrichment analysis revealed that darkturquoise genes were mainly enriched in glucosinolate biosynthesis, aminoacyl-tRNA biosynthesis, and ascorbate and aldarate metabolism pathways (Fig. 7D).

To pinpoint key AsA metabolism-related genes within this module, we selected the top 30 genes with the highest connectivity for gene network analysis (Fig. 7E, Table S4–S5). Results identified MIOX, a key enzyme in the inositol pathway, as the central gene in the co-expression network, underscoring its pivotal role in AsA biosynthesis in sweet potato. Further analysis highlighted four hub genes strongly associated with AsA content, all functionally linked to MIOX. These encode a kinesin-like protein (itf01g18300), TMV resistance protein (itf04g09550), ATP synthase (itf00g19700), and AT-hook motif protein (itf12g22780).

qRT-PCR validation

To evaluate transcriptome data reliability, six randomly selected genes involved in AsA biosynthesis and catabolism were validated using qRT-PCR. The qRT-PCR results showed consistency with transcriptomic data, confirming the reliability of RNA sequencing analysis (Fig. S1).

Discussion

AsA is a key water-soluble antioxidant and cofactor that maintains redox homeostasis, regulates immunity and metabolism in animals, and contributes to photosynthesis, respiration, and physiological metabolism in plants (Liu et al., 2021). Notably, AsA biosynthetic capacity varies greatly among species and varieties. For instance, ripe kiwifruit can contain up to 1108.76 mg/100 g FW AsA (Deng et al., 2022), compared with only 26.70 mg/100 g FW in apple (Lemmens et al., 2020) and 1320.90 mg/100 g FW in prickly pear (Ming et al., 2014). Such variation occurs not only between species but also among varieties within the same species. Lin et al. (2022) reported more than a fivefold difference in AsA content among kiwifruit varieties, and similar patterns have been observed in cucumber (Zhao et al., 2025).

In this study, examining sweet potato root development, we observed significant differences in AsA content among four cultivars (Fig. 2C). ‘Hangshu 2’ consistently maintained high AsA levels (peaking at 36.4 mg/100 g FW), while ‘Yanshu 25’ remained low (peaking at only 16.9 mg/100 g FW). Importantly, all cultivars displayed a ‘first increasing and then decreasing’ accumulation pattern, consistent with reported trends in raspberry (Hancock et al., 2007), kiwifruit (Liao et al., 2019), and peach (Imai et al., 2009).

Sugars, as key precursors for AsA biosynthesis, not only provide carbon skeletons for its synthesis but also regulate AsA metabolism-related enzyme genes through signal transduction pathways (Sawake et al., 2015). In this study, we observed significant differences in the correlation between soluble sugar content and AsA levels. Specifically, ‘Pushu 32’ showed a significant positive association (P < 0.05), whereas no such correlation was found in ‘Hangshu 2’ or ‘Ziluolan’ (Fig. 4A–D), consistent with previous findings in other crops (Massot et al., 2010; Yang et al., 2013). Notably, although AsA content in ‘Hangshu 2’ was significantly higher than in ‘Ziluolan’ (Fig. 2C), no significant difference in soluble sugar content was detected between them (Fig. 2E). This suggests that, beyond sugar metabolism, other regulatory factors, particularly the activities of key AsA biosynthetic enzymes, may play a dominant role in AsA accumulation. Starch, the principal storage compound in sweet potato roots, also displayed distinct spatiotemporal accumulation patterns. Previous studies reported large variation in starch content across developmental stages in sweet potato (Tang et al., 2023), consistent with the dynamic trends observed among the four cultivars in this study.

AsA accumulation in sweet potato depends on the coordinated regulation of biosynthesis, oxidative catabolism, and recycling (Zhao et al., 2025). In this study, GalLDH, the terminal enzyme in the L-galactose biosynthetic pathway, exhibited activity patterns that closely mirrored AsA accumulation in all four varieties (Fig. 3A). GalLDH activity was also significantly and positively correlated with AsA content in ‘Pushu 32’, ‘Hangshu 2’, and ‘Ziluolan’ (Fig. 4B–D), consistent with findings in tomato (Chen et al., 2024) and kiwifruit (Lin et al., 2022), reinforcing its central role in AsA biosynthesis. However, the AsA regulatory network shows species-specific variation. For example, in Rosa roxburghii, high AsA levels are mainly regulated by GalDH and DHAR (Yin et al., 2024), rather than GalLDH as in sweet potato and some other crops. This difference likely reflects evolutionary adaptations to distinct environments (Raut et al., 2025). As a root crop, sweet potato may rely more on the L-galactose pathway, with GalLDH as the key enzyme, while other species adopt alternative pathways under specific physiological conditions.

Regarding oxidative metabolism, APX and AAO exhibited cultivar-specific regulation. A significant positive correlation between AsA content and APX activity was observed in ‘Pushu 32’ and ‘Yanshu 25’, but not in ‘Hangshu 2’ or ‘Ziluolan’ (Fig. 4). Additionally, AsA regeneration via MDHAR and DHAR varied among varieties. The relatively low regeneration enzyme activity in ‘Yanshu 25’ was consistent with its lower AsA content. In contrast, a significant positive correlation between AsA content and MDHAR activity in ‘Ziluolan’ (Fig. 4D) was consistent with findings in strawberry (Cruz-Rus et al., 2011), highlighting the critical role of MDHAR in AsA recycling in sweet potato.

Transcriptome analysis provided a foundation for examining gene expression dynamics during sweet potato development and its regulatory role in AsA metabolism. PCA revealed significant temporal changes in the gene expression regulatory network across developmental stages (Fig. 5A). DEG analysis showed substantial transcriptomic differences among varieties at the same stage (Fig. 5B), indicating variety-specific expression patterns. Venn diagram analysis identified the most prominent transcriptome differences in the H5_vs_Y5 group, with 1744 up-regulated and 2390 down-regulated genes (Fig. 5C, D). Functional enrichment analysis revealed that these DEGs were mainly associated with photosystem I, photosynthesis, and related pathways. These results suggest that light conditions are a critical regulatory factor influencing AsA metabolism during sweet potato development (Fig. 5E, F), consistent with previous findings intomato, where AsA biosynthesis and metabolism are closely affected by light-related factors such as intensity, photosynthesis, photoperiod, and photoinhibition (Zushi et al., 2020).

AsA biosynthesis varies across plant species. In strawberry, AsA accumulation is regulated by different pathways depending on developmental stage: the L-galactose pathway predominates early, whereas the D-galacturonic acid pathway dominates at ripening (Cruz-Rus et al., 2011). In contrast, AsA biosynthesis in persimmon involves a more complex network involving four pathways, with the L-galactose pathway as the primary route (Wang et al., 2023). In this study, transcriptome data were systematically analyzed to construct expression profiles of genes related to AsA biosynthesis and metabolism during sweet potato root development (Fig. 6). Six key synthase genes were identified: PMI, PMM, HK, GME, and GalLDH in the L-galactose pathway, and MIOX in the inositol pathway. These findings indicate that the L-galactose and inositol pathways are the main metabolic routes regulating AsA biosynthesis in sweet potato, consistent with pathway distribution reported in date fruit (Lu et al., 2022).

WGCNA identified 30 functional modules with significant biological features, among which the darkturquoise module showed a significant positive correlation with AsA metabolism in sweet potato, particularly with GalLDH activity (Fig. 7B). KEGG enrichment analysis further clarified the biological function of this module (Fig. 7D). Gene network analysis identified the 30 most highly connected genes within the module, leading to the recognition of MIOX, a key enzyme-coding gene in the myo-inositol pathway (Fig. 7E). MIOX, a rate-limiting enzyme that converts inositol to D-glucuronic acid, is essential for plant AsA biosynthesis (Ntagkas et al., 2019). Our network analysis results are consistent with studies in tomato (Munir et al., 2020), suggesting that MIOX may also be an important candidate gene for As A synthesis in sweet potato. However, it is worth noting that this prediction requires further functional verification through molecular genetic methods (such as gene silencing or overexpression) to finally determine its regulatory role in sweet potato.

We further identified four genes functionally associated with MIOX: kinesin-like protein (itf01g18300), TMV resistance protein (itf04g09550), ATP synthase (itf00g19700), and AT-hook motif nuclear-localized protein (itf12g22780). Among them, ATP synthase is a pivotal enzyme in the mitochondrial electron transport chain (ETC), catalyzing ATP synthesis (Zancani et al., 2020). Since AsA protects mitochondria from oxidative damage by scavenging ROS (Chen et al., 2022), ATP synthase may regulate AsA synthesis by maintaining mitochondrial energy homeostasis, though this remains to be confirmed experimentally. The other three genes (itf01g18300, itf04g09550, and itf12g22780) have not been directly linked to AsA metabolism in plants. Therefore, their biological functions and regulatory mechanisms in sweet potato AsA synthesis will be the focus of future research.

Conclusions

In this study, the regulatory mechanisms of AsA metabolism in four sweet potato varieties were systematically examined using a combination of physiological, biochemical, and transcriptome analyses. The results showed significant varietal differences in AsA content, with ‘Hangshu 2’ consistently exhibiting high levels of AsA accumulation, while ‘Yanshu 25’ maintained relatively low levels. Metabolic pathway analysis indicated that the L-galactose and myo-inositol pathways are the two major routes for AsA biosynthesis in sweet potato. Integrated transcriptomic analysis and WGCNA identified MIOX (itf03g18270), a key enzyme-coding gene in the myo-inositol pathway, which may be an important candidate gene for AsA biosynthesis in sweet potato. Furthermore, four hub genes—encoding a kinesin-like protein, a TMV resistance protein, ATP synthase, and an AT-hook motif nuclear-localized protein—were found to be potentially involved in regulating MIOX and the AsA biosynthetic process. Therefore, these findings not only clarify the key biosynthetic pathways and candidate regulatory genes involved in AsA accumulation in sweet potato, but also provide a theoretical foundation and novel research directions for further functional validation, aimed at elucidating the complex regulatory network underlying AsA metabolism in root crops.
