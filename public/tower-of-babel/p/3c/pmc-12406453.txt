Safety and outcome of intravenous thrombolysis in ischemic stroke related to intracranial artery dissection
Authors: Wen Yang, Jian-Wei Wang, Mei-xia Zhang, Hong-Fang Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe clinical efficacy of intravenous thrombolysis (IVT) for acute ischemic stroke (AIS) associated with intracranial artery dissection (IAD) remains uncertain.Case summaryThis study presents a case of basilar artery dissection (BAD) in a 57-year-old Asian female patient devoid of previous risk factors for stroke who experienced a sudden onset of consciousness disturbance. The patient’s clinical condition deteriorated during IVT administration, but subsequent medical intervention and rehabilitative exercises facilitated eventual recovery of daily functioning. MRI eventually identified the etiology of the patient’s AIS.ConclusionAIS related to anterior circulation IAD may pose a relatively high risk of hemorrhagic transformation and symptom exacerbation during the early stages of IVT, but the impact on long-term prognosis remains uncertain. Consequently, further investigation is warranted to assess the safety and efficacy of IVT in such patients.Keywords: Intracranial artery dissection, Basilar artery dissection, Acute ischemic stroke, Intravenous thrombolysis, Rt-PA

Background

Intracranial artery dissection (IAD) constitutes a rare etiology of acute ischemic stroke (AIS) and stands as a significant contributor to strokes in young and middle-aged individuals [1]. Its incidence is elevated in the Asian population, with posterior circulation dissection being more prevalent [2, 3]. While vertebral artery dissection is relatively common, basilar artery dissection (BAD) is exceedingly rare, estimated at 0.25 per 100,000 person-years [4]. Presently, research on the etiology and management of cervicocranial artery dissection (CAD) predominantly focuses on cervical artery dissection (CeAD), with limited understanding of IAD. Both domestic and international guidelines lack precision regarding the efficacy and safety of intravenous thrombolysis (IVT) for IAD. This article presents a case of AIS associated with BAD treated with IVT, aiming to offer insights for future therapeutic approaches. The patient provided informed consent for publication of the case.

Case presentation

A 57-year-old woman was admitted to the emergency department due to sudden unconsciousness persisting for 44 min. The patient had no underlying diseases but reported posterior occipital pain following a neck massage several days earlier. The patient’s body temperature was normal and the blood pressure was 110/57mmHg. No abnormalities were observed in her heart, lung and abdomen. Neurological examination revealed light coma, with a Glasgow Coma Scale(GCS) score of 7(E1V2M4). The bilateral pupils were equal in size and had a sensitive response to light. With pain stimulation, the patient’s left upper limb and both lower limbs could withdraw, while the right upper limb remained inactive. The bilateral Babinski’s sign and meningeal irritation sign were negative.

The patient’s laboratory tests were basically normal. Head computerized tomography angiography (CTA) showed no cerebral hemorrhage or significant intracranial vessel occlusion for 49 min after the onset of symptoms. Following exclusion of contraindications to IVT, the patient received full-dose rt-PA 84 min after symptom onset, with a door-to-needle time of 40 min. However, 15 min post-IVT administration, the patient’s condition deteriorated abruptly, manifesting as light coma (GCS E1V1M4), unequal pupils(Left 5 mm, Right 3 mm) and diminished pupillary light reflex. The Babinski’s signs were positive bilaterally and meningeal irritation sign was negative. Head CT was performed 11 min after the exacerbation of symptoms while IVT was suspended. The CT revealed high signal density in the longitudinal fissure and bilateral tentorium cerebelli(Fig. 1.A). The neurologist, neurosurgeon and radiologist reviewed the images and considered contrast medium extravasation rather than subarachnoid hemorrhage. The patient continued to receive IVT while digital subtraction angiography (DSA) was prepared. DSA showed no obvious abnormalities in the intracranial and extracranial vessels. Subsequently, the patient was admitted to the intensive care unit. The treatment process was showed in Fig. 2.

The clinical course

Twenty-four hours post-IVT, head CT confirmed AIS in the left cerebellum and right temporal lobe(Fig. 1.B-D). On the eleventh day post-onset, head MR Showed infarction in the left brainstem and cerebellum, as well as in the right thalamus and temporal lobe(Fig. 1.E-H). The patient’s homocysteine, pretransfusion examination, tumor markers, thyroid function and immune markers were normal. Further investigations, including 24-hour dynamic electrocardiogram, ambulatory blood pressure monitoring, cardiac ultrasound, carotid artery ultrasound, and lung CT, yielded normal results. Therefore, we organized a multidisciplinary consultation. Experts found that the MRA showed abnormal dilation of the distal basilar artery(BA). Time of flight(TOF)-MRA showed that BA had a double-lumen sign, and a high signal could be seen on T1-weighted imaging(T1WI), suggesting the presence of intramural hematoma(Fig. 1.I-K). DSA images also confirmed the existence of double lumen of the BA, and there was a phenomenon of contrast agent retention in the false lumen (Fig. 1.L-M).

Following antiplatelet therapy, plaque stabilization, circulation enhancement, and anti-infective treatment, the patient’s consciousness improved significantly, with grade 2 limb muscle strength. Due to complications, the patient was discharged after 1 month. Her muscle strength returned to grade 4 in both upper limbs and grade 5 in both lower limbs. The modified Rankin Scale score (mRS) was 1 after 3 months of dual anti-platelet aggregation therapy. Re-examination of head MRI showed that the BAD basically disappeared.

Discussion

IAD refers to blood component infiltration into the vessel wall through damaged intima, culminating in wall dissection, hematoma formation, or spontaneous intracranial arterial wall hemorrhage. The underlying pathophysiology may involve the intracranial artery’s comparatively reduced elastic fiber content in the media, less adventitia, and absence of external elastic lamina, relative to extracranial arteries [5, 6]. IAD can cause headache, ischemic symptoms, subarachnoid hemorrhage, and compression symptoms. The specific etiology of IAD is still unknown. Epidemiologically, predisposing factors include hypertension, smoking, oral contraceptives, migraine, trauma history, recent infections, connective tissue disorders, fibromuscular dysplasia, and genetic factors [7, 8].

The patient in this case lacked conventional stroke risk factors but experienced neck massage before the onset of the disease, followed by pain in the occipital region. Notably, retrospective analysis revealed that over 80% of IAD cases manifest with headache [9]. S. Bhatia et al. reported a case of BAD presenting only with neck and occipital pain [10]. Headache attacks were usually caused by a direct tear in the wall of a blood vessel, or by the release of proinflammatory neurotransmitters from nerve endings around the artery [11]. Therefore, we needed to pay attention to the headache symptoms, which might reveal the cause.

The cause of AIS in the patient had not been clarified in the early stage. The following reasons were considered: First, the detection rate of BAD by head CTA was relatively low. Previous studies had shown that the current intravascular imaging (DSA, CTA, MRA) could not clearly show the direct signs of IAD, and the detection rate was only 32%−50% [12]. Secondly, radiologists had insufficient understanding of BAD and often overlooked the clinical manifestations when interpreting the images. Thirdly, cerebral angiography doctor only focused on whether there was stenosis in the vascular lumen, but paid little attention to the proximal stenosis of the BA, the distal dilation, and the retention of contrast agent in the false lumen. Finally, neurologists overly relied on radiologists.

Typical imaging features of IAD encompass eccentric arterial stenosis with external diameter dilation, beaded or segmental stenosis, intramural hematoma, double-lumen sign, intimal flap, and dissecting aneurysm formation. The 3D-SNAP sequence may enhance diagnostic accuracy by sensitively detecting intramural hematoma and the double-chamber sign [13]. The patient’s symptoms suddenly worsened during IVT, and combined with the characteristics of the infarction lesion, it was speculated that arterial to arterial embolism occurred during IVT. The pathogenesis of stroke due to IAD may include thromboembolism (arterial-to-artery embolism, intramural hematoma leading to occlusion of vasa vasorum and perforating vessel), hemodynamic mechanisms (hypoperfusion), or vascular injury leading to subarachnoid hemorrhage [14].

IVT is a safe and effective treatment for acute ischemic stroke. However, the paucity of IAD cases complicates definitive assessments of IVT safety and efficacy in this context. Theoretically, IVT may exacerbate intramural hematoma enlargement, promote dissecting aneurysm formation, or incite vessel rupture. A retrospective analysis of 8 reports on IAD treated with IVT, combined with our own case, revealed that 21% of the cases experienced hemorrhagic transformation, 32% experienced an exacerbation of early symptoms, but 58% of the patients had a good outcome as indicated by a modified Rankin Scale (mRS) score of ≤ 2 at three months(Table 1) [15–22]. Among the four patients with hemorrhage after IVT, three were identified as anterior circulation IAD, and two of them were IAD of the anterior cerebral artery. Of the six patients with neurological deterioration, four were in the anterior circulation. Two deceased patients both suffered from IAD in the anterior circulation. One of them received IVT combined with endovascular treatment. Reviewing the above studies, most IADs were detected through MRI and DSA examinations.

Conclusions

For individuals presenting with severe headache, consideration of IAD is warranted if hemorrhage is excluded by CT. Given the high rate of missed diagnoses, advanced imaging modalities and expert interpretation are indispensable. Based on the observations of our study, IVT for AIS caused by anterior circulation IAD may be have a relatively high risk of hemorrhagic transformation and early neurological deterioration, but the impact on long-term prognosis remains uncertain. Due to the small sample size of the study, conducting a multi-center registry study would be beneficial for further clarifying the efficacy and safety of IVT for IAD. Furthermore, a computer-aided diagnosis system based on deep learning is worthy of exploration.
