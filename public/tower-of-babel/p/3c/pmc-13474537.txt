Clinical outcomes of toxin-positive versus toxin-negative/NAAT-positive Clostridioides difficile infection in South Korea: a single-center retrospective cohort study
Authors: Seung Hoo Lee, Tae Yeul Kim, Hee Jae Huh, Sun Young Cho, Cheol-In Kang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCurrent guidelines recommend multistep algorithms incorporating nucleic acid amplification tests (NAATs) and toxin enzyme immunoassays (EIAs) for the diagnosis of Clostridioides difficile infection (CDI); however, the clinical significance of toxin-negative/NAAT-positive (toxin–/NAAT+) results remains uncertain. We aimed to compare clinical outcomes between toxin + and toxin–/NAAT+ cases.MethodsThis retrospective study included adults diagnosed with CDI between September 2020 and September 2025 at a tertiary hospital in South Korea. Patients were classified by toxin EIA and NAAT results into toxin + or toxin–/NAAT+ groups, and their outcomes were compared. The primary outcome was CDI recurrence within 90 days. Secondary outcomes included 60-day recurrence, 90-day all-cause mortality, resolution of diarrhea, and colectomy for CDI.ResultsIn total, 382 toxin + and 435 toxin–/NAAT+ were evaluated. CDI recurred in 55 patients (6.7%), including 35 (9.2%) in the toxin+ group and 20 (4.6%) in the toxin–/NAAT+ group (P = 0.011). Toxin positivity was significantly associated with recurrence (adjusted odds ratio [aOR], 1.99; 95% confidence interval [CI], 1.12–3.52; P = 0.019). Prior antibiotic exposure was also associated with recurrence (aOR, 2.24; 95% CI, 1.25–4.02; P = 0.007). Toxin positivity was not associated with 90-day mortality (aOR, 1.19; 95% CI, 0.85–1.69; P = 0.313).ConclusionsIn our cohort, toxin positivity was associated with an increased risk of 90-day recurrence but not with 90-day mortality. Our data indicate that incorporation of toxin EIA testing into CDI diagnostic algorithms could provide clinically relevant prognostic information.Trial registrationNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-026-13598-8.Keywords: Clostridioides difficile infection, Toxin positivity, Toxin-negative/NAAT-positive, Clinical outcomes

Introduction

Clostridioides difficile infection (CDI) is a major complication of antibiotic exposure and disruption of the gut microbiota, and it can lead to severe outcomes such as toxic megacolon, pseudomembranous colitis, colectomy, and death [1]. The global burden of CDI has increased over the past 30 years, particularly in countries with a high sociodemographic index [2]. Despite this, the optimal diagnostic approach for CDI remains controversial. Current guidelines recommend multistep algorithms combining toxin enzyme immunoassays (EIAs) and nucleic acid amplification tests (NAATs) for the diagnosis of CDI [3, 4]. However, it remains difficult to distinguish whether toxin EIA–negative but NAAT-positive cases (toxin–/NAAT+) represent true CDI with toxin levels below the detection limit of EIAs or C. difficile colonization [5–7]. Moreover, the necessity of antimicrobial therapy for toxin–/NAAT+ patients remains unclear. A recent quasi-experimental noninferiority study reported that implementation of reporting of C. difficile toxin results alone led to a sixfold reduction in CDI treatment, without a statistically significant increase in all-cause mortality among toxin–/NAAT+ patients [8]. In contrast, a recent meta-analysis found that treatment of toxin–/NAAT+ patients was associated with reduced all-cause mortality [9].

Whether clinical outcomes differ between toxin + and toxin–/NAAT+ patients is also an important clinical question that has yielded conflicting results across studies. A recent meta-analysis reported no significant difference in 30-day all-cause mortality between toxin + and toxin–/NAAT+ patients; however, the 60-day recurrence rate was significantly higher in toxin-positive patients [9]. Notably, most included studies were conducted in North America and Europe, where circulating C. difficile ribotype distributions differ substantially from those in East Asia [10], including South Korea, and the pooled estimates were largely driven by a single study from the United States that contributed the greatest weight and sample size [11]. Although two studies from South Korea were included, their sample sizes were insufficient to draw definitive conclusions regarding clinical outcomes [12, 13]. Therefore, we aimed to compare clinical outcomes, particularly recurrence, between toxin + and toxin–/NAAT+ patients in a larger cohort at a tertiary-care hospital in South Korea, to assess the prognostic implications of toxin positivity.

Methods

Study design, setting, and population

This retrospective cohort study was conducted at Samsung Medical Center, a 1,766-bed tertiary-care hospital in Seoul, South Korea. We retrospectively analyzed clinical data from adult patients (aged ≥ 19 years) who underwent routine C. difficile testing using glutamate dehydrogenase (GDH)/toxin A/B EIAs between September 1, 2019 and September 1, 2025. Patients with GDH+/toxin + or GDH+/toxin– were initially eligible, and the index date was defined as the date of GDH/toxin A/B testing. We excluded patients whose C. difficile testing was considered inappropriate, including those tested after recent laxative use or enema and those tested in the absence of new-onset diarrhea (defined as ≥ 3 unformed stools within 24 h). For patients with GDH+/toxin– results, inclusion additionally required a positive NAAT result on the same stool specimen within 7 days. Furthermore, we excluded patients with (1) a prior CDI episode within 90 days before the index date, (2) death within 14 days of the index date, or (3) loss to follow-up within 90 days of the index date.

Clinical data

Demographic characteristics and known risk factors within 60 days before the index date were collected, including the presence of a nasogastric tube, use of acid-suppressive agents (histamine-2 receptor blockers, proton pump inhibitors, and potassium-competitive acid blockers) and systemic antibiotic exposure (medication exposure defined as administration for ≥ 14 days during this period). Serum white blood cell count and creatinine level were obtained. Comorbidities were identified using International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision (ICD-10) codes, and the Charlson Comorbidity Index was calculated using the ICD-10 coding algorithm described by Quan et al. [14]. CDI therapeutic agents were also recorded. Data were extracted from the Clinical Data Warehouse DARWIN-C of Samsung Medical Center.

Laboratory methods

Stool samples were subjected to routine C. difficile testing using C. DIFF QUIK CHEK COMPLETE (TechLab, Blacksburg, VA, USA), an EIA for the simultaneous detection of GDH and toxin A/B. Samples with discordant results (GDH-positive/toxin-negative) were subsequently tested by NAAT using BD MAX Cdiff (BD, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA) at the attending physician’s request.

Severity of C. difficile infection

Severity of CDI was classified as follows: severe CDI was defined as the presence of leukocytosis (white blood cell count ≥ 15 × 10⁹ cells/L) and/or renal failure (serum creatinine ≥ 1.5 mg/dL); fulminant CDI was defined as the presence of shock (systolic blood pressure < 90 mmHg or vasopressor requirement to maintain systolic blood pressure ≥ 90 mmHg), ileus, and/or toxic megacolon [4].

Outcomes

The primary outcome was CDI recurrence within 90 days, as previous studies have commonly assessed recurrence over an 8- to 12-week follow-up period [15]. Recurrence was defined as the development of diarrhea with a positive toxin assay or NAAT at least 14 days after the index date. Secondary outcomes were 60-day recurrence, 90-day all-cause mortality, resolution of diarrhea, and colectomy due to CDI. Resolution of diarrhea was defined as the first day of two consecutive days with ≤ 2 stools per day, regardless of stool consistency.

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables are presented as counts with percentages, and continuous variables as medians with interquartile ranges (IQRs). Categorical variables were compared using Fisher’s exact test, and continuous variables using the Mann–Whitney U test.

To assess the primary outcome and secondary outcomes, we performed, if feasible, multivariable logistic regression analyses adjusting for the following covariates [16, 17]: age, sex, fever, Charlson Comorbidity Index, disease severity, presence of ileus, nasogastric tube use, use of acid-suppressive agents (histamine-2 receptor antagonists, proton pump inhibitors, and potassium-competitive acid blockers), antibiotic exposure, and receipt of CDI treatment. Missing data on diarrhea resolution status at day 14 were excluded from the logistic regression analysis. Sensitivity analysis for the primary outcome was performed in patients who survived to 90 days.

Time-to-event analyses were performed using Kaplan–Meier curves with comparisons by the log-rank test, and hazard ratios (HRs) were estimated using Cox proportional hazards models. The proportional hazards assumption was assessed using Schoenfeld residuals. Covariates included in the multivariable models were selected using backward selection.

Multicollinearity was assessed, and model calibration was evaluated using the Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test for all logistic regression models. A two-sided P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All analyses were conducted using R (version 4.5.2; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Ethics approval and consent to participate

This study was conducted in accordance with Declaration of Helsinki. The Institutional Review Board (IRB) of Samsung Medical Center granted exemption from review for this study owing to its retrospective design and the use of deidentified patient data (IRB no. 2026-01-034) and also waived the requirement for informed consent.

Results

Baseline characteristics

During the study period, 15,124 patients underwent GDH/toxin A/B testing, of whom 3,578 were toxin + or GDH+. Of these, 1,956 patients were excluded due to inappropriate specimens, including 22 patients given enema and 1,934 tested in the absence of diarrhea. A total of 2,425 samples from the excluded patients showed 722 toxin+/GDH+, and 648 toxin–/NAAT+ results. Among the remaining 1,622 patients, only toxin+ cases and toxin–/NAAT+ cases were included. In the 450 toxin+ cases, 1 patient had a prior CDI episode within 90 days, 33 died within 14 days, and 34 were lost to follow-up. In the 508 toxin–/NAAT+ cases, 2 patients had a prior CDI episode within 90 days, 35 died within 14 days, and 36 were lost to follow-up. After these exclusions, 382 toxin + and 435 toxin–/NAAT+ patients were included in the final analysis (Fig. 1).

No significant differences were observed between the groups in onset (hospital-acquired, 83.8% and 87.4%, P = 0.161), CDI severity (severe, 53.4% and 53.6%; fulminant, 29.3% and 28.5%; P = 0.958), ileus or toxic megacolon (7.1% and 5.7%, P = 0.475), pseudomembrane on colonoscopy (10/39 [25.6%] and 3/39 [7.7%], P = 0.065), Charlson comorbidity index (median 3 [IQR, 2–5] and 3 [IQR, 2–4], P = 0.739), nasogastric tube use (30.9% and 30.3%, P = 0.879), histamine-2 receptor antagonist use (9.9% and 9.7%, P = 0.907), proton pump inhibitor use (34.0% and 33.8%, P > 0.999), potassium-competitive acid blocker use (4.5% and 4.1%, P = 0.864).

CDI therapy within 7 days differed between the groups (P = 0.006). Treatment was more frequently administered in the toxin+ group than in the toxin–/NAAT+ group (95.5% versus 90.1%). Oral vancomycin was the most common regiment in both groups (62.6% versus 58.4%), followed by oral vancomycin plus metronidazole combination therapy (19.6% versus 14.5%).

Outcomes

Crude outcomes are shown in Table 2. A total of 55 patients (6.7%) experienced recurrent CDI within 90 days: 35 (9.2%) in the toxin+ group and 20 (4.6%) in the toxin–/NAAT+ group (P = 0.011). Among patients with recurrence, 24 (68.6%) in the toxin+ group remained toxin+, whereas 13 (65%) in the toxin–/NAAT+ group remained toxin–/NAAT+. A total of 174 patients (21.3%) died within 90 days, including 89 (23.3%) in the toxin+ group and 85 (19.5%) in the toxin–/NAAT+ group (P = 0.200). Resolution of diarrhea within 14 days was achieved in 280/308 (90.9%) patients in the toxin+ group and 342/379 (90.2%) in the toxin–/NAAT+ group (P = 0.795); the median time to resolution was not significantly different between these groups. Surgical treatment was required in 2 patients (0.5%) in the toxin+ group and 1 (0.2%) in the toxin–/NAAT+ group.

Toxin+ status was also significantly associated with 60-day recurrence (aOR, 2.96; 95% CI, 1.52–5.76; P = 0.001; Table S1). Toxin+ status was not significantly associated with increased mortality (aOR, 1.19; 95% CI, 0.85–1.69; P = 0.313; Table S2). Charlson Comorbidity Index ≥ 3, prior antibiotic exposure and severe or fulminant CDI were associated with increased mortality. Toxin+ status was not significantly associated with diarrhea resolution (aOR, 1.10; 95% CI, 0.65–1.84; P = 0.729; Table S3). Nasogastric tube use was a risk factor for persistent diarrhea.

Sensitivity analysis

Sensitivity analyses restricted to patients who survived to 90 days also demonstrated that toxin+ status was a significant risk factor for recurrence (aOR, 1.90; 95% CI, 1.04–3.47; P = 0.036; Table S4).

Time-to-event analyses

Kaplan–Meier curves were plotted for recurrence (Fig. 2), all-cause mortality (Figure S1) and resolution of diarrhea (Figure S2). Hazard ratios (HRs) were estimated using Cox proportional hazards models. Toxin+ status was associated with a higher hazard of 90-day recurrence (HR, 1.98; 95% CI, 1.14–3.43; P = 0.015; Table S5) and 60-day recurrence (HR, 2.74; 95% CI, 1.44–5.23; P = 0.002). However, it was not associated with 90-day all-cause mortality (HR, 1.16; 95% CI, 0.86–1.57; P = 0.320; Table S6) and resolution of diarrhea (HR, 1.04; 95% CI, 0.88–1.21; P = 0.676, Table S7).

Discussion

In this retrospective cohort, C. difficile toxin positivity was associated with an increased risk of CDI recurrence within 90 days and 60 days. In contrast, toxin positivity was not associated with 90-day all-cause mortality and 14-day diarrhea resolution, while underlying comorbidities and more severe disease were risk factors for 90-days mortality. Prior antibiotic exposure was a risk factor for both recurrence and mortality. In sensitivity analyses restricted to patients who survived to 90 days, toxin positivity remained a risk factor for recurrence. Time-to-event analyses using Cox proportional hazards models were consistent with these findings.

NAATs provide superior sensitivity and rapid turnaround times; however, they are limited in their ability to distinguish colonization with toxigenic C. difficile from true CDI, which may lead to overdiagnosis [18–21]. For this reason, the European Society of Clinical Microbiology and Infectious Diseases guidelines recommend multistep diagnostic algorithms for CDI that incorporate the highly specific toxin A/B EIA, either as an initial test in combination with GDH EIA or as a confirmatory assay following NAAT [3]. Beyond its diagnostic role, toxin A/B EIA may also provide prognostic information regarding recurrence risk [9], as confirmed in our cohort. In contrast, the Infectious Diseases Society of America guidelines allow NAAT to be used as a stand-alone diagnostic test for CDI, but only when preagreed institutional criteria for stool submission are in place, such as avoiding testing in patients receiving laxatives and restricting testing to those with unexplained, new-onset diarrhea (≥ 3 unformed stools within 24 h) [4]. The importance of establishing and enforcing such criteria is supported by our cohort data, in which more than half of patients with GDH-positive or toxin-positive results were tested inappropriately (1,956/3,578; 54.7%), most commonly in the absence of diarrhea. Moreover, over half of samples from these patients (1,370/2,425; 56.5%) met laboratory criteria for CDI (toxin + or toxin–/NAAT+), which may lead to overtreatment. Notably, toxin+ cases (n = 722) outnumbered toxin–/NAAT+ cases (n = 648). These findings suggest that, without preagreed institutional stool submission criteria, C. difficile testing is frequently performed inappropriately, underscoring the importance of appropriate stool submission criteria. Several strategies have been developed to reduce the submission of inappropriate specimens [22, 23], and their implementation may reduce CDI overdiagnosis and overtreatment in many institutions, including our center.

A recent systematic review and meta-analysis reported that toxin positivity was associated with higher 60-day recurrence [9]. This difference might be driven by the largest study [11], which reported a higher recurrence rate (15.5%) than observed in our cohort. In that study, ribotype 027 and 078 accounted for 59 and 14 of 605 isolates, respectively, and were more frequently detected in toxin+ cases than in toxin–/NAAT+ cases. Although ribotype may not be associated with disease severity [24, 25], ribotype 027 has been associated with reduced treatment responsiveness and a higher risk of recurrence [26], the observed differences in recurrence may reflect strain distribution rather than diagnostic classification alone. In Korea, hypervirulent ribotypes such as 027 have been reported infrequently [27, 28], and one recent multicenter study reported that ribotype 027 and ribotype 078 each accounted for 0.9% of C. difficile isolates (3 of 332, respectively) [29]. Although we did not perform strain typing, we have no specific reason to suspect that the epidemiology in our center differs substantially from that reported in other Korean hospitals. This is among the first studies to demonstrate that toxin+ cases are associated with a higher 90-day recurrence rate than toxin–/NAAT+ cases, in a setting where the impact of hypervirulent strains is negligible [30, 31].

Several studies have suggested that toxin–/NAAT+ cases may not uniformly require treatment, based on the lower mortality observed in this group in their cohorts [5, 8]. In our cohort, however, toxin status was not associated with 90-day mortality after adjustment, consistent with the result of the meta analysis [9]. Since hypervirulent strains that are associated with reduced treatment responsiveness and increase mortality [32, 33] are unlikely to be prevalent in this cohort, it is plausible that host-related factors were more strongly associated with an increased mortality. We found that comorbidities, antibiotic exposure and disease severity were associated with an increased mortality as previous studies [34, 35]. It should be noted that, although toxin–/NAAT+ results might not be specific for a definitive diagnosis of CDI, some patients in this group may require treatment, given that CDI itself has been associated with elevated mortality [36, 37].

This study has several limitations. First, because of the retrospective design, residual confounding and selection bias may remain despite multivariable adjustment. However, we excluded patients who died within 14 days of the index date to minimize immortal time bias. Second, we did not perform culture and ribotyping; therefore, we could not directly assess the contribution of ribotype 027 or other strains to recurrence in this cohort. However, no outbreaks of hypervirulent strains have been reported in Korea, and our center is unlikely to differ substantially from other Korean hospitals in this regard. Third, we did not determine CDI-attributable mortality, which may have limited an accurate comparison of mortality according to toxin status. However, because CDI often occurs in patients with substantial comorbidity, distinguishing CDI-attributable death from death due to underlying disease is inherently difficult and may be subjective.

In conclusion, in our cohort, toxin positivity was associated with an increased risk of recurrence but not with mortality and diarrhea resolution. Our data indicate that incorporation of toxin EIA testing into CDI diagnostic algorithms could provide clinically relevant prognostic information. Future studies are needed to identify and evaluate strategies to improve specimen appropriateness, thereby enhancing diagnostic accuracy.
