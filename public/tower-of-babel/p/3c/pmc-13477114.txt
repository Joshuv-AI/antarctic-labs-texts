Single-Fraction Postoperative Radiation Therapy for Post-traumatic Radioulnar Synostosis
Authors: Rhea Rakheja, Kimberly Hart, Nitin Vaishampayan, Ramesh Boggula, Steven Miller
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Post-traumatic radioulnar synostosis is a rare complication of forearm trauma, characterized by heterotopic bone formation between the radius and ulna, resulting in loss of forearm rotation. Surgical excision is the primary treatment, but recurrence through reformation of heterotopic bone remains a concern, particularly in patients with a prior osteogenic predisposition. Surgical removal of this bone bridge is the main treatment, but the condition can recur. We report the case of a man in his late 50s with post-traumatic left forearm radioulnar synostosis who underwent surgical excision followed by a single dose of radiation therapy (RT) (7 Gy) given within 48 hours of surgery to prevent recurrence. The patient experienced immediate improvement in passive forearm rotation after surgery, without neurovascular compromise, and tolerated radiation therapy without acute complications. This case adds to the small, published literature supporting postoperative low-dose RT in high-risk patients following radioulnar synostosis excision. It demonstrates the feasibility of same-day CT simulation and radiation delivery in the inpatient setting.

Introduction

Post-traumatic radioulnar synostosis occurs when abnormal heterotopic bone forms between the radius and ulna following a forearm or elbow injury. Risk factors for radioulnar synostosis include high-energy injuries, both-bone forearm fractures occurring at the same level, prolonged immobilization with delayed rehabilitation, Monteggia fracture-dislocations, extensive soft tissue disruption, traumatic brain injury, and certain surgical approaches, such as dual-incision techniques [1]. Radioulnar synostosis can cause partial loss of pronation and supination, substantially limiting the ability to perform activities of daily living.

Radioulnar synostoses are classified using the Hastings and Graham system, which categorizes lesions by anatomical location and guides surgical management. Type I lesions involve the distal radioulnar joint region and may be amenable to salvage procedures, such as the Sauvé-Kapandji or Darrach procedure. Type II lesions occur at extra-articular sites in the middle forearm, and Type III lesions involve the proximal radioulnar joint, with subtypes A, B, and C reflecting increasing degrees of complexity. For Type II and IIIA lesions, complete surgical excision remains the standard approach [1,2].

Surgical excision remains the standard treatment for radioulnar synostosis and is typically performed 6-12 months after the initial injury, once maturation of the synostotic bridge is confirmed on imaging [1]. Recurrence of heterotopic bone formation following excision is a recognized risk, particularly in patients with an osteogenic predisposition. The two principal adjuvant strategies to reduce the risk of heterotopic bone formation are nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) and perioperative low-dose radiation therapy (RT). The evidence for RT comes mostly from studies of hip replacement patients and has been applied to the forearm based on shared biological principles [3,4]. The published experience with RT specifically for forearm radioulnar synostosis is limited to a small number of case reports and series. This report adds an additional case to the literature and reviews the pertinent data supporting radiation therapy for the prevention of radioulnar synostosis after surgical excision.

Case presentation

The patient was a man in his late 50s who underwent surgical excision of a post-traumatic left forearm radioulnar synostosis (Figure 1). He had a long-standing history of this condition, which had resulted in progressive loss of forearm rotation and significantly limited his ability to perform daily activities. Conservative management had failed to improve his symptoms. His past medical history included left radial neuropathy, prior fractures of the left ulna and left fifth metacarpal, and sagittal synostosis. His past surgical history included open reduction and internal fixation of a left tibial shaft fracture and C5-C7 anterior cervical discectomy and fusion. The orthopedic surgery team performed the excision. Intraoperative fluoroscopy confirmed complete removal of the bone bridge, and the patient demonstrated improved passive range of motion immediately after surgery (Figure 2). No interposition graft was placed at the time of surgery.

The patient's postoperative course was uneventful, and he reported subjective improvement in forearm rotation compared with his preoperative baseline. He denied any new numbness, tingling, or weakness in the left upper extremity. He underwent evaluation by Radiation Oncology, and postoperative RT was recommended to prevent recurrence of radioulnar synostosis. He had no prior history of RT, connective tissue disorder, or implantable cardiac device.

Examination of the left upper extremity showed clean, dry, and intact wound dressings. The forearm compartments were soft, the radial pulse was strong, and the fingers were warm and well perfused. Motor function was intact in the left hand and forearm, and sensation was at his known baseline. He had pre-existing decreased sensation in the left hand due to his radial neuropathy. No new neurological deficits were identified. The risks and benefits of RT were discussed with the patient before treatment, including mild skin redness, swelling, and joint pain, as well as the small theoretical risk of developing a radiation-induced cancer. The patient agreed to proceed with treatment.

Before initiation of treatment, the patient underwent a planning CT scan for radiation treatment planning. A cradle was fashioned to stabilize the left arm, and a CT scan with 3 mm slices was performed of the left arm and cradle. The postoperative surgical bed of the left forearm was contoured on the planning CT as the clinical target volume (CTV). A two-field technique was used, with field apertures shaped to provide a 1-2 cm block-edge margin around the CTV to account for setup uncertainty and patient motion (Figure 3A). The patient received a single dose of 7 Gy using 6 MV photons, with the 100% isodose line covering the surgical bed (Figure 3B).

Radiation treatment was delivered approximately 48 hours after surgery, within the recommended prophylactic window of 72 hours. The patient continued to receive routine postoperative care concurrently, including DVT prophylaxis with subcutaneous enoxaparin, bowel regimen, and antiemetics as needed. Early mobilization and outpatient physical therapy were planned following discharge.

The patient was discharged after completing RT and continued his recovery under the care of the orthopedic surgery team. At the four-month follow-up visit, the patient was noted to have good pronation and supination of the left forearm. An X-ray of the left forearm obtained at follow-up demonstrated partial excision of the exostosis projecting laterally and anteriorly from the distal ulnar shaft. Outcomes, including forearm rotation measurements and long-term recurrence data, will be reported at future follow-up visits.

Discussion

Radioulnar synostosis refers to the pathological formation of bone in soft tissues between the radius and ulna, outside the normal skeletal architecture [5]. In the setting of post-traumatic radioulnar synostosis, recurrence following excision is driven by the recruitment of pluripotent mesenchymal stem cells that, under the influence of local postsurgical inflammatory signals, differentiate along an osteogenic pathway. Patients who have already formed synostosis have demonstrated this predisposition and are at elevated risk of recurrence after repeat surgical disruption of the same anatomical region [2].

RT targets osteoprogenitor cells during their early proliferative phase, before they undergo terminal osteogenic differentiation and become less radiosensitive [5]. Treatment within 72 hours is recommended because osteoprogenitor cells remain in this early proliferative phase and are most susceptible to radiation during this period [5]. The 72-hour postoperative window is supported by a randomized controlled trial by Gregoritch et al., in which preoperative (<4 hours before surgery) and postoperative (<72 hours after surgery) single-fraction RT were found to be similarly efficacious in hip arthroplasty, with the postoperative group receiving treatment within 72 hours of surgery [6].

This mechanism underlies the two core principles governing prophylactic RT in this context: the timing imperative (treatment should be delivered within 72 hours of surgery to intercept dividing progenitor cells) and the dose principle (low doses in the range of 7-8 Gy are biologically sufficient, as the target is a dividing progenitor cell population rather than an established tumor) [5]. The strongest evidence for radiation prophylaxis against heterotopic ossification comes from the hip arthroplasty literature, where multiple randomized controlled trials and meta-analyses have demonstrated its efficacy [4,7]. The application of this evidence to forearm radioulnar synostosis is supported by two foundational case series.

Abrams et al. described two patients with post-traumatic radioulnar synostosis who were treated with surgical excision and low-dose radiation therapy. Both patients recovered near-full ranges of motion without wound complications or recurrence [8]. Cullen et al. reported four similar patients treated with excision followed by a single fraction of 800 cGy (8 Gy). At follow-up ranging from one to four years, all four patients had good forearm rotation and no evidence of recurrent bone formation on imaging [9]. A more recent review by Osterman and Arief summarized this experience and supported the use of single-fraction RT at 7-8 Gy in patients with a high risk of recurrence, noting that no recurrences had been reported in the published series [2]. Not every patient who undergoes synostosis excision requires RT afterward. Bergeron et al. note that the recurrence rate after a first excision is low enough that routine prophylaxis is not always necessary [1]. However, patients who are at higher risk, such as those with a prior history of synostosis, high-energy injuries (i.e., motor vehicle accidents or falls from significant heights), or traumatic brain injury, are more likely to benefit from adjuvant treatment. The patient in this case had already developed abnormal bone formation once, which is one of the clearest indicators that he was at elevated risk of recurrence. This reasoning is consistent with the patient selection criteria used in the Cullen and Abrams series as well.

In this case, preoperative plain radiographs demonstrated a distal forearm synostosis, consistent with a Type I lesion according to the Hastings and Graham classification [1,2]. It is worth noting that, while Type I lesions are classically associated with salvage procedures such as the Sauvé-Kapandji or Darrach procedure, the treating surgeon elected excision based on the specific anatomy and intraoperative findings. The decision to pursue adjuvant RT following excision was made based on the patient's established osteogenic predisposition, regardless of synostosis type, as the risk of recurrent heterotopic bone formation after excision is driven primarily by patient-level factors rather than lesion location alone.

The dose of 7 Gy in a single fraction used in this case falls within the accepted prophylactic range of 7-8 Gy in one fraction, with 10 Gy in five fractions serving as an acceptable fractionated alternative. A meta-analysis by Pakos and Ioannidis demonstrated that doses below 6 Gy are associated with reduced efficacy, while single-fraction doses in the 7-8 Gy range achieve optimal prophylactic results [4]. Single-fraction delivery is preferred over fractionated regimens because it imposes less burden on the patient, does not delay early postoperative rehabilitation, and is logistically well suited to the inpatient setting.

The other main option for prophylaxis is NSAID therapy, most commonly indomethacin 75 mg, administered for six weeks after surgery. NSAIDs act by inhibiting COX-2-mediated prostaglandin synthesis and osteogenic differentiation. A systematic review by Henstenburg et al. found that both radiation and NSAIDs reduce the risk of heterotopic ossification after elbow trauma. However, neither is clearly superior to the other in head-to-head trials [7]. In this case, radiation was chosen over a prolonged NSAID course for several reasons: there is direct published precedent for RT in this specific condition, a single inpatient treatment is more practical than a six-week medication course, and the patient had a documented allergy to ibuprofen, limiting his NSAID options. Additionally, prolonged NSAID use carries well-established risks of gastrointestinal bleeding, renal toxicity, and cardiovascular complications, which can accumulate over the treatment course and may be poorly tolerated in older patients.

A relevant safety concern when using RT around the elbow and forearm is impaired bone healing. Hamid et al. conducted a prospective randomized trial of single-fraction RT following acute elbow trauma that was terminated early after the nonunion rate in the RT group reached 38%, compared with 4% in the control group [10,11]. This finding is important and deserves careful consideration in the context of this case. However, the study by Hamid et al. [10] is not directly applicable to the present case. That trial included patients who had undergone surgical fixation of acute fractures, meaning the bone was actively undergoing healing at the time radiation was delivered. Radiation is known to interfere with bone healing in that setting. In the current case, the surgery involved removal of already mature, consolidated bone, and there was no active fracture repair occurring at the radiation site. This important distinction likely explains why nonunion was not a complication in either the Cullen or Abrams series, in which patients also underwent excision of mature bone rather than acute fracture fixation.

The use of ionizing radiation for prophylaxis of a benign condition requires explicit justification of the risk-benefit balance, given the theoretical risk of radiation-induced malignancy associated with any exposure [5]. At the prophylactic doses used for heterotopic ossification (7-8 Gy to a small, localized field), the absolute risk of radiation-induced sarcoma is very low. In a patient in his late 50s, the typical latency period for radiation-induced malignancy of 10 or more years further attenuates the absolute actuarial risk. The functional consequence of recurrent synostosis - significant disability and the need for further surgery - is judged to outweigh this risk in high-risk patients. Informed consent, including explicit discussion of this risk, was obtained before treatment, in keeping with best practice for RT in benign disease.

Conclusions

This report demonstrates that single-fraction postoperative radiation therapy is a safe and practical adjuvant strategy for preventing post-traumatic radioulnar synostosis in high-risk patients. The absence of active fracture healing at the irradiated site in this setting distinguishes synostosis excision from acute fracture fixation. These findings support the use of RT without significant concern for nonunion. Further prospective data are needed to standardize patient selection and treatment protocols for this rare condition.
