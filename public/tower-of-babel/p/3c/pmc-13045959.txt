Introduction

Obturator hernia (OH) is a rare type of abdominal wall hernia that constitutes <1% of all abdominal hernias and is responsible for 0.2–1.6 % of the cases of mechanical small bowel obstruction[1,2]. It involves the protrusion of abdominal content through a defect in the obturator foramen, located between the obturator and the pectineus muscle. It usually contains the small bowel but may also include the appendix, bladder, omentum, or fallopian tubes[1,3]. Commonly affects elderly (70–90 years), thin, and multiparous women[3,4].

Early diagnosis is often challenging due to its nonspecific clinical features and inconclusive clinical examination, leading to delayed diagnosis and subsequently high morbidity and mortality. Clinical features are mostly nonspecific, often presenting as a mechanical bowel obstruction (90%), and the Howship–Rhomberg sign (pain in the anteromedial aspect of the thigh), which is positive in 20–50% of cases[5,6]. Contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CECT) scans of the abdomen and pelvis remain the cornerstone of diagnosis. Prompt surgical intervention is the definitive treatment.

We report a series (three cases) of small bowel obstructions due to right OH in elderly women who presented to the emergency department of a tertiary-level hospital in Nepal. This case report has been reported in line with the Process guidelines[7].

Case series presentation

Case 1

A 108-year-old, thin-built, frail lady presented to the emergency department with abdominal pain for 5 days, which was gradually progressive, dull aching, moderately severe, cramping in nature, and non-radiating. It was associated with multiple episodes of non-projectile, bilious vomiting. She also had abdominal distension and absence of passage of stool and flatus. There was no significant past surgical history. On clinical examination, she appeared ill, pale, and tachypneic, with a thin build. She was lying in bed with her right leg in a semi-flexed position. Abdominal examination revealed a soft, scaphoid abdomen with tenderness localized to the right iliac fossa and umbilical region. No palpable masses were noted, and her hernial orifices were intact. Digital rectal examination was unremarkable.

Laboratory investigations were within normal limits except for hemoglobin of 8.9 g/dl and serum potassium of 2.7 mmol/l. An abdominal supine X-ray showed centrally placed dilated bowel loops with signs of volvulus. Ultrasonography of the abdomen and pelvis was unremarkable. However, CECT of the abdomen and pelvis revealed herniation of a bowel loop on the right side, superior to the obturator internus muscle, located between the pectineus and obturator muscles (Fig. 1). There was focal dilatation of the proximal small bowel loop, suggestive of an obstructed right OH with small bowel herniation. Figure 1.CECT abdomen and pelvis showing herniation of bowel through the right OC.

CECT abdomen and pelvis showing herniation of bowel through the right OC.

The patient was resuscitated and taken for emergency laparotomy via a lower midline approach. Intraoperatively, a jejunal loop approximately 50 cm distal to the duodenojejunal (DJ) flexure was found herniating into the right obturator canal (OC), with a dilated proximal loop and collapsed distal bowel (Fig. 2). Manual reduction was performed after extending the OC via incision of the obturator membrane, carefully preserving the neurovascular bundle. Approximately 4 cm of jejunum was found inside the canal and was non-viable (Fig. 3); this segment was resected, and a two-layer end-to-end anastomosis was performed using polyglactin 3-0. The hernial defect was closed (the obturator membrane and surrounding fascia) with polypropylene-1 (Fig. 4). The postoperative course was uneventful. Early mobilization and feeding were initiated, and the patient was discharged on the eighth postoperative day due to her chest issues.Figure 2.Herniation of bowel through the OC and dilated proximal and collapsed distal loops. Case 2

A 78-year-old, thin-built, frail lady presented to the emergency department with abdominal pain for 10 days, which was centrally located and gradually progressive. The pain was associated with multiple episodes of non-projectile, bilious vomiting. She had not passed stool or flatus for the past 4 days.

On clinical examination, she appeared ill and emaciated. Her abdomen was soft and scaphoid, with tenderness over the right iliac fossa and umbilical region. No abdominal masses were palpable, and her hernial orifices were intact. Digital rectal examination was normal.

An abdominal supine X-ray showed centrally placed dilated bowel loops with evidence of volvulus. Ultrasonography of the abdomen and pelvis was unremarkable. CECT of the abdomen and pelvis revealed herniation of a small bowel loop on the right side, superior to the obturator internus muscle, lying between the pectineus and obturator muscles (Figs 5 and 6). These findings were suggestive of an obstructed right OH with herniation of a small bowel loop. Figure 5.CECT abdomen and pelvis axial view showing herniation of bowel through the right OC. Figure 6.CECT abdomen and pelvis coronal view showing herniation of bowel through the right OC and dilated bowel loop.

CECT abdomen and pelvis axial view showing herniation of bowel through the right OC.

CECT abdomen and pelvis coronal view showing herniation of bowel through the right OC and dilated bowel loop.

The patient was resuscitated and taken for exploratory laparotomy via a lower midline approach. Intraoperatively, a jejunal loop approximately 60 cm distal to the DJ flexure was found herniating into the right OC, with a dilated proximal loop and collapsed distal loop (Fig. 7). Manual reduction was performed after extending the OC by incising the obturator membrane, taking care to preserve the neurovascular bundle. Approximately 10 cm of jejunum was found within the canal, and the segment was non-viable (Fig. 8). The necrotic segment was resected, and a two-layer end-to-end anastomosis was performed using polyglactin 3-0. The hernial defect was closed (the obturator membrane and surrounding fascia) using polypropylene-1. The postoperative period was uneventful. Early mobilization and feeding were initiated, and the patient was discharged on the fifth postoperative day. Case 3

An 86-year-old female presented to the emergency department with complaints of abdominal pain for 5 days, which was initially localized to the right lower quadrant and later became generalized. The pain was associated with multiple episodes of non-projectile, bilious vomiting. She had not passed stool or flatus for the past 4 days. On clinical examination, she appeared ill, thin-built, and dehydrated. Abdominal examination revealed generalized tenderness with rebound tenderness. Digital rectal examination was normal. An abdominal supine X-ray showed centrally placed dilated bowel loops (Fig. 9). Ultrasonography of the abdomen and pelvis revealed mild free fluid in the pelvic cavity. CECT of the abdomen and pelvis demonstrated herniation of a small bowel loop on the right side, superior to the obturator internus muscle, lying between the pectineus and obturator muscles (Figs 10 and 11). The patient was in septic shock. She was resuscitated and taken for emergency laparotomy for sub-acute small bowel obstruction. A midline approach was used. Intraoperative findings included approximately 150 ml of feculent fluid in the pelvic cavity and a right-sided strangulated OH with perforation along the antimesenteric border, located approximately 150 cm distal to the DJ junction and 80 cm proximal to the ileocecal (IC) junction. A defunctioning loop ileostomy was created at the site of perforation on the right lower abdomen, and the hernial defect was closed (the obturator membrane and surrounding fascia) using polypropylene-1.

The patient was intubated and shifted to the intensive care unit. Unfortunately, on the second postoperative day, she expired due to cardiopulmonary arrest secondary to sepsis.

Discussion

The obturator foramen is a large opening present in the bony pelvis, formed by the pubis and ischium, and is covered by a thick membrane called the obturator membrane. The OC is 2–3 cm long and 1 cm wide, which is traversed by obturator vessels and nerves and lies between the obturator externus and the pectineus muscle[8]. OH is one of the rare abdominal hernias, which constitutes <1% of all abdominal hernias. It is a protrusion of abdominal content through a defect in the obturator foramen[1–3]. Arnaud de Ronsil first discovered it in 1724[9]. It usually contains the small bowel but may also include the appendix, bladder, omentum, or fallopian tubes. It is responsible for 0.2–1.6 % of all small bowel obstructions. The Left OC is protected by the sigmoid colon; thus right is commonly involved[1,2]. Our three cases were on the right side. Although rare, there are case reports of bilateral OH[8,10].

Commonly known as “little old lady’s hernia” because it most commonly affects elderly (70−90 years, all our cases were above 70 years), thin, and multiparous women (female-to-male ratio of 6:1). Mainly owing to wider pelvis and larger OC in females, enlargement of the OC after pregnancy, and age-related increased tissue laxness[1,3]. Conditions increasing the intraabdominal pressure, such as chronic cough, chronic constipation, ascites, multiparous, predispose to hernia[9–12].

The symptoms are non-specific, vague, and are usually in the form of signs of bowel obstruction, such as nausea, vomiting, abdominal pain, and a lack of bowel movement. Due to a high proportion of exhibiting Richter’s herniation of the bowel, it may present with a picture of intermittent bowel obstruction symptoms, further delaying the diagnosis. OH sacs often compress or irritate the obturator nerve running in the canal, giving rise to medial thigh pain known as the Howship–Rhomberg Sign, and is present in 20–50% of OH cases (one case was positive in our cases)[6,8]. Hannington–Kiff sign is the absence of an adductor reflex in the thigh, which is pathognomonic but rarely seen. A vaginal examination revealing a tender mass may also be helpful to the clinician in the female patient[10,12].

Commonly used radiological investigations are X-ray, ultrasonography, CECT, and MRI. CECT scan has high sensitivity and specificity, which helps in early diagnosis of OH, which shows herniation of bowel through the obturator foramen, which lies between the obturator muscles and pectineus. USG abdomen and pelvis can be very helpful in low-resource countries like Nepal, but it is user-dependent and the location of the hernia is deep, thus diagnosis by ultrasound may be difficult. MRI can be used in diagnosis, but is considered inferior to a CT scan[13].

Management of an OH consists of various surgical approaches. It can be an open approach and a minimally invasive laparoscopic approach[10,14]. Traditionally abdominal approach with a low midline incision allows the surgeon to establish the diagnosis, avoid any obturator vessels, give better exposure of the obturator ring, and facilitate bowel resection if necessary[1,14,15].

Simple closure of the hernial defect with interrupted sutures if the size is less than 1 cm, and placement of a synthetic mesh or omental patches (for contaminated cases) is required for defects larger than 1 cm[15]. Lower midline laparotomy with closure of the defect with suture done in all cases. In two cases, resection anastomosis was done and, in one case, we did a loop ileostomy.

Due to the rarity of these cases and the relatively recent emergence of laparoscopic hernia surgery, there is sparse literature on laparoscopic repair of OH. Both trans-abdominal and extra-peritoneal laparoscopic approaches have been described in the literature[14,15].

Laparoscopic repair has been shown to produce less post-operative pain, fewer pulmonary complications, and shorter hospital stays in such cases. As it occurs in elderly patients who have limited physiological reserve, a shift in practice from open repairs towards laparoscopic repair is advisable[15].

Delays in surgical treatment increased the risks of bowel strangulation, thus had high postoperative morbidity (17.6–86%) and mortality (0–47.6%), suggesting that prompt surgical treatment can reduce the morbidity and mortality associated with intestinal strangulation caused by OH[15,16]. Out of three cases, two cases were discharged eventually, while one mortality on the 2nd postoperative day. Conclusion

Our case series of right OH is a rare abdominal hernia causing bowel obstruction. Diagnosis is delayed due to nonspecific clinical features and inconclusive clinical examination. CT of the abdomen and pelvis is the gold standard in diagnosis. Although there are various surgical approaches, the abdominal approach with a low midline incision can be opted for as one of the effective treatment methods in OH. As it occurs in the elderly with comorbid conditions, and diagnosis is delayed, it has high morbidity and mortality.
