Facilitators of and barriers to patient and public involvement in building learning health systems in community health services settings: a scoping review protocol
Authors: Lillian Hung, Karen Lok Yi Wong, Ian Chan, Krisztina Vasarhelyi
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionThe development of learning health systems (LHSs) has often focused on optimally leveraging data. More attention should be paid to patient and public involvement or community engagement in forming learning communities that work together to build LHS. This scoping review aims to identify facilitators of and barriers to involving patients and the public in building LHSs in community health services settings.Methods and analysisWe will use the Joanna Briggs Institute’s scoping review methodology. We will review literature in English published from 1 January 2007 to 31 December 2022. The databases that will be searched are MEDLINE, CINAHL, Embase, Web of Science, Scopus, AgeLine, PsycINFO and Web of Science. Key inclusion and exclusion criteria include the following: we will only consider a learning community in a community health services context (eg, home care, long-term care, primary care); we will exclude literature on acute care settings; and we will consider any research designs apart from big data analytics. We will review all sources, including university student theses and dissertations. The review will proceed in three steps: (1) we will identify keywords and index terms from the MEDLINE and CINAHL databases; (2) using the keywords and index terms identified in step (1), we will search other databases and (3) we will handsearch the reference lists of the selected literature and will search for grey literature using Google. Two research assistants will screen the titles and abstracts separately, with reference to the inclusion criteria. Two researchers will then assess the full text of selected studies, also in reference to the inclusion criteria. We will present the findings in a charting table and provide a narrative summary.Ethics and disseminationThis work does not require ethics approval because the data for this scoping review are publicly available. The findings will be presented in a journal article and at conferences.Keywords: health services accessibility, health policy, public health

Introduction

The learning health system (LHS) model has the potential to revolutionise healthcare delivery by continuously improving care, promoting innovation and achieving better patient outcomes.1 LHS represents a shift towards a more patient-centred and efficient healthcare system that aims to improve health outcomes while reducing costs.2 However, realising these benefits requires the active engagement and meaningful involvement of patients and communities in developing and implementing LHSs in partnership with healthcare stakeholders, decision-makers and researchers. Such patient and public involvement (PPI) helps to ensure that the system design addresses the perspectives and needs of patients and their communities. Previous reviews of the literature have largely focused on data management; more attention should be paid to PPI or community engagement.

The Institute of Medicine proposed that implementing LHSs is crucial to achieving value-based healthcare. Value-based healthcare considers the outcomes that are most important to patients and focuses on delivering care that achieves those outcomes.3 LHSs are ‘systems that align science, informatics, incentives and culture for continuous improvement and innovation, with best practices seamlessly integrated into the delivery process and new knowledge captured as a by-product’.4 The principles of participatory leadership, inclusiveness and person-centredness are at the core of LHSs. However, the role of patient involvement and community engagement in LHS development remains unclear despite the emphasis on PPI in research and service improvement.

LHSs are designed to study care delivery in real-time, use lessons learnt to improve care, disseminate findings, and implement and evaluate evidence-based practices system-wide. In addition, LHSs offer innovative ways for patients to be actively involved in their care service improvement and research. Community engagement is a critical component of LHSs to ensure the active and meaningful involvement of community members, patients, point-of-care providers, administrators and researchers. As suggested by a recent local report, LHS should engage patients to ensure the healthcare systems are anchored on patient needs, perspectives and aspirations at all levels and focused on improving their care experiences.5

A community-based participatory approach and human-centred design methods will inform this scoping review. Such approaches are highly relevant because they prioritise the involvement of clinicians, patients and the public, which aligns with the focus on PPI in this scoping review. Community-based participatory approaches involve collaborating with relevant community members in a healthcare service improvement process, from problem identification to the implementation and evaluation of interventions.6 A community-based approach allows the health system to understand and prioritise the specific health needs of local communities. This results in more relevant and effective interventions. Human-centred design methods involve developing solutions to problems by understanding users’ needs and contexts.7 Human-centred design is an iterative process that involves continuous testing and improvement. This aligns with the concept of an LHS, which learns and improves over time.

LHSs promote dynamic knowledge integration, enabling rapid organisational learning and positively impacting care delivery, population health, and system performance. A culture of trust, safety, respect and shared learning is fundamental for successfully coproducing knowledge in an LHS. Stakeholder engagement is crucial for identifying acceptable solutions for the local context in LHS implementation. Barriers to LHS implementation may include resistance to change, lack of expertise and resources in knowledge translation, weak leadership support, technical challenges and unmet patient expectations.8 In addition, healthcare practitioners want understandable, trusted, and clinically useful data, tools, and interventions. Enablers of LHSs can include environments promoting respect, trust, collaboration and patient participation. Furthermore, regular and effective communication among stakeholders is critical to overcoming barriers to LHS implementation.

This scoping review aims to explore the reported literature on facilitators of and barriers to building LHSs with active patient and public contributions. The review will focus on community engagement, challenges and enablers for PPI. By understanding the facilitators of and barriers to LHS development, health systems can better design and implement LHSs that are effective, efficient and responsive to the needs of patients and communities. Ultimately, this can lead to better health outcomes for individuals and populations and a more sustainable and value-based healthcare system.

We conducted an initial search on 11 February 2023, of a small number of literature databases and the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) Database of Systematic Reviews and Implementation Reports. We did not find a systematic review on facilitators of and barriers to building a learning community for co-creating an LHS in a community setting.

We foresee that our scoping review will make two significant contributions. First, it will synthesise the most recent evidence on facilitators of and barriers to building a learning community for co-creating an LHS in a community health services setting. Also, we will include patient partners and family members with lived experience of accessing care in community health services settings throughout the scoping review process. Their contribution will enhance the relevance and quality of the scoping review. Scoping reviews are designed to map the existing literature comprehensively and identify key concepts, sources of evidence and research gaps in an emerging topic.9 In this case, our research questions focus on identifying facilitators of and barriers to PPI in building a learning community for the co-creation of LHSs in community health services settings. A scoping review is an optimal methodology for addressing the study questions. First, PPI in building a learning community for LHSs is an underexplored area; a scoping review provides an overview of existing literature to identify barriers, facilitators and strategies for LHS co-creation. Second, unlike systematic reviews, scoping reviews can include a variety of literature types, such as organisational reports and quality improvement projects about LHS development. The integration of diverse evidence, including clinician and patient partner perspectives, offers a comprehensive view that is essential for informing future research about meaningful collaboration.

Methods and analysis

Study design

This scoping review is guided by the JBI methodology for scoping reviews9 and builds on protocols we published previously.10 11 The JBI methodology provides guidelines for conducting scoping reviews, including steps for developing a protocol, searching and selecting literature, extracting data, and synthesising data. We chose the JBI instead of other scoping review methodologies because it is widely used worldwide in a range of disciplines, especially healthcare. Also, JBI scoping review steps are clear and easy to follow. The scoping review will be conducted from August 2023 to January 2024.

We suggest a 6-month time frame for a thorough and comprehensive analysis. First, the scoping review aims to explore a broad range of literature. A 6-month time frame allows for a more comprehensive review of relevant studies, including published articles, reports and other relevant sources. Second, gathering and organising the data for team analysis is a meticulous process. It involves reading, extracting relevant information and synthesising the findings. Allocating ample time for data collection and analysis helps us ensure accuracy, reliability and a robust review process. Third, iterative review process: This work involves an iterative process, where the research team may need to revisit the literature search and analysis to refine and explore emerging themes. Allowing 6 months provides the necessary flexibility to adapt the review process based on initial findings and feedback from team members.

Eligibility criteria

Participants

We will focus on studies that identify patients, peers, family partners, members of communities with an identified health or health equity concern, or members of the public at large as participants or stakeholders engaging in the co-development of an LHS in any community health service area.

Concept

The creation of a learning community requires the effective engagement and participation of a group of stakeholders with diverse backgrounds and perspectives. There is evidence that a variety of complex factors pose challenges to engaging and integrating community stakeholders in community health service initiatives, which, among others, may include contextual considerations, diverse perspectives and experiences related to the focal area, and limited financial and human resources to support engagement. Therefore, we anticipate that initiatives embarking on creating learning communities for building an LHS may also face challenges while also having opportunities to leverage strengths and supportive resources. In this scoping review, our aim is to map these facilitators of and barriers to patient and public engagement in building a learning community for co-creating an LHS. The core concepts are LHS, co-creation, stakeholder engagement and learning community.

Context

We will only consider learning communities in community health services contexts, which may include primary care, substance use and mental health services, long-term care, home health, assisted living and specialised services for specific patient populations (older adults, youth, pregnant persons, etc). We will exclude literature on acute care settings.

The motivation for this scoping review is to inform an initiative to form a learning community in a healthcare setting in Canada. Therefore, we aim to select studies most relevant to the Canadian context.

Types of studies

We will review literature in English published from January 2007 to December 2022. We selected 2007 for the start of the date range to be covered by our literature search because the first publication on LHSs was published in 2007.4 We will consider any research designs except big data analytics. Our preliminary search of the literature indicated that data analytics studies on LHS rarely discussed co-creation and building a learning community. We will review all sources, including university student theses and dissertations.

Search strategy

The JBI methodology suggests three steps.9 First, we will do a preliminary search using two databases related to our topic, MEDLINE and CINAHL (online supplemental file 1). The keywords we will use for the searches include LHS, co-creation and community engagement (online supplemental file 2). We will then review the titles and abstracts as well as the index terms of the searched studies. Second, we will search using all identified keywords and index terms. Third, we will screen the reference lists of all chosen studies for additional studies. The full search strategy for MEDLINE can be found in online supplemental file 1 of this protocol. We have sent our search strategy to our university medical librarian for feedback and will continue to work with her. The authors of this protocol (LH and KV) are experts on the topic of the scoping review, are familiar with the LHS literature and can supervise the team on specific reference searches.

Information sources

We will look for literature in the following databases: MEDLINE, CINAHL, Embase, Web of Science, Scopus, AgeLine, PsycINFO and Web of Science. We will also look for theses and dissertations via ProQuest. We will do phase searches through Google for grey literature (online supplemental file 3). Grey literature refers to literature that is published outside the conventional commercial or academic publishing channels.12 Some examples of grey literature include government documents and non-governmental organisation reports.

Study selection

All mapped literature will be saved into the reference manager tool, Zotero. Duplicates will be removed. Two research assistants will screen the titles and abstracts separately, with reference to the inclusion criteria, will look for the full text of the selected studies and will import the details of the citation of the selected studies into the JBI System for the Unified Management, Assessment and Review of Information.9

Two researchers will assess the full texts of the selected studies independently with reference to the inclusion criteria and exclude studies that do not meet the inclusion criteria. Reasons for exclusion will be documented.

We will continue fine-tuning the search and selection processes with assistance from the medical librarian. Reviewers may have different opinions on whether a study should be included. If this happens, we will discuss, seeking consensus. If an agreement cannot be reached, a decision from the lead author (LH) will be requested.

In the final article of the scoping review, we will present the results of the searches in detail, as well as the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses for Scoping Reviews flow diagram.

Data extraction and synthesis

We will use a charting table as a tool to record extracted data. Please see the charting table in online supplemental file 4. The data that will be extracted include author(s), publication year, country of the study, literature type, study design, study setting, study population, barriers to building a learning community for co-creating an LHS, as well as strategies to overcome the barriers or to facilitate building a learning community.

We will carry out a pilot test using the charting table. Two researchers will extract data from three studies separately and compare what they have extracted. We will adjust the charting table during the data extraction process if needed and report what changes we made.

We will use a charting table to show the extracted data as discussed. We will also provide a narrative summary of the findings and key themes.

Patient and public involvement

We will involve two patient partners and two family partners in the scoping review.

We will recruit our patient and family partners via the Community Engagement Advisory Network (CEAN), which is a community organisation that promotes the engagement of patient and family partners in research. More information about CEAN can be found at the organisation website (http://cean.vch.ca).

Patient and family partners will review the full text of the selected articles. Some of the patient and family partners have collaborated with the first author (LH) and second author (KLYW) previously on scoping reviews,11 13 14 have received training, and will be provided additional support if needed (eg, answering any questions they may have, checking in with them about their progress) to review the full text of the articles. New patient partners will receive training from an experienced patient partner. Patient and family partners will decide how many articles they want to review. We expect that each partner will review one to three articles. Gift cards will be provided to compensate patient and family partners for their expertise and time.

There will be regular biweekly in-person or Zoom meetings with patient and family partners. The authors will share the findings of the scoping review with patient and family partners and invite their feedback on the results. We will also invite patient and family partners to share the results within their networks. To thank our partners for their contributions, we will provide an honorarium and be coauthors of the scoping review article.

Ethics and dissemination

This work does not require ethics approval because the data for this scoping review are publicly available. The scoping review findings will be presented in a journal article and at conferences.
