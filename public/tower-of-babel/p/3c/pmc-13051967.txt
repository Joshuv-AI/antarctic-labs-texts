Precision Under Pressure: Scalp Block and Dexmedetomidine for Brain Abscess Drainage in a Patient With Complex Cyanotic Heart Disease
Authors: Sandeep Khatri, Ashish Raj Tiwari, Barsha Karki, Manisha Poudel, Anish Sah
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTCyanotic heart disease is a known risk factor for brain abscess. Anesthesia for burr‐hole drainage in these patients can be challenging due to complications like an increase in right‐to‐left shunt, arrhythmias, heart failure, and paradoxical air embolism. The risk of these complications can be decreased by choosing a scalp block with conscious sedation.Keywords: anesthesia, case report, dexmedetomidine, pulmonary stenosis, scalp block

Introduction

Pulmonary stenosis (PS) is mostly congenital in origin and is mostly associated with other cardiac anomalies, and can account for 7%–12% of congenital heart diseases (CHD) [1]. PS with an intact interventricular septum is rare, leading to right ventricle (RV) outflow tract obstruction, hypertrophy of the RV, elevated right heart pressure, and right‐to‐left shunt through an atrial septal defect (ASD) [2, 3]. This shunting is usually protective for pulmonary artery hypertension (PAH) [2]. Cardiac anomaly with right‐to‐left shunt is a known risk factor for the development of brain abscess, which typically presents with symptoms such as headache, fever, focal neurological deficits, and vomiting [4, 5]. Burr‐hole drainage of cerebral abscess can be performed under general anesthesia or regional anesthesia with sedation [6, 7]. Here, we present a case of a patient with cerebral abscess with PS and PAH who underwent burr‐hole drainage under scalp block with sedation.

Case Presentation

A 43‐year‐old male weighing 45 kg, a non‐smoker, non‐alcoholic, presented to the emergency room with the chief complaint of headache for 15 days, projectile vomiting and right‐sided weakness for 5 days. He had an episode of infective endocarditis 3 years back, for which he was admitted and had undergone treatment with antibiotics. Pulmonary stenosis (PS) with pulmonary artery hypertension (PAH) with atrial septal defect (ASD) with right‐to‐left shunt was diagnosed 3 years back. Pulmonary valve and ASD repair had been planned, but the patient did not follow up. He was on torsemide 20 mg twice daily and metoprolol 12.5 mg once daily, but was not compliant with his medications. He had functional limitations before the current illness as well, with shortness of breath on walking on level ground.

On examination, the patient was looking ill, lying supine in the preoperative holding area with a 20‐G cannula in situ in his left hand. He was conscious and oriented. His heart rate was 84 beats per minute, blood pressure was 120/70 mmHg, and he was afebrile. His oxygen saturation was 82% in room air, which increased to 91% with oxygen at 10 L/min via face mask. He had a grade 3 systolic murmur, which was more evident in the pulmonic area, with no signs of heart failure. Respiratory and abdominal examinations were normal. His pupils were equal and reactive to light. He had right‐sided weakness with a power of 4/5 with exaggerated reflexes in the right upper and lower limbs. Bilateral plantar reflexes were upgoing. Airway examination was normal.

Investigation and Treatment

He was polycythemic with hemoglobin of 23 g%, and mild thrombocytopenia with platelets of 112,000/mm3. Random blood glucose, renal functions, liver functions, and international normalized ratio (INR) were within normal range. Electrocardiography revealed a sinus rhythm with a heart rate of 64 beats/min, and right axis deviation. The right atrium was enlarged on chest X‐ray (Figure 1). Computed tomography (CT) scan revealed a single left parietooccipital ring‐enhancing, hypodense lesion, likely an abscess with surrounding vasogenic edema (Figures 2 and 3). The patient was shifted to the operating room. Electrocardiography, noninvasive blood pressure, and pulse oximetry were connected. A 20‐G arterial line was inserted in the left radial artery. A 7 Fr triple‐lumen central venous catheter was inserted in the right internal jugular vein after giving a superficial cervical plexus block under ultrasound guidance. The patient's baseline heart rate was 75 bpm, blood pressure was 110/80 mmHg, and SpO2 was 94% on oxygen at 10 L/min via face mask.

Scalp block (Figure 4) was performed using a total of 25 mL of 0.25% Bupivacaine to block the supratrochlear nerve (2 mL), supraorbital nerve (3 mL), zygomaticotemporal nerve (5 mL), auriculotemporal nerve (5 mL), and greater and lesser occipital nerves (5 mL each). Sedation was provided with dexmedetomidine, injecting a loading dose of 45 μg over 10 min and a maintenance dose of 0.7 μg/kg/h. Cefazolin 2 g and paracetamol 1 g were given intravenously. The patient was positioned supine with head turned to the right. The surgery lasted 90 min. A burr hole was made in the left occipital region, and 15 mL of thick pus was aspirated. Intraoperatively, heart rate ranged from 60 to 80 beats per minute, and blood pressure ranged from 100 to 140 mmHg systolic and 70 to 90 mmHg diastolic. The intraoperative course was uneventful. A total of 500 mL of 0.9% sodium chloride was given.

The patient was transferred to the intensive care unit (ICU) after the procedure for postoperative monitoring. Antibiotic coverage with ceftriaxone, metronidazole, and vancomycin was started, and paracetamol tablets were used for analgesia.

Outcome and Follow‐Up

Headache and vomiting subsided after the surgery. Motor power in the left upper and lower limbs was 4/5. The patient was transferred to the general ward on the first postoperative day after removal of the arterial line and central venous catheter. His ICU stay was uneventful. The patient was discharged on the third postoperative day and advised to follow up with neurosurgery as well as cardiac surgery.

Discussion

Scalp block with conscious sedation was the mode of anesthesia in this patient with severe pulmonary stenosis (PS), severe pulmonary artery hypertension (PAH), atrial septal defect (ASD), and right‐to‐left shunt. Invasive arterial pressure monitoring and central venous catheterization were also done.

Cyanotic heart diseases are an important risk factor for the development of brain abscesses, with more adverse outcomes [4, 5]. PS with ASD with an intact interventricular septum is a relatively rare form of cyanotic congenital heart disease. The increased right heart pressure due to the PS leads to a right‐to‐left shunt in the ASD. PS generally protects the pulmonary vasculature. However, PAH, although rare, has been documented even in patients with congenital severe PS [8].

Cyanotic heart diseases pose significant challenges to anesthetic management. An increase in the right‐to‐left shunt can lead to worsening of hypoxemia [9, 10]. The amount of blood that gets shunted depends on the difference in vascular resistances between the pulmonary and systemic circulations. Conditions that increase the pulmonary vascular resistance and hence right‐to‐left shunt must be avoided. This includes hypoxia, hypercarbia, pain, acidosis, hypothermia, and overinflation of lungs [9]. A sudden drop in systemic vascular resistance, as often seen with certain induction agents or neuraxial anesthesia, can increase the right‐to‐left shunt, increasing hypoxemia, decreasing cardiac output, and further worsening the shunt. The affected chambers are further predisposed to complications like arrhythmias and heart failure [10].

There have been various reports on the anesthetic management of a patient with cyanotic heart disease undergoing surgery for a brain abscess. Burr‐hole drainage of a brain abscess can be done under general anesthesia [6, 11, 12]. Possible hypoxia, hypercarbia, overinflation of lungs, and hypothermia during general anesthesia can lead to an increase in right‐to‐left shunt. Scalp block with sedation is another option for anesthetic management of burr hole, which could lead to a lower incidence of increased right‐to‐left shunt and shorter intensive care unit stay [13].

To our knowledge, this is the first report of anesthetic management of a patient with severe PS with severe PAH with ASD for burr‐hole drainage for brain abscess. Hemodynamic goals for a patient with PS include maintenance of preload, maintenance of contractility, and avoidance of an increase in pulmonary vascular resistance. ASD with a right‐to‐left shunt additionally warrants strict air‐bubble precautions, as introduction of an air bubble can lead to embolism into the systemic circulation.

A scalp block produces anesthesia to the ipsilateral side of the scalp. This provides excellent analgesia to the scalp, suited for surgeries like burr‐hole drainage of chronic subdural hemorrhage and brain abscess [14]. The brain itself is insensitive to pain and does not require analgesia. Dexmedetomidine provides anxiolysis, sedation, amnesia, and analgesia without significant respiratory compromise [15]. This makes dexmedetomidine an attractive choice for conscious sedation in procedures with regional anesthesia. Dexmedetomidine, however, can cause bradycardia and hypotension, so hemodynamic monitoring is paramount, especially in patients with preexisting heart diseases and those undergoing neurosurgery.

Conclusion

Scalp block with dexmedetomidine infusion is a viable option for anesthetic management for burr‐hole drainage of brain abscess. There can be a lower risk of hemodynamic instability and an increase in right‐to‐left shunt when compared to general anesthesia in patients with cyanotic heart disease.
