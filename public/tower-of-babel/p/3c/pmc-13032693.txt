Assessing the level of readiness for digital transformation in medicine: students’ perspectives on the use of artificial intelligence in health : a cross-sectional study
Authors: Effat Abbasi Montazeri, Javad Zarei, Bahareh Ghavami Hoseinpour, Amir Bahadori
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundArtificial intelligence (AI) is rapidly transforming healthcare by enhancing diagnostic accuracy, enabling personalized treatments, and improving patient outcomes. Medical students, as future healthcare providers and primary AI users, require adequate knowledge and readiness to integrate AI effectively in clinical practice. Despite growing global interest, little is known about the preparedness of medical students in Iran to adopt AI technologies.MethodsA descriptive cross-sectional survey was conducted among 321 students from medicine, dentistry, and pharmacy programs at Ahvaz Jundishapur University of Medical Sciences during the 2024–2025 academic year. Data were collected via a validated 22-item AI readiness scale covering four domains: cognition, competency, vision, and ethics. Descriptive and inferential statistics, including one-sample t-tests and Wilcoxon signed-rank tests, were applied based on data distribution. Correlation analyses explored relationships among readiness components.ResultParticipants demonstrated moderate cognitive readiness (mean = 3.03), indicating an average theoretical understanding of AI. Competency in AI application scored significantly above average (mean = 3.44, p < 0.001), reflecting confidence in practical use, particularly with digital health tools. The vision toward AI in medicine was positive (mean = 3.31, p < 0.001), although varied among students. Ethical awareness scored highest (mean = 3.69, p < 0.001), indicating strong sensitivity to AI’s ethical challenges. Significant positive correlations were found among all domains (r = 0.44 to 0.73, p < 0.01), with the strongest between cognition and competency. Despite general optimism, gaps remain in technical knowledge and regulatory understanding.ConclusionMedical students in this cohort demonstrate encouraging readiness to engage with AI, particularly in practical and ethical domains; however, foundational knowledge and technical literacy need to be strengthened. The findings underscore the urgent need to integrate interdisciplinary AI education, hands-on training, and legal-ethical instruction into medical curricula. These initiatives are essential to prepare future healthcare professionals for effective and responsible AI integration, ultimately enhancing the quality of patient care.Keywords: Artificial intelligence, Cognitive readiness, Technology acceptance, Student attitudes, Medical education

Introduction

The pivotal role of medical students and educational imperatives

Medical students, as future physicians and primary end-users of AI tools, play a pivotal role in ensuring the success of this digital transformation. As AI technologies become increasingly embedded in clinical environments, medical education must evolve to prepare future healthcare professionals for their effective use [1, 2, 3]. Applications such as robot-assisted surgical training, intelligent assessment feedback systems, and virtual simulation highlight AI’s growing role in both education and practice (4, 5). Integrating AI into the medical curriculum equips students with the knowledge and skills required to contribute to patient-centered, digitally advanced, and cost-effective healthcare systems (6, 7).

Numerous international studies suggest that students recognize the potential of AI in medicine (8). However, their practical knowledge and readiness to implement AI in clinical practice remain limited. For example, studies in Germany (9), China (Al-Gerafi et al., 2024), and Jordan (Rjoop et al., 2025) found that while most students were familiar with general-purpose AI tools (like ChatGPT), their knowledge of specialized clinical applications was often limited, and their primary source of AI knowledge was non-academic. This highlights the urgent need for structured AI education in academic curricula (10, 11).

Challenges, readiness assessment, and study focus

Despite its potential, the implementation of AI in healthcare faces challenges, including algorithmic opacity and complex operational frameworks (12), which may foster reluctance or resistance among healthcare professionals. Therefore, evaluating the perceptions, knowledge, and preparedness of future users, particularly medical students, is critical to ensuring successful and ethical adoption (13, 7, 14). A thorough understanding of AI enables students to critically evaluate AI-generated outputs and integrate them effectively into decision-making processes (15), which directly influences their future professional attitudes and clinical judgment quality (16, 17).

Consistent with established educational theories, evaluating learners’ prior knowledge and readiness is a crucial prerequisite before introducing new instructional content. This approach, particularly in the context of AI, aligns with constructivist principles and ensures that subsequent education is both relevant and effective (18–20). Several studies have emphasized the need to assess medical students’ knowledge, attitudes, and behaviors regarding AI integration into healthcare (21, 12). Assessing their readiness, including knowledge, attitudes, competencies, and ethical awareness (such as ethical considerations, health equity, and data security), is essential for guiding curriculum development and responsible AI integration in clinical care (22–24). Previous studies report generally positive attitudes toward AI and strong interest in formal training (25–28), though some concerns remain regarding employment implications and specialty choices (29–30).

Therefore, equipping medical students with the necessary competencies to integrate AI into clinical and educational settings is essential not only to overcome existing barriers but also to fully realize AI’s potential in improving patient care (31). Understanding students’ readiness can inform curriculum development and educational policy, paving the way for more innovative and adaptive medical education systems (32).

Although this topic is critically important, there has been limited research in Iran on medical students’ preparedness for adopting AI in clinical settings. This study addresses this gap by providing one of the first comprehensive assessments of Iranian medical students’ knowledge, attitudes, and readiness regarding AI integration into clinical practice. By focusing on this unique population, our study contributes novel insights into how future physicians in a developing country perceive AI, highlighting cultural, institutional, and educational factors that may shape adoption. For instance, our results suggest that the current lack of structured AI training (an educational factor) significantly impacts theoretical knowledge, while simultaneously revealing a strong ethical orientation (a cultural factor) among students toward AI implementation. The findings can inform curriculum designers, policymakers, and educators seeking to embed AI literacy into medical education and ensure that upcoming physicians are well-prepared to use emerging technologies safely and effectively.

Method

Study design

This descriptive cross-sectional study was conducted during the second academic semester of 2024–2025 at Ahvaz Jundishapur University of Medical Sciences, using a survey-based approach. The manuscript was prepared and reviewed for compliance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) checklist for cross-sectional studies to ensure transparency in reporting all relevant components.

Setting and participants

The study population included students enrolled in general medicine, dentistry, and pharmacy programs. Using a convenience-based purposive sampling approach, 321 students were recruited. The sampling aimed to include students who had minimal prior exposure to the study topic (AI in health). Recruitment was conducted voluntarily through online announcements (via student Telegram groups) and in-person appeals. All participants volunteered to take part in the study and provided written informed consent prior to data collection. Inclusion criteria were willingness to participate and prior exposure to the study topic. Exclusion criteria included incomplete questionnaire responses or withdrawal of consent at any stage.

The required sample size was formally determined using the formula for estimating a single population proportion in a cross-sectional study. Data collection instruments

Data were collected using a standardized “AI Readiness” scale comprising 22 items across four core dimensions:

Cognitive Readiness (Knowledge): (8 items, Q1–Q8) assessing fundamental conceptual understanding of AI.

Competency (Skills): (8 items, Q9–Q16) evaluating self-assessed practical ability in interacting with AI tools.

Vision (Attitude/Perspective): (3 items, Q17–Q19) measuring students’ outlook and acceptance (attitude) regarding the future integration of AI in healthcare.

Vision (Attitude/Perspective): (3 items, Q17–Q19) measuring students’ outlook and acceptance (attitude) regarding the future integration of AI in healthcare.

Ethics (Awareness): (3 items, Q20–Q22) assessing awareness of ethical and medico-legal implications of AI use.

Ethics (Awareness): (3 items, Q20–Q22) assessing awareness of ethical and medico-legal implications of AI use.

Responses were scored on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = Strongly Disagree to 5 = Strongly Agree). The reliability of the scale was confirmed by Karaca (2021) with a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.87 (33), and further validated in local studies (Reza Zadeh et al., 2023; Cronbach’s alpha = 0.94; content validity indices = 0.92 and 0.75) (34).

Data collection procedure

Questionnaires were administered both in-person and online via Google Forms. The design of the online survey was configured to mandate a response for every item (forced choice), preventing the submission of incomplete questionnaires. During in-person administration, data collectors confirmed that all items were addressed. This systematic approach ensured that the final dataset only included fully completed responses. Consequently, the reported response rate of 100% applies to the 321 fully usable questionnaires selected for the final analysis. Responses that were initiated but not completed, or those flagged as non-usable during preliminary screening (e.g., indicating withdrawal of consent or clear response pattern bias), were excluded prior to establishing the final analytic cohort.

Missing data handling

Due to the method of data collection (mandatory item completion in the online form and supervision during in-person completion), no item-level missing data occurred in the final dataset of 321 participants. Responses that were deemed non-usable or incomplete were excluded before data analysis, as per the defined exclusion criteria (i.e., case-wise exclusion was applied prior to the final cohort selection).

Incentives

To encourage participation, all students were informed that participation was voluntary and confidential. The primary incentive provided was the opportunity to contribute to the development of future educational policies regarding AI integration in their curriculum. Additionally, participants were offered a brief informational session on the study’s preliminary findings and the role of AI in their respective fields upon the study’s completion. No monetary or academic credit incentives were offered.

Data analysis

All collected data were compiled and analyzed using SPSS statistical software (version 26). The significance level was set at p < 0.05.

Statistical justification

The distribution of scores for each of the four components (cognitive readiness, competency, vision, and ethical awareness) was first assessed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov (K–S) test to determine the appropriate statistical approach (parametric vs. non-parametric).

For the cognitive readiness component, the K–S test indicated that the data followed a normal distribution (p 0.05). Consequently, a one-sample t-test was conducted.

For the remaining three components (competency, vision, and ethical awareness), the K–S test revealed a non-normal distribution (p < 0.05). Therefore, the Wilcoxon signed-rank test was applied, which is the non-parametric equivalent suitable for paired or one-sample comparison of non-normally distributed data.

Comparison against the neutral midpoint

Both the t-test and the Wilcoxon signed-rank test were used to compare the component mean/median scores against a fixed value of 3. This value was chosen because it represents the neutral midpoint of the 5-point Likert scale (neither agreement nor disagreement). This comparison was employed to provide an inferential judgment as to whether the students’ overall perception (in terms of knowledge, competency, attitude, or ethics) was statistically significantly positive (mean > 3) or negative (mean < 3) relative to the expected baseline neutrality.Descriptive statistics (means, standard deviations, minimum and maximum values) and the inferential p-values were calculated for each component to assess significant deviations from the midpoint. Descriptive and inferential statistics of ai readiness components

To assess students’ readiness across four core domains cognitive readiness, practical competence, forward-looking vision, and ethical awarenes both descriptive statistics and inferential tests were employed. Based on data distribution examined using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov (K–S) test, a one-sample t-test was applied for the “cognitive readiness” component, while the other three components were analyzed using the nonparametric Wilcoxon signed-rank test. The reference value for comparison was set at a median score of 3 (the neutral point on the Likert scale). Complete results are summarized in Table 2.

To assess students’ readiness across four core domains, statistical analysis revealed that for the cognitive readiness component, a one-sample t-test comparing the mean score with the neutral midpoint of 3 showed no significant difference (p = 0.435). The mean score of 3.033 (SD = 0.754) indicated a neutral and balanced level of conceptual understanding, suggesting that while students possess basic knowledge, their theoretical comprehension of artificial intelligence does not exceed the expected foundational level, highlighting the need for targeted strategies to strengthen fundamental AI literacy. For the practical competence component, the Wilcoxon signed-rank test indicated that students rated themselves above the neutral midpoint (p < 0.001), with a mean score of 3.440 (SD = 0.818), reflecting relative confidence in applying AI and interacting with its tools; this self-assessed competence may benefit from structured, skill-based training to align perceived and actual proficiency. Regarding the forward-looking vision component, the Wilcoxon test also revealed a significant positive deviation from the reference value of 3 (p < 0.001), with a mean score of 3.313 (SD = 0.923), indicating a generally positive outlook and appreciation for the integration of AI into future healthcare; however, the wide score range (1.00–5.00) suggests varying levels of exposure or familiarity with AI, emphasizing the importance of adaptable curricular design. Finally, for the ethical awareness component, the Wilcoxon test confirmed a significant positive deviation from the midpoint (p < 0.001), with a mean score of 3.690 (SD = 0.832), representing the highest level among all components and reflecting a strong ethical orientation toward the use of AI in medicine, underscoring the relevance of digital ethics education and case-based discussions to foster consistent ethical readiness across the student group.

Discussion

Overview of AI readiness and educational shortfalls

This cross-sectional study offers crucial insights into the readiness of future Iranian physicians, dentists, and pharmacists concerning the integration of Artificial Intelligence (AI) in healthcare. Our findings indicate a systemic disparity: students exhibit moderate familiarity with fundamental AI concepts but demonstrate notable weaknesses in understanding specialized processes and practical clinical applications. This pattern reflects an overall limited and unstructured exposure, suggesting insufficient readiness to effectively engage in professional environments increasingly shaped by AI.

This observation is largely consistent with several international studies, such as those by Hamdani et al. (2023) (35) and Lee & Chin (2023) (36), who reported similarly limited AI knowledge and cautious attitudes in their cohorts. Factors contributing to this gap in Iran likely include a lack of formal, centralized AI instruction, insufficient interdisciplinary content bridging foundational sciences with applied technologies, and general resource constraints. The significance of this finding lies in highlighting a systemic shortfall: current medical education frameworks have yet to position AI as a central, future-oriented component, an inadequacy that may hinder the effective implementation of emerging technologies and widen the gap between academic instruction and evolving clinical practice.

Critical reflection on cognitive readiness and competency

Our analysis reveals a critical disconnect between theoretical acceptance and practical capacity. Students’ neutral scores on the Cognitive Readiness component underscore that their theoretical comprehension does not exceed a foundational, self-acquired level. This contrasts with studies like those by Giovanner (2022) (37) and Bisdas (2021) (38) conducted in more resource-rich settings, which documented broader and more informed acceptance, possibly influenced by targeted educational reforms.

Furthermore, while students reported above-average proficiency in routine digital health applications (Competency) and purposeful use of online resources, their analytical skillsspecifically in interpreting algorithmic outputs and applying AI in clinical decision-makingremain underdeveloped. This indicates that competencies are primarily practice-based rather than conceptually grounded. These trends align with Lee & Chin (2023) (36) and Sabet et al. (2023) (39), who found that hands-on experience enhances user competence, but do not necessarily foster the critical thinking needed for complex AI interaction. Conversely, reports of lower practical competence, such as by Beigi et al. (2022) (40) or in low-resource settings like Zambia (Sabavierpandian et al., 2024) (41), may reflect more stringent infrastructural or educational limitations, highlighting the unique variance in readiness across global contexts.

The persistent low score in cognition, juxtaposed with acceptable self-reported competence, provides a crucial critical reflection point: it suggests that current Iranian medical education successfully develops general digital literacy, but critically fails to instill the algorithmic literacy necessary to understand and troubleshoot complex AI systems.

Attitudes and high ethical awareness: the role of cultural context

Students’ attitudes (Vision component) toward AI were generally positive and optimistic regarding potential benefits, aligning with favorable views documented by Beigi et al. (2022) (40) and Santos (2019) (8). This optimism is likely driven by general exposure to digital media and the promise of technological advancement, even in the absence of deep practical experience, as noted by Sabet et al. (2023) (39) and Mirzaei & Askouei (2022) (42).

Crucially, students displayed the highest scores in Ethical Awareness, particularly concerning the legal and moral use of health data. This strong positive finding requires critical reflection within the Iranian context. This high ethical score suggests that students’ readiness is heavily influenced by strong cultural and institutional values that emphasize confidentiality, professional duty, and accountability. This ethical foundation may be stronger than their technical capacity. This finding corroborates previous reports indicating improvements in ethical understanding following targeted education (Sabet et al., 2023; Ali & Moghari, 2024; Laupichler et al., 2024) (39, 43, 44). Nevertheless, lower specific knowledge regarding regulatory compliance and national legal standards indicates a gap between broad ethical principles (e.g., fairness) and concrete knowledge of local digital health legal frameworks.

Limitations

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, the cross-sectional design restricts the ability to draw causal inferences between variables. Second, the reliance on self-reported data introduces the possibility of social desirability and recall biases. Finally, the generalizability of the findings may be limited due to the specific demographic and institutional context of the participants. Future studies should consider longitudinal or mixed-methods designs and include more diverse populations across multiple institutions to enhance external validity.

Practical implications and recommendations

Based on these findings, we propose a series of actionable and strategic steps necessary to better integrate health technologies and artificial intelligence into medical education. A primary priority is the development of structured interdisciplinary educational programs that bring together students from medicine, dentistry, pharmacy, biomedical engineering, and data science to foster collaborative problem-solving skills essential for the future digital health ecosystem. To bridge the gap between theoretical knowledge and practical application, institutions should offer simulation-based workshops and case-based learning sessions that focus on real-world applications of AI and digital health tools, ensuring students gain necessary hands-on experience through interaction with AI-based tools like clinical decision support systems in a controlled learning environment. Furthermore, this requires incorporating either elective or core dedicated academic modules specifically focused on health technology literacy and the ethics of AI applications in clinical decision-making. These curricular changes must be supported by faculty development initiatives aimed at training educators on emerging technologies to ensure they can effectively teach and mentor students. Finally, to maintain program relevance and effectiveness, there must be continuous evaluation and feedback mechanisms established to systematically assess students’ competence and adapt training programs accordingly to the rapidly evolving technological landscape.

Conclusion

This study highlights that while medical students demonstrate foundational preparedness and a generally positive outlook toward AI, their overall readiness to effectively implement AI in clinical, educational, and research settings remains limited. Strengths include practical engagement with basic AI tools, yet significant gaps persist in understanding specialized concepts, analytical skills, and legal-ethical frameworks. These findings underscore the need for focused, structured educational programs to enhance students’ competencies and ensure the safe and effective integration of AI technologies in healthcare and medical education.
