Hydrogeochemical assessment of groundwater for agricultural suitability in the Ksour Mountains, Algeria
Authors: Bellal Hamma, Mohamed Faouzi Bekkouche, Foued Bouaicha, Khaled A Elnagdy, Ali Alzaed, Ayoub Barkat, Antonio Randazzo, Mekonnen Sileshi Andualem, Enas E Hussein
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe Ain-Sefra region, located in south-western Algeria has an arid climate and the groundwater is a vital resource for irrigation and domestic purposes. In the absence of other resources, conventional or otherwise, groundwater remains the driving force behind the region’s development. Recognizing the importance of groundwater in agriculture, this research utilized a combination of water quality indices and GIS techniques to map and evaluate the suitability of groundwater for agricultural purposes. Several physicochemical variables, such as major elements, electrical conductivity (EC), potential hydrogen (pH), temperature (T°), total dissolved solids (TDS) were measured from 43 water samples. Hydrochemical groundwater facies were Ca–Mg–SO4–Cl, Ca–Cl, Ca–Mg–HCO3 and Na–Cl, revealing processes of evaporation, reverse ion exchange and rock–water interaction. Additionally, the results show that the groundwater system is oversaturated with aragonite, calcite, dolomite, anhydrite, and gypsum, while halite is estimated to be undersaturated. A range of indices, such as electrical conductivity (EC), salinity, sodium absorption rate (SAR), sodium percentage (% Na), residual sodium carbonate (RSC), magnesium hazard (MH), Kelly ratio (KR), and permeability index (PI), were used to evaluate the groundwater suitability for irrigation. Although the groundwater is found to be suitable for agricultural use, salinity control measures are essential. Spatial variation maps of water quality indices reveal that groundwater from downstream part is highly mineralized, while low mineralized water observed in the western and southwestern parts.Keywords: Groundwater, Hydrochemical facies, Water–rock interaction, Geographic information systems (GIS)Subject terms: Environmental sciences, Engineering

Introduction

As a vital resource, water is essential for human survival and plays a key role in economic development and environmental preservation. The increasing pressure on natural resources, driven by growing domestic, industrial, and agricultural needs and population growth, poses a major challenge to the long-term sustainability of humanity1–3. Conserving these resources is crucial for guaranteeing the sustainability of natural and socio-economic systems. The increasing scarcity of surface water resources, particularly in arid and semi-arid regions such as Algeria, makes groundwater indispensable for agricultural activities4.

The Ain-Sefra region relies mainly on groundwater for its water supply. Since 2010, a large number of wells and boreholes have been drilled under the national fund for agricultural development and regularization (FNDRA). The number of wells an d boreholes has risen steadily in recent years, reaching over 900 by 2023 (Water Resources Department of Naama). This trend is attributable to the growing demand of water for irrigation, in particular for the creation and extension of irrigated perimeters, as well as for the supply of drinking water to the local population. Indeed, the overexploitation of groundwater, used for various purposes, has led to significant variations in groundwater quality5. Groundwater characteristics, including its suitability for various purposes, are largely influenced by a multitude of interacting factors. These include water–rock interactions, precipitation, weathering and evaporation processes, as well as anthropogenic activities. These elements contribute significantly to shaping the physico-chemical properties of groundwater, thus influencing its quality and availability6.

Assessing the quality of groundwater used for irrigation is essential for ensuring the long-term sustainability of agricultural practices, as water availability for irrigation depends on its qualitative and quantitative aspects7.

Numerous methods are commonly used to assess water suitability for irrigation, based on the analysis of major ion data. Analyzing the chemical composition of groundwater helps identify the key factors influencing its chemistry. Statistical tools like hierarchical cluster analysis, determination of saturation indices, and calculation of common water quality indices are used to characterize groundwater quality and identify spatial and temporal trends8–21.

This research aims to characterize the chemical composition of groundwater within Ain Sefra (South-West Algeria) region using the classic hhydrochemistry diagrams. Hierarchical cluster analysis (R-mode and Q-mode) and the calculation of saturation indices using PHREEQC software will be used to identify factors influencing the chemical composition.

Furthermore, the calculation of hydrogeochemical indices of groundwater quality can contribute to the successful assessment of groundwater suitability for irrigation uses and analyze the spatial patterns of parameters through the combination of remote sensing and Geographic Information Systems (GIS) within the applied methods.

Geological and hydrogeological setting of the study area

The study area is located in south-west Algeria (Fig. 1), positioned approximately 400 km south of the city of Oran and 30 km south-west of Naâma. It spans a total area of around 2700 km2 (UTM coordinates ranging from 681,740 W to 769,145 E and from 3,664,120 N to 3,610,150 S). This area is part of the Western Atlas domain, which is a mountain range located at the western edge of the Saharan Atlas, commonly referred to as the "Ksour Mountains It is bounded to the north by the cities of Naâma and Ain Ben Khelil, to the south by the town of Moghrar, to the east by the town of Boussamghoun in the province of El Bayadh, and to the west by the Algerian-Moroccan border.

Geographic and piezometric map of the study area (This figure was prepared using ArcGIS Desktop 10.8 (https://www.esri.com/en-us/arcgis/products/arcgis-desktop/overview).

The climatic conditions of any region represent one of the most important factors, as they play a contributing role in the natural recharge of groundwater systems, which consequently affects the socio-economic activities of the specific region19. Anyway, the climate of the study area is classified as semi-arid to arid, resulting in irregular precipitation from one year to the next. Winter periods, though short, are harsh and concentrated mainly in December and January. Summers are characterized by warm conditions, with higher temperatures generally prevailing from June to September. The average rainfalls in this region are around 183 mm/year. The average annual temperatures in the region are around 17.5 °C, and the rainy season typically lasts from March to May.

The Ain Sefra catchment is a significant component of the larger Oued Namous basin, which eventually flows into the Erg Occidental desert. The basin is surrounded by mountain ranges on its northern and southern sides, characterized by sedimentary deposits from the Mesozoic era, especially those dating back to the Jurassic period. The basin hosts several significant wadis, including Wadi Breidj, Wadi Sfssifa, Wadi Tirkount, and Wadi Tiout.

Traditionally, agriculture and pastoral activities have formed the primary economic basis of the region, making water resources indispensable for their sustenance. The region’s semi-arid to arid climate and limited rainfall necessitate access to groundwater to ensure the continued viability of agricultural and pastoral practices22,23.

The geology of the Ksour Mountains (Western Saharan Atlas) is characterized by a thick layer of sedimentary debris that spans a lengthy geological period from the Triassic to the recent Quaternary, with a significant emphasis on Mesozoic formations (Fig. 2). Numerous geological and geodynamic studies, including those by Ait Ouali24, Delfault25, Douihasni26, Kacemi27,28, Kazi-Tani29, Mansour 30, Meddah31,32, and Sebane33, have concentrated on the evolution of the Saharan Atlas, particularly during the Mesozoic era in this region. Triassic deposits are primarily comprised of multicolored clays with gypsum and salt, occasionally punctuated by basaltic flows, as witnessed by the Aïn Ouarka diapir34. The Lower and Middle Jurassic are marked by a substantial series of limestones, sandstones, and marls, suggesting marine depositional environments. A change in the sedimentary regime took place during the Upper Jurassic and Lower Cretaceous periods, leading to continental sedimentary deposits. These lithostratigraphic deposits include the Djara, Aissa, Tiloula, and Tiout formations.

Geological map26 (This figure was prepared using ArcGIS Desktop 10.8 (https://www.esri.com/en-us/arcgis/products/arcgis-desktop/overview).

The region’s primary structural features were formed during the Eocene and Oligocene epochs35, shaping the distinctive current morphology. Erosion-resistant Jurassic strata form prominent elongated ridges, while less resistant lower Cretaceous formations occupy broad intermediate synclines. These synclines are often capped by cornices of Cenomanian–Turonian limestone belonging to the Rhoundjaia formation36. The Tertiary period (Mio-Pliocene) is characterized by the deposition of red clays and sandstones, interspersed with gypsiferous levels, indicating a transition to continental environments. The Quaternary is made up of alluvial deposits and dune formations.

Hydrogeological investigations within the study area have revealed the presence of two potential stacked aquifer systems. The first aquifer system is a multi-layered formation located within Upper Jurassic silicoclastic rocks. It outcrops at the base of the Jebels Aissa, Mekter, Hirech, and Morhad mountains. The second, a multi-layered aquifer system housed within Lower Cretaceous detrital formations, is underlain by semi-permeable Neocomian strata, effectively isolating it from the underlying Jurassic aquifer.

Materials and methods

Sample collection and analysis

The hydrogeochemical traits of the Upper Jurassic and Lower Cretaceous aquifers was investigated in the study area. A reconnaissance survey and sampling campaign was conducted in May 2022.

Sampling Strategy

This campaign involved 43, water points (boreholes, wells, and springs) to assess spatial variability, with 6 representing the Upper Jurassic aquifer and 37 representing the Lower Cretaceous aquifer. The sampling locations were chosen to ensure comprehensive coverage of the entire study area (Fig. 1).

Sample Collection and Preservation

Adhering to established protocols37, each water point was purged for 15 min before sample collection to eliminate stagnant water and achieve representative groundwater samples. The samples were then stored in coolers at temperatures below 4 °C and transported to the Algerian Water Authority (ADE) laboratory in Tindouf for analysis.

Physicochemical Analysis

On-site measurements of temperature, pH, and electrical conductivity (EC) were taken using a HACH SL1000 multiparameter instrument. Laboratory analysis followed recommended methods38: (i) major ions, including calcium (Ca2+), magnesium (Mg2+), chlorides (Cl−) and bicarbonates (HCO3−) were analyzed using volumetric methods; (ii) sulfates (SO42−) and nitrates (NO3−) were determined by spectrophotometry (HACH DR6000); (iii) sodium (Na+) and potassium (K+) were measured using a flame photometer (JENWAY PFP7). By assessing the ionic balance, we confirmed the accuracy and consistency of our data. The minimal discrepancy observed (less than 5%) reinforces the reliability of our analytical methods and the quality of the resulting measurements.

Water typology

The Piper, Durov, and Stiff diagrams were used to assess water typology. These diagrams visualize the relative abundance of common ions in water samples, assuming anion and cation sums are equal. Ion concentrations are expressed as percentages.

Piper Diagram

The Piper diagram39 is a ternary plot showing primary anions (Cl−, CO32− + HCO3−, SO42−) in the lower right corner and primary cations (Ca2+, Mg2+, Na+, K+) in the lower left corner. A diamond plot in the center results from projecting data from the two triangles. Sample positions within the diamond indicate hydrochemical facies.

Durov Diagram

The Durov diagram40 is a composite plot with two ternary diagrams: one for primary cations (Ca2+, Mg2+, Na+, K+) on the left and one for primary anions (Cl−, CO32−, HCO3−, SO42−) on top. A central square binary plot shows the projection of the two ternary plots. A pH plot is added to the bottom, and a TDS plot is added to the right side.

Stiff Diagram

The Stiff diagram41 uses four parallel, horizontal axes extending from each side of a vertical zero axis. Anions and cations are plotted on the left and right sides of the vertical axis in meq/L. These shapes are often overlaid on a site map for quick evaluation of all samples on a single page. Major ion concentrations on the left and right sides of the plot centerline determine the shape and size of the graph.

Cluster analysis

Cluster analysis is a valuable tool for exploring relationships within a data set, enabling the identification and classification of objects or variables into distinct groups based on their similarities. Hierarchical clustering is a popular technique in earth sciences, especially for classifying hydrogeochemical data. It involves creating a hierarchy of clusters, starting from individual data points and merging them into larger clusters based on similarity measures. This hierarchical structure provides insights into the underlying patterns and relationships within the data16,42–47. This methodology involves a progressive consolidation of objects or groups of objects, starting with those with the highest level of similarity, and continuing iteratively until all objects are included in a hierarchical framework48. There are two primary modes of hierarchical analysis: R-mode and Q-mode49. Classification of samples according to their parameters is known as Q-mode classification, and classification according to their individuals is known as R-mode CAH.

The usual visual representation of agglomerative hierarchical clustering is the dendrogram. It should be stressed that the resulting cluster structure depends on both the similarity measure used and the linking method applied during the analysis. This underlines the importance of careful selection of these parameters.

In this study, hierarchical Q-mode and R-mode analyses were employed to categorize samples into distinct hydrochemical clusters. Ward’s agglomeration method was used to merge the initial clusters50.

Spatial analysis

The results of the analysis were used to create maps illustrating the spatial variation of different irrigation indices. These maps were generated using the inverse distance-weighted (IDW) interpolation tool in ArcGIS 10.8. To achieve this, the coordinates of each water point were measured in the field using a GPS device and then imported into a GIS to plot the well location map. The IDW method was subsequently employed to visualize the spatial distribution of indices like SAR, Na%, RSC, MH, KR, and PI.

Examination of the chemical parameters measured in the groundwater samples (Table 1) shows that the water temperature ranges from 17.9 to 46.0 °C. The pH values show a weakly alkaline profile, with an average between 6.7 and 8.5. EC stages from 500 to 6380 µS/cm.

Cation analysis of the groundwater samples found out the following dominance pattern: Na+ > Ca2+ > Mg2+ > K+. Calcium concentrations exhibited a huge range, spanning from 42.87 to 467 mg/l. In particular, 63% of the analyzed samples surpassed the World Health Organization’s recommended limit of 100 mg/l for calcium in drinking water58. Similarly, magnesium concentrations fell between 21 and 179 mg/L, with a mean value of 69.88 mg/L. Concerningly, 63% of the samples exceeded the desirable magnesium limit of 50 mg/L established by the WHO58. Sodium levels demonstrated an even greater range, varying from 16.9 to 1269 mg/L, with 56% of samples exceeding the recommended limit of 200 mg/L58. Potassium concentrations were comparatively lower, spanning from 1 to 42 mg/L, with the highest values observed in regions characterized by intensive agricultural practices.

Anion abundance within the groundwater samples followed the order SO42− > Cl− > HCO3−. Chloride concentrations exceeded the desirable limit of 250 mg/l58 in nearly 39% of the samples. Sulfate levels displayed significant heterogeneity, ranging from to 1465 mg/l, with 32% of samples surpassing the recommended limit of 250 mg/l. Bicarbonate concentrations also surpassed health recommendations outlined by the WHO58.

Assessment of hydro-geochemical facies

The overall characterization of groundwater consists in determining the hydrochemical facies, i.e. the different types of water, on the basis of ionic compositions and using different projections.

Piper trilinear diagram

Projection of major element concentrations on the Piper diagram (Fig. 3) provides valuable insights into the hydrochemical facies and the geochemical processes influencing the groundwater system. The analysis reveals that the majority of samples (63%) fall within the Ca–Mg–SO4–Cl field, indicative of extensive dissolution of evaporitic formations such as gypsum and anhydrite. This process releases significant amounts of sulfate, chloride, calcium, and magnesium ions into the groundwater, shaping its chemical signature and highlighting the dominant mineral dissolution mechanisms in the aquifer.

Piper diagram of analyzed samples.

Additionally, 21% of the samples are classified as Ca–Cl type waters, which are typically associated with the dissolution of chloride-rich minerals or the interaction with halite (NaCl) deposits. The presence of these waters suggests localized zones of halite dissolution or mixing with saline units. Furthermore, 12% of the samples belong to the Ca–Mg–HCO3 hydrochemical type, often linked to less saline conditions and more recent recharge areas where carbonate dissolution prevails, contributing bicarbonate ions to the groundwater.

A smaller proportion, 5%, of samples are of the Na–Cl type, generally related to the dissolution of gypsiferous formations, particularly calcium sulfate (CaSO4). This indicates areas where sulfate minerals are actively contributing to groundwater chemistry, reflecting ongoing mineral interactions.

Spatial distribution patterns reveal that groundwater samples from higher elevations are predominantly characterized by elevated Ca2+ and Mg2+ concentrations. This composition suggests the occurrence of a reverse ion exchange process, where sodium ions (Na+) in the groundwater displace calcium ions from the aquifer matrix. Such a process typically indicates fresh recharge waters interacting with mineral-rich formations at higher altitudes.

In contrast, lower elevation areas, which are characterized by slower groundwater flow and prolonged rock-water interactions, display elevated levels of Na+ and Cl− ions. These chemical signatures are typical of discharge zones where mineralized waters have accumulated over time, resulting in saline conditions. The dominance of these ions in lowland areas reflects the cumulative effects of dissolution, ion exchange, and evaporation processes that influence the water composition downstream.

Overall, the Piper diagram projections and the associated hydrochemical interpretations underscore the complex interplay of mineral dissolution, ion exchange, and hydrological flow regimes that collectively shape the geochemical evolution of the groundwater system across different terrains. This integrated understanding is crucial for managing water quality, assessing aquifer sustainability, and predicting future hydrochemical changes within the study area.

Origin of water mineralization

Hierarchical analysis in R mode

Cluster analysis, a statistical technique for identifying homogeneous groups within a data set, is widely used in scientific research to study relationships between variables. In the field of hydrochemistry, cluster analysis in R mode has proved particularly useful for characterizing water quality and identifying the sources and processes responsible for its chemical composition.

To investigate the relationships between different physicochemical parameters (Ca2+, Mg2+, Na+, K+, Cl−, SO42−, HCO3−, NO3−, EC, and T °C) of groundwater, R-mode cluster analysis was utilized. Ward’s method was employed for classification, and Pearson’s correlation coefficient was used as the similarity measure between variables.

Cluster analysis revealed four distinct groups (Fig. 9), each representing different hydrochemical and geochemical signatures within the groundwater system, offering valuable insights into the dominant processes and sources influencing water composition across the study region.

R-mode cluster.

The first group comprises bicarbonates, which are primarily associated with waters originating from carbonate formations. These bicarbonate-rich waters often result from the dissolution of carbonate minerals such as calcite and dolomite, indicative of areas where mineral–water interactions are dominated by carbonate weathering. The presence of high bicarbonate concentrations suggests active carbonate mineral dissolution, which plays a significant role in buffering pH levels and influencing the water’s overall chemical characteristics.

The second group includes calcium, electrical conductivity (EC), magnesium, and sulfates. These parameters reflect the influence of evaporitic formations, particularly gypsum and anhydrite, which are prevalent in the region. The high concentrations of these ions underscore their primary participation in groundwater mineralization, driven largely by water–rock interactions with salt deposits and evaporite layers. Elevated EC values in this group point to increased total dissolved solids (TDS), often associated with evaporation and concentration processes, highlighting the significant contribution of evaporitic minerals to the hydrochemical evolution of groundwater in the area.

The third group encompasses sodium, chloride, potassium, and temperature variables. These ions are characteristic of waters leached from gypsum-salt Triassic soils, where the high solubility of salts results in elevated concentrations. The high sodium and chloride levels reflect dissolution of halite and other soluble salts within the soil profile, with temperature influences potentially affecting mineral solubility and reaction rates. This group’s chemical profile indicates zones where salt leaching is prevalent, often linked to specific geological formations and climatic conditions that favor salt dissolution and transport.

The fourth group is distinguished by the presence of nitrates, which are dissociated from other chemical elements in the groundwater. The occurrence of nitrates in this group highlights the predominant influence of anthropogenic activities, such as agricultural practices, wastewater infiltration, and surface pollution. Elevated nitrate concentrations are often associated with human interventions and land use patterns, serving as indicators of potential contamination sources and posing concerns regarding water quality and health risks.

In summary, this cluster analysis effectively delineates the complex interactions between natural geological processes and anthropogenic impacts shaping the hydrochemical landscape. Recognizing these distinct groups enhances our understanding of the regional hydrogeochemical dynamics, aids in identifying zones of mineralization, and informs sustainable groundwater management strategies by distinguishing between natural mineralization processes and human-induced contamination sources.

Main hydrochemical processes

The interaction of groundwater with reservoir rocks naturally modifies the chemical composition of water. In order to specify the likely origin of each major component contributing to groundwater mineralization, we need to examine the relationships between the various major ions present. By analyzing the behavior of elements that have a common origin in the groundwater system, we can trace their pathways and understand the processes that contribute to overall chemistry. These processes generally include dissolution, weathering or ion exchange61.

Weathering and dissolution

The Ca2+/Mg2+ ratio in groundwater provides valuable information regarding the origin of these ions. According to Mayo and Loucks (1995)62, a Ca2+/Mg2+ ratio ≤ 1 indicates dissolution of dolomite, while a Ca2+/Mg2+ ratio ≥ 1 suggests dissolution of calcite. When this ratio exceeds 2, it is generally attributed to the dissolution of silicate minerals. Analysis of groundwater in the Ain Sefra region reveals that the majority of samples (51%) exhibit a Ca2+/Mg2+ ratio between 1 and 2, suggesting a predominant dissolution of calcite, with a secondary contribution (49%) from silicate minerals (Fig. 10). This observation suggests that the calcium present in these groundwaters could originate from two primary sources: the dissolution of calcite and the dissolution of silicate minerals.

Conversely, 39.50% of the samples exhibit an excess of calcium compared to sulfate. Ion exchange

Base exchange, a process where ions in groundwater are exchanged with those in rock significantly influences the evolution of groundwater chemistry in the study area. This process can occur during recharge or during extended periods of groundwater residence in the aquifer. The Chloro–Alkali Indices 1 and 2 (CAI.1 and CAI.2) and the [(Na+ + K+)–Cl−] versus [(Ca2+ + Mg2+)–(HCO3− + SO42−)] diagram can be used to evaluate ion exchange. According to Schoeller (1956), positive CAI.1 and CAI.2 values indicate an enrichment of Ca2+ or Mg2+ ions in the groundwater and a release of Na+ or K+ ions from the rock. Conversely, negative values suggest an exchange of Ca2+ or Mg2+ ions from the groundwater for Na+ or K+ ions from the rock.

Evaluation of groundwater quality for irrigation

In arid and semi-arid regions, the supply of irrigation water is one of the determining factors in agricultural production, both in terms of crop intensification and the extension of irrigated areas. In arid regions in particular, where water resources are limited or non-existent, groundwater is used. Today, agricultural development in these regions is faced not only with water scarcity, but also with new emerging challenges such as the growing threat of soil salinization, which can be assessed by electrical conductivity, and soil alkalinization. This situation stems from ionic interactions, involving sodium, calcium and magnesium, between irrigation water and soil. The accumulation of water–soluble salts in the soil root zone has negative repercussions on plant development. These salts impair soil permeability and aeration, as well as plant metabolism and osmotic processes. In order to assess the risk of soil salinization in the Ain Sefra region, a study of water suitability for irrigation is required, as this region is characterized by low rainfall, high evaporation and groundwater loaded with chlorides and sulfates. Many parameters are generally used to assess the suitability of water for irrigation, including: sodium absorption ratio (SAR), sodium percentage (%Na), residual sodium carbonate (RSC), magnesium hazard (MH), Kelly’s ratio (KR) and permeability index (PI)67. These parameters were used to assess water quality for irrigation in our study region.

Residual sodium carbonate (RSC)

High concentrations of carbonates and bicarbonates in irrigation water can contribute to the precipitation of calcium and magnesium, leading to an increase in sodium levels55. Moreover, elevated RSC values can adversely affect soil physical properties by promoting organic matter decomposition72,73. Groundwater is categorized into three classes based on RSC: unsuitable for irrigation (RSC > 2.5), adequate for irrigation (RSC < 1.25), and questionable for irrigation (RSC between 1.25 and 2.5)55. In this study, all groundwater samples exhibited RSC values below 1.25, indicating their suitability for irrigation (Table 4).

Magnesium hazard (MH)

The equilibrium between magnesium and calcium concentrations is a key factor in determining groundwater quality. Increasing magnesium concentration has a more detrimental effect on crop yields than calcium, as it causes soil alkalinization. Consequently, it is extremely important to quantify magnesium, an operation carried out by means of the magnesium hazard (MH) calculation74. The results obtained from this calculation were divided into two categories, namely values considered suitable and those deemed unsuitable. Values below 50 were classified as suitable, while those above this threshold were considered unsuitable. Analysis of the MH values (Table 4) shows that 74% of groundwater samples are with no magnesium hazard. However, 26% of groundwater samples were unsuitable for irrigation purposes.

Kelly’s ratio (KR)

Potassium Ratio (KR) is another important factor for evaluating groundwater quality for irrigation, especially considering sodium content. It has only two simple classifications: suitable and unsuitable. Water with a KR value less than 1 (KR < 1) is considered suitable for irrigation, while water with a KR value greater than 1 (KR > 1) is unsuitable56. Table 4 shows that 95% of the groundwater samples in the study area are suitable for irrigation, while 5% are unsuitable.

Permeability index (PI)

Another index for assessing waters for irrigation utilization is the permeability index (PI), which evaluates the effect of sodium, calcium, magnesium and bicarbonate on soil permeability due to long-term irrigation water57,75. He Permeability Index (PI) classifies groundwater into three categories: Class I (> 75%), Class II (25–75%), and Class III (< 25%). Water in Classes I and II is deemed suitable for irrigation, whereas Class III water is considered unsuitable. Our results indicate that all samples fall within Class II, signifying that the water is suitable for irrigation.

Conclusions

This study characterized the chemical quality of groundwater in the Ain-Sefra region, providing a basis for evaluating its suitability for irrigation. The study findings indicate a general increase in total mineralization of groundwater from upstream to downstream within the study area, following the flow direction (W–E). This trend suggests that the residence time of groundwater in the aquifer plays a significant role in mineral dissolution, leading to a gradual increase in salinity. Hydrochemical analysis using the Piper classification reveals that the dominant facies in the study area is Ca–Mg–SO4–Cl (62.80%), followed by Ca–Cl (20.90%), Ca–Mg–HCO3 (11.60%), and Na–Cl (4.70%). Comparing the groundwater types on the Durov diagram suggests that the variation in hydrogeochemistry within the study area is primarily attributed to simple mineral dissolution or water mixing processes. The chloroalkaline indices (CAI.1 and CAI.2) suggest a direct ion exchange between groundwater and the aquifer during its residence or movement. Analysis of characteristic ratios reveals that rock weathering is the primary factor influencing groundwater quality, with secondary contributions from evaporation and ion exchange. An assessment of saturation indices revealed that groundwater is supersaturated with respect to most mineral phases, primarily carbonates and evaporites. In contrast, the groundwater is undersaturated in relation to halite.

The irrigation indices (SAR, PI, KR, MH, Na%, and RSC) revealed that the groundwater was of excellent to moderate quality for irrigation, but salinity needs to be controlled. Spatial analysis of suitability indicators using the Inverse Distance Weighted (IDW) method enhanced our understanding of the spatial variability of these parameters. The resulting maps highlight high groundwater mineralization in the downstream section of the study area, while areas with low mineralization are situated in the west and southwest.

Based on the findings of this study, there are several recommendations that can be made to help improve the quality of groundwater in the Ain Sefra region. One of the main recommendations is to implement measures to control anthropogenic activities such as over-extraction of groundwater, improper waste disposal, and the use of fertilizers and pesticides in agricultural practices. This can help reduce the impact of human activities on the groundwater system, which can lead to contamination and degradation of water quality.

On the other hand, agricultural activities exert considerable pressure on the quality and quantity of available water resources. This situation calls for a study of underground resources in this region, which belongs to the arid climatic zone, characterized by irregular and scarce rainfall.

In addition, there is a need for continued monitoring and management of groundwater resources in the region to ensure that they are being used sustainably and that their quality is being maintained. This can be achieved through regular water quality testing, proper management of water extraction rates, and the implementation of best management practices in agricultural and other land use practices.

Ultimately, the study highlights the crucial need for future groundwater resource management in this region to consider salinity variations to ensure rational and sustainable utilization of this valuable resource. To optimize this management, it is recommended to integrate remote sensing and geographic information system (GIS) tools. These tools will enable a better understanding of spatiotemporal changes in terms of qualitative and quantitative aspects of groundwater.

Acknowledging the limitations of the Inverse Distance-Weighted (IDW) method ensures that our analysis remains transparent and paves the way for more robust future research. By considering advanced geostatistical methods such as kriging, future studies can achieve a more refined understanding of spatial variability. Additionally, we plan to increase the number of sample points and incorporate temporal variability in future studies to enhance the robustness and accuracy of our findings, incorporating these methods can enhance the accuracy of data interpretations and offer greater insights into the underlying processes.

Looking forward, future research in this area could focus on investigating the impact of climate change on groundwater resources in this region, as well as the implemention of innovative solutions such as artificial recharge and desalination to increase the availability of water resources in the region.
