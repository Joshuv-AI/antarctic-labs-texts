A benchmark dataset and interpretable deep learning framework for drug-induced developmental neurotoxicity prediction
Authors: Hongting Ma, Wenhui Zhang, Fengxi Liu, Rong Ni, Xue Wang, Yanying Sun, Xuelin Sun, Xiao Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Developmental neurotoxicity (DNT) represents a critical yet underevaluated toxicity endpoint within current chemical safety assessment frameworks, particularly in the context of drug development. In the present study, we constructed a standardized benchmark dataset comprising 2724 structurally curated compounds, including 1362 DNT-positive chemicals derived from the Developmental Neurotox Reference List (DNTREF) of the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) and 1362 property-matched presumed negative compounds (non-neuroactive drug-like compounds) selected from the ChEMBL database. Using this benchmark dataset, we systematically evaluated seven representative quantitative structure activity relationship (QSAR) modeling paradigms, covering traditional machine learning, deep neural networks, molecular language models, and graph neural networks. Across all models, the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) values ranged from 0.85 to 0.90 on an independent test set. Among these, a deep neural network based on MACCS fingerprints achieved the highest sensitivity in identifying DNT-positive compounds, supporting its utility in early-stage drug safety screening where minimizing false negatives is critical. Furthermore, SHAP-based interpretability analysis revealed key structural motifs associated with DNT, highlighting the roles of hydrophobic aromatic frameworks, electrophilic reactivity, and polar functional groups in modulating predicted neurodevelopmental risk. Collectively, this study provides a reproducible benchmark dataset and an interpretable QSAR framework that supports early DNT risk prioritization and offers actionable structural insights for medicinal chemistry optimization in drug discovery.

Introduction

Chemical developmental neurotoxicity (DNT) refers to the adverse effects that exogenous chemicals exert on central nervous system development following exposure during embryonic, perinatal, or early childhood stages.1,2 Such exposure can result in long-term, often irreversible neurobehavioral impairments, including cognitive deficits, learning disorders, behavioral abnormalities, and emotional dysregulation.1 Accumulating epidemiological and experimental evidence has linked prenatal and early-life exposure to pesticides, industrial chemicals, pharmaceuticals, and environmental pollutants with an increased risk of neurodevelopmental disorders, including autism spectrum disorder, attention deficit hyperactivity disorder, and intellectual disability.3–5 Given the highly orchestrated and vulnerable nature of neurodevelopment, even low-dose or short-term chemical exposure can cause persistent functional damage. Consequently, DNT has emerged as a critical toxicity endpoint of substantial public health relevance, thus it remains underevaluated in both chemical risk assessment and drug regulatory frameworks.6

Nowadays, the assessment of chemical DNT primarily depends on rodent developmental toxicity studies (OECD Guideline 426), neurobehavioral assays, and in vitro cellular models.7,8 These experimental approaches are generally time-consuming, costly, low-throughput, and subject to ethical constraints, making them ill-suited for large-scale safety screening of environmental chemicals and drug candidates. In order to address these limitations, the traditional quantitative structure–activity relationship (QSAR) models based on machine learning (ML) have been widely used in chemical toxicity screening.9–12 More recently, advanced algorithms, including deep neural networks and graph neural networks, have demonstrated promising performance in modeling a range of toxicological endpoints.13–17 These methods can automatically extract informative features from chemical structures, molecular descriptors, and high-throughput screening data, and have shown satisfactory predictive capability for endpoints with relatively abundant data, such as acute toxicity, cardiotoxicity, and respiratory toxicity.18–23

In the context of chemical DNT prediction, several computational modeling efforts have been reported in recent years. For instance, Gadaleta et al. explored a mechanism-informed neurotoxicity prediction framework by modeling the molecular initiating events from the perspective of the adverse outcome pathway (AOP).24 Zhang et al. constructed a DNT prediction model based on a conventional QSAR ensemble strategy and systematically analyzed the key structural features, showing the potential value of structural fragment-based modeling in DNT prediction.25 Despite these advances, chemical DNT remains comparatively underrepresented in current computational toxicology research. Existing DNT-related studies are often based on datasets with limited sample sizes, heterogeneous sources, and inconsistent labeling criteria, which can compromise model stability and hinder objective performance comparison across different algorithms. The lack of standardized and reproducible benchmark datasets therefore represents a major methodological bottleneck in this field. Moreover, from a drug development perspective, the evaluation of DNT is particularly critical for candidate compounds intended for use in pregnant women, children, or conditions requiring chronic central nervous system exposure, yet few computational models have been specifically developed or validated with drug-like chemical spaces in mind.

In response to these limitations, the present study develops a systematic computational framework for DNT prediction of chemicals. A systematically constructed, structurally curated, and reproducible DNT benchmark dataset with standardized data partitioning strategies is constructed to support objective model evaluation. Using this benchmark, multiple representative modeling paradigms, including traditional machine learning, deep neural networks, and graph neural networks, are systematically assessed for their applicability and predictive performance in DNT classification. In addition, SHAP-based interpretability analysis is employed to identify chemical structural features associated with DNT and to enhance the transparency of model predictions. This framework is designed to provide not only robust predictive performance but also actionable structural insights for medicinal chemistry optimization in early-stage drug discovery.

Materials and methods

Benchmark dataset preparation

In order to support the development and systematic evaluation of developmental neurotoxicity prediction models, a standardized and fully reproducible benchmark compound dataset was constructed. All data collection, structural preprocessing, and sample selection procedures were implemented through a unified chemical normalization pipeline (Fig. S1) to minimize the influence of labeling noise and structural bias on model training and evaluation.

Preprocessing of positive samples

Positive compounds were obtained from the Developmental Neurotox Reference List (DNTREF) released by the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency (U.S. EPA) through the CompTox Chemicals Dashboard.26 This reference list comprises chemicals supported by experimental or epidemiological evidence of developmental neurotoxicity. The original dataset contained 1482 entries.

All positive compounds underwent standardized structural curation. Records lacking valid SMILES representations were first removed. Molecular structures were parsed using RDKit (https://www.rdkit.org/), and entries that could not be successfully processed were excluded. Inorganic substances without carbon atoms were subsequently discarded. For multi-component entries, salt stripping and standardization were applied by retaining only the organic parent structure with the largest number of heavy atoms, whereas true mixtures containing multiple organic components of comparable size were excluded. Finally, structures were normalized and deduplicated. After these procedures, 1362 unique organic compounds were retained as DNT-positive samples.

Preprocessing of presumed negative samples and construction of a balanced benchmark dataset

The presumed negative samples were collected from ChEMBL (version 36, https://www.ebi.ac.uk/chembl/). To reduce the likelihood of inadvertently including neuroactive compounds, only chemicals with Anatomical Therapeutic Chemical (ATC) codes outside the nervous system category (ATC codes beginning with “N”). Entries without valid canonical SMILES were excluded. All negative candidates were subjected to the same structural normalization procedures applied to the positive set. The compounds with exact duplicate molecular structures matching those in the DNT-positive set were removed, resulting in 2250 candidate non-neuroactive presumed negative compounds.

A hard-negative matching strategy was employed to mitigate physicochemical bias associated with differences in molecular weight (MW) and lipophilicity (log P) when constructing the balanced dataset. MW and log P were selected as matching criteria because they critically influence blood–brain barrier permeability, placental transfer, and overall pharmacokinetic behavior, thereby helping to minimize exposure-related bias and focus on structure–neurotoxicity relationships. The MW and Crippen-calculated log P values were computed for all compounds. In the two-dimensional MW–log P space, one presumed negative compound was selected for each positive compound using a greedy nearest-neighbor matching approach without replacement. This procedure yielded a balanced benchmark dataset consisting of 1362 DNT-positive and 1362 hard-matched presumed DNT-negative compounds. Detailed data processing procedures are provided in the SI.

Data partitioning of the benchmark dataset

The final benchmark dataset comprised 2724 compounds represented by canonical SMILES and annotated with binary DNT labels. Comparison of MW (Fig. 1a) and log P (Fig. 1b) distributions between positive and presumed negative samples confirmed substantial overlap in key physicochemical property spaces. Additionally, the Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) based on MACCS fingerprints were used to visualize chemical space coverage (Fig. 1c), indicating that the dataset is suitable for model benchmarking with minimal structural bias.27 To further evaluate potential blood–brain barrier (BBB)-related physicochemical bias remaining after MW and log P matching, three additional descriptors associated with CNS permeability, including topological polar surface area (TPSA), hydrogen bond acceptor count (HBA), and hydrogen bond donor count (HBD), were analyzed for positive and negative compounds (Fig. S2). Shapiro–Wilk tests indicated that all descriptor distributions deviated from normality (all p < 0.001). Therefore, nonparametric Mann–Whitney U tests were performed. Statistically significant differences were observed between the two classes for TPSA, HBA, and HBD (all p < 0.001), with DNT-positive compounds generally exhibiting lower polarity-related descriptor values. However, Cliff's delta effect size analysis indicated only small effect magnitudes for TPSA (δ = −0.254), HBA (δ = −0.218), and HBD (δ = −0.294). These findings suggest that although residual BBB-related physicochemical differences remain after MW and log P matching, the overall imbalance is limited in magnitude and is unlikely to fully account for the observed predictive performance.

A three-stage data partitioning strategy was implemented to ensure objective performance assessment and reliable estimation of model generalizability. First, 20% of the complete dataset was randomly sampled as an independent test set. This subset was entirely excluded from model training, hyperparameter optimization, and model selection, and was reserved solely for final independent performance evaluation. Stratified sampling was applied to maintain a 1 : 1 ratio between positive and negative samples. The remaining 80% of the dataset was further randomly divided into a training set and a validation set at a ratio of 90 : 10. The training set was used for model training, whereas the validation set served for model selection, hyperparameter tuning, and preliminary performance assessment. Stratified sampling was also applied to preserve class balance. The final distributions of the training, validation, and independent test sets are summarized in Table 1.

To further evaluate model robustness under structure-aware conditions, a cluster-aware partitioning strategy was applied. MACCS fingerprints were generated for all compounds, followed by independent K-means clustering of positive and negative samples. Within each cluster, compounds were ranked by their distance to the cluster centroid and sequentially assigned to the training, validation, and independent test sets. This approach increased structural heterogeneity between subsets while preserving representative chemical space coverage and balanced class distributions.

Chemical space visualization and applicability domain analysis

Chemical space visualization and similarity-based applicability domain (AD) analysis were performed to characterize the structural coverage of the training, validation, and independent test sets and to delineate the domain in which model predictions are considered reliable. MACCS structural fingerprints were used as the primary molecular representation for similarity analysis. MACCS fingerprints are substructure key-based molecular descriptors derived from predefined fragment rules. Each compound is encoded as a 166 bit binary vector indicating the presence or absence of specific structural motifs and functional group patterns. Owing to their clear structural semantics and moderate dimensionality, MACCS fingerprints have been widely adopted for chemical similarity analysis and safety-related modeling tasks.28–30

MACCS fingerprints for all compounds were generated using RDKit, and pairwise structural similarities were quantified using the Tanimoto coefficient to construct a similarity matrix. Dimensionality reduction was subsequently conducted using UMAP and t-distributed Stochastic Neighbor Embedding (t-SNE) to embed the high-dimensional similarity space into two dimensions.31 The training, validation, and independent test sets were jointly projected into a unified low-dimensional space, enabling visual inspection of chemical space overlap and structural coverage among different data subsets.

The explicit AD classification rules were further defined based on MACCS–Tanimoto similarity thresholds.32 Threshold values of 0.4, 0.6, and 0.8 were evaluated to represent increasingly stringent similarity constraints. For each compound in the independent test set, the maximum Tanimoto similarity to any compound in the training set was calculated. Compounds with a maximum similarity exceeding a given threshold were classified as within the applicability domain, whereas those below the threshold were considered out of domain. This hierarchical thresholding strategy enabled a systematic assessment of model generalization stability and prediction reliability under varying degrees of structural similarity constraints. From a drug screening perspective, AD analysis provides critical guidance on whether a new chemical entity falls within the reliable prediction space of the model, thereby supporting informed decision-making in early-stage safety assessment.

Model construction and training strategy

Seven representative modeling paradigms were constructed and evaluated to systematically assess their applicability and predictive performance for DNT classification of chemicals. These paradigms encompassed traditional machine learning models, deep learning models based on molecular fingerprints and molecular language representations, and graph neural network models. All the model methods were implemented using Python 3.11.7.

Machine learning baseline models

Baseline machine learning models were developed using Support Vector Machine (SVM) and Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost) algorithms. SVM is a supervised learning method grounded in the principle of structural risk minimization and constructs a maximum-margin separating hyperplane in high-dimensional feature space, providing robust generalization for small-sample and high-dimensional datasets.33 XGBoost is an ensemble learning algorithm based on gradient boosting decision trees that iteratively fits residual errors to generate a strong classifier and has demonstrated strong performance in diverse QSAR modeling tasks.34 MACCS fingerprints were employed as input features for both SVM and XGBoost models to establish baseline performance for subsequent comparative analyses.

Deep learning models based on molecular fingerprints and transformer pre-training model

Within the deep learning framework, two types of fully connected deep neural network (DNN) models were constructed using MACCS fingerprints and molecular language representations as input features. DNNs learn hierarchical representations of input features through multiple layers of nonlinear transformations, enabling the modeling of complex structure–toxicity relationships.35 An initial DNN model based on MACCS fingerprints was developed to examine the potential performance gains achieved by deep learning over traditional fingerprint-based approaches.

In addition, molecular language representations were incorporated to capture higher-order semantic information encoded in SMILES strings. ChemBERTa, a Transformer-based pre-trained molecular language model, was employed to generate molecular embeddings via self-supervised learning on large-scale SMILES corpora. The ChemBERTa-zinc-base-v1 model, pre-trained on approximately 100 000 SMILES sequences from the ZINC database, was used in this study.36 The hidden-layer outputs provide 768-dimensional continuous molecular embedding vectors, which were subsequently used as input features for DNN-based DNT classification.

Graph neural network models

Three representative graph-based deep learning architectures, including GraphSAGE, AttentiveFP, and ChemProp, were employed to construct end-to-end molecular graph prediction models. GraphSAGE is an inductive GNN that updates node representations through neighborhood sampling and aggregation, facilitating efficient learning of node- and graph-level embeddings.37 In molecular graph modeling, GraphSAGE propagates atomic features across bonds through multilayer message passing to derive molecular representations. AttentiveFP introduces an attention mechanism that assigns adaptive weights to neighboring atoms, enabling the learning of more discriminative molecular representations and has shown robust performance in various molecular property and toxicity prediction tasks.38 ChemProp v2.2.3, a directed message passing neural network (D-MPNN) framework widely adopted in molecular property prediction, was incorporated as a representative modern message-passing architecture.39 ChemProp utilizes directed bond-centered message propagation to capture molecular structural information and has shown strong predictive performance across diverse pharmaceutical and toxicological applications. For all graph-based models, molecular structures were represented as atom-bond graphs, and molecular representations were learned automatically through message passing and aggregation without reliance on predefined molecular fingerprints.40,41

Model training and evaluation

Model hyperparameters were optimized using five-fold cross-validation on the training set. Preliminary model selection and performance assessment were conducted on the validation set. For deep learning models, an early stopping strategy was implemented by monitoring validation performance to prevent overfitting, and the optimal number of training epochs was determined based on validation set performance. The corresponding model weights were retained. The finalized models were subsequently evaluated on the independent test set to assess their predictive performance under previously unseen conditions. Detailed network architectures, hyperparameter settings, and training procedures for all models are provided in the SI.

Performance metrics and statistical analysis

A multi-metric evaluation framework was employed to comprehensively assess the classification performance and stability of the developed models for developmental neurotoxicity prediction. All performance evaluations were based on predictions obtained from the validation set and the independent test set.

Primary evaluation metrics included the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC), accuracy, recall, precision, F1-score, and Matthew's correlation coefficient (MCC). AUC was used to quantify the overall discriminative ability of the models across classification thresholds. MCC, which integrates true positives, true negatives, false positives, and false negatives, was adopted as a robust indicator of overall classification performance and is considered stable under both balanced and imbalanced class distributions. Their definitions and formulas are as follows:12345Here, TP represents true positives (predicted toxic and actually toxic), TN represents true negatives (predicted non-toxic and actually non-toxic), FP represents false positives (predicted toxic but actually non-toxic), and FN represents false negatives (predicted non-toxic but actually toxic).

Model interpretability analysis

Model interpretability analysis was performed to elucidate the structural factors driving model predictions and to enhance the transparency of the final classification model. The model that achieved the best performance on the independent test set was selected for interpretation using SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP), a game-theoretic feature attribution method that provides consistent and locally accurate explanations for complex machine learning models.42 SHAP is based on Shapley values from cooperative game theory and quantifies the contribution of each input feature to an individual prediction by estimating its marginal contribution across all possible feature combinations. This framework enables decomposition of model outputs into additive feature attributions, allowing both global and local interpretation of prediction behavior while maintaining theoretical consistency and model-agnostic applicability.

Global interpretation

Global SHAP analysis was conducted to quantify the overall importance of molecular features driving model predictions. For each feature, the SHAP value represents the direction and magnitude of its contribution to the predicted probability of developmental neurotoxicity. By averaging SHAP values across all compounds in the independent test set, globally important structural features and their general effect trends on DNT prediction were identified. This global interpretation provides insight into dominant structural motifs associated with increased or decreased predicted DNT risk of compounds and facilitates qualitative mapping of model behavior to known toxicological mechanisms.

Local interpretation

Local SHAP analysis was used to interpret individual compound predictions by quantifying feature-level contributions for each sample in the independent test set. For each compound, SHAP values were calculated to identify the key structural features driving its classification as DNT-positive or presumed DNT-negative. This compound-specific attribution supports mechanistic interpretation of predictions and enables qualitative analysis of structure–toxicity relationships at the molecular level.

All SHAP analyses were implemented based on the SHAP library in Python 3.11.7, using the model's predicted probability outputs as the input. Both global and local analysis results were utilized for result visualization and structural feature interpretation, supporting the scientific rationality and auditability of the model's predictions.

Results and discussion

Analysis of chemical space distribution and applicability domains

UMAP and t-SNE projections based on MACCS molecular fingerprints were employed to visualize the distributions of the training, validation, and independent test sets in chemical space. As illustrated in Fig. 2, both the validation, and independent test sets exhibited structural distributions highly consistent with that of the training set. Substantial overlap among the three subsets was observed, and no distinct clustering or separation patterns were evident. These results indicate that the randomly generated data partitions preserve comparable structural diversity and that the independent test set adequately represents the primary chemical space spanned by the training data. Accordingly, the data partitioning strategy did not introduce discernible systematic chemical bias.

We further evaluated the approximate structural coverage of the validation set and the independent test set using MACCS fingerprint-based Tanimoto similarity analysis (Fig. 2). Under a relatively stringent similarity threshold of 0.8, 46.3% and 47.3% of compounds in the validation set and independent test set, respectively, exhibited high structural similarity to compounds in the training set. When the threshold was reduced to 0.6, the proportion of compounds showing moderate structural similarity increased substantially to 96.8% and 95.1%, respectively. At a threshold of 0.4, all compounds in both subsets showed at least coarse structural similarity to compounds contained in the training data. Compounds falling outside the applicability domain under stricter similarity thresholds may be associated with reduced prediction reliability because these molecules are structurally less represented within the training chemical space. Under the stringent Tanimoto similarity threshold of 0.8, approximately 53–54% of compounds in the evaluation subsets were considered outside the applicability domain, suggesting that prediction uncertainty may increase for structurally distant molecules. Therefore, model predictions for out-of-distribution compounds should be interpreted with additional caution in practical screening applications.

Because MACCS fingerprints are relatively low-resolution binary descriptors, similarity values obtained from MACCS–Tanimoto analysis should be interpreted cautiously, particularly at lower thresholds. In this context, the current analysis was intended primarily as a broad assessment of chemical space overlap rather than a rigorous scaffold-level applicability domain definition. Nevertheless, the comparable similarity distribution profiles observed between the validation set and the independent test set suggest that both subsets maintain generally consistent structural relationships relative to the training dataset. Combined with the chemical space visualization analysis, these results support the overall structural representativeness and balance of the constructed DNT benchmark dataset for model benchmarking and external evaluation under the current experimental setting.

Among all models, DNN_MACCS exhibited numerically the highest recall (0.84, 95% CI: 0.80–0.88) while maintaining balanced performance (accuracy: 0.82, 95% CI: 0.79–0.85; F1-score: 0.82, 95% CI: 0.79–0.86; MCC: 0.64, 95% CI: 0.58–0.70). However, bootstrap confidence intervals (Table 2) indicate that the recall differences among the top fingerprint-based models are not statistically significant (overlapping CIs). XGBoost_MACCS and DNN_ChemBERTa showed comparable AUC (0.90, 95% CI: 0.87–0.92 and 0.89, 95% CI: 0.86–0.91, respectively) and recall (0.80, 95% CI: 0.75–0.85 and 0.82, 95% CI: 0.77–0.86), with no significant pairwise differences (DeLong test, p > 0.05 for all comparisons among SVM, XGBoost, DNN_MACCS and DNN_ChemBERTa). The incorporation of pre-trained molecular language representations in DNN_ChemBERTa did not result in consistent performance gains across evaluation metrics, suggesting that semantic information encoded by pre-trained embeddings did not substantially enhance DNT prediction under the current dataset size and labeling conditions.43,44

Among the graph-based deep learning models, GraphSAGE and AttentiveFP exhibited comparatively lower predictive performance under the current benchmark settings, with ROC–AUC values of 0.87 and 0.85, respectively, and relatively reduced MCC values (0.56 and 0.53). ChemProp achieved an ROC–AUC of 0.86 and an MCC of 0.59 on the independent test set, demonstrating competitive predictive performance. Notably, although ChemProp represents a widely adopted industry-standard molecular graph learning framework, its overall performance remained broadly comparable to that of the optimized fingerprint-based DNN_MACCS model rather than substantially exceeding it. These findings suggest that under the current dataset scale and benchmark settings, carefully optimized fingerprint-based deep learning models remain highly competitive relative to graph-based message-passing architectures. Nevertheless, these observations should be interpreted cautiously because the fingerprint-based and graph-based models were developed under different representation learning paradigms and optimization environments. In particular, graph neural network architectures generally involve more complex message-passing mechanisms and larger hyperparameter search spaces, which may require larger datasets and more standardized benchmarking conditions for rigorous comparison.45 The optimization protocols and model complexities differed across modeling paradigms in the current study. For example, the GNN models involved more complex hyperparameter search spaces and representation learning mechanisms than the fingerprint-based DNN models. Therefore, the observed performance differences should not be interpreted as definitive evidence of intrinsic superiority or inferiority between modeling architectures.

These results support the selection of DNN_MACCS as the final model for subsequent interpretability and mechanistic analyses, based on its comparatively higher sensitivity combined with stable overall classification performance. From a drug discovery perspective, the superior recall of this model makes it particularly suitable for early-stage safety screening, where minimizing false negatives is critical to avoid advancing potentially neurotoxic candidates.

To assess the statistical significance of performance differences among models, we computed 95% bootstrap confidence intervals (Table 2) and performed DeLong tests for AUC comparisons. The confidence intervals for AUC, accuracy, recall, F1-score and MCC overlapped substantially among the fingerprint-based models (SVM_MACCS, XGBoost_MACCS, DNN_MACCS, DNN_ChemBERTa), and DeLong tests revealed no statistically significant differences in AUC between any pair of these models (all p > 0.05). In contrast, all fingerprint-based models significantly outperformed the graph neural networks (GraphSAGE, AttentiveFP, Chemprop) in AUC (p < 0.01 for all comparisons). These results support the conclusion that the top fingerprint-based models perform comparably, and that the numerical advantage of DNN_MACCS in recall does not constitute a statistically validated superiority.

To evaluate the robustness of the developed models under structure-aware partitioning conditions, additional experiments were conducted using a cluster-aware split strategy based on MACCS fingerprint similarity. Compared with random splitting, all models exhibited moderately reduced predictive performance on the independent test set, reflecting the increased structural heterogeneity between training and evaluation subsets. Nevertheless, the models maintained reasonable discriminative ability, with AUC values ranging from 0.84 to 0.85 (Table S2). Among the evaluated models, XGBoost_MACCS achieved the highest recall (0.84), whereas DNN_MACCS maintained balanced overall performance with an AUC of 0.84 and MCC of 0.53. These findings suggest that the proposed framework retains acceptable robustness under more stringent structure-aware evaluation settings and that the predictive performance observed under random splitting was not solely driven by structural redundancy between subsets. The observed performance decrease under cluster-aware partitioning further suggests that random splitting may partially overestimate predictive performance due to structural similarity between subsets. Nevertheless, the retained discriminative ability under structure-aware conditions supports the robustness of the proposed modeling framework. Although entire-cluster hold-out strategies may provide a more stringent assessment of extrapolative generalization, the primary objective of the present benchmark is to support DNT prediction within a defined applicability domain rather than across unrestricted chemical space. As emphasized in OECD QSAR principles, reliable prediction requires explicit consideration of applicability-domain boundaries. Accordingly, applicability-domain analysis was incorporated into this study to characterize the chemical space in which model predictions are expected to be reliable. Future work may further investigate extrapolative performance under more stringent cluster hold-out protocols as larger and more diverse DNT datasets become available.

Analysis of the training stability and generalization ability of the DNN_MACCS model

To assess the training stability and potential overfitting risk of the selected DNN_MACCS model, systematic monitoring of the loss function, AUC, and accuracy trends on both the training and validation sets was performed throughout the training process. The model was trained for a maximum of 200 epochs with an early stopping strategy to prevent overfitting. Training was automatically terminated when no further improvement in validation performance was observed over consecutive epochs, and the model achieving the best validation performance was retained.

As shown in Fig. S3, the training loss decreased rapidly during the initial training phase, accompanied by simultaneous increases in AUC and accuracy, indicating efficient learning of the mapping relationship between molecular structural features and DNT labels. The validation AUC and accuracy reached their optimal values at approximately the 30th epoch and subsequently entered a stable plateau. In contrast, the training performance continued to improve slowly beyond this point, suggesting the onset of mild overfitting tendencies. Accordingly, the 30th epoch was selected as the optimal stopping point for the final model.

Under this optimal training configuration, the DNN_MACCS model achieved AUC values of 0.97 and 0.88 on the training and validation sets, respectively, with corresponding F1-scores of 0.92 and 0.85. The resulting training–validation AUC gap (0.09) and F1-score gap (0.07) remained within acceptable ranges, indicating that no pronounced overfitting occurred. These findings demonstrate that the DNN_MACCS model maintains favorable generalization stability while delivering strong predictive performance, supporting its suitability as the final model for developmental neurotoxicity prediction. The stable generalization behavior further reinforces its potential utility in practical drug screening applications where model reliability across structurally diverse compounds is essential.

Model interpretability analysis based on SHAP

To improve the transparency and interpretability of the DNN_MACCS model, SHAP analysis was performed to characterize the structural features contributing to model predictions at both global and local levels. It should be noted that the following analyses were intended to identify statistically associated structural patterns underlying DNT prediction rather than to establish direct causal biological mechanisms. Global SHAP analysis was used to evaluate overall feature contribution trends across the dataset, while local SHAP analysis was employed to interpret compound-specific prediction behavior.

Global feature contribution and structural pattern analysis

Global SHAP analysis was conducted for the best-performing DNN_MACCS model to evaluate the contribution patterns of individual MACCS fingerprint features (Fig. 4). The top-ranked features exhibited heterogeneous directional effects, indicating that the model prediction was influenced by both the presence and absence of specific structural patterns rather than by a limited set of universally toxicity-promoting fragments alone. The cumulative SHAP importance analysis revealed a highly concentrated feature importance distribution. The top 20 MACCS features accounted for 58.87% of the total absolute SHAP value, while the top 50 and top 100 features explained 89.70% and 99.55% of the total importance, respectively (Table S3 and Fig. S4). These results indicate that model predictions were primarily driven by a relatively limited subset of structural descriptors, whereas the majority of remaining features contributed minimally to the final predictions. Meanwhile, a substantial proportion of MACCS features exhibited negligible or zero SHAP contributions, suggesting that only a subset of structural descriptors substantially influenced model prediction behavior. Several features, including MACCS_050, MACCS_029, MACCS_058, MACCS_141, and MACCS_127, showed positive SHAP contributions at higher feature values, suggesting that the presence of these structural patterns was statistically associated with increased DNT prediction. In contrast, features such as MACCS_159, MACCS_146, MACCS_140, MACCS_161, MACCS_122, MACCS_166, and MACCS_153 exhibited inverse directional patterns, in which lower feature values contributed positively to DNT classification. These findings suggest that the DNN_MACCS model captures complex statistical relationships involving both structural enrichment and structural absence patterns within the dataset. Some positively associated features corresponded to aromatic or hydrophobic structural motifs that have previously been discussed in toxicological studies related to CNS exposure and chemical neurotoxicity. Conversely, several negatively associated features were linked to relatively polar or heteroatom-containing substructures that may reflect broader physicochemical contexts associated with reduced DNT prediction.

Data and model availability

The developmental neurotoxicity benchmark dataset constructed in this study includes standardized SMILES representations of all compounds, corresponding binary toxicity labels, and the fixed training, validation, and independent test set partitions used for model development and evaluation. These data are deposited in a public repository to ensure full reproducibility of the reported results. The dataset is expected to serve as a valuable resource for the computational toxicology and drug discovery communities, enabling further methodological development and benchmarking.

In addition, the scripts for MACCS fingerprint generation, the trained weights of the optimal DNN_MACCS model, and all scripts used for model training, inference, and SHAP-based interpretability analysis will be made publicly available.

All scripts for AD analysis and chemical space visualization using UMAP and t-SNE will also be released to support transparent assessment of model scope and result interpretation.

Strengths and limitations

This study presents several strengths in dataset construction, methodological design, and mechanistic interpretation.

First, a curated, standardized, and fully reproducible benchmark dataset for developmental neurotoxicity was established. In contrast to previous studies that relied on limited sample sizes, heterogeneous sources, and inconsistent labeling criteria, a unified and stringent workflow was applied for positive and negative sample selection, structural curation, physicochemical property matching, and fixed dataset partitioning, thereby providing a reliable benchmark foundation for objective methodological comparisons. The use of MW and log P matching specifically addresses potential pharmacokinetic bias, which is particularly relevant when translating computational findings to drug safety evaluation.

Second, multiple representative machine learning, deep learning, and graph neural network modeling paradigms were systematically benchmarked for the DNT prediction task. The comparative results delineate the performance characteristics of different modeling strategies and indicate that under the current experimental configuration and dataset scale, the fingerprint-based DNN_MACCS model demonstrated comparatively stable and balanced predictive performance.

Third, SHAP-based interpretability analysis was employed to identify key structural motifs statistically associated with model predictions. Although these associations do not establish causality, they provide hypothesis-generating structural insights that may inform future mechanistic investigations.

Several limitations should also be acknowledged. First, although the benchmark dataset constructed in this study is larger than those used in many previous computational DNT studies, the current sample size may still limit the full representation learning capacity of advanced graph-based deep learning architectures. While the additionally implemented ChemProp model demonstrated competitive predictive performance, graph neural network and message-passing models generally benefit from substantially larger datasets and broader chemical diversity for optimal representation learning. Therefore, the relative performance relationships observed in the present study may be influenced partly by dataset scale and benchmark configuration. In addition, recently emerging pretrained molecular foundation models and large-scale graph representation learning approaches were not comprehensively evaluated in the current work. Future studies incorporating transfer learning,46 multimodal molecular representations,47 and pretrained graph neural networks48 may further improve DNT prediction performance and generalizability as larger high-quality DNT datasets become available. Second, the presumed negative samples were primarily derived from non-neurological drugs in ChEMBL. Despite the use of ATC-based filtering and physicochemical property-matched sampling to reduce potential bias, the presence of yet-unreported developmental neurotoxicants cannot be fully excluded, which may introduce noise into model training. These compounds have not been experimentally validated for DNT, and their operational definition represents a limitation of the current benchmark. Third, the current dataset predominantly comprises pesticides, industrial chemicals, and drugs, with a limited representation of the full chemical space encountered in drug discovery, particularly for biologics and emerging modalities. Future work should expand the dataset to include a broader range of drug-like compounds in order to enhance chemical diversity and improve model generalizability in pharmaceutical applications. In addition, further investigation of prediction reliability, uncertainty estimation, and calibration performance for out-of-distribution compounds under stricter applicability domain definitions will be important for improving the practical robustness of DNT prediction models.

Conclusions

In this study, we established a systematically constructed and standardized benchmark dataset for chemical developmental neurotoxicity prediction. Using this dataset, we systematically benchmarked multiple representative modeling strategies, providing an objective basis for characterizing their predictive behavior and practical applicability in DNT prediction tasks. Under the current data scale, the fingerprint-based deep neural network model exhibited balanced discrimination performance and numerically high sensitivity (recall = 0.84, 95% CI: 0.80–0.88) for identifying potentially hazardous compounds, though pairwise comparisons with other fingerprint-based models did not reach statistical significance. It therefore serves as a practical baseline model for DNT-oriented prioritization. Nevertheless, further benchmarking under unified optimization conditions and larger datasets will be necessary to more rigorously compare different deep learning paradigms for DNT prediction. Through SHAP-based interpretability analysis, key structural patterns contributing to model predictions were identified and further summarized as hypothesis-generating toxicological associations. Importantly, these findings should be interpreted as statistical structure–prediction relationships rather than experimentally validated biological mechanisms. Overall, the benchmark dataset, systematic modeling evaluation framework, and interpretability strategy presented in this study provide a unified and extensible foundation for computational DNT prediction and may facilitate the incorporation of this underrepresented toxicity endpoint into early-stage chemical screening and risk assessment workflows.

Beyond the specific models evaluated in the current work, the proposed benchmark may also serve as a reusable community resource for future computational toxicology studies, including benchmarking of graph neural networks, pretrained molecular foundation models, transfer learning approaches, uncertainty-aware prediction methods, and mechanism-informed toxicological modeling. As larger and more diverse DNT datasets become available, the current benchmark framework may support continual dataset expansion and standardized external validation across different molecular representation learning paradigms. Although several models achieved relatively strong predictive performance under the present benchmark settings, important challenges remain regarding model generalizability, prediction reliability for structurally out-of-distribution compounds, and mechanistic interpretability. Addressing these issues will be essential for the future development of robust AI-assisted developmental neurotoxicity assessment frameworks. Collectively, the proposed framework offers not only reliable early-stage DNT risk prioritization but also interpretable structural insights that may support medicinal chemistry optimization and the development of safer drug candidates, particularly for vulnerable populations such as pregnant women and children.
