AI-powered tools in family medicine: Bridging technology and practice
Authors: Sultan Alnashmi Alqasim, Fahad M. Bindakhil, Mommed M. Alzahrani, Samar Fahad A Alhajri, Ghaida M. Alzaidi, Sara Fahad A. Alhajri, Noha Tashkandi, Assad M. Arafah
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectives:To assess the perceptions, attitudes, and readiness of family physicians in Saudi Arabia toward the adoption of artificial intelligence (AI) in clinical practice and to identify factors influencing its use.Methods:A cross-sectional, web-based survey was conducted between April and July 2025 among family medicine physicians across Saudi Arabia. A validated questionnaire captured demographic and professional characteristics, current and past AI use, perceived benefits and risks, factors influencing adoption, and preferred physician–AI collaboration models. Data were analyzed using descriptive statistics, bivariate tests, and multivariable logistic regression to identify independent predictors of AI use.Results:Of 400 invited physicians, 345 responded (response rate 86.3%). Overall, 45.8% reported using AI tools in the past year, most commonly ChatGPT (65.2%). AI use was more prevalent among physicians aged 25–34 years, those with ≤5 years of experience, Saudi nationals, residents, and those in tertiary hospitals. High awareness (70.2%) and willingness to learn (81.0%) were reported, with 81.6% supporting AI-related training. Key adoption drivers were reliability (80.4%), efficiency (70.3%), and ease of use (69.7%), while major barriers included lack of high-quality data (69.7%), inadequate algorithms (57.6%), and integration challenges (47.0%). Most respondents (76.5%) preferred a physician-led, AI-assisted model.Conclusion:Family physicians in Saudi Arabia show good engagement with AI and strong interest in training, alongside concerns over reliability, data quality, and workflow integration. Adoption is shaped by age, experience, and workplace setting. Addressing these barriers through targeted education, robust governance, and codesigned integration strategies could enhance AI uptake in family medicine.

Introduction

The integration of artificial intelligence (AI) into health care is a rapidly evolving reality, promising to reshape clinical practice, administrative workflows, and patient outcomes.[1] Family medicine stands at a critical juncture, poised to harness this technological wave to enhance its core functions. The challenge lies in navigating this transition thoughtfully, ensuring that technology serves the fundamental goals of patient-centered, equitable, and efficient care.[12]

The potential benefits of AI in the family medicine setting are profound and align with the Quintuple Aim of healthcare improvement.[3] AI-driven clinical decision support systems (CDSS) can augment diagnostic accuracy and facilitate evidence-based treatment planning for complex chronic conditions.[4] Concurrently, natural language processing (NLP) applications can reduce administrative burden faced by physicians, automating clinical documentation, summarizing patient records, and drafting responses to patient portal messages, thereby freeing clinicians to focus on direct patient interaction and complex medical decision-making.[15] Furthermore, AI-powered patient monitoring tools, including telemedicine platforms and wearable sensors, offer avenues for continuous care, remote follow-up, and early detection of clinical deterioration, particularly valuable in managing chronic diseases prevalent in primary care.[67]

Despite the promising potential of AI in healthcare, its integration faces substantial ethical and practical challenges. Key concerns include the risk of perpetuating health disparities due to biased or limited training data sets, the “black box” nature of complex models that undermines trust, explainability, and the need for robust data privacy, security, and governance frameworks.[89] In addition, a persistent gap exists between AI development in research settings and its practical deployment in primary care,[10] with limited involvement of frontline professionals leading to tools that may not fit clinical workflows or address key needs.[2] Usability issues, lack of interoperability with existing electronic health records, and high implementation costs further hinder adoption.[11]

Successful AI adoption in family medicine depends on clinicians’ perceptions, attitudes, and readiness.[3] While many physicians are optimistic, concerns persist about job security, skill erosion, and depersonalization of the patient-physician relationship.[1213] Understanding these perspectives is vital for developing training, fostering trust, and implementing strategies that empower rather than replace clinicians. Therefore, this study examines family physicians’ attitudes, perceived benefits, and challenges regarding AI adoption in Saudi Arabia to guide its responsible and effective integration into clinical practice.

Methods

Study design and setting

This is a cross-sectional, web-based survey conducted among family medicine physicians in Saudi Arabia. The survey was administered electronically between April 2025 and July 2025. Participation was voluntary, and informed consent was obtained electronically before survey initiation. The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Institutional Review Board of King Abdullah International Medical Research Center (KAIMRC) (IRB: 00000130225), and all procedures were conducted in accordance with the ethical standards of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Study participants

Eligible participants were family physicians practicing within Saudi Arabia. Physicians from nonfamily medicine specialties, practitioners outside Saudi Arabia, and medical students were excluded. Participants were recruited through simple random sampling, and all eligible physicians during the data collection period were invited to participate.

Data collection instrument

Data were collected using a structured, self-administered electronic questionnaire adapted from a previously validated survey developed by researchers at the Peking Union Medical College.[14] The instrument captured demographic and professional characteristics, experiences with clinical AI, and attitudinal perspectives towards its integration into family medicine practice. It included sections on: (i) demographic and professional information, such as gender, age group, nationality, job title, years of clinical experience, and hospital level; (ii) current and past use of decision-support clinical AI systems, including frequency of use, experiences with errors or adverse events, and perceived patient attitudes; (iii) attitudes toward clinical AI, assessed through agreement with a series of statements relating to its utility, efficiency, and future role in medicine, measured on a five-point Likert scale ranging from “strongly disagree” to “strongly agree”; (iv) factors influencing willingness to adopt AI and perceived challenges to its implementation, recorded in a multiple-response format; and (v) preferred models for the physician–AI working relationship. The questionnaire was distributed electronically to facilitate complete and accurate data capture.

Attitude scoring

To capture the attitudinal profile of respondents, Likert scale items were classified a priori into two distinct subscales: Perceived Benefits (e.g. knowledge, willingness to learn, efficiency, perceived usefulness) and Perceived Risks (e.g. potential job loss, replacement of physicians, reduced attractiveness of medicine). Items within each subscale were scored from 1 (“strongly disagree”) to 5 (“strongly agree”). For each participant, a Perceived Benefits Score was calculated as the mean of all benefit-related items, with higher scores indicating stronger endorsement of AI’s advantages. Similarly, a Perceived Risks Score was calculated as the mean of all risk-related items, with higher scores indicating greater concern regarding AI adoption. These subscale scores were used in comparative analyses across demographic and professional variables.

Sample size calculation

Using Epi Info software version 7.2.6.0, the sample size was calculated based on an anticipated AI system satisfaction rate of 74.3% (±7.78%). Assuming a response distribution rate of 50%, with 80% power, a 5% margin of error, and a 91% confidence interval, the minimum required sample was 220 participants. To account for a potential 10% rate of incomplete responses, the target sample size was increased to at least 244 participants. This sample size was considered adequate to provide reliable and precise estimates of AI implementation in clinical practice.

Statistical analysis

Data cleaning and coding were performed before analysis. Categorical variables were summarized as frequencies and percentages, and continuous variables as means and standard deviations. Group differences in continuous variables were assessed using independent-samples t-tests for two-group comparisons or one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) for comparisons involving more than two groups, following checks for normality and homogeneity of variance. Post hoc Tukey analyses were used for pairwise comparisons. Categorical variables were compared using Chi-square or Fisher’s exact tests, as appropriate. Univariate analyses were first performed to explore associations between demographic/professional characteristics and (i) ever-use of AI and (ii) subscale attitude scores. Variables with significant associations in univariate analyses were entered into multivariable logistic regression models to identify independent predictors of AI use. Odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were reported. All statistical tests were two-tailed, and a P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Analyses were performed using Jamovi (Windows version 2.6.23).

Results

A total of 400 family medicine physicians were invited to participate in the survey, of whom 345 consented and completed the questionnaire, yielding a response rate of 86.3%. The majority were Saudi nationals (82.0%), aged 25–34 years (59.7%), and residents (36.8%). The majority of respondents (97.1%) worked in the governmental sector, mainly in primary care hospitals (60.3%), and all specialized in family medicine, with 51.9% having ≤5 years of experience. Use of clinical AI systems in practice

Nearly half of the respondents (45.8%) reported using clinical AI tools in the past year, most commonly at least once a week (39.9%) or once a month (27.2%). Over one-third (36.1%) of AI users experienced errors or accidents, while most perceived patient attitudes as unclear (47.5%) or neutral (28.5%). The most frequently used AI tool was ChatGPT (65.2%), followed by medication-related applications (11.4%) and other tools such as Deepseek (7.0%) and POE (3.2%). Attitudes toward AI

Most participants demonstrated strong awareness and interest in clinical AI, with 70.2% acknowledging its wide application and 58.9% reporting good knowledge of the field. A large majority were willing to learn about AI (81.0%) and supported its inclusion in medical education (81.6%). However, fewer respondents viewed AI as superior to physicians, with only 19.0% considering it more accurate and 20.9% more efficient. While 38.6% believed that physicians who adopt AI would replace those who do not, most disagreed that AI would cause job loss (69.0%) or make medicine less attractive (65.8%), as shown in Table 3.

Perceptions, challenges, and preferred role of clinical AI in practice

Participants prioritized reliability/accuracy (80.4%), efficiency/speed (70.3%), and ease of use (69.7%) when considering clinical AI, with additional weight on interpretability (58.2%), privacy (53.2%), cost-effectiveness (44.3%), and widespread adoption (35.4%). Top perceived barriers were lack of high-quality training data (69.7%), inadequate algorithms/compute (57.6%), shortage of interdisciplinary talent (50.0%), integration difficulties (47.0%), regulatory gaps (45.5%), and limited understanding/acceptance among clinicians and students (40.9%). Most favoured a physician-led model with AI in an auxiliary role (76.5%), while many also supported AI completing care under physician supervision (45.5%). Discussion

This study offers a snapshot of Saudi family physicians’ perceptions, attitudes, and adoption of AI: clinicians are cautiously optimistic, engaged with emerging tools, and mindful of reliability, workflow fit, and preserving clinical judgement. Our survey indicates that AI is already a reality for a substantial portion of family physicians, with 45.8% reporting use of decision-support tools in the past year. Adoption was higher among younger physicians (25–34 years), those with <5 years’ experience, and practitioners in tertiary hospitals, suggesting digitally native, early-career clinicians are leading this shift.

Regarding attitude, most are willing to learn (81.0%) and support curricular integration (81.6%) yet remain sceptical that AI currently outperforms clinicians in accuracy or efficiency. Fears of widespread job loss are low, though many recognise a competitive edge for adopters. A notable gradient in perceived risk, higher among residents/specialists than consultants, likely reflects career-stage concerns versus the confidence of senior clinicians. Ultimately, physicians favour a collaborative model in which clinicians lead and AI assists (76.5%). In comparison with previous Saudi-based studies, our findings reveal both parallels and distinctions that provide valuable context for interpreting family physicians’ perspectives on AI adoption. For example, while our study demonstrated a substantially higher rate of AI use in the past year (45.8%) compared with Alkhatieb et al.’s study in Jeddah (25.9%), both cohorts exhibited strong recognition of AI’s potential benefits, particularly in relation to accuracy, efficiency, and workflow optimization. Similarly, our participants’ high willingness to learn about AI and support for structured AI training echoes the emphasis in Alkhatieb et al.[15] on the need for targeted education and interdisciplinary collaboration to bridge the gap between perceived value and real-world utilization. By contrast, Alsaedi et al.’s[16] nationwide survey across healthcare professions reported generally high AI awareness (mean = 3.96) but low prior experience (mean = 2.65), with top concerns centering on data management, regulatory issues, and professional practice impacts. These concerns resonate with our participants’ prioritization of reliability, interpretability, and privacy protection when considering AI adoption, indicating that despite variations in specialty, region, and usage rates, apprehensions about data quality, integration into clinical workflows, and safeguarding professional roles remain consistent themes.[16] Together, these comparisons suggest that while family physicians in our study are more engaged with AI than the broader physician or healthcare workforce in prior Saudi studies, the underlying enablers and barriers to adoption, particularly education, trust, and governance, are aligned.

Comparative analysis: A global perspective

Placing our findings within the international literature reveals both convergent themes and notable regional differences. The 45.8% adoption rate among Saudi family physicians appears relatively high compared to the approximately 19% usage reported among medical staff in a recent nationwide Chinese survey.[17] However, both studies identify a similar “intention-behavior gap,” where high levels of awareness and positive attitudes do not fully translate into widespread clinical use,[17] suggesting that systemic barriers may be the primary inhibitors of adoption globally.

The preferences of Saudi physicians align closely with those of their international counterparts. The strong consensus that AI should supplement, not replace, clinical judgment mirrors findings from a Canadian qualitative study, where stakeholders emphasized preserving the patient-provider relationship as a core value.[18] Similarly, the desire for AI to automate documentation and administrative tasks to reduce burnout is a high-priority application identified in Canada and a frequently cited benefit in systematic reviews.[1819]

The factors driving adoption also show global parallels. Our finding that younger, less experienced physicians are more likely to use AI is consistent with data from China, which showed that physicians under 45 had significantly higher adoption intentions.[17] The emphasis our participants placed on reliability, accuracy, and ease of use directly corresponds to the core tenets of technology acceptance models, such as performance and effort expectancy, which were identified as key facilitators in the Chinese context.[17] However, our study’s finding that perceived risk varies by professional seniority (resident vs. consultant) offers a more granular view than the Chinese study, which found the most significant risk-perception differences between professional roles (nurses vs. physicians).[17]

Broader implications: Ethics, equity, and the patient-doctor relationship

The integration of AI into family medicine carries profound implications that extend beyond clinical workflows. The ethical challenges highlighted in the literature, algorithmic bias, data privacy, and accountability, are paramount.[202122] The primary concern of our respondents regarding AI reliability (80.4%) intersects directly with the risk of algorithmic bias, where models trained on nonrepresentative data sets can perpetuate or even worsen health disparities, particularly for minority or structurally vulnerable populations.[23] For a national healthcare system like Saudi Arabia’s, deploying AI without rigorous bias mitigation could inadvertently undermine health equity goals.[24]

The “black box” nature of many AI models undermines trust and medicolegal clarity, a barrier repeatedly noted in international reviews.[25] Global accountability frameworks for AI-related harm remain unclear, with unresolved questions about liability among developers, institutions, and clinicians.[26] Clear governance and regulation, e.g., the GDPR and the EU AI Act, are essential to build confidence for broader adoption. Consistent with this, our respondents favored an auxiliary AI model that preserves the patient-doctor relationship and supports diagnosis and workflow efficiency.[27]

Barriers, facilitators, and limitations of current AI

The challenges to AI adoption cited by Saudi physicians are consistent with barriers identified in global systematic reviews.[2829] The top concerns were lack of high-quality training data, inadequate algorithms, and difficulty integrating AI into workflows, universally recognized obstacles.[30] This highlights that the challenges are not unique to any single healthcare system. The fact that over a third (36.1%) of our AI users had experienced errors highlights that concerns about reliability are not merely theoretical.

To overcome these barriers, the literature points to several key facilitators. First is the principle of co-design, where clinicians, patients, and other end-users are involved to ensure clinical relevance and usability.[31] Second, robust governance and institutional support are essential for providing the necessary infrastructure, funding, and technical assistance.[32] Third, as endorsed by 81.6% of our respondents, formal training and education programs are needed to build AI literacy, enabling clinicians to critically appraise and appropriately use AI tools.[33] Finally, AI tools must demonstrate clear value in improving patient outcomes.

Limitations

This study relies on self-reported behaviors and attitudes, introducing recall and social-desirability bias. The sampling frame was restricted to family physicians in Saudi Arabia, and the cohort was predominantly governmental-sector and primary-care based, which may limit generalizability. The questionnaire was adapted from prior work and, while structured, was not psychometrically re-validated in this setting. A longitudinal, mixed-methods study linking objective usage metrics and implementation outcomes to patient-level effects would strengthen causal inference and external validity.

Conclusion

This study found that 45.8% of family physicians in Saudi Arabia had used AI tools, with adoption more common among younger, early-career clinicians and those in tertiary settings. Participants reported high awareness, willingness to learn, and support for AI training, but also identified reliability, data quality, interpretability, and workflow integration as key challenges. Most favored a physician-led, AI-assisted model, reflecting the view that AI should complement rather than replace clinical decision-making. These findings highlighted the opportunities and barriers to AI adoption in family medicine and emphasized the need for targeted training, robust governance, and careful integration to support its effective use in practice.
