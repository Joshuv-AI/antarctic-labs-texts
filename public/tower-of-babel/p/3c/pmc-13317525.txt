Targeting neurovascular conflict in atypical vagal-glossopharyngeal neuralgia: illustrative case

Glossopharyngeal neuralgia (GPN) and vagal neuralgia are rare and often debilitating pain syndromes affecting cranial nerves (CNs) IX (glossopharyngeal) and X (vagus), respectively.1,2 While often considered separate entities, they can present with overlapping symptoms and are frequently discussed together due to their anatomical proximity and shared pathophysiological mechanisms.3 The estimated annual incidence of GPN is approximately 0.2–0.7 per 100,000 persons, with vagal neuralgia being even less common.4 The primary symptom is typically intense, paroxysmal throat, tongue, ear, or larynx pain, often triggered by swallowing, coughing, or speaking.5 However, atypical presentations can include dysphagia, dysphonia, syncope, cardiac arrhythmias, and even seizures, leading to diagnostic challenges.6

The prevailing theory regarding the etiology of GPN and vagal neuralgia involves neurovascular compression (NVC) of the glossopharyngeal and vagal nerves at their root exit zone (REZ) in the cerebellopontine angle (CPA).7 Aberrant arteries, most commonly the posterior inferior cerebellar artery (PICA) or, less frequently, the anterior inferior cerebellar artery (AICA), can compress and pulsate against the nerves, causing demyelination and ectopic impulse generation.8 The diagnostic workup typically includes a thorough neurological examination, high-resolution MRI to rule out structural lesions and assess for NVC, and diagnostic nerve blocks.9 Treatment options range from pharmacological management with anticonvulsants and analgesics to surgical procedures such as microvascular decompression (MVD), stereotactic radiosurgery (SRS), and, less commonly, rhizotomy.10

This report aims to provide a comprehensive review of vagal-glossopharyngeal neuralgia (VGPN), encompassing its heterogeneous clinical picture, pathophysiology, diagnosis, and treatment. We present a case report of a patient with an atypical presentation of VGPN successfully treated with MVD, accompanied by a 2D operative video demonstrating the surgical technique. The case is then discussed in the context of the current literature, highlighting the nuances of diagnosis, surgical management, and outcomes.

Clinical Presentation and Diagnosis

The diagnosis of VGPN is established through a combination of clinical presentation, neurological examination, and neuroimaging.11 The primary symptom is paroxysmal, lancinating pain along the distribution of the glossopharyngeal and vagal nerves. Painful episodes are typically brief, lasting from seconds to approximately 2 minutes, characterized by abrupt onset and termination. Such episodes may occur in clusters spanning days, weeks, or months and can manifest multiple times daily. While predominantly occurring during waking hours, episodes can occasionally awaken patients from sleep. Pain is often reliably triggered by specific stimuli involving pharyngeal or laryngeal stimulation. Swallowing, particularly of cold liquids, is a common elicitor. Other frequent triggers encompass speaking, coughing, yawning, chewing, sneezing, clearing the throat, touching the gums or oral mucosa, and sudden head movements. Palpable trigger zones may exist within the tonsillar fossa or pharynx.12 The International Classification of Headache Disorders, 3rd edition (ICHD-3), provides formal diagnostic criteria for GPN (code 13.2.1).13 These criteria, shown in Table 1, provide a standardized framework for diagnosis, crucial for both clinical practice and research consistency.13 However, these criteria strictly define the classical pain syndrome but fail to capture the broader spectrum of associated clinical characteristics. Associated symptoms may include dysphagia, dysphonia, hoarseness, syncope, bradycardia, hypotension, and, in rare instances, cardiac arrest,12 affecting approximately 10% of patients.14 This presentation, sometimes referred to as VGPN, results from an intense afferent barrage originating from CN IX, activating vagal nuclei (such as the nucleus tractus solitarius and dorsal motor nucleus of the vagus) in the brainstem, leading to heightened parasympathetic outflow.15 Cardiovascular events can occur during or immediately following a pain episode and may be life-threatening. The potential for serious autonomic complications elevates GPN beyond a simple pain disorder. Recently, hemi-laryngopharyngeal spasm (HeLPS) has been described both as an isolated disturbance and as a complementary feature in the context of VGPN, expanding the landscape of lower CN rhizopathies.16 Clinically, it is distinguished by a triad of progressively severe episodic coughing triggered by a suprasternal “tickle,” unilateral or circumferential throat contractions, and episodic stridor that may result in loss of consciousness.17 Because patients are typically asymptomatic between episodes, they often endure years of misdiagnosis before recognition. Differential diagnosis is crucial to exclude other potential causes of facial pain. High-resolution MRI examination with specific sequences to visualize CNs and surrounding vasculature is essential.18 MRI can identify potential causes of nerve compression, including vascular loops, tumors, and arteriovenous malformations. Careful evaluation of the REZ of CNs IX and X for evidence of NVC is warranted. CISS or FIESTA sequences are particularly valuable for visualizing CNs and their relationship to adjacent vessels.18,19 Glossopharyngeal nerve blocks with local anesthetic can provide temporary pain relief and support the diagnosis.20

Pathophysiology

The anatomical basis for the co-occurrence of GPN and vagal symptoms lies in the intimate proximity of the CN IX and X rootlets at the medulla.1 The predominant classical theory, accounting for up to 95% of cases, posits that pulsatile NVC at the root entry or exit zone triggers the condition.12,14 This compression induces focal demyelination at the transition zone between central and peripheral myelin, facilitating ectopic impulse generation and ephaptic cross-talk between fibers.19,21 These abnormal electrical discharges manifest as the characteristic paroxysmal, lancinating pain.

The posterior inferior cerebellar artery (PICA) is the most frequently implicated offending vessel, often due to anatomical variations such as a high vertebral artery origin or loops impinging on the supraolivary fossette.22 Less common culprits include the AICA, the vertebral artery, or local venous structures.14 Emerging evidence suggests functional segregation within the vagal rootlets: rostral rootlets likely mediate sensory phenomena such as coughing, while caudal rootlets govern motor and parasympathetic functions.17 The resolution of both GPN and concurrent HeLPS following MVD of both nerves reinforces the NVC hypothesis. Consequently, MVD serves as a targeted, cause-directed therapy that validates the underlying mechanical etiology for this complex of symptoms.3

Therapeutic Management

The management of GPN follows a stepwise escalation, beginning with pharmacological intervention and progressing to surgical options for medically refractory cases.23–25 First-line treatment typically involves carbamazepine (CBZ) or oxcarbazepine;26 however, while CBZ effectively manages initial symptoms in approximately 75% of patients, its efficacy often diminishes over time14 and is frequently limited by side effects such as hyponatremia, ataxia, and diplopia.26,27 Consequently, nearly 50% of patients eventually require surgical intervention.23–25 MVD, a nonablative procedure aimed at relieving vascular compression at the root entry zone of CN IX and X, remains a gold standard. MVD offers immediate postoperative pain relief in 85%–94% of cases and maintains long-term durability in 65%–97% of patients, with a recurrence rate between 7% and 15%.1,13,23,28–30 For patients who are poor surgical candidates or prefer noninvasive options, SRS provides a favorable safety profile, particularly regarding the preservation of lower CN motor function, although it may carry a higher risk of recurrence compared to MVD.30–32 Intracranial rhizotomy (sectioning of CN IX and upper sensory rootlets of CN X) serves as a potent alternative, providing high initial relief rates, yet it is often reserved for salvage therapy due to the significant risk of permanent neurological deficits, including dysphagia and hoarseness. Ultimately, the choice between these modalities necessitates a careful trade-off between procedural invasiveness, long-term efficacy, and the risk of CN morbidity.33–35

Illustrative Case

A 50-year-old woman was referred to the neurosurgical clinic with a 3-year history of paroxysmal episodes of impaired swallowing associated with sharp pain radiating to the right side of the throat and tongue; these symptoms had been mild until the past year, when they progressively increased in frequency and severity. At the time of neurosurgical evaluation, the patient reported continuous dysphagia. The patient had previously consulted an otolaryngologist who found no attributable causes at fiber-optic endoscopy. The patient denied any history of trauma, infection, or other neurological disorders, but additionally reported recurrent hoarseness and occasional wheezing, which were temporally unrelated to the dysphagia.

Neurological examination revealed mild hoarseness and slight depression of the soft palate on the right side. CN examination was otherwise normal.

MRI of the brain revealed an aberrating PICA that, after reaching the VII-VIII complex, descended to the upper and anterior aspect of the jugular foramen; here, it formed a loop between the rostral rootlets of CNs X and IX, which moderate distortion of the foramen’s content (Fig. 1). There was no evidence of tumors, demyelinating disease, or other structural lesions. Based on the clinical and radiological findings, a diagnosis of vagal-glossopharyngeal compression syndrome with neuralgia secondary to NVC was made.

Surgical Technique

Given the atypical clinical presentation—predominantly characterized by functional deficits including dysphagia and right-sided soft palate depression, alongside suspected concurrent vagal compression—the patient was deemed a poor candidate for both conservative pharmacological management and Gamma Knife radiosurgery. Consequently, surgical management was indicated upfront to directly resolve the underlying mechanical neurovascular conflict. Video 1 shows highlights of the procedure.

The patient underwent MVD via a right retrosigmoid approach. The patient was placed in the park-bench position, and a small retrosigmoid craniotomy was performed. The dura was opened, and the cerebellum was gently retracted to expose the CPA. Further arachnoid dissection was performed to better expose the region of the jugular foramen.

CNs IX and X were identified, and the PICA was visualized coursing between the nerves with gross compression of both CN IX and the rostral rootlets of CN X (Video 1). The PICA was sharply dissected free using microsurgical tools under high magnification before interposing Teflon felt between the PICA itself and CNs IX and X to prevent future compression (Fig. 2). During the procedure, intraoperative neurophysiological monitoring was used to minimize the risk of complications. Brainstem auditory evoked potentials were used to monitor the function of the auditory pathway, while electromyography was used to monitor the function of the lower CNs. The dura was closed watertight, and the bone flap was replaced.

The postoperative course was uneventful. The patient remained completely free of neuralgia, and the right-sided palate depression had visibly resolved by postoperative day 1. A postoperative CT scan ruled out any acute complications. The patient experienced complete resolution of her preoperative symptoms and was discharged on 3rd postoperative day 3. At the 6-month follow-up appointment, the patient was pain free and also reported resolution of both dysphagia and hoarseness; no more episodes of wheezing had occurred since the procedure.

Discussion

Vagal-glossopharyngeal compression syndrome is a rare and enigmatic condition, posing considerable diagnostic and therapeutic challenges to clinicians.1,3,16,36 The case we present underscores the critical importance of considering this diagnosis in patients presenting with otherwise unexplained symptoms such as dysphonia, dysphagia, and laryngopharyngeal spasms, especially alongside complaints of unilateral throat pain. High-resolution MRI is an indispensable tool for identifying potential NVC affecting the lower CNs, enabling a more targeted and effective treatment strategy.1,18,37

Notwithstanding its limited incidence, the management and outcomes of GPN have been extensively described and researched; earliest descriptions of glossopharyngeal and vagal neuralgia originate from Chawla and Falconer’s report in 1967;2 their work provided a foundational understanding of these conditions, emphasizing the diagnostic challenges inherent in differentiating them from other causes of facial pain and highlighting the scarcity of effective treatment options available at the time. In 1981, Rushton and colleagues published their own series of 217 patients, detailing the wide array of clinical presentations of both glossopharyngeal and vagal neuralgia.6 More surgery-oriented research subsequently arose, highlighting the role of MVD. Resnick and Jannetta detailed the surgical technique involved in MVD for GPN in their 1995 series, reporting the outcomes of patients who underwent MVD, noting a high rate of pain relief.38 Similar contributions were made by Ferroli et al., who conducted a retrospective study of 31 patients undergoing MVD for primary GPN.7 Rey-Dios and Cohen-Gadol reported that MVD achieved an approximate success rate of 80%, while also acknowledging a complication rate of approximately 10%.1 In their work, Ma et al. offered detailed insights into the long-term efficacy and prognostic factors influencing MVD for GPN.36 Their study stands out for its extended follow-up period beyond the initial postoperative phase, providing a more comprehensive understanding of the durability of MVD outcomes. Finally, in 2020, Honey et al. presented a compelling case of vagus-associated neurogenic cough caused by vascular encroachment of the vagus nerve, introducing the concept of VANCOUVER (vagus-associated neurogenic cough occurring due to unilateral vascular compression of its root) syndrome.16 This study expanded the understanding of atypical presentations of vagal neuralgia. Their findings emphasized the need for clinicians to consider vagal nerve involvement in patients presenting with unexplained cough, thereby broadening the scope of diagnostic possibilities.39–43

The same procedure for VGPN presents a higher risk of complications compared to MVD for trigeminal neuralgia. This is primarily due to the proximity of the lower CNs to vital brainstem structures. Potential complications include dysphagia, hoarseness, aspiration pneumonia, and CSF leakage.37 As a result, careful patient selection, meticulous surgical technique, and experienced neurosurgical expertise become paramount to optimizing outcomes and minimizing potential risks.

Overall, MVD has evolved as a well-recognized technique for treating GPN with good outcomes in the management of neuralgic pain;44 however, its efficacy in other clinical features due to compression of lower CNs (dysphagia, dysphonia, HeLPS, etc.) is more sparsely described, and recommendations are lacking for an adequate therapeutic path exhibiting such disturbances.

Observations

The case hereby presented highlights an atypical manifestation of GPN. While classical GPN is overwhelmingly characterized by paroxysmal pain as the primary symptom, this patient presented primarily with continuous dysphagia and objective functional deficits. Furthermore, her secondary symptoms of hoarseness and wheezing raised a strong clinical suspicion of concomitant vagal nerve (CN X) compression at the level of the jugular foramen. This specific constellation of episodic laryngopharyngeal symptoms secondary to vagal neurovascular conflict is highly characteristic of the recently described HeLPS syndrome. Recognizing this concurrent presentation was critical; it dictated the shift away from palliative, pain-focused therapies such as medical management or radiosurgery toward MVD, which definitively addresses the underlying pathogenetic mechanisms responsible for the functional motor deficits.

Lessons

VGPN is a rare and intricate condition that presents significant challenges in both diagnosis and treatment. MVD stands out as an effective surgical approach for carefully selected patients who exhibit NVC of the glossopharyngeal and vagal nerves. Meticulous patient selection via thorough clinical evaluation and supportive MRI examination, a highly refined microsurgical technique, and proper follow-up are essential to optimize outcomes and reducing the likelihood of complications. Moving forward, further research is essential to deepen our understanding of the underlying pathophysiology of VGPN and to develop even more effective and targeted treatments for this debilitating condition. The case presented here, along with the accompanying 2D operative video, can be a valuable resource to further strengthen the surgical awareness and skills in the treatment of VGPN MVD. This information contributes to the collective efforts aimed at improving patient outcomes and quality of life for those affected by this rare neurological disorder.
