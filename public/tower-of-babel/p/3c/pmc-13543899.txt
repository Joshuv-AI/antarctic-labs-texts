Fatty change in rotator cuff muscles following reverse total shoulder arthroplasty: does the humeral component design matter?
Authors: Gaku Matsuzawa, Taku Hatta, Eiji Itoi, Toshitake Aizawa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundFatty change progression is an important factor influencing treatment decisions for shoulder disorders. Appropriate evaluation and management of fatty change in the rotator cuff muscles and of its relationship to reverse total shoulder arthroplasty (rTSA) warrant careful consideration. This study aimed to examine the association between post-operative fatty change progression in individual rotator cuff muscles and humeral component design after rTSA.MethodsThis retrospective study included 203 patients with large to massive rotator cuff tears or cuff tear arthropathy who underwent rTSA. Of these, 41 patients without glenoid-sided lateralization and with at least 2 years of follow-up imaging were included in the analysis. Patients were treated with either inlay or onlay humeral components. Fatty change progression was assessed using the Goutallier classification for each rotator cuff muscle and was categorized as mild or severe. Post-operative outcomes included active range of motion and patient-reported outcome measures, including the American Shoulder and Elbow Surgeons score and the Constant Murley score. Associations between implant design, fatty change in each rotator cuff muscle, and clinical outcomes were analyzed. The Minimal Clinically Important Difference (MCID) was explored for variables independently associated with outcomes in the primary multivariable models.ResultsSixteen patients underwent inlay and 25 underwent onlay humeral component implantation. Fatty change progression rates were similar between groups for the supraspinatus and teres minor but were lower with the onlay design for the infraspinatus (ISP) and subscapularis (SSC). The onlay design was independently associated with lower post-operative Goutallier grades of the ISP (odds ratio, 0.07; 95% confidence interval, 0.02-0.33) and SSC (odds ratio, 0.11; 95% confidence interval, 0.03-0.44). Post-operative fatty change correlated with pre-operative ones. Fatty change in individual muscles was not associated with post-operative range of motion. Greater post-operative SSC fatty change was independently associated with lower patient-reported outcome measure scores. All patients with mild SSC fatty change or those treated with an onlay component achieved the MCID. In contrast, MCID achievement rates in patients with severe SSC fatty change treated with an inlay component were 56% for the American Shoulder and Elbow Surgeons score and 44% for the Constant Murley score.ConclusionGreater fatty change progression in the ISP and SSC was associated with the use of an inlay humeral component. An association between post-operative fatty change progression and clinical outcomes was observed in the SSC but not in the other rotator cuff muscles.

Fatty change of the rotator cuff muscles is an important factor influencing treatment decisions for shoulder disorders. Although the shoulder function after reverse total shoulder arthroplasty (rTSA) is primarily driven by the deltoid muscle function, with a reduced contribution from the rotator cuff muscles compared with the native shoulder,1 residual rotator cuff function still contributes to rotational function. Fatty change of the rotator cuff muscles has been reported to progress over time in the absence of repair.7,14,36 It has also been associated with tear progression,16 an increased risk of retear,6,12 and reduced muscle strength.10 Earlier surgical intervention prior to fatty change may be associated with improved post-operative outcomes.34 Several studies have reported the influence of pre-operative fatty change on post-operative outcomes following rTSA.46,49 The relationship between fatty change and implant-related factors has also been investigated.23,39 However, fatty change progression differs among individual muscles,12 and its clinical relevance after rTSA remains unclear. Given the distinct advantages and limitations of inlay and onlay humeral designs, further evidence is needed to guide implant selection and optimize clinical outcomes. Furthermore, studies addressing the causes and clinical implications of post-rTSA fatty change progression are limited,45 and no previous study has investigated whether the relationship between post-operative fatty changes in individual rotator cuff muscles and clinical outcomes differs between inlay and onlay humeral designs.

The primary aim of this study was to examine the association between post-operative fatty change progression in individual rotator cuff muscles and humeral component design after rTSA. A secondary aim was to evaluate associations among post-operative muscle quality, humeral component design, and clinical outcomes. We hypothesized that post-operative muscle quality would differ among individual rotator cuff muscles and would be associated with the humeral component design.

Methods

Patients

Between 2019 and 2023, a retrospective single-institution study included 203 patients with large to massive rotator cuff tears (RCTs) or cuff tear arthropathy who underwent rTSA. Among these, 116 patients without glenoid-sided lateralization were selected to evaluate the influence of humeral-sided lateralization. Fifty-seven cases were excluded for surgical reasons, including concomitant muscle or tendon transfer (n = 43), unfamiliar humeral components (n = 7), or complications such as recurrent dislocation (n = 4), periprosthetic fracture (n = 2), and infection (n = 1). Cases with irreparable subscapularis (SSC) tendon tears (n = 3) were also excluded to avoid the potential confounding effect of repair status. Additional exclusions included follow-up less than 2 years (n = 12), including 3 patients who were lost to follow-up, and absence of post-operative computed tomography (CT) or magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) examinations (n = 6), which were part of the standard post-operative evaluation protocol for rTSA at our institution. Consequently, 41 shoulders in 41 patients were included in the final analysis. Radiologic evaluation

Rotator cuff muscle quality was evaluated pre-operatively and at 2 years post-operatively using the Goutallier classification13 on CT or MRI (Fig. 2). Fatty change progression was defined as an increase of at least 1 grade post-operatively. Grades 0 to 2 were considered mild and grades 3 to 4 severe. Because grade 4 is the highest grade on the 0 to 4 scale, cases with a baseline grade of 4 were not evaluable for progression. Fatty change was assessed on sagittal oblique images at a level of 13.5 mm medial to the suprascapular notch to minimize implant-related halo artifacts, based on prior evidence showing a strong correlation with more medial assessments.47 Two shoulder experts independently reviewed all CT and MRI images. Surgical technique

All procedures were performed by 2 senior surgeons (G.M. and T.A.) under general anesthesia using a deltopectoral approach with the patient in the supine position. The proximal portion of the pectoralis major was temporarily released and subsequently restored to its native insertion. Intraoperative assessment demonstrated that no intact infraspinatus (ISP) was observed, partial SSC tears were present in 9 patients, and no complete tears of the ISP, SSC, or teres minor (Tm) were identified. Partial SSC tears were identified in 9 patients. In all cases, the SSC was detached and repaired to the lesser tuberosity with the arm positioned in 40° of external rotation (ER). A standard baseplate and a 36-mm glenosphere were used in all patients. The selection of inlay or onlay humeral components was determined by the operating surgeons without predefined criteria. Patients were therefore treated with either inlay (Aequalis Reversed II, Stryker, Kalamazoo, MI, USA) or onlay (Ascend Flex System, Stryker, Kalamazoo, MI, USA) components, defined as the inlay and onlay groups, respectively. The humeral component was implanted with 20° of retroversion.

Post-operative management

All patients underwent supervised rehabilitation once or twice weekly for 6 months. The operated arm was immobilized in a sling in abduction and neutral rotation for 3 weeks, except during rehabilitation. Pendulum and elbow mobilization exercises were initiated immediately after surgery. Passive elevation and ER were introduced at 2 weeks, and active forward elevation (FE) and ER were started at 4 weeks. Internal rotation (IR) was restricted to the level of the greater trochanter for 6 weeks. Thereafter, stretching and strengthening exercises for both external and internal rotation were initiated.

Clinical evaluation

Active range of motion (ROM), including FE, ER with the arm at the side, IR, and patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs) were assessed pre-operatively and at 2 years post-operatively. PROMs included the American Shoulder and Elbow Surgeons (ASES) score and the Constant Murley score. IR was evaluated according to the Constant Murley methodology (IR score). Outcomes were analyzed according to implant type (inlay vs. onlay humeral component) and fatty change severity of each rotator cuff muscle, including the supraspinatus (SSP), ISP, Tm, and SSC.

Statistical analysis

All analyses were performed using R software (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria), with statistical significance defined as P < .05. Continuous variables were compared using appropriate parametric or non-parametric tests, and categorical variables were compared using Fisher's exact test. Pre-operative ROMs and PROMs were compared between the implant groups, and standardized mean differences (Cohen's d) were calculated. Post-operative Goutallier grades were analyzed using ordinal logistic regression. For each muscle, the post-operative grade was modeled as the dependent variable, with pre-operative grade and implant design as covariates. Odds ratios with 95% confidence intervals were estimated using proportional odds cumulative logit models. Associations between post-operative muscle quality and clinical outcomes were evaluated using baseline-adjusted multivariable linear regression models including implant design and post-operative Goutallier grade as independent variables, with post-operative ROM and PROMs as outcomes. Additional models excluding fatty change variables were fitted to assess their incremental contribution beyond implant design. Because of the limited sample size, covariates in the primary models were restricted to reduce overfitting. Age, sex, and body mass index (BMI) were included only in sensitivity analyses. Exploratory Minimal Clinically Important Difference (MCID) analyses were performed using a distribution-based approach, with thresholds defined as 0.5 standard deviations of the pre-operative ASES score and Constant Murley score. These analyses were limited to variables independently associated with post-operative PROMs in the primary multivariable models to minimize overinterpretation given the sample size. MCID achievement rates for other variables were evaluated in supplementary analyses. Standardized mean differences (Cohen's d) were calculated to assess baseline differences in pre-operative clinical measures according to implant design. P values were additionally adjusted using the Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate procedure with a false discovery rate of 0.05 in analyses involving multiple comparisons. Two senior shoulder surgeons independently reviewed all radiographs, although they were not blinded to implant design. One surgeon repeated the assessment at least 1 week later and was blinded to the initial evaluation. Interobserver and intraobserver reliability of Goutallier grading were assessed using weighted kappa coefficients with 95% confidence intervals.

Results

Of the 41 patients, 16 were treated with inlay and 25 with onlay humeral components. No major complications, including scapular spine or acromial fractures, were observed. Scapular notching was grade ≤1 in all cases, with no significant differences between groups at the 2-year follow-up. Demographic characteristics, including age, sex, BMI, and follow-up period, did not differ significantly according to humeral component design. Pre-operative clinical measures, including FE, ER, IR score, ASES score, and Constant Murley score, did not differ significantly according to implant design. Image evaluation of fatty change

In ordinal logistic regression analyses of the rotator cuff muscles, use of an onlay component was independently associated with a lower post-operative Goutallier grade in the ISP and SSC (both P < .001). The post-operative Goutallier grade was associated with the corresponding pre-operative value across all muscles (Table III). Clinical outcomes

In multivariable models adjusted for baseline function, an onlay implant design was positively associated with post-operative ER, ASES score, and Constant Murley score (Table IV). These associations remained unchanged after additional adjustment for post-operative Goutallier grades of the SSP, ISP, Tm, and SSC (Table V). No muscle fatty change was associated with post-operative ROM. Greater post-operative SSC fatty change was independently associated with lower post-operative ASES scores and Constant Murley scores, whereas no other muscle showed an association with post-operative PROMs (Table V). Similar results were observed in sensitivity analyses adjusting for age, sex, BMI, and scapular notching, even after correction for multiple testing (Appendix Table A3 and A4). The MCID values for the ASES score and Constant Murley score were 4.1 points and 6.6 points, respectively. All patients with mild SSC fatty change achieved the MCID for both the ASES score and the Constant Murley score. Similarly, MCID achievement was observed in all patients treated with an onlay humeral component. Discussion

The primary finding of this study was that post-operative fatty change progression and its association with the humeral implant design varied among individual rotator cuff muscles. Previous studies have suggested that fatty change in rotator cuff muscles may progress over time under certain conditions and may vary among individual muscles. The relationship with clinical outcomes has been a matter of interest.

The SSP demonstrated the highest prevalence of severe fatty change both pre-operatively and post-operatively, and severity was not associated with implant type. Previous studies have shown that fatty change of the SSP continues to progress in untreated cases or after retear following repair.12,28 Because the present study involved patients with large to massive RCTs treated with rTSA, the SSP was left unrepaired, resembling a non-repaired condition. Consequently, SSP fatty change progression was observed in more than 80% of cases, irrespective of implant type, and these findings showed similar trends to previous reports. However, considering the favorable outcomes after rTSA despite irreparable SSP tears,56 the clinical implications of these findings remain uncertain.

Progression of ISP fatty change appears to vary according to its functional status. Worsening has been reported in cases without repair or with retear,12,13,16 whereas stability has been observed following satisfactory repair12,14 or after anatomic total shoulder arthroplasty.29 From a biomechanical standpoint, a Grammont-type inlay humeral component without glenoid-sided lateralization results in humeral medialization.1

In contrast, lateralized designs have been shown to increase the external rotator moment compared with non-lateralized implants.5,15,24, 25, 26 Although the present study did not clarify whether differences in implant design alter ISP loading or length during daily activities, the onlay humeral implant may be associated with preventing ISP fatty change progression, possibly through a greater ISP moment than the inlay design.

Advanced fatty change of the Tm has been associated with inferior clinical outcomes after rTSA.20,45,49 Therefore, understanding both pre-operative Tm quality and its post-operative course is important in this setting. Previous studies have reported limited Tm fatty change progression following surgery. In patients with an intact Tm undergoing arthroscopic rotator cuff repair, no significant post-operative progression of Tm fatty change has been observed.20 Additionally, compensatory hypertrophy of the Tm has been reported in RCTs involving the ISP.18,44 Although several studies have suggested that post-operative progression of Tm fatty change is uncommon,20,28 its course after rTSA remains unclear. In line with these reports, progression of Tm fatty change was observed in less than one-third of cases in the present cohort, and only 10% of patients progressed to severe post-operative fatty change. In addition, no association was identified between humeral component design and progression of Tm fatty change. Several factors might contribute to these results. The present cohort included no cases with severe Tm fatty change requiring additional muscle transfer, which may have limited post-operative fatty change progression. Alternatively, intrinsic anatomic or structural characteristics of the Tm may contribute to its relative resistance to fatty change.

The SSC fatty change progression has also been reported to be associated with higher retear rates and inferior post-operative outcomes,33,58 supporting the importance of its assessment. Regarding the post-operative course of SSC tears, previous studies have reported inconsistent findings, suggesting that fatty change may be prevented by successful repair27 or may continue to progress to some extent despite repair.41 In anatomic total shoulder arthroplasty, post-operative SCC fatty change has been reported to progress regardless of whether the tendon was managed with a peel or an osteotomy.30 In addition, this progression appears to be influenced by tear size17 and elapsed time, indicating that the severity and post-operative behavior of SSC pathology may vary according to the underlying condition. Consequently, the natural history and post-operative course of SSC fatty change remain controversial. In rTSA, lateralization has been reported to increase the moment of the anterior rotator cuff5 or SSC.24, 25, 26 Accordingly, the relationship between the SSC quality and implant designs, as well as their clinical implications, remains an important issue. In this study, although severe SSC fatty change was rare pre-operatively, progression was common after surgery, especially with inlay humeral components. Previous studies in other joints have demonstrated that muscles affected during surgical exposure may exhibit fatty change progression.52 In addition, progression has been reported to occur to some extent even in repaired muscles, whereas greater progression is observed in unrepaired muscles.55 Although these findings should be interpreted cautiously across different muscles or anatomical regions, detachment and repair of the SSC from the lesser tuberosity may potentially mimic a repaired SSC tear. In addition, insufficient restoration of the SSC moment may functionally resemble incomplete repair. Although these possibilities could not be fully evaluated in the present study, these factors may have been associated with the post-operative progression of SSC fatty change.

In contrast to progression, improvement in Goutallier grade was observed only in a small number of onlay cases for all muscles except the Tm, whereas no improvement was observed in the inlay group. However, the small number of improved cases limits interpretation, and it remains unclear whether these findings reflect true improvement or measurement variability.

Several studies have reported greater improvement in ER with onlay than with inlay humeral components.8,31,35 Although lateralized rTSA may improve ER in cases of ER deficit,3,23 this effect may depend on pre-operative muscle quality of the external rotators, including the ISP and Tm.4,45,49 Therefore, implant design should be interpreted in relation to fatty change of the external rotators when evaluating post-operative outcomes. Severe fatty change of the Tm has been associated with inferior post-operative ER and PROMs after rTSA.4,49 Tm may functionally compensate for ISP deterioration with respect to ER,19,44 whereas pre-operative ISP fatty change appears more closely related to post-operative strength than to ROM.10,29 In our cohort, although the inlay design was associated with progression of ISP fatty change, ER was not related to the ISP Goutallier grade, and Tm quality remained preserved regardless of implant design. Preservation of Tm quality may therefore help maintain post-operative ER. Further evaluation of strength in relation to muscle quality may provide additional insight.

Although the inlay design was associated with progression of SSC fatty change, post-operative IR remained unrelated to implant design or to progression of SSC fatty change. The SSC contributes substantially to shoulder IR.11,51 However, although SSC fatty change may influence IR,40 neither SSC repair42 nor implant design8,35,60 has been associated with post-operative IR after rTSA. Persistent IR limitation after rTSA likely reflects multiple factors,21 including implant impingement22 and posture abnormalities such as kyphosis.37 This multifactorial background may explain why post-operative IR was not associated with SSC fatty change or implant design despite implant-related differences in SSC fatty change progression. Advanced evaluation methods, such as 3-dimensional (3D) dynamic assessment of IR, may provide further insight into this interpretation.38,53

Zhou et al reported superior short-term PROMs with onlay compared with inlay designs.59 In contrast, other studies have reported minimal differences between these designs.31,35 These discrepancies may reflect differences in follow-up duration or implant configuration, suggesting that potential early advantages of onlay designs may diminish over time. Onlay implants may also improve internal rotational strength.60 In addition, the SSC function appears important after rTSA. SSC repair has been associated with improved PROMs, whereas SSC fatty change has been linked to reduced muscle strength and impaired function.10,15,33,58 These findings were similar to our results showing an association between SSC fatty change and post-operative PROMs. This may be explained by the deterioration of SSC quality, which adversely affects IR strength and IR-related activities of daily living.10,51 Therefore, implant selection that minimizes progression of SSC fatty change may help optimize post-operative outcomes.

Exploratory MCID analyses showed similar trends to the primary multivariable regression findings, with post-operative SSC quality and humeral component design associated with clinically meaningful improvement. Given that analyses were limited to variables associated with post-operative PROMs and did not include anchor-based methods, the findings should be interpreted with caution.

Although reproducibility of the Goutallier classification has been reported as inconsistent in some studies,32,43 others have demonstrated good reliability for CT9,43 and MRI47,48 assessment and no significant differences between 2-dimensional and 3D evaluations.54 Post-operative evaluation after rTSA may be complicated by implant-related halo artifacts.2 In the present study, fatty change was assessed on sufficiently medial oblique sagittal images where artifacts were minimal, yielding excellent interobserver and intraobserver reproducibility with high kappa values. Thus, this imaging approach appears to provide a valid assessment of fatty change in our cohort. Although post-operative 3D evaluation remains challenging, future studies using reliable 3D imaging techniques may allow more precise assessment.

This study has several limitations. Although a previous report found no association between rotator cuff muscle volume and clinical outcomes after rTSA,57 muscle volume was not assessed in the present study. In addition, surgeon-directed implant selection and unblinded assessment of fatty changes may have introduced selection and observer bias. The strict inclusion criteria and limited sample size may also have reduced generalizability and increased the risk of a type II error. Larger studies are needed to further clarify the relationship between lateralization and fatty change while accounting for muscle volume and other potential confounders.

Conclusion

Greater fatty change progression in the ISP and SSC was associated with the use of an inlay humeral component. An association between post-operative fatty change progression and clinical outcomes was observed in the SSC but not in the other rotator cuff muscles.
