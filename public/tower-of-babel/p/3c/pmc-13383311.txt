A pleiotropic single-molecule, sustained-release regenerative scaffold enables coordinated repair after ischemic stroke
Authors: Li Ruan, Wenzhe Du, Huaqi Wang, Yifu Ma, Fangling Sun, Feng Tian, Zunjing Liu, Jiajia Xue
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIschemic stroke induces acute oxidative damage followed by prolonged inflammation and incomplete tissue reconstruction, creating a need for local therapies that provide sustained neuroprotection and repair support. Here, an injectable hybrid scaffold is developed that integrates a brain-compliant hydrogel with short electrospun nanofibers for sustained local delivery of morroniside (MOR), a pleiotropic small molecule with antioxidant, anti-inflammatory and pro-regenerative activities. The hydrogel provides early MOR release to mitigate acute injury, while the nanofibers enable sustained delivery together with extracellular-matrix-mimetic cues that support angiogenesis, neurogenesis, and axonal remodeling. In vitro, MOR enhanced endothelial and neuronal survival and promoted their migration and lineage-specific differentiation under ischemic conditions. In a rat cortical stroke model, MOR-loaded scaffolds significantly improved motor recovery, reduced infarct volume, enhanced vascularization, suppressed glial scarring, and facilitated axonal regeneration. Transcriptomic analysis revealed broad microenvironmental reprogramming, including suppression of inflammatory pathways and enrichment of PI3K-AKT-related repair signaling with increased p-PI3K and p-AKT immunofluorescence. These results demonstrate that coupling a structurally engineered scaffold with a small molecule supports multiple repair-associated responses following ischemic stroke and offers a translational strategy for repairing ischemic brain injury.Keywords: Ischemic stroke, Hybrid scaffold, Hydrogels, Short electrospun fibers, MOR

Introduction

Ischemic stroke, the most prevalent subtype of stroke, remains a leading cause of mortality and long-term disability worldwide, affecting more than seven million people each year [1,2]. Although thrombolysis and mechanical thrombectomy can restore perfusion in selected patients, their clinical utility is constrained by narrow therapeutic windows and their inability to mitigate secondary neurodegeneration or compensate for insufficient endogenous repair [[3], [4], [5]]. As a result, most patients exhibit incomplete functional recovery, highlighting the pressing need for therapeutic strategies that couple rapid neuroprotection with sustained support for tissue regeneration [6].

The post-stroke regenerative process is inherently dynamic and progresses through multiple, temporally overlapping phases. The acute phase is dominated by excessive oxidative stress, excitotoxicity, and widespread neuronal death, followed by a subacute phase characterized by neuroinflammation, glial activation, and delayed apoptosis [[7], [8], [9], [10]]. As the lesion microenvironment begins to shift toward recovery, endogenous neural stem and progenitor cells initiate neurogenesis, angiogenesis, axonal sprouting, and synaptic remodeling, processes essential for reconstructing neural circuits and restoring function [[11], [12], [13]]. Injectable biomaterial scaffolds have emerged as a promising strategy to modulate the evolving post-stroke microenvironment, as their physicochemical properties and cargo-release profiles can be rationally engineered to match the evolving biological demands of the injured tissue [[14], [15], [16]]. In current designs, this is often achieved by incorporating multiple therapeutics (e.g., small molecules and biologics such as growth factors) within hydrogels, as well as microspheres, nanoparticles, or micelles embedded within the hydrogels [[17], [18], [19], [20]]. Such composited systems provide controlled, phase-specific release via differential diffusivity or material degradation kinetics [[21], [22], [23], [24]]. However, such multi-component scaffolds inevitably introduce structural and compositional complexity and require incorporation of biologics with limited stability and potential immunogenicity, posing substantial challenges for chemistry, manufacturing, and control (CMC), and ultimately for clinical translation.

Morroniside (MOR), an iridoid glycoside derived from Cornus officinalis, offers a compelling alternative due to its broad and phase-relevant pharmacological activities [25]. In the acute phase of stroke, MOR acts as a potent antioxidant, scavenging reactive oxygen species and upregulating endogenous enzymes such as superoxide dismutase and glutathione peroxidase to reduce oxidative damage and neuronal apoptosis [26,27]. MOR also stabilizes the blood-brain barrier by maintaining tight junction integrity and reducing edema and vascular permeability [25]. During the subacute phase, MOR attenuates neuroinflammation by inhibiting microglial and astrocytic activation and suppressing pro-inflammatory cytokines including tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), interleukin-1β (IL-1β), and IL-6 [26,27]. In the regenerative phase, MOR promotes neural stem/progenitor-cell proliferation and neuronal differentiation in the subventricular zone and peri-infarct cortex [28,29], enhances neurite extension, axonal sprouting, and synaptogenesis, and exerts pro-angiogenic effects that support revascularization and tissue remodeling [30,31]. Thus, MOR represents a single small-molecule therapeutic capable of modulating distinct biological processes across multiple phases of post-stroke recovery.

Motivated by this unique alignment between MOR’s stage-specific activities and the temporal trajectory of brain repair, we developed a hydrogel-nanofiber composite scaffold engineered for sustained, long-term release of MOR. The injectable hydrogel provides a soft, tissue-compliant matrix that conforms to the irregular geometry of stroke cavities and enables an early burst release of MOR, ideal for counteracting acute oxidative stress and inflammation while preserving blood-brain barrier integrity. Embedded within this matrix, biodegradable short electrospun nanofibers composed of polycaprolactone and type I collagen (PCL/COL-I) supply extracellular-matrix-mimetic mechanical cues and facilitate sustained, localized release of MOR over extended periods to support neurogenesis, axonal outgrowth, angiogenesis, and synaptic reconstruction. Compared with previously reported multi-component constructs, this single-molecule scaffold reduces formulation complexity while integrating sustained local MOR delivery with structural support for post-stroke repair.

Results and discussion

The angiogenetic and neuroprotective efficacy of MOR

Post-ischemic neovascularization is essential for restoring micro-perfusion in the ischemic penumbra [32,33]. To evaluate the angiogenic capacity, an oxygen-glucose deprivation (OGD) model was established by culturing human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs) in glucose-free medium under hypoxic conditions (<1% O2) for 1 h. The migratory activity of HUVECs was first assessed using a scratch assay. OGD-treated cells were cultured in 24-well plates until forming an adherent monolayer, after which a linear wound was created and different concentrations of MOR (i.e., 0.2, 0.6, 1.2, and 1.9 mM) were added, with cells without MOR as the Blank control group. After 48 h, MOR treatment significantly accelerated wound closure in a concentration-dependent manner within 0.2–1.2 mM, while 1.9 mM showed no further enhancement compared with 1.2 mM (Fig. 1a and b), demonstrating the pro-migratory effect of MOR on endothelial cells.

Cell proliferation was then evaluated by culturing OGD-treated HUVECs with different concentrations of MOR, followed by Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) analysis at day 1 and day 3. MOR treatment induced a dose-dependent increase in cell activity (Fig. 1c), confirming its proliferative effect on HUVECs. Angiogenic activity was also evaluated using a tube formation assay, where OGD-treated HUVECs were cultured on Matrigel with different concentrations of MOR for 4 h. MOR treatment significantly enhanced vascular network formation, with increased tube length, branching points, and tube numbers in a concentration-dependent manner from 0.2 to 1.2 mM, while 1.9 mM showed no additional effect (Fig. 1d and e; Fig. S1). These results demonstrate that MOR promotes endothelial migration, proliferation, and vascular network assembly under ischemic conditions, highlighting its potent pro-angiogenic activity.

We then investigated the neurogenic potential of MOR using SH-SY5Y cells with neuron-like characteristics. The cells were subjected to OGD for 1 h and subsequently cultured with different concentrations of MOR. CCK-8 analysis at day 1 and day 3 showed significantly higher optical densities (O.D.) in the MOR-treated groups than those in the blank control (Fig. S2; Fig. 1f). Neuronal differentiation was further assessed by culturing SH-SY5Y cells for 10 days in the presence or absence of 10 μM all-trans retinoic acid (RA), a differentiation inducer, with or without MOR treatment. MOR promoted neurite elongation and morphological transition toward neuron-like phenotypes (Fig. 1g and h; Fig. S3). Quantitative analysis revealed that 1.2 mM MOR produced the greatest increase in average neurite length, with no significant difference from 1.9 mM, both with and without RA, indicating enhanced differentiation and neurite extension. These results highlight the neurogenic activity of MOR and its potential to promote neurovascular repair.

Physicochemical properties of the hybrid scaffolds and release kinetics of MOR

To enable controlled release of MOR, a hybrid scaffold was fabricated by dispersing 1 mM MOR and 3 mg/mL MOR-loaded short electrospun PCL/COL-I nanofibers (average length of 93.3 ± 39.3 μm, Figs. S4 and S5) in an injectable hydrogel made from thiolated hyaluronic acid/heparin-gelatin and crosslinked with polyethylene glycol diacrylate (PEGDA) (Scheme 1; Fig. 2a). The encapsulation efficiency (EE) and drug loading content (DLC) of MOR in both the hydrogel and short fiber (per scaffold: 350 μL) were separately quantified by high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) analysis. The hydrogel exhibited EE of 89.08 ± 2.91% with DLC of 3.61 ± 0.12%, whereas the short fiber showed EE of 44.17 ± 5.92% with DLC of 4.21 ± 0.56% (Fig. S6). Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images showed uniaxially aligned arrays of the as-spun nanofibers with smooth surface and uniform diameter, measuring ca. 406 ± 29 nm for pristine fibers (Fig. S7) and ca. 445 ± 21 nm for MOR-loaded fibers (MOR-Fibers) (Fig. 2b). Upon cryosection and ultrasonic dispersion, short MOR-Fibers were obtained. HUVECs were cultured in medium containing different concentrations of short MOR-Fibers to evaluate the cytocompatibility. The results showed that a concentration of 3 mg/mL was appropriate for dispersing short nanofibers in the hydrogel precursor, as 5 mg/mL inhibited endothelial cell proliferation (Fig. S8). After embedding short fibers, the average pore size of the hydrogel increased from ca. 57 μm to ca. 96 μm in the hybrid scaffold (Fig. 2c; Fig. S9), enhancing porosity and facilitating nutrient exchange and cell infiltration. Fluorescence imaging of rhodamine B-labeled fibers confirmed their uniform dispersion throughout the hydrogel matrix (Fig. 2c; Fig. S10).

An injectable anisotropic nanofiber/hydrogel hybrid scaffold co-loaded with MOR supports post-stroke repair through a simple and controllable material system. Following administration in an ET-1-induced stroke model, the scaffold is associated with M2 macrophage polarization (anti-inflammation), enhanced CD31-positive vessel formation and reduced GFAP-positive gliosis (angiogenesis), and improved axonal regeneration and remyelination (neurogenesis), ultimately facilitating ischemic brain repair.

For hydrogels designed for brain repair, the injectability, brain-compliant stiffness, swelling behavior, and degradation rate are critical for ensuring safe delivery and long-term functionality in vivo. We evaluated the gelation state, injectability, and extrusion force of the hybrid scaffolds at 0, 10, and 20 min (Figs. S11 and S12). After incubation at 37 °C for 10 min, the hybrid scaffold remained in a flowing state without complete gelation, maintaining injectability through a clinically relevant 26 G needle as well as through a microsyringe for intracranial injection in vivo. The corresponding extrusion forces were approximately 2.8 N and 6.2 N, respectively. The hybrid scaffolds achieved complete gelation for 20 min at 37 °C. At this stage, the scaffold still retained injectability through a clinically relevant 26 G needle, with an extrusion force of approximately 7.4 N. However, it could no longer be injected through the microsyringe for intracranial administration in vivo. Therefore, the hydrogel precursor solution was pre-incubated at 37 °C for 10 min prior to injection through microsyringe for intracranial administration in vivo, facilitating in situ gelation under physiological conditions after delivery into the brain tissue. From the rheological measurements, pristine hydrogel and hybrid scaffolds showed comparable storage moduli (G’), ranging from 140 to 146 Pa (Fig. S13), indicating that the incorporation of short fibers did not significantly affect the scaffold stiffness. This range was consistent with the mechanical compliance of native brain tissue, which is beneficial for minimizing mechanical mismatch and reducing secondary tissue damage [34]. The fully gelled hybrid scaffold exhibited shear-thinning behavior (Fig. S14). Furthermore, the equilibrium swelling ratio in PBS decreased from 21.9 ± 2.7% in the pristine hydrogel to 14.5 ± 3.0% in the hybrid scaffold (Fig. S15), indicating that the fiber incorporation restricted volumetric expansion and thereby reduced the risk of elevated intracranial pressure. After soaking in PBS for 1 month, the pristine hydrogel retained only 25.5 ± 3.3% of its mass, whereas the hybrid scaffold retained 39.3 ± 11.1% due to slower degradation of the embedded nanofibers (Fig. 2d). Importantly, this degradation period over one month ensures structural stability within lesion cavity, supporting sustained tissue regeneration and functional recovery.

The release of MOR from hybrid scaffold + MOR (350 μL) was assessed over 4 weeks in PBS, which was quantified using HPLC. The MOR-loaded hydrogel without short fibers (Hydrogel + MOR) served as the control group. As shown in Fig. 2e, both groups exhibited an initial burst release, with 19.8 ± 1.5% and 18.2 ± 2.7% of MOR released within the first 7 days. The hybrid scaffold exhibited a sustained drug release profile, characterized by an initial relatively rapid release followed by a sustained, diffusion-limited release extending over 4 weeks. After 4 weeks, the cumulative MOR release reached 113.8 ± 9.1 μg for the Hydrogel + MOR group and 157.1 ± 23.7 μg for the Scaffold + MOR group. Importantly, during the subsequent 3 weeks, MOR continued to be released from the hybrid scaffold at a steady rate. These results suggest that the incorporation of nanofibers enhanced MOR retention and delayed release, likely by introducing additional diffusion barriers and strengthening drug-matrix interactions.

Modulation of inflammatory response, angiogenesis, and neuronal cell behavior

Following ischemic stroke, excessive activation of pro-inflammatory microglia and macrophages exacerbates tissue damage and impedes neural regeneration, highlighting the importance of effective immune modulation in establishing a regenerative microenvironment [35,36]. To evaluate the immunomodulatory effect of the scaffolds, RAW264.7 cells were subjected to OGD for 1 h and then co-incubated with pristine scaffold or MOR-loaded scaffold (Scaffold + MOR). Following incubation for 24 h, cells were polarized toward classically activated (M1) macrophages by lipopolysaccharide (LPS, 100 ng/mL) or toward alternatively activated (M2) macrophages by IL-4 and IL-10 (20 ng/mL each) for 72 h. The untreated cells served as the control group. Reverse transcription quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR) was then used to analyze M1 macrophages markers (cluster of differentiation 86 (CD86), TNF-α) and M2 macrophages markers (CD206). As shown in Fig. 3a, compared with the LPS group and pristine scaffold, Scaffold + MOR markedly downregulated CD86 and TNF-α, indicating inhibition of pro-inflammatory polarization. In parallel, compared with the IL-4/IL-10 group and pristine scaffold, Scaffold + MOR significantly upregulated CD206 expression, demonstrating its efficacy in promoting anti-inflammatory and pro-regenerative phenotypes. Importantly, such early-phase immune modulation is critical to minimize secondary damage after ischemia and to accelerate the transition toward tissue repair. Together with HPLC analysis of released MOR, these in vitro assays indicate that released MOR retained measurable structural integrity and biological activity under the tested conditions (Fig. S16).

Revascularization is essential for restoring nutrient delivery and supporting neural regeneration following stroke [37,38]. To evaluate the angiogenic potential of the scaffold under ischemic conditions, HUVECs were subjected to OGD for 1 h and then cultured with culture medium (Control), pristine scaffold (Scaffold), or MOR-loaded scaffold (Scaffold + MOR). Cell proliferation was assessed on days 1, 3, 5, and 7 using the CCK-8 assay. As shown in Fig. 3b, both Scaffold and Scaffold + MOR groups promoted higher proliferation compared with Control. Notably, at days 3 and 5, Scaffold + MOR further enhanced cell activity compared with Scaffold, attributable to the pro-angiogenic effect of MOR. Live/dead staining at day 7 confirmed the enhanced cell viability in Scaffold and Scaffold + MOR groups, showing minimal cell death (Fig. 3c). Functional angiogenesis was evaluated by tube formation assay. Scaffolds were incubated in serum-free medium for 24 h, and the extracts were collected to treat OGD-injured HUVECs seeded on Matrigel. After 6 h of incubation, cells in the Scaffold + MOR group formed extensive capillary-like networks with greater branching complexity and tubular integrity compared to Control and Scaffold groups (Fig. 3d). From the quantitative analysis, the Scaffold + MOR group showed significantly higher vessel area, total tube length, and junction number, while the Scaffold group showed no significant difference from Control (Fig. 3e). Together, these results demonstrate that the MOR-loaded hybrid scaffold enhanced endothelial proliferation, viability, and functional angiogenesis.

Following stroke, promoting neuronal cell migration into the lesion core and supporting their survival and differentiation are essential for functional recovery [39,40]. To assess the neural regenerative potential of the scaffold, SH-SY5Y neuroblastoma cells were subjected to OGD to simulate ischemic injury and subsequently evaluated for migration, proliferation, and differentiation. In a Transwell migration assay, OGD-treated cells were seeded in the upper chamber, while the lower wells were filled with culture medium containing extracts from different treatment groups. After 24 h, the number of migrated cells was significantly increased in both the MOR and Scaffold + MOR groups compared to the pristine scaffold group, indicating that MOR promoted chemotactic migration of neuronal cells (Fig. 4a and b). Cell proliferation was monitored on days 1, 3, 5, and 7 using the CCK-8 assay. As shown in Fig. 4c, MOR improved proliferation relative to the Control group, consistent with its known neuroprotective effects. Notably, the Scaffold + MOR group showed significantly higher proliferation than the pristine scaffold, confirming the contribution of sustained MOR release. Live/dead staining further confirmed high cell viability across all groups, with significantly higher viability observed in the Scaffold + MOR group compared to the pristine scaffold (Fig. 4d).

For neuronal differentiation, OGD-treated cells were cultured in standard medium or in medium supplemented with 10 μM RA, while the scaffolds were placed in Transwell inserts. After 10 days of culture, neuron-specific class III beta-tubulin (Tuj1) immunostaining revealed that even in the absence of RA, the Scaffold + MOR group exhibited markedly longer neurite extensions than other groups (Fig. 4e and f; Figs. S17 and S18), indicating that MOR directly promoted neuronal differentiation under ischemic conditions. In the presence of RA, neurite outgrowth in the Scaffold + MOR group was further enhanced, with an average neurite length of 87.8 ± 28.1 μm, significantly greater than those in the Control (43.9 ± 14.3 μm) and pristine scaffold (45.3 ± 16.2 μm) groups (Fig. 4e and f). Collectively, these results demonstrate that the MOR-loaded hybrid scaffold effectively promoted neuronal migration, proliferation, and differentiation under ischemic conditions, highlighting its potential to support neural regeneration in the ischemic brain.

Functional recovery and tissue remodeling in a stroke model

To evaluate the therapeutic efficacy of the scaffold in vivo, a rat model of cortical ischemic stroke was established by stereotactic injection of endothelin-1 (ET-1) into the motor cortex. Three days post-injury, animals received intracortical injections of PBS (Control group), pristine scaffold (Scaffold), or MOR-loaded scaffold (Scaffold + MOR). After evaluation using a 7-day tail suspension test in rats, MOR-loaded scaffolds treatment improved abnormal behavioral manifestations in a concentration-dependent manner within 0.2–1.2 mM, while 1.9 mM showed no further enhancement in forelimb flexion and body twisting compared with 1.2 mM. Therefore, 1.2 mM was selected as the final implantation dose of MOR (Fig. S19). Subsequently, the experimental timeline and surgical procedure are shown in Fig. 5a and b. Motor function was assessed using the ladder rung walking test and rotarod performance on days 1, 7, and 28 post-treatment. As shown in Fig. 5c and Videos S1-S7, the Scaffold + MOR group exhibited a substantial reduction in error rate during the ladder test. Typically, at day 7, both Control and Scaffold groups showed high error rates (ca. 15.3%), whereas the MOR-treated group showed a reduced rate of 12.7 ± 4.9%. This improvement continued over time, reaching 4.9 ± 3.5% by day 28, compared to 11.2 ± 1.6% (Control) and 8.8 ± 0.6% (Scaffold). Similarly, the rotarod test revealed significantly prolonged motor endurance in the Scaffold + MOR group, with fall latency increasing from 15.8 ± 4.8 s on day 7 to 22.1 ± 7.3 s on day 28, 1.9-fold and 1.5-fold higher than Control and Scaffold groups, respectively.

Histological analysis at day 28 further confirmed structural improvement. Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining revealed large infarct cavity in the Control group, which were visibly reduced in the Scaffold + MOR group with more cellular infiltration (Fig. 5d). Quantitative analysis showed a 69.4 ± 2.4% reduction in cavity area with MOR treatment, which was 16.4% and 4.2% smaller than those in the Control and Scaffold groups, respectively (Fig. S20). Bielschowsky’s silver staining was used to evaluate axonal integrity. Limited axonal fibers were observed in the Control group, while the Scaffold and Scaffold + MOR groups displayed progressively denser axonal networks (Fig. 5e). Quantitatively, axon density in the Scaffold + MOR group was 2.1-fold and 1.2-fold greater than in the Control and Scaffold groups, respectively (Fig. S21), although still below levels observed in the Sham group. These results indicate that the MOR-loaded scaffold significantly enhanced both functional recovery and structural brain repair after ischemic injury. The therapeutic benefit could be attributed to a combination of mechanical support, sustained MOR release, and facilitation of host cell infiltration.

Axonal regeneration and remyelination are critical for restoring functional neural circuits following stroke [41,42]. To elucidate the anatomical basis underlying functional recovery, axonal regeneration and remyelination were evaluated by immunofluorescence staining for neurofilament 200 (NF200, axonal marker) and myelin basic protein (MBP, myelination marker) in both infarct and peri-infarct regions at 28 days post-treatment. In the infarct core, the Control group exhibited sparse axonal structures and minimal MBP expression (Fig. 6a). Scaffold implantation moderately increased axonal ingrowth, while the Scaffold + MOR group showed the most extensive NF200 staining. Quantitative analysis revealed that the mean fluorescence intensity of NF200 in the Scaffold + MOR group was 6.0-fold higher than in the Control group and 1.3-fold higher than in the Scaffold group (Fig. 6b). Similarly, the number of NF200-positive cells was 5.8-fold and 1.3-fold higher than in the Control and Scaffold groups, respectively (Fig. 6c). Despite these improvements, NF200 expression remained below that of the Sham group, indicating incomplete structural restoration. MBP staining showed limited remyelination in all injured groups. While the Scaffold + MOR group exhibited slightly higher MBP expression and cell counts than the other treatment groups, the difference was not statistically significant in the infarct core. These findings suggest that the treatment promoted axonal ingrowth more robustly than myelin restoration in the severely damaged infarct core.

In peri-infarct regions, more pronounced effects were observed. Neurons in the Control group exhibited extensive degeneration and apoptosis. Implantation of the pristine scaffold partially preserved neuronal morphology in the peri-infarct region and facilitated neuronal migration into the lesion cavity. The MOR-loaded scaffold further enhanced neuronal integrity in surrounding tissue, with a greater number of neurons actively migrating into the cavity and exhibiting early signs of remyelination. Quantitative analysis showed that the fluorescence intensities of NF200 and MBP were 1.9-fold and 1.5-fold higher, respectively, in the Scaffold + MOR group compared to the Control group, and 1.4-fold and 1.3-fold higher than those in the Scaffold group (Fig. 6d and e). The corresponding increases in NF200- and MBP-positive cell counts further support scaffold-mediated neuronal maturation beyond the lesion boundary. Taken together, these findings demonstrate that the MOR-loaded hybrid scaffold promoted axonal regeneration and facilitated remyelination, particularly in peri-lesional areas. These neuroanatomical improvements contribute to the observed functional recovery following stroke.

In parallel, we evaluated the capability of the scaffold to modulate the post-stroke microenvironment, focusing on vascular remodeling and glial scar formation, two processes that critically influence neuroregeneration. Angiogenesis restores oxygen and nutrient supply to ischemic tissue, whereas glial scarring, which arises from the proliferation and hypertrophy of reactive astrocytes and the accumulation of extracellular matrix molecules such as chondroitin sulfate proteoglycans, can form a physical and biochemical barrier that inhibits axonal regrowth and tissue remodeling [43,44]. To assess these results, brain sections were stained for CD31 (an endothelial marker) and glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP, a marker of reactive astrocytes) at 28 days post-treatment. In the infarct core, the Control group exhibited sparse CD31-positive vessels and strong GFAP expression, indicative of limited angiogenesis and pronounced glial activation (Fig. 7a). Scaffold implantation moderately increased vascular density and reduced GFAP levels. These effects were further enhanced in the Scaffold + MOR group, which displayed abundant, well-organized vasculature and substantially attenuated GFAP staining. Quantitative analysis showed that CD31 fluorescence intensity increased 1.7-fold in the Scaffold group compared to Control, and an additional 3.1-fold in the Scaffold + MOR group relative to the scaffold-only group (Fig. 7b). GFAP expression was significantly reduced in the MOR-treated group (Fig. 7c), indicating effective suppression of reactive astrogliosis.

In the peri-infarct regions, similar improvements were observed. Blood vessels in the Control and Scaffold groups were thin and disorganized, and GFAP-positive cells were densely distributed along the cavity-tissue boundary. In contrast, the Scaffold + MOR group exhibited significantly greater vascular density and fewer GFAP-positive cells (Fig. 7d and e). Quantitative analysis revealed that the number of GFAP-positive cells in the Scaffold + MOR group was reduced by 33.6% compared to that in the Scaffold group, and by 47.7% compared to the Control group. Similarly, the number of CD31-positive cells was significantly higher in the Scaffold + MOR group than in the Scaffold group, which in turn was higher than in the Control group. Together, these results suggest that the MOR-loaded hybrid scaffold was associated with enhanced angiogenesis and reduced glial scar formation, thereby creating a more permissive microenvironment for neuronal infiltration and tissue remodeling. Despite these promising findings, certain limitations should be acknowledged. The relatively small animal cohort and the absence of additional in vivo control groups, such as free MOR or Hydrogel + MOR, limit definitive attribution of the therapeutic effect to individual scaffold components. Further validation in larger-scale studies will be necessary to confirm reproducibility and translational potential.

Transcriptomic insights into scaffold-mediated regeneration

To elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying the therapeutic effects of the hybrid scaffold, transcriptomic analysis was performed on brain tissues collected 28 days after treatment. Principal component analysis (PCA) revealed clear separation between the untreated Control and Scaffold + MOR group, indicating scaffold + MOR-associated transcriptional reprogramming (Fig. 8a). Notably, replicates within the control and scaffold groups clustered closely, suggesting that the scaffold possessed favorable biocompatibility. Hierarchical clustering revealed distinct expression profiles, with a notable increase in protein-coding genes in the Scaffold + MOR group (Fig. 8b). Differential gene expression was compared between the Scaffold + MOR group and the untreated Control group. Volcano plot analysis identified 239 significantly upregulated and 1402 downregulated genes in the Scaffold + MOR group (Fig. 8c), indicating substantial transcriptomic remodeling. In contrast, only minimal transcriptional changes were observed between the Control and Scaffold groups, supporting the biocompatibility and low immunogenicity of the pristine scaffold. Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis showed that the differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were primarily associated with cytokine regulation, wound healing, and extracellular matrix organization (Fig. 8d). Kyoto encyclopedia of genes and genomes (KEGG) pathway analysis further identified significant enrichment in the phosphoinositide 3-kinase/protein kinase B (PI3K-AKT) signaling pathway, along with nuclear factor kappa-B (NF-κB), cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction, Ras-related protein 1 (Rap1), and nucleotide-binding oligomerization domain (NOD)-like receptor signaling pathways (Fig. 8e). Among these, the PI3K-AKT signaling pathway exhibited a notable enrichment score, suggesting that it may be one of the pathways involved in scaffold-mediated regeneration. To provide correlative protein-level support, immunofluorescence staining was performed for phosphorylated and total PI3K and AKT. Compared to the Control group, the Scaffold + MOR group showed increased fluorescence intensity for both phosphorylated PI3K (p-PI3K) and AKT (p-AKT) in the infarct core and peri-infarct regions (Fig. 8f and g). Quantitative analysis revealed a 1.2-fold increase in the p-PI3K/PI3K ratio and a 1.6-fold increase in the p-AKT/AKT ratio relative to the Control group (Fig. S22). These data suggest an association between Scaffold + MOR treatment and PI3K-AKT-related repair signaling. This pathway has been widely reported to play key roles in inflammation regulation, angiogenesis, and neuroprotection. However, because RNA-seq was performed at a single time point and immunofluorescence does not establish pathway causality, the present data cannot prove that PI3K-AKT activation mediates the therapeutic effects. Further validation using RT-qPCR, Western blot analysis, and pathway inhibition experiments would be required to establish a causal mechanism. Given that this study focuses on biomaterial design and functional evaluation and is limited by sample availability, such mechanistic investigations will be addressed in future work. Despite the lack of direct evidence for PI3K-AKT activation, the transcriptomic findings provide valuable mechanistic insights into the regenerative effects of the scaffold. The MOR-loaded scaffold was associated with enrichment of pro-repair pathways, including PI3K-AKT signaling, together with reduced inflammatory activity and enhanced angiogenic and neuroregenerative programs. Importantly, these molecular signatures were concordant with the histological and functional improvements observed in vivo, suggesting that the scaffold promotes post-stroke recovery by establishing a permissive regenerative microenvironment.

Conclusion

In this study, we have developed a multifunctional injectable hybrid scaffold composed of short electrospun PCL/COL-I nanofibers embedded within a brain-mimetic hydrogel matrix, co-loaded with MOR, a pharmacologically stable small molecule with clinically relevant regenerative potential. This platform was designed to replicate the structural and mechanical features of brain extracellular matrix while enabling sustained MOR release through differential incorporation in the hydrogel and fiber compartments. In vitro, the scaffold promoted M2 macrophage polarization, enhanced endothelial and neuronal cell migration, and supported neuronal differentiation under ischemic conditions. In a rat stroke model, the MOR-loaded scaffold significantly improved behavioral outcomes, reduced infarct volume, and enhanced axonal regeneration, remyelination, and vascularization while suppressing glial scar formation. Transcriptomic profiling and immunostaining suggested that PI3K-AKT-related signaling may be associated with these repair responses, although causal pathway involvement remains to be validated. Together, these findings demonstrate that the integration of a structurally engineered scaffold with a pleiotropic small molecule exerts coordinated beneficial effects on repair-associated immune, vascular, and neural responses. This strategy offers a clinically translatable platform for promoting functional repair of ischemic brain injury and may serve as a generalizable platform for central nervous system regeneration.

Experimental section

Materials

PCL, Mw = 80,000 g/mol, MOR, hexafluoroisopropanol (HFIP), ethanol, and acetic acid were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA). The injectable hydrogel precursor (HyStem-HP) was obtained from Advanced BioMatrix (Carlsbad, CA, USA). COL-I was supplied by Solarbio Life Sciences (Beijing, China). Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) and SH-SY5Y medium were purchased from HyClone (Logan, UT, USA). Fetal bovine serum (FBS) and Live/Dead viability/cytotoxicity assay kits were obtained from Thermo Fisher Scientific (Waltham, MA, USA). Antibodies including Tuj1 (1:500, BioLegend, Cat. No. 801202), NF200 (1:500, Sigma-Aldrich, Cat. No. N4142), MBP (1:500, Abcam, Cat. No. ab40390), CD31 (1:200, Abcam, Cat. No. ab182981) and GFAP (1:500, Cell Signaling Technology, Cat. No. 12389) were obtained from Abcam (Cambridge, UK) and Thermo Fisher. Deionized water was prepared using a Milli-Q purification system (Millipore, Burlington, MA, USA).

The bioactivity of MOR

All experimental groups were treated with MOR at the following concentrations: 0.2, 0.6, 1.2, and 1.9 mM in drug-containing culture medium. In the OGD model, HUVECs were exposed to OGD for 1 h in an anaerobic chamber (<1% O2) before subsequent experiments. Specifically, HUVECs subjected to OGD were seeded into 24-well plates (1 × 104 cells/well). A linear wound was generated in the cell monolayer using a sterile 200 μL pipette tip. Different concentrations of MOR were added to co-incubate for 48 h. Then cells were fixed (3% glutaraldehyde), permeabilized (0.1% Triton X-100), blocked (1% BSA), and stained with Alexa Fluor 555-phalloidin and DAPI. Quantification analysis of remaining wound area was calculated by using ImageJ. Meanwhile, post-OGD, cells were seeded into 24-well plates (1 × 104 cells/well) and coincubated with different concentrations of MOR. Cell proliferation was assessed on day 1 and 3 using the CCK-8 assay. To assess angiogenic potential, Matrigel was diluted with serum-free DMEM (1:1 ratio, v/v) on ice and added into 96-well plates (50 μL) at 37 °C for 45 min to allow gel polymerization. OGD-treated HUVECs (3 × 104 cells/well) were seeded onto Matrigel and incubated with different concentrations of MOR for 4 h. Then endothelial networks were stained with calcein-AM, and tube length, branching points, and tube count were quantified using Image J.

Following OGD preconditioning, SH-SY5Y were plated in 24-well culture plates (1 × 104 cells/well) and coincubated with various concentrations of MOR. Proliferation kinetics were evaluated at day 1 and day 3 intervals using the CCK-8 assay. For neuronal differentiation, SH-SY5Y preconditioned under OGD were seeded into 24-well plates (5 × 103 cells/mL) and coincubated with different concentrations of MOR and RA (10 μM) for 10 days. Then, cells were fixed and immunostained with Tuj1 antibody followed by Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated secondary antibodies. Cell nuclei were counterstained with DAPI. Neurite outgrowth was visualized using confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM) and quantitatively analyzed with ImageJ software.

Fabrication and characterization of hybrid scaffolds

PCL and COL-I (1:1, w/w) were dissolved in HFIP (10%, w/v). For drug loading, 1% (w/v) MOR was added. Electrospinning was conducted at 18 kV with a 1 mL/h flow rate onto a rotating drum (1300 rpm) for 6.5 h. Resulting nanofibers were cryosectioned and ultrasonicated for dispersion (ca. 93.3 ± 39.3 μm length). Hydrogel precursor was prepared by dissolving 10 mg thiol-modified hyaluronic acid/heparin and 10 mg gelatin in PBS. Crosslinking was initiated with 5 mg PEGDA in 0.5 mL PBS at 37 °C for 20 min. For fabricating the Scaffold + MOR group, 1 mM MOR and 3 mg/mL MOR-loaded nanofibers were added to the precursor before crosslinking. Pristine scaffolds (1 mL hydrogel containing 3 mg/mL PCL/COL-I nanofibers) without MOR served as controls.

The morphology and diameter of the nanofibers were examined using scanning electron microscope (Hitachi S4800), while elemental composition was assessed by Energy Dispersive X-ray Spectroscopy (EDS). Surface wettability was evaluated by static water contact angle measurements using deionized water droplets. The morphology of the short nanofibers was characterized by optical microscopy, and the fiber-length distribution following cryosectioning and ultrasonication was quantitatively analyzed using Image J.

Gelation behavior of hybrid scaffolds was recorded at predetermined time points (0, 10, and 20 min) at 37 °C. To evaluate injectability, the hybrid scaffolds at different crosslinking stages were injected through a clinically relevant 26 G needle as well as a microsyringe pump needle (25 μL gas-tight microsyringe). Consistently, the extrusion force required for injection was quantitatively measured using a texture analyzer. In addition, the shear-thinning behavior of the fully gelled hybrid scaffolds (d = 5 mm) was characterized by rheological analysis. Because in vivo administration was performed using the 10 min pre-incubated precursor before complete gelation, injectability was primarily determined by precursor flowability and extrusion force rather than post-extrusion recovery of a fully gelled scaffold. Rheological properties of the hydrogel and hybrid scaffold were characterized using a rheometer under frequency sweeps ranging from 0.1 to 15 Hz at a constant strain of 10%. Swelling behavior was assessed by immersing rhodamine B-labeled hydrogel precursors loaded into cylindrical conduits (2 mm in diameter) in PBS at 37 °C for 24 h. In vitro degradation was quantified by measuring the percentage of weight loss at 5-day intervals in PBS.

Calibration curve for MOR was established using HPLC at a detection wavelength of 240 nm. Serial standard solutions (2.525–505.0 μg/mL) were prepared from MOR stock solution, and peak areas were recorded. A linear regression between concentration and peak area was used to construct the calibration curve (R2 > 0.99). DLC and EE of MOR in the hydrogel and short fiber were quantitatively determined. For the hydrogel, MOR was homogeneously mixed with the hydrogel precursor solution (350 μL, 1 mM MOR) prior to crosslinking, followed by gelation. The formed hydrogels were thoroughly washed with deionized water, and all washing solutions were collected to quantify the amount of unencapsulated MOR. For the short fibers, MOR-loaded fibers (3 mg/mL) were completely dissolved in HFIP (350 μL), and the total MOR content was quantified by HPLC, thereby determining the actual drug loading. Drug release from MOR-loaded hydrogel and hybrid scaffolds (350 μL; 3 mg/mL short fibers; 1 mM MOR) was analyzed using HPLC at predetermined time points between 1 and 28 days, based on a pre-established calibration curve of HPLC. The release study was conducted in 350 μL of PBS (pH 7.4, 37 °C) to provide a small-volume, physiologically buffered environment relevant to local intracerebral delivery. This volume is within the reported order of magnitude of the adult rat cerebrospinal fluid compartment (below 500 μL). Given the aqueous solubility of MOR (∼200 mM), the drug concentration throughout the release period remained far below its solubility limit, ensuring sink conditions and enabling accurate characterization of sustained release behavior. In addition, characteristic HPLC peaks of free MOR (200 μg/mL) and released MOR collected from scaffolds (350 μL, 3 mg/mL short fibers, 1 mM MOR) at day 14 were compared. HPLC analysis confirmed that the released MOR concentration was adjusted to 200 μg/mL, equivalent to the free MOR group, to evaluate whether MOR maintained its structural integrity and stability following sustained release from the scaffolds.

Modulation of macrophage polarization and HUVECs proliferation and angiogenesis by the hybrid scaffolds

RAW264.7 cells were subjected to OGD by culturing in glucose-free medium within an anaerobic chamber (<1% O2) for 1 h. Post-OGD, cells were seeded into 24-well plates (2 × 104 cells/well) and assigned to three treatment groups: Control (no treatment), Scaffold (hydrogel with 3 mg/mL PCL/COL-I nanofibers), and Scaffold + MOR (hydrogel with 1.2 mM MOR and 3 mg/mL PCL/COL-I nanofibers). For polarization studies, cells were treated with LPS (100 ng/mL) to induce M1 macrophages polarization or a combination of IL-4 and IL-10 (20 ng/mL each) to promote M2 macrophages polarization. Scaffolds were co-incubated with the macrophages for 72 h under each condition. Total RNA was extracted and reverse-transcribed into complementary DNA (cDNA). RT-qPCR was performed to assess the expression levels of polarization-related markers: CD86 (M1 macrophages), TNF-α (M1 macrophages) and CD206 (M2 macrophages), using GAPDH as the internal control.

HUVECs were exposed to OGD for 1 h. Different scaffolds (200 μL) were placed into 24-well plates after sterilization, then HUVECs were seeded onto scaffolds with a density of 1 × 104 cells per well. Cell proliferation was assessed on days 1, 3, 5, and 7 using the CCK-8 assay. Cytocompatibility was evaluated on day 7 by Live/Dead staining with calcein-AM and propidium iodide (PI), followed by fluorescence microscopy. To assess angiogenic potential, scaffold extracts were prepared by incubating scaffolds in 500 μL of serum-free DMEM for 24 h, followed by filtration through a 0.22 μm membrane. OGD-treated HUVECs were seeded onto Matrigel and incubated with the respective extracts for 6 h. Capillary-like structures were stained with calcein-AM, and tube length, branching points, and tube count were quantified using ImageJ.

In vitro migration, proliferation and differentiation of SH-SY5Y cells

SH-SY5Y neuroblastoma cells were cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS at 37 °C. To simulate ischemic conditions, cells were subjected to OGD. For migration analysis, a Transwell assay was conducted using inserts with 3 μm pore size in a 24-well plate. After OGD, SH-SY5Y cells (1 × 104) suspended in serum-free medium were seeded into the upper chambers, while the lower wells were filled with complete DMEM containing scaffold extracts. After 24 h, migrated cells on the lower surface of the membrane were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, stained with 0.5% crystal violet, and imaged under an optical microscope. Cell proliferation and cytocompatibility were assessed using the CCK-8 assay (days 1, 3, 5, and 7) and Live/Dead staining (day 7), respectively, observed by fluorescence microscopy. For neuronal differentiation, OGD-treated SH-SY5Y cells were seeded into 24-well plates with differentiation medium containing 10 μM RA. Hybrid scaffolds (200 μL per well) were placed in the upper chambers of Transwell inserts above the cells. After 10 days of co-culture, cells were fixed and immunostained with Tuj1 antibody followed by Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated secondary antibodies. Cell nuclei were counterstained with DAPI. Neurite outgrowth was visualized using CLSM and quantitatively analyzed with ImageJ software.

In vivo ischemic stroke model and scaffold implantation for brain repair

All animals were purchased from SPF Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China) and housed under specific pathogen-free conditions at a controlled temperature of 22 ± 2 °C and relative humidity of 50–60%, with a 12 h light/dark cycle. All animal procedures were approved by the Xuanwu Hospital Ethics Committee (approval number: XW-20231009-1). Adult male Sprague-Dawley rats (280-300 g) were acclimated and trained for one week to walk across a 100-cm horizontal ladder with irregular rungs. Ischemic stroke was induced by stereotactic injection of 800 pmol of ET-1 (in 2 μL of saline) into three cortical sites (anterior-posterior = −1.0 mm, +1.0 mm, +3.0 mm; mediolateral = −2.5 mm; dorsoventral = −2.3 mm). Three days after stroke induction, 10 μL of either PBS or hydrogel precursor solution containing short fibers were injected into the cortex at anterior-posterior = +1.0 mm using a microsyringe at a flow rate of 1.2 μL/min. In a preliminary dose-optimization experiment, the tail suspension test was performed to evaluate neurological deficits after treatment with MOR-loaded scaffolds containing different MOR concentrations (0.2–1.9 mM), and 1.2 mM was selected for subsequent in vivo experiments. Animals were randomly assigned to four groups: (1) Sham (skull exposed without injection), (2) Control (injection of PBS), (3) Scaffold (hydrogel with short fibers), and (4) Scaffold + MOR (hydrogel with both MOR and MOR-loaded short fibers). Six surviving successfully modeled animals per group were included for behavioral analyses. After behavioral testing, three animals per group were used for histology and immunofluorescence, and the remaining three animals per group were used for RNA-seq.

To assess motor function recovery, rats underwent behavioral testing on days 1, 7, and 28 following ischemic stroke model establishment. The ladder rung walking task was used to evaluate forelimb coordination and motor impairment. After a 3-day training period, animals were tested for forelimb errors, defined as paw slips or missteps, and results were reported as error-to-step ratios. Motor coordination was further assessed using the rotarod test conducted at a constant rotation speed of 26 rpm. Each rat performed three trials per session, and the time until the animal fell from the rotating rod was recorded. The average of the three trials was used for analysis.

At 28 days after scaffold injection, rats were transcardially perfused with saline followed by 4% paraformaldehyde. Brains were harvested, fixed, cryoprotected in 30% sucrose, and sectioned at a thickness of 30 μm. H&E staining and Bielschowsky’s silver staining were performed to assess cellular infiltration and nerve fiber integrity at the site of ischemic injury. For immunofluorescence analysis, brain sections were incubated with primary antibodies against NF200, MBP, CD31, and GFAP. Fluorescent images were acquired and quantitatively analyzed using ImageJ software.

For transcriptomic profiling, peri-lesional brain tissue was harvested from anesthetized rats and immediately preserved in RNA stabilization solution. Total RNA was extracted and subjected to high-throughput sequencing. Differential gene expression analysis was performed using DESeq2 with the thresholds of |log2 fold change (FC)| ≥ 1 and p value < 0.05. GO and pathway enrichment analyses were conducted to identify biological processes and signaling cascades associated with scaffold-mediated tissue regeneration. Additional immunostaining was performed to examine phosphorylated and total PI3K and AKT levels as correlative readouts of PI3K-AKT-related signaling.

We numbered the animals and allocated them into groups using a simple randomization of excel-generated random numbers. The experimenters were blinded to the treatments for data collection and analysis. The exclusion criteria were: (i) unsuccessful establishment of the cortical ischemic stroke model (absence of neurological deficits). (ii) accidental death or moribund state during the experimental procedure. During the experimental period, the overall mortality rate of the rat cortical ischemic stroke model was approximately 50%. The causes of death mainly included intraoperative and postoperative complications, such as anesthetic accidents, intracranial hemorrhage, and severe cerebral edema resulting from ischemia-reperfusion injury. Statistical analyses were performed using Origin software, GraphPad Prism 8.0, and R software (v3.4.3). For comparisons between two groups, a t-test was employed. For comparisons involving more than two groups, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used, followed by post-hoc analysis when appropriate.

Statistical analysis

All experiments included at least three parallel samples, and data were presented as the mean ± standard deviation. Statistical analyses were performed using Origin software, GraphPad Prism 8.0, and R software (v3.4.3). For comparisons between two groups, a t-test was employed. For comparisons involving more than two groups, one-way ANOVA was used, followed by post-hoc analysis when appropriate. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05. The level of significance was indicated as: ∗P < 0.05 and n.s., not significant.
