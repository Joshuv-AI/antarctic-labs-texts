Milestones, revisited: Iterative and nonlinear identity development in Gen‐Z
Authors: William Warton, Olivia Bruce, David Moseley, Michelle L. Byrne, Kelly‐Ann Allen, Ainsley Hughes, Beth P. Johnson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study examined how sexual and gender identity development experiences, commonly operationalized as “milestones,” were navigated by Gen‐Z LGBTQ+ young people. Using a life‐history calendar approach, milestone‐related experiences were treated as heuristic sites of meaning‐making rather than fixed developmental benchmarks. Participants were a sample of 51 Australian LGBTQ+ young people (ages 16–26), purposively recruited from a larger quantitative study, who participated in semi‐structured interviews conducted from May to September 2023. Participants reflected on their identity development experiences across childhood and adolescence. Interviews were analyzed using reflexive thematic analysis. Findings indicate that identity‐related experiences were often iterative and context‐dependent, with meanings shifting across time, relationships, and social environments. Rather than unfolding as linear sequences, experiences such as self‐identification, disclosure, and relationships were frequently revisited, deferred, reinterpreted, or resisted. Relational contexts, visibility, and experiences of affirmation or invalidation shaped how and when these experiences acquired significance. The study offers qualitative insight into dimensions of identity development that complement population‐level milestone research and have implications for research, policy, and practice.

INTRODUCTION

Formative sexual and gender identity development (SGID) experiences, commonly termed “milestones,” are events or realizations that mark important moments in a person's understanding and exploration of their LGBTQ+ identity (Baams et al., 2025; Hall et al., 2021). For many young people, these experiences do not occur as discrete, time‐bounded events; instead, they may unfold gradually, iteratively, and be revisited over time (deMayo et al., 2025; Dolan & Garvey, 2024; Hereth et al., 2020; Parmenter et al., 2022). Milestone approaches have been valuable for comparing generational cohorts and documenting population‐level patterns in the timing and sequencing of SGID‐related experiences and their associations with stress and mental health (Baams et al., 2025; Bishop et al., 2024; Katz‐Wise et al., 2017; Puckett et al., 2022; Rendina et al., 2019; Rosario et al., 2011; Scandurra et al., 2021). Systematic reviews of the milestone literature demonstrate a focus on first occurrence and age‐based summaries (see Baams et al., 2025; Hall et al., 2021); however, this focus can under‐represent how experiences are revisited, reinterpreted, or remain absent and can obscure the relational and sociohistorical contexts through which experiences gain meaning. In some contexts, particularly when findings are summarized as average ages or typical sequences, milestone indicators may be read as implying normative progressions, even though such interpretations exceed what the measures themselves are designed to capture. The current study uses life‐history calendar interviews to examine how Gen‐Z LGBTQ+ young people narrate, revisit, and contextualize these experiences across the life course, offering qualitative insight into dimensions of identity development that are not well captured by first‐occurrence indicators alone.

Brief historical overview of scholarship on identity development

Sexual identity development (SID) refers to the ongoing processes of understanding and integrating patterns of sexual attraction, behaviors, and experiences to form one's sexual orientation (Parmenter et al., 2022). A person's sexual orientation is characterized by different factors, such as attraction, sexual behavior, romantic connection, and self‐identification (Mustanski et al., 2014; Savin‐Williams, 2019). Meanwhile, gender identity development (GID) refers to the ongoing process of developing and exploring gender expression (e.g., visual gender presentation) and body (Dolan & Garvey, 2024; Hereth et al., 2020).

Over the past century, scholarship on SGID has shifted from pathologizing LGBTQ+ identities to recognizing them as part of natural human diversity (see Drescher, 2020 for a review). Early 20th‐century conceptualizations of SGID were shaped by religious, legal, and medical discourses that imposed a hetero‐cis‐normative lens, assuming a binary alignment of sexuality, where both gender identity and sexual orientation align with traditional gender roles (Moore & Reynolds, 2018; Robinson & Davies, 2015; Savin‐Williams, 2019). The early work on SGID was often articulated through stage‐based models that assumed relatively linear progressions and universal endpoints (e.g., Cass, 1979; Coleman, 1982; Troiden, 1989). These models were later critiqued for inadequately capturing variability in the lived experiences of SGID and for overlooking sexual identities beyond lesbian/gay, gender identity more broadly, and race/ethnicity, socioeconomic status, age, and cultural context (Cordes Galbraith, 2022; D'Augelli, 1994; Goodrich & Brammer, 2021; Mustanski et al., 2014). Subsequent scholarship beyond these early models has demonstrated social, relational, and contextual influences and heterogeneity in SGID pathways (Bilodeau & Renn, 2005; Floyd & Stein, 2002; Maguen et al., 2002; Rosario et al., 2004; Savin‐Williams, 1998). This shift laid important groundwork for later milestone approaches, which sought document variability in the timing and sequencing of identity‐related experiences at the population level.

Conceptualizing “Milestones”

Milestones measures are not a proxy for LGBTQ+ identity development more generally. Rather than offering a unified theory of SGID, milestone research operationalizes commonly reported first‐occurrence experiences (e.g., initial awareness, self‐identification, disclosure, or engagement in relationships) to situate identity‐related experiences within developmental time. This approach emphasizes that the timing, order, and salience of these experiences may vary across individuals as a function of sociohistorical, demographic, and contextual factors (Baams et al., 2025; Bishop et al., 2024; Layland et al., 2023).

In some contexts, particularly when first‐occurrence indicators are interpreted as benchmarks or achievements, milestone findings may be read in ways that align with broader heteronormative or homonormative narratives emphasizing linear progression, visibility, and definitional closure. Such readings tend to center trajectories culminating in stable self‐identification, disclosure, or relationships, while rendering less visible experiences characterized by ambiguity, nondisclosure, recurrence, or shifts in identity labels (cf. Katz‐Wise, 2015; Katz‐Wise et al., 2024). This limitation is especially salient for populations whose developmental pathways are less well represented in existing milestone research, including nonbinary, gender diverse, and asexual individuals (Dolan & Garvey, 2024; Kelleher et al., 2023, 2025). Moreover, identity‐related experiences, particularly for TGNC individuals, may change in salience or meaning over time, especially during periods of social or medical transition, such as the initiation of gender‐affirming care (Del Toro et al., 2025; Fowler et al., 2024). Importantly, these normative readings exceed what milestone measures themselves are designed to capture. By treating milestone‐related experiences as narrative anchors rather than endpoints, the present study resists these interpretations and foregrounds how identity development is shaped through ongoing negotiation within relational and sociohistorical contexts.

Evolving identities

Research consistently demonstrates that fluidity in sexual and gender identity is both common and multi‐form (Diamond & Butterworth, 2008; Katz‐Wise et al., 2024; Katz‐Wise & Todd, 2022). For some individuals, shifts in identity occur as new language, communities, or interpretive frameworks become available, while others describe contextual movement in how identities are understood or expressed across time and social settings (Cosgrove, 2021; Galupo et al., 2017). For example, plurisexual individuals often report that existing labels inadequately capture their experiences and therefore provide additional context or qualification when describing their identities (Dyar et al., 2015; Galupo et al., 2015). Such patterns are closely tied to broader social change, as evolving cultural vocabularies and practices expand the range of intelligible identity options, with implications that vary by generation and intersectional location (Hagai et al., 2020). Contemporary frameworks, including Sexual Configurations Theory (van Anders, 2015) and the Emergent Model for Nonbinary Identity Development (Dolan & Garvey, 2024), conceptualize identity as comprising multiple interacting dimensions, offering ways to theorize sexuality and gender beyond fixed or singular categories.

These processes are also shaped by the contemporary technological landscape. Digital spaces provide unprecedented access to information, new contexts for identity exploration, and opportunities for connection with other LGBTQ+ people (Cover, 2019; Fox & Ralston, 2016; Noon et al., 2024). Although earlier generations (e.g., Gen‐X and Millennials) also used online platforms for identity exploration, such as Usenet or early forum groups (Dhoest & Van Ouytsel, 2022; McLean & Schubert, 1995), Gen‐Z has come of age in an environment where always‐available, mobile, and highly networked social media platforms are deeply embedded in everyday life. Within this context, Gen‐Z young people are increasingly exploring nontraditional sexual and gender identities (e.g., pansexual, nonbinary) and revising or refining identity labels over time (Bochicchio et al., 2023; Hyde et al., 2019; Marino et al., 2024; Watson et al., 2020). For instance, a recent study conducted by the Kinsey Institute in collaboration with the dating app Feeld found that Gen‐Z users were more likely than older cohorts to report changes in identity labels across time (Lehmiller & Feeld, 2024).

Together, this body of work underscores that SGID is often iterative, context‐dependent, and responsive to shifting social conditions. Queer theory has long argued that identity categories are historically contingent and open to change, deriving meaning through opposition to an imagined “Other” instead of reflecting fixed essences (Butler, 1990; Hagai & Zurbriggen, 2022; Rubin, 1984). Conceptualizing identity development as emergent and ongoing aligns with broader developmental perspectives that emphasize negotiation and change across the life course (Branje et al., 2021; Meeus, 2023). While milestone research has been instrumental in documenting when identity‐related experiences first occur and how timing varies across cohorts, it is less well suited to capturing how identities are continually reinterpreted, renegotiated, or reconfigured over time—particularly within contemporary generational contexts such as those characterizing Gen‐Z.

Process‐oriented alternatives for understanding milestones

Given the shifting landscape in how SGID is understood, particularly within Gen‐Z, an effective approach to conceptualizing identity development is as an emergent and processual phenomenon rather than as a predetermined sequence. Process‐oriented perspectives in identity research emphasize identity as an ongoing narrative and relational project shaped through person–environment transactions of socialization, recognition, and constraint (Luyckx et al., 2025; Tebbe & Budge, 2016). From this view, developmental events matter not only because they occur, but because their meanings are formed, evaluated, and revised across time, relationships, and settings. This process lens also foregrounds identity continuity and coherence in identity development, allowing periods of delay, nonoccurrence, or change to be interpreted as meaningful dynamics rather than the absence of development (Luyckx et al., 2025). A life‐history calendar (LHC) approach is well suited to examining these processes, as it elicits temporally ordered narratives while enabling participants to layer context, revisit earlier interpretations, and describe periods of nonoccurrence or suspension over time (DeHart, 2021; Fisher, 2013; Hereth et al., 2020). Within this process, milestone‐related experiences can be understood as prompts for ongoing cycles of meaning‐making rather than as developmental endpoints, aligning with accounts of identity development as iterative, relational, and context‐bound.

The current study

While milestone approaches remain useful for anchoring identity‐related experiences to developmental time and context, they are less well suited to capturing how experiences are revisited, reinterpreted, or acquire changing significance across the life course. These limitations may be particularly salient in the context of Gen‐Z, whose identity development unfolds within social and digital environments that foreground ongoing exploration, contextual movement, and evolving identity language (Bochicchio et al., 2023; Cover, 2019; Hyde et al., 2019; Marino et al., 2024). Accordingly, while we retain the term “milestones” for continuity with existing scholarship, the present study does not treat these experiences as fixed achievements or developmental endpoints. Instead, we approach milestone‐related experiences as contextually situated events that acquire meaning through participants' ongoing sense‐making within relational and sociohistorical contexts. This framing allows attention to both first occurrences and subsequent reinterpretations, as well as to experiences of delay, nonoccurrence, or change that are not readily captured by first‐age indicators alone.

To examine these processes, we employ a life‐history calendar (DeHart, 2021), which facilitates participant recall by situating identity‐related experiences alongside personal, relational, and environmental events across time. In doing so, this study seeks to extend existing milestone research by illuminating dimensions of identity development, such as iteration, context, and meaning‐making, that complement population‐level findings. Guided by this approach, the study addresses the following research questions: In what ways do Gen‐Z LGBTQ+ young people construct, interpret, and re‐negotiate their SGID experiences across their life course?How do relational, sociohistorical, and structural contexts shape, enable, or constrain these SGID experiences?

METHOD

Approach to inquiry

Conceptualizing SGID as an ongoing, context‐dependent process has direct implications for how such experiences can be studied. Approaches that privilege meaning‐making, relational context, and temporal revision require methods capable of capturing narrative continuity and change without presupposing fixed sequences or endpoints. Accordingly, the present study adopts a qualitative, interpretive design that allows participants to describe how identity‐related experiences are constructed, revisited, and situated within broader life‐course and sociohistorical contexts. Rather than adopting an a priori position that milestone approaches are invalid, we use participants' narratives to examine the conditions under which milestone framings align with lived experience and the conditions under which they obscure iterative, relational, or suspended trajectories.

Our study was conducted within a constructivist–relativist epistemological framework, which assumes that knowledge is actively co‐constructed between participants and researchers, while always situated in cultural, social, and historical contexts (Moon & Blackman, 2014). Analysis followed an exploratory, primarily inductive approach, allowing themes to be developed from participants' accounts while remaining informed by existing scholarship on SGID. Consistent with reflexive thematic analysis, we recognize that findings are not discovered but actively produced through our interpretive engagement with the data. This approach was selected to support a careful examination of how participants describe, interpret, and revisit identity‐related experiences across time and context. Accordingly, the epistemological orientation guided both the design of the interview procedure and the analytic process. We characterize the present work as exploratory rather than confirmatory as no preregistration or point predictions were specified.

Sample

A sample of 51 Australian LGBTQ+ (all sexually and gender diverse) young people (ages 16–26; M = 20.80 years, SD = 3.01) participated in a semi‐structured interview on their SGID experiences. We recruited a purposive subsample from a larger quantitative study on milestones (Warton et al., 2025), which captured participant demographic details and ages (or age ranges) for initial SID milestone experiences. We drew on the principle of information power to guide sample size adequacy (Malterud et al., 2016). Given that our study addressed a broad aim, included participants with heterogenous SGID experiences, and sought to interrogate and refine existing milestone models, a larger sample was warranted. The size of the dataset allowed us to capture the diverse and sometimes contradictory identity pathways of Gen‐Z LGBTQ+ young people while enabling in‐depth qualitative analysis. Materials: Life‐history calendar

A LHC (DeHart, 2021) was employed to capture identity processes, as it supports the recall of life events and experiences within a visual, nonlinear, branching timeline, aiding participants in situating their SGID experiences within specific sociohistorical and environmental contexts. A template of the LHC used is available online (https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.17319280).

Theoretical justification

The use of the LHC aligns with a life‐course perspective (see Elder, 1998), which emphasizes multiple temporalities and contingencies and recognizes that the impact of events varies depending on when they occur in someone's life. Thus, both ontogenetic (individual development) and sociogenic (sociohistorical) time must be considered in understanding SGID. By mapping out personal histories, the LHC allows participants to connect their identity processes with external socio‐political, environmental, and personal events. Indeed, the experiences of different LGBTQ+ cohorts are shaped by the visibility and acceptance of sexual and gender diversity in that era, creating distinct social contexts for SGID (Bränström & Pachankis, 2021; Hammack et al., 2018; Russell & Fish, 2019).

This method is also well aligned with an intersectional perspective. The LGBTQ+ community is heterogeneous, and sexual and gender identity intersect with other social identities and statuses (Cerezo et al., 2020; Nicholson et al., 2022). Intersectionality (see Crenshaw, 1991) highlights how systems of oppression (e.g., internalized stigma, violence, discrimination, exploitation, disempowerment, and cultural imperialism) intersect with identity‐based prejudices (e.g., racism, classism, sexism), where sociodemographic factors (e.g., race or ethnicity, socioeconomic status, sexual identity, gender identity, ability or disability status, age, immigrant or citizenship status, and religion or faith) create unique, compounding stress. The LHC facilitates the mapping of these intersecting identities, enabling participants to narrate how discrimination, violence, and other forms of oppression are embedded within the context of their personal events.

Importantly, the LHC is not used in the current study to reify “milestones” as fixed stages, but to reposition them as heuristic sites of meaning that could be experienced, re‐experienced, or ignored entirely. This shift allows us to foreground the variability, negotiation, and contextual embeddedness of identity development. Consistent with our earlier critique of linear and essentialist frameworks, SGID events merely served as entry points into participants' narratives, while the LHC situated these identity experiences within broader life‐course and contextual trajectories.

Implementation

Building on this theoretical foundation, the LHC was implemented to facilitate the interviews by aiding participant recall through visual timelines, layers, and prompts (DeHart, 2021; Glasner & van der Vaart, 2009; Nelson, 2019). Compared to traditional questionnaires, the LHC has been found to be beneficial for eliciting complex details across the life course and has been found to be reliable through test–retest assessment (Engel, Keifer, Thompson, et al., 2001; Engel, Keifer, & Zahm, 2001). It has been tested across diverse age groups, racially diverse populations, and socio‐political contexts (Axinn et al., 2020; Goldbach et al., 2023; Quandt et al., 2020), including sexual and gender diverse young people (Fisher, 2013; Hereth et al., 2020).

In the current study, contextual prompts included holidays, jobs, personal and family events, school events, national or world events, and significant relationships (such as friendships or other close connections). The LHC template also incorporated example prompts from SID research (e.g., self‐identity, coming out, sexual activity; Hall et al., 2021), where participants could mark multiple entries per event type, including repeats, reversals, and absences; participants could also add episodes as they revisited, reframed, or bypassed events. Importantly, given the lack of research on the intersection of SGID and to resist trans‐normative assumptions, GID events were not prelisted and instead participant generated. Participants were also invited to add other meaningful events, including pubertal and bodily changes, ensuring that the framework captured any dimensions of identity development that the participants felt were significant.

Measures from recruitment survey

The following measures were used in the larger quantitative study that the subsample from the current study was drawn from (Warton et al., 2025).

Demographics

The demographic data collected included participants' age, sexual identity, gender identity, sex assumed at birth, current school year level, race or ethnicity, religious identity, disability status, and neurodivergence self‐identification. Participants had the option to select multiple responses for race or ethnicity, sexual identity, and gender identity. For sexual identity, participants were asked, “What do you sexually identify as? Please select all that apply,” with response options including Lesbian, Mostly attracted to girls, Gay, Mostly attracted to boys, Straight (Heterosexual), Bisexual, Queer, Questioning, Asexual, Pansexual, “My preferred sexual identity (or at least one of them) isn't listed. I identify as:” [open text field], and Prefer not to say. For gender identity, participants were asked, “What is your gender identity? Please select all that apply,” with the options including Male or man, Female or woman, Nonbinary, Genderfluid, Trans or gender diverse, “My gender identity (or at least one of them) isn't listed. I identify as:” [open text field], and Prefer not to say.

Formative sexual identity events

Eleven questions assessed SID by examining the age(s) surrounding their earliest remembered events (if experienced or applicable) for attraction, self‐identity, same‐gender sexual activity (with the same gender or sex or someone who is nonbinary or genderfluid), opposite‐gender sexual activity, same‐gender romantic relationship (with the same gender or sex or someone who is nonbinary or genderfluid), opposite‐gender romantic relationship, disclosure (to anyone), and coming out to an immediate family member. For example, “How old were you when you first consensually engaged in sexual activity with someone of the same sex as you, or someone who is non‐binary or genderfluid?” Each event generated two types of data: binary data indicating whether this experience had occurred or not, and continuous data specifying the age (or ages, in years) at which these events took place. Participants also had the option to select “I have not experienced this yet,” or “This is not relevant to me,” or “This is not applicable to me,” or “I have not done this yet” where appropriate. These survey ages were used as prompts during interviews rather than endpoints; first‐age indicators were not privileged over subsequent episodes or nonoccurrence.

Procedure

Study procedures were approved by the Monash University Human Research Ethics Committee (ID: 37035). Interviews were conducted online via Zoom in Australia from May to September 2023, with an average duration of 59.23 minutes (range: 27.12–112.62 minutes), and were audio‐recorded and transcribed verbatim using auto‐transcription software. Participants received $30 for completing the interview. Participants joined the interview alone and in an environment that was comfortable and private for them. Participants provided verbal consent to participate after being read the consent script. An additional step was included for mature minors (i.e., 16‐ and 17‐year‐olds) to ensure they understood what was involved in the study. Mature minors were asked to describe what they understood of the study, procedure, and their rights to withdraw. Researchers then made an informed decision about whether these participants were able to provide informed consent based on their level of understanding. The primary interviewer (conducted ~78% of the interviews) was a first‐year PhD student with prior research experience interviewing and working with LGBTQ+ populations. A second interviewer with prior research experience working with LGBTQ+ populations was also recruited. The interviewers (WW and AH) shared their screen displaying the LHC, which organized events visually with contextual cues (e.g., school events, friendships) and SGID prompts along the left margin. The LHC grouped columns by calendar year and displayed months within each year, with a text box below for notes and date marking. The columns prompted temporal recall, while the rows supported thematic or episodic recall without implying order.

While the dates for SID events or experiences obtained from the recruitment survey served as a guide, participants ultimately directed the interview. The interviewer explored each event as narrated by participants, attending to repeats, reversals, catalysts, constraints, and context; timing was captured as ranges when precise dates were uncertain. Prompts were used only when necessary (e.g., “who were the people involved in this [event]?”; “were there other significant events or context around this [event] that you felt impacted it?”; for a full list refer to Supplemental Material Table S2). Participants could refine dates and share their life history, with the interviewer marking dates on the LHC, allowing participants to observe and contribute throughout.

We had two researchers conducting the interviews. As a procedural check to assess methodological adherence, Cohen's kappa was used to measure agreement on event notation and temporal windowing on the LHC during the interviews. Each interviewer independently reviewed and completed a LHC for five interviews (10% of the projected sample) from the other interviewer before progressing with the remaining scheduled interviews, achieving a procedural agreement of 88.21% on notation (not interpretive coding), indicating strong consistency in the interview process. Please note, while at first this may appear inconsistent with a reflexive thematic analysis approach, this kappa statistic does not refer to the coding of the interview transcripts; it is for how information (e.g., SGID event name, year and month of occurrence) was recorded in the LHC by the researchers conducting the interviews. This was not directly important to the analysis process but rather added a layer of certainty that the interviews were being conducted to the same standard given the sample size.

Analytical process

Data from the LHC interviews were analyzed using thematic analysis with both a reflexive and codebook approach within a contextualist framework (Braun & Clarke, 2021a, 2021b, 2024). The reflexive thematic analysis approach was chosen because it aligns with critical qualitative research principles, recognizing knowledge as contextual, subjective, and partial, with reflexivity as a core aspect (Koro‐Ljungberg & Cannella, 2017). In this paradigm, knowledge is understood as being contextual, subjective, and inherently partial, with reflexivity recognized as an essential aspect of the research process. We adopted an inductive, bottom‐up logic to explore how participants navigated SGID events across their lives. In this way, events functioned as temporal and contextual reference points within the LHC, helping participants situate their identity experiences across ontogenetic and sociogenic time. These reference points were not treated as sequential or cumulative stages but as narrative anchors through which participants articulated change, continuity, and reinterpretation. In our interviews, we prompted participants to reflect on their experiences of SGID events, while our analysis focused on constructing the broader patterns of meaning surrounding them.

The analytic process was both iterative and reflexive rather than linear. Two coders (WW and OB) immersed themselves in the transcripts and LHCs, initially coding a subset of five transcripts each (~10% of the sample). All transcript coding was done through NVivo 14, used with permission from Lumivero (2023). Both coders then met to calibrate their approaches, discussing similarities and differences, and developed a preliminary shared coding framework. While reflexive thematic analysis typically emphasizes organic, evolving coding, this framework was not a rigid guide but an iterative tool that changed as each coder engaged with the data. After the initial shared coding process, each coder independently coded the remaining half of the sample (23 transcripts each). Weekly meetings were held to engage with the content of the transcripts, and discuss, consolidate, and refine codes, guiding and evolving the coding process. This collaborative approach, lasting two months, resulted in 78 codes across the 51 transcripts. Each version of the iterative codebook with tracked changes and annotations is publicly available (https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.17319280).

Reflexive practice and positionality

The primary and secondary authors, who led the coding and analysis, were both Gen‐Z, White, straight, cisgender, able‐bodied doctoral researchers. This positioning afforded both insider and outsider perspectives relative to the research: They shared a generational location with participants and, as allies, had witnessed many of the developmental experiences described by participants unfold within their own social worlds. At the same time, they hold structural privilege within academia and society and do not share the marginalized identities at the center of this study. In response, analytic decisions were discussed within a multidisciplinary research team that included sexually diverse, nonbinary, and neurodivergent members, as well as a clinical psychologist with extensive experience working with LGBTQ+ young people.

Reflexivity was primarily enacted through ongoing analytic dialogue during weekly research meetings rather than through formal memoing. Throughout coding and theme development, we discussed how prior assumptions about SGID milestones shaped attention and interpretation, and how these assumptions shifted through engagement with the data. The most salient assumption in our early coding was a homonormative expectation that identity development would primarily culminate in same‐gender intimacy and relationships. Participants' accounts complicated this framing: Intimacy was not always present within developmental narratives, and opposite‐gender relationships were present as meaningful sites of exploration, safety, or concealment, including in ways that co‐occurred with queer identification.

These tensions were brought into focus through the diversity of the research team, which fostered intersubjective recognition and reflexive dialogue (see Levitt, 2024), supporting a more nuanced analysis while resisting claims to neutrality or objectivity. These team discussions examined when interpretations risked reproducing hetero/homonormative or trans‐normative assumptions (e.g., privileging disclosure as progression), informed refinement of code definitions, supported the identification of not experiencing a milestone as analytically meaningful (e.g., deliberate nondisclosure), and contributed to theme development that reflected participants' meaning‐making processes rather than mapping onto normative trajectories. The specific identities of research team members will not be disclosed given the Trump administration's policing of academic freedom through visa control (Weninger, 2025) and ongoing attempts to dismantle institutional mechanisms supporting LGBTQ+ equity (Dawson & Kates, 2025).

Theme construction

Codes were exported from NVivo into the online collaborative software Miro (2024), where we began visually clustering related codes by conceptual similarity. Rather than assuming codes naturally “belonged together,” we treated clustering as a creative and interpretive process, continually questioning our assumptions and negotiating points of convergence and divergence as a team. For each cluster, we developed a higher‐order organizing concept, acknowledging these concepts were actively constructed rather than passively “emerging” from the data. From these clusters, we drafted six preliminary themes. Weekly team meetings were used to reflexively interrogate these drafts, drawing on our research questions while remaining alert to ways our positionalities shaped interpretation. Throughout, we returned to the coded extracts in NVivo to check that our interpretations remained coherent and adequately grounded in participants' accounts. Through this recursive process of construction and refinement, the preliminary themes were consolidated into three themes and one underlying subtheme. All co‐authors reviewed and debated the final thematic structure, offering further insight and refinement to the quotes used and the theme names. Participants were also invited to anonymously provide feedback on the final themes to further test resonance, though no feedback was received.

RESULTS

The following analysis was guided using a reflexive thematic approach that treated milestone‐related experiences as sites of meaning‐making rather than discrete or time‐bounded events. For each event prompt (e.g., self‐identification, disclosure, relationships), we documented episodes, context, catalysts, constraints, and temporal ranges described by participants. Interpretations were developed through ongoing engagement with participants' LHCs and interviews, with particular attention to how experiences were revisited, reinterpreted, or suspended across time.

Given the tendency for developmental narratives to be organized chronologically, we remained attentive to the risk of reproducing stage‐like descriptions in our analysis. To address this, we avoided privileging first‐occurrence ages, foregrounded repeated and absent events, and framed time as contingent and relational rather than sequential. Through this process, we constructed three analytic themes describing mechanisms through which identity‐related experiences acquired meaning: (1) iteration, (2) empowerment via visibility, and (3) invalidation. An additional subtheme cut across these mechanisms, relationships as catalytic contexts, which illustrate how relational dynamics influence milestone experiences across participants' accounts. All participant names used in representative quotes are pseudonyms to protect their identities.

Theme 1: Identity as iterative work

Participants described identity‐related experiences as unfolding through ongoing processes of reassessment and revision, rather than as singular or final moments. Across accounts, self‐identification and related experiences were revisited as meanings, labels, and social contexts changed over time. Iteration, rather than chronological age, characterized how participants narrated these trajectories. For instance, Taylor (23, Lesbian, Queer, Asexual, Genderfluid) described how their early choice of a bisexual identity label reflected a moment of clarity, but one that eventually shifted as their sense of self evolved, “It felt right at the time, but until it didn't. If that makes sense.” This process of reassessment was echoed by Bailey (17, Asexual, Agender), who noted that their choice in identity labels and description “was all very tentative… I was very much like this can change, I'm not so sure that this is who I am.” These reflections highlight how identity‐related experiences were reassessed and redefined throughout their development in response to personal experiences and new understandings of gender or sexual identity.

Theme 2: Queer visibility as a mechanism of empowerment

Throughout the identity development process, queerness emerged as a powerful source of empowerment, resilience, and support for participants. Exposure to queer identities, the visibility of queer communities, and the relationships formed in them played pivotal roles in helping participants navigate the challenges of coming out, self‐identifying, and exploring their sexual or gender identities. Within this theme, participants highlighted how visibility functioned across multiple contexts. We present four contexts of schools, family, online spaces, and intersectional queer communities, as interrelated sites where visibility enabled empowerment. These are not discrete subthemes but illustrate how empowerment through visibility operated across multiple, intersecting domains.

Community and school experiences

For participants who encountered visible queer peers or supportive school environments, identity exploration was described as more accessible and less isolating. Morgan (24, Lesbian, Mostly attracted to girls, Queer, Gender diverse, Nonbinary, Agender) recalled,There was like, another kid in my class also, who like identified as like as genderqueer. And it was like it was, that's also another thing that helped me, like, come out during that time, to my friends. Like helping them see, I'm like, okay, I'm like safe, to come out.

There was like, another kid in my class also, who like identified as like as genderqueer. And it was like it was, that's also another thing that helped me, like, come out during that time, to my friends. Like helping them see, I'm like, okay, I'm like safe, to come out.

In Morgan's account, disclosure was closely tied to perceived safety and recognition within their immediate environment. The presence of others with similar identities helped normalize their experiences and shaped the timing and emotional tone of disclosure.

The presence of LGBTQ+ affinity groups (e.g., Gay‐Straight Alliance) in schools, when available, provided valuable support and a sense of community for queer students. However, their impact was often limited by institutional barriers. As Jade (24, Lesbian, cis‐Female) noted, “people were supportive, but they were very slow to do anything about it, which was really frustrating.” Though well‐received, these LGBTQ+ affinity groups often faced challenges like limited staff support, infrequent meeting times, slow administrative changes, and resistance to broader institutional reforms, such as gender‐neutral bathrooms or inclusive uniforms.

In some cases, responsibility for sustaining these spaces fell to students themselves. TJ (17, Queer, Nonbinary) recalled how they took on the responsibility of running one of these groups and became a mentor to younger students, “a lot of younger people that were a part of this, and so very quickly, I became a mentor to these people, because they had no one else.” There was also risk involved with being a group member, that could lead to further social exclusion or marginalization within school, “if people know you're in the GSA, they're like, ‘Okay, you're probably queer’, which isn't always like the safest situation for people” (Jade, 24, Lesbian, cis‐Female).

Several students reported that their schools actively hindered or completely shut down efforts to organize these groups. Some students even faced backlash when staff attempted to create such spaces, “one staff member in particular tried to set up an LGBT group on campus, and we ended up getting so many complaints about it… so it pretty quickly got shut down” (Mikaela, 24, Lesbian, cis‐Female). This reflects broader challenges in creating inclusive environments for queer students in schools, “I'd grown to understand that there's only so much that teachers can do even if they mean the best, they still have bosses” (Logan, 23, Bisexual, Gender diverse, Nonbinary). These accounts illustrate how visibility functioned as a conditional resource, empowering when supported by institutions, but potentially risky or burdensome when responsibility for visibility fell onto students themselves.

The absence of queer representation in certain schools did not simply delay identity exploration; it meant that certain experiences (e.g., self‐identification or disclosure) were entirely suspended until later in life. Harley (22, Queer, Pansexual, Nonbinary, Genderqueer) reflected, “If like, I knew that non‐binary people existed in high school, I think I would've come out a lot sooner.” Similarly, Sage (25, Bisexual, Queer, Nonbinary, Agender) shared that they “knew very little about queerness as a general proposition until after [they] left school, because… school never talked about it. Queerness was just not addressed in any way, shape, or form.” The absence of discourse around queerness in these environments delayed self‐exploration, leading participants to feel isolated and invalidated in their identities,I didn't feel like my own feelings were valid, I never went to seek that information out myself… I didn't feel like I had anybody that I could talk to about it safely. So it just never happened until after I got out of school and I'd been in university for a few years and I'd met some more people. (Sage, 25, Bisexual, Queer, Non‐binary, Agender).

Parental and family experiences

Parental support, while often discussed, was primarily framed as a space for acceptance and providing autonomy for self‐exploration. If a participant's parents were supportive, it was usually in the form of accepting them and giving them the space to explore their identity independently—particularly for GID. For example, Cyrus (19, Lesbian, Queer, Transmasculine, Nonbinary, Genderqueer) described how their mother was supportive but struggled to provide guidance, stating, “she wasn't super good at helping but was genuinely very, very supportive.” However, when it came to more direct support, queer family members played a pivotal role. Mikaela (24, Lesbian, cis‐Female) shared how disclosing their identity to their gay uncle brought crucial clarity,I disclosed it to my uncle who's gay and I said, I don't really know—but I want to know… so he took me out clubbing to one of the gay bars and that was the first time I'd had a reaction to being kissed since my best friend in high school, and then it all just clicked.

Queer family members, through shared experiences and being role models, offered the most impactful forms of support, guiding participants through critical moments of self‐discovery. It is important to note that not all participants experienced this kind of support. Some reported feelings of invalidation or rejection from their families, which created significant challenges in their identity development and added complexity to their experiences of self‐exploration.

Online experiences

Social media emerged as a critical site of queer visibility, particularly for participants who lacked access to queer identities or communities offline. These platforms enabled early encounters with queer identities, histories, and narratives, allowing participants to recognize themselves in others and to imagine identity possibilities that had previously felt unavailable. For many, this initial recognition was foundational. As Maddy (23, Queer, Mostly attracted to girls, cis‐Female) described, “It's how I first found out about queer as being an option. Social media platforms functioned as critical gateways to self‐awareness, offering opportunities to learn about the experiences of others, see diverse representations of queerness, and begin to envision a future where they could exist openly and authentically, “just seeing like, other queer people exist [on social media]… wait, queer people can be happy? We get to be happy as well?” (Rami, 18, Queer, Nonbinary).

Intersectional communities

For some participants, queer support networks were further enriched when they aligned with their intersectional identities, including their racial, cultural, and gender experiences. As Alex (23, Bisexual, cis‐Female) shared, having “queer friends who are also people of color, having more people that like, their intersectionalities line up with my intersectionalities was beneficial.” These connections allowed them to navigate the complexities of being queer while also managing the unique challenges of coming from a religious or migrant household; “I didn't feel like I had to explain like, what coming from religious household was like, what coming from like a migrant household is like, what like code‐switching meant.” Here, code‐switching marked not only the negotiation of culture, but also what counts as coming out or disclosure, as these experiences shift when one must constantly recalibrate identity across contexts.

Similarly, Oli (21, Lesbian, Demisexual, cis‐Female) described the challenges of connecting with predominantly White queer communities at her university, emphasizing that their cultural background and experience of being queer in Vietnam did not align with the mainstream narratives often presented in Australian queer spaces, “we didn't really share the same experience as a queer person.” In these contexts, common milestone indicators, such as when or how one first comes out (especially to family) were often inaccessible. White, nonreligious queer spaces offered no frame for experiences like those of Oli and Alex, differentiating their identity development trajectory from commonly reported experiences.

Sasha (24, Asexual, Mostly attracted to girls, Nonbinary) extended this point in the context of disability, explaining, “I'm on several [Facebook groups] for different queer stuff, and different chronic illness stuff… we can now find these little communities where everyone has the same experience… they can really give you practical advice and just be really affirming.” These experiences underscore the significance of intersectional queer communities in fostering authentic connections and offering nuanced support, which is especially vital for those whose identities are shaped by multiple, sometimes conflicting, aspects of their lived experience.

In summary, across contexts visibility functioned as a relational and contextual resource rather than a singular influence. Exposure to queer identities through peers, family members, online platforms, and community spaces supported exploration, validation, and a sense of belonging. Participants' accounts illustrate how identity‐related experiences were shaped by whether visibility was present, absent, or constrained within their environments, influencing how and when these experiences were understood and articulated. Importantly, visibility was rarely experienced in isolation. Its power was derived from being embedded in relationships with peers, mentors, family members, and online communities, which catalyzed identity‐related experiences. In this sense, visibility functioned not only as an enabling condition for exploration, but also as a relational mechanism that re‐staged when and how identity development experiences carried meaning.

Theme 3: Invalidation stifles milestone meaning

Invalidation and discrimination, whether experienced from family, school, or media, posed significant emotional distress for participants as they navigated the complexities of their SGID. These experiences often constrained self‐affirmation and identity exploration, shaping how participants understood, expressed, and sustained their sense of self over time. For some, invalidation went beyond disagreement or misunderstanding; it undermined their ability to feel recognized as themselves. As Cyrus (19, Lesbian, Queer, Transmasculine, Nonbinary, Genderqueer) reflected, “so much of my queerness is very integral to me, to how I view the world, and how I present… not being seen as queer meant I don't think I felt like I was being seen as myself.” This disconnection from being seen and recognized as their true selves carried an emotional toll, contributing to feelings of isolation, confusion, and self‐doubt.

For many participants, family relationships were a central site of invalidation. Parents often dismissed or minimized their identities, framing them as phases or problems to be resolved rather than experiences to be affirmed. These dynamics were particularly pronounced for participants with plurisexual or nonbinary identities, whose fluidity was frequently interpreted as evidence of uncertainty or inauthenticity. For example,I had always felt invalidated if… my gender expression shifted, and then I would think that I was actually just going through a phase… I would have periods of yeah, just feeling very, like, androgynous, and at that time, I would align myself with being non‐binary, but then I would kind of transition back to wanting to be more feminine, and then I just felt as though like, my mum was right, this is actually just a phase, and I felt like an imposter for wanting to use they/them pronouns and stuff like that… when I would revert back to dressing more femininely, [mum] would kind of tell me that like, see I told you it was a phase—and then I wouldn't be able to argue with that. (Dylan, 19, Lesbian, Genderfluid).

In some cases, invalidation escalated into outright rejection. This kind of invalidation impacted identity development more broadly through shaping how participants viewed and affirmed their own identity, and navigated coming out to future people after facing rejection, often encouraging caution, concealment, or emotional withdrawal.

Beyond the family, educational environments were another prominent site of invalidation. Discrimination and stigma within schools, religious institutions, and social circles often created barriers to self‐expression for many participants, forcing them to suppress or hide their identities to avoid judgment or hostility. Similarly, Emerson (17, Mostly attracted to girls, Gay, Queer, Pansexual, Asexual, Questioning, Nonbinary, Genderfluid) reflected on the challenges of navigating transphobia and transmedicalism within the TGNC community as a nonbinary person. Across these accounts, invalidation operated as a pervasive and cumulative force. Whether encountered through family interactions, school environments, media representations, or community norms, the denial or conditional recognition of participants' identities shaped how safe, legitimate, or sustainable their self‐understandings felt. Rather than moving smoothly through these developmental experiences, participants described how invalidation and discrimination altered progress, often destabilizing the meaning of these events themselves, making a relationship or identity feel temporary, unsafe, or illegitimate. These experiences highlight how recognition and affirmation were not simply supportive add‐ons, but central conditions shaping how participants navigated and sustained their developing sense of self.

DISCUSSION

This study extends milestone‐based research on SGID by examining how experiences—commonly operationalized as milestones—are constructed, revisited, and interpreted across time and context among Gen‐Z LGBTQ+ young people. Guided by a process‐oriented and interpretive approach, the analysis foregrounds milestones as an ongoing, relational form of meaning‐making rather than as fixed developmental benchmarks. Consistent with prior scholarship, milestone frameworks remain valuable for documenting when identity‐related experiences first occur and for enabling comparability across cohorts (Baams et al., 2025). However, the present findings highlight aspects of identity development that are less readily captured by first‐occurrence indicators alone, including how identity milestones are reinterpreted, deferred, recur, and refused. By using milestone prompts as narrative entry points within life‐history calendar interviews, this study illustrates how identity‐related experiences acquire significance through meaning‐making processes embedded in relational, sociohistorical, and generational contexts. In doing so, the findings align with emerging models that conceptualize identity development as iterative and context‐dependent (e.g., Dolan & Garvey, 2024; van Anders, 2015). By decentering the primacy of “first‐time” occurrence, this qualitative approach underscores the importance of a milestone as being fluid, iterative, and shaped by shifting environments: a finding that requires ongoing consideration in quantitative milestone research.

Reframing milestones as intersecting and processual

Rather than unfolding as independent or sequential trajectories, sexual and gender identity processes were often described as mutually constitutive, with shifts in one domain prompting reinterpretation in the other. This pattern aligns with prior work emphasizing the iterative and dynamic nature of participants' intersecting SGID experiences, which influence each other in highly individualized and dynamic ways (Hereth et al., 2020). For example, in the current study, participants frequently described how changes in their understanding of their gender identities shaped how they made sense of attraction, relationships, or labels, and vice versa. These interdependencies highlight the value of attending to identity development as a coordinated process rather than as a series of discrete, domain‐specific events.

Additionally, many participants reported the absence or deliberate deferral of experiences, such as coming out to family or engaging in sexual activity. While milestone research has long acknowledged variability in timing and sequencing (Baams et al., 2025; Hall et al., 2021), participants' narratives underscore the importance of understanding nonoccurrence and deferral as meaningful aspects of identity trajectories, particularly when experiences are constrained by safety concerns, cultural expectations, or social environments. Despite the inherent variability in these experiences, the notion of revisiting, renegotiating, or bypassing a milestone remains underrepresented in the literature. For Gen‐Z, this absence of fixed indicators is especially relevant, as they increasingly embrace fluidity in identity and often experience milestones at nontraditional times or choose to forgo them altogether in favor of more authentic expression. These young people are resisting fixed labels and exploring their SGID in nonlinear, recursive ways, aided by increased access to information and digital queer communities that encourage contextual experimentation and meaning‐making, allowing them to redefine and interpret their identities in dynamic, personal ways.

Interpreting self‐identification as a dynamic indicator

Participants' accounts also highlight the dynamic nature of self‐identification, suggesting caution in treating identity labels as static indicators of developmental status. While the notion of fluidity in identity is not new in milestone research (Katz‐Wise et al., 2024; Katz‐Wise & Todd, 2022), it is rarely acknowledged in the broader literature (Baams et al., 2025; Hall et al., 2021). For many participants, adopting a particular sexual or gender identity label was not a singular or final event, but part of an ongoing process of exploration, clarification, and revision. This adds needed context to recent quantitative work with Gen‐Z that found identity fluidity and label change was common, likely indicating a period of identity exploration (Katz‐Wise et al., 2023). In the current study, changes in self‐identification were often accompanied by renewed or re‐contextualized acts of disclosure, shifts in relationships, or changes in how attraction and embodiment were understood.

Milestone research has been instrumental in documenting when individuals first adopt particular identity labels and how these timings vary across cohorts (Baams et al., 2025; Bishop et al., 2024; Hall et al., 2021). However, participants' narratives suggest that the meaning attached to these first identifications may change over time, and that subsequent reinterpretations are central to identity development. From this perspective, age‐based indicators capture important temporal information but do not fully represent how identity labels are negotiated, sustained, or revised across contexts. These findings point to the value of longitudinal or intensive longitudinal methodological approaches that can trace continuity, change, and suspension in identity‐related experiences over time. Rather than replacing existing milestone approaches, such methods may complement quantitative indicators by illuminating how self‐identification operates as a relational and iterative process embedded within broader life‐course dynamics.

Visibility as a contextual condition for identity timing and meaning

Similar to prior research, the findings of this study highlight the central role of queer connection, belonging, and visibility in shaping identity processes, with participants emphasizing the importance of queer representation in schools, media, and personal relationships for affirmation, mentorship, and support (Cerezo & Bergfeld, 2013; McCormick et al., 2015; Ong et al., 2025; Wernick et al., 2014). The significance of improved queer representation is best understood through experiences of witnessing and mirroring. In line with Devor's (2004) work, and recent scholarship on nonbinary identity development (Dolan & Garvey, 2024), witnessing occurs when individuals feel seen and validated in their true gender identities by others; meanwhile, mirroring refers to when individuals see their identities reflected in others who share their lived experiences. For many of the participants, the affirmation they received—whether from family, friends, or members of queer communities—was crucial in shaping their sense of self and enhancing their confidence in their identity. Importantly, this visibility did not merely affirm identity but actively reshaped when, how, and whether milestones such as coming out or self‐identification could occur.

Participants emphasized the importance of having allies and mentors who not only acknowledged but actively supported their gender and sexual identities. This aligns with literature indicating that inclusive queer communities function as key contexts for resilience and well‐being, particularly for those navigating multiple and sometimes conflicting aspects of identity (Ghabrial, 2017; Singh et al., 2014), though this is not without challenges within LGBTQ+ spaces (McCormick & Barthelemy, 2021). Mirroring played an equally important role in fostering a sense of belonging and connection. Participants described the empowerment of encountering others with similar gender or sexual identities, especially within intersectional queer spaces where racial, cultural, and gender similarities were present. These shared experiences allowed individuals to see themselves in others, alleviating feelings of isolation and clarifying self‐understanding. In these spaces, participants often found mirrors—other queer or nonbinary individuals who validated their experiences and affirmed that they were not alone.

Invalidation as a contextual constraint on identity expression

Empowerment and invalidation operated as opposing conditions shaping participants' identity work. Where visibility created openings for identities to be renegotiated or re‐timed, discrimination and stigma narrowed or constrained these possibilities. Framing empowerment and invalidation as contextual dimensions of meaning‐making underscores that identity processes are contingent on the social environments through which they occur. Enduring challenges for LGBTQ+ young people include family rejection, violence, and homelessness (Baams et al., 2019; McGeough & Sterzing, 2018), and persistent discrimination in schools (Kaufman & Baams, 2022). Participants' accounts of school‐based invalidation reflect a broader wave of anti‐LGBTQ+ mobilizations that target education, restricting content, and resources (Ferfolja & Ullman, 2020; Graff & Korolczuk, 2022; Thoreson, 2022). These contexts amplify structural stigma and erase visibility at critical sites like schools, limiting opportunities for affirmation and self‐expression.

Within a minority stress framework (Brooks, 1981; Meyer, 1995, 2003), distal constraints (e.g., discrimination, bullying, and policy or organizational censorship) feed proximal processes (e.g., anticipatory rejection, identity concealment). Together, these dynamics help explain why identity expression unfolds unevenly across time and space, rather than as a fixed or predictable sequence. Participants' accounts show that in hostile environments, processes of self‐definition are not halted so much as displaced, deferred, or rendered temporarily unsafe, underscoring how structural conditions shape the rhythms and meanings of identity work.

Limitations

The findings of this research should be interpreted in light of several considerations regarding scope and context. First, the sample comprises Gen‐Z LGBTQ+ young people residing in Australia, and the findings are therefore situated within a specific sociohistorical and cultural context. Gen‐Z has come of age during a period marked by expanded identity language, heightened visibility of sexual and gender diversity, and the pervasive influence of digital and social media environments. As such, the processes described here reflect how SGID is negotiated under contemporary conditions and should not be assumed to generalize unproblematically to earlier or later generational cohorts or to contexts with different cultural, legal, or institutional landscapes.

Second, the current sample is limited by an over‐representation of those assumed female at birth. Those assumed male at birth who are gender and sexually diverse are a challenging population to draw from in young samples due to a more rigid enforcement of gender norms for boys and men, with harsher social sanctions against gender nonconformity (Jackson et al., 2021; Mason et al., 2025). Future research should focus more on exploring the SGID experiences in Gen‐Z samples assumed male at birth to help bridge this gap.

Finally, although the study intentionally repositioned milestones as heuristic prompts rather than developmental benchmarks, the use of milestone‐based prompts necessarily draws on constructs derived from prior literature. While participants frequently resisted, revised, deferred, or bypassed these prompts, the framing may still have shaped which experiences were most readily articulated. Accordingly, the findings speak most directly to how commonly studied milestone‐related experiences are reinterpreted and renegotiated across time and context, rather than to the full range of identity processes that may fall outside existing milestone frameworks. Future research may benefit from designs that further decenter predefined identity events and allow identity processes to be elicited without reference to established milestone categories.

Implications

Milestone frameworks have a demonstrated utility in clinical practice (Tazza et al., 2023) and as self‐help resources (Brazelton & Sparrow, 2006). However, these frameworks risk constraining identity development if treated as standardized, age‐based benchmarks. Our findings suggest that their value lies not in prescriptive sequencing or measurement, but in offering flexible, heuristic entry points for reflection and meaning‐making. They can provide useful guidance for individuals, families, support people, and clinicians to help explore and narrate identity experiences, rather than to measure them against static norms. Uses can include building understanding, supporting adjustment, and coping to reduce isolation, uncertainty, and anxiety (Tazza et al., 2023). The evolving nature of SGID can be a source of uncertainty and self‐doubt for LGBTQ+ young people. Framing milestones as re‐visitable, recursive, and fluid narrative points allows young people to represent complexity and to construct self‐understandings that foster coherence and self‐acceptance. Clinicians supporting people in this process can draw upon the language and insights contained within identity experiences to support young people as they explore self‐narratives, anticipate changes over time, and manage feelings of isolation by hearing about the journeys of others.

At the policy level, understanding the personal experiences of people as they navigate milestones can strengthen and focus policy priorities. The findings of this study support initiatives such as increasing and diversifying representation of SGID in media, the importance of queer spaces and opportunities for connecting with an LGBTQ+ community (particularly in rural and small community contexts), and education initiatives aimed at increasing LGBTQ+ visibility and providing information about change over the life course for identity development. Here, milestones should not be understood as universal endpoints but as context‐dependent moments of articulation, recognition, or relational visibility that are enabled (or constrained) by institutional, cultural, and policy environments.

In clinical practice, therapeutic support for SGID should be nonpathologizing, de‐stigmatizing, and grounded in affirmation of identity fluidity and diversity (Rees et al., 2021). Rather than privileging the timing or sequence of experiences, clinicians can use milestone narratives as open‐ended dialogic touchpoints. Experiencing milestones can be associated with improved mental health (Puckett et al., 2022), but our findings suggest this is because they provide opportunities for meaning‐making and social validation, not because they occur at a particular time or in a fixed order. The therapist's role in SGID is to provide a safe space for the individual to explore thoughts and feelings about experiences, relationships, and self. The therapist reflects the person's words and affects, bearing witness and supporting them to find their own narratives about themselves and their identity at various points across their life course. Practically, this entails (in no implied sequence): cultivating awareness of life‐course variability and the cultural specificity of SGID (Rees et al., 2021); collaborating to co‐construct frameworks for understanding experience; normalizing ongoing reinterpretation of identity and embracing uncertainty as part of development rather than a deviation; advocating for affirming environments within families and communities; helping clients situate their narratives among diverse queer experiences without assuming a single trajectory; and maintaining reflective, flexible spaces in which identities, emotions, and meanings to evolve over time.

CONCLUSION

The present study contributes to ongoing debates about how sexual and gender identity development is conceptualized by examining how experiences commonly described as milestones are constructed, revisited, and interpreted across time and context among Gen‐Z LGBTQ+ young people. Rather than supporting stage‐based or achievement‐oriented models, the findings illustrate identity development as an iterative and context‐dependent process, shaped through relationships, social conditions, and changing interpretive frameworks. In doing so, this study highlights limitations of milestone approaches that rely on fixed sequences or first‐occurrence indicators, particularly when applied to generational contexts characterized by expanded identity vocabularies, digital connectivity, and ongoing exploration.

Consistent with a reflexive and constructivist orientation, the findings are offered as analytic interpretations developed through engagement with participants' life‐history narratives, rather than as claims about universal developmental pathways. Across accounts, identity development was described less as movement toward stable endpoints and more as a continuous negotiation of meaning, belonging, and recognition across different contexts and moments in the life course. Milestone‐related experiences, such as self‐identification, disclosure, or relationships, often acquired significance through reinterpretation, recurrence, deferral, or refusal, underscoring the importance of attending to how identity processes unfold over time rather than when they first occur. The findings also underscore the importance of social environments in shaping identity work. Affirming relationships, visible queer communities, and supportive institutional contexts facilitated exploration and meaning‐making, whereas invalidation and structural constraint narrowed or destabilized these processes. Together, these patterns underscore the importance of policies, practices, and cultural conditions that support inclusion, recognition, and safety for LGBTQ+ young people, particularly within educational, familial, and digital spaces.

By reframing milestones as heuristic entry points rather than developmental benchmarks, this study extends existing SGID research and offers a process‐oriented perspective that complements population‐level milestone findings. More broadly, it contributes to a growing body of work that conceptualizes identity development as relational, situated, and ongoing, providing a framework better aligned with the lived realities of contemporary queer young people.
