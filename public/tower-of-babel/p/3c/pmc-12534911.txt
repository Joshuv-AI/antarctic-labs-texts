Dissemination experiences and preferences from HIV service delivery organizations
Authors: Maria Pyra, Morgan Purrier, Dennis Li, Kathryn Macapagal, Nanette Benbow
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundBetter understanding of how deliverers use research and select interventions can further reduce the gap between developing and delivering effective intervention and implementation strategies, especially in the field of HIV.MethodsWe interviewed a convenience sample of Midwest health organizations and health departments who are involved with HIV treatment and/or prevention services. Using an iterative, rapid qualitative analysis, we identified key steps in the process of prioritizing health needs, selecting interventions, and disseminating or receiving information about interventions.ResultsIn order to prioritize areas for interventions, organizations used community assessments, developing leaderships buy-in, considered staff capacity, accessed funding, and created partnerships. Once a priority areas was developed, interventions were usually developed by the organization or adapted from pre-existing interventions to meet local needs. Organizations preferred to receive information from trusted broker agencies or from peer organizations. There was a strong desire to evaluate and share results from home-grown interventions but evaluation capacity and funding were limiting factors.ConclusionsThere are several ways to improve dissemination and knowledge sharing between researchers and practitioners. Researchers can design flexible and adaptable interventions, with a range of dissemination materials available to broker agencies. Deliverers can partner with researchers for evaluation, while funders can specifically support evaluation and dissemination, including peer-to-peer learning.Keywords: HIV, Implementation science, Dissemination, Deliverers

Background

The fields of dissemination and implementation science have developed many frameworks on how research is disseminated and interventions are taken up by organizations [1–3]. A better understanding of this dissemination gap, is necessary to successfully end the HIV epidemic (EHE) in the U.S. [4], which has persisted at nearly 30,000 new infections annually since 2018 [5], despite the existence of highly effective treatment and prevention interventions. Many of the existing dissemination frameworks focus on the pathway of researcher-initiated intervention being disseminated or ‘pushed’ out to health delivery organizations; it is worth noting the growing focus on co-development and engaging community in intervention design, often referred to as designing for dissemination [6, 7].

In contrast, there is less literature on the perspective of those delivering health interventions, particularly around HIV, and the factors that facilitate ‘pulling in’ innovations into their practices. Bach-Mortensen et al., using a systematic review, and Aarons et al., using a survey, both identify several organization level factors regarding the uptake of evidence-based interventions [8, 9]. Specific to HIV, Owczarzak et al. found that training, need for adaptation, and lack of real-world evidence were barriers to uptake of evidence-based interventions [10, 11]. In their systematic review of diffusion of health service interventions, Greenlagh et al. [12] identified several research priorities including, on the other hand, understanding how interventions are developed by and shared among health service organizations.

Increased understanding of the process of selecting and implementing new intervention and implementation strategies by healthcare systems and health departments can allow researchers develop more effective dissemination of evidence-based interventions and implementation strategies. In this study, we conducted qualitative interviews to assess how delivery organizations in the Midwest use or would like to use research of HIV-related interventions and strategies, which we refer to for simplicity as ‘programs’.

Methods

Study Population & Procedures

We recruited participants affiliated with health service organizations and state or local health departments across the Midwestern United States that provided HIV prevention and/or treatment services. Eligible participants held roles in clinical service delivery, administration, or program leadership and had direct experience implementing HIV-related services. We employed a purposive and snowball sampling strategy [13], identifying initial contacts through professional networks and organizational affiliations. These individuals were contacted via email and encouraged to refer others who met the study criteria. Interested participants completed an eligibility screener and electronic informed consent prior to scheduling a one-on-one virtual interview with a trained member of the study team. Interviews were conducted via videoconference, audio-recorded with permission, and professionally transcribed verbatim. Participants received a $100 gift card as compensation.

Measurement

We developed a semi-structured interview guide (see Appendix), informed by Greenhalgh et al. [4] and the updated Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research (CFIR 2.0) [5]. The guide was designed to elicit perspectives on both individual- and system-level factors influencing implementation. Using real-world examples from their organizations, as well as hypothetical situation, participants were asked about each step in the process from identifying a need for a program or intervention, the selection, development, and/or adaptation of that program, and subsequent evaluation and/or dissemination of the program, with attention on how research is or could be used at each step.

Analysis

We conducted a rapid qualitative analysis informed by established rapid inquiry methods [14], grounded in thematic analysis. Two team members independently reviewed all transcripts and developed a preliminary codebook incorporating both deductive codes (derived from the interview guide and CFIR 2.0) and inductive codes emerging from the data. All transcripts were double-coded, with discrepancies resolved through discussion. Through iterative debriefings, structured summaries, and thematic matrices, the team synthesized findings across interviews until thematic saturation was achieved. Capitalized terms were altered to maintain anonymity.

This study was reviewed and deemed exempt by the Northwestern IRB (STU00220236).

Results

Our final sample included 11 individuals from 8 distinct organizations (42% response rate), including 2 state health departments and 6 community health centers or federally qualified health centers (FQHCs) all from urban or suburban jurisdictions. We found that participants used specific processes used to select and implement HIV-related programs, which included assessing needs, securing resources, and attaining leadership support. Organizations then located, adapted, or developed intervention, with varying degrees of evaluations and subsequent dissemination. Additionally, participants described their preferences for receiving information. These processes were often iterative or occurred simultaneously, rather than following a linear progression (Fig. 1), and are described in detail below.

Iterative implementation process

Implementation Considerations

A key first step was assessing the needs of their respective population, as organizations were almost universally focused on being able to meet needs of a specific community. This process was succinctly captured by one participant:

I think that the driving part of it is what is the need for a program? What are the needs of the patients? What are we seeing that keeps coming up and coming up and coming up? So, can we develop something that will be helpful for our patients, because this keep coming up? I think that’s a number one, probably. Then, two is what we already have in place and what research stuff could help us improve what we’re already doing. ID21

Almost all participants described using assessments, which could be informal, such as visiting clinic waiting rooms or talking with frontline staff or more structured, such as feedback from Community Advisory Boards or Ryan White assessments, as critical to decide priority areas to address,.

It does and so, what I tell my staff is that, “You may look up and I’m sitting in the waiting room talking to people. It’s okay; I’m not trying to find out who didn’t room them correctly; who didn’t get a copy of their insurance care. I truly want to hear, because my background is social work. Of course, I want to be in the waiting room.” ID25

Although multiple priority areas might be identified, the decision to move forward with an intervention was often guided by practical considerations. Availability of funding was foremost, though leadership buy-in and staff capacity were also mentioned. Notably, the processes of developing a program and securing funding were often intertwined.

It is a combo. We have, I would say, over the last five years worked hard to be much more proactive instead of sitting around and waiting to the right funding opportunity to come our way...At the beginning of the strategic plan period or the beginning of a fiscal year, we will say, “Where are we wanting to grow and how,” and have those listed and then, we have to have our prospectors have that front and center as they go seek out and try to mix our interests with what’s out there to be funded. Sometimes on the other end, there are not enough and, of course, we’re very proactive in saying, “We’re going to do this no matter what and we’ll figure out the funding later.” ID24

Funding was primarily considered in terms of external funders, though the cyclical nature of grant funding remained a notable concern. Other participants spoke of how an intervention may sustain itself for an organization.

The stability for it. And that's just because either HRSA, SAMHSA grants tend to be like multi-year grant cycles… And currently there are very limited grants opportunities that will address the needs for the populations that we have here at ORGANIZATION. And again, the population that we serve is very unique. ID18

In the business sense, how it can generate income for the agency but then, in the community sense, just how it can improve and impact, bridge the gap for many people that live in the communities that we service. ID20

Once a need was identified, the program must then align with the organization’s mission and gain the support of leaders.

I think the biggest factor is going to be leadership buy-in. Really, just being intentional about what it is that we’re asking, so that when we present our request to leadership, we can really speak to how this service or this initiative can positively impact the services here at ORGANIZATION, in multiple ways. ID20

Sites acknowledged that even with leadership support or funding, programs may not move forward due to limited organizational capacity. Particularly, sites mentioned that external funding opportunities were commonly not pursued due to being overly burdensome or time-intensive. There was a general sense that these organizations were already overburdened. Relatedly, some sites welcomed partnering on research grants while others felt that these brought extra requirements and were difficult to support, ideas revisited in the partnering conversation below.

Capacity, unfortunately. I think that we would love to bring in more projects. We actually have to say no more often than we have to say yes. That's because there's only—my boss is at capacity, I'm at capacity. And it took me a long time to understand this, I would rather do something small that has a greater impact than have a bunch of this little programs that have no impact ID18

[I] said, “If you want to do research, then you pay for an extra person to take some of the burden off of us, so we can think about it.” ID22

Developing external partnerships was often a step in the implementation process but depended on the project. There did not appear to be a consistent process; instead, sites seemed to reach out on a case-by-case basis. Regarding academic partnerships, most sites had positive reports, seeing these partnerships as helpful for developing programming, improving evaluations, and supporting dissemination.

You know, what's unique about that project is that we did it in combination with an academic healthcare institution, which is why there are publications about it, that is not typically our route of distribution. ID19

Types of initiatives

Once the organization had decided to implement a program, the next step was to select or develop the intervention. Broadly, there were three types of programs described: home-grown, grant-mandated, or pre-existing. Primarily, sites preferred to develop their own programs, driven by the desire to tailor interventions to specific populations or contexts (described below under Adaptations). These home-grown projects were also seen as an important way to foster staff creativity and motivation.

“Oh, we need to improve employment.” We’re going to hire someone to do some of these employment activities and maybe those activities are part of an existing model that’s out there that’s evidence informed or evidence based. But I would say, “No, for the most part, we’re going to hire people and let them do it and it’s not necessarily based on a model. It’s what people need.” ID23

One supervisor highlighted how home-grown programs were a way for them to motivate their staff, even as they presented funding challenges..

I think it's both and we're really lucky that we—like, I have staff that work for me that are passionate about best practice alerts in emergency rooms, passionate about that. When I say that word, do you know what I'm talking about? Passionate that that is how we are going to, like, people are not coming in for HIV rapid tests to like AIDS service organizations….That is not really a CDC initiative. There's very little funding that I can carve out from any grant to do that…. So we carved it out and we figured it out and they are doing that and it's impressive. ID26

Using new innovations was often seen as a way for organizations to empower and engage their staff, an important consideration that may not be met by pre-existing programs.

I think ORGANIZATION encourages staff to do QI, like quality improvement projects and we have a process improvement team that does a lot of QI projects on their own… So, they supported us in coming up with a program design so that we have the base number; we have a way to measure it. So, I think that’s encouraged at ORGANIZATION to go share out and that team helps with that. ID21

Developing a home-grown intervention took teamwork and energy, and (as described further under Dissemination) was often supplemented by learning from other organizations.

There’s no specific tool book; we were just making sure writing everything down. It was sort of oral lead-in for the injectable. Figuring out with PHARMACY, how quick do they send it and can we do the same day?....So, it was looking at working with our nurses, working with our providers….a lot of with the nurses and their supervisor. When they met together, sort of talking through this whole workflow of the injectables. It was a lot of communication, talking and communication with other teams....I may have attended one or two meetings or trainings, but I don’t feel like there was a lot of information out there and I was hungry to know what other people were doing. I think everyone was on the same page as, like we don’t know. I felt like when we went and asked, no one was really doing it, except for us. I’m sure it wasn’t just us, but it felt that way, so we figured it out on our own. ID21

Second, sites also reported using interventions or processes as defined by their funders. Compared to the empowerment of home-grown interventions, there was a sense of less freedom to adapt or innovate around these required interventions.

I know on the HIV side, there are a lot of required things you have meet on a monthly basis versus the HIV prevention side. We just didn’t have those requirements, but we knew we need to develop flows on our own, because there wasn’t anything set in place to say, “This is when you need to do this, this, this and this.” ID21

But they also have to have time to do it. The funder literally has to put it in the grant. If the funder said, “Get your hair cut every week, the ORGANIZATION would salute and go, ‘We’re getting our hair cut every week.’” But if they’re not required, they may not be doing it, because there’s other pressures on them. ID22

Finally, sites were asked about using pre-existing or externally developed interventions. There as varying levels of experience with these programs. Some participants expressed a need for existing materials when starting a new intervention.

But like having a guide like that would be amazing. And if one already exists please point me to it. ID28

While some had positive experiences, the predominant sense was that pre-existing interventions were considered a starting point or general framework, from which organizations would select or adapt certain components.

The biggest one is reading it and understanding it and not necessarily duplicated, because if we duplicate it we're going to just be set up for failure. The population that we serve is very unique and is often not—how can I say this? It's generally not the population that this interventions, or workflows, or programs have seen in the past. So, that's why we will take an intervention or programming and take the structure of it and build it within. And from there we just run pilots and see if it's working or no ID18

I think we’re really good at taking the framework and modifying it to meet the needs of the specific population that we want to serve. ID23

It at least gives us a starting point to be able to run the program, so we have an idea. Then, life happens or the group happens and then you do have to make some variances on there. We bought for the person to come five out of five appointments, but we realized that we may only be able to get [all the sessions]. We worked with that to be able to make sure we’re still running the program the way that it’s supposed to, but it may not be in five sessions. ID25

Other individuals felt that existing interventions, especially those tied to research partnerships, felt too constraining.

Hmm, I think I’m going to say, “No.” Outside of really general HRSA guidelines, grant funding guidelines and things of that sort, we’ve really been blessed enough to envision what that work looks like here, at ORGANIZATION and cater it to, let’s just say, the services that we have available. ID20

When you start bringing science into it, people feel hampered and that it is overly rigid and it’s not quite client centered, even if it is. There’s a cultural thing... ID24

Participants were very concerned with the need to adapt an intervention to their local context, whether due to different target populations or priorities. Reiterating their interest in meeting community need in the first step of the implementation process, participants believed took pride in understanding and connecting with those they served.

I feel like all these super great organizations, when they’re implementing some of these evidence-based interventions is almost like a choose your own adventure model to it. For instance, you put it together, you know, this is the intervention. These are things I need to think about if I’m changing the population or this is something I need to think about if we don’t have access to electronic health records; right? We’d have to get it from a clinical partner. What kind of pieces do we need to put in place or, in an urban setting and not the suburban place that you did it. What are the factors that we need to think about to do this? Because, as an organization, we are picking and choosing as we go along and, while that would be great for evidence-based intervention, I also feel like there could be some sort of manual put together.... So, you’re going to implement your own evidenced-based intervention, these are all the things that you need to think about. ID23

In particular, one participant highlighted that pre-existing interventions were valuable for providing language to clinicians, with institutional sources carrying greater influence among provides. However, community components were more likely to need adaptation.

And I honestly can't think of a time where we've used like a guide of theirs of how to operate in the community just because it's not—everything's very region specific…So, it has to be effective and it can't be harmful. So, the clinician stuff, guidelines for clinicians, we use that constantly. And it works great because when we're dealing with providers if they're told from the feds, it has more teeth in it than if they're pulled from us. ID28

Once an intervention was developed and being delivered, sites wanted the ability to evaluate their programs. However, there was variation in access and ability to conduct evaluation. Some sites had in-house evaluation teams; other relied on external partners or were limited to the requirements of reporting established by funders. Participants wanted evaluation to be built-in before a program was implemented, though this was not often the actual process.

For the SAMHSA, yes. There is an evaluation component. So we are partnered with PARTNER to do the tracking of the data and the evaluation for this particular program. ID18

So, we do have an internal quality and evaluation unit who works with us. And that's a constant conversation for us with any project we're doing. So really the base of a lot of our evaluation work are those reportable measures that we have to report back to the CDC on EHE funds and prevention funds. ID28

Dissemination – receiving information

The selection process was often iteratively related to dissemination, in terms of receiving information. In other words, there was a desire to connect and learn from peers – specifically those who were doing similar work – as they considered how to develop or adapt a new program. In particular, there were several examples mentioned of meetings facilitated by funding or larger umbrella organizations to bring sites together for these specific purposes.

So, I went around to agencies to shadow them, to find out how they did things. The state helped with that. “Here’s people you should follow or talk to,” and they were really good at pushing things forward… One of the things I thought about for a long time was almost when you have an organization that’s a little less experienced at a certain thing, but has the connections to the community and the care and love, to almost get them a mentor organization. ID22

We talk to pharma just about product and what’s the best way to do that and aid our providers. We’ve looked at CDC guidelines; we’ve looked at HRSA; we’ve looked at all of those guidelines to then, be able to start doing that. Then, we reached out to HEALTH DEPARTMENT, who’s like us and said, “Send me your....procedure.” She said, “Okay.” That’s how, because—a lot of the time, people, yes, I understand there’s room for research and coming up with new guidelines. Sometimes we need to do it and we need to do it quickly. Why recreate the wheel on something that has a very successful rate and funding their program? ID25

Participants expressed a strong interest in staying current with new programs science, but they faced challenges such as limited time and an overwhelming number of training opportunities. To address these limitations, sites adopted various strategies, such trusted ‘information brokers’, like AETC, NASTAD, and the Ryan White Conference, which provided regular seminars, updates, and emails.

Ultimately, I feel like we really lean into the AETC and, let’s just say, the training platforms that are available through our Ryan White funded agencies ID20

We rely very heavily on NASTAD when we have questions or when we need to be connected to someone, and we rely very heavily just on the information and spotlights that they put out about promising practices and programs....We also though, you know, we monitor the CDC's TRAIN system for research and topics and emerging things that are coming out, and we utilize that system as well as their CTS or capacity building and training request system for new interventions and training related to those when they come out, if they're appropriate and interesting ID19

We asked specifically about the CDC compendium as a source for interventions. Many participants had never heard of the compendium or used similar tools from other sources.

Others had used the compendium, usually in grant writing.

We used it in funding applications to find an intervention that fit what we were applying for, since it also has a website of evidenced-based interventions and we had used that one, as well ID23

We do. We use that in a few places. And the first one that comes to my mind is when we developed our Rapid Start guidelines for the state, we were using that. ID28

There were also observations that such online resources become unwieldly without regular pruning.

There's so much information... It's just PDF after PDF after PDF that people just chalked up there, here's PrEP guidance, breastfeeding guidance… And again, I love it. Like I'll go and I'll find what I'm looking for and then I'll open it and I'll read it. I'll read the whole PDF. I don't know going forward there's got to be a way for CDC and big people to make some of these guidance documents and information sharing and best practices shorter and the websites easier to navigate because it's never going to stop. While all this suggested a need for effective dissemination to organizations, there was no consensus on how sites wanted to learn about interventions. Preferred modality – paper, videos, etc. – varied greatly by individual and learning type, though there was sense that younger staff preferred video.

There are extremes. Like there are people that love to write and I printed same-day initiation for PrEP and it's like 300 pages long. That is hard. That is really hard to read. And I also have found like three pages long implementations, which is lacking details. There is one that I found to be a perfect medium that was about 30 pages long. It had infographics, and it had bullet points, and it had more like goal-achieving and less task oriented, because if this is going to be implemented by the organization, those texts are going to be different. So, it's mostly the suggestions that an organization should consider to successfully implement that intervention or module. ID18

But I think they would say, "I want a seven-minute YouTube video, or when I go to conferences instead of a 45 minute panel discussion, I need micro learning." ID26.

What did clearly emerge was that multiple formats are needed to truly understand and decide to adopt a program – initially a one-pager or brief document, then if interested, a webinar or more detailed manual.

I think just a snapshot either by slides or a snapshot in a one-pager and maybe a link to more information or it’s more details on your website. Don’t give me a toolkit right away. I don’t know what I’m going to do with that. Then, be open to chatting with me about, “Okay, so you implemented this in an urban area, but I live in a smaller town that’s got some rural aspects to it. Talk me through what you think needs to be changed so that I could do this. Otherwise, I’m just going to speculate on my own and be like, ‘Well, maybe this doesn’t work for X, Y and Z so I’m going to change these things without knowing those are critical components,’ but some of the others could be changed.” ID23

An emphasis on flexibility or adaptability was also important. This ties back to the desire and expectation from these organizations that they would have to tailor any intervention to meet their particular community; finding interventions designed with adaptation in mind was a clear advantage.

Then after that meeting, providing a model that, one, demonstrates again the program or the initiative, but then, two, provides space that allows for flexibility. ID20

Reiterating the desire to learn from peers, once a potential program was identified, talking to those in similar roles who had experience with the program was considered very important. This could also take the form of site visits. Then, for those who decided to adopt a program, more detailed manuals or videos were desired.

But also by the people that are actually doing the intervention, not necessarily from who developed it, because I think who developed it, it's very black and white on the writing. But from partner organizations that are implementing this intervention. So we can hear about the successes, challenges, or we can get some, like, tips of the implementation aspect of it. ID18

I would want to hear about what the findings are and how does it work. What does it look like on the ground? How well does it work with our priority communities; right? How intensive—how much of a ramp up is there for it? Just logistical stuff. Who would I reach out to? Ideally, I would reach out to the folks who created it and/or have piloted it. ID24

There was a strong sense that representation of community in the intervention team, and to consider inclusive perspectives in presentations. It was also recommended that slides and materials be shared after presentations, especially as staff may not always be able to attend live.

I don't want to sit there and read a whole paper….I'll look to see like what type of pictures or person they're utilizing for whatever they're representing. So if it's HIV care team and it's a picture, what does that care team look like?...So I think when I see the pictures that look closely more related to the work that's actually being done in our state or throughout the different population, that's like, okay, that's something I'm interested in… I also like to see straightforward….Like just get straight, I mean, because we don't have a lot of time either…. So let's just get straight to the point, all the fluff, they can leave out the fluff. ID27

Dissemination – sharing information

Participants largely wanted to be able to share their successful interventions with others in the field and, as seen throughout the examples below, highlighted the ways in which funders could expedite this exchange. However few sites were able to do so and many reported barriers.

It’s always a challenge—I think the city—they used to have quarterly meetings of people they funded. They were so wonderful...But they were really great and they were great for my staff to pull my staff in, where I could introduced them. ID22

Funders did not always support dissemination and organizations rarely had additional funds to support dissemination. There was also a connection to the lack of evaluation, as described above.

It's the same thing with the biomedical conference that I was awarded the scholarships to attend and learn of initiatives. The grants don't want to pay for it. So it's very institutional barriers of, like, doing the work and getting it out there, but if it's sadly not within the organization or even the funders to establish and present, it's not going to go anywhere because there is no—it's not a priority. The priority is you're given this money, you have the scopes, and that's it. So we have loads of data, loads, loads, and loads of data for some of the work that we're not funded for. And we want to get it out there, but there's no one out there that's saying like, "Yeah, let us help you get it out there to make noise, or to raise awareness, or to potentially open up for new money." ID18

For us, we were rarely able to go to conferences, because we didn’t have the funding to go. If we went, it was because somebody helped pay for it sometimes. Once in a while, people could go. ID22

Sites spoke well of funders that supported this work, either through a grant or by convening sites togethers.

But typically for other strategies, or activities, or approaches, we really leverage our federal organizations in order to disseminate findings and best practices. We primarily do that work through NASTAD for our HIV and viral hepatitis work, or through NCSD for our STI work. ID19

So, we actually have done quite a few things for PROGRAM. We did a NASTAD PROGRAM promising practices like brief with NASTAD. We luckily in State we can travel a lot and we dedicate a lot of money to travel. So we've presented PROGRAM, I think at the Ryan White Conference…. And our project officers definitely know about it and will link us to other states. ID26

There was also recognition that these opportunities to present and attend were important growth experiences for staff. Participants were less likely to report disseminating in scientific journals or academic writing.

I think it gets shared in that ORGANIZATION really loves to go to conferences and talk to people. ORGANIZATION is involved in multiple community committees and taskforces so they go and talk about the program and what they’re doing. We’re not as good at writing something up and putting it on our website that says, “This is the model that we’re doing.” ID23

And I said there are very few people here that still publish. It's not like a thing that I see as much as I used to. ID26

Discussion

Overall, the implementation process was strongly guided by a desire to meet community needs, from initial identification of the priority area to selection of a program, which was most often designed for or adapted to the local community. Outside research played little role in these initial steps. Organizations did want to learn about research and interventions, but to deal with time constraints, relied on trusted information brokers. Once they learned of a research -based intervention, there were preferences for a range of materials from introductory to detailed, preferably with insights from real-world use by peer organization. Finally, organizations wished to disseminate out the results of their own programs, but faced barriers in terms of evaluation and funding.

Consistent with these results, other researchers have also found lack of fit or the need to adapt as a key barrier to uptake of evidence-based interventions [8, 10, 11]. Our results were also in line with prior work showing resistance to ‘top down’ or pre-existing interventions, particularly without evidence of real-world effectiveness [11].

This work suggests several ways to better integrate intervention and implementation research and improve dissemination (Fig. 2). Much intervention work has a source-based approach [15], emphasizing the development of an intervention which is then pushed out and marketed. Most of the organizations had a more user-based approach [15], focused on their own assessments and selection of an intervention/program.

Recommendations for researchers and those supporting practitioners

Therefore, research-based interventions must be: 1) aligned with a local need, 2) flexible and adaptable to different contexts, and 3) shared in a range of formats. In practice, earlier and sustained community-academic partnerships could address the first point. Regarding flexibility, researchers should consider distinguishing the essential and modifiable components, or, as Hawe et al. describe, the form vs function of their intervention [16]. Going further, researchers can create different versions for different contexts and audiences. Finally, for the last point, researchers should consider developing a range of products for dissemination, from brief, interest-generating one-pagers or videos, to full webinars or manuals. Researchers should also highlight real-world experiences of delivering the intervention and identify useful brokers (ATECs or professional organizations) who can distribute or host the materials. However, given the paradox that researchers have been urged to ‘design for dissemination’ for several years and yet the dissemination gap persists, these approaches may be insufficient [6, 7, 17]. This may indicate additional barriers, such as funding constraints or challenges in developing community partnerships necessary for this type of intervention development [18–23]; additional work in this area would be informative.

While the dissemination gap persists, however, this work also highlights how organization-developed interventions could be better leveraged and disseminated between organizations. These gaps can be addressed by: 1) improving evaluation, through funding and leveraging academic partnerships (as highlighted in the recent CDC guidance [24]); 2) explicitly including funds for dissemination; and 3) funding networking between organizations. Increasing the dissemination of these pragmatic and context-appropriate interventions could rapidly increase implementation scale-up of HIV prevention and treatment interventions. Paradoxically, while funders can be important contributors to better dissemination, many organizations saw external funding, and particularly research funding, as overly taxing and difficult to apply for.

This study involved health service organizations in the Midwest, mostly in urban or suburban settings, using a convenience sample, limiting generalizability. Organizations who did not respond may have different experiences. However, findings were robust across organizations and themes were saturated. Additional data from other areas, particularly the South and rural settings, and organization types are needed.

Conclusion

We found the service organizations have intentional processes for selecting and developing interventions that may not align with research processes. There are numerous ways researchers and funders could improve synergy between those processes, including improving the evaluative and dissemination capacity of service organizations and their locally designed programs, and designing flexible interventions with an expectation of adaptation.
