Parent PTSD, social support and differential associations with facets of anxiety sensitivity
Authors: Kristen Bermingham, Christopher Latourrette, Karen G. Chartier, Spit for Science Working Group, Ananda B. Amstadter, Kaitlin Bountress
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground: Anxiety Sensitivity (AS), the belief that anxiety experiences have negative implications, is a prospective predictor of posttraumatic stress disorder (PTSD) and has been linked to changes in PTSD in experimental studies. AS is comprised of three sub-components: cognitive, physical, and social concerns. PTSD is moderately heritable and parental PTSD may increase risk for these AS facets both directly and through environmental influences like reduced social support.Objective: The present study will examine whether Parent PTSD is associated with reduced social support and in turn facets of AS.Method: Participants were recruited from an ongoing longitudinal study of college student mental health; 94 individuals with current alcohol use disorder (AUD), PTSD, both, or neither (i.e. trauma-exposed controls) were included.Results: Cross-sectional analyses revealed that offspring of parents with PTSD reported less social support in their families of origin, which was associated with higher AS-cognitive concerns. There was a direct effect of parent PTSD on physical concerns, but no effects of parent PTSD or support on social concerns.Conclusions: Offspring of parents with PTSD who perceive less family support may be at higher risk for internalising problems via increased negativity, and may not feel that their caregivers are reliable sources of emotional regulation. Therefore, reduced support may explain the path between parent PTSD and cognitive AS concerns. In contrast, it is possible that simply having a parent with PTSD who perhaps displays outward symptoms of anxiety increases risk for more AS physical concerns. Together, these findings suggest that offspring of parents with PTSD are a high-risk group on whom family-based interventions bolstering social support and AS (cognitive)-based interventions ought to be focused.

Introduction

Anxiety Sensitivity (AS), the belief that anxiety experiences have negative implications (O’Bryan et al., 2022; Reiss et al., 1986), is a prospective predictor of posttraumatic stress disorder (PTSD) symptoms (Short et al., 2020). AS has three sub-components: cognitive (e.g. lack of cognitive control is a sign of going crazy), physical (e.g. physical sensations are a sign of a heart attack), and social concerns (e.g. fear of publicly observable anxiety) (Shabahang et al., 2021). Changes in AS drive changes in PTSD in experimental work, with cognitive concerns being particularly important, potentially by influencing the belief that anxiety symptoms are harmful (El Jamal et al., 2025; Short et al., 2023).

Thus, understanding drivers of AS is important to reducing risk for PTSD. PTSD has family influences (Afari et al., 2021; Fel et al., 2022; Marchese & Huckins, 2023), and given AS’s links to PTSD, parental PTSD may increase risk for AS – via genetic and environmental effects. Parents with PTSD are often less emotionally available, perceive their children more negatively, report finding parenting more stressful, and have children with poorer emotion regulation (Christie et al., 2019; Zitzmann et al., 2024). Thus, parents with PTSD may parent in a less supportive manner, and pass on an anxious disposition. We test via cross-sectional analyses the hypotheses that those reporting parental PTSD will also report higher AS on all three facets, via reduced familial social support, over and above covariates.

Method

IRB approval was granted by Virginia Commonwealth University and informed consent was obtained. Data come from participants recruited to be part of a PTSD-AUD study from an ongoing longitudinal study of college student mental health; 94 individuals with current AUD [13], PTSD or sub-clinical PTSD [12], both [12], or neither (i.e. trauma-exposed controls) [57] were included. Procedures are detailed elsewhere (Latourrette et al., 2025). A clinical interview via Zoom assessed DSM-5 Criterion A trauma exposure. A web-based survey and a lab visit were then completed. The assessment included biological sex (73.4% female), participant report of lifetime trauma via the Life Events Checklist (M: 5.18, SD: 3.05), biological parent PTSD (27.7% reported they believed at least one biological parent had PTSD, coded 1 = yes), childhood social support via a modified Medical Outcomes Study Social Support Survey (sum of the 8 items; M: 29.59; SD: 4.95; Cronbach Alpha .87), and AS via the Anxiety Sensitivity Index-3 to capture the physical concerns subscale (M: 5.19, SD: 4.48; Cronbach Alpha: .82), cognitive concerns subscale (M: 3.90, SD: 4.28; Cronbach Alpha: .85), and social concerns subscale (M: 8.62, SD: 4.84; Cronbach Alpha: .76).

Path models were estimated, within Mplus Version 8, using the ML estimator, and missing data were estimated using Maximum Likelihood estimation. Parent PTSD was a predictor of social support, and social support and parent PTSD were included as predictors of the three facets of anxiety sensitivity. All paths controlled for the effects of sex and number of traumas. The predictors and outcomes were allowed to freely correlate.

Results

The final path model and results are depicted in the Figure. The model was fully saturated, so no fit statistics were provided. In predicting social support, those with a parent with PTSD reported receiving less support. The covariates of sex and number of experienced traumas were non-significant (NS). For physical concerns, the effect of social support was NS, but there was a direct effect of parent PTSD such that having a parent with PTSD was associated with higher physical concerns. Females and those with more traumas also reported higher scores. For cognitive concerns, those receiving less social support also reported higher AS cognitive concerns.1 There was no effect of parent PTSD or the number of traumas. Females also reported higher scores on this subscale. Finally, for social concerns, neither parent PTSD nor social support exerted significant effects. Females reported higher values on this subscale. The number of traumas was also not associated with values on this subscale.

Discussion

Offspring of parents with PTSD reported less social support in their families of origin, which was associated with higher AS-cognitive concerns. There was a direct effect of parent PTSD on AS-physical concerns, but no effects of parent PTSD or support on social concerns.

To our knowledge, no work had examined the links among parental PTSD, social support and facets of AS. Facets of AS are critical to understand, as they are associated with mental health. For example, the cognitive concerns subscale is related to depression and suicidality (Saulnier et al., 2018; Smith et al., 2022). Physical concerns are one mechanism by which intolerance of uncertainty is associated with greater PTSD hyperarousal symptoms and during the pandemic, COVID-related fear (METİN et al., 2025; Warren et al., 2021), and the social concerns scale was associated with social anxiety fear of negative evaluation (Allan et al., 2018; Brooke & Intrieri, 2023). Offspring of parents with PTSD who perceive less family support may be at higher risk for internalising problems via increased negativity, and may not feel that their caregivers are reliable sources of emotional regulation (i.e. Parent PTSD may impact social support and in turn cognitive AS). In contrast, it is possible that simply having a parent with PTSD who perhaps displays outward symptoms of anxiety increases risk for more AS physical concerns. The lack of association with social concerns could be due to low power or a true null effect.

These findings should be interpreted in light of limitations. Our sample was small, limiting power to detect small effects. Participants were college students or had completed all or at least some college, limiting generalisability. We had a small number of males, but did detect some effects of sex on AS, underscoring future work. These data were cross-sectional, so we cannot be confident about the direction of effects, although it is unlikely that young adult AS impacted parent PTSD and family of origin social support. Additionally, although individuals with a parent with PTSD reported less support, we cannot be confident that it was the parent with PTSD who provided less support; it may be that it is parent PTSD, co-parenting with someone with PTSD, or both that is associated with less support. Finally, although offspring reports of parent PTSD tend to be reliable(Yehuda et al., 2006), this is not the gold standard in terms of reporting of potential PTSD (i.e. clinical interview is the gold standard). Together, these findings suggest that offspring of parents with PTSD are a high-risk group on whom family-based interventions bolstering child social support and cognitive-based interventions to improve AS ought to be focused.
