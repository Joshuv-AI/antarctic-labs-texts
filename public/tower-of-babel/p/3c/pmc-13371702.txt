Instrumental assessment of Sit-to-Stand in Parkinson's disease: a scoping review
Authors: Marco Trucco, Roberta Ditella, Anastasia Dhimitriadhi, Beatrice Occhetto, Martina Ballesio, Mirko Job, Marco Testa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionParkinson's disease (PD) is a growing neurological challenge. The Sit-to-Stand (STS) transition is a key proxy for functional independence and fall risk. While sensor technology offers objective STS assessment, the methodological landscape is highly heterogeneous, lacking standardized protocols. This scoping review systematically maps the literature on the technological assessment of the STS transition in PD, focusing on sensors, tasks, settings, and variables. Additionally, the review details device accessibility and ecological validity in home-based settings, as these are two important elements for large-scale application and implementation in standardised protocols for both clinical assessment and real-world monitoring.MethodsA scoping review following Joanna Briggs Institute and PRISMA-ScR guidelines. Five major databases (PubMed, Embase, CINAHL, Scopus, Web of Science) and citation tracking were used to identify studies from 2015 to 2025. Eligibility criteria included studies with PD patients of any stage undergoing instrumental STS transition assessment. We mapped data according to sensors, tasks, settings, and variables. We performed specific subanalyses on Inertial Measurement Units (IMUs)—distinguishing research-grade 'kinematic' sensors from consumer 'Mobile' devices—and assessed ecological validity (unsupervised vs. supervised) in home-based settings.ResultsFrom 7,368 records, 77 studies were included. Four dominant trends emerged: (1) Technology: IMUs were used in 87% of the studies. (2) Protocol: the Timed-Up-and-Go (TUG) test (60%) was more prevalent than pure STS tasks (22%), (3) Setting: Most assessments occurred in outpatient settings (79%), followed by home-based settings (25%). (4) Functional variables: despite technological sophistication, 'duration' remained the primary variable followed by linear acceleration and angular velocity. Moreover in the home setting, while 74% of studies achieved high ecological validity (unsupervised), a "scalability gap" emerged: 63% still utilized complex research-grade IMUs.ConclusionInstrumental STS transition assessment in PD is a rapidly expanding field. It is dominated by IMU sensors, but fragmented by protocol heterogeneity and a critical “scalability gap” in home monitoring. Complex variables still require non-scalable sensors, while scalable mobile devices have not yet been validated for advanced metrics. Background

Parkinson's disease (PD) represents one of the most significant neurological challenges of our time and is often described as a "neurological pandemic" [1]. Epidemiological projections are stark: the global prevalence, estimated at 12 million people in 2025, is expected to double to over 25 million by 2050 [2]. Owing to its progressive neurodegenerative nature, PD manifests with a wide spectrum of motor and nonmotor symptoms that severely compromise mobility, fall risk, autonomy, and quality of life for people with Parkinson's disease [3, 4]. However, the current standard of care, which often relies on episodic, in-clinic visits (e.g., every 6–12 months), is increasingly strained and ill suited to manage PD effectively [5, 6]. This model struggles to capture the fluctuating nature of symptoms and fails to provide continuous patient support [7, 8].

Technology is one potential solution to address this issue. The integration of digital health technologies, facilitated by the omnipresence of sensors, widespread connectivity, and increasing digital literacy, offers a viable solution to the scalability crisis [9, 10]. These tools can enable objective, remote patient monitoring and 'digital phenotyping', providing unbiased, quantifiable data capable of revolutionizing clinical practice, monitoring, and research [5, 7]. Therefore, identifying functional biomarkers that are both clinically meaningful and robustly measurable is fundamental.

The Sit-to-Stand (STS) transition has emerged as a high-value target for this purpose. The STS is not merely a single movement; it is a fundamental prerequisite for daily life, is performed multiple times per day, and is biomechanically demanding, requiring the complex coordination of force generation, postural control, and balance [11–15]. In patients with PD, STS performance is frequently and progressively impaired [16–18]. This impairment is not only a symptom but also a direct precursor to critical adverse outcomes, serving as a powerful proxy for functional independence, global postural control, and fall risk [9, 16, 19].

Although traditional clinical assessments are useful, as demonstrated by the Five Times Sit-to-Stand (5TSTS) [16], technology-supported rehabilitation approaches offer numerous advantages, as described above. These include the collection of longitudinal, objective data; the ability to capture complex and multidimensional parameters; and the facilitation of patient engagement and data handling in clinical trials.

While previous reviews have examined the clinimetric properties of various devices, a comprehensive mapping of the methodological landscape—spanning the full range of technologies, protocols, and contexts—is currently lacking. Therefore, this scoping review aims to systematically map the literature on technological assessments of the STS transition in PD. Crucially, to ensure a comprehensive mapping of the methodological landscape, the scope of this review explicitly includes both standalone STS tasks (e.g., the 5TSTS) and STS transitions embedded within more complex motor protocols, most notably the Timed Up and Go (TUG) test. To achieve this goal, we addressed four primary key questions: (1) the sensors used, (2) the tasks/tests performed, (3) the acquisition settings, and (4) the functional variables analyzed. Furthermore, to address known barriers to clinical translation, considering the widespread diffusion of inertial measurement units (IMUs) in daily life [20, 21], we conducted two specific subanalyses focusing on (a) technology accessibility (distinguishing 'Kinematic' research-grade IMUs from 'Mobile' consumer devices) and (b) ecological validity in the home-based setting (distinguishing high-ecology unsupervised home monitoring from low-ecology supervised assessments). The ultimate goal of this review is to provide researchers and clinicians with a clear synthesis of the current evidence, thereby informing the development of standardised protocols for STS assessment that can clearly differentiate between in-depth clinical assessment and scalable, real-world monitoring of autonomy and prediction of fall risk.

Methods

Protocol and registration

The present scoping review was conducted in accordance with the Joanna Briggs Institute methodology for scoping reviews [22]. The Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) checklist was used for reporting the manuscript [23]. The study protocol was registered a priori on 27 May 2025 in Open Science Framework Registries. https://archive.org/details/osf-registrations-vyfwz-v1.

Eligibility criteria

Articles were eligible for inclusion if they met the following Population, Concept and Context (PCC) criteria:

The population included patients of any age or sex who were diagnosed with PD at any phase of the disease or parkinsonism.

Context: studies conducted in all types of settings.

Context: studies conducted in all types of settings.

This scoping review included any study design. No language or geographical restrictions were applied. We used DeepL Translate (online) to translate non-English studies. Studies were included if at least 80% of the population met the inclusion criteria. Studies that did not meet the PCC criteria were excluded.

Study selection process

We searched the following databases for literature up to May 13th, 2025: MEDLINE (via PubMed), Embase, CINAHL, Web of Science and Scopus. We considered any study design published within the last 10 years (as of January 1, 2015). In addition, we used Web of Science to implement backward and forward citation tracking strategies for eligible studies. All the search strategies were developed and implemented by an expert librarian.

Supplementary material 1 reports the full search strategy for these databases.

Duplicates were eliminated via the Deduplicator function of “Systematic Review Accelerator” [24].

Two reviewers (AD and RD) independently performed the study selection process, first by title/abstract and second by full text. Disagreements were resolved by consensus or by the decision of a third author (MT). We used the online electronic systematic review software package Rayyan QCRI to organize, track and perform the selection process [25]. All reasons for excluding any full-text source of evidence were recorded and reported.

Data extraction process

Data extraction was conducted independently by two reviewers (AD and RD) via a standard Excel spreadsheet developed a priori according to the population, concept, context (PCC) framework. The process was carried out in a double-blinded manner, and any disagreements were resolved by consensus or by the decision of a third author (MT).

We extracted the following data from each study: article name, first author, year of publication, country, study design, population, sex, age, phase of disease, type of setting, type of sensor and location, type of task/test involving a Sit-to-Stand transition, and functional variables/parameters.

Data synthesis

The results are presented both quantitatively and thematically. The quantitative analysis involved the collection of data, which were summarized and reported descriptively.

Characteristics such as country, study design, year of publication, sex, age and phases of disease were reported solely in the table for contextual clarity. In contrast, the type of sensor, type of test/task, type of setting and functional variables were both discussed in the text and summarized in tables, providing a clear, accessible overview.

Furthermore, a focus was placed on IMUs, which are the most widely used sensors, for which we identified two subcategories: kinematic analysis IMUs and mobile device IMUs. This distinction was based on the specific intended use of the technology, characterizing “kinematic-analysis IMUs” as systems specifically developed for research-grade assessment of human movement, and “mobile-device IMUs” as systems developed for general use by individuals in their activities of daily living.

For each subcategory, the following data were analyzed and summarized through tables: parameters, sensor location, type of task/test, and type of setting.

Finally, with respect to the home-based setting, two reviewers (AD and RD) examined the ecological validity of the assessment, classifying it as high or low on the basis of the following factors: real-life context and absence of supervision (in person or via videoconference). Specifically, a "high ecological validity" rating was strictly assigned only if both evaluated factors were positive. If either of these conditions was not satisfied, the study was assigned a "low" rating.

The findings were discussed and reported in a table, combining them with considerations of IMU costs.

Results

An overview of the study selection process is provided in Fig. 1. We identified 7,368 records from databases (PubMed n = 678; Embase n = 4,109; CINHAL n = 308; Scopus n = 1,378; Web of Science = 895) with 2,600 duplicates. After screening 4,768 titles and abstracts, 4,643 studies were excluded.

PRISMA flowchart

We reviewed 125 full texts and retrieved 6 additional studies through citation tracking implemented with the Web of Science. For excluded reports, the reason was explained according to population, concept, context and duplicates. Ultimately, 77 studies met the inclusion criteria for this scoping review.

Supplementary materials 2 reports the references to the included studies, and supplementary material 3 reports the references to the excluded studies, along with the reasons for their exclusion.

Characteristics of the included studies and patients

All included studies were published in English and had different study designs, of which most were observational studies (n = 47/77; 61%).

The included studies were conducted in 17 countries, with the majority in Italy (n = 18; 23.4%) and the USA (n = 15; 19.5%). The publication of studies was evenly distributed over the past decade. Additionally, an analysis considering the COVID-19 period with the subsequent increase in technology use [26], which corresponds to the midpoint of the research period, revealed a similar distribution (period 2015–2019: 46.8%; period 2020–2025: 53.2%).

Among the seventy-seven included studies, 54 reported the sex of a total of 2,037 patients, with 700 females (34%) and 1,337 males (66%). This distribution is consistent with the prevalence data for PD among females and males.

67 studies report the age of the patients and are divided according to mean age into two categories: ≤ 65 with 43% of the patients and > 65 with the remaining 57%.

The disease phase was reported according to the Hoehn & Yahr (H&Y) scale and divided into early, middle and advanced stages. In accordance with our primary aim to map the methodological landscape of STS assessment, the H&Y scale was selected as the principal clinical indicator for this review. Across the 77 highly heterogeneous studies included, the H&Y scale was the most consistently reported metric, providing a robust and universally recognized indication of overall disease severity and clinical applicability. While we acknowledge that additional clinical characteristics—such as MDS-UPDRS III scores, disease duration, or specific stratifications by faller versus non-faller status—are critical for establishing the direct clinical value of these sensors, their reporting was highly inconsistent and fragmented across the literature. Attempting to aggregate these diverse and inconsistently reported variables would have introduced significant confounding bias.

Twenty-eight studies (36%) did not report H&Y scores; most other studies considered patients in the middle stage, and none considered a sample consisting solely of patients in the advanced stage (Table 1).

We observed that the studies employed various tests or tasks; the most commonly used test was the TUG test (60%), followed by the STS test (22%).

The results were found in every type of setting: inpatients (4%), outpatients (79%) and home-based (25%) (Table 2).

Focus on inertial measurement units

In-depth analysis was performed on IMUs depending on the IMU category (kinematic analysis (78%) or mobile devices (22%)), and the following were analyzed: parameters, sensor location, type of test/task, and setting.

Regarding sensor configurations, we expanded our analysis to categorize IMUs into single-sensor and multi-sensor setups, providing clarity on the complexity of data acquisition. However, it is important to note that specific multi-sensor anatomical combinations, wearability methods (e.g., elastic straps, belts), and commercial brands utilized were extremely heterogeneous across the included studies. Due to this high variability, synthesizing these elements into a summary table was not feasible; nevertheless, all granular details regarding manufacturers, specific anatomical combinations, and device fixation methods are fully reported in Supplementary Material 4.

Focus on the home-based setting: ecological validity

With respect to home-based setting, we observed that all assessments were conducted with IMUs, and occasionally, other sensors were used as well. We classified the studies as having either high ecological validity (74%) or low ecological validity (26%).

We then examined the proportion of studies employing the STS test that were classified as having high ecological validity (75%) and the proportion observed among studies using the TUG test (80%).

Furthermore, we explored the aspects of accessibility and transparency, distinguishing kinematic analysis sensors (63%) as specific research instruments and mobile devices (37%) as accessible and transparent instruments, with reference to the detected parameters and their ecological validity.

Discussion

This scoping review systematically mapped the scientific literature pertaining to the use of technology for the quantitative assessment of the STS transition in patients with PD. The analysis of 77 included studies has delineated a comprehensive landscape of a rapidly evolving field of research, one characterized by significant methodological heterogeneity but also by remarkably clear technological and contextual trends. The results confirm that while the technology for objective assessment is widely utilized, its translation into scalable and ecologically validated clinical protocols remains a significant challenge. This discussion reports the principal findings, contextualizes them within the current clinical and research landscape, delineates the strengths and limitations of this review, and, finally, articulates the implications for both rehabilitative practice and future research.

Summary of principal findings

First, a clear technological dominance of IMUs was observed. With 87% of the studies employing this technology, IMUs are considered the instrument of choice for kinematic assessment outside of traditional motion capture laboratories [27]. This finding confirmed our hypothesis and justified our subanalysis on IMUs, as other systems, like force platforms (9%) and optical systems (17%), are largely restricted to laboratory settings as reference standards.

Second, task/test dominance was identified. The STS transition has not been primarily studied as a standalone motor task (e.g., the STS test or 5TST, 22% of studies). Rather, it is more frequently analyzed as a subcomponent of the TUG test, a protocol employed in 60% of the included studies.

Third, dominance setting emerged. The vast majority of research (79%) is conducted in outpatient settings. This strongly suggests that instrumental assessment is viewed primarily as an extension of traditional clinical assessment, performed within a controlled, supervised environment. Conversely, exploration of the home-patient setting, which represents the frontier for long-term monitoring and true ecological assessment, remains significantly rare (25%) [28]. This highlights a critical gap between the potential of wearable technology and its current implementation in patients’ daily lives.

Finally, the analysis of the extracted metrics revealed a dominance of variables.

Regardless of the sophistication of the sensor, STS duration remains the most widely used parameter, measured in 46 out of 67 studies conducted using IMUs, 9/13 using optical systems and in all studies utilizing force platforms (7/7). Although duration is a highly sensitive clinical indicator of axial bradykinesia that effectively distinguishes patients with PD from healthy controls [10, 16, 29], the field often uses advanced technologies simply to replace a stopwatch. To truly improve clinical care, research must systematically explore the complex kinematic parameters made available by these technologies. For example, an increase in mediolateral sway and a reduction in fluidity (jerks) immediately after the STS are powerful predictors of postural instability and fall risk [30–32], whilst reduced trunk flexion reflects a biomechanical loss of anticipatory momentum, providing direct targets for personalised rehabilitation [16, 17, 32–34].

Interpretation of trends and methodological implications

The joint interpretation of these findings reveals a series of critical gaps and trade-offs that define the current challenges in the field.

Protocol heterogeneity: the TUG versus pure STS challenge

The predominance of the TUG (60%) over pure STS tests (22%) is a logical methodological choice. The TUG test is one of the most widely used and validated assessment tools for fall risk in PD patients, and its instrumentation allows for the analysis of the entire motor sequence [35]. While the inclusion of all protocols containing an STS transition was correct for the mapping objective of this review, the resulting reliance on the TUG comes at a methodological cost. This heterogeneity implies that for future studies analyzing specific variables, it will be critical to determine whether data from pure STS and TUG-embedded transitions can be aggregated or if doing so introduces a significant confounding bias. As highlighted in the literature, the STS transition within a TUG test is biomechanically distinct from a pure STS test (e.g., 5TSTS). The former is an STS-to-Walk transition, incorporating motor anticipation, horizontal momentum generation, and immediate step initiation, with different postural control strategies than the latter, which is an STS transition aimed at achieving a stable standing posture [33, 36]. Aggregating data from these two distinct tasks limits the clinical understanding of specific motor deficits, as the STS-to-Walk phase in the TUG masks the pure anti-gravity force generation and balance recovery required to simply stand up.

On the other hand, focusing on the pure STS test (like the 5TSTS or 30 s-CST) retains fundamental clinical value [16, 17]: it is a test that isolates the ability to rapidly generate force against gravity and control balance during a self-generated perturbation. Furthermore, it is a test that can be performed on a larger number of patients, including those with greater disabilities, who might be unable to complete a TUG safely, making it an ideal candidate for monitoring more frail populations [37].

Demographic gaps: the search for the "Invisible Patient"

Our demographic analysis revealed critical gaps in both reporting and inclusion. The fact that 36% of studies failed to report the disease stage (H&Y scale) is a significant methodological flaw that prevents the contextualization of results and impedes any clear aggregated data synthesis.

Even more strikingly, 0% of the included studies recruited samples consisting exclusively of patients in advanced disease stages (H&Y 4–5). While the exclusion of H&Y stage 5 patients (who are wheelchair-bound or bedridden) is methodologically understandable given their inability to perform the STS test, the total absence of specific data on the H&Y 4 subgroup was unexpected and represents a major limitation of the current body of literature [38]. Patients at this stage, despite severe disability, are often still able to perform the STS transition, and the assessment of this capacity would be highly valuable. Moreover, patients at this H&Y stage may no longer be able to complete a TUG safely but may still be able to perform a pure STS test. This highlights the importance of using this simpler task to gain insights into fall risk and to inform personalized rehabilitation. It is precisely for this subgroup—which is at the highest risk of falls and loss of independence—that a qualitative, instrumental assessment of STS could be maximally informative for defining fall risk and designing assistive interventions [39]. The inclusion of this cohort would also permit essential comparisons with less advanced cohorts to understand the biomechanical progression of STS deficits. The current literature, which focuses predominantly on patients in the middle stage of the disease (39%), effectively overlooks the very patients with the greatest clinical need.

"Variable gap" between kinematic and mobile IMUs

Our subanalysis of IMU-based studies (Table 4) confirmed a stark division in the field, which is dominated by "research-grade" kinematic sensors (78%). This indicates that research is still focused primarily on laboratory-grade precision and proof-of-concept validation rather than on clinical scalability [5].

This technological division creates a clear "variable gap”. Complex kinematic variables that are fundamental to understanding the motor strategy (e.g., momentum transfer vs. flexion strategy), such as the ROMs of the trunk, knee, and hip, are captured exclusively by kinematic systems (e.g., 40% for trunk ROM vs. 0% for mobile devices) [40]. This gap is not merely a methodological preference but an intrinsic technological limitation: calculating the ROM requires measuring the relative angles between two body segments, thus necessitating a multisensor setup (e.g., one sensor on the trunk and another on the thigh) [41]. Mobile devices, which are typically single-unit systems (e.g., a smartphone or a smartwatch), cannot acquire these data [42].

This technical distinction highlights a fundamental clinical trade-off. While complex metrics like trunk ROM are invaluable for personalizing clinical rehabilitation (e.g., correcting flexion deficits), unsupervised home monitoring requires robust, simpler variables like sway and jerk, which directly correlate with real-world fall risk.

Interestingly, however, a niche use for mobile devices in measuring sway was identified (27 vs. 2% for kinematic devices). This finding, although derived from a small number of studies, is of fundamental strategic importance. This insight suggests a potential role for mobile devices not as tools to replicate the complex biomechanics of kinematic systems but as "stability meters" following the transition [30, 31, 43]. Given that postural instability (quantified as sway after a task) is a key predictor of fall risk in PD patients, the ability to monitor this specific variable with a ubiquitous and low-reactivity device (a smartphone) represents a crucial direction for developing ecological monitoring and fall prevention strategies.

The "scalability gap" in the home-based setting

The analysis of the home-patient setting (n = 19) yielded the most surprising and perhaps most critical finding of this review (Table 5). First, the majority of these studies (74%) were classified as having high ecological validity (i.e., conducted without supervision), confirming the technical feasibility of remote monitoring [44, 45].

In-depth analysis of ecological validity in the home-patient setting

However, contrary to expectations, we discovered a "scalability gap." The home-based setting is not dominated by mobile devices. Instead, 63% of studies conducted at home still utilize "research-grade" kinematic sensors. This result is paradoxical and suggests that current high-ecology home monitoring is still an extension of the laboratory, requiring the deployment of expensive, complex-to-position, and nonscalable devices to the general patient population.

The analysis of the variables extracted in this context confirms the paradox. Kinematic sensors succeed in capturing complex variables (e.g., trunk ROM, jerk, and power), even in high-ecology settings. Conversely, mobile devices have a fragmented performance: advanced metrics such as power and CoP displacement were only acquired (n = 2/7 for both) in low-ecology studies (i.e., supervised by a researcher at home).

The implication is stark: a critical trade-off exists among scalability, ecological validity, and data richness. To obtain rich biomechanical data in an unsupervised manner, the current literature relies on expensive kinematic sensors. To use scalable mobile devices, supervision still appears necessary to ensure a correct setup and the acquisition of advanced data. This "scalability gap" is the primary barrier preventing the translation of instrumental STS assessment into routine clinical practice and large-scale home monitoring [44, 46]. Our scoping limits the observation of interest to the objective evaluation of kinematic parameters of the STS transition, excluding a broader field of interest that uses wearable sensors. These sensors, which are based on artificial intelligence algorithms that are capable of managing noise and inconsistencies in mobile device data, are able to recognize everyday activities [47, 48].

Strengths and limitations

This scoping review is the first to specifically and granularly map the literature on the instrumentation of the STS transition in PD. The strengths of our study lie in several methodological choices [49]. First, the adoption of targeted subanalyses allowed us to move beyond a simple mapping exercise to provide critical insights. Specifically, the distinction between kinematic and mobile devices revealed the crucial trade-off between precision and accessibility. Second, the rigorous classification of ecological validity (high vs. low) on the basis of a real-world context and the presence of supervision represents an innovative methodological contribution. This classification allowed us to unveil the "scalability gap" in the home setting, a key finding of this review. Finally, the inclusion of both forward and backward citation tracking allowed for the retrieval of 6 additional studies, strengthening the comprehensiveness of our search.

Furthermore, as highlighted by our analysis of sensor configurations, a critical methodological limitation across the literature pertains to algorithmic validation. While many studies utilized commercial kinematic sensors with proprietary algorithms (e.g., APDM, Xsens), a significant portion of the literature lacks transparent, concurrent validation against gold-standard reference systems (such as optical motion capture or force plates) specifically within the context of the PD STS transition. This reliance on "black-box" proprietary algorithms or unvalidated custom scripts limits cross-study comparability and diagnostic transparency.

Although no language limits were set during the database search, a potential limitation is the exclusion of "gray literature" (e.g., conference proceedings, doctoral theses, unpublished study protocols). This may have led to the omission of relevant data, especially concerning emerging technologies or methodological failures that are not reported in peer-reviewed articles.

Additionally, as an inherent limitation of our scoping methodology, this review focused almost exclusively on technical aspects—namely, the devices used and the variables extracted—rather than on providing a pooled synthesis of clinical outcomes (e.g., comparing PD versus controls or mapping associations with clinical scales). This predominantly methodological approach was a deliberate choice to prevent the substantial confounding bias that would arise from aggregating highly heterogeneous protocols.

Implications for future research

The results of this review not only map the present state of the field but also trace a clear route for future research by identifying three methodological gaps that must be addressed and that directly inform future prospective studies.

Test/task Gap Research should move beyond the generalized use of the TUG test and focus on the validation of pure STS protocols (such as the 5TSTS or 30 s-CST) in both laboratory and ecological contexts while assessing the relevance of the acquired data. Future studies should isolate the STS transition to characterize deficits in force generation, speed, and stability and correlate these specific metrics with fall risk rather than relying on aggregated measures.

Implications for clinical practice

For physiotherapists and clinicians working daily with PD patients, our results suggest both enthusiasm and caution. The enormous volume of research on IMUs (87% of studies) confirms that the future of motor assessment is quantitative. However, this review demonstrates that the widespread use of smartphone apps for an in-depth biomechanical assessment of the STS strategy (e.g., sway) is not yet a validated solution in the literature.

Clinicians must be aware that the majority of the literature is based on "research-grade" sensors (78% of IMUs) and tests (TUG, 60%) that do not isolate the transition. Therefore, the purchase of expensive kinematic sensors for outpatient practice may be justified only if the clinician intends to perform a deep biomechanical analysis (e.g., of ROM) to personalize a rehabilitation intervention, as highlighted by our "variable gap" analysis.

For a clinician who desires simpler monitoring, using a smartphone to measure the duration of a 5TSTS is certainly more accurate than a manual stopwatch, but the true added value (e.g., measuring posttask sway) is still supported by only a small number of studies.

While traditional clinical scales (e.g., MDS-UPDRS) or manual stopwatch tests are inherently subjective, prone to ceiling effects, and limited to episodic assessments, instrumented STS provides an unparalleled sub-clinical resolution. It allows clinicians to objectively capture micro-alterations in postural control—such as subtle increases in jerk or mediolateral sway—long before they manifest as overt clinical falls or noticeable functional decline. This level of granularity effectively shifts the paradigm from reactive management to proactive, personalized fall prevention.

The high feasibility (74%) of unsupervised monitoring (Table 5) is a powerful proof-of-concept for the development of dedicated applications. This finding suggests that once the "scalability gap" is bridged, the objective measurement of STS at home will become a viable clinical tool for remote monitoring [25], the titration of pharmacological therapy, and the long-term evaluation of rehabilitation effectiveness.

Conclusions

Instrumental analysis of the STS transition in PD is a fertile and rapidly growing field of research, that may offer significant clinical benefits far beyond the capabilities of traditional observational scales. The literature is largely dominated by the use of IMU sensors in outpatient settings; however, it remains fragmented, with a predominant focus on the TUG test—which does not adequately capture transitional movements—and a notable lack of data concerning patients in advanced stages of the disease.

Although unsupervised home monitoring is technically feasible [28, 35], our review has identified a critical "scalability gap": the richest and most meaningful biomechanical metrics are still derived from expensive and complex "research-grade" sensors [37], whereas scalable mobile devices struggle to provide advanced data in ecological contexts.

Future research must prioritize not only the development of robust algorithms for mobile devices to translate quantitative STS assessment from the laboratory to the patient's daily life but also, perhaps more importantly, the evaluation of the clinical relevance of the acquired variables. This step is fundamental for defining standardized protocols that clearly distinguish their purpose: on the one hand, ecological monitoring—longitudinal, low-burden, and focused on predictive metrics for fall risk and the disease’s course (e.g., duration, daily repetition count, sway, linear acceleration, angular velocity); on the other hand, in-depth clinical assessment, which is based on complex biomechanical metrics (e.g., ROM, power, jerk), is aimed at designing personalized rehabilitation interventions and effectively integrating fall prevention into treatment goals.
