Conserved Functions of LARP1 Proteins in Eukaryotes
Authors: Farnaz Mansouri‐Noori, Mark A Bayfield
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTLa and La‐related proteins (LARPs) are conserved RNA‐binding proteins that share a characteristic La motif (LaM) and have important functions in RNA metabolism. Members of the LARP1 family bind a cohort of mRNAs encoding factors involved in the process of mRNA translation, including ribosomal protein mRNAs (RP mRNAs). These mRNAs can contain a sequence of 5–15 pyrimidines in their 5′UTRs, immediately following the m7G cap, and are named 5′ terminal oligopyrimidine (5′TOP) mRNAs. The DM15 domain of human LARP1 has been suggested to specifically recognize this motif, thereby affecting 5′TOP mRNA translation and stability. However, the specific function of LARP1 in this context remains unclear. Intriguingly, the 5′TOP motif is not found in RP mRNAs in C. elegans and yeast, while LARP1 orthologs in some systems lack the characteristic DM15 domain, suggesting that essential functions of LARP1 family members may precede the emergence of the 5′TOP motif and the DM15. In this work, we review studies in humans and several model organisms where we draw parallels between reported RNA binding modes and functions of different LARP1 orthologs. Introduction

The La and La‐Related Protein Superfamily

La and La‐related proteins (LARPs) are a conserved family of RNA‐binding proteins with important and diverse functions in RNA metabolism. They are classified into five subfamilies including genuine La (LARP3), LARP1 (including LARP1a/b, previously referred to as LARP1/2), LARP4 (including LARP4a/b, previously referred to as LARP4/5), LARP6 and LARP7 (Bousquet‐Antonelli and Deragon 2009). With the exception of genuine La from Alveolates (Deragon 2020; Kerkhofs et al. 2022), La proteins contain two main RNA binding motifs: an N‐terminally located La motif (LaM) and an adjacent RNA recognition motif (RRM1) that together give rise to the La module, which folds into an RNA binding pocket. This V‐shaped binding cleft is best characterized to accommodate the terminal uridylates (UUU‐3′OH) found in nascent polymerase III transcripts (Kotik‐Kogan et al. 2008; Stefano 1984; Teplova et al. 2006). The RRM1 is also hypothesized to contact other spatially distinct regions on its RNA substrates (Bayfield et al. 2010; Maraia et al. 2017).

Several unique LARPs have been characterized since the original classification of genuine La. The LaM is the unifying structural element found in all LARPs, but these proteins also harbor other motifs that are specific to each sub‐family and allow for interaction with different substrates. Amongst the other families (LARPs), LARP1 (Fonseca et al. 2015; Lahr et al. 2017; Stavraka and Blagden 2015; Tcherkezian et al. 2014), LARP4 (Küspert et al. 2015; Schäffler et al. 2010; Yang et al. 2011) and LARP6 members (Cai et al. 2010; Dermit et al. 2020; Martino et al. 2015) are cytoplasmic and interact with mRNAs, whereas LARP7 members are nuclear and interacts with non‐coding RNAs (Eichhorn et al. 2016, 2018; Uchikawa et al. 2015; Yang et al. 2022). Structural studies characterizing the LaM of human LARP1 (hLARP1) (Kozlov et al. 2022, 2024), hLARP4 (Cruz‐Gallardo et al. 2019), hLARP6 (Martino et al. 2015) and hLARP7 (Eichhorn et al. 2016, 2018; Uchikawa et al. 2015; Yang et al. 2022) have confirmed an overall similarity with the LaM of human La (hLa) (Dock‐bregeon et al. 2021). This review focuses on the LARP1 family and its links to the stability and translation of mRNAs, including ribosomal protein mRNAs (RP mRNAs) and related to this, 5′TOP mRNAs.

5′ Terminal Oligopyrimidine Sequences and Their Regulation by mTOR

While the first transcribed nucleotide of most mRNAs is usually a purine (Schibler et al. 1977; Schibler and Perry 1977), a special subset of cellular mRNAs in numerous metazoans harbor a stretch of 5–15 pyrimidines in the 5′UTR, immediately after the m7G cap, and are consequently named 5′ terminal oligopyrimidine (5′TOP) mRNAs (Avni et al. 1997; Iadevaia et al. 2008; Meyuhas and Kahan 2015). The first nucleotide in the 5′TOP motif is an invariant cytidine (Avni et al. 1997; Meyuhas 2000), and this unique +1C has been hypothesized to drive the regulation of 5′TOP mRNA expression through trans‐acting factors. 5′TOP mRNAs code for proteins that are important in the process of mRNA translation, including ribosomal proteins and translation initiation and elongation factors. These mRNAs are noted for being highly and efficiently transcribed and translated in an evolutionary conserved manner (van den Elzen et al. 2022). The 5′TOP motif is found in humans, other vertebrates and Drosophila, while a partial variant has been proposed in Arabidopsis (Scarpin et al. 2020). On the other hand, the 5′TOP sequence has not yet been reported in C. elegans (Danks et al. 2015; Meyuhas and Kahan 2015) or yeast (Meyuhas and Kahan 2015). Given that the divergence of a common ancestor with Arabidopsis precedes the divergence between humans and yeasts (Figure 1), it is intriguing that the 5′TOP sequence has been conserved from fruit flies to humans, yet appears to have been lost in C. elegans and yeasts. 5′TOP sequences have been intimately linked to RP mRNA translation and stability (van den Elzen et al. 2022), and much research has been dedicated to how trans‐acting factors recognize this motif to regulate protein production, cell proliferation and growth.

LARP1 orthologs are evolutionarily conserved. A. thaliana , which shares a common ancestor with yeasts and humans, has both short‐ and long‐forms of LARP1. S. cerevisiae and S. pombe only possess the short form with a single C‐terminally located LaM, while C. elegans, D . melanogaster, H . Sapiens, M . musculus and D. rerio have long forms of LARP1 containing a DM15 region (Bousquet‐Antonelli and Deragon 2009). Control of expression of 5′TOP mRNAs has been extensively linked to the mechanistic Target of Rapamycin (mTOR) signaling pathway, as treatment with mTOR inhibitors, such as rapamycin, selectively inhibits the translation of mRNAs coding for ribosomal proteins and other factors involved with translation (Jefferies et al. 1994; Terada et al. 1994). Thus, 5′TOP and 5′TOP‐like mRNAs account for the majority of the mTOR signaling pathway substrates (Hsieh et al. 2012; Thoreen et al. 2012). mTOR is the kinase subunit of the multi‐subunit mTORC1 and mTORC2 complexes (Foster and Fingar 2010; Jacinto 2008). The two complexes have different responses to allosteric inhibition by rapamycin: an acute exposure to rapamycin only blocks mTORC1, whereas both mTORC1 and mTORC2 are fully inhibited by compounds that are structurally similar to adenosine triphosphate (ATP), including Torin1 and Torin2 (Thoreen et al. 2009). Functionally, the activity of mTORC1 is central to phosphorylation of S6 kinases (S6K) and eIF4E‐binding proteins (4EBPs). Therefore, mTOR signaling and how it regulates 5′TOP mRNA translation is an important aspect of cell survival and growth. Also, many RNA‐binding proteins are phosphorylated by mTOR complexes and have regulatory functions that are involved with related processes. Various proposed trans‐acting factors that significantly and selectively regulate 5′TOP mRNA translation have been reported as long as three decades ago (Slobin and Rao 1993). Several of the candidate 5′TOP effectors that have been proposed over the years include hLa (Cardinali et al. 2003; Crosio et al. 2000; Pellizzoni et al. 1996), AUF1 (Kakegawa et al. 2007), TIA‐1 (Damgaard and Lykke‐Andersen 2011) and LARP7 (Markert et al. 2008), but definitive evidence for the regulatory roles of these proteins in 5′TOP mRNA translation has been lacking.

LARP1 Family

Our understanding of LARP1 function is recent compared to what has been known for the genuine La proteins (Deragon and Bousquet‐Antonelli 2015), and proposed functions for LARP1 family members have been controversial (Berman et al. 2021). Most studies have linked hLARP1 to the inhibition of 5′TOP mRNA translation initiation via sequestration of the m7G cap and 5′TOP sequence through a C‐terminal DM15 region (Bousquet‐Antonelli and Deragon 2009; Deragon and Bousquet‐Antonelli 2015; Marchler‐Bauer et al. 2013; Ponting et al. 2001), even as some have proposed hLARP1 can promote mRNA translation (see below).

hLARP1 contains multiple phosphorylation sites (Hong et al. 2017; Jia et al. 2021) and interacts with mTORC1 (Fonseca et al. 2015; Hong et al. 2017; Tcherkezian et al. 2014) as one of its direct substrates. Therefore, phosphorylation of hLARP1, downstream of mTORC1, may play a role in differential regulation of 5′TOP mRNA translation. Additionally, altered modes of association of the DM15 region with the m7G cap/5′TOP sequence may allow for hLARP1 to toggle between various roles (Bayfield et al. 2019; Cassidy et al. 2019; Hong et al. 2017; Nguyen et al. 2024).

Interestingly, some LARP1 orthologs in Arabidopsis (LARP1b and LARP1c) and all LARP1 orthologs in S. cerevisiae (Slf1p and Sro9p) and S. pombe (Slr1p) do not possess a DM15 region. Studies on hLARP1 suggest that the N‐terminal region of the protein (containing the LaM) is dispensable for the inhibition of 5′TOP mRNA translation (Philippe et al. 2017). It is possible that the hLARP1 LaM could, therefore, have roles that overlap with other eukaryotic LARP1 proteins missing the DM15 region. The evolutionary conservation of LARP1 proteins across eukaryotes, despite key differences in their structural features and target substrates, make their proposed roles in mRNA translation and stability intriguing areas of study.

Human LARP1

Initial Characterization

Related to the arrangement of RNA binding domains, LARP1 family members are classified into two types: the LARP1‐L (long‐form) and LARP1‐S (short‐form) (Deragon and Bousquet‐Antonelli 2015; Zhang et al. 2023) (Figure 1). The long‐form was thought to contain a La module (LaM + RRML5) and a C‐terminal DM15 motif, while the short‐form only contains a C‐terminal LaM (Deragon and Bousquet‐Antonelli 2015). Humans contain two variants of LARP1‐L: hLARP1a and hLARP1b, which were previously characterized as LARP1 and LARP2, respectively. There are two isoforms of hLARP1a encoding 1019 (NCBI: NP_056130.2) and 1096 (NP_291029.2) amino acid proteins, and three isoforms of hLARP1b encoding proteins of 914, 512, and 358 amino acids long (Burrows et al. 2010; Schwenzer et al. 2021). hLARP1a is the predominant form in humans which is described to be 40 times more abundant in HeLa cells (Burrows et al. 2010), and amongst the two isoforms of hLARP1a, the 1096 amino acid isoform is dominantly expressed in cancer and other tested cell lines, including HEK‐293 T, even though the 1019 amino acid isoform is more extensively referred to in the literature (Schwenzer et al. 2021). While several LARP1 orthologs were originally suggested to possess the La module, consisting of the LaM and an adjacent RRM, recent structural work in humans suggests that the region immediately C‐terminal to the LaM does not adopt an RRM fold (Kozlov et al. 2022). A ribosome binding region (RBR) (Saba et al. 2024), however, has been suggested to exist beyond the LaM and an adjacent PABP‐interacting motif (PAM2), prior to the DM15 region. Should the expected La module RRM, typically found immediately C‐terminal to the LaM, also be missing in other LARP1‐L type proteins, these isoforms may be more generally similar to the LARP1‐S form than previously anticipated.

The first human LARP1 study reported that hLARP1 interacts with PABP in an RNase‐independent manner and that it associates with ribosomal subunits (Burrows et al. 2010). Depletion of hLARP1 leads to a considerable increase in the ratio of subpolysomes (40S, 60S, and 80S) to polysomes; therefore, it was further suggested that hLARP1 is involved with mRNA translation initiation. Since overexpression of hLARP1 in HeLa cells contributes to mesenchymal‐like or migratory phenotypes, it was also proposed that hLARP1 facilitates the synthesis of proteins required for cancer‐related phenotypes. hLARP1 was further shown to associate with mRNA poly(A) tails, specifically recognizing the 3′ termini, to stabilize mRNAs (Aoki et al. 2013). Additionally, the depletion of hLARP1 in HEK293 cells led to a considerable decrease in steady state levels of several 5′TOP mRNAs, further suggesting that hLARP1 selectively stabilizes this subset of mRNAs.

hLARP1 Functions in mRNA Translation and Stability

The specific function of hLARP1 has been the subject of some debate, starting with work reporting that it either enhances (Tcherkezian et al. 2014) or represses (Fonseca et al. 2015) the translation of 5′TOP mRNAs. These studies identified hLARP1 in a proteomic screen for factors that associate with the mRNA 5′ cap in an mTOR‐dependent manner (Tcherkezian et al. 2014) using m7GTP affinity chromatography coupled with quantitative mass spectrometry (Sonenberg et al. 1978). Furthermore, hLARP1 co‐immunoprecipitates with eIF4A, but not eIF4E or eIF4G. eIF4A is also reported to enhance the interaction of 5′TOP mRNAs with hLARP1 through helicase‐independent functions (Shichino et al. 2024). hLARP1 knockdown followed by polysome profiling results in an increase in the height of the 80S peak and a decrease in polysomes (Tcherkezian et al. 2014), similar to other reports (Burrows et al. 2010), indicative of defects in translation initiation caused by hLARP1 knockdown. It was concluded that hLARP1 depletion significantly decreases the abundance of 5′TOP mRNAs, while treatment with mTOR inhibitors did not lead to similar depletion of these mRNAs (Tcherkezian et al. 2014). 5′TOP mRNAs are further suggested to undergo low basal levels of translation, instead of being translationally silenced, during starvation (Schneider et al. 2022).

In contrast to findings by Tcherkezian et al. (2014), Fonseca et al. (2015) proposed a model where hLARP1 acts as a cap‐binding protein that sequesters the mRNA cap under mTOR inhibition, preventing eIF4F assembly and therefore mRNA translation initiation. Their findings suggested that hLARP1 competes with eIF4G for binding to 5′TOP mRNAs, directly engaging the cap and the adjacent 5′TOP motif. Knockdown of hLARP1 also decreased the inhibitory effects of rapamycin/Torin1 and amino acid starvation on 5′TOP mRNA translation. Therefore, hLARP1 was proposed to play a crucial role in inhibiting the translation of these mRNAs downstream of mTORC1, while also having a positive role in maintaining 5′TOP mRNA stability. Notably, overexpression of hLARP1 significantly increased the half‐lives of mRNAs coding for RPL29, RPL36 and PABP. It was concluded that hLARP1 acts as a bimodal regulator of 5′TOP mRNAs, repressing their translation during mTOR inhibition while also stabilizing them for active translation under favorable conditions.

While the 5′TOP sequence is sufficient for blocking mRNA translation during mTOR inhibition (Gentilella et al. 2017; Hochstoeger et al. 2024), hLARP1 knockout in HeLa cells may either fully (Jia et al. 2021) or only partially (Hochstoeger et al. 2024) rescue the translation of 5′TOP mRNAs. The inhibitory effects of hLARP1 on 5′TOP mRNA translation, therefore, may be secondary to the effects of eIF4E‐binding proteins 1 and 2 (4EBP1/2) during mTOR inhibition, while loss of hLARP1 results in selective destabilization of 5′TOP mRNAs independent of 4EBP1/2 (Hochstoeger et al. 2024). Related to its functions in mRNA stability, hLARP1 is also crucially important for anchoring 5′TOP mRNAs to stress granules and to some extent P‐bodies (Wilbertz et al. 2019), while pyrimidine‐rich sequences in the 5′UTR, regardless of whether they are at the extreme 5′ end or not, can drive 5′TOP mRNA localization in a hLARP1‐dependent manner (Goering et al. 2023), which could further affect RNA stability. hLARP1 is also proposed to selectively mediate 5′TOP mRNA stability through interaction with free 40S ribosomal subunits (Gentilella et al. 2017), while the RBR, which binds the 40S subunit, is not specifically responsible for this, given that mutations to this region did not affect 5′TOP stability (Saba et al. 2024). Under mTOR inhibition, the 40S‐hLARP1 complex is suggested to stabilize classic 5′TOP mRNAs, such as RP mRNAs, along with those involved in ribosome biogenesis, metabolic processes and mitochondrial functions (Fuentes et al. 2021). It is possible that the association of 40S‐hLARP1 and 80S‐hLARP1 with translationally inactive 5′TOP mRNAs may help stabilize a readily available pool of mRNAs primed for immediate translation initiation under growth‐stimulating conditions (Fuentes et al. 2021; Ogami et al. 2022; Saba et al. 2024; Schneider et al. 2022). Therefore, several studies have linked hLARP1 to both promotion and inhibition of 5′TOP mRNA translation and controlling RNA stability.

Distinct Regions of hLARP1 Are Linked to Various Functions and Binding Modes

In addition to interacting with 5′UTRs of specific and diverse mRNAs, hLARP1 has been proposed to interact with the mRNA poly(A) tail. While the DM15 region is thought to be mainly responsible for binding to the cap and 5′TOP motif, residues within the LaM, known for recognizing terminal uridylates in genuine La (Kotik‐Kogan et al. 2008; Teplova et al. 2006), enable hLARP1 to bind poly(A) RNA (Kozlov et al. 2022, 2024). Interestingly, LaM amino acids (310–540, hLARP1a 1019 amino acid isoform) have been implicated in interacting with both the 5′TOP motif and mRNA poly(A) tail (Al‐Ashtal et al. 2019). Structural work suggests that residues Q323 to P410 (1019 amino acid isoform) adopt a folded structure that binds poly(A) RNA (Kozlov et al. 2022); it is possible that these residues plus additional amino acids beyond this region, particularly 410–540 (1019 amino acid isoform), could also contribute to hLARP1's capacity to bind to the 5′TOP motif (Al‐Ashtal et al. 2019). Notably, a co‐crystal structure of the LaM bound to oligo(U) RNA revealed that the same amino acids responsible for binding to oligo(U) are also involved in binding to oligo(A) RNA (Kozlov et al. 2024). The isolated LaM, together with the PAM2 motif and a few C‐terminal residues following this region, also mediates 3′ poly(A) length protection and mRNA stabilization in HEK293 cells through an interaction between the PAM2 motif and PABP (Mattijssen et al. 2021). Furthermore, hLARP1 participates in global poly(A) tail lengthening, which most substantially stabilizes 5′TOP mRNAs (Baptissart et al. 2023; Mattijssen et al. 2017; Ogami et al. 2022; Park et al. 2023).

Moreover, reports indicating that the LaM of hLARP1 recognizes the 5′TOP sequence independently of the mRNA cap, while also binding poly(A) sequences, suggest that hLARP1 can simultaneously engage both ends of 5′TOP mRNAs (Al‐Ashtal et al. 2019). hLARP1 LaM (amino acids 310–540, hLARP1a 1019 residue isoform) was shown to form intermediate protein‐RNA complexes with 5′TOP‐containing RNAs and poly(A) RNA in in vitro competition assays. This supports a model where binding of hLARP1 to the 5′TOP motif and the poly(A) tail of the same mRNA could enhance translation, possibly by circularizing the mRNA transcript to facilitate ribosome recycling, while the DM15 region specifically binding the Cap/+1C could lead to repression of translation. Related to this, it is important to note that although LARP1‐S isoforms lack the DM15 region, these orthologs are still linked to mRNA translation (Jennings et al. 2023; Kershaw et al. 2015; Mansouri‐Noori et al. 2023; Schenk et al. 2012; Sobel and Wolin 1999).

While recent structural studies (Kozlov et al. 2022; Smith and Silvers 2024) in humans have confirmed that hLARP1 LaM amino acids (323–410, hLARP1a 1019 residue isoform) bind a poly(A) RNA (Kozlov et al. 2022), they also show that a penultimate guanine at the 3′ end of the poly(A) sequence leads to a three‐fold increase in binding (Kozlov et al. 2024), hence providing evidence for guanylation of 5′TOP mRNA poly(A) tails, which may protect them from rapid deadenylation (Chang et al. 2014; Lim et al. 2018). Other studies have shown that the hLARP1 RBR (amino acids 660–724, hLARP1a 1096 amino acid isoform), residing between the LaM/PAM2 and DM15 region, binds within the mRNA entry channel of the 40S small ribosomal subunit, interacting with the 18S rRNA and ribosomal proteins RPS2, RPS3, RPS17 and RPS30, to form a translationally inactive complex. This further allows for the 60S subunit to join, forming weakly associated LARP1‐80S complexes that interact with non‐translating 5′TOP mRNAs through the LAM/PAM2 and DM15 domains (Saba et al. 2024). Interestingly, mutations to the RBR do not affect 5′TOP mRNA translation and stability in contrast to mutations to LAM/PAM2/DM15 (Saba et al. 2024). Another structural study mapping the interaction between hLARP1 and 18S rRNA, resolved to the mRNA entry channel of the 40S ribosome, further supports the idea that hLARP1 binding to the 40S subunit is incompatible with active translation. However, it was proposed that the 5′TOP motif may facilitate the displacement of hLARP1 from the entry channel, thereby explaining the high rates of 5′TOP mRNA translation observed under normal physiological conditions and active mTOR signaling (Wolin et al. 2025). This is consistent with the model in which phosphorylation of the regions adjacent to the DM15 causes hLARP1 to release the mRNA cap and +1C of 5′TOP mRNAs. In contrast, under conditions of mTOR inhibition, hLARP1 is suggested to bind more stably to 18S rRNA, as well as to 4EBP1, together preventing the formation of the eIF4F complex on the mRNA (Wolin et al. 2025).

Other work has suggested that hLARP1's interaction with its target mRNAs, here including 5′TOP mRNAs, non‐5′TOP mRNAs and mitochondrial function‐related transcripts, increases under mTOR inhibition when these mRNAs are translationally repressed, and that this interaction is specifically dependent on PABP (Smith et al. 2021). Mutations to the hLARP1's PAM2 motif led to an overall decrease in RNA binding while mutations to the DM15 region did not, supporting a model where the LaM/PAM2 and DM15 domains interact with distinct regions of a target mRNA. These findings suggest that a dominant mode of RNA binding through the LaM/PAM2 may be in effect, while the DM15 domain may serve as an additional regulatory region that is activated when binding to certain mRNAs such as the 5′TOPs. Notably, inhibiting the interaction of hLARP1 with PABP leads to decreased RNA binding by hLARP1, indicating that its association with PABP may allow for broader mRNA binding beyond transcripts containing 5′TOP motifs (Smith et al. 2021).

In sum, a variety of RNA binding modes linked to the several domains have been hypothesized to coordinate hLARP1 function on various targets (Figure 2). The DM15 binding mode is hypothesized to modulate access of eIF4E to the 5′TOP Cap/+1C motif (Lahr et al. 2015, 2017) via phosphorylation of hLARP1 regions adjacent to the DM15 (more below). The LaM‐centric RNA binding mode, in collaboration with a PAM2‐associated interaction with PABP, is hypothesized to control hLARP1 binding to poly(A), with associated functions in RNA stability, for both 5′TOP and non‐5′TOP mRNAs. This binding mode may be dispensable for repressing 5′TOP mRNA translation (Philippe et al. 2017), even if this region has also been linked to the 5′TOP motif or other pyrimidine‐rich sequences (Al‐Ashtal et al. 2019).

Phosphorylation of hLARP1

The phosphorylation status of hLARP1 has been proposed to allow for the protein to toggle between different regulatory functions. hLARP1 is a direct substrate of mTORC1 and Akt/S6K1, and contains 26 serine/threonine phosphorylation sites distributed across seven rapamycin‐sensitive clusters (Hong et al. 2017; Jia et al. 2021). Related to this, hLARP1 is proposed to bind near the 3′ end of the 5′UTR (Hong et al. 2017), close to the AUG start codon, where a pyrimidine‐rich translational element (PRTE) is located in some 5′TOP mRNAs (Hsieh et al. 2012). hLARP1 also associates with the 3′UTR of mRNAs encoding ribosomal proteins and other translation‐related factors (Hong et al. 2017). Upon phosphorylation, hLARP1 is hypothesized to dissociate from the 5′UTR, relieving its repressive effect on translation, and instead bind to the 3′UTR (Hong et al. 2017).

Several phosphorylation clusters have been classified (Jia et al. 2021). Phosphorylation sites in cluster 6, located upstream of the DM15 domain, are particularly sensitive to rapamycin which impairs RNA binding and hLARP1's inhibitory effects on mRNA translation. In contrast, residues in clusters 4 and 5, located in the mid‐region of hLARP1, are suggested to enhance hLARP1's association with mTORC1. Therefore, phosphorylation of different identified clusters may differentially regulate the LaM and DM15 domain of hLARP1, leading to distinct functional outcomes. Thus, while the inhibitory effects of hLARP1 on mRNA translation are primarily attributed to its C‐terminal region, including the DM15 domain, hLARP1 may remain bound to 5′TOP mRNAs under growth‐stimulating conditions to act as a nucleation site for recruitment of translational factors (Al‐Ashtal et al. 2019; Cassidy et al. 2019; Fuentes et al. 2021; Gentilella et al. 2017; Hong et al. 2017; Saba et al. 2024; Tcherkezian et al. 2014).

Diversity of hLARP1 RNA Targets

Transcription start site sequencing has shown that hLARP1's function extends to transcripts with TOP‐like motifs that are not classified as 5′TOP mRNAs. These findings have led to the description of a metric called TOPscore, which quantifies pyrimidine‐richness at transcription start sites (Philippe et al. 2020). TOPscore was defined as a mean length of consecutive C/U residues up to 6 nucleotides at each analyzed position, since TOP sequences longer than 6 nucleotides failed to increase the degree of translation repression by hLARP1. Intriguingly, it was proposed that the 5′TOP sequence is an extreme example of a broader set of 5′ regulatory sequences. Thus, hLARP1 recognizes a wide range of 5′ sequences, with a requirement for a +1 C or U and as few as two additional pyrimidines. Interestingly, an increase in the length of the polypyrimidine sequence (up to 6 nucleotides) correlates with an increased level of repression by hLARP1, a finding that challenges the common notion that 5′TOP motifs have a binary function. Therefore, mRNAs with 5′TOP or TOP‐like sequences can be regulated to varying degrees, potentially by different regions of hLARP1, and this may have led to some contradictory conclusions regarding the functions of hLARP1.

Connections to Disease States

Several studies have linked hLARP1 to tumor survival and cancer progression. Reports suggest that hLARP1 binds the non‐5′TOP BCL2, BIK and mTOR mRNAs and differentially regulates their stability through contacts to pyrimidine‐rich sequences in the 3′UTR (Hopkins et al. 2016; Mura et al. 2015). The RNA interactome of hLARP1 includes 3000 transcripts enriched for 5′TOP and other mRNAs involved in cancer pathways (Mura et al. 2015). Several groups have shown that hLARP1 is overexpressed in cancer cells with a net effect that leads to a pro‐growth enhanced protein expression and a poor prognosis in cervical squamous cell carcinoma (Mura et al. 2015), colorectal cancer (Desi et al. 2022; Ye et al. 2016), hepatocellular carcinoma (Li et al. 2025; Zhu et al. 2023), multiple myeloma (Seibert et al. 2023), gastric carcinoma (Liu et al. 2024), head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (Xu et al. 2025) and anaplastic thyroid carcinoma (Yin et al. 2025).

In colorectal cancer cells, hLARP1 is proposed to bind to approximately 400 transcripts that contain binding sites in their 3′UTRs; importantly, these transcripts are not enriched for 5′TOP‐containing RP mRNAs (Desi et al. 2022). Thus, in cancer cells, dysregulated hLARP1 may be targeted to different mRNA 3′UTRs, while its phosphorylation may also differ from its normal state (Desi et al. 2022; Hong et al. 2017). Other reports have identified hLARP1 as a target of the oncogenic transcription factor MYC (Desi et al. 2022; Seibert et al. 2023), and a GCN2‐dependent target of ATF4 during a sustained response to nutrient starvation. hLARP1 is also suggested to colocalize with GCN1 (Wu et al. 2020; Young and Wek 2016), which is the upstream activator of GCN2 (Sattlegger and Hinnebusch 2005). Therefore, abnormal hLARP1 function downstream of some transcription factors may allow for altered phenotypes under disease states.

hLARP1 has additionally been implicated in mitochondrial fitness and regulation of oxidative phosphorylation through functions linked to cancer cell survival (Ma et al. 2024). Knockdown of hLARP1 in A2780 ovarian cancer cells disrupts the translation of the mitochondrial transcription initiation factor TFB2M and respiratory chain complex II subunit SDHB, leading to mitochondrial dysfunction following PKI‐402 treatment, which inhibits PI3K and mTORC1/2. Interestingly, this was coupled to a role for hLARP1 in stabilizing the TFB2M mRNA through its LaM. Overexpression of a hLARP1 Q333A LaM point mutant failed to restore TFB2M mRNA levels, while full‐length hLARP1 and a mutant lacking the DM15 domain achieved a partial rescue. Therefore, hLARP1, which was suggested to be recruited to the mitochondrial outer membrane, could be involved in maintaining mitochondrial function during PI3K/mTOR inhibition or mitigating the effects of their pharmaceutical inhibition (Ma et al. 2024). hLARP1 has also been reported to regulate the expression of mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA)‐encoded proteins (To et al. 2019), similar to reports in S. cerevisiae (Kershaw et al. 2015) and Drosophila (Y. Zhang et al. 2016). hLARP1 is required for full oxidative phosphorylation capacity as its knockout in erythroleukemic K562 cells leads to a partial reduction in oxygen consumption and increased cell death (To et al. 2019). Related to hLARP1's role in binding to and regulating the expression of mRNAs coding for factors involved in multiple aspects of metabolism, including glycolysis and oxidative phosphorylation, its overexpression in cancer is thought to allow for tumor cells to survive conditions of fluctuating nutrient and oxygen availability (Mura et al. 2015). The interaction of hLARP1 with such transcripts is also suggested to be essential for normal cellular function during embryonic development and tissue regeneration, where the heterozygous loss of hLARP1 has been implicated in neurodevelopmental disorders likely due to reduced activity in fetal brains (Chettle et al. 2024). Related to functions with respect to mitochondrial fitness, hLARP1 is also implicated in aging (Lu et al. 2023; Qi et al. 2025), consistent with the involvement of the protein in mTOR signaling, which has previously documented links to longevity and epigenetic aging (Horvath et al. 2019; Kennedy and Lamming 2016; Lu et al. 2016).

Additionally, multiple studies have linked hLARP1 with viral pathogenesis. RyDEN, an interferon‐stimulated gene that attenuates the replication of several viruses, including the dengue virus, was found in a complex with hLARP1 and PABP (Suzuki et al. 2016). Specifically, RyDEN interferes with the translation of the dengue virus by interacting with the 3′UTR of the viral RNA. It was suggested that binding of RyDEN to this region blocks cellular RNA‐binding proteins, such as hLARP1 and PABP, from binding to the same region, which could otherwise stabilize the viral RNA and promote its expression. Other reports characterizing the SARS‐CoV‐2 RNA‐protein interactome and upregulated cellular RNAs during viral infection suggest that hLARP1 has antiviral functions in this context (Lee et al. 2021; Schmidt et al. 2021), and may interact with the SARS‐CoV‐2 nucleocapsid protein (Gordon et al. 2020). eCLIP analysis revealed that hLARP1 binds the SARS‐CoV‐2 RNA at multiple locations enriched in oligopyrimidine TOP‐like sequences, found within the first 70 nucleotides of the RNA or the viral 5′ leader (Schmidt et al. 2021). Knockout of hLARP1 led to higher levels of viral RNA and a corresponding increase in the production of infectious virus, while transient hLARP1 re‐expression reduced viral particle production to levels seen during wildtype hLARP1 expression. Additionally, inhibiting mTORC1/2 results in a reduction in viral replication, where phospho‐proteome studies have shown hLARP1 undergoes active phosphorylation as a result of SARS‐CoV‐2 infection (Bouhaddou et al. 2020; Klann et al. 2020). Other studies have linked hLARP1 to maintenance of 5′TOP mRNA expression during global translational inhibition caused by the SARS‐CoV‐2 Nsp1 protein (Lee et al. 2021; Maurya et al. 2023; Rao et al. 2021), as well as links to HCV (Bley et al. 2024; Chatel‐Chaix et al. 2013; Plissonnier et al. 2019) and Zika virus (Scaturro et al. 2018) infection. Given that hLARP1 is either dysregulated in cancer or that it is involved with the cellular response to viral infection, a mechanistic understanding of its function is valuable where hLARP1 can be considered as a therapeutic target.

Summary of Function

Studies on hLARP1 by various groups converge on themes of mRNA translation and stability, especially for RP mRNAs. The precise role(s) of hLARP1 in these processes is puzzling in the complex human system, with an expanding and often contradictory body of research shaping our current understanding (Table 1) (Berman et al. 2021). Since their initial characterizations, several LARP1 orthologs have also been linked with these processes; however, the mechanisms by which these conserved proteins coordinate involvement with such processes remain elusive. We propose that a review and contextualization of conserved functions of simpler LARP1 orthologs, as discussed below, may be informative in our attempts to unravel the complexities of this class of factors in humans.

LARP1 Characterization in Different Models

A . thaliana

Arabidopsis has three LARP1 orthologs: one of which includes both the La module and the DM15 region, while the other two contain a single LaM (Bousquet‐Antonelli and Deragon 2009). Initial studies in Arabidopsis showed that overexpression of either short form of LARP1 (LARP1b and LARP1c), but not the DM15‐containing LARP1a, leads to precocious leaf senescence and upregulation of several senescence‐associated and defense‐related genes (B. Zhang et al. 2012). LARP1c‐GFP accumulation in cytoplasmic foci was also sensitive to cycloheximide treatment, suggesting that short‐form LARP1c could be involved with processes related to translation. Subsequent work revealed that the long‐form LARP1a in Arabidopsis (referred to as LARP1 hereafter) functions in coupling mRNA translation and decay during heat stress (Merret et al. 2013). In response to such stress, Arabidopsis reprograms gene expression by downregulating general translation (Larkindale and Vierling 2008), which is linked to the decay of 25% of its mRNA transcriptome. LARP1 was proposed to play a critical role in this process by recruiting the 5′‐3′ exonuclease and XRN1 ortholog XRN4 to translating polysomes to promote co‐translational mRNA decay. Notably, LARP1 binding to XRN4 was insensitive to nuclease treatment, suggesting a direct link between LARP1 function and mRNA decay in this system.

More recent studies suggest that Arabidopsis LARP1 is involved with mRNA translation in a TOR‐dependent manner (Scarpin et al. 2020, 2022). The classic 5′TOP motif is not characterized in Arabidopsis. However, TOPscore analysis revealed that several mRNAs, involved in diverse steps of ribosome biogenesis (i.e., rDNA transcription, rRNA processing and ribosome complex assembly), along with a few cytosolic RP mRNAs and plant‐specific mRNAs, contain TOP‐like motifs that are regulated by TOR signaling in a LARP1‐dependent manner (Philippe et al. 2020; Scarpin et al. 2020, 2022). Here, TOR inhibition resulted in a significant reduction in 91% of the cytosolic RP mRNA and 37% of the mitochondrial mRNA abundance and broad translational repression of RP mRNAs, coupled with decreased phosphorylation of LARP1 and hyperphosphorylation of eIF4G, consistent with general repression of mRNA translation. Importantly, the translational efficiency of mRNAs involved in translation, particularly those encoding cytosolic ribosomal proteins, was significantly reduced in larp1 mutant seedlings. Therefore, the TOR‐LARP1‐5′TOP axis is proposed to be conserved in Arabidopsis, linking LARP1 activity to TOR signaling and regulation of TOP‐like mRNAs in this system. Arabidopsis LARP1 and hLARP1, therefore, share functions related to mRNA translation, specifically of translation‐associated mRNAs under the control of the TOR pathway, with added links related to direct recruitment of exonucleases associated with co‐translational decay under periods of stress. Even though human LARP1 has been linked to mRNA stability, direct engagement of mRNA decay factors has not yet been reported.

Other evolutionary studies have identified members of the La, LARP1 and LARP6 families across various plant species, while LARP4 and LARP7 family members were absent (Xiang et al. 2024; Zhang et al. 2023), consistent with earlier findings (Deragon 2020). In an analysis of 16 plant species, 188 La and LARP genes were identified (Zhang et al. 2023), including 32 genuine La proteins, 80 LARP1 proteins and 76 LARP6 proteins. As mentioned earlier, this study further classified LARP1 members into two sub‐categories: LARP1‐L (long‐form) and LARP1‐S (short‐form) (Zhang et al. 2023). While the long‐form contains both a LaM and a C‐terminal DM15 domain, the short‐form only contains a C‐terminal LaM. Curiously, amongst the identified LARP1 orthologs in plants, the short‐form protein is observed more frequently. Therefore, it is interesting to note that plants, which share a common ancestor with yeasts and humans (Figure 1), possess both the long and short forms of LARP1. Given that LARP1‐S is the only form to exist in some yeasts (Deragon and Bousquet‐Antonelli 2015) and LARP1‐L in most higher eukaryotes, including humans, Arabidopsis may be an interesting model organism for studying the evolutionary divergence of LARP1 members.

C. elegans

Two distinct LARPs exist in C. elegans : one that clusters with the LARP1 family and another which relates to a metazoan‐specific LARP4 branch (with closer homology to LARP4b). The C. elegans LARP‐1 was described to include a C‐terminal “LARP1 domain” (Nykamp et al. 2008), which was the first characterization of the now renamed DM15 motif. Full‐length LARP‐1 was shown to bind homopolymers of poly(U) and poly(G) in vitro, but not poly(A) or poly(C) RNA. The isolated N‐terminal of LARP‐1, containing the LaM, maintained binding to poly(G) but not poly(U), while a C‐terminal fragment containing the DM15 region bound weakly to both poly(G) and poly(U). Here, the authors suggested early on that the LaM and DM15 may cooperate to enhance RNA‐binding activity of LARP1 family members. Notably, the lack of in vitro binding to poly(A) RNA could suggest a necessity for interaction with PABP with respect to potential engagement of mRNA poly(A) tails in C. elegans , but this has not been tested. It is also noteworthy to mention that hLARP1 does not bind to poly(C) RNAs (Al‐Ashtal et al. 2019), or those containing poly(C), poly(G) or poly(U) sequences at their 3′ ends (Aoki et al. 2013).

C. elegans LARP‐1 was further linked to gene expression in the germline, as larp‐1 null mutant worms have a defect in oogenesis. Similar to Arabidopsis, C. elegans LARP‐1 was linked to RNA stability, as it colocalized with P bodies (Nykamp et al. 2008) together with the C. elegans decapping enzyme homolog DCAP‐1 (Cohen et al. 2005; Lall et al. 2005) and the putative translational repressor and Scd6 homolog CAR‐1 (Albrecht and Lengauer 2004; Anantharaman and Aravind 2004). The abundance of several mRNAs linked to signaling and sexual development (i.e., lip‐1 (MAPK phosphatase), let‐60 (RAS), mpk‐1 (MAPK kinase) and fem‐3 (sex determination protein) (Zanin et al. 2010)) was additionally shown to increase in larp‐1 null worms, further arguing for functions related to RNA stability (Nykamp et al. 2008; Zanin et al. 2010). Interestingly, the less extensively studied human LARP1 family member, hLARP1b, is expressed at significantly lower levels than hLARP1a in most cell types, with the notable exception of the testes (Nawaz 2023). This pattern suggests a potentially underexplored connection between LARP1 family members and gametogenesis, aligning with suggested roles for LARP1 proteins in germline development in C. elegans and Drosophila. It is also noteworthy that, similar to the proposed roles of hLARP1 in mitochondrial fitness and aging, C. elegans LARP‐1 is suggested to promote local protein synthesis by driving liquid–liquid phase separation of translation machinery and RNA‐binding proteins into mitochondria‐associated translation organelles, which help protect mitochondrial health and promote longevity (Bai et al. 2025).

An interesting venue for further investigation in C. elegans includes identifying LARP‐1 target mRNAs at a global level. Since 5′TOP mRNAs are not described in C. elegans, while the LARP1 ortholog in this system possesses a DM15 region, it would be informative to uncover the functional significance of the DM15 domain in this system, which may lead to some understanding of early or understudied functions of this domain. C. elegans may be an intermediate organism, in an evolutionary sense, between yeasts (lacking both the 5′TOP motif and LARP1 proteins with a DM15 region) and higher eukaryotes (containing both the 5′TOP motif and long‐form LARP1 proteins). Further studies in C. elegans could therefore enhance our understanding of LARP1‐associated RNA binding modes.

D. melanogaster

Two LARP species have been identified in Drosophila: dLarp (CG42551) belonging to the LARP1 family, and CG11505 belonging to the LARP4 (with closer homology to LARP4b) family. Early studies in Drosophila characterized the LARP1 ortholog in this system, dLarp, as a cytoplasmic Hox target protein with functions related to cytoskeleton structure and integrity (Chauvet et al. 2000). dLarp contains a La module and two tandem repeats of a DM15 motif at its C‐terminal end, and was reported to function in male meiosis and mitochondrial inheritance (Ichihara et al. 2007). Notably, detached spindle microtubules in dLarp mutant flies resembled phenotypes observed in PABP mutants (Ichihara et al. 2007), and later studies confirmed that dLarp exists in an RNA‐independent complex with PABP (Blagden et al. 2009). While both PABP and dLARP mutants showed related meiotic defects, PABP mutants result in severe cell death while dLARP null flies are viable, leading to the hypothesis that dLarp may function on a subset of PABP‐bound mRNAs (Blagden et al. 2009).

More recent studies have linked dLarp to the growing body of literature connecting LARP1 family members to mitochondrial function (see above and Table 1). dLarp has been hypothesized to enhance localized translation of a subset of nuclear‐encoded mitochondrial proteins through an A‐Kinase Anchoring Protein (AKAP) named MDI, for “Mitochondrial DNA Insufficient” (Zhang et al. 2016). In MDI knockout cells, several nuclear‐encoded mitochondrial factors showed significantly reduced mRNA levels in polysomes and decreased de novo protein synthesis, but this was rescued by the expression of a Tom20‐dLarp fusion protein constitutively targeting dLarp to the mitochondrial outer membrane. It was suggested that the MDI‐dLarp complex promotes the translation of mtDNA replication factors, mitochondrial ribosomal proteins and electron‐transport chain subunits. Furthermore, while phosphorylation of dLarp does not affect its stability or localization, it was proposed to inhibit its function as a translational activator (Zhang et al. 2019). It remains to be determined whether dLarp is also involved with the translation and/or stability of cytoplasmic RP mRNAs as it has been described for LARP1 orthologs in other systems, and if this interaction is regulated through the DM15/5′TOP +1C binding mode.

S. cerevisiae

Amongst characterized yeast LARP1 family members, the S. cerevisiae LARP1 orthologs Slf1p and Sro9p, as well as the S. pombe ortholog Slr1p (see below), belong to the short‐form LARP1 sub‐category, similar to the Arabidopsis LARP1b and LARP1c proteins (Bousquet‐Antonelli and Deragon 2009). These factors therefore provide an opportunity to study LaM‐associated RNA binding within the LARP1 family, independent of the DM15 region. Similar to hLARP1, Slf1p and Sro9p function in the cytoplasm and associate with translating ribosomes (Sobel and Wolin 1999). Both proteins bind highly expressed mRNAs, and Slf1p is proposed to enhance the stability of target mRNAs in a LaM‐dependent manner (Schenk et al. 2012). Yeast strains lacking either Slf1p or Sro9p are less sensitive to the effects of paromomycin and cycloheximide, which affect the elongation phase of translation (Singh et al. 1979; Sobel and Wolin 1999). Before its characterization as a LARP1 family member, Slf1p was also linked to copper detoxification since, in a dose‐dependent manner, it increases the ability of yeast cells to process copper sulfide and allow for its depletion (Schenk et al. 2012; Yu et al. 1996). Also, Sro9p was reported to function as a chaperone that represses the yeast heme activator protein Hap1 (Lan et al. 2004). Additionally, functions in transcription (Tan et al. 2000) and cytoskeleton organization (Imai et al. 1996; Kagami et al. 1997) have been reported for Sro9p.

The two S. cerevisiae LARP1 paralogs, which have evolved as a result of gene duplication (Wolfe and Shields 1997), share a 37% overall, and 57% LaM, similarity in amino acid sequence (Kershaw et al. 2015). While Slf1p is predominantly cytoplasmic, Sro9p shuttles between the nucleus and the cytoplasm, potentially bound to its target mRNAs (Röther et al. 2010; Schenk et al. 2012). Key point mutations in the LaM, or its deletion, lower Slf1p's association with polysomes (Kershaw et al. 2015). Other functions such as RNA binding, target mRNA stabilization, and copper detoxification are also LaM‐dependent for Slf1p (Schenk et al. 2012). Polysome profiling shows that while Slf1p primarily sediments with monosomes and polysomes, Sro9p tends to associate with subpolysomes and polysomes (Kershaw et al. 2015; Sobel and Wolin 1999). This may indicate that Slf1p preferentially interacts with elongating ribosomes, whereas Sro9p could affect initiation as well as elongation events (Kershaw et al. 2015). Additionally, Sro9p binds a wider breadth of mRNAs, including all that Slf1p binds to; however, Sro9p is also approximately 13.5 times more abundant than Slf1p (Jennings et al. 2023). Interestingly, in nearly all studies, both Slf1p and Sro9p have been reported to bind highly expressed mRNAs, including those coding for RP mRNAs, which are functionally similar to 5′TOP mRNAs (Jennings et al. 2023; Kershaw et al. 2015; Schenk et al. 2012; Sobel and Wolin 1999). Despite these findings, their interaction with RP mRNAs has not yet been specifically studied in S. cerevisiae . Thus, this remains an intriguing area for further investigation given the link established between LARP1 and RP mRNA regulation (in humans, Arabidopsis and S. pombe [see below]).

Slf1p and Sro9p have also been reported to associate with RNAs that are upregulated in response to copper toxicity (Jennings et al. 2023; Schenk et al. 2012; Yu et al. 1996) and oxidative stress (Jennings et al. 2023; Kershaw et al. 2015), events which involve reprogramming of mRNA translation. Both proteins immunoprecipitate components of the mRNA translation machinery, such as eIF4E, eIF4G and PABP, as well as ribosomal proteins Rps3p and Rpl35p. Notably, Slf1p and Sro9p remain associated with these factors during oxidative stress (Kershaw et al. 2015). Slf1p plays a crucial role in this context, involving the translation and stability of the upregulated stress response mRNAs (Jennings et al. 2023; Kershaw et al. 2015; Schenk et al. 2012). Acute stress leads to an increased expression of specialized mRNAs and increased likelihood of ribosome collisions during the elongation phase of mRNA translation. Slf1p has been proposed to play a role in resolving ribosome collisions and preventing frameshifting, such that stress response mRNAs could be effectively translated in this context (Jennings et al. 2023; Kershaw et al. 2015).

Analysis of the transcripts bound by both Slf1p and Sro9p suggests that their targets are also enriched for mRNAs involved with mitochondrial functions (Kershaw et al. 2015). Slf1p and Sro9p are further involved in promoting survival fitness in S. cerevisiae, which is chronically infected with a double‐stranded RNA virus called L‐A (Nakayashiki et al. 2005; Wickner et al. 2013). Despite being presumed to be harmless, the infection leads to proteotoxic stress and conditional lethality when viral attenuation pathways are suppressed. In cooperation with other proteins, Slf1p alleviates the consequences of proteotoxic stress to increase cell survival, while Sro9p suppresses viral protein accumulation in response to high levels of viral translation (Chau et al. 2023).

S. pombe

S. pombe contains a single short‐form LARP1 ortholog named Slr1p. Studies in this system have suggested that Slr1p is involved with both the translation and stability of mRNAs that encode ribosomal proteins and other components of the translational apparatus (Mansouri‐Noori et al. 2023). Notably, Slr1p does not have a DM15 domain, yet its target RNAs are functionally equivalent to 5′TOP mRNAs. A motif discovery search revealed that these mRNAs contain a 5′ proximal AC‐rich (5′PAC) motif which resides close to the start AUG, where hLARP1 has also been shown to bind in the 5′UTR of translation‐related mRNAs (Hong et al. 2017). This is consistent with a role for Slr1p in binding to pyrimidine‐containing sequences, within its target mRNAs' 5′UTRs, analogous to the 5′TOP/PRTE sequence that is found in higher eukaryotes. Proteomic studies showed that Slr1p interacts with several factors linked to mRNA translation, such as the translation initiation factors eIF4G (Tif471p in S. pombe) and eIF3i (Sum1p), as well as the major 5′ to 3′ mRNA exonuclease and XRN1 homolog Exo2p, in an RNA‐independent manner. Slr1p‐associated mRNAs are less abundant and have shorter half‐lives in wildtype cells in comparison to slr1‐ cells, suggesting that Slr1p has a role in the destabilization of RP mRNAs. On the other hand, depletion of polysomes in the presence of the translation initiation inhibitor harringtonine and lower expression of 5′PAC motif‐containing reporters in slr1‐ cells argue for a positive role for Slr1p in translation initiation. Furthermore, mutagenesis of the Slr1p LaM residue Q332, known for recognizing terminal uridylates in genuine La (Q20) (Kotik‐Kogan et al. 2008; Teplova et al. 2006) and poly(A) RNA in hLARP1 (Q333) (Kozlov et al. 2022, 2024), suggests this highly conserved residue is responsible for the recognition of the 5′PAC motif in S. pombe (Mansouri‐Noori 2025). These findings highlight the versatility of RNA‐binding modes through the LaM. Together, functions in mRNA translation and stability may converge on a role for Slr1p in co‐translational mRNA decay, similar to what has been proposed for LARP1 in Arabidopsis. Future work in S. pombe, delineating functions of Slr1p in response to stress or with respect to its post‐translational modification status, may shed light on its complex roles.

Conclusion

Common Themes

A number of functions have been proposed for hLARP1 (Figure 2), but the underlying mechanisms and their context dependency are still a source of uncertainty. The most extensively studied function is a role in the regulation of mRNA translation, especially related to 5′TOP and 5′TOP‐like transcripts, through the direct engagement of the m7G cap and adjacent +1 cytidine, with post‐translational modifications around the DM15 likely influencing a ligand‐ready state that is hypothesized to control binding and associated inhibition of translation initiation (Bayfield et al. 2019; Cassidy et al. 2019; Fonseca et al. 2015; Hong et al. 2017; Jia et al. 2021; Lahr et al. 2017; Philippe et al. 2017). Reflection on work performed on LARP1 family members in other systems provides some clues on areas for future focus, in the hope that the varied functions ascribed to hLARP1 might be better understood.

RNA Binding by the LaM and Associated Regions

The role of the LaM is well‐established as the primary RNA binding site in most members of the LARP superfamily, but to date has received less attention relative to the DM15 for hLARP1. The absence (short‐form LARP1 proteins) or uncertainty (hLARP1) for the presence of an RRM immediately C‐terminal to the LaM sets the LARP1 family apart from other LARPs, and the potential RNA binding mode ascribed to LARP1 orthologs are varied. The LaM of hLARP1 has been most extensively linked to poly(A) RNA binding (Aoki et al. 2013; Kozlov et al. 2022; Mattijssen et al. 2021; Park et al. 2023), similar to hLARP4, but also to guanylated poly(A) sequences (Kozlov et al. 2024) as well as pyrimidine‐rich sequences similar to 5′TOP motifs (Al‐Ashtal et al. 2019). Work in other systems linking LARP1 to PABP (Drosophila; Blagden et al. 2009) may align with a link to mRNA poly(A) tails, while work in S. cerevisiae and S. pombe are also consistent with binding of internal mRNA sequences (Jennings et al. 2023; Mansouri‐Noori et al. 2023). Therefore, it appears that LaM‐dependent functions of LARP1 family members may be directly (interaction with pyrimidine‐containing sequences in humans; Al‐Ashtal et al. 2019; Schmidt et al. 2021 and S. pombe; Mansouri‐Noori et al. 2023) or indirectly (poly(A) tail binding leading to increased mRNA stability; Table 1) permissive to translation, while the DM15 binding mode is inhibitory. Clarity on these questions will likely rely on assessment of direct interactions in cellulo as well as attempts to integrate context dependent interactions that may change as a consequence of post‐translational modifications (more below).

Active Versus Passive Roles in RNA Decay

A recurring theme in LARP1 studies in metazoans is LARP1 interaction with PABP. Combined with work proposing direct binding of hLARP1 to the 3′ end of poly(A) tails (Aoki et al. 2013; Kozlov et al. 2024) and the lower abundance of some mRNAs in LARP1 knockdown or knockout cells (humans) (Aoki et al. 2013; Fonseca et al. 2015; Fuentes et al. 2021; Gentilella et al. 2017; Hopkins et al. 2016; Mura et al. 2015; Tcherkezian et al. 2014), multiple groups have proposed that LARP1 family members stabilize mRNA clients by protecting them from decay. On the other hand, certain mRNAs have been shown to increase in abundance in LARP1‐depleted cells (humans, Arabidopsis, C. elegans , and S. pombe) (Hopkins et al. 2016; Lee et al. 2021; Mansouri‐Noori et al. 2023; Merret et al. 2013; Nykamp et al. 2008; Schmidt et al. 2021; Zanin et al. 2010) and there is evidence that both long‐ (Arabidopsis) and short‐form (S. pombe) LARP1 members make direct, protein–protein interactions with homologs of the human 5′ to 3′ exonuclease XRN1 (XRN4 in Arabidopsis, Exo2p in S. pombe) (Mansouri‐Noori et al. 2023; Merret et al. 2013). These active roles for LARP1 family members in 5′ to 3′ exonuclease recruitment may link the LARP1 family to co‐translational mRNA decay, even if this has not yet been studied for hLARP1.

Post‐Translational Modifications of LARP1

hLARP1 is a downstream target of mTOR, and phosphorylation has been proposed as a mechanism by which the DM15 may toggle 5′ cap binding modes (Bayfield et al. 2019; Cassidy et al. 2019; Fonseca et al. 2015; Hong et al. 2017; Jia et al. 2021; Philippe et al. 2017). Multiple TOR‐associated phosphorylation sites have been mapped adjacent to both the hLARP1 DM15 and LaM, as well as to some analogous sites in LARP1 from S. pombe. Given that hLARP1 is not an obligate mTOR target, its phosphorylation by other kinases, as well as additional post‐translational modifications, represents an understudied aspect of LARP1 function. Such modifications likely enable functional switching of LARP1 downstream of TOR and other signaling pathways, a concept that remains plausible yet largely unexplored.

LARP1 in Mitochondrial Function and Gametogenesis

While most described functions for LARP1 have focused on 5′TOP or 5′TOP‐like mRNAs, an expanding body of literature has linked LARP1 family members to mRNAs coding for factors destined for the mitochondria (human, Drosophila, S. cerevisiae ; see Table 1). Work in C. elegans and Drosophila has also linked LARP1 mutants to sterility defects (Blagden et al. 2009; Ichihara et al. 2007; Nykamp et al. 2008; Zanin et al. 2010), and it is notable that the less studied hLARP1b is more highly expressed in the testes (Nawaz 2023).

In conclusion, proposed roles for hLARP1 are diverse and complex (Figure 2). Comparative studies of LARP1 family members in other systems may both help clarify these roles and provide a foundation for exploring additional hLARP1 functions that remain uncharacterized. Key future directions include examining the evolutionary divergence between LARP1‐S (LaM only) and LARP1‐L (LaM + DM15) orthologs, identifying target RNAs/motifs and modes of RNA binding, characterizing protein–protein interactions, investigating altered functional states under stress conditions, and determining post‐translational modification patterns downstream of various signaling pathways.
