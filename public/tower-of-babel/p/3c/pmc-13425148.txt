Comparative Proteomics Reveals Conserved and Lineage-Specific Molecular Toolkits in Lophotrochozoan Biomineralization
Authors: Yaru Chen, Jie Li, Zhangjie Peng, Fujin Lv, Yue Wang, Wen Teng, Chenrui Li, Linlin Zhang, Toni Gossmann
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAnimal biomineralization has evolved repeatedly, yielding diverse skeletal structures. A key question is whether this diversity stems from molecular convergence or a shared ancestral toolkit. Studying lophotrochozoans—a group with carbonate, phosphate, iron, and mixed mineralization systems—provides an ideal framework for addressing this issue but remains underrepresented in comparative omics studies. Here, we present a genome assembly and shell matrix proteome of the phosphate-biomineralizing brachiopod Lingula anatina CN, derived from a geographically distinct population. By integrating these data with comparative proteomic analysis across seven lophotrochozoan lineages representing diverse mineralization chemistries, we provide novel insights into the molecular basis of biomineralization. Sequence-based comparisons reveal extensive lineage specificity of shell matrix proteins, with little conservation across distant taxa. In contrast, domain-level analyses uncover a broadly conserved repertoire of functional modules—including EF-hand, von Willebrand factor type A, collagen-related, chitin-binding, and ferritin-like domains—recurrently recruited into shell matrix proteins across lineages. Importantly, similarity in shell matrix protein composition correlates more strongly with mineralization chemistry than with phylogenetic distance. Specifically, phosphate-mineralizing systems share a higher proportion of conserved shell matrix proteins and domains across deep evolutionary distances than do carbonate-based systems, indicating that mineral chemistry exerts a dominant selective pressure on the evolution of biomineralization-associated proteins. Lineage-specific innovations appear to arise primarily through domain shuffling, expansion of low-complexity regions, and gene family diversification. These findings support a model wherein lophotrochozoan biomineralization is underpinned by a shared ancestral molecular toolkit, differentially filtered and elaborated according to mineral chemistry, providing new insights into the early evolution of animal skeletal systems.Keywords: Lingula anatina, genomics, comparative proteomic, biomineralization, toolkits, evolution

Animal biomineralization underlies diverse skeletal structures, yet the relative roles of phylogeny and mineral type in shaping shell matrix proteins (SMPs) remain poorly understood. By comparing SMPs across phosphate-, carbonate-, iron-, and mixed-mineral systems in seven lophotrochozoan species, we show that, while SMP sequences are highly lineage-specific, structural domains are broadly conserved, forming a modular toolkit. Importantly, SMP composition aligns more closely with mineralization chemistry than with phylogenetic distance, revealing pervasive convergent evolution. These findings provide new insights into the molecular and evolutionary principles governing animal biomineralization, highlighting how functional constraints of mineral types drive convergence and innovation in SMP repertoires.

Introduction

Biomineralization represents a pivotal evolutionary innovation in metazoan history, emerging widely during the Cambrian Explosion and profoundly shaping the structure and function of modern marine ecosystems (Knoll 2003; Pan et al 2019). Among metazoans, the superphylum lophotrochozoa exhibits the greatest diversity of biomineralization strategies, encompassing calcium carbonate–(Benjamin et al 2012; Immel et al 2015; Gilbert et al 2022; Liu et al 2024), calcium phosphate–(Zhang et al 2024; Ge et al 2025), iron- (Faivre et al 2015; Liu et al 2022), and mixed mineral–based systems (Zhang et al 2012; Zhao et al 2018; Bai et al 2023) with evolutionary origins traceable to the early Cambrian (Liang et al 2023). This combination of deep phylogenetic divergence and mineralogical heterogeneity makes lophotrochozoans a uniquely powerful framework for examining the evolutionary principles governing animal biomineralization.

Within lophotrochozoans, brachiopods, mollusks, annelids, and related lineages display strikingly distinct mineral chemistry despite shared ancestry (Gilbert et al. 2022). The brachiopod Lingula, long regarded as a “living fossil,” has persisted since the early Cambrian (∼520 Ma) and survived all five mass extinctions (Emig 2003; Zhang et al 2009). Its shell is composed of an apatite–chitin composite that closely resembles the mineral composition of vertebrate bones and teeth, yet phosphate biomineralization is rare among extant invertebrates (Murdock et al 2011; Gilbert et al. 2022). Together with carbonate- and iron-mineralizing systems in other lophotrochozoan lineages, Lingula provides an evolutionary anchor for exploring how mineral composition interacts with phylogenetic history to shape skeletal systems (Williams et al 1994; Knoll 2003; Neary et al 2011; Zhang et al 2023).

Animal shells are organic–mineral composites dominated by the mineral phase, whereas the organic shell matrix typically constitutes less than 1% of shell weight (Agbaje et al 2019). Despite its low abundance, the shell matrix—particularly shell matrix proteins (SMPs)—plays a central role in shell formation by providing organic scaffolds (Clark et al 2020) and regulating crystal nucleation, growth (Zheng et al 2023), and polymorph selection construction (Falini et al 1996; Addadi et al 2006; Ponce and Evans 2011). Advances in high-throughput transcriptomic and proteomic approaches have enabled large-scale identification of SMPs across diverse biomineralizing taxa (Jackson et al 2015; Fuyun et al 2024; Wong et al 2025). In Lingula, the first genome and shell proteome established a foundational catalog of SMPs associated with phosphate biomineralization (Luo et al 2015), while proteomic studies in annelids and mollusks have revealed extensive lineage-specific diversification of SMP repertoires (Kocot et al 2016; Marin 2020; Ge et al. 2025).

Comparative proteomic analyses within mollusks have demonstrated that similar skeletal structures can arise from markedly different protein repertoires, highlighting rapid evolutionary turnover driven by gene co-option, domain shuffling, and lineage-specific innovation (Jackson et al. 2010; Aguilera et al 2017; Zhang et al 2021; Liu et al 2023). Studies of specialized mineralized structures, such as the iron-mineralized scleritome of the scaly-foot snail (Wong et al. 2025) and mineralized radulae in mollusks (Liu et al. 2022), have further illustrated the repeated recruitment of preexisting genes into novel biomineralization contexts. Together with recent proteomic investigations of phosphate biomineralization in annelids (Ge et al. 2025), these studies have begun to extend comparative perspectives beyond single taxa or mineral systems, revealing substantial molecular diversity underlying biomineral formation.

At the same time, comparative analyses have uncovered recurring molecular features shared across disparate biomineralization systems. A limited set of conserved protein domains—including chitin-binding (CB), carbonic anhydrase (CA), von Willebrand factor type A (VWA), tyrosinase, and EF-hand domains—has been repeatedly identified in SMPs across mollusks and other biomineralizing metazoans, supporting the existence of a deeply conserved “core biomineralization toolkit” (Zhao et al. 2018; Murdock 2020; Yarra et al 2021; Shimizu et al 2024). Genomic and proteomic analyses further suggest that such conserved components may be differentially recruited and modified across lineages, giving rise to distinct mineralization strategies (Luo et al. 2015; Yuan et al 2022; Lewin et al 2024). Notably, however, most available data derive from carbonate-based systems, and the molecular basis and evolutionary relationships of phosphate biomineralization remain comparatively underexplored.

Consequently, the extent to which molecular similarities observed among different biomineralization systems reflect convergent evolution or broader evolutionary patterns shaped by shared ancestry and mineral chemistry remains incompletely understood. While previous studies have provided valuable insights into individual lineages and mineral systems (Luo et al. 2015; Arivalagan et al 2017; Ge et al. 2025; Wong et al. 2025), a systematic comparison of shell matrix proteomes spanning the full phylogenetic breadth of lophotrochozoa and the diversity of mineralization types within this superphylum has not yet been undertaken. As a result, the relative contributions of phylogenetic distance and mineral composition to the evolution of biomineralization-associated proteins remain to be evaluated at a macroevolutionary scale.

Here, we address this gap by integrating a high-quality genome and shell matrix proteome of Lingula anatina with a comprehensive comparative analysis of eight publicly available lophotrochozoan shell proteomes. This dataset encompasses seven phylogenetically distinct lophotrochozoan lineages and seven mineralization types. By systematically comparing protein composition, domain architecture, and patterns of evolutionary conservation across mineralization strategies, we evaluate whether lophotrochozoan biomineralization is underpinned by a putatively ancestral molecular toolkit that is differentially retained, modified, and elaborated in relation to mineral chemistry. Together, our analyses establish a unified, domain-centric framework for investigating the deep evolutionary origins and diversification of biomineralization systems in animals.

Results

Comparative Genomics of L. anatina Geographically Distinct Populations

We generated 33.27 Gb of PacBio HiFi reads (∼105× coverage) from a single SMART cell on the PacBio Revio platform, yielding a high-quality genome assembly of LinAna CN spanning 324.7 Mb, with a contig N50 of 18.41 Mb (Table S1). The assembled contigs were anchored into pseudochromosomes using the high-quality LinAna3.0 assembly as a reference (Fig. 1a; Fig. S1), resulting in 153 contigs with a scaffold N50 of 28.10 Mb (Table S1). The assembly demonstrated high completeness (99.6% complete genes via BUSCO) and includes 77.14 Mb (23.73%) of repetitive elements, dominated by DNA transposons (15.21 Mb, 4.68%) (Fig. S2 and Tables S1 and S2). We predicted 19,444 high-confidence protein-coding genes with an average of 8.6 exons per gene, and functional annotation assigned terms to 96.45% of genes using NR (Non-Redundant Protein Database), Swiss-Prot (Swiss-Prot Protein Sequence Database), GO (Gene Ontology), KEGG (Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes), Pfam (Protein Families Database), and InterPro (Table S1). Genes were categorized into 26 functional groups based on Eukaryotic Orthologous Groups (KOG) (Fig. S2a) and activities spanning DNA metabolism, immune responses, development, and catalytic functions, with localizations to key cellular structures. Quantitative analyses of GO term distributions provide a systematic overview of functional diversity in the L. anatina genome (Fig. S2b), offering a detailed view of its structural and functional landscape and establishing a comprehensive resource for investigating the evolutionary adaptations and unique biology of this brachiopod.

We generated a genome assembly from a geographically distinct (Chinese) population. Notably, genome-wide comparison reveals a relatively low sequence similarity (∼0.73) between assemblies (Fig. S3 and Tables S2 to S4). Repetitive regions and gaps observed among these genomes further reflect the structural diversity across populations. Figure 1b visualizes the geographic origins of L. anatina genomes. The L. anatina CN population, sourced from Rizhao, China, demonstrates significant geographic distance from its Japanese counterparts (L. anatina JP). Genetic distance analysis further quantifies these differences, with the Chinese population (LinAna CN) diverging at genetic distances of 0.0759 from Japanese population (LinAna JP), underscoring significant interpopulation differentiation attributed to geographic isolation. Mapping analyses provide further evidence of genomic heterogeneity between populations (Fig. S4). The mapping ratio between the Chinese L. anatina CN and the Japanese populations (L. anatina JP) is notably lower, reflecting high genomic divergence and substantial genetic diversity between these populations. In comparison to other species, such as Magallana angulata versus Magallana gigas, and regional variations within the same species (Chinese M. gigas vs. Japanese M. gigas), the significantly lower mapping ratio for L. anatina indicates that geographic isolation and historical divergence have played pivotal roles in shaping genomic differentiation between the Chinese and Japanese populations (Fig. S4). Together, these results indicate that the LinAnaCN assembly represents a high-quality genomic resource capturing considerable population divergence, providing a robust foundation for downstream analyses of gene content and shell matrix proteomes.

Phylogenetic Relationships and Gene Family Evolution

Comparative genomic analysis of L. anatina across 40 metazoans, including lophotrochozoans, ecdysozoans, deuterostomes, and basal metazoans, positioned it firmly within brachiopods and revealed distinct patterns of gene family evolution. The L. anatina (CN) lineage exhibited 43 gene family expansions and 212 contractions, contrasting with a related brachiopod lineage (2,011 expansions, 55 contractions), while other lophotrochozoans such as Phoronid Phoronis australis and Bryozoa Bugula neritina showed lineage-specific expansions (Fig. S5a). Deuterostomes largely retained conserved gene family profiles. GO enrichment analysis indicated that expanded families are enriched in intracellular structures, metabolism, and nucleic acid binding, whereas contracted families are associated with extracellular matrix (ECM) biosynthesis and sulfotransferase activities (Fig. S5b and c). Notably, several expanded or contracted gene families overlap with those implicated in biomineralization, such as intracellular organelle and proteoglycan biosynthetic process terms, including chitin synthases, tyrosinases, carbonic anhydrases, and collagen-related genes, suggesting that lineage-specific expansions and losses may contribute to divergent mineralization strategies. For example, contraction of ECM-modifying enzymes in L. anatina may reflect specialization for phosphate-based shell formation, while expansions in intracellular regulatory genes could support high metabolic and secretory demands of mineral deposition. These findings reveal the evolutionary uniqueness of L. anatina, highlight lineage-specific adaptations in gene content, and provide a functional framework linking gene family dynamics to the diversification of biomineralization strategies across lophotrochozoans.

Ultrastructure and Proteome of the L. anatina Shell

We employed multi-scale analyses to characterize the ultrastructure and proteome of the phosphate-calcium shell in L. anatina, providing insights into its unique biomineralization mechanisms. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) revealed a hierarchical shell structure with alternating organic and inorganic layers (apatite microcrystals), with a distinct spatial patterning of phosphate deposition. Regional differences were observed between shell margin and central regions: the margin shell (∼300 µm) exhibited an internal layer of dot-like apatite crystals and an outer surface that was smooth with occasional pores (Fig. 2a, e to h). In contrast, the central region (∼1.5 cm) displayed an inner surface composed of rod- and botryoid-shaped apatite crystals, an outer surface formed of laminated sheets, and a clearly defined layered structure in cross-section (Fig. 2a, a to d). The shell comprises three main layers, similar in overall layering to molluscan shells: an outer periostracum (∼4 µm) of chitin and organic matrix, which exhibits a cross-linked network externally (Fig. 2a, d1), a primary layer (∼40 µm) predominantly composed of glycosaminoglycans (GAGs) with minor amounts of apatite, and a secondary laminated layer of apatitic laminae, which constitutes the main mineralized zone (Iwata 1981; Williams et al. 1994). Raman spectroscopy, X-ray diffraction (XRD), and energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) analyses confirmed fluorapatite as the dominant mineral, with compositional variation in calcium (Ca), phosphorus (P), and fluorine (F) between margin and center (Fig. 2b to d), distinguishing Lingula shells from the calcium carbonate shells of mollusk.

To investigate the molecular basis of phosphate biomineralization, proteins were extracted in acid-soluble and acid-insoluble fractions (Fig. 3a). SDS-PAGE revealed compositional differences, highlighting SMP complexity. LC-MS/MS analysis against the L. anatina genome identified 97 shell proteins based on Label-free quantification (LFQ) (Fig. 3b and Table S5), of which 13 were novel, 29 lacked functional annotation, and 55 were annotated (Fig. 3d). We found that most of shell proteins had only one unique peptide hit and many of them lack signal peptides (Fig. 3c). Based on structural domains and predicted functions (Marin 2020; Luo et al 2022), proteins were classified into seven categories: ECM/ECM-binding, biomineralization enzymes, saccharide-binding, immunomodulatory, divalent cation-binding, cytoplasmic/nuclear, and unknown proteins including low-complexity domains (LCDs) or repetitive low-complexity domains (RLCDs) (Fig. 3d). Protein–protein interaction (PPI) network analysis was performed on key shell proteins, such as elongation factor 1-alpha (XP_013405897.1), histone H2A.V(XP_013415122.1), polyubiquitin (XP_013414193.1), histone H3.3(XP_013416026.1), and histone H4(XP_013416063.1) (Fig. 3e). However, these proteins belong to the cytoplasmic/nuclear category, and their specific mechanisms require further investigation. The most enriched domains (P < 0.05) were PF00008 (EGF-like), PF01391 (collagen triple helix, 20 copies), PF13385 (concanavalin A-like lectin/glucanase), PF01607 (chitin-binding peritrophin-A), and PF13330 (Mucin-2 WxxW repeats) (Fig. 3f), suggesting their central role in forming the ECM and phosphate-calcium shell framework (Liu et al 2021). GO enrichment analysis implicated shell proteins in cytoskeletal organization, organelle structure, protein and ion binding, catalytic activity, intracellular architecture, and transmembrane transport (Fig. 3g). KEGG pathway analysis highlighted 13 enriched pathways (P < 0.05), mainly ECM-receptor interaction, complement and coagulation cascades, and ABC transporters (Fig. 3h). Collectively, these results reveal a complex molecular network underpinning shell formation. The integration of ultrastructural and proteomic data demonstrates that the phosphate-calcium biomineralization system of L. anatina represents a distinct evolutionary solution within lophotrochozoans, offering a valuable comparative framework for understanding the evolution of diverse mineralization strategies.

Domain analysis of 54 SMPs (Table S2), after excluding housekeeping proteins, showed that the most abundant domains were EGF, collagen triple helix repeats, and thrombospondin type 1 (TSP_1) (Fig. 4), consistent with an ECM-derived shell matrix (Ozbek et al 2010). Additional domains, including PF00090 (TSP_1), PF00084 (sushi/SCR), PF00054 (laminin G), and PF00059 (lectin C-type), were also observed, indicating potential multifunctionality in biomineralization and other cellular processes (Arivalagan et al 2017; Marin 2020). A notable fraction of proteins contained low-complexity or repetitive domains, many of which remain uncharacterized. Lingula SMPs were enriched in glycine and alanine residues compared with other brachiopods and mollusks, consistent with glycine-rich collagens expressed in the mantle (Margaret 1977; Mayumi et al 1991; Williams et al. 1994; Luo et al. 2015). Several collagen types (α-I, III, IV, VII) incorporated EGF-like domains and glycine-rich regions. Chitin-binding (ChtBD2) domains were detected in multiple proteins, indicating the presence of collagen–chitin composite components within the shell matrix. In addition, LFQ analysis indicates that, among the SMP classifications, proteins such as divalent cation-binding proteins, saccharide-binding proteins, and unknown proteins exhibit relatively high abundance (Table S6). These observations define the structural and domain composition of Lingula SMPs without inferring specific mechanistic roles.

Comparative Proteomic Analysis Across Lophotrochozoan Species

Comparative proteomic analyses were conducted to assess the degree of conservation of SMPs in L. anatina relative to six other lophotrochozoan species, including articulate brachiopods, annelids (polychaetes), and mollusks (polyplacophores, gastropods, bivalves, and cephalopods), representing major evolutionary lineages and biomineralization systems within lophotrochozoa (Fig. 5a and Table 1; Data S1 to S7). Sequence homology analysis revealed an absence of universally conserved SMPs across all seven taxa examined. At the primary sequence level, none of the identified SMPs was shared by all lophotrochozoan species included in this comparison. As the number of taxa considered increased, the number of shared SMPs declined sharply. Specifically, only four SMPs were shared among six species (putative tyrosinase-like protein tyr-3, SCO-spondin, uncharacterized protein LOC112041919, zonadhesin), three among five species (peroxidasin-like, mucin-5AC, uncharacterized protein LOC106161601), and five among four species (calcineurin B homologous protein 1, A disintegrin and metalloproteinase with thrombospondin motifs adt-1, basement membrane-specific heparan sulfate proteoglycan core protein, uncharacterized protein LOC106160759, kunitz-type serine protease inhibitor Bt-KTI), whereas the majority of homologous SMPs were restricted to comparisons involving three or fewer species. This pattern indicates limited sequence-level conservation of SMPs across broad taxonomic scales. Within mollusks, five SMPs (putative tyrosinase-like protein tyr-3, SCO-spondin, uncharacterized protein LOC106161601, CD109, carbonic anhydrase) were conserved among the molluscan representatives analyzed, whereas overlap between mollusks and brachiopods was substantially lower (only one SMP putative tyrosinase-like protein tyr-3), further underscoring pronounced inter-lineage divergence in SMP repertoires. Consistent with this trend, L. anatina possessed 22 SMPs with no detectable homologs in any of the other species examined, highlighting a substantial lineage-specific component of its shell proteome (Fig. 5b). These unique SMPs were predominantly uncharacterized and frequently contained low-complexity repetitive regions enriched in alanine-, glycine-, serine-, threonine-, aspartic acid-, and glutamic acid-rich motifs (Fig. 4). Such compositional features distinguish the Lingula shell proteome from those of other lophotrochozoans. Collectively, these results demonstrate that SMP repertoires exhibit weak conservation at the sequence level across lophotrochozoan species, with lineage-specific components constituting a major fraction of the L. anatina shell matrix proteome.

Given the pronounced divergence of SMPs at the primary sequence level, we further assessed conservation at the structural domain level across seven lophotrochozoan species. In contrast to weak sequence conservation, domain-based analyses revealed markedly higher conservation, indicating retention of shared functional modules despite extensive protein turnover (Fig. 5c). Notably, the EF-hand domain was detected in all species examined, representing the most broadly conserved component of lophotrochozoan shell proteomes and underscoring its central role in calcium handling during biomineralization. Beyond this universally shared domain, multiple domains showed broad but non-universal distributions across distantly related lineages, including ECM-associated, ion-binding, and redox-related modules. Several domains, such as the ChtBD2 and tyrosinase, were shared between phosphate-mineralizing brachiopods and carbonate-mineralizing mollusks, indicating conservation across mineralization chemistries. Brachiopods (including Lingula) and annelids shared a particularly rich repertoire of domains, encompassing F5/8 type C, trypsin inhibitor-like cysteine rich, concanavalin A-like lectin/glucanases, laminin G, WD domain, EGF-like, DOMON, TSP_1, CUB, calponin homology (CH), ferritin-like, lectin C-type, sushi repeat, and animal heme peroxidase domains. Within brachiopods, L. anatina was further characterized by an enrichment of EGF domain-containing collagenous proteins. Together, these results demonstrate that while SMP repertoires are highly lineage-specific at the sequence level, their domain architectures are comparatively conserved across lophotrochozoans, highlighting domain-level conservation as a unifying feature of shell biomineralization systems.

Comparative Proteomic Analyses Across Lophotrochozoan Biomineralization Types

To evaluate whether biomineralization chemistry influences SMP composition, we further compared published shell matrix proteomes across seven distinct lophotrochozoan biomineralization systems, including calcium carbonate-, calcium phosphate-, iron-based, and mixed mineral types (Fig. 6 and Table 1; Data S1 to S3 and S6 to S9). SMP similarity differed markedly between phosphate- and carbonate-based systems. In pairwise comparisons, 33 of 54 L. anatina SMPs (61.66%) had detectable homology with at least one other species. The highest similarity was observed with the phosphate-mineralizing polychaete Sternaspis chinensis (32.06%), whereas similarity to the phylogenetically closer calcitic brachiopod Magellania venosa was lower (18.18%) (Figs. 5b and 6a). Mantel tests indicate that SMP similarity is most strongly correlated with mineral chemistry (Pearson's r = 0.8504, P = 0.0153), whereas correlations with batch effect and phylogenetic distance are weak and non-significant (Fig. S9 and Tables S8 to S12).

The SMPs of the amorphous iron phosphate-mineralizing shield of the polychaete S. chinensis did not exhibit the highest similarity with the phosphate-shelled L. anatina (44.44%). Instead, they displayed higher similarity to iron sulfide (FeS)-enriched scales (81.16%) and magnetite (Fe3O4)-mineralized radulae (64.00%) (Fig. 6b). This pattern may suggest a relatively more conserved set of SMPs involved in iron-associated mineralization processes, potentially linked to the formation of specialized biomineralized structures such as radulae, scales, or shields. Comparisons of magnetite-mineralized radulae (eg Acanthopleura loochooana) revealed markedly higher similarity to iron sulfide-enriched scales (51/69, 73.91%) than to phylogenetically closer aragonitic cephalopods (4/77, 5.17%) (Fig. 6c). The SMP sequences of the distantly related, iron sulfide-mineralized scales (eg Chrysomallon squamiferum) showed unexpectedly high similarity (exceeding 97.10%) when mapped to SMPs from other lophotrochozoans, indicating a high degree of conservation in the evolution of C. squamiferum scales (Wong et al. 2025). Concurrently, these iron sulfide-mineralized scales also exhibited considerable similarity to the amorphous iron phosphate-mineralizing polychaete S. chinensis (49.62%) (Fig. 6d). The high degree of similarity spanning large phylogenetic distances and distinct mineral chemistries strongly suggests the occurrence of convergent evolution in SMPs (Fig. 6a to d).

Within carbonate-based systems, the calcitic brachiopod M. venosa showed higher similarity to the predominantly calcitic–aragonitic bivalve M. gigas, although overall similarity remained below 20.00% (Fig. 6e). The SMPs of the aragonitic-shelled Nautilus pompilius, when compared with other mineralization types, showed the highest similarity to those of the iron sulfide-enriched scales (44.93%), followed by those of the phosphate-shelled L. anatina (33.89%) (Fig. 6f). Notably, the SMPs of M. gigas—a species with a mixed calcite–aragonite shell—did not show the strongest similarity to those of the purely calcitic M. venosa or the aragonitic N. pompilius. Instead, they exhibited higher similarity to the SMPs of iron sulfide-enriched scales (43.48%), while showing lower matching rates with other lophotrochozoan biomineralization types (Fig. 6g). Collectively, these patterns indicate that mineralization chemistry exerts a stronger constraint on SMP composition than phylogenetic proximity. The SMPs associated with calcium carbonate shells may follow more complex evolutionary trajectories, influenced by factors such as crystal polymorphism (calcite vs. aragonite), diversity in shell microstructure, and environmental adaptations (eg deep-sea vs. shallow-water habitats).

Given the influence of mineralization chemistry on SMP composition at the sequence level, we further assessed differences at the structural domain level across seven lophotrochozoan biomineralization systems. Distinct mineralization types were associated with characteristic domain repertoires (Fig. 6h; Data S10 to S18). For example, ferritin domains were detected in both phosphate-mineralizing systems and magnetite-enriched radulae, consistent with their established role in magnetite biomineralization in A. loochooana (Liu et al. 2022). Additional domains, including PF14670 (coagulation factor Xa inhibitory site), PF07645 (calcium-binding EGF), and PF12662 (complement Clr-like EGF), were identified across phosphate- and iron-based systems, suggesting shared contributions to mineral deposition. Within phosphate-mineralizing systems, a particularly rich repertoire of domains was observed, including PF12661 (human growth factor–like EGF), PF00024 (PAN domain), PF02140 (galactose-binding lectin), PF00681 (plectin repeat), PF12947 (EGF domain), PF00005 (ABC transporter), and PF17902 (SH3 domain), indicating their likely functional significance in phosphate biomineralization. Notably, collagen triple helix repeats and spectrin repeats, previously reported in phosphate-based systems, were also present in iron-rich radulae and scales (Fig. 6h), suggesting that these scaffold-associated domains represent deeply conserved features that can be co-opted for diverse mineral types, including iron-based biomineralization.

Discussion

Despite extensive studies on lophotrochozoan biomineralization, the relative roles of phylogeny and mineral type in shaping SMP composition remain unclear. Here, by comparing SMPs across seven species representing phosphate-, carbonate-, iron-, and mixed-mineral systems, we observed two patterns. First, SMP sequences are highly lineage-specific, showing limited conservation even among closely related taxa, whereas structural domains are broadly conserved, revealing domain-level biomineralization toolkits. Second, pairwise comparisons indicate that SMP composition is more strongly constrained by mineralization chemistry than by phylogenetic distance. These findings provide new insights into the evolutionary mechanisms shaping animal skeletal diversity.

First, we generated a high-quality genome assembly of the Chinese strain of L. anatina (LinAna CN) and performed comparative genomics analysis with other geographically isolated populations. However, the assembly we present is a reference-guided pseudochromosome assembly based on LinAna3.0, which has certain limitations. In particular, its long-range structural accuracy has not been independently validated. The observed genetic distance of 0.0759 between the Chinese and Japanese populations, along with a significantly lower sequence mapping rate, highlights the substantial impact of geographic isolation and historical divergence in shaping genomic differences between these populations. This finding underscores the critical role of local adaptation and population-specific evolutionary pressures in driving genetic diversity. Comparative analyses with other species, such as M. angulata and M. gigas, further indicate that L. anatina populations exhibit an even greater degree of genomic divergence, likely reflecting their unique ecological or evolutionary contexts. In summary, the LinAna genome assembled in this study serves not only as a high-quality reference for this species but also as a crucial genomic tool for studying brachiopod evolutionary biology. The discovery of genomic heterogeneity among populations deepens our understanding of population-specific adaptation processes and lays the groundwork for future comparative studies on the adaptive significance of genomic diversity in response to environmental and geographic pressures. Future research focusing on the functional implications of these genomic divergences will provide deeper insights into the evolutionary history and biological uniqueness of geographically isolated L. anatina populations.

Second, a prominent pattern emerging from our analyses is that, despite high divergence at the primary sequence level, SMPs retain relatively conserved functional domains across seven lophotrochozoan species (Fig. 5b). As the number of taxa compared increases, the proportion of shared SMPs declines sharply, reflecting rapid sequence evolution and extensive lineage-specific innovation. This pattern is consistent with observations in the cephalopod N. pompilius, where 52 of 78 SMPs are lineage-specific, underscoring the independent evolution of SMP repertoires (Zhang et al. 2021). At the domain level, conserved modules are widely maintained. For example, the EF-hand domain is universally present across all examined lophotrochozoan species, highlighting the central role of calcium regulation in biomineralization (Huang et al 2007; Nikapitiya et al 2010). Previous studies indicate that Lingula (phosphate-based) shares an ancestral toolkit—including BMP signaling, chitinase, and peroxidase—with carbonate-mineralizing mollusks (Luo et al. 2015; Lewin et al. 2024). Consistent with this, we found that domains such as the chitin-binding ChtBD2 and tyrosinase are shared between phylogenetically distant phosphate-mineralizing brachiopods and carbonate-mineralizing mollusks (Fig. 5c), supporting prior reports that, although brachiopod and mollusk shells are structurally analogous, their formation relies on a conserved complement of biomineralization proteins (Wernstrm et al 2022). This “deep homology” strongly suggests derivation from a common biomineralizing ancestor. Furthermore, the extensive domain repertoire shared between brachiopods and annelids indicates that, despite rapid turnover in SMP amino acid sequences, key functional domains are retained as independent evolutionary modules. Through mechanisms such as domain shuffling, co-option (Aguilera et al. 2017), gene family expansions, and subsequent sub-functionalization (Aguilera et al 2014), these ancient, functionally defined domains can be recombined to generate novel protein variants adapted to specific biomineralization demands. This conservation of structural domains amid sequence-level divergence likely explains how highly divergent SMPs can still construct functionally analogous mineralized structures, revealing a modular evolutionary mechanism underlying lophotrochozoan biomineralization.

Finally, our analyses reveal that SMP composition correlates more closely with mineralization chemistry than with phylogenetic distance. This cross-lineage similarity across different mineral types strongly suggests potential convergent evolution or shared constraints, driven by the biochemical and physical constraints of mineral deposition, such as ion availability and crystallization dynamics (Fig. 6). This finding aligns with recent studies (Wong et al. 2025), which suggest that the SMP repertoire of C. squamiferum scales may exhibit high evolutionary conservation. The striking similarity observed across distant phylogenetic lineages could be driven by multiple factors. While convergent evolution remains a plausible explanation, this pattern could also be influenced by shared functional constraints among iron-based systems and a potentially limited repertoire of biomineralization-related SMPs in these organisms. Moreover, redundancy or annotation bias in public databases might also contribute to the observed similarity. Therefore, although the results strongly suggest functional conservation or convergent evolution, alternative explanations should not be disregarded. Independent lineages have repeatedly recruited or optimized functionally analogous SMPs (and their domains) to meet these shared mineralization challenges. This pattern is also evident in comparative proteomics of calcitic shells from Cirripedia and bivalves (Yuan et al. 2022). Despite being phylogenetically distant—Cirripedia in arthropoda and bivalves in mollusk—both lineages independently evolved calcium carbonate shells during the Cambrian and show parallel expansions of gene families associated with shell formation. These observations underscore the strong selective influence of mineralization type and the convergent evolution of SMP repertoires in response to similar biomineralization demands (Yuan et al. 2022; Yuan et al 2024).

Our analyses indicate that phosphate-based SMPs in the brachiopod Lingula are more conserved with phosphate-mineralizing annelids than with carbonate-mineralizing brachiopods. This pattern likely reflects fundamental differences between calcium phosphate and calcium carbonate biomineralization in terms of mineral assembly (Zhang et al. 2024; Jin et al 2025), ultrastructure(Olev 2022), mechanical properties (Schmahl et al 2008; Merkel et al 2009), and responses to environmental factors such as pH and ion availability (Gilbert et al. 2022). For example, molluscan calcium carbonate shells typically form extracellularly in the mantle cavity via precipitation of amorphous calcium carbonate (ACC) from seawater, followed by nucleation on a chitinous matrix (Duboisset et al 2022; Gilbert et al. 2022). In contrast, phosphate mineralization in Lingula involves intracellular synthesis of apatite precursors within mantle epithelial vesicles, which are subsequently transported to the site of mineral deposition (Williams et al. 1994). Differences in mantle cell types between carbonate- and phosphate-mineralizing systems further contribute to these distinctions (Mu et al 2023). Together, these divergent physicochemical and cellular contexts likely constrain SMP composition, promoting functional convergence among distantly related taxa facing similar mineralization challenges.

However, in comparative proteomic studies utilizing public datasets, detection limits and batch effects represent critical technical constraints that must be carefully considered when evaluating differences in SMPs. The inherent sensitivity of mass spectrometry may result in the non-detection of low-abundance SMPs. The proteomic data integrated in this study were derived from independent experiments, with variations in sample preparation protocols, instrument platforms, and detection sensitivities. Such technical heterogeneity can lead to the systematic omission of lowly expressed yet functionally important SMPs in certain species. This may subsequently affect the comprehensive identification of a “core SMP toolkit” and the accurate inference of lineage-specific protein loss/gain events during comparative analysis. Besides, SMPs often evolve rapidly and are enriched in low-complexity and repetitive regions, which may reduce the sensitivity of sequence similarity-based approaches such as BLAST for detecting deep homology. When integrating multi-source datasets, batch effects can become a significant confounding variable. Although we have assessed and found their influence to be relatively weak through statistical methods such as Mantel tests, completely eliminating batch effects remains challenging. They continue to be a source of uncertainty in cross-dataset comparisons. Future studies employing prospective, standardized, and cross-species synchronous proteomic experiments will allow for more effective control of these technical variations. This will enable a more precise elucidation of the molecular evolutionary mechanisms driving the diversification of biomineralization strategies within a more homogeneous technical context.

Conclusions

Our analyses reveal that lophotrochozoan SMPs are highly lineage-specific at the sequence level but retain conserved functional domains, forming a modular biomineralization toolkit that can be recombined to generate lineage-specific repertoires while maintaining analogous shell functions. Moreover, SMP composition correlates more strongly with mineralization chemistry than with phylogenetic distance: phosphate-based SMPs in Lingula are more similar to those of phosphate-mineralizing annelids than to carbonate-mineralizing brachiopods, and convergent SMP patterns are observed across iron- and carbonate-mineralizing systems. These results indicate that the evolution of lophotrochozoan biomineralization reflects the combined influence of modular domain conservation and mineral type–driven convergent evolution, providing a framework for understanding the diversity of animal skeletal structures.

Materials and Methods

Sample Collection and Sequencing

Specimens of L. anatina (30 ± 5 mm in length) were collected from the intertidal zone in Rizhao, China. All organisms were transferred to the laboratory and acclimated for 2 weeks in aquaria containing 60 L of seawater (10 °C, salinity 18‰). The animals were fed mixed marine algae (mainly Chrysomonadales) at the concentration of 105 cells/mL (25 mL per individual of L. anatina per day) twice a day. Only intact, similarly sized, and healthy individuals displaying normal activity were selected for to perform the Illumina and PacBio sequencing. The total genomic DNA was extracted by SDS method and followed by chloroform purification, examination of the quantity and quality through Qubit and Agilent bioanalyzer instrument. The qualified genomic DNA was used to construct libraries.

First, to estimate genome complexity, genomic DNA was sheared into 350-bp fragments using a physical fragmentation method. A short-insert sequencing library was then constructed and sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform (Illumina, Inc., San Diego, California, United States), yielding a total of 33 Gb of paired-end 150-bp (PE150) reads. Second, the PacBio library was prepared following the manufacturer's standard protocol (Pacific Biosciences, Menlo Park, California, United States), which included DNA fragmentation with a g-TUBE, DNA repair, ligation of dumbbell adapters, exonuclease digestion, and size selection of target DNA fragments using a BluePippin system. Circular consensus sequencing mode was employed for sequencing, generating three SMART cells of data and 33.3 Gb of HiFi reads with an approximate sequencing depth of 105×.

To facilitate comprehensive annotation of the genome assembly, transcriptome sequencing was performed for L. anatina. Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen). RNA concentration and quality were further assessed using a Qubit fluorometer and agarose gel electrophoresis, respectively. The RNA-seq library was constructed with the VAHTS Universal V8 RNA-seq Library Prep Kit for Illumina (Vazyme, Cat. No. NR605) and subsequently sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform (Illumina, Inc., San Diego, California, United States) in PE150 mode.

Genome Assembly and Assessment

Genome size, repetitive sequence content, and heterozygosity were first estimated using Illumina short-read data. K-mer frequency analysis (k = 27) was conducted with Jellyfish (Marçais and Kingsford 2011) and GenomeScope2.0 (Rhyker Ranallo-Benavidez et al. 2020). Based on the Illumina-derived K-mer profile, the genome size of L. anatina was estimated to be 296 Mb, with a heterozygosity of 1.99%. The primary genome assembly was initially generated from PacBio long-read data using hifiasm v0.16 with default parameters (Cheng et al 2021). Subsequently, a haploid sub-assembly was reconstructed using HaploMerger2 (Huang et al 2017). Chromosome-scale scaffolding was then performed using the L. anatina reference genome (Gerdol et al 2018) with RagTag v2.1.0 (Alonge et al 2022), resulting in a reference-guided pseudochromosome assembly. A Circos plot was generated to visualize the genomic features of the major pseudochromosomes using Circos v0.69-8 (Krzywinski et al 2009). Genome assembly completeness was evaluated using BUSCO v5.1.3 (Simão et al 2015) with the “metazoa_odb10” gene set (Waterhouse et al. 2018).

Genome Annotation

Repetitive sequence annotation was performed via a combined strategy of de novo repeat library prediction and homology-based comparison. First, a de novo repeat library was constructed using RepeatModeler v 2.0.7 with default parameters. Long terminal repeat (LTR) retrotransposons were specifically identified in the genome using LTR_FINDER v1.07 and LTR_retriever v2.9.0, both run with default settings. The de novo repeat library and LTR-specific library were then merged and redundancy-removed to generate a final, non-redundant repeat library. RepeatMasker v 4.2.1 was subsequently employed to annotate repetitive sequences in the L. anatina genome, with parameters set as -frag 100000 -gc 33.37 -lcambig -xsmall -gff; the analysis integrated both the RepBase database and the species-specific de novo library.

Three complementary approaches were used to predict protein-coding genes: homology-based annotation, transcriptome-guided annotation, and ab initio gene prediction. Homologous protein sequences were retrieved from the Swiss-Prot database and the Nr database. For transcriptome-guided annotation, transcriptome reads were aligned to the genome assembly using Hisat2 v2.2.1 with parameters –skip 8 –qc-filter; StringTie2 v1.3.6 was then used to assemble aligned reads into transcript models and generate the training dataset for gene prediction. Ab initio prediction was conducted on the repeat-masked genome assembly using Augustus v3.5.0 with default parameters. Finally, gene structure annotation was refined using BRAKER3, and the completeness of the resulting gene set was evaluated with BUSCO v5.1.3. The functional annotation was performed on the longest protein isoforms using standard bioinformatic tools against seven databases. Specifically, NR annotation was carried out with DIAMOND v2.1.16 blastp (e-value < 1 × 10−5) to obtain functional annotations and species distribution; Swiss-Prot annotation was conducted by BLAST v2.14.0+ against the Swiss-Prot database for standardized functional descriptions; COG/KOG annotation involved DIAMOND blastp searches against the KOG database, followed by functional classification and visualization; eggNOG annotation used DIAMOND v2.1.8 alignment against the eggNOG database, with orthology-based functional refinement via a local database; InterPro annotation provided domain and motif information. We have no dedicated haplotype purging applied. Only conservative filtering based on contig length and coverage was used, minimizing the risk of over-collapsing allelic variation.

Analysis of Population Sample Distribution and Genetic Distance

Samples of the Chinese population (L. anatina CN) analyzed in this study were collected from the coastal waters of Rizhao, Shandong Province, China. The genomic data for the Japanese populations used for comparison (LinAna3.0 and LinAna2.0) were downloaded from the NCBI database; these samples originated from the waters near Amami-Oshima Island and the Ryukyu Islands, Japan. The geographic distribution map of the populations was plotted using the maps and ggplot2packages in R software (v4.3.1), with coastline geographic data sourced from the public Natural Earth dataset. Sampling site coordinates were as follows: L. anatina CN: 119.4°E, 35.3°N and Japanese population site: 28.440583°N, 129.667608°E. To assess the level of genetic differentiation between populations, calculations were based on genome-wide single nucleotide polymorphisms. First, sequencing data from the Japanese population samples were aligned to the L. anatina CN genome and the Japanese reference genome (LinAna3.0), respectively, using BWA-MEM (v0.7.17). Variant detection was performed using the SAM tools/BCF tools pipeline, and the pairwise genetic distance (FST) and average nucleotide divergence (Da) were calculated based on high-quality SNP sites. Furthermore, to intuitively compare genomic similarity between different populations or species, we performed a genome sequence mapping rate analysis. Sequencing reads from the Japanese population were aligned to the L. anatinaCN genome, M. angulata, and M. gigas genomes (as outgroups for comparison) using BWA-MEM. The unique mapping rate was calculated using SAM tools flag stat. The statistical results are presented in a bar chart, plotted using the ggplot2package in R, and the significance of differences between groups was assessed using a t-test (P < 0.05).

Comparative Genomics Analysis

Gene models were refined using AGAT (v1.5.0) and GETA (v2.7.1), followed by orthologous groups identified from protein sequences of all selected species (27 lophotrochozoans and 15 other animals) using OrthoFinder (v2.5.5) with the MMseqs2 (v18-8cc5c) search method and an inflation parameter of 2. Single-copy orthologs were extracted for phylogenomic analysis. Protein alignments were generated with MAFFT (v7.505) using the L-INS-i strategy, converted to codon alignments, and filtered with Gblocks (v0.91b) under relaxed parameters. A maximum-likelihood species tree was inferred from the concatenated alignment using IQ-TREE2 (v2.4.0) under the best-fit model (GTR + F + R9) with 5,000 ultrafast bootstraps. Divergence times were estimated using the MCMCtree program in PAML (v4.9i) with the approximate likelihood method. Five independent runs were performed, and convergence was assessed by comparing branch lengths. Gene family expansion and contraction were analyzed with CAFE (v5.1.0). Orthogroups present in at least three species were used. The global birth-death rate (λ) and error model were estimated from a filtered dataset, which was then applied to analyze the complete set of families.

Crystal Identification and Morphology Observation

Shell samples were collected and sent to the SEM facility at the Institute of Oceanology, Chinese Academy of Sciences, for critical point drying and gold sputtering coating. The surface and vertical surface of the shell were examined by SEM (Hitachi-S-3400N, 5 kV). Energy-dispersive spectroscopy (EDS, IXRF 550i) was performed on selected samples to determine their elemental composition. The Raman spectra were examined at an excitation wavelength of 532 nm three times for 20 s in the range of 0∼4000 cm−1 using a confocal Raman spectrometer (Apha 300R+, WlTec, UIm, Germany). To identify the crystal structure, XRD analysis was employed to characterize the overall crystalline phases. The measurements were conducted using a Bruker D8 Advance X-ray diffractometer with a scanning range of 2θ = 15° to 65°. The raw XRD data collected by the Diffrac Plus Control software were exported using Export File V1.0 and subsequently analyzed with Jade 5 software for phase identification and crystallographic examination.

SMP Extraction

Fifty individual shells of L. anatina were collected and then rinsed in Milli-Q water. Superficial epibionts and periostracum were removed by incubating intact shells in NaClO (1%, vol/vol) for 24 h and then rinsed five times with Milli-Q water. The collected fragments were pulverized (approximately 1 g) after air-drying. The powdered samples were decalcified in cold dilute acetic acid (15%, vol/vol) about 60 h at 4 °C. The solution (final pH around 4.2) was centrifuged at 12,000 × g (30 min) and was rinsed five times with Milli-Q water. Acid-soluble proteins (ASP) from the supernatant were precipitated by adding trichloroacetic acid/acetone (1:10) with 0.07% β-mercaptoethanol at −20 °C for 12 h. After mixing and centrifuging, the same amount of acetone was added for washing the pellet. The pellet containing ASP was resuspended in the reducing buffer (8 M carbamide). On the other hand, the insoluble pellet from the acetic acid solution, which contained acid-insoluble proteins (AIP), was rinsed with Milli-Q water and then resuspended in reducing buffer. ASP and AIP samples were mixed with 5× loading buffer respectively and boiled for 5 min. Extracted proteins were separated on 12.5% SDS-PAGE gel (constant current 14 mA, 90 min), visualized with Coomassie Blue G-250 staining. The protein concentrations were quantified by a BCA Protein Assay Kit (Bio-Rad, United States). Afterward, protein bands were excised and in-gel digested with trypsin. Protein digestion was performed by trypsin. The digest peptides of each sample were desalted on C18 Cartridges (Empore SPE Cartridges C18 [standard density], bed I.D. 7 mm, volume 3 mL, Sigma), concentrated by vacuum centrifugation. The dried peptides were then reconstituted in 0.1% formic acid for LC-MS/MS analysis by a Q-Exactive mass spectrometer coupled to an EASY nLC 1200 system (Thermo Fisher Scientific) for 65 min. The MS raw data for each sample were combined and searched using the MaxQuant 1.6.14 software for identification and quantitation analysis. Trypsin was selected as the digestive enzyme, with a maximum of two missed cleavages allowed. Carbamidomethylation (C) was set as a fixed modification and oxidation (M) as a variable modification. Mass tolerances were set as follows: 20 ppm for the first search, 6 ppm for the main search (precursor ions), and 20 ppm for MS/MS fragment ions. The searches were conducted against the protein sequences of L. anatina models. The false discovery rate (FDR) thresholds for both protein and peptide identification were set at ≤0.01. For quantification, razor and unique peptides were used with a 2-min match-between-runs time window. LFQ was applied with a minimum ratio count of 1. Protein abundance was determined using both intensity-based absolute quantification (iBAQ) and LFQ algorithms: iBAQ intensity represented absolute protein expression levels within samples. The pipeline for protein extraction, identification, and quantitative analysis is provided in Fig. S5.

Identification and Quantitation Analysis of L. anatina Shell Proteins

Identification of proteins from above was performed using blastp searches against the NCBI database to find homolog sequences. Molecular weight (MW, Da) and isoelectric points (pIs) of sequences were computed using the ExPASy ProtParam tool (https://web.expasy.org/protparam/). The secreted proteins were predicted by SignalP-5.0 (https://services.healthtech.dtu.dk/services/SignalP-5.0/). Conserved domains and LCDs were predicted using SMART (http://smart.embl-heidelberg.de/) and InterproScan (Jones et al 2014; Finn et al 2017). The detection and alignment of repeats in protein sequences were performed using PlaToLoCo (https://platoloco.aei.polsl.pl/). All of these programs were run using default settings and thresholds. GO terms were mapped, and sequences were annotated using the software program Blast2GO. GO enrichment was established using TBtools (https://github.com/CJ-Chen/TBtools-II). Following annotation steps, the studied proteins were blasted against the online Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) database (https://www.genome.jp/kegg/) to retrieve their KEGG orthology identifications and were subsequently mapped to pathways in KEGG. Enrichment analysis was applied based on the Fisher’s exact test, considering the whole quantified proteins as the background dataset. Benjamini–Hochberg correction for multiple testing was further applied to adjust derived P-values. Only functional categories and pathways with P-values under a threshold of 0.05 were considered as significant. The PPI information of the studied proteins was retrieved from STRING software (http://string-db.org/) by their gene symbols. The results were downloaded in the XGMML format and imported into Cytoscape software (http://www.cytoscape.org/, version 3.2.1) to visualize and further analyze functional PPI networks. Additionally, the pipeline for quantitative shell proteomics extraction and the workflow of identification and quantitation analysis for the shell proteomic data are comprehensively listed in Figs. S6 and S7, respectively.

Comparative Proteome Analysis

Interspecies protein comparison was performed using blastp v2.13.0 + 79, with homology detection criteria set to a cutoff e-value < 1 × 10−5, percent identity ≥ 25% to 30%, and query coverage ≥ 70% to 80%. The SMP database utilized in the search comprised SMP sequences obtained from six lophotrochozoans, including M. venosa (Jackson et al. 2015), S. chinensis (Ge et al. 2025), C. squamiferum (Wong et al. 2025), N. pompilius (Zhang et al. 2021), A. loochooana (Liu et al. 2022, 2024), and M. gigas (Zhao et al. 2018). This study designed comparative analyses from two distinct perspectives. First, a comparative analysis across different biomineralizing lineages was performed using the following seven species: L. anatina (shell), M. venosa (shell), S. chinensis (shield), C. squamiferum (shell), N. pompilius (shell), A. loochooana (shell), and M. gigas (shell). Second, a separate comparative analysis across different biomineral types was conducted, specifically focusing on fluorapatite in L. anatina (shell), calcite in M. venosa (shell), amorphous iron phosphate in S. chinensis (shield), iron sulfide in C. squamiferum (scales), aragonite in N. pompilius (shell), magnetite in A. loochooana (radula), and a mixture of calcite and aragonite in M. gigas (shell). This includes the newly generated data for L. anatina (calcium phosphate) from this work, as well as data for six additional species (eight proteomes) obtained from public resources (Table S7). The SMPs were searched according to their potential biomineralization functions and the predicted domains in their protein sequences (Marin 2020; Luo et al. 2022). Blastp results indicate interspecies SMP similarity with at least one significant match and were visualized with a Circos plot using Circos v0.69-8 (Krzywinski et al. 2009). Conserved functional domains were predicted using InterProScan, and domains identified against SMART and Pfam databases with e-values < 0.01 were obtained. The occurrence frequency of all domains has been standardized based on the total number of SMPs identified for each species (Data S10 to S18). Additionally, the acquisition and analysis workflows for the comparative proteomics data are comprehensively listed in the Fig. S8. Correlations among protein datasets were quantified using the Mantel test accounting for phylogenetic distance, and effect sizes and P-values were reported (Advanced Cor link was performed using the OmicStudio tools at https://www.omicstudio.cn/tool).
