Efficacy and tolerability of linezolid as an adjunctive treatment for nontuberculous mycobacterial infections in patients with adult-onset immunodeficiency syndrome: a prospective cohort study
Authors: Pakpoom Phoompoung, Natthakit Chavapradit, Suporn Foongladda, Niphon Chirapapaisan, Yupin Suputtamongkol, Nasikarn Angkasekwinai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPatients with nontuberculous mycobacterial (NTM) diseases associated with anti-interferon gamma autoantibodies (anti-IFN-γ auto-Abs) often experience recurrences, despite prolonged treatment, which requires intravenous anti-mycobacterial therapy. Linezolid (LZD) shows a potential as an adjunct treatment, but there is limited data on its long-term efficacy and safety.MethodsWe conducted a prospective study from December 2017 to April 2022, to evaluate the efficacy of a nine-month oral LZD regimen in reducing NTM treatment failure, defined as relapse and the need for intravenous anti-mycobacterial therapy for suspected uncontrolled NTM or death as well as to determine its tolerability.ResultsAmong 40 adults (≥18 years) with NTM diseases and anti-IFN-γ auto-Abs treated with linezolid, 10 (25%) discontinued treatment early due to adverse events (AEs), including one death from suspected uncontrolled diseases at month seven. Of the 30 patients who completed the nine-month course, only five maintained their initial dose. The remaining 25 required dose modification: four temporarily paused treatment due to AEs, nine reduced to a half dose due to AEs, and 12 reduced their dose because of stable disease. By month nine, the cumulative incidence rates of relapse and treatment failure were 22.5 and 15.0%, respectively; consequently, 77.5% and 85.0% of patients remained free from the relapse and treatment failure. Overall, 24 patients (60%) reported a total of 31 AEs, with anemia and peripheral neuropathy being the most frequent.ConclusionOur findings suggest that linezolid could be a valuable adjunct treatment for nontuberculous mycobacteria diseases in patients with anti-interferon gamma autoantibodies. However, close monitoring for adverse events is essential.

Introduction

Adult-onset immunodeficiency (AOID) syndrome is an acquired immunodeficiency caused by anti-interferon gamma autoantibodies (anti-IFN-γ auto-Abs) in non-human immunodeficiency virus infected individuals [1]. It has been recognized as an emerging disease in several Asian countries, including Thailand [2]. Patients with anti-IFN-γ auto-Abs often present with disseminated opportunistic infections (OIs) caused by various intracellular organisms, such as varicella-zoster virus, Salmonella, or Cryptococcus. However, nontuberculous mycobacteria (NTM), particularly Mycobacterium abscessus, are by far the most common and difficult-to-treat pathogen [1,2].

Patients with AOID who develop NTM diseases require extended oral anti-mycobacterial therapy, often necessitating multiple courses of intravenous (IV) rescue anti-mycobacterial treatment due to worsening of symptoms and requiring extensive long-term monitoring [2,3]. A long-term study conducted at Siriraj Hospital on patients with anti-IFN-γ auto-Abs revealed that only one in five patients achieved a sustained clinical remission for nine months, despite undergoing prolonged antimicrobial therapy of a median duration of 27.4 months [3]. Most patients continued to experience active disease, with relapses or reinfections occurring at a median interval of 8.3 months after treatment [3]. This highlights the urgent need for more potent anti-mycobacterial treatments for NTM diseases in patients with anti-IFN-γ auto-Abs.

Linezolid (LZD), an oxazolidinone antibiotic, is approved by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) for treating multi-drug resistant Gram-positive bacterial infections [4]. It also has significant anti-mycobacterial activity against both multidrug-resistant Mycobacterium tuberculosis (TB) and NTM [5,6]. In 2018, the World Health Organization (WHO) reclassified LZD as a Group A medication, recommending it as a first-line agent in treatment regimens for multidrug-resistant/rifampicin-resistant (MDR/RR)-TB [7]. Despite its proven in-vitro efficacy against both slow and rapid-growing NTM, the correlation between laboratory susceptibility testing and clinical performance remains unclear. The FDA has approved a dosage of 1,200 mg daily of LZD for up to 28 days. This limitation is due to the risk of adverse events (AEs) with longer use, such as myelosuppression, peripheral neuropathy, and optic neuropathy. However, for anti-tuberculous treatment, a dose of 600 mg daily has shown similar efficacy to the 1,200 mg daily [8]. To date, only one retrospective study has reported on the use of LZD for treating patients with anti-IFN-γ autoantibodies and NTM diseases [9]. This prospective clinical study aims to evaluate the efficacy and tolerability of a nine-month oral LZD regimen in reducing treatment failure of NTM diseases in patients with anti-IFN-γ auto-Abs.

Methods

We conducted a prospective study from December 1, 2017, to April 30, 2022, to evaluate oral LZD as an adjunctive therapy in adult patients with AOID and NTM disease. Inclusion and exclusion criteria are shown in supplementary appendix 1. All enrolled cases received follow-up care and treatment for nine months. The study protocol was approved by the Siriraj Institutional Review Board (SIRB) (COA No. Si 268/2017) on May 15, 2017 and was registered on ClinicalTrial.gov on July 18, 2017 (NCT03220074). This research has been adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki.

Study procedures

After obtaining written informed consent, demographic data and clinical characteristics of NTM diseases were recorded. A baseline neurological examination, including sensory evaluation, was performed by a clinical investigator. An ophthalmologist conducted a baseline eye examination. Laboratory examination is detailed in the in supplementary appendix 1.

Patients received a daily oral dose of 600 mg of linezolid (LZD), administered either once daily or in two divided doses, as an adjunct to their existing anti-mycobacterial regimen. Patients weighing less than 45 kg received a reduced dose of 300 mg. The plan treatment duration was nine months, a period chosen based on previous studies indicating a median time to relapse of approximately nine months [3]. The LZD dose could be reduced to 300 mg daily in cases of stable disease-defined as the improvement or resolution of NTM-related symptoms/signs (e.g. lymphadenopathy, reactive skin rash, or reactive arthritis) alongside normalized CRP levels. Dose reduction was also permitted for adverse events (AEs) such as cytopenia, peripheral neuropathy, or optic neuropathy. While temporary treatment interruptions (up to 12 weeks) were allowed, permanent discontinuation were mandatory for severe AEs or if dose reintroduction was not feasible. Vitamin B6 or erythropoietin (EPO) were not routinely administered. Patients were evaluated every six weeks for the first six months, then every three months through month nine. Compliance was assessed at each visit via pill counts of unused medication. Beyond nine-month, subsequent therapy was determined by the primary physician.

LZD-related adverse events (AEs) were monitored through clinical and laboratory assessment at baseline and all follow-up visits. Events were graded according to the Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE) version 4.03 (supplementary appendix 2). Termination criteria and comprehensive ophthalmological assessment protocol are detailed in supplementary appendix 2 and 3, respectively.

Anti-IFN-γ auto-Abs concentrations in serum were measured as described (supplementary appendix 4) [3,10]. Mycobacterial cultures and identification followed standard recommended procedures. M. abscessus was not further classified into subgroups. The susceptibility of NTM to LZD was assessed using broth microdilution by Thermo Scientific™ Sensititre™. The following Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) breakpoints were used to define susceptibility to linezolid for both rapid and slow growing mycobacteria: susceptible (MIC ≤ 8 μg/ml), intermediate (MIC =16 μg/ml), and resistant (MIC ≥ 32 μg/ml) [11].

Outcome measurement

The primary efficacy outcome was the cumulative incidence of relapse and treatment failure, over nine months. Relapse was defined as the worsening of existing symptoms or the development of new NTM-related clinical manifestations-such as lymphadenopathy, fever, or skin rash-regardless of whether it was clinical or culture-proven. Treatment failure was defined as a relapse requiring intravenous antimycobacterial therapy for suspected NTM disease or death. The secondary safety outcome was the proportion of patients who experienced adverse event due to linezolid during the nine-month study period. Adverse events (AEs) associated with LZD included, but were not limited to, peripheral neuropathy, optic neuropathy, and myelosuppression. Disseminated disease was defined as the isolation of NTM in normally sterile site, such as blood or bone marrow or lymph node, or in two or more of distinct non-contiguous organs.

Sample size calculation and statistical analysis

Based on our previous study, approximately 20% of NTM diseases in patients with AOID resolved with long-term, non-LZD treatment [3]. Another study reported a 50% response rate with LZD combination therapy [9]. Based on these findings, we hypothesized that adding LZD would increase the proportion of patients with stable disease, who do not require intravenous anti-mycobacterial treatment to 60%. To achieve this, a sample size of 45 participants was needed, accounting for a 5%Type 1 error and a 10% loss-to-follow-up rate. This same sample size was sufficient for evaluating adverse events, as prior studies reported a 60% rate of AEs with 600 mg daily dose of LZD [12,13].

Patient characteristics and safety data were analyzed using descriptive statistics. Categorical data are presented as frequencies or percentages, while continuous data are presented as the mean with standard deviation or the median with interquartile range (IQR). Comparisons of categorical variables between groups were performed using Fisher’s exact test. Statistical significance was defined as a p-value < 0.05. All data were analyzed using the R software version 4.5.3 (R Foundation, http://www.r-project.org).

Results

A total of 40 patients with AOID and NTM diseases were enrolled in this study; 67.5% were female. The median age was 53 years (IQR 47, 59) and the median BMI was 21.37 kg/m2 (IQR 20.63, 25.6). All patients were tested positive for anti-IFN-γ auto-Abs, with the median time to diagnosis of 12 months (IQR 1, 36) and the median concentration of antibodies expressed in optical density (OD) of 3.75 (IQR 2.95–4.15). Seven patients (17.5%) had at least one co-morbidity including two patients with diabetes mellitus, and one patient each with systemic lupus erythematosus, hypertension, nasopharyngeal cancer, nephrotic syndrome, ischemic heart disease, chronic kidney disease, and chronic hepatitis B infection. Overall, patients had received anti-mycobacterial treatment for a median of 13 months (IQR 1, 32) prior to enrollment. Eleven patients (27.5%) were newly diagnosed with AOID and were experiencing their first episode of NTM diseases. At enrollment, 33 patients (82.5%) had active NTM diseases and 29 (72.5%) had recently completed a course of intravenous (IV) anti-mycobacterial drugs. The median interval between the last IV dose and study entry was 1 month (IQR 0.5, 7.5). The administration of parenteral antimicrobials prior to linezolid initiation was not part of the study protocol. While the standard treatment for NTM (specifically RGM) typically involves a combination of parenteral imipenem and amikacin, specific data regarding the exact antimicrobials agents administered before study entry was not recorded.

Baseline demographics and clinical characteristics of the NTM diseases were presented in Table 1. The most common NTM isolated before enrollment were rapid-growing mycobacteria (RGM), identified in 33 patients (82.5%) and M. abscessus being the most frequent (27 patients, 67.5%). During LZD treatment, three patients were also treated for other opportunistic infections: one each for Talaromyces marneffei, herpes simplex virus (HSV), and varicella-zoster virus (VZV). LZD susceptibility testing was performed on 24 NTM isolates (M. abscessus [n = 16], M. fortuitum [n = 2], MAC [n = 3], M. chelonae [n = 1] and M. kansasii [n = 1], NTM spp. [n = 1]). Only seven (29%) of these strains were susceptible to LZD (M. abscessus [n = 2], M. fortuitum [n = 2], and MAC [n = 1], M. kansasii [n = 1], and NTM spp. [n = 1]). The MIC distribution of linezolid ranged from 2 to > 32, µg/mL, as illustrated in Supplementary Figure 1. While M. abscessus was not identified at the subspecies-level, phenotypic antimicrobial susceptibility testing (AST) for clarithromycin was available for 18 isolates; of these, only 55% were susceptible (MIC≤ 2 µg/mL).

LZD treatment and efficacy outcome

Among the 40 patients, 14 (35%) received LZD 600 mg once daily, 23 (57.5%) received 300 mg twice daily, and 3 (7.5%) received 300 mg daily due to a body weight less than 45 kg. Most patients received a combination of two anti-mycobacterial agents, typically a macrolide (clarithromycin or azithromycin) and a fluoroquinolone (levofloxacin, ciprofloxacin, or moxifloxacin). Additional antibiotics included rifampicin (n = 2), clofazimine (n = 2), doxycycline (n = 2), and ethambutol (n = 1).

During the 9-month study period, 10 patients (25%) discontinued LZD prematurely due to adverse events (AEs), including one death from suspected uncontrolled NTM diseases seven months after enrollment. Of the 30 patients completing the full 9 months, five maintained their initial dose, while 25 underwent dose modification: four temporarily paused treatment due to AEs, nine reduced to a half dose due to AEs, and 12 reduced their dose due to stable disease (Figure 1). The median duration of LZD treatment for all patients was 24 weeks (IQR 18, 36).

The cumulative incidence of relapse increased from one patient (2.5%) at 6 weeks to nine patients (22.5%) at 9 months. Among the nine patients who relapsed, two had culture-confirmed M. abscessus infection approximately six months after enrollment, involving the same species as before enrollment. The cumulative incidence of treatment failure increased from 2.5% at week 6 to 15.0% by 9 months. Overall, most patients remained free from the relapse (77.5%) and treatment failure (85.0%) at the 9-month study period (Table 2). Relapse appeared more common among patients who discontinued LZD prematurely (5/10, 50.0%) than among those who continued LZD until nine months (4/30, 13.3%), but this difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.190). Safety outcome

Among the 40 patients, 24 (60.0%) experienced a total of 31 adverse events (AEs) episodes. The cumulative incidence of AEs rose from 15.0% at 6 weeks to 47.5% at 12 weeks, and 50.0% at 18 weeks, peaking at 60.0% by 6 months and remaining stable through 9 months. Five patients (12.5%) experienced multiple AEs: two with anemia and peripheral neuropathy; one with anemia, thrombocytopenia, and peripheral neuropathy; one with pancytopenia; and one with peripheral neuropathy and nausea/vomiting.

As shown in Figure 2, hematological toxicities were the most common (15 events in 11 patients; 27.5%), followed by peripheral neuropathy (11 events in 11 patients; 27.5%). Other AEs includes subclinical optic neuropathy (n = 2), increased intracranial pressure (n = 1), exfoliative dermatitis (n = 1), and nausea/vomiting (n = 1).

The median onset of hematologic toxicity was 12 weeks (IQR: 6, 12 weeks). Most hematological events improved, allowing LZD to be continued via dose reduction or temporarily discontinuation followed by resumption at a half-dose. Peripheral neuropathy had a later median onset of 18 weeks (IQR: 12, 24 weeks). Overall, 10 patients (25%) experiences serious AEs necessitating permanent LZD discontinuation, primarily due to peripheral neuropathy (n = 4), anemia (n = 2), exfoliative dermatitis (n = 1), intracranial hypertension (n = 1), subclinical optic neuropathy (n = 1), and nausea/vomiting (n = 1) (Figure 2).

Discussion

Previous studies have shown that using LZD as an adjunctive therapy for pulmonary M. abscessus disease improves sputum conversion rates [14,15]. Additionally, a retrospective study from Thailand demonstrated that adding LZD 600–1200 mg daily to the treatment of disseminated NTM in AOID patients for a median of six months resulted in a 75% complete or partial response rate [9]. Based on these findings, we hypothesized that adjunctive oral LZD treatment could accelerate NTM pathogen clearance and prevent treatment failure in patients with AOID.

Our study’s results confirmed that RGM is the most common OI, accounting for 82.5% of cases [3,9]. Over the nine-month study period, 77.5% and 85.0% of patients remained free from relapse and treatment failure, which is consistent with the findings of the aforementioned Thai study [8]. However, the primary outcomes of the two studies differed: our study also focused on treatment failure, defined as the relapse with requiring intravenous anti-mycobacterial treatment, while the previous study assessed treatment response based on changes in clinical symptoms and signs. Our study also reported a low incidence of subsequent culture-proven NTM diseases, specifically M. abscessus, in only 5% (2 out of 40). Despite these differences, both studies consistently reported positive outcomes. These results suggest that LZD has the potential to improve clinical outcomes for NTM treatment in patients with AOID. Since a majority of patients (72.5%) received intravenous antibiotics for active disease prior to enrollment, this suggests that linezolid primarily served as maintenance therapy following parenteral treatment. However, its role within this cohort was not limited to post-parenteral maintenance.

Notably, the observed short-term improvement could also be attributed to close patient monitoring and better adherence to oral anti-mycobacterial treatment during the study. Therefore, more clinical studies with a randomized controlled design, a larger sample size, and longer follow-up durations are needed to confirm the efficacy of adjunctive oral LZD in this population. Although in vitro susceptibility testing of NTM isolates from our patients showed only 29% susceptible to LZD, the clinical outcomes did not correlate with these in vitro findings. Furthermore, clinical efficacy data for NTM in support of the current CLSI breakpoints are very scarce [16].

Tolerability is a primary concern with long-term LZD treatment. The rate of adverse events (AEs) in our study was higher than previously reported, with 25% of the patients permanently discontinued LZD due to AEs, compared to 18% discontinuation rate in previous systematic reviews of patients with MDR-TB or XDR-TB who received LZD (300–1200 mg) for 1–36 months [12]. Linezolid exhibits significant inter-individual exposure variability due to several factors. While ethnicity has been considered as a potential contributor to pharmacokinetic differences between Asian and non-Asian population, a systematic review found inconsistent results [17]. The main factors associated with a higher risk of linezolid overexposure are older age, low body weight, and renal dysfunction [18]. Additionally, potential drug-drug interactions with co-administered medications can differ between TB and NTM treatment regimens. Notably, macrolides, a cornerstone of NTM treatment, are potent P-glycoprotein inhibitors and have been reported to increase the area under the curve (AUCs) of linezolid by 44% [19].

Our study found hematological toxicity rate of 27.5%, which is higher than the 19.5% reported in previous systematic review [12] and 2% observed in the Zenix study, where patients received LZD 600 mg daily for 26 weeks [20]. However, direct comparison between studies is challenging due to variation in patient populations and underlying factors contributing to anemia, such as inflammation, which is common in AOID patients. Consistent with several previous studies, most hematologic AEs in our study were manageable through dose reduction or temporarily discontinuation with subsequent resumption at the reduced dose [12,20]. Previous systematic reviews have linked myelosuppression to LZD dosage and high minimum free concentrations (fCmin) above 1.38. This is more common in patients receiving 600 mg once daily compared to 300 mg twice daily (27% vs. 20%, respectively) [21].

Neuropathy is known adverse event of LZD treatment, with previous reviews reporting rates of 24%–34% [12,20]. Our prospective study found it developed later than myelosuppression (median onset of 18 vs. 12 weeks). Although its incidence was similar, peripheral neuropathy led to a higher rate of permanent LZD discontinuation than hematological toxicity (36.4% vs. 18.2%). This highlights the need for close neurological monitoring throughout treatment since neuropathy has been reported as irreversible in some cases, even after LZD withdrawal [22]. The underlying cause of neuropathy may differ from that of myelosuppression, as one study found no link between LZD trough concentration and neurologic toxicity [23,24]. However, a direct comparison between our patient group (NTM diseases in AOID) and those with MDR-TB is limited due to differences in co-administered medications and potential drug-drug interactions [25]. No cases of symptomatic optic neuropathy or lactic acidosis were observed in our study. While research has identified potential biomarkers for LZD-associated neuropathies [26], no neuroprotective strategies are currently available to prevent these adverse events.

A notable limitation of our study is its design as a prospective clinical trial without a placebo or comparator group. Consequently, we were unable to conclusively determine whether linezolid has a direct impact in reducing treatment failure in patients with NTM diseases. Additionally, a separate analysis of RGM versus SGM outcomes was not performed as there were only four SGM cases- a sample size too small for meaningful comparison. While our findings offer valuable insights, further research, particularly randomized controlled trials with placebo or comparator arm are needed to establish the true efficacy of linezolid in this context.

Conclusion

A daily oral dose of 600 mg LZD as an adjunctive treatment for NTM diseases showed potential efficacy in reducing relapse and treatment failure over a nine-month period. However, given the commonality of adverse events, close monitoring and follow-up are necessary.
