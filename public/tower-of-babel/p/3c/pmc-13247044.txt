Interactions across hemispheres in prefrontal cortex reflect global cognitive processing
Authors: Megan E McDonnell, Akash Umakantha, Ryan C Williamson, Matthew A Smith, Byron M Yu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBrain functions involve processing in local networks as well as modulation from brainwide signals, such as arousal. Dissecting the contributions of populations of neurons to these functions requires knowledge of interactions between brain areas. We investigated these interactions using dual hemisphere recordings of prefrontal cortex in monkeys performing a spatial memory task. To tease apart global processing from local interactions, we applied a novel statistical approach called pCCA-FA (a combination of probabilistic canonical correlation analysis and factor analysis) to analyze trial-to-trial variability in neuronal activity. We found substantial shared variability among neurons within each population, much of which was actually shared across populations and linked to an arousal process. Our work presents a path by which we can leverage multi-area recordings to reveal aspects of brain functions that are hidden in single-area recordings.Subject terms: Neural decoding, Cognitive neuroscience

Introduction

Brain functions such as attention1,2, decision making3, and working memory4,5 rely on coordination among neurons within brain areas as well as across brain areas. Thus, the spiking activity of individual neurons can be influenced by neurons from the same brain area, neurons in other brain areas, global modulatory signals, and more6. Teasing apart the impact of each of these influences on neural activity would enable a deeper understanding of how neurons in different brain areas interact to give rise to such brain functions.

To dissect the various influences on neural activity, we can consider how the activity of one neuron covaries with that of other nearby neurons. Many studies have examined how the activity of pairs of neurons in a single brain area covaries from trial-to-trial by computing spike count correlation (rsc)7,8. These pairwise correlations have been widely used as a window to investigate a range of phenomena from cognitive states to circuit architectures9,10. To go beyond pairs of neurons, a related body of work has identified the population-wide covariability structure in single brain areas. These population-level approaches have been used to study the trial-to-trial variability underlying sensory encoding11,12, attention13,14, motor control15–17, learning18–20, decision-making21–23, network structure24–26, and more27. Taken together, these studies inform our understanding of how neural activity is shared among neurons within a brain area.

Given that most brain functions rely on interactions across brain areas, it is likely that some component of the activity of each brain area is shaped by interactions with other brain areas. Developments in neural recording technology have enabled simultaneous recordings of populations of neurons in multiple brain areas28–30, which give rise to the possibility of distinguishing interactions across brain areas from interactions solely within a single brain area31–34. Simultaneous recordings from multiple brain areas have been leveraged to study widespread brain functions such as sensory processing35–40, motor control41–44, learning45–47, decision-making48–50, working memory51,52, arousal53,54, and attention55–60. However, most statistical methods for analyzing populations of neurons are not designed to distinguish activity shared across areas from activity shared solely among neurons within each area.

Here, we seek to identify components of neural activity that involve interactions across two areas (i.e., an across-area component), as well as interactions solely within each area (i.e., a within-area component). We simultaneously recorded spiking activity from each of the two hemispheres (here referred to as two areas) of prefrontal cortex (PFC) in macaque monkeys while they performed a spatial memory task. Utilizing a statistical method we developed called pCCA-FA (a combination of probabilistic canonical correlation analysis and factor analysis), we found substantial shared variance among neurons in PFC, much of which involved neurons across both areas. In fact, the across-area component of PFC activity was larger than the within-area component. We then uncovered a link between the across-area component and a brainwide arousal process. Our work motivates a reinterpretation of shared variance among neurons within a brain area as arising from multiple sources, both within that area as well as via interactions across the brain.

Results

To support perception, cognition, and action, each neuron’s activity can be shared with other neurons in the same brain area, as well as with neurons in other brain areas. Consider the spiking activity of two distinct populations of neurons, recorded simultaneously from two brain areas (area 1 and area 2, Fig. 1a). For each neuron, we would like to identify which aspects of its activity are shared with neurons in the other area (pink arrow) and which aspects are shared exclusively with neurons in the same area (blue arrow). To understand how the activity of neuron 1 in area 1 is shared with other neurons (i.e., to quantify the relationships described by the pink and blue arrows in Fig. 1a), we would like to partition its trial-to-trial spike count variance into an across-area component (termed across-area variance; Fig. 1b, pink), a within-area component (termed within-area variance; Fig. 1b, blue), and a component independent to this neuron (termed independent variance; Fig. 1b, gray).

Understanding neuronal variability using spike count variance.

a Given two populations of neurons, we seek to understand what aspects of each neuron’s activity (e.g., area 1, neuron 1, shaded triangle; box indicates spike count bin) are shared with neurons in the other area (pink arrow) versus only with other neurons in the same area (blue arrow). b By considering how a neuron’s activity covaries with other neurons in the same area and the other area, we can partition its spike count variance into a component that is only shared with neurons in the same area (within-area variance, blue partition), a component that is shared with neurons in the other area (across-area variance, pink partition), and a component that is not shared with any other recorded neuron (independent variance, gray partition).

Mean spike count correlation does not adequately partition shared variance

We simultaneously recorded spiking activity from neurons in both hemispheres of dorsolateral PFC to understand how neural activity was shared either across or within areas (Fig. 2a). We began by characterizing the pairwise interactions among neurons within each area using rsc. The spike counts used to compute rsc were taken from a one-second window during the delay period of a memory-guided saccade task (Fig. 2b). Throughout this work, we combined trials across conditions after removing condition-specific means, and removed slow-timescale trends to avoid spurious correlations61 (see Methods, Supplementary Fig. 1).

The mean rsc among pairs of recorded neurons in a brain area is commonly taken to indicate the strength of shared activity among neurons. We computed rsc for each pair of neurons within each area (Fig. 2c, left and middle, example session), then averaged over the pairs to obtain a within-area mean rsc per session (right PFC within-area mean rsc = 0.029, left PFC within-area mean rsc = 0.033, example session). In the majority of sessions, we found that within-area mean rsc values were positive (Fig. 2d, top and middle).

To understand how activity is shared across areas, one approach is to compute rsc between pairs of neurons across areas (i.e., one neuron in each area). Mean rsc decreases for pairs of neurons as the physical distance between the neurons increases62–64, and has been shown to be smaller for pairs of neurons across versus within brain areas65. We observed an across-area mean rsc that was close to zero (Fig. 2c, right; d, bottom). This might be taken to suggest that neurons within areas interact more strongly than across areas (Fig. 2d; p = 1.0e − 13, pooled across animals and brain areas; one-tailed t-test), and that there are little to no interactions across the hemispheres of PFC.

However, there are several limitations to mean rsc measurements. First, by averaging over all pairs of neurons (either across or within areas), we ignore the breadth of rsc distributions (Fig. 2c). For example, if there are pairs of neurons with strong positive correlations and also pairs with strong negative correlations, the mean rsc might be close to zero even though many neuron pairs exhibit strong correlations. One way to identify neuron pairs with strong positive and negative correlations is to consider the relative tuning preferences of the neurons66–70. Indeed, when we computed mean rsc among subsets of across-area neuron pairs with similar tuning (Fig. 2e, top) and opposite tuning (Fig. 2e, bottom), we found that similarly tuned pairs of neurons exhibited positive rsc (mean rsc = 0.019; p = 1.6e − 10, pooled across animals; one-tailed t-test), whereas oppositely tuned pairs of neurons exhibited negative rsc (mean rsc = − 0.013; p = 1.6e − 7, pooled across animals; one-tailed t-test). Second, the within-area mean rsc does not reveal whether the activity shared among neurons in an area is also shared with other areas. Rather, all co-fluctuations among neurons in a brain area are collapsed into the within-area mean rsc. As such, within-area mean rsc could reflect interactions with other areas and/or global neuromodulation. Third, mean rsc (either across- or within-area) is not designed to identify the many possible population-wide co-fluctuations among neurons. Several distinct changes in population-wide co-fluctuations can result in the same change in mean rsc71,72. By collapsing all interactions in one metric, we are unable to separately characterize the many influences on neural activity. Here we desire to leverage the simultaneity of our dual PFC recordings to dissect the multiple population-wide influences on across- and within-area activity (as in Fig. 1b).

pCCA-FA partitions across- and within-area shared variance

To explicitly partition across- and within-area variance, we can leverage statistical methods that take into account all simultaneously-recorded neurons together. In a single brain area, we can identify population-wide co-fluctuation patterns using factor analysis (FA) or related methods73. While this provides a more detailed view of population-wide covariability than mean rsc72, these methods do not reveal whether activity fluctuations are shared with other brain areas. When recording from two brain areas simultaneously, we can identify variance shared among neurons across areas by employing canonical correlation analysis (CCA) or related methods33. However, neither CCA nor its probabilistic variant, pCCA74, are designed to also identify variance shared only among neurons within each area (see Methods). Given that none of the aforementioned methods are designed to partition variance into both across-area and within-area components, a new approach is needed.

To address this need, we developed a dimensionality reduction method for two simultaneously recorded brain areas called pCCA-FA (a combination of probabilistic canonical correlation analysis and factor analysis), which partitions the variance of each area’s activity into an across-area component, a within-area component, and a component independent to each neuron. pCCA-FA can be applied to any two populations of simultaneously recorded neurons, and here we focus on the case where each population is recorded in a distinct brain area (Fig. 3a, area 1 and area 2). We seek to identify latent variables that capture co-fluctuations among neurons across areas (Fig. 3a, z, pink), as well as latent variables specific to each area that capture co-fluctuations exclusively among neurons within each area (Fig. 3a, area 1: z1, light blue; area 2: z2, dark blue). In total, pCCA-FA identifies three sets of latent variables: one across-area set, and a separate within-area set for each area. Because the within-area latent variables are specific to each area, the number of within-area latent variables for each area need not be the same.

pCCA-FA approximates neural activity as a weighted sum of the across-area latent variables (Fig. 3b, pink) and the within-area latent variables (Fig. 3b, light and dark blue for areas 1 and 2, respectively). The co-fluctuation patterns comprise the weights, which are estimated from the neural activity and fixed across all trials. The values of the latent variables can vary from trial to trial. Each co-fluctuation pattern can be represented as a vector in a population activity space, where each coordinate axis represents the activity of one neuron (Fig. 3c). Whereas the across-area co-fluctuation patterns are paired across areas (Fig. 3c, pink), the within-area co-fluctuation patterns are separate for each area (i.e., not paired across areas) (Fig. 3c, light and dark blue for areas 1 and 2, respectively).

For each neuron, we can quantify the proportion of its spike count variance explained by each set of latent variables. pCCA-FA partitions each neuron’s variance into a component that is described by the across-area latent variables (Fig. 3d, pink) and a component that is described by that area’s within-area latent variables (Fig. 3d, light and dark blue for areas 1 and 2, respectively). The residual variance not accounted for by across- or within-area latent variables is independent to each neuron (Fig. 3d, gray). Thus, pCCA-FA explicitly partitions the spike count variance of each neuron into across-area, within-area, and independent components (as in Fig. 1b).

To summarize the outputs of pCCA-FA, we define the following two metrics. First, to understand the extent to which a particular set of latent variables explains a neuron’s spike count variance, we define the across- and within-area percentage of variance shared among neurons (%sv). The values of this metric reflect the relative sizes of the bars in Fig. 3d. Across-area %sv is defined as the proportion of spike count variance explained by the across-area latent variables, averaged across neurons in each brain area (see Eq. (8)). Within-area %sv is defined analogously using the within-area latent variables (see Eq. (9)). Thus, each brain area has both an across-area %sv and a within-area %sv. Second, we quantify the complexity of the interactions described by each set of latent variables by computing its shared dimensionality (dshared)25,75. A larger dshared would indicate more complex interactions among neurons, whereas a smaller dshared would indicate less complex interactions among neurons. Across- and within-area dshared are defined as the number of latent variables required to explain the across- or within-area variance, respectively (see Methods).

Before applying pCCA-FA to neural recordings, we performed extensive model validation. We verified that pCCA-FA accurately identified the ground truth %sv and dshared using simulated data with properties chosen to be similar to our neural recordings (Supplementary Figs. 2–3). We also found that pCCA-FA required fewer trials to recover the ground truth %sv and dshared than pCCA (Fig. 3e, f) because pCCA-FA has fewer parameters than pCCA (Supplementary Fig. 4). Further, pCCA-FA outperformed other dimensionality reduction techniques (pCCA and FA) on neural recordings (Supplementary Fig. 4). Thus, the pCCA-FA framework successfully captures how neurons in two brain areas covary by partitioning variance into across- and within-area components.

Across-area interactions were substantial and often greater than within-area interactions

To identify across- and within-area variance in our PFC recordings, we fit pCCA-FA to the same neural activity (with the same preprocessing) as in the rsc analyses (Fig. 2). For each session, pCCA-FA estimated a single set of across-area latent variables common to both areas and a separate set of within-area latent variables for each area. From the mean rsc values in Fig. 2, one might expect to uncover a large within-area component and small across-area component using pCCA-FA.

To determine if this was the case, we computed across- and within-area %sv and dshared. For the same example session as in Fig. 2c, we found that a substantial portion of variance was shared across areas (14.97% and 17.47%, for right and left PFC, respectively), compared to the variance shared exclusively within each area (5.65% and 7.47%, for right and left PFC, respectively) (Fig. 4a). This trend was evident across sessions, with the across-area %sv being larger than the within-area %sv (Fig. 4b; p = 2.6e − 7, pooled across animals and brain areas; paired one-tailed t-test; see Supplementary Fig. 5 for a shuffle control). Thus, substantial across-area interactions were present in the PFC activity and identified by pCCA-FA (see also Supplementary Fig. 1e for the across-area correlation values computed using pCCA-FA), contrary to the intuition from measuring pairwise across-area rsc. This discrepancy can be understood by considering the sign of pairwise correlations among neurons (Fig. 2e, see Math Note A of ref. 72). Mean rsc averages over positive and negative correlations found in individual pairs of neurons, whereas %sv accounts for both positive and negative correlations among neurons. Note that the values of across- and within-area %sv (like mean rsc) can depend on neuronal properties, including their physical distance and tuning preferences.

Having found that neurons tended to more strongly covary across areas than within each area, we next considered the complexity of these interactions. We found that the across-area dshared was also larger than the within-area dshared (Fig. 4c; p = 3.1e − 6, pooled across animals and brain areas; paired one-tailed t-test). This indicated that, in addition to being stronger, the interactions we identified across areas were more complex than the interactions within each area. We further note that these findings are not a consequence of statistical bias in measuring across- versus within-area %sv and dshared (Supplementary Fig. 2).

Across- and within-area components involved different co-fluctuation patterns

We next sought to understand the population-wide co-fluctuation patterns represented by each set of latent variables, as they provide insight into the nature of the co-fluctuations among neurons (Fig. 5a). There are many possible population-wide co-fluctuations. Perhaps the simplest example is all neurons sharing the same-signed loading onto a latent variable, such that the population would all increase their activity together or decrease their activity together. We examined whether this was the case in our PFC recordings. For this analysis, we focused on the first co-fluctuation pattern of each ordered set, as it reflected a large proportion of the across- or within-area variance (Supplementary Fig. 6). We found that all neurons increasing their activity together or decreasing their activity together was not consistent with the across- or within-area co-fluctuation patterns we identified (Fig. 5b, Supplementary Fig. 7).

We next asked whether across- and within-area co-fluctuation patterns were similar to each other by computing the angle between them in population activity space (θestimated; Fig. 5a). They might be similar if two different processes (one global and one local) had a similar effect among the neurons. Alternatively, each process might engage distinct circuit mechanisms, yielding different co-fluctuation patterns. In an example session, we found that the across- and within-area co-fluctuation patterns appeared visually distinct, and produced a θestimated of 72° (Fig. 5b). In all sessions, we found that the across- and within-area co-fluctuation patterns had large θestimated (Fig. 5c), often close to 90°, which would indicate distinct co-fluctuation patterns.

To further assess the extent to which the measured θestimated reflected true differences in the across- and within-area co-fluctuation patterns, we created a simulation in which we defined the ground truth angle between across- and within-area co-fluctuation patterns (θsim, see Methods, Supplementary Fig. 3). We simulated activity in each area with a specified value of θsim that was otherwise matched to the neural recordings in terms of the number of neurons, latent variables, and trials (see Methods). We found that, for any given θsim, the resulting distributions of θestimated (Fig. 5d, top) were narrower than what we observed in the neural recordings (Fig. 5c). Instead, if we sampled a different value of θsim for each simulated dataset uniformly from 45° to 90°, we recovered a distribution of θestimated (Fig. 5d, bottom) that was similar to that observed in the neural recordings (p = 0.40, pooled across animals and brain areas; paired two-tailed t-test). We confirmed that the observed variation in θestimated from session to session was not related to the number of neurons or trials recorded (Supplementary Fig. 8). Furthermore, we determined that the variation in θestimated did not arise solely from sampling different subsets of neurons from session to session, although it still likely contributed (Supplementary Fig. 9). Together, these results indicate that across- and within-area components involved different co-fluctuation patterns that varied in their similarity from session to session.

Across-area latent variables were related to pupil diameter

Given that we found substantial across-area variance (Fig. 4) involving different co-fluctuation patterns than the within-area component (Fig. 5), we considered whether the across-area variance might be related to a brainwide process. Arousal is known to modulate activity of neurons throughout the brain53,54,76–78. In addition, arousal state has been linked to changes in both the tonic diameter of the pupil79,80 and event-related changes in pupil diameter81,82. Changes in pupil diameter have also been linked to arousal-related changes in neural activity in both cortical83–86 and subcortical79,87–89 regions. Given that arousal has been found to be a brainwide phenomenon, we wondered if it was related to the across-area variance we observed. Recall that the across-area component is defined as variance that is shared with one or more neurons in the other area (regardless of whether that variance is also shared with neurons in the same area). We therefore asked how well the across- and within-area latent variables identified by pCCA-FA explained variance in the pupil diameter (Fig. 6a).

To address this question, we measured the trial-by-trial pupil diameter during the delay period and adjusted the pupil diameter values to remove slow fluctuations in an analogous manner to the neural activity, yielding one pupil diameter measure per trial (see Methods, Supplementary Fig. 10a, b). We used the across- or within-area latent variables identified by pCCA-FA (i.e., either d across-area latent variables or dm within-area latent variables for area m; see Methods) to compute a trial-by-trial regression of the pupil diameter for each set of latent variables (Fig. 6b). Across sessions, we found that the across-area latent variables explained substantially more variance in the pupil diameter (mean r2 = 0.25) than the within-area latent variables (right PFC, mean r2 = 0.038; left PFC, mean r2 = 0.030) (Fig. 6c; p = 1.3e − 30, pooled across animals and brain areas; one-tailed t-test). Furthermore, we found that the across-area latent variables explained variance in the event-related changes in pupil diameter observed just after target onset (Supplementary Fig. 10; p = 1.1e − 5, pooled across animals and brain areas; one-tailed t-test). Thus, the across-area latent variables may be related to multiple aspects of pupil-linked arousal. Taken together, these results are consistent with a link between the coordinated activity across brain areas and global cognitive processes such as arousal.

Discussion

In this work, we asked to what extent neural activity is shared across versus within hemispheres of PFC by considering how the spike count variance of the two brain areas covaried from trial-to-trial. Applying a novel statistical approach called pCCA-FA, we found that of the substantial shared trial-to-trial variability among PFC neurons, a large proportion involved neurons in both areas. The component of neural activity shared among neurons in both areas (across-area variance) was larger in magnitude (%sv) and complexity (dshared) than the component shared only among neurons in one area (within-area variance), in contrast to what was suggested by mean rsc analyses. Additionally, we found that the across-area component was related to pupil diameter, a common readout of arousal state. Our approach thus leveraged multi-area recordings in PFC to uncover signatures of global cognitive processing that were otherwise hidden in pairwise and single-area analyses.

Across-area variance may arise from a variety of circuit motifs. First, it may come from circuits that provide common inputs to both hemispheres of PFC. One such pathway involves the encoding of brainwide cognitive states, such as arousal. Arousal state modulation is thought to propagate through brainwide signaling that originates in the locus coeruleus90,91. The locus coeruleus projects to both hemispheres of PFC92,93 and influences pupil diameter94, a common readout of arousal state. Correlations between neural activity and pupil diameter have been observed across the brain36,43,54,95, supporting the notion that the across-area variance we observed in PFC may be in part due to common inputs originating in the locus coeruleus. Common input to both hemispheres of PFC may also arise through circuit motifs involved in sensory processing and motor control. Each hemisphere of PFC represents both visual fields17, and common motor plans have been shown to impact neural activity across the brain36,41,43. Second, variance shared across hemispheres may arise from direct anatomical projections96, which have been shown to be spatially patterned97. Interactions across the hemispheres are central to various brain functions such as working memory51,52, motor control41,42, sensory perception40, and spatial attention56,57.

Although we emphasized the variance shared across areas, we also identified a substantial amount of variance that was shared only among the neurons within each area. We labeled this variance as within-area, but we note that it could be shared with other non-recorded brain areas. The within-area variance we observed may originate through a variety of circuit motifs. First, representations of visual fields in PFC are topographically arranged with a contralateral bias, resulting in each hemisphere of PFC encoding different, although partially overlapping, information98–100. Second, internal states like spatial attention tie neurons together with nearby receptive fields, especially those occupying the same hemisphere of visual space. For example, previous studies of visual area V4 during spatial attention tasks have identified attention-related components of neural activity that are hemisphere-specific56,57. This type of selective modulation may account for some of the within-area variance we report here. Third, within-area variance may result from constraints on patterns of neural activity imposed by the cortical circuitry (e.g., horizontal connections) in each hemisphere101,102. In network models, spatial clustering architecture that may underlie local circuits within a hemisphere can have a profound influence on trial-to-trial variability24–26,103,104.

Underlying the across- and within-area circuit motifs are various cellular and circuit mechanisms, which have been implicated in the shaping of correlations among neurons both across and within brain areas. First, the structure of inhibition is known to shape correlations among neurons, where the influence of inhibition depends on its spatial scale26,105 and timing relative to excitation106–109. Second, changes at the level of the synapse, including synaptic depression110 and connection strength111, can also regulate correlated variability. Third, neuronal correlations are influenced by neuromodulators such as acetylcholine112–114 and norepinephrine91, which together have wide-ranging modulatory effects including altering the strength of thalamocortical inputs115 and regulating global brain state116. Finally, the strength of excitatory synaptic connectivity has been linked to the strength of correlation117. Anatomically, long-range horizontal connections within a brain area118–120, across brain areas97, and across the corpus callosum96, are all weaker than local recurrent connectivity. However, the strength of the detected correlations need not be directly linked to the strength of the anatomical connection between hemispheres or among distant neurons121. For example, weak neuromodulatory inputs could drive strong correlations among neurons by influencing the much stronger local circuit mechanisms that exist in local circuitry. Our analyses using pCCA-FA, like any other assessment based solely on spiking activity, do not explicitly distinguish between these cellular and circuit mechanisms. Instead, we focused on the spiking variability yielded by these mechanisms and dissected the components of spiking variability that are shared across versus within brain areas.

With the identification of within-area variance, pCCA-FA allowed us to isolate components of neural activity that were not shared between the two hemispheres of PFC. Previous work has described a gating of signals across areas via a communication subspace122. pCCA-FA identified what would be termed a communication subspace between the two hemispheres of PFC: we found distinct across- and within-area co-fluctuation patterns, indicating that not all signals were shared between areas. In other words, the existence of the within-area latent variables in our PFC recordings indicated that a communication subspace exists between the hemispheres.

pCCA-FA improves upon CCA and its probabilistic variant (pCCA) in identifying across-area interactions. First, CCA and pCCA are designed only to identify across-area variance and do not partition the remaining variance into a component that is shared among neurons within each area from variance that is independent to each neuron. Thus, pCCA-FA provides a more thorough dissection of the population activity in two brain areas. Second, even in cases where only across-area variance is of interest, pCCA-FA produces a more accurate estimate of across-area variance than pCCA (or CCA), especially in the limited data regime (Fig. 3e, f). This is due to the pCCA-FA model having a smaller number of parameters than pCCA (see Eqs. (2) and (4), and Supplementary Fig. 4). The tradeoff is the increased computation time of pCCA-FA compared with pCCA for jointly identifying the across- and within-area dimensionalities in pCCA-FA compared to identifying only the across-area dimensionality in pCCA (or CCA). This can be partially mitigated by employing efficient hyperparameter optimization techniques123,124. For these reasons, we advocate for the use of pCCA-FA in the place of pCCA or CCA for studying interactions between brain areas.

pCCA-FA fills an unmet need in the set of exploratory tools tailored for studying the trial-to-trial variability of neural activity. When studying neurons from a single brain area, FA is among the simplest approaches for separating variance that is shared among neurons from variance that is independent to each neuron. For two brain areas, pCCA-FA is among the simplest methods for separating variance that is shared across areas from variance that is only shared among neurons within each area. To incorporate the concept of time, one can consider using Delayed Latents Across Groups (DLAG)124 or related methods32,125–130 to separate activity time courses that are shared across areas from those that are shared only among neurons within each area. pCCA-FA can be extended in the following ways. First, one may seek to identify latent variables that are explicitly related to task stimuli or the animal’s behavior by using a targeted dimensionality reduction approach95,131–134 or by applying pCCA-FA to raw spike counts. Second, one may seek to identify non-linear interactions between brain areas135–138. As multi-area population recordings continue to increase in prevalence28–30, pCCA-FA and related methods will become increasingly essential for dissecting the rich structure of across- and within-area interactions.

Methods

Animal model

Three male rhesus macaque monkeys (Macaca mulatta) were used in this study. One animal was sourced from Tulane National Primate Research Center (Monkey Wa) and two animals were from Covance (Monkey Pe and Monkey Sa). They were 10 (Monkey Pe), 7 (Monkey Wa), and 7 (Monkey Sa) years old at the time of data collection. All experimental procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committees of Carnegie Mellon University and the University of Pittsburgh, and were in accordance with the United States National Research Council’s Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals.

Surgical preparation

Each animal was surgically implanted with a titanium headpost, which was fixed onto the skull using titanium screws. This was necessary to limit head movement such that the position of the eyes could be tracked and neural activity recorded. Animals were then each implanted with two 96-electrode “Utah” arrays (Blackrock Neurotech, Salt Lake City, UT). Electrode arrays were placed on the prearcuate gyrus just anterior to the arcuate sulcus, one in each hemisphere. Surgeries were performed in sterile conditions under general anesthesia using isoflurane.

Electrophysiological methods

Animals were positioned 36 cm from a 21” cathode ray tube monitor with 1024 × 768-pixel resolution and a refresh rate of 100 Hz. Task stimuli were generated using custom-designed software in Matlab (Mathworks, Natick, MA) that utilized the Psychophysics toolbox extensions139–141. Signals from the implanted electrodes were band-pass filtered (0.3–7500 Hz) and then digitized at 30,000 Hz. For each electrode, spiking waveforms were defined as a 52-sample (1.73 ms) window of the filtered voltage signal triggered by the signal crossing a predefined threshold, and stored for offline analysis. The threshold was defined as a multiple of the root-mean-square voltage of a brief epoch of the raw signal on each electrode collected at the beginning of each session. All behavioral data and neural activity were recorded using a Grapevine recording system (Ripple, Salt Lake City, UT) for further offline processing and analysis.

Behavioral task

Animals were trained to perform a standard memory-guided saccade task142. At the beginning of each trial, a 0.5 degree diameter blue fixation circle appeared at the center of a gray screen. The animal initiated fixation within an invisible window (Monkey Pe: 2.4, Monkey Wa: 1.8, Monkey Sa: 2.9 degrees radius) centered on the fixation circle and then 200 ms later a white target flashed in the animal’s periphery (Monkey Pe: 11.8 or 17.5 degrees from fixation depending on the session, Monkey Wa: 11.6 degrees from fixation, Monkey Sa: a single value in the range of 8.0–15.6 degrees from fixation depending on the session, with one session containing multiple randomly interleaved distances in this range) at one of 4, 8, or 16 locations depending on the session. The white target remained on the screen for either 100, 200, or 400 ms (depending on the session) after which it was removed from the screen. The animal maintained fixation for a delay period drawn randomly from a uniform distribution between 1.5 and 3 seconds. After the delay, the fixation circle disappeared, which served as the go cue for the animal to initiate a saccade toward the remembered location where the white target flash had appeared. The animal had 500 ms (Monkey Sa: 400 ms) to initiate a saccade, and an additional 200 ms to reach the target location. Successful completion of a saccade was defined by the animal maintaining fixation within an invisible window (Monkey Pe: 4.1, Monkey Wa: 5.9 or 7.0, Monkey Sa: 4.1 degrees radius) centered at the target location for at least 100 ms. A liquid reward was delivered for a saccade to the correct location. For a subset of sessions, a dim white circle was flashed at the target location after saccade initiation to assist the animal in target acquisition. Trials were pseudo-randomized in blocks during which the animal was required to correctly complete all target directions before beginning a new block. In total, we collected 42 sessions of data (16 in Monkey Pe, 10 in Monkey Wa, and 16 in Monkey Sa). For each session, we considered only trials in which the animal successfully acquired the saccade target (i.e., correct trials). The number of correct trials per session for Monkey Pe was 729.8 ± 173.4 (mean ± standard deviation), for Monkey Wa was 463.5 ± 170.1, and for Monkey Sa was 850.1 ± 284.2.

Preprocessing of neural activity

We used a previously trained and published neural network to classify waveforms as spikes or noise143. Briefly, the network was trained on human-sorted waveforms to distinguish between waveforms putatively of neural origin and waveforms not of neural origin. Using this network, we removed threshold crossings that were unlikely to be of neural origin, and refer to the remaining waveforms as spikes. The spikes from each electrode on the Utah array were treated as multi-unit activity, and no further differentiation of single units was performed. Thus, each electrode was treated as one neural unit. Using multi-unit activity tends to yield higher correlation values than single unit activity8, and this applies equally to the across-area and within-area measurements.

We removed electrodes that had very low firing rates or very high trial-to-trial variability. To do this, we first binned neural activity by counting spikes during the target presentation and delay period between target onset and saccade initiation cue. We then removed electrodes with mean spike count lower than 2 spikes/second or Fano factor greater than 10 during this time window. This served to eliminate electrodes contaminated by recording artifacts, or units that were not present for the entirety of a recording session. We also removed electrodes affected by artifactual cross-talk due to electrical coupling. For each pair of electrodes, we flagged spikes as coincident if they occurred within 100 μs of each other. If either electrode in the pair had 20% or more of its spikes flagged as coincident, we flagged that pair as having artifactual crosstalk. This procedure provided an index for each electrode of how many other electrodes it was coincident with. We then iteratively removed the electrode coincident with the highest number of other electrodes until no crosstalk remained, which ensured that the fewest possible number of electrodes were removed. After this process the number of remaining electrodes in Monkey Pe was 78.3 ± 7.8 and 79.5 ± 8.5 (mean ± standard deviation), in Monkey Wa was 85.3 ± 8.1 and 25.2 ± 4.4, and in Monkey Sa was 62.6 ± 9.8 and 75.3 ± 13.2 for right and left PFC respectively.

After the above preprocessing steps, the spiking activity was binned into spike counts. The bin was defined as the one second of the delay period immediately preceding the earliest possible saccade initiation cue in the session, such that each trial contributed one bin. For example, if the earliest possible saccade initiation cue in a given session was 1.5 s after the target disappeared, the bin would be defined as 0.5–1.5 s after the target disappeared for every trial in that session. This one-second window aggregated the interactions across and within brain areas observed during the delay period on the trial-to-trial timescale.

Computing signal correlation

In Fig. 2e, we investigated if the distribution of across-area rsc, despite having a mean near zero, contained pairs of neurons with positive rsc and other pairs of neurons with negative rsc, in a way that was systematically related to the neurons’ task tuning. To do this, we identified each neuron’s tuning across all of the saccade target locations. We determined whether each pair of neurons had similar or opposite tuning by computing their signal correlation. This was defined as the Pearson correlation between the two neurons’ raw trial-averaged responses to each of the possible targets (4, 8, or 16 depending on the session) during the one-second window described above. To assess significance for each neuron pair, we generated a chance distribution of correlation values by using a permutation test (shuffling the target location labels). We then labeled a neuron pair as having significant signal correlation (positive for similarly tuned and negative for oppositely tuned pairs) if their signal correlation was more extreme than the 99th percentile of the chance distribution. Neuron pairs not meeting this criterion were excluded from this analysis.

Combining trials across conditions

To increase the number of observations for model fitting, we combined trials across conditions (saccade target direction). To focus our analyses on trial-to-trial variability, for each neuron, we first subtracted the condition mean from the binned spike counts in each condition and then combined residual spike counts across conditions. Because the spike count variance can differ across conditions, the residual spike counts can still contain a small amount of condition-specific signal. An alternative approach is to z-score the spike counts for each neuron and condition before combining across conditions63. We repeated the key analyses related to the identification of shared variance among neurons in Figs. 2 and 4 using condition-specific z-scoring and found qualitatively similar results. Note, the computation of signal correlation (above) was performed before the subtraction of condition-specific means.

Separation of slow and fast components

In this work, we were interested in trial-to-trial variability of neural activity on a timescale of seconds. As we investigated interactions between left and right PFC, it soon became apparent that both areas also contained a component that varied slowly on a timescale of minutes to hours. While slow-timescale variability presents its own set of scientific questions (e.g., ref. 54), these fluctuations complicated our analyses by introducing dependencies between one trial and the next, violating the basic independence assumption of correlation analysis (including regression, Pearson correlation, pCCA, and pCCA-FA). Therefore, we removed the slow component from the neural activity (see Supplementary Fig. 1), and focused all analyses in this work on faster-timescale trial-to-trial variability, except where noted in Supplementary Fig. 1 and for computing signal correlation. We computed the slow component for each session using a centered boxcar filter of length 25 trials, computed for each remaining electrode after the preprocessing described above. We then subtracted this component from the residual spike counts to remove slow-timescale trends that could have induced spurious correlations61. We performed this preprocessing procedure independently for each neuron. All analyses in this study were performed on the residual faster-timescale component, except where noted.

Preprocessing of pupil diameter

Eye position and pupil diameter were monitored using monocular infrared tracking at a 1000 Hz sample rate (EyeLink 1000, RS Research, Mississauga, Canada). On each trial, we measured the pupil diameter during the delay period, from target offset until the saccade initiation cue. Each trial was smoothed using a median filter with a 50 ms window (i.e., the median value in a 50 ms centered window was subtracted from each sample) to remove high frequency noise.

For the analyses in Fig. 6, we computed mean smoothed pupil diameter (arbitrary units) over the full time window described above to obtain the most robust estimate of the pupil diameter on each trial. This yielded one average pupil diameter value per trial. Like the preprocessing of neural activity, we removed slow-timescale fluctuations in pupil diameter using a centered boxcar filter of length 25 trials. This yielded what we termed the trial-by-trial pupil diameter (Supplementary Fig. 10a, b).

To ensure pupil data quality, we computed the variance across trials of the trial-by-trial pupil diameter. We then removed outlier sessions that exceeded a variance threshold of two times the interquartile range of the variance values across sessions. This was performed for each animal individually. This procedure excluded one session from Monkey Wa from analyses of the pupil (Fig. 6, Supplementary Fig. 10), resulting in n = 9 sessions for analysis. To ensure pupil diameter was on a similar scale across sessions, we z-scored the trial-by-trial pupil diameter for each session by subtracting the mean and dividing by the standard deviation across trials in the session.

Probabilistic canonical correlation analysis (pCCA)

A commonly used approach for identifying interactions between brain areas is canonical correlation analysis (CCA)33. Briefly, CCA attempts to find a co-fluctuation pattern in area 1 and a co-fluctuation pattern in area 2, such that neural activity along these co-fluctuation patterns is maximally correlated across areas. Further co-fluctuation patterns can be found subject to the constraint that they are uncorrelated with previous co-fluctuation patterns. The correlations along these co-fluctuation patterns are known as canonical correlations (ρ). We developed a model called pCCA-FA (a combination of probabilistic canonical correlation analysis and factor analysis (FA144)), which explicitly partitions covariance of neural activity into an across-area component, a within-area component, and a component independent to each neuron. The covariance of neural activity in area 2 is decomposed into analogous across-area (W2W2T), within-area (L2L2T), and independent neuron (Ψ2) components. In the pCCA-FA model, variance shared with neurons in the other area is assigned to the across-area component (appearing in the pCCA-FA model as Wm for each area m, and corresponding to latent variable z, which involves both areas), regardless of whether that variance is also shared with neurons in the same area. Consequently, variance is assigned to the within-area component only when it is not shared with any neuron in the other area (appearing in the pCCA-FA model as Lm, and corresponding to latent variable zm, which only involves area m).

pCCA-FA can be viewed as a combination of two existing latent variable methods, namely pCCA, which finds co-fluctuation patterns that maximize correlation between two populations, and FA, which maximizes covariance between neurons within a single population. Importantly, pCCA-FA explicitly identifies both types of covariance in a single probabilistic framework. The key difference between pCCA-FA and pCCA is that pCCA-FA constrains the full covariance matrices identified by pCCA (e.g., Ψ1 in Eq. (2)) as low-rank plus diagonal (e.g., L1L1T+Ψ1 in Eq. (4), see Supplementary Fig. 4). It follows that pCCA-FA has fewer parameters than pCCA, and is thus more robust in the regime of low trial counts (i.e., able to recover ground truth with fewer trials; Fig. 3e, f). With sufficient trials, pCCA-FA and pCCA identify the same across-area co-fluctuation patterns.

Measuring %sv and dshared

dshared

We used the metric, dshared, to measure the complexity of across- and within-area interactions. For a given pCCA-FA model, there are four instances of dshared, an across-area and a within-area instance, for each of the two areas. Note that dshared measures the dimensionality of interactions shared among neurons and excludes activity related to each neuron’s independent variance. We measured dshared in an analogous way to ref. 25, but adapted for across- and within-area dimensionalities.

Briefly, the first step in measuring across-area dshared is to fit the loading matrices Wm with d * across-area latent variables, where d * is the number of latent variables that maximizes the cross-validated data likelihood. Across-area dshared is defined for each area m as the minimum number of latent variables needed to explain at least 95% of the across-area shared covariance in WmWmT, where dshared ≤ d *. A 95% threshold was used in order to focus on latent variables that explain a large amount of shared variance. Note that although there is a single set of d * across-area latent variables, it is possible to obtain a different value of across-area dshared for each area. We analogously defined within-area dshared for each area using the procedure described above by replacing WmWmT with LmLmT.

Simulated data generation

To validate the pCCA-FA model (Fig. 3e, f), as well as generate chance distributions (Fig. 5d), we generated simulated data xm for each area m from the pCCA-FA model according to Eq. (4). This involved defining ground truth model parameters μm, Wm, Lm, and Ψm for each area m.

For model validation in Fig. 3e, f, we defined these parameters in the following way. We generated random loading matrices Wm and Lm for each area m using independent draws from a standard normal distribution. We scaled the %sv in each loading matrix by adjusting the singular values of the across- and within-area loading matrices (Wm and Lm) relative to each other to achieve the desired ratios (≈ 15% across-area and ≈ 10% within-area). The independent variance values (i.e., entries of Ψm) were then scaled to achieve a level of 75%. Each simulated dataset had 30 neurons per area, 5 across-area latent variables (d = 5), and 3 within-area latent variables per area (dm = 3 for each area m). For each trial count configuration, we generated 30 sets of ground truth parameters and a single simulated dataset per set of parameters.

For Fig. 5d, we defined the ground truth model parameters to be copies of those that were estimated from each of the neural recordings, with one manipulation, described below. For the chance distributions in Fig. 5d and the simulated data in Supplementary Fig. 3, we specified the angle between across- and within-area co-fluctuation patterns (θsim). To achieve this, we held the across-area loading matrix (Wm) constant and set the first column of the within-area loading matrix (Lm) to be a vector that was θsim degrees apart from the first column of Wm. The remaining columns of the within-area loading matrix were randomly generated from a standard normal distribution, and then orthogonalized relative to the first column (i.e., the first column remained fixed and the remaining columns were set to be mutually orthogonal to it).

Fitting pCCA-FA and pCCA to simulated data

To fit pCCA-FA to simulated data for the analysis in Fig. 3e, f, we used the EM algorithm described above. Across- and within-area dimensionalities were estimated in a cross-validated manner. Candidate dimensionalities for across- and within-area ranged from 1 to 10 (inclusive), for both across- and within-area latent variables. After the across- and within-area dimensionalities were selected, a final pCCA-FA model was fit with the specified dimensionalities using all simulated trials. The parameters of this final model were used to compute %sv and dshared.

To fit pCCA, we used an analogous EM algorithm, presented in ref. 74. We fit pCCA to the same sets of simulated data as pCCA-FA. The across-area dimensionality was estimated in a cross-validated manner. Candidate dimensionalities for the across-area latent variables ranged from 1 to 10 (inclusive). After the across-area dimensionality was selected, a final pCCA model was fit with the specified dimensionality using all simulated trials. The parameters of this final model were used to compute across-area %sv and dshared.

Pupil diameter regression

To explain pupil-related variance using the latent variables estimated by pCCA-FA, the across-area latent variables Ez∣x1,x2 and the within-area latent variables Ez1∣x1 and Ez2∣x2 were estimated using Eq. (7). We regressed either the across-area or the within-area (right or left PFC) latent variables onto the trial-by-trial pupil diameter using a linear regression model for each set of latent variables. We then assessed r2, the proportion of variance in the trial-by-trial pupil diameter explained by the regression model.

To compute chance distributions, we repeated the procedure above, except that we performed the regression using latent variables estimated from the neural activity from a different session than the one in which we measured pupil diameter (i.e., the latent variables from session i were used to explain variance in the pupil diameter from session j, where i ≠ j). Trials were truncated in the session with more trials to ensure equal trial numbers. Chance distributions contained 240 samples for Monkeys Pe and Sa with 16 sessions, and 72 samples for Monkey Wa with 9 sessions. We report the 95% confidence intervals of this chance distribution (Fig. 6c, gray bars, termed shuffle). An alternative method to generate the chance distribution involves performing a within-session shuffle, in which the latent variables from session i are used to explain variance in the pupil diameter from session i but with permuted trial labels. This procedure yielded a similar, but slightly more lenient, chance distribution.

To control for differences in the number of across- and within-area latent variables, we repeated the entire procedure using only one predictor (i.e., the top across- or within-area latent variable) rather than the full set of predictors (i.e., d across- or dm within-area latent variables). This yielded similar results (p = 4.3e − 22; pooled across animals and brain areas, n = 82 brain areas, 2 per session; one-tailed t-test on the differences in r2 for each session).

Statistical tests and reproducibility

Data preprocessing was performed using custom code in MATLAB (Mathworks, Natick, MA). Criteria for inclusion of units are described in Methods section: Preprocessing of neural data. All analyses were performed in Python (version 3.8.18). Statistical tests (t-test) were performed using the SciPy (version 1.10.0, https://scipy.org) statistics module. Sample sizes and p-values are directly reported in the corresponding figure caption. Averaged data shown in figures are averaged across sessions (n = 42) or brain areas (n = 84, 2 per session), noted in each caption. For data shown in histograms, means are marked with triangles. Detailed descriptions of plotted data are provided in each caption. Code to reproduce the figures and statistics is publicly available on GitHub at https://github.com/meganmcd13/figures-dual-pfc.
