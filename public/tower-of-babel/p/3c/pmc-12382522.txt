Serial cardiac magnetic resonance imaging to assess myocardial inflammation in PET-positive cardiac sarcoidosis under immunomodulatory therapy: a retrospective observational study
Authors: Julia Mareen Treiber, Jan Sebastian Wolter, Sören J Backhaus, Erika Platt Siefert, Andreas Rieth, Steffen D Kriechbaum, Philipp Klemm, Ingo Tarner, Ulf Mueller-Ladner, Samuel Sossalla, Andreas Rolf
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectiveTo evaluate changes in cardiac magnetic resonance (CMR) tissue characteristics in patients with active cardiac sarcoidosis (CS) confirmed by positron emission tomography (PET)-CT undergoing immunomodulatory therapy (IMT), and to explore their potential use for inflammation monitoring.DesignRetrospective observational cohort study.SettingTertiary care referral centre in Germany.ParticipantsFrom a cohort of 47 patients with CS, 24 patients with PET-confirmed active myocardial inflammation and complete baseline and follow-up CMR imaging after ≥6 months of IMT were included.Primary and secondary endpointsPrimary outcome: Changes in CMR-derived tissue characteristics (T1, T2 mapping, late gadolinium enhancement (LGE) mass). Secondary outcomes: Changes in functional (ejection fraction (EF) and global longitudinal strain (GLS)) and morphological parameters (end-diastolic/systolic volume indices (EDVi/ESVi)).ResultsPatients with PET-confirmed active CS show increased global T1 and T2 compared with healthy volunteers. Over the course of IMT, significant reductions in global T2 (median (IQR): 39 (38–41) ms vs 37 (36–39) ms; p=0.002), LGE-region T2 (43 (40–46) ms vs 41 (38–42) ms; p=0.003), and relative LGE mass (23% (17–38) vs 15% (8–32); p=0.006) were observed. No significant differences were found in EF (p=0.78), GLS (p=0.49), EDVi (p=0.56), ESVi (p=0.28) or native T1 values (p=0.23).ConclusionIn patients with PET-confirmed active CS undergoing IMT, serial CMR demonstrated measurable changes in T2 mapping and LGE parameters, suggesting a potential role for CMR tissue characterisation in monitoring myocardial inflammation. However, due to the observational design and absence of a control group, causal treatment effects cannot be confirmed. Further prospective studies are needed to validate the utility of CMR for treatment monitoring in CS.Keywords: Magnetic resonance imaging, Cardiovascular imaging, Inflammation, CARDIOLOGY

Introduction

Cardiac sarcoidosis (CS) is a potentially life-threatening, often unrecognised inflammatory heart disease that can lead to ventricular arrhythmia (VA), heart failure (HF) and death.1 Standard treatment is based on immunomodulatory therapy (IMT) targeting inflammation in order to avoid or at least minimise irreversible myocardial damage and arrhythmia.2 Therefore, it is crucial to make an early diagnosis, which includes both the extent of myocardial damage and, importantly, the activity of myocardial inflammation.

Cardiac magnetic resonance (CMR) is an excellent and safe non-invasive method to evaluate both of these parameters. It also overcomes the sampling error of endomyocardial biopsy, which is especially high in CS.3 4 Beyond the typical intense, almost mass-like late gadolinium enhancement (LGE), T1 and T2 mapping allow for quantitative assessment of chronic remodelling and oedema5,7 and are known to detect early stages of CS before echo- and electrocardiography are conspicuous.4 8 Elevated T2 in particular seems to be best suited to detect active inflammation,9,15 and it has been demonstrated that T2 mapping can be utilised for disease monitoring and therapy assessment in various inflammatory conditions.16 17 Nevertheless, the detection of active disease and potentially reversible myocardial damage only by CMR can be challenging. Therefore, diagnosis is usually based on a combination of CMR and 18-fluorodeoxyglucose (18FDG) positron-emission tomography (PET)-CT, which is an integral part of the diagnostic algorithm recommended by the Heart Rhythm Society (HRS) and the American Heart Association.2 18

The reliability of monitoring inflammation and tissue characteristics by CMR in CS patients receiving IMT has not yet been established. This study aimed to describe changes in myocardial tissue characteristics by serial CMR in patients with PET-positive CS undergoing IMT and to explore their potential role in assessing disease activity over time.

Methods

Study population

Between January 2017 and August 2023, we identified all patients with CMR findings consistent with CS who also had positive myocardial 18FDG uptake at baseline PET-CT2 19 and whose IMT was initiated at our institution. Although the absence of LGE does not fully exclude CS, only examinations showing LGE patterns consistent with CS in conjunction with supportive clinical features were included in the analysis. The follow-up CMR examinations were also conducted at our CMR unit. All patients were discussed in an interdisciplinary board to adjudicate the diagnosis and make the treatment decision. In accordance with current treatment recommendations,2 patients with active inflammation received IMT based on glucocorticoids, starting with an initial daily dosage of 60 mg over 7 days, followed by a weekly dose reduction until a maintenance dose of 7.5 mg was reached. In cases of therapy-refractory VA or highly symptomatic HF, methotrexate (MTX) 15 mg weekly or azathioprine 2 mg per kg body weight was added. IMT was accompanied by standard HF and device therapy, if indicated.

Follow-up CMR and clinical assessment were performed after at least 6 months or if a patient’s clinical status worsened during therapy. In certain cases, an 18FDG-PET-CT scan was performed to determine the current inflammatory activity.

Data were collected in the BioCVI registry, which was approved by the ethics committee of the University of Giessen.

CMR acquisitions

All CMR scan protocols followed the Society of Cardiovascular Magnetic Resonance recommendations.20 21 All examinations were performed on a 3 Tesla scanner (Skyra, Siemens Healthineers, Erlangen, Germany) in the head-first supine position with an 18-array coil.

CINE imaging

Retrograde ECG-gated steady-state free precession (SSFP) cine images were used routinely for functional analysis. Typical scan parameters were echo time (TE) 1.38 ms, repetition time (TR) 3.15 ms, flip angle 50°, bandwidth 962 Hz/px, field of view (FOV) 380 mm, voxel size 1.8 mm × 1.8 mm, slice thickness 8 mm, interslice gap 2 mm and temporal resolution 30 ms. The myocardium was covered from base to apex with 11–15 short-axis and with 3 standardised long-axis (2-, 3- and 4-chamber view) stacks. For volumetric measurements, trabecularisation was excluded. 2D left ventricle (LV) strain was assessed by feature tracking in all long-axis scans for global longitudinal strain (GLS) and in additional short-axis scans for global circumferential strain (GCS). Contour motion and strain curves were checked for plausibility.

Native T1 and T2 mapping

T1 and T2 maps were generated in 3 short-axis scans (basal, midventricular and apical). To standardise the acquisition of the 3 mapping slices, a 5-into-3 planning approach was applied. Native T1 mapping was performed by modified look locker sequences (MOLLI 3 (2)3 (2)5) with the following typical scan parameters: TE 1.14 ms, TR 3.1 ms, bandwidth 108 Hz/px, FOV 350 mm, voxel size 1.4 mm × 1.4 mm × 8.0 mm, slice thickness 8 mm, adiabatic inversion pulse, 11 inversion times and ECG-gated antegrade SSFP single-shot readout with 50° flip angle.

T2 maps were generated using ECG-gated antegrade T2 prep SSFP sequences (TE 1.34 ms, TR 4.2 ms, flip angle 12°, voxel size 1.8 mm × 1.8 mm, slice thickness 8 mm and T2 prep with 0, 30 and 55 ms).

Native global T1 and T2 times were determined in the midventricular septum. A region of interest at least 2 voxels wide was placed in the centre of the septum as recommended by the ConSept method22 in an automatically generated parametric T1 and T2 map, and mean values were calculated. To avoid partial volume effects of the blood pool, the accuracy of motion correction of the native magnitude images was carefully checked. For this method, the following institutional normal values were established in a cohort of 64 healthy volunteers: native T1=1121 ± 28 ms and native T2=37 ± 2 ms.

T1 and T2 values within areas of LGE were assessed using ROI analysis, based on a previously published approach for regional myocardial tissue characterisation in CS and myocarditis.23 For follow-up, regional measurements within the same LGE-positive region were used.

Late gadolinium enhancement

For LGE inversion recovery-segmented gradient echo sequences were acquired 10–15 min after intravenous injection of gadolinium-dota (Dotarem, Guerbet, Villepinte, France; 0.15 mmol/kg bodyweight). The 9–11 short-axis and 3 long-axis scans were located in the same position as in cine imaging. Typical scan parameters were TE 1.97 ms, TR 3.5 ms, flip angle 20°, bandwidth 289 Hz/px, FOV 370 mm, voxel size 1.3 mm × 1.3 mm × 8.0 mm, and slice thickness 8 mm. For quantitative assessment, a deviation of 5 SD from a reference region was defined as pathological, and LGE mass and relative LGE mass (rLGEmass) were calculated.

Post-processing

Image analysis was performed on a commercially available workstation (CVI42, Calgary, Canada) by two experienced examiners (ARo 17 years and JT 7 years). The following parameters were measured at both baseline and follow-up:

Functional parameters: LV and right ventricular (RV) ejection fraction (EF), GLS, GCS

All tissue characteristics were compared with our cohort of 64 healthy volunteers.

18FDG-PET-CT

After a fasting period of 18 hours, 18FDG was applied as a radioactive tracer for differentiation between normal and inflammatory myocardial tissue using the PET-CT technique (Geminin TF 64, Philips Healthcare, Eindhoven, Netherlands). The last meal before the fasting period was low in carbohydrates. 15 minutes before 18FDG application, the patient received 50 IU/kg bodyweight heparin to suppress the tracer uptake in normal myocardium. Focal 18FDG uptake matching LGE in CMR is an indication of CS.

Statistics

Normally distributed metric parameters are presented as mean±SD and in cases of non-normal distribution, as median and IQR. Categorical variables are presented as absolute frequencies and percentages. Normality of the data was determined by the Shapiro-Wilk test. Groups were compared using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test for non-normally distributed dependent data. A p-value < 0.05 was considered to be statistically significant. All tests were computed using Stata (Stata Corp, College Station, Texas, USA).

Patient and public involvement

There was no direct involvement of patients or the public in the conduct of this study.

Results

Following HRS recommendations and subsequent adjudication by an interdisciplinary board, 44 patients with active myocardial inflammation confirmed by 18FDG uptake in the myocardium were identified and defined as the baseline cohort for tissue characterisation at first diagnosis. 3 patients met the diagnostic criteria for CS but showed no evidence of inflammatory activity on PET and were therefore not included in the analysis of therapy-associated changes. 24 of the baseline cohort underwent follow-up CMR and were classified as the follow-up cohort. 3 follow-up scans had to be excluded due to poor image quality after device implantation; thus, 21 patients remained for full examination (see figure 1). The median time to follow-up CMR scan was 260 days.

In the follow-up group, all patients received IMT either by glucocorticoids, initially 60 mg/day (96%) or by additional MTX (15 mg/week)/azathioprine (2 mg/kg body weight) (58%). A pacemaker or cardioverter-defibrillator (ICD) was implanted in 9 patients (37.5%) of the follow-up group and in 21 patients of the baseline cohort (48%).

9 patients in the follow-up cohort had available baseline and follow-up 18FDG-PET-CT data. We descriptively compared metabolic activity and tissue characteristics. In the whole CS cohort with 47 patients (44 PET-positive and 3 PET-negative), 5 patients died (11%) and 10 had malignant events (1 survived due to sudden cardiac death (SCD) (2%), 5 had appropriate ICD therapy (11%) and 4 experienced VA (9%)) during an observation period of 3 years. In the follow-up cohort, 2 patients died; 1 had SCD and 1 patient had malignant arrhythmia after discontinuing long-term immunosuppressive therapy. The majority of patients showed significant clinical improvement in New York Heart Association (NYHA) functional class. At follow-up, only 3 patients remained in NYHA class III. While tissue characteristics indicative of myocardial inflammation improved in all patients, those with persistent NYHA III symptoms had advanced HF (EF <45%) at baseline, possibly explaining the limited clinical response despite imaging improvement.

Functional and morphological parameters: Reduced EF (<50%) at baseline was recorded in 16 patients (67%). Among the 24 patients who underwent a follow-up scan, no significant improvement in EDVI, ESVI, EF or strain parameters was observed (table 3).

Discussion

The present results suggest that CMR may contribute to the evaluation of treatment effects of IMT in patients with CS, showing trends that may be in line with those observed using PET-CT. The main findings of the present study are:

Global T2 and T2 in LGE regions, as well as the LGE amount, show a statistically significant but small decrease following IMT.

Global T2 and T2 in LGE regions, as well as the LGE amount, show a statistically significant but small decrease following IMT.

At baseline, therapy-naive PET-positive CS patients showed modest but statistically significantly elevated T1 and T2 compared with healthy volunteers.

At baseline, therapy-naive PET-positive CS patients showed modest but statistically significantly elevated T1 and T2 compared with healthy volunteers.

CS patients undergoing therapy do not show significant changes in functional and morphological parameters.

CS patients undergoing therapy do not show significant changes in functional and morphological parameters.

Imaging of inflammation is a key method to assess CS disease activity and the effect of therapy. CS treatment includes IMT with high-dose glucocorticoids that have the potential for major side effects.19 25 Therefore, identifying those patients who will benefit most from IMT is pivotal. T1 and T2 mapping by CMR are established methods of tissue characterisation and the evaluation of myocardial fibrosis and water content.7 26 27 They can detect early stages of cardiac involvement in CS and other inflammatory diseases and add relevant prognostic information.828,33 T2 mapping provides information on the amount of myocardial oedema and inflammation as confirmed by endomyocardial biopsy and seems to be superior to traditional T2-weighted sequences.12 Therefore, T2 mapping is widely used as a non-invasive biomarker for inflammation in myocarditis or other inflammatory heart diseases.8,1327 30 34

Our findings of elevated native T1 and T2 mapping parameters in patients with active CS are consistent with previously published data. Puntmann et al found higher T1 and T2 times in 53 biopsy-proven CS patients, irrespective of age, symptoms or time since diagnosis.8 Greulich et al validated these findings even in the absence of LGE and in patients with preserved EF.28 In a hybrid CMR/FDG-PET-CT approach, they observed increased mapping values despite the absence of LGE in 4 PET-positive patients.35 Despite this evidence, PET-CT is still included in guideline recommendations to confirm or rule out a CS diagnosis.2 35 To comply with this recommendation, we only included those patients who were PET-positive in our analysis.

However, there are no clear recommendations for an optimal assessment of treatment response in CS, and there is uncertainty regarding CMR and PET-CT parameters in terms of the optimal examination procedure and follow-up period. To the best of our knowledge, there is just one small case series of 8 patients with CS reported by Crouser et al who observed the resolution of an abnormal T2 signal in response to IMT.36 Further data addressing more than 1 tissue characteristic are lacking. We observed a reduction in both global T2 and focal T2 in LGE-positive regions in the majority of examinations following IMT. Although we cannot determine causality due to the lack of a control group without IMT, the observed T2 changes, given the known sensitivity of T2 mapping to myocardial oedema, may be indicative of a therapeutic response and resolution of myocardial inflammation. Although our data cannot prove treatment success, the present findings suggest changes in myocardial tissue characteristics consistent with a reduction in inflammatory activity. The utility of tracking inflammation via CMR has been confirmed in other inflammatory heart conditions.16 33 37 In a cohort of 46 patients with acute myocarditis, Spieker et al showed that elevated T2 and the amount of myocardium with elevated T2 times were significant prognostic factors for unfavourable outcome, and in follow-up examinations T2 decreased with clinical recovery.33 Nevertheless, there are only a limited number of studies that address the response to IMT. Usman et al found elevated T2 in patients with biopsy-proven acute allograft rejection and normalisation in follow-up scans after successful treatment.37 In systemic sclerosis, Panopoulus et al showed a decrease in T2 ratio, T2 mapping values and LGE in follow-up CMR after IMT.16 These findings are in good agreement with the outcome of our analysis. Recently, we found that regional ECV within LGE-positive areas can differentiate CS from active and chronic myocarditis of other aetiologies.23 In agreement with our previous findings, we also detected a reduction in focal ECV in response to therapy that possibly reflects decreasing inflammation even in areas with focal LGE.

Although global native T1 and ECV remained unaffected during follow-up, we noticed a decrease in the amount of LGE. The quantification of LGE is of paramount significance since areas with LGE serve as the main trigger of VA, which represents a significant prognostic factor, especially in CS.28 38 Recent findings by Athwal et al highlight that not only the presence but also specific patterns of LGE are strongly associated with adverse clinical outcomes, including VA and HF events, independent of EF or overall LGE burden.39 Traditionally, LGE has been interpreted as an indication of irreversible replacement fibrosis. Our results suggest that LGE in CS can be partially attributed to active inflammation and oedema and therefore might also be potentially reversible, similar to the oedema in acute myocardial infarction. This observation is supported by the findings of Panopoulus et al in SSc patients, who also found a decrease in LGE but no changes in T1.16 Interestingly, there was no improvement in functional parameters like GLS or EF.

It is of particular importance to differentiate whether clinical deterioration in patients with CS is due to progression of HF or an increase in myocardial inflammation. Our findings indicate that changes in global and regional T2 may reflect alterations in myocardial inflammation following IMT in CS. However, these observations remain hypothesis-generating, and further studies are needed to confirm their suitability as monitoring tools. Regional T2 may help reflect the focal nature of CS, as reductions in T2 appeared more pronounced in areas of LGE. A concurrent decline in ECV in these regions could suggest that LGE may, in part, be influenced by reversible oedematous extracellular volume expansion. Although only a limited number of follow-up PET-CT examinations were available to assess activity status, we observed good agreement with CMR findings. In order to minimise unnecessary radiation exposure and reduce costs, follow-up assessments using CMR may represent a reliable option to describe both the inflammatory status and chronic remodelling processes. Although our results are insufficient to justify clinical decision-making based on isolated tissue characteristics, serial CMR examinations may still support the former. Nevertheless, optimal patient evaluation should always be guided by a multimodal approach that integrates clinical, imaging and laboratory findings.

Limitations

There are some principal limitations to our study due to the observational nature of our investigations. One limitation is the lack of a quantitative reference method for imaging inflammation. Our findings are only suggestive of a response to therapy and a decrease in inflammation. To better assess potential causality, a comparison group without PET-confirmed inflammation would have been valuable. However, given the rarity of CS, conducting a prospective controlled study remains a major challenge. In addition, we exclusively included LGE-positive patients. This may limit the representativeness of the study population, as there are CS patients without LGE who primarily exhibit active myocardial inflammation. Further, we did not abide by a strict follow-up schedule, so patients received IMT of varying duration until follow-up CMR. We did not observe a relevant number of patients with relapse during the 1-year observation, so we cannot estimate T2 values that are significant for disease relapse. Although septal T1 and T2 mapping are well-established and reproducible techniques, no standardised approach currently exists for quantifying tissue characteristics within LGE-positive regions. As a result, longitudinal assessment of regional changes is challenging and may not reliably reflect true myocardial alterations. Due to considerable overlap with healthy values, global T1 and T2 may be limited in detecting characteristic focal inflammation in CS. Regional parameters more often exceeded cut-offs, and over 50% of patients showed >2 ms T2 reduction under IMT, supporting the value of serial measurements for individual decision-making. However, given the small absolute changes in T2, random variation within the range of measurement variability cannot be ruled out.

A further limitation is the low number of follow-up examinations. CS patients frequently need device therapy that complicates follow-up examinations by CMR. These patients are principally at higher risk for worsening HF and therefore of particular interest. Additionally, our data do not allow for conclusions regarding the optimal therapy.

Conclusion

Imaging of myocardial inflammation and tissue characterisation by CMR, particularly using T2 mapping and LGE quantification, may provide insights into treatment-related changes in CS. However, given the observational nature of this study, these findings should be considered hypothesis-generating and require confirmation in prospective controlled trials. CMR tissue characteristics may support therapy assessment but should not be used as standalone decision triggers—a multimodal clinical approach remains essential.
