Chronic expanding hematomas arising over a decade post primary total hip arthroplasty
Authors: Chuanlong Wu, Hongyi Wang, Teng Yu, Qiyuan Bao, Junxiang Wen, Jiong Zhang, Zhihong Liu, Jianmin Feng, Weibin Zhang, Chuan He
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundChronic expanding hematoma (CEH) is an infrequent yet serious complication following total hip arthroplasty (THA). Mismanagement of this condition can result in severe consequences. The purpose of this study is to conduct a retrospective analysis of patients who developed CEH after THA at our institution over the past 20 years, complemented by a review of the existing literature. This comprehensive approach aims to contribute valuable clinical insights into the diagnosis and management of CEH.MethodsIn this study, we conducted a retrospective study of patients who had undergone THA within the past two decades and subsequently developed CEH during their follow-up period at our institution. The data collected encompassed fundamental patient demographics, including age, gender, and specifics regarding the primary THA implants. Additionally, we gathered preoperative, postoperative, and follow-up imaging studies. Following the data compilation, a thorough literature review was performed to aggregate and analyze the published cases of CEH occurring post-THA.ResultsOur follow-up data identified five patients who developed CEH more than ten years after undergoing THA. In one case, CEH manifested subsequent to a traumatic event, while the remaining four cases were non-traumatic. The outcomes following revision surgery were heterogeneous: two patients showed no indications of recurrence throughout a least follow-up period of over one year, one patient eventually underwent amputation, and two patients developed periprosthetic joint infection (PJI), an ongoing management challenge. Our literature review revealed nine previously reported cases with similar clinical features. A summary is as follows: Etiology: While often linked to trauma or surgery, a definitive cause is not always present. It is hypothesized that inadequate hemostasis during the initial procedure may contribute to the development of CEH. Time Course: The condition typically evolves slowly over an extended period of years. Mechanism: The underlying mechanism remains unclear. Diagnosis: MRI: T2-weighted sequences exhibits a combination of hypointense and hyperintense signals reflecting a blend of fresh and old blood, indicative of recurrent hemorrhage. Isointense or slightly high signals on T1-weighted sequences. Histological Features: Characterized by three distinct aspects: a peripheral wall densely encapsulated in fibrous tissue; fresh and mobile blood clots; and a central zone of loose connective tissue formation. Differential Diagnosis: Includes inflammatory pseudotumors, hemophilia, and malignant neoplasms, among others. Treatment: The gold standard of care involves complete surgical resection, inclusive of the capsule. Given CEH's propensity for progressive bone resorption, it is recommended that once identified, patients undergo regular follow-ups every 2–3 months. Should significantly bone loss be detected, surgical excision of the mass should be contemplated.ConclusionCEH is a rare complication that can arise following THA, typically exhibiting a slow progression over a period of years. Given the potential for devastating consequences if mishandled, vigilant follow-up care is essential to ensure timely identification and immediate treatment of CEH upon detection.The translational potential of this articleThis study enhances diagnostic accuracy, optimizes treatment, improves prognosis, reduces medical costs, and promotes the update of guidelines for the rare disease CEH after THA.

Introduction

Total hip arthroplasty (THA) surgery, a landmark achievement in orthopedic surgery during the 20th century, has emerged as the cornerstone treatment for severe hip joint disorders [1]. Forecasts indicate that by 2030, the annual volume of total hip arthroplasty in the United States is projected to surge to 850,000 procedures, marking a consistent upward trajectory that mirrors the broader global trend [2]. Chronic expanding hematoma (CEH) is an uncommon complication following THA, representing a rare occurrence in the spectrum of joint replacement surgery. CEHs have been documented to occur in various parts of the body, with a predilection for the chest [[3], [4], [5]], muscles of the arms and legs [6,7], and subcutaneous tissues [8], often in the absence of any preceding surgical intervention. Typically, these hematomas manifest after trauma and then progress gradually over time. The low prevalence and scarcity of published reports have historically limited the awareness of CEH among orthopedic surgeons. In 2011, Goddard et al. [9] documented the first case of CEH following total hip arthroplasty. Distinguishing these cases from inflammatory pseudotumors often seen after THA, the patients presented with three quintessential histological features: a peripheral wall encircled by a dense fibrous tissue; fresh and mobile blood clots/blood; and a central zone characterized by the formation of loose connective tissue [10].

Over recent years, while there have been occasional case reports of CEH following THA, the rarity of these publications and a tendency to focus on successful cases—since scholars typically prioritize reporting positive outcomes—has meant that the gravity of CEH has not garnered the attention it deserves. The purpose of this study is to conduct a retrospective analysis of patients with CEH post-THA who were treated at our institution in recent years, and to synthesize all similar cases reported in the literature. This comprehensive approach aims to contribute additional clinical insights into the diagnosis and management of CEH.

Literature search and selection

Following the data collection, a thorough literature review was performed to aggregate and analyze the published cases of CEH occurring post-THA. A systematic literature review was conducted using the PubMed and Embase databases on Jan 1, 2025. Several combinations of terms and expressions were evaluated, including both MeSH and free text terms. By analyzing the articles retrieved from each combination, we selected the following final search expression: (“Total hip arthroplasty” OR “Total hip replacement”, “Chronic expanding hematoma” OR “CEH” OR “hematoma”). Additionally, we manually searched the reference lists of the identified articles. We selected articles, written in the English language, in which scientific detail and reporting were sufficient to enable our understanding of the material. Finally, 8 studies including 9 CEH cases after THA were included in our study.

Case 1

A 63-year-old woman underwent left THA due to femoral head necrosis, utilizing a ceramic-on-ceramic bearing in the procedure. Her postoperative recovery was uncomplicated and uneventful. A decade post-THA, she presented with unexplained left hip pain that intensified with ambulation (Table 1). Comprehensive laboratory tests, including inflammatory markers and coagulation profiles (Table 2), were within normal limits, and bacterial cultures of the synovial fluid were sterile. Imaging studies, including radiographs and computed tomography (CT), disclosed peri-prosthetical osteolysis encircling the lesser and greater trochanter (Fig. 1a–c). MRI depicted a isointense or slightlyhyperintense signals on T1-weighted sequences and a nontypical combination of hypointense and hyperintense signals on T2-weighted sequences (Fig. 1d–f). Following consultations with pathologists, radiologists, and orthopedic oncologists, CEH was diagnosed, prompting the patient to have a complete excision of the mass, inclusive of the capsule. The mass, responsible for the left hip swelling, was a conglomerate of liquid and solid elements. Post-aspiration of sanguineous fluid from the mass, it deflated and was resected along with the enveloping capsular tissue (Fig. 2a–d). Upon intraoperative examination, it was confirmed that the ceramic components were free from fractures and that the femoral prostheses were firmly secured. The patient then underwent a comprehensive revision of the acetabular component, while the femoral stem was preserved. A Delta ceramic liner accompanied by a metal sleeve and a ceramic femoral head was employed for the procedure (Fig. 2f). Histopathological examination of the specimen revealed fibrin and blood cells, aligning with the diagnosis of a hematoma (Fig. 2e). Subsequent postoperative surveillance confirmed the absence of hematoma the recurrence (Fig. 2g). Case 2

A 50-year-old female who had previously received a right THA for post-traumatic femoral head necrosis, employing a metal-on-polyethylene articulation, presented with unexplained right hip pain 21 years post-THA, which increased with ambulation and was accompanied by progressive swelling in the right hip region(Table 1). Radiographic findings indicated osteolysis adjacent to the lesser trochanter and acetabular side (Fig. 3a). CT scans exposed a sizable peripheral mass encircling the hip prosthesis (Fig. 3b). MRI demonstrated a isointense signal in T1-weighted images(Fig. 3c) and a typical hybrid of hypointense and hyperintense signals on T2-weighted images, presenting a mosaic-like appearance (Fig. 3d and e). The mass, inclusive of the enveloping capsular tissue, was radically excised (Fig. 4a–c). Intraoperative frozen section analysis was in line with a hematoma diagnosis. Histopathological assessment disclosed the presence of fibrin and red blood cell clumps, with no evidence of malignancy (Fig. 4d). Subsequent follow-up CT scans confirmed no reoccurrence of the hematoma (Fig. 4f). Case 5

Discussion

CEH is an uncommon postoperative complication following THA, and within the scope of this study, we detail five such cases. Given its infrequency, securing a diagnosis presents a significant challenge, often resulting in delayed intervention. Our diagnostic approach to CEH was anchored in radiological features and histopathological outcomes, aligning with patterns observed in prior cases documented in medical literature. Despite these efforts, the success rate in treatment remains subdued. Among our cases, two patients experienced effective treatment, with follow-ups extending over two years; however, two others developed periprosthetic joint infection (PJI) and continue to receive treatment, and one patient ultimately required amputation. Consequently, it is essential to distill the pathogenesis, etiology, diagnostic approaches, and therapeutic insights related to CEH to improve clinical management.

CEH was initially described by Friedlander in 1968 [12], in the case of a 22-year-old male who had incurred a gunshot wound to the left thigh during his service in the U.S. military in Vietnam in 1966, which had healed completely. Upon subsequent examination, a localized swelling in the left lower leg was identified, likely present for 4–5 years without a definitive traumatic event. The patient hypothesized it may have originated from a basketball game, which constitutes the earliest documented report of CEH. In 1980, Reid et al. [10] formally introduced the concept of CEH in the Journal of JAMA, detailing six cases predominantly involving internal organs and characterizing CEH by three key features: a central blood clot, a wall of granulation tissue, and peripheral dense fibrous tissue. Goddard et al. [9] later documented the first case of CEH following total hip arthroplasty. The patient, a woman who had undergone a MOP THA at the age of 59 due to rheumatoid arthritis (RA), returned six years later for revision surgery due to a loose acetabular cup. Nine years post-revision, a substantial swelling on the right buttock and thigh emerged without any history of trauma. Laboratory investigations excluded coagulation disorders and hemophilia. Three years subsequent to the diagnosis, the patient underwent excision of the mass and joint debridement. Postoperatively, there was localized swelling and fluctuation in the buttock area, and aspiration extracted 200 mL of sanguineous fluid. Over a three-year follow-up period, the mass did not reoccur, and the osteolytic lesion remained stable. Despite undergoing joint debridement, the patient was capable of bearing weight and ambulating with the assistance of a walker. The exact cause of CEH remains elusive. It is hypothesized that inadequate hemostasis during surgery, potentially due to unsealed minor blood vessels from the initial procedure, could result in chronic bleeding and subsequent hematoma formation [13]. Trauma is also considered a potential trigger for CEH, although in the majority of our cases, a clear traumatic event was not identified. There is a school of thought that CEH might be associated with the byproducts of metal ions or wear debris from prosthetic components [14]. Among our cases, three involved a metal-on-polyethylene bearing surface, while two had a ceramic-on-ceramic interface. Review of the literature reveals a variety of interfaces: three cases with ceramic-on-polyethylene [11], two with metal-on-metal [15,16], and three with metal-on-polyethylene [9,17], with one case lacking specific details [18]. We speculate that the generation of metal ions or other wear particles may also be an important cause of CEH formation. The chronic and continuous release of metal ions or other wear particles around the implant acts as a chronic inflammatory stimulus, triggering a series of inflammatory responses, including the recruitment of immune cells, activation of fibroblasts, and release of pro-inflammatory cytokines [1,2]. The initial hematoma serves as a reservoir for metal ions and inflammatory mediators, gradually diffusing into the surrounding tissues and triggering chronic inflammation, fibroblast activation, and collagen deposition, ultimately leading to the formation of a fibrous capsule [3,4]. The disease's progression is typically slow, spanning months to years; the average time to the development of CEH in our patient cohort was 15 years, and the average across seven reported cases in the literature was 15.4 years. The underlying mechanism remains unclear, but it is widely accepted that the degradation of blood cell components within a blood clot can impact surrounding tissues and provoke an inflammatory response. This inflammation can enhance the permeability of blood vessel walls and also increase bleeding from the dilated capillaries within the granulation tissue, leading to the formation of a hematoma [19]. Bone alterations and destruction may stem from repeated hemorrhagic events, a phenomenon observed in patients with hemophilia [20]. In this report, the prothrombin time (PT) and activated partial thromboplastin time (APTT) for the five cases were found to be within the normal range, contrasting with the prolonged APTT typically seen in individuals with von Willebrand disease (VWD) and hemophilia. Coagulation factor activity was not assessed in these cases because the APTT results were normal. However, these findings do not entirely exclude the potential for mild VWD in these patients.

MRI is pivotal in diagnosing CEH, typically manifesting as a isointense or slightlyhyperintense signals on T1-weighted sequences and/or a combination of hypointense and hyperintense signals on T2-weighted sequences. Some patients will exhibit a distinct "mosaic sign" in T2-weighted imaging, which reflects a blend of fresh and old blood, suggesting recurrent hemorrhagic events [21]. This feature was present in all our patients. Moreover, CT scans are instrumental in delineating the mass's dimensions, morphology, and its relationship to surrounding tissues, which is crucial for surgical planning [22]. However, differentiating hematomas from malignant soft tissue tumors can be difficult based solely on clinical and radiological characteristics [23]. Histological examination is often necessary for a definitive diagnosis, which typically reveals three classic features: (1) a peripheral wall made up of dense fibrous tissue, (2) fresh and mobile blood clots/blood, and (3) a central region composed of loose connective tissue [10]. It is imperative to distinguish CEH from malignant tumors, hemophilia, and inflammatory pseudotumors. Coagulation function tests were conducted for all patients to exclude hemophilia, and postoperative pathology excluded the presence of malignant tumors in these patients. The distinction between CEH and inflammatory pseudotumors is not always distinct, as there is potential for concurrent conditions in many patients. Precise differential diagnosis is vital for devising treatment strategies and for prognostic prediction.

The gold standard for treating CEH is complete surgical excision, inclusive of the capsule, which is essential for reducing recurrence rates and establishing a definitive histological diagnosis [24].To mitigate the risk of excessive intraoperative bleeding, preemptive measures such as preoperative angiographic assessment and arterial embolization of surrounding vessels are often undertaken. In our series, two patients who underwent complete resection and simultaneous one-stage revision have remained recurrence-free for over a year. However, two patients experienced recurrent hematomas postoperatively and subsequently developed infections, potentially linked to incomplete resection during the initial surgery; another patient ultimately required amputation. These outcomes highlight the critical nature of early detection and intervention.

Within the literature, five patients who underwent complete mass resection and one-stage replacement have shown no recurrence over a mean follow-up period of 2.4 years [15,17,18,25]. One patient had a hematoma recurrence three months post one-stage revision, but no further recurrence was observed after additional vascular embolization, with a subsequent nine months of follow-up [16]. One patient, due to poor renal function, underwent hematoma excision under local anesthesia only. The condition recurred after 6 months. The patient then underwent hematoma excision again under general anesthesia. No recurrence was observed during the 6-month follow-up period afterward [26]. Another patient underwent one-stage spacer implantation followed by two-stage prosthetic revision, with no recurrence noted during a two-year follow-up [15]. In one case involving mass excision and joint debridement, no recurrence was reported after three years of follow-up [9]. The higher success rates noted in the literature compared to our case series may be attributed to several factors. Some of our cases were identified at a later stage, characterized by more severe hematomas and bone dissolution, and the initial surgical interventions were not as comprehensive. Furthermore, there is a potential for reporting bias, with a tendency among researchers to focus on and report their more successful outcomes. An additional consideration is the shorter follow-up periods in the literature, with two cases having follow-ups of less than one year, and one case only having a six-month follow-up, which may not fully capture the long-term outcomes and complications.

Our research and a comprehensive literature review have highlighted that CEH can result in progressive bone dissolution, emphasizing the need for vigilant postoperative surveillance for patients who have undergone THA. Timely mass excision is imperative once bone loss is identified. A stark example from our case series is the fifth patient, who showed only mild femoral osteolysis during an outpatient visit two years prior, but experienced a catastrophic deterioration leading to amputation within the subsequent two years. This underscores the critical importance of regular imaging follow-ups for assessing treatment efficacy and early detection of recurrence.

This study has numerous strengths and clinical translation values. To our knowledge, it is the first to systematically report multiple cases of CEH after THA and conduct a comprehensive literature review and analysis. Meanwhile, this study has significant clinical translation significance. Firstly, it helps to improve the diagnostic accuracy of CEH after THA. By summarizing and optimizing imaging examination methods, we can further reduce the rates of missed and misdiagnoses, achieve early identification, and thereby enhance diagnostic precision and establish early diagnostic criteria for CEH after THA. Secondly, it can optimize treatment plans. Early diagnosis allows for timely determination of the optimal surgical timing, reducing the risk of complications. Moreover, personalized treatment plans can be developed based on the characteristics of the hematoma, thereby improving therapeutic efficacy. Thirdly, it can improve prognosis. After early diagnosis and optimized treatment, the incidence of complications such as PJI can be reduced, and functional recovery can be promoted. Lastly, the above measures can effectively shorten hospital stays and reduce the risk of reoperation, thereby lowering medical costs. Meanwhile, the results of this study provide a basis for updating the management guidelines for CEH after THA and will promote the standardization and normalization management. In summary, this study holds significant clinical translation value in terms of diagnosis, treatment, prognosis, cost control, and guideline updates for CEH following THA, thereby contributing to the improvement of overall therapeutic efficacy.

Despite our efforts, this study has several limitations. First, due to the rarity of the cases, this study only included five patients. This may lead to insufficient representativeness of the research results, making it difficult to comprehensively reflect the true incidence, clinical characteristics, and treatment outcomes of CEH in patients after THA. To address this limitation, we reviewed nine additional cases reported in the literature to supplement the small sample size. Secondly,the follow - up time of patients varies greatly, ranging from 7 months to 39 months, and some follow - up times in the literature are also short. A short follow - up time may not be able to detect long - term complications or recurrence of CEH in a timely manner, affecting the accurate evaluation of treatment effects. Therefore, future research needs to expand the sample size and follow - up time to verify the conclusions. Additionally, in the future, we will promote the establishment of multicenter studies or large-scale joint replacement databases (such as joint replacement registries) to collect more case data for analysis, which can further improve the diagnosis and treatment of CEH after THA.

CEH, while a rare complication, has the potential to cause significant functional disability and diminish the quality of life. Delayed or improper treatment can escalate to severe consequences. Our study contributes valuable insights into the diagnosis and management of CEH post-THA. Future research endeavors should concentrate on preventative strategies, such as refining surgical techniques to minimize postoperative bleeding, and on the development of more sensitive diagnostic tools for the early detection of CEH. Moreover, delving into the molecular mechanisms of CEH is essential for a deeper comprehension of its pathogenesis and for devising targeted therapeutic approaches. In conclusion, heightened awareness is essential; once CEH is detected, regular follow-ups must be conducted to facilitate early identification and expedite treatment.
