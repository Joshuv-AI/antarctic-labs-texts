A novel percutaneous aspiration sclerotherapy for staged volume reduction of giant single bulla in the treatment of COPD combined with pulmonary bullae
Authors: Wusheng Deng, Chao Li, Yun Jiang, Xiao Hu, Yufei Jiang, Gang Jiang, Yanjun Hu, Cheng Li, Hui Zhang, Jiazhou Yu, Yongliang Jiang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe efficacy of a novel technique-percutaneous aspiration sclerotherapy for staged volume reduction of giant single bulla-in patients with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) combined with pulmonary bullae remains unclear.MethodsA retrospective study was conducted on 40 COPD patients with pulmonary bullae who underwent the novel percutaneous aspiration sclerotherapy for staged volume reduction of giant single bulla technique. Pulmonary bullae size, pulmonary function, blood gas analysis, six-minute walk test (6MWT), modified Medical Research Council (mMRC) score and St. George ‘s Respiratory Questionnaire (SGRQ) were assessed before and after the procedure. Related complications were also recorded.ResultsThe average size of the giant bullae in the 40 patients was 12.67 ± 2.49 cm. Compared with preoperative data, PaO2, forced expiratory volume in one second (FEV1), FEV1%, forced vital capacity (FVC), FVC%, 6MWT, mMRC score, and SGRQ score were all significantly improved in 40 patients after surgery (p < 0.001); The diameter of the pulmonary bullae was significantly smaller after surgery (p < 0.001); PaCO2 decreased postoperatively, but no statistical significance (p > 0.05); Pneumothorax was detected in 14 patients. Twelve patients with hemoptysis had small amounts of hemoptysis; eight patients had a fever, and nine patients experienced chest pain.ConclusionPercutaneous aspiration sclerotherapy for staged volume reduction of giant single bulla is a feasible treatment for pulmonary bullae, involving a straightforward procedure. Nevertheless, its long-term clinical efficacy remains to be further validated in follow-up studies.Keywords: COPD, Pulmonary bulla, Volume reduction, Percutaneous

Introduction

Pulmonary bullae, are characterized by the formation of air-containing cavities with a diameter greater than 1 cm within the lung tissue [1, 2]. A giant pulmonary bulla (GEB) is a type of bullous lung tissue lesion, generally defined as a lung bulla larger than one-third of the unilateral chest cavity [3]. It is often secondary to chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) [4]. GEB is reported as rare abroad, with an incidence of only 0.21/100,000 per year [5]; however, it may be relatively common in China, given that nearly 100 million COPD patients in the country. Since pulmonary bullae cause irreversible lung tissue damage, affected patients may experience symptoms such as chest tightness, shortness of breath, haemoptysis and chest pain. Additionally, pulmonary bullae often lead to spontaneous pneumothorax [6], which may be life-threatening in severe cases. Symptomatic cases often require surgical intervention.

COPD patients with GEB often have poor lung function, which seriously affects their quality of life [7]. Drug treatment options are limited, and surgical intervention treatment is often necessary [8]. Traditional surgical treatments mainly include thoracotomy or thoracoscopic surgery, which aim to relieve the compression on normal lung tissue by removing bullae [9, 10]. However, these methods have limitations [11–13]. For example, they are invasive, require general anesthesia, and carry a risk of damaging normal lung tissue during the procedure. High-risk patients, such as those aged 70 years or older, those with underlying cardiopulmonary diseases, or those with poor lung function (forced expiratory volume in one second (FEV1) < 50%), often cannot tolerate these surgeries. Furthermore, many complications occur after surgery, including persistent air leakage lasting more than seven days, atrial fibrillation, the need for postoperative mechanical ventilation, and pneumonia [5, 9, 14].

In recent years, less invasive therapeutic methods for treating pulmonary bullae have emerged [15–17]. Endobronchial valve (EBV) implantation is a novel bronchoscopic treatment method aimed at achieving safer, more reversible and minimally invasive lung volume reduction for patients with heterogeneous emphysema [18, 19]. A prospective, non-randomized, single-center study [20] found that endoscopic EBV implantation is applicable for surgical GEB patients. Patients’ lung function and quality of life significantly improved within 24–48 h after procedure. EBV treatment improve symptoms, activity tolerance, and quality of life for pulmonary bullae patients. However, lung function may deteriorate after EBV treatment for patients with positive bypass ventilation. Furthermore, in patients with severe COPD complicated by pulmonary bullae, well preserved lung parenchyma may be damaged.

For patients with pulmonary bullae unsuitable for other treatment methods such as EBV or surgical treatment, the injection of autologous blood may be considered. Hetzel et al. [21] used three-dimensional navigation technology to determine the puncture position and direction, guiding the infusion of autologous blood into bullous emphysema through bronchoscopy under fluoroscopic guidance. This reduced lung volume and improved respiratory distress. However, as this mechanism triggers an inflammatory response, early pneumonia may occur, accompanied by adverse events such as fever, hypoxaemia, and tachycardia. Internal medicine thoracoscopy has been used to treat subpleural pulmonary bullae. A study [22] found that internal medicine thoracoscopy was used for cauterisation and coagulation treatment of 30 cases of pulmonary bullae (diameter 2–5 cm, narrow pedicle, low tension). About 70% cases achieved cessation of air leakage and lung re-expansion 24 h after surgery, with an 89.3% success rate two years post-surgery. However, thoracoscopy in internal medicine is complex and challenging to perform. For large pulmonary bullae or those in special locations or with widespread distribution, internal medicine thoracoscopy cannot completely remove or treat them, which affects the outcome of the treatment. In addition, recurrence rates are higher.

Simple bullectomy and EBV therapies are associated with limitations including extensive trauma, high recurrence rates, and inconsistent therapeutic efficacy [23, 24]. This leaves an urgent unmet clinical need for highly effective, minimally invasive treatment strategies. Percutaneous puncture aspiration sclerotherapy for volume reduction meets these clinical requirements and is supported by evidence-based medical evidence. A prospective open-label, single-arm, non-randomised investigation was conducted to explore the safety and therapeutic efficacy of intrabullous adhesion plication for GEB. Relevant findings confirmed significant improvements in pulmonary function and physical exercise endurance following this intervention [25]. Overall, percutaneous puncture aspiration sclerotherapy for volume reduction has a significant therapeutic impact in the management of pulmonary bullae.

This article comprehensively analyzes the applications, efficacy, and safety of minimally invasive percutaneous volume-reduction techniques for the treatment of pulmonary bullae, offering novel insights and references for clinical practice.

Methods

Inclusion criteria

The definition of GEB should conform to the following criteria: (1) A single GEB: occupying a unilateral chest volume of ≥ 30% or with a diameter of ≥ 5 cm that compresses normal lung tissue and causes a significant decline in lung function (FEV1 decreased by ≥ 20% compared to the expected value); (2) Multiple pulmonary bullae, where one bulla is dominant (accounting for more than 50% of the volume), and the others have little impact on lung function; (3) Mature and stable pulmonary bullae. Imaging shows intact pulmonary bullae walls, with no signs of infection or progressive enlargement, and a duration of more than three months. (4) The patient is unable to tolerate surgical procedures: The patient is unable to withstand the risk of open chest surgery due to advanced age, poor cardiopulmonary reserve (FEV1 < 50% of the expected value), and the presence of serious comorbidities such as severe coronary heart disease and pulmonary arterial hypertension. (5) The symptoms are significant, including persistent dyspnoea (mMRC score ≥ 2), recurrent pneumothorax or infection, and ineffective conservative treatment. (6) After inhaling bronchodilators, the FEV1/forced vital capacity (FVC) ratio is less than 70%.

Exclusion criteria

Patients were excluded for the following reasons: (1) Diffuse, homogeneous pulmonary bullae. Multiple bullae that are widely distributed and of uniform size. They lack clear volume reduction targets and have low expectations for postoperative functional improvement. (2) Acute respiratory system diseases: including respiratory infections, acute asthma attacks and acute exacerbation of COPD. (3) Immature/active pulmonary bullae: The bullae have thin walls, irregular shapes, or recent signs of high risk, such as rupture, bleeding, or infection. (4) End-stage cardiopulmonary failure: extremely poor lung function (FEV1 < 30% of the predicted value and Diffusing Capacity of the Lungs for Carbon Monoxide (DLCO) < 40% of the predicted value), heart function grade IV (NYHA classification), or severe pulmonary hypertension (mPAP ≥ 35 mmHg). (5) Systemic contraindications: coagulation dysfunction (INR > 1.5, platelet count < 50 × 10^9/L), severe liver and kidney dysfunction (Child-Pugh class C, eGFR < 30 ml/min/1.73 m²), or advanced malignant tumours.

The Medical Ethics Committee of Hunan Provincial People’s Hospital (The First Affiliated Hospital of Hunan Normal University) approved this study (Approval No.: [2025]-305).

Statistical analysis

The SPSS 22.0 software package was used for the analysis, with the experimental results first being subjected to homogeneity of variance and normality tests. Quantitative data that followed a normal distribution and had exhibited homogeneity of variance were expressed as the mean ± standard deviation (x ± s). Two independent sample t-test was used to compare the means of two sets of quantitative data, while a one-way analysis of variance was used to compare the means of three or more sets of quantitative data. A p-value of less than 0.05 was used as the standard for determining whether there was a statistical difference.

Patient enrollment and selection

The patient selection flowchart is presented in Fig. 3. Initially, a total of 49 patients were assessed for potential eligibility. Among them, 3 patients with multiple bullae, 2 patients with acute respiratory system diseases, and 1 patient with systemic contraindications were excluded according to the study criteria. Consequently, 43 patients were considered eligible for further evaluation. During the follow-up period, 2 patients were lost to follow-up, and 1 patient was excluded due to incomplete clinical data. Ultimately, 40 patients were enrolled in the final analysis of this study.

Complications

Regarding the complications, pneumothorax occurred in 14 patients (35.0%). Chest tube placement was performed on 12 patients; and the remaining two patients presented with simple occult pneumothorax, which improved with oxygen therapy alone. Twelve patients presented with mild hemoptysis, of whom three required clinical intervention in the form of haemostatic agents and bed rest. Eight patients experienced fever, of whom only two infected individuals required antibacterial treatment. and nine patients experienced chest pain. The average length of hospital stay was 9 days, and the average drainage time for postoperative pneumothorax patients was 6 days. The results are shown in Table 3.

Discussion

We conducted a clinical study including 40 patients with COPD and pulmonary bullae. We found that a novel percutaneous aspiration sclerotherapy for staged volume reduction of giant single bulla proved effective and safe for treating COPD combined with pulmonary bullae. Among the 40 patients assessed in the efficacy analysis, the diameter of the pulmonary bullae was significantly reduced and the 6MWT was significantly increased postoperatively compared to preoperative values. The pneumothorax complication rate was 35.0%.

Unlike traditional surgical bullectomy which involves resecting pulmonary bullae through a thoracotomy, the intrabullous fixation technique adopted in this study does not require such invasive procedures. This technique involves CT-guided puncture of pulmonary bullae under local anesthesia, followed by injecting a fixative and implementing internal fixation, achieving the goal of reducing the volume of pulmonary bullae. Results showed significant improvement in patients’ postoperative symptoms and pulmonary function, with FEV1% and FVC% markedly increased compared to preoperative levels. We used the 6MWT and the SGRQ scores to evaluate the patients’ quality of life postoperatively, results also showed significant improvement.

Recently, several clinical studies have evaluated CT guided percutaneous pulmonary bullae reduction surgery in COPD patients with pulmonary bullae [7, 15, 30]. Li et al. [25] reported that using percutaneous puncture technology to treat 36 pulmonary bullae patients significantly improve their pulmonary function and exercise tolerance. Among these patients, three patients could barely walk preoperatively achieved 6MWT results of 42 m, 72 m, and 528 m postoperatively. Complications included ipsilateral pneumothorax in 22 cases, only two of which were tension pneumothorax, and six cases had subcutaneous emphysema. There were no cases of bronchopleural fistula or death, and the treatment was effective in long term. CT guided percutaneous pulmonary bullae reduction surgery is minimally invasive, has a high success rate, and improves lung function.

Currently reported sealants for percutaneous puncture treatment of pulmonary bullae include porcine fibrin sealant, autologous blood, doxycycline, and medical adhesive [19, 21, 31, 32]. Lauromacrogol injection is a kind of foam hardener commonly used in the treatment of varicose veins. It has a strong stimulating effect, which damages the vascular endothelium, causing endothelial cell fibres proliferation and vascular occlusion [33]. Zhao et al. [34] reported treating 19 patients with pulmonary bullae via CT-guided percutaneous injection of lauromacrogol; the results showed that the volume of pulmonary bullae decreased significantly, and their dyspnea symptoms improved or treating 19 patients with pulmonary bullae via CT-guided percutaneous injection of lauromacrogol. We innovatively applied sclerosing agents (lauromacrogol, autologous blood, and thrombin powder), which were injected into pulmonary bullae in stages to gradually block their leakage channels. Our results demonstrated that injection of lauromacrogol into pulmonary bullae significantly reduced their volume postoperatively.

Pneumothorax is a common complication of percutaneous aspiration sclerotherapy for staged volume reduction of giant bullae. To reduce the risk, the puncture should ideally avoid traversing normal lung tissue. Yang et al. [35] reported a 26% pneumothorax rate in 73 patients treated with CT-guided percutaneous OB glue occlusion. However, our results showed that the incidence of pneumothorax was 35%, higher than previous studies. This may because our procedure involves three stages with multiple punctures and procedures such as the injection of sclerosing agents and aspiration. These procedures may directly cause the rupture of pulmonary bullae and gas leakage into the pleural cavity.

This study examined the blood gas analysis of patients with pulmonary bullae and found PaCO₂ declined postoperatively without statistical significance. Following significant reduction, the surrounding lung tissue re-expands, thereby increasing lung ventilation and correcting the ventilation/blood flow imbalance. The non-significant decrease in PaCO₂ might be because patients’ baseline PaCO₂ was normal, and fewer had carbon dioxide retention. In our study, the mMRC and St. George’s scores of patients decreased significantly, indicating that this treatment improved respiratory function and quality of life in COPD patients.

This study applies percutaneous aspiration sclerosis staged pulmonary bulla volume reduction surgery to COPD patients with pulmonary bullae. This approach is innovative and provides valuable clinical guidance. Precise positioning is achieved under CT guidance, requiring only local anaesthesia and no special patient positioning; Compared to surgical procedures, it avoids lung tissue resection and suturing, incurring no additional surgical trauma. Furthermore, it does not require auxiliary mechanical ventilation, which significantly shortens the time required for lung recruitment and causes less damage to lung function. The puncture needle penetrates directly into pulmonary bullae without exposing, cutting or suturing lung tissue, thereby reducing bleeding. Most importantly, it is more cost-effective than surgical procedures.

As a considerable proportion of patients with COPD present with multiple bullae, our technique is primarily applicable to those with a single dominant bulla. Patients with multiple bullae were mostly excluded from the study. In patients with multiple pulmonary bullae, intercommunication and mutual interactions may exist between the bullae. This complicates air leakage, making it hard to seal off the air leakage at the base of the pulmonary bullae [36]. Furthermore, the presence of multiple bullae necessitates additional puncture sites, which substantially increases the incidence of postoperative complications such as persistent air leakage and pneumothorax. Future studies will explore the applicability of our technique in patients with multiple relatively independent pulmonary bullae.

The present study has several limitations. First, the sample size is relatively small. Second, as a single-center retrospective study, which may not fully reflect the patient characteristics and treatment outcomes across different regions and healthcare levels, potentially resulting in selection bias. Moreover, no comparative analysis was performed against standard regimens such as bullectomy, EBV placement and conservative therapy. Third, although this study provides evidence that staged volume reduction therapy with percutaneous puncture sclerotherapy is effective in the short term for treating pulmonary bullae, long-term follow-up (> 6–12 months) is not done, and the long-term efficacy remains unclear. Fourth, the incidence of pneumothorax is relatively high. We will further optimize operational procedures and workflows to reduce the incidence of pneumothorax.

Conclusions

Percutaneous aspiration sclerotherapy for staged volume reduction of giant single bulla is a feasible treatment for pulmonary bullae, involving a simple procedure. Our technique is minimally invasive, has expanded indications and is repeatable. Nevertheless, its long-term clinical efficacy remains to be further validated in follow-up studies.
