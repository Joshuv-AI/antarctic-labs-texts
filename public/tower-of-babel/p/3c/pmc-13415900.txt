Exploring the Predictive Value of National Residency Matching Program (NRMP) Rank in Residency Outcomes
Authors: Emily Kwon, Mingzhuo Pei, Annie Xu, Rohini Ravi Bahethi, Evelyne Kalyoussef, Wayne Daniel Hsueh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Purpose:To examine the relationship between the National Residency Matching Program (NRMP) rank list position and otolaryngology residency performance.Methods:Five consecutive graduating classes at a single institution’s otolaryngology residency training program (2021-2025) were evaluated (n = 18) using their complete 5-year data. Correlation coefficients were calculated to assess the relationship between NRMP rank and in-service exam scores, research productivity, total case logs, milestone evaluations, selection as Administrative Chief Resident, receipt of teaching awards, and post residency position. Milestone evaluations were based on faculty ratings across all 7 core competencies on a 1- to 7-point scale, with an average score calculated for each resident. Spearman’s rho was used to evaluate correlations between NRMP rank and both in-service scores and milestone evaluations. Chi-square tests were conducted to assess associations between NRMP rank and categorical outcomes, including selection as Administrative Chief Resident, receipt of the teaching award, and post residency position.Results:The average NRMP rank of the residents was 8.778 (range: 1-26). There was a significant correlation between higher NRMP rank and higher milestone evaluations (ρ = −.544, P = .020). However, no significant correlations between NRMP rank and in-service exam scores, research productivity, total case logs, selection as Administrative Chief Resident, receipt of the teaching award, or post residency position were found.Conclusion:Our results suggest that better-ranked residents receive higher milestone evaluation scores. However, NRMP rank alone may not reliably predict performance on in-service exam scores, chief resident selection, receipt of teaching awards, or position after residency training.

Introduction

Otolaryngology is one of the most competitive residency programs in the U.S. According to the National Residency Matching Program (NRMP), only 85.3% of otolaryngology residency spots were filled by U.S. medical students in the 2000 match. 1 In contrast, 99.7% of spots offered in the 2024 match were filled by U.S. medical students and 90 US students went unmatched.2,3 The high demand for otolaryngology residencies allows academic programs to carefully select applicants with the potential to contribute to advancements in research, clinical innovation, and leadership within the field. Decisions are made on United States Medical Licensing Examination (USMLE) scores, letters of recommendation, academic performance, research achievements, personal statements, and interview performance. Before 2022, one of the main tools used to assess applicants to otolaryngology programs was the USMLE Step 1, a standardized exam taken by U.S. medical students upon completion of pre-clinical courses. However, since 2022, the USMLE has started recording scores as pass/fail, making it essential to understand the relationship between how applicants are selected, and their performance in residency. 4

The NRMP employs a computer algorithm to facilitate the residency matching process. Each institution generates an NRMP rank list, which represents its assessment of an applicant’s potential for future success. A significant amount of time and effort is spent by otolaryngology residencies in order to prepare what they believe is the most accurate rank list. However, there are limited opportunities to determine if these rankings accurately predict residency performance. Research on the correlation between otolaryngology applicant rank and future success is scarce. While retrospective studies in otolaryngology have investigated this relationship, they have yielded inconclusive results.5,6 This inconsistency extends to other specialties, including emergency medicine, obstetrics and gynecology, internal medicine, and anesthesiology, where studies on NRMP rank and residency performance have also produced mixed findings.7-11

Thus, our study aims to evaluate our own program’s relationship between rank list position and resident performance. We hypothesize that the NRMP number positively correlates with residency performance, as dictated by research productivity, milestone evaluations, case log totals, selection as chief resident, receipt of teaching award, and position after residency metrics.

Materials and Methods

This study evaluated the relationship between residency performance measures and NRMP rank list position in 5 consecutive classes (2021-2025) at our institution’s residency program. During residency, residents receive multiple evaluations of their clinical, surgical, and interpersonal skills from faculty evaluators. The 6 types of evaluations used in this study to assess performance were research productivity, milestone evaluations, case log totals, selection as a chief resident, receipt of a Golden Apple teaching award, and position after residency training. Research productivity was calculated as the total number of manuscripts the resident has published during their residency training. At the end of each 3-month rotation, residents received evaluations from all faculty. Faculty rated each resident using a 1- to 7-point scale (7 = best) on the 6 Accreditation Council for Graduate Medical Education (ACGME) General Competencies: Patient Care (including clinical and surgical skills), Medical Knowledge, Practice-Based Learning and Improvement, Interpersonal and Communication Skills, Professionalism, and Systems-Based Practice. Residents in any year of training were eligible for the Golden Apple teaching award, which is considered an annual teaching honor that is anecdotally recognized across medical institutions. The teaching award recognizes resident physicians who are deemed impactful, passionate, and student-centric and served as further subjective assessments of resident performance. In addition, case log totals and post residency positions (eg, private practice vs fellowship) were collected. In total, the records of 18 residents (all residents in 5 consecutive classes) at our residency training program were retrospectively reviewed.

Correlation coefficients were calculated to assess the relationship between NRMP rank and in-service exam scores, milestone evaluations, selection as Administrative Chief Resident, and receipt of the teaching award. Spearman’s rho was used to evaluate correlations between NRMP rank and in-service scores, milestone evaluations, and research productivity. Fisher’s exact tests were conducted to assess associations between NRMP rank and categorical outcomes, including selection as Administrative Chief Resident, receipt of the teaching award, and post residency positions. For the above comparisons, a P-value of <.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance. This study was approved by Rutgers University’s Institutional Review Board (Pro2024000998).

Results

Rutgers NJMS had 18 residents who completed residency training between 2021 and 2025. The average NRMP rank of the residents was 8.778 (range: 1-26). A statistically significant moderate negative correlation was found between NRMP rank and milestone evaluations (ρ = −.544, P = .020), indicating that better-ranked residents received higher milestone evaluation scores (Figure 1A). However, there was no significant correlation between NRMP rank and in-service exam scores (ρ = .056, P = .826), research productivity (ρ = −.133, P = .599), or total case logs (ρ = −.163, P = .519; Figure 1B-D). Fisher’s Exact tests revealed no significant associations between NRMP rank and selection as Administrative Chief Resident (P > .05), receipt of the teaching award (P > .05), or post residency position (P > .05).

Discussion

Every year, residency programs across the United States attempt to bring the most qualified and promising medical students to their institution. The NRMP is a computer algorithm used to streamline the residency matching process and involves the creation of an NRMP rank list at each institution. Typically, residency programs use a combination of USMLE scores, letters of recommendation, academic performance, research achievements, personal statements, interview performance, and program signaling when determining their NRMP rank list. With these measures, this rank list serves as a representation of the institutions’ prediction of the future success and potential of each applicant. Countless hours are spent interviewing these applicants and creating the rank list each year. However, previous studies have been inconclusive about whether a resident’s NRMP rank position appropriately predicts their residency performance in otolaryngology.

Previous studies examining the relationship between the NRMP rank list and otolaryngology residency performance have yielded mixed results, utilizing various metrics to assess resident success.5,6 Ezeh et al, 6 for instance, analyzed 7 years of residency classes (28 residents total) and reported an average resident rank position of 9.7 (range: 1-22). Similarly, Bent et al. 5 investigated 8 residency classes with a total of 32 residents, although only 24 of them had graduated at the time of their analysis, and an average rank was not specified. Our study’s average rank of 8.778 (range: 1-26) is comparable to that observed by Ezeh et al. 6 These prior investigations employed metrics such as chief resident selection, resident teaching awards, in-service exam scores, and research productivity to gauge performance.

Our findings partially align with Bent et al. 5 and Ezeh et al. 6 in that we also found no significant correlation between match-rank order and chief resident selection, resident teaching awards, in-service exam scores, or research productivity. This consistency across studies, despite variations in cohort size and specific timeframes, suggests that for these particular performance indicators, the initial NRMP rank may not be a strong predictor. In addition to these performance-based metrics, our study also examined whether NRMP rank was associated with post residency career path decisions, such as pursuing fellowship versus entering private practice. We found no significant relationship between match rank and these career outcomes—an area not explored by either Bent et al. 5 or Ezeh et al. 6 This finding may further support the idea that factors beyond initial match position influence long-term career trajectories.

However, unlike Bent et al., who examined both faculty and resident evaluations, and Ezeh et al., who focused solely on faculty evaluations, our study utilized milestone evaluations—a standardized, competency-based framework introduced by the ACGME and the American Board of Otolaryngology in 2013, with an updated version released in 2021.5,6,12,13 Notably, Bent et al. 5 conducted their analysis before the introduction of milestones, and Ezeh et al. 6 did not incorporate milestone data into their evaluation metrics. Milestones provide an objective assessment of resident development across multiple dimensions, including rotation-based performance, academic achievement, and on-call competency. Not all members of our Clinical Competency Committee who complete the milestones assessment are involved in the final ranking position of residents. Our analysis revealed a statistically significant positive correlation between higher NRMP rank position and milestone evaluation scores. This unique finding suggests that NRMP rank at our institution may predict aspects of overall resident success as captured by milestone performance—despite no observed correlation with other traditional performance metrics. The significance of milestone evaluations as indicators of long-term success warrants further investigation.

While the data is inconclusive on the correlation between NRMP rank position and resident performance in otolaryngology, research in other specialties offers contrasting perspectives. Sklar and Tandberg 7 found that higher NRMP ranks correlated with perceived stronger performance in emergency medicine residents 9 ; however, a more recent retrospective study in 2019, showed position on NRMP rank list was of little value in predicting performance. Similarly, Olawaiye et al. 9 observed a positive correlation between NRMP rank and clinical performance in OB/GYN residents. Beninato et al. 10 examined 6 rank lists from an academic surgical residency program in 1992 to 1997 and found that graduates who were ranked highly at the time of the residency match were more likely to pursue an academic career. Bowe et al. 11 developed a preliminary ranking tool incorporating cognitive and non-cognitive traits and found a correlation between applicant rank and residency performance in internal medicine and anesthesiology. Cognitive traits included USMLE scores, medical school grades, and Alpha Omega Alpha status and non-cognitive traits included interpersonal and communication skills and professionalism. These findings highlight the possibility that the predictive value of NRMP rank may vary across specialties.

Beyond NRMP rank, other studies have explored the predictive power of various residency application components. More recent work in otolaryngology has focused on objective measures of surgical competency. Chen et al. 14 utilized a smartphone application to assess resident operative performance in otolaryngology, offering a potentially more granular assessment of surgical skill development. Daly et al. 15 found that an interviewer’s impression of a candidate possessing an “exceptional trait” and a USMLE Step 1 score > 570 were predictive of success during otolaryngology residency. Beyond otolaryngology, a review of 80 studies by Kenny et al. 16 emphasized the strong association between standardized exam performance and medical school grades with measures of physician performance across specialties. Conversely, they found a weaker association for deans’ letters, reference letters, and interviews, despite their common use in resident selection. A separate review for orthopedic residencies by Egol et al. 17 reported that high USMLE Step 1 scores correlated with improved surgical skill ratings, and medical school clinical honors predicted higher overall resident performance. This difference across specialties may be due to variations in training requirements, evaluation methods, or the relative importance of specific skills.

This retrospective review has several limitations. First, it assessed a small cohort of residents at a single institution during a period of program growth in residency position number, which limits the generalizability of our data. The data presented here covers the classes of 2021 to 2025. In 2020, a new NJMS residency program was formed through the integration with an osteopathic program, and thus we were unable to extract the complete data for the classes of 2017 to 2020. This merger and the resulting data limitations explain the smaller sample size. The non-significant findings observed between predictors and outcomes in this study could be due to the limited statistical power afforded by the small sample size. However, small sample sizes are a limitation commonly noted in similar studies on this topic. 6 Additionally, the analysis of research productivity is limited to publications in peer-reviewed journals and does not include other forms of scholarly output such as abstracts, poster presentations, or book chapters. Similarly, our analysis of post-residency positions was limited to immediate fellowship or private practice choices, without long-term follow-up to determine whether graduates eventually entered academic medicine, university-affiliated positions, or remained in private practice. In addition, there are inherent changes in the interview and ranking process which are unique to a program’s individual admissions committee and the changing values and culture of residency programs, which cannot be controlled for in a study. Consequently, these dynamic and individualized factors pose a substantial challenge for future meta-analyses across institutions, limiting the generalizability of findings on the predictive validity of the NRMP rank.

One important consideration when evaluating the predictive power of the NRMP rank position in highly competitive specialties like otolaryngology is the overall excellence and homogeneity of the applicant pool. 18 Given the rigorous selection process for otolaryngology residency, applicants who reach the interview stage are inherently highly accomplished. This restriction of range in applicant quality may make it inherently difficult to discern statistically significant differences in future performance based solely on subtle variations in rank order, especially for outcomes that represent general success. While our study found that better-ranked residents received higher milestone evaluation scores, future research should explore whether these differences represent clinically significant distinctions in performance or are minor variations within an already exceptional cohort. The difficulty in separating differences among highly qualified candidates suggests that factors beyond the traditional application metrics, such as individual learning styles, resilience, or specific program-resident “fit,” might play an increasingly important role in determining long-term success in these competitive fields.

Future directions may include exploring whether additional milestone-based assessments could be developed as another tool in the residency selection process. In the field of neurosurgery, a pilot study developed a milestone-based tool to evaluate sub-interns. Faculty and students found milestone scores useful for differentiating applicants objectively. 19 In our study, NRMP rank position showed a statistically significant correlation with milestone evaluations—an objective, structured framework for assessing resident development—but not with other performance measures such as teaching awards, research productivity, in-service exam scores, or case log volume. This suggests that milestone evaluations may uniquely reflect the competencies valued during the resident selection process. As the transition of Step 1 to pass/fail reduces the number of standardized academic metrics available for evaluating medical students, the development of pre-residency, student-level milestone frameworks may provide a more structured and competency-based method to assess medical students earlier in training and support more holistic residency selection.

Conclusion

Our results suggest that at our institution, higher-ranked medical students may receive higher milestone evaluation scores as residents. However, NRMP rank alone may not reliably predict performance on in-service exams, chief resident selection, research productivity, increased case logs, receipt of teaching awards, or post residency career path. This study contributes to the ongoing discussion regarding the predictive value of NRMP rank in otolaryngology. While our findings suggest a potential link between rank and milestone evaluations, the overall picture remains complex. Future research, including larger sample sizes, longitudinal follow-up, and more objective performance measures are needed to better understand the factors contributing to successful residency training. Further exploration of the specific milestone evaluation components correlating with NRMP rank is also warranted. With the move away from using standardized exam scores as a major deciding factor in residency applicant selection and a changing applicant pool, understanding alternative predictors of resident success will become of greater interest and importance.
