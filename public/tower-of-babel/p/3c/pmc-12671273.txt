Assessing the Impact of Drawing in Biology Lecture Classes on Student Learning
Authors: Stephanie L Madison, Sarah Layne, Caleb Wilkinson, Khoa Pham, Haley Sims
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTOne type of active learning technique that instructors can incorporate is the use of various drawing activities. This study investigates the impact of drawing during biology lecture classes on student learning. Undergraduate students in two lecture sections of an introductory cellular and molecular biology course completed worksheets that either required them to draw (learner‐generated) or required them to interpret a drawing (instructor‐generated). The four topics that were assessed were amino acid polymerization, nucleotide polymerization, cellular respiration, and photosynthesis. Student learning was assessed using multiple‐choice and short‐answer exam questions. Students who completed the learner‐generated worksheet related to amino acid polymerization performed significantly better than students who completed the instructor‐generated worksheet. Differences in student learning were not statistically significant for the other three topics; however, trends indicated that for some topics, the learner‐generated worksheet increased student understanding while for other topics the students who completed the instructor‐generated worksheet performed better. These findings indicate that it is important to carefully align the type of drawing activity with the complexity of the topic. When the drawings were too complex, students did not gain as much from creating their own images. Instructors are encouraged to thoughtfully integrate various types of drawing activities into their courses to increase student learning.Keywords: amino acid polymerization, biology, cellular respiration, drawing, nucleotide polymerization, photosynthesis, scholarship of teaching and learning

Introduction

It is widely accepted that active learning improves student learning in STEM courses, and instructors can incorporate a wide range of active learning strategies into their classes [1]. One type of active learning technique instructors can employ is utilizing drawing‐related activities. Drawing activities can utilize a range of drawings from instructor‐generated to learner‐generated [2]. Instructor‐generated drawings are provided by the instructor and then students are asked to interpret the drawings [2]. When a student is given a blank piece of paper and instructed to create their own drawing, this is referred to as a learner‐generated drawing activity [2].

Multiple studies have utilized learner‐generated drawings. Some researchers have used learner‐generated drawings to assess student understanding, identify student misconceptions, and recognize gaps in student knowledge [3, 4, 5, 6, 7]. Others have shown that having students sketch their ideas will improve their ability to construct models [8, 9]. Learner‐generated drawings have also been used to promote student learning [10, 11]. Students that generated their own drawings have scored higher on posttests than students that did not create their own drawings [12, 13, 14, 15, 16, 17]. Students that reported that they studied by creating drawings have been shown to receive higher course grades than students that did not draw while studying [18]. Since learner‐generated drawing activities can sometimes also involve retrieval practice, one study showed that having students draw did not further improve student learning compared to retrieval practice alone [19]. In another study, utilizing instructor‐generated drawings improved student learning and reduced cognitive load, whereas students that created their own drawings had an increased cognitive load and did not show an increase in understanding [20]. Overall, there are some mixed results for incorporating drawing activities into classes [2]. This suggests that the benefits of having students generate their own drawings might be topic dependent.

Biology lends itself to having students draw in class [2, 21]. Students may make observations and draw realistic figures of various organisms [2]. This has been shown to improve student knowledge and attitude [22]. Students can draw cellular and molecular structures that are not visible to the naked eye [2, 23]. Students can also visualize cellular processes by creating abstract drawings to represent what occurs in each process [2].

In this scholarship of teaching and learning project, the main research question was to determine whether having students draw during biology lecture classes would improve student learning. Two categories of drawing were assessed: drawing structural formulas and drawing abstract representations of processes. First, does having students draw the structures of polymers improve their understanding of polymerization? Specifically, amino acid polymerization and nucleotide polymerization were assessed. Second, does having students draw abstract representations of biological processes improve their understanding of the processes? Abstract drawings of cellular respiration and photosynthesis were created.

Methods

Instructor‐Generated and Learner‐Generated Worksheets

Instructor‐generated and learner‐generated worksheets were developed for each topic (Amino Acid Polymerization, Nucleotide Polymerization, Cellular Respiration, and Photosynthesis).

Amino Acid Polymerization Worksheets

The top half of both the instructor‐generated and learner‐generated worksheets was identical. Both worksheets showed the structures of alanine and glycine. Students were asked to circle and label the amino group, the carboxyl group, and the R‐group on alanine. On the bottom half of the instructor‐generated worksheet, the students were shown the structure of a short peptide with the following sequence: glycine–alanine–glycine. On the bottom half of the learner‐generated worksheet, the students were asked to draw the structure of a short peptide with the following sequence: glycine–alanine–glycine. On both the instructor‐generated and learner‐generated worksheets, students were asked to label all the peptide bonds, label the N‐terminus, label the C‐terminus, and draw a box around alanine.

Nucleotide Polymerization Worksheets

The top half of both the instructor‐generated and learner‐generated worksheets was identical. Both worksheets showed a ribonucleotide and gave students instructions. Students were asked to circle and label the 5‐carbon sugar, the nitrogenous base, and the phosphate group on the nucleotide shown. They were also asked to number all five carbons on the sugar (1′‐5′). The students then had to determine if the nucleotide was a deoxyribonucleotide or a ribonucleotide. On the bottom half of the instructor‐generated worksheet, the students were shown the primary structure of a nucleic acid created by connecting three copies of the nucleotide shown on the top half of the worksheet. On the bottom half of the learner‐generated worksheet, the students were asked to draw the primary structure of a nucleic acid by connecting three copies of the nucleotide shown on the top half of the worksheet. On both the instructor‐generated and learner‐generated worksheets, students were asked to label the 5′ end of the nucleic acid, label the 3′ end of the nucleic acid, and circle one phosphodiester linkage.

Cellular Respiration Worksheets

The instructor‐generated worksheet had an abstract drawing of eukaryotic cellular respiration. Inputs and outputs were listed next to various symbols that represented various steps and components involved in cellular respiration. The PowerPoint slide shown during lecture provided the instructions. Students were told that the edges of the paper represented the cell membrane. Students were asked to label the inner membrane, outer membrane, intermembrane space, mitochondrial matrix, glycolysis, pyruvate processing, citric acid cycle, electron transport chain, and ATP synthase. They also had to draw + symbols where protons accumulate.

The learner‐generated worksheet was a blank piece of paper. The PowerPoint slide shown during lecture provided the instructions. Students were told that the edges of the paper represented the cell membrane. Students were asked to create and label an abstract drawing of eukaryotic cellular respiration. They had to draw one mitochondrion and label the inner membrane, outer membrane, intermembrane space, and mitochondrial matrix. They were given symbols to represent glycolysis, pyruvate processing, citric acid cycle, electron transport chain, and ATP synthase. They were told to draw each symbol in the correct location in the cell and write the inputs and outputs for each. They also had to draw + symbols where protons accumulate.

Photosynthesis Worksheets

The instructor‐generated worksheet had an abstract drawing of eukaryotic photosynthesis. Inputs and outputs were listed next to various symbols that represented various steps and components involved in photosynthesis. The PowerPoint slide shown during lecture provided the instructions. Students were told that the edges of the paper represented the inner membrane of a chloroplast. Students were asked to label the thylakoid membrane, thylakoid space, stroma, photosystem I, photosystem II, Calvin cycle, electron transport chain, and ATP synthase. They also had to draw + symbols where protons accumulate.

The learner‐generated worksheet was a blank piece of paper. The PowerPoint slide shown during lecture provided the instructions. Students were told that the edges of the paper represented the inner membrane of a chloroplast. Students were asked to create and label an abstract drawing of eukaryoticphotosynthesis. They had to draw one thylakoid and label the thylakoid membrane, thylakoid space, and stroma. They were given symbols to represent photosystem I, photosystem II, Calvin cycle, electron transport chain, and ATP synthase. They were told to draw each symbol in the correct location in the chloroplast and write the inputs and outputs for each. They also had to draw + symbols where protons accumulate.

Implementation

One instructor (an author on this manuscript) taught two lecture sections of an introductory cellular and molecular biology course during the Spring 2017 semester. The morning lecture section was larger than the afternoon lecture section. After each topic was covered in lecture, students were given either an instructor‐generated worksheet or a learner‐generated worksheet to complete during class (Table 1). Worksheets were collected and then the correct answers were discussed at the end of class.

Assessment of Learning

IRB approval was obtained (UTK IRB‐17‐03715‐XP). One hundred and two students consented, so their exam answers were analyzed for Exam 1, Exam 2, and the Final Exam. The number of participants was slightly lower for each topic based on which students were in attendance when the worksheet was completed. All analyses were completed using Excel. Instructor‐Generated and Learner‐Generated groups were compared for each relevant multiple‐choice and short‐answer question using 2 × 2 chi‐square analysis (Table S1).

For each student, a total score per topic was calculated. F‐tests were used to compare the variances between Instructor‐Generated and Learner‐Generated groups for each topic. All the groups had equal variances except for the Amino Acid Polymerization scores. Mean scores for Instructor‐Generated and Learner‐Generated groups were compared using a two‐sample t‐test assuming equal variances (except for Amino Acid Polymerization scores that used a two‐sample t‐test assuming unequal variances).

Results and Discussion

To determine whether having students draw during biology lecture classes improves student understanding of the concepts, students completed either a learner‐generated worksheet or an instructor‐generated worksheet in class. Learner‐generated worksheets required students to draw and then label their drawing, while instructor‐generated worksheets only required students to label a drawing printed on the worksheet. Exam questions were used to assess student understanding of the concepts.

Understanding the Polymerization of Amino Acids and Nucleotides

Four exam questions related to amino acid polymerization were analyzed. For all four questions, a higher proportion of students that completed the learner‐generated worksheet answered correctly compared to the students that completed the instructor‐generated worksheet (Figure 1). Only one question was statistically significant (Table S1). When comparing mean scores (total score for all four questions), students that completed the learner‐generated worksheet (mean 3.15, SD 0.91) scored significantly higher than students that completed the instructor‐generated worksheet (mean 2.45, SD 1.35, Figure 2).

N1–N4 assessed nucleotide polymerization. Only A4 had a significant difference between the two worksheet groups (p < 0.05).

Mean score ± SD on questions related to each topic based on which type of worksheet they completed (Instructor Generated or Learner‐Generated). Only mean scores for amino acid polymerization were significantly different (p < 0.05).

Four exam questions related to nucleotide polymerization were analyzed. For three out of the four questions, a higher proportion of students that completed the learner‐generated worksheet answered correctly compared to the students that completed the instructor‐generated worksheet (Figure 1). None of the questions were statistically significant (Table S1). When comparing mean scores (total score for all four questions), students that completed the learner‐generated worksheet (mean 3.04, SD 0.96) scored higher than students that completed the instructor‐generated worksheet (mean 2.93, SD 1.11); however, the difference was not significant (Figure 2).

Overall, drawing a short peptide improved student understanding of the polymerization of amino acids. The instructor noted that there was more discussion when students were completing the learner‐generated worksheet compared to the instructor‐generated worksheet because students were trying to determine how the amino acids would connect together. Drawing a nucleic acid did not improve student understanding of the polymerization of nucleotides to the same extent as drawing a short peptide improved their understanding of amino acid polymerization. This could be due to the differences in drawing complexity. The structure of a nucleotide is a lot more complex than the structures of glycine and alanine. The instructor noted that there were student complaints while they were completing the learner‐generated worksheet for nucleotide polymerization. Some students were overwhelmed with drawing the nucleic acid, so there was not as much discussion about how to connect the nucleotides compared to the amino acid polymerization discussions. This could indicate that the nucleotide polymerization learner‐generated worksheet increased student cognitive load too far, thus reducing the benefits of drawing [20].

Understanding the Processes of Cellular Respiration and Photosynthesis

For the eight cellular respiration and eight photosynthesis exam questions, results varied depending on the type of question being asked. One type of question asked students to recall the reactants and products for each step of cellular respiration or photosynthesis. The second type of question asked students to identify the location of steps or components of cellular respiration and photosynthesis.

For both cellular respiration and photosynthesis, three exam questions related to identifying reactants and products were analyzed. For all six questions, a higher proportion of students that completed the learner‐generated worksheet answered correctly compared to the students that completed the instructor‐generated worksheet (Figure 3). None of the questions were statistically significant (Table S1). When comparing mean scores for cellular respiration (total score for all three questions), students that completed the learner‐generated worksheet (mean 1.26, SD 1.03) scored higher than students that completed the instructor‐generated worksheet (mean 0.93, SD 0.87); however, the difference was not significant (Figure 2). When comparing mean scores for photosynthesis (total score for all three questions), students that completed the learner‐generated worksheet (mean 1.71, SD 1.04) scored higher than students that completed the instructor‐generated worksheet (mean 1.30, SD 1.00); however, the difference was not significant (Figure 2).

C1–C3 assessed cellular respiration knowledge related to recalling the reactants and products for each step. P1–P3 assessed photosynthesis knowledge related to recalling the reactants and products for each step. None of the differences were statistically significant.

For both cellular respiration and photosynthesis, five exam questions related to identifying locations of steps and components were analyzed. For four out of the five cellular respiration questions and three out of the five photosynthesis questions, a higher proportion of students that completed the instructor‐generated worksheet answered correctly compared to the students that completed the learner‐generated worksheet (Figure 4). None of the questions were statistically significant (Table S1). When comparing mean scores for cellular respiration (total score for all five questions), students that completed the instructor‐generated worksheet (mean 2.78, SD 1.53) scored higher than students that completed the learner‐generated worksheet (mean 2.57, SD 1.61); however, the difference was not significant (Figure 2). When comparing mean scores for photosynthesis (total score for all five questions), students that completed the instructor‐generated worksheet (mean 2.53, SD 1.49) scored higher than students that completed the learner‐generated worksheet (mean 2.25, SD 1.39); however, the difference was not significant (Figure 2).

Overall, the effect of worksheet type varies depending on the question type. None of the differences were significant; however, it is important to recognize which students completed each type of worksheet. The morning lecture section completed the learner‐generated worksheet for cellular respiration and the instructor‐generated worksheet for photosynthesis, and the afternoon lecture section completed the instructor‐generated worksheet for cellular respiration and the learner‐generated worksheet for photosynthesis (Table 1). For the lecture‐generated worksheets, students had to write the inputs and outputs for each step or component of the process. For the instructor‐generated worksheets, the inputs and outputs were already listed next to each symbol on the drawings. Although not significant, students performed better on all questions asking them to match reactants and products to the correct step of each process when they had completed the learner‐generated worksheet in class. This means it was beneficial for students to think about and then write out the inputs and outputs for each step and component of the process. On the other hand, students that had completed the instructor‐generated worksheet tended to but not significantly perform better on questions that asked them to recall the location of steps or components of the process. This indicates that when it comes to associating steps with the appropriate location, it was more beneficial for students to label an abstract drawing than create their own abstract drawing. Perhaps, students had a hard time visualizing the spatial organization of each process in the drawings that they created. The instructor recalled that students completing the learner‐generated worksheet were hesitant to begin their drawings and a number of students ended up spending too much time trying to make their drawings more realistic and detailed than needed. Again, this might indicate the learner‐generated worksheets increased their cognitive load [20]. Altogether, these results suggested that the ideal worksheet for reviewing cellular respiration or photosynthesis would provide the abstract drawing where students need to label the steps/components as well as list the inputs and outputs for each step/component.

Conclusions

In conclusion, utilizing drawing activities during biology lecture classes can increase student learning. Specifically, when students are given the structures of amino acids and asked to draw a short peptide during class, they will perform better on assessments related to amino acid polymerization. Even though the other three topics examined (nucleotide polymerization, cellular respiration, and photosynthesis) did not yield any statistically significant results, there were noticeable trends in the data. The lack of significant differences could have been a result of the small sample size for one of the lecture sections, as well as the differences in the level of drawing complexity. Out of the four learner‐generated worksheets, the one related to amino acid polymerization was the least complex. It could be that if the requested drawings are more complex, students would benefit more from being given a scaffold for their drawing than just blank space on a paper to draw from scratch. Instructors should include drawing activities during lecture to increase active learning, thus improving student learning; however, they should evaluate each concept carefully to determine where on the instructor‐generated to learner‐generated drawing scale would best fit the topic and knowledge background of their students.
