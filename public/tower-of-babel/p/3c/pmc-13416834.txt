Fluorination-mediated solvation remodeling of ethylene carbonate-free carbonate electrolytes affords stable 4.6 V-class lithium metal batteries
Authors: Fulu Chu, Lixian Wang, Xin Xue, Yu Xuan, Zehao An, Tong Yu, Wenshuo Hou, Longwei Liang, Linrui Hou, Changzhou Yuan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
High-voltage lithium metal batteries (LMBs) represent the most promising route to next-generation high-energy-density energy storage, yet their practical deployment is severely bottlenecked by conventional ethylene carbonate (EC)-based electrolytes, which suffer from a narrow electrochemical window below 4.3 V, unstable electrode–electrolyte interphases, uncontrolled Li dendrite growth, and a high Li+ desolvation barrier from strong EC–Li+ chelation. Herein, we propose an EC-free fluorination strategy to construct a series of carbonate electrolytes, with the optimized fully fluorinated solvent formulation of 3,3,3-trifluoropropylene carbonate (TFPC) and fluoroethyl methyl carbonate (FEMC) to precisely tailor the Li+ solvation into an F-solvent/anion-enriched structure. This design enables the formation of the robust LiF-rich dual interphases on both the Li anode and 4.6 V LiNi0.9Co0.05Mn0.05O2 (NCM90) cathode, delivering a 95% average coulombic efficiency over 150 cycles in Li‖Cu cells, and 82.3% (25 °C) and 82.6% (50 °C) capacity retention after 100 cycles at 1C in LMBs, along with outstanding rate and wide-temperature-tolerance properties. More essentially, this contribution offers a rational design paradigm in electrolyte chemistry for high-performance EC-free electrolytes toward next-generation high-energy-density LMBs.

Introduction

The ever-growing demand for high-energy-density energy storage systems, driven by the rapid development of electric vehicles, portable electronics, and grid-scale energy storage, has spurred intensive research into next-generation rechargeable batteries.1–3 Lithium metal batteries (LMBs), which employ lithium metal anodes (LMAs) with an ultra-high theoretical specific capacity (3860 mAh g−1) and the lowest redox potential (−3.04 V vs. the standard hydrogen electrode), are widely recognized as the most promising candidate to break the energy density ceiling of commercial graphite-based lithium-ion batteries (LIBs).3–6 To fully unlock the high-energy-density potential of LMBs, high-voltage cathode materials including 4.6 V-class Ni-rich ternary materials (LiNi0.8Co0.1Mn0.1O2, NCM811; LiNi0.9Co0.05Mn0.05O2, NCM90) and 5.0 V-class spinel LiNi0.5Mn1.5O4 (LNMO), have emerged as the core technical route,7,8 as they can deliver a theoretical energy density exceeding 450 Wh kg−1 at the cell level, a ∼30% improvement over the commercial 4.3 V LIB system.9,10

However, the practical deployment of high-voltage LMBs is severely hindered by two inherent and irreconcilable bottlenecks, both rooted in the limitations of conventional carbonate electrolytes.8,11,12 On the cathode side, commercial electrolytes suffer from severe oxidative decomposition at the electrode–electrolyte interphase (EEI) at operating voltages above 4.5 V, leading to continuous electrolyte consumption, transition metal dissolution from the cathode, and rapid capacity fading.13–17 On the anode side, the unstable solid electrolyte interphase (SEI) formed on the highly reactive lithium metal surface cannot suppress continuous parasitic side reactions and uncontrolled lithium dendrite growth, which triggers internal short circuits, low coulombic efficiency (CE), and critical safety hazards.

It is worth emphasizing that ethylene carbonate (EC, Fig. 1a), the indispensable core component of nearly all commercial carbonate electrolytes, has become the fundamental source of the above bottlenecks, despite its high dielectric constant for lithium salt dissolution and moderate SEI film-forming ability.12,18,19 First, EC has a high melting point of 36.4 °C, which is too high compared to that of its counterpart propylene carbonate (PC, −48.8 °C, Fig. 1b and S1, SI).18,20,21 This narrows the operating temperature range of the electrolyte, causes severe salt precipitation at low temperatures, and deteriorates low-temperature ion transport kinetics. Second, the strong chelation between EC and Li+ results in an extremely high desolvation energy barrier,22,23 which further exacerbates the sluggish interfacial reaction kinetics in a wide-temperature scenario. Third, the low anodic oxidation limit of EC (∼4.3 V vs. Li/Li+) makes it unable to adapt to advanced high-voltage cathodes (>4.5 V), and its continuous oxidative decomposition at high voltages is the main cause of cathode interface failure.11,12 Therefore, eliminating EC from the electrolyte system and further developing EC-free high-voltage electrolytes has become an inevitable trend for next-generation high-energy-density LMBs.

To address these intractable challenges, extensive research efforts have been devoted to developing advanced high-voltage electrolyte systems. High-concentration electrolytes (HCEs)24 and localized high-concentration electrolytes (LHCEs)25 have been widely explored, as they can construct an anion-dominated Li+ solvation structure, broaden the electrochemical stability window, and promote the formation of a robust inorganic-rich SEI. Nevertheless, their inherent drawbacks, including prohibitive cost from massive usage of expensive lithium salts, high viscosity, low ionic conductivity, poor electrode wettability especially at low temperatures, and poor compatibility with industrial battery manufacturing processes, severely limit their practical commercialization. Fluorinated carbonate electrolytes have emerged as another promising strategy, as fluorine substitution can significantly enhance the anodic oxidation stability of solvents, reduce the Li+ desolvation energy barrier, and facilitate the formation of a LiF-rich stable SEI.26–28 However, the most reported fluorinated electrolyte studies only focus on single fluorinated solvent modification, and are still based on EC-containing electrolyte systems, which cannot fundamentally solve the inherent defects of EC. Yan et al. demonstrated that tris(2,2,2-trifluoroethyl) carbonate (TFPC) exhibits superior film-forming capability compared with the state-of-the-art fluoroethylene carbonate (FEC) additive. An electrolyte formulation of 1 M LiPF6 in EC/DEC with 10 wt% TFPC enables the construction of a mechanically robust SEI, which effectively stabilizes silicon anodes that undergo drastic volume variation during cycling.29 Zhang et al. further verified the application potential of TFPC in high-voltage LiNi0.5Mn0.3Co0.2O2/graphite battery systems.30 From a theoretical perspective, Nayak et al. investigated the heterogeneous microstructures and ion transport dynamics of 1.0 M LiPF6 in TFPC-based electrolytes via molecular dynamics simulations, providing fundamental insights into the solvation behavior of fluorinated carbonate electrolytes.31 In terms of fluorinated linear carbonates, Naylor et al. developed a non-flammable electrolyte using fluoroethyl methyl carbonate (FEMC) and validated its operational feasibility in LiNi0.6Mn0.2Co0.2O2/-graphite lithium-ion cells.32 More importantly, there is a lack of systematic and in-depth mechanistic analysis of the synergistic effect of cyclic and linear fluorinated carbonates, especially their synergistic regulation on the Li+ solvation structure and the simultaneous stabilization of both the anodic SEI and cathodic electrolyte interphase (CEI) layers. To date, it remains a great challenge to develop an EC-free electrolyte that can simultaneously achieve dendrite-free lithium deposition, high-voltage cathode compatibility (over 4.5 V), and wide-temperature operation for practical LMBs.

Herein, we proposed a fluorination design strategy for EC-free carbonate electrolytes, to address the core bottlenecks of high-voltage LMBs via precise solvation structure regulation and synergistic dual interphase engineering. A series of EC-free electrolytes were constructed through progressive fluorination modification, including the base electrolyte (PE, Fig. 1c) of 1 mol L−1 (M) LiPF6 in propylene carbonate (PC)/ethyl methyl carbonate (EMC), the single-fluorinated electrolyte (TE, Fig. 1d) of 1 M LiPF6 in TFPC/EMC, and the fully fluorinated synergistic electrolyte (TF, Fig. 1e) of 1 M LiPF6 in TFPC/fluoroethyl methyl carbonate (FEMC). The results systematically demonstrated that the gradient introduction of fluorinated cyclic and linear carbonate could precisely tailor the Li+ solvation structure, transforming the solvent-dominated solvation sheath into F-solvent/anion-enriched structures, which significantly reduces the Li+ desolvation energy barrier. More importantly, the synergistic effect of cyclic and linear fluorinated carbonates enabled the simultaneous construction of a robust, LiF-rich SEI on the LMA and a dense, anti-oxidative CEI on the high-voltage LiNi0.9Co0.05Mn0.05O2 (NCM90) cathode, fundamentally inhibiting interfacial parasitic reactions and lithium dendrite growth. Benefiting from this rational design, the optimized TF electrolyte eliminates the inherent defects of EC, enables dendrite-free lithium deposition with an average CE of over 95.7%, and supports ultra-stable long-term cycling of 4.6 V Li‖NCM90 cells. This work not only breaks the performance ceiling of traditional EC-based electrolytes in high-voltage LMBs, but also provides a systematic theoretical guidance and practical design paradigm for the development of EC-free electrolytes for next-generation high-energy-density battery systems.

Experimental section

Chemicals and materials

Lithium hexafluorophosphate (LiPF6), propylene carbonate (PC), ethyl methyl carbonate (EMC), 3, 3,3-trifluoropropylene carbonate (TFPC), and fluoroethyl methyl carbonate (FEMC) were purchased from Macklin (Shanghai, China). The base electrolyte (PE) was formulated with 1 mol L−1 LiPF6 dissolved in a PC/EMC mixture (3 : 7 by volume). The TE electrolyte was prepared using the same salt concentration in a TFPC/EMC binary solvent (3 : 7 by volume), while the TF electrolyte employed a TFPC/FEMC solvent blend (3 : 7 by volume) with identical LiPF6 content.

CR2025/2032-type coin cells were assembled in an argon-filled glove box (H2O < 0.1 ppm, O2 < 0.1 ppm) using a polypropylene separator (Celgard 2500) and 40 µL of each electrolyte. The NCM90 cathode was fabricated by mixing the active material, Super P conductive carbon, and polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) binder in N-methyl-2-pyrrolidone (NMP) to form a homogeneous slurry. The slurry was cast onto aluminum foil, dried at 110 °C under vacuum for 12 h, and punched into circular electrodes with a diameter of 12 mm. A piece of 450 µm-thick lithium metal foil served as both the reference and counter electrode. As for Li‖Cu cells, the potential window was 0–1.0 V (vs. Li/Li+), while Li‖NCM90 cells were cycled between 2.8 V and 4.6 V (vs. Li/Li+).

Electrochemical measurement

Galvanostatic charge/discharge tests of all cells were performed on a CT2001A battery test system (LAND Electronics, Wuhan, China). Cyclic voltammetry (CV), linear sweep voltammetry (LSV), electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS), and Tafel plot measurements were all conducted on an IVIUM electrochemical workstation (Ivium Technologies, The Netherlands). For Tafel plot analysis, Li‖Li symmetric cells were tested in the voltage range of −0.2 V to 0.2 V at a scan rate of 1 mV s−1. EIS measurements were performed with a voltage amplitude of 10 mV over a frequency range of 105 Hz to 0.1 Hz. LSV tests were carried out using Li‖stainless steel cells, by scanning from open-circuit potential to 5.0 V at 1 mV s−1. CV measurements were conducted at a scan rate of 0.1 mV s−1.

Material characterization

The morphologies and microstructures of the electrodes were characterized by field-emission scanning electron microscopy (FESEM, JEOL-6300F), transmission electron microscopy (TEM) and high-resolution TEM (HRTEM, JEOL JEM-2100 Plus, operating at 200 kV). Energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDX) coupled with both FESEM and TEM was used for elemental mapping analysis. The crystalline phases of fresh and cycled NCM90 cathodes were examined by X-ray diffraction (XRD, Rigaku Ultima IV). X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS, ESCALAB 250) with an Al Kα X-ray source was employed to analyze the chemical composition and bonding evolution of electrode electrolyte interphases (EEIs) on both electrode surfaces. For the ex situ characterization, cells were disassembled in an argon-filled glove box. The harvested electrodes were rinsed with EMC to remove residual electrolyte salts, dried under vacuum, and stored in sealed containers before transfer to the characterization instruments. The ionic conductivity of these electrolytes was measured using a calibrated conductivity meter (Leici DDSJ-318, China). Raman spectra of these electrolytes were acquired using a Renishaw System 2000 spectrometer with a 532 nm laser wavelength.

Computational methodology

All density functional theory (DFT) calculations were performed using the Gaussian 09 software package.33 Molecular geometries were first optimized at a small basis set level to obtain the most stable configurations, followed by single-point energy calculations using a larger basis set with the GD3BJ dispersion correction (em = gd3bj). Molecular orbital energies were extracted from the single-point calculation results in Hartree units.

Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations of the electrolyte systems were conducted using the Materials Studio software with the COMPASS II force field to describe the interatomic interactions. The simulation protocol consisted of four consecutive steps: (1) energy minimization using the steepest descent algorithm; (2) gradual annealing from 600 K to 300 K; (3) 5 ns NPT ensemble equilibration at 300 K and 1 atm, controlled by the Nosé–Hoover thermostat and Parrinello–Rahman barostat; and (4) 100 ps production NPT simulation at 300 K. Molecular trajectories were recorded every 1 fs for subsequent analysis. Typical solvation structure models were constructed based on the MD simulation results, and the corresponding desolvation energies were calculated accordingly.

Results and discussion

Design principle of EC-free gradient fluorinated carbonate electrolytes

To construct a whole EC-free gradient fluorinated electrolyte system, progressive substitution of non-fluorinated carbonate solvents with their fluorinated counterparts is the core design principle of this work. This aims to fundamentally address the inherent bottlenecks of conventional EC-based electrolytes and PC-based EC-free electrolytes for high-voltage LMBs. Considering basic physicochemical properties and the relatively high viscosity of fluorinated cyclic carbonates (Fig. S1, S2 and SI),18,20,34 all three electrolyte formulations maintain a fixed cyclic-to-linear carbonate volume ratio of 3 : 7 and a consistent LiPF6 concentration of 1 M. Thus, this ensures a single variable of fluorination degree for mechanism analysis. Moreover, the measured ionic conductivity at 25 °C is 9.15 mS cm−1 for the PE electrolyte, 6.61 mS cm−1 for the TE electrolyte, and 3.27 mS cm−1 for the TF electrolyte (Fig. S3, SI).

The base electrolyte (PE) was designed as the EC-free control group, using PC as the cyclic carbonate matrix to completely replace EC (Fig. 1f). PC was selected due to its high dielectric constant (ε = 64.4), excellent lithium salt dissolution capability, wide liquid-phase temperature range (−48.8–242 °C), and room-temperature liquid state,20,21 which fundamentally avoids the high melting point, narrow operating temperature range, and strong Li+ chelation of EC. However, this non-fluorinated EC-free base electrolyte suffers from two fatal defects that limit its application in high-voltage LMBs. First, the low anodic oxidation limit of PC (∼4.3 V vs. Li/Li+) and ethyl methyl carbonate (EMC, ∼4.5 V vs. Li/Li+) leads to severe continuous oxidative decomposition of the electrolyte on the surface of 4.6 V-class high-voltage cathodes, triggering transition metal dissolution and rapid capacity fading.11,18 Second, the poor compatibility of PC with LMAs results in uncontrollable reductive decomposition at the anode interface, failing to form a dense and stable SEI layer.21 This further induces uncontrolled lithium dendrite growth, continuous parasitic side reactions, and low CE during cycling.

To address the high-voltage intolerance of the PE system, the single-fluorinated electrolyte (TE) was constructed via isomolar substitution of PC with the fluorinated cyclic carbonate TFPC, while retaining the non-fluorinated linear solvent EMC and the fixed cyclic-to-linear ratio (Fig. 1g). The strong electron-withdrawing effect of the trifluoromethyl group in TFPC significantly elevates the anodic oxidation stability of the cyclic carbonate matrix,26,29 broadening the electrochemical stability window of the electrolyte, which effectively adapts to the operating voltage of 4.6 V-class high-voltage cathodes. Meanwhile, the fluorinated structure of TFPC can optimize the reductive decomposition behavior of the cyclic solvent on the lithium metal surface, promoting the formation of fluorine-rich SEI components to a certain extent. Nevertheless, the TE system still fails to achieve stable cycling of high-voltage LMBs, as the non-fluorinated linear solvent EMC remains the core defect source. The low oxidation resistance of EMC still causes oxidative decomposition at high voltage, and its poor compatibility with LMAs cannot support the formation of a continuous and robust SEI, resulting in unmitigated lithium dendrite growth and unstable interfacial behavior during long-term cycling.

On this basis, the fully fluorinated synergistic electrolyte (TF) was developed, the optimized EC-free formulation via isomolar substitution of EMC with the fluorinated linear carbonate FEMC, realizing full fluorination modification of both cyclic and linear carbonate components (Fig. 1h). This gradient full fluorination design achieves a perfect synergy between the fluorinated cyclic and linear carbonates. TFPC acts as the high-dielectric matrix to ensure lithium salt dissolution and ultra-high anodic oxidation resistance, while FEMC inherits the low viscosity and excellent electrode wettability of EMC, and further enhances the anodic stability and reductive film-forming ability via fluorine substitution.35,36 More importantly, the synergistic effect of dual fluorinated carbonates enables precise regulation of the Li+ solvation structure. It transforms the solvent-dominated solvation sheath in the PE system into an F-solvent/anion-enriched structure, significantly reduces the Li+ desolvation energy barrier, and provides sufficient precursors for the in situ formation of a robust LiF-rich SEI on the anode and a dense anti-oxidative CEI on the cathode. This design simultaneously solves the two core bottlenecks of high-voltage oxidative decomposition and lithium dendrite growth, thus achieving synchronous stabilization of both anode and cathode interfaces in 4.6 V class LMBs.

Molecular-level investigation of gradient fluorinated electrolytes

To fundamentally elucidate the rationality of the gradient fluorination design at the molecular level, the intrinsic physicochemical properties of the five carbonate solvents (EC, PC, TFPC, EMC, and FEMC) were systematically investigated via DFT calculations (Fig. 2). The electrostatic potential (ESP) distribution (Fig. 2a–e) directly reflects the electron cloud density and reactive sites of each solvent molecule.37 The non-fluorinated EC and PC exhibit more uneven ESP distribution with large extremum differences (ΔESP, Fig. 2f), while the fluorinated TFPC and FEMC show significantly enhanced positive potential regions around the trifluoromethyl groups, with smaller ΔESP and lower molecular dipole moments (Fig. 2g). A quantitative two-dimensional (2D) profile comparing the ESP extrema of all investigated solvent molecules is provided (Fig. S4, SI) to further clarify the difference in their Li+ solvation capabilities. This fluorination-induced charge redistribution optimizes the ion–dipole interaction between solvent molecules and Li+, decreases the Li+-solvent coordination strength, and facilitates Li+ desolvation and uniform surface adsorption of component molecules to form a continuous interphase.

(f) ΔESP and (g) dipole moment comparisons of various solvents. (h) Highest occupied molecular orbital–lowest unoccupied molecular orbital (HOMO–LUMO) energy levels and (i) binding energies of different electrolyte components.

The highest occupied molecular orbital (HOMO) and lowest unoccupied molecular orbital (LUMO) energy levels are critical indicators of the antioxidant and reductive film-forming ability of the solvents.8,38 The non-fluorinated EMC exhibits HOMO/LUMO levels of −7.92/1.04 eV, and PC exhibits levels of −8.15/0.58 eV (Fig. 2h). After fluorination, the HOMO/LUMO levels of FEMC decrease to −8.50/0.49 eV, and those of TFPC drop to −8.73/0.25 eV. The lowered HOMO levels confirm that fluorination significantly enhances the anodic oxidation stability of the solvents, enabling high compatibility with high-voltage cathodes. Meanwhile, the reduced LUMO levels indicate that fluorinated carbonates are more prone to preferential reductive decomposition on the LMA to form a high-quality SEI.

Furthermore, the binding energy between Li+ and each solvent molecule was calculated (Fig. 2i, S5 and SI). The fluorinated TFPC and FEMC show a moderate Li+ binding strength of −2.10 eV and −1.81 eV compared with non-fluorinated carbonates (−2.31 eV for PC and −2.02 eV for EMC), which not only ensures sufficient lithium salt dissolution and stable Li+ solvation, but also effectively reduces the Li+ desolvation energy barrier, accelerating interfacial reaction kinetics.39,40 These DFT calculation results fully verify the rationality of the gradient fluorination design at the molecular level, and lay a theoretical foundation for the subsequent solvation structure regulation and interfacial stabilization.

Solvation structures of gradient fluorinated electrolytes

To elucidate the regulatory effect of this gradient fluorination design on the Li+ solvation environment, the solvation structure of the three electrolytes was characterized via MD simulations. As shown in Fig. 3, the evolution of the Li+ solvation environment, including with increasing fluorination degree, is revealed by performing MD simulations on the PE, TE and TF electrolyte systems. All systems are equilibrated for 5 ns to obtain stable simulation snapshots, radial distribution functions (RDFs), coordination number statistics, and typical Li+ solvation sheath structures. For the non-fluorinated PE system (Fig. 3a–c), the RDF curve shows a typical solvent-separated ion pair (SSIP) structure. Li+ is mainly coordinated with the oxygen atoms of PC and EMC molecules, with a total solvent coordination number of ∼3.4 (Fig. S6, SI), and few PF6− anions participate in the first solvation sheath of Li+. This solvent-dominated solvation structure leads to preferential decomposition of solvent molecules at the electrode interface,41,42 which is the root cause of unstable SEI formation, severe oxidative decomposition at high voltage, and uncontrolled Li dendrite growth.

After replacing PC with fluorinated cyclic carbonate TFPC, the single-fluorinated TE system (Fig. 3d–f) shows an obvious evolution of the solvation structure. This indicates that the introduction of TFPC weakens the strong coordination between non-fluorinated solvents and Li+, promotes the entry of PF6− anions into the first solvation sheath, and transforms the solvation structure from a pure solvent-dominated to partial anion-participated configuration.

This solvation structure evolution is further amplified in the fully fluorinated TF system (Fig. 3g–i). The dual fluorination of both cyclic and linear carbonates further optimizes the Li+ solvation sheath environment, with more PF6− anions entering the first solvation shell to form an F-solvent/anion-enriched solvation structure (Fig. S6, SI). This optimized structure brings two core advantages for high-voltage LMBs. On the one hand, the increased participation of PF6− in the solvation sheath reduces the total solvent coordination of Li+, promotes anion-derived/F-involved interfacial reactions, and provides sufficient fluorine sources for the in situ formation of LiF-rich EEIs.28,43 On the other hand, the enhanced coordination between TFPC and Li+ facilitates the Li+ desolvation and preferential decomposition of fluorinated carbonates at the anode interface, further enriching the fluorine content of the interphase. This gradient evolution of the solvation structure directly determines the subsequent interfacial stabilization effect and electrochemical performance of the three electrolyte systems.

Electrochemical kinetics of gradient fluorinated electrolytes

To further verify the solvation structure evolution revealed by MD simulations and elucidate the regulatory effect of gradient fluorination on the electrochemical reaction kinetics of the EC-free electrolyte systems, the three electrolytes were systematically characterized via Raman spectroscopy, LSV, CV, Tafel plot analysis, variable-temperature EIS, and the distribution of relaxation times (DRT) deconvolution.

To experimentally verify the modulated solvation microenvironment, Raman spectroscopy was performed on different electrolytes, with full-range spectra (Fig. S7, SI) and magnified characteristic bands (Fig. 4a–c). In the 1800–1860 cm−1 region corresponding to the CO stretching of cyclic carbonates,44 the TF and TE systems show a higher fraction of both free and Li+-coordinated TFPC molecules compared with conventional carbonates (Fig. 4a). This indicates the intrinsic solvation behavior of the fluorinated solvent toward Li+. In the 880–940 cm−1 region (Fig. 4b),45 the TF electrolyte presents a high proportion of free FEMC and an obvious trend of Li+ solvation with FEMC, whereas PE and TE electrolytes show more pronounced free EMC species. In the 500–560 cm−1 region (Fig. 4c), the TF electrolyte features a markedly intensified free FEMC signal originating from its weak solvating capability, together with a distinct coordinated FEMC peak.45 In comparison, more coordinated EMC species enter the primary solvation shell in PE and TE electrolytes, which matches well with the solvation species statistics from MD simulations.

LSV measurements were first performed to evaluate the high-voltage stability of the three electrolyte systems (Fig. 4d). For PE and TE electrolytes, noticeable oxidative decomposition currents are detected at potentials exceeding 4 V, while TF shows a negligible current increase in the same potential range. When the potential is further increased to 4.6 V (the inset of Fig. 4d), the oxidation currents of PE and TE increase obviously, in sharp contrast to the stable current response of TF. Notably, even at the 5.0 V cut-off potential, TF still exhibits the lowest current density among the three samples. These results collectively demonstrate that TF exhibits the best oxidation resistance and the widest electrochemical window, which is critical for high-voltage LMB applications.

The reductive decomposition behavior of the electrolytes, which directly determines the SEI formation process on the LMA, was investigated via LSV tests using Li‖Cu cells (Fig. 4e). The reductive current density of the non-fluorinated PE electrolyte increases sharply at 0.08 V (vs. Li/Li+), while the onset reduction potential shifts positively to 0.1 V for the TE electrolyte and 0.15 V for the TF electrolyte. Meanwhile, at a fixed potential of 0.1 V (vs. Li/Li+), the reductive current of the PE electrolyte is only 0.75 mA, significantly lower than those of both the TE electrolyte (1.26 mA) and the TF electrolyte (1.83 mA) (Fig. S8, SI). The positively shifted onset reduction potential and the higher reductive current indicate that fluorinated carbonates undergo preferential and controllable reductive decomposition on the Cu surface, which is consistent with the lowered LUMO energy levels of TFPC and FEMC from theoretical calculations, and lays the foundation for the formation of a robust SEI.

The anodic oxidation stability of the three electrolytes as a core parameter for high-voltage LMB compatibility was further evaluated with the CV tests (Fig. 4f). The non-fluorinated PE electrolyte exhibits a distinct irreversible oxidation peak at 5.1 V (vs. Li/Li+) (Fig. S9, SI), corresponding to the oxidative decomposition of non-fluorinated PC and EMC solvents. By contrast, the oxidation decomposition behavior is significantly suppressed in the single-fluorinated TE electrolyte, and the fully fluorinated TF electrolyte shows the highest anodic stability, with no obvious oxidation current below 5.5 V (vs. Li/Li+). This result confirms that the combination of TFPC and FEMC endows the electrolyte with excellent high-voltage compatibility indeed, which is in good agreement with the lowered HOMO energy levels of fluorinated carbonates from DFT calculations.

To quantitatively evaluate the Li+ transport and desolvation kinetics of the three electrolytes, variable-temperature EIS tests were conducted from 30 °C to 70 °C to calculate the Li+ charge transfer activation energy (Ea,ct) during the desolvation process (Fig. 4g). The fully fluorinated TF electrolyte exhibits the lowest desolvation activation energy (56.87 kJ mol−1) among the three systems, which is attributed to the moderate Li+ binding strength of fluorinated carbonates and the optimized anion-enriched solvation structure.19,46 This result is also well corroborated by the ESP distribution and Li+ binding energy calculations. The optimized charge distribution of fluorinated solvents reduces the energy barrier for Li+ to detach from the solvation sheath, thus accelerating the interfacial charge transfer process and facilitating the electrochemical reaction kinetics.

Tafel plot analysis and DRT deconvolution of EIS were further performed to gain in-depth insight into the interfacial reaction kinetics. As seen from the full Tafel comparison among PE, TE and TF electrolytes (Fig. S10, SI), the PE electrolyte, benefiting from low solution viscosity and high ionic conductivity, exhibits a higher exchange current density (i0) than the TE electrolyte and reaches a level close to the TF system. This indicates the favorable interfacial mass transport properties and charge transfer kinetics at the EEI of the TF electrolyte. DRT deconvolution was used to resolve the complex impedance into distinct relaxation processes corresponding to Li+ migration through the SEI (RSEI), interfacial charge transfer (Rct), and bulk ion diffusion (Rdiffusion).47,48 Compared with the PE and TE electrolytes, the TF electrolyte shows significantly reduced impedance in all relaxation processes, especially the charge transfer resistance and SEI film resistance (Fig. 4h, S11 and SI). Correspondingly, the three electrolyte systems exhibit distinct lithium-ion solvation sheath structures, which give rise to different energy barriers for ion transport in the bulk electrolyte, desolvation at the electrode–electrolyte interface, and mass transfer during electrochemical reactions (Fig. 4i). The PE electrolyte encounters a substantial transport barrier, which inevitably deteriorates its ion transport kinetics and subsequent lithium deposition behavior.19 In contrast, the TF electrolyte enables a lower energy barrier for interfacial desolvation and electrochemical reactions, thereby delivering superior electrochemical performance and enabling uniform, smooth lithium deposition. These results confirm that the gradient fluorination design not only optimizes bulk ion transport behavior but also constructs low-impedance, stable EEIs, collectively accelerating the overall electrochemical reaction kinetics of high-voltage LMBs.

Li deposition performance in fluorinated electrolytes

The cycling stability and deposition/dissolution reversibility of the LMA are core prerequisites for the practical application of high-voltage LMBs, which are directly determined by the structure and stability of the in situ formed SEI. The gradient fluorination design proposed in this work has been verified to optimize the Li+ solvation structure and accelerate electrochemical reaction kinetics. The Li deposition/dissolution reversibility, long-term cycling stability, and Li deposition morphology of the three EC-free electrolytes were further systematically evaluated via the Li‖Cu cells, Li‖Li cells, and scanning electron microscopy (SEM) characterization, thus elucidating the regulatory effect of gradient fluorination on the interfacial stability of the LMA.

CE is the most critical parameter to quantify the reversibility of Li deposition/dissolution,49 which can be evaluated via Li‖Cu cells at a current density of 1 mA cm−2 with a fixed areal capacity of 1 mAh cm−2 (Fig. 5a). For the non-fluorinated PE electrolyte, the cell delivers an average CE of only 80.2% in the initial 30 cycles, followed by a rapid CE decay. This severe performance degradation is attributed to the unstable SEI derived from the solvent-dominated solvation structure, which cannot suppress continuous parasitic reactions between the electrolyte and highly reactive Li metal, leading to massive consumption of active Li and electrolyte. For the single-fluorinated TE electrolyte, the Li‖Cu cell achieves a significantly improved average CE of 81.9% under the same test conditions, and maintains stable cycling for over 100 cycles before accelerated capacity fading. This confirms that the introduction of fluorinated cyclic carbonate TFPC optimizes the SEI formation process to a certain extent, but the residual non-fluorinated EMC still leads to continuous interfacial deterioration during long-term cycling. In sharp contrast, the fully fluorinated TF electrolyte enables a remarkable average CE of 94.7% and maintains highly stable cycling for over 200 cycles with negligible CE fluctuation. Upon further increasing the current density to a practically relevant level of 3 mA cm−2, the PE electrolyte fails to withstand high-current operation and exhibits a sharp capacity decay after only 20 cycles. The monofluorinated TE system can sustain cycling for 70 cycles but shows a significant deterioration in reversibility, while the TF electrolyte maintains continuous stable cycling with high CE, delivering a cycle life exceeding 150 cycles. Calculation of the average CE reveals that the TF system achieves a high value of 95.8%, demonstrating remarkably enhanced stability of LMAs (Fig. 5b). This significant improvement in Li deposition/dissolution reversibility directly confirms that the gradient fluorination design promotes the formation of a robust and stable SEI on the Li metal surface, which is in excellent agreement with the LUMO energy level calculations and experimental reductive decomposition behavior. To further evaluate the practical application potential under high-energy-density conditions, cycling tests were extended to higher areal capacities of 3 mAh cm−2 (Fig. S12). At 3 mAh cm−2, the TF electrolyte maintains stable Li plating/stripping with a high average CE of 97.3% and low polarization, significantly outperforming the PE and TE electrolytes.

To further investigate the long-term interfacial stability of the gradient fluorinated electrolytes during repeated Li plating/stripping, Li‖Li symmetric cells were assembled as well and subjected to galvanostatic cycling tests at various current densities. The cells with PE, TE, and TF electrolytes at 0.5 mA cm−2@1.0 mAh cm−2 exhibit a cycling lifespan of ∼200 h, 1100 h, and 1390 h, respectively, with distinct differences in voltage hysteresis evolution (Fig. 5c). For the non-fluorinated PE electrolyte, the cell shows obvious voltage fluctuation after only 160 h of cycling, indicating the formation of uncontrolled Li dendrites and an unstable SEI, which brings a severe risk of internal short circuit. The cell with the single-fluorinated TE electrolyte delivers a prolonged cycling lifespan, but similar voltage fluctuation appears after cycling for 800 h, followed by rapid failure within 1100 h. This is because partial fluorination modification cannot fully suppress the continuous growth of Li dendrites and the accumulation of dead Li during long-term cycling. Impressively, after full substitution of non-fluorinated PC and EMC with fluorinated TFPC and FEMC, the cell with the TF achieves an ultra-long stable cycling lifespan of over 1350 h, with ultralow and stable voltage hysteresis throughout the cycling process.

The rate performance of the LMA was further evaluated at stepwise increased current densities from 0.5 mA cm−2 to 5 mA cm−2, to verify the superiority of the fully fluorinated TF electrolyte under harsh operating conditions. The cells with PE and TE electrolytes exhibit significantly higher polarization potentials at all current densities, and even premature failure occurs at a high current density of 5 mA cm−2. Nevertheless, the cell with the TF electrolyte maintains a low and stable voltage hysteresis even at high current densities, and delivers excellent cycling stability without performance degradation. These results confirm that the high-quality SEI derived from the fully fluorinated electrolyte effectively inhibits parasitic reactions between the LMA and the electrolyte, reduces the accumulation of dead Li and interfacial impedance, and thus endows the LMA with excellent long-term cycling stability and rate capability.

The structure and stability of the SEI are widely recognized as the decisive factor for Li deposition morphology.19,49 To directly visualize the regulatory effect of the gradient fluorination design on Li deposition behavior, Li‖Cu cells were disassembled after 10 cycles at 1 mA cm−2@1.0 mA h cm−2 to characterize the top-view (Fig. 5e–g) and cross-sectional (Fig. 5h–j) morphology of the deposited Li. For the PE electrolyte (Fig. 5e and h), the Cu current collector surface is covered with numerous slender and disordered Li dendrites after cycling, presenting a loose and porous structure. This severe dendrite growth and mossy Li formation are direct evidence of an unstable SEI, which cannot guide uniform Li nucleation and deposition, leading to continuous side reactions, massive dead Li formation, and rapid performance degradation. For the TE electrolyte, the deposited Li presents a block-like morphology with reduced dendrite content, but there are still irregular pits and a small amount of dendritic Li on the surface (Fig. 5f). The cross-sectional image further reveals that the deposited Li layer still has a large range of porous structures in the inner region (Fig. 5i), despite the improved surface morphology compared with the PE system. This porous Li layer with a high specific surface area aggravates the direct contact between the electrolyte and active Li, leading to continuous electrolyte decomposition and active Li loss, which is the main reason for the accelerated capacity fading of the TE cell during long-term cycling. In addition, the thick and inhomogeneous SEI formed in the TE hinders Li+ migration, further inducing the formation of electrically isolated dead Li and reducing the cycling lifespan of LMA.

In sharp contrast, the Li deposited in the TF electrolyte exhibits an ultra-dense, smooth, and dendrite-free morphology, with no visible cracks or pores on the surface (Fig. 5g). The cross-sectional image further confirms that the deposited Li layer is highly compact with uniform thickness, without the porous structure observed in the PE and TE systems (Fig. 5j). This uniform and dense Li deposition is attributed to the robust LiF-rich SEI derived from F-solvent/anion-enriched solvation structures of the TF electrolyte, which can guide homogeneous Li+ flux and nucleation, effectively suppress Li dendrite growth, and isolate the direct contact between the electrolyte and LMA.50,51 Combined with the morphological evolution of the deposited Li layer in the three electrolytes, it can be concluded that the high-quality SEI is directly derived from the optimized Li+ desolvation process induced by the full fluorination design, which in turn delivers significantly improved Li cycling reversibility and stability.

Cycling performance and interfacial chemistry in fluorinated electrolytes

After verifying the significant improvement due to the fluorination design in LMA stability and Li deposition reversibility, the electrochemical performance of the three EC-free electrolytes in high-voltage Li‖NCM90 cells were further evaluated to elucidate the regulatory effect of fluorination on the CEI and structural stability of the 4.6 V-class Ni-rich cathode. The practical application potential of the TF electrolyte was comprehensively verified via room-temperature long-term cycling, rate capability, and high-temperature cycling tests, combined with multi-scale interfacial and structural characterization studies.

Li‖NCM90 cells were assembled with the PE, TE, and TF electrolytes, respectively. The cells were first activated at 0.1C for three cycles within the voltage range of 2.8–4.6 V at a room temperature of 25 °C, followed by long-term cycling at 1C (Fig. 6a). During 100 cycles, the cell with the PE electrolyte delivers an initial discharge specific capacity of 228 mAh g−1, which rapidly decays to 154 mAh g−1 after 100 cycles, corresponding to a low capacity retention of only 67.5%. This severe capacity fading is attributed to the continuous oxidative decomposition of non-fluorinated PC and EMC on the Ni-rich cathode surface at a high cut-off voltage of 4.6 V,8,52 which is consistent with the low anodic stability of the PE electrolyte revealed by LSV tests. The cell with the TE electrolyte shows significantly improved specific capacity and cycling stability compared with the PE group, which is ascribed to the enhanced anodic stability brought by the TFPC. Notedly, the cell with the TF electrolyte exhibits the best electrochemical performance, with an initial discharge specific capacity of 226.5 mAh g−1 and a retained capacity of 186.4 mAh g−1 after 100 cycles, achieving a high capacity retention of 82.3%. This remarkable cycling stability confirms that the combination of TFPC and FEMC enables excellent compatibility with the 4.6 V high-voltage Ni-rich cathode, which is in good agreement with the elevated HOMO energy levels and widened electrochemical stability window of the fluorinated carbonates.

To evaluate the rate capability of cells with different electrolytes and their capacity recovery ability after high-current operation, galvanostatic charge–discharge tests were performed at stepwise increased rates of 0.2, 0.5, 1, 2, and 5C, followed by returning to 0.2C (Fig. 6b). The cell with the PE electrolyte delivers discharge specific capacities of 219.5, 186.1, 157.2, 136, and 118.1 mAh g−1 at 0.2, 0.5, 1, 2, and 5C, respectively, with a recovered capacity of 196.5 mAh g−1 when the rate returns to 0.2C. The cell with the TE electrolyte shows improved rate performance, with specific capacities of 219.9, 208.7, 192.7, 178.3, and 157.9 mAh g−1 at the corresponding rates, and a recovered capacity of 203.6 mAh g−1. Impressively, the cell with the TF electrolyte exhibits the most outstanding rate capability, delivering ultra-high specific capacities of 223.8, 221.6, 211.8, 200.1, and 180.8 mAh g−1 at 0.2, 0.5, 1, 2, and 5C, respectively, and an almost 100% capacity recovery of 223.2 mA h g−1 when the rate returns to 0.2C. The capacity advantage of the fully fluorinated TF electrolyte becomes more prominent with increasing current density. This superior rate performance is attributed to two core factors. On the one hand, the thick, loose, and low ionic conductivity SEI/CEI formed in the PE leads to extremely high interfacial impedance and severe polarization at large current densities, which hinders normal charge–discharge processes. On the other hand, the TF facilitates Li+ desolvation and uniform Li deposition, constructing a thinner, more uniform, and high-quality SEI and CEI with high ionic conductivity. This significantly reduces the interfacial charge transfer resistance due to the low desolvation activation energy and fast electrochemical reaction kinetics of the TF electrolyte.

As is well known, the elevated-temperature (≥50 °C) operation accelerates electrochemical kinetics but inevitably aggravates electrolyte–electrode parasitic reactions, forming a thick and inhomogeneous CEI/SEI, inducing severe active Li loss, corrosive HF generation, transition metal (TM) dissolution from Ni-rich cathodes, and gas evolution, all of which deteriorate battery performance.12,53,54 To further figure out the ability of fluorinated electrolytes to mitigate these issues, the high-temperature performance of Li‖NCM90 cells were evaluated with the three electrolytes at 50 °C within 2.8–4.6 V (Fig. 6c). Over 100 cycles, the PE electrolyte cell delivers an initial discharge capacity of 240.8 mAh g−1 which sharply decays to 138.4 mAh g−1, with an ultra-low capacity retention of only 57.5% (Fig. S13, SI). It also shows a significant capacity drop from 223.1 to 189.5 mAh g−1 when the current density increases from 0.1C to 1C, indicating severe interfacial degradation under high-temperature and high-voltage conditions. The TE electrolyte delivers improved cycling stability (Fig. S14, SI), while the TF achieves the best high-temperature performance of an initial capacity of 243 mAh g−1, 82.6% capacity retention (200.9 mAh g−1) after 100 cycles, and negligible capacity decay during the rate switch from 0.1C to 1C (Fig. S15, SI).

Notably, the EC-free electrolyte design also endows the TF system with excellent low-temperature performance. At −20 °C, the TF-based battery delivers a reversible capacity of 112 mAh g−1 at 0.1C and maintains stable cycling at 1C, retaining 118 mAh g−1 after 90 cycles with a high average CE of 96.1% (Fig. S16, SI). In stark contrast, the monofluorinated TE-based battery shows severely degraded low-temperature performance. It only delivers a maximum capacity of 80.9 mAh g−1, and experiences continuous and rapid capacity fading, dropping to 64.2 mAh g−1 after 70 cycles and only 46 mAh g−1 after 100 cycles, with a low average CE of 83.1%. The PE electrolyte also achieves significantly better low-temperature cycling stability than the TE electrolyte due to the low viscosity and high ionic conductivity, with capacity retention slightly lower than the TF electrolyte (Fig. S16, SI). The corresponding galvanostatic charge–discharge profiles further validate the significantly improved low-temperature high-voltage performance of TE electrolyte with a flat discharge voltage plateau and much smaller polarization (Fig. S17, S18 and SI). This remarkable performance gap clearly demonstrates the superiority of the gradient fluorination EC-free electrolyte design for low-temperature LMB applications. Low-temperature rate measurements further reveal the critical influence of electrolyte transport properties. The PE electrolyte exhibits significantly superior rate performance at low temperature relative to the TE electrolyte, with capacity delivery slightly lower than or close to that of the TF electrolyte (Fig. S19, SI). At a high rate of 5C, both TE and TF fail to release effective reversible capacity due to deteriorated mass transport, while the PE system still retains a certain capacity output, verifying that ionic conductivity and solution viscosity are indispensable design considerations for electrolytes targeting low-temperature high-rate applications. Further contact ignition tests reveal that high-content fluorinated and flame-retardant components also effectively improve the operational safety of the TF system (Fig. S20, SI). Overall, fluorine substitution significantly enhances the thermal stability and low-temperature adaptability of carbonate solvents, and the fully fluorinated electrolyte outperforms conventional carbonate electrolytes in high/low-temperature performance, enabling superior thermal stability and long lifespan for high-voltage LMBs.

To unravel the underlying mechanism behind the excellent cathode performance enabled by the fully fluorinated TF electrolyte, the structural evolution and interfacial chemistry of the cycled NCM90 cathode were systematically characterized via multi-scale techniques. TEM was first employed to directly visualize the morphology and thickness of the CEI layer on the cycled NCM90 cathode (Fig. 6d–f). After 200 cycles in the PE electrolyte, the NCM90 cathode is covered by a thick and rough CEI layer with a thickness of ∼14.1 nm (Fig. 6g). This is derived from the severe and continuous oxidative decomposition of the non-fluorinated electrolyte at high voltage. After 200 cycles in the TE electrolyte, the NCM90 cathode presents a relatively uniform but still thick CEI layer with a thickness of ∼7.4 nm (Fig. 6h). In sharp contrast, after 200 cycles in the TF electrolyte, the NCM90 cathode exhibits an ultra-thin, dense, and uniform CEI layer with a thickness of only ∼4.7 nm (Fig. 6i). This thin and robust CEI not only facilitates fast Li+ transport across the CEI, but acts as a physical barrier to isolate the direct contact between the electrolyte and the highly active cathode surface, thus suppressing continuous parasitic reactions.

To elaborately analyze the chemical composition of the CEI on the NCM90 cathode, XPS was performed after 10 cycles in different electrolytes (Fig. 6g–i). In the C 1 s spectra of all three electrolytes (Fig. 6g), distinct chemical states of carbon are detected, including C–C/C–H, C–O, CO, and C–F (organic fluorine).12,55 In the O 1s spectra (Fig. 6h), the CO peak of the TF electrolyte is weaker than those of the TE and PE electrolytes, while the C–O signal is significantly stronger, indicating that the oxidative decomposition of organic electrolyte components is effectively suppressed in the TF electrolyte. In addition, a higher content of LiF is detected in the F 1 s spectrum of the TF electrolyte (Fig. 6i), which confirms that more inorganic fluorine-containing species are generated to form a more stable CEI. These results demonstrate that the full substitution of non-fluorinated PC and EMC with fluorinated TFPC and FEMC promotes the formation of a robust CEI on the cathode surface. It effectively inhibits the oxidative decomposition of the electrolyte and organic components, thus improving the cycling lifespan of the Ni-rich cathode material at high voltage.

Besides, XRD and SEM were further employed to evaluate the effect of the three electrolytes on the bulk structural stability of NCM90 cathodes after long-term high-voltage cycling (Fig. S21, SI). The intensity ratio of the (003) to (104) diffraction peaks (I003/I104), a key descriptor for the structural stability of Ni-rich layered cathodes, was calculated to quantify structural degradation.7,52 A higher ratio indicates superior structural integrity with suppressed Li+/Ni2+ cation mixing and irreversible phase transition, while a lower ratio signifies severe structural collapse. The pristine NCM90 has an I003/I104 ratio of 1.32, which decreases to 1.05, 1.22, and 1.30 for cathodes cycled in the PE, TE, and TF electrolytes, respectively (Fig. S22). These results confirm that the TF electrolyte effectively mitigates Li+/Ni2+ cation mixing and preserves the native layered structure of NCM90 even under 4.6 V high-voltage operation. Cross-sectional SEM images further verify the structural protection effect of the TF electrolyte (Fig. S23–S25, SI). For the cycled cathode in PE, widespread intergranular cracks and severe structural collapse are observed in most secondary particles (Fig. S23, SI). These detrimental cracks drastically increase electrode–electrolyte contact area, aggravate interfacial side reactions, and cause loss of electrical contact between active materials, directly evidencing the inability of conventional carbonate electrolytes to withstand the 4.6 V high cut-off voltage. The cycled cathode in TE shows only minor intergranular microcracks in partial secondary particles, with significantly inhibited structural degradation (Fig. S24, SI). In sharp contrast, the cycled cathode in TF maintains fully intact secondary particles with no obvious intergranular cracks (Fig. S25, SI), confirming that the TF electrolyte effectively protects the Ni-rich cathode from structural degradation even at a high voltage of 4.6 V.

Collectively, the fully fluorinated TF electrolyte promotes the formation of a more stable, thinner, and more uniform LiF-rich CEI on the high-voltage NCM90 cathode surface, while the non-fluorinated PE electrolyte forms a thick and inhomogeneous CEI. The robust and high-quality CEI facilitates fast Li+ migration, effectively protects the cathode material from structural collapse, and suppresses parasitic side reactions of the electrolyte under high-voltage and high-temperature conditions. Combined with the stable SEI on the LMA, the TF electrolyte simultaneously realizes the synchronous stabilization of both anode and cathode interfaces, thus significantly improving the cycling stability, rate capability, and high-temperature performance of 4.6 V high-voltage LMBs.

Conclusion

In summary, an EC-free fully fluorinated electrolyte with TFPC and FEMC is developed in this work for 4.6 V high-voltage LMBs, to solve the core bottlenecks of severe electrolyte oxidation and unstable EEIs in conventional systems and elucidate the regulatory effect of this gradient fluorination design. Benefiting from the synergistic effect of fluorinated TFPC and FEMC, the Li+ solvation structure was tailored into an F-solvent/anion-enriched configuration, reducing the Li+ desolvation energy barrier, and enabling the formation of robust LiF-rich interphases on both the Li metal anode and NCM90 cathode. Such optimized electrolytes achieve excellent Li deposition reversibility with an average CE of 95.8% over 150 cycles in Li‖Cu cells, ultra-long cycling stability over 1200 h in Li‖Li cells, and a high capacity retention of 82.3% (25 °C) and 82.6% (50 °C) after 100 cycles at 1C in 4.6 V Li‖NCM90 cells. More significantly, our contribution here provided an efficient yet simple design strategy for high-performance EC-free electrolytes, and rationally clarifies the underlying mechanism of gradual fluorination toward high-energy-density LMBs.
