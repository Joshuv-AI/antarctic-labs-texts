Isothermal-microwave pretreatment enhances the quality and oxidative stability of cold-pressed oil from short-term germinated sesame seeds
Authors: Run-Yang Zhang, Deng-Ju Cai, Lin-Xuan Wu, Wen-Ting Yin, Yu-Xiang Ma, Shu-Guang Gao, Hua-Min Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Enhancing the oxidative stability of cold-pressed sesame oil using green methods without high-temperature treatments remains challenging. Short-term germination is a common but limited solution. In this study, isothermal-microwave pretreatment (80 °C, 0–60 min) was applied to short-term germinated sesame seeds, followed by cold pressing. Results showed that moderate-duration pretreatments (10–30 min) yielded higher oil extraction rates (81.06%–82.22%) than the 0-min pretreatment (79.71%). Moreover, the β-tocopherol, γ-tocopherol, sesamolin, and total phenolics contents of the oils increased by 17.22%–35.14%, 10.90%–17.28%, 16.18%–25.60%, and 7.24%–26.19%, respectively, resulting in enhanced radical scavenging capacity and oxidative stability. This was because isothermal-microwave pretreatment promoted their migration into the oil bodies. Furthermore, the oil obtained from short-term germinated seeds pretreated with isothermal-microwave for 15 min also exhibited better frying stability than oil from seeds without pretreatment. Thus, this work presents a promising approach for producing high-quality cold-pressed oil with enhanced oxidative stability.

Introduction

Sesame (Sesamum indicum L.), an ancient oilseed crop, has been cultivated and processed for thousands of years worldwide (Khaldi, Tounsi, Balti, Chaker, & Kechaou, 2024; Qu, Zhao, Li, & Lv, 2025). Hot-pressed sesame oil is obtained by pressing sesame seeds after roasting at 130–200 °C (Mostashari & Mousavi Khaneghah, 2024; Gholamhoseini & Dolatabadian, 2024). Although this oil has a desirable flavor and is highly favored by consumers, its use is limited to flavoring applications, which restricts the market scale. Moreover, high-temperature processing might adversely affect the protein quality of the resulting sesame cake (Chang et al., 2024; Yin et al., 2023). In contrast, cold pressing is a low-temperature, solvent-free method for extracting oil. Developing cooking oils from cold-pressed sesame oil is a promising path for the sesame processing industry, as it preserves natural nutrients, increases added value, supports sustainable production and offers economic-related opportunities for both growers and processors (Hu et al., 2024; Huang et al., 2023; Tiozon et al., 2023). However, it remains a challenge to enhance the oxidative stability of cold-pressed sesame oil using green technologies that avoid high-temperature treatments.

Short-term germination is considered to improve the oxidative stability of cold-pressed sesame oil. This process involves soaking the seeds to allow water absorption and incubating them at 28–30 °C for a short duration (< 24 h) (Chang et al., 2024; Hu et al., 2023; Huang et al., 2023; Qu et al., 2025). It is derived from normal germination (25–35 °C, > 72 h), which can enhance the phenolic acids in sesame seeds (Gholamhoseini & Dolatabadian, 2024; Hu et al., 2024; Huang et al., 2023). Short-term germination can also increase total phenolic content in sesame seed by 2.1-fold (Dossou et al., 2021; Tiozon et al., 2023). However, it has limited potential for further development, as prolonged germination inevitably leads to a significant reduction in oil yield and an increase in free fatty acid content (Chang et al., 2024; Dossou et al., 2021; Yang et al., 2025). Therefore, more research is required to further enhance product quality.

Isothermal-microwave and traditional microwave pretreatment are both green methods based on microwaves, but they differ significantly. Isothermal-microwave processing employs a high-precision infrared temperature sensor for real-time monitoring of the sample, and the system automatically modulates microwave power based on the instantaneous temperature feedback to maintain the sample at the target temperature during irradiation (Chen et al., 2024; Qin et al., 2025). Traditional microwave pretreatment, which typically employs fixed-power microwave equipment without temperature control, has been extensively studied in research on hot-pressed oil production (Hu et al., 2023; Huang et al., 2023; Mohamed Ahmed et al., 2020). This method can enhance the antioxidant capacity of oil by promoting the migration of antioxidants into the oil; however, it relies primarily on thermal effects, which commonly result in seed temperatures exceeding 110 °C (El-Beltagi et al., 2022; Muangrat, Chalermchart, Pannasai, & Osiriphun, 2020; Yang et al., 2025; Yin et al., 2023). Hence, traditional microwave pretreatment is unsuitable for cold-pressed sesame oil production, as it cannot avoid high temperatures (Chang et al., 2024; Gholamhoseini & Dolatabadian, 2024). Therefore, this study attempts the use of isothermal-microwave equipment to maintain processing temperatures, ensuring that the production process complies with the standards for cold-pressed sesame oil. Moreover, the application effect needs to be comprehensively evaluated. This approach mainly may face challenges in the practical implementation, such as limited scalability to industrial production, variability across sesame cultivars, and increased processing costs. Nonetheless, it still might present a promising green strategy for enhancing the quality of cold-pressed sesame oil.

To further improve the quality and oxidative stability of cold-pressed oil from short-term germinated sesame seeds, the isothermal-microwave was applied to ensure that the pretreatment and pressing processes met the temperature requirements (80 °C) for cold-pressed processes. The influence of isothermal-microwave pretreatment on the major components and antioxidant constituents of short-term germinated sesame seeds, as well as on the lipid profile, stability, and functional properties of the resulting cold-pressed oil, were systematically analyzed. This research might provide a new green strategy for enhancing the quality of cold-pressed sesame oil.

Materials and methods

Materials and chemical reagents

The sesame seed (Zhuzhi-22) were obtained from the Henan Academy of Agricultural Sciences (Zhengzhou, China). Soybean oil and sunflower oil (Golden Dragon Fish® brand) were purchased from Yihai Kerry Investments Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). The analytical reagent, i.e., methanol, n-hexane, acetonitrile, anhydrous ethanol, petroleum ether, isopropanol, and butylated hydroxytoluene (BHT), and methylene chloride were purchased from Tianjin Kemiou Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd. (Tianjin, China). Harman (1-methyl-9H-pyrido[3,4-b]indole, 98%) and norharman (9H-pyrido[3,4-b]indole, 98%) were sourced from Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Inc. (Santa Cruz, CA, USA). Sesamin (>98%) and sesamolin (>98%) were acquired from Sigma-Aldrich (Shanghai, China). The chromatography pure reagent, i.e., acetonitrile, methanol, isopropanol, dichloromethane was purchased from Merck (Darmstadt, Germany). Glutaraldehyde fixative for electron microscopy (Product number: 02607-BA) and embedding resin (Product number: 90529–77-4) was purchased from Structure Probe, Inc. (West Chester, Pennsylvania, USA). Osmium Tetroxide was purchased from Ted Pella, Inc. (Redding, CA 96003, USA).

Oils preparation from sesame seeds pretreated with short-term germination and isothermal-microwave

Short-term germinated sesame seeds were prepared following a previously published study (Chang et al., 2024). Briefly, sesame seeds were rinsed five times and then soaked in distilled water (1,5, w/v) for 2 h. The seeds were evenly distributed in a tray to a depth of 1 cm and incubated in the dark at 30 °C and 95% relative humidity for 12 h using a constant temperature and humidity incubator (LHS-250H, Shengyuan Co., Ltd.). Then, drying was carried out at 50 °C until a moisture content of (8.0 ± 0.1)% was achieved, measured in compliance with ISO standard 662 (2016). The short-term germinated sesame seeds were then subjected to isothermal-microwave pretreatment using a MAS-II Plus microwave system (Sineo Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China) at a constant sesame seed temperature of (80 ± 1.0) °C for varying durations: 0, 5, 10, 15, 30, 40, 50, and 60 min. The resulting samples were labeled as SS-GM0, SS-GM5, SS-GM10, SS-GM15, SS-GM30, SS-GM40, SS-GM50, and SS-GM60, respectively. The time gradient was established based on transmission electron microscope observations of oil body damage, aiming to systematically investigate oil quality changes following different pretreatment durations. The temperature calibration of the equipment was performed by using a standard thermometer to measure the temperatures of multiple pure substances with known melting points while they were subjected to treatment at set temperatures. The SS-GM0 served as the untreated control for germinated seeds. Separately, sesame seeds without any pretreatment were labeled SS-C, and served as the overall control sample. The aforementioned sesame seeds, namely SS-C, SS-GM0, SS-GM5, SS-GM10, SS-GM15, SS-GM30, SS-GM40, SS-GM50, and SS-GM60, were directly pressed at room temperature using an 6YZ-180 oil press (Bafang, Zhengzhou, China) under 60 MPa for 20 min per cycle, with three consecutive pressing cycles performed. The resulting oils were labeled as CSO, CSO-GM0, CSO-GM5, CSO-GM10, CSO-GM15, CSO-GM30, CSO-GM40, CSO-GM50, and CSO-GM60, respectively.

Micromorphology of sesame seed

Pretreatment followed previously published methods (Chang et al., 2024; Yin et al., 2023). Briefly, sesame seed tissue (1 mm3) was placed in a centrifuge tube containing 2.5% glutaraldehyde solution at 4 °C for 24 h. After discarding the fixative, sample was rinsed by PBS (phosphate buffer saline) buffer, post-fixed by osmium tetroxide (1%), washed by PBS (0.1 mol/L, pH 7.4). Sequential dehydration was performed using 30%, 50%, 70%, and 90% ethanol, followed by two changes of absolute ethyl alcohol. Sample was infiltrated with acetone: embedding agent (1,1, v/v) for 3 h, then with a 1:3 (v/v) mixture for 4 h, and finally immersed in pure embedding agent overnight at 37 °C. The sample was placed into embedding molds filled with fresh embedding agent and polymerized at 70 °C for 12–48 h. The resulting resin blocks were stored for subsequent sectioning. Ultrathin sections of 70–90 nm were cut from the resin blocks of embedded samples, stained with uranyl acetate (8–15 min) and lead citrate (5–10 min). After air-drying, sample was examined by a HITACHI-HT7800 transmission electron microscope (Hitachi High-Tech, Tokyo, Japan).

Lipidomic analysis using ultra-performance liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (UPLC-MS/MS)

Pretreatment and detection method followed previously published studies (Qu et al., 2025; Wu et al., 2025). For the pretreatment of sesame seeds: Freeze-dried seeds were ground, and 1 mL of methyl tert-butyl ether:methanol (3,1, v/v) containing an internal standard mixture was added. A Following 15 min of vortexing, 200 μL ultrapure water was added, and the mixture was vortexed (1 min) and centrifuged (12,000 rpm, 10 min). The 200 μL organic layer was collected, dried under vacuum, and reconstituted in 200 μL solvent S1 (acetonitrile:isopropanol, 1:1, v/v) for UPLC-MS/MS analysis. For the pretreatment of oil: A 10 mg oil sample was dissolved in solvent S1 and vortexed. The solution was mixed with an internal standard in solvent S1, vortexed, and centrifuged at 4 °C to obtain the supernatant for analysis. Following the pretreatment described above, 120 μL of supernatant was analyzed by UPLC-MS/MS system. Chromatographic separation was carried out on an ExionLC AD UPLC system with a Thermo Accucore™ C30 column (2.6 μm, 2.1 × 100 mm). The mobile phase consisted of solvent A (acetonitrile:water, 60:40 v/v, with 0.1% formic acid and 10 mmol/L ammonium formate) and solvent B (acetonitrile:isopropanol, 10:90 v/v, with 0.1% formic acid and 10 mmol/L ammonium formate), at a flow rate of 0.35 mL/min, column temperature of 45 °C, and injection volume of 2 μL. Mass spectrometry was performed on a Sciex QTRAP 6500+ system with an ESI source in both positive and negative ion modes using Analyst 1.6.3 software. Data were acquired in multiple reaction monitoring mode with nitrogen as the collision gas, and declustering potential and collision energy were optimized for each transition.

Determination of major endogenous antioxidants of sesame seeds and oils

Tocopherols content of sesame seeds and oils

Tocopherols content of sesame seeds

The sesame seed samples required prior extraction of liposoluble constituents using a Soxhlet apparatus with petroleum ether at 40 °C. The solvent was then removed by vacuum rotary evaporation to obtain the liposoluble constituent. The liposoluble constituent was determined according to the ISO 9936 (2016). Briefly, it was dissolved in hexane–isopropanol (99,1, v/v) in an amber volumetric flask, with 0.1 g of BHT added to inhibit oxidation. It was then analyzed using a Waters e2695 HPLC system (Waters Corporation, MA, USA) equipped with an NH₂ column (250 × 4.6 mm, 5 μm) and a Waters 2475 fluorescence detector. The flow rate was 0.8 mL/min, the column temperature was maintained at 40 °C, and fluorescence detection was performed at an excitation wavelength of 298 nm and an emission wavelength of 325 nm.

Tocopherols content of cold-pressed oils

The tocopherols content of the cold-pressed oil samples was determined according to ISO 9936 (2016). Briefly, the oil was dissolved in a hexane-isopropanol and analyzed using a HPLC system, as described in Section 2.5.1.1.

Sesamin and sesamolin content of sesame seeds and oils

The analysis was performed following the method described by Huang et al. (2023). Briefly, 250 mg of oils or seeds liposoluble constituent (preparation method was described in 2.5.1.1) was dissolved in n-hexane, filtered (0.22 μm membrane), and analyzed by HPLC (Waters 2695 with 2475 fluorescence detector, MA, USA). Separation was performed on a Sunfire Prep Silica column (4.6 × 250 mm, 5 μm) using isocratic n-hexane/tetrahydrofuran (98:2, v/v) at 0.8 mL/min, 30 °C, with 10 μL injection. Fluorescence detection: excitation wavelength, 295 nm; emission wavelength, 330 nm. Analytes were identified by comparing their retention times to those of authentic standards, and quantified using calibration curves constructed from peak area versus standard concentration.

Total phenols content of sesame seeds and oils

Detection method followed previously published study (Zhang et al., 2025). Briefly, the oils or seeds liposoluble constituent was dissolved in n-hexane and reacted with 10% sodium carbonate and Folin–Ciocalteu reagent for 90 min. The absorbance at 710 nm was recorded on an X8 spectrophotometer (Shanghai Metash Instruments Co., Ltd., China) and reported as milligrams of gallic acid equivalents (GAE) per 100 g of oil.

Phenols compounds composition by UPLC–Orbitrap-MS of sesame seeds and oils

Phenolic profiles were analyzed using a modified method from Hou et al. (2024). For sesame seeds, samples were ground, mixed with 0.5 mL of 80% aqueous methanol containing 0.2% vitamin C, vortexed, ultrasonically treated for 30 min, and centrifuged to obtain the supernatant for analysis. For oils, 50 mg was transferred to a centrifuge tube, mixed with an equal volume of methanol containing 0.2% vitamin C, vortexed, incubated at −20 °C for 30 min, and centrifuged to obtain the supernatant for analysis. The sesame seed and oil extracts were analyzed by UPLC–Orbitrap-MS (Thermo Fisher Scientific) using an ACQUITY UPLC HSS T3 column (1.8 μm, 2.1 × 50 mm) at 40 °C. The mobile phase consisted of water and acetonitrile, both containing 0.1% acetic acid, under a gradient from 90:10 (v/v) at 0–2 min to 40:60 at 6 min, held until 8 min, followed by re-equilibration to initial conditions by 12 min. Mass spectrometry was performed in negative ion mode on a Q Exactive instrument with a heated ESI source. Data were acquired and processed using Xcalibur 4.1 and TraceFinder 4.1 Clinical (Thermo Scientific).

Determination of physicochemical properties of oils

The peroxide value and acid value were measured following ISO 3960 (2017) and ISO 660 (2020), respectively. Color value was assessed by an PFXi880 automatic colorimeter (Lovibond, UK) following ISO 27608 (2010). The non-enzymatic browning index (BI) was measured based on the method of Hu et al. (2024). Briefly, oil was dissolved in n-hexane (1,8, w/v), and the absorbance (420 nm) was recorded as the indicator of BI by a X8 UV-VIS spectrophotometer (Shanghai Metash Instruments Co., Ltd., China).

Free radical scavenging capacity of oils

The assay performed following the protocol of Segoviano-León et al. (2025). For DPPH (2,2-Diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl) radical scavenging assay, oil (0.1 mL) of was mixed with 0.1 mmol/L DPPH solution (3.9 mL) of and incubated in the dark for 30 min; absorbance was then measured at 510 nm. For the ABTS (2,2′-Azino-bis(3-ethylbenzothiazoline-6-sulfonic acid)) radical scavenging assay, the ABTS·+ working solution was prepared by oxidizing ABTS with potassium persulfate and incubating in the dark for 12 h. The solution was diluted to an absorbance of 0.70 ± 0.05 at 734 nm. Then, 0.1 mL of sample was mixed with 3.9 mL of the ABTS·+ solution, incubated in the dark for 10 min, and the absorbance at 734 nm was measured. For the FRAP (ferric reducing antioxidant power) assay, the working FRAP reagent was prepared fresh by mixing acetate buffer (300 mmol/L, pH 3.6), TPTZ solution (10 mmol/L in 40 mmol/L HCl), and FeCl₃·6H₂O solution (20 mmol/L) at a ratio of 10:1:1 (v/v/v). A sample solution (0.1 mL) was added to 3.9 mL of the FRAP reagent, vortexed, and incubated at 37 °C for 30 min in the dark. The absorbance was then measured at 593 nm against a reagent blank.

Oxidative stability index of oils

The oxidative stability index (OSI) of the oils was assessed using a Rancimat 734 instrument (Metrohm, Herisau, Switzerland) according to ISO 6886 (2016). Briefly, 3 g of oil was placed in a glass reaction vessel, then inserted into a light-tight heating well. The sample was heated at 110 °C under an airflow of 20 L/h, and the induction period was taken as the OSI value.

Thermostability of oils

Thermogravimetric analysis was performed according to previously published methods (Gao, Chen, He, Shi, & Zeng, 2024). 10 mg of oil was analyzed by a NETZSCH STA-449C analyzer (Germany). The air flow was 50 mL/min and heating rate was 10 °C/min. The mass loss as a function of temperature was noted, and the thermogravimetric data was used to create the derivative thermogravimetric curve.

Frying performance of oils

Deep frying procedure

The frying procedure was carried out according to previous studies (Lou et al., 2025; Zhang, Kong, Qin, Liu, & Wang, 2024). Briefly, fresh tofu was cut into uniform pieces (15.0 ± 0.1 g), and three pieces were fried per cycle in a deep fryer (HX-81, 290 × 440 × 310 mm; Heng Xing, China) at 160 °C for 1 min. Before introducing the next batch, a 30-min interval was allowed, during which the fried tofu was placed in a wire basket over the fryer to drain excess oil for 15 min, followed by 5 min of oil stirring to ensure homogeneity. After this interval, the fresh batch was added to initiate the next frying cycle. Oil samples were collected at various frying time points and used for analysis.

Determination of fatty acid composition

The determination was performed according to AOCS method Ce 1-62 (2009). Methyl esters were prepared from 500 mg of oil and analyzed by an 7890 A gas chromatography system (Agilent, Santa Clara, USA) equipped with an HP-88 column (100 m × 0.25 mm, 0.2 μm film thickness). The relative proportion of each fatty acid was calculated using the area normalization method.

Determination of polar component, p-anisidine values, and iodine value

The polar component was measured immediately after each frying batch using a Testo 270 edible oil quality tester (Khor et al., 2020). The p-anisidine value (p-AV) and iodine value were measured following ISO 6885 (2016) and ISO 3961 (2024), respectively.

Determination of heterocyclic amine content

The determination followed the method of Yang et al. (2025). Briefly, oil was mixed with acetonitrile, vortexed for 5 min, sonicated for 10 min, and centrifuged. The supernatant was then combined with n-hexane and re-centrifuged to separate the acetonitrile layer. The extract was purified using a PCX solid-phase extraction column and eluted with methanol containing 5% (v/v) ammonium hydroxide. The eluate was dried under nitrogen, reconstituted in methanol, and filtered (0.22 μm) prior to analysis. Heterocyclic amines were quantified by HPLC–ESI-MS/MS using an ACQUITY UPLC BEH C18 column (Waters) with a gradient of ammonium formate (10 mmol/L, pH 6.8) and acetonitrile. MS detection was performed in positive ion mode.

Determination of volatile flavor compounds

The determination followed the method of Yin et al. (2023) using headspace solid-phase microextraction (HS-SPME). 3 g of oil and 10 μL of 4-nonanol (internal standard) were placed in a sealed vial and incubated at 60 °C for 20 min with stirring. The fiber was then exposed to the headspace for 50 min and desorbed in a GC injector (250 °C, splitless mode, 5 min). Compounds were separated on an HP-5MS capillary column using helium as carrier gas and analyzed by GC–MS in electron ionization mode (m/z 33–400). Tentative identification was based on NIST 17 library matching and retention indices from a C5–C30 n-alkane series. Relative contents (%) were calculated by area normalization.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using Origin 9.1 (OriginLab, Northampton, MA, USA) and SPSS Statistics 23.0 (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA). The data were obtained from three independent experiments. Results are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Duncan's multiple range test (p < 0.05) was applied for pairwise comparisons, and Pearson's correlation analysis was used to assess relationships between variables.

Results and discussion

Effect of isothermal-microwave pretreatment on the seed morphology and the oil yield

As observed from the TEM images (Fig. 1), the cellular structures of both raw sesame seeds (SS-C) and germinated sesame seeds without isothermal-microwave pretreatment (SS-GM0) remained highly intact, with clearly defined cell walls and distinct organelles. The oil bodies appeared as regular circles or ellipses, uniform in size and densely packed. As the duration of isothermal (80 °C) microwave pretreatment increased from 5 to 30 min (i.e., SS-GM5, SS-GM10, SS-GM15, SS-GM30), the morphology of oil bodies within the sesame seeds gradually became irregular, and the cell walls progressively degraded. Consequently, intracellular contents may begin to leak and interact more frequently with each other. Following microwave pretreatment exceeding 30 min (SS-GM40, SS-GM50, SS-GM60), the oil bodies underwent complete morphological transformation, and the overall cellular architecture was almost completely destroyed, resulting in abundant cell wall fragments (Hu et al., 2023; Muangrat et al., 2020; Qin et al., 2025). These structural changes might not only have influenced the efficiency of oil migration during mechanical pressing but also intensified interactions among intracellular components. This might further have affected both the physicochemical properties and the profile of minor constituents in the extracted oil. As shown in Table S1 (Supplementary Material), the moisture content of sesame seed samples showed no significant variation (8.06%–8.24%, p = 0.836); the oil samples also showed no significant difference in moisture content (0.44%–0.47%, p = 0.949). Despite the slight reduction in oil content of sesame seeds after germination, the oil yields (Table S2, Supplementary material) from isothermal-microwave pretreated samples (42.30%–43.94%) were nearly equivalent to that of the CSO-GM0 (42.25%) and CSO (43.99%) (p = 0.716). However, CSO-GM15, CSO-GM30, and CSO-GM45 exhibited significant higher oil extraction rate (81.06%–82.22%) compared to CSO-GM0 (79.71%) and CSO (80.57%) (p = 0.039). Effect of isothermal-microwave pretreatment on the lipidomics of seeds and oil

In order to verify the impact of pretreatment on lipid composition, based on the results of oxidative stability and antioxidants content in following section, representative sesame seed samples (SS-GM15, processed at the optimal isothermal-microwave duration, together with the control samples SS-C and SS-GM0) and corresponding oil samples (CSO, CSO-GM0, CSO-GM15) were selected for lipidomic analysis. As shown in the Fig. 2A, there were clearly differences in lipid quantities between sesame seeds and sesame oil. Among glycerolipids, triacylglycerols (TG) and diacylglycerols (DG) were present in significantly higher amounts in sesame seeds compared to sesame oil, whereas other glycerolipid types such as digalactosyldiacylglycerol (DGDG) and monogalactosyldiacylglycerol (MGDG) were almost undetectable in sesame oil (p < 0.001). Additionally, free fatty acids (FFA) and phospholipids (PLs), i.e., phosphatidic acid (PA), phosphatidylethanolamine (PE), and phosphatidylcholine (PC), were generally found increased concentrations in sesame seeds than in sesame oil. This indicated that during oil pressing, some lipids were either removed or transformed, leading to changes in the types and quantities of lipids in sesame oil (Mostashari & Mousavi Khaneghah, 2024; Qu et al., 2025; Wu et al., 2025). Fig. 2B revealed that germination pretreatment (SS-GM0) reduced the proportion of TG relative to SS-C. This reduction was might due to increased lipase activity during germination, resulting in the breakdown of TG into FFAs and other lipids. The proportions of PLs such as PC and PE increased, indicating that germination promoted cell membrane metabolism and restructuring (Chang et al., 2024; Gholamhoseini & Dolatabadian, 2024; Hu et al., 2023). Germination also altered the proportions of other lipids, including lysophospholipids (LPC, LPE, etc.) and sphingolipids (Cer, HexCer, etc.). Subsequent isothermal-microwave pretreatment further decreased the TG proportion in germinated seeds (SS-GM15), possibly because it accelerated lipid degradation and conversion. It also increased the proportions of PLs, particularly LPC and LPE, suggesting enhanced cell membrane metabolic activity (Escalante-Aburto et al., 2025; Huang et al., 2023; Qu et al., 2025). Changes in the proportions of sphingolipids and sterol esters (CE) suggested structural remodeling of cell walls and membranes. These changes in seed lipid composition consequently influenced the lipid profile of cold-pressed sesame oil (Dossou et al., 2021; Wu et al., 2025). In particular, CSO-GM0 exhibited a lower TG proportion compared to CSO, with CSO-GM15 having the lowest TG content. Phospholipid levels increased in line with these changes. Fig. 2C and D provided further insights into which lipid components in the oil samples underwent obvious changes during processing. Compared to CSO and CSO-GM0, CSO-GM15 exhibited a reduced relative abundance of specific glycerides, including TG(16:0/18:1/18:2), TG(16:1/16:1/18:2), TG(16:0/18:0/18:2), DG(18:1/18:2), and DG(16:0/18:3). In contrast, the proportions of some PLs, particularly PE(18:1/18:1) and PI(18:1/18:1) were increased. Additionally, the levels of FFAs rose notably, primarily FFA(18,1) and FFA(18,2). Therefore, germination combined with isothermal-microwave pretreatment had a more pronounced effect on the lipid composition of cold-pressed sesame oil compared to germination alone, primarily by reducing TG content and increasing the levels of PLs and FFAs. Effect of isothermal-microwave pretreatment on the endogenous antioxidants

As shown in Fig. 3, the application of isothermal-microwave pretreatment for varying durations following germination pretreatment exerted a pronounced influence on the levels of common minor constituents in both sesame seeds and cold-pressed sesame oils. An appropriate pretreatment duration (e.g., 15 min) enhanced the concentrations of these compounds in the oil, whereas prolonged exposure (>40 min) led to a marked decline (Aragão & Maximo, 2024; Muangrat et al., 2020; Yin et al., 2023). Specifically, regarding sesame lignans, the sesamin content of SS-C (4494.65 mg/kg) slightly less than that in SS-GM0 (4575.82 mg/kg). However, no significant differences were observed between SS-C, SS-GM0, and samples treated with isothermal-microwave for 5 to 40 min (SS-GM5 to SS-GM40) (p = 0.138). A similar trend was observed in cold-pressed sesame oils, where sesamin levels ranged from 3877.29 to 3976.36 mg/kg, with no significant differences among them (p = 0.083). For sesamolin in sesame seeds, SS-GM5, SS-GM10, and SS-GM15 exhibited comparable levels, which were slightly higher than those in SS-GM0 and significantly higher than in SS-C, SS-GM50, and SS-GM60. In cold-pressed sesame oil, CSO-GM15 (2827.43 mg/kg) and CSO-GM30 (2843.73 mg/kg) contained significantly higher sesamolin than CSO (1655.33 mg/kg) and CSO-GM0 (2264.11 mg/kg) (p < 0.001). With increasing duration of isothermal-microwave treatment, sesamin and sesamolin exhibited minor losses, might due to oxidative degradation or transformation during the pretreatment process (Amakhmakh et al., 2024; Escalante-Aburto et al., 2025; Xu et al., 2026). In terms of γ-tocopherol, SS-GM0 (503.64 mg/kg) exhibited a higher value than SS-C (463.73 mg/kg), due to germination enhancing enzymatic activity within the sesame seeds. This enhancement not only promoted the biosynthesis of tocopherols but also facilitated the breakdown of cell walls and the release of their constituents, thereby contributing to increased content and migration of γ- and β-tocopherol (Aragão & Maximo, 2024; El-Beltagi et al., 2022; Wang et al., 2025). The γ-tocopherol content gradually decreased as the duration of isothermal-microwave pretreatment extended. The changes of γ-tocopherol in sesame seeds also led to corresponding changes in the oils (Escalante-Aburto et al., 2025; Muangrat et al., 2020). The CSO from SS-C had lower γ-tocopherol content than all others. The SS-GM0 had the highest γ-tocopherol content than other sesame seed samples, however, the γ-tocopherol content of CSO-GM0 (780.16 mg/kg) was lower than many oils, such as CSO-GM5, CSO-GM10, CSO-GM15, and CSO-GM30. The content of β-tocopherol was lower than that of γ-tocopherol but followed a similar trend. For instance, CSO-GM10 (640.30 mg/kg) and CSO-GM15 (607.86 mg/kg) showed significantly higher β-tocopherol content compared to CSO-GM0 (473.80 mg/kg) and CSO (329.27 mg/kg) (p < 0.001). Hence, based on germination pretreatment, isothermal-microwave with moderate pretreatment times (e.g., 10 or 15 min) disrupted the cellular structure, promoting the migration of tocopherols into the oil bodies (El-Beltagi et al., 2022; Escalante-Aburto et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2025). This allowed more tocopherols to be harvested along with the oil during pressing, finally increasing the tocopherol content of the oil (Aragão & Maximo, 2024; Huang et al., 2023; Koishi et al., 2025). However, as the duration of isothermal-microwave extended, it might exacerbate the oxidative degradation of tocopherols in both the seeds, thereby reducing tocopherols content in oil.

In terms of total phenols content, SS-C (960.33 mg/kg) and SS-GM0 (1093.99 mg/kg) exhibited relatively low values. The application of isothermal-microwave pretreatment to germinated sesame seeds further increased the total phenolic content, with the highest value observed in SS-GM15 (1235.62 mg/kg). For the oil samples, CSO-GM15 (348.72 mg/kg), CSO-GM30 (374.97 mg/kg), and CSO-GM40 (368.61 mg/kg) showed significantly higher contents compared to CSO (203.29 mg/kg), CSO-GM0 (297.16 mg/kg), and other oil samples subjected to germination and isothermal-microwave pretreatment (ranging from 316.06 mg/kg to 336.74 mg/kg) (p < 0.001). The reasons for these changes are as follows: during germination, polyphenols in sesame seeds may have become more extractable due to the breakdown of cell walls, while some enzymatic reactions may have generated new phenolic compounds (Amakhmakh et al., 2024; Qu et al., 2025). Short-duration isothermal-microwave pretreatment disrupted the cellular structure, facilitating the release of more total phenols from the cells and thereby increasing the phenolic content in the oil (Escalante-Aburto et al., 2025; Qin et al., 2025). However, prolonged isothermal-microwave treatment, both in seeds and oil samples, might have led to oxidative polymerization of some phenolic compounds, forming larger molecules and reducing the extractable total phenolic content. According to Table S3 (see supplementary material), SS-GM0 and SS-GM15 showed clearly increases in trans-ferulic acid, caffeic acid, vanillic acid, p-hydroxycinnamic acid, and vanillin compared to SS-C. Additionally, new components such as luteoloside, isorhamnetin, and isoliquiritigenin were detected. This may be attributed to the breakdown of cell walls promoted by germination and microwave treatment, making these compounds more extractable (Hou et al., 2025; Huang et al., 2023). After pressing to produce sesame oil, components such as phthalic acid, 3,4-dihydroxybenzoic acid, and 4-hydroxybenzoic acid were not detected. This could be due to the poor lipid solubility of some polyphenolic components, which may not have been extracted along with the oil during pressing (Hou et al., 2025; Mostashari & Mousavi Khaneghah, 2024; Wang et al., 2025). In SS-GM15, the content of trans-ferulic acid increased clearly compared to SS-GM0, while the content of trans-cinnamic acid decreased markedly. This suggests that the effects of isothermal-microwave pretreatment were dual: on one hand, it promoted the migration of certain components, while on the other hand, it may have caused oxidation of some compounds or facilitated reactions with non-lipid components, leading to their consumption and a subsequent decrease in oil (El-Beltagi et al., 2022; Mostashari & Mousavi Khaneghah, 2024; Wu et al., 2025).

Effect of isothermal-microwave pretreatment on the physicochemical properties of oils

As shown in Fig. 4A, although the acid values and peroxide values of all oil samples were far below the quality standards for edible oils, the effects of different pretreatments on these values warranted attention. The acid value of CSO-GM0 (1.61 mg/g) was markedly greater than that of CSO (0.83 mg/g), indicating that the germination promoted the formation of free fatty acids (Abrante-Pascual, Goicoechea-Oses, & Nieva-Echevarría, 2025; Lou et al., 2025; Qin et al., 2025). The acid values of CSO-GM5 to CSO-GM60 slightly higher (between 1.74 and 1.87 mg/g) than that of CSO-GM0, suggesting that isothermal-microwave pretreatment slightly accelerated the hydrolysis of glycerides in sesame seeds, leading to increased generation of free fatty acids (Yin et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2025). Nevertheless, all these acid values remained far below the acid value limit for edible oils (3.00 mg/g), which is acceptable. Different with acid value, CSO-GM0 (1.62 mmol/kg) had lower peroxide value than CSO (2.02 mmol/kg), might due to phenolic antioxidants generated during germination, which scavenged oxidation products. The peroxide values for CSO-GM5 to CSO-GM10 (1.38–1.67 mmol/kg) were significantly lower than those for CSO-GM30 to CSO-GM60 (3.02–5.16 mmol/kg), and even markedly lower than CSO (p < 0.001). This suggests that moderate isothermal-microwave pretreatment could reduce oxidation by enhancing antioxidant content. In contrast, prolonged pretreatment might promote lipid oxidation, potentially because it severely disrupts oil body structures, thereby promoting free radical formation and accelerating oxidative reactions (Abrante-Pascual et al., 2025; El-Beltagi et al., 2022; Mohamed Ahmed et al., 2020). As shown in Fig. 4B, the browning index of oil from the sesame seeds subjected to isothermal-microwave pretreatment (CSO-GM5 to CSO-GM60) ranged from 0.076 to 0.097, slightly higher than that of CSO-GM0 (0.070) and CSO (0.050). This increase might be attributed to the acceleration of Maillard reactions and oxidation processes in sesame seeds during pretreatment, leading to the formation and accumulation of pigmented compounds; alternatively, it could result from enhanced migration of such pigments into the oil phase (Amakhmakh et al., 2024; Gholamhoseini & Dolatabadian, 2024; Qin et al., 2025). Similar trends can also be observed in the oil color (yellow) values. The yellow value of CSO-GM0 (27.5) was higher than that of CSO (6.2), which may be attributed to the production of colored compounds, such as carotenoids and flavonoids, during the germination process, leading to a darker color of oil. For CSO-GM5 to CSO-GM15, the relatively high yellow values suggested that moderate microwave pretreatment promotes the formation of pigments and their migration into the oil (Gholamhoseini & Dolatabadian, 2024; Hu et al., 2023). However, with further extension of the pretreatment time (>30 min), the yellow values obviously decrease, indicated that prolonged treatment may lead to the degradation or transformation of pigments, disrupting their structures and resulting in a lighter color.

Effect of isothermal-microwave pretreatment on the free radical scavenging ability of oils

The DPPH radical scavenging activity of all oils (Fig. 4C) exhibited a concentration-dependent trend. The IC₅₀ value of CSO-GM0 was clearly lower than that of CSO, which was attributed to the increased levels of antioxidant compounds—such as tocopherols and polyphenols—generated during the short-term germination of sesame seeds (Gao et al., 2024; Muangrat et al., 2020). These antioxidants migrated more efficiently into the oil, thereby enhancing the antioxidant capacity of CSO-GM0. Following short-term germination, isothermal-microwave pretreatment further reduced the IC₅₀ values of CSO-GM5 to CSO-GM30, indicating even stronger radical scavenging activity (Escalante-Aburto et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2024). However, with prolonged isothermal-microwave pretreatment (>30 min), the IC₅₀ values of CSO-GM40, CSO-GM50, and CSO-GM60 increased markedly, suggesting a decline in the oil's antioxidant capacity. Similar trends were observed in the FRAP reducing power and ABTS+ radical scavenging activity (Fig. 4D). Samples subjected to isothermal-microwave pretreatment for ≤30 min (e.g., CSO-GM5 to CSO-GM30) demonstrated higher radical scavenging efficiencies, whereas treatments exceeding 30 min led to an obviously reduction in scavenging activity (Amakhmakh et al., 2024; Hou et al., 2024). These changes likely reflected alterations in endogenous antioxidants of oils. Short-duration isothermal-microwave treatment appeared to facilitate the release and diffusion of antioxidants from sesame seeds, promoting their effective transfer into the oil phase. In contrast, extended microwave exposure accelerated oxidative reactions, depleting antioxidant constituents in the seeds and consequently reducing their concentration in the extracted oil, which ultimately diminished the oil's overall antioxidant capacity (Chang et al., 2024; Gao et al., 2024). These findings support the results reported in the previous section.

Polar component and heterocyclic amine content of different oils

The polar component content (Fig. 5A) in oils increased during simulated frying, indicating occurring thermal degradation and oxidation (Yang et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2024). Within 8 h of frying, the total polar component content of CSO-GM15, CSO-GM0, and CSO showed minor differences. However, after 12 h of frying, CSO-GM15 exhibited a clear lower value in polar component (9.70%) than CSO-GM0 (12.27%), CSO (14.30%), SFO (13.03%), and SBO (22.20%). This was because CSO-GM15 contained higher levels of endogenous antioxidants, which inhibited lipid oxidation and slowed the formation of polar compounds (Hou et al., 2025; Lou et al., 2025). Overall, CSO-GM15 demonstrated better oxidative stability than CSO and comparable performance to SFO, with its polar compound formation being more controlled than SBO. As shown in Fig. 5B, heterocyclic amine (HCA) formation in all oils increased with frying time, reflecting the thermal degradation of amino acids and creatine under high temperature. After 12 h of frying, CSO-GM15 exhibited the lowest HCA content (3.91 μg/kg), following CSO-GM0 (4.44 μg/kg), CSO (7.22 μg/kg), SFO (11.18 μg/kg), and SBO (19.54 μg/kg). While germination alone (CSO-GM0) reduced HCA levels compared to untreated CSO, the addition of 15-min isothermal-microwave pretreatment further lowered HCA content, suggesting improved antioxidant capacity or altered lipid composition that limits Maillard reaction pathways (Gholamhoseini & Dolatabadian, 2024; Khor et al., 2020; Yin et al., 2023). Changes in the chemical properties of different oils

During the simulated frying process, the peroxide value (Fig. 5C), anisidine value (Fig. 5D), iodine value (Fig. 5E), and acid value (Fig. 5F) of all oils changed clearly, reflecting oxidative degradation and hydrolysis (Koishi et al., 2025; Segoviano-León et al., 2025). CSO exhibited the highest peroxide value, followed by CSO-GM0, while CSO-GM15 maintained lower levels, indicating that CSO-GM15 underwent less oxidation during frying. This beneficial effect can be attributed to the synergistic action of short-term germination and isothermal-microwave pretreatment. Specifically, compared to no pretreatment (CSO), germination promotes the biosynthesis of natural antioxidants of sesame seeds, such as sesamol and tocopherols, thereby enhancing the antioxidants content and oxidative stability of the oil (CSO-GM0). Furthermore, the isothermal-microwave pretreatment facilitates the migration of these germination-induced antioxidants into the oil phase without degrading heat-sensitive phenolics, ultimately improving the antioxidant capacity of CSO-GM15 (Abrante-Pascual et al., 2025; Xu et al., 2026; Zhang et al., 2024). The anisidine value, which reflects secondary oxidation products, rose sharply in SBO and SFO, while CSO-GM15 exhibited significantly lower increases than CSO, CSO-GM0, and even SFO, indicating superior resistance to lipid peroxidation (p < 0.001). CSO exhibited the most pronounced decline in iodine value after frying, followed by CSO-GM0, indicating extensive degradation of unsaturated fatty acids due to oxidation. In contrast, CSO-GM15 retained a higher iodine value, demonstrating superior preservation of double bonds during frying. This was because, compared to CSO and CSO-GM0, the oil obtained from germinated sesame seeds subjected to isothermal-microwave pretreatment (CSO-GM15) exhibited suppressed oxidation of unsaturated fatty acids owing to its higher levels of endogenous antioxidants (El-Beltagi et al., 2022; Muangrat et al., 2020; Yang et al., 2025). This observation indicated that isothermal-microwave pretreatment helped inhibit the oxidative degradation of unsaturated fatty acids in oil. The acid value increased gradually in all oils, but CSO-GM15 had consistently lower values than CSO and CSO-GM0, suggesting less hydrolysis and triglyceride breakdown (Gholamhoseini & Dolatabadian, 2024; Hu et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2024). Overall, CSO-GM15 outperformed CSO and CSO-GM0 in oxidative stability and thermal resistance, showing comparable or better performance than SFO and SBO (Aragão & Maximo, 2024; Koishi et al., 2025; Wu et al., 2025). These results indicate that constant-temperature microwave pre-treatment effectively enhances the quality and safety of sesame oil during high-temperature cooking by reducing oxidation and hydrolysis.

Oxidative stability and thermal stability of different oils

The oxidation induction period (Fig. 6A) reflects the resistance of oils to oxidative degradation under accelerated conditions, with a longer time indicating higher oxidative stability (Chang et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2025). Among the tested oils, CSO-GM15 exhibited the longest oxidation induction period (11.38 h), significantly exceeding that of CSO (7.75 h), CSO-GM0 (9.57 h), SBO (5.58 h), and SFO (4.32 h) (p < 0.001). This indicates that the constant-temperature microwave pre-treatment (15 h) substantially enhanced the oxidative stability of sesame oil. This is consistent with the analysis in the previous chapters. Compared to untreated CSO and germinated but non-microwave-treated CSO-GM0, CSO-GM15 showed improved antioxidant capacity, likely due to the activation or increased migration of natural antioxidants such as sesamol and sesamin into the oil phase during microwave processing (Amakhmakh et al., 2024; Hu et al., 2023; Qin et al., 2025). The superior performance of CSO-GM15 over both common cooking oils (SBO and SFO) further highlights its potential for extended shelf life and better performance in high-temperature applications (Mohamed Ahmed et al., 2020; Wu et al., 2025). These results demonstrate that the combination of germination and microwave treatment effectively delays lipid oxidation, making CSO-GM15 a more stable and safer oil for culinary use. Thermal stability is also a crucial property of oils and is closely linked to oxidative stability, as the products of thermal decomposition can influence free radical formation and peroxide accumulation, thereby affecting the extent of oil oxidation (Qin et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2025). Based on the thermogravimetric (Fig. 6B) and derivative thermogravimetric curves, the thermal stability of CSO-GM15 was higher compared to CSO, CSO-GM0, SBO, and SFO. The thermogravimetric curve of CSO-GM15 showed a slower mass loss rate at elevated temperatures, indicating greater resistance to thermal degradation (Abrante-Pascual et al., 2025; Gao et al., 2024; Koishi et al., 2025). In the derivative thermogravimetric curves, the main decomposition peak of CSO-GM15 occurred at a higher temperature than that of CSO and CSO-GM0, suggesting enhanced thermal stability due to the microwave pre-treatment. Compared to SBO and SFO, CSO-GM15 exhibited a more stable profile with a narrower and sharper decomposition peak, reflecting a more uniform and less reactive lipid structure (Khaldi et al., 2024; Tiozon et al., 2023). The shift in decomposition temperature toward higher values for CSO-GM15 implies that the constant-temperature microwave treatment may have promoted structural changes such as antioxidant enrichment or improved molecular packing, thereby delaying oxidation and pyrolysis (Abrante-Pascual et al., 2025; Aragão & Maximo, 2024; Koishi et al., 2025). Overall, CSO-GM15 demonstrated superior thermal stability, outperforming both untreated sesame oil and common cooking oils, which highlights the effectiveness of microwave pre-treatment in enhancing the heat resistance of sesame oil during high-temperature applications (Aragão & Maximo, 2024; Zhang et al., 2024). Volatile flavor compounds of different oils

Based on the flavor profiles (Fig. 7) and volatile compound contents at different frying times, CSO-GM15 exhibited a more stable and desirable flavor development compared to CSO, CSO-GM0, SBO, and SFO during simulated frying. At 0 h, CSO-GM15 showed no detectable volatile compounds, similar to other oils, indicating no initial off-flavors (Hu et al., 2024; Yin et al., 2023). However, after 6 h of frying, CSO-GM15 generated characteristic aroma compounds such as 2-heptenal (E), furan, 2-pentyl, and 2-undecenal, which are associated with pleasant roasted and nutty notes, while maintaining lower levels of oxidation-derived aldehydes like hexanal and heptanal compared to CSO and CSO-GM0. At 12 h, CSO-GM15 continued to produce moderate amounts of these desirable volatiles without clearly accumulation of rancid or burnt odorants such as 4-nonanone or cyclobutanone, 2,2,3-trimethyl-, which were elevated in SBO and SFO. In contrast, CSO and CSO-GM0 showed higher levels of aldehydes like 2-decenal (E) and nonanal at later stages, suggesting faster lipid oxidation and off-flavors formation (Koishi et al., 2025; Wu et al., 2025; Yin et al., 2023). This indicated that the constant-temperature microwave pre-treatment enhanced the antioxidant capacity of sesame oil, delaying oxidative degradation and preserving its natural aromatic profile (Abrante-Pascual et al., 2025; Yang et al., 2025). Conclusion

In this study, isothermal-microwave pretreatment was employed to enhance the quality and oxidative stability of cold-pressed oil derived from short-term germinated seeds, while ensuring adherence to the low-temperature requirement for cold-pressed process. The results revealed that isothermal-microwave pretreatment, when applied for a moderate duration (e.g., 15–30 min), disrupted cellular structures within the seeds, facilitating enhanced oil migration during extraction and leading to higher oil yields. Short-term germination pretreatment increased the endogenous antioxidants in sesame seeds, and moderate isothermal-microwave pretreatment further slightly enhanced their levels. The most crucial point was that isothermal-microwave pretreatment with moderate duration notably promoted the migration of these antioxidants into the oil and thus improved oil quality. Prolonged isothermal-microwave treatment durations (>40 min) were found to potentially exacerbate oxidative degradation due to the lack of protection by complete cellular structures, leading to a decline in these antioxidants. The physicochemical properties of the oils, including acid values, peroxide values, and color indices, were also affected by the isothermal-microwave pretreatment, with moderate durations promoting better oxidative stability and desirable sensory attributes. Specifically, the oil obtained from short-term germinated seeds pretreated with isothermal-microwave for 15 min exhibited an 18.91% longer oxidation induction period and showed better frying stability compared to that from the 0-min pretreatment seeds. Crucially, the isothermal-microwave pretreatment maintained the seed temperature below 80 °C, avoiding the overheating associated with conventional microwave processing or roasting. It meeting the low-temperature requirements essential for cold-pressed sesame oil production. Therefore, this research provides a new strategy for improving the quality and oxidative stability of cold-pressed sesame oil. The resulting oil is expected to extend beyond low-temperature cooking and may also be suitable for domestic short-term frying. However, its refining processes and real application scenarios (e.g., baking, prolonged frying, moist-heat cooking) need more research in future to facilitate commercialization.
