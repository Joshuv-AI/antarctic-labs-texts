Impact of scleral cautery on limbal vasculature after cataract surgery assessed using optical coherence tomography angiography
Authors: Shuichiro Eguchi, Tatsuaki Amari, Yusuke Oniyanagi, Tetsuro Oshika
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
We investigate the influence of scleral cautery during cataract surgery on limbal vascular density and remodeling using anterior segment optical coherence tomography angiography (AS-OCTA). Twenty eyes of 20 patients who underwent cataract surgery with a sclerocorneal incision were included. Patients were divided into two groups: non-cautery (n = 10) and cautery (n = 10). The area around the incision site was scanned using AS-OCTA before surgery and at 1, 3, 5, 7, 14, 21, 28, and 90 days postoperatively. Images were analyzed to depict conjunctival vasculature (surface to a depth of 200 μm) and intrascleral vasculature (depth of 200 to 1000 μm). Vascular density was evaluated using ImageJ software. In the non-cautery group, intrascleral vascular density significantly increased during the wound-healing period up to 21 days postoperatively. Cautery application completely diminished this effect, resulting in significantly reduced intrascleral vascular density in the cautery group compared to the non-cautery group until 5 days after surgery. On the seventh day and later, intrascleral vascular density in the cautery group recovered, but the vascular pattern did not return to its preoperative state even at 90 days after surgery. Conjunctival flap vascular density was reduced for 28 days after surgery, with cautery application further decreasing conjunctival vascular density. AS-OCTA enabled separate observation of conjunctival and intrascleral vasculature. Intrascleral blood flow significantly increased after cataract surgery, but scleral cauterization markedly blocked this effect. The vascular reconstruction process following cataract surgery continued for almost a month, with cautery application leading to prolonged vascular disruption and altered vascular patterns.

Introduction

Blood vessels play a critical role in several phases of wound healing: hemostasis, inflammation, proliferation, and remodeling1–4. First, they constrict to minimize blood loss, then facilitate the infiltration of immune cells. They also deliver oxygen and nutrients essential for cell growth and angiogenesis, and support the remodeling of the matrix. By enabling these cellular processes and promoting tissue regeneration, blood vessels are indispensable for successful wound healing.

In cataract surgery performed using a sclerocorneal incision, cautery is commonly applied to the episclera to manage bleeding. Although the influence of cautery on surgically induced astigmatism has been studied5,6, there is a lack of research investigating its impact on the vasculature patterns. Understanding how cautery alters limbal blood vessels is important, as changes in this highly vascularized region may influence postoperative wound healing and visual outcomes. It has been reported that cataract surgery is one of the two most common surgeries associated with surgically-induced scleral necrosis, second to pterygium excision, where excessive cauterization on the sclera can disrupt intrascleral vasculature, leading to local ischemia7. The evaluation of changes in intrascleral vasculature can offer invaluable information on such processes, but its direct observation is impossible with conventional examination modalities.

Optical coherence tomography angiography (OCTA) is a non-invasive imaging technique that can visualize blood vessels at various tissue depths, enabling the combined assessment of both deep and superficial vasculatures8–10. While OCTA is commonly used to evaluate retinal and choroidal blood flow, anterior segment OCTA (AS-OCTA) can visualize blood vessels in the iris, conjunctiva, and sclera11–14. In this study, we employed AS-OCTA to investigate the effects of cautery on vascular density and the remodeling process following cataract surgery.

Patients and methods

Patients

Twenty eyes of 20 patients who underwent surgery for age-related cataract between January and October 2020 were included in the study. None of the eyes included in the study had ocular or systemic diseases that could potentially affect the postoperative wound-healing process. Excluded conditions were glaucoma, uveitis, allergic conjunctivitis, and other inflammatory diseases. Patients with diabetes or a previous history of ocular surgery were also excluded from the study. Furthermore, subjects using systemic or topical medications known to influence surgical outcomes were not included. The patients were followed up at least for 3 months after surgery.

This study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the institutional review board of Eguchi Eye Hospital. Prior to undergoing treatment, all patients provided informed consent, demonstrating their voluntary participation and understanding of the study’s purpose, procedures, and potential risks.

Surgery

All eyes underwent phacoemulsification and implantation of an intraocular lens (IOL) by a single surgeon (S. E.). The surgical procedure began with the creation of a fornix-based conjunctival peritomy at the superior limbus, followed by the administration of sub-Tenon anesthesia. The study population was divided into two groups based on the application of cautery to bleeding vessels on the sclera. In 10 eyes, bleeding vessels within a 2.5 mm x 2.5 mm area on the sclera were cauterized using a wet-field coagulator set at 20% power (Centurion Vision System, Alcon Laboratories, Inc., Fort Worth, TX, USA). In another 10 eyes, cautery treatment was not applied during the surgical procedure. In both groups, a 2.4 mm sclerocorneal incision was made, through which phacoemulsification and in-the-bag implantation of a monofocal IOL (Clareon, CNA0T0, Alcon Laboratories, Inc.) were performed. The incision was self-sealing, and no sutures were required to close the surgical wound.

For the first week after surgery, patients received topical 0.5% levofloxacin and 0.01% betamethasone sodium phosphate four times a day, and 0.1% diclofenac sodium three times a day. From one week to three months postoperatively, the regimen was changed to topical 0.5% levofloxacin and 0.1% fluorometholone four times a day, while continuing 0.1% diclofenac sodium three times a day.

Examinations

Anterior segment photography and AS-OCTA examinations were conducted by a single examiner (Y.O.) before and 1, 3, 5, 7, 14, 21, 28, and 90 days after surgery. For the AS-OCTA examination, a swept-source OCT system (PLEX Elite 9000; Carl Zeiss Meditec, Dublin, CA, USA) was used with a 10-diopter optical adaptor lens. This device has a central wavelength of 1040–1060 nm, a bandwidth of 100 nm, an A-scan depth of 3.0 mm in tissue, and a full-width at half-maximum axial resolution of approximately 5 μm in tissue. The instrument performs 100,000 A-scans per second. With the patient instructed to gaze downward, the area surrounding the wound was scanned to acquire AS-OCTA images. The scan was performed using a 3 × 3-mm retina scan mode, which reportedly correspond to an approximately 6 × 6-mm area in the anterior segment11. En face images were generated using a built-in software, and the OCTA images were automatically segmented. Superficial- and deep-layer flow images were developed with en face maximum projection from the conjunctival epithelium to a depth of 200 μm (mainly conjunctival composition) and from a depth of 200 μm to 1000 μm from the conjunctival epithelium (mainly intrascleral composition), respectively, as previously reported11,12. The projection-resolved algorithm in the built-in software was used when developing the deep-layer en face images.

From the en-face images of each layer at each time point obtained by AS-OCT, the cornea and eyelids were excluded, and the vascular density was measured using image processing software (ImageJ version 1.52, National Institute of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA). The analysis area was set by applying a 1024 × 1024 pixel rectangular box, binarization was performed using Otsu’s thresholding method, and the density was calculated by dividing the number of pixels in the vessel area by the total number of pixels. The images within ImageJ were corrected using band-pass and top-hat filters. To observe changes in vascular density before and after surgery, the vascular density on each observation day after surgery was converted to a percentage (%) based on the vascular density before surgery.

Statistical analysis

Numerical data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) unless otherwise specified. For the statistical analysis, the Kruskal-Wallis test adjusted with the Bonferroni correction was performed to assess the changes in vascular density before and after surgery, while the Wilcoxon test was used to compare the vascular density differences between the non-cautery and cautery groups. JMP software (JMP 15.2.0, SAS Institute, Inc., Cary, NC, USA) was used for the statistical analysis, and p < 0.05 was considered a significant value.

Figure 1 illustrates the time course of AS-OCTA images depicting the conjunctival vessels surrounding the cataract surgery incision. In the non-cautery eye, the vascular network was disrupted at the edge of the fornix-based conjunctival flap on the first postoperative day. By the third postoperative day, blood flow had significantly increased at the conjunctival flap edge, indicating active remodeling of the conjunctival vessels. Subsequently, the conjunctival vessels reconnected to the limbal vascular network, and the overall vascular pattern returned to a state closely resembling the preoperative status. In the cautery eye, the conjunctival vascular network overlying the coagulated scleral area completely disappeared on the day following surgery, with blood flow interrupted over a wide area. The reconstruction of blood vessels was protracted, with a broad avascular region and sparse vascular density persisting even at 90 days postoperatively.

Figure 2 shows the time course of AS-OCTA images of the intrascleral vessels. In the non-cautery eye, vascular density remarkably increased over the entire measurement field from day 1 to 5 after surgery. The vessels on the limbal side of the incision were observed on day 1 but became scarce from day 5 throughout the study period. In the cautery eye, the vascular network in the cauterized area was absent until 5 days after surgery. On the seventh postoperative day, remodeling of the vascular network from the surrounding area began, and the avascular area gradually decreased. In both non-cautery and cautery eyes, new corneal marginal vascular arcade formation near the limbus occurred at 7 days after surgery, and vascular density gradually increased. However, the pattern of the reconstructed vascular network at 90 days after surgery differed from that observed before surgery.

The vascular intensity was quantitatively analyzed. In the non-cautery group, the conjunctival vascular density at 5, 14, and 90 days after surgery was significantly lower than the preoperative level (p < 0.05), with a decrease rate of approximately 22% at 14 days postoperatively (Fig. 3). In the cautery group, the conjunctival vascular density at all postoperative time points was significantly lower than the preoperative value (p < 0.01), with a decrease rate of approximately 40% on the day after surgery. There was no significant difference between the non-cautery and cautery groups at any time point.

Discussion

The findings in the non-cautery group indicated that intrascleral blood flow significantly increased following surgery, with vascular density remaining significantly higher than preoperative levels from day 1 to day 21. The vascular density was particularly elevated within the first postoperative week. These observations are consistent with previous histological studies of wound healing processes. In a cataract surgery model, Hikichi et al.15 reported that connective tissue extended through the full thickness of the surgical wound by postoperative day 3. By day 5, the connective tissue had become dense and aligned with the lamella, with further progression of the wound healing process observed by day 7. Similarly, in a cutaneous wound healing model, Tonnesen et al.16 demonstrated that angiogenic capillary sprouts invade the fibrin/fibronectin-rich wound clot and organize into a microvascular network throughout the granulation tissue within a few days. Thus, the observations in the non-cautery group of the present study likely represent the active wound-healing processes that occur within the first few days following surgery.

The application of cauterization to the sclera nearly eliminated the vasculature in the treated area, counteracting the surgery-induced vascular enhancement observed in the non-cautery group. Consequently, the vascular density in the cautery group was significantly lower than that of the non-cautery group from day 1 to day 5 postoperatively. However, the blood flow in the cautery group recovered after this initial period, with vascular density from day 7 to 21 significantly surpassing preoperative levels. These findings suggest that the wound-healing process in the cautery group was initially suppressed immediately following surgery but became active approximately 1 week postoperatively.

Both the non-cautery and cautery groups exhibited a sustained increase in intrascleral vascular density compared to preoperative values throughout the study period. Moreover, the pattern of the reconstructed vascular network at 90 days preoperatively differed from that observed preoperatively. These findings suggest that even small incision cataract surgery, employing a 2.4-mm sclerocorneal incision, exerts prolonged effects on the intrascleral vasculature following the surgical intervention.

The conjunctival vasculature was also affected by the surgery and cautery. In the non-cautery group, blood flow in the fornix-based conjunctival flap was diminished on the first postoperative day, but active remodeling of the conjunctival vessels occurred by the third postoperative day. On day 5, the conjunctival vessels reconnected to the limbal vascular network, and the overall vascular pattern returned to a state closely resembling the preoperative status. In contrast, the conjunctival vascular network overlying the coagulated scleral area in the cautery group nearly disappeared on the day following surgery, with blood flow undetectable over a wide area. The reconstruction of blood vessels was prolonged, as indicated by a wide avascular area observed at 28 days post-surgery and sparse vascular density persisting even at 90 days post-surgery. These differences between the non-cautery and cautery groups are intriguing, as the conjunctival vessels were not directly cauterized during surgery. It may be possible that heat from the cautery device indirectly affected the conjunctival vasculature, or that some chemical interaction occurred between the sclera and conjunctiva after the flipped conjunctiva was repositioned. Currently, we do not have definitive explanations for these results, and further investigation is warranted to elucidate the underlying mechanisms.

The interpretation of the avascular area observed with OCTA in the present study may remain open to debate. Two possible explanations exist: either the vasculature in the affected area was completely eliminated, or the vessels exhibited extremely slow blood flow that was undetectable by OCTA. If the latter explanation holds true, cauterization may have merely induced temporary blood stagnation, followed by reperfusion within a few days. However, the current study’s findings suggest otherwise. The remodeling of both conjunctival and intrascleral vasculature in the cautery group was prolonged, and the vascular network pattern after reconstruction differed substantially from the preoperative pattern. Consequently, we propose that the observed phenomena represent genuine vascular disappearance and remodeling, rather than a transient reduction of blood flow.

Cataract surgery is one of the most common causative procedures of surgically induced scleral necrosis, following pterygium excision7. The scleral stroma receives blood and nutrition supply through choroidal blood vessels and the episcleral vascular network17,18. Excessive cauterization of the sclera leads to delayed wound healing and direct necrosis of ocular tissue19,20. Postoperative ischemia can create conjunctival retraction, producing a Dellen-like lesion in the surrounding bare sclera that further enhances scleral melting21. Risk factors for scleromalacia are scleral exposure, Dellen formation, and ischemia from excessive cautery on episcleral vasculature or by compression induced by a tight band or explant after scleral buckling surgery18,19,21. These phenomena can result in corneal astigmatism, irregular shape, tear film instability, and corneal epithelial abnormalities, which lead to long-term suboptimal visual function. Furthermore, if these changes are severe, they could even cause significant and irreversible vision loss.

Electrocauterization is a surgical technique that disrupts vasculature by applying heat generated from an electric current. This method involves using a device to deliver heat directly to tissue, causing coagulation that effectively seals blood vessels and prevents bleeding. The process begins with an electric current heating the tip of a cautery device, which is then applied to the targeted tissue, leading to the denaturation and coagulation of proteins. This heat-induced coagulation forms a clot that seals the vessel, stopping further bleeding. Besides sealing blood vessels, electrocautery can also destroy abnormal tissue growths, making it useful for excising such growths during surgery. Although electrocautery offers precision and allows for controlled tissue damage, minimizing harm to surrounding healthy tissue, the heat application can still damage adjacent tissues. Devices can be adjusted to control the temperature and energy level based on surgical goals. Therefore, it is crucial to avoid excessive electrocautery in cataract surgery, where the primary aim is hemostasis rather than the removal of unwanted tissue.

The current study has several limitations. Firstly, while the OCTA device for the posterior segment of the eye is equipped with a sophisticated eye-tracking system that compensates for eye movement by continuously monitoring the location of retinal vessels, this system sometimes does not work properly with anterior segment scanning. In our study, we captured slit-lamp photographs of all eyes, and scanning locations were manually adjusted referring to the major conjunctival vessels, which is likely less accurate than the automated eye-tracking method. The development of an eye-tracking system specific to AS-OCTA is needed. Secondly, we assumed that the superficial layer (from the conjunctival epithelium to a depth of 200 μm) comprises the conjunctiva, and the deep layer (from a depth of 200 μm to a depth of 1000 μm) mainly comprises the sclera, as reported previously11,12. Cataract surgery has been reported to induce significant swelling of the bulbar conjunctiva, from approximately 200 μm preoperatively to 350 μm at 1 week postoperatively, which remained at a higher value until 1 month after surgery22. Thus, it appears that our segmentation may have failed to capture the vascular signals from the full thickness of the conjunctiva in the early postoperative period. However, conjunctival vascular signals are most intense immediately beneath the conjunctival epithelium and become very sparse toward the Tenon’s capsule, as shown by B-mode images of AS-OCTA11–14. Therefore, segmentation down to a depth of 200 μm seems to be sufficient to assess conjunctival blood flow. On the other hand, the scleral thickness close to the corneal limbus is reported to be 500 μm to 800 μm23. Surgical invasion can cause some swelling of the sclera, and the sclerocorneal incision is usually placed 2 to 3 mm apart from the limbus. Therefore, it can be assumed that the vessels located from a depth between 200 μm and 1000 μm consist of episcleral and scleral vessels, and it is unlikely that vascular signals from the ciliary body were included in this segment.

In conclusion, this study investigated the time course of changes in conjunctival and intrascleral vasculature following cataract surgery and analyzed the impact of scleral cauterization on vascular density and the remodeling process. Our findings demonstrate that cataract surgery induces significant changes in both conjunctival and intrascleral blood flow, with scleral cauterization leading to prolonged vascular disruption and delayed remodeling. Our study showcases the utility of AS-OCTA as a valuable tool for evaluating wound healing processes after ocular surgery, offering insights into the vascular changes that occur during the postoperative period. Future research should focus on elucidating the mechanisms underlying these vascular alterations and their long-term implications for ocular health and surgical outcomes.
