Intradural extramedullary cavernous malformation: A common lesion in an uncommon location
Authors: Jonathan Alexander, Jasmine Leewong, Christian Burgos-Sanchez, Benjamin Rosen, Erik Dedekam, Daniel Bess
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntradural extramedullary cavernous malformations (IECM) are an uncommon manifestation of a typically intracranial vascular lesion, with few reports describing their imaging characteristics. Hemorrhage is a frequent presenting feature, often resulting in subarachnoid blood products and clinical deterioration. We present a 78-year-old female undergoing treatment for low-stage endometrial carcinoma who was found to have layering subarachnoid hemorrhage on lumbar spine MRI. Subsequent contrast-enhanced imaging of the neuroaxis revealed multicompartment intraspinal hemorrhage and a 1.6-cm intradural, extramedullary mass at T7 with cord compression. The lesion was surgically resected for presumed metastasis. However, pathology demonstrated only hemorrhage and fibrous tissue without malignancy, favoring cavernous malformation. Retrospectively, characteristic imaging findings such as lack of internal enhancement and stigmata of prior hemorrhage help differentiate this from metastasis. This case highlights an atypical location and presentation of a common vascular lesion, emphasizing the need to include cavernous malformation in the differential for intradural extramedullary masses.Keywords: Cavernous malformation, Cavernoma, Intradural extramedullary, Spinal hemorrhage, Neuroradiology, MRI

Introduction

Cavernous malformations are common non-neoplastic lesions of venous origin which are frequently an incidental finding in the intracranial space. Spinal cavernous malformations, however, are a rarer entity, representing only 5% of all cavernous malformations [1] and 5%-12% of spinal vascular lesions [2,3]. Intradural extramedullary cavernous malformations, referred to as IECMs, are an exceedingly rare subset as approximately 3% of spinal cavernous malformations [1].

Symptoms attributed to spinal cavernous malformations classically arise from either cord compression or hemorrhage. Compared to cerebral cavernous malformations, IECMs have increased propensity for subarachnoid hemorrhage given the uncovered, exposed position within the spinal subarachnoid space, not having overlying cord tissue to cover friable tissue and contain extension of hemorrhage into other compartments [4]. These blood products may be detected before the lesion itself and have variable signal characteristics based on chronicity. IECMs have the potential to exert significant mass effect upon the spinal cord due to lesion growth and recurrent hemorrhage and thrombosis. Thus, severe clinical symptoms may develop with IECM, correlating with subarachnoid hemorrhage and spinal cord compression.

Cavernous malformations have a characteristic “popcorn” appearance, best appreciated on MRI with mixed T1/T2 intensity and thin T2 hypointense rim [2,5,6]. Expansion of the cord or mass effect is common depending on the location of the lesion [2]. Further MRI characteristics are related to the extent, age, and location of associated blood products. They are typically occult on digital subtraction angiogram (DSA) due to the inherent slow vascular flow. However, DSA serves to eliminate higher flow vascular lesions [3,7]. From a pathologic perspective, cavernous malformations are comprised of dilated sinusoids with a thin endothelial lining and no intercalating neural cells, unlike vascular tumors with neoplastic growth of vascular endothelium [3,8].

Of those intradural extramedullary cavernous malformations reported in available literature, the majority are described from a surgical perspective and focus primarily on symptoms and operative findings. As a result, these lesions present a challenging diagnostic dilemma on preoperative imaging, but identification is critical for surgical planning to treat cord compression and prevent recurrent hemorrhage. Furthermore, recognition of IECM imaging characteristics extends the differential beyond neoplastic etiologies. To that benefit, here we report a case of a thoracic IECM causing extensive hemorrhage and compressive spinal myelopathy that required surgical excision. Ultimately, this case report aims to strengthen the radiological literature for characterizing IECMs by demonstrating multicompartment multilevel hemorrhage, cord compression, and syrinx as secondary imaging characteristics of IECM.

Case presentation

The subject for our case is a 78-year-old female patient with a recent diagnosis of stage IB endometrial adenocarcinoma, who presented to the emergency department with a complaint of abdominal pain. Initial imaging demonstrated no acute cause for the patient’s presenting symptoms other than her known malignancy. Subsequently, admission was undertaken for acute pain management. Workup over the next few days revealed an additional complaint of insidious-onset lower extremity weakness, present for an indeterminate amount of time per the patient’s daughter. New lower extremity muscle spasms and back pain developed a couple days after initial admission. A lumbar MRI was obtained in pursuit of a cause for these neurological symptoms, such as subacute cauda equina nerve root compression. Instead, imaging revealed multicompartment layering blood products of varying ages, but no obvious source in the lumbar spine (Fig. 1).

Multicompartment spinal hemorrhage on MRI. 1.5 Telsa MR images of the lumbar spine, including sagittal T2-weighted (A) and sagittal T1-weighted (B) sequences demonstrating a dorsal subdural hematoma compressing the lower thoracic spinal cord (arrow) and layering subarachnoid blood products at the lumbosacral junction (asterisk).

Subsequent contrast-enhanced MR imaging of the remainder of the neuroaxis revealed a 1.6 cm intradural extramedullary mass at T7 (Fig. 2). Additionally, there was further multicompartment spinal hemorrhage, resulting in compression and edema of the spinal cord as well as an inferiorly projecting syrinx. This lesion demonstrated low T1 and T2 signal without significant internal enhancement apparent on either axial or sagittal postcontrast sequences. There were additionally prominent foci of layering low signal compatible with hemosiderin. Susceptibility-weighted imaging of the spine was not obtained during initial presentation and thus not included for evaluation. Subtle focal intracranial subarachnoid hemorrhage was also present, possibly redistributing from spinal subarachnoid hemorrhage (Fig. 3). Digital subtraction angiography (DSA; not shown) did not reveal any abnormal vascular supply to the region. At this time, the differential was broad and included cavernous malformation, capillary hemangioma, hemorrhagic metastasis from patients known uterine malignancy, and meningioma.

Intradural extramedullary cavernous malformation on MRI. 1.5 Tesla MR images, including sagittal T1-weighted precontrast (A), T2-weighted (B), and T1-weighted postcontrast (C) sequences of the thoracic spine demonstrating a nonenhancing extramedullary mass (arrow) at T7 with characteristic “popcorn” appearance and low T2 signal hemosiderin rim. There are surrounding blood products with cord compression, edema, and a small syrinx extending inferiorly (curved arrow).

Intracranial subarachnoid hemorrhage on MRI. 1.5 Tesla MR images, including axial T2/FLAIR (A) and susceptibility-weighted imaging (“SWI”, B) sequences demonstrating T2/FLAIR hyperintense and SWI hypointense signal at the right marginal sulcus (arrow) reflecting subarachnoid hemorrhage.

Due to the high-grade stenosis and associated symptoms from mass effect and hemorrhage, neurosurgery elected to resect the mass. The patient underwent T7 laminectomy and resection without complication. Intraoperative findings confirmed that the mass was located in the extramedullary space and again demonstrated multicompartment blood products. The leading diagnosis at the time of resection was metastasis, but unexpectedly, surgical pathology demonstrated non-malignant fibrous tissue with mixed chronicity hemorrhage and necroinflammatory debris (Fig. 4) in keeping with the diagnosis of a cavernous malformation.

Histopathologic appearance of the cavernous malformation. Multiple hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) stained slides at various magnifications including 20× (A), 100× (B), and 200× (C). The 20× magnification slide (A) demonstrates a fragment of the lesion containing both clotted blood in the upper left (arrow) and necroinflammatory debris toward the bottom (asterisk). The 100× magnification slide (B) shows the clotted blood with fibrin strands and admixed leukocytes, and the 200× magnification slide (C) shows scattered necroinflammatory debris.

Subsequent imaging follow-up demonstrated expected postoperative changes without mass recurrence after 6 months. Her recovery was essentially unremarkable despite some residual radiculopathy, and she slowly recovered full use of lower extremity function.

Discussion

Although rare within the spinal canal, a cavernous malformation is an important imaging differential consideration for a hemorrhagic intradural extramedullary spinal lesion. IECM, albeit a benign entity, was associated with high morbidity in this patient with severe multicompartment hemorrhage and spinal cord compression requiring surgical resection. Given the history of uterine malignancy, hemorrhagic metastasis was also a serious differential consideration. Differentiating the 2, while difficult prospectively, may have influenced the decision for or timing of surgery.

Though accompanied by several nonspecific findings, this example of a spinal cavernous malformation demonstrated many imaging features characteristic of cavernous malformations typically seen intracranially. The mixed T1/T2, so-called “popcorn” appearance, and thin T2 hypointense hemosiderin ring can be seen across intracranial, intramedullary and intradural extramedullary cavernous malformations [2,5,6]. The heterogeneous T1 and T2 signal intensity on MRI signifies varied stages of blood due to the propensity for thrombosis and recurrent hemorrhage, whereas the presence of a T2 hypointense peripheral hemosiderin ring may represent progressive central clearance of hemosiderin [6]. Contrast enhancement of IECM varies widely, but is usually modest when present in a heterogenous or peripheral pattern [3,5]. These collective findings highly suggest a cavernous malformation and emphasize the importance of recognizing a common lesion in an atypical location, as in the case of the patient presented here.

A relatively unique manifestation of this case’s presentation was the presence of asynchronous multicompartment hemorrhage across multiple cord levels and intracranially. While spinal subarachnoid hemorrhage is common among reported IECMs, subarachnoid hemorrhage and superficial siderosis extending intracranially have been infrequently documented as associated imaging findings of IECMs (Table 1). CNS superficial siderosis–specifically the accumulation of subpial hemosiderin secondary to recurrent subarachnoid hemorrhage–is apparent on MRI as a T2 and GRE hypointense rim [3,[9], [10], [11], [12]]. Intracranial subarachnoid hemorrhage has been reported with IECMs at the level of C2-C3, C5-C6, and T10 root [1,8,13]. Below the cervical spine, intracranial superficial siderosis has been documented with IECMs at T12, L1, and below the conus medullaris at L2 [[9], [10], [11]]. Hemorrhage from IECM other than subarachnoid hemorrhage is rarely reported (Table 1). One case demonstrated isolated intramedullary hemorrhage associated with a C5-C6 IECM [14]. Another case reported a T10-11 IECM with only subdural blood products [15]. Based on the current literature of IECMs summarized in Table 1, our case uniquely presents atypical hemorrhage of an IECM extending beyond the spinal subarachnoid space and involving the subdural and intracranial subarachnoid spaces. In accordance with those reported cases of atypical hemorrhage associated with IECM, our patient underwent surgical resection of the IECM and had marked clinical improvement of symptoms postoperatively.

Hemorrhage, as a common coexisting and nonspecific clinical condition, may therefore present with non-localizing symptoms. Imaging targeted to one area of concern may result in only apparent hemorrhage of an affected compartment while the lesion and further extension of hemorrhage lie outside the field of view. We have demonstrated that such a circumstance occurred with the initial imaging for our patient. Evaluation for causative lesions throughout the entire neuroaxis is critical to complete the workup for spontaneous hemorrhage. Furthermore, hemorrhagic non-neoplastic lesions should be kept on the differential when such findings are in isolation on initial imaging.

As previously stated, a cavernous malformation was not the only lesion on the differential when our patient’s lesion was initially imaged. Additional entities included in the differential for intradural and extramedullary spinal lesions include capillary hemangioma, arteriovenous malformation or fistula, solid primary neoplasm, and metastases (Table 2). Meningiomas and schwannomas are the most common of these entities. Solid tumors such as these demonstrate avid enhancement, which is rarely seen in IECM. Meningiomas may display an enhancing dural tail, while paragangliomas and myxopapillary ependymomas mostly occur along the cauda equina [16]. A capillary hemangioma is a low flow vascular lesion consisting of clustered capillaries with flattened endothelium and interposing fibrous septa [6,17,18]. Capillary hemangiomas also avidly enhance and may display perimedullary vascular T2 signal voids [5,6,17,18,]. Arteriovenous malformation (AVM) and fistula (AVF) are high flow shunt lesions with AVM containing a vascular nidus. Perimedullary vascular T2 signal voids are present in AVMs and AVFs. Dilated draining veins are distinguishing features of AVMs and AVFs, which may demonstrate early filling on MR angiography [3,6]. While cavernous malformations are typically occult on digital subtraction angiogram (DSA) as in our case and a similar case of a IECM causing subarachnoid hemorrhage and cord compression by Poudel et al. [3,7], DSA remains critical in the work up to differentiate from other high flow vascular entities and tumors. Infrequently, subarachnoid hemorrhage may present as a focal or loculated hematoma or clot mimicking the appearance of an IECM with similar mixed T1 and T2 signal, mass effect, and lack of enhancement [19].

Differential considerations for hemorrhagic intradural extramedullary lesions.

Ultimately, this case of a thoracic intradural extramedullary cavernous malformation causing extensive hemorrhage did require surgical excision to treat compressive spinal myelopathy and stop progression of hemorrhage. This case strengthens the preoperative imaging differential for an intradural extramedullary lesion with associated hemorrhage and establishes cavernous malformation as an important benign diagnosis to consider with the possibility for significant morbidity. As demonstrated in the imaging of this case, we aim to further establish multicompartment multilevel hemorrhage, cord compression, and syrinx as secondary imaging characteristics of IECM in the radiological literature that can aid in preoperative diagnosis of IECM.
