Climate Change Enhances the Success of Marine Invasive Species
Authors: Nicola S. Smith, William W. L. Cheung
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTClimate change and invasive species both independently threaten marine biodiversity but can also interact. Invasive species often possess traits that not only promote their success in novel environments but may also confer resilience to climate‐driven ocean change, allowing them to respond favorably to a changing climate. Here, drawing on 67 studies across 30 non‐native species, we present moderate but consistent evidence that climate change enhances invasion success—particularly through increased abundance and establishment—across multiple temporal scales and ocean regions, while recognizing taxonomic and geographic biases in the available data. Even after accounting for moderators, unexplained heterogeneity remained among studies, suggesting strong context dependency in the effects of climate change on invasion success. Warming and extreme events emerged as key drivers of invasion success. We also identify ocean hotspots for invasions globally, with the cold and warm temperate Northwest Pacific emerging as major donor regions, and the Mediterranean Sea and Northern European Seas as primary recipient regions. Our study suggests that in the future, invasive species are likely to pose a greater threat to ocean biodiversity than previously thought, underscoring the urgent need for institutional capacity building, broader surveillance, predictive modelling, and adaptive management strategies across global marine ecosystems.

Introduction

Climate change and invasive species pose significant, independent threats to biodiversity (Jaureguiberry et al. 2022). These stressors can also interact. Invasive species can facilitate climate change and climate change can facilitate bioinvasions (Walther et al. 2009; Diez et al. 2012; Bezabih Beyene et al. 2022; Sorte 2023). In the latter instance, it is widely thought that climate change will increase invasion success (Dukes and Mooney 1999; Diez et al. 2012; Sorte 2023). Invasive species share traits and life‐history characteristics that make them successful in a novel environment while these may also allow them to respond favorably to a changing climate (Dukes and Mooney 1999; Diez et al. 2012; Sorte 2023). For example, having broad environmental tolerances in ocean ecosystems allow marine invasive species to withstand and potentially thrive under expected changes in ocean temperature and chemistry with climate change (Sorte 2023). Likewise, pioneering life‐history characteristics like rapid growth and maturation, high fecundity, and rapid colonization allow invasive species to capitalize on changes in the biotic and abiotic environments caused by increased frequency and/or intensity of extreme events, like hurricanes and marine heatwaves, which are associated with climate change (e.g., Sorte, Fuller, and Bracken 2010; Diez et al. 2012; Sorte 2023).

Synthesis of empirical evidence testing the hypothesis that climate change enhances invader success remains limited, especially in marine ecosystems. Existing quantitative reviews highlight the context dependency of this hypothesis, underscoring the challenges in generating broadly applicable, testable predictions (Sorte et al. 2013; Stephens et al. 2019). Moreover, marine and freshwater ecosystems are frequently analyzed together as ‘aquatic ecosystems’, despite fundamental differences in the ability of organisms to respond to climate change in each system (e.g., greater scope for species range shifts in marine than freshwater ecosystems) (Sorte et al. 2013; Stephens et al. 2019). Further, while several studies have proposed specific mechanisms linking climate change to invasions in freshwater systems, no equivalent exists for the ocean (Rahel and Olden 2008). A comprehensive, quantitative review of the effects of climate change on invasive species in marine ecosystems is urgently needed and could yield significant insights into the future state of the ocean contending with these stressors.

In this study, we conduct a systematic literature review and global meta‐analysis of the effects of climate change on marine invasions. Specifically, we address six key questions: (1) does climate change generally facilitate marine invasions? (2) which stage(s) of the invasion process are most strongly affected by climate change? (3) which measure of invasion success is most strongly influenced by climate change? (4) which functional group(s) are most strongly affected by climate change? (5) which climate stressor elicits the greatest response in invasive species? and (6) are there marine invasion hotspots globally?

Results and Discussion

Climate Change Effects on Invasive Species

Climate change has numerous effects on the ocean. In the studies included in our analyses, these changes included increased ocean temperatures, seawater freshening, increased intensity of extreme events (i.e., hurricanes and marine heatwaves), and ocean acidification (Table 1). We found moderate but consistent evidence that climate change enhances marine invasion success, though the magnitude and significance of effects varied across study types and temporal scales (p = 0.023; Table 2, Model 1; Figure 1). Specifically, invasion success under climate change rose by 5273% (lnRR = 3.984) in models and was associated with a 6301% (lnRR = 4.159) increase in observational studies relative to conditions prior to the appearance of a climate stressor, with these large relative increases partly reflecting low initial values before climate change effects occurred. Short‐term experiments showed, on average, a 75% (lnRR = 0.56) increase in invasion success relative to the control group but displayed large variation in effect size among studies, with the 95% confidence interval including zero (Figure 1; Table 2, Model 1). This latter finding suggests greater context dependency in short‐term experiments in which experiments likely cover a wider range of conditions (e.g., representatives of all functional groups in the dataset are included in experiments) than observational studies and models. Notably, significant heterogeneity remained among studies in our initial model of climate change effects, even with the inclusion of study type as a moderator, suggesting that some unmeasured factors (see other moderators explored below) are still unaccounted for in this analysis (Table 2, Model 1; p < 0.001).

To improve interpretability, we report both the untransformed log response ratio (lnRR) and its back‐transformed percentage change. However, back‐transforming the mean of the log response ratio can bias the estimate of the mean response ratio when study sample sizes are small, and can overestimate effects if an unweighted response ratio is used, as in our analysis (Hedges et al. 1999; Lajeunesse and Forbes 2003). The back‐transformed percentages should therefore be interpreted with caution and alongside the corresponding lnRR values.

Temporal scale and study design were systematically confounded: all experiments were short‐term (< 1 year), observational studies spanned medium time scales (≥ 1 year but < 50 years), and models covered long‐term projections (≥ 50 years but < 100 years; Figure 1). Despite this structural bias that constrained direct comparisons, our analysis suggests a pattern of invader success increasing substantially with longer time horizons under climate change (Figure 1). These findings support the prevailing hypothesis that climate change facilitates invasion success and suggest, at least preliminarily, that short‐term studies may underestimate the cumulative impacts of invasions under climate change. However, this interpretation remains tentative because temporal scale is confounded with study type in our dataset. We therefore interpret this pattern cautiously. Although observational studies are compared directly with experiments and models here, the former does not imply causality. Non‐climate covariates, such as degree of habitat modification/anthropogenic disturbance, nutrient loading, etc. can artificially inflate lnRRs in observational studies. Long‐term experimental studies are therefore urgently needed to disentangle the effects of temporal scale and study type.

There was moderate evidence that invader abundance is magnified under climate change (Figure 2C,D; Table 2, Model 3). Among the biological responses evaluated, invader abundance was consistently higher under climate change—rising by 205.7% (lnRR = 1.1175) in short‐term experiments and was associated with an extraordinary 44,259% (lnRR = 6.0949) increase in medium‐term observational studies (Figure 2B; Table 2). These large back‐transformed lnRR values should be interpreted cautiously because increases in invader abundances are often compared to a very small baseline or to instances in which the invader was initially not detected in the area. For example, the invasive Pacific oyster, Crassostrea gigas , was initially not detected in tidal flats in parts of the northern Wadden Sea (i.e., 0 individuals per m2) but with warming, within 8 years, their abundance increased to 1.6, 6.4, and 27.8 individuals per m2 (Diederich et al. 2005). The magnitude of these relative increases would likely be smaller if calculated against baselines in which invaders are already present and/or abundant in the environment. Nevertheless, that mid‐term observational studies show such strong increases in abundance but not establishment or spread suggests two possible interpretations. First, invasive species may increase in abundance without forming viable, self‐sustaining populations if environmental conditions remain suboptimal for reproduction, or if population growth is driven by continued propagule supply. Second, invasive species may establish but fail to spread due either to dispersal limitation or to habitat filtering in which there is an environmental mismatch between invader traits and the abiotic environment beyond the initial introduction site.

Other metrics, including phenology, distribution, and performance, trended positive but had greater response variability in which the 95% confidence intervals included zero. This suggests that the metric used to measure invasion success greatly influences the conclusions drawn about the effects of climate change on invasion success. Other metrics like phenology may show more response variability to climate change because they are typically influenced by several interacting factors such as whether the timing of a developmental event was delayed or advanced by climate change and the subsequent effect of that change on invader fitness.

Climate change also tended, on average, to enhance invader establishment in some circumstances (Figure 2A). We found weak evidence that climate change is associated with a near doubling (99.5%) (lnRR = 0.6905) of establishment success in short‐term experiments (Figure 2A). For an increase in abundance and subsequent establishment, an invasive species must arrive, survive local abiotic conditions and resident species interactions, and successfully reproduce. A relaxation of native biotic resistance with climate change and/or increased advantages of pioneering life‐history characteristics shared among invasive species that aid in establishment and population growth like rapid colonization and high fecundity may explain these results (Dukes and Mooney 1999; Sorte, Fuller, and Bracken 2010; Sorte et al. 2013; Sorte 2023). Importantly, these findings indicate the potential for the early stages of invasion as particularly sensitive to environmental change and highlight the need for greater monitoring, early detection, and rapid response (Figure 2A; Table 2; See management implications section below for details).

Despite numerous predictions that climate change will increase the geographic distribution of invasive species (e.g., Hellmann et al. 2008; Rahel and Olden 2008), this trend was positive but with substantial variation within observational studies and models, which both included zero in the 95% confidence intervals (Figure 2D,E). This large variation may be due to at least two possibilities. First, the realized introduced environmental niche of an invader often changes as the invasion progresses (Liu et al. 2025). Thus, differences in invasion timelines among taxa in our dataset—even within the same ~50 years temporal bins of our analysis—may account for some species showing large increases in distribution under climate change while others do not. Second, most marine invasive species fail to fully occupy their predicted environmental niche space in their introduced range within reasonable timeframes (i.e., 200 years) (Liu et al. 2025). This suggests that despite climate change creating otherwise, suitable environmental niche space for invasive species, this space may remain unfilled, thereby reducing the magnitude of anticipated range expansions. Overall, our findings suggest that while most habitats may become favourable to (and therefore potentially occupied by) invasive species with a changing climate, there are still many others that will remain or will become inhospitable (Figure 2D,E). Nevertheless, given the above findings, efforts to slow invasive population spread should be second to early detection and rapid response (EDRR) measures (see management implications section below for details).

There was little to no evidence that invasion success increases with climate change across the functional groups examined in experiments (p = 0.173), observational studies (p = 0.191) or models (p = 0.458) (Figure 2F–H; Table 2, Model 4). This suggests that climate change effects may not be unique or more pronounced among trophic levels, and contrasts with previous studies that demonstrate trophic amplification of climate change effects (e.g., Kwiatkowski et al. 2019). However, we are cautious in our interpretation due to the relatively small number of trophic groups (and sample sizes within those groups) as well as the lack of vertebrate predators, which can have substantial ecological impacts (e.g., Indo‐Pacific lionfishes, Pterois volitans and P. miles ), presented here. These findings may therefore only apply to largely invertebrate‐dominated systems.

There was moderate evidence that ocean warming was positively associated with invasion success in medium‐term observational studies, where success increased by 44,259% (lnRR = 6.0949) (Figure 2J; Table 2, Model 5). Our findings reinforce the central role of temperature in shaping marine ecological dynamics, particularly through its influence on ectotherm metabolism, reproduction, and species interactions (Kordas et al. 2011). Further, this correlation suggests that although many native species are negatively impacted by rising temperatures, ocean warming has the opposite effect on most invasive species. This finding points to the notion put forward by Sorte et al. (2013) that invasive species are typically below their physiological optima in invaded habitats—which in this case is temperature—and therefore are likely to thrive with increasing ocean temperatures. Ocean acidification and seawater freshening are also important climate‐driven stressors (IPPC 2022). Unfortunately, insufficient studies examined the effects of these stressors to permit generalizations (Table 1).

There was weak evidence that increased intensity and/or frequency of extreme events associated with climate change, like hurricanes and marine heatwaves, promotes the success of invasive species in short‐term experiments (98.9% increase; lnRR = 0.6876; Figure 2I; Table 2, Model 5). On the one hand, extreme events can improve the success of invasive species if they disproportionately, adversely affect resident native species in ways that free up limiting resources for invader establishment (e.g., Davis et al. 2000). For example, the invasive sea vine, Halophila stipulacea, was able to dominate the benthic community following large declines in native seaweeds with hurricane passage in Puerto Rico (Hernández‐Delgado et al. 2020). On the other hand, extreme events can negatively affect invasive species if they reduce native prey or habitat‐forming species upon which invaders depend. This context‐dependency of the effect of extreme events could explain some of the variance in effect size exhibited in both experiments and observational studies. Further, variability in the temporal scale required for community re‐equilibration and succession following a major disturbance due to differences in response time among biological systems and/or the severity of the event may also explain some effect size variance and can, in fact, underestimate the true effect of extreme events on invasion success.

Hotspots of Marine Invasions

Most invasive species in our dataset originated from the Cold Temperate Northwest Pacific and the Warm Temperate Northwest Pacific marine provinces of the world, particularly along the coasts of Japan, Korea, and eastern China (Figure 3, see Method). These regions are well‐known for their dense human populations, intense maritime activity, and extensive aquaculture. These factors likely contribute to high propagule pressure (i.e., the number of individuals released into a new area and the number of discrete release events); and hence, increased probability of a non‐ native species establishing in a new region (Lockwood et al. 2005). High propagule pressure allows species to overcome demographic and environmental stochasticity encountered in the recipient region (Lockwood et al. 2005). Further, high propagule pressure can be positively associated with high genetic variation in the introduced population, thereby increasing the potential for adaptation to novel selection pressures in the recipient region (Lockwood et al. 2005). In contrast, the primary recipient regions of marine invasions were concentrated in the Mediterranean Sea and the Northern European Seas, with up to 17 species recorded in some marine provinces (Figure 4). These areas are recognized global hotspots of marine invasions (Galil et al. 2014; Zenetos and Galanidi 2020) likely due to a combination of location‐related traits that are known to facilitate non‐native species invasions like warming waters (Stachowicz et al. 2002) and extensive coastal development (Bulleri and Chapman 2010), as well as factors positively correlated with propagule pressure like high shipping traffic (Keller et al. 2011). Indeed, recent regional analyses of invasion patterns have also noted the high number of invasive taxa in the Northern European Seas (i.e., North Sea and Baltic Sea) (Cuthbert and Briski 2021). Notably, there is a clear directional pattern, with species predominantly moving from Northwest Pacific donor regions to recipient regions in the temperate Northeast Atlantic (Figure 3). One important caveat here is that under‐sampled regions such as the Global South (tropics) may appear less invaded simply due to data gaps and not necessarily due to properties intrinsic to these regions (e.g., high species diversity/biotic resistance) or to less shipping activity/propagule pressure. Also, this analysis does not account for emerging areas of increased risk, such as the Arctic, where the consequences of climate change are driving dramatic elevations in shipping activity while changes in the temperature and salinity of Arctic ports may permit species to survive in previously inhospitable areas (Saebi et al. 2020).

Publication Biases and Other Sensitivity Analyses

The overall conclusions of our meta‐analysis are robust to the bias that most researchers only publish significant results, as indicated by the large magnitude of the Rosenthal fail‐safe number (47,694) (see Methods). However, we uncovered substantial biases in where and what types of climate stressors are examined. Climate change effects on invasive species were investigated in only a third (4) of the 12 marine realms identified by Spalding and colleagues (Spalding et al. 2007), and most of these studies (54 out of 67) occurred in the Temperate Northern Atlantic (See Method, Table 1). This geographic bias is exacerbated by the observation that 52 of the 54 studies in the Temperate Northern Atlantic investigated the effects of a single climate stressor: ocean warming (Table 1). Finally, although we detected taxonomic bias in our dataset where studies on the Pacific oyster were dominant, removing this species did not qualitatively change our main conclusions (Figure S1). In fact, the effect size was notably larger when studies on the Pacific oyster were excluded, suggesting that our main conclusions are conservative (Figure S1).

Future Research Directions

Our meta‐analysis highlights several areas for future research and gives rise to a testable prediction. The studies in our analysis focused primarily on marine invertebrates, which are often considered to be easier to work with, at least in experiments. More research that analyzes the responses of invasive vertebrates to climate change is needed. Also, studies examined invader responses in relatively shallow waters, most taking place in < 50 m. Studies on deep sea organisms are lacking and may highlight the importance of other stressors, like ocean deoxygenation, in these colder habitats where hypoxia is more common (Helly and Levin 2004). Although not the focus of this study, few researchers have examined the combined effects of multiple climate stressors on invasive species. Interactions between climate stressors have been documented (e.g., Harvey et al. 2013) and may change our conclusions on the effects of climate change on invasive species where non‐additive interactions are possible. It is also important to disentangle the association between study type and temporal scale (Figure 1), in which long‐term experiments are particularly lacking. Geographic bias and a tendency to focus solely on the effects of ocean warming in temperate climates must also be addressed. Studies conducted on tropical species, particularly in the Global South, are imperative to obtain a fuller understanding of the effects of invasive species in the world's oceans under climate change. Lastly, studies that track the fate of a single invasive population through all stages of the invasion process are needed to truly understand the mechanisms driving broad patterns of invasion success under climate change. In terms of testable predictions, our findings suggest that the establishment stage of marine invasions is likely to be most sensitive to climate change in which invaders are most likely to respond by increasing in abundance.

Invasion Management Implications

Both invasive species and climate change are recognized as dominant drivers of environmental change globally (Jaureguiberry et al. 2022). Every ocean of the world, including Antarctica (Avila et al. 2020) now contain invasive species while the effects of climate change in marine systems are ubiquitous. Positive interaction between these stressors creates an emerging global issue in which the scale of the problem is daunting. Given that the early stages of invasion are the most sensitive to a changing climate, targeting introduced species with known invasion potential before they become abundant is the most prudent management action. Lists such as “100 of the world's worst invasive alien species” compiled by the IUCN (Lowe et al. 2000) are particularly useful entry points to managing the problem, and such lists should be regularly updated and made systems specific and regionally relevant. Further, we call for increased investment globally in early detection systems for potential non‐native invaders. This could include the use of emerging technology and tools such as routine eDNA surveillance in marine systems and citizen science reporting apps for easily recognizable non‐native species (e.g., iNaturalist and iMapInvasives). Similarly, early prevention could include extensive ballast water monitoring and water treatment programs. The most up‐to‐date information relevant to EDRR measures should be made more readily available through regular regional training workshops, conferences, and open‐access publishing. Quantitative (e.g., species distribution models) and semi‐ quantitative (e.g., AS‐ISK) invasive species risk assessments that incorporate the potential effects of climate change should be conducted regularly at both regional and subregional scales while routine evaluations of how the effectiveness of management measures may change with a changing climate should also be undertaken. We emphasize that efforts to evaluate invasive species impacts and quantify intervention effectiveness should strive to use generalized effect sizes (as we have done here; e.g., log response ratio, Hedges' g, odd ratios) which allow for comparisons across taxa, systems, and response variables. We need to be on the lookout for “sleeper species” or non‐native species already established in an area that may become invasive once changing environmental conditions precipitated by climate change favor their increased abundance and spread (O'Uhuru et al. 2024). In this latter instance, consistent monitoring of key, established non‐native species should be prioritized so that early signs of increased abundance can be quickly addressed. Importantly, none of the above is possible without institutional capacity building, especially in small island developing states. Above all, we must put pressure on governments to support policies that reduce the use of fossil fuels and slow the rate of a warming planet.

Conclusion

We conclude that there is moderate but largely consistent evidence that climate change facilitates marine invasions. Biological invasions are already considered one of the leading causes of biodiversity loss, along with climate change. But these factors are often considered independently. Here, due to a changing climate, we show that marine invasive species are likely to have a larger negative effect on native biodiversity than previously thought, underscoring the urgent need for institutional capacity building, increased biosecurity measures, broader surveillance, predictive modelling, and adaptive management strategies across global marine ecosystems.

Methods

Study Selection, Data Extraction, and Coding

On September 24th, 2021, we conducted a systematic search of the peer‐reviewed literature for studies that quantified the effects of climate change on marine invasive species. We followed the protocol outlined in the PRISMA (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta‐ analyses) statement. We searched two bibliographic databases, that is, Aquatic Sciences and Fisheries Abstracts Database Guide (ASFA) and ISI Web of Science, using the search terms “marine” AND “climate change” AND “invasive” OR “introduced” OR “non‐native” OR “non‐ indigenous” OR “exotic” OR “alien” for all available years through 2021 (Figure S2). Because searches of each database rendered 10's of 1000's (ISI Web of Science) to over 2 million (ASFA) publications, we limited our analysis to the first 1000 publications per database when studies were sorted by relevance. We also searched the references list of publications included in our analysis and other major reviews to identify additional relevant studies (Figure S2).

We used several inclusion and exclusion criteria when selecting publications for analysis. First, the publication must examine a marine system. Second, the publication must quantify a biological response to a single climate stressor. We did not include studies that quantified socioeconomic impacts or effects of multiple climate stressors acting simultaneously on an invasive species. This is because the former is beyond the scope of this review while the latter may introduce context dependencies that preclude generalizations. Third, the publication must be an experiment, observational study, or model. Fourth, the publication had to examine the response of a non‐indigenous invasive species. Here, we defined invasive species as a species occurring outside its native range due to human action that has established and spread. We did not include studies of native species with invasive properties, that is, “native invaders”. Finally, the publication must be available in English.

For each study, we collected the following data: (1) study type (experiment, observational study, model), (2) type of biological response of the invader (changes in invader abundance, per capita impact on native taxa, individual performance (e.g., somatic growth rate, heat shock protein expression), phenology, invasive species richness), (3) temporal scale (short: < 1 year; medium: ≥ 1 year but < 50 years; long: ≥ 50 years but < 100 years), (4) invader taxon, (5) invader functional group (epibenthic fouling community, predator, primary producer), (6) invader scientific name, (7) invader native range, (8) climate stressor (acidification, extreme event, ocean warming, seawater freshening), (9) invasion stage (introduction, establishment, spread) or invader impact, (10) study location, and (11) invader introduced range. When a publication included the results of multiple, independent studies, we included a separate effect size for each study (see Effect size).

We included a total of 67 studies, originating from 15 publications in our analyses (Figure S2; Table S1). The top two reasons that studies were excluded from our analyses were: (1) the study was aquatic but not marine (i.e., a freshwater study), and (2) the study did not explicitly examine invasive species in combination with a climate stressor. The number of studies per publication ranged from one to 18, with a median of two. We determined a priori that a minimum of N = 3 studies was needed to be included in statistical analyses. A total of 30 non‐native species was included in our analysis (Table S1).

Effect Size

We used the natural logarithmic response ratio (lnRR) as an effect size for several reasons, which is calculated as follows: lnRR=X¯EX¯C where X¯E and X¯C are the average measured response in the experimental and control treatments, respectively (Hedges et al. 1999; Lajeunesse and Forbes 2003). The lnRR is often used in meta‐analyses in ecology because it does not require knowledge of standard deviation or sample size when performing an unweighted meta‐analysis (e.g., Sorte, Williams, and Carlton 2010). This feature is important because many of the studies included in our analysis did not report the standard deviation. Another advantage is that the lnRR can be used for non‐experimental data where the aim is to compare the means of two groups as a measure of an effect (Gerstner et al. 2017). We could, therefore, include observational studies in our analysis in which the two groups compared are the means before versus after a climate stressor was observed, although we acknowledge that these two groups are not fully independent. A third advantage is that the lnRR is easily back transformed to provide estimates of average percent change in an effect, thus allowing us to interpret results in a biologically meaningful way.

However, lnRR can only be used if the mean effect of the control or treatment group is not zero. As our dataset included some zeros, we substituted zero for a very small non‐zero value, as suggested in other studies (Thapa et al. 2018). Specifically, when presented with a zero, we substituted it with 0.002, which is half of the smallest non‐zero value in our dataset.

Traditional meta‐analyses use weighted effect sizes in which the contribution of an individual study to the overall mean effect size is typically weighted by the inverse of its sampling variance (Gurevitch and Hedges 1999). This allows for more weight to be given to more precise studies (i.e., those with larger sample sizes) than less precise studies (i.e., those with smaller sample sizes) (e.g., Smith and Côté 2019). However, there are several reasons to weigh studies equally, including to avoid underestimation of effect sizes and increased Type 1 errors (Shurin et al. 2002). Also, in our dataset there was considerable diversity in study systems and biological responses to climate stressors, which ranged from the biochemical effect of an invasive seaweed on a native mussel over hours (Vieira et al. 2021) to changes in settlement date of an invasive oyster with climate warming over decades (King et al. 2021). Consequently, the expected variance among studies in our dataset differs considerably and is more likely due to the underlying biology of the system than to differences in statistical precision. An unweighted meta‐analysis is therefore appropriate because it emphasizes diversity among systems and moderators, but not pooled effect precision. Further, several studies in our dataset did not report a standard deviation, precluding the ability to perform a parallel weighted meta‐analysis for comparison. For all these reasons, we performed an unweighted meta‐analysis.

Research Questions

We had six a priori questions, five of which are presented in Figure S2. We also asked if there are invasion hotspots globally? This question was separated into two components: (1) Are certain regions of the world more likely to be a source or donor of invasive species? and (2) Are certain regions of the world more likely to be the recipient of invasive species?

Hotspots of Marine Invasions

We adopted the global marine biogeographic classification system developed by Spalding and colleagues (Spalding et al. 2007) to define marine provinces in our analysis. This system delineates the world's coastal and shelf waters into 62 distinct marine provinces based on shared biogeographic characteristics of marine ecosystems, including patterns in species composition, oceanographic features, evolutionary history, and endemism at the species level. To determine invasion hotspots, we used four online invasive species databases (i.e., CABI Invasive Species Compendium, Global Invasive Species Database, Nemesis, and World Register of Introduced Species). Specifically, we determined the source and recipient regions or countries for an invasives species in our list based on the online databases and then matched them with the corresponding marine province from Spalding and colleagues (Spalding et al. 2007). Finally, we tallied the number of species per province. We excluded cryptogenic species, that is, species whose native range is unclear, from this analysis. Our dataset included five cryptogenic species (i.e., Asparagopsis armata , Pontogammarus maeoticus, Pontogammarus maeoticus, Rugulopteryx okamurae, and Semimytilus algosus).

Statistical Analysis

Models

To test our five hypotheses related to invasion success and climate change, we constructed five separate mixed‐effects models (multilevel meta‐regressions) in which each a priori explanatory variable (or moderator) was treated as a fixed effect while study was treated as a random intercept to account for instances in which multiple effect sizes originated from a single study (see Nakagawa and Santos 2012; Noble et al. 2017) (Figure S2). In all cases except for one (see Figure S2), we subset the data by study type because our analysis showed that study type was a significant predictor of effect size (see Results; Figure S2). We did not expect a common, overall effect of climate change on invasion success across all study types to be meaningful due to diverse methods, time scales, and taxa; hence, we did not perform a random effects model of the full dataset. We also did not construct random‐effects models across various subgroupings because we did not record within‐study variance due to data limitations and instead used an unweighted model (see Effect size).

Assessing Heterogeneity

We used restricted maximum‐likelihood estimation to measure residual heterogeneity among effect sizes (i.e., the variability among true effects that is not accounted for by the explanatory variables or moderators in the model, 𝜏2) (Viechtbauer 2010). Cochran's Q test was used to test the significance of 𝜏2 in mixed‐effect models (Hedges and Olkin 1985) while we used the QM statistic to determine whether explanatory variables in mixed‐effects models accounted for some of the heterogeneity among studies.

Assessing Publication Bias and Other Sensitivity Analyses

The largest potential source of Type I error in meta‐analysis is publication bias (Harrison 2011). We assessed for publication bias using Rosenthal's Fail‐safe N approach, which calculates the number of missing studies with non‐significant results that we would have to locate and incorporate into our analyses for the significance level of our analyses to fall below a particular alpha level. In our case, alpha was set at 0.05. Because we used an unweighted meta‐analysis, we did not also assess for bias by looking for asymmetry in the funnel plot, which is non‐informative in this instance. Another source of potential bias in our study is that many studies examined the effects of climate change on the invasion success of the Pacific oyster, Crassostrea gigas . To determine how sensitive our main conclusion was to studies conducted by this taxon, we removed all studies on the Pacific oyster from our dataset and then re‐ran our main mixed effects model in which study type was a fixed effect and study was a random intercept.

We conducted all analyses using the metafor package (Viechtbauer 2010) in R (v. 4.2.1; R Development Core Team 2014). We extracted original data needed to calculate effect sizes from figures in studies using ImageJ (Schneider et al. 2012), when necessary.
