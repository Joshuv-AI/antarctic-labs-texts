Structural design and cytotoxic profiling of a novel 1,3,4-thiadiazole derivative targeting MCF-7 and HCT-116 cell lines: synthesis, cytotoxicity, and in silico study
Authors: Mostafa Sayed, Ehdaa Mohammed, Doha H. Aboubaker, Ahmed A. El-Rashedy, Reda Hassanien, Mahmoud S. Tolba, Mostafa Ahmed
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Cancer remains a leading cause of mortality worldwide, necessitating the continuous development of more effective and selective therapeutic agents. Among heterocyclic scaffolds, 1,3,4-thiadiazole derivatives have attracted considerable attention due to their diverse pharmacological properties and favorable physicochemical characteristics. In this study, a novel thiadiazole-based compound namely 2-((5-acetamido-1,3,4-thiadiazol-2-yl)thio)-N-(naphthalen-2-yl)acetamide, was successfully synthesized in excellent yield (99%) and high thermal stability and was fully structurally characterized using FT-IR, NMR, and mass spectrometry. The anticancer potential of the synthesized compound was evaluated in vitro against three human cancer cell lines, namely breast adenocarcinoma (MCF-7), colorectal carcinoma (HCT-116), and hepatocellular carcinoma (HepG-2), using the MTT assay. The compound exhibited pronounced cytotoxic activity against MCF-7 and HCT-116 cell lines, with IC50 values of 1.07 ± 0.03 µM and 2.09 ± 0.55 µM, respectively, demonstrating superior potency compared to the reference drug doxorubicin. In contrast, no significant activity was observed against HepG-2 cells, indicating a degree of selectivity. These findings highlight the potential of the 1,3,4-thiadiazole scaffold as a promising platform for the development of novel anticancer agents with enhanced efficacy and selectivity. In silico investigations, including molecular docking, molecular dynamics simulations, and MM/GBSA analysis, revealed stable binding of the synthesized compound within the VEGFR2 active site, supported by favorable binding free energy and key ligand–residue interactions.

Introduction

The increasing prevalence of antimicrobial resistance continues to pose a serious threat to global public health, necessitating sustained efforts toward the discovery of novel therapeutic agents with improved efficacy and safety profiles.1,2 In this context, heterocyclic compounds have emerged as indispensable tools in medicinal chemistry, owing to their structural diversity and broad spectrum of biological activities. Among these, thiadiazole derivatives have attracted significant attention due to their versatile pharmacological properties and synthetic adaptability3–5 due to their diverse biological activities and structural versatility. Structurally, thiadiazoles are five-membered heterocycles incorporating sulfur and nitrogen atoms, and their derivatives have demonstrated a wide array of biological activities, including antimicrobial,6 antioxidant,7 anticonvulsant,8 nematocidal,9 antiviral,10 anticancer,11 antiureolytic12 and anti-parasitic activities.13–23

Despite significant advances in cancer therapeutics, the treatment of malignant diseases remains a major clinical challenge.24,25 Conventional chemotherapy is often limited by poor selectivity and severe adverse effects, primarily resulting from damage to normal tissues.26,27 Neurotoxicity and other systemic toxicities associated with commonly used anticancer drugs further emphasize the urgent need for safer and more selective therapeutic alternatives.28 Therefore, the development of novel anticancer agents capable of selectively targeting tumor cells while minimizing off-target effects is of paramount importance.29 Among the different thiadiazole isomers, 1,3,4-thiadiazole has emerged as a particularly valuable scaffold in drug design due to its favorable electronic properties and ability to interact with diverse biological targets. Numerous studies have reported that 1,3,4-thiadiazole derivatives exhibit a wide range of biological activities, including antimicrobial, anti-inflammatory, anticonvulsant, antitubercular, and anticancer effects.30–33 The biological versatility of this scaffold is largely attributed to its capacity for structural modification, allowing the incorporation of diverse substituents to fine-tune pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic properties. Recent research has focused on the strategic functionalization of thiadiazole derivatives to enhance their biological activity and selectivity.34 In this regard, N-substituted chloroacetamide moieties have been identified as promising functional groups with notable biological relevance, particularly in antimicrobial applications23,35–41 that N-substituted chloroacetamides are highly effective and selective as microbial reagents. Furthermore, advances in computational chemistry, including molecular docking and molecular dynamics simulations, have significantly contributed to understanding ligand–target interactions and guiding the rational design of bioactive molecules.42,43 These approaches have facilitated the identification of key structural features responsible for improved biological performance. Structure–activity relationship (SAR) investigations have revealed that the biological activity of thiadiazole derivatives is strongly influenced by substitution patterns and electronic characteristics of the core structure.44 Substitution at the 2- and 5-positions of the 1,3,4-thiadiazole ring has been shown to play a crucial role in modulating anticancer activity.45,46 Additionally, the incorporation of aromatic moieties and heterocyclic hybrids has been reported to enhance lipophilicity, cellular uptake, and target binding affinity, thereby improving overall therapeutic potential.47,48 Despite these advances, the development of thiadiazole-based compounds with enhanced selectivity and superior anticancer activity remains an ongoing challenge.49 In particular, the design of hybrid molecules combining thiadiazole with bioactive fragments capable of improving cytotoxic potency and selectivity toward specific cancer cell lines is still underexplored.50

Motivated by our earlier work demonstrating the antimicrobial potential of thiadiazole-based compounds,51 the present study was undertaken to explore a structurally distinct naphthalene analogue with potential anticancer activity. The strategic introduction of a naphthalene moiety, a privileged scaffold in medicinal chemistry known for its enhanced lipophilicity and π–π stacking capability, was designed to shift the biological profile from antimicrobial to anticancer activity. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first report of a naphthalene-thiadiazole acetamide hybrid with potent activity against MCF-7 and HCT-116 cell lines. In the present study, we report the synthesis, full characterization, and biological evaluation of a novel 1,3,4-thiadiazole derivative incorporating a naphthalene-based acetamide moiety. The anticancer activity was investigated in vitro against colorectal carcinoma (HCT-116), hepatocellular carcinoma (HepG-2), and breast adenocarcinoma (MCF-7) cell lines. Computational studies, including molecular docking, MD simulations, and MM/GBSA, were employed to elucidate the binding mode and stability within the VEGFR2 active site.

Results and discussion

Chemistry

The target compound, 2-((5-acetamido-1,3,4-thiadiazol-2-yl)thio)-N-(naphthalen-2-yl)acetamide (6), was successfully synthesized in excellent yield (99%) through a concise and efficient multi-step synthetic pathway, as outlined in Scheme 1. The overall strategy relied on the construction of the 1,3,4-thiadiazole core followed by sequential functionalization to introduce both acetamide and naphthalene moieties, affording the final hybrid structure in excellent yield. The synthesis commenced with the cyclization of thiosemicarbazide (1) with carbon disulfide (2) in the presence of anhydrous sodium carbonate under reflux conditions in ethanol, leading to the formation of 2-amino-5-mercapto-1,3,4-thiadiazole (3).52 Mechanistically, this transformation proceeds via the nucleophilic attack of the terminal amino group of thiosemicarbazide on carbon disulfide, forming a dithiocarbazate intermediate. Subsequent intramolecular cyclization, accompanied by elimination of hydrogen sulfide, results in the formation of the thiadiazole ring system. Acidification of the reaction mixture promotes protonation and precipitation of the desired heterocyclic product in high yield, confirming the efficiency of the cyclization process. In the next step, compound 3 underwent acylation with acetic anhydride in acetic acid to afford 2-acetamido-5-mercapto-1,3,4-thiadiazole (4).53 This reaction involves nucleophilic attack of the amino group on the electrophilic carbonyl carbon of acetic anhydride, leading to the formation of the corresponding acetamide derivative. The introduction of the acetyl group not only enhances the stability of the molecule but also modulates its electronic properties, which may influence its subsequent reactivity and biological activity. The high yield obtained for this step reflects the smooth and selective nature of the acylation process. The final step involved the S-alkylation of compound 4 with 2-chloro-N-(naphthalen-2-yl)acetamide (5) in the presence of fused sodium acetate in ethanol under reflux conditions. In this transformation, the thiol group of compound 4 is deprotonated under basic conditions to generate a reactive thiolate anion, which acts as a strong nucleophile. This thiolate species subsequently undergoes an SN2-type nucleophilic substitution with the electrophilic methylene carbon of the chloroacetamide derivative 5, resulting in the formation of a thioether linkage and yielding the target compound 2-((5-acetamido-1,3,4-thiadiazol-2-yl)thio)-N-(naphthalen-2-yl)acetamide (6). The absence of detectable side products and the excellent yield (99%) highlight the high efficiency and selectivity of this alkylation step, which can be attributed to the good leaving ability of the chloride ion and the strong nucleophilicity of the thiolate intermediate.

Overall, the developed synthetic route proved to be efficient, high-yielding, and reproducible, enabling the successful preparation of the target thiadiazole derivative with excellent purity. The combination of mechanistic understanding and comprehensive spectroscopic characterization confirms the successful formation of the designed molecular architecture, which serves as a promising scaffold for further biological evaluation.

Anticancer activity

Cytotoxicity of the target compound 6

Accordingly, thiadiazole derivative 6 was examined in vitro to quantify their inhibitory activity against antitumor cell lines such as HCT-116 (Colorectal carcinoma), HepG-2 (Hepatocellular carcinoma), and MCF-7 (Human Breast Adenocarcinoma) by using the MTT method. Doxorubicin, one of the common small-molecule anti-cancer drugs, was employed for comparison. The data generated were utilized to construct a dose–response curve, allowing for the determination of the concentration of test compounds required to kill 50% of the cell population (IC50). Cytotoxic activity is reported as the mean IC50 from three independent experiments. The results are presented in Table 1. The examined compound showed several degrees of inhibitory effects on tested human tumor cells. Notably, compound 6 exhibited remarkable selectivity for breast (MCF-7) and colorectal (HCT-116) cancer cells over hepatocellular carcinoma (HepG-2) cells, with no significant activity observed against HepG-2 (IC50 > 100 µM). This selectivity is noteworthy, as many anticancer agents show broad-spectrum cytotoxicity without differentiation between cancer types. The observed selectivity may be attributed to differential expression of VEGFR2 or related kinases in these cell lines, or to cell-type-specific differences in drug uptake, metabolism, or efflux. This selectivity profile is particularly encouraging for the development of targeted therapies with reduced off-target effects.

Numerous compounds that incorporate the 1,3,4-thiadiazole scaffold in combination with other heterocycles have been reported in the last six years as potential anticancer agents. Notably, the pyridine derivatives,54 exhibited significant antiproliferative activity against cancer cells. Among the thiadiazoles, derivatives that are substituted at the 2 and 5 positions of the thiadiazole nucleus are the most prevalent and demonstrate the highest antiproliferative potency. A variety of these compounds have proven especially effective in the treatment of breast cancer, particularly against the MCF-7 cell line, exhibiting pro-apoptotic behavior55 and also displays cytotoxic effects on A549 lung cancer cells. This compound induces apoptosis in a concentration- and time-dependent manner, as confirmed by lactate dehydrogenase release, annexin V labeling, and TUNEL assays. The underlying mechanism of action involves the downregulation of Bcl-XL during short-term treatment and the upregulation of Bax during long-term treatment, both of which correlate with increased caspase-3 activity. Additionally, the inhibition of Akt/PKB activation plays a role in late-phase apoptosis. Furthermore, thiadiazole exhibits anti-angiogenic activity in a nude mouse model (Fig. 1). The anticancer activity of other 1,3,4-thiadiazoles derivatives was investigated.56 These derivatives demonstrated cytotoxic effects on various human cancer cell lines, including those derived from bladder cancer, non-small cell lung carcinoma, rectal adenocarcinoma, and breast cancer (T47D). Among them, compound 7, was particularly effective against HCV29T cells, exhibiting IC50 values of 3.7 and 1.1 µg mL−1, respectively. In comparison, cisplatin, the reference drug, exhibited significantly greater toxicity with an IC50 of 0.7 µg mL−1. Structure–activity relationship analysis indicated that aryl derivatives were generally more active than their alkyl counterparts, while the impact of the specific substituents on the aryl ring appeared negligible. Furthermore, linking the aryl ring via –CH2– or –OCH2– did not enhance the anticancer potency of these compounds.56 A more comprehensive quantitative structure–activity relationship analysis illuminated that the electron properties of the 1,3,4-thiadiazole ring were the most critical determinants of activity in these derivatives. The type of substitution at the fifth carbon influenced the charge distribution of this moiety, and molar refractivity (CMR) emerged as another significant parameter affecting the activity of 1,3,4-thiadiazole derivatives.57 Thiadiazole derivatives exhibit promising anticancer activity, with the specific structure influencing their effectiveness. The presence of an aromatic ring, particularly at the 5th position, enhances this activity. Substituents on the aromatic ring and amino group also play a crucial role in determining the anticancer potency. Thiadiazoles possess unique properties that make them useful scaffolds in medicinal chemistry. The presence of a sulfur atom in the thiadiazole structure enhances lipophilicity, while its mesoionic nature enables these compounds to more effectively traverse cellular membranes. As bioisosteres of pyrimidines, their derivatives can potentially interact with DNA and RNA. As mesoionic compounds, thiadiazoles can readily cross cell membranes and interact with biological compounds in unique ways. The broad range of activities exhibited by thiadiazole-containing compounds is testimony to their utility.58 Thiadiazole-containing drugs, such as the diuretics acetazolamide and methazolamide, as well as antibiotics cefazedone and cefazolin sodium, are already in clinical use. Additionally, a growing body of evidence highlights numerous thiadiazole derivatives that demonstrate anticancer activity in various in vitro and in vivo models. Furthermore, several thiadiazole-containing compounds have advanced to clinical trials, either as standalone therapies or in combination with established anticancer drugs.

Anticancer mechanism of thiadiazole.

Structure function relationship and mechanism of thiadiazol

A comparative analysis of compound 6 with our previously reported phenyl and tolyl analogues51 reveals the critical role of the naphthalene moiety in conferring anticancer activity. While these analogues exhibited only antimicrobial activity (MIC values of 2.40–4.80 µg mL−1 against various pathogens), compound 6 demonstrates potent cytotoxicity against cancer cell lines. This dramatic shift in biological activity underscores the importance of the naphthalene moiety in mediating interactions with cancer-specific targets such as VEGFR2, which is not a primary target for the antimicrobial phenyl/tolyl analogues.

Target selectivity and binding affinity are improved by the bicyclic naphthalene structure, which is especially well matched to the spatial restrictions of hinge areas in enzymes like VEGFR-2.59,60 A crucial component of ligand-target binding interactions, lipophilicity is increased by the naphthyl group. Furthermore, lipophilicity has a major impact on solubility, absorption, distribution, and elimination qualities. The compounds' “drug-like” properties are a result of their high lipophilicity values.61 According to Evren report,62 naphthyl-thiazole compounds containing a hydrazone moiety exhibit strong anticancer activity. Naphthyl-imidazole thiazole analogs, which are potent EGFR inhibitors, were created by Deng.63 The naphthalene derivative exhibits anticancer activity through a variety of mechanisms, including (i) inhibiting topoisomerase-II,64 (ii) intercalating to G + C regions of DNA64 (iii) blocking PDGF binding on fibroblasts via cell surface receptor,65 and (iv) inhibiting growth factor receptor binding.66 Interestingly, there is a little study in the literature on compounds with naphthalene units embedded in a thiazole scaffold.67 In the current work, the coupling of naphthalene moiety with thiazole core through azomethine function has been carefully constructed to examine the impact of medicines' steric properties on anticancer activity. Incorporating linkers such as hydrazides or enamides with certain methylene spacing improves metabolic stability while preserving target engagement (e.g., blocking tubulin beta polymerization).68 Pyrimidine, the fundamental building block of essential nucleic acids, uses the thiadiazole nucleus, a five-membered ring with one sulfur and two nitrogen atoms, as a bioisostere. As a result, the substance can easily pass through cellular membranes and obstruct DNA replication.69 The molecule's capacity to establish certain hydrogen bonds with active site residues is determined by the configuration of the sulfur and nitrogen atoms.70 Cytotoxicity against cancer cell lines is significantly increased by adding functional groups (such as an electron-withdrawing phenyl or halobenzyl group) at position-2 of the thiadiazole ring.71 While the compound demonstrates potent cytotoxicity against cancer cell lines, future studies should include non-cancerous cell lines (e.g., MCF-10A for breast, CCD-18Co for colon) to determine the therapeutic selectivity index and confirm the safety profile.

Computational biology study

Molecular dynamic and system stability

To better understand the dynamic behavior and stability of the ligand–protein complex, molecular dynamics (MD) simulations were carried out.72,73 This technique provides valuable insights into conformational changes, interaction persistence, and system equilibration over time. Ensuring the stability of the simulated systems is essential to avoid artifacts and to validate the reliability of the obtained trajectories. The structural stability of the systems was first evaluated using root-mean-square deviation (RMSD) analysis of the protein backbone atoms over the simulation time. The apo-protein system exhibited an average RMSD value of 1.41 ± 0.19 Å, whereas the compound 6–VEGFR2 complex showed a slightly lower average value of 1.36 ± 0.17 Å (Fig. 2A). This observation indicates that ligand binding does not destabilize the protein structure; instead, it promotes a more stable conformational state throughout the simulation. To further examine residue-level flexibility, root-mean-square fluctuation (RMSF) analysis was performed.74 The apo-protein displayed relatively higher fluctuations, with an average RMSF of 3.28 ± 0.26 Å, reflecting increased mobility in the absence of the ligand. In contrast, the ligand-bound system exhibited significantly reduced fluctuations, with an average RMSF value of 0.94 ± 0.48 Å (Fig. 2A and B). The statistical significance of the observed differences between the apo and ligand-bound systems was confirmed using Student's t-test (p < 0.05 for all pairwise comparisons over the 50 ns trajectory), indicating that the observed reductions are not attributable to random thermal fluctuations. While the absolute differences in RMSD (Δ = 0.05 Å), Rg (Δ = 0.10 Å), and SASA (Δ ≈ 425 Å2) are modest, they are statistically significant and biologically meaningful, as they are accompanied by a 70% reduction in RMSF at the binding pocket residues (Fig. 2B). This reduction in flexibility suggests that compound 6 stabilizes key regions of the protein, particularly around the binding site. The overall compactness and folding behavior of the systems were assessed using the radius of gyration (Rg).75 The apo-protein showed an average Rg value of 19.93 ± 0.09 Å, while the ligand-bound complex displayed a slightly lower value of 19.83 ± 0.07 Å (Fig. 2C). The decrease in Rg upon ligand binding indicates a more compact and structurally stable conformation of the protein. Additionally, the solvent-accessible surface area (SASA) was analyzed to evaluate changes in protein surface exposure to the solvent.76 The apo system exhibited a higher average SASA value (14922.24 Å2) compared to the compound 6–VEGFR2 complex (14497.13 Å2) (Fig. 2D). This reduction in SASA suggests decreased solvent exposure and the formation of a more tightly packed hydrophobic core in the presence of the ligand.

While the observed differences in RMSD (Δ = 0.05 Å), Rg (Δ = 0.10 Å), and SASA (Δ ≈ 425 Å2) between the apo and ligand-bound systems are modest in absolute magnitude, their statistical and biological significance warrants discussion. The RMSD values for both systems fall well within the range typically reported for stable protein simulations (≤3.0 Å), and the small absolute difference reflects that VEGFR2 is a structurally robust kinase domain that does not undergo large-scale conformational reorganization upon ligand binding. This is consistent with the “conformational selection” model, where ligands preferentially bind to pre-existing ground-state conformations rather than inducing global unfolding. The statistical significance of these differences was confirmed using Student's t-test (p < 0.05 for all pairwise comparisons over the 50 ns trajectory), indicating that the observed reductions are not attributable to random thermal fluctuations. Importantly, the biological relevance of these subtle changes is supported by the accompanying RMSF data, where ligand binding reduced backbone fluctuations by approximately 70% (from 3.28 ± 0.26 Å to 0.94 ± 0.48 Å) in the binding pocket region. This marked stabilization of specific residues, rather than global protein compaction, is the primary driver of enhanced binding affinity. Similarly, the modest Rg reduction (0.5%) combined with the more substantial SASA decrease (2.8%) suggests that ligand binding induces localized hydrophobic packing around the binding site without compromising the overall tertiary fold. These findings align with prior VEGFR2-inhibitor studies, where similarly subtle Rg changes (typically 0.1–0.3 Å) were associated with nanomolar binding affinities and significant functional inhibition. The observed reduction in structural fluctuations and maintenance of conformational integrity throughout the simulation support the strong and stable interaction of the ligand within the catalytic binding site.

Binding interaction mechanism based on binding free energy calculation

To quantitatively evaluate the binding affinity of compound 6 toward the VEGFR2 receptor, the molecular mechanics/generalized Born surface area (MM/GBSA) approach was employed. This method is widely recognized for providing reliable estimates of ligand–protein binding free energies by incorporating both molecular mechanics energies and solvation effects.77 The binding free energy (ΔG_bind) was calculated by extracting representative snapshots from the MD simulation trajectory and decomposing the total energy into its individual components. As summarized in Table 2, the calculated ΔG_bind value for the compound 6–VEGFR2 complex was found to be significantly negative (−45.90 ± 0.30 kcal mol−1), indicating a thermodynamically favorable binding process. A detailed analysis of the contributing energy terms reveals that the interaction is predominantly driven by van der Waals (ΔE_vdW = −50.07 ± 0.24 kcal mol−1) and electrostatic interactions (ΔE_elec = −40.47 ± 0.38 kcal mol−1), both of which contribute favorably to the overall binding energy. These negative values reflect strong non-covalent interactions between the ligand and key residues within the active site. The gas-phase interaction energy (ΔG_gas = −90.55 ± 0.41 kcal mol−1) further supports the strength of these interactions. In contrast, the solvation free energy term (ΔG_solv = 44.64 ± 0.25 kcal mol−1) exhibited a positive value, indicating an unfavorable contribution due to desolvation effects upon ligand binding. However, this penalty is effectively compensated by the strong favorable gas-phase interactions, resulting in an overall negative binding free energy. These findings demonstrate that the stability of the compound 6–VEGFR2 complex arises primarily from hydrophobic and electrostatic interactions within the binding pocket. The significant contribution of van der Waals forces suggests an optimal geometric fit of the ligand within the receptor cavity, while electrostatic interactions further enhance binding specificity. Overall, the MM/GBSA results corroborate the strong binding affinity and stability of compound 6, supporting its potential as an effective VEGFR2-targeted anticancer agent.

Identification of the critical residues responsible for ligands binding

To further elucidate the molecular basis of ligand recognition and stabilization within the VEGFR2 active site, a per-residue energy decomposition analysis was performed based on the MM/GBSA calculations. The results, illustrated in Fig. 3, reveal that several residues within the catalytic binding pocket play a crucial role in stabilizing compound 6 through favorable energetic contributions. Notably, residues such as Asp8 (−0.55 kcal mol−1), Val42 (−0.94 kcal mol−1), Ala60 (−0.433 kcal mol−1), Val61 (−0.431 kcal mol−1), and Glu79 (−4.79 kcal mol−1) contribute to the initial stabilization of the ligand within the binding cavity. Additional hydrophobic and van der Waals interactions are observed with residues including Leu80 (−0.149 kcal mol−1), Ile82 (−1.177 kcal mol−1), Leu83 (−1.319 kcal mol−1), and Ile86 (−0.369 kcal mol−1), which help anchor the ligand in an optimal orientation. Further contributions arise from residues such as Val92 (−0.378 kcal mol−1), Val93 (−0.979 kcal mol−1), Val108 (−0.616 kcal mol−1), Ile109 (−0.449 kcal mol−1), and Val110 (−1.604 kcal mol−1), reflecting the importance of hydrophobic interactions in maintaining ligand stability. Importantly, residues located deeper within the binding pocket, including Ile162 (−2.403 kcal mol−1), His163 (−1.533 kcal mol−1), and Asp183 (−3.172 kcal mol−1), exhibit more pronounced energy contributions, indicating their critical role in strengthening ligand binding through a combination of electrostatic and hydrogen-bonding interactions. Moreover, residues such as Cys161 (−0.722 kcal mol−1), Cys182 (−2.133 kcal mol−1), Phe184 (−1.249 kcal mol−1), and Leu156 (−0.501 kcal mol−1) further support ligand stabilization via hydrophobic contacts and π-related interactions. The cumulative contribution of these residues highlights a well-defined interaction network that governs ligand recognition and binding affinity. Overall, the per-residue decomposition analysis demonstrates that the binding of compound 6 is stabilized by a synergistic combination of hydrophobic, electrostatic, and hydrogen-bonding interactions involving key residues within the VEGFR2 active site. These findings provide valuable structural insights that can guide the rational design and optimization of future thiadiazole-based inhibitors with enhanced potency and selectivity.

Ligand–residue interaction network profiles

VEGFR2-compound 6 complex

The optimization of ligand–protein interactions is a central objective in rational drug design, as structural modifications can significantly enhance bioavailability, reduce toxicity, and improve pharmacokinetic behavior.78 Vascular endothelial growth factor receptor 2 (VEGFR2), a receptor tyrosine kinase encoded by the KDR gene, plays a pivotal role in angiogenesis by mediating endothelial cell proliferation and migration upon activation by its ligand, VEGF.79 Consequently, inhibition of VEGFR2 has emerged as an effective strategy in anticancer therapy. The binding mode of compound 6 within the catalytic active site of VEGFR2 was analyzed to elucidate the key interactions responsible for its stability and activity. As depicted in Fig. 4, the ligand adopts a well-defined orientation within the binding pocket, enabling multiple stabilizing interactions. The acetamide moiety extends into the active site region and participates in hydrogen bonding interactions with critical residues such as Asp183 and Ile162, contributing significantly to binding specificity and stability. The naphthalene ring system of compound 6 plays a crucial role in hydrophobic recognition, forming π–alkyl interactions with residues including Val42, Ala60, and Val110. These interactions enhance the ligand's anchoring within the hydrophobic cavity of the receptor. In addition, π–sulfur and π–sigma interactions are observed between the aromatic system of the ligand and residues such as Cys182 and Lys60, further reinforcing the ligand–protein complex. The combination of hydrogen bonding, hydrophobic contacts, and π-related interactions results in a stable and energetically favorable binding configuration. The spatial complementarity between compound 6 and the VEGFR2 active site highlights the importance of both the thiadiazole core and the appended naphthalene moiety in mediating strong and specific interactions. The interaction profile of compound 6 within the VEGFR2 binding pocket underscores its potential as a promising inhibitor, with a binding mode that supports both stability and biological activity. These findings provide valuable structural insights for the future design and optimization of thiadiazole-based anticancer agents targeting VEGFR2.

Principal component analysis (PCA)

Principal component analysis was performed to quantify the collective motions of VEGFR2 systems. The first two principal components (PC1 and PC2) account for 68.4% of the total positional variance in the apo system (PC1: 47.2%, PC2: 21.2%) and 72.1% in the compound 6–VEGFR2 complex (PC1: 51.3%, PC2: 20.8%). The higher cumulative variance captured in the complex indicates that ligand binding reduces the dimensionality of the conformational space, restricting the protein to a smaller set of essential motions. The trace of the covariance matrix, representing total Cα fluctuations, decreased from 42.7 nm2 (apo) to 31.8 nm2 (complex), a reduction of 25.5%. This quantitative decrease, together with the tighter clustering of PC projections shown in Fig. 5 and Table S1, confirms that compound 6 suppresses global atomic fluctuations and stabilizes the VEGFR2 structure. These metrics provide objective support for the enhanced stability inferred from visual inspection of the PCA scatter plot.

Dynamics cross-correlation matrices (DCCM) analysis

To investigate the conformational dynamics and coordinated motions of VEGFR2 upon ligand binding, dynamic cross-correlation matrix (DCCM) analysis was conducted based on the Cα atomic fluctuations throughout the simulation trajectories (Fig. 6). This analysis allows for the identification of correlated and anti-correlated motions between residue pairs. In the DCCM maps, regions exhibiting positive correlations (synchronized motion) are represented in yellow to red, whereas regions displaying negative correlations (opposing motion) are depicted in blue to black. Molecular electrostatic potential (MEP) analysis and correlation with binding interactions

To visualize the charge distribution across the molecular surface and predict regions susceptible to electrophilic or nucleophilic interactions, molecular electrostatic potential (MEP) analysis was carried out for compound 6 using the B3LYP/3-21G level of theory following geometry optimization. The MEP map serves as a valuable tool for identifying potential binding sites, as it simultaneously illustrates the size, shape, and electrostatic character of the molecule through a color gradient. In the generated map (Fig. 8), regions of negative electrostatic potential (depicted in red) indicate sites favorable for electrophilic attack, whereas regions of positive potential (depicted in blue) are prone to nucleophilic attack. Correlation with docking and MD interaction profiles

The MEP analysis reveals three distinct electrostatic features that directly correspond to key binding interactions observed in the molecular dynamics simulations: (1) Negative potential around the carbonyl oxygen atoms (red regions, −0.05 to −0.08 a.u.): the acetamide carbonyl oxygen (CO) exhibits significant negative electrostatic potential. This electron-rich center acts as a hydrogen bond acceptor, forming stable hydrogen bonds with the backbone NH of Asp183 and the side chain of Ile162 within the VEGFR2 active site (see Fig. 4 and per-residue decomposition in Fig. 3). The calculated hydrogen bond occupancy from MD trajectories exceeded 85% for the Asp183 interaction, directly correlating the MEP-predicted electron density with experimentally observed binding stability, (2) positive potential around the amide NH groups (blue regions, +0.04 to +0.07 a.u.): The two amide protons (δ 12.63 and 10.31 ppm in 1H NMR) display strong positive electrostatic potential, making them hydrogen bond donors. In the MD simulations, these NH groups engage in water-mediated hydrogen bonds with the carboxylate side chain of Glu79 and the backbone carbonyl of Cys182. The MM/GBSA decomposition identified Glu79 as a major contributor to binding free energy (−4.79 kcal mol−1), consistent with the MEP-predicted hydrogen bond donor capacity. (3) Delocalized negative potential over the thiadiazole ring and sulfur atoms (−0.03 to −0.06 a.u.): the 1,3,4-thiadiazole core, particularly the sulfur atoms, exhibits moderate negative potential. This region participates in π–sulfur interactions with Cys182 (per-residue contribution: −2.133 kcal mol−1) and π–alkyl interactions with Val42 (−0.94 kcal mol−1) and Val110 (−1.60 kcal mol−1). The MEP-predicted electron richness around the thiadiazole sulfur aligns with its role as a weak hydrogen bond acceptor and polarizable center for hydrophobic contacts.

Correlation with anticancer activity

The MEP features also rationalize the observed structure–activity relationship. The strong negative potential on the acetamide carbonyl explains why the acetamide moiety is essential for activity—its removal would eliminate the key hydrogen bond with Asp183, consistent with prior SAR studies on thiadiazole-based VEGFR2 inhibitors. Similarly, the positive potential on the naphthalene-linked amide NH correlates with its involvement in a water-bridged interaction network; compounds lacking this NH (e.g., N-methylated analogs) show reduced cytotoxicity (IC50 > 10 µM in preliminary unpublished data). The balanced electron distribution—neither too polarized nor too diffuse—supports the compound's ability to cross cell membranes (log P ∼2.8) while maintaining specific polar interactions, contributing to its low micromolar IC50 values against MCF-7 (1.07 µM) and HCT-116 (2.09 µM). In summary, the MEP analysis provides electronic-level rationalization for the specific ligand–residue interactions identified in the docking and MD studies, bridging computational chemistry with observed biological activity.

Conclusions

In this study, a novel 1,3,4-thiadiazole-based hybrid incorporating acetamide and naphthalene functionalities was successfully designed and synthesized via an efficient multi-step protocol, affording the target compound in excellent yield (99%) and purity. Comprehensive structural characterization confirmed the successful construction of the desired molecular architecture. The in vitro anticancer evaluation demonstrated remarkable cytotoxic activity, particularly against MCF-7 (IC50 = 1.07 µM) and HCT-116 (IC50 = 2.09 µM) cell lines, significantly superior to the reference drug doxorubicin, while showing negligible activity toward HepG-2 cells (IC50 > 100 µM), indicating a promising degree of selectivity. This selectivity is noteworthy and suggests cell-type-specific targeting. Computational investigations provided further insight into the molecular basis of this activity. Molecular docking and 50 ns molecular dynamics simulations revealed that compound 6 forms a stable complex within the VEGFR2 active site, maintaining structural integrity throughout the simulation period. MM/GBSA binding free energy calculations (−45.90 kcal mol−1) confirmed favorable interaction energetics, primarily driven by van der Waals and electrostatic contributions. Residue decomposition analysis identified key amino acids contributing to ligand stabilization, while PCA and DCCM analyses demonstrated reduced conformational flexibility upon ligand binding, supporting enhanced system stability. MEP analysis further supported these findings by revealing that the electron-rich acetamide carbonyl and the positive electrostatic potential around the amide NH groups directly correspond to key hydrogen-bonding interactions with Asp183 and Glu79 in the VEGFR2 active site. The integration of experimental and computational findings highlights the significant potential of this thiadiazole-based hybrid as a promising and selective anticancer candidate. The synergistic combination of the thiadiazole core, lipophilic naphthalene moiety, and flexible thioether linkage appears to play a crucial role in enhancing cellular uptake, target interaction, and biological performance. Future studies will focus on in vitro kinase assays, flow cytometry-based mechanistic studies, and systematic SAR exploration. These results provide valuable insights for the rational design of future thiadiazole-derived anticancer agents, warranting further mechanistic investigations and in vivo studies to fully explore their therapeutic potential.

Experimental

Synthesis of the target compounds

2‐Amino-5-thiol-1,3,4-thiadiazole (3)82

A suspension of thiosemicarbazide (0.25 mol) in absolute ethanol was treated with carbon disulfide (0.25 mol) in the presence of anhydrous sodium carbonate (0.25 mol). The reaction mixture was heated under reflux with continuous stirring for 1 h, followed by further heating on a steam bath for an additional 4 h to ensure completion. The progress of the reaction was monitored by thin-layer chromatography (TLC) using a solvent system of toluene/ethyl acetate/formic acid (5 : 4 : 1). Upon completion, the solvent was removed under reduced pressure, and the resulting residue was dissolved in water. The aqueous solution was then acidified with concentrated hydrochloric acid, leading to the precipitation of the desired product. The solid was collected by filtration, washed with water, and dried to afford compound 3 as yellowish crystals in 89% yield; m.p. 233 °C.

2-Acetylamino-5-mercapto-1,3,4-thiadiazole (4)53

Compound 3 (7 mmol) was dissolved in acetic acid (4 mL), and acetic anhydride (1 mL) was added dropwise under stirring. The reaction mixture was maintained at 100 °C for 20 min. After completion, the mixture was allowed to cool and then poured into ice-cold water, resulting in the formation of a precipitate. The solid product was filtered, thoroughly washed with water to remove residual reagents, and dried to yield compound 4 as a yellow solid in 90% yield; m.p. >300 °C.

Synthesis of 2-((5-acetamido-1,3,4-thiadiazol-2-yl)thio)-N-(naphthalen-2-yl)acetamide (6)

A mixture of compound 4 (1 g, 7.5 mmol) and fused sodium acetate (0.82 g, 10 mmol) was suspended in ethanol. To this mixture, a solution of 2-chloro-N-(naphthalen-2-yl)acetamide (5) (2.68 g, 8 mmol) in ethanol was added dropwise with stirring. The reaction mixture was then heated under reflux for 2 h. The formation of the product was observed within the first 10 min as a precipitate began to appear. After completion of the reaction, the mixture was cooled, and the solid product was collected by filtration. The crude product was purified by recrystallization from a mixture of ethanol and chloroform to afford compound 6 in excellent yield. Anticancer study

Cell culture

This study employed colorectal carcinoma (HCT-116), hepatocellular carcinoma (HepG-2), and breast cancer (MCF-7), Via a holding firm for vaccines and biological goods (Viscera), we were able to secure the cell lines from ATCC. The cells were cultured in DMEM (Corning, Kennebunk, ME, USA) supplemented with 10% Fetal Bovine Serum (Eurx, Gdansk, Poland) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin. Culturing took place in 100 mm plates (Sarstedt, Newton, NC, USA) within an incubator that maintained optimal growth conditions—5% CO2, a temperature of 37 °C, and humidity levels between 90–95%. Upon reaching 80% confluence, the cells were detached from the plate surface using 0.05% trypsin supplemented with 0.2% EDTA (Gibco, San Diego, CA, USA). The cell count was determined using a hemocytometer, and cells were subsequently seeded at a density of 5 × 105 cells per well in six-well plates (Sarstedt, Newton, NC, USA) with 2 mL of DMEM. Only cells achieving 80% confluence were utilized for further experimentation.

Cytotoxicity assay

The MTT assay was used to evaluate the cytotoxicity of Thiadiazole derivatives on three different cell line types: colorectal carcinoma (HCT-116), hepatocellular carcinoma (HepG-2), and breast cancer (MCF-7). Computational biology

System preparation and molecular docking

The codes 4ASD,84 the crystal structure of the vascular endothelial growth factor receptor 2 (VEGFR2) was obtained from the protein data bank and constructed using UCSF Chimera.85 The pH was adjusted and tuned to 7.5 using PROPKA.86 ChemBioDraw Ultra 12.1 was used to sketch the extracted 2D structure. The 2D structure was optimized for energy minimization using the steepest descent approach and the MMFF94 force field in the Avogadro program.87 Hydrogen atoms were eliminated with UCSF chimaera prior to docking.

Molecular docking

AutoDock Vina88 was used to calculate the docking process, and Gasteiger partial charges89 were applied at that time. The AutoDock atom types were delineated using the AutoDock graphical user interface provided by MGL tools. The AutoDock Vina grid center coordinates for x, y, and z are −24.49, −4.86872, and −10.7642. The search space size was set to 20 Å × 20 Å × 20 Å in each of the three examples, with an exhaustive value of 8. Using the Lamarckian genetic technique90 docked conformations were produced in decreasing order of docking energy.

Molecular dynamic (MD) simulations

The use of Molecular Dynamic (MD) simulations into the study of biological systems allows for the exploration of atoms and molecules' physical motion, which is not easily accessible by other means.91 The knowledge gained from running this simulation offers a detailed viewpoint on the dynamical evolution of biological systems, including molecular affiliation and conformational changes.91 The PMEMD engine on the GPU, which is part of the AMBER 18 package, was used to run the MD simulations for every system for 50 ns.92
