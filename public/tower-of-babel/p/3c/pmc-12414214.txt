Role of stroke volume in patients presenting with uncomplicated anterior STEMI
Authors: Muntaser Omari, Natasha James, Andrew Brown, Kadhim Kadhim, Bilal Bawamia, Adam McDiarmid, Mohammad Alkhalil
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundStroke volume is an established echocardiographic marker but has not been widely studied in patients with ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI). We aimed to evaluate stroke volume in a cohort of uncomplicated anterior STEMI and to assess its prognostic role in those with severe left ventricle (LV) systolic dysfunction.Methods and resultsThis is a single-centre retrospective analysis of consecutive patients presenting with anterior STEMI who underwent uncomplicated primary percutaneous coronary intervention. Stroke volume was measured using echocardiography within in-hospital admission. The primary endpoint was cardiovascular mortality. Of 3592 patients with STEMI, 616 were included. The mean age was 65±14 years (76% males). The average left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) and stroke volume were 39±11% and 62±18 mL, respectively. There was a modest relationship between stroke volume and LVEF (r=0.33, p<0.001), which was not maintained in patients with severe LV dysfunction (r=0.11, p=0.19). On multivariate analysis, stroke volume was associated with cardiovascular death at 12 months (HR 0.92, 95% CI 0.86 to 0.98, p=0.015) in patients with severe LV systolic dysfunction. Receiver operating characteristic analysis demonstrated an area under the curve of 0.80 (95% CI 0.70 to 0.91, p<0.001) in this group with negative predictive value of 99% for cardiovascular mortality.ConclusionsStroke volume is a readily and useful echocardiographic marker to assess prognosis in patients with anterior STEMI and severe LV systolic dysfunction. It identifies with a high degree of certainty those patients who are likely to survive despite their severe LV systolic dysfunction.

Introduction

Patients presenting with ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI) remain at increased risk of future major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE).1 2 While procedural and postprocedural care, including intensive secondary prevention, have resulted in a reduction in future adverse cardiovascular events,25 the event rate following anterior STEMI remains relatively high.6 7 This group is known to have larger infarct size and is associated with a twofold increase in MACE compared with non-anterior STEMI.6 In fact, emerging technologies are increasingly targeting anterior STEMI, given the degree of myocardium at jeopardy, aiming to reduce MACE in this high-risk group.810 A better understanding of the potential mechanisms leading to adverse events would allow future targeted therapies that are tailored to patients’ needs.

Cardiac MRI and invasive coronary physiology have been shown to identify high-risk patients.11 12 However, they are not routinely available in day-to-day clinical practice. Echocardiography, on the other hand, is easily accessible and is part of routine care in patients post-STEMI.2 Impaired left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) is a well-established risk marker of MACE13; however, the stunning phenomenon that can occur immediately following STEMI would not allow precise prediction of future events and reduce the ability of LVEF in determining adverse events.14

Stroke volume (SV) is an established marker to quantify cardiac output and is commonly used to aid diagnosis and assess prognosis in patients with valvular heart disease.1517 However, SV is not widely used when assessing patients presenting with acute myocardial infarction (MI). It may serve as an additive metric to evaluate prognosis in patients presenting with STEMI, beyond left ventricle dysfunction.

Therefore, the aims of the present study were to assess the role of SV in patients presenting with anterior STEMI and to determine whether SV is an independent predictor of cardiovascular mortality in patients with severely impaired left ventricle dysfunction.

Method

Study design

This was a single-centre, retrospective, observational study and was conducted according to the Declaration of Helsinki and Good Clinical Practice guidelines. Anonymised clinical and echocardiographic data were collected from the institutional database that is used for standard clinical care, as previously described.18 The clinical database is overlooked by dedicated data managers who prospectively ensured clinical data are accurate and completed accordingly.

Study population

Between January 2017 and December 2020, consecutive patients who were admitted to the Freeman Hospital, Newcastle upon Tyne, UK, via the primary percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) pathway following STEMI presentation, were screened for inclusion in this study. STEMI was defined according to the European Society of Cardiology guidelines and as the occurrence of ongoing chest pain for at least 30 min associated with ST-segment elevation in at least two contiguous leads.1 Patients were accepted to be transferred to the catheterisation laboratory after being triaged by an experienced coronary care unit (CCU) nurse coordinator and following review of clinical history alongside ECG. Patients were preloaded with aspirin 300 mg by the ambulance crew and received P2Y12 inhibitor on arrival to the Freeman Hospital (predominantly prasugrel 60 mg). Ticagrelor 180 mg was used when there were precautions to using prasugrel, such as age >75 years, low body weight <60 kg and previous stroke or transient ischaemic attack.

Primary PCI was performed according to current guidelines using right radial artery whenever possible.1 Heparin was used universally in all cases according to body weight. The use of thrombectomy and glycoprotein (GP) IIb/IIIa inhibitors was at the operator’s discretion. Following the procedure, patients were admitted to CCU for observation, and secondary prevention treatment was commenced by the attending cardiologist.

The current study only included patients presenting with uncomplicated anterior STEMI. Patients who presented with cardiogenic shock or required mechanical or non-invasive ventilatory support were excluded from the study. Similarly, patients with no flow-limiting lesions were also not included in the analysis. Additionally, patients who had procedural complications, resulting in prolonged hospital admission, or stayed in hospital for more than 72 hours were excluded from the analysis. Transthoracic echocardiography

Transthoracic echocardiography was performed within 72 hours of hospital admission. LVEF was estimated using the Simpson methodology when possible. In cases where the outline of the endocardial border was not clear, visual assessment by an experienced sonographer was recorded and confirmed by specialist cardiologists. Severe LV systolic dysfunction was defined as ejection fraction (EF) <35%.

SV was calculated by multiplying the left ventricular outflow tract (LVOT) cross-sectional area (CSA) and velocity time interval (VTI) using the following equation as previously described16 17 19 20:

Stroke volume=CSA(LVOT)*VTI(LVOT)

LVOT diameter was measured in the parasternal long-axis view below the level of the aortic annulus perpendicular to the long axis of the aorta in mid-systole.16 17 19 20 LVOT VTI was obtained from the Doppler signal in the apical five-chamber view after careful consideration to ensure that this measurement was done from the same location, whereby the LVOT diameter was recorded. LVOT diameter and LVOT VTI were measured ‘offline’ and were recorded by an experienced sonographer who was not aware of the aim of the study. LVOT VTI was averaged over five consecutive beats in patients with atrial fibrillation. Patients with significant valvular pathologies of more than moderate severity (stenosis or regurgitation) in any loci, including tricuspid regurgitation, were excluded from the study. Patients with suboptimal echo images, including those with poor endocardial borders, or unavailable Doppler signal were not included in the current analysis (figure 1).

Study endpoints

The primary study endpoint was cardiovascular mortality at 12 months following index presentation. Secondary endpoints included all-cause mortality and the composite endpoint of sudden death and the requirement for secondary prevention implantable cardioverter–defibrillator (ICD) at 12 months. Mortality data were provided by the Office for National Statistics and SV was assessed as an independent predictor of both cardiovascular and all-cause mortality.

Optimal heart failure treatment was defined according to the discharge medications and stratified according to the degree of LV systolic dysfunction. Patients who were discharged on the combination of beta blocker and ACE inhibitors (ACEi) or angiotensin II receptor blockers (ARB) with EF≥40%, or beta blocker, ACEi (or ARB) and mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists for those with EF<40% were considered to be on optimal heart failure treatment.

Statistical analysis

Continuous data were assessed for normality of distribution using the Shapiro-Wilk test, including visual assessment of histogram and skewness measurement, and presented mean±SD (if data were normally distributed) and compared using the unpaired t-test. For non-normally distributed data, this was presented as median accompanied by IQR and compared using Mann-Whitney U test. Categorical data were presented as absolute number and percentages (%), and frequency comparisons were made using the χ2 test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. Correlations were assessed using Pearson R coefficient unless one or both covariates violated its assumption, then Spearman rank test was used. Kaplan-Meier curves and the associated log-rank test were performed to determine the relationship between SV and cardiovascular and all-cause mortality. Cox regression HR was determined after adjustment to age, gender, body mass index (BMI), standard cardiovascular risk factors, haemoglobin, creatinine, obstructive pulmonary disease, previous MI, previous revascularisation, cerebrovascular events, peripheral vascular disease, haemodynamic on arrival, ischaemia time, multivessel disease, radial approach, use of GPIIb/IIIa, LV function, troponin and discharge medications. All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS V.28.0 (SPSS), and a p value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Over the study period, 3592 patients were transferred to the Freeman Hospital via the primary PCI pathway. Of those, 976 patients had uncomplicated anterior STEMI, but only 616 patients had echocardiogram results that met the study inclusion criteria (figure 1).

The mean age was 65±14 years and patients were predominantly male (76%). The average ischaemia time was 6.5 hours and the majority of patients had their primary PCI via the right radial approach (92%). The LV systolic function was 39±11% and 153 patients (24.8%) had severe LV systolic dysfunction (<35%).

The SV followed a relatively normal distribution pattern (figure 2), with an average of 62±18 mL in the whole cohort. Patients with low SV (defined as less than the mean) were less likely to be male (67% vs 85%, p<0.001), had lower BMI (27±5 vs 29±5, p<0.001) and lower LVEF (37±10 vs 42±10, p<0.001) compared with those with normal SV. There was no difference in clinical and procedural characteristics between the two groups, except for the use of GPIIb/IIIa (table 1). The difference in SV was derived from both higher LVOT VTI (15.8±3.1 vs 20.7±3.9, p<0.001) as well as larger LVOT diameter (2.0±0.2 vs 2.2±0.2, p<0.001) in patients with normal versus low SV.

The primary endpoint of cardiovascular death at 12 months occurred in 3.4% (21 out of 616 patients) of the whole cohort, of which the rates of cardiovascular death were 7.2% and 2.2% in patients with severe and non-severe LV dysfunction, respectively. The causes of cardiovascular death were heart failure (43%), sudden death (43%) and recurrent MI (14%). The distribution of death aetiologies was not identical in patients with severe versus non-severe LV dysfunction. Heart failure deaths (82%) dominated patients with severe LV dysfunction, while sudden death (70%) was the most common cause in those with non-severe LV dysfunction. All-cause mortality at 12 months was 3.9% (24 out of 616 patients) in the whole cohort, and 8.5% and 2.4% in patients with severe and non-severe LV dysfunction, respectively. When assessing the relationship between SV and the primary endpoint in patients with severe LV dysfunction, SV was associated with cardiovascular death at 12 months on univariate analysis (HR 0.93, 95% CI 0.88 to 0.97, p=0.003) and even after adjusting for other significant predictors in multivariate analysis model (HR 0.92, 95% CI 0.86 to 0.98, p=0.015) (table 3). A similar relationship was evident between SV and all-cause mortality. SV had a significant association with all-cause mortality on univariate (HR 0.96, 95% CI 0.92 to 1.00, p=0.029) and multivariate analyses, after adjustment for multiple other predictors (HR 0.95, 95% CI 0.90 to 1.00, p=0.039) (table 4). However, there was no significant difference in SV among patients who received secondary ICD treatment or sustained sudden cardiac death and those who did not (63±16 vs 55±16, p=0.28).

Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis using SV demonstrated an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.80 (95% CI 0.70 to 0.91, p<0.001) in predicting cardiovascular mortality for patients with severe LV systolic dysfunction (figure 4). Using different thresholds, including optimal ROC analysis-based cut-off, the sensitivity and negative predictive value of low SV in predicting cardiovascular mortality were excellent and calculated as 91% and 99%, respectively (table 5). Using a threshold of 50 mL among patients with severe LV dysfunction, the primary endpoint was more frequent in those with low versus high SV (15.6% vs 1.1%, p<0.001) (figure 5).

Discussion

This is the largest study that evaluated the role of SV, as measured on echocardiography, in patients presenting with anterior STEMI who underwent uncomplicated primary PCI procedure and uneventful in-hospital stay. The main findings from this study can be summarised as follows: first, SV followed normal distribution and had modest correlation with LVEF; second, this relationship was not maintained in patients with severe LV systolic dysfunction; third, SV was an independent predictor of cardiovascular and all-cause mortality but not the composite of sudden cardiac death and secondary ICD treatment; and fourth, SV was a reliable measurement to effectively rule out cardiovascular death with optimal threshold of 45–50 mL.

Patients with acute MI remain at increased risk of future adverse events, despite being treated with primary PCI and guidelines-directed optimal medical treatment. Advances in preprocedural and intraprocedural management alongside better pharmacological treatments have led to a reduction in mortality and morbidity.32123 Nonetheless, their residual risk remains significant, and there is a lack of easily available prognostic marker that may help tailor treatment to reduce this residual risk. Cardiac MRI, particularly mapping technique, provides additional prognostic information that discerns high-risk patients presenting with STEMI.11 21 24 25 Similarly, invasive coronary physiology using index microcirculatory resistance identifies patients who are at risk of in-hospital complications as well as long-term adverse outcomes.2628 However, these tests are not readily available in every cardiac centre and are associated with additional costs that may limit their widespread use.

In contrast, echocardiography is an essential assessment for patients postacute MI and is recommended to be done prior to hospital discharge. SV is routinely measured on echocardiogram and widely used in assessing patients with valvular heart disease, but has not been systematically studied in patients presenting with STEMI. We elected to investigate the role of SV in patients with anterior STEMI. This group is at heightened risk compared with non-anterior, which may be related to the larger myocardium at jeopardy.6 Additionally, we further evaluated the impact of low SV in patients with anterior STEMI and severe LV systolic dysfunction. Identifying a subgroup within this high-risk group would aid physicians in formulating a management plan and provide patients with important prognostic information about their future risk.

Our study highlighted that SV was an independent predictor of both all-cause and cardiovascular mortality in patients with severe LV systolic dysfunction. This was evident even after adjusting for multiple variables that are known to be associated with worse prognosis following STEMI presentation. Moreover, SV was able to characterise patients with severe LV systolic dysfunction. It added a second domain to understand future risk beyond LVEF measurement. Notably, the relationship between EF and SV was not present in our cohort of anterior STEMI and poor LV function. This may suggest a competing risk exerting a different mechanism that contributes to adverse outcomes, beyond LV systolic dysfunction. Previous cohort studies have highlighted a stepwise increase in the risk of cardiovascular death, heart failure and arrhythmia according to LV systolic dysfunction.13 It may be reasonable to speculate that deaths in patients with severe LV systolic dysfunction are mechanistically related to either heart failure or arrhythmia, including sudden cardiac deaths. Nonetheless, our data did not demonstrate any association between SV and the secondary endpoint of sudden cardiac death or ICD implantation. While this may make arrhythmia a less likely mechanism, our study did not systematically evaluate the burden of arrhythmia in patients with low SV. In fact, the Vest Prevention of Early Sudden Death Trial (VEST) showed that the wearable cardioverter–defibrillator did not lead to a significantly lower rate of arrhythmic death than control in patients with recent MI and an EF of 35% or less.29 Future studies may add further insights into the role of arrhythmia as a potential mechanism of adverse events in this cohort.

Our study highlighted that mortality related to heart failure was common, particularly in patients with severe LV dysfunction. Optimal heart failure treatment was associated with almost 90% risk reduction of mortality, highlighting a potential modifiable cause of death. Moreover, the separation in Kaplan-Meier curves happened very early and almost within 2 months following hospital discharge. It is important to highlight that the included patients were considered well, following their STEMI presentation, and had uncomplicated procedures and uneventful in-hospital stays. It remains to be determined whether early follow-up—specifically at 4 weeks posthospital discharge—or intensifying medical treatment by adding glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) or angiotensin receptor neprilysin inhibitor (ARNI) treatment may change outcome. This targeted group of low SV was relatively small and accounted for 40% of patients with anterior STEMI who developed severe LV systolic dysfunction. Importantly, those with ‘normal’ SV had a high negative predictive value (99%) and effectively excluded future adverse events. Characterising patients using simple measurements on echocardiogram would certainly allow better understanding of the mechanisms of future risk and enable us to tailor treatment to reduce this risk.

Our study has several limitations that need to be highlighted. First, this is a single-centre retrospective analysis, and such design has inherent limitations that need to be considered when interpreting the results. Second, measurements of SV are prone to error, particularly when it comes to LVOT diameter measurement and the assumption of circularity of LVOT area. Nonetheless, it is a well-validated echocardiographic marker and well established in the work-up for valvular heart disease. Finally, our study did not capture heart failure hospitalisations. Therefore, we would not be able to ascertain whether low SV was associated with increased risk of readmission with heart failure.

Conclusions

SV is a readily and useful echocardiographic marker to assess prognosis in patients with anterior STEMI and severe LV systolic dysfunction. It identifies with a high degree of certainty those patients who are likely to survive despite their severe LV systolic dysfunction.
