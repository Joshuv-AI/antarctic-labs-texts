Transcription factor activity profiling uncovers prognostic subtypes and a novel oncogenic role for PRDM4 in NSMP endometrial carcinoma
Authors: Sisi Liu, Min Sun, Xiuying Wang, Wen Feng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAmong the four molecular subtypes of endometrial carcinoma (EC), the no specific molecular profile (NSMP) group is the largest, comprising roughly 40–50% of cases. Patients within this subtype follow markedly different clinical courses, yet no reliable tools exist to separate those who will do well from those at risk of recurrence. Because transcription factors (TFs) sit at the top of gene regulatory hierarchies, their activity patterns may capture biological differences that conventional expression signatures miss.MethodsWe analyzed transcriptomic, mutation, and clinical data from TCGA-UCEC. An EC-specific regulatory network was built with ARACNe-AP, and per-sample TF protein activity was estimated using VIPER. Consensus clustering on the activity matrix defined novel subtypes within the NSMP population. We characterized these subtypes through pathway, immune infiltration, and mutation analyses. A LASSO-Cox model based on TF activities was trained and tested in an independent GEO cohort, then combined with clinical variables into a nomogram. The top-ranked master regulator, PRDM4, was investigated by immunohistochemistry, shRNA knockdown, overexpression experiments, and chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP-qPCR).ResultsThree subtypes (C1–C3) emerged with clearly separated survival curves (log-rank P < 0.0001). C1 tumors showed active immune signaling, frequent PTEN/PIK3CA mutations, and good outcomes. C3 tumors were enriched for epithelial-mesenchymal transition and hypoxia programs, carried an immunosuppressive microenvironment dominated by regulatory T cells and M2 macrophages, harbored more TP53 mutations, and had the worst prognosis. A nine-TF risk score achieved 3-year AUC values of 0.775 (training) and 0.579 (validation). The accompanying nomogram showed good calibration and decision-curve performance. PRDM4 was the master regulator most strongly linked to the aggressive C3 phenotype. In functional assays, PRDM4 drove proliferation, migration, and invasion while suppressing apoptosis. ChIP-qPCR confirmed direct binding of PRDM4 to the LATS2, VEGFA, and ITGA5 promoters.ConclusionsTF activity profiling splits NSMP EC into prognostically meaningful groups and yields a workable risk model. PRDM4 functions as an oncogenic transcription factor in this context, offering a candidate biomarker and potential therapeutic target for high-risk NSMP patients.Keywords: Endometrial carcinoma, NSMP subtype, Transcription factor activity, PRDM4, Prognostic model, Transcriptional regulatory network

Introduction

Endometrial carcinoma (EC) ranks among the most common gynecological malignancies globally, and its incidence has climbed steadily over the past two decades [1, 2]. Rising rates of obesity, metabolic syndrome, and population aging are the principal drivers of this trend [3, 4]. Most patients diagnosed at an early stage do well, but a meaningful fraction presents with advanced or recurrent disease where treatment options are limited and outcomes remain poor [5, 6]. Better molecular stratification—capable of flagging high-risk patients early and guiding individualized therapy—is an unmet clinical need. Current screening and diagnostic strategies for EC remain limited in their ability to identify high-risk populations, underscoring the importance of molecular-based approaches [7].

The molecular classification of EC took a decisive step forward with large-scale genomic profiling, which defined four subtypes: DNA polymerase epsilon (POLE) ultramutated, microsatellite instability-high (MSI-H), copy number low (no specific molecular profile, NSMP), and copy number high (serous-like) [8–11]. This taxonomy has been adopted by major international guidelines and has reshaped how clinicians assess prognosis and plan treatment, with ongoing refinements in classification and therapeutic decision-making [12, 13]. POLE-mutated tumors carry an excellent prognosis irrespective of histological grade; MSI-H tumors show intermediate outcomes and potential sensitivity to immune checkpoint inhibitors; copy number high tumors behave aggressively [14–16]. The NSMP subtype, however, remains a management challenge—it is the largest group, accounting for roughly 40–50% of all EC cases, yet the least well-defined.

NSMP is essentially a diagnosis of exclusion: no POLE exonuclease domain mutations, microsatellite stability, and no widespread somatic copy number alterations [17–19]. This negative definition inevitably sweeps together patients with very different biology. Some follow indolent courses rivaling the POLE subtype; others develop aggressive recurrences and distant metastases [20–22]. The subtype has accordingly been called the “gray zone” of EC molecular classification [23, 24]. No consensus biomarkers or therapeutic targets exist for it, and standard clinicopathological parameters—grade, stage—offer insufficient prognostic resolution. There is a clear need to crack open this heterogeneous group and find biologically grounded features that enable finer stratification.

Most transcriptomic subtyping efforts have relied on differential gene expression [23, 24]. Such analyses are informative but capture downstream consequences of dysregulated programs rather than the upstream drivers themselves [25, 26]. Transcription factors (TFs) sit at the top of gene regulatory hierarchies: by binding regulatory elements and modulating coordinated gene networks (regulons), they govern proliferation, differentiation, epithelial-mesenchymal transition, immune evasion, and metabolic reprogramming—processes central to tumor initiation, progression, and treatment resistance [27–31]. Critically, a TF’s functional activity depends not only on its own mRNA level but on post-translational modifications, protein-protein interactions, subcellular localization, and cofactor availability [32, 33]. Measuring TF mRNA therefore often misrepresents true protein-level activity, motivating computational alternatives.

Two algorithms address this gap. ARACNe (Algorithm for the Reconstruction of Accurate Cellular Networks) uses information-theoretic principles to build context-specific regulatory networks, identifying statistically significant mutual information between TFs and targets while pruning indirect edges via the data processing inequality [34]. VIPER (Virtual Inference of Protein-activity by Enriched Regulon analysis) then infers TF activity by testing whether a TF’s regulon is coordinately shifted in a given sample’s expression signature [35]. This approach has proven more robust and biologically coherent than direct mRNA quantification and has successfully identified master regulators and clinically relevant subtypes across multiple cancer types [36]. Its application to the NSMP heterogeneity problem in EC, however, has not been explored.

The PRDM (PRDI-BF1 and RIZ homology domain containing) family of transcription factors has attracted growing interest in cancer biology. PRDM proteins function as epigenetic regulators—either through intrinsic histone methyltransferase activity or by recruiting chromatin-modifying complexes—and modulate gene expression programs critical for cell fate and tumorigenesis [37]. Several family members have been implicated as oncogenes or tumor suppressors in various malignancies. PRDM4, however, remains virtually unstudied in solid tumors, and its role in EC is unknown.

In this study we set out to address these gaps through a multi-layered investigation. We constructed an EC-specific transcriptional regulatory network and inferred TF protein activity across NSMP patients using the ARACNe-VIPER framework. From the resulting activity profiles we performed unsupervised clustering to identify novel subtypes with distinct prognoses and molecular characteristics, and pinpointed the master regulators and pathways defining each group. We then built and externally validated a TF activity-based prognostic risk model using LASSO-Cox regression, integrating it with clinicopathological variables into a nomogram for individualized survival prediction. Finally, we identified PRDM4 as a key master regulator of the aggressive subtype and validated its oncogenic function through loss- and gain-of-function experiments, with chromatin immunoprecipitation confirming its direct transcriptional targets. Our findings provide a framework for resolving NSMP prognostic heterogeneity, a practical tool for risk stratification, and, to the best of our knowledge, the first characterization of PRDM4 as an oncogenic driver and potential therapeutic target in EC.

Materials and methods

Data acquisition and preprocessing

Transcriptomic data (STAR-Counts workflow), somatic mutation files (Masked Somatic Mutation, MAF format), and clinical annotations for the TCGA uterine corpus endometrial carcinoma (UCEC) project were downloaded through the TCGAbiolinks R package. Molecular subtype labels came from the TCGA PanCancer Atlas, accessed via the TCGAquery_subtype function. We kept only primary tumor samples and removed normal tissue and duplicates.

Raw counts were restricted to protein-coding genes, converted to transcripts per million (TPM) to account for gene length and library size, and log₂(TPM + 1)-transformed. Genes expressed above 1 TPM in fewer than 10% of samples were discarded. The TCGA STAR-Counts workflow incorporates robust internal normalization; to further reduce technical variation, the remaining matrix was quantile-normalized with the normalizeBetweenArrays function from the limma package.

Patients classified as “copy number low” (NSMP) with complete survival data formed the training cohort; patients identified as POLE ultramutated, MSI-H, or copy number high (serous-like/p53-abnormal) were excluded. The TCGA-UCEC project comprises mixed histologies, predominantly endometrioid carcinoma (~ 80%) together with serous and mixed types; our molecular subtyping specifically isolated the NSMP group from this histologically diverse cohort. A separate EC expression dataset was obtained from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) via the GEOquery package, processed identically, and used for external validation.

Regulatory network construction

A curated list of human transcription factors was downloaded from AnimalTFDB 4.0 (http://bioinfo.life.hust.edu.cn/AnimalTFDB4/) and intersected with genes in the normalized matrix to define the regulator set, yielding a final set of 1634 transcription factors used for network construction.

We ran ARACNe-AP on the full TCGA-UCEC tumor expression matrix (all molecular subtypes pooled) to maximize statistical power for detecting robust, generalizable TF–target dependencies, which are often conserved across related tumor states. Sample-specific context was subsequently restored at the activity-inference stage by VIPER, which evaluates regulon enrichment within each individual NSMP tumor relative to the cohort mean. The algorithm estimates mutual information (MI) between each TF and every other gene, then applies the data processing inequality (DPI) to prune indirect edges. Settings: 100 bootstrap iterations, DPI tolerance = 0, MI P-value threshold = 1 × 10⁻⁸. The output is a collection of regulons—each linking one TF to its predicted targets along with mode of regulation (activation or repression) and interaction confidence.

Transcription factor activity inference

TF protein activity was inferred for each NSMP sample with the VIPER algorithm. Rather than relying on a TF’s own mRNA level, VIPER asks whether the TF’s regulon is coordinately shifted in a given sample, producing a normalized enrichment score (NES) that serves as a proxy for protein-level activity.

Because VIPER infers activity from the coordinated behavior of co-regulated gene sets rather than absolute expression levels, it is inherently robust to variation in tumor purity, which is generally high across TCGA samples. For each sample, a gene expression signature was calculated relative to the NSMP cohort mean. These signatures, together with the ARACNe-derived network, were passed to the viper function in the viper R package. The resulting sample × TF activity matrix was used in all downstream analyses.

Consensus clustering

Unsupervised clustering was carried out on the TF activity matrix with ConsensusClusterPlus. We ran 1,000 subsampling iterations (80% of samples per iteration), using hierarchical clustering with Ward.D2 linkage and 1 − Pearson correlation as the distance metric.

The optimal cluster number was chosen by jointly inspecting: (i) the consensus matrix heatmap for block-diagonal clarity; (ii) the cumulative distribution function (CDF) curves; (iii) the delta area plot, looking for the point where additional clusters yield diminishing returns; and (iv) per-item consensus scores.

Survival analysis and clinical associations

Overall survival (OS) was measured from diagnosis to death or last follow-up. Kaplan-Meier curves were drawn with the survival package and compared by log-rank test; plotting used survminer. Associations between subtypes and clinical variables (age dichotomized at the median, grade G1/G2 vs. G3, FIGO stage I–II vs. III–IV) were tested with chi-squared or Fisher’s exact tests.

Master regulator identification

TFs with significantly different activity across subtypes were detected by one-way ANOVA (multi-group) or t-test (pairwise). P-values were corrected with the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure. TFs passing FDR < 0.05 and |activity difference| > 1 were designated master regulators.

Pathway and functional enrichment

Target genes of subtype-defining master regulators were extracted from the ARACNe network. GO annotation (biological process, cellular component, molecular function) and KEGG pathway enrichment were performed with clusterProfiler (adjusted P < 0.05). We also ran gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) against the MSigDB Hallmark collection, ranking genes by expression difference between subtypes.

Immune microenvironment profiling

Immune composition was assessed two ways. CIBERSORTx with the LM22 matrix estimated proportions of 22 immune cell types from bulk expression. In parallel, single-sample GSEA (ssGSEA) via the GSVA package scored immune pathway activities including T cell exhaustion, interferon response, and antigen presentation. Between-subtype differences were evaluated by Kruskal-Wallis test.

Mutation landscape analysis

MAF files were processed with maftools. Oncoplots displayed the mutation spectrum per subtype. Frequencies of recurrently mutated drivers (TP53, PTEN, ARID1A, PIK3CA, CTNNB1, KRAS, PIK3R1, FGFR2, PPP2R1A) were compared across subtypes using Fisher’s exact test with FDR correction.

Prognostic risk model

Model building followed two steps. Univariate Cox regression screened TFs for association with OS (P < 0.05). Significant TFs then entered LASSO-penalized Cox regression (glmnet package). Ten-fold cross-validation selected the regularization parameter λ. We adopted lambda.min, which yields the model with the minimum cross-validated error, to maximize feature richness and predictive performance during biomarker discovery; lambda.1se would have produced an overly sparse model retaining too few transcription factors to capture the prognostic signal. Model evaluation and external validation

Predictive accuracy was assessed by Kaplan-Meier analysis with log-rank testing and time-dependent ROC curves (timeROC package). AUC was reported at 1, 3, and 5 years. The same formula and cutoff were applied unchanged to the GEO validation cohort, and the same metrics were recalculated.

Independent prognostic value and nomogram

Univariate and multivariate Cox models tested whether the risk score remained prognostic after adjusting for age, grade, and FIGO stage. A nomogram predicting 3- and 5-year OS was built from the independent predictors using the rms package. Calibration was checked by bootstrap resampling (1,000 iterations). Clinical usefulness was quantified by decision curve analysis (DCA), which plots net benefit against threshold probability.

Clinical specimens and immunohistochemistry

Formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded EC tissues were collected from the Department of Gynecologic Oncology at The First People’s Hospital of Lianyungang. NSMP status was confirmed by immunohistochemistry for mismatch repair proteins (MLH1, MSH2, MSH6, PMS2) and POLE mutation screening. The study received ethics committee approval, and all patients provided informed consent.

PRDM4 staining was performed on 4 μm sections. After deparaffinization, rehydration, and citrate-buffer antigen retrieval (pH 6.0), endogenous peroxidase was blocked with 3% H₂O₂. Sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with anti-PRDM4 primary antibody, then with HRP-conjugated secondary antibody. DAB served as chromogen; hematoxylin provided counterstain. Expression was scored by the H-score method (range 0–300).

Cell lines and gene manipulation

HEC-1 A (ATCC) and Ishikawa (Cell Bank, Chinese Academy of Sciences) cells were maintained in McCoy’s 5 A and DMEM/F12, respectively, each supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin-streptomycin, at 37 °C with 5% CO₂. Mycoplasma testing was performed routinely.

For knockdown, two independent shRNAs targeting PRDM4 (shPRDM4#1, shPRDM4#2) and a non-targeting control (shNC) were packaged into lentiviral vectors in HEK293T cells and used to transduce HEC-1 A cells. For overexpression, full-length PRDM4 cDNA was cloned into a lentiviral vector; Ishikawa cells received either OE-PRDM4 or empty vector. Stable lines were selected with puromycin (2 µg/mL, 7 days). Efficiency was confirmed by qRT-PCR and western blot. Both HEC-1 A and Ishikawa are well-characterized endometrioid adenocarcinoma cell lines that broadly align with the NSMP/copy-number low molecular profile (PTEN-mutated, microsatellite-stable, TP53 wild-type, with PIK3CA pathway alterations), although neither was prospectively classified as an NSMP patient sample.

Proliferation assays

CCK-8: cells were plated at 2 × 10³ per well in 96-well plates. At days 0–5, CCK-8 reagent (10 µL) was added, incubated 2 h, and absorbance read at 450 nm. Five replicates per condition; three independent repeats.

Colony formation: 500 cells per well in 6-well plates, cultured 14 days, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, stained with 0.1% crystal violet. Colonies ≥ 50 cells were counted. Three replicates per experiment.

Migration and invasion assays

Transwell chambers (8 μm pore; Corning) were used, with Matrigel coating for invasion. Upper chamber: 5 × 10⁴ cells in 200 µL serum-free medium. Lower chamber: 600 µL medium with 20% FBS. After 24 h (migration) or 48 h (invasion), non-migrated cells were removed; migrated cells were fixed, stained with crystal violet, and counted in five random fields at 200×. Three replicates.

Flow cytometry

Cell cycle: cells were fixed in 70% ethanol overnight, treated with RNase A (50 µg/mL), stained with propidium iodide (50 µg/mL) for 30 min, and analyzed by flow cytometry. Phase distribution was quantified with ModFit LT.

Apoptosis: cells were double-stained with Annexin V-FITC/PI per kit instructions and analyzed within 1 h. Total apoptosis = early (Annexin V⁺/PI⁻) + late (Annexin V⁺/PI⁺). All assays in triplicate.

qRT-PCR

RNA was extracted with TRIzol (Invitrogen) and reverse-transcribed (PrimeScript RT Kit, Takara). qPCR used TB Green Premix Ex Taq II on a QuantStudio system. Relative expression was calculated by 2⁻ΔΔCt with GAPDH as reference. Primers are listed in Supplementary Table S1. Triplicate reactions, three independent experiments.

Western blotting

Protein was extracted in RIPA buffer with protease/phosphatase inhibitors, quantified by BCA assay, separated by SDS-PAGE (20–40 µg per lane), and transferred to PVDF membranes. Blocking: 5% non-fat milk in TBST, 1 h. Primary antibodies (PRDM4, LATS2, VEGFA, ITGA5, β-actin) were applied overnight at 4 °C; HRP-conjugated secondaries for 1 h at room temperature. Bands were detected by ECL. β-actin served as loading control. Antibody details are in Supplementary Table S2.

ChIP-qPCR

Cells were cross-linked with 1% formaldehyde (10 min), quenched with glycine, and sonicated to 200–500 bp fragments. Immunoprecipitation used anti-PRDM4 or normal rabbit IgG (negative control) overnight at 4 °C, captured with protein A/G beads. After washing, cross-link reversal, and DNA purification, enrichment at target promoters was measured by qPCR and expressed as percent input. Primer sequences for LATS2, VEGFA, and ITGA5 promoter regions are in Supplementary Table S1.

Statistical analysis

Bioinformatic work was done in R version 4.2.3; experimental data were analyzed in GraphPad Prism 9.0. Two-group continuous comparisons used Student’s t-test or Wilcoxon rank-sum test depending on normality. Multi-group comparisons used one-way ANOVA with Tukey’s post hoc or Kruskal-Wallis with Dunn’s correction. Categorical data were tested by chi-squared or Fisher’s exact test. Survival was analyzed by Kaplan-Meier with log-rank comparison; Cox regression was used for univariate and multivariate prognostic modeling. LASSO handled high-dimensional variable selection. In vitro experiments were repeated at least three times; results are shown as mean ± SD. All tests were two-sided; P < 0.05 was the significance threshold.

Survival separation was pronounced (log-rank P < 0.0001; Fig. 2D). C1 patients had the best overall survival, C2 was intermediate, and C3 fared worst. Clinically, C3 was over-represented among high-grade (G3) and advanced-stage (III–IV) cases, while C1 skewed toward low-grade, early-stage disease (Fig. 2E). A heatmap of the 20 most differentially active TFs showed clear subtype-specific patterns: risk-associated factors such as PRDM4, MAFK, and EGLN1 were elevated in C3, whereas protective factors including ZNF747, SPIB, and EAF2 dominated in C1 (Fig. 2F).

Distinct molecular programs and immune landscapes define each subtype

Differential activity analysis between C3 and C1 identified master regulators on both sides (Fig. 3A). PRDM4, MAFK, EGLN1, SMYD2, and LATS2 were upregulated in C3; SPIB, ZNF747, EAF2, HSF5, IKZF3, and ASCL4 were higher in C1.

B Pathway enrichment results: C3 is dominated by EMT and hypoxia; C1 by immune activation. C Immune cell proportions across subtypes estimated by CIBERSORTx (*P < 0.01; ***P < 0.001; ns, not significant). D Mutation frequencies of recurrent driver genes by subtype

Pathway enrichment of the corresponding regulons painted a coherent picture. C3-associated regulators governed epithelial-mesenchymal transition, hypoxia adaptation, angiogenesis, and TGF-β signaling—programs linked to metastatic potential and treatment resistance. C1-associated regulators were tied to adaptive immunity, T cell activation, antigen processing, and interferon-γ signaling (Fig. 3B).

Immune deconvolution matched these pathway results. C1 tumors contained more CD8⁺ T cells, NK cells, M1 macrophages, and dendritic cells—hallmarks of an immunologically active microenvironment. C3 tumors instead showed elevated regulatory T cells and M2 macrophages, pointing to immune suppression (Fig. 3C).

Mutation profiles added another layer of distinction. PTEN and PIK3CA mutations, common in endometrioid tumors with good prognosis, were more frequent in C1. TP53 and PPP2R1A mutations, markers of aggressive biology, were enriched in C3 (Fig. 3D).

A nine-TF risk model stratifies NSMP patients

Univariate Cox regression flagged 16 TFs as prognostically significant (P < 0.05). LASSO-Cox regression narrowed these to nine with non-zero coefficients (Fig. 4A–B). The resulting risk score was calculated for each patient.

Splitting the training cohort at the median risk score separated patients into high- and low-risk groups with markedly different survival (log-rank P < 0.0001; Fig. 4D). A heatmap ordered by risk score showed a smooth transition from protective to adverse TF activity patterns (Fig. 4C). Time-dependent ROC analysis yielded AUC values of 0.745, 0.775, and 0.744 at 1, 3, and 5 years (Fig. 4E).

When the same formula and cutoff were applied to the GEO validation cohort, the high-risk group again trended toward worse survival (Fig. 4F). AUC values were 0.643, 0.579, and 0.631 at 1, 3, and 5 years (Fig. 4G)—lower than in the training set, as expected given the smaller sample size, but directionally consistent.

The risk score is independently prognostic and supports a clinical nomogram

In univariate Cox analysis, age, grade, FIGO stage, and the TF risk score were each associated with OS. Multivariate analysis confirmed that the risk score retained independent prognostic value after adjustment for all three clinical covariates (Fig. 5A).

Proliferation: CCK-8 assays showed reduced growth after PRDM4 depletion and accelerated growth upon overexpression (Fig. 7B).

Migration and invasion: Transwell assays demonstrated that knockdown suppressed both capacities, while overexpression enhanced them (Fig. 7C).

Cell cycle and apoptosis: Flow cytometry revealed G0/G1 arrest and increased apoptosis in knockdown cells, with the opposite pattern in overexpressing cells (Fig. 7D).

Downstream targets: Based on the ARACNe network, we examined LATS2, VEGFA, and ITGA5. Both mRNA (qRT-PCR) and protein (western blot) levels of all three genes dropped upon PRDM4 knockdown (Fig. 7E). ChIP-qPCR confirmed that PRDM4 directly occupied the promoter regions of each gene (Fig. 7F), establishing it as a transcriptional activator of effectors involved in proliferation control, angiogenesis, and integrin-mediated adhesion.

Discussion

The NSMP subtype sits in an awkward position within the EC molecular classification: it is the largest group yet the least well-defined, and clinicians lack reliable markers to distinguish patients who will do well from those headed for relapse. Our approach was to move upstream—from gene expression to the transcription factors that shape it—and ask whether TF activity patterns could resolve this heterogeneity in a biologically coherent way.

The three subtypes we identified are not merely statistical clusters. Each carries a distinct molecular signature that maps onto known biology. C1 tumors display active adaptive immunity, frequent PI3K-pathway mutations, and favorable outcomes—a profile consistent with immunologically engaged, endometrioid-type disease. C3 tumors look fundamentally different: EMT and hypoxia programs are upregulated, the immune microenvironment is suppressive, and TP53 mutations are more common. This constellation resembles the aggressive serous-like profile, despite these tumors being classified as NSMP by current criteria. The implication is clinically important: some patients labeled NSMP may harbor biologically high-risk disease that the existing four-category system does not flag. C2 occupies a middle ground in both molecular features and survival, suggesting a transitional state or mixed biology.

Using TF activity instead of raw expression has both practical and biological justification. On the practical side, TF activity integrates information from dozens to hundreds of target genes, making it less susceptible to noise. Biologically, TFs represent causal regulatory nodes—they do not merely correlate with phenotype but actively shape it. Our results support this reasoning: the subtypes defined by TF activity showed tighter coupling to functional pathway states and immune composition than would typically emerge from expression-based clustering of a comparably sized cohort.

The nine-TF prognostic model performed well in the training data (3-year AUC = 0.775) and showed directionally consistent, though attenuated, performance in the validation cohort (3-year AUC = 0.579). We acknowledge that a 3-year AUC of 0.579 indicates limited discriminative ability in this specific validation cohort. Beyond the smaller sample size, several factors likely contribute: (1) differences in technical platforms (RNA-seq in TCGA versus microarrays in many GEO cohorts), which introduce systematic biases; (2) cohort heterogeneity, as patient populations and underlying biology may differ even within the NSMP definition; (3) residual batch effects from differing sample handling across institutions despite normalization; and (4) potential differences in disease stage, treatment, or follow-up protocols. The primary value of the external analysis was therefore to confirm directional consistency of survival separation rather than to establish definitive predictive accuracy, and larger, prospectively collected, multi-center cohorts will be required to determine the model’s true generalizability. That said, the risk score added prognostic information beyond age, grade, and stage in multivariate analysis, which suggests it captures biology not already encoded in standard clinical variables. The nomogram combining these factors showed good calibration and net benefit on decision curve analysis, making it a reasonable candidate for prospective testing.

PRDM4 drew our attention because it was the master regulator most strongly associated with the poor-prognosis C3 subtype, and because almost nothing is known about its function in solid tumors. The PRDM family has been studied primarily in hematological malignancies and developmental biology, where members like PRDM1 and PRDM16 play well-characterized roles. PRDM4 has largely been ignored. Our functional data fill part of this gap: knockdown reduced proliferation, migration, and invasion while promoting apoptosis; overexpression did the opposite. These effects were consistent across two cell lines and multiple assay types, lending confidence to the conclusion that PRDM4 acts as an oncogene in EC.

The mechanistic picture, while still incomplete, points to a coherent downstream program. ChIP-qPCR showed that PRDM4 directly binds the promoters of LATS2, VEGFA, and ITGA5, and expression of all three genes tracked with PRDM4 levels. VEGFA drives tumor angiogenesis and already has approved inhibitors in clinical use. ITGA5 mediates cell-matrix interactions relevant to invasion and metastasis. LATS2 is a Hippo pathway kinase whose role in cancer is context-dependent—it can be tumor-suppressive or, when dysregulated, contribute to proliferative signaling. The fact that PRDM4 simultaneously activates genes spanning angiogenesis, adhesion, and growth control may explain why C3 tumors display such broadly aggressive behavior. These programs may further intersect with canonical metastatic effectors: PRDM4 could indirectly promote invasion by modulating matrix metalloproteinases such as MMP1 [38] and MMP2 [39], while its enrichment of TGF-β signaling [40] in C3 tumors may reinforce EMT and remodel the surrounding stroma. Such activity is consistent with the emerging concept that transcriptional regulators shape pre-metastatic niches and the metastatic microenvironment [41, 42], positioning PRDM4 as a potential upstream coordinator of a pro-metastatic program in NSMP EC. Whether PRDM4 acts through its PR/SET domain’s intrinsic methyltransferase activity or by recruiting coactivator complexes remains to be determined; ChIP-seq and proteomic interaction studies would be informative next steps.

The nomogram, by integrating the TF-based risk score with age, grade, and stage, could in principle assist clinicians in tailoring adjuvant therapy intensity, refining surveillance schedules, and informing patient counseling for NSMP patients. We emphasize, however, that it remains a research tool: rigorous prospective, multi-center validation in real-world settings is essential before any clinical translation can be considered. From a therapeutic standpoint, several observations are worth noting. The immune-hot profile of C1 raises the question of whether these patients might benefit from checkpoint inhibitors—a hypothesis that could be tested retrospectively in existing immunotherapy trial cohorts. For C3, the dependence on EMT and angiogenesis programs suggests vulnerability to anti-angiogenic agents or EMT-targeting strategies. PRDM4 itself is a transcription factor and therefore a challenging drug target by conventional means, but indirect approaches—degradation via PROTACs, disruption of protein-protein interactions, or targeting its downstream effectors like VEGFA—offer potential paths forward.

We acknowledge several limitations. The analysis is anchored in TCGA data, and the external validation cohort was small. Multi-center prospective studies with larger sample sizes are needed to confirm the subtyping system and risk model. Our TF activity inference used bulk transcriptomics, which averages over intratumoral heterogeneity; single-cell approaches could reveal whether the C3-associated regulatory program operates uniformly across tumor cells or is confined to specific subpopulations. A further limitation concerns the molecular context of our cell models: although HEC-1 A and Ishikawa broadly resemble the NSMP/endometrioid profile, they were not prospectively defined as NSMP patient-derived lines, so the functional findings should be interpreted with appropriate caution. In addition, while our ChIP-qPCR data confirm direct binding of PRDM4 to the LATS2, VEGFA, and ITGA5 promoters and their expression correlates with PRDM4 levels, we did not perform rescue experiments demonstrating that these targets functionally mediate PRDM4’s oncogenic phenotypes; this represents association rather than definitive causation and is an important avenue for future work. The functional experiments were performed in vitro only. While the consistency across knockdown and overexpression in two cell lines is reassuring, xenograft or genetically engineered mouse models would strengthen the case for PRDM4’s in vivo relevance. Finally, the ChIP-qPCR data confirm promoter binding at three loci but do not provide a genome-wide view of PRDM4’s regulatory targets—ChIP-seq would be a logical extension.

In summary, TF activity analysis provides a principled approach to decipher the mechanism of NSMP in endometrial carcinoma. The three subtypes identified here have distinct biology, different prognoses, and potentially different therapeutic vulnerabilities. The accompanying risk model translates these findings into a format compatible with clinical use. And PRDM4 emerges as a previously overlooked oncogenic transcription factor whose biology in EC warrants further investigation—both as a prognostic marker and as a starting point for therapeutic development.
