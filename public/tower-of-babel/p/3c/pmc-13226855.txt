Immunotherapy related organizing pneumonia mimicking metastatic progression in a patient with endometrial cancer: A case report
Authors: Hao Li, Soe Paing Winn, Yiqing Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•ICI-related OP can mimic metastatic disease with atypical, mass-like, and FDG-avid pulmonary lesions.•Tissue biopsy is the gold standard to distinguish immune-related adverse events from true cancer progression.•Persistently negative ctDNA can serve as a noninvasive indicator suggesting a non-malignant process.•Corticosteroids and ICI discontinuation often lead to complete radiographic resolution of organizing pneumonia.

Introduction

Immune checkpoint inhibitor (ICI)–related pneumonitis is a well-described immune-related adverse event, occurring in up to 10% of patients treated with immune checkpoint inhibitors (Naidoo et al., 2017, Nishino et al., 2016). It can manifest with a wide spectrum of radiographic patterns, commonly as interstitial pneumonia, organizing pneumonia (OP), and in severe cases, acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS). Organizing pneumonia typically appears as peripheral or bronchocentric ground-glass opacities; while it also can manifest as consolidation, with air bronchograms, or vascular structures coursing through the lesions (Naidoo et al., 2017, Nishino et al., 2016).

This report describes a patient with high-grade serous endometrial carcinoma receiving adjuvant maintenance ICI immunotherapy who developed bilateral lung masses concerning for metastatic disease, ultimately diagnosed as ICI-related organizing pneumonia with complete resolution on corticosteroid treatment.

Case presentation

Patient history

A 60-year-old African American woman was diagnosed with high-grade serous carcinoma of the endometrium following routine gynecology examination and pap smear. She was taken by gynecology oncologist for surgical staging with total abdominal hysterectomy, bilateral salpingo-oophorectomy and pelvic and para-aortic lymph node dissection. Final pathology revealed pT2 disease with cervical stromal involvement and pN2mi disease with micrometastatic involvement of both pelvic and para-aortic lymph nodes. No distant metastatic disease was identified at diagnosis.

Tumor immunohistochemistry demonstrated aberrant p53 expression (null pattern) and a high proliferative index, with a Ki-67 of approximating 95%. HER2 testing revealed equivocal immunohistochemistry (2+), with fluorescence in situ hybridization confirming HER2 amplification. Mismatch repair protein expressions were proficient.

The patient’s past medical history was notable for hypertension and there was no family history of cancer.

Adjuvant treatment

Eight weeks after surgery, the patient started to receive adjuvant chemotherapy with carboplatin and paclitaxel, with immune checkpoint inhibitor dostarlimab. Patient completed 6 cycles of chemotherapy, and was continued on maintenance dostarlimab.

Nine weeks after initiation of dostarlimab, the patient developed immune-related thyroiditis, initially presenting as subclinical hyperthyroidism and subsequently progressing to hypothyroidism within two months. This was managed initially with observation and subsequently with levothyroxine under endocrinology guidance.

Surveillance and detection of lung lesions

The patient remained in clinical remission until routine surveillance imaging, with a contrast-enhanced CT of the abdomen, and pelvis performed 19 months after diagnosis revealed new bilateral pleural-based masses and rounded masses/consolidation in the lower lobes. The rounded parenchymal masses measured 4.0 × 3.1 cm in the left lower lobe and 4.6 × 4.0 cm in the right lower lobe. The pleural-based lesions measured 6.8 × 2.8 cm in the left lower lobe, 3.7 × 1.7 cm in the right lower lobe, along with a paravertebral lesion adjacent to the right lower lobe (Fig. 1a-c). The official radiology reading interpreted these as lung, pleural, and paravertebral metastatic disease. The patient remained asymptomatic throughout this period, reporting no cough, dyspnea, fever, or weight loss. Serial circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) testing performed as part of routine surveillance remained persistently negative, including specimens obtained 20 weeks prior to the CT and 1 week after CT. The case was subsequently reviewed at the thoracic tumor board, where the lesions were considered highly suspicious for metastatic disease, and recommendation was to pursue navigational bronchoscopy biopsy.

Diagnostic work-up

Transbronchial fine-needle aspiration and transbronchial biopsy of the right lower lobe rounded mass yielded only blood and bronchial cells. Transbronchial biopsy of the left lower lobe rounded lesion demonstrated benign respiratory epithelium with mild acute and chronic inflammation and a foreign-body–type giant cell reaction. No malignancy was identified. Bronchoalveolar lavage samples were negative for malignant cells and showed numerous neutrophils and macrophages.

Given persistent concern for malignancy despite non-diagnostic bronchoscopy, a CT-guided core needle biopsy of the left pleural based mass was performed. Histopathology revealed features consistent with organizing pneumonia with non-necrotizing granulomatous reaction and no evidence of malignancy. Bacterial, fungal, and mycobacterial cultures were negative, as was serum QuantiFERON testing for tuberculosis. Evaluation for systemic sarcoidosis with the serum angiotensin-converting enzyme level was negative. A subsequent pulmonology consultation also ruled out sarcoidosis possibility. The medical oncologist considered the diagnosis to be ICI-related autoimmune pneumonitis, and started the patient on corticosteroid treatment.

Management and outcome

Dostarlimab was held, and prednisone 40 mg daily was initiated. The patient remained asymptomatic, and corticosteroids were gradually tapered over two months. Follow-up chest CT 2 months after the start of prednisone demonstrated near-complete resolution of the bilateral lung lesions, including the right paravertebral lesion, with restoration of normal aeration (Fig. 1e-g). The patient continues to show no evidence of cancer recurrence on imaging or ctDNA surveillance (repeated 1 month after the start of prednisone), now 24 months since initial surgery, and 4 months since the diagnosis of ICI-related pneumonitis.

Discussion

Incorporating immune checkpoint inhibitor (ICI) in the locally advanced, recurrent, and metastatic endometrial carcinoma has been established as a standard practice, based on a series of recent studies and publications (Powell et al., 2024). In particular, the adjuvant treatment on this patient is based on the Ruby trial, in which patients with high-risk serous carcinoma, with para-aortic lymph nodes involvement, even without residual disease after surgery were evaluated. Compared with chemotherapy alone, the addition of dostarlimab significantly reduced the risk of disease progression in both the mismatch repair–deficient/microsatellite instability–high (dMMR/MSI-H) population (HR 0.28; 95% CI 0.16–0.50) and the mismatch repair–proficient/microsatellite stable (pMMR/MSS) population (HR 0.76; 95% CI 0.59–0.98). The risk of death was substantially lower among patients with dMMR/MSI-H disease (HR 0.32; 95% CI 0.17–0.63), while a favorable trend toward improved overall survival was observed in the pMMR/MSS population (HR 0.79; 95% CI 0.60–1.04) (Powell et al., 2024). This patient’s tumor had positive Her-2 expression, and pMMR status, raising an alternative adjuvant treatment option of adding trastuzumab to chemotherapy backbone. The immunotherapy based regimen was selected in this case, based on the availability of higher level evidence (phase III study) supporting an immunotherapy-based approach, and the category 1 recommendations from the National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) guideline (Fader et al., 2020).

ICI–induced pneumonitis occurs in a minority of patients but can exhibit diverse radiographic patterns. In the initial paper published by Nishino et al. in 2016, the radiographic patterns observed were cryptogenic organizing pneumonia (COP) in 13, nonspecific interstitial pneumonia (NSIP) in 3, hypersensitivity pneumonitis (HP) in 2, and acute interstitial pneumonia (AIP)/acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) in 2 patients (Nishino et al., 2016). Although pneumonitis with interstitial infiltration pattern, and pneumonitis with ground glass opacity pattern, are most commonly recognized as pattern of concern, it is less well registered in the minds of the practitioners that organizing pneumonia, taking a pattern mimicking tumor mass, should also be considered as possible immunotherapy related changes. PET avidity in inflammatory lesions further complicates interpretation, as SUV values in inflammation or infection may overlap with that in malignancy. Consequently, biopsy remains the gold standard for diagnosis when feasible.

With those radiology findings, one another significant phenomenon under consideration is pseudoprogression, in which imaging suggests tumor progression despite the absence of true tumor growth (Chiou and Burotto, 2015). Although pseudoprogression most commonly involves the primary tumor, it may also occur at distant sites, raising concern for metastatic disease. This phenomenon is thought to result from immune cell infiltration, necrosis, edema, and inflammation within affected tissues (Jia et al., 2019). The reported incidence of pseudoprogression across solid tumors is relatively low, generally affecting fewer than 10% of patients receiving ICIs, and predominantly reported in melanoma and lung cancer (Chiou and Burotto, 2015).

The key to the differentiation of true progression, pseudopregression, and ICI-related pneumonitis is pathology evaluation. In addition to navigational bronchoscopy with biopsy of the rounded masses, a CT-guided biopsy of a pleural-based lesion was performed, as this lesion appeared radiographically more suspicious for malignancy, with the absence of air bronchograms. The pathology showed organization pneumonia with non-necrotizing granulomatous reaction, which was the most commonly reported pathological findings in ICI-related pneumonitis (Larsen et al., 2019). Both Larsen et al. and Naidoo et al. identified organizing pneumonia as the predominant pattern of ICI-related pneumonitis (7/9 and 6/6 cases, respectively), generally associated with subclinical or mild disease and occasionally accompanied by non-necrotizing airspace granulomas (Larsen et al., 2019, Naidoo et al., 2020). Less common patterns described were diffuse alveolar damage, acute fibrinous pneumonitis, and chronic interstitial inflammation (Larsen et al., 2019).

Organizing pneumonia is a form of parenchymal lung disease that represents a nonspecific reparative response to preceding alveolar injury and is pathologically characterized by buds of granulation tissue composed of fibroblasts and myofibroblasts embedded in connective tissue, with varying degrees of organization, involving the alveoli, alveolar ducts, and respiratory bronchioles (Cottin and Cordier, 2012, Prasla et al., 2025). It may be idiopathic, termed cryptogenic organizing pneumonia (COP), or occur secondary to infections, medications, malignancy, or autoimmune conditions (Prasla et al., 2025). An extensive infectious workup in our patient was negative, effectively excluding infection as the etiology of the organizing pneumonia.

In this case, the other critical piece of evidence is the persistent negative ctDNA. Using ctDNA for monitoring minimal residual disease is a new practice that has been developed recently, and its prognostic value for future recurrence has been evaluated in colon cancer and cervical cancer (Mayadev et al., 2025, Soueidy et al., 2024). The prognostic value, referring to its sensitivity, specificity, positive and negative predictive values on endometrial cancer has not been well studied though multiple clinical trials including ctDNA analysis have been registered (Capasso et al., 2025). Prior studies in lung cancer seemed to show correlation of ctDNA kinetics with true progression, not in pseudoprogression (Guibert et al., 2017). While false-negative ctDNA results can occur—particularly in low-volume disease—serial negativity in this patient increased confidence in a non-malignant etiology and supported a therapeutic approach to suppress inflammation.

Once the diagnosis is established, management of immune checkpoint inhibitor–induced organizing pneumonia primarily involves suspension of the offending immunotherapy and initiation of corticosteroids. Treatment is guided by severity, ranging from close monitoring without steroids for mild (grade 1) disease to high-dose intravenous corticosteroids with additional immunosuppressive agents for severe or refractory cases (Puzanov et al., 2017). As our patient was asymptomatic, oral prednisone at 0.5 mg/kg dose was able to result in complete radiographic resolution of pulmonary lesions.

Conclusion

Immune-related pneumonitis can manifest with a wide array of imaging findings, and reported here is a rare case of pulmonary consolidations and pleural-based masses which were also FDG-avid on PET imaging. Tissue sampling is essential for accurate diagnosis, and the most common pathological manifestation is organizing pneumonia. Serial negative ctDNA detection was consistent with the non-malignant etiology and correlated with the clinical course, suggesting its valuable negative predictive value.
