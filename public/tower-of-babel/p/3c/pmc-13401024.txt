Structure-property correlation study on biocompatibility of wire arc additively manufactured nitinol alloy
Authors: Kaviya M, Vignesh M, Sahil Gaba
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryWire arc additive manufacturing (WAAM) provides a cost-effective approach for fabricating NiTi alloys for biomedical applications. In this study, a NiTi alloy wall was fabricated using WAAM and systematically evaluated for its microstructure, mechanical properties, corrosion resistance, and biocompatibility. EBSD analysis showed mainly high-angle grain boundaries, while XRD confirmed the dominant B2 austenitic phase with minor B19′ martensite. Microhardness increased from 282 to 380 HV with increasing build height, while tensile strength ranged from 550 to 615 MPa. XPS analysis revealed a TiO2-rich passive surface layer, which enhanced wettability, as indicated by contact angles of 29.93° and 25.95°. Electrochemical testing measured a corrosion rate of 0.013 mm year−1. Hemolysis was below 1%, and MG-63 cell viability reached 91.18%. These results show that WAAM-fabricated NiTi exhibits favorable structural integrity and biocompatibility, making it suitable for potential orthopedic implant applications.

Introduction

Nickel-titanium (NiTi) alloy is a shape memory alloy (SMA) that exhibits the shape memory effect (SME) and superelasticity due to a reversible martensitic transformation between the B2 austenite and B19′ martensite phases.1 NiTi alloy can recover its original geometry in response to thermal or mechanical stimuli, providing exceptional functional versatility.2 NiTi alloy exhibits excellent corrosion resistance, good fatigue life, favorable biocompatibility, and high damping capacity, making it suitable for applications that require long-term performance under cyclic or variable loading conditions.3 NiTi alloy has been widely used across various engineering industries for its unique functional and mechanical properties. It is used in aerospace, automotive, and robotics industries in components such as couplings, actuators, and fasteners that require high energy absorption and reliable performance under cyclic loading conditions4 and also used in the biomedical field in devices such as cardiovascular stents, orthodontic archwires, orthopedic implants, and surgical instruments, where its superelasticity, corrosion resistance, and biocompatibility contribute to minimally invasive surgical procedures and improved functional performance. Among these biomedical applications, NiTi alloy has attracted increasing interest for orthopedic implants due to its superelasticity, corrosion resistance, and mechanical compatibility with bone tissue.5

Additive manufacturing (AM) is an advanced manufacturing approach that builds components layer-by-layer directly from digital models, enabling the fabrication of complex geometries and customized structures with improved material efficiency. This capability has made AM particularly attractive for materials that are difficult to process using conventional manufacturing techniques.6,7 AM has become a valuable method for producing NiTi alloy components with intricate geometries and customized designs that are difficult to achieve using traditional manufacturing techniques due to the alloy’s poor machinability.8 Several AM methods, including powder bed fusion (PBF), laser metal deposition (LMD), and directed energy deposition (DED), have been investigated for the fabrication of NiTi alloys.9,10 Among these, wire arc additive manufacturing (WAAM), which uses a continuous wire feedstock and an electric arc as the heat source, has gained increasing attention for processing metallic materials. Compared to powder-based AM techniques, WAAM offers benefits such as lower material costs, reduced contamination risks, higher deposition rates, and better material utilization.11,12 WAAM enables the production of near-net-shape components and medium-to large-scale metallic structures with enhanced manufacturing efficiency. Recent studies have further demonstrated the successful application of WAAM to stainless steels, titanium alloys, and nickel-based superalloys, highlighting its capability to produce dense deposits with controlled microstructure and application-specific mechanical properties.13,14,15 These advantages position WAAM as a promising technique for the fabrication of customized NiTi alloy components for advanced engineering and biomedical applications.9,16

Several studies have investigated the influence of WAAM processing conditions on the structural characteristics and mechanical behavior of NiTi alloys. Resnina et al. fabricated a multi-layer NiTi alloy wall using WAAM and reported compositional variations caused by substrate dilution, the formation of Ti-rich dendritic phases in the initial deposition layers, and a columnar-to-equiaxed grain transition along the build height.17 Similarly, Vaghasia et al. produced a layered NiTi alloy structure and observed build-dependent microstructural refinement, accompanied by an increase in microhardness from 236.56 HV at the base to 316.78 HV in the upper layers, indicating the influence of thermal gradients during WAAM.18 Singh et al. further demonstrated that appropriate control of interlayer temperature significantly reduces porosity and improves the deposition quality of WAAM-fabricated NiTi structures.19 While these studies provide important insights into the process-structure relationships of WAAM-fabricated NiTi alloys, investigations using other AM techniques have also explored the potential of NiTi alloys for biomedical and orthopedic applications. For example, NiTi alloy fabricated using laser powder bed fusion (LPBF) has been investigated to evaluate its mechanical behavior and corrosion resistance, which are critical factors for biomedical applications due to the potential release of nickel ions from the alloy surface.10 Furthermore, DED has been employed to fabricate NiTi alloy structures with titanium gradient layers and hydroxyapatite coatings to improve biocompatibility and suitability for orthopedic implant applications20 Gorgin Karaji et al. reported that additively manufactured porous NiTi alloy exhibited favorable in vitro biological performance, including improved cell proliferation and osteogenic response for orthopedic application.21 Perumal et al. reported that LPBF-fabricated NiTi alloy exhibited improved corrosion resistance at higher processing energy density, which was attributed to the formation of a stable TiO2-rich passive layer and reduced manufacturing defects.22

These studies demonstrate that NiTi alloys can be successfully produced through different AM routes while maintaining favorable biocompatibility and corrosion resistance for biomedical applications. However, research on WAAM-fabricated NiTi alloys has primarily focused on processing optimization, microstructural evolution, and mechanical behavior, whereas systematic investigations of their biological performance remain limited.

The novelty of this study lies in the investigation of the NiTi alloy fabricated by the WAAM process and its biocompatibility. The fabricated material was evaluated through electrochemical corrosion testing, surface wettability analysis, hemocompatibility, and cytocompatibility assessment. In addition, the microstructural characteristics and porosity features of the deposited alloy were examined to understand their influence on the observed response. The results establish a relationship between processing-induced structural features and the performance of WAAM-fabricated NiTi alloy, supporting its potential for orthopedic implant applications. The study indicates that WAAM offers a viable, cost-effective route for fabricating NiTi alloy components for biomedical implant applications.

Crystallographic characterization by EBSD

Electron backscatter diffraction (EBSD) analysis was conducted to further clarify the grain structure, grain boundary character, local misorientation, and grain size distribution of the WAAM-fabricated NiTi alloy, as shown in Figure 4. The grain boundary map (Figure 4A), indicates that high-angle grain boundaries (HAGBs, >10°) are predominant, accounting for approximately 72.17% of the boundaries, while low-angle grain boundaries (LAGBs, 2°–10°) account for about 27.83%. The dominance of HAGBs indicates the development of well-defined grain interfaces within the deposited structure, while the presence of LAGBs may relate to subgrain formation and local recovery caused by repeated thermal cycling during layer-by-layer deposition.26 The microstructure map also shows a heterogeneous grain morphology, reflecting variations in solidification and reheating conditions during the WAAM process. The Kernel Average Misorientation (KAM) map (Figure 4B) displays generally low local misorientation within most grain interiors, with higher values concentrated near grain boundaries and interfacial regions. This indicates that local lattice distortion and stored strain are mainly localized at boundary networks rather than evenly distributed throughout the matrix.27 Such behavior aligns with the cyclic heating and cooling inherent to WAAM processing, where thermal gradients can promote local strain accumulation at grain boundaries. The grain-size distribution histogram (Figure 4C) reveals a broad range of grain sizes, with an average grain size of 125.11 ± 43.26 μm, indicating controlled heterogeneous grain growth.28 The coexistence of both finer and coarser grains reflects local differences in cooling rate and repeated reheating during deposition. The EBSD results confirm that the WAAM-fabricated NiTi alloy developed a well-defined, thermally evolved grain structure characterized by dominant HAGBs, stable grain interiors, and a varied grain size distribution.Figure 4EBSD analysis of the WAAM-fabricated NiTi alloy(A) grain boundary map, (B) KAM map, and (C) grain size distribution. Phase identification using XRD

X-ray diffraction (XRD) was performed on the bottom, middle, and top regions of the wall, as shown in Figure 5. The diffraction patterns obtained from all regions are predominantly characterized by the B2 austenitic NiTi phase. The reflections observed at 2θ = 43.22°, 62.72°, and 76.28° were indexed to the (110), (200), and (211) planes of the B2 phase, in agreement with standard Joint Committee on Powder Diffraction Standards (JCPDS) card no. 00-018-0899 for NiTi alloy. The diffraction patterns from the three build regions show no notable shift in peak positions, suggesting that the phase composition remains stable with increasing build height despite repeated thermal cycling during WAAM deposition. Minor variations in relative peak intensities are attributed to build-height-dependent changes in grain orientation and microstructure during WAAM deposition. This behavior further suggests that local compositional segregation was limited and did not substantially change the average lattice parameter of the dominant B2 matrix. Additionally, repeated reheating during successive layer deposition likely promoted partial homogenization. A low-intensity diffraction peak was detected at 2θ = 37.18° attributed to the plane of the B19’ martensite phase, indicating the presence of a minor retained martensitic constituent within the deposited structure. Similar diffraction patterns and phase features have been reported for WAAM-produced NiTi alloy.29.30 The XRD results indicate a predominantly B2 austenitic matrix with a minor B19 martensitic contribution in the WAAM-fabricated NiTi alloy. Micro-CT porosity analysis

Micro-computed tomography (micro-CT) was employed to evaluate the internal integrity and porosity features of the WAAM-fabricated NiTi alloy. Figure 6A displays the reconstructed three-dimensional specimen geometry, showing a continuous and well-consolidated bulk structure without visible macroscopic cracks or major internal discontinuities. Figure 6B shows the segmented pore distribution within the analyzed volume, indicating only a limited number of isolated internal defects. Quantitative analysis revealed a total porosity of approximately 0.204 vol%, corresponding to a relative density of 99.80%, confirming the near-dense nature of the deposited alloy. The pore population was predominantly composed of closed pores (0.161 vol%), while the open porosity fraction remained low (0.043 vol%). The predominance of closed pores indicates that defects are largely discrete rather than interconnected. This is beneficial, as interconnected porosity is more detrimental to structural integrity and may facilitate fluid ingress in physiological environments.31,32 The low pore content indicates effective melt pool consolidation and sufficient remelting between successive deposited layers during WAAM processing. Additionally, the absence of significant interconnected defects shows that lack-of-fusion porosity was effectively minimized under the chosen deposition conditions. The porosity level observed in this study falls within the range reported for optimized wire-arc and related arc-based AM systems, where dense builds with limited internal defects have been achieved following process parameter optimization.33,34 The micro-CT findings show that good internal build quality with minimal volumetric defects, which benefits subsequent structural performance and the reliability of biomedical components.Figure 6Micro-CT analysis of the WAAM-fabricated NiTi alloy(A) Reconstructed 3D volume and (B) pore distribution within the analyzed volume.

Mechanical properties

The micro-hardness test was performed in accordance with ASTM E384-17, using a load of 200 gf and a dwell time of 10 s. Measurements were performed across the eight layers of the WAAM-fabricated NiTi alloy wall, with three indents taken at each layer. The micro-hardness profile showed a clear increase with build height (Figure 7A). The lowest micro-hardness value of 282 HV was observed in the bottom layer, while the middle layers ranged from 307 to 347 HV, and the topmost region reached approximately 380 HV. This progressive increase indicates microstructural variation along the build direction during WAAM deposition. The lower region experiences higher heat extraction through the substrate and a steeper thermal gradient during the initial stages of deposition, which can promote heterogeneous solidification and comparatively lower hardness values. As the build height increases, repeated thermal cycling and gradual changes in heat flow conditions modify local solidification behavior, leading to progressive microstructural homogenization and increased micro-hardness in the upper layers.35 The XRD analysis indicates a predominantly B2 austenitic phase with a minor B19′ martensitic contribution. The comparatively lower hardness in the bottom region can be associated with a greater influence of martensitic features and transformation-induced effects, whereas the higher hardness in the upper layers corresponds to a more stable B2-dominated matrix.24 Thus, the observed hardness variation arises from the combined influence of thermal history, microstructural evolution, and phase constitution along the build direction.Figure 7Mechanical performance of WAAM-fabricated NiTi alloy at different regions of the deposited wall(A) Microhardness across layers; (B) tensile strength and elongation at the bottom, middle, and top regions. Data are represented as mean ± SD.

The tensile properties of the WAAM-fabricated NiTi alloy improved with increasing build height, as illustrated in Figure 7B. The bottom region exhibited an ultimate tensile strength (UTS) of approximately 550 MPa with an elongation of 9.5%, while the middle region showed 576 MPa and 12.3%, respectively. The top region demonstrated the highest tensile performance, with an UTS of about 615 MPa and an elongation of 14.6%. The comparatively lower strength and ductility observed in the bottom region can be linked to rapid heat extraction through the substrate during the initial stages of deposition, which promotes local microstructural heterogeneity and residual thermal effects. As wall height increases, repeated thermal cycling and a gradual reduction in thermal gradient alter the solidification behavior, leading to improved microstructural uniformity and a corresponding increase in tensile strength and elongation.36 The improved tensile response in the upper region also indicates strong interlayer metallurgical bonding within the deposited wall. XPS

X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) was used to analyze the surface chemistry of the WAAM-fabricated NiTi alloy, as the outer passive layer plays a crucial role in its corrosion resistance and biocompatibility.39 The spectra are presented in Figures 8A–8D. The survey scan in Figure 8A confirms the presence of Ni, Ti, and O as the main surface elements, along with a small carbon peak near 285 eV, which is typically attributed to atmospheric exposure. No significant additional elemental peaks were observed, indicating minimal surface contamination. As shown in Figure 8D, the Ti 2p spectrum exhibits characteristic peaks at 458 and 464 eV. Additionally, the O 1s peak centered at 530 eV in Figure 8C is consistent with oxygen bonded to metal oxides. The combined Ti 2p and O 1s signals suggest the presence of a titanium oxide-based passive layer on the alloy surface.40 Nickel peaks remain detectable in the Ni 2P region (Figure 8B), confirming the underlying NiTi alloy chemistry after WAAM processing. However, the co-occurrence of Ti and O signals indicates that the outermost surface is mainly covered by the passive oxide layer rather than exposed nickel-rich metallic regions. This behavior is typical of NiTi alloys and is related to the preferential oxidation of titanium, which forms a stable passive layer. The formation of a TiO2-rich passive layer is advantageous because it acts as a barrier against chemical attack in physiological environments and enhances surface stability.22 The XPS findings indicate that the WAAM-fabricated NiTi alloy retains the characteristic passivated surface state of NiTi alloys, beneficial for corrosion resistance and biomedical applications.Figure 8X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy spectra of the fabricated NiTi alloy surface(A) Survey spectrum showing the overall elemental composition, (B) high-resolution Ni 2p spectrum, (C) high-resolution Ti 2p spectrum, and (D) high-resolution O 1s spectrum

Biocompatibility study

Contact angle and surface wettability

The wettability of the NiTi alloy was evaluated to determine its interaction with physiological fluids (simulated body fluid, SBF), as surface wetting behavior is important in early biological events at the implant interface, such as protein adsorption and initial cell attachment.41 Figure 9 shows the contact angles (29.93° and 25.95°) of the two analyzed regions of the deposited wall, which were determined using the static sessile-drop method as per ASTM D7334-08(21). The low contact angles indicate rapid droplet spreading, consistent with the surface’s hydrophilicity (<90°).42 The observed low contact angles are consistent with the measured surface roughness (Ra = 0.178–0.192 μm), where the inherent surface topography increases the effective contact area, thereby promoting liquid spreading and enhancing wettability. XPS analysis also confirmed the presence of a TiO2-rich surface layer, which increases surface energy and improves hydrophilicity. The combined effect of surface topography and oxide layer formation creates favorable conditions for protein adsorption and early cellular interaction.40,43 The surface’s hydrophilic nature facilitates protein adsorption and early cellular interactions, both of which are essential for effective tissue integration in NiTi alloy implants.44 The contact angles determined in the present study are consistent with those of clinically used NiTi alloys.45 Thus, NiTi alloy fabricated by the WAAM process exhibits surface-interaction properties that can be utilized for biomedical applications.Figure 9Surface wettability of WAAM-fabricated NiTi alloy evaluated by contact angle measurements(A) Contact angle: 29.93° and (B) Contact angle: 25.95° measured on WAAM-fabricated NiTi alloy.

Maintaining high cell viability under maximum exposure conditions indicates that the WAAM process does not compromise the alloy’s intrinsic biocompatibility. This behavior can be attributed to the formation of a chemically stable, adherent TiO2-rich surface layer that acts as a protective barrier and regulates interfacial interactions between the material and the biological environment.43 The presence of this passive film limits direct exposure of the substrate, suppresses excessive ion release, and contributes to a controlled surface chemistry, thereby minimizing adverse cellular responses.51,52 In addition, the combined influence of surface chemistry and surface characteristics resulting from the WAAM process provides a stable interface that supports cell adhesion and viability. Nickel ion release behavior

Nickel ion release is an important parameter in evaluating NiTi alloys, as the leaching of Ni2+ ions can influence the biological response of the material.44 In additively manufactured NiTi alloys, particularly those fabricated by WAAM, microstructural heterogeneity, and surface characteristics can affect ion release behavior. Nickel ion release from the WAAM-fabricated NiTi alloy samples was measured using inductively coupled plasma-mass spectrometry (ICP–MS) under simulated physiological conditions, and the results are presented in Figure 14. The measured nickel ion concentrations were 0.064 mg/L at day 1 and 0.112 mg/L at day 7, showing an increase in ion concentration with immersion time under static conditions. The release behavior is governed by the alloy’s surface chemistry. The formation of a TiO2-rich passive layer on the NiTi alloy surface, as confirmed by XPS, acts as a barrier that prevents the dissolution of nickel ions into the surrounding medium.54 In addition, surface heterogeneity and localized microstructural variations associated with the WAAM process contribute to the observed increase in ion concentration over time. The obtained nickel ion release falls within the range reported for NiTi alloys, including additively manufactured NiTi alloy materials.55 Similar nickel ion release behavior has been reported for conventionally processed wrought NiTi alloys used in biomedical applications56 The WAAM-fabricated NiTi alloy exhibits controlled nickel-ion release under the tested conditions, indicating stable surface characteristics and suitability for biomedical implant applications.Figure 14Nickel ion release (mg/L) from WAAM NiTi at day 1 and day 7Data are represented as mean ± SD.

Biocorrosion of WAAM NiTi alloy

The electrochemical corrosion behavior of the WAAM-processed NiTi alloy under simulated physiological conditions was assessed using potentiodynamic polarization tests. Samples were taken from three regions along the build height (bottom, middle, and top) to investigate potential variations in corrosion response, and the representative polarization curve is illustrated in Figure 15. The corrosion parameters derived from Tafel extrapolation for each region are summarized in Table 5. The corrosion response shows only minor variation with build height, with corrosion rates ranging from 0.0105 to 0.0156 mm year−1. The average corrosion rate across the three regions was around 0.013 mm year−1, reflecting a relatively low degradation rate of the WAAM-fabricated NiTi alloy under simulated physiological conditions. This behavior is typical of NiTi alloys and is mainly due to the formation of a stable TiO2 passive film on the alloy surface, which provides effective corrosion protection.39 The Icorr value obtained falls within the lower range of corrosion currents reported by Zuo et al. for additively manufactured NiTi alloys, indicating comparable corrosion kinetics for AM-processed NiTi alloys.57 This observation suggests that the WAAM process has no effect on the alloy’s inherent corrosion behavior and that the NiTi alloy produced by wire arc deposition retains its typical passive-film-controlled characteristics, which are suitable for biomedical applications. The post-corrosion behavior of the alloy was further examined using SEM-EDS, with resulting micrographs and elemental maps shown in Figure 16. The SEM images show that corrosion pits are localized, frequently observed near melt pool edges and adjacent regions, while the surrounding areas remain largely unchanged. This indicates that corrosion occurs through localized breakdown of the passive film rather than uniform material removal. Such behavior is associated with local variations in surface and oxide-film stability, consistent with reported corrosion mechanisms for NiTi alloys.51,58 Analysis of EDS revealed the presence of Ni and Ti in near-equiatomic ratios, along with considerable oxygen enrichment throughout the analyzed surface. The detected oxygen is associated with oxide formation during corrosion exposure, which is further supported by XPS analysis indicating a TiO2-rich passive layer. Minor chlorine signals observed in the EDS mapping are due to trace chloride residues from the SBF. Limitations of the study

Future studies should focus on understanding the long-term in vivo response to the corrosion-fatigue interaction and the implant stability of WAAM-fabricated NiTi alloy under physiological conditions. Further investigation into the influence of the WAAM thermal history and post-processing treatment on surface integrity and biocompatibility would be useful for assessing their potential in orthopedic implant applications.

Method details

Fabrication of NiTi alloy wall through WAAM

The NiTi alloy wire, with a 1.2 mm diameter and near-equiatomic composition, was used as the feedstock wire for WAAM. Fabrication was performed using a GMAW-based WAAM system (ESAB MIG 400i CCCV), and the molten pool was shielded by Ultra High Purity (UHP) argon shielding gas (99.99% purity) at a flow rate of 20 L/min. The chemical composition of the NiTi feedstock wire used in this study is presented in Table 1. To ensure metallurgical compatibility during deposition, a commercially available titanium plate was selected as the substrate due to its strong interfacial bonding with the NiTi alloy.59 The WAAM experimental setup is illustrated in Figure 1. More details on the experimental setup are given in Figure S1 in the supplementary file.

The substrate was preheated to 150 °C before the wall building to stabilise the melt pool, promote interlayer bonding and minimise thermal gradients during the process. Preheating also helps reduce residual stress development and support stable phase formation during the initial stages of deposition.60 The preheat temperature was monitored using a non-contact infrared thermometer prior to deposition. An interlayer delay of 5 min was maintained to control interpass temperature and prevent excessive heat accumulation during successive layer deposition, thereby ensuring stable melt-pool behavior and consistent microstructural evolution. Preliminary experimental trials were conducted to optimise the WAAM process parameters and ensure stable arc behavior (as detailed in Figure S2; Table S1 in the Supplementary Information). The optimised parameters (listed in Table 2) were then used to fabricate an eight-layered, dense NiTi alloy wall measuring 80 × 25 × 5 mm. Figure 2 shows the fabricated NiTi alloy wall and the corresponding sampling regions along the build height, which were subsequently subjected to metallurgical, mechanical, and biological evaluations.

Metallurgical and mechanical characterisation

Postprocessing of the fabricated NiTi alloy wall was carried out along the build direction using Wire Electrical Discharge Machining (WEDM), and specimens were cut from it for various testing procedures. Three regions representing the bottom, middle, and top of the build were extracted, each machined to 5 × 5 mm to characterise its metallurgical properties. The specimens were embedded in acrylic resin and prepared following standard metallographic procedures for microstructural analysis. Polishing was performed using SiC abrasive papers ranging from 320 to 2000 grit, followed by polishing with a diamond suspension to achieve a scratch-free surface. To reveal grain boundaries and solidification structures, the polished surfaces were etched with an H2O: HNO3: HF solution. Optical analysis was performed using a Dewinter Victory-800 inverted metallurgical microscope equipped with a 5 MP digital imaging system. Microstructural and chemical analyses at higher resolution utilised a Zeiss Sigma field-emission scanning electron microscope with an Oxford INCA EDS detector, allowing for the determination of grain morphology and element distribution within the deposited layers. Electron Backscatter Diffraction (EBSD) analysis was conducted using a field-emission scanning electron microscope (Thermo Fisher FEI Quanta 250 FEG) equipped with an EBSD detector. Data were acquired with a step size of 0.6 μm at an accelerating voltage of 20 kV and a tilt angle of 70°. EBSD data were analyzed using Oxford AZtecHKL software to evaluate grain morphology. Phase identification was conducted using a Panalytical X’Pert PRO X-ray diffractometer with Cu-Kα radiation (1.5406 Å). Measurements were taken over 2θ = 10°–80° with a step size of 0.02° and a scan rate of 2°/min to identify phase constituents and assess phase evolution during WAAM. Micro-computed tomography (micro-CT) analysis was performed using a SkyScan 1273 system (Bruker, Belgium). The scans were conducted at an accelerating voltage of 130 kV and a current of 300 μA using a 1 mm filter. The acquisition was carried out with a pixel size of 12.08 μm, a rotation step of 0.3° over 360°, an exposure time of 1800 ms, and frame averaging of 2. Cross-sectional volumes from the deposited wall were analyzed, and the reported porosity values represent the averaged response of the scanned volume. Image reconstruction and quantitative analysis were performed using Bruker CTAn software. The reconstructed data were analyzed to evaluate internal porosity and volumetric characteristics of the fabricated samples. A Micro-Vickers hardness tester (HDNS–Kelly Instruments) was used to perform microhardness testing in accordance with ASTM E384-17. The applied load was 200gf, with a dwell time of 10 s. Microhardness was measured at each built height, using three indents per layer to evaluate variations along the deposited wall. A universal testing machine (FIEE-50 kN, Fuel Instruments and Engineers Pvt. Ltd., India) was used to perform tensile testing on dog-bone-shaped specimens extracted from the bottom, middle, and top regions of the deposited wall along the build direction. Micro tensile specimens with dimensions of 16 mm × 6 mm × 2 mm (overall length × gauge length × gauge width) were prepared, and testing was carried out under controlled loading conditions until fracture to evaluate the mechanical behavior of the fabricated samples.

Surface and biocompatibility evaluation

Surface characterisation

Surface chemical composition and elemental states of the fabricated NiTi alloy samples were analyzed using X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) (ULVAC-PHI VersaProbe 4) equipped with a monochromatic Al Kα radiation source. Prior to analysis, the sample surface was sputtered with Ar+ ions to remove surface contaminants. Both survey and high-resolution spectra were acquired to identify elemental composition and related chemical states. Surface roughness was assessed using a contact profilometer (ACCRETECH SURFCOM TOUCH). Measurements were taken along the build direction of the deposited wall at various locations, and the reported roughness parameters reflect averaged values derived from the recorded surface profiles.

Contact angle and wettability analysis

The wettability behavior of NiTi alloy processed by WAAM was measured using a static sessile drop contact angle method to assess liquid-solid interactions and surface hydrophilicity. The measurements were conducted with the contact-angle goniometer (Micro Capture Pro, Version 2.3) using an optical imaging module with a 10 MP resolution and an automatic algorithm that accurately fits the baseline to within 0.3°. The wettability measurements were conducted after sonication of the specimens in ethanol and deionised water to remove the surface contaminants. The samples were washed and left to dry in the ambient laboratory environment. The 35 μL of a droplet of simulated body fluid (SBF) was applied to the surface with the help of a micro-syringe, and the angle of contact was measured after 10 s of stabilisation. The SBF composition adhered to the Kokubo formulation to reproduce the ionic equilibrium of human physiological fluid by maintaining a pH of approximately 7.40 ± 0.02. The ionic concentrations of the prepared SBF solution are summarised in Table 3.

Hemocompatibility testing

Hemocompatibility was evaluated using an in vitro erythrocyte-induced hemolysis assay conducted in accordance with ISO 10993-4. In the red blood cell suspension preparation, 5 mL of the Ethylenediaminetetraacetic Acid (EDTA)-treated peripheral whole blood was diluted in 15 mL of sterile Phosphate Buffer Solution (PBS) (pH 7.4) and centrifuged at 3500 rpm at 25°C. The erythrocyte pellet obtained was washed 5 times with PBS in each case. The pellet was re-dispersed in 5 mL of PBS and centrifuged at 3500 rpm for 5 min at 25°C to wash off the plasma residues. The last pellet was resuspended in 10 mL PBS to obtain a homogeneous red blood cell stock. Before exposure, the WAAM-processed NiTi alloy samples in the various build regions were sterilised by immersion in 70% ethanol and air-drying under a biosafety hood. To set up the assay, 0.8 mL of the RBC suspension was placed in 2 mL microtubes, either with 0.2 mL of PBS (negative control) or with 0.01% Triton X-100 (positive control). The NiTi alloy samples were then placed in 1.0 mL of the RBC dispersion. The tubes, including the controls, were incubated at 37 °C, then centrifuged at 3500 rpm for 20 min at 25 °C. Color change of the supernatant was visually assessed, and 150 μL of each tube was added to a 96-well microplate to measure absorbance at 570 nm with the Cytation 5 plate reader to determine the amount of released hemoglobin. The percentage of hemolysis was calculated using Equation 1, in accordance with ISO 10993-4 standard,(Equation 1)Hemolysis(%)=ODsample−ODNCODPC−ODNC×100

Cytocompatibility testing

Cytotoxicity was evaluated using an Acridine Orange/Propidium Iodide (AO/PI) staining assay. MG-63 cells were seeded at a density of 3 × 103 cells per well in a 96-well plate and incubated for 24 h prior to exposure. The samples were prepared by immersing NiTi alloy specimens in 1% FBS-containing media and incubating for 48 h under 5% CO2. Following treatment, the cells were stained with Acridine orange (10 μg/mL) and Propidium iodide (50 μg/mL) and incubated for 10 min at room temperature. After staining, the cells were washed with 1× PBS to remove excess dye, and fluorescence images were acquired using a Biotek Cytation 5 imaging system in green and red channels. Green fluorescence corresponds to viable cells, while red fluorescence indicates non-viable cells. All measurements were performed in triplicate, and the results are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Quantitative cell viability was calculated from integrated fluorescence intensities using Equation 2.(Equation 2)Cellviability(%)=GreenGreen+Red×100

Nickel ion release test

Nickel ion release from the WAAM-fabricated NiTi alloy samples was measured by inductively coupled plasma–mass spectrometry (ICP–MS) under physiologically relevant conditions, in accordance with ISO 10993-12 and ISO 10993-18. Before extraction, the samples were rinsed with ultrapure water to remove surface contaminants. Each sample was immersed in 10 mL of 1× Hank’s Balanced Salt Solution (HBSS) in metal-free containers, and the extraction was performed under static conditions at 37 ± 1 °C. Nickel ion release was assessed at two time points: 24 h (Day 1) and 168 h (Day 7). Blank controls, consisting of HBSS incubated under identical conditions without test samples, were used to account for background contamination. For each time point, three independently extracted samples (n = 3) were analyzed. At each interval, the extraction medium was collected and acidified with approximately 2% (v/v) trace metal-grade nitric acid to stabilise dissolved ions. The solutions were analyzed using ICP–MS with appropriate calibration standards, and internal standard correction was applied to ensure accuracy. Nickel concentrations were determined from calibration curves (R2 ≥ 0.999), and the values reported represent the mean concentrations after blank subtraction, expressed in mg/L (ppm).

Electrochemical corrosion behavior

A potentiodynamic polarisation test was carried out to assess the corrosion behavior of NiTi alloy samples produced by WAAM under simulated physiological conditions. All measurements were performed using a CS310M potentiostat/galvanostat (CorrTest Instruments, China) in accordance with ASTM F2129 standards. A typical three-electrode system was employed, with the WAAM-fabricated NiTi alloy specimen as the working electrode, a platinum wire as the counter electrode, and an Ag/AgCl reference electrode. The mechanically polished electrode surface was ultrasonically cleaned with ethanol, then rinsed with deionised water to remove surface contaminants. A sealed electrochemical cell was used to control the working electrode’s exposed surface area before testing, ensuring a consistent measurement interface. All measurements were conducted with the SBF (composition provided in Table 3) maintained at 37 °C. Samples were pre-polarised by electrochemical stabilisation until an Open Circuit Potential (OCP) was reached after 30 min. Potentiodynamic scanning was then performed at 1 mV/s, starting 250 mV above the recorded OCP and covering a sufficient potential range to evaluate the material’s passivation behavior and breakdown tendency.

Quantification and statistical analysis

All experimental measurements were conducted in triplicate (n = 3), and the results are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Microhardness measurements were taken using three indentations at each deposited layer of the WAAM-fabricated NiTi alloy wall. Hemolysis percentage, cell viability, nickel ion release, and electrochemical corrosion were assessed from three independent experiments performed under identical testing conditions. Quantitative EBSD analysis was performed with Oxford AZtecHKL software, and micro-CT porosity analysis was performed with Bruker CTAn software. Graphs were generated, and quantitative data were analyzed with OriginPro software.
