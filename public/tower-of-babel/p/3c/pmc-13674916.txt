Intraorbital wooden foreign bodies: A retrospective study of 30 cases
Authors: Chandana Chakraborti, Nabanita Barua, Juhi Gupta, Manisha Agarwal, Chandrima Dasgupta, Mrinal Kanti Ghosh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Purpose:Intraorbital Wooden foreign bodies (IOrbWFB) can remain trapped or undetected in imaging, leading to potentially serious complications. We aimed to study 30 consecutive cases of intraorbital wooden foreign body (IOrbWFB), their clinical profile, and management in a tertiary care hospital.Methods:A retrospective observational study of 30 cases at our center from March 2020 to December 2024 was conducted. Data were collected from hospital records and reviewed after obtaining institutional ethical clearance.Results:Out of the 30 cases, 14 patients presented with features of orbital cellulitis, and seven cases presented with orbitocutaneous fistula (OCF). The time interval between injury and initial presentation ranged from 1 day to 7 years. A total of 16 cases presented within a week of injury, among which four cases presented early within 24 hours. A foreign body was detected in 17 cases by computed tomography (CT), but 10 cases were misdiagnosed on the initial CT scan. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) was performed in 13 cases, including suspicious cases and when symptoms were not resolving. Eight patients underwent surgery multiple times, of which two cases underwent surgery three times, and five cases underwent surgery twice.Discussion:The size, location, and number of IOrbWFB should be evaluated thoroughly using imaging studies. They are often misdiagnosed on imaging; hence, a high index of suspicion should be maintained in cases with a history of trauma, regardless of the time interval between presentation and trauma. Primary surgery should be performed with proper exposure, and the foreign body should be removed intact to have a better outcome. The study will help to avoid missed diagnosis and misdiagnosis of IOrbWFB.Conclusion:Thorough history taking, detail examination, appropriate imaging and proper surgical planning are essential is all cases of suspected IOrbWFB.

Penetrating orbital injuries with or without retained intraorbital foreign bodies are rare, and the nature of the FB may be organic, inorganic, or metallic. Metallic FB and glass particles are usually well tolerated because of their inert nature, but intraorbital wooden foreign bodies (IOrbWFBs) are more susceptible to infections and visual complications.[1] Delayed presentation and inadequate history may further complicate this scenario. Localization of the IOrbWFB is difficult, and fragment retentions are not uncommon even after extraction. Due to its porous consistency and organic nature, wood is an excellent medium for microorganisms, making it a bacterial growth medium.[2] The retained matters may remain asymptomatic for a long time and may result in orbital cellulitis, abscess, fungal infections, discharging orbitocutaneous fistula (OCF), and restricted extraocular motility (EOM). Computed tomography (CT), magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), and ultrasonography (USG-B scan) are the common imaging modalities to detect IOrbWFB. Broken IOrbWFB may result in piecemeal removal during surgery.[3] We report the clinical features and management of 30 cases of IOrbWFB and review the relevant literature.

Aims and objectives

To study the clinical profile, management, and outcome of 30 cases of IOrbWFB trauma.

Materials and Methods

A retrospective observational study was done at our tertiary eye care center. The study adhered to the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki as amended in 2013. The study was cleared by the institutional review board approval and ethical committee.

Consecutive 30 cases of IOrbWFBs treated during the study period (March 2020 to December 2024) were included in this study. Patients with intraorbital FB other than wood and one patient with OCF who refused surgery were excluded from the study. Data was collected from the oculoplasty clinic, in-patient records, and the OT register of the department. Clinical profile, imaging modalities, treatment, and outcome were evaluated and tabulated.

Results

Among the 30 cases, 70% (n = 21) were male, and 30% (n = 9) were female. The mean age was 37 years (range: 1–70 years). The majority were affected in the young and middle-aged adult group (18–60 years), as 63.3% (n = 19). Left-sided injuries were 46.6% (n = 14), while 53.3% (n = 16) involved the right orbit. Injuries in 13 cases were caused by a stick, in seven cases by bamboo, in six by a tree branch (including palm tree and coconut branch), in three by a wooden splint following a fall on the ground at home, and in one by a twig. The most frequent site of injury was found to be the superomedial quadrant of the orbit, followed by the inferior and least of all superolateral quadrants.

Lacerated wound (LW) (30%, n = 9) and proptosis (36.6%, n = 11) were the most common presenting features, while OCF was noted in 23.3% (n = 7) of cases. The time interval between injury and initial presentation ranged from 1 day to 7 years. A total of 16 cases presented within a week of injury, among which four cases presented early within 24 hours. CT was done in all cases and was helpful in diagnosing FB in 17 (56.6%) cases. But 10 cases had been misdiagnosed on the initial CT scan as air pockets. MRI was done to confirm the diagnosis in 10 (33.3%) cases, and when symptoms were not resolving in three (10%) cases despite conservative management and surgery. All cases were treated surgically except for one, as he refused surgery. Further microbiological study was done and revealed microorganisms in a few cases. Staphylococcus aureus was found to be the most common microorganism. Eight cases underwent multiple surgeries due to retained pieces of FB. Surgery was performed three times in one case, three times in two cases, and two times in five cases. Cases presenting with orbital cellulitis resolved on removal of the foreign body but OCF persisted in two cases. Proptosis (n = 10, 33%) was predominate presenting symptom.

The mean time interval of presentation varied widely from a few hours to 7 years, so the arithmetic mean did not represent the mean of the data. A 32-year-old female presented with complaints of erythematous swelling of the right lower lid area following trauma with a palm tree branch 3 years back. Noncontrast CT (NCCT) scan revealed a linear hyperdense shadow in the right inferior extraconal space. Well-encapsulated FB was removed under local anesthesia (LA) in two pieces [Fig. 1].

Symptoms worsened even after intervention. There was proptosis, chemosis, and congestion of the conjunctiva with a fixed dilated pupil. CT scan reported emphysema of the right orbit. Discussion

Orbital injury with WFBs usually occurs accidentally, owing to injury with sticks, falls, and while working in the field. The WFBs usually include tree branches, corn stalks, bamboo, and wood. Detection of IOrbWFBs remains a challenging task, especially in cases with trivial injury and unreliable history.[4]

A careful history is needed to suspect IOrbWFB but is often difficult to obtain, especially in children and in intoxicated patients, as they are often unable to provide a reliable history.

IOrbWFB can lead to chronic conditions like purulent inflammation, abscess, fistula formation, pathologies like granulomatous tissue reaction, osteomyelitis, and Central nervous system (CNS) complications. Our study found a male preponderance (70% males); other studies reported a similar result.[5] We found that adult males (18–60 years) were affected more frequently (40%, n = 12) while working in fields and woodwork, since we are primarily an agronomic society. A similar finding was seen in a study conducted by Taş and Top.[6] Children and the elderly are affected accidentally, as being fragile they are more prone to falls.

The right orbit (53.3%) was found to be more frequently affected than the left (46.6%). As the body’s natural protective response is hyperextension of the neck during a fall or bending forward during an assault, the superomedial quadrant is the most commonly affected site, followed by the inferior and least of all superolateral quadrants.[6] Hansen et al. pointed out that the anatomical structure of the orbit plays an important role in the intracranial extension of trauma.[7]

Retained IOrbWFB can lead to granulomatous inflammation, which results in OCF formation. In our series, seven patients presented with OCF, and two of them also had abscess formation. Imaging procedures often fail to delineate fistulas. The extent of the fistula tract should be preoperatively delineated by appropriate imaging modalities to completely excise the tract. Removal of IOrbWFB without proper exploration of the wound might lead to tissue damage and residual foreign materials.[5] In the case of an OCF, we suggest entering through the fistulous tract (entry wound) and irrigating the tract with normal saline, which helps to loosen the FB from surrounding adhesions and thereby helps in the smooth removal of an intact WFB.

Detection of intraorbital organic foreign bodies remains a challenging task, especially in cases with trivial injury. The imaging modality of choice in a WFB is a great topic of debate. Radiographs usually fail to detect IOrbWFB[3] as they are usually radiolucent, containing gas in the matrix. CT often fails to detect IOrbWFB due to densities of different kinds of wood (dry and green) ranging from 1110 HU to 2446 HU.[78910111213] Presentation of a WFB on a CT scan varies from an area of low attenuation in the initial stages to an area of high attenuation in the later stages due to high water content. So, a recently introduced WFB may be missed on a CT scan.[14] However, the use of contrast and proper windowing can better differentiate wood from air and bone fragments, thus helping in detecting any coexisting fracture.[15] CT scan is preferred as it evaluates the presence of an underlying mass or inflammatory process.[161718] Identification of the inflammatory response can assist the viewer in identifying the retained foreign body because the actual splinter may be difficult to visualize. In our study, CT scan was helpful in diagnosing IOrbWFB in 56.6% cases, 33.3% as a suggestive diagnosis, and missed in 10% cases. MRI was performed to confirm the diagnosis in 33.3% cases, and when symptoms were not resolving in three cases (10%) despite conservative management. Shelsta et al .[8] also concluded quantitative CT as preliminary radiography used as diagnosis of IOrbWFB, whereas Taş and Top[6] study showed similar findings.

USG is rarely used as a useful tool to detect IOrbWFB, showing a linear echogenic focus with acoustic shadowing.[3] Both fresh and old WFB appear hypo intense on an MRI, due to high air content in fresh wood and high moisture content in old wood after fluid imbibition from surrounding tissues. T1-weighted images are preferred over proton density and T2-weighted images, because they provide better contrast between fat and wood. Thus, MRI is needed after a CT scan, as seen in a study by Li J et al.[19] symptomatically in the early postoperative period. In a few cases, radiologists reported FBs as air pockets. If the WFB is dry, it is a common mistake that can happen from both an ophthalmologist and a radiologist. In an emergency, when imaging was done by residents, in a few suspicious cases, we have consulted senior radiologists; in a few cases, MRI was advised. Especially in small FBs forming granuloma or abscess, in those cases, MRI is better.

For first-line imaging, NCCT is Investigation of choice (IOC). Especially in children. If it fails to detect, then MRI. If the patient presents late, where the WFB is encased by soft tissue and imbibition of water and soft tissue modeling makes visualization difficult in CT.

In our series, all cases were managed successfully except two cases, where one refused surgery, and in case 19, fistula persisted even after 3rd attempt of exploratory orbitotomy. As WFB can cause bacterial as well as fungal infection, it is resistant to conservative management, hence surgery is required. This was also demonstrated in astudy by Kang SJ et al, surgery was done in all cases after proper imaging, and the approach was according to the location of FB and the quadrant of the globe affected.[20] Great care was taken to remove the whole intact foreign body in order to prevent re-surgery for multiple retained pieces, as it poses more complications due to tissue fibrosis. A similar finding was seen in a study conducted by Li J et al.[19]

Conclusion

Often the detection of IOrbWFB is a challenging task; multiple imaging techniques may be needed for the proper localization of some foreign bodies. Thus, careful history taking and a thorough clinical examination of the periorbital skin and conjunctiva are needed. The patients should be well informed beforehand about the limitations of the diagnostic ability and the pros and cons of conservative management. They should be properly counseled about the difficulties of removing such FB and the possible need for re-surgery for the removal of the residual pieces. Repeat imaging after removal may be required. Radiologists need to be aware of the various imaging appearances of WFBs and should be able to successfully localize the retained wood.
