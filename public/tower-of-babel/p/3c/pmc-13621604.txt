A multi-view feature fusion framework with interpretable graph convolution for predicting microbe-drug associations
Authors: Lisha Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPredicting associations between human microbes and drugs (MDA) is a critical step in drug development and precision medicine. Although various computational approaches have been proposed, many existing models still struggle to reveal the key features and interaction mechanisms that drive their predictions, and their decision processes remain difficult to interpret and validate biologically. To address these limitations, we propose IDEAL (Interpretability-Driven Evolvable Attentive Learning for Microbe-Drug Association), a multi-view framework that integrates drug network topological attributes, BERT-encoded drug semantics, drug fingerprints, microbe genome sequence attributes, BERT-encoded microbe semantics, and microbe metabolic pathway attributes. We further introduce GNNExplainer into the graph convolutional network (GCN) pipeline to identify influential drug-microbe relations and feed the resulting structural signal back into graph refinement. In addition, we employ Elastic Weight Consolidation (EWC) to support parameter-efficient adaptation when biological data grow over time while mitigating catastrophic forgetting. Extensive experiments on three public datasets demonstrate that the proposed method achieves strong predictive performance, offers interpretable structural explanations, and provides a practical transfer-learning route for evolving MDA benchmarks. The source code for our model is publicly available at https://github.com/Luoxidu02/IDEAL.Keywords: Microbe-drug association prediction, Multi-view feature fusion, Interpretable graph convolutional network, Continual learning, Pre-trained language model, Computational biology

Introduction

Microbial communities–including bacteria, viruses, fungi, and protozoa–are widespread throughout the human body, inhabiting organs such as the skin, mouth, and gut. These microbes are essential for metabolic balance, vitamin synthesis, and protection against pathogens. Disruption of the microbiome, or dysbiosis, is linked to diseases like obesity, diabetes, and cancer. Importantly, microbes and drugs influence each other: microbes can affect drug metabolism and efficacy, while drugs can alter microbial composition, as observed in several examples, such as the impact of intestinal microbiota on drug metabolism (PMID: 32409589) [1] and the modulation of gut microbiota by antibiotics (PMID: 33651651) [2]. The rise of drug-resistant strains further complicates this relationship, underscoring the need to clarify microbe–drug associations for precision medicine.

Traditional experimental methods for studying these interactions are often slow and costly. As a result, computational approaches based on heterogeneous data integration and machine learning have become valuable tools for prioritizing potential microbe-drug associations. To date, numerous computational methods have been developed for this task. For instance, EGATMDA [3] constructs multiple biological networks and employs a hierarchical graph attention network, with both node-level and graph-level attention, to learn and aggregate features for microbe-drug association prediction. Similarly, GCNMDA [4] integrates graph neural networks with probabilistic modeling to capture intrinsic node similarity, thereby strengthening structural consistency in drug-microbe association prediction. Graph2MDA [5] exploits multi-modal graphs incorporating diverse drug and microbe attributes and applies a variational graph autoencoder to generate informative embeddings that enrich the feature space for prediction. Furthermore, SCSMDA [6] combines structure-enhanced contrastive learning with a self-paced negative sampling strategy, enabling the model to learn more discriminative representations and thus achieve higher predictive accuracy in drug-microbe association tasks. Recent advances in chemical informatics have also demonstrated the power of graph neural networks and transformer models for molecular property prediction and reaction modeling [7–9]; these studies provide useful insights for improving drug representations in association prediction settings.

However, due to the inherent "black-box" nature of deep learning, while these methods achieve high prediction accuracy, they suffer from poor interpretability, making it difficult to uncover the key biological features and interaction mechanisms driving their predictions. In the field of bioinformatics, interpretability is particularly important, and many scholars have attempted to address this issue. For instance, although XGDAG [10] generates interpretable subgraphs, its interpretability is still constrained by the complexity of the subgraphs, the quality of knowledge graph data, and the fact that different interpretability methods may yield divergent explanations, making prediction results difficult to understand in an intuitive and consistent manner. In microbial drug interaction prediction, although GCNATMDA [11] introduces an attention mechanism to enhance interpretability, the attention weights are not explicitly presented, and the multi-source feature fusion makes it difficult to trace the origin of results. Furthermore, the attention mechanism lacks validation mapping to biological mechanisms, thus limiting the interpretability of the model. Similarly, while the negative sample sampling strategy in SCSMDA has been optimized, there is usually no interpretability mechanism to explain why certain negative samples are selected or how they influence the final prediction.

Additionally, current approaches for fusing heterogeneous features often rely on simplistic methods like concatenation or averaging, which fail to adaptively weigh the contributions of different modalities to a specific entity. In our model, we address this by introducing novel features; drug fingerprints and names supplement sequence-based representations with implicit physicochemical properties, while the inclusion of metabolic pathway information enriches microbial feature embeddings with biological mechanism context. Our designed gated attention mechanism, tailored for multi-modal biological features, then adaptively learns the importance weights of different feature modalities for each drug and microbe entity, enabling a more refined and controllable feature fusion.

Furthermore, certain common features are prevalent across different types of drugs or microbes. For instance, common ring systems such as benzene and pyridine frequently appear across different drug molecules [12]; additionally, different bacterial species commonly share a set of conserved core genes, which are widely used in phylogenomic analyses [13]. However, existing static models often overlook these commonalities, necessitating complete retraining with every new data arrival. This process is not only inefficient but also leads to forgetting of previously learned features, making it difficult to meet the dynamic growth needs of biological data. By contrast, incremental learning can retain acquired knowledge while efficiently integrating new data, thereby substantially improving a model’s scalability and generalization. For example, the incremental representation learning approach proposed by Jha et al. can continuously update node representations in biomedical bipartite networks without retraining the entire model [14]. However, in the domain of drug–microbe interaction prediction, studies employing incremental learning remain relatively scarce.

In summary, this study makes three primary contributions to address the key challenges in microbe-drug association prediction. First, we propose an explainability-driven graph optimization framework in which GNNExplainer is used not only for post-hoc interpretation but also to extract task-relevant structural signals that refine the heterogeneous graph. Second, we design a multi-view gated attention mechanism that adaptively fuses diverse features, including drug network topology, chemical fingerprints, and microbe genomic attributes, to produce more precise and controllable entity representations. Third, we introduce an EWC-based continual-learning strategy for dynamic data adaptation. Experimental results validate the effectiveness and robustness of the proposed framework, with particularly strong performance on the MDAD and aBiofilm benchmarks.

Related works

Explainability in graph neural networks

Predicting interactions between biological entities–such as drugs, targets, proteins, and genes–is a cornerstone of modern bioinformatics. While Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) have demonstrated exceptional performance on these tasks, their inherent opacity often hinders their real-world utility, as understanding the "why" behind a prediction is as crucial as the prediction itself. Consequently, GNN explainability methods, such as GNNExplainer [15], have been widely adopted to bridge the gap between predictive power and biological insight.

In the landscape of biomolecular interaction prediction, these interpretability techniques are almost universally applied as a post-hoc tool for model validation and hypothesis generation. For instance, in Drug-Target Interaction (DTI) prediction, a model like iNGNN-DTI [16] utilizes a cross-attention module to learn and visualize the most influential interactions between drug substructures and protein residues, thereby explaining its binding predictions. Similarly, the IHDFN-DTI model [17] uses an attention mechanism in its heterogeneous feature fusion module to generate attention weights that identify the most influential molecular regions driving the predicted interaction. This trend extends to other critical areas. In Protein-Protein Interaction (PPI) prediction, MGPPI [18] utilizes a gradient-based explanation method called Grad-WAM. It analyzes gradients from the final graph convolutional layer to calculate the contribution of each amino acid residue, effectively highlighting the key binding sites driving the predicted interaction. Another approach is seen in the PPI-BAN model [19], which employs a bilinear attention network for interpretability. This mechanism generates attention weight maps that identify significant interaction sites. These weights are then mapped to specific amino acid residues, providing a clear visualization of the key areas driving the interaction. In LncRNA-disease association prediction, the X-LDA framework [20] offers interpretability by applying integrated gradients to predefined "graph patches." This method calculates attribution scores to highlight which specific topological patterns in the graph, rather than just individual nodes, are most influential in driving a prediction. In drug-drug interaction prediction, the SA-DDI model [21] offers interpretability through a substructure attention mechanism. This allows the model to automatically identify and highlight the key molecular substructures within each drug. Recent studies on adjacent biomedical association tasks further clarify why richer representation learning matters. Zhao et al. use a heterogeneous information network for lncRNA-miRNA prediction, showing how cross-type topology can strengthen association inference [22]. Zhao et al. also apply geometric deep learning to drug repositioning, which is relevant to our graph-based modeling of biomedical entities even though the target task differs [23]. Zhang et al. propose a multi-perspective molecular representation framework for drug discovery, reinforcing the importance of richer drug-side features in our setting [24]. Zhao et al. further introduce motif-aware hierarchical attention for miRNA-disease association prediction, highlighting how structured attention can improve both representation quality and interpretability [25]. Beyond biomedical applications, attention-based architectures have also demonstrated strong explanatory potential in image segmentation with boundary-aware mechanisms [26], action recognition through uncertainty-guided attention [27], and weakly supervised temporal localization through cross-category consensus learning [28], underscoring the broad applicability of attention-based designs for interpretable prediction systems.

A unifying theme across these diverse applications is that explainability serves as a downstream, analytical step. The rich information generated by the explainer–be it a subgraph, a set of important nodes, or attention weights–is used to build human trust and guide future experiments but is not mechanistically looped back to refine the model’s underlying graph representation. The input graph, which often contains noisy or incomplete information, remains static throughout the training and inference process. To the best of our knowledge, the concept of "closing the loop" by leveraging these explanations to dynamically optimize the graph structure itself remains a significant, underexplored avenue in biological interaction prediction. Our work directly addresses this limitation by proposing an explanation-driven framework where importance scores are not merely an output for human interpretation but are a core component in a feedback mechanism to re-weight and prune the graph, thereby enhancing both predictive accuracy and the fidelity of the model’s reasoning.

Incremental learning on graph-structured data

Incremental learning aims to enable a model to learn from new data without catastrophically forgetting previously learned knowledge. Classic methods to mitigate this issue include replay-based methods [29], parameter isolation methods [30], and regularization-based methods [31]. The last category, most relevant to our work, achieves this by penalizing changes to parameters deemed important for previous tasks. A pioneering method in this area is Elastic Weight Consolidation (EWC) [32], which utilizes the Fisher Information Matrix to quantify parameter importance.

In the domain of graph data, researchers have adapted these strategies to address the unique challenges of non-i.i.d. and structurally dependent observations. For example, Zhou et al. proposed an experience replay framework for GNNs (ER-GNN) [33], which maintains a buffer of historical nodes for replay during training on evolving graphs. Similarly, other studies have explored knowledge distillation to preserve both feature and topological information, as demonstrated in GraphSAIL [34]. However, the application of these techniques to knowledge transfer between different but related biomedical graph datasets remains underexplored. This gap is especially evident in domains with rapid data accumulation, such as microbe-drug association research. As documented in a comprehensive 2022 review by Wang et al. [35], the field has experienced explosive data growth. The foundational MDAD database [36] (5,505 associations) was published in 2018, and the aBiofilm database [37] added another 5,027 associations within the same year. By 2020, with the further addition of the DrugVirus database [38], the total number of known associations had more than doubled. This rapid accumulation of data over just two years equates to a Compound Annual Growth Rate (CAGR) of more than 46%. This rapid, burst-like publication cadence—where the knowledge base can nearly double in a single year—renders static models instantly obsolete and makes reliance on full, computationally expensive retraining impractical. This underscores the need for incremental learning approaches. Our study therefore explores how EWC can enable a model pretrained on a large dataset to adapt to a new, smaller dataset while retaining useful prior knowledge.

In bioinformatics, the rapid and continuous accumulation of data has spurred the adoption of incremental learning across various domains. For example, continual learning has been used to process massive scRNA-seq datasets that exceed RAM capacity, outperforming static models in cell classification by up to 10% [39]. In the realm of medical diagnostics, Few-Shot Class-Incremental Learning (FSCIL) is used to update retinal disease recognition models with new diseases from very few samples, overcoming data scarcity and achieving state-of-the-art performance without forgetting prior knowledge [40]. In another example focused on medical data privacy, non-storage continual learning enables the long-term clinical deployment of diagnostic models. Researchers have used these methods to update a model for classifying retinal diseases, allowing it to learn new conditions sequentially without needing to access or store previous patient data, thereby mitigating catastrophic forgetting under strict privacy constraints [41]. Furthermore, The Continual Class-Specific Impression (CCSI) framework [42] pioneers a data-free approach to class-incremental learning in medical imaging. It circumvents privacy and storage issues by synthesizing data for past classes directly from the model’s parameters. This enables continuous model updates for new diseases without accessing original patient data, effectively preventing catastrophic forgetting. Closer to our focus on association prediction, the MISSIM model [43] uses a Broad Learning System (BLS) to continually refine its predictions of miRNA-disease associations as new data emerges. This incremental approach avoids costly, full retraining while preserving previously learned knowledge. These studies motivate our EWC-based incremental learning design for microbe–drug association prediction.

To further improve both interpretability and predictive capability, we incorporate GNNExplainer into our training pipeline and use its edge-level explanations to refine the graph structure. Incremental learning via elastic weight consolidation

To evaluate the model’s ability to acquire new knowledge without catastrophically forgetting previously learned information, we designed an incremental learning experiment. In this scenario, a model pre-trained on a source dataset (Task A, e.g., MDAD) is fine-tuned on a smaller, distinct target dataset (Task B, e.g., DrugVirus). To mitigate catastrophic forgetting, we employ Elastic Weight Consolidation (EWC), a regularization strategy that protects parameters crucial to the source task.

Second, we introduce internal alignment MLPs initialized as identity mappings in the aligned space and update them jointly during EWC fine-tuning. Both and act row-wise on each entity feature vector, so the entity order is preserved throughout the transformation.

Importantly, no re-indexing or entity matching is performed during alignment. If the i-th row of a target feature matrix corresponds to drug (or microbe) i before alignment, the i-th row after alignment still corresponds to the same entity because the MLPs transform each row independently and do not permute rows. Consequently, the correspondence between mapped features and target-dataset drugs/microbes is guaranteed by the original dataset indexing used to build the adjacency matrix and train/test splits. For example, when DrugVirus is treated as the target dataset, the alignment modules preserve the original 175 drug rows and 95 microbe rows exactly; only the feature dimensions are transformed, not the entity identities or their ordering.

Handling dataset-specific node numbers

The number of nodes can differ across datasets, which changes the shapes of graph-dependent inputs such as the adjacency matrix , the normalized propagation matrix , and the node-feature matrix . These matrices are therefore rebuilt for each target dataset rather than transferred as trainable model parameters. In contrast, the transferable parameters protected by EWC are the size-invariant neural-network weights, including the modality projection MLPs after feature-space alignment, the GCN weight matrices and , and the decoder parameters. Since these trainable weights depend on feature and hidden dimensions rather than on N, they can be warm-started from the source task once the input feature dimension has been aligned. For each target dataset, the model applies the transferred weights to the newly constructed target graph and computes embeddings with target-specific shape . Thus, changes in the numbers of drugs or microbes affect only the dataset-specific input tensors and output embedding rows, while the EWC regularization is applied only to compatible trainable parameters with fixed dimensions.

Training and evaluation protocol

During the fine-tuning phase, the target-dataset model is warm-started from the source-dataset weights, while the alignment modules are optimized with a smaller learning rate than the main GCN and decoder. The optimization objective combines three signals: performance on the new target dataset, rehearsal on the source-task training split, and the EWC penalty. We evaluate the fine-tuned model on the held-out target-task fold to assess knowledge acquisition and on the corresponding source-task fold to track knowledge retention. For reference, we also report a Scratch baseline, which denotes direct training on the target dataset from random initialization under the same fold split and evaluation protocol, without source pretraining or EWC.

Results

In this section, we present a comprehensive evaluation of the proposed framework, beginning with its performance comparison against state‑of‑the‑art baselines on multiple datasets. We then examine the contribution of key modules through ablation studies, followed by case analyses using five representative drugs and five representative microbes to illustrate the biological relevance of the predicted associations.

For a fair and robust comparison, all methods were evaluated using a 5-fold cross-validation strategy. The results detailed in Table 2 show that our method achieves the best mean performance among the compared methods on both the MDAD and aBiofilm datasets, with AUC scores of 0.9765 and 0.9867, respectively. This strong performance on the primary bacterial and fungal association tasks can be attributed to our model’s core architectural innovations. The gated attention mechanism for multi-view feature fusion enables adaptive integration of diverse biological information, while the GNNExplainer-driven graph optimization proactively refines the network topology to amplify biologically relevant signals, thereby enhancing predictive accuracy for these domains. On the DrugVirus dataset, our model under the standard training setup achieves a competitive but not leading AUC of 0.8847. This result is intrinsically linked to our model’s specialized structural design. The feature encoders in our framework–particularly for microbial genome sequences and metabolic pathways–are inherently optimized for the complex cellular structures and functional modules of bacteria and fungi. In contrast, viruses, which are acellular and lack their own metabolism, present a fundamentally different biological entity. Consequently, the rich feature representations that empower our model’s performance on MDAD and aBiofilm encounter a natural domain gap when applied to viral data, limiting the model’s peak performance on the DrugVirus benchmark.

First, we validated the effectiveness of our Gated Attention fusion module by comparing it against several alternative attention-based fusion strategies. The results, detailed in Table 4, unequivocally demonstrate its superiority. For instance, on the aBiofilm dataset, our Gated Attention model achieved a nearly perfect AUC of 0.9867, consistently outperforming Dynamic Composite Attention (0.9809), Hybrid Attention (0.9789), and Dynamic Layer Attention (0.9560). A similar trend was observed on the MDAD dataset, where Gated Attention (AUC 0.9765) showed a significant margin over its closest competitor. This performance advantage is attributed to the Gated Attention mechanism’s ability to learn entity-specific importance weights for each feature modality, allowing for a more nuanced and adaptive integration of heterogeneous information compared to less flexible fusion methods.

Second, we investigated the impact of our novel explanation-driven graph refinement process. As shown in Table 5, this component is critical to the model’s success. The baseline model without any explainer feedback (‘No explainer‘) achieved an AUC of only 0.9287 on the MDAD dataset. By incorporating our GNNExplainer-based refinement, the performance surged to an AUC of 0.9765, representing a remarkable improvement of approximately 4.8%. We also tested an alternative explainer, GradCAM, which improved the AUC to 0.9518 but still fell short of the GNNExplainer approach. This substantial performance gap underscores that our method of using GNNExplainer not just for post-hoc analysis but to actively reconstruct the graph topology is highly effective at amplifying task-relevant signals and pruning noise.

Finally, to justify our choice of the graph encoder, we compared our GCN-based architecture with a model using a Graph Attention Network (GAT) encoder, with results presented in Table 6. The GCN encoder demonstrated markedly superior performance on all three benchmarks. On the MDAD dataset, the GCN-based model (AUC 0.9765) significantly outperformed the GAT-based model (AUC 0.8958). Similarly large performance gaps were observed on the aBiofilm (0.9867 vs. 0.9330) and DrugVirus (0.8847 vs. 0.8320) datasets. This consistent outperformance suggests that for the specific characteristics of our constructed heterogeneous graph, the direct and efficient neighborhood aggregation mechanism of GCN is more effective than the weighted aggregation of GAT.

In summary, these ablation studies confirm that each of our primary design choices–the Gated Attention fusion, the GNNExplainer-driven graph refinement, and the GCN encoder–plays an indispensable and synergistic role. The removal or replacement of any of these components leads to a significant degradation in performance, validating their collective importance in achieving the state-of-the-art results of our proposed framework.

To demonstrate our model’s interpretability, we employed GNNExplainer [15] to deconstruct the reasoning behind the high-confidence prediction linking Tamoxifen to Hepatitis C Virus (HCV). The analysis revealed a sophisticated inference process built on both broad induction and direct deduction. On the virus side, the model contextually defined HCV within a "viral community" characterized by shared vulnerabilities. It captured a "family weakness" by connecting HCV to its Flaviviridae relatives like Dengue virus (importance: 0.371), suggesting susceptibility to similar mechanisms [56]. Simultaneously, it learned a "phenotypic weakness" by linking HCV to other hepatotropic viruses like Hepatitis A and B (0.461), identifying a shared dependency on the liver’s cellular environment [57]. Concurrently, the model’s reasoning on the drug side followed two distinct and complementary paths. First, it established a broad pharmacological analogy by positioning Tamoxifen as a "neighbor" to the broad-spectrum antiviral agent Galidesivir (0.331), a known inhibitor of RNA viruses including members of the Flaviviridae family [58]. This suggested that Tamoxifen likely possessed general anti-RNA virus properties, akin to an entire class of drugs. More critically, however, GNNExplainer uncovered a direct and powerful inference shortcut that provided a more precise rationale. This shortcut was a two-step transitive path built upon a known drug-microbe association, (CYT107 → HCV, importance: 0.309), which has been explored in clinical contexts for its immunomodulatory effects in chronic HCV infection [59], and a crucial drug-drug similarity the model learned, (Tamoxifen ↔ CYT107, importance: 0.306). The logic here is compellingly direct: given that CYT107 is known to affect HCV, and Tamoxifen is identified as highly similar to CYT107, the model infers that Tamoxifen is also likely to affect HCV. This second path moves beyond general analogy to a specific, evidence-based precedent. In essence, the GNNExplainer shows that the model’s prediction is not based on a single piece of evidence but on a logical closure where the broad, community-based induction is anchored and strongly reinforced by a precise, deductive shortcut, making its reasoning both transparent and highly plausible.

Conclusion

In this study, we propose a novel framework that integrates multi-view features, explainability feedback, and continual learning to address three major challenges in microbe-drug association (MDA) prediction: accuracy, interpretability, and model adaptability. By deeply integrating multi-source heterogeneous data–including drug chemical structures and semantic information, as well as microbial genomic and metabolic pathway data–and employing a gated attention mechanism for adaptive fusion, our model constructs more comprehensive and precise entity representations.

The core innovations of this work are twofold. First, we pioneer the use of GNNExplainer’s explanation results to optimize the graph structure itself. By extracting the importance scores of critical edges from the explanations of known associations and feeding them back into the adjacency matrix, the model is guided to focus on more biologically relevant connections. This new paradigm of "explainability-driven performance enhancement" not only significantly improves prediction accuracy but also enhances the biological traceability and credibility of the model’s decision-making process. Second, we introduce a continual learning strategy using Elastic Weight Consolidation (EWC) to effectively address the challenges posed by the dynamic growth of biological data. This mechanism enables the model to efficiently learn from new data without catastrophically forgetting previously acquired knowledge, thereby significantly improving its scalability and long-term usability in real-world, dynamic environments.

Extensive experimental results on three public datasets–MDAD, aBiofilm, and DrugVirus–demonstrate that our model performs strongly on key metrics such as AUC and AUPR, especially on the bacterial and fungal benchmarks. Despite these encouraging results, our study has several limitations. The model’s performance depends on the completeness and quality of the multi-view data, the two-stage explanation-and-refinement pipeline increases computational overhead, and the current validation remains limited to benchmark datasets plus literature-supported case studies rather than prospective experimental verification.

Looking ahead, we plan to explore three primary future directions. First, we aim to incorporate active learning strategies to enable the model to screen and recommend the most valuable unknown associations for investigation, thereby guiding wet-lab experimental design. Second, we will evaluate the framework on more realistic temporal-update settings and external validation cohorts, including true dataset-version updates when such benchmarks become available. Third, we will extend the current bipartite framework to a tripartite host-microbe-drug interaction network in order to uncover more complex systems-level mechanisms relevant to personalized medicine and pharmacomicrobiomics.

Data availability

In this study, we utilized three experimentally validated microbe-drug association datasets: MDAD, aBiofilm, and DrugVirus. To ensure consistent evaluation and fair comparison with baseline methods, the majority of our data was retrieved from the repository provided by Long et al. [4] (https://github.com/longyahui/GCNMDA). Specifically, the following data matrices and attributes were obtained directly from this source: Association Data: The adjacency matrices and entity name lists for MDAD, aBiofilm, and DrugVirus. Drug Features: The drug structural similarity matrix (Sd) and drug network topological attributes (Fd). Microbe Features: The microbe functional similarity matrix (Sm) and microbe genome sequence attributes (Fm). For clarity, the original experimentally validated datasets are described as follows: MDAD: Constructed by Sun et al. [36], containing 2470 associations between 1373 drugs and 173 microbes. aBiofilm: Developed by Rajput et al. [37], focusing on anti-biofilm agents. Original repository: http://bioinfo.imtech.res.in/ manojk/abiofilm/. DrugVirus: Created by Andersen et al. [38], providing broad-spectrum antiviral data. Original database: https://drugvirus. info/. To further enhance the feature representation, we incorporated additional biological features and semantic information calculated in this study. The sources and processing methods for these supplementary features are: Metabolic Pathways: Used to construct the microbial metabolic attributes (Fm meta). We implemented an automated data retrieval pipeline using Python. First, the UniProt Taxonomy API (https://www.uniprot.org/) was queried to map microbial names to their corresponding KEGG organism codes. Subsequently, the specific metabolic pathway lists were retrieved via the KEGG RESTful API (https://www.kegg.jp/kegg/rest/). Finally, the retrieved pathway identifiers were transformed into numerical feature vectors using Feature Hashing. Drug Chemical Structures: Used to construct the drug molecular substructure attributes (Fd smiles). We retrieved the Canonical SMILES (Simplified Molecular Input Line Entry System) strings for all drugs from the PubChem database (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/). These chemical structure representations were then processed using the RDKit Python package to generate Morgan fingerprints (radius=2), which encode the topological environments of atoms into binary feature vectors. Pre-trained Language Model: Used to generate the semantic attributes for drugs (Fd bert) and microbes (Fm bert). The pre-trained BERT model weights (bert-base-uncased) were obtained from the Hugging Face model hub (https://huggingface. co/bert-base-uncased). All processed feature matrices, along with the source code for data preprocessing and model training, are publicly available at our GitHub repository: https://github.com/Luoxidu02/IDEAL.
