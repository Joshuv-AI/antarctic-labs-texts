Improving glycaemic outcomes with subsidised access to diabetes technology in a young adult population with type 1 diabetes
Authors: Andrew‐Hyun Lee, Gabrielle Stokes, Kaye Farrell, Deborah Jane Holmes‐Walker
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimsThe availability of diabetes technologies has increased, although access can be limited for young people with type 1 diabetes (T1D). In Australia, access to subsidised continuous glucose monitors (CGM) has expanded significantly, while insulin pump access remains limited. This study evaluated the impact of changes in availability of diabetes technologies on glycaemic outcomes in young adults with T1D.Methods418 with T1D aged 15–25 years attending a young adult diabetes clinic in Sydney were reviewed between July 2019 and June 2024. The primary outcome was change in glycaemia as measured by glycosylated haemoglobin (HbA1c) across 5 years.ResultsCGM use increased from 29.4% to 76.2% over the study duration, and uptake increased in all sociodemographic groups likely due to universal subsidisation. Insulin pump use remained unchanged (52–57%), as pump access remains limited by cost. An increase in pump therapy was only observed in those of the lowest sociodemographic cohort, attributed to expansions in the insulin pump program targeted to low‐income households, although a reduction in usage was observed in middle socio‐economic groups. Use of hybrid closed loop (HCL) insulin pumps increased from 5.1% to 35.4% in line with CGM uptake. With increased uptake of technologies, median HbA1c improved from 68 mmol/mol (8.4%) to 64 mmol/mol (8.0%) (p < 0.001). CGM and HCL demonstrated independent benefits to glycaemia. Rates of severe hypoglycaemia and diabetic ketoacidosis were low at 1.08 and 4.90/100 person‐years, respectively. Pump therapy was associated with reduced DKA.ConclusionsOverall, improvements in glycaemia in young adults were achieved with greater accessibility to subsidised diabetes technologies.

BACKGROUND

Type 1 diabetes (T1D) is a chronic condition associated with significant disease burden, including in the adolescent and young adult population. 1 Changing life events in this period including education, occupation and social expectations are often accompanied with reduced health care engagement and increased diabetes‐related hospitalisation. 2 A range of diabetes technologies including continuous glucose monitoring (CGM), insulin pumps and hybrid closed loop (HCL) systems can translate to benefits to glycaemia 3 , 4 and quality of life 5 in young adults. Despite this, uptake and acceptance of technologies remain a challenge in this cohort. 6 , 7

Access to diabetes technologies has increased in many countries. In Australia, access to CGM was introduced in 2017 with subsidisation for continuous and flash glucose monitoring for T1D under 21 years, followed by concession card holders for low‐income earners in 2019, and subsequently universal subsidisation for T1D in July 2022. CGM devices subsidised through the National Diabetes Supply Scheme include Medtronic™ G3/G4 (the latter from June 2023), Dexcom™ G5/G6 (the latter from March 2022) and Freestyle Libre™/Libre 2 (the latter from August 2021).

By contrast, access to insulin pump therapy in Australia remains predominantly through private health insurance or self‐funding. Subsidisation through the government insulin pump program has been available since 2008, initially for financially disadvantaged families with children under 18 years, and later expanded to 21 years from April 2023. Pumps available through this program were the Medtronic™ 670G pumps, with the 770G added in June 2021, and subsequently changed to the YpsoPump™ as the sole option in late 2023.

HCL insulin pump systems have been available since 2019 with the Medtronic™ MiniMed 670G, and algorithm upgrades (780G) available in Australia in late 2022. Alternate HCL systems with the Tandem™ t:slim X2 Control IQ and YpsoPump™ CamAPS FX became available in Australia in 2022 and 2023, respectively.

This study aimed to evaluate the impact of changes in diabetes technology availability on glycaemic outcomes in young adults with T1D aged 15–25 years attending a specialised young adult diabetes clinic at a tertiary hospital, with comparison against historical control data from the same clinic. 8

METHODS

Study design and population

A retrospective cohort study was conducted in adolescents and young adults aged 15–25 years with T1D attending a young adult diabetes service at Westmead Hospital, Sydney Australia, from July 2019 to June 2024. A year was defined as the first day of July to the last day of June the following year to reflect dates of changes in accessibility to diabetes technologies in Australia. Individuals who were pregnant were excluded from this study.

Key features of the young adult service include access to a multidisciplinary team at a late‐finishing afternoon clinic, diabetes educator as coordinator, text reminders prior to appointments, rebooking of missed appointments and a central mobile phone contact including support for sick days. Data collected included age, gender, country of birth, diabetes duration, visit frequency, glycosylated haemoglobin (HbA1c), insulin delivery mode, events of severe hypoglycaemia (hypoglycaemia requiring assistance, loss of consciousness or seizure) and admissions for diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA).

Deidentified data routinely extracted annually from electronic medical records and consented for research (HREC/08/HNE/379; SSA/09/WMEAD/269; 2019 PID00927) were utilised for this study. The primary source of data was obtained through electronic diabetes assessment forms prospectively completed by individual clinicians at each visit. Unique numerical identifiers and password‐protected files were used to maintain patient privacy and confidentiality. The data underwent preliminary assessment prior to analysis to determine accuracy, with clinical notes reviewed to retrieve any incomplete entries.

Measures

The primary outcome of the study was change in glycaemia as measured by HbA1c across 5 years to evaluate the impact of change in availability of diabetes technologies in the adolescent and young adult population. Secondary outcomes were to assess the annualised incidence of severe hypoglycaemia and DKA, and the effects of sociodemographic status utilising Socio‐Economic Indices for Areas (SEIFA) 9 on glycaemic outcomes.

Insulin therapy was categorised as either multiple daily subcutaneous insulin (MDI) or insulin pump therapy depending on the predominant therapy used by the individual in the past month prior to clinic review. Insulin pump therapy was further categorised as hybrid closed loop (HCL) if automated insulin delivery occurred by communication with a compatible CGM, or as manual mode if insulin delivery was only determined by the pump's preset variables. Suspend before hypoglycaemia functions (such as Basal‐IQ from the Tandem™ t:slim X2 pump) were defined as manual mode as insulin delivery is not automated except for suspension of delivery when glucose levels are low.

Inputs on an electronic assessment form at each clinic visit were used to identify episodes of DKA and severe hypoglycaemia. Episodes of DKA were also identified through ICD codes. Each episode of DKA and severe hypoglycaemia was verified through an assessment of the individual's electronic medical record including clinic letters, emergency department and discharge correspondences.

Point of care HbA1c was measured utilising the AFINION™ 2 Analyzer at each visit or obtained from local pathology providers where the visit was conducted by video telehealth. Telehealth was predominantly utilised during COVID‐19 restrictions. Glycaemic data were obtained by a download of pump and/or CGM data at the time of clinic review. The CGM outcomes of time in range, time below range and time above range were categorised as per international recommendations. 10 Glycaemic targets for the clinic were HbA1c ≤53 mmol/mol (7.0%), and CGM time above range ≥ 70% and time below range <5%.

SEIFA is a socio‐economic measure by geographic area determined by the Australian Bureau of Statistics, 9 which categorises the population into five groups. SEIFA 1 represents the area of least socio‐economic advantage, and SEIFA 5 refers to the area of most advantage.

Statistical analyses

Data were analysed using SPSS version 25. Continuous variables were expressed as median and interquartile range (IQR) unless otherwise specified, and categorical data were given in absolute numbers and percentages. For the primary outcome, changes in median HbA1c over time were analysed with a Kruskal–Wallis test, or analysis of variance (ANOVA) when comparisons were made across multiple groups. Comparisons in rates of DKA, severe hypoglycaemic events and use of diabetes technologies were analysed with Pearson's chi‐square test. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. Pairwise comparisons of insulin delivery mode (MDI, manual mode insulin, HCL) were adjusted with Bonferroni correction.

RESULTS

Over the study period of July 2019 to June 2024, 418 adolescents and young adults aged 15–25 years (195 men, 223 women) with T1D were reviewed in the Young Adult Diabetes clinic with 3185 visits (Table 1). Median visit frequency was 3.0 per year, except in 2023 where visit frequency had reduced to 2.0 per year (p = 0.01). Median age was 21.1 years (IQR 19.1–23.5 years). Uptake of continuous glucose monitors (CGM) increased throughout the study duration from 29.4% in 2019 to 76.2% in 2023 (p < 0.001) (Table 1). CGM use increased in all SEIFA groups (p < 0.001 for all), with the greatest increase observed in SEIFA 1 (Figure 1a), noting expansion of CGM subsidisation for low‐income households in 2019. Although the SEIFA 5 group had the overall highest rates of CGM use over the 5 years, there was no statistical difference in CGM use across the SEIFA groups at study end in 2023. When the analysis was stratified by age, CGM use was higher in those aged ≤21 years (54.6%) compared with the older cohort (48.9%) (p = 0.001; Tables S1 and S2).

Rates of multiple daily subcutaneous insulin (MDI) and insulin pump use remained unchanged across the duration of the study (Table 1). Use of hybrid closed loop (HCL) systems increased, with a concomitant decrease in manual mode insulin delivery, with use of HCL surpassing manual mode insulin delivery in 2023. The SEIFA 5 group exhibited the highest rates of insulin pump use overall at 60.5% (p < 0.001). Only SEIFA 1 exhibited a statistically significant increase in use of insulin pump therapy during the study duration (p = 0.049), while the SEIFA 2 + 3 group demonstrated a reduction in use (p = 0.002; Figure 1b).

Median HbA1c improved from 68 mmol/mol (8.4%) in 2019 to 64 mmol/mol (8.0%) in 2023. Proportion achieving a target HbA1c of 53 mmol/mol (7.0%) or less was static at 17–18% until an increase was observed in 2023 to 26.8% (Table 1).

HbA1c was significantly lower in all CGM users (63 mmol/mol [7.9%]) compared to those who did not use CGM (73 mmol/mol [8.8%]) (p < 0.001; Figure 2a). The effect remained when evaluating MDI users alone (to exclude the effect of pump systems on glycaemia), with CGM users exhibiting a lower HbA1c (64 mmol/mol [8.0%]) than those who did not (73 mmol/mol [8.8%]) (p < 0.001). Those who did not use CGM were less likely to be on an insulin pump (42.5%) compared with CGM users (64.6%) (p < 0.001).

When stratified by insulin delivery mode, median HbA1c in all users was lowest in HCL users at 58 mmol/mol (7.5%) (p < 0.001), while HbA1c in MDI or manual mode insulin pump users did not differ at 68 mmol/mol (8.4%) and 72 mmol/mol (8.7%), respectively (p = 0.10) (Figure 2b). The proportion with HbA1c ≤53 mmol/mol (7%) was significantly greater in those using HCL (28.8%; p < 0.001). When analysing CGM users alone (to exclude the effect of CGM on glycaemia), HbA1c was lowest in those using HCL systems (58 mmol/mol [7.5%]), compared to those on MDI (64 mmol/mol [8.0%]), and highest in those using manual mode insulin pumps (68 mmol/mol [8.4%]) (p < 0.001).

Median HbA1c was lowest in the SEIFA 5 group at 65 mmol/mol (8.1%) and highest in SEIFA 1 at 73 mmol/mol (8.8%) (p < 0.001) (Figure 3). Similarly, the largest proportion with HbA1c ≤53 mmol/mol (7%) was observed in the SEIFA 5 group and lowest in SEIFA 1 (respectively, 21.9% and 13.6%, p < 0.001). HbA1c improved in all groups over time, although the SEIFA 2 + 3 cohort did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.06). There was no difference in HbA1c across the study duration by age, excepting 2021, where a lower HbA1c was observed in the younger cohort (65 mmol/mol [8.1%]) compared to those >21 (70 mmol/mol [8.5%]) (p = 0.035).

Rates of severe hypoglycaemia were low at 1.08/100 person‐years, and incidence did not differ by mode of insulin delivery (p = 0.955) nor CGM use (p = 0.766). Hypoglycaemia rates remained unchanged over the duration of the study (p = 0.889) (Table S3).

Incidence of DKA was 4.90/100 person‐years. Rates of DKA were highest in those using MDI at 7.03/100 person‐years, compared to those on pump therapy at 2.98/100 person‐years (p < 0.001). There was no difference in DKA rates for HCL compared with manual mode insulin pump delivery (p = 0.503), and no change in DKA frequency was seen with CGM use (p = 0.323). Rates of DKA remained unchanged over the duration of the study (p = 0.156) (Table S3).

DISCUSSION

A glycaemic target of <53 mmol/mol (7%) in young adults with T1D is difficult to achieve. Diabetes technologies have been shown to improve glycaemia in T1D, 3 , 4 although their real‐world benefits are challenged by limitations to access and adherence. This retrospective cohort study demonstrates the uptake of diabetes technology in young adults from a single‐centre youth‐orientated diabetes service, in parallel with changes to subsidisation of devices in Australia.

CGM use significantly increased in our cohort from 29.4% to 76.2% over 5 years, with the greatest increases observed in 2020 and 2022 which correlate with changes in subsidisation in Australia. Notably, CGM uptake significantly increased regardless of socio‐economic status, with equal distribution of CGM usage across all SEIFA groups in the final year of the study. In addition, in the year prior to extending CGM access to all ages (2021), the greatest disparity in CGM use by age was observed, highlighting the impact of subsidised access to the uptake of CGM. A quarter of young adults in our clinic have elected not to use CGM, with concerns about accuracy, alarm fatigue, adhesive reactions, difficulty maintaining sensor adhesion or dislike of attachments as the main reported barriers to continued use. 6 , 11 We however hypothesise that further increases in use are likely to occur with continued improvements in CGM technology and continued subsidisation.

By contrast, uptake of insulin pumps has remained stagnant at 56% over the study period, although this has increased from our previous study of the same clinic with 40% pump use in 2012. 8 The ongoing lack of subsidisation for pump therapy except for those in the lowest socio‐economic bracket remains an issue in Australia. The current funding model explains the increased utilisation of pump and HCL technology in the least advantaged (SEIFA 1). Uptake particularly observed in the latter half of the study in the SEIFA 1 group may have occurred due to expansion of the insulin pump program in 2020 (with increased numbers of devices subsidised) and 2023 (with expanded eligibility for those aged 19–21 years). Conversely, discouraging reductions in pump use in the SEIFA 2 + 3 group appear to represent a demographic experiencing a gap in accessibility to government funding and the inability to afford private health insurance.

The benefits of diabetes technologies to glycaemic outcomes are well documented 12 , 13 , 14 which has been reassuringly reflected in our real‐world data. In particular, CGM and HCL users consistently demonstrated improved glycaemic outcomes, with the effects of CGM and HCL observed to have independent benefits on glycaemia. Insulin pumps in manual mode did not offer significant glycaemic benefit over MDI in comparison with randomised controlled studies, 15 although notably they did provide a reduction in time in hypoglycaemia compared to MDI without an increase in DKA, as similarly observed in previous studies. 16

With the significant uptake in CGM and HCL, a corresponding improvement in median HbA1c was observed over the study duration. The exception occurred in 2020 where a deterioration in glycaemia may have occurred secondary to COVID‐19, which has also been demonstrated in other similar T1D populations. 17 This occurred despite retained visit frequency with implementation of regular telehealth. It is also worth noting that HbA1c at commencement of this study was no different when compared to the same clinic in 2001–2012 (median HbA1c 70–72 mmol/mol [8.5–8.7%]). 8 Despite the emergence of multiple diabetes technologies prior to 2019, uptake remained low and limited by cost until subsidies were introduced. It is therefore likely that improvements in overall glycaemia in this clinic population resulted once substantial uptake of diabetes technology had occurred by the end of the 5 years.

Rates of DKA and severe hypoglycaemia were unchanged over the study duration, and increases in DKA were not observed over the COVID‐19 period, unlike in other populations. 18 A reduction in DKA incidence was observed in those on insulin pump therapy, but otherwise, diabetes technologies were not associated with a reduction in DKA nor severe hypoglycaemia in our study. The study was, however, unlikely to have been sufficiently powered due to the low event rate, with DKA rates in this cohort of 50/1000 person‐years being sustained well below the population rate in this age group of 164/1000 person‐years. 2 , 8 , 19

Lastly, no consistent associations between glycaemia and age were demonstrated in our cohort. This is in contrast to two previous evaluations using the Australian Diabetes Data Network (ADDN) registry (a registry which encompasses several T1D clinics in Australia including this cohort), which had previously found highest HbA1c in emerging adults (20–25 years). 20 , 21 International cohorts have also found that young adults aged above 19 years did not attain HbA1c at levels achieved earlier in childhood and adolescence. 22 In our study, expansions in CGM subsidisation regardless of age since 2022 may explain the absence of the same finding in this study. Notably, the year prior to universal CGM access which had demonstrated the greatest disparity in CGM and HCL use by age was the only year where a difference in HbA1c by age was observed, with significantly lower HbA1c in the younger cohort with higher uptake of CGM technology.

A limitation of our study lies in the nature of registry data, with limits to completeness of data and accuracy, and potential for incomplete capture of all DKA and severe hypoglycaemia. Percentage of time using CGM was also not reliably quantified in this study. The study may also not be generalisable to all individuals with T1D due to the resources available in a youth‐specific diabetes clinic. Previous comparisons to the wider ADDN registry data revealed higher DKA incidence 23 and possibly higher HbA1c 19 , 24 in the wider T1D population aged 15–25. Qualitative data regarding barriers to technology use and identification of psychosocial factors including finances, social supports, diabetes distress and eating disorders which impact self care need to be captured in the registry and addressed.

CONCLUSION

Despite the challenges faced with managing T1D in adolescents and young adults, access to diabetes technologies through subsidisation models can act as catalysts for improving glycaemic outcomes in this dynamic and socio‐economically diverse population. Gaps in the Australian healthcare system exist, particularly with regards to insulin pump therapy and use of automated insulin delivery, which was shown to further improve glycaemic outcomes in this age group. There is an urgent need to address socio‐economic disparities of access to insulin pump therapy through continued expansion of funding and government policy changes.
