Analgesic effects of transversus abdominis plane and quadratus lumborum blocks are not clinically meaningful for laparoscopic nephrectomy: systematic review and network meta-analysis
Authors: Boohwi Hong, Yumin Jo, Sujin Baek, Woosuk Chung, Chahyun Oh, Seyeon Park
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundUltrasound-guided abdominal wall blocks are increasingly used to enhance postoperative analgesia in laparoscopic nephrectomy. Among these, the transversus abdominis plane (TAP) block and the quadratus lumborum (QL) block have emerged as promising techniques. However, no comprehensive review has yet compared the analgesic efficacy of these two regional approaches.MethodsAn extensive search was conducted across MEDLINE, Embase, Cochrane Library, Web of Science, and Google Scholar to identify randomized controlled trials comparing the postoperative analgesic effects of the TAP block, QL block, and systemic analgesia. The primary outcome was 24-hour opioid consumption, standardized to intravenous morphine milligram equivalents (MME). Secondary outcomes included postoperative pain scores assessed using a 0–10 Visual Analog Scale (VAS). A minimal clinically important difference (MCID) was defined as a reduction of 10 mg MME or 1 point on the VAS.ResultsTwelve studies were included. Both TAP and QL blocks significantly reduced opioid consumption compared to systemic analgesia (mean difference [95% confidence interval, CI] QL, −11.42 mg [−18.88 to −3.97]; TAP, −10.88 mg [−17.49 to −4.26]). However, the 95% CI did not meet the predefined MCID of −10 mg. Similarly, improvements in postoperative pain scores did not reach clinical significance.ConclusionsWhile TAP and QL blocks demonstrated a significant analgesic effect compared to systemic analgesia, the clinical relevance of this benefit may be limited.

INTRODUCTION

Laparoscopic nephrectomy, a minimally invasive surgical procedure to remove kidneys, has gained widespread acceptance due to its advantages over open surgery, including reduced postoperative pain, shorter hospital stay, and quicker recovery [1]. Despite these benefits, effective management of postoperative pain remains a crucial aspect of patient care. Inadequate pain control can lead to complications, delayed recovery, and prolonged hospitalization [2]. Therefore, optimizing analgesic techniques is essential to enhance patient outcomes and overall satisfaction.

The transversus abdominis plane (TAP) block and quadratus lumborum (QL) block are two regional anesthesia techniques increasingly employed to manage postoperative pain in patients undergoing laparoscopic nephrectomy. The TAP block involves the injection of local anesthetics into the plane between the internal oblique and transversus abdominis muscles, providing analgesia to the anterior abdominal wall [3,4]. On the other hand, the QL block targets the fascial plane adjacent to the quadratus lumborum muscle, potentially offering more extensive analgesia that includes the visceral and somatic nerves [5].

While both blocks have emerged as promising analgesic techniques, the comparative effectiveness between TAP and QL blocks remains uncertain. The TAP block is considered easier to perform with a more straightforward anatomical target, while the QL block, although technically more challenging, may provide more comprehensive analgesia [5,6]. Understanding the comparative effectiveness of these blocks is crucial for guiding clinical practice and optimizing patient care in laparoscopic nephrectomy.

Network meta-analysis (NMA) offers a robust methodological framework to compare the clinical effectiveness of multiple interventions simultaneously, even in the absence of direct head-to-head trials [7]. By synthesizing data from various studies, NMA enables a comprehensive evaluation of the relative efficacy of different treatment strategies. Despite their increasing use, no comprehensive review has compared the analgesic effectiveness of the TAP and QL blocks in laparoscopic nephrectomy. This study therefore aimed to evaluate their effects using a NMA.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

The protocol for this systematic review and network meta-analysis was prospectively registered with the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO; registration number: CRD42023407851). The study was conducted and reported in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines of NMA to ensure transparency and methodological rigor in the preparation of this manuscript [8].

1. Eligibility criteria and literature search

To address the clinical question of whether the TAP and QL blocks reduce postoperative pain in laparoscopic nephrectomy, the authors applied the PICO-SD (Population/Intervention/Comparison/Outcome/Setting/Duration) framework to develop a comprehensive search strategy. The population included adult patients (≥ 18 years) undergoing laparoscopic nephrectomy under general anesthesia. The interventions of interest were the TAP and QL blocks, and comparisons were made against a control group receiving systemic analgesia without any regional block. Studies were excluded if they involved open nephrectomy or patients with neurological disorders potentially affecting pain perception or analgesic response. A systematic literature search was conducted in MEDLINE, Embase, the Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials, Web of Science, and Google Scholar. Two reviewers independently performed the search and resolved any discrepancies through discussion. The initial database search was conducted in April 2023, followed by an updated search in May 2024 to ensure inclusion of the most recent studies. In addition to database searches, a manual review of the reference lists of the included articles was performed to identify additional relevant studies. Articles published between February 2000 and May 2024 in peer-reviewed journals were considered eligible. The overall review process was carried out between May and June 2024. The original PROSPERO registration included the thoracic paravertebral block (TPVB) and erector spinae plane block (ESPB) as comparator interventions. However, preliminary screening revealed that the number of eligible randomized controlled trials involving TPVB and ESPB in laparoscopic nephrectomy was insufficient for meaningful analysis. Therefore, these interventions were excluded from the updated search, and the final analysis focused solely on the TAP and QL blocks. This revision has been recorded in the PROSPERO amendment history.

The search strategy combined MeSH terms and free-text keywords relevant to the target procedure and interventions. Search terms included: ((laparoscopic nephroureterectomy [MeSH] OR \”laparoscopic nephrectomy\” OR \”retroperitoneoscopic renal surgery\” OR \”laparoscopic donor nephrectomy\” OR \”robot-assisted nephrectomy\” OR \”urological laparoscopic surgery\”)) AND ((\”Quadratus Lumborum block\” OR \”Trans-muscular QL block\” OR \”Anterior QL block\” OR \”QL block\” OR \”transversus abdominis plane block\” OR \”TAP block\”)).

To identify only randomized controlled trials (RCTs), filters were applied based on publication type and study design. Reference lists of included studies were manually screened for additional eligible articles. The retrieved records were managed using EndNote software (Clarivate).

2. Data extraction

Two independent reviewers performed data extraction using a standardized form developed a priori. Each study was reviewed twice to ensure consistency and accuracy of the collected information. For continuous outcomes, such as pain scores and opioid consumption, means and standard deviations (SDs) were obtained. When only medians and interquartile ranges (IQRs) were reported, the authors applied the estimation method described by Wan et al. [9] to convert values into approximate means and SDs. If data were presented solely in graphical format, the original figure were enlarged (approximately 300%–400%) and extracted numerical values using a virtual ruler, aligning scale units precisely with axis markings. Measurements were taken at the midpoint of each bar or point, ensuring perpendicular alignment for accuracy. Effect sizes and standard errors were then derived from the processed data. Additional variables—including block technique, anesthetic agent, sample size, participant characteristics, and intervention details—were extracted and summarized using a predesigned data collection sheet.

3. Quality assessment

The methodological quality of the included RCTs was assessed independently by two reviewers using the Cochrane Risk of Bias tool [10]. Domains evaluated included random sequence generation, allocation concealment, blinding (participants, personnel, and outcome assessors), completeness of outcome data, selective reporting, and other potential sources of bias. Discrepancies between reviewers were resolved through consensus or, when necessary, consultation with a third author. All assessments were performed using RevMan 5.4 software (Cochrane Collaboration). Each study was assigned an overall risk of bias rating categorized as low, unclear, or high.

4. Outcome definitions and interpretation

The primary outcome was cumulative opioid use during the first 24 hours post-surgery, standardized as intravenous (IV) morphine milligram equivalents (MME) using the following conversions: IV morphine 1 mg = IV fentanyl 10 μg = IV sufentanil 2 μg = IV tramadol 10 mg. Secondary outcomes included pain intensity scores measured on a 0–10 Visual Analog Scale (VAS) at predefined postoperative intervals: early (0–6 hours), middle (6–18 hours), and late (18–24 hours). For studies reporting multiple time points, values closest to 1, 12, and 24 hours were selected. Minimum clinically important differences (MCID) were set a priori as 10 mg MME for opioid consumption and 1 point on the VAS for pain scores [11,12].

5. Data synthesis and statistical analysis

A network meta-analysis was performed using a random-effects model under the frequentist framework. Statistical analysis was conducted in R software (version 4.0.3; R Foundation for Statistical Computing) using the “netmeta” and “dmetar” packages [13,14]. Treatment effects were expressed as mean differences (MDs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Heterogeneity and inconsistency were quantified using the I² and Q statistics. Treatment efficacy was ranked using both P-scores and the surface under the cumulative ranking curve (SUCRA), derived from resampling with 100,000 simulations. Scores ranged from 0 to 1, where higher values indicated greater analgesic efficacy.

6. Certainty assessment

The certainty of evidence for the primary and secondary outcomes of this meta-analysis was assessed using the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) approach. Five key domains were considered: risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision, and publication bias. Based on these domains, the quality of evidence was categorized into four levels: high, moderate, low, and very low. Summary of findings tables and evidence profiles were generated using the GRADEpro GDT software (https://gradepro.org). Downgrading decisions for each outcome were made according to explicit GRADE criteria. For example, imprecision was downgraded when the CI included the predefined MCID, and inconsistency was judged based on heterogeneity indices (I² > 50%). In addition, the quality of evidence was assessed using CINeMA (Confidence in Network Meta-Analysis) developed by the Institute of Social and Preventative Medicine, University of Bern, Switzerland, via the official web application (https://cinema.ispm.unibe.ch). Publication bias was assessed using Egger’s test and visual inspection of funnel plot asymmetry.

7. Subgroup analysis

To explore possible effect modifiers, exploratory subgroup analyses were conducted based on (1) the use regular postoperative non-opioid analgesics, (2) surgical approach (retroperitoneal vs. transperitoneal), and (3) timing of block administration (before vs. after surgery). For studies that employed both approaches (mixed), the surgical approach was classified as retroperitoneal.

RESULTS

1. Baseline characteristics of the included studies

A comprehensive literature search yielded 502 records, from which 465 studies remained after removing duplicates. Screening of titles and abstracts based on predefined eligibility criteria identified 54 studies for full-text review. After thorough assessment by four independent reviewers, 12 studies were included in the final analysis (Fig. 1). The included studies originated from seven countries: India (2), the United States (1), Turkey (1), China (5), Italy (1), South Korea (1), and Denmark (1), with two studies involving robot-assisted nephrectomy. Key characteristics of the included trials, including block type, surgical approach, timing of block administration, and analgesic regimen are summarized in Table 1 [15–26]. Additionally, Table 2 presents an overview of the number of included studies and enrolled patients, categorized by outcome. For consistency, two intervention arms from a single study [24] comparing different QL approaches were merged in accordance with Cochrane recommendations.

2. Methodological quality and risk of bias

Most studies were judged as low risk for attrition, reporting, and other biases. Allocation concealment was generally adequate; however, random sequence generation was unclear in three studies [22,24,26]. One study showed high performance bias [20]. Overall, moderate risk of bias was identified in performance and detection domains. These findings are illustrated in Fig. 2. A comparison-adjusted funnel plot showed evidence of a visually symmetric plot of opioid consumption and pain scores. The results of Egger’s regression test of outcomes also showed no significant publication bias (Supplementary Figs. 1–4). The certainty of evidence was rated as moderated-to-low in nature according to the GRADE system (Table 2), and the confidence rating of each comparison using CINeMA is described in Supplementary Fig. 5.

3. Heterogeneity and consistency test

Heterogeneity across studies was substantial, as indicated by high I² values and significant Q statistics, justifying the use of a random-effects model (Table 2). Due to the lack of direct comparisons between the TAP and QL blocks, formal assessment of consistency using methods such as node-splitting was not feasible. However, the reasonable similarity in clinical and methodological characteristics across studies supports the assumption of transitivity, which underlies the validity of indirect comparisons. In addition, the similarity in effect estimates between pairwise and network analyses further supports the plausibility of a coherent network.

4. Efficacy outcomes

Six studies each contributed opioid consumption data for the TAP and QL blocks. In pairwise analyses, both blocks showed a statistically significant reduction in opioid consumption compared to the control; however, the effect sizes of –11.07 (95% CI: –21.65 to –0.49) and –11.91 (95% CI: –20.48 to –3.34), respectively, were not considered clinically meaningful (Supplementary Fig. 6). Similarly, in the NMA, which included indirect comparisons, the QL block showed an effect size of –11.42 (95% CI: –18.88 to –3.97) and the TAP block showed –10.88 (95% CI: –17.49 to –4.26), indicating that the results were not substantially different from the result of pairwise comparisons. The upper 95% CI for both blocks did not meet the predetermined MCID of –10 mg, indicating that the reductions were not clinically significant (Fig. 3). Pain scores at each time point showed statistically significant differences compared to the control in NMA, with the exception of the QL block during the early period. However, these differences did not meet the MCID of 1 or more (Fig. 3). As no head-to-head comparisons between the TAP and QL blocks were available for any of the outcomes assessed, network league tables were constructed based solely on indirect estimates to enhance interpretability (Table 3).

5. Ranking hierarchy

Table 4 summarizes the ranking of each intervention based on P-scores across outcomes. The QL block ranked highest for opioid consumption (P score = 0.7732), as well as for middle (0.7594) and late-period pain scores (0.8636). Meanwhile, the TAP block demonstrated comparable P-scores—notably surpassing the QL in the early postoperative period (0.7664 vs. 0.7082)—suggesting a largely similar analgesic profile between the two blocks. Given these overlapping probabilities, the distinction in efficacy between the QL and TAP blocks remains equivocal, precluding definitive conclusions regarding superiority. These findings are further illustrated by the SUCRA plots in Fig. 4, which visually depict the cumulative ranking probabilities for each outcome.

6. Subgroup analysis

When restricting the analysis to nine studies that did not include regular non-opioid analgesics, the QL block [21,22,24–26] showed a MD of −10.81 mg (95% CI: −20.29 to −1.34) compared to the control, and the TAP block [15,16,18,20] showed a MD of −11.66 mg (95% CI: −21.54 to −1.78) (Supplementary Fig. 7). Compared to the main analysis, these results suggest that excluding non-opioid agents does not substantially alter the overall interpretation.

In the subgroup analysis based on surgical approach, greater opioid-sparing effects were observed in studies using the transperitoneal approach (seven studies; Supplementary Fig. 8). In this subgroup, the QL block [21–23] reduced opioid consumption by a MD of −16.50 mg (95% CI: −29.49 to −3.51), and the TAP block [16,17,19,20] by −13.67 mg (95% CI: −23.31 to −4.03). In contrast, among studies employing the retroperitoneal approach (five studies; Supplementary Fig. 9), the QL block [24-26] demonstrated a MD of −8.31 mg (95% CI: −19.62 to 2.99), while the TAP block [15,18] showed a MD of −5.56 mg (95% CI: −18.84 to 7.71). Since the CIs for both interventions crossed zero in the retroperitoneal subgroup, the analgesic effects of the QL and TAP blocks in this surgical setting may be negligible.

Regarding the timing of block administration, blocks performed after surgery appeared to provide greater analgesic effects compared to those performed before surgery. In the subgroup comprising studies where blocks were administered postoperatively, the TAP block [15,17] demonstrated a MD of −14.56 mg (95% CI: −17.11 to −12.01) (two studies; Supplementary Fig. 10). The QL block data were not available for this subgroup. In contrast, among studies in which blocks were administered preoperatively, the TAP block [16,18–20] showed a MD of −9.04 mg (95% CI: −17.82 to −0.26), while the QL block [21–26] showed a MD of −11.64 mg (95% CI: −19.57 to −3.71) (10 studies; Supplementary Fig. 11).

DISCUSSION

This network meta-analysis suggests that in patients undergoing laparoscopic nephrectomy, both the QL and TAP block significantly reduce postoperative opioid consumption and pain score. However, the effect sizes were not clinically meaningful. QLB and TAP blocks showed comparable postoperative analgesic effects in laparoscopic nephrectomy.

The MCID for morphine consumption was set at 10 mg and for pain scores at 1, based on previous literature [27–29]. The MCID is a representative method for determining clinical significance by setting a specific threshold for measured outcomes as a valid criterion for effective judgment. If changes in clinical trial results exceed this threshold, they are considered to have clinical significance [11,12,30]. Unfortunately, although the authors’ meta-analysis identified statistically significant differences, it did not demonstrate clinical relevance. Considering the commonly anticipated effect sizes in RCTs assessing peripheral and truncal nerve blocks—IV MME of 9 mg over 0–24 hours, and a 2-point reduction in pain on the Numeric Rating Scale (0–10)—our criteria were slightly more lenient [31]. Nevertheless, the results failed to meet the thresholds for minimal clinically important differences. Interestingly, the present findings are generally consistent with two recent meta-analyses that evaluated the QL and TAP blocks in nephrectomy patients [32,33]. However, those studies did not assess outcomes against predefined thresholds of clinical importance. By incorporating MCID-based evaluation, the present analysis offers a distinct interpretive framework—demonstrating that even statistically significant benefits may not translate into meaningful clinical impact. This distinction underscores the importance of contextualizing effect sizes when guiding clinical decision-making.

TAP and QL blocks are often considered secondary options and are selectively advised in situations where basic analgesics cannot be used [34], intrathecal morphine is not an option [35], or epidural block is not feasible [36]. Additionally, for certain laparoscopic surgeries, these blocks have shown inconsistent results or lack of sufficient evidence, leading to their exclusion as first-line analgesic methods [37–39]. Currently, there are no procedure-specific recommendations or ERAS (Enhanced Recovery After Surgery) guidelines specifically addressing laparoscopic nephrectomy. Based on the number and outcomes of the studies included in the authors’ analysis, it seems unlikely that TAP or QL blocks can be recommended as a part of multimodal analgesia for this procedure.

The QL block has been investigated for its potential to provide postoperative analgesia in various surgical procedures, including laparoscopic surgeries [40]. A systematic review and meta-analysis conducted by Uppal et al. [41] evaluated the efficacy of the QL block for abdominal wall and hip surgeries in providing postoperative pain relief compared to a placebo and other analgesic techniques. The analysis revealed that while the QL block does offer analgesic benefits over a placebo for abdominal wall surgeries, the benefits are marginal when compared to other regional analgesic techniques such as the TAP block. Specifically, for laparoscopic nephrectomy, the QL block reduced opioid consumption and improved early mobilization and recovery of intestinal function. However, the analgesic advantages were not significant enough to establish a clear clinical superiority over other established methods. The included studies utilized various QL approaches (lateral, posterior, anterior) and reported inconsistent outcomes, making it difficult to draw definitive conclusions about its efficacy. Although the QL block is generally considered safe, some studies reported adverse events such as quadriceps weakness [42–44] and symptoms of local anesthetic systemic toxicity such as metallic taste, tinnitus, arrhythmia [45]. These potential risks must be weighed against the marginal benefits observed.

The QL block offers a theoretical advantage over the TAP block by providing both somatic and visceral analgesia. This is due to the potential spread of local anesthetic into the thoracic paravertebral space, blocking somatic nerves and the lower thoracic sympathetic trunk [6,46,47]. While this may provide superior analgesia compared to the TAP block, which primarily targets somatic pain, the clinical relevance of this theoretical benefit remains uncertain. The authors’ analysis also demonstrated similar effect sizes between the QL and TAP blocks, and the ranking probabilities of the two groups made it difficult to determine superiority. This suggests that the theoretical hypothesis of the QL block being superior is unlikely to translate into clinically significant benefits.

Unlike the present analysis, a pairwise meta-analysis evaluating the QL block, which included studies covering various types of renal surgeries, demonstrated clinically significant results, with a MD of −17.58 (95% CI: −23.14 to −12.02) [48]. While the findings appeared promising, the overall outcomes were notably influenced by one study performed on renal transplantation recipients, where the effect size was significantly larger compared to other studies [49]. Notably, the results of that particular study were not reproduced in a recent well-conducted RCT on living donor renal transplantation [50]. This discrepancy underscores the need for cautious interpretation of the earlier findings.

In the present study, direct comparisons of both blocks were not available, which precluded the use of the net splitting approach to check for consistency between direct and indirect comparison. As an alternative, pairwise comparisons were performed focusing solely on direct comparisons and compared these results with those of the network meta-analysis, which included indirect comparisons. The resulting effect sizes were nearly identical, suggesting that indirect comparisons did not introduce meaningful inconsistency and supporting the overall homogeneity of the findings. A prior meta-analysis comparing the TAP and QL blocks in various abdominal surgeries also reported similar findings [51]. Although the study reported a statistically significant difference between the blocks, the magnitude of the difference was small and unlikely to translate into meaningful clinical benefit.

The choice of surgical technique, transperitoneal or retroperitoneal nephrectomy, may influence postoperative outcomes. The retroperitoneal approach avoids the peritoneal cavity, reducing adhesion risks and offering direct access to posterior tumors and hilar structures. However, CO₂ insufflation may lead to subcutaneous emphysema and increased tension at the incision site [52]. Interestingly, a recent RCT reported no significant difference in pain patterns between the two approaches, yet painkiller consumption was lower in the retroperitoneal group [53]. Meanwhile, the authors’ subgroup analysis revealed that the opioid-sparing effects of both TAP and QL blocks were more pronounced in patients undergoing transperitoneal nephrectomy, whereas those undergoing retroperitoneal surgery did not show statistically significant benefit. Specifically, in retroperitoneal cases, the CI for both blocks crossed zero, indicating a lack of clear analgesic effect. This discrepancy underscores the need for further research into technique-specific regional blocks to optimize perioperative analgesia, in alignment with ongoing efforts to refine multimodal strategies tailored to surgical context.

Non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) are theoretically associated with nephrotoxicity due to cyclooxygenase inhibition, which suppresses the synthesis of prostaglandins—key mediators of renal vasodilation. This mechanism can lead to reduced renal blood flow and a decrease in the glomerular filtration rate, prompting longstanding concerns regarding NSAID safety in nephrectomy patients [54]. However, several studies have suggested that short-term NSAID use may not significantly impair renal function, highlighting the importance of considering duration of use when evaluating associated risks [55,56].

In this network meta-analysis, only three studies consistently administered basic analgesics such as acetaminophen [17,19,23]. Similar omissions of basic analgesics have been noted in numerous RCTs evaluating regional analgesia [57,58]. This represents a critical methodological limitation, as the efficacy of regional techniques should ideally be assessed within the framework of a standardized multimodal analgesic regimen. Furthermore, none of the studies reviewed incorporated a combination of acetaminophen and NSAIDs. Given that the combination of basic analgesics plays a pivotal role in multimodal strategies and may exert synergistic effects [59–61], future trials evaluating regional blocks should adopt more consistent and comprehensive background analgesic protocols.

This study has several potential limitations. First, the number of included studies was limited, and there was substantial heterogeneity among them. Despite the deliberate restriction of the study population to RCTs involving patients undergoing laparoscopic nephrectomy, notable variations were observed in the use of non-opioid analgesics, the timing of block administration, and surgical techniques. Therefore, further analyses incorporating more robust and comprehensive evidence are needed to validate these findings [62]. Second, the authors’ analysis primarily focused on analgesic outcomes. While this was intended to provide targeted guidance on pain management strategies, future studies should aim to include a broader range of clinically relevant outcomes—such as postoperative complications and quality of recovery—to better evaluate the overall impact of regional analgesia interventions. Lastly, the authors were unable to include additional comparisons with other regional techniques [63,64], as expanding the intervention groups was not feasible due to the limited number of studies and the specific regional techniques available in this surgical context.

In conclusion, while the TAP and QL blocks provided measurable analgesic effects, their clinical relevance in laparoscopic nephrectomy appears to be limited. Although subgroup analyses suggested potential variation by surgical approach and block timing, these findings were limited by small sample sizes and inconsistent use of basic non-opioid analgesics. Further well-designed trials are needed to clarify the role of regional analgesia within optimized multimodal strategies for this procedure.
