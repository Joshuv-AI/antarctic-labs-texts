Effect of Chronic Dolutegravir Administration on the Trace Amine Profile in Wistar Rats
Authors: Natasha Henning, Tracy A. Kellermann, Carine Smith
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDolutegravir (DTG), an integrase strand inhibitor, is currently used as the first-line treatment for HIV. Despite relatively poor tissue penetration, the risk of adverse effects in metabolic and excretory systems should be considered. The trace aminergic system and trace amines are emerging as relevant role players in many chronic diseases that are commonly diagnosed but poorly understood. Trace amines are biogenic amines that are endogenously produced and can also be ingested by the intake of trace amine-rich food. Trace amines are known to differentially regulate inflammatory and neurological outcome.ObjectiveThis study investigated the effects of DTG on the trace amine profile in a wistar rat model.MethodsA total of 24 healthy wistar rats were randomly divided into four experimental groups: male and female controls and male and female DTG-treated. Blood and tissue samples were collected following a 12-week DTG administration study. Liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectroscopy (LC-MS/MS) was used to determine trace amine concentrations in urine, plasma, brain, and gastrointestinal tissue.ResultsCurrent data illustrate that polyamines differ significantly (p < 0.05) between males and females in various matrices. DTG significantly (p < 0.05) reduced jejunal tyramine and urinary synephrine levels.ConclusionData do not raise major concerns about DTG in the context of the trace amine profile. However, given the importance of the dysregulated trace amine profile in various diseased states, including HIV, current data warrant clinical investigation to further evaluate the significance of DTG-associated effects on the trace amine profile.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s40268-024-00484-4.

Introduction

Dolutegravir (DTG), an integrase strand inhibitor, is approved by the United States Food and Drug Administration (FDA) and is currently recommended as a first-line treatment for treatment-naïve and treatment-experienced HIV-infected patients [1]. Good cellular uptake has been demonstrated for DTG in in vitro models [2], but relatively poor tissue penetration was reported from in vivo models [3]. However, since DTG is largely metabolised by the liver and excreted in faecal matter and urine [4], and since the microbiomes in these compartments are major contributors to trace amine synthesis, the risk for adverse effects in these organs or compartments should be considered.

In this context, the trace aminergic system is emerging as a relevant role player in many chronic diseases that are commonly diagnosed but poorly understood. Trace amines are biogenic amines that are endogenously produced (in small amounts by the host and relatively large amounts by the microbiome) and also ingested via trace amine-rich foods [5]. Modulation of the trace amine profile has been implicated in neuropsychiatric, neurological and gastrointestinal disorders [6, 7].

Of specific relevance, although DTG is generally very well tolerated, the majority of adverse effects reported for DTG that lead to its discontinuation in a small number of patients involve the neurological and/or gastrointestinal systems [8, 9]. In the context of neurophysiology, trace amine dysregulation has been linked to neurocognitive disorders such as Alzheimer’s disease (AD) and neuropsychiatric symptomology [10, 11]. These overlap with the neurological adverse events reported by people living with HIV after treatment with DTG. Furthermore, in the context of gastrointestinal health, differential roles for different trace amines have been reported in the context of irritable bowel syndrome [12–14] and other gastrointestinal disorders [15], again pointing to adverse outcomes in the face of a dysregulated trace amine profile. Of further interest to the HIV context, our group recently demonstrated oestrogen can affect the trace amine secretion profile of gut commensals and probiotic microbes [16], which is in line with women reporting relatively more adverse effects of DTG [17, 18]. Thus, we proposed modulated trace amine signalling as a potential source of dysregulation in the context of DTG-associated adverse effects. A recent report of a dysregulated trace amine profile (altered tryptophan metabolism and increased faecal levels of phenylethylamine and polyamines) in HIV patients [19] aligns with our theory of a role for trace amines in DTG-associated adverse outcomes, but specific evidence is lacking. Thus, we evaluated the effect of chronic DTG administration (in the absence of a potential confounding effect of HIV infection itself) on the trace amine profile across different tissues in rats.

Materials and Methods

Animals

All protocols were ethically cleared by the Research Ethics Committee: Animal Care and Use (REC: ACU) of Stellenbosch University (reference number ACU-2021-22035). Animal housing and experiments were carried out in accordance with the National Institutes of Health Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals. A total of 24 healthy 7-week-old wistar rats (12 females and 12 males; average body mass 185 ± 26.6 g) were divided into two weight-matched groups, namely controls (n = 6 males, n = 6 females) and DTG-administered (n = 6 males, n = 6 females).

DTG was extracted from (50 mg) Olegra® DTG sodium tablets (Aurobindo Pharma, South Africa) and purity was confirmed at >99% using high-pressure liquid chromatography (electronic supplementary material [ESM] Methods—Extraction of dolutegravir from (50 mg) Olegra® dolutegravir sodium tablets). The final dose of DTG was administered 24 h prior to killing and endpoint sample collection. One entire (1 cm3) jelly block was hand-fed directly to each rat to ensure that the full dose was consumed.

Sample Collection

At protocol endpoint, rats were killed by sodium pentobarbital overdose (200 mg/kg, intraperitoneal injection). Ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA)-anticoagulated blood was collected via aortic puncture, centrifuged, and plasma aliquoted and stored at −20 °C until analysis. Urine was extracted directly from the bladder and stored at −20 °C until analysis. The left hemisphere of the brain, as well as the duodenum, jejunum and ileum, were excised and snap frozen in liquid nitrogen, before storage at −80 °C until analysis.

Sample Analysis

DTG concentrations were determined in plasma, as well as various body compartments. The methodology and findings in terms of DTG plasma and tissue distribution only have been presented and comprehensively discussed in a previous publication [24]. Briefly, of relevance to the current study, we demonstrated that endpoint plasma DTG concentrations ranged from 407 to 2143 ng/mL, which closely aligns with the plasma range of 662–2556 ng/mL previously reported in HIV+ humans chronically treated with DTG [25]. This accuracy of the dosing strategy employed provides support for the relevance of trace amine-related results reported in this study, which was an expanded investigation on the plasma and tissues from the same rodents reported on in our previously mentioned publication.

Reagents

Tyramine hydrochloride (T898493), phenethylamine (P321335), tryptamine (T894600), rac-synephrine (S920000), rac-octopamine hydrochloride (O239750), cadaverine (C058000), 1,4-diaminobutane (putrescine) [D416025], 3-iodothyronamine hydrochloride (I720500) and the internal standards phenethylamine-d4 (P321336) and 1,4-butane-1,1,2,2,3,3,4,4-d8-diamine-d8 (putrescine-d8) [D416027] were purchased from Toronto Research Chemicals (Toronto, ON, Canada); agmatine sulphate salt (A7127), spermidine (49761), spermine (S3256), trichloroacetic acid (TCA) and liquid chromatography-mass spectroscopy (LC-MS)-grade ammonium formate were purchased from Merck KGaA (Darmstadt, Germany); LC-MS grade acetonitrile (ACN) and methanol (MeOH) were purchased from ROMIL Ltd (Cambridge, UK); formic acid and acetic acid were purchased from Fischer Chemicals (New Hampshire, USA); and ammonium acetate was purchased from Labchem (Johannesburg, South Africa). Ultrapure water was produced from a Synergy® water purification system (Merck KGaA, Darmstadt, Germany).

Liquid Chromatography-Tandem Mass Spectroscopy Equipment

LC-MS/MS analysis was conducted on a SHIMADZU 8040 triple quadrupole-mass spectrometer (SHIMADZU, Kyoto, Japan) connected to a SHIMADZU Prominence LC system (Kyoto, Japan). The system consisted of an LC-20ADXR solvent delivery system, Nexera XR SIL-20AXR autosampler and CTO-20A column oven. The analytes were chromatographically resolved on an Agilent Poroshell 120 EC-C18 (3.0 × 100 mm, 2.7 μm) and an Agilent ZORBAX Eclipse XDB-C8 (4.6 × 150 mm, 5 μm). A multiple reaction monitoring (MRM) event was created for each analyte containing several transitions (ESM Table 1). One transition was used for quantification and a second transition was used for confirmation. The acquisition was set in positive electrospray ionisation mode. Argon was the collision-induced dissociation gas, delivered at 230 kPa. The electrospray ionisation parameters were 3 L/min nebulising gas flow, desolvation line temperature at 250 °C, heating block temperature at 400 °C, interface voltage of 4.50 kV, interface current of 11.10 µA, and 15 L/min drying gas flow.

Chromatographic Conditions

The column temperature was set at 30 °C and 10 μL of extracted sample was injected onto the column. The autosampler temperature was set at 15 °C. The needle rinse was H2O:MeOH (20:80, v/v). Two different chromatographic methods were developed due to varying polarities of the analytes under investigation.

Method 1: Mobile phase A composition was 5 mM ammonium formate and 0.1% formic acid in ultrapure water (Synergy, Merck), while mobile phase B consisted of 5 mM ammonium formate and 0.1% formic acid in MeOH:H2O (95:5, v/v). Chromatographic separation was carried out using a gradient elution program and an Agilent Poroshell 120 EC-C18 (3.0 × 100 mm, 2.7 μm) with a flow rate of 0.4 mL/min. The gradient started at 5% mobile phase B held for 0.5 min, mobile phase B was increased to 95% until 4 min and held at 95% until 5 min, reduced to 5% mobile phase B until 6 min and re-equilibrated at 5% mobile phase B for 3 min, giving a total run time of 9 min.

Method 2: Mobile phase A composition was 5 mM ammonium formate and 0.1% formic acid in ultrapure water (Synergy, Merck), while mobile phase B consisted of 5 mM ammonium formate and 0.1% formic acid in ACN:H2O (95:5, v/v). Chromatographic separation was carried out using a gradient elution program and an Agilent ZORBAX Eclipse XDB-C8 (4.6 × 150 mm, 5 μm) with a flow rate of 0.4 mL/min. The gradient started at 30% mobile phase B and was increased to 95% mobile phase B over 3 min, held at 95% until 5.5 min, reduced to 30% mobile phase B until 6.5 min and re-equilibrated for 3 min, giving a total run time of 9.5 min.

Preparation of Stock Solutions and Internal Standards

Stock solutions of 1 mg/mL were prepared by dissolving tyramine (TYR), β-phenylethylamine (PEA), tryptamine (TRP), ρ-synephrine (SYN), ρ-octopamine (OCT), 3-iodothyronamine (T1AM), putrescine (PUT) and cadaverine (CAD) in MeOH. Stock solutions of 1 mg/mL were prepared by dissolving agmatine (AGM) in H2O. The first working stock (WS) was prepared by spiking 10 μg/mL of each trace amine stock solution into MeOH:H2O (1:1, v/v) to prepare a pooled WS containing all the trace amines. Subsequent calibration standards were prepared from the WS by serial dilution (1:1) with MeOH:H2O (1:1, v/v). Three quality control (QC) samples (QC high, medium and low) were prepared in the same manner as the calibration standards. The QCs were included to verify the calibration curves. Aliquots of all WSs were stored at −80 °C. Calibration curves were prepared on the day of extraction from frozen WS. Trace amines are endogenous compounds, and matrices free of trace amines could not be sourced, therefore calibration standards and QCs were prepared in a solvent. The calibration standards were prepared by spiking a blank solvent with the trace amine WSs not exceeding 5% (v/v) of the final volume. For the quantification of trace amines in urine and plasma, the calibration standards and QCs were prepared in water. Calibration standards and QCs for tissue analysis were prepared using ACN:H2O (70:30, v/v) with 0.2% formic acid. The calibration curves prepared ranged from 1.98 to 1000 ng/mL, but specific ranges for each analyte are summarised in ESM Table 2. Internal standard stock solutions were prepared at 1 mg/mL by dissolving PEA-d4 and PUT-d8 in MeOH. An internal standard WS was prepared in MeOH containing concentrations of 1.6 μg/mL PUT-d8 and 0.4 μg/mL PEA-d4.

Preparation of Calibration Curves

Each analytical batch included calibration standards in duplicate, a blank sample (blank solvent with internal standard) and one double blank sample (only blank solvent), as well as QCs prepared in duplicate. Acceptance criteria for calibration standards and QCs within every analytical batch while analysing samples was an accuracy of between 80% and 120% with a percentage coefficient of variation (%CV) of ≤20%. Calibration curves were generated by plotting the peak area ratio of analyte to internal standard versus analyte concentration. The calibration standards were used to construct a calibration curve using a quadratic 1/C or 1/C2, where C = concentration, weighted regression. Due to limitations of the extraction method and limited sample volume that prevented further optimisation, calibrations curves could not be generated for spermidine (SPD) and spermine (SPM); thus, results achieved for these trace amines are semi-quantitative and are only included to show presence or absence in a particular matrix. Method robustness evaluation criteria and results are presented in ESM Tables 3–7).

Sample Extraction

Due to the relative novelty of our tissue trace amine analysis, we provide detailed extraction methods for the various matrices in the ESM (ESM Methods—Preparation of samples for trace amine analysis). A solid phase extraction (SPE) protocol was developed using a method previously published, with minor modifications [26]. The original method was modified by increasing the amount of internal standard spiked from 10 to 50 μL and introducing additional sample preparation before extraction to ensure sample suitability. The SPE protocol utilised the Supel-clean™ LC-WCX SPE cartridges with 100 mg bed wt. and 1 cc barrel. Briefly, the cartridges were conditioned with 1 mL MeOH followed by two 1 mL equilibration steps with 50 mM ammonium acetate pH 5.2. Calibration standards, QCs and samples were buffered with 50 mM ammonium acetate pH 5.2 (200 μL sample: 800 μL buffer) and individually spiked with 50 μL internal standard WS (PEA-d4 at 0.4 ng/mL and PUT-d8 at 1.6 μg/mL). Following sample loading, the cartridges were washed with 1 mL 5% MeOH in H2O, 1 mL 10% MeOH in H2O, and 1 mL 20% MeOH in H2O. Analytes were eluted using ACN with 5% formic acid. The eluent was evaporated to dryness under vacuum utilising the Genevac miVac Duo Sample Concentrator at 40 °C. Samples were reconstituted in 200 μL MeOH:H2O with 0.1% formic acid, vortexed vigorously and transferred to 96-well plates to be analysed using LC-MS/MS.

Data and Statistical Analysis

Data acquisition and processing was performed using LabSolutions version 5.109 (Shimadzu Corporation, Kyoto, Japan). Microsoft Excel version 16.54 (Microsoft Corporation, Redmond, WA, USA) was used to calculate the means, standard deviation (SD) and %CV. The tissue analyte concentrations obtained during LC-MS/MS analysis (ng/mL) were normalised by tissue weight in grams per millilitre homogenising solvent. CAD and PUT concentrations in the urine, duodenum and jejunum fell above the upper limit of quantification (100 ng/mL for urine and 5000 ng/g tissue) for most of the collected samples. Their concentrations should be considered as semi-quantitative as these were determined by extrapolation of the standard curve. Since the concentrations of SPD and SPM were semi-quantitative, they were excluded from statistical analysis. GraphPad Prism 9.4.1 software (GraphPad Software, Inc., San Diego, CA, USA) was used for statistical analysis. Data are presented as mean ± SD. Distribution of data was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk normality test. Statistical analyses included two-way ANOVA with Tukey’s multiple comparison test and Mann–Whitney U test for ileal trace amine concentrations. A p-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Limited or Low Abundance of Trace Amines in Rodent Plasma and Brain Tissue

The concentrations and type of trace amines detected were largely dependent on the matrix under investigation. In plasma, despite the high sensitivity of the method employed, only four trace amines were detected. TYR and TRP were detected at concentrations just below the limit of quantification (3.91 and 1.95 ng/mL, respectively). Plasma PUT concentrations ranged between 50.0 and 100 ng/mL, in line with a previous report in rodents [27]. SPD concentrations were inferred semi-quantitatively and were <2000 ng/mL. Given the extremely low abundance of trace amines in plasma [27], which is in line with literature—where plasma trace amine levels are seldom reported—these data were excluded from statistical analysis.

Of the panel selected for analysis, only three trace amines (SPD, PUT and CAD) were detected in the brain (ESM Table 8). Discussion

The use of rodents on a standardised diet negated confounding effects of dietary trace amines, which is a strength of the current study design, but species differences in microbiome composition between rodents and humans are a limitation to data interpretation. Thus, we refrained from discussing the absolute concentrations of trace amines detected, instead focusing on the relative abundance of trace amines within specific synthesis pathways. Nevertheless, the relevance of sex- or DTG-associated differences observed for specific trace amines, and the mechanisms involved, are most likely conserved across species.

Current data present novel findings in this context. First, the trace amine profile seemed relatively resistant to modulation by DTG, which is in line with its relatively good tolerance profile, although synthesis of a limited number of trace amines seemed to be similarly altered, which may have significance. Second, levels of some trace amines differed significantly between males and females in various matrices.

Trace amine concentrations vary in distinctive tissue compartments [30], likely as a result of the different trace amine-secreting microbiome in that compartment and/or the relative abundance of substrate and vital enzymatic catalysts, as was also observed in the current study. For example, the relatively sparse and low concentrations of trace amines measured in brain samples may be ascribed to the known rapid degradation of trace amines in the brain in particular [31]. Furthermore, when considering a subclass of trace amines (the polyamines), concentrations appear to be under tight homeostatic control, especially in the brain [32, 33]. This tight homeostatic control of CAD and PUT in the brain, and the lack of perturbation by DTG in the current study, suggest that DTG administration does not predispose to neurological disorders in HIV via direct action of CAD or PUT. Of course, since the small sample size necessitated a multi-analyte detection approach (and did not allow for additional runs on diluted samples), we cannot make conclusions on the potential roles of other trace amines in the brain. Follow-up studies are required to fully elucidate the effect on DTG on, for example, SPD, which was also detected in the brain.

In this study, the polyamines (AGM, CAD, PUT, SPD and SPM) were the trace amines most abundantly detected in gastrointestinal tissues. Anti-inflammatory responses have been linked to increased production of these polyamines in the gastrointestinal tract and brain [34, 35]. For example, AGM (a precursor to PUT) was reported to attenuate mucosal damage; of further interest, it was demonstrated that the protective effects declined at higher concentrations [36]. This is supported by evidence that AGM’s neuroprotective, anti-apoptotic, anti-inflammatory and anti-oxidant properties were only observed at low concentrations [37, 38]. Of relevance to this study, the metabolites produced downstream from AGM metabolism—PUT, SPM and SPD—were consistently detected. This potentially suggests that tight regulation of AGM concentrations (to prevent accumulation) could promote metabolite (PUT, SPD and SPM) formation. This notion is supported by previous data from our group, which linked disruption of tight junction protein expression and distribution profile to accumulation (lack of metabolism) of AGM after its experimental addition to (HT-29) human gut epithelial cells in culture [16], while the same effect was not observed in larval zebrafish, where AGM metabolism would be intact [14]. In this model, AGM seemed to protect from intestinal inflammation, likely due to the action of its anti-inflammatory metabolites (metabolites of AGM [PUT, SPD and SPM] have been demonstrated to accelerate epithelial cell renewal and to increase the abundance of anti-inflammatory macrophages [39]). Related to this, relatively higher levels of the AGM metabolite PUT were also observed in male versus female brains in the current study (independent of DTG treatment), which may suggest that the male brains exhibited a relatively more protective trace amine profile than the female brains, which may translate to a higher vulnerability of the female blood–brain barrier tight junction proteins, and thus also of the brain itself.

Limited and contradictory published data on basal concentration ranges of polyamines in various rat matrices meant the tissue concentrations detected in this study could not be contextualised. Nevertheless, given the known regulatory role of these trace amines in general inflammatory and redox status, the fact that concentrations of these trace amines were not significantly affected by DTG treatment is encouraging.

However, in contrast, subtle but consistent treatment effects were observed in both urine and gastrointestinal tissues. Generally, trace amine concentrations seemed slightly lower in the DTG group when compared with the control group, although only reaching statistical significance for TYR in the gut and SYN (a downstream metabolite of TYR) in urine. This trend for lower abundance was most notable in the urine and, to some extent, in the jejunum, for the trace amines TYR, PEA, TRP, AGM and CAD. In this context, it is important to note that microbes in both the urinary and gut microbiomes contribute to both trace amine synthesis and metabolism. In HIV, significant microbial dysbiosis has been described. For example, higher abundance of Enterococci and Lactobacilli genera (both prominent contributors to TYR synthesis) was recently reported in a comprehensive study on pro-inflammatory dysbiosis of microorganisms and their metabolites in HIV [19]. Although TYR and its metabolites were not specifically reported, the current data showing reduced TYR in response to DTG suggest that DTG may in fact normalise an HIV-associated increase in TYR abundance, which has been linked to poorer outcome in the context of intestinal inflammation [14]. Similarly, the same HIV study also reported higher fecal levels of l-tryptophan (precursor to TRP), as well as higher levels of CAD, PUT, SPD and PEA, in HIV+ patients versus controls. Current data on PEA, TRP and CAD again suggest a relative normalisation by DTG. Furthermore, it is important to note that trace amines and traditional monoamine neurotransmitters are synthesised from shared substates. For example, an increase in TYR or PEA synthesis from tyrosine or phenylalanine may negatively affect dopamine synthesis. Similarly, an increased TRP synthesis from tryptophan may negatively impact serotonin synthesis. Thus, in terms of the neurological adverse outcomes in HIV, it seems that these reported outcomes may be due to HIV-associated dysbiosis, and that DTG may in fact play a beneficial role in this context, rather than exacerbating neurological complications.

However, no one maladapted role player or mechanism can explain all DTG- or HIV-associated changes in trace amine levels. In addition to microbial and metabolite abundance, the balance between metabolite synthesis and metabolism needs to be considered, as well as the roles of enzymes such monoamine oxidases and (host and bacterial) decarboxylases, before firm conclusions on mechanisms may be made.

For example, a reduction in activity of the enzyme AADC, which metabolises the amino acids tyrosine, phenylalanine and tryptophan into TYR, PEA and TRP, respectively, has been reported, in the non-HIV literature, to be linked to neurological symptoms such as behavioural disorders, mood disturbances, insomnia and gastrointestinal symptoms [40], which (although not highly prevalent) are the most prominent intolerability reactions ascribed to DTG use [8, 17]. Furthermore, given the central role of AADC in cellular redox status [41], which is also dysregulated in HIV, the possibility of HIV- or DTG-induced alteration of AADC function, and thus trace amine profile, should be further elucidated in purpose-designed studies. Nevertheless, current data do not seem to support any undesired effects of DTG on the trace amine profile in the HIV context.

Sex effects were not as prominent in the archetypal trace amines (PEA, TYR, TRP) in most of the matrices investigated. Nonetheless, there were significantly higher urine concentrations of TRP in female versus male rats. This observation supports our earlier report of Lactobacilli monocultures producing more TRP in the presence of oestrogen [26]. Moreover, it has been reported that the female urinary microbiome is dominated by the Lactobacilli genus [42]. This could potentially explain the sex difference seen with greater TRP concentrations in females. Interestingly, in another study, it was demonstrated that bacterially derived TRP in female mice resulted in increased mucus production, which had a positive effect on preventing barrier disruption [43]. Given the known high incidence of gastrointestinal adverse events in people living with HIV, males in particular might thus benefit from supplementation with Lactobacilli-based probiotics. Despite detecting TRP in male ileac tissue, the smaller size of ileac tissue mass excised from females meant ileac TRP concentrations could not be determined. In addition, due to the low concentrations of TRP in the duodenum and jejunum, the effect of DTG could not be determined in either of these sections. However, given the beneficial effects of TRP, it is a positive outcome that DTG does not seem to disrupt TRP production in either males or females.

The sex differences observed for inflammation-associated trace amines (CAD, AGM and PUT) consistently demonstrated higher levels in females versus males, independent of DTG. When considered in isolation, it is not clear whether an increase in this particular combination of trace amines may suggest a relatively better or worse inflammatory status in females. The significance of these data remains to be further elucidated by assessment of inflammatory or redox parameters.

The fact that DTG dose was not adjusted for body size (to align with clinical practice) may have resulted in a relatively higher dosing of DTG to females in the current study. We have previously suggested a potential vulnerability of adipose tissue to DTG in these rodents, which we ascribed to a relative DTG overdosing in smaller-sized bodies rather than to sex per se [24]. In line with this, despite the relatively higher dose exposure of females to DTG in the current study, data do not suggest a greater risk for adverse outcome in females versus males. Thus, data support an interpretation of favourable tolerance of DTG, in terms of the trace amine system, in both males and females.

Conclusion

The current data presented expand on the available literature by illustrating concentrations of various trace amines in different wistar rat biological matrices. In the context of antiretroviral therapy (ART), despite the requirement for confirmation in a larger, human cohort, and in the presence of HIV infection, current data suggest some DTG-associated changes in the trace amine profile, but, overall, support an interpretation that DTG is unlikely to result in detrimental maladaptation of trace amine signalling. Nevertheless, given the complex interplay of multiple role players and the substantial size of the global population potentially impacted by DTG treatment, current data warrant more comprehensive assessment in appropriate models to fully elucidate the effect of DTG on the trace amine profile, as well as activity of enzymes involved in their synthesis or metabolism. In addition, we demonstrated statistically significant differences in trace amine profiles between male and female wistar rats, independent of DTG treatment. This is particularly relevant given that a dysregulated trace amine panel has been linked to several neurological and gastrointestinal disorders, both of which are reported more frequently by women using ART. Together, these data suggest that greater female incidence of these adverse symptoms may be due to a relatively less advantageous trace amine profile in females in general, rather than specific adverse reactions to DTG.
