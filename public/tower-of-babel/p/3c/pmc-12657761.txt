Multi-omics reveal beneficial effects of chrysanthemum stalks on growth performance and immune function in Hu sheep
Authors: Wanhao Cai, Yaping Xu, Wangda Cheng, Jianqiang Xu, Xiaozhong Chen, Hongbiao Fei, Xiaotao Chen, Chong Wang, Huiling Mao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractChrysanthemum stalks (CS), a plant by-product, often face underutilization and resource wastage. This study aimed to explore the effects of partially replacing peanut vines (PV) in the diet with CS on the growth performance, rumen fermentation performance and microbiota, as well as the plasma physiology, biochemistry, and metabolism of Hu sheep. Twenty-seven 6-month-old male Hu sheep (weighing 39.4 ± 0.5 kg; mean ± SD) were randomly assigned to three groups: 1) basal diet (CON); 2) CS replaced 7.88% of PV (low substitution [LS]); and 3) CS replaced 15.57% of PV (high substitution [HS]). The feeding trial lasted for 91 d. Replacement of peanut vines with chrysanthemum stalks significantly increased dry matter intake in both LS and HS groups as compared to the CON group (P = 0.021; linear effect, P = 0.050), while average daily weight gain only increased significantly in the LS group (P = 0.020; quadratic P = 0.042). The concentrations of volatile fatty acids (VFAs) and ammonia nitrogen (NH3–N) were the highest in rumen of LS group (P < 0.05; quadratic effect, P < 0.05). The LS group also had the highest relative abundance of Prevotella (P = 0.011; quadratic P = 0.001). The rumen metabolomic analysis showed elevated levels of flavonoids, terpenoids, and amino acids in the LS group (P < 0.05). Additionally, the LS group exhibited significantly higher levels of total antioxidant capacity, immunoglobulin A, and immunoglobulin M (P < 0.05; quadratic P < 0.05), while tumor necrosis factor-α and interferon-γ levels were lower (P < 0.05; quadratic P < 0.05). The plasma metabolomic analysis revealed increases in phospholipids, amino acids, and indole derivatives in the LS group (P < 0.05). In general, CS improved the growth performance of Hu sheep by increasing VFA concentrations and promoting beneficial bacteria like Prevotella in the rumen. Additionally, flavonoids and terpenoids in CS were positively associated with immune response and antioxidant capacity. These beneficial effects were more pronounced in the LS group compared to the HS group, highlighting the potential of CS as a roughage substitute for improving sheep growth performance and immune function.Keywords: Hu sheep, Chrysanthemum stalk, Growth performance, Immune function, Microbiome, Metabolome

Introduction

With the growth of the global population and the increasing demand for animal protein, feed supply faces unprecedented challenges. According to projections by the Organization for Economic Cooperation and Development and the Food and Agriculture Organization of the United Nations (OECD-FAO, 2023), global feed demand will continue to rise, which may threaten the stability of livestock production and cause fluctuations in food prices, thereby increasing market uncertainty. Consequently, seeking and developing new high-quality feed resources has become a key strategy to address this challenge. In this context, the development and utilization of by-products from economic crops as high-quality feed resources are particularly important.

Chrysanthemum is a traditional flower and medicinal herb in China with a long history of cultivation. It is valued for its ornamental, medicinal, and culinary uses (Yuan et al., 2020). In 2016, the cultivated area of chrysanthemum in China amounted to 7228 ha, and this area has been on an upward trend in recent years, with a sales value of more than 1.5 billion Chinese yuan (Chang et al., 2019; Chao, 1984). Chrysanthemum stalks (CS), as the non-medicinal part of chrysanthemums, are mostly discarded after the medicinal part is harvested, resulting in serious waste of resources and environmental pollution (Cui et al., 2023, Cui et al., 2023). Some studies have shown that CS are rich in terpenoids, flavonoids, polyphenols, polysaccharides and other active ingredients with antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and immunomodulatory biological activities (Hadizadeh et al., 2022; Peng et al., 2019). In addition, CS are rich in proteins, amino acids, fats and other nutrients, and can serve as a good non-conventional feed resource (Cui et al., 2024). Thus, utilization of CS could provide substantial economic and environmental benefits.

Evidence from non-ruminant studies has demonstrated the anti-inflammatory and antioxidant potential of CS extract. Li et al. (2022) indicated that CS extract can inhibit the expression of inflammatory cytokines in zebrafish. Additionally, CS extract has shown protective effects on the retinal function of diabetic rats and can slow down the progression of diabetes (Hu et al., 2012). These findings indicated that CS extract has strong antibacterial, anti-inflammatory, and antioxidant properties.

By contrast, studies in ruminants have provided direct evidence of its nutritional and functional benefits. Mao (2010) reported that with increasing CS feeding, the average daily gain (ADG) of lambs increased significantly, along with a significant increase in plasma catalase (CAT) activity and total antioxidant capacity (T-AOC), and a significant decrease in malondialdehyde (MDA) content. Another study of CS on beef cattle had the same findings (Hou, 2021).

As a key breed in China’s sheep industry, Hu sheep are highly valued for their meat quality and adaptability. However, the physiological responses of Hu sheep to CS supplementation remain unclear, limiting its practical application. This study applied metabolomics, microbial sequencing, and other multi-omics technologies to evaluate the effects of CS on the growth performance, immune function and antioxidant capacity of Hu sheep. By evaluating growth performance, rumen microbiota, and plasma metabolomics, we aim to determine the optimal CS inclusion level in Hu sheep diets, providing a sustainable feed strategy.

Materials and methods

Animal ethics statement

All experimental procedures were approved by the Animal Protection and Utilization Committee of Zhejiang Agriculture & Forestry University, Hangzhou, China (ZAFUAC202443, February 2024) and were in accordance with the university’s animal research rules. All experimental animals, design, and management were conducted in compliance with the “Animal Research: Reporting of In Vivo Experiments” (ARRIVE) guidelines (https://arriveguidelines.org). All efforts were made to minimize animal suffering, including the use of humane endpoints and standardized housing conditions.

Animals and experimental design

Twenty-seven male Hu sheep with a mean body weight (BW) of 39.40 ± 0.53 kg (mean ± SD) at the age of six months were randomly allotted to three groups: 1) CON, a control ration with 100% peanut vines (PV); 2) low substitution (LS), where 7.88% of PV was replaced with CS; 3) high substitution (HS), where 15.57% of PV were replaced with CS. Three sheep with similar BW within each group were placed and housed in one pen (3 m × 2 m), which was considered as an experimental replicate. The PV used in this study were sourced from rural areas of Zhengyang County, Henan, China, and the CS were obtained in October 2023 from a Chrysanthemum morifolium planting base in Tongxiang, Zhejiang. The CS were first cut into 1 to 2 cm segments using a cutter, then baled with a binding machine and wrapped in polyethylene film. Finally, they were stored in a cool, shaded indoor area at 15 to 25 °C with 50% to 70% relative humidity for long-term preservation. The chemical compositions and main biologically active components of the CS are presented in Table 1. The concentrate-to-forage ratio of the diets was 35:65 and they were formulated to meet the minimum nutrient requirements for energy, protein, minerals, and vitamins based on the NRC (2007) (Table 2). All sheep had free access to diets (fed twice daily at 08:00 and 15:00) and fresh water. The amount of feed offered and refused was recorded every day and dry matter intake (DMI) calculated, and BW was weighed on the first day and the last day. After a 7-d adaptation period, the entire feeding trial lasted for 91 d.

Sample collection and measurements

Chemical analyses of diets and feces

The dry matter (DM; method 930.15), ether extract (EE; method 920.39), crude protein (CP; method 991.20), and crude ash (method 942.05) in feces and diets were analyzed following AOAC (2019). Dry matter content was determined by drying the samples at 105 °C for 3 h in a forced-air oven (DHG-9240A, Shanghai Yiheng Scientific Instruments Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China), and all analyses were conducted on a DM basis. Ether extract content was determined using a Soxhlet extraction apparatus (BÜCHI Extraction System B-811, BÜCHI Labortechnik AG, Flawil, Switzerland). Nitrogen content in feces and diets was determined by the Kjeldahl method, and CP content was calculated as nitrogen × 6.25. Crude ash content was determined by combusting the samples at 550 °C for 4 h in a muffle furnace (SX2-4-10A, Shanghai Jinghong Laboratory Instrument Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China). Organic matter (OM) content was calculated as DM minus crude ash. Neutral detergent fiber (NDF) and acid detergent fiber (ADF) contents were determined according to the procedure described by Van Soest et al. (1991).

Sampling and metabolomics analysis of CS

A five-point sampling scheme was used, collecting 200 g from each of five CS bales to form a composite sample. This procedure was repeated to generate six independent replicates. Before untargeted metabolomics analysis, each replicate was gradually thawed at 4 °C, followed by extraction of 100 mg sample with 1 mL of pre-chilled methanol-acetonitrile-water solution (2:2:1, v/v). The sample was centrifuged at 18,000 × g for 20 min at 4 °C, and the supernatant was dried at 25 °C for 16 h using a vacuum concentrator (SPD140P2, Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc., Waltham, MA, USA). The dried sample was then re-dissolved in 100 μL of acetonitrile/water (1:1, v/v) solvent, and centrifuged again at 18,000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was collected and analyzed using ultra-performance liquid chromatography (UHPLC) (Agilent 1290; Agilent Technologies Inc., Santa Clara, CA, USA) coupled to a quadrupole time-of-flight (AB Triple TOF 6600; AB Sciex, Framingham, CA, USA) for liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) analysis to identify the metabolites and expression level in CS. Metabolites were separated on a C18 column (2.1 × 100 mm, 1.8 μm) maintained at 40 °C, with a flow rate of 0.4 mL/min and an injection volume of 2 μL. The mobile phase consisted of solvent A (water with 25 mM ammonium acetate and 0.5% formic acid) and solvent B (methanol). The gradient program was as follows: 0 to 0.5 min, 5% solvent B; 0.5 to 10 min, linear increase to 100% solvent B; 10.0 to 12.0 min, 100% solvent B; 12.0 to 12.1 min, linear decrease to 5% solvent B; and 12.1 to 16 min, 5% solvent B. Metabolites were analyzed in both in both positive and negative electrospray modes with information-dependent acquisition (m/z 100–1200) (Zhang et al., 2025). Calculation of the relative proportion of biologically active components was based on the ratio of the expression level of each metabolite to the total expression level of all metabolites (Applied Protein Technology Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China).

Rumen fermentation parameters

At the end of the experiment, two sheep of each replicate were slaughtered. Sheep were humanely euthanized via captive bolt stunning followed by exsanguination, in compliance with the Animal Epidemic Prevention Law of the People’s Republic of National People’s Congress of the People’s Republic of China (2021) and institutional guidelines (ZAFUAC202443). The rumen contents were strained through four layers of compressed gauze to obtain the rumen fluid, and the pH value of the rumen fluid was determined using a pH meter (Ph818M, Hong Kong Smart Sensor Ltd., Hong Kong, China). The rumen fluid was stored at −80 °C until further analysis. To 1 mL of rumen fluid, 200 μL of 25% metaphosphoric acid was added and mixed by manually vortexing. The mixture was centrifuged at 12,000 × g at 4 °C for 10 min. The supernatant was subjected to gas chromatography (Agilent 7890B) equipped with a flame ionization detector (FID) to determine the concentrations of volatile fatty acids (VFAs). Separation was performed on a DB-FFAP capillary column (30 m × 0.25 mm × 0.25 μm; Agilent Technologies). Nitrogen was used as the carrier gas at a flow rate of 30 mL/min. The injector and detector temperatures were set at 260 °C, and the column oven was maintained at 220 °C (Hu et al., 2005). Both ammonia nitrogen (NH3–N) concentration and microbial protein (MCP) content were determined using a microplate reader (Powerwave XS, BioTek Instruments, Winooski, VT, USA). NH3–N concentration was determined according to the colorimetric method described by Verdouw et al. (1978), which is based on the phenol–hypochlorite reaction that produces an indophenol blue complex measurable at 700 nm. Finally, MCP content was assessed via the purine method (Zinn and Owens, 1986), in which MCP yield was calculated from RNA content.

Nutrient apparent digestibility

Fecal samples were collected from each sheep at 03:00, 09:00, 15:00, and 21:00 daily during a 3-d (49–51 d) collection period, based on previous studies showing a stable digestibility following a 7-week adaptation period (Farenzena et al., 2017). Fecal samples were pooled in equal amounts by weight and dried at 65 °C for 72 h prior to nutrient digestibility analysis. The collection and processing of feed samples were conducted using the same protocol as for fecal samples. The nutrient digestibility was determined using the acid insoluble ash (AIA) method. Briefly, the samples were ground to allow passage through a 40-mesh sieve (425 μm; Royalstar Electronic Appliance Group Co., Ltd., Hefei, Anhui, China). The AIA, an internal marker for determination of apparent nutrient digestibility, was analyzed according to Van Keulen and Young (1977). Determination of rumen microorganisms

Total DNA of the rumen fluid samples were extracted by utilizing the E.Z.N.A. Soil DNA kit (Cat. No. D5625–01; Omega Bio-Tek Inc., Norcross, GA, USA) and DNA quality was verified by A260/A280 ratio (1.8-2.0) via Nanodrop. The V3–V4 regions of the bacterial 16S rRNA gene were amplified employing the forward primer 338F (5′-ACTCCTACGGGAGGCAGCAG-3′) and the reverse primer 806R (5′-GGACTACHVGGGTWTCTAAT-3′). PCR amplification was carried out with the following cycling conditions: 95 °C for 3 min; 30 cycles of 95 °C for 30 s, 55 °C for 30 s, and 72 °C for 45 s; and a final extension at 72 °C for 5 min. The PCR products were quantified with the Quant-iTPicoGreen dsDNA Assay kit (Cat. No. P7589; Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA). The amplicon libraries were generated by pooling in an equal ratio and Sequencing was performed on Illumina MiSeq platform (Majorbio Bio-Pharm Technology Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China).

Microbiota bioinformatics analysis was conducted using QIIME 2 (Bolyen et al., 2019). The DADA2 denoise-paired method was applied to process the sequences by quality filtering, denoising, merging, and removing chimeras. Reads with an average Q-score <20, ambiguous bases, or length <200 bp were discarded, and chimeras were removed during the denoising step. After quality control, paired-end 2 × 300 bp sequencing generated >45,000 reads per sample, which were used for downstream analysis. Amplicon sequence variants (ASVs) were clustered by closed-reference operational taxonomic units (OTUs) picking at 97% similarity using the Greengenes 16S reference database (gg_2022). Alpha diversity metrics, including Shannon (1948), Simpson (1949), Chao1 (Chao, 1984), were calculated using QIIME 2. The similarity of microbial community structures among samples was assessed using principal coordinates analysis (PCoA) based on the weighted UniFrac distance algorithm (Lozupone et al., 2007). Analysis of similarities (ANOSIM) was performed to evaluate the significance of microbial community differences among groups, and the Wilcoxon rank-sum test was used to compare community differences among groups, with P-value adjusted for multiple testing using the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) correction.

Analysis of rumen fluid and plasma metabolomics

Metabolites were extracted from rumen fluid and plasma as the method described by Zhang et al. (2024) and the prepared samples were used for LC-MS analysis. The LC-MS/MS analysis was performed using a Thermo Fisher Scientific UHPLC-Q Exactive HF-X system equipped with an ACQUITY HSS T3 column (100 mm × 2.1 mm, 1.8 μm; Waters Corp., Milford, MA, USA) at Majorbio Bio-Pharm Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Raw peak extraction, database filtering and baseline calibration, peak alignment, deconvolution analysis, peak identification and peak area integration were processed and analysed using Progenesis QI (Waters Corp.) software (Miao et al., 2024). Metabolite identification was performed by querying the Majorbio database and in addition the data were analyzed via the Majorbio cloud platform (https://cloud.majorbio.com).

The R package “ropls” (Version 1.6.2) was then utilized for orthogonal partial least squares discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA), along with a 7-cycle interactive validation to assess the model’s stability. Metabolites with a variable importance in the projection (VIP) score greater than 1 and a P-value less than 0.05 were identified as significantly different metabolites, based on the VIP scores from the OPLS-DA model and P-value from Student’s t-test. Differential metabolites between LS and CON groups were mapped into their biochemical pathways through metabolic enrichment and pathway analysis based on the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) database (http://www. genome.jp/kegg/). The receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve was plotted with the true positive rate (sensitivity) on the y-axis and the false discovery rate (1-specificity) on the x-axis. An area under the curve (AUC) value greater than 0.8 was considered indicative of a potential biomarker associated with CS feeding.

Results

Growth performance

As shown in Table 3, partial replacement of PV in the diet with CS significantly increased the DMI in Hu sheep (P = 0.021), with a linear trend observed (P = 0.050). In addition, increasing the replacement of PV with CS resulted in a quadratic increase in the final BW (P = 0.008) and the ADG (P = 0.042), with both indices being highest in the LS group (P < 0.05). However, the feed conversion ratio (FCR) in the HS group was higher than those in the other two groups (P = 0.011), with a linear increase in FCR (P = 0.006).

Rumen fermentation parameters

Although there was no significant difference in the NH3–N in the rumen of the HS group, its MCP content was significantly higher than that of the CON group (P = 0.006). Nutrient apparent digestibility

Partial replacement of PV in the diet with CS had a significant effect on the apparent digestibility of Hu sheep. Interestingly, increasing CS replacement of PV quadratically increased all the apparent digestibility indicators (P < 0.05) (Table 5). Compared with the CON group, the apparent digestibility of DM, OM, CP, NDF, and ADF in the LS group were increased by 3.02%, 3.52%, 1.95%, 4.19% and 4.48% (P < 0.05).

Ruminal microbial community characteristics

There were no significant differences in alpha diversity indices of rumen microbial community among the three groups (P > 0.05, Table S1). Principal coordinates analysis based on the weighted_unifrac distance algorithm showed that the bacterial community was clearly separated between LS and CON groups (P = 0.047, Fig. 1A), but not between HS and CON groups (P = 0.877, Fig. 1B). At the phylum level, the dominant phyla were Bacteroidota and Firmicutes (Fig. 1D). The relative abundance of Bacteroidota increased quadratically with increasing levels of CS replacement (P = 0.009), whereas the relative abundance of Firmicutes decreased quadratically (P = 0.012). The relative abundances of Bacteroidota (Wilcoxon rank-sum test P = 0.050) and Firmicutes (Wilcoxon rank-sum test P = 0.062) exhibited a trend toward differential abundance (Table 6). Furthermore, the Firmicutes to Bacteroidota ratio (F:B ratio) of the LS group was the lowest (P = 0.044, Fig. 1C). At the genus level, the dominant genera were Prevotella, Rikenellaceae_RC9_gut_group, norank_f__F082, Succiniclasticum, Christensenellaceae_R-7_group (Fig. 1F). The relative abundance of Prevotella increased quadratically with increasing levels of CS replacement (P = 0.001). Differential bacteria in genus level were Prevotella, the relative abundance of Prevotella (Wilcoxon rank-sum test P = 0.011) was the highest in the LS group; the relative abundances of Rikenellaceae_RC9_gut_group (Wilcoxon rank-sum test P = 0.075) and unclassified_f__Prevotellaceae (Wilcoxon rank-sum test P = 0.096) also exhibited a trend toward differential abundance (Table 6). Based on Spearman’s co-occurrence network analysis, it was observed that the LS group (Fig. 1G) has a denser network with more edges compared to the other two groups (Fig. 1F and H), indicating potentially more complex inter-colony interactions within the LS group.

Analysis of ruminal metabolomics

According to the above results, the sheep in the LS group had better growth performance, rumen fermentation efficiency, and apparent digestibility. Therefore, a further ruminal metabolomic analysis was performed between the LS and CON groups. A total of 1969 metabolites were detected in both of the LS and CON groups. According to the results of OPLS-DA (R2Y = 0.998, cumulative Q2 = 0.434), a significant difference in rumen metabolites between the two groups was presented (Fig. 2A). Then, ruminal differential metabolites were screened by P-value (P < 0.05), VIP (VIP ≥1), and fold change (FC ≥ 1 or FC ≤ 0.5). A total of 354 rumen differential metabolites were detected, of which 302 were up-regulated metabolites and 51 were down-regulated metabolites (Fig. 2B). Metabolic pathway analysis based on significantly different rumen metabolites revealed the enrichment of six metabolic pathways (Fig. 2C), such as “pyrimidine metabolism”, “nucleotide metabolism”, “phenylalanine, tyrosine and tryptophan biosynthesis”, “valine, leucine and isoleucine biosynthesis”, “valine, leucine and isoleucine degradation”, and “aminoacyl-tRNA biosynthesis”. A total of 14 differential metabolites were identified across enriched KEGG metabolic pathways (Table S2), based on the “HMDB Class” classification, the main categories include pyrimidine nucleotides (deoxycytidine monophosphate [dCMP], thymidine, deoxyuridine), purine nucleotides (2′-deoxyadenosine 5′-phosphate [dAMP], xanthosine, deoxyinosine), keto acids and derivatives (acetoacetic acid, prephenate), carboxylic acids and derivatives (L-isoleucine, L-valine), as well as L-tryptophan, 2-isopropylmalic acid (2-IA), orotic acid, and 4-hydroxyphenylpyruvic acid (4-HA), which belong to the classes of indoles and derivatives, fatty acyls, diazines, and benzene and substituted derivatives, respectively. Additionally, a variety of flavonoids and terpenoids were identified among the 354 detected metabolites, all of which had AUC scores greater than 0.8 in the ROC analysis, indicating their potential use as biomarkers for CS-fed animals (Table S3).

Relationships between rumen microorganisms, rumen metabolome, and plasma metabolome, and their interpretation of LS and CON groups phenotypes

To understand the interrelationships between the screened rumen metabolites, plasma metabolites, rumen microorganisms and phenotypic indicators of Hu sheep, Spearman analysis was carried out among them. Analysis of the rumen microbiome revealed that Prevotella was the most abundant genus in the rumen and the only genus among the top ten in terms of relative abundance to exhibit a significant change in abundance. As illustrated in Fig. 4 (Table S6) and Fig. 5 (Table S7), the correlation between the rumen microbiome and phenotypic indices revealed several significant positive relationships involving the Prevotella. Prevotella was positively correlated with ADG and DMI in growth performance, all indices of apparent digestibility, acetate and isobutyrate in rumen fermentation indices, ALP and AST in plasma liver function indices, as well as IgM in plasma immunity indices. Additionally, a significant negative correlation was observed between Prevotella and TNF-α. The correlation analysis between the rumen metabolome and phenotypic indices revealed strong associations between DMI, liver function indices, antioxidant indices, and immune indices in Hu sheep with various differential metabolites in the rumen. Additionally, the apparent digestibility indices and rumen fermentation indices correlated strongly with 2-isopropylmalonic acid as well as flavonoids differential metabolites and terpenoids differential metabolites in the rumen. Correlations between plasma metabolomes and phenotypic indices revealed strong associations between apparent digestibility indices, liver function indices, antioxidant and immune indices, and various plasma differential metabolites. Significant correlations were observed between DMI and metabolites, which excluded glycerophospholipids in Hu sheep. Rumen fermentation indices showed significant correlations with AA, CDP-ethanolamine, as well as organic sulfuric acids and derivatives. Correlations between Prevotella and both rumen and plasma metabolomes revealed significant correlations between Prevotella and 2-IA, flavonoids and terpenoids in the rumen. Prevotella exhibited significant correlations with L-isoleucine, LAB, 5-hydroxyindoleacetic acid, N-epsilon-acetyl-L-lysine, 2-hydroxycinnamic acid, P-coumaraldehyde, AA and CDP-ethanolamine, as well as glycerophospholipids in the plasma. In addition, plasma metabolites of flavonoids and terpenoids—such as 5-hydroxytryptophol sulfate, 5-(3′,5′-dihydroxyphenyl)-gamma-valerolactone sulfate, P-coumaraldehyde, and 2-hydroxycinnamic acid—showed significant correlation with apparent digestibility, rumen fermentation parameters, antioxidant and immune parameters.

Discussions

Growth performance

The chemical composition of CS includes beneficial components, such as flavonoids, terpenoids, and polyphenols, as well as a variety of alcohols, ketones, and aldehydes. These components contribute to the formation of the desired flavor profile, and these flavor compounds can enhance DMI by improving palatability (Kaneko et al., 2017; Ma et al., 2011). In the current study, dietary substitution of CS increased the DMI of the sheep, which might be due to the flavoring compounds in CS potentially enhancing the palatability of the diets and increasing the Hu sheep’s willingness to feed. However, further research is needed to confirm the direct impact of flavor compounds on DMI in this context. Mao (2010) found that using chrysanthemum stalks as a partial replacement for rice straw in the diets of weaned lamb increased the final BW and ADG. However, the final BW and ADG were not changed in the HS group, while the FCR was significantly higher than those in the other two groups in this study. Mirzaei-Alamouti et al. (2021) reported that the reduction of NDF content in lamb’s diet from 270 to 220 g/kg DM decreased the FCR, but did not affect the final BW and ADG. The NDF and ADF contents of CS were higher than those of PV, and high-fiber feeds increase the volume of rumen contents, which may lead to a greater sense of satiety in ruminants (Dado and Allen, 1995). If the high-fiber diet exceeds the fiber degradation capacity of the rumen of Hu sheep, it will cause digestive burden and reduce the absorption efficiency of nutrients (Buxton and Redfearn, 1997; Hao et al., 2021). Previous research has demonstrated that bioactive compounds derived from plants, when administered at high concentrations, can exert detrimental effects on ruminant metabolism, potentially increasing the burden on the digestive system and impairing nutrient absorption efficiency (Jaiswal et al., 2020). Chronic exposure to elevated doses of plant secondary metabolites, such as flavonoids and terpenoids, may induce toxicity, inhibiting the activity of ruminal microorganisms and ultimately compromising the growth performance and productivity of ruminants (Formato et al., 2022; Tedeschi et al., 2021). However, a limitation of this study was the small numbers of pens used as the experimental unit for growth performance assessment, which may reduce statistical power. Future trials should include more pens per treatment to enhance reliability. Furthermore, although sheep in the HS group had higher DMI, their growth performance was limited, potentially due to restricted fiber digestion and the consumption of high levels of plant bioactive compounds.

Rumen fermentation parameters

There was no difference in rumen pH among the groups, and the rumen pH of all groups was higher than the threshold value for subacute ruminal acidosis (pH > 5.6) as defined by Ramos et al. (2021). Thus, replacing PV with CS did not cause abnormal rumen fermentation. NH3–N levels can indicate the degradation of feed protein in the rumen and the utilization of NH3–N by microorganisms (Zeng et al., 2023). NH3–N levels were significantly higher in the LS, while no significant change was observed in the HS group. However, MCP levels were significantly higher in both groups. These findings were align with Mao (2010), suggesting that CS can promote the conversion of NH3–N to MCP in the rumen. The higher CP content in CS compared to PV contributed to the enhanced production of rumen MCP (Zhang et al., 2020). Additionally, the crude protein digestibility (CPD) of LS group was significantly higher, which further elevated NH3–N and a decrease in the efficiency of nitrogen utilization in the rumen. Taken together, these factors led to an overall decrease in nitrogen efficiency in the LS group.

Acetate, propionate, and butyrate are the main products of rumen fermentation and important energy sources for ruminants (Bergman, 1990). In this study, the concentrations of TVFA, acetate, propionate, and butyrate in the rumen of the LS group were significantly elevated, while no significant differences were observed in the HS group. The increased DMI in LS group likely contributed to a higher supply of fermentable substrates in the rumen (Paddick et al., 2019). Additionally, the increased abundance of Prevotella in the rumen improved the degradation efficiency of protein and starch (Zhang et al., 2024). These factors collectively led to the concentration of more VFA in the LS group. However, the VFA concentration in the HS group did not significantly increase, which may be related to the overloading of fiber intake and insufficient NH3–N in the rumen (Zhao et al., 2013). The growth of fiber-degrading bacteria depends on adequate NH3–N (Gleason et al., 2022). In the HS group, as the fiber intake increased, the limited NH3–N might have restricted bacterial activity and reduced VFA concentrations (Bedford et al., 2020).

Nutrient apparent digestibility

The dry matter digestibility (DMD), organic matter (OMD), neutral detergent fiber digestibility (NDFD), acid detergent fiber digestibility (ADFD), and CPD in the LS group was significantly higher than those in the CON group. The increase of apparent digestibility was closely related to the increase of DMI, and the correlation analyses also confirmed the positive correlation between DMI and apparent digestibility indicators. Furthermore, flavonoids, terpenoids and other active compounds in the rumen of the LS group were positively correlated with the apparent digestibility, which may be derived from CS. Many studies (Chen et al., 2015, Chen et al., 2015; Dorantes-Iturbide et al., 2022; Sun et al., 2017; Yang et al., 2007) reported that flavonoids and terpenoids extracted from chrysanthemums significantly enhanced the apparent digestibility of the animals. Higher DMI was positively correlated with increased bioactive uptake, enzyme secretion, and gut motility, providing associative insights into the improved nutrient digestion observed (Chen et al., 2023). However, the apparent digestibility exhibited a quadratic relationship with increasing CS replacement levels. This phenomenon may be related to a compensatory mechanism in the body’s metabolism of bioactive compounds such as flavonoids and terpenoids (Aoi et al., 2021). The excessive intake of bioactive compounds in the HS group may have exceeded the metabolic capacity, potentially limiting their beneficial effects on digestibility. The moderate fiber content in the LS group supported the high apparent digestibility by providing adequate rumen fill without overwhelming its digestive capacity. This balance enhanced energy supply efficiency and sustained the activity and population of fiber-degrading bacteria (Krause et al., 2003; Lechartier and Peyraud, 2010). However, de Souza et al. (2018) found that animals can fully digest fiber to meet their energy requirements when DMI is reduced, which is inconsistent with the results of the present study. This discrepancy may be due to the differences in fiber structure and the presence of antinutritional factors between CS and PV, as well as the digestive-promoting active ingredients in CS (Reed et al., 1990; Smeriglio et al., 2017). In contrast, apparent digestibility in the HS group did not change significantly, possibly due to the higher fiber content in their diet. High-fiber diets require more mechanical and chemical digestive processes, which consume additional energy for mastication, rumination, and microbial fermentation. This increased unproductive energy expenditure reduces the actual efficiency of feed utilization (Cao et al., 2021; Sari et al., 2015). However, the digestibility estimates were based on just 72 h of fecal collection, whereas Sun et al. (2017) recommended sampling for at least 7 days in 6 animals. Therefore, this shorter sampling period should be considered a limitation.

Ruminal microbial community

At the phylum level, the relative abundance of Bacteroidota in the LS group quadratically increased, while Firmicutes quadratically decreased. Bacteroidota are typically more abundant in diets with higher proportions of roughage or coarse fiber (Cui et al., 2023, Cui et al., 2023), which is consistent with the findings of this study. Chen et al. (2016) indicated that Firmicutes dominate under low organic load conditions, whereas the relative abundance of Bacteroidota significantly increases at the onset of organic overload. The significant increase in OMD in the LS group may explain the observed changes in the relative abundances of Bacteroidota and Firmicutes. However, the abundance trends of Firmicutes and Bacteroidota in the HS group reversed as the CS substitution level increased. This may be due to the specific antimicrobial effects of flavonoids, which promote Firmicutes while inhibiting Bacteroidota (Perestrelo et al., 2019). Higher flavonoid intake in the HS group likely increased Firmicutes abundance and suppressed Bacteroidota. Despite the higher fiber intake in the HS group, the modulation of microbiome structure by flavonoids resulted in the observed balance between these two phyla, potentially overriding the expected fiber-driven shifts.

At the genus level, the relative abundance of Prevotella was significantly higher in the LS group. Spearman correlation analysis further revealed its central role in enhancing growth performance, digestive efficiency, ruminal fermentation, hepatic function, and immune responses. Prevotella plays an important role in the degradation and assimilation of proteins and polysaccharides, as well as in the concentration of VFAs (Jami et al., 2013). The significant increases in CPD, OMD, and VFA levels in the LS group observed in this study further support this viewpoint. The differential metabolites in the rumen were rich in flavonoids, terpenoids, and amino acids, which have been shown to increase the relative abundance of Prevotella through amino acid metabolism (Xue et al., 2020), flavonoids (Liu et al., 2023), and terpenoids (Poudel et al., 2019), aligning with the findings of this study. In the HS group, the relative abundance of Rikenellaceae_RC9_gut_group tended to increase, whereas unclassified_f__Prevotellaceae tended to decrease. This microbial shift may reflect competitive interactions in the fiber-rich environment, where Rikenellaceae_RC9_gut_group demonstrates superior substrate utilization efficiency, thereby outcompeting unclassified_f_Prevotellaceae (Qiu et al., 2019).

Ruminal metabolomics

Variations in rumen metabolite levels affect both rumen function and host health (Ametaj et al., 2010). Metabolomic analysis revealed significant differences in rumen metabolites between the LS and CON groups, specifically in amino acids, nucleotides, flavonoids, and terpenoids. Crude protein is broken down by microorganisms in the rumen into small peptides and amino acids (Abbasi et al., 2018). These amino acids play a crucial role in the synthesis of proteins and peptides (Mariz et al., 2018). The significantly higher CPD observed in the LS group was also associated with notable alterations in ruminal amino acid metabolism. L-Tryptophan, an essential amino acid, serves as a key precursor for protein synthesis by rumen microbes (Mohammed et al., 2003). L-Tryptophan and its metabolites, such as 5-hydroxytryptamine (5-HT), regulate feeding behavior, immune responses, and nervous system functions (Huether et al., 2012; Miao et al., 2019; Zhao et al., 2019), which is consistent with the findings of this study. Prephenate is a precursor to the biosynthesis of phenylalanine and tyrosine, which is converted to phenylpyruvate by prephenate acid dehydratase, and then leads to the production of L-phenylalanine (Mahlstedt et al., 2010). Additionally, prephenate can be converted into 4-HA via prephenate dehydrogenase. In tyrosine biosynthesis, 4-HA is an important intermediate in tyrosine metabolism, representing a crucial step (Schwinck and Adams, 1959). Correlation analysis revealed a significant positive association among prephenate, ruminal NH3–N, and MCP. This suggests that elevated NH3–N may support phenylalanine biosynthesis by providing additional nitrogen, thereby potentially promoting the microbial synthesis of both phenylalanine and tyrosine. However, the specific mechanism underlying the positive coordination among NH3–N, MCP, and prephenate remains unclear. Thus, feeding CS can increase substrates for microbial protein synthesis and enhance phenolic compounds and other aromatic amino acid derivatives by rumen microbes. This may contribute to the antioxidant capacity and immune regulation of animals.

L-Isoleucine and L-valine are branched-chain amino acids (BCAAs) and essential amino acids. In the rumen, they can be metabolized into VFAs, which provide the animal with energy (Dehority et al., 1958). L-Isoleucine and L-valine are converted into intermediates such as pyruvate and succinyl-coenzyme A (CoA) during the BCAA degradation, which then enter the tricarboxylic acid (TCA cycle) (Van Den Hende et al., 1963). This was further supported by the positive correlation of these amino acids with butyrate. In addition, L-isoleucine showed a significant positive correlation with CPD in the present study, thus suggesting that these BCAAs promote fiber digestion and protein synthesis, playing a crucial role in maintaining the metabolic balance of rumen microbes (Van Den Hende et al., 1963). 2-isopropylmalic acid is a key intermediate in the leucine biosynthesis pathway. It is synthesized from acetyl-CoA and α-ketoisovalerate through the catalytic action of α-isopropylmalate synthase, an enzyme whose activity is regulated by leucine through negative feedback (Nie et al., 2018). 2-isopropylmalic acid shows a significant positive correlation with both NH3–N and CPD. A higher NH3–N content helps provide an adequate nitrogen source for protein synthesis, while a higher CPD contributes to improving the efficiency of nitrogen utilization. The positive correlation among these three factors promotes the biosynthesis of leucine, thereby accelerating the body’s energy metabolism. Acetoacetic acid is a key ketone body produced during the degradation of BCAA. It plays a crucial role in ketogenesis and is converted into acetyl-CoA, and this acetyl-CoA then enters the TCA cycle to provide energy (Youm et al., 2015). Interestingly, both the biosynthesis and degradation pathways of BCAAs were significantly enriched in this study. These results suggested that CS supplementation prompts rumen microbes to regulate BCAA metabolism to meet the demands of protein and energy. Therefore, CS can significantly increase the CPD in Hu sheep, thereby improving the amino acid metabolism in the rumen. This enhanced amino acid metabolism interacts with higher levels of NH3–N, further promoting the production of MCP. The KEGG pathway analysis indicated that the addition of CS affected pyrimidine and nucleotide metabolism. The increased availability of nucleic acids in the rumen may indicate greater availability of MCP (Leng and Nolan, 1984), which is in line with the findings of this study. Rodríguez et al. (2000) demonstrated that increased DMI significantly elevates the nucleic acid content in the rumen, which aligns with the positive correlation between nucleic acids and DMI observed in this study. The upregulation of nucleic acid metabolites may be attributed to the higher CP content in CS. High nitrogen diets can increase the content of nucleic acid in rumen fluid (McAllan and Smith, 1969), so as to promote the rapid proliferation and metabolism of rumen microbes. This microbial activity further elevated the nucleic acid content in the rumen. The mechanism of dCMP and dAMP downregulation remains unclear, but it may be related to DNA synthesis requirements in rumen microbes or to components in dCMP and dAMP metabolism (Mantsavinos and Canellakis, 1959).

In the current study, substantial amounts of flavonoids and terpenoids were detected in the rumen. Similarly, Yu et al. (2023) found various flavonoid compounds in the rumen of dairy cows after supplementing with citrus flavonoid extract. Flavonoids and terpenoids have known antimicrobial properties and can reduce the activities of methanogen and pathogenic bacteria in the rumen (Cushnie and Lamb, 2005; Mahizan et al., 2019). This reduction in methane production could improve the rumen fermentation efficiency and direct more carbon to propionate and other VFAs (Olagaray and Bradford, 2019). The positive correlations among flavonoids, terpenoids, and rumen fermentation parameters observed in this study were consistent with these effects. Furthermore, these compounds not only reduce harmful bacteria but also increase the relative abundance of beneficial bacteria. For instance, Prevotella abundance demonstrated significant positive correlations with both flavonoid and terpenoid concentrations, suggesting a potential mechanistic link between these phytochemicals and improved apparent digestibility. Flavonoids may improve digestive efficiency by neutralizing ruminal free radicals, mitigating epithelial oxidative stress, and stabilizing the microbial ecosystem, thereby enhancing nutrient absorption (Liu et al., 2020). Using combined criteria (P < 0.05, VIP >1, AUC >0.8) effectively identifies flavonoid and terpenoid metabolites as potential CS biomarkers, but the lack of external validation may lead to overfitting. Therefore, these findings should be considered exploratory.

Plasma physiology and biochemistry indicators

Total antioxidant capacity is a key biomarker for assessing oxidative stress status and health risks (Vona et al., 2019). In this study, T-AOC levels in the LS group were significantly elevated, whereas SOD levels were markedly reduced. The antioxidant components in CS, such as polyphenols and flavonoids, effectively enhanced the body’s ability to combat oxidative stress (Duh et al., 1999; Orzuna–Orzuna et al., 2023). These components may enhance overall antioxidant capacity by scavenging free radicals in the bloodstream, thereby reducing oxidative stress and cellular damage (Pérez-Torres et al., 2021). However, the observed decrease in SOD activity, despite the overall increase in antioxidant capacity, seems paradoxical given that SOD represents the first line of defense in the body’s antioxidant system (Ighodaro and Akinloye, 2018). The contrasting trends between T-AOC and SOD levels suggested that CS may regulate systemic antioxidant pathways, potentially activating other antioxidant mechanisms in the LS group to compensate for reduced SOD activity. CS extracts can influence the expression and activity of antioxidant enzymes through complex biochemical pathways (Li et al., 2022). Future studies will be needed to determine the activities of other antioxidant enzymes and examine the expression of related genes to elucidate the underlying mechanisms.

IgA and IgM are essential for mucosal and humoral immunity, and elevated levels generally indicate an enhanced response to antigenic stimulation (Chen et al., 2020). In this study, the levels of IgA and IgM in the LS group were significantly increased, indicating that feeding CS could enhance the immune defense ability of Hu sheep. This enhancement may be due to the fact that the polysaccharides in CS may promote the synthesis and release of immunoglobulins by activating immune cells or strengthening intestinal barrier function (Han et al., 2021; Tao et al., 2018). Furthermore, the reduction of inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α and IFN-γ, which are typically upregulated in the inflammatory responses, indicates that the inflammatory state of the body has been alleviated (Shen et al., 2018). TNF-α and IFN-γ were significantly reduced in the LS group, suggesting that CS has anti-inflammatory properties, which is consistent with other studies (Zhang et al., 2019).

Although the AST, ALT, and ALP levels in the LS and HS groups were significantly higher, the values were within the reference range for liver function in sheep as defined by the Laboklin laboratory in Germany (https://laboklin.com/en/specialist-information/reference-values). The positive correlation between liver markers and rumen flavonoids and terpenoids suggests that this increase may be due to liver metabolism of these compounds, transported from the rumen via the bloodstream, which is consistent with findings of metabolic stress from plant extracts (Lepczyński et al., 2017; Kalantar, 2018; Zhang et al., 2022). Due to their short-term physiological variability (Picotte et al., 2009), plasma parameters may limit the interpretation of their association with apparent digestibility when samples are collected at different time points. The decline in total protein in the HS group may result from anti-nutritional factors in CS that impair protein digestion and absorption. The high CS proportion may have exceeded the tolerance threshold for Hu sheep and reduced the total protein levels.

Plasma metabolomics

Plasma metabolomics provide valuable insights into an animal’s growth performance, feed conversion efficiency, and overall health status (Toral et al., 2023). GPCho (18:1/16:1) and PC (16:0/18:1[11Z]) are both classified as lecithin, and serve as important precursors for the synthesis of AA. Arachidonic acid and its metabolites play key roles in enhancing immune responses and promoting the resolution of inflammation (Das, 2018). The positive correlation between AA and plasma immune indicators in this study also supports this viewpoint. The upregulation of AA and the downregulation of GPCho (18:1/16:1) and PC (16:0/18:1[11Z]) may reflect a potential mechanism through which CS-derived flavonoids and terpenoids influence phospholipid A2 metabolism. This enzyme activity promotes the release of AA from the sn-2 position of these phospholipid molecules (Joshi et al., 2016). The differential metabolites detected in glycerophospholipid metabolism were categorized into five types: 1-acyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphocholine, lecithin, CDP-ethanolamine, PE, and phosphatidyl-N-dimethylethanolamine (PDME). Among these, CDP-ethanolamine and 1-acyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphocholine were significantly upregulated, while PE, PDME, and lecithin were markedly downregulated. 1-Acyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphocholine is known to activate a variety of immune cells, and its upregulation can alleviate oxidative stress by increasing the expression of intracellular antioxidant enzymes (Lu et al., 2019). Cytidine diphosphate ethanolamine–ethanolamine, a precursor in PE synthesis, plays a crucial role in mitochondrial biogenesis and muscle oxidative capacity (Selathurai et al., 2015). The reduction in PE, PDME, and lecithin may trigger compensatory cellular responses, such as upregulating alternative antioxidant pathways or promoting the synthesis of alternative phospholipids, to maintain cellular antioxidant function and energy metabolism (Carrasco et al., 1996; Fu et al., 2021). Since most of the detected phospholipid molecules were upregulated, these changes may enhance cellular membrane integrity and lipid-mediated signaling in Hu sheep.

L-Threonine is an essential amino acid that is involved in protein synthesis, mucosal proteins production, and plays a key role in maintaining intestinal barrier integrity and immune function (Hou and Wu, 2018; Ji et al., 2019). Therefore, increased L-threonine levels may indicate that CS enhances mucin synthesis, strengthening intestinal barrier function. L-Threonine can be converted into L-isoleucine by enzymes, which may explain the elevated levels of L-isoleucine in the plasma levels. L-2,4-diaminobutyric acid, a non-protein amino acid, is essential for DNA and RNA synthesis and contributes to cell proliferation and tissue repair. The upregulation of LAB indicated that CS may enhance cellular metabolism, thereby promoting cell proliferation (Chen et al., 2015). Ectoine, a non-enzymatic antioxidant molecule, protects cells from oxidative stress by scavenging free radicals (Bownik and Stępniewska, 2016; Brands et al., 2019). The downregulation of ectoine may be due to the antioxidant components in CS, which reduces oxidative stress and inflammation, thus decreasing the Hu sheep’s reliance on ectoine for cellular protection. N-epsilon-acetyl-L-lysine, the acetylated form of L-lysine, was found to be upregulated, suggesting that active components in CS may influence lysine acetylation and thus modulate gene expression. This process could contribute to enhancing the body’s antioxidant and anti-inflammatory responses (Rahman et al., 2004). 5-Hydroxyindoleacetic acid, a primary metabolite of 5-HT, was detected in this study. Interestingly, we also identified the 5-HT precursor L-tryptophan in the rumen, suggesting that feeding CS may enhance tryptophan metabolism in multiple systems in Hu sheep, potentially improving appetite and immune function (Huether et al., 2012). The aminoacyl-tRNA biosynthesis pathway, which plays a crucial role in protein translation and synthesis (Gomez and Ibba, 2020), was significantly enriched in both the rumen and plasma. This further indicates that feeding CS can significantly improve the protein synthesis level of the body.

Interestingly, while numerous flavonoids and terpenoids were detected in the rumen, they were not found in their original form in the plasma. Flavonoids are absorbed and transported to the liver, where they are converted into glucuronide, sulfate, or methyl-conjugates before being excreted through urine or feces (Ahmadipour et al., 2017, 2015; Kalantar et al., 2018). As demonstrated in the current study, although no original forms of flavonoids and terpenoids were detected in the plasma, we did identify their glucuronide, sulfate, or methyl-conjugated metabolites. Correlation analysis showed that these metabolites were significantly correlated with apparent digestibility, rumen fermentation parameters, antioxidant, and immune indicators. This finding indicated that these compounds were metabolized in the liver and then entered the systemic circulation, suggesting their potential systemic role in modulating digestibility, fermentation, oxidative balance, and immune responses, although the underlying mechanisms remain hypothetical. However, as a supervised method, OPLS-DA may risk overfitting; thus, results should be interpreted cautiously and validated with independent datasets in future studies.

Conclusions

In conclusion, the multi-omics technologies demonstrated the feasibility of CS as a feedstock for Hu sheep. Active components such as flavonoids and terpenoids in CS improved the growth performance and immune function of animals. The related metabolites in the rumen and blood of Hu sheep further supported this conclusion. The specific mechanisms of action are shown in Fig. 6. Current findings suggested that replacing 7.88% of PV with CS on a dry matter basis yields had the most effective results.
