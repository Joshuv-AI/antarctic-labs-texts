Single Treatment Boiling Histotripsy Focused Ultrasound Ablation Neither Negates nor Enhances the Activity of α-CD40 in a Pancreatic Cancer Model
Authors: Mariia Stepanechko, Awndre Gamache, Frederic Padilla, Madison Nemshick, Lydia Kitelinger, Claire Conarroe, Matthew DeWitt, John A Hossack, Timothy N. J. Bullock
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Objective:Pancreatic adenocarcinoma (PDAC) tumors are often unresectable and do not respond to standard anti-cancer treatments. Boiling histotripsy (BH) is a mechanical focused ultrasound ablation regimen that can target non-operable tumors. BH is a non-invasive procedure that might synergize with immunotherapies being developed for PDAC. We reasoned that BH-mediated release of tumor antigen and damage-associated molecular patterns would augment tumor immunity and cooperate with α-CD40 therapy.Methods:We explored the efficacy of BH ablation either as monotherapy for PDAC or in combination with systemically administered α-CD40 agonistic antibodies in controlling tumor outgrowth. We further assessed the changes in the tumor immune compartment and the ability of BH to release tumor antigens by flow cytometry.Results:A single BH treatment could not control tumor growth and had limited independent immunostimulatory properties. BH effectively liberated tumor antigen into the tumor microenvironment for acquisition by local phagocytes but did not promote its presence in the tumor-draining lymph nodes. BH did not activate either tumor- or lymph node-resident conventional dendritic cells. BH did not impede the ability of α-CD40 immunotherapy to reduce the tumor burden and promote the infiltration of M1-like macrophages and IFN-γ+ CD8+ T cells to the tumor microenvironment, suggesting that antibody access to the ablated tissue was not obstructed.Conclusion:Our findings indicate that PDAC tumor progression cannot be halted by BH ablation using sub-total ablation, and that the BH treatment regimen utilized in this study has limited immunogenicity.Significance:Our discoveries attest to the need for optimizing BH ablation protocols to unlock its anti-tumor potential.

Introduction

PANCREATIC adenocarcinoma (PDAC) is a rare but highly deadly type of cancer that is responsible for 8% of all cancer-related deaths. Often unresectable due to its anatomic location and poorly responsive to standard treatments, PDAC is an aggressive disease with a combined 5-year survival rate of only 13% [1]. Desmoplastic stroma is a hallmark of PDAC [2] that can interfere with chemotherapy delivery [3], [4] and restrict infiltration of cytotoxic T cells and NK cells [5]. Aside from this, PDAC tumors are characterized by an immunosuppressive microenvironment associated with the presence of protumorigenic M2-like tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs), myeloid-derived suppressor cells, and regulatory T cells (Tregs), which can further hinder the anti-tumor immune response [6]. Therefore, it is critical to develop complex treatment approaches that target both these features of PDAC.

CD40 belongs to the tumor necrosis factor superfamily and serves as a costimulatory molecule on the surface of dendritic cells (DCs), monocytes, macrophages (MΦs), and B cells. The binding of an appropriate ligand, CD40L (CD154) expressed by CD4+ T cells or NK cells, boosts the antigen-presenting capabilities of CD40+ DCs, activates MΦs, and promotes survival and class switching in B cells [7], [8]. Stimulation of CD40 with agonistic monoclonal antibodies in PDAC models can lead to MΦ-mediated tumor control [9] or DC-mediated initiation of adaptive anti-tumor immune response [10], [11], [12]. The efficiency of various combinations of α-CD40 stimulation and immune-, chemo- or radiotherapy have been tested preclinically and in clinical settings. Whereas the combination of α-CD40 monoclonal antibodies (mAbs) and chemotherapy was efficacious in mouse models [10], [11], recent clinical trial (NCT03214250) did not report a prolonged survival of patients treated with this regimen [13]. The limited efficacy of this approach could be explained by the inherent heterogeneity of PDAC, due to which only a fraction of patients may realize the full benefit of chemoimmunotherapy regimens [13]. While the pursuit of specific biomarkers can help identify the group of patients likely to respond to therapy, it is also necessary to develop other treatment options that are less dependent on the genetic and molecular profiles of the PDAC.

Ablative focused ultrasound (FUS) non-invasively delivers concentrated acoustic energy into a small focus to locally destroy the targeted tissue, thus it serves as an attractive candidate for tackling unresectable and desmoplastic PDAC tumors. Ablative FUS includes multiple modalities that have different mechanisms of action – from mechanical tissue fragmentation (intrinsic, shock-scattering, or boiling histotripsy) to thermal denaturation (thermal FUS or HIFU) [14]. Thermal FUS is a clinically safe palliative treatment in patients with advanced PDAC [15], [16] and preliminary studies suggest an ability to elicit abscopal effects in combination with chemotherapy [17], [18]. Nonetheless, thermal ablations may cause pancreatitis [19], [20] and skin burns [19], [21], [22], highlighting the opportunities for leveraging non-thermal protocols to avoid these adverse effects. Mechanical FUS (mFUS) can be a safer alternative with no dose-limiting toxicities and, in combination with chemotherapy, promote a partial response in PDAC patients as described in a phase one clinical trial [23]. Additionally, mFUS treatment is a recently approved modality to treat the tumors in liver, which is a common organ for PDAC metastasis. A case report demonstrated that mFUS could restrict the growth of both treated and untreated colorectal liver metastasis, thus suggesting an abscopal effect [24]. Overall, the therapeutic potential of mechanical FUS in PDAC is currently being investigated in several clinical trials (NCT05262452, NCT05601323, NCT06282809).

This present study focuses on the mFUS modality termed “boiling histotripsy” (BH), which utilizes high-pressure millisecond pulses to produce vapor bubbles within the focal spot leading to tissue fractionation [25]. BH treatment displays an anti-fibrotic potential in an animal model of liver fibrosis [26] and reduces the stiffness of fibrotic human prostatic hyperplasia tissue [27], suggesting it can be a suitable treatment option for targeting stiff PDAC tumors. Furthermore, BH has been shown to elicit immunomodulatory effects [28], [29], [30], [31], [32], [33], [34] and synergize with available immunotherapies [30], [31], [35] to control tumor growth and prolong survival. Particularly, in certain tumors, BH treatment has been reported to release danger signals that can stimulate innate immune response [29], [33], shift MΦ polarization from M2 towards M1 [29], [31], increase maturation and activation of dendritic cells [31], [32], promote trafficking of tumor antigen to the tumor-draining lymph nodes [34], and enhance T cell infiltration and activation [30], [31], [33]. Furthermore, BH can potentiate the effects of immune checkpoint blockade in neuroblastoma [30] and breast cancer [31] and enhance the efficacy of intratumoral α-CD40 stimulation in melanoma [35]. The latter combination might be particularly promising in the context of PDAC due to the previous evidence of α-CD40 immunotherapy in controlling this disease [9], [10], [11], [12], [36]. BH can be delivered multiple times without decreased efficacy [31], which further strengthens the rationale for using this modality in therapeutic combinations for PDAC therapy. Additionally, the combined potential of BH to lower the tumor burden and stimulate the immune system could reduce the effective dose of α-CD40 mAbs, thus preventing the dose-limiting toxicity of this immunotherapy [37].

Here, we used a custom ultrasound-guided FUS system to deploy partial volume BH and assessed the therapeutic potential of this BH regime as monotherapy for PDAC or part of a combination strategy with α-CD40 mAbs. We also investigated the immunomodulatory properties of BH therapy by assessing the infiltration of cells associated with the immune control of PDAC. Lastly, we introduced fluorescent proteins to tumor cells and used this model to study the fate of tumor antigens after BH treatment which are crucial for driving T cell responses. Overall, our findings provide new insights into the effects of BH on PDAC TME, which may be helpful in developing local ablative approaches to enhance anti-cancer immune responses and establishing rational therapeutic combinations.

Methods

Mice

6–8-week-old C57BL/6J mice were purchased from The Jackson Laboratory (Jax #000664). Mice were housed in a controlled environment on a 12:12 light-dark cycle and supplied with food and water ad libitum. All mouse experiments were conducted in compliance with the guidelines of the University of Virginia and approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee, Protocol # 4270.

Cell Lines

The KPC7940b cell line originating from the LSL-KrasG12D/+, LSL-Trp53R172H/+;Pdx1Cre mouse model was obtained from the Beatty Laboratory [38]. We engineered the KPC7940b cell line to stably express mCherry and ZsGreen fluorescent proteins using custom-made lentiviral particles (VectorBuilder, VB231002–1295ytz). KPC7940b mCherry/ZsGreen cells were expanded post-transduction, selected for clones resistant to geneticin (0.8 mg/ml; Gibco #10131035), and sorted using a cell sorter (BD, Influx) to enrich for double-positive population. Cells were maintained in high glucose DMEM+L-Glutamine (Gibco, #11965092) supplemented with 10% FBS (Corning #35–010-CV) at 37 °C and 5% CO2. Thawed cells were cultured for up to 3 passages before implantation.

Tumor Implantations

Depending on the experiment, 250 K of parental KPC7940b or transduced KPC7940b mCherry/ZsGreen cells were resuspended in 100 μL of 1:1 mixture of Matrigel (Corning, #356231) and DPBS (Gibco, #14190144) and injected subcutaneously (s.c.) into the right flank of mice. Tumor outgrowth was measured via digital calipers. Tumor volume was calculated using the formula (1) V=π6(l⋅w)32 where V is tumor volume, l is tumor length, and w is tumor width. Fourteen days post-implantation, mice were randomized into treatment groups with the matched starting tumor volume. Mice were monitored throughout the study and humanely euthanized at the specified time points, once the tumors had reached the maximum permitted size (4000 mm3) or if wet ulcers had developed.

In Vivo Ultrasound-Guided Boiling Histotripsy Set-Up

A custom ultrasound-guided FUS system was used to deliver BH treatment. The system is composed of 4 single elements focused transducers (model SU-102, Sonic Concept, Bothell, WA, USA), each of 33 mm diameter and 55 mm radius of curvature, 3.78 MHz center frequency. The four transducers are embedded within a solid resin around a 5.08 cm-wide circular central opening, and are confocally aligned, for a total equivalent single active aperture of 66 mm (Fig. 1(a)). The four transducers are connected to a 600 W, four-channel power amplifier (TPO, Sonic Concept, Bothell, WA, USA). To adjust for mechanical tolerance in transducers’ positioning, the phases of three of the transducers have been adjusted with respect to the fourth one to generate maximum peak pressure in water at low power. For targeting, a high-resolution imaging array (MS200, center frequency 30 MHz, FUJJIFILM Visualsonics) is housed in the central opening of the probe (Fig. 1(a), (b)), so that the focal spot is contained within the imaging plane on a VisualSonics 2100 ultrasound preclinical imaging system. The ablation system is composed of 3D mechanical stages controlled by an Arduino and driven by a custom user interface developed in MATLAB (MathWorks, Inc.) for treatment planning.

The therapeutic probe was first calibrated with a fiber optic hydrophone (HFO-690, Onda). The measurements were conducted in degassed water at room temperature. The acquired waveforms were deconvoluted using Soniq, Onda’s proprietary software. The focal zone at −6 dB of peak negative pressure (PNP) was measured to be roughly elliptical, with a width of 1 mm and length of 1.5 mm, for a volume less than 1 mm3 (Fig. 1(c). The scan was conducted in a relatively low-output regime (10 W to each transducer), resulting in a PNP of approximately 12MPa (Fig. 1(d)). To estimate the peak positive (PPP) and negative pressures in a boiling histotripsy setting, focal pressure was measured using the fiber optic probe hydrophone with 115 W on each of the 4 transducers (460 W total). PNP was measured to be −28 MPa, and PPP = 49 MPa (Fig. 1(e)).

BH Treatment

Mice underwent sham or BH treatment on day 14 post-implantation. Three days before the treatment all the fur surrounding the tumor was removed with depilatory cream (Nair) to prevent the interference of the fur with the ultrasound waves. On treatment day mice were anesthetized by receiving an intraperitoneal (i.p.) injection of ketamine hydrochloride (50 mg/kg; Ketaset, Zoetis) and dexmedetomidine hydrochloride (0.25 mg/kg; Dexmedesed, Dechra) in sterilized 09% saline (Hospira, Pfizer), after which they underwent BH treatment with a custom ultrasound-guided FUS system. Mice were positioned above the water bath on a plane holder connected to the 3D mechanical stages. The holder had a hole in its center where the tumor was positioned (Fig. 1(b)). The tumors were imaged by moving the mice over the imaging probe along their rostro-caudal axis. In each treatment plan, safety margins of 1.5 mm from tumor edges were defined, then tumor tissue within the margins was ablated. For the BH treatment, the four transducers were driven with 115 W (460 W total), with 5 ms pulses, a pulse repetition frequency (PRF) of 2HZ, for 20–30 s total duration, resulting in 40 60 pulses per treatment point. A total of 2 to 4 planes spaced by 1 mm were treated in each tumor dependent on the tumor size. Within each plane, treated points were separated by 1–1.5 mm. For most treatment points, the development of hyperechoic marks within the focal spot was observed using B-mode imaging during exposure of FUS energy, as illustrated in (Fig. 1(f); Supplementary movie 1; Fig. S1). The treated area generally became hypoechoic within minutes of treatment and the gross consistency of the treated tumors was softer. After BH treatment a s.c. injection of atipamezole hydrochloride (1 mL in 9 mL saline, 100 μL s.c., Revertidine, Modern Veterinary Therapeutics) was used to reverse the effect of dexmedetomidine hydrochloride. Sham treatment comprised of fully submerging the flank tumor in the water bath for 10 minutes.

Histology

Resected tumors were embedded in OCT compound (Tissue-Tek, Sakura Finetek USA) in a cryomold and cut into 5 μm sections. The sections were next stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) and NADH-diaphorase staining following standard protocols.

Antibody Injections

On day 16 post-implantation 100 μg of agonistic anti-mouse CD40 antibodies (FGK4.5; Ichorbio, #ICH1073) or rat IgG2a isotype control antibodies (2A3, BioXCell #BE0089) were diluted in 0.9% saline and administered i.p. to the appropriate treatment groups.

BFA Injections

Depending on the experiment, 250ug of Brefeldin A (BioXtra, Sigma Aldrich # B6542–25MG) diluted in 0.9% saline was administered i.p. 6h prior to harvest to prevent cytokine secretion from the cells.

Flow Cytometry

Depending on the experiment, axial and brachial tumor-draining lymph nodes (tdLNs) on the right side together with corresponding contralateral non-draining lymph nodes, and/or flank tumors were collected at the specified time points.

LNs were first manually homogenized (Wheaton, Tenbroeck Tissue Grinder #62400–518) and filtered through the 100 μm filter mesh (Genesee Scientific # 57103) to generate single-cell suspensions. Such suspensions were spun down at 1200 RPM for 5 min (Eppendorf 5180) to pellet the cells, which were next transferred to a 96-well plate (Corning #3897) for staining.

Tumors were minced and then enzymatically digested for 45 min at 37 °C in RPMI media (Gibco #11875093) supplemented with 10% FBS, 0.5 mg/mL Collagenase A (Roche #10103578001), and 0.5 mg/mL DNAse I (Roche #10104159001). Upon digestion, tumors were homogenized and filtered through the 100 μm filter mesh. Such samples were next centrifuged at 1200 RPM for 5 min. The obtained pellets were resuspended in 10 mL of 1x PBS, underlaid with 10 mL of Lympholyte-M cell separation media (Cedarlane Labs #CL5035) and centrifuged at 1000 g for 10 min with low acceleration and no brake. The interface was collected, diluted with 1x PBS to obtain the final volume of 50 mL, and centrifuged at 800 g for 10 min. Supernatants were next discarded and the cells were transferred to a 96-well plate (Corning #3897) for staining.

The cells were first washed with 1x PBS and next stained with viability dye Fixable Live/Dead Blue (Invitrogen, # L23105) for 20 min at 4 °C, followed by 15 min exposure to anti-mouse CD16/32 diluted in 2% normal mouse serum solution (Valley Biomedical, #AS3054) at 4 °C to block Fc gamma receptors. Then, cells were washed with FACS buffer, spun down, and resuspended in a master mix made of Brilliant Stain Buffer (BD, #3563794) and fluorescent monoclonal antibodies for surface markers. Depending on the experiment, cells were stained for 40 min at 4 °C for the following surface markers: CD45, MHCII, CD3, CD4, CD11c, CD8α, Ly6G, XCR1, F4/80, CD11b, CD86, SIRP-α, CD19. Antibody information, source, catalog number, and dilution factor can be found in Table SI. After the wash step with FACS buffer, cells were fixed in 1x BD FACS Lysis Buffer (BD, #349202) for 10 min at room temperature, washed, and resuspended in FACS buffer for collection.

The samples subjected to intracellular and intranuclear staining were washed with FACS buffer upon surface staining and next processed using eBioscience FOXp3/Transcription Factor Staining Buffer Set (Invitrogen, #00–5523-00). Cells were first fixed for 55 min at 4 °C with a solution consisting of Fix/Perm Diluent and Fix/Perm Concentrate at a 3:1 ratio. Afterward, cells were washed with 1x Perm/Wash Buffer, spun down, and stained for iNOS, FOXP3, IFN-γ, and CD206 in 1x Perm/Wash Buffer. Antibody information, source, catalog number, and dilution factor can be found in Table SI. After the staining, cells were washed with 1x Perm/Wash Buffer and fixed with 1x BD FACS Lysis Buffer as described above.

Flow cytometry was performed using Cytek Aurora Borealis (Cytek Biosciences) and SpectroFlo v3.0.3 software (Cytek Biosciences). Data were analyzed using FlowJo 10 software (FlowJo, LLC). Within the FlowJo software, the FlowAI plugin [39] was used to perform the quality control of the FSC data as indicated.

Statistical Analysis

GraphPad Prism 9 (GraphPad Software, Inc) was used for statistical analysis. A mixed effect model with Šidák correction was fitted to the tumor outgrowth data. Mantel-Cox test was used to determine the significance between the survival of different treatment groups. An unpaired t-test with Welch’s correction was performed while comparing two groups of area under the curve (AUC) or flow cytometry data. ROUT’s outlier test with Q = 0.1% was performed prior to any statistical analysis of flow cytometry data to identify definitive outliers. Tukey’s multiple comparison test was used to assess the difference between three and more groups of data that passed the Brown-Forsythe test (i.e., had equal SD values). Dunnett’s T3 multiple comparison test was employed for data that did not pass the Brown-Forsythe test. Symbols (*, **, ***, ****) denote p values < 0.05, 0.01, 0.001, or 0.0001, respectively.

Results

Boiling Histotripsy Monotherapy Fails to Control the Outgrowth of Subcutaneous KPC PDAC Tumors

Based on previous reports showing that intrinsic histotripsy provides transient growth control of subcutaneous Pan02 pancreatic tumors [40] and our data on boiling histotripsy (BH) in the context of melanoma [34], we tested the efficacy of BH in a well-established model of PDAC, KPC7940b [38]. Established tumors underwent BH treatment on day 14, upon reaching a treatable volume of ≥ 100 mm3 (Fig. 2(a)). BH was administered using a custom four-transducer system, which allowed targeting the tissue of less than 1 mm3 of volume, ensuring precise targeting. Tumor tissue within established safety margins was ablated by treating the points spaced by 1–1.5 mm. The ablative success of BH was confirmed by performing H&E and NADH-diaphorase staining on selected tissues resected 2 h post-treatment. BH treatment resulted in disrupted tissue integrity and decreased viability of the tumors, which were not evident in untreated samples (Fig. 1(g)). Assessment of the overall survival after ablation indicated that BH did not provide any benefit or negative effect compared to sham-treated controls (Fig. 2(b)). Similarly, we did not observe any differences in the tumor growth between the two groups (Fig. 2(c), (d)) nor in the integrated tumor burden calculated as the area under the curve (AUC) between days 14–32 of the experiment (Fig. 2(e)). This indicates that although BH delivered under these treatment conditions is a safe procedure, this monotherapy cannot restrain the tumor growth in this model, despite ablative success.

Boiling Histotripsy Does Not Augment the Anti-Tumor Potential of α-CD40 Immunotherapy

Although BH was not effective for tumor control as a monotherapy, we next asked whether it could be complemented with α-CD40 immunotherapy to yield a positive effect on survival and tumor growth control, as was previously shown in a melanoma model [35]. We hypothesized that the antigen release caused by BH ablation [34] would be captured by DCs in the tdLNs, which could next be licensed by CD40 stimulation and subsequently prime the anti-tumor T cell response. Alternatively, preconditioning of the TME with α-CD40 can increase the infiltration of inflammatory monocytes/macrophages with the potential to reduce tumor fibrosis [38], which might augment the activity of BH. On the other hand, the efficacy of this timing might be compromised if α-CD40-induced maturation of DC minimizes their capacity to engulf antigen after BH treatment [41]. To discern between these possibilities, we first exposed mice to BH or Sham treatment and administered systemic α-CD40 agonistic antibodies or control IgG (Fig. 3(a)) either 48 h before or after BH treatment. We determined that α-CD40 monotherapy significantly slowed tumor growth (Fig. 3(b)), decreased tumor burden (Fig. 3(c)), and prolonged survival (Fig. 3(d)). The addition of BH, however, did not alter any of these outcomes, and all the groups that received α-CD40 mAbs demonstrated similar levels of tumor growth control and overall survival (Fig. 3(b)–(d)). Importantly, we did not observe a significant difference between the survival of any of the groups that received both treatments (Fig. 3(d)). Overall, BH treatment does not augment PDAC tumor growth control provided by α-CD40 immunotherapy.

Boiling Histotripsy Treatment Enhances Immune Cell Infiltration While α-CD40 Therapy Elicits an Immunostimulatory Effect in PDAC Tumor Microenvironment

By creating a tissue homogenate, BH could destroy both cancer and immune cells in the treated area and thus temporarily reduce the number of infiltrating immune cells in the TME. Conversely, a previous report on intrinsic histotripsy demonstrated alterations in the immune composition of PDAC tumors at 7 and 14 days post-treatment [40], which prompted us to assess the immune composition of tumors treated with BH. Although BH did not alter the outcome of α-CD40 immunotherapy, we were also keen on elucidating whether pretreatment with BH introduced any changes in the immune subsets associated with immunotherapy. To this end, we treated mice with either therapy, or in combination, and then resected tumors for flow cytometry analysis 6 days after the last treatment (Fig. 4(a); gating strategy provided in Fig. S2), allowing us to assess the effect of BH on components of the adaptive immune response, which is necessary for tumor growth control in PDAC [10], [11], [12].

The infiltration of DCs to the TME is associated with tumor control [42], thus in the first step we decided to investigate whether DC representation was altered by any treatment. Tumors treated with BH alone had the highest number of infiltrating conventional DCs (cDCs), which constituted a trending difference relative to Sham treatment and a significant difference from a group that received α-CD40 treatment (Fig. 4(c)). This result was independent of the tumor weight, which was similar between all the groups (Fig. S3), thus it was not caused solely by the debulking of the tumor tissue with BH. At the same time, BH pretreatment did not affect the cDC counts in the combination group, which were similar to the group treated with immunotherapy only (Fig. 4(c)). Conventional DCs can be further divided into XCR1+ conventional type 1 DCs (cDC1s) and SIRP-α+ conventional type 2 cells (cDC2s), which are responsible for priming CD8+ [43], [44] and CD4+ T cells [45], [46], respectively. We observed that the total number of cDC1s and cDC2s isolated from the tumors displayed a similar trend to total cDC counts (Fig. 4(d), (e)). Whereas the percentage of total cDCs remained similar between Sham and BH-treated tumors, both α-CD40-treated groups showed substantial reductions in the number and proportion of cDC infiltrating treated tumors (Fig. 4(c)). This discrepancy between the total counts and percentages suggests that at this timepoint BH promotes the infiltration of various immune cell subsets without altering the overall proportions. The reduction in total cDCs in α-CD40 treated tumors was caused by the absence of cDC1s, which could have migrated to lymph nodes upon licensing. Meanwhile, the levels of cDC2s remained stable between all the groups (Fig. 4(a), (d), (e)). Interestingly, an influx of XCR1− SIRP-α− DCs can be noted in α-CD40-treated tumors (Fig. 4(a)), which may be immature DCs. Overall, these data indicate that BH promotes cDCs accumulation, while α-CD40 stimulation specifically decreases cDC1 representation in the TME.

Given the increase in cDC1s after BH, we also examined T cells and their activation status. We observed that combination therapy resulted in a ~2-fold increase in cytotoxic CD8+ T cell numbers compared to Sham and α-CD40 treated groups (Fig. 5(a)). The numbers of CD4+ T cells, that may be primed by cDC2s [47], were not statistically different between groups (Fig. 5(b)), even though we noted the elevated counts of cDC2s in the TME post-BH (Fig. 4(d)). We did not observe any difference in the CD8+ and CD4+ T cell proportions after any of the therapies (Fig. 5(c), (d)). Surprisingly, BH treatment alone increased the numbers (Fig. 5(e)) of FOXP3+ regulatory T cells (Tregs) without altering their proportional representation within either the CD4+ T cell population or the total CD45+ cell population in the TME. In contrast, the inclusion of α-CD40 reduced the proportion of CD4+T cells that have FOXP3+ regulatory phenotype (Fig. 5(f), (g)), which was consistent with the previously published reports on α-CD40 monotherapy and combination with immune checkpoint inhibitors [12], [36]. When considered together, the combination of BH and α-CD40 mAbs lead to a significant 4.5-fold increase in the CD8+/Tregs ratio relative to Sham or BH-treated tumors and even a trending rise compared to α-CD40 treatment alone (Fig. 5(h)). To understand whether this alteration in CD8+/Tregs ratio was accompanied by an increase in T cell function activity, we interrogated T cells for the production of IFN-γ. There was a strong trend in the number, but not proportion, of IFN-γ+ CD8+ T cells in the tumor after the combination treatment (Fig. 5(i), (k)), indicating that the alteration in CD8+/Treg ratio correlates with an increase in IFN-γ production. We also found that despite BH’s contribution to IFN-γ+ CD8+ T cell counts, the increase in IFN-γ production in both CD8+ and CD4+ T cells was a result of CD40 stimulation (Fig. 5(i)–(l)). As BH alone does not attract more CD8+ T cells nor affects IFN-γ production, we lastly asked whether it influences the exhaustion status of these cells. Since PD-1 can be transiently expressed during the early phase of T cell activation, and thus serve as an activation marker [48], we defined 2 populations based on their PD-1 expression – medium and high. PD-1med CD8+ T cells were more common in the combination therapy group, whereas their proportion of CD8+ T cell compartment remained similar to tumors treated only with α-CD40 mAbs (Fig. S4(a)). Meanwhile, the representation of the PD-1high exhausted CD8+ T cells was not significantly different across all the groups, despite the combination group showing trending increases in cell counts compared to Sham (Fig. S4(b)). Upon quantifying the per-cell expression of PD-1 on total cytotoxic T cells by geometric mean fluorescence (GMF) intensity, we noted that BH treatment did not elevate the exhausted state of the cells nor interfere with α-CD40-mediated reduction in PD-1 expression (Fig. S4(c), (d)). Altogether, BH alone does not improve the representation nor activation status of T cells in the TME.

Given that focused ultrasound ablation can shift MΦ polarization status in breast cancer model [49], whereas α-CD40 immunotherapy promotes tumoricidal M1 phenotype specifically in the context of pancreatic cancer [9], [50], [51], we lastly assessed the effect of BH and α-CD40 treatment on the MΦ compartment in our study. Although the combination of BH and α-CD40 mAbs resulted in the trending increase in MΦ counts, none of the therapies provoked changes in the overall MΦ representation among CD45+ cells in the TME (Fig. 6(b)). In this model, almost no iNOS+ (M1-like) cells were found in either the Sham cohort or day 8 after BH ablation (Fig. 6(a), (c)) indicating that BH does not independently alter MΦ polarization. The numbers of CD206+ (M2-like) MΦs were elevated whereas the percentage remained unaltered (Fig. 6(a), (d)). Consistent with the previous reports, we noted a shift toward M1-like cells upon α-CD40 administration and a large loss of M2-like MΦs (Fig. 6(a), (c), (d)). Importantly, these immunomodulatory effects only occurred in groups treated with α-CD40.

In summary, BH promotes the infiltration of myeloid and effector immune cells without altering their relative representation within specific subsets. Thus, BH displays little immunomodulatory effects in pancreatic tumors 8 days after the treatment as an independent therapy, nor does it negate the effects of α-CD40 immunotherapy. The trending increase in the number of IFNγ-expressing CD8+ T cells after BH is insufficient to influence tumor outgrowth kinetics compared to the effects of α-CD40 alone.

Boiling Histotripsy Promotes the Release of Tumor Antigens Which Are Subsequently Engulfed By Neutrophils and Ly6G−F4/80lowCD11b+ Cells

Tumor antigen release is the first step in the cancer-immunity cycle, which can lead to T cell-mediated anti-tumor immune response [52]. Thus, to understand how BH might be driving larger T cell responses, we questioned whether BH-mediated ablation promotes antigen release in pancreatic tumors, similar to that which we described for melanoma [34]. To answer this question, we engineered KPC7940b cells to express two fluorescent proteins ZsGreen (ZsG) and mCherry (mCh), which would enable us to track the fate of surrogate tumor antigens. Although only ZsG had previously been successfully used in tumor antigen studies in the context of melanoma [53], [54], [34] and pancreatic cancer [42] we also used mCherry to validate our results, as these two proteins reportedly displayed different uptake patterns by immune cells in the TME [55]. We implanted the transduced cells (Fig. S5) s.c., treated the developed tumors with BH, and resected the tumors and axial and brachial lymph nodes for flow cytometry (Fig. 7(a); gating strategy provided in Fig. S6). We found that BH treatment resulted in an over two-fold increase in the proportion of ZsG+ CD45+ cells in the TME and a trending increase in the ZsG+ cell counts (Fig. 7(b), (c)). Surprisingly, we found few mCh+ CD45+ cells in the TME in either Sham or BH group (Fig. S7), which might be a consequence of the weaker intensity and stability of mCherry [55], as well as the autofluorescence of phagocytic cells in the red channels. Due to this, we focused only on ZsG uptake in further analysis.

We examined ZsG acquisition by cDCs, neutrophils, MΦs, and potentially monocytes (Ly6G−F4/80lowCD11b+) in the TME. We did not include B cells in the analysis as they were scarcely present in the TME (data not shown). We observed only trending changes in the numbers of ZsG+ neutrophils and Ly6G−F4/80lowCD11b+ 24 h post-BH treatment, whereas the counts of ZsG+ cDCs and MΦs remained similar between both groups (Fig. 8(a)–(d); top section). Whereas BH significantly elevated the proportions of ZsG+ cells in all subsets except MΦs (Fig. 8(a)–(d); middle section), neutrophils and Ly6G−F4/80lowCD11b+ were mostly responsible for antigen uptake after the ablation, as twice as many acquired ZsG relative to Sham-treated tumors (Fig. 8(a)–(d); bottom section). This result is consistent with the fact that neutrophils and Ly6G−F4/80lowCD11b+ displayed a trend towards increased infiltration into the tumors 24 h after BH treatment (Fig. S8), as compared to the increase in cDCs we observed 8 days after ablation.

Overall, BH ablation results in tumor antigen release in pancreatic TME which can next be acquired by antigen-presenting cells (APCs) and phagocytic cells, mostly neutrophils and Ly6G−F4/80lowCD11b+.

Tumor Antigen Released As a Result of Boiling Histotripsy Ablation Is Not Found in the Tumor-Draining Lymph Nodes

Given that ZsG can be tracked to draining lymph nodes in the pancreatic cancer model [42] and our previous findings on BH-induced ZsG drainage to lymph nodes in the context of melanoma [34], we questioned whether BH enhances antigen acquisition in the draining lymph nodes in our model of PDAC. Thus, we analyzed tdLNs resected 24 h post-BH treatment (Fig. 7(a); gating strategy provided in Fig. S6). In tdLNs ZsG was associated with cDCs and B cells, but no significant increases in ZsG presence in APCs were observed after BH treatment (Fig. 9(a), (b); left and middle). The GMF of ZsG within APCs also did not increase, indicating that ZsG was not being phagocytosed at a greater level on a per-cell basis (Fig. 9(a), (b); right section). At the same time, we did not observe any changes in the level of CD86 and MHCII molecules on ZsG+ cDCs both in the tdLNs and TME (Fig. S10; gating strategy provided in Fig. S9). As CD86 and MHCII are usually upregulated after the activation of cDCs and during the maturation phase [56], our results indicate that BH does not potentiate activation of these cells. Altogether, these data suggest that while our BH regimen can effectively release tumor antigens from pancreatic tumors, it does not promote activation of cDCs and subsequent drainage of antigens to the lymph nodes 24 h post-treatment, perhaps explaining the limited immunomodulatory potential of our ablative approach.

Discussion

In this study, we determined that BH monotherapy has limited independent immunostimulatory properties in PDAC tumors and ultimately fails to control their growth. We established that BH can (i) attract different inflammatory and regulatory immune subsets and (ii) effectively release tumor antigens locally without enhancing the activation status of cDCs or antigen drainage to the tdLNs. We also demonstrated that BH needs complementary treatment, such as α-CD40 immunotherapy to promote the infiltration of tumoricidal M1-like MΦs and effector IFN-γ+ CD8+ T cells to the TME.

Given the therapeutic and immunomodulatory potential of BH in various cancer models [28], [29], [30], [31], [32], [33], [34], we examined the efficacy of a single round of BH ablation in subcutaneous PDAC tumors. The treatment parameters were selected based on the appearance of a hyperechoic region in preliminary trials, although there is always a stochastic response due to cumulative dose with BH regimes. As such, we cannot definitively confirm that the applied regimen corresponds to a standard boiling histotripsy regime characterized by a strong shocked wavefront, as described in [57]. Differences in waveform characteristics and biological response, especially the absence of NADH-positive staining in the treated area, may suggest that the histotripsy exposure conditions in our study differ in some respects from the canonical BH regime. Notably, we employed a higher frequency transducer than typically used in BH studies, in order to achieve precise spatial control of the treatment zone, which may also have influenced the bioacoustic effects. However, previous reports acknowledge that histotripsy occurs on a spectrum with numerous potential waveforms and tissue interactions, and likely can lead to differences in tissue response and ultimately immunological consequences between studies [25].

Potential differences in bioacustic effects could explain why, despite observing successful cavitation via ultrasound imaging, we found that BH alone could not control tumor growth nor prolong the survival of mice. Our results are supported, however, by the data obtained in an orthotopic model of PDAC treated with pulsed HIFU (pHIFU), where FUS ablation did not influence the survival [58]. On the other hand, such observations go against the previously published report on the effects of intrinsic histotripsy in Pan02 tumors [40]. Of note, Pan02 tumors have limited desmoplastic reaction and high prevalence of neoantigens, opposite to KPC-derived cell lines [59], used in the present study and paper published by Mouratidis et al. [58] While a high number of neoepitopes is usually not enough to induce anti-tumor immune response in Pan02 tumors [59], a successful liberation of multiple antigens could boost the immune response, explaining the positive outcomes of the study reported by Hendricks-Wenger et al. [40].

It is possible that the BH ablation of the center of the tumor does not restrict the growth of the outer layer and that a more aggressive approach targeting tumor peripheries may be needed to physically halt further tumor expansion. Such a result also suggests that in our model the immunomodulation that occurs after BH is insufficient in form or function, as previous positive outcomes of BH treatment were linked to the changes in the immune milieu of the tumors [28], [30], [31]. Indeed, we did not observe detectable changes in the proportions of immune cells in the TME 8 days after BH monotherapy. Interestingly, we noted trending changes in the numbers of infiltrating cDCs, which can have practical implications, as DC deficiency can contribute to the limited adaptive immune response in PDAC [42]. cDC mobilization with FLT3 ligand, on the other hand, can lead to the increased infiltration of CD8+ T cells, which can result in disease stabilization and has been reported to increase response to radiation treatment of PDAC [11], [42]. Supporting this concept, in this current study the increase in cDC was accompanied by a parallel relative increase in the numbers of CD8+ T cells in the treated tumors. While this increase was not significant compared to previous report on pHIFU in orthotopic PDAC [58], a different time point chosen by Mouratidis et al. (12 days following ablation vs 8 days in the present study) and differences in the FUS set-up and protocol could account for disparities in the results. In contrast to our previous studies with BH in a melanoma model, we did not observe increases in the activation state of cDC in either the tumor or the tdLN after BH, which may be a consequence of the dysfunctional state of cDC evident in PDAC [42].

At the same time, we noticed an increase in the numbers of M2-like MΦs and Tregs after BH. Although these cells are associated with immunosuppressive TME [6], they are also involved in the wound-healing process [60], [61]. Lesions created after BH ablation could be viewed as a wound that needs to heal [62]. As M2-like MΦs are responsible for removing the debris [63], their increased accumulation post-BH can be linked to the clearance of the ablated region. The infiltration of Tregs can also facilitate tissue repair by further skewing MΦs toward a prohealing phenotype [64]. Trending increases in the numbers of various immune subsets without the changes in their proportions suggest that BH can elicit a mixed effect on the immune milieu in the TME. This point is additionally supported by the reports on the increased release of the anti-inflammatory cytokine IL-10 post-BH [28] and downregulated signaling via HMGB1, NF-κB, IL-6, and TLR pathways with increasingly prolonged timepoints after treatment with intrinsic histotripsy [40]. Therefore, it is important to consider the potential immunosuppressive aspects of wound healing post-BH treatment in future studies, and this may contribute to the lack of an increase in IFNγ+ CD8+ and CD4+ T cells after BH ablation alone. Altogether, our studies did not reveal quantifiable differences in the representation of immune cell subsets in BH-treated tumors. Nonetheless, we cannot discount the possibility that spatial reorganization of innate and adaptive immune cells occurs proximal to lesions generated by BH.

Another possible explanation for why BH displays limited immunomodulatory effects in PDAC would be the lack of the bioeffects ascribed to FUS ablations such as antigen drainage to the tumor-draining lymph nodes [65]. We determined that whereas antigen release and its uptake by phagocytic cells and cDCs occurred in the treated tumor, we did not observe a corresponding rise in ZsGreen presence in the tdLN. This stands contrary to our previous report on melanoma tumors [34], where similar treatment parameters were used. The opposite outcomes documented in these studies might be due to the less fibrotic nature of melanoma tumors and lower collagen levels leading to more tissue destruction. Additionally, the lack of noticeable change in antigen drainage could be caused by differences in the lymphatic system in both models. Instead, we believe that antigen release post-BH is insufficient to enhance cDC maturation and antigen trafficking to the lymph nodes. Altogether, there is a significant deficit in antigen drainage to tdLNs in this model of PDAC and it cannot be improved by the BH regime employed in this study.

Based on our immunological observations of BH monotherapy, we next explored its efficacy in combination with agonistic α-CD40 mAbs. We found that combination therapy decreased tumor burden and prolonged survival as compared to BH monotherapy, but these positive outcomes were the function of α-CD40 immunotherapy. Nonetheless, we established that therapeutic sequencing of BH and α-CD40 is safe whether delivered prior to or after BH, in contrast to the deleterious effects of the α-CD40 mAbs administered before chemotherapy [66] or as a monotherapy in gliomas [67]. We also observed that only combination therapy could significantly increase the numbers of total CD8+ T cells as well as IFN-γ+ CD8+ T cells in the TME relative to Sham treatment. This positive outcome was coupled with the fact that α-CD40 treatment given post-BH reduced the proportions of Tregs and their numbers, resulting in the highest ratio of CD8+ /Tregs and CD4+/Tregs in the TME. Furthermore, the combination therapy significantly enhanced the numbers of M1-like and diminished M2-like MΦs, resulting in the highest ratio of M1/M2-like cells in this group. Given this apparent cooperativity between BH and α-CD40 at the immunological level, it is quite surprising that increased tumor control is not apparent in the combination treatment group. This suggests that either the increased presence and activity of effector lymphocytes is insufficient to impede tumor growth, or alternatively the mode of α-CD40-mediated control of tumor is independent of T cell immunity at this stage [9]. Altogether, our data indicate that while BH does not interfere with α-CD40 stimulation, the latter is required to modulate the immune compartment of the TME. At the same time, preconditioning the TME with BH prior to immunotherapy can help in shaping the immune response. Pertaining to these conclusions, Singh et al. showed that only the combination of both BH and intra-tumoral α-CD40 could slow down tumor growth in a melanoma model, yet they did not observe any major differences in the immune compartment between groups treated with immunotherapy alone or BH and α-CD40 mAbs [35]. Therefore, the BH regimen used here may have limitations as an immunostimulant in combination with α-CD40, consistent with data obtained in a different tumor model and with different FUS instrument [35].

Conclusion

We demonstrate that a single BH treatment is not an effective monotherapy for PDAC with the treatment parameters used here. Future studies will be needed to determine whether the apparent wound-healing response that attracts different immunostimulatory and inhibitory populations to the TME and/or poor antigen drainage to the tumor-draining lymph nodes are responsible for this outcome. We also show that BH ablation does not impede and can cooperate to a certain extent with immunomodulatory therapies such as α-CD40 stimulation to enhance the immune infiltration and effector activity in the TME. However, additional unknown barriers persist to prevent cooperative control of tumor growth in this model of PDAC.
