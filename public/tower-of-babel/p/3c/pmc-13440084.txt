Perceived stress, stress-related symptoms, and support-based coping in healthcare students: insights from a cross-sectional study in the United Arab Emirates
Authors: Abubakker Siddiq Mohamed Hameed, Janet Chichu James, Sandhra Treesa Santhosh, Anugraha Lisa Ciju, Syeda Kaunain Fatima, Aaesha Rannath, Latifa Alsaad, Liju Susan Mathew, Jayakumary Muttappallymyalil
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHealthcare students experience significant stress due to rigorous academic demands, frequent assessments, and clinical responsibilities. Prolonged stress can negatively impact academic performance, cognitive abilities, and mental well-being. Understanding stress levels and coping mechanisms among healthcare students is essential for developing targeted interventions to enhance their well-being and academic success. This study aims to assess the prevalence of stress and explore the coping strategies employed by healthcare students.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted among healthcare students at a medical university in Ajman, UAE. A self-administered questionnaire was used to collect sociodemographic data, stress levels using the Perceived Stress Scale (PSS-10), and coping mechanisms. The final sample consisted of 938 students, recruited using convenience sampling. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the data, and chi-square tests were performed to assess associations between stress levels and sociodemographic variables. Data analysis was conducted using IBM® SPSS Statistics version 29.0.ResultsAmong the participants, 71.1% experienced moderate stress, while 13.8% reported high stress. Stress levels were significantly associated with college affiliation (p = 0.009) and nationality (p = 0.044). The most commonly reported stress-related manifestations were difficulty sleeping (44.5%), feeling overwhelmed (44.1%), and appetite loss (42.5%). Coping strategies varied, with 42.9% of students sometimes relying on parental guidance, 40.5% seeking support from mentors, and 43% turning to friends for stress relief. A significant association was found between coping mechanisms and perceived stress levels (p < 0.001), indicating that students who frequently sought social support experienced lower stress levels.ConclusionsThe study highlights the high prevalence of stress among healthcare students and emphasizes the critical role of social support in stress management. Institutional interventions, such as mentorship programs and stress management workshops, could be effective in reducing stress and improving student well-being. Future research should explore the long-term effectiveness of different coping mechanisms and the impact of structured academic interventions on stress reduction.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-026-09585-x.

Background

Stress is a significant challenge faced by healthcare students, driven by demanding academic requirements, frequent assessments, and the intensity of clinical training [1–3]. Unlike students in other disciplines, healthcare students must integrate theoretical learning with practical application in high pressure environments, often with early exposure to patient care [4]. This combination contributes to sustained psychological stress, which can adversely affect academic performance, concentration, and overall well-being [5], with many students at risk of burnout and reduced motivation [6, 7].

In the United Arab Emirates (UAE), these challenges are further intensified by the rapid expansion of the healthcare system and the increasing demand for healthcare professionals. Early clinical exposure and evolving healthcare needs, including the rising burden of non-communicable diseases, contribute to elevated academic and clinical stress among students [8].

The prevalence of stress among healthcare students is well established, with studies consistently reporting moderate to severe stress levels in a substantial proportion of students [9, 10]. For instance, a study reported that 77% of medical students experienced moderate stress, highlighting the magnitude of the issue [11]. This trend reflects increasing academic expectations, expanding curricula, and competitive learning environments [12–15], along with concerns related to clinical competence, professional identity, and future career prospects [16].

The consequences of stress extend beyond academic performance and significantly affect psychological and cognitive functioning [4, 6, 14–19]. High stress levels are associated with burnout, emotional exhaustion, and a reduced sense of accomplishment, which may impair both learning outcomes and patient care skills [4, 8, 12, 17, 20]. In addition, chronic stress can negatively impact memory, problem solving ability, and clinical decision making [21], and may increase the risk of mental health disorders such as anxiety and depression [22, 23].

Stress levels among healthcare students are influenced by multiple factors, including gender and year of study. While several studies report higher stress levels among female students [4, 7, 14, 24–28], others suggest that differences may reflect variations in emotional expression and coping styles rather than true differences in stress burden [25]. Some studies also report no significant gender differences, emphasizing the role of resilience, academic pressure, and social support systems [5, 11, 12, 24]. Similarly, stress varies across academic years, with early year students facing transition related challenges and senior students experiencing stress related to clinical responsibilities and career uncertainty [6, 17, 26].

Coping strategies play a critical role in moderating stress. Adaptive strategies such as effective time management, structured study routines, and social support are associated with lower stress levels and improved academic outcomes [13, 29], whereas maladaptive strategies such as avoidance, procrastination, and substance use are linked to poorer outcomes [29].

In response, medical institutions in the UAE have introduced wellness programs, counseling services, and peer support initiatives. While these interventions have demonstrated effectiveness in reducing stress and improving well-being [18, 26], barriers such as stigma, limited time, and reluctance to seek help continue to restrict their utilization [5, 22, 30, 31]. This highlights the need for proactive institutional approaches, including integration of mental health education, enhanced faculty engagement, and normalization of discussions around stress and well-being.

While the prevalence of stress in healthcare students is well documented, further research is needed to understand how students perceive and manage stress in their academic environments. This study aims to assess stress levels, common stress manifestations, and coping strategies among healthcare students. Specifically, the study seeks to: (1) assess the levels of stress among healthcare students, (2) identify common stress manifestations, and (3) examine the social support coping strategies employed by these students. By analyzing these factors, the study will provide insights that can inform targeted interventions to improve student well-being and academic success.

Materials and methods

Study design, setting, and sampling

This cross-sectional study was conducted among healthcare students at Gulf Medical University in Ajman, United Arab Emirates. The initial sample size of 652 was estimated using the formula Z^2(PQ)/L^2, with adjustments made for a 10% non-response rate, resulting in a final sample size of 717. After excluding responses that did not fulfill the inclusion criteria or had incomplete responses, the final sample size that was analyzed was 938. A convenience sampling technique was employed to recruit participants who were enrolled in various programs across the six colleges under Gulf Medical University. We used a large sample size (n = 938) and included students from various healthcare programs and years of study, which we believe helps provide a broad representation within the university’s healthcare student population.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Students were eligible for inclusion if they were: (1) enrolled in any of the healthcare programs at Gulf Medical University during the study period, (2) aged 18 years or older, and (3) willing to voluntarily participate by providing informed consent. Exclusion criteria included: (1) incomplete questionnaire responses.

Questionnaire development

A self-administered questionnaire was developed to assess perceived stress and coping strategies. The questionnaire included sections on sociodemographic variables such as age, gender, nationality, program, and year of study to explore correlations between stress levels and various factors. The Perceived Stress Scale (PSS-10) was used to measure stress levels, consisting of 10 items rated on a four-point Likert scale. Coping strategies were also assessed using a Likert scale, with categories based on common coping mechanisms.

The Perceived Stress Scale (PSS-10), a widely used and validated instrument developed by Cohen et al. (1983), was used to assess perceived stress. The scale includes 10 items rated on a 5-point Likert scale. Although the original tool does not prescribe cutoff points, we followed the categorization provided in the scoring guidelines of the PSS-10 published by the New Hampshire Department of Administrative Services [32] to classify stress into low (0–13), moderate (14–26), and high (27–40) levels. No additional factor analysis was conducted, as the PSS-10 has consistently demonstrated good psychometric properties in international settings.

Coping mechanism measurement

Coping mechanisms were assessed using a self-reported Likert scale, where participants indicated the frequency with which they employed various coping strategies. The scale consisted of five response options ranging from Never to Very Often. Each participant selected the option that best described their use of specific coping strategies. These strategies were based on common social and emotional support mechanisms, specifically parental support, sibling support, mentor guidance, and peer/friend support. These four strategies were selected because prior research has highlighted the protective role of social networks in mitigating academic stress among healthcare students.

Unlike the Perceived Stress Scale (PSS-10), which was categorized into low, moderate, and high stress levels, the coping strategies were treated as categorical variables for analysis. This approach allowed for a detailed examination of the coping strategies employed by students and their association with perceived stress levels.

The full questionnaire, including the newly developed questions on stress manifestations and coping strategies, is provided as Supplementary File 1.

Content validation and piloting

The content of the questionnaire other than PSS-10 was validated by three experts from the university, who reviewed the questions for clarity, relevance, and accuracy in measuring the intended constructs of perceived stress and coping strategies. Following the validation process, a pilot study was conducted with five students who were not part of the final sample. This allowed testing the questionnaire’s clarity, ease of use, and ability to measure the intended outcomes. Based on the pilot test, modifications were made to enhance the questionnaire’s readability and overall effectiveness.

Ethical issues and data collection procedure

The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of the Gulf Medical University (IRB-COM-STD-27-JAN-2024). Helsinki’s declarations were strictly adhered to, ensuring that all participants provided informed consent before participating in the study. Participants were briefed about the study’s objectives and assured that their participation was voluntary, with the option to withdraw at any time without penalty. Data collection took place between September 2024 and December 2024 in various student-populated areas of the university, such as classrooms, the library, and the food court. This period corresponded to the mid to late phase of the academic semester and overlapped with assignment deadlines, class tests, and examination preparation. Eligible participants were approached and invited to complete the questionnaire via a QR code, which linked directly to the validated online questionnaire hosted on Google Forms. To prevent duplicate entries, only one response per participant was allowed by requiring them to log in using their university email account before accessing the form. Clear instructions were provided to participants on how to access and complete the questionnaire. No personally identifiable information was requested to maintain anonymity and confidentiality, and all responses were kept confidential.

Data management and statistical analysis

Data was collected via QR codes that participants scanned to access the questionnaire, and responses were gathered from various student-populated areas within the university. After data collection, the responses were exported from Google Forms into an Excel spreadsheet. The data was then coded and cleaned, excluding any incomplete or invalid entries. Data analysis was performed using IBM® SPSS Statistics version 29.0 (Chicago, IL) in December 2024. Descriptive statistics were used for the sociodemographic variables and perceived stress levels. Chi-square tests were conducted to assess the relationships between sociodemographic factors (such as gender, program of study, and year of study) and perceived stress levels. The Perceived Stress Scale (PSS) score was calculated by summing the responses to all 10 items.

Results

In this study, 938 students from a Medical University in Ajman participated. The majority were aged 20 years or older (57.7%) and female (72.8%). Most students were in Year 2 (37.1%), followed by Year 1 (29.7%). The largest proportion was enrolled in the College of Medicine (47.4%). Participants were predominantly from the Southeast Asian Region (43%), followed by the Eastern Mediterranean Region (36.9%) (Table 1).

Discussion

The study examined the stress levels and coping strategies among healthcare students at a Medical University in Ajman, contributing to the body of research on psychological distress experienced by students in demanding academic settings. The findings align with numerous previous studies, emphasizing the widespread occurrence of stress in Health Professional Education and its association with various factors.

The present study found that 71.1% of students reported moderate stress, while 13.8% experienced high stress. These results corroborate findings from studies in Florida and Mangalore, India, where the majority of students experienced moderate stress levels [11, 25]. Similarly, a study from India highlighted that 91% of medical students reported high stress, underscoring the inherent academic pressure in medical education [14]. Furthermore, studies from China and Malaysia observed moderate to high stress levels, particularly during the later years of medical education, a trend reflected in this study.

Age differences in stress levels have been explored in various studies with mixed results. The study did not find a significant association between age and stress, aligning with other research suggesting that coping mechanisms, rather than age, might play a more crucial role in managing stress. Some studies, however, reported that older students tend to experience lower stress due to more developed coping mechanisms [33].

Gender differences in stress levels also remain a point of debate. Our study found no significant gender differences (p = 0.317), contrasting with studies from Spain and Saudi Arabia, which reported higher stress levels among female students [14, 34]. Interestingly, studies from Greece suggest that while females report greater emotional distress, males often exhibit higher levels of academic disengagement [26]. These findings suggest that the manifestation of stress may be more complex than simple gender differences and may depend on the support systems and coping strategies available.

Regarding year of study, this study showed no statistically significant difference in stress levels across academic years (p = 0.396). Despite this, Year 2 students reported the highest proportion of high stress (15.8%), while Year 4 students experienced the most low stress (20%). These findings differ from a study in India, where final-year students reported the highest stress levels, likely due to the increasing academic burden as graduation approaches [35].

Nationality differences played a significant role in perceived stress, with Southeast Asian Region (SEAR) students reporting higher levels of moderate stress (72.7%) and high stress (15.4%), suggesting that academic pressures, cultural adjustment issues, and family expectations might contribute to stress among international students. This finding aligns with research from Nepal, where students in similar contexts experienced high academic stress levels [36]. Moreover, the study population at GMU is predominantly expatriate, which may expose students to additional stressors such as cultural adaptation and uncertainty about residency and educational opportunities [37].

College affiliation was another key factor influencing stress levels. This study found that the students from the College of Medicine had the highest proportion of moderate stress (73.7%), reflecting the demanding nature of medical education and early clinical exposure. This result is consistent with findings from China, where medical students reported moderate to high stress levels [38]. Dental students at GMU, however, experienced a slightly more favorable stress profile compared to their counterparts in Saudi Arabia, where dental students reported higher levels of perceived stress [39].

Sleep disturbances, appetite loss, and feelings of being overwhelmed were common symptoms reported by students experiencing high stress, with 44.5% reporting difficulty sleeping, 42.5% appetite loss, and 44.1% feeling overwhelmed. These findings align with research linking stress to sleep disturbances and emotional exhaustion, which are commonly observed in Health professional students [18, 20]. Similarly, studies from Saudi Arabia and Nepal reported significant correlations between poor sleep quality and high stress levels [9, 16]. Furthermore, research from King Abdulaziz University highlights the high prevalence of sleep disturbances and psychological distress among healthcare students, reinforcing the need for targeted mental health interventions [40].

This study found that students primarily relied on social support from family, mentors, and friends to manage stress. This aligns with prior research emphasizing the protective effects of strong social networks in mitigating academic stress [13, 21, 34]. These findings further indicate that students who frequently sought guidance from mentors and friends had significantly lower stress levels, which supports studies that highlight the role of mentorship in reducing psychological distress among students [41, 42]. Additionally, parental and sibling support was associated with lower stress levels, mirroring previous research findings on the role of familial support in coping with academic pressures [15, 43]. The findings also indicate that variations in the frequency and consistency of utilizing coping strategies may influence stress levels, with irregular engagement in support systems potentially contributing to the predominance of moderate stress observed among students.

Studies in Spain found that students who employed structured coping mechanisms, such as goal setting and planning, reported significantly lower stress levels compared to those relying primarily on emotional support [34]. A study in Greece also found that students with higher emotional intelligence were more likely to adopt problem-focused coping mechanisms, leading to better academic performance and reduced stress [26]. This suggests that while emotional support provides short-term relief, long-term stress reduction may require active problem-solving approaches.

Given the high stress levels observed in this study, institutional interventions such as mentorship programs, stress management workshops, and peer counseling initiatives are critical in alleviating student distress. Previous studies have shown that structured wellness programs and faculty mentorship significantly reduce stress among healthcare students [12, 27]. Additionally, the significant association between mentorship and lower stress levels in this study reinforces findings that highlight the importance of faculty support in reducing student stress [14, 20]. Research from the United States suggests that integrating resilience training and mindfulness programs into medical curricula can improve students’ ability to handle stress, which could be beneficial for healthcare students experiencing high levels of psychological distress [15, 43].

Limitations

This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. Firstly, it was conducted at a single medical university in Ajman, limiting the generalizability of the results to other institutions with different academic structures and student demographics. Additionally, the reliance on self-reported data introduces the possibility of response biases, such as social desirability or recall bias, which may have affected the accuracy of the reported stress levels and coping strategies. The cross-sectional nature of the study provides only a snapshot of stress and coping mechanisms at one point in time, preventing an understanding of how these factors evolve throughout students’ academic journeys. Furthermore, the timing of data collection represents an important limitation, as it was conducted during the mid to late academic semester and overlapped with periods of increased academic demand, including assignment deadlines, class tests, and examination preparation. This may have influenced the reported stress levels. In addition, the study did not account for individual factors such as pre-existing psychological conditions or concurrent illnesses, which may have acted as confounding variables. Additionally, while the study explored social support as a coping mechanism (parental, sibling, mentor, and peer support), it did not assess other coping strategies, including problem-focused, self-leadership, or maladaptive strategies, which may have provided a more comprehensive perspective on stress management. Lastly, cultural factors play a significant role in stress perception and coping mechanisms, and since this study was conducted in a specific regional context, the findings may not be fully applicable to students in different cultural settings. Future research should incorporate longitudinal designs, diverse samples, and a broader range of stressors and coping mechanisms to provide a more comprehensive understanding of stress among healthcare students. Additionally, further studies within similar institutional settings are recommended to validate these findings and explore contextual variations in stress and coping strategies.

Conclusion

Our study confirms that stress is a prevalent issue among healthcare students and highlights the critical role of coping strategies in managing psychological distress. The findings emphasize the importance of structured institutional interventions, particularly mentorship programs and peer support, in fostering student well-being. Comparisons with prior research suggest that while social support is a key coping mechanism, self-leadership, problem-solving, time-management strategies, and workshops on stress management may be more effective in long-term stress management. Future research should further explore how these coping mechanisms evolve over time and assess the long-term effectiveness of institutional interventions in reducing stress among healthcare students.
