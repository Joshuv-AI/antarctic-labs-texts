Healthcare Inequity in Spinal Cord Injury: Out of Pocket and Access Barriers in Paraplegic and Tetraplegic Patients in Iran
Authors: Ali Alavi-Badabsari, Alireza Ahmadi-veshno, Seyyed Pouyan Zadparast, Delaram Fadaee Heydarabadi, Enayatollah Homaie Rad
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveSpinal cord injury (SCI) imposes substantial long-term physical, psychological, as well as financial burdens on patients in Iran, where high out-of-pocket (OOP) payments remain a major barrier to care. This study examined inequalities in healthcare utilization and OOP expenditures among patients with paraplegia and tetraplegia.MethodsThis cross-sectional analyzed data from the 2023 International Spinal Cord Injury Survey for Iran, including 191 patients from the Guilan Province. Data on healthcare utilization and OOP spending for consultations, rehabilitation, medications, diagnostics, mental health, and inpatient services were analyzed. Inequality was assessed using Gini and concentration indices, and further explored through decomposition analysis and recentered influence function regression.ResultsInpatient care accounted for the highest OOP cost (US$516.27/month), followed by rehabilitation (US$178.65/month). Mental health services were the least utilized and most inequitable, with a concentration index of 0.49, indicating pro-rich access. Education, marital status, and place of residence significantly influenced the disparities, with rural and less-educated patients experiencing greater barriers.ConclusionPatients with SCI in Iran face substantial financial inequities, and wealthier groups enjoy greater access to essential services. Policy reforms aimed at expanding insurance coverage, decentralizing rehabilitation, and subsidizing high-cost care are urgently required to promote equitable healthcare access and improve outcomes.Keywords: Spinal cord injuries, Health expenditures, Health equity, Health care utilization, Iran

INTRODUCTION

Spinal cord injury (SCI) is a harmful neurological disorder. It can induce many substantial impairments in somatosensory and autonomic functions.1) According to the World Health Organization, SCIs impact between 13 and 33 million individuals in the world, and around 250,000 to 500,000 new cases emerge each year.20) Over the last 30 years, its global prevalence has increased from 236 to 1,298 cases per million people.1) The data indicates that the incidence rate of traumatic SCIs in Iran is approximately 9 cases per 100,000 individuals. Furthermore, the prevalence of SCIs in Iran is about 318.45 cases per million people.20) Based on these reported data, SCI affects a large number of patients. To receive medical services, patients are required to pay fees such as physical rehabilitation services, medicine, and psychiatric care services.3) The amount of money that is directly paid by individuals is called out-of-pocket (OOP). The analysis of OOP plays a key role in health system policies and the performance of insurance. A lower amount of OOP reflects efficiency in the healthcare system. Based on the statistics from the World Health Organization, the rate of OOP for health is around 24% in the world. In many developing countries, OOP is the major way of financing healthcare, and this percentage increases to up to 50% in these countries.10) According to the World Health Report, equity in health systems, responsiveness and efficiency, and fairness in healthcare financing are essential goals. Understanding the numbers of OOP expenses helps us achieve these goals.15) For instance, OOP accounts for 67% of total healthcare system expenses in Pakistan, 44.9% in the Republic of Moldova, and 62.6% in India.11,19,21) In Ethiopia, many families are responsible for covering their own healthcare costs, which poses a significant health challenge. These OOP expenses account for approximately one-third (34%) of all healthcare spending, making it one of the highest rates in the world. This situation arises because a large number of people lack sufficient health insurance.2) Iran’s National Health Accounts show that OOP payments by families account for about 55% of the total financial resources used to fund the health system. Patients with SCIs experience long-term disabilities; as a result, these patients are frequent users of medical services. Therefore, it is important to gather detailed information about OOP payments. This information will provide health policymakers with a better understanding, helping them to make better decisions for planning and distributing resources effectively, especially for patients who are unable to afford the costs of treatment.10,13) In developing countries, healthcare resources are often insufficient, making it difficult for individuals to access the services they need. Furthermore, statistics show that the percentage of the global population spending more than 10% of their household income on healthcare rose from 9.4% in 2000 to 12.7% in 2015, totaling approximately 927 million people.18) When the payment system relies on OOP expenses, access to healthcare services becomes heavily influenced by individuals' economic status and income.5) One of the primary goals of international health policy is to implement a more equitable payment system than OOP expenses. When patients are unable to afford treatment costs, they may resort to cheaper and less effective options, such as traditional medicine. Alternatively, they might abandon their treatment altogether or fail to complete it.11) Furthermore, direct OOP payments for healthcare negatively impact how households allocate their income towards essential needs like food, housing, clothing, education, and other essential living necessities.9) As previously mentioned, SCI is a long-term condition that imposes significant financial burdens on affected patients. Understanding the OOP expenses incurred by patients with SCI is essential for policymakers to enhance funding and treatment processes for this population. This study aims to quantify OOP payments for healthcare services, particularly focusing on rehabilitation, psychiatric care, and medication among SCI patients in Iran. Additionally, it will analyze how these costs impact healthcare access and treatment adherence, while also addressing the equity challenges present in the current healthcare system.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

This was a cross sectional study. Data of International Spinal Cord Injury Survey for Iran (InSCI-IR) in 2023 was used in the study. The survey was conducted in Guilan province, Iran and contained information about 191 SCI patients who were faced with untreated paraplegia and tetraplegia. The data covered near 60% of all registered SCI patients in the province.

InSCI-IR data were based on the health information system of Poursina Hospital of Rasht, a referral center for traumatic SCI cases across Guilan Province. The registry includes comprehensive clinical and demographic data on patients with traumatic spinal injuries from an initial pool of 1,066 eligible patients between the years of 2015 and 2023.

A part of InSCI questionnaire focuses on both the utilization and payments of various health services. It covers consultation services such as visits to general practitioners, specialists, mental health services; diagnostic services including imaging and laboratory tests; medical treatment services such as surgeries, hospitalizations, bedsore treatment and acute trauma care; rehabilitation services like physiotherapy, occupational therapy hydrotherapy, and medication-related costs for both prescribed and non-prescribed drugs.

To measure inequity and inequality in utilization and OOP of services, two main indicators were used: Gini and concentration index. Both of these indicators are rooted in welfare economics and share a similar mathematical foundation; however, they differ in theoretical focus. The Gini coefficient originates from general income inequality theory and is based on the Pigou-Dalton principle of transfers, which emphasizes inequality in the distribution of OOP (or utilization of services) regardless of ability outcomes. In contrast, the concentration index is grounded in the theory of horizontal equity in health economics, focusing on how OOP or healthcare utilization are distributed across socioeconomic groups.

The Gini coefficient measures inequality in income distribution by plotting the Lorenz curve and comparing it to a line of perfect equality. It reflects how far the actual income distribution deviates from perfect equality, with values ranging from 0 (complete equality) to 1 (maximum inequality). It is widely used in economics to summarize income inequality in a single metric.

The concentration index, while mathematically similar, measures inequality in health variables ranked by socioeconomic status. Its values also range from −1 to +1, where 0 represents perfect equity in health distribution. The index reflects not only the level of inequality but also its direction—whether it favors the rich or the poor—making it particularly relevant in health equity research. The Lorenz and concentration curves visually depict inequality in the distribution of income and health-related variables, respectively. The closer the curve is to the 45-degree line (line of equality), the more equitable the distribution. A larger distance from this line indicates greater inequality. If the concentration curve lies below the equality line, the variable is concentrated among the wealthier; if it lies above, it favors the poorer groups. These curves help interpret both the magnitude and direction of inequality across socioeconomic strata.

To estimate the socioeconomic status of households, a wealth index was constructed using principal component analysis based on asset ownership data. Each household received a wealth score derived from a range of assets and amenities, including ownership of items such as a car, laptop, smartphone, television, computer, washing machine, refrigerator, microwave, vacuum cleaner, radio, air conditioner, bicycle, and motorcycle. Additional indicators included access to health and complementary insurance, internet connectivity, various sources of drinking water (e.g., piped, spring, bottled), and the number of rooms in the household. This index served as the basis for classifying households into different socioeconomic groups. To assess the extent and sources of inequality, we used the Blinder-Oaxaca decomposition method. This approach breaks down the observed differences in health outcomes between socioeconomic groups into explained and unexplained components. In this study, variables such as age, sex, education level, place of residence, trauma mechanism, and mode of transport to emergency care were included in the model to determine their individual contributions to the observed disparities. The share of each factor in driving inequality was calculated and reported as a percentage.

To analyze the equity of OOP expenditures and service utilization, all data were converted in monthly form. To calculate concentration index, the conindex command was employed using STATA SE v 17.1 (StataCorp LLC, College Station, TX, USA).12) As the utilization variables were bounded the Erreygers corrected concentration index was applied to ensure appropriate handling of the data’s bounded nature.4) To explore the socioeconomic determinants of inequality in OOP expenditures and service utilization, we employed the recentered influence function (RIF) regression approach. This method allows the estimation of the effects of explanatory variables on distributional statistics, such as the concentration index. Unlike traditional decomposition methods, RIF regression directly models the influence of covariates on the outcome’s inequality index, treating the concentration index as the dependent variable. The analysis was conducted using the rifreg command in Stata, which estimates the partial effects of each variable.16) The study received ethical approval from the ethics committee of Guilan University of Medical Sciences (ethics code: IR.GUMS.REC.1405.001). All monetary values were converted from Iranian Rials (IRR) to U.S. dollars (US$) using an exchange rate of 272,000 IRR per US$1, which was the prevailing rate at the time of the study.

RESULTS

TABLE 1 presents descriptive characteristics of 191 individuals with SCI. The majority of participants were male (n=149, 78.01%), while females comprised 21.99% (n=42). Most participants resided in urban areas (n=108, 56.54%), with the remainder living in rural settings (n=83, 43.46%). Regarding marital status, 61.26% (n=117) of the participants were married, 22.51% (n=43) were single, 8.38% (n=16) were widowed, and 7.85% (n=15) were separated. In terms of education, 13.09% (n=25) had no formal education. Primary and lower secondary education levels were reported by 21.47% (n=41) and 21.99% (n=42), respectively. Higher secondary education was less common (n=3, 1.57%). A notable proportion had post-secondary education (n=53, 27.75%) or higher education (n=27, 14.14%). Employment status revealed that only 35.08% (n=67) of the participants had paid employment before the SCI. Regarding the time of return to work after injury, a majority (n=112, 58.64%) never resumed work. Only 5.24% (n=10) returned immediately after injury, while 34.03% (n=65) resumed work after a delay of several months. The level of SCI was incomplete in 78.01% (n=149) and complete in 21.99% (n=42). The type of SCI was paraplegia in 64.92% (n=124) and tetraplegia in 35.08% (n=67). Regarding the cause of injury, traffic accidents were the most common cause (n=80, 41.88%), followed by falls from a height greater than one meter (n=94, 49.21%), work-related accidents (n=47, 24.61%), leisure activities (n=8, 4.19%), falls from less than one meter (n=11, 5.76%), sports (n=2, 1.05%), and violence (n=1, 0.52%). In terms of assistance, 73.30% (n=140) of participants reported having an assistant. The most common of the assistance were family members (n=137, 71.73%), followed by friends (n=31, 16.23%) and professional assistants (n=38, 19.90%). Only 2.09% (n=4) were institutionalized.

Regarding OOP expenditures, the concentration index was significantly positive for several categories. specifically, consultation services (0.09±0.04), drugs and medications (0.15±0.05), and mental health services (0.49±0.16) showed statistically significant pro-rich inequalities. inpatient and treatment services also demonstrated a substantial positive concentration index (0.41±0.24), suggesting a tendency toward higher OOP payments among the rich, although this association was only marginally significant. conversely, rehabilitation and diagnostic services showed no significant inequality in OOP.

In terms of service utilization, significant pro-rich inequalities were observed for rehabilitation (0.23±0.04), diagnostic services (0.12±0.05), and inpatient and treatments (0.27±0.08). These findings suggest that these services are disproportionately used by wealthier populations. In contrast, no significant inequality was detected for the utilization of consultation, mental health, or drug-related services. The decomposition analysis of the concentration index identified several significant contributors to inequality in both OOP expenditures and health service utilization among individuals with SCI (TABLE 5). In terms of OOP expenditures, age was significantly associated with both consultation and medication costs. Older age contributed to lower inequality in consultation expenditures (coefficient: −5.869±3.213) but to higher inequality in drug and medication costs (coefficient: 11.036±6.200). Educational level also showed a clear role: individuals with higher secondary education (coefficient: −528.140±252.355) and high education (coefficient: −294.870±177.843) had a significantly negative contribution to inequality in consultation costs. Moreover, those with post-secondary education significantly reduced inequality in mental health-related OOP expenditures (coefficient: −822.170±327.051). Marital status was also influential. Being married increased inequality in consultation expenditures (coefficient: 212.440±118.710), while it reduced inequality in drug and medication costs (coefficient: −312.100±138.888). In addition, widowed or separated individuals showed a negative contribution to inequality in medication costs (coefficient: −490.230±252.657). Regarding service utilization, higher levels of education were the most consistent contributors to inequality. Individuals with higher secondary education were associated with lower use of rehabilitation (coefficient: −445.780±267.918), diagnostic services (coefficient: −769.210±394.563), and inpatient care (coefficient: −2,250.900±444.259), thereby contributing to inequality in utilization. Similarly, post-secondary education was significantly associated with reduced use of rehabilitation services (coefficient: −291.130±136.535). The constant term in the models for rehabilitation (coefficient: 765.250±330.092) and inpatient services (coefficient: 1,608.760±684.906) was also significant, reflecting baseline inequality unexplained by the observed variables.

DISCUSSION

Reaching justice in healthcare for patients with chronic high-cost conditions is considered as one of the most significant challenges in health systems of developing countries. This study is aimed to discover Inequalities in healthcare access and expenditures in patients with SCI in an Iranian population in Guilan province. Our results highlight the systematic disparities in healthcare expenditures and access to services among these patients stemming from their socioeconomic variables.

One of the most concerning aspects of inequality within patients is significant OOP payment burden that accounts for a huge share of household’s healthcare finances. It’s been documented by Mirabedini et al.10) that OOP payments and unofficial payments make up around 50% and 35% of all health-related expenditures in Iran. It is also indicated in our study that the biggest share of these costs in SCI patients belongs to treatment and hospitalization services, followed by rehabilitation courses and medication expenses. These findings align with those already demonstrated by literature from Iran and Pakistan.11,14)

The detailed analysis of us indicated that the wealthier groups are favored in case of health expending inequalities, a result that can be confirmed by previous studies in Iran. These studies revealed that insurance participation is significantly regressive especially for low-income individuals. It means that a higher portion of household’s income should be spent for insurance fees and health expenses in lower socioeconomic groups.14) In contrast, a Switzerland based study showed that the sufficiency of insurance coverage plays a significant role in filling the OOP gap within different socioeconomic groups.6) This comparison emphasizes the role of insurance system in reaching equal access to health care services regardless of individual’s financial status and also the need of a structural reform in Iran’s healthcare system.

Many individuals with SCI struggle not only with physical challenges but also with serious mental health problems like depression, anxiety and post-traumatic stress disorder.23) However accessing to mental health services, which is important for their quality of life, is highly associated to socioeconomic level of households that leads to a significant access gap in favor of the individuals with higher ability to pay. These findings highlight the underestimation of mental related needs in physically injured population in our healthcare system. Although inequality in utilization of special services is still observed even in an advanced healthcare system like Switzerland, but accessing to primary healthcare is guaranteed regardless of the socioeconomic status in those systems.6) This difference indicates that Iran’s healthcare system faces evident challenges to provide accessible mental health support for SCI patients and OOP payments remains the leading barrier to reaching these services. This can result in severe consequences such as worsening physical health, increased length of treatment, isolation and elevated risk of suicide.

Another contributor to access and OOP inequality is geographical status of the patient. Accessibility of patients living in rural areas is limited due to the centralization of medical and rehabilitation structures in urban areas. This inequality aligns with findings of another study in Iran which showed the inequal distribution of human resources and medical structures in favor of urban areas. This situation obligates the rural patients to spend time and extra money to reach those specialized centers in major cities.17) Although the described problem is not exclusive to Iran and as what was showed in another study, rural patients in other vast countries like Canada are forced to travel within cities to seek specialized medical services.22)

Meanwhile, socioeconomic factors other than income play roles in the complexity of inequalities, a notable example of these in our study is the level of education. Rises in education level of patients result in reduction of inequalities in mental health services expenditures but also is related to lower usage rates of rehabilitation and hospital services. This pattern may represent lower needs of medical treatments in these individuals due to less severe injuries. Accordingly, some studies in Iran have shown that individuals with higher levels of education are more successful in cost reduction strategies as a result of better navigating the health care system. But this privilege may not necessarily means better access to specialized services by these individuals but instead a form of substitution with alternatives.8) Marital status is also an important social factor, married individuals experience greater equality gap in mental health related services but lower inequality in medication expenditures that can be caused by more financial support from family in medication costs. In addition, marital support in both forms of financial and emotional can lead to better management of medical expenditures especially in recurrent expenses such as medications. In contrast, in one-time services like consultations this support may not be sufficient or it may be in different patterns.

Compared to other chronic diseases, like Multiple Sclerosis, studies indicated catastrophic expenditures in those patients that highlights similarities with our results. Individuals with multiple sclerosis are also struggle with a great burden of expanses due to their chronic disease.7) These similarities underscore the necessity of systematic reforms in health policy. In order to achieve this, a reassessment of Health Transformation Plan is crucial to guarantee coverage of essential and high expenses services for patients.

Although this study presents important insights, it faces some limitations that should be considered when interpreting the results. The cross-sectional design of the study prevents the creation of a valid causal relationship between socioeconomic factors and health outcomes. In other words, it cannot be determined if poverty necessarily results in worsened healthcare condition or vice versa. Also focusing on a single province decrease the generalizability of the results. Because social, economic and cultural factors vary widely within different provinces of Iran, these findings may not represent the overall situation in the country. On the other hand, reliance on self-reported survey increases the risk of recall bias especially in small costs that can be easily forgotten. Although big OOP payments are unlikely to be missed. Additionally, this study did not fully examine informal payments even though they account for a significant share of financial burden in these cases.

CONCLUSION

This study highlights the considerable inequities in healthcare access and financial burden among SCI patients in Iran. OOP payments, particularly for inpatient treatment and rehabilitation services, remain the largest financial barrier, disproportionately affecting patients from lower socioeconomic groups. The pro-rich distribution of expenditures and utilization underscores systemic inefficiencies in the health financing structure, with rural residents and less-educated individuals facing the greatest disadvantages. Beyond financial costs, inequities in mental health service access reflect a critical gap in addressing the holistic needs of SCI patients. These findings emphasize the urgency of structural reforms to Iran’s healthcare system, including broader insurance coverage, financial risk protection, and decentralization of rehabilitation services. Policymakers must prioritize equitable financing and targeted interventions to reduce the catastrophic burden of OOP payments. Ensuring fair and affordable access to essential services is critical for improving health outcomes and quality of life among SCI patients.
