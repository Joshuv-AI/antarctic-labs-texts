Salinity-adapted and core microbiota along the soil salinity gradients encircling the Tarim Basin
Authors: Xiaojing Liu, Qiong Jia, Qiyong Tang, Yanpeng Ding, Zhidong Zhang, Meiying Gu, Wei Huang, Ning Wang, Lijuan Zhang, Jing Zhu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSoil salinization affects nearly one-tenth of global drylands and imposes strong environmental filters on microbial communities. The Tarim Basin in Xinjiang, China, is one of the world’s largest inland basins and an extreme saline-arid ecosystem, yet a basin-scale understanding of its microbiota remains lacking. In this study, we collected 132 soil samples along a salinity gradient spanning more than 3,000 km encircling the Tarim Basin. By combining amplicon sequencing with measurements of soil physicochemical properties, we aimed to unravel the salinity-adapted microbiota and core microbial taxa characteristic of this extreme environment.ResultsOur results showed that bacterial communities, rather than fungal communities, were strongly shaped by soil salinity. Extremely saline soils exhibited reduced bacterial diversity and were enriched in halophilic and halotolerant taxa, including Natronomonas, Salinimicrobium, Stenotrophomonas, Salinibacter, Halorussus, and Halomicrobium. In contrast, taxa such as Arthrobacter, Rubrobacter, Rubellimicrobium, and Fusarium declined with increasing salinity. Soil cation concentrations (Na+, K+, Ca2+, Mg2+), major anions (Cl−, SO42−), and electrical conductivity all increased significantly along the salinity gradient. Despite this strong environmental filtering, we identified a set of potential core microbiota consistently present across all soils, dominated by Halomonas, Salinimicrobium, Natronomonas, Rubrobacter, and members of the Palleronia-Pseudomaribius group. Their persistent occurrence across the basin suggests that these taxa play key roles in sustaining ecosystem functions within the extreme saline-arid environments of the Tarim Basin.ConclusionsOur basin-scale analysis reveals that soil salinity significantly contributes to microbial community assembly in the Tarim Basin, selecting for specialized halophilic and halotolerant taxa while reducing overall bacterial diversity. The discovery of core microbiota with known salt-adaptation mechanisms highlights the presence of a stable microbial backbone across heterogeneous saline landscapes. These findings provide new ecological insights into how extreme salinity shapes microbiome structure and identify candidate microbial groups potentially contributing to ecosystem stability and plant stress resilience in arid saline environments.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s40793-026-00918-3.

Introduction

Approximately one-tenth of the Earth’s dryland surface is affected by soil salinization, and this area is projected to increase globally by 6.7% by 2100 [1]. Saline soils are defined as non-sodic soils containing sufficient soluble salts to adversely affect the growth of most crop plants [2]. Beyond reducing agricultural productivity, salinization also threatens ecosystem functioning and land sustainability in arid and semi-arid regions worldwide [3]. Soil microorganisms play central roles in nutrient and biogeochemical cycling, where their life and death dynamics, together with stress-adaptive traits, shape the patterns of these cycles [4]. In particular, soil salinity can exert strong selective pressure on microbial communities, leading to the enrichment of taxa associated with salt tolerance, including those linked to halophilic enzyme strategies [5]. Although many studies have characterized microbiomes in saline soils, most have focused on agricultural lands [6, 7], coastal area [8, 9], or experimental manipulations of soil salinity [10]. Investigations of natural saline soils with minimal human influence, however, can provide valuable insights into how salinity acts as an environmental filter shaping microbial community composition.

The Tarim Basin, located in southern Xinjiang, is China’s largest inland river basin (> 1 million km2) and also the largest inland basin globally [11]. It is a hyper-arid region dominated by the Taklamakan Desert, where minimal precipitation (only 50–80 mm annually) and strong salinity create an extreme environment with sparse vegetation [11, 12], making it an ideal natural laboratory for studying the mechanisms of soil salinity formation and its effects on microbial communities in saline-arid ecosystems [13]. Most studies investigating microbial communities in the Tarim Basin have focused on specific habitats or processes, such as the Tarim River microbiome assembly dynamics [14], plant-associated microbiota [15], and microbial groups involved in nitrogen and carbon cycling [16], as well as the responses of soil microbiota to external disturbances [17]. However, due to the limited spatial scope of previous studies, a comprehensive understanding of the microbiome across the Tarim Basin, particularly the composition and functional potential of salt-adapted microbial taxa, remains lacking.

The core microbiota are defined as the group of microorganisms that are characteristic of a specific host or environment [18]. The quantification of the core microbiota often relies on abundance-occurrence methods, which identify taxa that exceed both a minimum relative abundance threshold and a minimum frequency of occurrence across samples, reflecting the idea that more abundant taxa tend to be more consistently present and ecologically stable within a community [18]. Because of their ecological significance, defining the core microbiota has been a major focus in various systems, including the pig gut [19], human gut [20], wetlands [21], grasslands [22], reforestation ecosystem [23]. Therefore, identifying the core microbiota in the Tarim Basin could provide valuable insights into the microbial taxa adapted to its extreme salinity and aridity, and their roles in maintaining ecosystem stability.

In this study, we investigated 44 sites encircling the core region of the Tarim Basin, including the Taklamakan Desert, covering a total distance of over 3,000 km (Fig. 1). At each site, three adjacent soil samples were collected as replicates, resulting in 132 soil samples in total. These samples span a salinity gradient from low to high, and were classified as non-saline, slightly saline, moderately saline, highly saline, and extremely saline according to the FAO standard [24]. We performed amplicon sequencing and measured the chemical properties of all soil samples. Specifically, we aimed to address two key hypotheses: (1) Increasing soil salinity acts as an environmental filter that enriches microbial taxa putatively associated with salt adaptation. (2) A consistent core microbiome, shaped by salinity-selective pressure, exists in the Tarim Basin and underpins key ecosystem functions.

Methods

Study site and soil sampling

From April to May 2023, soils were collected from 44 sites encircling the Tarim Basin, Xinjiang, China (N 36°49′-41°58′; E 75°08′-89°35′; altitude 813–1628 m) (Fig. 1 and Fig. S1). At each site, three soil replicates were taken, resulting in a total of 132 soil samples. At each site, the surface crust and loose sand were carefully removed before soil collection. Sterile stainless steel shovels and disposable gloves were used throughout to avoid cross-contamination. Approximately 5 kg of soil was collected from a depth of 0–20 cm after removing the top 2–3 cm of surface material. For each site, approximately 5 kg of soil was collected for the measurement of physicochemical properties (stored at room temperature), and 0.25 g was subsampled into Eppendorf tubes and stored at -20 °C for subsequent total DNA extraction.

Measurement of soil physicochemical properties

In total, twelve soil physicochemical properties were measured for each soil sample: sodium (Na), potassium (K), sulfate (SO42−), chloride (Cl−), bicarbonate (HCO3−), electrical conductivity (EC), pH, soil organic matter (SOM), salinity, available phosphorus (P), calcium (Ca), and magnesium (Mg). Sodium and potassium were determined using a flame photometer [25]. Sulfate was analyzed by the barium sulfate turbidimetric method [26], and chloride was measured using the Mohr method [27]. Bicarbonate was quantified by the acid-base titration method [28]. Soil EC and pH were measured using a conductivity meter and a pH meter, respectively. Soil organic matter was determined by the potassium dichromate oxidation-photometric method [29]. Soil salinity was analyzed using the gravimetric method [30]. Available phosphorus was determined by the molybdenum-antimony colorimetric method [31]. Calcium and magnesium were measured using the EDTA complexometric titration method [32]. Based on soil salinity content, the soils were classified into different salinity levels: samples with salt content less than 3 g/kg were defined as non-saline; 3–6 g/kg as slightly saline; 6–10 g/kg as moderately saline; 10–20 g/kg as highly saline; and greater than 20 g/kg as extremely saline.

DNA extraction and amplicon sequencing

Genomic DNA was extracted from 0.25 g of soil using the PowerSoil DNA Isolation Kit (Mo Bio Laboratories, Carlsbad, CA, USA) following the manufacturer’s instructions. The V3-V4 regions of the bacterial 16 S rRNA gene were amplified using primers 341 F (5′-CCTAYGGGRBGCASCAG-3′) and 806R (5′-GGACTACNNGGGTATCTAAT-3′), each tagged with a unique barcode. The fungal ITS2 region was amplified using primers ITS3 (5′-CTTGGTCATTTAGAGGAAGTAA-3′) and ITS4 (5′-GCTGCGTTCTTCATCGATGC-3′).

Each PCR reaction (30 µL total volume) contained 15 µL of Phusion® High-Fidelity PCR Master Mix (New England Biolabs, USA), 0.2 µM of each primer, and approximately 10 ng of template DNA. Thermal cycling conditions were as follows: initial denaturation at 98 °C for 1 min; 30 cycles of 98 °C for 10 s, 50 °C for 30 s, and 72 °C for 30 s; and a final extension at 72 °C for 5 min. PCR products were mixed with 1× loading buffer containing SYBR Green and visualized on a 2% agarose gel.

Amplicons with the expected size were mixed in equimolar ratios and purified. Sequencing libraries were then constructed and indexed using the NEB Next® Ultra™ II FS DNA PCR-free Library Prep Kit (NEB/E7430L). Library quality was assessed using a Qubit fluorometer and real-time PCR for quantification, and a bioanalyzer for size distribution. The libraries were pooled and sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform using a 2 × 250 bp paired-end sequencing cycle. Negative controls (no template) were included during DNA extraction and PCR amplification; these controls yielded no detectable amplicon bands on agarose gels, confirming the absence of significant kit or reagent contamination (Novogene Bioinformatics Technology Co., Ltd.).

Bioinformatic analysis

Raw paired-end reads were assigned to each sample based on unique barcodes, and the barcode and primer sequences were trimmed. Paired-end reads were then merged using FLASH (v1.2.11) [33], which efficiently merges overlapping paired-end reads generated from the same DNA fragment to produce raw tags. Quality filtering of the raw tags was performed using fastp (v0.23.1) [34] to obtain high-quality clean tags. Chimera sequences were identified by comparing the tags against reference databases, SILVA (16 S, version 138.2) and UNITE (ITS, version 10.0), using the UCHIME algorithm [35], and subsequently removed to generate the final set of effective tags. Amplicon Sequence Variants (ASVs) were obtained from the effective tags using the DADA2 module implemented in QIIME2 (v2022.2) to denoise the sequences. Taxonomic annotation of ASVs was performed in QIIME2 using the SILVA database for 16 S/18S sequences and the UNITE database for ITS sequences. For unclassified or irregular regions, taxonomic assignment was performed against the MicroNT sub-library, derived from the NCBI NT database and containing archaeal, fungal, viral, and bacterial sequences. The sequencing depth ranged from 42,630 to 182,795 reads for bacteria and from 38,085 to 248,757 reads for fungi. ASVs with fewer than 10 total reads were removed (Novogene Bioinformatics Technology Co., Ltd.).

Statistical analysis

Raw sequencing reads were normalized to relative abundance, which was used for all subsequent statistical analyses [36]. The effects of soil salinity and sampling location on bacterial and fungal community composition were evaluated using the PERMANOVA test and stratified PERMANOVA with sampling site stratified. Linear mixed model was applied to assess the effects of salinity level on bacterial and fungal diversity, as well as on soil physicochemical properties, with sampling site included as a random factor to account for the nested design (three replicates per site). For each salinity level, the average relative abundance of all phyla and genera was calculated, and the top 20 bacterial phyla, 15 fungal phyla, and the top 20 bacterial and fungal genera were presented. LEfSe analysis was performed to identify biomarker genera associated with each salinity level. The top 100 ASVs with the highest relative abundance were selected for Spearman correlation analysis with all soil physicochemical parameters [37]. P-values were adjusted using the false discovery rate (FDR) method, and ASVs significantly correlated with more than six parameters were visualized in a heatmap. Given the strong co-variation among soil physicochemical variables, the number of correlated parameters should be interpreted as an indicator of response to broader environmental gradients rather than independent associations (Fig. S2). Core microbiota were defined based on three criteria: (1) mean relative abundance > 0.01; (2) occurrence in more than 80 samples; and (3) no significant variation in relative abundance across salinity levels [18]. The core microbiota network was visualized using Gephi [38].

Results

Overall effects of soil salinity on microbial communities

Soil salinity explained R2 = 0.05 in both bacterial and fungal community composition. This effect was statistically significant based on PERMANOVA, but not significant when assessed using stratified PERMANOVA with permutations constrained within sampling sites (Fig. 2a, b and Fig. S4). Sampling location (R2 = 0.43 and 0.39, respectively) significantly influenced the composition of bacterial and fungal communities (Fig. 2a, b). In Fig. 2a and b, samples are colored by salinity level to illustrate the independent contribution of salinity beyond geographic distance, despite geography being the dominant factor. Soil salinity did not significantly influence either bacterial or fungal diversity (Fig. 2c, d and Fig. S5).

The dominant bacterial phyla were Pseudomonadota, Actinobacteriota, Firmicutes, Bacteroidota, Halobacterota, Chloroflexi, and Gemmatimonadota, with Halobacterota showing the highest relative abundance in extremely saline soils and the lowest in moderately and non-saline soils (Fig. 3a). Fungal communities were primarily composed of Ascomycota, followed by Basidiomycota (Fig. 3b).

Key microbial taxa of different soil salinity levels

For the dominant bacterial genera, extremely saline soils exhibited the highest relative abundance of Alcaligenes, followed by Stenotrophomonas and Achromobacter. Highly saline soils were dominated by Palleronia-Pseudomaribius, Achromobacter, and Gramella. In moderately saline soils, Ralstonia showed the highest relative abundance, followed by Escherichia-Shigella and Achromobacter. Slightly saline soils were enriched in Lactobacillus, Nitrosarchaeum, and Escherichia-Shigella. Non-saline soils were dominated by Nitrosarchaeum, Candidatus Nitroscosmicus, and Domibacillus (Fig. 3c).

For the dominant fungal genera, extremely saline soils showed the highest relative abundance of Lulwoana, Leptospora, and Stolonocarpus. Highly saline soils were dominated by Herpotrichia, Trichophaeopsis, and Berkeleyomyces. Moderately saline soils exhibited higher abundances of Collariella and Subramaniula. Slightly saline soils were enriched in Subramaniula, Gloeoporus, and Dimorphosporicola. Non-saline soils showed the highest relative abundance of Immersiella, Marcelleina, and Dimorphosporicola (Fig. 3d).

LEfSe analysis revealed that the biomarker bacterial genera for extremely saline soils were Natronomonas, Salinimicrobium, Stenotrophomonas, Bacteroides, Halorussus, Halomicrobium, and Salinibacter (Fig. 3e). The biomarkers for highly saline soils were Achromobacter, Halomonas, Marinobacter, Alifodinibius, Planococcus, and unidentified Actinomarinales. For moderately saline soils, the dominant biomarker genera were Rubrobacter, Tunicatimonas, Truepera, Pseudomonas, and Sphingobium. Slightly saline soils were characterized by Pontibacter, Lactobacillus, Sphingomonas, Microvirga, Arthrobacter, Massilia, and Rubellimicrobium. In non-saline soils, the biomarker bacterial genera were Tumebacillus, Adhaeribacter, Domibacillus, and MND1 (Fig. 3e).

ANCOM-BC analysis showed that, compared with non-saline soils, the top differentially abundant bacterial genera were as follows: in extremely saline soils, Bacillus and Marinobacter; in highly saline soils, Marinobacter, Parabacteroides, and Bacillus; in moderately saline soils, Niastella, Bacillus, and Natronomonas; and in slightly saline soils, Planococcus, Sphingoaurantiacus, Sphingomonas, and Truepera (Fig. S3a).

For fungi, LEfSe analysis the biomarker fungal genera for extremely saline soils were Cladosporium, Paraphoma, Malassezia, and Lachancea. Highly saline soils were characterized by Alternaria, Trichophaeopsis, Rhizophlyctis, Tausonia, Bathanamyces, Ascobolus, and Coprinopsis. Moderately saline soils showed enrichment of Naganishia, Collariella, Aureobasidium, and Alternaria. Slightly saline soils were dominated by Aspergillus, Stachybotrys, Spiromastigoides, Subramaniula, Oedocephalum, Diversispora, Dimorphosporicola, Iodophanus, Neocamarosporium, Curvibasidium, and Gloeoporus. Non-saline soils were characterized by Stolonocarpus, Monosporascus, Herpotrichia, Lasiodiobolium, Cephalophora, Talaromyces, Canariomyces, Mortierella, Claroideoglomus, Podospora, Clitopilus, and Coniochaetis (Fig. 3f).

ANCOM-BC analysis showed that, compared with non-saline soils, the top differentially abundant fungal genera were as follows: in extremely saline soils, Cephaliophora, Lachancea, Oedocephalum, and Alternaria; in highly saline soils, Paraphoma, Neocosmospora, and Alternaria; in moderately saline soils, Oedocephalum, Lophiostoma, Mortierella, and Alternaria; and in slightly saline soils, Lophiostoma, Cladosporium, and Alternaria (Fig. S3b).

Soil physicochemical properties and their highly correlated microbial taxa

Given the strong collinearity among salinity-related variables (Na+, Ca2+, K+, Mg2+,Cl−, SO42−, EC), we used EC and total salinity as representative proxies for the salinity gradient in the main analyses. The other variables showed consistent patterns and support the same conclusions. Overall, the concentrations of major cations (Ca2+, K+, Mg2+, and Na+), anions (Cl− and SO42−), and electrical conductivity (EC) decreased significantly from extremely saline to non-saline soils (Fig. 4a). In the extreme saline condition, these parameters, such as Ca2+, K+, Mg2+, EC, Na+, SO42−, and salt levels, were at least three times higher compared to other salinity conditions (Fig. 4a). Soil pH decreased significantly with increasing salinity (Fig. 4). In contrast, soil organic matter (SOM) were markedly higher in non-saline and slightly saline soils than in soils with higher salinity (Fig. 4a). The concentrations of HCO3− and soil phosphorus (P) were not significantly influenced by soil salinity level (Fig. 4a). Accordingly, these two parameters were not significantly correlated with other soil properties. In contrast, Ca2+, K+, Mg2+, Na+, Cl−, SO42−, and EC were all significantly positively correlated with each other, and were significantly negatively correlated with soil pH (Fig. S2).

ASV47 (unidentified Halomicrobiaceae), ASV31 (Natronomonas), and ASV39 (Natronomonas) were significantly negatively correlated with soil concentrations of Mg2+, SO42−, Cl−, total salt, EC, Na+, and SOM, but positively correlated with soil pH (Fig. 4b). In contrast, ASV9 (Arthrobacter), ASV41 (unidentified Micrococcaceae), ASV5 (unidentified Micrococcaceae), fungal ASV5 (Stachybotrys), ASV42 (Rubrobacter), ASV78 (unidentified Actinobacteria), ASV48 (Rubrobacter), ASV76 (Rubellimicrobium), ASV105 (unidentified Planococcaceae), and fungal ASV6 (Fusarium), as well as overall bacterial diversity, exhibited opposite correlation patterns to the ASVs described above (Fig. 4b).

Potential core microbiota across all soils

A set of microbial taxa were consistently detected across all soils with different salinity levels. Among these, 34.21% belonged to Pseudomonadota, including ASV79 (Palleronia-Pseudomaribius), ASV72 (Pseudomonas), ASV71 (Halomonas), ASV7 and ASV1 (Escherichia-Shigella), ASV62 (Devosia), ASV61 (Palleronia-Pseudomaribius), ASV6 and ASV24 (Halomonas), ASV38 (Idiomarina), ASV23 (unidentified Gammaproteobacteria), ASV2 (Ralstonia), and ASV3 (Stenotrophomonas). 23.68% were affiliated with Bacteroidota, including ASV91 and ASV87 (unidentified Balneolaceae), ASV51, ASV49, ASV44, ASV4, ASV22, and ASV17 (Salinimicrobium), as well as ASV25 (unidentified Rhodothermaceae). 21.05% were assigned to Actinobacteriota, including ASV92 and ASV74 (Rubrobacter), ASV77, ASV58, and ASV19 (unidentified Nitriliruptoraceae), ASV35 (Kocuria), ASV26 (unidentified Actinobacteria), and ASV15 (unidentified Actinomarinales). 10.53% were affiliated with Halobacterota, including ASV47 (unidentified Halomicrobiaceae), and ASV39, ASV31, and ASV29 (Natronomonas). 5.26% belonged to Firmicutes, including ASV40 and ASV21 (unidentified Bacillaceae). Additionally, ASV90 (PAUC43f_marine_benthic_group, Gemmatimonadota) and ASV83 (Truepera, Deinococcota) were also identified as shared taxa across all soil salinity levels (Fig. 5, Table S1).

Discussion

Soil salinity reduces bacterial diversity while elevating cation concentrations

Our results showed that with increasing soil salinity, the concentrations of cations (Ca2+, K+, Mg2+, and Na+) and anions (Cl− and SO42−) increased significantly. This pattern is consistent with previous studies reporting that saline irrigation enhances cation uptake by plants [39]. Similarly, Yan et al. [40] found that saline-water irrigation markedly increased soil cation concentrations, including Na+, Ca2+, and Mg2+, and that excessive Na+ accumulation could further deteriorate soil structure, such as by promoting crack formation. In contrast, Ca2+ accumulation was shown to enhance soil aggregation [40]. Other studies have also demonstrated that higher soil salinity not only increases cation concentrations but also reduces soil SOM concentrations [41]. However, in our study, the soils with the highest salinity exhibited the highest SOM content. This discrepancy may stem from differences in ecosystem context: Chen et al. [41] examined cultivated cotton fields, where elevated salinity degraded soil structure and accelerated SOM loss. In contrast, our study focused on natural saline soils in the Tarim Basin, where high salinity likely suppressed microbial metabolic activity, thereby reducing SOM decomposition and leading to its accumulation. However, alternative factors may also contribute to the observed relationship between salinity and SOM, including differences in soil texture or sediment composition, as well as spatial confounding (e.g., unmeasured environmental gradients associated with geographic distance).

High osmotic stress and ion toxicity can adversely affect soil microorganisms by disrupting membrane homeostasis, altering enzyme conformation, and disturbing the intracellular K+/Na+ balance [42]. These stresses can lead to reductions in soil microbial diversity and biomass [43]. Previous studies examining the effects of soil salinity on microbial diversity have also reported that increasing salinity significantly decreases both bacterial and fungal diversity [44, 45]. Most studies conducted in the Tarim Basin have focused on the relationships between soil salinity and environmental factors rather than on microbial responses [11, 46, 47]. Another study in the Tarim Basin found that soil salinity did not significantly influence soil microbial diversity; however, this could be attributed to the small sample size (n = 18), which may not have adequately captured the spatial heterogeneity of the region [16]. By analyzing 132 samples collected across the Tarim Basin, our study provides a more comprehensive assessment, revealing that bacterial, but not fungal, diversity decreases with increasing soil salinity. We acknowledge that geographic location (sampling site) was the dominant factor shaping microbial community composition; nonetheless, salinity acted as an additional environmental filter independent of spatial structure.

Stenotrophomonas, Salinibacter, Marinobacter, Pleosporales, and Sordariales show increased relative abundance in extremely saline soils

Our results showed that the overlapping microbial taxa between the top abundant bacterial genera and the biomarker bacteria in extremely saline soils included Stenotrophomonas and Salinibacter. Stenotrophomonas has been widely reported to enhance plant salt tolerance in crops such as Arachis hypogaea [48], tomato [49], and soybean [50]. This may be attributed to the high salt tolerance of Stenotrophomonas spp., which can retain proteolytic activity even under 3% NaCl [51]. The primary mechanism is the accumulation of compatible solutes such as trehalose or glucosylglycerol [52, 53]. In addition, the diffusible signal factor (DSF) quorum-sensing system in Stenotrophomonas spp. can further enhance the accumulation of osmoprotectants, including glucosylglycerol and trehalose, under saline conditions [54, 55]. When comparing with non-saline soils, Marinobacter was among the top five differentially abundant bacterial ASVs across all saline soils. This finding is consistent with numerous studies reporting that this genus is commonly found in saline environments [56, 57].

Salinibacter was another genus strongly selected under high soil salinity. In contrast to the moderate salt tolerance of Stenotrophomonas spp., Salinibacter can withstand far higher salinities (15–30% NaCl), making it a true extremely halophilic bacterium [58]. This genus also shares several traits with halophilic archaea, likely due to horizontal gene transfer, acquiring archaeal-like features such as an acidic proteome, specialized ion transporters, and gene exchange systems that function effectively in hypersaline environments [59]. As a result, Salinibacter spp. are widely distributed across hypersaline environments worldwide, including salt fields and crystallizer ponds [60]. Their ability to thrive under such extreme salinity is associated with a “salt-in” strategy, whereby cells accumulate inorganic ions [61], structural modifications of the DNA polymerase III β-subunit that maintain replication stability in high-salt conditions [62], and the regulatory flexibility to shift between “salt-in” and “salt-out” osmoregulatory strategies when exposed to fluctuating salinity [63]. This has been demonstrated in a study conducted in the hyper-arid Atacama Desert, where metagenomic sequencing revealed that Salinibacter spp. did employ a salt-in strategy by accumulating intracellular KCl to balance osmotic pressure [64].

Overall, in our study, bacteria were more sensitive to soil salinity than fungi, as there was no overlap between the major fungal genera and the fungal biomarkers identified by LEfSe analysis. Two fungal orders, Pleosporales and Sordariales (such as Alternaria as shown by ANCOM-BC analysis), showed higher relative abundances in soils with extreme salinity. Species belonging to Pleosporales have frequently been isolated from saline environments, including saline–alkali soils [65], saline lakes [66], and salt marshes [67]. Species from Sordariales have also been detected in salt-affected habitats, such as halophytes in desert ecosystems [68] and hypersaline lagoons [69]. However, not all species within Pleosporales or Sordariales are salt-tolerant or halophilic, many exhibit only limited halotolerance or are present in these environments due to ecological, rather than physiological, adaptations.

Natronomonas was positively correlated with soil salinity, while Arthrobacter, Stachybotrys, Rubrobacter, Rubellimicrobium, and Fusarium were negatively correlated

Among the three ASVs that positively co-varied with soil salinity, two belonged to Natronomonas spp. Natronomonas is a genus of typical halophilic archaea [70]. The mechanisms of Natronomonas have evolved to cope with extreme salinity by adopting a classic salt-in strategy, maintaining high intracellular salt concentrations stabilized by highly acidic, low-pI proteins that remain functional in hypersaline cytoplasm [71, 72]. In addition, Natronomonas possesses ether-based membrane lipids and a complex glycosylated cell envelope-featuring Thr-rich repeat proteins and abundant lipid-anchored surface proteins, that together protect the cell against salt- and alkali-induced destabilization [73]. It also produces the compatible osmolyte 2-sulfotrehalose and encodes extensive ion and nitrogen transport systems to overcome metal and nutrient limitations in soda lakes, enabling this archaeon to thrive in high-salt, high-pH environments [72].

In contrast, Arthrobacter, Stachybotrys, Rubrobacter, Rubellimicrobium, and Fusarium were negatively correlated with increasing soil salinity. Arthrobacter species are known to occur in arid environments [74] and can adopt a coccus morphology to withstand desiccation stress [75]. Stachybotrys, in contrast, is a moisture-dependent saprotrophic fungus typically found in wet or water-damaged environments [76, 77]. Rubrobacter has been identified as a biomarker of land-use history in a large-scale biogeographic study in China [78]. Rubellimicrobium commonly inhabits nutrient-rich soils associated with plants [79]. Fusarium is a well-known saprotrophic pathogenic fungus, capable of producing mycotoxins and acting as an opportunistic human pathogen [80]. Together, their ecological preferences for moist, nutrient-rich, or moderate-stress environments likely explain their decline under high salinity, where osmotic stress and reduced resource availability diminish their competitiveness.

Salinimicrobium, Natronomonas, and Halomonas as potential core microbiota of the Tarim Basin

In this study, we used a correlation-based co-occurrence network. Based on the topological structure, highly correlated sets of microbes were defined as the core. Before proceeding with the discussion, it is important to note that this core was defined using relative abundance, and therefore may not fully represent the true ecological function. It should also be noted that our core microbiota definition relied on sample occurrence rather than site-level prevalence; the nested sampling design (three replicates per site) may have influenced the identification of core taxa, and future studies incorporating site-level prevalence thresholds could provide a more robust assessment. We also acknowledge that despite the inclusion of negative controls, trace levels of contamination from kits or reagents cannot be entirely excluded, particularly in low-biomass samples; however, the consistent detection of core taxa across high-biomass samples and their ecological relevance to saline environments support their authenticity. Six ASVs within the core microbiota belonged to Salinimicrobium spp. (Table S1). The presence of Salinimicrobium in Xinjiang Province has been reported in previous studies [81–83]. However, despite being a halophilic bacterium, its relative abundance was not significantly affected by soil salinity and remained consistent across all soils in our study. One possible explanation is that Salinimicrobium inhabits a wide range of saline environments, including saline soils [84], seawater [85], and salt lakes [86], and therefore may not be particularly sensitive to salinity fluctuations.

Another core genus is Halomonas, a moderately halophilic bacterium (0.5–2.5 M NaCl) with highly versatile metabolic capabilities, including nitrate respiration and nitrogen fixation under low-nutrient conditions [87, 88]. It also secretes exopolysaccharides and forms biofilms, which enhance its tolerance to environmental stresses such as drought, radiation, and temperature fluctuations [88]. Whole-genome sequencing further revealed that Halomonas possesses genes involved in compatible solute biosynthesis, ion transport and pH regulation, and plant hormone production, all of which can contribute to improving plant salt tolerance [89].

Last but not least, Natronomonas spp. were also identified as core microbiota; however, as discussed earlier, the ASVs assigned to this genus showed strong variation along the soil salinity gradient. This apparent contradiction is likely due to the use of amplicon sequencing, which provides only genus-level taxonomic resolution. Different Natronomonas species exhibit markedly different salinity tolerances. For example, Natronomonas pharaonis is highly sensitive to salinity shifts and grows optimally at 3.5–4.5 M NaCl [71], whereas Natronomonas halophila has a much lower optimal range of 1.0-3.5 M NaCl [90]. Taken together, the shared traits of these core taxa suggest that salinity acts as an important environmental filter shaping the microbiome of the Tarim Basin. Given that hypersaline systems are often strongly associated with archaeal communities, a dedicated archaeal-focused analysis represents an interesting future direction to further unravel the ecological roles of halophilic archaea in the Tarim Basin (Fig. S5).
