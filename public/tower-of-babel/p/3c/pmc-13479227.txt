Risk of neurodevelopmental disorders associated with paternal use of valproate during spermatogenesis
Authors: Neda Razaz, Jonas Soderling, Torbjörn Tomson, Julie Werenberg Dreier, Jakob Christensen, Marte-Helene Bjørk, Jannicke Igland
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundRegulatory authorities have raised concerns about potential teratogenic and neurodevelopmental disorders (NDDs) risks associated with paternal valproate use during spermatogenesis, leading to restrictions. We aimed to investigate the association between paternal valproate exposure during spermatogenesis and the risk of NDDs in offspring in Sweden and Norway.MethodsWe conducted a population-based cohort study of two nationwide cohorts from Sweden and Norway, including all singleton live births (≥22 gestational weeks) from 2007 to 2020. Paternal monotherapy exposure to valproate, lamotrigine or levetiracetam was defined as ≥1 prescription filled for only one of these medications during the 3 months prior to conception. NDDs were identified from health registers from age 1 through 2022. Cox proportional hazards models were used to estimate adjusted HRs (aHRs), accounting for relevant confounders. A random-effects meta-analysis combined results from both countries.ResultsThe Swedish cohort included 1588 children with paternal valproate and 3093 with lamotrigine/levetiracetam monotherapy, while the Norwegian cohort included 463 and 1109, respectively. Compared with paternal lamotrigine/levetiracetam monotherapy, paternal valproate exposure was not associated with increased pooled risk of any NDD (aHR, 1.06; 95% CI 0.81 to 1.22), including autism spectrum disorder (aHR, 1.29; 95% CI 0.89 to 1.85), attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (aHR, 0.98; 95% CI 0.76 to 1.25), intellectual disability (aHR, 1.20; 95% CI 0.65 to 2.21; Sweden only) or psychological development disorders (aHR, 1.28; 95% CI 0.84 to 1.97). Findings remained consistent in dose–response analyses and when restricted to fathers with epilepsy.ConclusionsIn this large Nordic study, paternal valproate use during spermatogenesis was not associated with increased risk of NDDs in offspring, suggesting current regulatory restrictions may warrant re-evaluation.Keywords: EPILEPSY, NEUROEPIDEMIOLOGY, EPIDEMIOLOGY

WHAT THIS STUDY ADDS

In this population-based cohort study of 2051 children born to fathers who used valproate monotherapy during spermatogenesis in Sweden and Norway, no significant association was found between paternal valproate use and neurodevelopmental disorders, including autism spectrum disorder, attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorders, intellectual disability or psychological development disorders, compared with paternal use of lamotrigine or levetiracetam monotherapy.

Introduction

Epilepsy affects at least 45–50 million people globally and accounts for over 0.5% of disease burden.1 Valproate is a highly effective treatment for epilepsy,2 3 particularly idiopathic generalised epilepsies, but it is also used for other indications, such as migraine and mood stabilisation. Due to growing evidence of its teratogenic effects,4 the European Medicines Agency (EMA) restricted its use in pregnant women and women of reproductive age in 2014,5 with stricter regulations introduced in 2018.6

Given valproate’s established teratogenicity following maternal exposure and results from some preclinical studies,7 concern has also emerged about potential male-mediated reproductive toxicity. Recently, the UK Medicines and Healthcare Products Regulatory Agency (MHRA) and the EMA’s Pharmacovigilance Risk Assessment Committee issued precautionary guidance for valproate use in men.8 9 The recommendations were based on an EMA-commissioned observational study using Scandinavian register data. The IQVIA study by Colas et al. suggested a 50% increased risk of neurodevelopmental disorders (NDDs) in offspring of men treated with valproate monotherapy during spermatogenesis (ie, the 3 months before conception) compared with those exposed to lamotrigine or levetiracetam.9 10 Although it was noted that this association may be confounded by the underlying indication11 and causality could not be established, the MHRA and EMA have nonetheless issued precautionary measures on the use of valproate in males.

A recent systematic review, motivated by the EMA-commissioned study, examined six population-based studies on paternal antiseizure medication (ASM) exposure and NDDs and found no evidence of increased risk.12 Two high-quality studies from Sweden (2006–2016)13 and Denmark (1997–2017)14 included in this review partially overlapped with cohorts used in the EMA study (Denmark from 1997 until 2018 and Sweden from 2006 until 2018); notably, the large Danish study, based on a subset of the same data, also found no increased risk of NDDs.11 14 Human studies on teratogenesis are observational and prone to selection bias and confounding by unmeasured variables; therefore, findings must be replicated to be reliable. While such evidence exists for valproate use in women,4 it is lacking for behavioural teratogenesis from valproate exposure in men. To address these conflicting findings, we aimed to investigate the association between paternal valproate monotherapy during spermatogenesis and the risk of NDDs in offspring in Sweden and Norway.

Methods

Study design and study population

We conducted a population-based cohort study of two nationwide cohorts from Sweden and Norway of all singleton live births at ≥22 completed gestational weeks identified in the Medical Birth Registers, between 1 January 2007 and 31 December 2020, in Sweden and between 1 January 2010 and 31 December 2018, in Norway. We excluded children with implausible gestational age (≤154 or ≥315 days), no registered father in the Medical Birth Registers, or who died or emigrated before 1 year of age. We also excluded children whose mothers used valproate from 30 days before the last menstrual period (LMP) to birth, those whose fathers were exposed to more than one ASM during spermatogenesis or had no exposure to valproate, lamotrigine or levetiracetam. This left 4681 children for analysis in Sweden and 1572 in Norway (figure 1).

Data sources

Swedish data sources

The Swedish Medical Birth Register has recorded antenatal, obstetric and neonatal care data for over 98% of births in Sweden since 1973.15 The National Patient Register includes inpatient care from 1987 and outpatient specialist care from 2001, with diagnoses coded using International Classification of Diseases, Ninth Revision (ICD-9) (1987–1996) and ICD-10 (from 1997).16 The Prescribed Drug Register contains data on all dispensed medications nationwide, classified by the Anatomical Therapeutic Chemical (ATC) system, since 1 July 2005.17 The Multi-Generation Register links individuals born in Sweden since 1932 (and residing in the country since 1961) to their biological or adoptive parents.18 Sociodemographic data were obtained from the Total Population Register and the Education Register.19

Norwegian data sources

The Norwegian Medical Birth Registry includes data on births in Norway since 1967. Registration of pregnancies in the registry is mandatory for all pregnancies with gestational length ≥12 weeks. The registry includes the personal identification number for the mother and the father, information on the mother’s cohabitation status, the mother’s health before and during pregnancy, complications during pregnancy and childbirth and the child’s vital status and health up to 1 year after birth. The Norwegian Patient Registry includes data on all inpatient and outpatient contacts in specialist healthcare, and from 2008 onwards, with diagnostic codes coded according to ICD-10.20 21 The Norwegian Prescribed Drug Registry contains information on all dispensed prescriptions in Norwegian pharmacies from 2004 and onwards. Each dispensed prescription includes information on the active substances, coded according to the ATC system and the amount of the drug dispensed. For reimbursed prescriptions, the indication for use is coded according to ICD-10 codes if the drug has been prescribed in specialist healthcare and International Classification of Primary Care version 2 codes if the drug has been prescribed in primary care. Information on death and emigration during follow-up was obtained from Statistics Norway.

Medication exposure during spermatogenesis

Paternal monotherapy with valproate, lamotrigine or levetiracetam was defined as filling ≥1 prescription for only one of these medications (ATC codes: N03AG01, N03AX09, N03AX14) from 120 days before the first day of the LMP to LMP+14 days (ie, covering spermatogenesis and accounting for early prescriptions), without filling of prescriptions for other ASMs (ATC: N03A or N05BA09) during this period. The mean daily valproate dose was calculated by dividing the total milligrams dispensed in the exposure period by the number of days in the exposure period and was categorised as high dose (>750 mg/day) or low dose (≤750 mg/day).

Neurodevelopmental disorders in the child

Offspring diagnoses of NDDs from the first birthday were extracted from the National Patient Register in both Sweden and Norway. Using the same definition as in the Colas et al. study, NDD included intellectual disability (ID: ICD-10: F70–F79), psychological development disorders (F80–F83), autism spectrum disorder (ASD: F84, excluding F84.2–F84.4), and attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD; F90.0, F98.8).10

Offspring were considered to have the NDD if they had at least one recorded diagnosis (based on the listed diagnostic codes). To reduce the risk of misclassification, diagnoses of NDD made during the first year of life were disregarded, as children under 1 year old are rarely diagnosed with NDD. Therefore, the study follow-up began at 1 year of age.

Covariates

For each child, we collected data on birth year, sex, maternal early pregnancy body mass index (BMI, kg/m²), parental education level (Sweden only), smoking during pregnancy, parental cohabitation, parental age at birth and parental history of epilepsy and psychiatric disorders. Gestational age was estimated primarily using early second-trimester ultrasound (>88%), with alternatives being the LMP (7%) or postnatal assessment (5%). Maternal and paternal age at delivery were calculated as the date of delivery minus the parents’ birth date. Information on cohabitation with the child’s father was obtained at the first antenatal visit. Mothers were classified as smokers if they reported daily smoking at the first antenatal visit or at 30–32 gestational weeks; non-smokers reported no smoking at both points. BMI was calculated using weight measured at antenatal care registration and self-reported height, categorised according to WHO guidelines.22 In Sweden, paternal epilepsy was defined as a diagnosis recorded before 120 days prior to the mother’s LMP using ICD-9 code 345 (excluding 345.29) or ICD-10 code G40. In Norway, paternal epilepsy was additionally identified using the Medical Birth Register and by the presence of reimbursement codes for epilepsy in ASMs prescriptions before 120 days prior to the LMP. Psychiatric disorders were identified as any diagnosis of mental disorders (ICD-9: 290–315; ICD-10: F00–F99) recorded before LMP−120 days. Information on parental history of epilepsy and psychiatric disorders was obtained from the patient registers, available from 1978 in Sweden and from 2008 in Norway. Psychotropic medication use was identified based on prescriptions filled 120 days before LMP to LMP+14 days (ATC codes: N05A, N05B (excluding N05BA09), N06A, N06BA01, N06BA02, N06BA04, N06BA09, N06BA11, N06BA12).

Statistical analysis

We compared the baseline characteristics of children born to fathers using valproate monotherapy vs lamotrigine/levetiracetam monotherapy. Using Cox proportional hazards regression, we estimated HRs with 95% CIs for NDDs, comparing valproate-exposed children with those exposed to lamotrigine/levetiracetam. Dose-event analyses were also conducted with comparison of high dose and low dose of valproate vs any dose of lamotrigine/levetiracetam. Follow-up for each NDD began at age 1 year and continued until NDD diagnosis, death, emigration or study end (31 December 2022, in Sweden, and 31 December 2019, in Norway). To account for potential clustering among siblings, we used clustered robust standard errors by paternal ID. Adjustments were made for child sex, birth year, maternal cohabitation status and parental characteristics (age at birth, psychiatric history, psychotropic medication use and epilepsy diagnosis). In Sweden, we additionally adjusted for parental education level (unavailable in Norway). Analyses were performed separately for Sweden and Norway, and combined using random-effects meta-analysis via the inverse variance method with restricted maximum likelihood. In addition, we restricted our main analysis to the cohort of fathers with epilepsy and compared valproate-exposed children to lamotrigine/levetiracetam-exposed children within this group.

Data analysis was conducted using SAS V.9.4 for Sweden and Stata V.18.0 for Norway and the meta-analysis. All p values were two-sided, with statistical significance set at p<0.05. No adjustments were made for multiple comparisons, so results are considered exploratory. The analysis was performed in May 2025.

Results

The Swedish cohort included 1595 live births to fathers using valproate monotherapy and 3097 exposed to lamotrigine or levetiracetam monotherapy during spermatogenesis. The Norwegian cohort included 463 exposed to valproate and 1109 to lamotrigine or levetiracetam.

The characteristics of children born exposed to paternal use of valproate for both Sweden and Norway are shown in table 1. Compared with fathers receiving lamotrigine or levetiracetam monotherapy, fathers receiving valproate monotherapy were on average slightly older at the time of birth (larger proportion >40 years), more likely to live without a partner, had a higher frequency of epilepsy, but a lower prevalence of psychiatric diagnoses and use of psychotropic medication (table 1).

Discussion

In this population-based study from Sweden and Norway, including more than 2000 fathers using valproate in monotherapy in the spermatogenic period, no evidence of a substantially increased risk of any NDDs, including ASD, ADHD, intellectual disability or disorders of psychological development, compared with children of fathers using lamotrigine/levetiracetam, was found. These findings remained consistent across dose-event analyses and when restricting the analysis to fathers with epilepsy.

Therefore, despite a substantial overlap in study population and the use of similar methodologies, our estimates were not in accordance with the increased risk reported in the Colas et al. study.10 In our study, the aHR of NDDs following paternal valproate use was 1.03 (95% CI 0.82 to 1.30) in Sweden and 1.20 (95% CI 0.70 to 2.04) in Norway, with a combined estimate of 1.06 (95% CI 0.85 to 1.30), compared with offspring exposed to paternal lamotrigine/levetiracetam. In comparison, the Colas et al. study, which included 841 children from Sweden and 325 from Norway exposed to paternal use of valproate during spermatogenesis, reported higher risks (aHR, 1.54 (95% CI 0.95 to 2.51) and 1.76 (95% CI 0.83 to 3.71), respectively), with a pooled estimate that also included Danish data of 1.50 (95% CI 1.09 to 2.07). There are several methodological differences between our study and the study by Colas et al, including the definition of outcome, exposure window, adjustment method and covariate selection. That study applied a narrower exposure window, starting 3 months (ie, 3×28 days period) prior to LMP+14 (equivalent to 70 days prior to LMP), while our study included prescriptions up to 120 days prior to LMP as exposure in spermatogenesis. A wider exposure window (as in our study) could potentially dilute risk estimates, particularly if fathers stopped using valproate well before conception. However, they study by Colas et al. also considered prescriptions dispensed up to 6 months prior to LMP, if the calculated duration of use overlapped with the 3-month window. Therefore, differences in exposure definitions are unlikely to explain the weaker associations observed in our results. Additionally, we employed a stricter definition of neurodevelopmental disorders (ASD, ID, ADHD and psychological development disorders), whereas they study by Colas et al. included broader conditions such as dyslexia, tic disorders, hyperkinetic disorders and dystonias. However, a sensitivity analysis from Colas et al. using a stricter definition yielded results similar to their primary analysis.9 10,9,10

For ASD specifically, our pooled risk estimate was (aHR 1.29; 95% CI 0.89 to 1.85), whereas Colas et al. reported a significantly higher estimate (aHR 2.70; 95% CI 1.19 to 6.17, Sweden only).10 The reason for this discrepancy is unclear but may relate to differences in adjustment strategies. Notably, the regression-adjusted pooled estimate for NDD (aHR 1.25; 95% CI 0.95 to 1.66) in the Colas et al study was weaker than the estimate obtained using propensity score weighting (aHR 1.50; 95% CI 1.09 to 2.08) and none of the adjustment methods included paternal epilepsy. In contrast, we adjusted explicitly for paternal epilepsy and restricted analyses to fathers with epilepsy to control for potential epilepsy-related confounding. Additionally, their estimates included adjustments for maternal obesity and smoking, while our Swedish analyses adjusted for parental education and cohabitation.9 23 Although in our study, point estimates were slightly above 1.0 without reaching statistical significance (particularly in the Norwegian cohort), our larger sample size strengthens our conclusion that any potential risk associated with paternal valproate exposure is likely very small, if it exists at all, or may reflect residual confounding due to unmeasured factors such as lack of adjustment for parental education.

Other studies support our results. A nationwide cohort study using Danish national registers analysed 1 235 353 singletons, including 1336 children of fathers who used valproate during spermatogenesis.14 The results suggested no association between paternal valproate exposure and the risk of congenital malformations or NDD in offspring. A follow-up paper from the Danish group applied a similar design as in our study, including only monotherapy cases, but still found no increased risk of NDD.11 Furthermore, our findings align with a previous Swedish register-based data (based on a subset of the same data used in our study and in the Colas et al. study),13 and a recent systematic review,12 both of which found no significant association between paternal ASM use, including valproate, during conception and adverse developmental outcomes in offspring. Unlike the Colas et al. study, the Swedish study13 used a narrower definition of NDD (limited to ASD, ADHD and intellectual disability), restricted to fathers with epilepsy (thereby addressing confounding by indication present in previous studies), and compared paternal ASM exposure only to unexposed fathers, rather than comparing across different ASMs and indications.

Overall, the wide variability in NDD risk estimates following paternal valproate exposure during spermatogenesis is striking, given the similar populations and analytic methods across studies. In contrast, maternal valproate exposure during pregnancy has been consistently linked to increased NDD risk using data from the same registries,13 even when using robust control groups, such as mothers who discontinued valproate before pregnancy,24 unexposed siblings25 and unexposed children of women with epilepsy.26 A few small clinical studies and case reports have linked valproate to lower semen parameters, including reduced sperm count.27 However, a recent large international cohort study found no evidence of an association between valproate and infertility in men with epilepsy or bipolar disorder in real-world settings.28

Strengths and weaknesses of the study

The key strengths of this study lie in the nation-wide population-based coverage of the Swedish and Norwegian registries with minimal loss to follow-up and thus good generalisability of the observations.13–15 Prescription records of ASM have been shown to be highly correlated with self-reported use,29 and dose of ASM estimated from prescription fills is a good proxy for blood levels and thus for biological exposure in pregnancy.30 In our study, we restricted our cohort to fathers with epilepsy, while most previous studies compared valproate treatment regardless of the underlying disease, which may introduce bias by confounding by indication. Furthermore, the Swedish and Norwegian data used in this study partially overlapped with the Colas et al. study cohorts, but with an expanded cohort of valproate-exposed fathers. Finally, we adjusted for cohabitation/marital status and both parents’ education (Sweden only) as proxies for socioeconomic status. A limitation of our study is that we rely on register data for the offspring outcomes, which have not been formally validated. Nevertheless, this would not invalidate our comparisons between valproate and lamotrigine/levetiracetam exposed to the same registers. By including only children diagnosed in hospital or outpatient care, we likely overrepresented the more severe cases, so our findings may not generalise to milder conditions like mild ASD or ADHD. Unlike maternal exposure, paternal exposure carries some uncertainty, as some children may have unregistered biological fathers. However, there is no reason to expect this proportion to differ between valproate-exposed and lamotrigine/levetiracetam groups. We did not account for the use of valproate in fathers before the spermatogenic period; hence, the control group could have been using valproate previously in life. It is unknown if the duration of valproate exposure in men, including use during the development of germ cells before sperm production, can influence risks of epigenetic changes in the sperm. In our study, Norway had notably shorter median follow-up time compared with Sweden, likely under-ascertained later-onset NDDs, yielding few cases for some subtypes, and potentially attenuating associations. Finally, the dose-event analysis did not reveal any consistent pattern, but high valproate doses may simply be a proxy for unmeasured confounders; future studies should examine dose effects across all ASMs.

Conclusion

Our findings reassure that paternal valproate use around conception is not associated with adverse neurodevelopmental outcomes in offspring. This evidence supports counselling men with epilepsy (or other indications), without applying the stringent valproate restrictions placed on women of childbearing potential. Alongside findings from a recent Danish register study, these results warrant reconsideration of regulatory precautions for the use of valproate in male patients of fertile age.
