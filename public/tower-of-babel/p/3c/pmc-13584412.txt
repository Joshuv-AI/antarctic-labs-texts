Prevalence of molecular markers related to artemisinin partial resistance and ACT partner drug resistance in the Plasmodium falciparum population in Kongo Central, DRC
Authors: Marius Ahm Stauning, Madalena Raposo, Nono Mvuama, Baudouin Matela, Antoinette Tshefu, Sissel Manscher Iversen, Guylain Sundisa, Aline Maliwani, Cally Roper, Nono Koka, Caterina Guinovart, Jørgen Kurtzhals, Jean Okitawutshu, Michael Alifrangis, Henry Ntuku, Helle Hansson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundArtemisinin-based combination therapies (ACTs) are the cornerstone of Plasmodium falciparum malaria treatment. Recent reports have documented P. falciparum with mutations associated with artemisinin partial resistance in eastern DRC, raising concerns about reduced drug susceptibility. This study examines the prevalence of mutations linked to reduced ACT efficacy in P. falciparum isolates collected December 2024–February 2025 in the Kongo Central Province, western DRC.MethodDried blood spots were obtained from children under two years of age attending targeted health facilities and testing positive for malaria with rapid diagnostic tests. Diagnosis was confirmed with qPCR and Illumina-targeted amplicon sequencing was used to analyse mutations in the pfk13, pfubp-1, pfcoronin, pfmdr-1 and pfcrt genes.ResultsPfk13 Q613E classified by WHO as potential marker of artemisinin partial resistance was found in a single sample, while pfubp-1 mutations D1525E and E1528D, also suspected of being linked to artemisinin partial resistance were found in 20% and 22% of the sequencing positive samples, respectively. N-Y-S-N-D and N-F-S-N-D were the dominating pfmdr1 haplotypes while the pfcrt wildtype C-V-M-N-K haplotype was highly dominant as well (90%).ConclusionOur results suggest no immediate concern of maintaining artemisinin and ACTs as first-in-line treatment for malaria in Kongo Central, DRC. However, the findings are of concern and continued molecular surveillance is needed to monitor emerging resistance patterns and allow for a timely response.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12936-026-06022-8.

Introduction

Artemisinin-based drugs are a cornerstone in the global effort to control malaria [1, 2]. The drugs are highly effective and used to treat both severe and uncomplicated cases. To avoid selection of resistance by large-scale use, the World Health Organization (WHO) recommends artemisinin-based combination therapies (ACTs). In ACTs artemisinin-derivatives are paired with a partner drug of either amodiaquine, lumefantrine, mefloquine, piperaquine, sulfadoxine-pyrimethamine or pyronaridine. Of these are artemether-lumefantrine (AL), artesunate-amodiaquine (AS-AQ), and artesunate- pyronaridine (AS-PYR) recommended as first and second line treatments in DRC, with AL and AS-AQ being the most frequently used [3]. So far, ACTs have retained their high efficacy against P. falciparum infections in sub-Saharan Africa (SSA), but P. falciparum harbouring mutations associated with artemisinin partial resistance have been documented, particularly in East Africa. [4–11] Their presence, even in small numbers is alarming since the global burden of malaria is primarily in SSA and widespread use of ACTs may promote the spread of resistance. Reduced efficacy of first-in-line treatment in SSA would have negative consequences, both for regional growth and stability, and for the collected global efforts to control and eliminate malaria [12]. Molecular surveys tracking the prevalence of both artemisinin and partner drug resistance are thus needed to inform and support timely response [13]. WHO classifies molecular markers of antimalarial drug resistance according to the following domains; genetic epidemiological-, laboratory- and clinical- evidence [14]. Markers are considered validated if evidence is available in all domains, candidate markers if two of three domains are available and potential markers if evidence is only available from a single domain. A well-established site for mutations related to artemisinin partial resistance in P. falciparum is the propeller domain of the Kelch-13 gene (pfk13) in which pfk13 F446I, N458Y, C469Y, M471I, Y493H, R539T, I543T, P553L, R561H, P574L, C580Y, R662I, and A675V are validated markers, while several other markers are classified as either candidate or potential markers [14]. Additionally, studies have further shown that mutations in the P. falciparum ubp-1 and coronin genes (pfubp1 and pfcoronin) may equally introduce reduced artemisinin efficacy [15–20]. Of special interest are the pfUBP-1 mutations D1525E, E1528D, R3138H, and V3275F and the pfcoronin mutations G50E, R100K, and E107V [14–20]. Furthermore, mutations in the P. falciparum chloroquine resistance transporter gene (pfcrt) and P. falciparum multidrug resistance protein 1 gene (pfmdr1) are related to ACT partner drug resistance [14]. Single observations of the validated artemisinin partial resistance markers pfk13 mutations A675V and R561H have recently been reported in the eastern part of the Democratic Republic of Congo (DRC) in two independent studies which analysed samples collected in 2020–21 and 2022–23, respectively [6, 8]. The discovery is alarming as DRC can form a bridge between East and West Africa, since the country is large, centrally located, and shares borders with nine different countries. Validated markers of artemisinin partial resistance have not been recorded in western or central DRC, but the latest molecular surveillance in Kongo Central analysed samples obtained in 2020 and the current prevalence is therefore uncertain. Another approach to monitor drug efficacy is by using therapeutic efficacy studies (TES). The latest published TES in Kongo Central was performed in 2017–2018, and it raised concerns about the efficacy of AL, endorsing the need for continued surveillance [21]. This study examined the prevalence of molecular markers related to artemisinin partial resistance and ACT partner drug resistance in P. falciparum obtained from children < 2 years with malaria in the Kongo Central province of DRC December 2024 – February 2025.

Methods

Study design and sites

The study is a cross-sectional study collecting samples in eight of the 31 health zones of the Kongo Central province in DRC; Kimpese, Gombe-Matadi, Sona-Bata and Ngidinga, Kwilu-Ngongo, Mbanza-Ngungu, Boko-Kivulu and Kisantu health Zones (Fig. 1). Health Zones were selected based on accessibility and logistical considerations for conducting the study. Malaria is hyper-endemic in the area with > 300 confirmed cases pr. 1000 inhabitants [3].

Study participants and sample collection

Samples were collected from malaria suspected symptomatic children < 2 years of age testing positive with malaria Rapid Diagnostic Test (mRDT) (First Response® Malaria Ag. HRP2, Premier Medical Corporation Private Limited, Gujarat, India) at public health facilities between December 2024 and February 2025. Finger-pick blood samples were collected on Whatman filter paper (GE Healthcare, Chicago, IL), prior to administration of anti-malaria treatment. DBS was dried and individually packed with silica beads at room temperature at the individual health facilities. Samples were hereafter stored at a central lab at 0–4 °C before final shipment and analysis at University of Copenhagen. Demographic information was captured using standardized questionnaires, administered by healthcare professionals. Healthcare professionals taking part in study sampling received training in the study methodology in pre-study training sessions.

Sample size

Using, n = (1.962 × P x (1—P))/e2, with P being the expected true proportion and e the desired precision apparent prevalence can be calculated with 95% CI and 0.05 precision by including 73 study participants when assuming a true prevalence of artemisinin partial resistance markers of maximum 5% [22]. Small population adjustment was not considered required. To account for false positives with RDT screening as well as difficulties in obtaining enough DNA for sequencing in low-parasitemia patients we estimated a need to increase sample size with 50%, giving a final sample size of minimum 110 study participants.

Molecular methods

Plasmodium DNA was extracted using the QIAamp® 96 DNA Blood Kit (QIAGEN, Venlo, The Netherlands). Presence of P. falciparum DNA in extracts was examined with quantitative real-time-polymerase-chain-reaction (qPCR) using the AriaMx platform (Agilent, Santa Clara, USA) and primers targeting VAR-genes [23]. Ct-values < 35 was considered positive. Sequencing was performed with Illumina-targeted amplicon sequencing, at the Flow Cytometry & Single Cell Core Facility, University of Copenhagen following protocols by Nag et. al. supplemented with amplifications of pfUBP1 and pfcoronin (supplemental table S1) [24]. Quality trimming with minimal accepted Phred score of 20, keeping only reads with ≥ 99% certainty in nucleotide determination was performed with Trimmomatic [25]. Final analysis was hereafter done through an in house Python and R based bioinformatical pipeline with P. falciparum 3D7 genome as reference genome [25]. Nucleotide calling was preformed independently for the forward and reverse sequencing strand of each sample. Only nucleotides in positions covered by > 50 reads after quality trimming and with ≥ 20% of these reads representing the specific nucleotide was considered present in a sequencing strand of a given sample. P. flaciparum3D7 lab-strains were used as positive controls, while uninfected blood, and sterile demineralized water, were negative controls. Individual genotypes were determined for each codon of all genes, and haplotypes was constructed for pfmdr1 and pfcrt. Codons with mixed genotypes were counted as mutant when determining proportion of samples carrying genotype mutations. Mixed infections were excluded when constructing pfmdr1- and pfcrt- haplotypes.

Statistic methods

Statistical analysis was performed with R version 4.3.2, and the following packages “gtsummary,” “gtsave,” “tidyverse,” “Dplyr,” “flextable,” “openxlsx” and “readxl.” Data are stratified according to study sites and summarized with percentages for categorical variables, and medians and interquartile ranges (IQR) for continuous variables. Differences between studies sites were analysed using Fisher's exact test or Pearson’s Chi-squared test for categorical variables and Kruskal-Walli’s rank sum test for continuous variables. P < 0.05 was considered significant.

Molecular results pfK13

A total of 34 different non-synonymous mutations were found in pfk13. The potential marker of artemisinin partial resistance pfk13 Q613E was found in a single sample from Ngidinga (Table 2). The pfk13 Q613E mutation was found in both the forward and reverse sequencing strand of the sample. The sample contained a total of 4489 reads passing the predefined quality thresholds that covered pfk13 codon 613, and of these did 3618 reads support the Q613E mutation. Several unclassified mutations were also seen in pfK13 (Table 2). These all passed the quality trimming and thresholds for nucleotide calling (Table 2). Notable among these, are findings of pfk13 C447Y and pfK13 V534A because mutations in neighbouring codons are validated markers of artemisinin partial resistance (Table 2).

Molecular results pfUBP1

A total of 33 different non-synonymous mutations were found in pfubp1 (Table 2), with pfubp1 D1525E and pfubp1 E1528D, both suspected to be linked to artemisinin partial resistance, in 20% and 22% of the sequencing positive samples, respectively (Table 2). Of these, eight samples contained both pfubp1 D1525E and pfubp1 E1528D (2.7% of samples which were sequencing positive in both codons). No significant differences in pfubp1 D1525E and pfubp1 E1528D prevalences were seen between study sites (p = 0.07 and p = 0.3), although pfubp1 D1525E was not found in Kimpese. Sequencing success at codon 1525 and 1528 of pfubp1 in Kimpese was comparable to the other sites (59% success, overall, 56%, range within sites 75%-42%). However, Kimpese is a smaller site only accounting for 7.7% of the total samples (Table 1).

Molecular results pfcoronin

A total of 12 different non-synonymous mutations were found in pfcoronin (Table 2). No classified markers related to artemisinin partial resistance were found. Of the unclassified mutations passing the quality trimming and threshold for nucleotide calling are finding of pfcoronin R100G interesting, as the related pfcoronin R100K mutation was recently classified as a potential marker of artemisinin partial resistance by WHO (Table 2).

Molecular results pfmdr1 and pfcrt

A total of 18 and 8 different non-synonymous mutations were found in pfmdr1 and pfcrt, respectively. (Table 2). The most frequent haplotype in pfmdr1 codon 86–184-1036–1042-1246 was N-Y-S–N-D (58.5% of sequencing positive samples with full non-mixed haplotypes) followed by N-F-S–N-D (Table 3). The most prevalent pfcrt codon 72–76 haplotype was the wildtype C-V-M–N-K (89.9% of sequencing positive samples with full non-mixed haplotypes, Table 3). Several unclassified mutations passing the threshold for nucleotide calling were further seen in both genes (Table 2).

Discussion

On the positive side we found no validated or candidate markers of artemisinin partial resistance, suggesting no immediate concern for the continued use of ACTs in Kongo Central. On the other hand, we found several markers worth mentioning for their potential clinical impact. The finding of pfk13 Q613E is of concern as pfk13 Q613E was recently classified as a potential marker of artemisinin partial resistance [14]. Single sample observations of pfk13 Q613E were previously documented in western DRC in samples collected in 2020 and 2019, respectively [8, 26]. The mutation was however not adopted in the WHO classification at the time of these studies and therefore not recognized as significant [8, 26]. Validated and candidate markers of artemisinin partial resistance have been documented with low prevalence in eastern DRC, but the latest examinations in western DRC did, in line with our results, not find any of these markers [6, 8, 26–28]. A similar picture is reported in northern Angola and southern Congo in regions bordering our study area [29–31]. The pfubp1 D1525E and E1528D mutations found in approximately 20% of the samples are also of concern. Both mutations previously have been linked to reduced artemisinin efficacy, and have been found in ACT-treatment failure cases from Kenya, Angola, Uganda and Liberia [15, 16]. The exact molecular pathway of pfUBP-1 mediated resistance is not fully resolved but it has been suggested that pfUBP-1 acts as an essential compartment protein and interactor in the pfK13-EPS15 complex, and that mutations in pfubp-1 can introduce reduced susceptibility to artemisinin- derivatives by modulating parasite haemoglobin endocytosis [17, 18]. Other studies of P. yoelii orthologues of pfUBP-1 in rodent malaria have suggested that UBP1 modulation of the MDR1 pathway can mediate high-level resistance to the ACT partner drugs mefloquine, lumefantrine, and piperaquine. [32] It should, however, also be noted that pfubp1 has not yet been evaluated in the WHO classification of molecular markers of artemisinin partial resistance. The findings should thus be interpreted with caution and future studies examining the possible clinical consequences of these markers would be of high value. Prevalence of pfubp1 has not previously been examined in DRC, but low prevalence of pfubp1 D1525E has recently been documented in Ghana (4.9%), while low prevalences of E1528D have been documented in Ghana and Nigeria (7.4–1.6%) [9, 10]. Knowledge of pfubp-1 mutation rates in Africa is however limited as the markers have only been investigated in few countries. We did not find any pfcoronin markers of concern. To our knowledge pfcoronin has not previously been examined in DRC. Knowledge of pfcoronin prevalence in Africa is generally limited, but pfcoronin R100K was observed in ACT treatment failure patients in a recent study in Nigeria [11]. In summary, our results suggest no immediate concern of maintaining artemisinin and ACTs as first-in-line treatment for malaria in Kongo Central, DRC. However, continued molecular surveillance is needed, as dispersal of mutated P. falciparum strains is an ongoing and dynamic process. Awareness of other factors essential for rational drug use must also be maintained. These include drug quality, administration practices and composition of circulating Plasmodium species but are outside the scope of this study. A striking difference in qPCR positivity between Boko—Kivulu and the other study sites was seen in our results (Table 1). We found no systematic difference in study population or clinical practice that could explain this, but audits of clinical practice could be relevant to include in future studies.

Limitations

This study has some limitations. The study population is homogenous, only including symptomatic children < 2 years. Even though this is an interesting study population, drug use and malaria exposure may differ among age groups. Continued surveillance including more age groups, community data, and asymptomatic cases would therefore be valuable. Nevertheless a recent study in Kinshasa comparing parasite drug resistance genotypes among infections in children and unrelated pregnant women found no systematic difference between population subgroups [28]. A larger sample size would possibly also be required to test the differences between study sites found in our study, while more sampling points throughout the year would be required to test potential seasonal fluctuations. The initial mRDT screening in our study can be influenced by both HRP2 persistence and/or pfhrp2/pfhrp3 deletion but compared to the gold standard microscopy mRDT enabled the inclusion of more health care facilities and cost-effective increasement in sample size. Finally, are the knowledge of mutations involved in anti-malaria drug resistance rapidly growing and the WHO compendium of resistance markers are continuously updated. It is likely that future studies should include mutations that are currently not in our pipeline.

Conclusion

As no validated or candidate markers of artemisinin partial resistance was found our results suggest no immediate concern of maintaining artemisinin and ACTs as first-in-line treatment for malaria in Kongo Central, DRC. The finding of pfk13 Q613E, pfubp1 D1525E and pfubp1 E1528D is however of concern. Continued molecular surveillance is needed to track the development and allow for a timely response in case of rising rates. Future studies examining clinical impact of these possible ACT resistance markers would also be of high value.
