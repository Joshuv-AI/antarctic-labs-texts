In silico approaches for discovering microbial antiviral defense systems
Authors: Lixu Jiang, Yansheng Li, Baocai Xie, Lianrong Wang, Shi Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractProkaryotes possess a remarkably diverse and dynamic repertoire of antiviral defense systems, enabling them to withstand phage predation. However, their frequent horizontal gene transfer, extensive sequence diversity, modular genomic organization, and rapid evolution make purely experimental discovery challenging. Coupled with the massive influx of microbial genomes from high-throughput sequencing, computational strategies have become indispensable complementary tools that can enhance the efficiency and scope of defense systems discovery. In this review, we categorize computational approaches into four major strategies: (i) Sequence homology-based methods, which reliably annotate known defense systems through protein sequence similarity but are limited in detecting highly divergent or novel systems; (ii) Structure-guided approaches, which leverage conserved protein folds to uncover remote homologs and single-gene defense proteins, providing sensitivity beyond sequence-based identification, albeit at high computational cost; (iii) Genomic context-based strategies, which exploit gene co-localization and defense islands to uncover multi-gene defense clusters and previously uncharacterized defense modules; and (iv) Artificial intelligence-powered methods, which integrate sequence-derived embeddings with genomic context information to predict low-homology proteins and reconstruct candidate defense systems at scale, enabling discovery of novel systems beyond the reach of conventional approaches. We further discuss emerging tools and frameworks, such as the conserved gene cluster discovery tool and genomic foundation models, which hold strong potential to extend conventional approaches for identifying novel defense systems and supporting the generative design of synthetic modules. By comparing methodological principles, strengths, and limitations, this review provides a practical framework for the systematic exploration of microbial immune systems, guiding applications such as rational phage therapy, microbiome engineering, and synthetic biology.Keywords: antiviral defense systems, defense islands, bacterial immunity, in silico discovery, artificial intelligence

Introduction

In the context of frequent and varied phage attacks, prokaryotes have developed diverse antiviral defense mechanisms collectively referred to as the prokaryotic immune system. These systems are central to the evolutionary arms race between microbes and their viral predators, shaping both genome evolution and ecological dynamics across microbial communities. These systems include both well-characterized mechanisms such as CRISPR-Cas [1], toxin-antitoxin (TA) [2], restriction-modification (R-M) [3, 4], and abortive infection (Abi) [5], and more recently discovered systems, including DNA phosphorothioate (PT)-based restriction-modification [6–10], bacteriophage exclusion (BREX) [11], defense island system associated with restriction-modification (DISARM) [12], cyclic-oligonucleotide-based antiphage signaling system (CBASS) [13], and Zorya [14]. Phages, in turn, have evolved sophisticated counter-defense strategies to circumvent bacterial immune systems, including anti-CRISPR proteins (Acrs) and anti-restriction proteins [15, 16].

This ever-expanding repertoire of microbial defense systems has significantly advanced our understanding of host-virus interactions in microbial communities. Beyond fundamental insights, these systems have also inspired transformative tools in genome editing, synthetic biology, and therapeutic applications. A prominent example is the CRISPR-Cas system, which has revolutionized genome editing. It holds broad implications for antibacterial therapy, phage therapy, the protection of industrial microbial strains, and the study of host-virus dynamics in diverse environments. Furthermore, integrating CRISPR-Cas systems with Acrs enables precise modulation of genome editing activity, thereby expanding their potential for safe and tunable biotechnological applications. In addition, the ever-expanding repertoire of newly discovered defense systems represents a valuable reservoir of diverse molecular mechanisms, offering immense potential for the development of novel biotechnological tools and applications [17, 18]. However, experimental discovery of defense systems remains a major bottleneck, largely due to the immense scale of microbial genomes and the intrinsic complexity of these systems [14]. Defense systems frequently display dynamic horizontal gene transfer, extensive diversity, modular genomic organization, and rapid evolutionary turnover, all of which pose substantial challenges to their systematic discovery [14, 19, 20]. These complexities underscore the urgent need for robust and scalable computational strategies to enable the systematic identification and characterization of defense systems across large-scale genomic datasets.

To address this challenge, numerous computational approaches have been developed for discovering and predicting antiviral defense systems shaped by the ongoing evolutionary arms race between bacteria and phages [21, 22]. Among these, sequence homology-based tools such as DefenseFinder [23], CRISPRCasFinder [24], TAfinder [25–27], and PADLOC (Prokaryotic Antiviral Defence LOCator) [28, 29] have been instrumental in annotating known systems by identifying conserved protein sequences. However, such methods often fail to identify remote homologs when sequence identity is low, leading to reduced sensitivity. In contrast, structure-guided approaches enhance detection power by leveraging conserved three-dimensional motifs, thereby uncovering distant evolutionary relationships that extend beyond sequence similarity [30, 31].

Nevertheless, both sequence- and structure-based homology approaches are inherently limited in their capacity to identify novel systems that lack recognizable features. To overcome this limitation, genomic context-based discovery, particularly through the analysis of defense islands, has emerged as a powerful alternative. By identifying protein families that are consistently co-localized with known defense genes across large-scale microbial genomes, this strategy has substantially accelerated the discovery of new antiphage systems [14, 20, 32–36]. More recently, artificial intelligence (AI)-driven models such as DeepDefense and DefensePredictor [37, 38] have enabled scalable prediction of prokaryotic defense systems by incorporating high-dimensional representations that extend beyond the capabilities of conventional methods. Foundation models like Evo further demonstrate the potential of deep learning (DL) in designing synthetic biological systems, including CRISPR-Cas modules [39]. Building on these advances, predictive models trained on core defense hotspots are now capable of uncovering previously overlooked genomic diversity and cryptic defense elements.

In this review, we provide a systematic classification of computational approaches for the discovery of antiphage defense systems into four principal categories: sequence homology-based methods, structure-guided prediction, genomic context-based strategies, and AI-powered predictive models. We discuss their underlying principles, strengths, and limitations, and propose future directions for enhancing sensitivity, scalability, and integrative capability. By offering a structured overview of current methodologies, this review aims to serve as a practical roadmap for researchers seeking to elucidate the complex landscape of microbial immune defenses through computational approaches.

Microbial antiviral defense systems

To appreciate the rationale underlying different computational strategies, it is essential to understand the biological complexity of microbial antiviral defense systems. The molecular mechanisms and detailed biology of each system have been extensively reviewed elsewhere [3, 20, 40, 41] and are therefore not covered in detail in this review. Prokaryotic defense systems are remarkably diverse and widely distributed across bacterial and archaeal genomes. Classical mechanisms include defense mediated by second messenger signalling pathways [13, 42–44], the production of antiviral chemical compounds [45–47], the incorporation of reverse transcription of small RNAs into their defensive processes [20, 48], Abi mechanisms [49], innate immune mechanisms, and adaptive immunity mechanisms (Fig. 1) [3, 6, 7, 50]. The CRISPR-Cas system represents an adaptive immune mechanism wherein small RNA molecules guide sequence-specific nucleases to cleave invading nucleic acids, thereby providing heritable and precise defense against previously encountered phages. R-M systems representing an innate immune mechanism rely on a dual-function strategy in which foreign DNA is recognized and enzymatically cleaved by restriction endonucleases, while host DNA is protected through sequence-specific methylation. Abi is a form of altruistic programmed cell death triggered during phage invasion， which halts viral replication and protects the surrounding microbial population.

Diversity of prokaryotic antiviral defense systems. These antiphage mechanisms encompass a wide range of bacterial defense strategies, including innate immune systems such as R-M, BREX, and DNA phosphorothioate-based systems (e.g. Tdp, Dnd, Ssp); adaptive immune systems such as CRISPR-Cas; Abi systems, including AbiZ, AbiB, Lit, and RexAB, as well as TA modules that can mediate Abi-like responses; chemical defense systems, which naturally produce DNA-intercalating small molecules such as daunorubicin, doxorubicin, epirubicin, idarubicin, mitoxantrone, and acriflavine that block phage replication; signaling-based systems such as CBASS and Thoeris; and retron-based systems, exemplified by Retron Ec48. Beyond these characterized systems, numerous cryptic and uncharacterized layers of antiphage defense continue to be discovered, reflecting the remarkable diversity and complexity of bacterial antiviral immunity.

These classical systems have laid the foundation for the discovery of an increasing number of mechanistically diverse antiviral defense systems. Comprehensive and up-to-date information on bacterial antiviral defense systems is available through the online “List of Defense Systems” catalog of DefenseFinder and the complementary PADS Arsenal database (Table 1) [23, 51]. For a deeper understanding of phage-encoded counter-defense strategies, including anti-CRISPR and other antidefense proteins, researchers can refer to dbAPIS [52], a dedicated database that systematically curates phage-encoded inhibitors of bacterial immunity. Together, these open-access resources form a valuable foundation for investigating the complex interplay between microbial defense systems and viral countermeasures. Recent studies have revealed that the defense arsenal encoded within the prokaryotic pan-genome is far more diverse than previously anticipated [14, 20]. Comparative genomic analyses have demonstrated that defense systems are frequently encoded in clusters, forming so-called defense islands, which are genomic regions characterized by statistically significant nonrandom clustering of defense-related genes across diverse prokaryotic genomes [14, 20, 32]. These islands often include mobile genetic elements and exhibit high variability across taxa, contributing to their functional diversity while complicating their identification by traditional methods [32, 34]. Additionally, many defense systems are embedded within mobile genetic elements, facilitating horizontal gene transfer and promoting evolutionary innovation [19, 83]. Some defense systems are also encoded within prophages or phage genomes, serving as evolutionary hotspots for the emergence and dissemination of new defense mechanisms [84]. Importantly, the number of encoded defense systems correlates with phage resistance level, suggesting their cumulative and occasionally redundant contributions to antiviral protection [85, 86].

The identification of microbial antiviral defense systems poses significant challenges due to their inherent biological complexity. The identification of antiphage defense systems remains difficult because of several intrinsic features: high sequence variability and rapid evolution driven by phage-host arms races; modular and noncanonical gene architectures shaped by frequent horizontal gene transfer; and the widespread lack of functional annotation, particularly among newly discovered or lineage-specific genes. Moreover, many defense systems are organized as multigene operons or embedded within defense islands, necessitating integrative genomic context analyses beyond simple sequence similarity searches [3, 20, 23, 36, 87]. To address these challenges, computational approaches need to integrate multiple layers of biological information, including three-dimensional structural features, genomic neighborhood context, evolutionary conservation, and increasingly, AI-driven pattern recognition from large-scale genomic datasets. By accounting for these biological complexities, researchers can more effectively evaluate, refine, and integrate computational strategies for the systematic discovery of microbial defense systems.

Homology-based prediction of antiphage defense systems

Identification of defense protein homologs

Traditional identification of bacterial antiphage defense systems relied heavily on labor-intensive and costly wet-lab experiments. Although effective, these methods were both time-consuming and resource-intensive. In contrast, bioinformatics-driven strategies have significantly accelerated the discovery of antiviral defense systems by providing high-throughput and cost-effective alternatives. Among these, protein sequence homology-based approaches remain central to most computational tools, identifying proteins homologous to known defense genes across diverse bacterial genomes. This approach has greatly expanded the repertoire of characterized defense systems, providing insights into underlying mechanisms, novel variants, and evolutionary trajectories of bacterial immunity. Homology-based prediction leveraging sequence similarity therefore remains a cornerstone of the prediction of defense systems. To facilitate the identification of antiviral defense-related genes, we categorized representative protein sequence homology-based tools based on their algorithmic strategies and typical applications. BLASTp, a classic and widely adopted pairwise alignment program, provides accurate and interpretable results for homolog detection but is less suited for large-scale analyses due to its relatively high computational cost [97]. In contrast, fast sequence similarity search tools, such as DIAMOND and MMseqs2, are designed for ultra-rapid alignment of protein sequences against large databases [89, 95]. These tools implement optimized k-mer indexing and seed-and-extend heuristics, enabling efficient analysis of large-scale metagenomic datasets. However, their lower sensitivity limits the detection of remote homologs, particularly when functional divergence is high. To address this limitation, iterative and profile-based methods such as PSI-BLAST enhance sensitivity by constructing position-specific scoring matrices (PSSMs) across multiple search rounds [96, 99]. HMMER and HHsearch offer even higher sensitivity by employing profile Hidden Markov Models (HMMs) or HMM-HMM comparisons, enabling the identification of distant homologs and conserved domains [90, 91, 100, 101]. These approaches are particularly effective when coupled with curated domain databases such as Pfam, making them valuable tools for defense gene discovery. While profile-based tools provide deep evolutionary insights, they are computationally more demanding and may require substantial time and memory on large datasets. Therefore, researchers should balance speed and sensitivity when selecting appropriate tools (Table 2).

Homology-based tools for the Identification of antiphage defense systems

While the previous section discussed general tools for predicting homologous defense-related proteins, this section focuses on specialized prediction tools tailored specifically for identifying bacterial defense systems. To systematically identify CRISPR-Cas systems, several dedicated tools have been developed, each leveraging distinct computational strategies. CasLocusAnno combines PSI-BLAST, the Markov Cluster Algorithm (MCL), and the Maximum Continuous Cas Subcluster (MCCS) model to achieve accurate detection and subtyping of known CRISPR-Cas loci [92]. CRISPRdisco integrates CRISPR array identification via minCED with downstream Cas protein verification, enabling complete system reconstruction [93]. CRISPRCasFinder [24], built on the Macromolecular System Finder (MacSyFinder) [102] framework modeling CRISPR–Cas genetic architectures and the minimal set of protein components required, utilizes HMM-based Cas gene detection (CasFinder) and provides detailed classification of CRISPR-Cas subtypes. Other tools, such as HMMCAS and CRISPRone, also rely on HMMs of Cas protein families to detect a broad range of CRISPR-Cas components sensitively [88, 94]. CRISPRone [88] is a comprehensive tool for the annotation and prediction of CRISPR-Cas systems in prokaryotic genomes. It provides detailed annotations of CRISPR arrays (repeat-spacer units), cas genes with their respective types and subtypes, as well as antirepeats such as tracrRNA fragments in type II systems. CRISPRone offers an online prediction service for user-provided genomic sequences and incorporates a false-CRISPR detection pipeline to reduce false positives. All results, including both validated CRISPRs and curated false-CRISPR elements, are freely available through the CRISPRone website (Table 2).

In addition to CRISPR-Cas systems, TA modules represent another important class of bacterial defense against viruses. TAfinder is a dedicated tool for the genome-wide identification of type I-VIII TA systems in bacteria and archaea [25]. It integrates homology-based protein searches using both BLASTp and HMMER3, supported by an extensive set of profile HMMs covering toxin (207) and antitoxin (228) domains. Beyond sequence similarity, TAfinder incorporates operon structure analysis to improve the accuracy of TA loci prediction. By combining domain-level detection with gene neighborhood context, TAfinder enables comprehensive annotation and classification of diverse TA modules in microbial genomes. TAfinder accepts either annotated genomes (GFF format) or unannotated genomic sequences (FASTA format) as input. TASmania [98] is another specialized resource for TA system discovery, complementing TAfinder with a broader and more exploratory approach. It analyzes over 41 000 EnsemblBacteria assemblies and uses a curated set of 369 toxin and 305 antitoxin HMM profiles to identify known and putative TA loci (types I-IV, with side hits for types V-VI). TASmania employs a “guilt-by-association” strategy, organizing loci into pseudo-operons (“popTA”) and clustering HMM profiles to reduce redundancy while uncovering unexpected TA combinations. This strategy yields over 2.1 million candidate TA loci across diverse bacterial phyla, providing an extensive resource for comparative genomics. In addition, several complementary databases emphasize specialized curation and functional annotation. The T1TAdb represents the first comprehensive platform dedicated to type I TA systems, offering detailed annotations of toxin mRNAs, antitoxin asRNAs, regulatory elements, and secondary structures for ~1900 loci across ~500 bacterial strains [103]. Similarly, RASTA-Bacteria was an early tool for identifying toxin and antitoxin protein domains but has been discontinued since 2011 [104]. Together, these resources enrich the TA research landscape (Table 2).

For a more comprehensive view of bacterial defense strategies, integrated tools such as DefenseFinder [23] and PADLOC [28] have been developed. DefenseFinder is a dedicated tool for the identification of known prokaryotic antiviral defense systems from genomic sequences. It is built upon the MacSyFinder framework, which employs a two-step procedure: (i) identification of candidate defense-related proteins using profile HMM-based homology searches, and (ii) application of genetic architecture rules to validate system presence. Each antiviral system is represented as a customized model composed of curated HMM profiles and system-specific decision rules that define required (mandatory), optional (accessory), and excluded (forbidden) components. DefenseFinder currently includes models for 152 distinct antiviral systems, incorporating both existing domain profiles (e.g. Pfam, COG) and custom-built HMMs. The tool supports flexible system architectures, enabling variability in gene organization, particularly for complex systems. Notably, updated CasFinder v3.0.0 models for CRISPR-Cas types I-VI and 33 subtypes are fully integrated into DefenseFinder. The tool is available via both a command-line interface and a web server. It accepts input in either protein FASTA or nucleotide FASTA format (Table 2).

PADLOC (Prokaryotic Antiviral Defence LOCator) is a comprehensive tool for identifying diverse prokaryotic antiviral defense systems based on profile HMMs, enabling detection of CRISPR arrays and retron ncRNAs [28]. The core PADLOC software, implemented in R, utilizes a curated database (PADLOC-DB) containing thousands of HMMs with defined scoring thresholds and system classification models written in YAML. In version 2.0.0, PADLOC-DB includes 5027 HMMs representing over 700 protein families, which cover a broad spectrum of defense mechanisms. PADLOC predicts defense system genes based on HMM matches, which are subsequently mapped to system models to determine the presence of complete systems. A user-friendly web server is also available, enabling users to analyze bacterial and archaeal genomes, plasmids, phages, and metagenomes.

Owing to their distinct advantages, these specialized tools for predicting antiviral defense systems predominantly rely on HMMs. By modeling sequence variability within probabilistic frameworks, HMMs provide a powerful approach for recognizing diverse members of protein families. Additionally, established resources such as the Pfam database offering high-quality preconstructed HMMs, substantially simplify and accelerate the development of these prediction tools. These characteristics collectively make HMMs a reliable foundation for identifying and annotating defense-associated proteins.

Prediction performance of homology-based tools

Comprehensive tools for defense systems

PADLOC, which contains more than 180 system definitions as of the current release, is a comprehensive and widely adopted tool for the systematic identification and annotation of bacterial and archaeal anti-phage defense systems. Benchmarking against the reference dataset curated by Doron et al. [14] demonstrated that PADLOC achieved a high recall rate of ~97% for multi-gene defense systems, successfully identifying most systems reported in the original study and detecting additional homologs beyond those previously annotated. In contrast, sensitivity for single-gene systems such as Shedu was lower (89%) due to necessary trade-offs between specificity and detection thresholds, reflecting the inherent challenges of accurately detecting single-protein defense systems [29]. PADLOC’s design is based on a curated library of HMM profiles combined with system classification rules, enabling interpretable predictions and robust annotation. This framework also enabled the identification of six new Doron system subtypes (e.g., Druantia type IV, Hachiman type II, Lamassu type II, Septu type II, Thoeris type II, Zorya type III) and an additional putative system, Hma, through genomic co-localization and context analysis. The PADLOC web server further enhances accessibility by supporting diverse input formats and a broad range of targets, including bacterial, archaeal, viral, metagenomic, and plasmid sequences. Typical genome analyses (~5000 proteins) finish in under 5 min, with optional CRISPRDetect scanning contributing a modest increase in runtime.

Despite its strengths, PADLOC’s reliance on synteny constraints limits its ability to identify systems fragmented across contigs, and single-gene defense systems are inherently more susceptible to both false positives and false negatives. Many defense proteins contain ubiquitous domains (e.g. ATPase domains in PtuA, retrons, and Old nucleases), which may lead to occasional overlapping system classifications (<1% overlap in reported system calls). These ambiguities can often be resolved through manual curation of scoring parameters and genomic context, inspection of HMM and target coverage metrics, or integration with complementary tools such as HHpred [105], AlphaFold2 [106], ColabFold [107], DALI [108], and Foldseek [109] for fine-grained domain-level or structure-guided annotation. PADLOC's scoring thresholds are tuned for higher sensitivity to maximize the discovery of potential defense homologs, with the expectation that users will perform downstream analyses for validation [28]. Overall, PADLOC provides a robust, interpretable, and efficient platform for high-confidence identification of multigene defense systems, while also supporting exploratory discovery efforts in large-scale genomic and metagenomic datasets.

DefenseFinder is a bioinformatics platform designed for the systematic detection of all currently known prokaryotic antiphage defense systems. First released in 2021 with 60 curated systems, the tool has rapidly expanded to cover 152 systems (v2.0.0), reflecting the accelerating pace of discoveries in this field [23, 110]. DefenseFinder integrates HMM-based protein profiles with system-specific decision rules to achieve high-confidence detection. Benchmarking against the dataset from Doron et al. showed excellent sensitivity and specificity for multigene systems, with sensitivity ranging from 97.4% (DISARM) to 99.4% (Septu) and specificity from 96.7% (Septu) to 99.97% (Thoeris). When compared to PADLOC for the 12 systems detected by both tools, DefenseFinder showed strong agreement, with concordance values ranging from 76% (Septu) to 97.7% (Wadjet).

The tool offers both a command-line interface, installable as a Python package for large-scale genome analysis, and a web service for smaller-scale or occasional use. Both interfaces accept protein multi-FASTA files or nucleotide FASTA input and generate outputs listing detected defense systems and the proteins involved. Optional outputs allow users to examine all hits to antiviral proteins, including those outside complete systems, providing a broader view of antiviral defense potential. The web service additionally provides a visualization of results. DefenseFinder is optimized for conservative detection of complete systems, and its modular design supports frequent model updates, enabling the tool to remain current with rapidly evolving literature. In addition to system detection, DefenseFinder now provides a community-curated knowledge hub (https://defensefinder.mdmlab.fr) containing system-specific pages. These pages summarize each system’s discovery, genomic architecture, molecular mechanism, distribution, 3D structure, experimental validation, and references. The knowledge base is hosted on a GitLab repository to facilitate open community contributions and expert verification.

Despite its strengths, DefenseFinder faces several challenges: (i) some systems lack sufficient experimental validation limiting comprehensive accuracy assessment; (ii) rapidly evolving classifications can lead to closely related systems (e.g. Pycsar and CBASS) being modeled at broader functional levels before subtype boundaries are fully resolved; and (iii) detection performance may be constrained for systems with only a few validated representatives or atypical genetic architectures. Nevertheless, DefenseFinder provides a powerful, scalable, and transparent platform for annotating and exploring the expanding diversity of bacterial and archaeal antiviral defense systems, making it a valuable resource for both large-scale genomic studies and in-depth investigations.

In terms of performance comparison, both PADLOC and DefenseFinder achieve similarly high sensitivity (recall) for multigene defense systems, typically exceeding 97%. However, when considering specificity, DefenseFinder provides clearly defined high-specificity metrics (96.7%–99.97%), resulting in fewer false positives. PADLOC is tuned toward higher sensitivity, favoring the discovery of potential homologs, which may come at the cost of slightly increased false positives. In contrast, DefenseFinder emphasizes precision, minimizing false positives but potentially missing some atypical or partially annotated systems. Accordingly, PADLOC is better suited for exploratory analyses or large-scale genome scans aimed at annotating defense systems and discovering novel or incompletely characterized systems, whereas DefenseFinder is more appropriate for high-confidence annotation and mechanistic studies where minimizing false positives is critical. Importantly, tools such as PADLOC and DefenseFinder do not reduce defense system prediction to a simple binary classification problem. Instead, they perform multiclass, interpretable annotations, assigning proteins to specific system types and subtypes and providing scoring metrics for each prediction. This design allows researchers to evaluate prediction confidence and interrogate complex genomic contexts, rather than relying solely on yes/no classification outputs.

Specific tools for TA and CRISPR-Cas systems

TAfinder 2.0 is a specialized computational tool developed alongside TADB 3.0 [25] for high-confidence prediction of TA loci. Its reference database is extensively curated, incorporating 921 TA-related publications and more than 530 experimentally validated TA loci. Using a stringent homology-based strategy, TAfinder 2.0 relies exclusively on confirmed TA pairs and applies a conservative BLAST H-value cutoff of >0.36 to minimize false positives. This conservative design prioritizes precision over exploratory discovery. When applied to 34 789 fully sequenced prokaryotic genomes (34 257 bacterial and 532 archaeal), TAfinder 2.0 identified 211 697 putative TA loci across all eight known TA types (I-VIII). Type II TA systems were the most abundant, with Escherichia coli strains harboring an average of 14 loci (99.1% coverage across 2795 genomes) and Mycobacterium tuberculosis strains averaging over 50 loci, reflecting both its extensive coverage and the richness of its curated dataset.

TASmania, in contrast, was developed as a large-scale discovery platform emphasizing breadth and the identification of novel TA systems [98]. The tool mines more than 41 000 bacterial assemblies in the EnsemblBacteria database and integrates curated HMM models for toxins and antitoxins (369 toxin and 305 antitoxin HMM profiles), which are clustered into higher-level groups to reduce redundancy. TASmania applies a “guilt-by-association” strategy to organize hits into pseudo-operon structures, enabling the identification of novel TA pairs. Comparative benchmarking using reference strains demonstrated that TASmania recovers nearly all TAfinder-predicted loci while identifying many additional candidates, particularly within uncharacterized families. Experimental validation in M. tuberculosis confirmed robust toxicity for six of 11 TASmania-specific candidates, highlighting its potential for novel discovery.

Together, TAfinder 2.0 and TASmania provide complementary functionality: TAfinder 2.0 offers a conservative, high-confidence framework for robust TA annotation and comparative genomics, whereas TASmania is optimized for large-scale exploration and the discovery of previously uncharacterized TA systems. Notably, neither tool frames its predictions as a strict binary classification task. Instead, both perform sequence similarity searches and locus-level assembly to assign TA system candidates, producing graded evidence rather than a simple “positive/negative” output.

Beyond TA systems, CRISPR-Cas systems represent another major class of prokaryotic defense mechanisms, and a variety of computational tools have been developed to predict and annotate them. HMMCAS [94] provides a fast and interactive web-based service for Cas protein annotation using HMMER3.1 and a comprehensive Cas protein HMM library. It can sensitively detect Cas proteins and infer putative Cas operons and their types. However, HMMCAS alone does not resolve cas locus architectures or subtypes with the same level of accuracy as more recent tools. In contrast, CasLocusAnno [92] combines the MCL with the MCCS strategy to improve cas locus identification. When evaluated against the curated annotations of Makarova et al. [111], CasLocusAnno achieved higher accuracy (89.1%) than MacSyFinder [102] (83.3%) and is also capable of recognizing fused Cas proteins. In addition, CRISPRone [88] systematically evaluates CRISPR-Cas systems across complete archaeal and bacterial genomes, integrating genomic context and spacer diversity to improve prediction accuracy. By distinguishing isolated CRISPRs and cas loci from false-CRISPR elements, it identified 3224 suspicious orphan CRISPRs (27% of initial predictions) and classified them into tandem repeats, STAR-like elements, simple repeats, and unknown types. Compared with traditional repeat-structure-based tools (e.g. CRT [112], CRISPRFinder [113]), which are prone to high false-positive rates, CRISPRone substantially reduces misannotation, with approximately half of false-CRISPRs filtered through tandem repeat detection. This curation enhances reliability and provides a robust foundation for accurate genome-wide CRISPR-Cas annotation [88].

CRISPRdisco [93] is an automated pipeline that identifies CRISPR repeats and cas genes in genome assemblies, classifies them into all six major types and 23 recognized subtypes (including putative V-U systems), and evaluates the completeness of each locus. In benchmarking analyses, CRISPRdisco achieved 94% concordance with CRISPRdb [114] for repeat detection and up to 99% agreement with the curated Cas annotations of Makarova et al. [111], highlighting its high reliability. A notable advantage of the tool is its ability to detect “split arrays,” which were extremely common in type III systems (73%). In terms of computational performance, the pipeline required only 2–5 min per replicon when parallelized with 10 CPUs, with runtime scaling nearly linearly with the number of input sequences. Overall, CRISPRdisco provides reproducible and accurate predictions with strong classification capacity, though subtype assignment can be affected in incomplete genomes due to its dependence on intact cas gene architecture.

CRISPRCasFinder [24] is an integrated pipeline for simultaneous detection of CRISPR arrays and Cas proteins, combining an improved CRISPR array finder (CRISPRFinder v4.2) with MacSyFinder-based Cas detection and updated HMM profiles. In benchmarking on a curated dataset of 400 genomes, predictions generated by CRISPRCasFinder were compared against expert-curated annotations in CRISPRdb (used as the reference set) and evaluated alongside PILER-CR, CRT, and CRISPRDetect. Across this test panel, precision, recall, and F-measure ranged from 0.921 to 0.982, 0.935 to 0.987, and 0.932 to 0.978, respectively, indicating broadly comparable performance among the four tools. When evaluated against the updated Cas system classification of Makarova et al. CRISPRCasFinder (CasFinder v2.0) also showed high concordance, with Jacquard coefficients ranging from 77% to 96%. The tool adopts a conservative calling strategy, requiring the presence of at least three cas genes for Class 1 systems by default, reducing false positives in Cas operon predictions. In practical performance tests, the web server processed a typical bacterial genome (4–6 Mb) within approximately 1–2 minutes, and completed 50 MB multi-FASTA test files in roughly 5 minutes. Standalone runtimes scale reasonably with genome size, though memory consumption is higher than that of some alternative tools. CRISPRCasFinder integrates CRISPR array detection and Cas system prediction within a unified framework. For CRISPR arrays, it quantifies repeat conservation using an entropy-based conservation index (EBcons) and assigns evidence levels (1–4) based on EBcons values and spacer similarity. This design effectively filters out short or spurious repeat arrays; arrays with EBcons ≥70 and low spacer similarity are classified as high-confidence candidates. Limitations include potential subtype misassignments in incomplete or misassembled genomes and relatively higher memory requirements for large-scale analyses.

Taken together, comparative evaluation of these five pipelines highlights that no single tool uniformly outperforms the others across all metrics, but each offers distinct advantages depending on the application. In terms of overall accuracy, CRISPRCasFinder and CRISPRdisco generally demonstrate among the highest balances of sensitivity and specificity, supported by quantitative benchmarks and their integration of both repeat and Cas detection. CRISPRone provides a unique contribution by systematically filtering false-CRISPR arrays, addressing one of the major sources of false positives in large-scale genomic analyses. From a computational perspective, the tools span a spectrum from highly efficient (HMMCAS, CasLocusAnno) to more resource-intensive (CRISPRCasFinder), and performance varies markedly with genome completeness: fragmented assemblies or metagenomic datasets remain challenging, often leading to higher false-negative rates for subtype assignment or complete system reconstruction. Notably, these approaches implicitly frame CRISPR-Cas prediction as a binary classification problem at the feature level (e.g. presence/absence of Cas proteins, valid vs. spurious repeats), whereas system-level annotation is inherently a multiclass task involving assignment to specific types and subtypes. Thus, tool selection should be guided by dataset characteristics and research objectives: CRISPRCasFinder and CRISPRdisco are well suited for comprehensive and reliable system-level analyses, whereas CRISPRone is particularly valuable for large-scale surveys where minimizing false-positive CRISPR arrays is a major priority.

Structure-guided discovery of antiphage defense systems

Prediction of single-gene defense systems

Sequence-based homology prediction often struggles to detect remote protein homologs when sequence identity is low, thereby limiting its sensitivity for identifying highly divergent defense systems. Although HMM-based approaches offer greater sensitivity than direct sequence alignment, they still depend on detectable sequence conservation and may overlook deeply diverged evolutionary relationships. In contrast, structure-based homology detection provides a more robust alternative by capturing conserved protein folds and domain architectures that persist over long evolutionary timescales, enabling the detection of functionally conserved defense proteins across diverse lineages. Incorporating structural information substantially enhances the accuracy of protein annotation in metagenomic studies, resulting in improvements that can reach up to 70% [31]. In addition, Duan et al. applied structural features to identify antiphage defense systems and counter-defense systems, though applications of structural approaches in this field remain relatively limited. These findings underscore the potential of structure-based approaches in addressing the limitations of sequence homology-based methods (Fig. 2a) [30].

Defense systems were first predicted using PADLOC, and proteins located within the 1–15 kb intervals between annotated defense systems were extracted as candidates. These proteins were clustered using USEARCH based on sequence homology, and a representative sequence from each cluster was selected for structural modeling with AlphaFold2. Non-redundant phage proteins of unknown function were extracted from viral genome databases, clustered to generate representative families, and filtered to remove overly long, families lacking enrichment, and proteins with known functions. To identify putative antidefense proteins, all candidate proteins were iteratively co-folded with host defense proteins (e.g. Thoeris components) through an iterative AlphaFold2-Multimer screening pipeline, applying progressively stricter confidence thresholds. Final high-confidence candidates were selected for downstream experimental validation.

Among the limited studies applying structural similarity to antiviral defense discovery, the work by Duan et al. represents a prominent example. The authors retrieved 331 144 metagenome-assembled genomes (MAGs) and used PADLOC [28] to annotate known defense systems. Proteins located within the 1–15 kb intervals between these systems were extracted, clustered by sequence similarity, and filtered to obtain 10 620 unannotated proteins (≥200 amino acids) for structure prediction using AlphaFold2 [106]. These predicted structures were then aligned against known single-gene defense proteins using US-align [115] to identify structurally similar yet sequence-divergent homologs (Fig. 2a). Through this pipeline, they discovered BxaP, a previously unknown anti-phage defense protein found within genomic loci encoding BREX and RM systems but functioning independently to confer phage resistance. A comparable structural-screening framework was applied to >285 000 phage proteins to identify structural homologs of known anti-defense proteins such as Acrs, anti-RecBCD factors, and anti-RM proteins.

Overall, structure-guided discovery provides a powerful complement to sequence-based methods by enabling the detection of remote homologs and uncovering single-gene defense proteins that would otherwise remain hidden. Its strength lies in capturing conserved protein folds and functional architectures that transcend sequence divergence, thereby broadening the scope of identifiable defense components. Nevertheless, current applications are largely confined to single proteins and are insufficient for resolving the higher-order organization of multigene systems, where operon-level context and modular architectures are essential for defense functionality. To address these limitations, future methodological advances should aim to integrate structural homology searches with system-level delineation, enabling both fine-grained detection of divergent proteins and a comprehensive understanding of complex antiviral defense systems. Additionally, improving the scalability and computational efficiency of structural comparison tools will be critical for routine application in large-scale metagenomic surveys. Together, these directions point toward the development of hybrid pipelines that integrate structural features with contextual genomic features, ultimately enhancing the sensitivity of computational defense system discovery.

Potential strategies for addressing current limitations

Despite its potential, the current structure-guided approach remains limited in its ability to identify multigene antiviral defense systems, which are prevalent in prokaryotic genomes and often rely on gene co-localization and coordinated function. To overcome this limitation and broaden the scope of antiviral defense system discovery, we propose a two-step optimization strategy that integrates structural homology detection with system-level modeling. This integrated approach would improve both the sensitivity and scalability of structure-based pipelines.

First, it is crucial to optimize the structural comparison phase following protein modeling. Since AlphaFold2 and AlphaFold3 offer state-of-the-art structure prediction accuracy, the performance of downstream discovery depends heavily on the choice of alignment tools. Existing structural alignment tools vary significantly in their strengths, and selecting the appropriate one hinges on specific research priorities, such as precision, throughput, and flexibility. Developing pipelines that integrate accurate structure modeling with efficient comparison remains a challenge, particularly in large-scale screening.

Leveraging advanced protein structure comparison tools, including Dali [116], TM-align [117], FAST [118], FoldSeek [109], MAMMOTH [119], TM-Vec [31], and FATCAT [120, 121] (Table 3), can significantly improve the sensitivity of remote homology identification. High-precision alignment tools, such as Dali, TM-align, and US-align are ideal for identifying remote homologs via global or local structural alignments, albeit at higher computational cost. US-align integrates accurate and efficient structural alignment within a unified framework, supporting pairwise and multiple alignments across proteins, RNA, DNA, and macromolecular complexes [115]. FAST and FoldSeek enable high-throughput structural searches, achieving substantial speed gains with only minimal sensitivity loss. Flexible alignment tools, such as MAMMOTH and FATCAT, detect local distortions or structural rearrangements beyond rigid-body alignment. MAMMOTH is highly effective for local alignment, assisting in pinpointing specific structural similarities. FATCAT offers flexible alignment by allowing twists and gaps between aligned fragment pairs (AFPs), and evaluates statistical significance using P-values from extreme value distributions [121]. AI-driven methods, such as TM-Vec, can infer structural similarity directly from sequences, enabling remote homology detection without requiring full 3D modeling. TM-Vec has demonstrated higher accuracy in CATH homology retrieval (88%) compared to FoldSeek (85%) [31]. When combined with DeepBLAST, TM-Vec enables structure-based alignments by identifying structurally homologous regions between proteins using only sequence data [31]. Collectively, these tools offer complementary advantages that can be leveraged to tailor structure comparison strategies based on analysis scale and resolution needs [31, 109, 115–121] (Table 3). A modular pipeline combining high-accuracy methods for critical targets and high-speed tools for initial filtering may offer optimal performance.

Second, to address the limitation of structure-based methods in identifying only single-gene defense systems, we propose extending the pipeline by incorporating a system-level identification step inspired by MacSyFinder. While structural alignment excels at detecting distant homologs of known defense proteins, it cannot determine whether these proteins are part of coordinated multigene systems. MacSyFinder is a rule-based framework specifically designed for the system-level identification of multigene molecular systems in prokaryotic genomes. To bridge this gap, we suggest defining system models based on structurally identified core components of known defense systems and applying genomic architecture rules derived from MacSyFinder, such as gene co-localization, order, and orientation. If all core components are present in a genome and satisfy these spatial constraints, the locus can be inferred to encode a candidate multigene defense system.

This strategy links structure-level homology detection with operon-level genomic context, enabling the identification of modular and context-dependent antiviral systems that structure-based methods alone may overlook. By combining the sensitivity of structure-guided protein prediction with the organizational modeling principles of MacSyFinder, this approach proposes a comprehensive structure-guided framework for discovering both single-gene and multigene antiviral defense systems in microbial genomes.

Structure-guided discovery of phage-encoded anti-defense proteins

Building upon the structural approaches applied to predict single-gene defense systems, recent studies have also employed structure-driven strategies to identify viral antagonists of bacterial immunity. In particular, a structure-guided reverse screening pipeline was established and validated, starting from ~32 million phage proteins, which were clustered and filtered to yield 38 700 representative families (each with ≥40 homologs and proteins <200 amino acids). Representative sequences were systematically co-folded with host immune proteins using AlphaFold2-Multimer [122], with iterative predictions per pair to assess confidence and consistency. Candidates were prioritized based on reproducible high-confidence scores and further refined through homolog consistency checks and interface size evaluation, effectively reduced false positives inherent to AlphaFold2-Multimer (Fig. 2b). Experimental validation confirmed seven phage-derived inhibitor families that block host immunity by inserting flexible loops into active-site pockets of immune proteins, thereby obstructing catalytic residues. Remarkably, some of these inhibitors also suppressed homologous immune proteins in plants and animals, underscoring the deep evolutionary conservation of immune mechanisms and highlighting phage genomes as rich reservoirs of immune-modulatory proteins [123].

This discovery not only enhances our understanding of virus-host coevolution but also opens up translational opportunities, as such inhibitors could be exploited as research tools or developed into novel immune modulators. Overall, this work demonstrates the strong potential of structural prediction to uncover biologically relevant viral inhibitors from vast protein spaces, while also emphasizing the necessity of integrating computational pipelines with experimental validation. Importantly, this reverse structural approach parallels the strategies previously used to identify bacterial defense proteins, yet shifts the perspective from predicting immune components to uncovering their viral antagonists, thereby providing a complementary framework for dissecting virus-host interactions.

Genomic context-based discovery of novel defense systems in defense islands

Novel defense systems located between known defense systems

Traditional sequence and structure similarity-based approaches often fail to identify entirely novel antiphage defense systems. To overcome this limitation, genomic context-based analysis has emerged as an alternative strategy. This approach is based on the observation that defense-related genes tend to co-localize within specific regions of the genome, known as defense islands. Such colocalization is not random; rather, it often reflects underlying functional associations, evolutionary recruitment, or co-regulation. These regions frequently contain a mosaic of both known and uncharacterized genes, providing a fertile ground for discovering novel defense systems through guilt-by-association strategies [14, 20, 48, 64, 124–126].

Defense islands are characterized by a significant enrichment of known defense genes (e.g. RM systems, TA modules, CRISPR-Cas) alongside mobile genetic elements (MGEs), such as phage integrases, transposases, and plasmid-related genes. These nonrandom associations suggest coevolution or functional interactions. Furthermore, detailed protein domain analysis of genes in these islands reveals both divergent homologs of known defense components and entirely novel genetic organizations, supporting the hypothesis that defense islands are hotspots for the emergence of new systems [32, 34, 35].

A landmark study by Doron et al. established a computational framework for systematically mining defense systems within defense islands [14]. Their three-stage pipeline consisted of identifying protein families enriched near known defense systems, assembling multi-gene candidate systems using sequence-based clustering and reconstructing conserved genomic adjacency patterns across species, and validating predicted systems experimentally through heterologous expression in E. coli or Bacillus subtilis.

Specifically, Doron et al. analyzed over 14 000 pfam families across more than 45 000 bacterial and archaeal genomes. For each pfam, they calculated two metrics: the defense score (the frequency of co-localization with known defense genes) and the defense context variability score (the diversity of neighboring defense gene combinations). Pfams with a defense score ≥ 65% and defense context variability ≥0.1 (277 in total) were selected as potential defense-related anchors. For each anchor pfam, neighboring genes within ±10 ORFs were extracted and clustered using OrthoMCL to define homologous gene clusters. To reduce redundancy, only the top 10% largest clusters, those that are frequent across multiple species, counting each species once, were retained. Edges between clusters were defined when one cluster directly followed another without intervening frequent clusters; their weights reflected co-occurrence frequency across species, and only the top 10% thickest edges were kept. In each genome, the maximal path containing the anchor pfam and composed of the retained clusters and thickest edges was recorded. Conserved paths observed across multiple genomes were regarded as candidate multigene systems.

Candidate systems with similar gene composition and order were subsequently merged, and only those present in multiple distinct species were further analyzed. Functional annotation of system components was performed using HHpred, Phyre2, PSI-BLAST, and the NCBI Conserved Domain Database, followed by manual curation to exclude mobile genetic elements, metabolic operons, or previously known defense systems absent from the initial dataset. To enhance discovery coverage, a second iteration incorporated gene families from newly identified systems into the positive pfam set, allowing iterative expansion of defense-associated gene contexts. This genomic context-based strategy ultimately revealed 41 candidate single-gene or multi-gene systems were identified, 28 of which displayed broad phylogenetic distribution. Experimental validation confirmed that 9 of 26 successfully cloned systems conferred strong phage resistance, underscoring the effectiveness of iterative defense island mining in uncovering novel antiviral defense systems (Fig. 3a).

Building upon this foundation, Gao et al. developed a domain-independent framework that bypasses reliance on annotated domains. Their method began with approximately 8.7 million genes located within 10 kb or 10 ORFs of known defense genes, which were clustered into 600 000 seed sequences. Seeds were predicted to be defense-related if they frequently co-occurred with known defense genes and exhibited high genomic defense context variability. This approach led to the identification of 7472 candidate defense genes, 23% of which lacked any annotated domains or contained only domains of unknown functions (DUFs), and 37% contained noncanonical domains. From these, 48 prioritized systems were heterologously expressed in E. coli, and several showed antiphage activity against diverse phage types. This study highlights how domain-independent identification based on co-localization and contextual diversity can reveal entirely novel defense systems beyond the scope of existing annotations [20] (Fig. 3b).

Extending genomic co-localization frameworks for defense system discovery, Millman et al. applied MMseqs2 to cluster proteins from 38 167 bacterial and archaeal genomes and computed defense scores based on both Pfam and COG annotation [64]. Anchor clusters were defined for predicting multi-gene defense systems based on criteria such as a defense score ≥ 0.4, q-value ≤0.05, and presence in at least four genera. Using this approach, they identified 21 previously unknown multi-gene defense systems, several of these systems encoded domains homologous to eukaryotic antiviral proteins such as ISG15, SEFIR, and dynamin-like domains, revealing deep evolutionary parallels [64]. In parallel, they investigated reverse transcriptase (RT)-associated retrons, showing that RTs often act as anchor genes within functional defense loci, conferring phage resistance via Abi mechanisms. These results both expanded the catalog of microbial anti-phage systems and clarified the long-debated defensive role of retrons in microbial immunity [48].

In summary, genomic context-based analysis of defense islands has emerged as a powerful framework for discovering both single-gene and multi-gene antiphage systems. By leveraging the co-occurrence patterns of defense genes in microbial genomes, these approaches circumvent the reliance on sequence homology and structural similarity, enabling the discovery of entirely novel defense systems. Collectively, these studies have substantially expanded the known defense repertoire and provide a scalable blueprint for future discoveries.

New defense systems embedded within known defense systems

While previous genomic context-based studies have largely focused on genes located between known defense systems within genomic defense islands, these approaches may still overlook novel antiviral components that are genetically embedded within the known systems themselves. To account for this, Payne et al. introduced a novel computational strategy termed “guilt-by-embedding,” which infers the potential defense functions of previously uncharacterized genes based on their consistent presence within established defense architectures [87].

This strategy was applied to Hma (Helicase, Methylase, ATPase) defense systems, which were identified across more than 230 000 bacterial and archaeal genomes using PADLOC. The authors allowed the presence of up to three embedded genes between canonical Hma components to account for potential modular insertions. After initial annotation, known defense systems were excluded, and the remaining embedded sequences were deduplicated using Seqkit. Non-redundant protein sequences were clustered via MMseqs2 and grouped into homologous families using HHsearch. For gene cassettes embedded in more than five independent Hma loci, HMM models were constructed and scanned across the genome database. Those present in at least 100 genomes were retained, yielding 17 high-confidence candidate systems for further validation (Fig. 3c).

Experimental assays confirmed seven previously uncharacterized antiviral systems, each conferring measurable phage resistance when expressed in heterologous hosts. These results demonstrate that even within well-characterized defense systems, embedded genetic modules can encode additional layers of antiviral functionality that would be overlooked by previously reported genomic context analyses. Importantly, this “guilt-by-embedding” approach complements “guilt-by-association” strategies by interrogating internal organizational features of defense loci rather than relying solely on adjacent genomic neighborhoods.

Methodologically, the guilt-by-embedding strategy introduces several important innovations. It redefines system boundaries by allowing internal insertions, thereby capturing the modular and recombinogenic nature of defense systems. Furthermore, the toolchain, from PADLOC to HHsearch, is modular and broadly applicable to other multigene systems. While initially developed for Hma-like systems, this embedding-based search strategy can be generalized to other complex modular systems, such as retrons, BREX, and CBASS, particularly those located in dynamic genomic regions like genomic islands or prophages. This expands its applicability to a wider range of defense architectures and facilitates deeper exploration of system modularity and nested organization.

Nevertheless, this approach is not without limitations. It remains dependent on known systems as anchor frameworks, and may therefore overlook defense modules not associated with canonical systems. Additionally, genes embedded through horizontal gene transfer or genomic rearrangement could confound functional inference. Rare systems present in only a handful of genomes may also be filtered out by stringent frequency thresholds.

In summary, the guilt-by-embedding framework expands the scope of genomic context-based discovery by shifting the focus from genetically linear adjacency to internal organization. Together, the guilt-by-embedding and guilt-by-association approaches reveal that defense islands are stratified repositories of both canonical and cryptic antiviral functions, underscoring the layered complexity of the prokaryotic immune repertoire.

Methodological insights

Across various approaches, ranging from guilt-by-association methods (which include Pfam-based and domain-independent predictions), to the more recent guilt-by-embedding strategy, a unifying conceptual framework emerges: novel defense systems can be discovered by leveraging nonrandom genomic organization rather than relying on strict sequence or structural homology. These approaches exploit the evolutionary principle that defense genes are often clustered, co-regulated, or modularly inserted within dynamic genomic regions, transforming local genomic context into a predictive signal (Fig. 3).

The advantages of genomic defense context-based discovery methods are clear. These methods leverage the co-localization of defense genes and their neighboring genes within defense islands, enabling the identification of novel systems that might be overlooked by traditional homology-based approaches. First, they bypass the inherent bias of annotation-dependent pipelines, enabling the detection of genes with unknown domains or with highly divergent homologs. Second, by focusing on genomic defense neighborhoods and conserved architectures, these methods are particularly well-suited for identifying multi-gene defense systems whose collective function may not be inferred from individual components. Third, modularity-aware strategies such as guilt-by-embedding extend discovery into the interior of characterized systems, uncovering cryptic antiviral layers that traditional flanking analyses would overlook.

However, these strategies also face important limitations. They remain dependent on known systems as anchors, which may lead to underperformance when defense modules do not cluster within defense islands or are not associated with canonical defense loci. Additionally, high-frequency thresholds may filter out rare but functionally relevant systems, while horizontal gene transfer and genomic rearrangements can introduce confounding signals. Moreover, the reliance on co-localization alone does not guarantee functionality, emphasizing the necessity of experimental validation.

Looking forward, several directions could enhance the utility of genomic context-based discovery. For instance, integrating structural prediction or co-evolutionary coupling analyses could improve discrimination between true functional modules and spurious associations. Expanding databases with experimentally validated defense systems will will enhance the discovery of novel defense systems, broadening the scope of defense islands and increasing the potential for new system detection using this method. In summary, genomic context-based methods represent a powerful paradigm shift in the discovery of novel antiviral defense systems. By transforming the genome itself into a discovery map, these approaches have already significantly broadened the prokaryotic immune repertoire and provide a fertile ground for the development of next-generation computational tools.

MGEs: challenges and opportunities in antiviral defense system discovery

MGEs, including plasmids, prophages, transposons, and integrative conjugative elements (ICEs), constitute an important yet challenging reservoir for discovering novel antiviral defense systems. Their multilayered architecture, where insertion sequences, transposons, integrons, and other mobile modules are frequently nested within plasmids or ICEs, introduces substantial complexity into computational defense system mining. MGEs often encode diverse defense-related modules, such as R-M systems, TA pairs, and CRISPR-Cas loci, that contribute to host antiphage immunity or promote MGE persistence [127]. Because MGEs and host defense systems share intertwined evolutionary histories, genomic context-based filtering strategies designed to remove spurious associations (e.g., excluding MGE-dense regions to avoid false positives) may inadvertently eliminate authentic defense loci embedded within or adjacent to MGEs [14]. This presents a methodological dilemma: while filtering is necessary to reduce noise, overly stringent exclusion of MGE-associated regions risks missing bona fide defense systems that preferentially mobilize or evolve within these highly dynamic genomic environments.

At the same time, MGEs have been increasingly recognized as active reservoirs and vectors of antiviral systems, playing a pivotal role in bacterial defense [19, 83, 128]. Many prophages, conjugative elements, and their parasitic satellites encode defense modules homologous to host-encoded systems, directly contributing to phage resistance [61, 128]. For example, Vassallo et al. demonstrated that intact MGEs frequently localize to defense-enriched genomic hotspots, serving as major distributors of defense systems and shaping the evolutionary dynamics of bacterial immunity [5].

Beyond mere carriage, MGEs actively facilitate the horizontal transfer of defense genes, enabling their dissemination across diverse bacterial lineages [21, 84]. This genetic mobility contributes to lineage-specific diversification of immune repertoires. In natural environments, such as in marine Vibrio populations, MGE-mediated variation in defense modules leads to phage resistance differences among clonal strains, independent of changes in phage receptor genes [129]. These findings highlight the essential role of MGEs in reshaping bacterial defense strategies in response to environmental pressures [129–132].

Large-scale comparative genomics has further revealed that MGEs preferentially integrate into core defense hotspots (cDHSs), which are conserved genomic loci enriched with antiviral systems. These regions harbor a wide variety of known and putative defense modules, such as the newly identified “Shango” system, and are often surrounded by mobile elements [33, 36, 133]. This suggests that MGEs not only promote the exchange and recombination of defense modules but also shape the genomic landscape for modular immunity.

To fully harness this hidden potential, future discovery pipelines should re-evaluate MGE-rich regions rather than discarding them. Systematic exploration of cDHS and MGE-associated loci holds immense promise for uncovering previously overlooked, mechanistically diverse antiviral systems. In summary, MGEs act as both obstacles and drivers of innovation in bacterial immunity. While their evolutionary entanglement complicates detection, their roles in dissemination, diversification, and modularization make them indispensable players in shaping the prokaryotic defense landscape.

Applications of AI in predicting antiviral defense systems

AI-powered prediction of counter-defense systems

AI, particularly machine learning (ML) and DL, has become a powerful tool for discovering counter-defense systems encoded by phages, such as anti-CRISPR (Acr) proteins. These systems often exhibit high sequence diversity and lack conserved domains, making them challenging to identify using conventional homology-based approaches. To address this, a series of ML-based models have been developed to predict Acrs based on diverse sequence-derived features, evolutionary signals, and contextual information.

Among these, DeepAcr employs a deep neural network architecture that integrates convolutional and recurrent layers to learn hierarchical sequence patterns from one-hot encoded protein sequences. Notably, DeepAcr does not rely on explicit feature engineering or genomic context, enabling its applicability across CRISPR-Cas subtypes not included in the training data. The model is trained on curated positive samples from Anti-CRISPRdb and manually defined negative samples, and uses techniques such as dropout, cosine annealing, and early stopping to avoid overfitting. Benchmarking results demonstrated that DeepAcr can generalize to novel Acr families, highlighting the potential of pure sequence-based DL approaches in the discovery of functionally diverse counter-defense elements [134].

In contrast, PaCRISPR integrates evolutionary information by extracting PSSMs through PSI-BLAST, capturing conservation features critical for Acr recognition. These features are fed into an ensemble of support vector machines (SVMs), each trained with different hyperparameters and feature representations. The final prediction is derived from the averaged outputs of multiple base models, improving both robustness and interpretability. Comparative evaluations demonstrated that PaCRISPR achieves high precision and recall across diverse bacterial genomes, offering a complementary strategy to sequence-based neural models [135]. Another noteworthy model employs the ExtraTreesClassifier, an ensemble learning method based on randomized decision trees, to integrate features such as amino acid composition, sequence complexity, and physicochemical properties. Trained on experimentally validated Acrs and non-Acrs, this model demonstrated superior performance in identifying novel Acrs from unannotated genomes [136].

Beyond Acrs, recent studies have extended AI applications to a broader class of viral antidefense systems. A representative framework employed a BERT-based protein language model (PLM) for the numerical encoding of viral proteins, followed by convolutional neural networks for the classification of infection outcomes and interpretable ML for identifying protein pairs that modulate bacterial immunity. Using this strategy, eight novel antidefense proteins from Vibrio phages were predicted and experimentally validated. They counteract a broad range of bacterial defense systems, including AbiH, AbiU, Septu, CBASS, Retron, and DRT. Intriguingly, one antidefense protein was found to concurrently inhibit both Retron and AbiH, despite their lack of homology. While this approach relies on high-quality infection phenotype data, it provides a scalable and interpretable framework for mapping virus-host defense dynamics, with potential applications in phage therapy and synthetic biology [137].

AI-powered prediction of antiviral defense systems

In contrast to anti-CRISPR prediction, the application of AI to uncover prokaryotic antiviral defense systems is still emerging. However, several tools already demonstrate strong predictive capacity through innovative model architectures (Fig. 4). DeepDefense [37] represents one of the first DL-based frameworks specifically designed to identify and annotate prokaryotic antiviral defense systems, with a particular focus on Doron defense cassettes such as Thoeris, Gabija, Zorya, Druantia, Kiwa, Lamassu, Septu, Shedu, Hachiman, and Wadjet [14]. Unlike traditional HMM-based methods that rely primarily on sequence homology, DeepDefense combines convolutional neural networks and bidirectional gated recurrent units to capture both local sequence motifs and long-range contextual features, enabling the identification of immune-related proteins even when sequence similarity is low. The pipeline adopts a two-stage classification strategy: the first module filters out unrelated proteins (initial binary classification), while the second performs multiclass annotation to assign proteins to known system types or flag them as novel candidates (multiclass prediction). To improve reliability, model calibration techniques such as Deep Open Classification (DOC) and temperature scaling are incorporated, allowing the rejection of uncertain predictions. Furthermore, predicted proteins are clustered into genomic “cassettes” based on defense gene colocalization, which enhances robustness and reduces false positives compared to single-gene predictions. Benchmark analyses demonstrate that DeepDefense can identify up to three times more immune cassettes than HMM-based tools such as PADLOC, particularly by capturing low-homology or previously uncharacterized systems, while maintaining scalability for high-throughput genome and metagenome screening.

AI-powered discovery of antiviral defense systems. Current AI models for identifying defense systems include Deepdefense, DefensePredictor, CRISPRCasTyper, CRISPRcasIdentifier, and Evo. Evo is a genomic foundation model that, after being fine-tuned on CRISPR-Cas datasets, has demonstrated the ability to predict and generate defense systems. As a result, Evo holds promise for predicting and designing a broader range of antiviral defense systems through further fine-tuning on large-scale defense-related datasets.

DefensePredictor [38] represents a ML-based framework designed to systematically identify bacterial antiviral defense systems from genomic data. The model was developed using a large curated dataset comprising ~244 000 defense-related genes annotated by DefenseFinder and ~14 million non-defense genes with functional GO/COG annotations, which were reduced by clustering to mitigate redundancy. Each gene was encoded by integrating PLM embeddings from ESM2 (150 M parameters) with genomic context features, including neighboring genes and sequence composition statistics, yielding a comprehensive 3319-dimensional feature representation. These features were used to train a gradient boosting classifier (LightGBM), optimized with extensive hyperparameter tuning using Optuna. Model outputs represent gene-level defensive probabilities, which are further aggregated across operons to identify candidate defense loci. It also integrates SHAP value analysis to provide feature-level interpretability. Applied to 69 E. coli strains, DefensePredictor identified 45 novel experimentally validated systems.

Beyond these broadly targeted tools, ML has also been applied to the systematic classification of CRISPR-Cas systems, one of the best-characterized antiviral defense mechanisms. CRISPRCasTyper integrates traditional HMM-based Cas gene detection with a gradient boosting classifier (RepeatTyper) that assigns CRISPR-Cas subtypes from the tetramer composition of CRISPR repeat sequences [138]. This allows for subtype assignment even in the absence of adjacent cas operons (e.g. orphan or distant arrays). CRISPRCasTyper supports synteny visualization, handles circular genome boundaries, and reports the modular completeness of the system (adaptation and interference). Benchmarking against a manually curated set demonstrated a median subtype-typing accuracy of 98.6%, and the software scales effectively to large genome and metagenome datasets. Complementarily, CRISPRcasIdentifier employs an ensemble of classifiers, including SVM, Classification and Regression Tree (CART), and Extremely Randomized Trees (ERT), to predict not only known Cas proteins but also potentially missing components, based on learned association rules [139]. The model incorporates MCL clustering, MUSCLE-based alignments, and HMM-based profiling, and has shown robust performance in subtype identification across both bacterial and archaeal systems. Its nested cross-validation and feature importance analysis enhance both generalizability and transparency.

Prediction performance of AI-based tools

Specific tools for CRISPR-Cas systems

CRISPRCasTyper [138] represents one of the most advanced tools for the automated prediction and classification of CRISPR-Cas systems, addressing the long-standing challenge of system diversity and continuous expansion of subtype definitions. Built on a curated dataset derived from manually annotated CRISPR-Cas systems (covering 31 subtypes and later extended to >40 000 repeats), the tool integrates profile HMM searches of 680 Cas protein families with a ML module (repeatTyper) based on XGBoost, which classifies orphan or distant CRISPR arrays. In benchmark analyses (Makarova et al.’s Cas annotations [111]), CRISPRCasTyper achieved a median accuracy of 99.5% across 19 subtypes, outperforming CRISPRCasFinder [24] (93.9%). For all 31 subtypes, CRISPRCasTyper achieved a median accuracy of 98.6%. Its computational efficiency is notable, with bacterial genomes (2–6 Mbp) processed in under 1 min and large metagenomic assemblies within 10 min, making it suitable for large-scale studies. The tool demonstrated robust performance in real metagenomic datasets, successfully assigning subtypes to 74% of orphan CRISPR arrays and revealing ecological distribution patterns of CRISPR-Cas systems across environments. Nevertheless, limitations remain, including lower accuracy for rare subtypes due to a reliance on frequent updates to capture novel variants, as well as updates to HMMs for problematic cas genes. Notably, CRISPRCasTyper does not address a binary classification task, but rather a multiclass prediction problem involving subtype-level discrimination, which underscores the complexity of CRISPR-Cas system identification in genomic and metagenomic contexts.

CRISPRcasIdentifier [139] was developed to provide an interpretable and accurate framework for the automated classification of CRISPR-Cas systems. The tool was trained on curated CRISPR-Cas gene cassettes and ~68 594 Cas protein sequences [111, 140], which were clustered to build multiple sets of HMMs (HMM1-HMM5). Each cassette was represented as a feature vector based on maximum HMM bit-scores across Cas protein families and classified using ML algorithms, including CART, SVM, and ERT in a one-vs-the-rest multiclass setting. To account for missing or highly divergent proteins, subtype-specific regression models were further trained to predict normalized bit-scores, providing indirect evidence of absent Cas proteins. This design enabled both high predictive accuracy and biological interpretability, as feature importance analyses and regression outputs consistently recovered known Cas signature genes and functional associations.

In terms of predictive performance, nested cross-validation demonstrated balanced accuracies above 95% for most models, while external benchmarking on a dataset of 6098 CRISPR-Cas cassettes [140] from 4974 archaeal and bacterial genomes (Makarova et al.) yielded an adjusted balanced accuracy of 0.89 and a macro-averaged F-score of 0.91, outperforming existing tools such as CRISPRdisco, CRISPRcasFinder, MacSyFinder, CRISPRone, and HMMCAS. Regression modules (ERT) demonstrated low mean absolute errors (<0.05) for normalized bit-score predictions in most cases, confirming the effectiveness of subtype-specific regressors for predicting missing or unknown Cas proteins. Homology searches also confirmed several predictions of distant Cas homologs. Although the method is computationally intensive during training due to large-scale HMMs construction and nested cross-validation with grid search, inference on new genomes is relatively efficient. CRISPRcasIdentifier performs best on complete gene cassettes and demonstrates strong generalization, but fragmented metagenomic assemblies and rare subtypes remain challenging, potentially leading to false negatives or misclassifications. Importantly, CRISPRcasIdentifier addresses a multiclass prediction problem with regression-based evidence inference, highlighting its suitability for capturing the complexity of CRISPR-Cas system diversity.

Taken together, CRISPRCasTyper and CRISPRcasIdentifier are both designed for the automated detection and subtyping of CRISPR-Cas systems, yet they employ complementary strategies, leading to distinct strengths and limitations. CRISPRCasTyper integrates HMM-based detection of Cas proteins with operon models and a supervised repeat classifier (repeatTyper) to subtype CRISPR arrays. This design prioritizes rapid and scalable inference, particularly for orphan arrays, but its repeat-based classifier offers limited mechanistic interpretability, relying primarily on HMM and synteny evidence. In contrast, CRISPRcasIdentifier represents each gene cassette as a feature vector of maximum HMM bit-scores across Cas protein families and applies multiple ML classifiers (CART, SVM, and ERT) alongside per-protein regressors for missing-protein inference. This approach provides both high predictive accuracy and biological interpretability, as feature importance and regression outputs consistently recover known signature genes, although the model training phase is computationally intensive due to large-scale HMM construction and nested cross-validation.

In terms of predictive performance, CRISPRCasTyper achieves a median per-subtype accuracy of ~98.6% for well-represented classes and can annotate typical bacterial genomes in under one min, with large metagenomic assemblies (60–100 Mbp) processed in less than 10 min. CRISPRcasIdentifier demonstrates strong internal cross-validation performance (>0.95 balanced accuracy) and achieves a macro-averaged F-score of 0.91 on an external benchmark of 6098 curated cassettes, while accurately inferring missing Cas proteins (mean absolute error < 0.05).

Both tools have limitations. Bias in training data and class imbalance affect the prediction of rare or newly discovered subtypes. Genome fragmentation or split cassettes can increase false negatives, with CRISPRCasTyper adopting a conservative approach that may undercall small or fragmented loci, while CRISPRcasIdentifier performs best on intact cassettes. For CRISPRcasIdentifier, HMM design and granularity influence detection sensitivity and downstream subtype classification, and generalization to diverse metagenomic datasets remains limited, as evidenced by a drop in performance from internal cross-validation (>0.95) to external benchmarks (0.89).

From a practical perspective, CRISPRCasTyper is preferable for high-throughput surveys targeting well-represented subtypes where rapid processing is essential. CRISPRcasIdentifier is more suitable for analyses involving incomplete gene cassettes, atypical operons, or cases where interpretability and missing-component inference are required. For critical applications, combining both tools, integrating HMM bit-scores, synteny information, repeatTyper probabilities, and ML evidence, provides the most reliable strategy to minimize false positives and false negatives.

Comprehensive tools for antiviral defense systems

In benchmark evaluations, Deepdefense [37] demonstrated strong predictive performance across diverse sequence homology levels. At high homology (80–95), accuracy ranged 0.93–0.95, ROC-AUC 0.980–0.994, and weighted AUPRC 0.943–0.973, indicating reliable prediction for moderately divergent proteins. Performance decreased at lower homology (50–60), with accuracy 0.83–0.90, ROC-AUC 0.973–0.982, and AUPRC 0.839–0.90. Further reductions (30–40 homology) showed substantial drops in accuracy for sparsely represented classes, highlighting limited generalization to highly divergent sequences.

Despite these strengths, several methodological and practical limitations are evident. First, the reliance on curated training data (21 196 validated immune proteins balanced with an equal number of negatives) introduces biases, as some systems (e.g. Lamassu) remain underrepresented relative to abundant ones like Wadjet. This class imbalance, only partially mitigated through weighted sampling, may lead to higher sensitivity for well-sampled subtypes while reducing performance on rare systems. Second, the model's accuracy declines as sequence homology decreases, limiting its ability to generalize to distant homologs and raising concerns about applications to highly diverse or fragmented metagenomes. Third, interpretability is more constrained compared to HMM-based approaches, since deep neural network features are abstract and not directly linked to biological mechanisms. Although calibration and confidence thresholds help identify potential novel candidates, predictions ultimately require external validation. Fourth, computational costs during model development are substantial: hyperparameter optimization with Bayesian Optimization with Hyperband (BOHB) across multiple architectures and ensemble construction demands significant resources, although the final inference stage is scalable and has been applied to tens of thousands of plasmids, thousands of bacterial and archaeal genomes, and numerous phage samples.

Applications to real-world datasets highlight both promise and limitations. When applied to 7308 bacterial genomes, Deepdefense identified 8751 candidate defense cassettes and 1608 putative novel systems. Compared to HMM-based methods such as PADLOC [28], it showed markedly higher sensitivity: in one test across 76 genomes, it detected 885 immune-related proteins versus 199 by PADLOC, with limited overlap. This demonstrates a strong capacity to detect divergent or weakly homologous immune proteins that escape HMM detection. At the same time, this increased sensitivity raises the likelihood of false positives, especially in fragmented metagenomic assemblies where cassette formation is hindered by incomplete genomic context.

In conclusion, Deepdefense performs both binary and multiclass prediction tasks and extends these to the cassette level, achieving high sensitivity and specificity for known immune proteins while enabling the discovery of divergent candidates. Its key strengths lie in its ability to detect low-homology proteins, ensemble calibration for uncertainty management, and automated cassette construction that reduces single-gene noise. However, limitations include dataset bias, reduced generalization at low homology, high computational costs during training, and limited interpretability compared to traditional HMM-based approaches. In real genomic and metagenomic applications, the method uncovers substantially more candidates than HMM-based pipelines, but at the expense of potentially higher false-positive rates. Thus, Deepdefense is best viewed as a complementary discovery tool that excels at proposing novel candidates but requires careful calibration and external validation to ensure biological accuracy.

In terms of predictive performance, DefensePredictor [38] achieved high precision and recall on the held-out test set, with an average precision (AP) of 0.95. At the protein family level, the model correctly identified more than half of the members in 30 out of 40 defense-related HMM clusters, though ~25% of families still exhibited false negatives. When benchmarked against alternative approaches, DefensePredictor markedly outperformed the guilt-by-association method (AP = 0.38), HMM-based remote homology searches (AP = 0.33), and even embedding-based homology detection using ESM2 (AP = 0.70). These results highlight its superior ability to identify truly novel defense genes, including those lacking detectable sequence homology or falling below conventional coverage thresholds. The model’s probabilistic scoring framework further enables stringent thresholding; for instance, high log-odds scores(e.g. log-odds >7.2) strongly correlated with experimental validation (r = 0.94) allowing users to prioritize high-confidence candidates. When applied to 69 diverse E. coli strains, DefensePredictor identified over 700 unique defense proteins, with 42% of the >200 low-homology candidates subjected to functional testing were experimentally validated, demonstrating that the tool can successfully recover truly novel defense genes. Moreover, DefensePredictor detected substantially more systems than DefenseFinder (27 versus 6.2 per genome in a representative dataset), and the discovery curve did not saturate across thousands of genomes, underscoring its potential to expand the repertoire of prokaryotic immune systems.

From a methodological perspective, DefensePredictor exhibits several advantages. Its sensitivity stems from leveraging PLM embeddings that capture distant sequence relationships beyond traditional alignment, while specificity is enhanced by incorporating genomic context such as GC content and gene orientation. Although model training and embedding generation are computationally intensive, inference is efficient, enabling the pipeline to process bacterial genomes within minutes, making it suitable for large-scale metagenomic surveys. However, limitations remain. The training data were primarily derived from E. coli and systems detectable by DefenseFinder, introducing possible dataset biases that may restrict generalization to distantly related species. A 25% false-negative rate at the protein family level underscores the limited sensitivity of DefensePredictor in detecting highly divergent proteins. Moreover, while SHAP analyses improved interpretability, PLM-derived features remain abstract, limiting mechanistic insights compared to homology-based methods.

When applied to a set of 69 diverse E. coli strains, DefensePredictor effectively identified candidate antiviral defense proteins, including over 700 unique proteins, of which more than 200 had little or no detectable homology to known defense proteins. Although applying stringent probability thresholds can reduce false positives, predictions still require downstream validation, as some may represent false positives， partially annotated or truncated systems, or genes not adequately expressed in the tested host strain. DefensePredictor primarily functions as a discovery-oriented tool, classifying genes as defensive versus nondefensive and enabling the reconstruction of operons and broader system predictions. Its strengths in sensitivity, scalability, and identification of novel candidates make it a powerful complement to homology- and genomic context-based methods, while careful thresholding and experimental follow-up remain essential to confirm biological relevance.

When considered side by side, Deepdefense and DefensePredictor highlight complementary strengths and limitations in sensitivity, specificity, computational cost, and dataset applicability. Deepdefense demonstrates excellent performance on curated datasets, achieving near-perfect ROC-AUC under high sequence similarity and robust accuracy at moderate similarity levels. Its ensemble calibration strategies further mitigate overconfidence and improve rejection of unrelated proteins. However, its sensitivity declines on highly divergent or low-homology sequences, and class imbalance in the training data favors abundant systems over rare ones. By contrast, DefensePredictor exhibits consistently strong sensitivity and precision, outperforming homology-based and cooccurrence-based methods, and effectively recovers known systems while identifying novel candidates, some of which have been experimentally validated. Its use of PLM embeddings enables detection of distant homologs beyond traditional alignment approaches, and inference is computationally efficient, although model training and embedding generation remain resource-intensive. Both tools employ strategies to limit false positives-calibrated ensembles in DeepDefense and probabilistic thresholds in DefensePredictor.

Overall, both Deepdefense and DefensePredictor significantly enhance the capacity of AI-based pipelines to detect antiviral defense systems beyond the reach of HMM-driven tools, but their limitations underscore the need for careful interpretation. Deepdefense excels at systematic calibration and cassette-level inference but is limited by dataset bias, high computational costs, and poor generalization to low-homology contexts. DefensePredictor offers high sensitivity, scalability, and discovery potential but also faces challenges due to biased training datasets, residual false negatives, and limited interpretability of PLM-derived features. In real genomic and metagenomic applications, both methods uncover substantially more candidate systems than traditional HMM-driven approaches, but this comes with increased risks of false discoveries. Thus, these AI tools should be viewed as complementary discovery frameworks: they suggest possible defense mechanisms and identifying novel candidate systems, but their outputs require rigorous thresholding, integration with other evidence, and downstream experimental validation to ensure biological accuracy in complex microbial datasets.

From prediction to design: leveraging genomic foundation models

Recent advances in genome-scale foundation models, particularly Evo and Evo2 [39, 141], showcase strong capabilities across DNA, RNA, and protein modalities. Evo, built on the StripedHyena architecture, combines attention mechanisms with data-controlled convolution operators to capture long-range dependencies in complex genomes. The model excels across multiple biological scales, from molecular and macromolecular complexes to full genomes, surpassing traditional Transformer-based architectures in modeling multigene systems and the structural organization in microbial genomes. These features make Evo particularly effective for uncovering the modularity and gene synteny that define many antiviral defense systems.

The functional potential of Evo has been experimentally validated. When fine-tuned on a curated dataset of 72 831 CRISPR-Cas loci, Evo accurately learned the genomic architecture of these systems and successfully generated functional synthetic CRISPR-Cas operons (Fig. 4). Similarly, training on the IS200 and IS605 datasets enabled Evo to generate functional RNA-protein and DNA-protein complexes, marking a significant milestone in collaborative molecular design using language models. These results demonstrate that foundation models can move beyond sequence classification to support rational system design, bridging computational biology and synthetic biology.

Building on this progress, Evo2 [141] advances genomic foundation modeling to unprecedented scale. Trained on 9.3 trillion nucleotides spanning bacteria, archaea, eukaryotes, and viruses, Evo2 incorporates the StripedHyena2 architecture, which integrates attention with multiple convolutional operators to efficiently capture patterns across up to one million nucleotides. This architecture enables the model to represent hierarchical biological structures, ranging from local sequence motifs and regulatory elements to large-scale genomic organization, achieving greater accuracy and efficiency than Transformer-based approaches. Notably, Evo2 sets a new standard in variant effect prediction, accurately evaluating both coding and noncoding mutations, and demonstrates emergent interpretability by autonomously learning features such as exon-intron boundaries, transcription factor binding sites, protein structural motifs, and prophage regions.

Beyond predictive modeling, Evo2 also exhibits generative capabilities at the genome scale. It can generate realistic mitochondrial, bacterial, and eukaryotic genomes while preserving functional and structural elements. Guided sequence generation further enables controllable design of epigenomic patterns, highlighting its potential for genome engineering. To support broad adoption, the developers released all model parameters, inference tools, and the OpenGenome2 dataset (https://huggingface.co/datasets/arcinstitute/opengenome2), making Evo2 one of the largest and most accessible genomic foundation models to date. Together, these features position Evo2 as a transformative platform for both predictive genomics and rational genome design.

These advances provide a powerful basis for extending foundation models beyond canonical applications, paving the way for the systematic discovery and design of prokaryotic antiviral defense systems. Building upon the core capabilities of Evo and Evo2, these genome-scale foundation models have the potential to learn multilayered representations of prokaryotic antiviral defense systems beyond CRISPR-Cas. Fine-tuning on curated defense-related datasets, such as defense islands, core defense hotspots, and MGEs that drive horizontal gene transfer, enables the models to capture both intrinsic features of defense genes and their genomic-contextual characteristics. Moreover, Evo’s ability to model long-range dependencies and gene synteny allows it to recognize modular arrangements and co-occurrence patterns among defense genes, delineate system boundaries, and infer combinatorial associations within defense islands. These representations further encode evolutionary signals, including patterns of horizontal gene transfer and the co-localization of defense genes with accessory or mobile elements. Collectively, this multiscale learning framework supports not only the accurate annotation and classification of known defense systems, such as CRISPR-Cas, RM, and Abi, but also the generation of novel defense systems that have yet to be observed in nature.

The success of this framework critically depends on the availability of domain-specific, high-resolution training data. Future efforts should prioritize the systematic curation of defense-associated loci, including defense islands, defense-encoding MGEs, and associated regulatory modules and effector modules. Integrating these defense-related datasets into the Evo training pipeline could significantly enhance the model’s ability to identify rare or atypical systems, accelerate the discovery of new antiviral mechanisms, and support the rational design of synthetic immune platforms in microbial hosts. In summary, the generative power of foundation models like Evo represents a transformative opportunity for microbial immunity research. By fusing integrating comprehensive genomic datasets with advanced modeling architectures, these approaches establish the groundwork for next-generation platforms in the discovery and design of antiviral defense systems, with far-reaching applications in synthetic biology, phage therapy, and genome engineering.

Conclusions and future perspectives

In this review, we have systematically summarized recent computational advances in the discovery of bacterial antiphage defense systems. We categorized the existing tools and resources based on four major predictive strategies: sequence homology, structural similarity, genomic context analysis (e.g. defense islands), and AI-based approaches, and discussed their respective strengths, limitations, and potential synergies. A comprehensive list of tools, pipelines, databases, and repositories has been compiled (Table 4), serving as a reference for researchers in the field [23–25, 28, 30, 37–39, 51, 52, 88, 92, 94, 98, 103, 138, 139, 141–143]. Among these approaches, sequence homology- and genomic context-based strategies remain dominant due to their robustness and biological interpretability, whereas structure-guided methods are still limited by high computational costs. Meanwhile, AI-based tools, though still in early stages for defense system discovery, provide promising performance and scalability when supported by well-curated training data and suitably designed deep learning architectures.

Sequence homology-based and structure-guided methods achieve high accuracy in annotating known defense systems and their conserved components, particularly for systems with well-defined conserved domains. However, their ability to detect highly divergent or novel defense systems is limited, as these approaches rely heavily on existing databases and sequence homology to known defense proteins. Structure-guided prediction methods, by capturing conserved protein folds, can reveal single-gene defense candidates with low sequence similarity but conserved structures, providing a distinct advantage for identifying potential novel single-gene systems. Nevertheless, these approaches remain limited in resolving the overall architecture of multigene or modular defense systems.

Genomic context-based approaches, such as defense island mining and co-occurrence analyses, are particularly effective for discovering multigene clusters and previously uncharacterized defense modules. By leveraging anchor genes or known defense genes, these methods can pinpoint candidate novel systems across genomes at scale. However, their performance fundamentally depends on the accurate annotation of anchor genes, and the predicted systems generally require experimental validation to confirm their defensive activity.

Artificial intelligence-based methods, particularly DL models, offer high sensitivity and can integrate information from defense genes and genomic context. They are capable of detecting low-homology proteins and complex system architectures, thereby enabling the discovery of novel defense systems that are difficult to capture with conventional methods. These approaches are especially suitable for large-scale, high-throughput genomic analyses, but their predictive performance depends on high-quality training data, development costs are relatively high, and interpretability remains limited.

Looking ahead, the future of AI-driven computational discovery of antiviral defense systems hinges critically on two key factors: (i) the integration of high-quality, curated defense datasets, including defense islands, MGEs, and core defense hotspots; and (ii) the development of DL models capable of capturing both the intrinsic features of defense genes and their genomic context. Moreover, models such as Evo can leverage long-range dependencies and gene synteny to recognize modular arrangements and co-occurrence patterns among defense genes, delineate system boundaries, infer combinatorial associations within defense islands, and capture evolutionary signals such as horizontal gene transfer and the co-localization of defense genes with accessory or mobile elements. The growing availability of experimentally annotated defense modules, combined with the emergence of genomic foundation models such as Evo and Evo2, provides a robust foundation for this next phase of computational exploration.

In a landmark study, genome-scale language models Evo1 and Evo2 were fine-tuned on bacteriophage genomic datasets and subsequently employed to design complete phage genomes. This approach successfully generated 16 viable, artificially designed phages that exhibited substantial evolutionary novelty, with some outperforming the natural ΦX174 in adaptability and lytic kinetics. Notably, a cocktail of these AI-generated phages rapidly overcame resistance in three E. coli strains tolerant to ΦX174, highlighting their potential utility for phage therapy against multidrug-resistant bacteria [144]. Beyond the direct biomedical relevance, these findings highlight the capacity of genome language models to capture long-range dependencies, modular organization, and evolutionary signals across genomes. Within the context of antiviral defense system research, this progress further illustrates the prospects of foundational genome models to enable generative exploration of modular architectures and to inspire the rational design of synthetic defense systems. Although such applications remain aspirational, the shift from predictive modeling to genome-level generation represents a critical step toward integrating computational discovery with synthetic biology, ultimately accelerating the research and engineering of microbial immune systems.

Recent advances in de novo conserved gene cluster detection provide promising avenues to complement traditional genome context-based strategies for antiviral defense discovery. A representative example is Spacedust, a tool specifically developed for the systematic identification of conserved gene clusters across microbial genomes [145]. Leveraging the observation that functionally related genes in prokaryotes and viruses tend to be colocalized, Spacedust combines sensitive structure-based homology search (via Foldseek) with newly developed clustering and gene-order conservation statistics, enabling high sensitivity in detecting partially conserved and remotely homologous gene clusters. In an all-versus-all analysis of 1308 bacterial genomes, Spacedust identified 72 843 conserved gene clusters covering 58% of 4.2 million genes. Importantly, it successfully recovered 95% of antiviral defense clusters previously annotated by PADLOC, demonstrating its robustness in identifying biologically meaningful gene neighborhoods relevant to antiviral immunity.

These results highlight that conservation of gene neighborhoods can serve as a powerful signal for inferring functional associations. For the discovery of antiviral defense systems, this capability holds particular promise as it addresses a central limitation of genomic context-based approaches: distinguishing genuine multigene defense modules from background genomic variation. By being reference-free and structure-aware, Spacedust offers a scalable framework to systematically discover conserved gene clusters, including highly divergent or previously unannotated defense modules. Integrating Spacedust with conventional defense island and co-occurrence analyses could therefore substantially improve the sensitivity and generalizability of genome-context approaches.

Importantly, recent studies have identified antidefense islands, genomic regions in bacteriophages or the leading region of plasmids enriched in counter-defense genes (e.g., anti-CRISPR and antirestriction) that cluster into functional modules promoting immune evasion [146–149]. These genomic elements mirror the organization of bacterial defense islands and highlight the molecular arms race between host and phage at the genomic scale. Beyond the level of individual genomes, recent theoretical work has introduced the concept of panimmunity, which posits that microbial communities collectively maintain a diverse arsenal of defense and counter-defense genes through horizontal gene transfer. A stochastic Lotka–Volterra model demonstrated that even under the continual turnover of bacterial and phage strains, sufficiently high rates of horizontal gene transfer enable the stable persistence of diverse defense/counter-defense genes in the community pan-genome, akin to an “island migration” model where genes move between hosts rather than species between islands [150]. These insights suggest that defense-counterdefense dynamics extend from modular genomic architectures to community-level gene pools, which are maintained by gene flow. Concurrently, AI models have been successfully applied to predict phage-host interactions [151, 152], indicating that genome-scale interaction patterns can be learned. Building upon these advances, future AI-driven frameworks that jointly consider defense islands, antidefense islands, and the ecological dynamics of panimmunity may map and predict multiscale interaction networks between bacterial defense systems and phage counter-defense strategies. Such integrative models would advance our understanding of molecular arms races and facilitate the rational design of defense or counter-defense systems for microbiome engineering, phage therapy, and synthetic biology.

Recent genomic analyses reveal how bacterial defense systems interact with mobile genetic elements and shape the dissemination of antimicrobial resistance. For instance, in Staphylococcus haemolyticus, a major reservoir of resistance genes, the defensome, including RM, Abi, and CRISPR-Cas systems, is highly modular and frequently associated with MGEs. Notably, genomes harboring CRISPR-Cas systems tend to carry fewer mobile elements and antimicrobial resistance determinants, highlighting a trade-off between immunity and horizontal gene acquisition. This immune-mobilome interface not only influences the evolutionary trajectories of opportunistic pathogens but also underpins their role in spreading resistance within microbial communities [153]. In parallel, advances in phage therapy demonstrate that AI models can accelerate the identification, optimization, and rational design of therapeutic phages, moving beyond labor-intensive empirical screening toward predictive frameworks that consider host range, resistance, and evolutionary robustness [144]. Coupled with emerging synthetic biology capabilities for rapid phage engineering, these approaches pave the way for rationally optimized phage therapeutics against multidrug-resistant bacteria [154]. Moreover, machine learning-driven approaches are transforming gene-editing technologies, enabling the discovery and optimization of novel CRISPR-Cas variants. Taken together, these advances illustrate that AI can serve as a unifying framework across scales, from predicting genomic interaction networks to guiding therapeutic phage design and engineering synthetic defense or counter-defense modules, bridging fundamental microbiology with translational applications in antimicrobial resistance management and synthetic biology [155].

In conclusion, the discovery of bacterial antiviral defense systems is entering a transformative phase driven by the convergence of sequence- and structure-based homology searches, genomic context mining, and AI-powered prediction. While homology- and genomic context-based approaches remain essential for reliable annotation of known defense systems and modular defense clusters, structure-guided strategies and de novo cluster detection tools such as Spacedust expand the accessible search space by enabling sensitive detection of remote or highly divergent systems. In parallel, emerging AI models, including DefensePredictor, DeepDefense, and Evo, demonstrate the growing potential of AI not only to predict defense components but also to generate new defense modules, marking a conceptual a shift to generative exploration. Realizing this potential will require the integration of high-quality, experimentally validated defense datasets with scalable DL architectures capable of capturing multiscale genomic features. Moreover, incorporating ecological frameworks, such as panimmunity, antidefense islands, and the immune-mobilome interface, can extend computational inference beyond individual genomes to community-level dynamics of defense and counter-defense. Ultimately, the synergy between computational discovery and experimental innovation promises to accelerate the identification, rational design, and translational application of novel antiviral defense systems, forging new links between fundamental microbiology, therapeutic phage engineering, and synthetic biology.
