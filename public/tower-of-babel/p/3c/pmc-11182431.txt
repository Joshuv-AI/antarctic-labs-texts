Transanal minimally invasive surgery for benign and malignant rectal lesions: midterm outcomes from a tertiary center
Authors: Mahmood Al-Dhaheri, Fajer Al-Ishaq, Ali Toffaha, Mohamed Abu Nada, Amjad Parvaiz, Mohamed Kurer
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractBACKGROUND: Although transanal minimally invasive surgery (TAMIS) for rectal neoplasia has gained wide acceptance, the mid-term and long-term outcomes are not widely reported in the literature.OBJECTIVE: Describe the mid-term outcomes of patients who underwent TAMIS for benign and malignant rectal lesions in a single center.DESIGN: Retrospective cohort studySETTINGS: Tertiary referral centerPATIENTS AND METHODS: Demographic, clinical, and oncological outcomes of patients who underwent TAMIS between January 2015 and December 2022 were prospectively collected. The indication for TAMIS was based on the National Comprehensive Cancer Network guidelines. The follow up for the cancer patients included clinical examination, tumor markers every 6 months and MRI rectum at the end of one year. In addition, colonoscopy and CT scan at years one and three and a final CT scan and colonoscopy at year five.MAIN OUTCOME MEASURES: Mid-term oncological and clinical outcome.RESULTS: Thirty elective TAMIS procedures included adenocarcinoma for 33.3% (n=10) of the patients, 20% (n=6) neuroendocrine tumor and the 40% (n=12) were adenomatous lesions. Negative resection margins were achieved in all malignant lesions. Perioperative complications occurred in 2 patients (6.6%), one patient had breaching into the peritoneal cavity, and postoperative hypotension occurred in another patient. The median follow-up time was 23 months (range: 5–72 months). Two patients with adenoma and positive margins developed recurrent adenoma (6.6%) and one patient with initial polypectomy biopsy of adenocarcinoma, had TAMIS with histopathology of adenoma and distant metastasis had developed.CONCLUSIONS: TAMIS for local excision of rectal neoplasia is a valid option with favorable mid-term outcomes provided there is adherence to careful selection criteria.LIMITATIONS: Retrospective nature and small number of the patients.

INTRODUCTION

Total mesorectal excision (TME) remains the current best procedure for curative resection of rectal cancer.1 The management of rectal tumors has undergone significant advances in the past few decades with the development of new surgical tools and techniques. The use of minimally invasive techniques (MIS) such as laparoscopy and robotic platforms in TME operations has been widely adopted due to evidence from multiple large randomized controlled trials demonstrating their effectiveness.2–8 However, TME surgery is associated with significant morbidity as well as having functional impacts on quality of life. In addition, with the introduction of screening programs, an increasing number of patients present with early asymptomatic rectal lesions requiring an alternative approach to TME in suitable patients.

Transanal endoscopic microsurgery (TEM), introduced over three decades ago, allows for local excision of rectal lesions with benefits such as better functional outcomes, less morbidity, and faster recovery.9 Although it had the potential to be widely adopted, the high cost of setup and lengthy learning curve prevented it from gaining broader acceptance. More recently, transanal minimally invasive surgery (TAMIS) has emerged as an effective alternative to traditional surgery and TEM. Since its introduction in 2010, TAMIS has gained wider acceptance and popularity due to its favorable surgical outcomes, shallow learning curve, low cost, easier setup, and greater flexibility during the procedure. Several studies have reported its safety and feasibility in the short term.10–12 Despite these advantages, there is a scarcity of data on the mid- and long-term outcomes of TAMIS.13 We present the short and midterm outcomes in consecutive patients undergoing TAMIS procedure at our institution for both benign and malignant rectal lesions.

PATIENTS and METHODS

This is a retrospective review of prospectively collected data of consecutive patients who underwent a TAMIS procedure between January 2015 and December 2022 at a single institution, Hamad General Hospital, Doha, Qatar. All malignant or suspicious lesions underwent MRI rectum, CT staging and were discussed by the multidisciplinary team. Demographic and clinical data were collected, including age, gender, ASA, preoperative diagnosis and tumor location. Perioperative data were also collected, including operative time, length of hospital stay, and postoperative complications. Oncologic outcomes, including disease-free survival and local recurrence rates, were evaluated according to our protocol for surveillance.

Malignant lesions are deemed appropriate for a TAMIS procedure with local excision if the patient had mobile rectal tumors, less than 3 cm in size, not extending beyond the submucosa, with well-to-moderate differentiation, <30% of the rectal circumference, no evidence of nodal involvement, and low-risk histopathological features.

Patients with malignant lesions had a clinical examination every three months for the first one year and every six months for the next five years. Carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA) levels were measured every 6 months for 5 years. MRI rectum, colonoscopy and computerized tomography (CT) scan were performed at one year. Additionally, another had two colonoscopies with two CT scans at the third and fifth year. For adenomatous lesions, the patients had the same clinical follow up in addition to colonoscopy at one year and with subsequent follow up depending on the colonoscopy findings.

The specifics of TAMIS have been described in our initial experience.14 Descriptive statistics were used to summarize demographic and clinical data. Continuous variables were reported as medians and interquartile ranges. Categorical variables were reported as frequencies and percentages.

RESULTS

Of the 30 consecutive patients who underwent TAMIS for rectal tumors or polyps between January 2015 and December 2022, 73% were male with a median age of 56 years (minimum-maximum: 27–87 years) (Table 1). Fifty percent of the tumors were located within 5 centimeters of the anal verge while 46.6% were between 5 centimeters to 10 centimeters and one case (3.6%) was 18 centimeters from the verge. Thirty-three percent of the patients had adenocarcinoma, 20% neuroendocrine tumor, 40% were adenomatous lesions and 6.6% were inflammatory polyps (Table 2).

The median follow-up time was 23 months (minimum-maximum: 5–72 months). R0 was achieved in 100% of the malignant cases, and two of the benign cases (6.6%) had positive margins. The two (6.6%) patients with positive margins had histopathology of tubular adenoma with high grade dysplasia after TAMIS, developed recurrent adenoma and both underwent re-TAMIS with no further recurrence during the follow up period. Another patient with initial histopathology of adenocarcinoma postpolypectomy, underwent TAMIS. The histopathology post-TAMIS was tubulovillous adenoma and distant metastasis developed. There was no specimen fragmentation in the malignant and adenoma lesions. Four patients required further treatment: two patients sent for TME because of upstaging from cT1 on MRI to pT2 after TAMIS, and another two patients with recurrent adenoma required another TAMIS. One patient required a completion proctectomy because of familial adenomatous polyposis.

Two patients suffered adverse perioperative events (6%) in our series. One case of peritoneal breach was treated laparoscopically during the index procedure and another patient developed postoperative hypotension for which he went to the intensive care unit. No surgical cause for his problem was identified and the patient made a full recovery.

DISCUSSION

TAMIS is a natural extension of MIS, and thus has a shallow learning curve which has led to wide adoption of the technique in colorectal practice. Since the first publication in 2010 several studies have shown its safety and feasibility with good short-term outcome.10–12

The TAMIS procedure was introduced in our practice in 2015, when a consultant colorectal surgeon of the team visited a high-volume center in the USA where the procedure was started with good outcomes shown early. Subsequently, the co-inventor of the technique directly supervised the first five procedures in our institute. We used the selection criteria as published in National Comprehensive Cancer Network guidelines to keep the quality assurance and standard of care.15

Our results showed favorable short and mid-term outcomes. The patients and their pathologic characteristics were comparable to others in the published literature.10–13 Hospital length of stay, operative time, and overall complications were also equivalent.10–13

The specimens for cancer cases were submitted as an en bloc resection without fragmentation in any case. Two patients with adenoma and positive margins developed recurrence. Both underwent re-excision using TAMIS and no further recurrence was noted during the follow-up period. There was one patient who developed lung and liver metastasis one year after the TAMIS procedure. This patient was a 40-year-old male referred from a private hospital after having endoscopic polypectomy. The histopathology showed moderately differentiated adenocarcinoma, extending into the superficial submucosa, and the invasive adenocarcinoma was 0.5 mm from the deep cauterized margin. He underwent TAMIS. Histopathology post-TAMI was tubule-villous adenoma, completely excised with negative margins. The closest margin was 4 mm. One year later, digital rectal examination, colonoscopy, and MRI showed no local recurrence; however, CT scan showed distant metastasis. Our multidisciplinary team recommended chemotherapy. The patient traveled to their home country and there was no further follow up. The last encounter with the patient was 13 months after TAMIS. These oncologic findings are comparable when compared to the literature where recurrence rate of 5% to 26% have been reported.11,13,16

Some of the benign rectal polyps in our institute are excised endoscopically using either endoscopic mucosal resection or endoscopic submucosal dissection. Neither procedure requires general anesthesia and are safe in very selective cases.17 However, the TAMIS procedure has the advantage of affording a full-thickness excision of the lesion, which in turn offers better local disease staging.

The current study has some limitations. The small sample size spanned over 7 years with highly strict criteria for inclusion may limit its generalizability. Another drawback of this study is the lack of functional outcome data (urological, sexual and bowel function) which is important especially when compared to other procedures. Additionally, in our practice endoanal ultrasound is not performed routinely, unlike other centers where they perform endoanal ultrasound for early rectal tumors. We rely instead exclusively on MRI for rectal cancer staging. Although use of MRI is highly accurate for rectal staging, it may be less accurate with tumors located in the lower part of the rectum and hence an adjuvant endoanal ultrasound is often recommended.

However, to our knowledge, the current data represents the largest single-center experience in the Middle East region. Although this is a small cohort over a span of 7 years, the overall clinical and oncological outcomes are acceptable, and this should encourage other centers to adopt the technique. The safety of the technique and favorable clinical and oncological outcomes are underpinned by the strict selection criteria of the patients and standardized approach to training of the staff undertaking such procedures.14

The TAMIS procedure remains an important tool in the management of early rectal tumors as well as benign lesions. A well-structured approach for acquiring the skill set as well as strict adherence to selection of the patient, results in acceptable short-term outcomes as well as good long-term oncological results.
