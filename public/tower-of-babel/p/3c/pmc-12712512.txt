Prone-Position Physical Examination of the Ankle for Chronic Ankle Instability: A Technical Note
Authors: Tristan Duguay, Thais Dutra Vieira, Gérard Saillant, Ronny Lopes
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Ankle sprain can lead to chronic ankle instability. Although radiologic imaging contributes to the diagnosis, the most critical step remains a thorough physical examination and patient history. This examination is also essential for identifying risk factors for surgical failure. A rigorous and complete clinical assessment is therefore mandatory before surgical indication. In this article, we describe our complete technique for physical examination of the ankle in the prone position for chronic ankle instability evaluation.

Ankle sprains are common and can lead to chronic ankle instability (CAI), a condition that presents diagnostic challenges.1 Radiologic tests, including radiography, dynamic radiography, ultrasonography, magnetic resonance imaging, and computed tomography arthrography, are valuable tools in the assessment of CAI. However, physical examination remains the primary—and possibly the most important—step in establishing this diagnosis. When surgery is indicated after failed conservative treatment, accurate topographic diagnosis of lesions is crucial for planning the appropriate surgical approach. Although physical examination is well validated for acute ankle ligament injuries, its reliability in chronic cases remains more controversial and less studied.2

Most clinical tests described in the literature have been performed in the supine position,1 but examination in the prone position offers several advantages, which will be discussed in this technical note. This article provides an update on the physical examination for diagnosing CAI and outlines our technique for conducting the entire examination in the prone position (Video 1).

Subtalar and Tibiotalar Laxity Evaluation

To assess laxity, with the patient prone and the knee extended, the examiner stabilizes the talus by grasping the lateral malleolus with one hand. The other hand cups the calcaneus, applying controlled lateral forces in the coronal plane. Tibiotalar laxity is evaluated by grasping the fibula higher. This is the only position that allows for the isolated evaluation of both subtalar and tibiotalar laxity (Fig 2).Fig 2Subtalar (A) and tibiotalar (B) laxity evaluation. Tibiotalar Mobility Evaluation

The subsequent examination is performed with 90° of knee flexion. It is essential to ensure that the hindfoot is fully relaxed before proceeding. To achieve this, the examiner can position his or her knee at the base of the patient’s leg, allowing it to rest completely. The examiner places one hand around the calcaneus and heel and places the other hand over the anterior distal tibia. The foot is moved in dorsiflexion and plantar flexion while the sagittal motion of the ankle is observed (Fig 3).Fig 3Tibiotalar mobility evaluation. Tibiotalar mobility is easily assessed by the examiner, positioned at the patient's side, by performing plantar flexion and dorsiflexion of the tibiotalar joint.

Calcaneofibular Ligament Evaluation

Talar tilt3 can be assessed by applying forced varus to the ankle. The examiner mobilizes the calcaneus with 2 fingers, with the bi-malleolar clamp being fixed with the other hand (Fig 5). The presence of abnormal coronal laxity is assessed. At this point, it is easy to ask the patient to turn over partially to visualize the frontal laxity for himself or herself by making a comparative examination with the healthy side. The patient then becomes fully aware of the problem.Fig 5Talar tilt can be assessed by mobilizing the calcaneus with 2 fingers, with the bi-malleolar clamp being fixed with the other hand. Syndesmosis Test

All syndesmosis tests4 can be easily performed in the prone position by applying a lateral rotation component to the ankle, except for the cross-leg test. To perform the Frick test, the lower hand holds the leg stationary and the upper hand exerts a forced lateral rotation of the ankle (Fig 6A). The squeeze test consists of lateral compression of the leg using one hand. To perform this compression movement, the fingers are placed on the fibula, the thumb is placed on the tibia, and the hand is placed just above the midpoint of the calf. The results of both tests are positive if pain is felt (Fig 6B).Fig 6Syndesmosis evaluation. (A) Frick test. Evaluation of Associated Lesions

Anterior and posterior impingement,5 which are frequently associated with CAI, are systematically assessed during clinical evaluation to determine whether they should be addressed if surgical treatment for CAI is performed. Clinical diagnosis of posterior impingement is based on the onset of posterior pain during sudden plantar flexion of the ankle. Anterior impingement can be assessed by performing the Molloy test: The examiner dorsiflexes the ankle and applies firm thumb pressure over the anterolateral joint line to provoke symptoms (Fig 7). To evaluate fibular instability, inversion against resistance is performed, allowing for easy visualization or palpation of anterior displacement of the peroneal tendons.Fig 7Evaluation of anterolateral conflict with Molloy test. The examiner positions the foot in forced dorsiflexion and applies pressure with the thumb in the anterolateral angle of the ankle. The solid arrow indicates the distal load, and the dotted arrow indicates joint movement.

The Silfverskiöld test distinguishes between gastrocnemius and soleus tightness. Dorsiflexion is measured first with the knee extended (gastrocnemius engaged), then with the knee flexed at 90° (gastrocnemius relaxed). In the prone position, we perform an inverted Silfverskiöld test to quantify ankle dorsiflexion with the knee in 90° of flexion by applying increased force to the foot (Fig 8A). The test is then repeated with the leg extended to reassess dorsiflexion (Fig 8B). A difference of 20° indicates a positive test finding, suggesting gastrocnemius retraction.Fig 8Inverted Silfverskiöld test. Discussion

This technical note outlines the assessment of CAI with the patient in the prone position. Although most clinical tests described in the literature have been performed in the supine position,1 prone positioning offers several advantages: it is easy to perform, requires only 1 position for the entire examination, allows the patient to remain relaxed, allows assessment of Achilles tendon retraction, and is the only method for assessing associated subtalar laxity.

CAI a common condition, and its surgical management has advanced significantly in recent years with the development of minimally invasive arthroscopic techniques. Surgical indications are based on a thorough physical examination, which can be challenging for inexperienced surgeons. A methodical and comprehensive evaluation is essential for making an accurate diagnosis, identifying risk factors for failure, and considering differential diagnoses.

At present, there are no data on the diagnostic performance of the different tests in the physical examination of CAI in terms of sensitivity and sensitivity. The diagnostic performance has been studied in acute sprains but not in CAI, with previous studies showing that the sensitivity of clinical tests increased with the delay between examination and acute trauma.2,6,7 This could be even more true in the physical examination of CAI, with previous studies showing that the sensitivity of clinical tests increased with the delay between examination and acute trauma. This effect may be even more pronounced in CAI, potentially leading to improved performance of these tests. Several studies could be carried out to investigate this, including intraobserver and interobserver correlation studies on the talar-tilt test and anterior drawer test in the prone position, as well as comparative studies between prone, supine, and hanging-leg physical examinations.

In conclusion, this technical note shows the feasibility of performing a comprehensive physical examination for CAI with the patient in the prone position, allowing for a single position throughout the assessment. This approach enables a thorough evaluation of associated subtalar laxity.
