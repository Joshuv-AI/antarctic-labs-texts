Active-fluidics versus gravity-fluidics system in phacoemulsification for type 2 diabetes mellitus without diabetic retinopathy: a retrospective observational study
Authors: Jingrui Wang, Yi Ma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe efficacy and safety profile of the active-fluidics system (AFS) in diabetic cataract patients remains inadequately characterized. This study specifically evaluated AFS versus gravity-fluidics system (GFS) in type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) patients without diabetic retinopathy (DR), evaluating early visual recovery, corneal edema patterns, and macular thickness changes.Patients and methodsIn this retrospective observational study, a total of 105 eyes of 105 T2DM patients without DR undergoing phacoemulsification at Tianjin Union Medical Center (November 2020–June 2024) were enrolled. Patients were stratified into AFS (n = 61) and GFS (n = 44) groups based on intraoperative fluidics availability. Both groups underwent standardized 2.4-mm clear corneal incision phacoemulsification using the Centurion system. Intraoperative parameters including cumulative dissipated energy (CDE), ultrasound time, total aspiration time (AT), irrigation fluid and total surgical time were recorded. Outcome measures included best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA), central corneal thickness (CCT), endothelial cell density (ECD), endothelial cell loss (ECL), and central macular thickness (CMT). Cystoid macular edema (CME) was defined as > 30% CMT increase from baseline.ResultsIntraoperative parameters were comparable between groups (all P > 0.05). At postoperative week 1, BCVA was statistically lower (better) in the AFS group (0.08 ± 0.11 vs. 0.13 ± 0.13 logMAR, mean difference − 0.05, 95% CI − 0.09 to 0; P = 0.046), though this 0.05 logMAR difference is below the minimal clinically important difference for cataract surgery. CCT was significantly lower in the AFS group at week 1 (577.21 ± 40.01 vs. 594.93 ± 31.40 μm, P = 0.016) and month 1 (562.64 ± 34.74 vs. 580.95 ± 40.79 μm, P = 0.014), with convergence by month 3 (P = 0.534). No intergroup differences were detected in ECD, hexagonal cell percentage, or coefficient of variation at any time point (all P > 0.05). ECL percentages at month 3 showed a numerical trend toward higher loss in the AFS group (12.3% vs. 8.6%), but this did not reach statistical significance (P = 0.092; 95% CI − 0.62 to 8.08). CMT changes were comparable between groups (all P > 0.7), with CME occurring in 2.9% overall.ConclusionIn T2DM patients without DR, AFS was associated with statistically earlier but clinically modest visual recovery at week 1 and reduced early corneal edema compared with GFS. However, the modest effect sizes, borderline ECL trend, and retrospective single-center design limit definitive clinical recommendations. Introduction

The global prevalence of diabetes mellitus has reached epidemic proportions, with the International Diabetes Federation estimating 537 million adults living with diabetes in 2021, projected to rise to 783 million by 2045 [1]. Among the numerous ocular complications, cataract develops earlier and more frequently in diabetic patients compared to non-diabetic individuals [2]. Diabetic cataract surgery is associated with increased intraoperative and postoperative complications, necessitating more complex management strategies [3].

The corneal endothelium is particularly vulnerable in diabetes. Studies have consistently shown that diabetic patients experience greater corneal endothelial cell loss (ECL), more severe postoperative corneal edema, and delayed wound healing following cataract surgery compared to their non-diabetic counterparts [4, 5]. Furthermore, diabetic patients face a substantially elevated risk of postoperative cystoid macular edema (CME), which occurs in approximately 1.8% of patients without diabetic retinopathy (DR), rising to over 6% in those with non-proliferative disease [6]. These complications significantly compromise surgical outcomes and visual rehabilitation.

The centurion phacoemulsification system (Alcon Laboratories, Fort Worth, TX, USA) offers two distinct fluidics modalities: the active-fluidics system (AFS), which maintains target intraocular pressure (IOP) through automated infusion regulation, and the gravity-fluidics system (GFS), which relies on bottle height [7]. Previous investigations have demonstrated that AFS effectively protects corneal endothelium, minimizes disturbance to ocular blood flow—particularly retinal circulation—and reduces complication rates [8, 9].

Whether these advantages extend specifically to diabetic patients remains uncertain. The compromised blood-aqueous barrier, impaired autoregulation of ocular blood flow, and reduced corneal endothelial reserve in diabetes render these patients exceptionally sensitive to IOP fluctuations during surgery [10, 11]. While AFS may offer potential advantages for this population, though this remains to be specifically demonstrated through its precise IOP maintenance, dedicated clinical evidence comparing fluidics systems exclusively in diabetic patients remains scarce [12]. Notably, the landmark randomized controlled trial by Luo et al. [8] established AFS superiority in the general age-related cataract population, yet its diabetic subgroup (comprising 32–35% of participants) was not separately analyzed. Khokhar et al. [12] prospectively evaluated AFS in diabetic eyes but included mixed DR stages without stratification.

To our knowledge, no study has specifically focused on the predominant clinical scenario: diabetic patients without DR undergoing phacoemulsification, who represent approximately 60–70% of the diabetic cataract surgical population. We therefore conducted this retrospective comparative study to evaluate early visual recovery, corneal edema patterns, and macular thickness changes following AFS versus GFS in type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) without DR.

Patients and methods

Study design and population

This retrospective comparative study was conducted at the Department of Ophthalmology, Tianjin Union Medical Center, Tianjin, China. The study was conformed to the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Ethics Committee of Tianjin Union Medical Center (approval number: [2020C02]). Given the retrospective nature of the study, the requirement for informed consent was waived. The study was reported in accordance with the STROBE (Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology) guidelines for retrospective cohort studies.

We reviewed the medical records of patients with T2DM who underwent uncomplicated phacoemulsification cataract surgery between November 2020 and June 2024. 105 patient allocation to the AFS (n = 61) or GFS (n = 44) was determined by intraoperative system availability and surgeon preference. To minimize selection bias, we included only patients who met predefined eligibility criteria and had complete follow-up data.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria: (1) Diagnosis of T2DM according to the World Health Organization 2011 criteria [13]. (2) Age-related cataract with nuclear hardness grade III according to the Emery-Little classification system [14]. (3) Non-DR as confirmed by preoperative fundus examination and optical coherence tomography. (4) Complete clinical and follow-up data available.

Exclusion criteria: (1) Pre-existing ocular comorbidities including DR, diabetic macular edema, glaucoma, uveitis, or other retinal diseases. (2) History of previous intraocular surgery or ocular trauma. (3) Intraoperative complications requiring conversion to alternative surgical techniques. (4) Incomplete follow-up data or loss to follow-up.

Surgical procedures

All surgeries were performed by a single experienced surgeon using the Centurion Vision System (Alcon Laboratories, Fort Worth, TX, USA) with standardized technique. The fluidics system selection was determined by surgeon preference based on patient availability and system readiness. Standardized surgical technique was employed for all patients: A 2.4-mm clear corneal incision was created at the temporal limbus. Continuous curvilinear capsulorhexis approximately 5.0–5.5 mm in diameter was performed, followed by hydrodissection and hydrodelineation. Phacoemulsification was conducted using the torsional mode with an Intrepid Balanced tip. Nucleus disassembly employed the divide-and-conquer technique: The nucleus was divided from the center toward the periphery using a chopper into multiple small nuclear segments, which were then sequentially emulsified and aspirated using torsional ultrasound. Torsional power was dynamically adjusted intraoperatively according to nuclear hardness, torsional ultrasound emulsification at 60% power, pulse mode duty cycle 50%. Residual cortical material was removed using an angled irrigation/aspiration tip. A foldable acrylic intraocular lens (IOL) was implanted in the capsular bag [15].

For patients in AFS group, target intraocular pressure (IOP) was set at 50 mmHg using the Active Fluidics Control mode. The system automatically adjusted infusion pressure to maintain the preset IOP throughout the procedure. The bottle height displayed in the AFS interface represents the calculated equivalent value (approximately 15 cm per 11 mmHg) and was recorded for documentation purposes. For patients in GFS group, bottle height in the GFS group was initially set at approximately 90 cm above the patient’s eye level and adjusted intraoperatively as needed; irrigation pressure was gravity-dependent. Achieved bottle heights were extracted from Centurion system logs. Identical surgical parameters were used for both groups: vacuum level 450 mmHg, aspiration flow rate 45 cc/min, and torsional ultrasound power adjusted according to nuclear density. All patients received the same ophthalmic viscosurgical device and balanced salt solution for irrigation/aspiration [16, 17]. Intraoperative parameters recorded from the Centurion system log included: cumulative dissipated energy (CDE, %·seconds), ultrasound time (seconds), total aspiration time (seconds), total surgical time (minutes), irrigation fluid usage (mL), vacuum level (mmHg), and bottle height (cm). Total aspiration time was defined as total effective phacoemulsification time—from initial groove creation through cortical cleanup—as automatically recorded by the system. Postoperative topical regimen was standardized for all patients. Levofloxacin 0.5% eye drops, 4 times daily for 2 weeks; Tobramycin-dexamethasone ointment, once daily for 1 week; Prednisolone acetate 1% eye drops, tapered over 4 week. Patients were followed-up at 1 week, 1 month, and 3 months postoperatively. All examinations were performed by an independent ophthalmologist masked to the fluidics system used.

Outcome measures

Given the retrospective design, outcome measures were not prospectively designated as primary or secondary. We systematically extracted the following variables from medical records: Intraoperative parameters: vacuum level (mmHg), bottle height (cm), CDE (%), ultrasound time (seconds), total aspiration time (AT, seconds), irrigation fluid usage (mL) and total surgical time (minutes). Visual recovery: BCVA measured by Snellen chart and converted to logarithm of the minimum angle of resolution (logMAR) units at 1 week, 1 month, and 3 months postoperatively. Corneal status: Central corneal thickness (CCT, µm) by non-contact specular microscopy or anterior segment OCT; corneal endothelial cell density (ECD, cells/mm²), endothelial cell loss (ECL) percentage (%), coefficient of variation (CV, %), and percentage of hexagonal cells (HC, %) by specular microscopy. Macular evaluation: Central macular thickness (CMT, µm) by spectral-domain OCT; cystoid macular edema (CME) defined as > 30% CMT increase from baseline with or without cystoid changes on OCT. Intraocular pressure (IOP) and complications: IOP by non-contact tonometry; incidence of corneal edema, Descemet’s membrane folds, and other adverse events.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS version 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Normality of data distribution was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Continuous variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and compared between groups using independent samples t-test. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies (%) and compared using Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test as appropriate. Longitudinal changes within each group were analyzed using repeated measures analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons. Given the retrospective nature of this study, no a priori sample size calculation was performed. Instead, we report effect sizes with 95% confidence intervals (CI) as the primary metric for interpreting clinical significance of observed differences, consistent with current methodological recommendations [18, 19]. Cohen’s d was calculated as the mean difference divided by the pooled standard deviation, with values of 0.2, 0.5, and 0.8 interpreted as small, moderate, and large effect sizes, respectively. The 95% CI indicates the range within which the true population effect is likely to lie with 95% certainty; statistical significance was inferred when the 95% CI excluded the null value. For transparency, post hoc power estimates are provided in Supplementary Table S1; however, we emphasize that our primary interpretations rely on confidence intervals and clinically meaningful effect sizes rather than post hoc power, as the latter is derivative of observed data and has been criticized as inappropriate for interpreting completed research results [18]. A propensity score-adjusted analysis was additionally performed for the primary outcomes (BCVA and CCT) to address potential confounding by age, HbA1c, and diabetes duration (Supplementary Table S2). A two-sided P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline demographics and characteristics

A total of 105 eyes of 105 patients with T2DM were included in the analysis: 61 eyes in the AFS group and 44 eyes in the GFS group. All surgeries were completed without intraoperative complications, and no cases required conversion to alternative surgical techniques. Baseline demographic and clinical characteristics were comparable between the two groups (Table 1). There were no significant differences in gender distribution, age, laterality (right/left), duration of diabetes, or preoperative glycated hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c) levels (all P > 0.05).

Visual acuity outcomes

Both groups demonstrated significant improvement in BCVA from baseline (Table 3). Preoperative BCVA was comparable between groups (P = 0.578). The BCVA was statistically lower (better) in the AFS group at postoperative week 1 (0.08 ± 0.11 vs. 0.13 ± 0.13 logMAR, mean difference − 0.05, 95% CI − 0.09 to 0; P = 0.046; Cohen’s d = 0.42). This early visual advantage represents approximately one Snellen line difference (logMAR 0.05). However, this 0.05 logMAR mean difference (− 0.05, 95% CI − 0.09 to 0) corresponds to approximately one Snellen line and is below the minimal clinically important difference (MCID) of 0.1–0.2 logMAR established for cataract surgery outcomes. By postoperative month 1 and month 3, BCVA was comparable between groups (both P > 0.05).

CCT at different time points

Preoperative CCT was comparable between the AFS group and GFS groups (P = 0.886), confirming baseline equivalence. The AFS group demonstrated significantly lower CCT at postoperative week 1 and month 1, with convergence by month 3 (Table 4). At week 1, the mean difference was − 17.72 μm (95% CI − 32.10 to − 3.34; P = 0.016; Cohen’s d = 0.48). At month 1, the mean difference was − 18.50 μm (95% CI − 33.16 to − 3.83; P = 0.014; Cohen’s d = 0.50). The 17–18 μm differences at week 1 and month 1 exceed the 10 μm threshold associated with clinically detectable corneal edema, suggesting a modest but potentially meaningful reduction in stromal hydration. By month 3, CCT was comparable between groups (P = 0.534; Cohen’s d = 0.13).

Corneal endothelial cell parameters

Preoperative and postoperative ECD, endothelial cell loss (ECL), CV, and HC percentage were comparable between groups at all time points (all P > 0.05) (Table 5). Both groups experienced significant ECL from baseline. The 95% confidence interval crossing zero precludes definitive conclusions regarding equivalence between groups. Given the borderline P value and the lower confidence bound approaching zero, equivalence cannot be definitively concluded.

Postoperative complications

Adverse events were infrequent in both groups (Table 7). At postoperative month 1, one patient in each group developed exacerbated dry eye with mild corneal epithelial damage. Cystoid macular edema (CME) was identified in one GFS group patient at month 1, with one additional GFS patient and one AFS patient identified at month 3. The overall incidence of CME was 2.9% (3/105), with no significant difference between groups (AFS: 1.6%, 1/61; GFS: 4.5%, 2/44; P > 0.05). No serious adverse events, including end ophthalmitis, retinal detachment, or persistent corneal decompensation, occurred in either group. These findings suggest comparable macular outcomes between fluidics systems in this population, though the low event rate limits definitive safety conclusions.

Discussion

This retrospective comparative study evaluated active-fluidics (AFS) versus gravity-fluidics (GFS) systems in type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) patients without diabetic retinopathy (DR) undergoing phacoemulsification. Our findings indicate that AFS was associated with statistically earlier visual recovery and reduced early corneal edema compared with GFS. However, the modest effect sizes, borderline endothelial cell loss (ECL) trend, and retrospective single-center design limit definitive clinical recommendations. These observations contribute to the evidence base for fluidics selection in diabetic cataract surgery but require prospective validation [8, 9].

The AFS group in our study demonstrated statistically lower BCVA at postoperative week 1 in diabetic patients without DR, consistent with the AGSPC trial findings in the general cataract population [8]. The landmark AGSPC trial by Luo et al. established that AFS achieves superior BCVA at postoperative day 1 and week 1 in the general age-related cataract population [8]. However, the 0.05 logMAR mean difference corresponds to approximately one Snellen line and falls below the minimal clinically important difference of 0.1–0.2 logMAR established for cataract surgery outcomes [20, 21]. By month 1, this between-group difference was no longer detectable, and visual outcomes converged by month 3. The clinical relevance of this transient advantage should be interpreted cautiously. This convergence suggests that the primary benefit of AFS lies in accelerating early recovery rather than altering ultimate visual outcomes. For diabetic patients, who often experience delayed wound healing and prolonged inflammation, earlier recovery of even modest visual function may facilitate return to daily activities or timely second-eye surgery [4, 5]. Nonetheless, we refrain from claiming clinically meaningful visual superiority, as the magnitude and duration of the observed difference are limited.

The most robust finding in our study was the significantly lower central corneal thickness (CCT) in the AFS group at postoperative week 1 and month 1. This 17–18 μm difference exceed the 10 μm threshold associated with subjectively detectable corneal edema, representing approximately 45% less corneal swelling, suggests that fluidics system selection may influence early corneal recovery, independent of energy delivery beyond the effects of ultrasound energy exposure. However, CCT converged by month 3, indicating that the observed advantage reflects accelerated functional recovery rather than sustained structural protection. The dissociation between CCT and endothelial cell density (ECD) observed in our study carries important clinical implications. While CCT distinguished groups as early as week 1, ECD showed no intergroup differences at any time point. Endothelial cell loss (ECL) percentages at month 3 showed a numerical trend toward higher loss in the AFS group (12.6% versus 9.0%), though this did not reach statistical significance (P = 0.053; 95% CI − 0.1% to 7.3%). The confidence interval (CI) including zero precludes definitive conclusions regarding equivalence or non-inferiority [22]. This pattern suggests that CCT may serve as a more sensitive early indicator of corneal functional recovery than ECD, but does not establish AFS superiority in endothelial protection. Our findings align with prior reports of reduced corneal edema with AFS [12, 23, 24], though we extend these observations specifically to diabetic patients without retinopathy. Khokhar et al. [12] observed reduced corneal edema with AFS in a mixed diabetic population, but did not stratify by DR status or quantify temporal recovery patterns. A recent meta-analysis confirmed greater corneal ECL and prolonged corneal edema in diabetic versus non-diabetic patients following phacoemulsification [5]. The temporal profile of CCT reduction paralleled the early BCVA difference, supporting internal consistency. However, the mechanism underlying this association—presumed to involve improved anterior chamber stability with AFS—remains speculative in our retrospective design, as direct intraoperative measurements were not obtained.

CMT profiles were comparable between groups at all-time points, with cystoid macular edema occurring in 2.9% overall. These findings are consistent with reported CME incidence in non-proliferative diabetic populations [25], and suggest that fluidics system selection does not substantially influence macular outcomes in this subgroup. The theoretical risk that AFS-maintained higher IOP might exacerbate macular edema in diabetic patients has limited its adoption in this population [7, 26]. Compared with non-diabetic patients, individuals with diabetes have a higher incidence of postoperative macular edema after cataract surgery, experience more difficulty in recovery, and consequently suffer significantly compromised surgical outcomes [27]. Our findings expanded the evidence base beyond the general population studied in the AGSPC trial [8]. However, the low event rate limits statistical power for definitive safety conclusions, and we caution against overinterpreting these observations as proof of equivalent macular safety.

The observed differences in our study may be partially explained by its fundamental engineering advantage: maintenance of stable IOP through automated infusion pressure regulation, as opposed to gravity-dependent, manually adjusted bottle height in GFS [17, 21]. Diabetic patients exhibit impaired autoregulation of ocular blood flow and compromised blood-aqueous barrier integrity, which may render them more vulnerable to IOP fluctuations [10, 11]. The stable anterior chamber dynamics provided by AFS may reduce turbulent fluid flow, mechanical stress on the corneal endothelium, and inflammatory mediator release, potentially contributing to observed recovery differences [16, 28, 29]. The comparable ECL between groups, despite divergent CCT profiles, suggests that AFS primarily protects endothelial functional reserve rather than preventing cell death. This distinction is clinically relevant: while ECL loss is permanent, functional recovery (as reflected by CCT normalization) determines the duration of postoperative visual impairment.

Strengths of this study include the focused, clinically relevant population (DR-free T2DM); standardized surgical technique with a single experienced surgeon; comparable intraoperative parameters between groups, isolating fluidics system effects; and comprehensive longitudinal assessment of both anterior and posterior segment outcomes. Our findings support several evidence-based considerations for diabetic cataract surgery. AFS may be preferentially selected for T2DM patients without DR, particularly when rapid visual recovery is prioritized (e.g., bilateral sequential surgery, professional visual demands). CCT monitoring at postoperative week 1 may serve as a valuable early indicator of surgical stress and recovery trajectory, supplementing or even supplanting early ECD assessment in resource-limited settings. Macular safety concerns should not restrict AFS use in DR-free patients, given the comparable CMT profiles observed.

This study has several limitations inherent to its retrospective design. First, the absence of a non-diabetic comparator precludes determination of whether observed effects are specific to diabetes or generalizable to all patients. Our findings demonstrate AFS associations within diabetic patients, not diabetes-specific advantages. Second, considering the inherent limitations of retrospective research in sample size, a post hoc power analysis was performed (Supplementary Table S1). The effect sizes (Cohen’s d) and power varied greatly. We acknowledge that the post hoc power analysis relies on the actual effect size obtained from the collected data. All positive outcomes yielded a Cohen’s d around 0.5, indicating a minimum moderate effect size. A modest sample size (n = 105) provides adequate power for detecting CCT differences (post-hoc power 72%) at 1 week post-op but was underpowered for ECL comparisons. Given the borderline P = 0.053 in ECD and P = 0.092 in ECL at 3 month post-op and the lower confidence bound approaching zero, equivalence cannot be definitively concluded, and larger studies with longer follow-up are warranted to clarify this outcome. Third, non-random allocation may introduce potential selection bias. While baseline comparability and propensity-adjusted analyses support the robustness of our findings (Supplementary Table S2). Fourth, single-surgeon and single-center design enhance internal validity but limit generalizability. Surgical technique preferences and patient populations may differ across settings. Fifth, patient-reported outcome measures were not collected, which precludes assessment of subjective visual recovery or quality-of-life impacts. Finally, our exclusion of patients with diabetic retinopathy restricts applicability to this higher-risk subgroup, which merits dedicated investigation given the elevated CME rates observed in proliferative disease. Future prospective randomized trials focused specifically on diabetic patients—incorporating patient-reported outcomes and longer follow-up—are needed to strengthen the evidence. Studies should also evaluate AFS safety across varying stages of DR to define its limits in higher-risk eyes. Additionally, tailoring AFS IOP settings to individual factors—such as baseline endothelial cell count, diabetes duration, or glycemic control—could help optimize outcomes in this heterogeneous population.

Conclusion

In this retrospective study of T2DM patients without DR, AFS was associated with statistically earlier visual recovery and reduced early corneal edema compared with GFS, while macular thickness parameters were comparable between groups. However, the modest effect sizes (0.05 logMAR, ~ 18 μm CCT), borderline ECL trends (P = 0.092), and single-center design limit definitive clinical recommendations. These findings contribute to the evidence base for fluidics selection in diabetic cataract surgery but require validation in larger, prospective, multi-center studies before supporting practice change. CCT monitoring may serve as a practical early indicator of corneal recovery in this population, pending further investigation.
