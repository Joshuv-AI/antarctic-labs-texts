Cryo-EM structure of the native assembled Mfa type V pilus from the periodontal pathogen Porphyromonas gingivalis
Authors: Satoshi Shibata, Hideyuki Matsunami, Kazuhisa Ouhara, Yuri Taniguchi, Makoto Tokoro Schreiber, Alejandro Villar-Brillones, Koji Nakayama, Mikio Shoji, Matthias Wolf
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Porphyromonas gingivalis is a primary pathogen causing periodontal disease. The cell has two kinds of type V pili, the Fim pilus and the Mfa pilus, both of which play essential roles in colonization, biofilm formation, and pathogenicity. The functional polymerized structure of the Fim pilus is known, whereas the structure and assembly mechanism of the Mfa pilus remain unclear. Here, we show the structure of the polymerized recombinant Mfa1 stalk pilin determined by cryo-electron microscopy at 3.0 Å resolution. The atomic model of the Mfa1 filament reveals that Mfa1 pilins polymerize by protease-mediated strand exchange and retain a Ca2+ ion in the metal-binding pocket, which modulates immune recognition of the Mfa pilus by human cells. Furthermore, we elucidated the three-dimensional architecture of the streptococcal-binding region on the Mfa pilus. Our results further strengthen evidence that protease-mediated strand exchange is the universal assembly mechanism of type V pili. Our structure of the polymerized Mfa pilus, which represents the functional state on the cell surface, provides targets for antimicrobial drug design to treat periodontal disease and P. gingivalis-related systemic diseases.

Introduction

Biofilm formation is a general strategy for bacteria to survive in challenging environments, such as those within host tissues. Establishing environmental surface-microbe, host-microbe, and microbe-microbe interactions by adhesive pili is essential for biofilm formation. Their attachment ability directly influences the virulence and pathogenicity of bacteria. Bacterial pili have been classified by their assembly mechanism as chaperone-usher, curly, type IV, sortase-dependent, type V, and electrically conductive pili (e-pili)1–3. Type V pili are unique to Gram-negative bacteria belonging to the class Bacteroidia and are composed of anchor, stalk, and accessory (adaptor and tip) pilin subunits. Structurally conserved type V pilins have an N-terminal domain (NTD) and a C-terminal domain (CTD). Each domain consists of a β-sandwich fold arranged in a transthyretin-like arrangement3. Pilins are exported to the cell surface as lipoproteins via the Sec translocon, the Lol pathway, and lipoprotein-sorting machinery4–6. Monomeric pilin is anchored on the outer membrane by an N-terminal “anchor strand” containing a conserved cysteine residue, which serves as the lipidation site. The pilins located at the base and along the stalk have “donor strands” in their C-terminal domain that are essential for pilin polymerization. In contrast, among the accessory pilins, the tip pilin located at the distal end of the pilus and the adaptor pilin connecting the tip to the stalk lack donor strands3,7. The crystal structure of the stalk pilin FimA from Porphyromonas gingivalis revealed that the donor strand in monomeric FimA is pinned under the loop following its anchor strand, which contains a conserved protease cleavage site. We refer to this state as the “self-complementing” form. Furthermore, structural analysis of polymerized FimA by cryo-EM has clarified that the major type V pilus is assembled by protease-mediated strand exchange7. Loop cleavage of the monomeric pilin at Arg46 by arginine-specific cysteine proteinases (R gingipain: RgpA and RgpB) secreted from P. gingivalis triggers release of the donor strand from the pilin subunit, exposing a hydrophobic groove in the CTD. We refer to this state as “donor strand-extended”. Two cleaved pilin subunits in their donor strand-extended form can polymerize by initial interaction of the C-terminus of the flipped-out donor strand from the donor pilin with the hydrophobic groove in the CTD of the acceptor pilin. Embedding of the donor strands in the hydrophobic groove of the acceptor pilin releases the anchor strand from the NTD of the acceptor pilin. This frees the added subunit from the cell membrane, thereby sequentially elongating the pilus. In the final, stable, polymerized state, the donor strand fully replaces the anchor strand of the acceptor pilin7.

Infection with the oral pathogen P. gingivalis causes not only the development and progression of chronic periodontitis in adults but is also linked to several systemic diseases, such as diabetes, rheumatoid arthritis, cardiovascular disease, pancreatic cancer, and Alzheimer’s disease8,9. P. gingivalis contains two distinct forms of type V pili that are the long major Fim pilus (0.3–1.6 µm) and the short minor Mfa pilus (80–120 nm), both of which are critical virulence factors for host colonization and evasion of the innate immune system10–12. Fim and Mfa pilins are expressed from distinct operons with similar structures, composed of genes encoding stalk pilins (in the major pilus, this is fimA; in the minor pilus, mfa1), anchor pilins (major: fimB; minor: mfa2), accessory pilins (major: fimC, fimD, fimE; minor: mfa3, mfa4, mfa5), and regulatory genes13–19. Both pili attach bacterial cells to extracellular matrix proteins on oral epithelia and play an essential role in the co-aggregation of P. gingivalis with other bacterial pathogens in the oral biofilm20. The interaction between P. gingivalis and oral streptococci involves the association of Mfa1 with the streptococcal cell surface protein SspB, which is a member of the streptococcal antigen I/II21–23, and of FimA with streptococcal secreted glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH)24,25. Crystallography of Mfa1, Mfa2, Mfa3, Mfa4, and Mfa5 showed that these pilins share the double β-sandwich domains fold with other type V pilins3,16,26,27. The crystal structure of Mfa1 revealed electron density consistent with the coordination geometry of a divalent alkali metal ion modeled as Ca2+ in the protruding loop of the C-terminal domain16. Among the Mfa pilins, the anchor pilin Mfa2, the stalk pilin Mfa1, and the accessory pilin Mfa3 have donor strands, whereas Mfa4 and Mfa5 lack them. Accordingly, Mfa4 and Mfa5 are thought to constitute the tip of the pili, while Mfa3 is considered to function as a linker connecting the stalk to the tip complex28.

The structure of the polymeric state of Mfa1 remains unclear. In the present work, we determined the polymerized Mfa1 stalk pilin by cryo-EM, elucidated the importance of the C-terminal strand of Mfa1 in pilin polymerization, and revealed the surfaces of Mfa1 involved in microbe-microbe interactions at near-atomic resolution. Our structure provides insights about functional surfaces relevant as immune epitopes and for pilus adhesion, which are essential for the development of antibacterial drugs.

Results and discussion

Mfa pilus polymerization relies on protease-mediated strand exchange

The full-length Mfa1 stalk pilin subunit of P. gingivalis strain ATCC 33277 is composed of 563 amino acids, including a secretion signal sequence (residues 1–19), a lipidation site at Cys20, and an Rgp cleavage site at Arg49 (Fig. 1A). We cloned and overexpressed the N-terminal His-tagged recombinant Mfa1 (rMfa1) containing residues 21–563 in Escherichia coli and then purified the subunit in monomeric form. We have previously succeeded in structure determination of polymerized FimA by cryo-EM after in vitro treatment of monomeric recombinant FimA pilin (rFimA) with the arginine-specific protease RgpB from P. gingivalis7. Using the same strategy, we purified, polymerized, and concentrated rMfa1 by polyethylene glycol precipitation followed by ultracentrifugation. Imaging by transmission electron microscopy (TEM) of the negative-stained rMfa1 filaments showed a twisted ribbon structure (Fig. 1B), which was similar in morphology to the Mfa pili isolated from pilus-defective (FimA− and Mfa1−) mutant P. gingivalis cells containing a plasmid expressing Mfa1 (Supplementary Fig. 1). We determined the structure of polymerized rMfa1 by single particle cryo-EM using the same workflow previously applied to FimA. The two-dimensional (2D) class average images generated from cryo-EM images show segments of the polymerized filamentous rMfa1 structure, which is 54 Å wide and contains 4–5 rMfa1 subunits per image segment (Fig. 1C, D). Further image analysis allowed us to create a three-dimensional (3D) electron potential map of the rMfa1 filament at 3.0 Å resolution by averaging images from 267,383 filament segments representing different views. The cryo-EM map enabled us to build an atomic model containing three Mfa1 pilin subunits using the monomeric crystal structure of pMfa1Δ9 (PDB 5NF3)16 as a template (Fig. 1E). The fitted atomic model spanning Ala50 to Leu563 depicts the geometry of the rMfa filament. The N-terminal chain Ser21 to Arg49 was cleaved by the RgpB, and the C-terminus Leu563 was found embedded in the CTD of the anterior acceptor subunit (Fig. 1F and Supplementary Fig. 2A). The helical parameters of the rMfa1 filament were calculated from the atomic pilin subunit models of the polymerized unsymmetrized Mfa1 filament by measuring the relative translational and rotational relationships between neighboring subunits. Based on these measurements, rMfa1 pilins formed a right-handed helical filament with a helical twist of 68.8 degrees (5.2 subunits per turn) and a rise of 74.2 Å (pitch 388.3 Å per turn). The topologies of the self-complementing monomeric and the polymeric states of Mfa1 are illustrated in Fig. 1G and H. The pMfa1Δ9 structure depicts the monomeric state of Mfa1, although the C-terminal nine amino acids (strand βC) have been removed (Fig. 1G). Both NTD and CTD of monomeric Mfa1 are folded in β-sandwich motifs, each consisting of two β-sheets16. The anchor strand β1 is embedded in sheet 1 of the NTD, and part of the donor strand β14 augments sheet 3 of the CTD (Fig. 1G). In the polymeric state, the overall topology is preserved, but the anchor strand β1 and the donor strand β14 are replaced by the donor strands β14 and βC of the posterior donor subunit, respectively (Fig. 1H). With this replacement, the orientations of the β-strands also change from the monomeric state, where β14 is antiparallel to β13, to the polymeric state, where βC becomes parallel to β13. A comparison of the monomeric Mfa1 structure with its polymeric state shows that the donor strands flip out of the CTD at the loop containing Pro539 (Supplementary Fig. 2B). This observation confirms that protease-mediated strand exchange is a common polymerization mechanism for Fim and Mfa pili in P. gingivalis. Protease-mediated strand exchange is also observed in the polymerization of chaperone-usher pili. However, in chaperone-usher pili, the N-terminal β-strand is inserted into the adjacent pilin subunit in a manner that forms an antiparallel β-sheet29,30.

Assignment of numerical hydrophobicity values to each amino acid of the assembled Mfa1 model with the Kyte-Doolittle method31 illustrates the complementary pattern of hydrophobicity between donor strands β14-βC and the hydrophobic grooves in the acceptor subunit (Supplementary Fig. 3) and hence explains pilus polymerization by domain swapping. Note that elongation requires the release of the short anchor strand, which remains attached to the lipid membrane. The C-terminal region from Thr543 to Leu563, including donor strands, contains 11 hydrophobic amino acids. These amino acids on the donor subunit face hydrophobic patches in the acceptor subunit’s groove. The replacement of the anchor strand and β14 of the acceptor subunit with donor strands β14-βC of the donor subunit creates new hydrogen bond pairs and additional hydrogen bonding interactions between βC and the β1-β2-loop in the polymeric state (Supplementary Fig. 4). His62, Val64, and Gly65 on the N-terminal β1-β2-loop region are now within the hydrogen-bonding distance of Ser558 and Val556 on the donor strand. No amino acid residue pairs suggestive of covalent cross-linking were observed.

Thermal stability of the Mfa pilus

Although the polymerized major Fim pilus subunits are not covalently linked, they form a stable biopolymer. Its donor strand is oriented in the groove of the acceptor pilin via a hydrophobic interaction pattern, and it is reintegrated into the β-sheet by establishing hydrogen bonds7. Our cryo-EM structure shows that the same general mechanism assembles the minor Mfa pilus. Additionally, in the Mfa pilus, the N-terminal β1-β2-loop region following the new N-terminus post cleavage (Ala50) is located close to the region where the donor strand was bound in the self-complementing monomeric acceptor subunit (Supplementary Fig. 2A). We tested the thermal and chemical stability of the rMfa1 filament in which its N-terminal β1-β2-loop appears to firmly embed the donor strand within the β-sheet of both the NTD and CTD (Supplementary Fig. 4). Incubation of rMfa1 filaments at 70 °C for 10 min in SDS buffer containing β-mercaptoethanol resulted in a ladder pattern separated by PAGE and detected by western blot (Fig. 2A). In contrast, incubation at 80 °C or higher resulted in complete dissociation into monomers. Under the same conditions, the Fim pilus was stable at 80 °C for 10 min incubation7. The result shows that the rMfa1 filament has slightly lower thermal stability than the Fim pilus. The interaction between the β1–β2 loop and the donor strand does not appear to contribute to increased filament thermal stability. In the FimA filament, no interaction between the donor strand extending from the neighboring subunit and the β1–β2 loop was observed, and instead the donor strand interacted with CTD residues 322–326 of the acceptor subunit7. The Mfa1 C-terminus is essential for initiation of strand-exchange

C-terminal deletion mutant analysis of FimA suggested that the C-terminal residues of the donor strand play a key role in initiating strand exchange with the acceptor pilin3,7. To test the importance of the C-terminal residues of the donor strand for Mfa1 polymerization, we created pilus-defective (FimA− and Mfa1−) mutant cells containing a plasmid expressing only either full-length Mfa1, Mfa1 without the last two residues (Δ562-563), or the last one (Δ563). A ladder pattern was detected by Western blot only in samples from cells expressing full-length Mfa1 (Fig. 2B). While we observed Mfa1 pili on the surface of the cells expressing full-length Mfa1 by electron microscopy (arrows in Fig. 2C–G), plasmids expressing C-terminal truncated Mfa1 failed to complement the ability of defective mutant cells to assemble Mfa1 pili. These results suggest that the C-terminus of the Mfa1 donor strand is essential for strand exchange in the type V pilus assembly mechanism. The C-terminus of the donor strand is thought to initiate binding to the acceptor pilin, resulting in the displacement of the anchor strand and subsequent strand exchange. The spontaneous nature of polymerization in vitro suggests that the intramolecular interaction of the donor strand is unstable, allowing its release upon RgpB-mediated cleavage of the β1–β2 loop, and that intermolecular donor strand interactions are thermodynamically more favorable than the intramolecular interaction within the NTD. The differences in β-strand orientation in the CTD between the monomeric and polymeric states may also contribute to differences in structural stability between the two states. A ladder pattern has been observed for sortase-mediated covalent pili, in which pilin subunits are covalently linked through isopeptide bonds32. In contrast, the ladder patterns of Mfa and Fim pili under partially denaturing conditions reflect polymerization via strand exchange. In this assembly mechanism, the donor pilin provides a β-strand that inserts into the β-sheet of the adjacent acceptor pilin with its C-terminal end, forming stable intermolecular interactions. Noncovalent interactions, including extensive hydrogen bonding within the β-sheet, mediate the stabilization of the polymeric structure. These findings indicate that, unlike sortase-dependent pili, Mfa filaments maintain structural stability without covalent links.

A metal ion is not essential for Mfa1 assembly and stability

The crystal structure of the monomeric Mfa1(PDB 5NF3) contains electron density that was interpreted as a metal ion based on its coordination geometry in a loop that is part of the proline-rich region between residues 503–53916. However, the bound ion type was not identified. Our cryo-EM map of the polymerized rMfa1 revealed electron density at the binding site of the presumed alkali metal found in the same loop region (Fig. 1E, F and Supplementary Fig. 2C). Ca2+ binding is required to assemble and stabilize the pseudopilus from Klebsiella oxytoca and the archaellum from Methanococcus maripaludis33,34. Treatment of archaella with EGTA or EDTA disrupted the interaction between archaellins, leading to filament depolymerization. Thus, we investigated the role of metal-binding in the polymerization and stability of the Mfa pilus. The experiment with rMfa1 filaments treated with EDTA showed that rMfa1 filaments are stable in a buffer containing 50 mM EDTA (Fig. 3A). We created a pilus-defective (FimA- Mfa-) mutant expressing Mfa1 with alanine substitutions in four amino acids (Asp507, Asp509, Asn512, and Glu514 (DDNE/A)) that were predicted to be involved in metal ion binding by the polymerized Mfa1 structure (Supplementary Fig. 2C). The results showed that the mutant cell had pili on its surface, and a ladder pattern was detected by western blot analysis in whole-cell fractions (Fig. 3B, C). These results suggest that the metal ion is not required for polymerization nor for the stability of Mfa1. To identify the bound metal ion, we subsequently performed inductively coupled plasma atomic emission spectroscopy (ICP-AES) using the wild-type rMfa1 filament and the recombinant metal-binding-defective Mfa1 filament containing alanine substitution DDNE/A at a protein concentration of 1 mg/ml (Table 1). Qualitative analysis of the spectroscopic data revealed Ca2+, Mg2+, and S2- in both samples. The signal intensities of Ca2+ and Mg2+ were approximately seven- and two-fold higher in the wild-type rMfa sample than in the DDNE/A rMfa1 sample, respectively. Cu2+, Fe2+, Zn2+, and Mn2+ were undetected in either sample. There was no significant difference in the signal intensity of S2-, which is derived from sulfur-containing amino acids, between the two samples. More precise quantitative analysis of Ca2+ and Mg2+ ions showed that the 1 mg/ml concentration of the wild-type rMfa1 sample contained 1200 ± 95 ug/L Ca2+ and 36.6 ± 3.8 ug/L Mg2+, while the DDNE/A rMfa1 sample at the same concentration contained 287.5 ± 9 ug/L Ca2+ and 34.55 ± 2.2 ug/L Mg2+. Therefore, we conclude that the rMfa pilus preferentially binds Ca2+. Both Ca2+ and Mg2+ are present in saliva and gingival crevicular fluid, but Ca2+ concentrations are relatively higher than those of Mg²⁺; in gingival crevicular fluid, Ca²⁺ and Mg²⁺ concentrations are approximately 135 µM and 8 µM, respectively35,36. The Ca2+ binding site is involved in the human innate immune response

The recognition of Mfa and Fim pili by the host involves Toll-like receptor (TLR) 2 and TLR437–39. Mfa1 induces IL-6 and IL-8 proinflammatory cytokine production in human bronchial epithelial cells, and a molecular docking simulation predicted that the proline-rich region between residues 503–539 containing the Ca2+ binding site in Mfa1 interacts with TLR240. Therefore, we investigated whether disruption of Ca2+ binding by alanine substitutions in Mfa1 affects the immune recognition of the Mfa pilus by human cells. To determine differences in the cellular immune response, human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs) were incubated with 10 µg/ml purified polymerized wild-type rMfa1 or DDNE/A rMfa1 filaments for 48 h, and IL-6 levels in the cell culture supernatant were quantified. We observed that IL-6 production did not differ significantly between the cell cultures treated with wild-type rMfa1 filaments and the untreated control. In contrast, incubation of HUVECs with DDNE/A rMfa1 filaments stimulated IL-6 production more strongly than the wild-type Mfa1 filament (Fig. 3D). The structural change of the proline-rich region by alanine substitutions may have altered Mfa1 recognition by HUVECs. The Mfa1 structure containing Ca2+ ion presumably reduces immune recognition in the host. In P. gingivalis, the structure of lipid A is indicated as a means to evade the host’s immune system41. The Mfa1 structure with complexed Ca2+ ion in the Mfa pilus is considered a new strategy to evade the host’s immune response.

Interaction surface structure of the native Mfa1 filament with the streptococcal antigen I/II family protein SspB

P. gingivalis co-colonizes the oral cavity together with streptococci in a common biofilm20,42. Adhesion to streptococci is established by the interaction of Mfa1 with the streptococcal cell-surface Antigen I/II family proteins SspA and SspB43. Antigen I/II family proteins are composed of the N, A, V, P, C, and CWA domains (Supplementary Fig. 5A)44. SspB Mutation analysis and an inhibition assay of P. gingivalis adherence to S. gordonii with synthetic peptides have suggested that the polypeptide region 225–400 of Mfa1 is required for adherence of P. gingivalis to the SspB adherence region (BAR) 1167–1193 in the C-terminal domain of S. gordonii SspB21–23,45. An in-silico docking analysis suggested a putative binding cleft in Mfa121. Mapping of the SspB interaction region of Mfa1 onto the atomic model of polymerized three Mfa1 subunits shows its 3D location on the Mfa pilus (Fig. 4A). A structural comparison of the putative SspB binding cleft of the monomeric state of Mfa1 with its polymeric state shows that the α-helix 343–362 has moved to a different position (Fig. 4B). The other parts of the binding cleft are structurally conserved, as they overlay precisely in this alignment. We predicted the interaction between the polymerized Mfa1 structure and SspB using a blind docking analysis with the GRAMM (Global Range Molecular Matching) web server46. We analyzed the C-terminal domain 915–1407 of SspB, which includes the BAR domain whose structure has been determined by X-ray crystallography (PDB: 7L0O, Supplementary Fig. 5)47. The analysis suggested possible interactions between the binding cleft of Mfa1, which contains the α-helix, and the vicinity of the BAR domain of SspB; however, no direct helix–helix contact between the BAR domain and Mfa1was supported by the docking analysis (Supplementary Fig. 5B). The crystal structure of the C-terminal domain may not fully represent its native conformation. Furthermore, helix–helix interactions between the BAR and Mfa1 domains may contribute to the initiation of a stable SspB-Mfa1 association, and intermediate states in this binding process may not be captured by our docking simulation. Synthetic polypeptides such as the BAR peptide, comprising the adherence-promoting region of SspB, inhibit the binding of SspB to Mfa1 and reduce the P. gingivalis-S. gordnii biofilm formation48. Characterization of Mfa1 and FimA Pilins

We have determined the polymeric structures of the Mfa1 and FimA7 stalk pilins, which are important for the pathogenicity of P. gingivalis. The structural and functional characterization of these pilins is summarized in Table 2. The biogenesis of the type V pilus is distinct from other types of pili. Both pilins contain a secretion signal sequence and a lipobox motif for export to the cell surface as lipoproteins through the Sec or Tat translocation pathways, ABC transporter, and an unknown lipoprotein sorting mechanism6. After cleavage by the serine protease Rgp at Arg49 (Mfa1) or Arg46 (FimA) in the β1β2-loop, which follows the anchor strand, pilins are polymerized by a sequential strand-exchange mechanism (Fig. 5). Sequence comparison revealed limited sequence identity between Mfa1 and FimA (17%), despite their structural similarity. The C-termini and the sequences of the N-terminal strands that are cleaved off have low similarity. The double β-sandwich domain structure in a transthyretin-like arrangement is a common feature of type V pilins3. The differences in filament width and helical parameters are determined by the molecular dimensions of their pilin subunits. In Mfa1, the calcium-binding site is located within a 50-residue proline-rich region between strands β13 and β14 of sheet 3. In contrast, the corresponding region in FimA is only 17 residues long and lacks a proline-rich sequence7. Although not all binding sites have been identified, multiple molecules, including microbial and salivary ligands, human extracellular matrix and cell-surface proteins, and immune-regulatory molecules, have been reported28. In particular, P. gingivalis pili activate TLR2 and induce pro-inflammatory cytokines, thereby promoting periodontal tissue destruction and exacerbating disease progression49, whereas Mfa pilus suppresses or modulates host immune responses and is important for establishing the persistent colonization of P. gingivalis39. Methods

Strains, Media, and growth condition

P. gingivalis ATCC 33277 cells were grown under anaerobic conditions (80% N2, 10% CO2, 10% H2) at 37 °C in enriched brain–heart infusion (BHI) broth or on enriched tryptosoya (TS) agar plates supplemented with 5 µg/mL hemin (Sigma-Aldrich, MO, USA) and 0.5 µg/mL menadione (Sigma-Aldrich). Luria–Bertani (LB) broth and LB agar plates were used to grow E. coli strains. Antibiotics were added at the following concentrations: ampicillin (50 or 100 µg/mL for E. coli) and erythromycin (Em; 10 µg/mL for P. gingivalis).

P. gingivalis mutant strains, plasmids, and primers used in this study are listed in Supplementary tables 1, 2, and 3, respectively.

Construction of recombinant Mfa1 proteins in E. coli

To generate recombinant Mfa1 and Mfa1 with alanine substitutions in the four amino acids Asp507, Asp509, Asn512, and Glu514 (DDNE/A) with N-terminal His-tag, PCR products were amplified with a primer pair of pET15bMfa1Fw/pET15bMfa1Rv using pTCB-Mfa1 and pTCB-Mfa1-DDNE-to-AAAA, respectively, as DNA templates, which were cloned into pUC118. Cloned DNAs were checked by DNA sequencing analysis. Each lower NdeI-XhoI fragment from the above cloned plasmids was inserted into the corresponding sites of the pET15b vector (Novagen, WI, USA), resulting in pET15b-Mfa1 and pET15b-Mfa1-DDNE-to-AAAA, respectively. Each recombinant plasmid was transformed into E. coli BL21(DE3). Recombinant proteins in E. coli were expressed in LB medium containing 1 mM IPTG at 20 °C for 18 h.

Purification of recombinant Mfa1 and RgpB protease

E. coli cells expressing recombinant proteins were resuspended in 0.5 mM phosphate buffer, pH 7.0, containing 0.3 M NaCl and lysed using BugBuster reagent (Merck, Darmstadt, Germany). Recombinant Mfa1 with an N-terminal His-tag was purified by affinity chromatography using Talon resin (Takara Bio, Shiga, Japan). The purified proteins were concentrated and buffer-exchanged into PBS using an Amicon Ultra centrifugal filter with a 30 kDa molecular weight cutoff (Merck). His-tagged RgpB protease was expressed in P. gingivalis strain 662i6H50 and purified similarly.

Construction of mfa1::cepA fimX-E::ermF mutant

To generate a targeting vector of pGEM-T Easy fimX-E::ermF, the upstream region of the fimX gene (0.42 kb) and the downstream region of the fimE gene (0.40 kb) were PCR-amplified from chromosomal DNA of P. gingivalis ATCC 33277 using pairs of fimXupFw/fimXupBw and fimEdwFw/fimEdwBw primers, respectively, and cloned into the pUC118 vector (Takara, Kusatsu, Japan), resulting in pUC118-fimXup and pUC118-fimEdw, respectively. DNA fragments from SphI-BamHI (0.42 kb) of pUC118-fimXup and from PstI-SacI (0.40 kb) of pUC118-fimEdw were placed at corresponding sites of pGEM-T Easy hbp35::ermF (pKD740)51, resulting in pGEM-T Easy fimX-E::ermF (pKD1402). pKD1402 was digested with ApaI, and the linearized DNA fragment was introduced into a mfa1::cepA mutant (KDP225)3 by electroporation to generate the Emr transformant, resulting in the mfa1::cepA fimX-E::ermF mutant (KDP1111).

Construction of plasmids for complementation

To create complementation plasmids containing the mfa1 gene, the coding regions were amplified with the forward primer mfa1compFw and the reverse primers mfa1compRv, mfa1 compRv (Δ563), mfa1 compRv (Δ562-563), mfa1 or compRv (Δ561-563), respectively. Each PCR product was cloned into pUC118, yielding vectors pUC118-mfa1, pUC118-mfa1 (Δ563), pUC118-mfa1(Δ562-563), pUC118-mfa1 (Δ561-563), respectively. To create a complementation plasmid containing the mfa1 DDNE/A gene, the coding regions were amplified using primer pairs of mfa1 compFw/Mfa1DDNEto 4 ARv and Mfa1DDNEto 4 AFw/mfa1compRv, with ATCC 33277 genome as DNA template, and purified. Furthermore, an amplified PCR product with the primer pair mfa1 compFw/mfa1 compRv, using the above two purified PCR products as templates, was cloned into pUC118, yielding pUC118-mfa1DDNE to 4A. The porK gene region (SalI-XbaI) of pBSSK-cat pro-porK-rgpB ter (pKD955)52 was replaced with the SalI-XbaI DNA fragment containing the coding regions of mfa1, mfa1 (Δ563), mfa1 (Δ562-563), mfa1 (Δ561-563), or mfa1 DDNE/A from the above plasmids to yield pBSSK-cat pro-mfa1-rgpB ter, pBSSK-cat pro-mfa1(Δ563)-rgpB ter, pBSSK-cat pro-mfa1(Δ562-563)-rgpB ter, pBSSK-cat pro-mfa1(Δ561-563)-rgpB ter, pBSSK-cat pro-mfa1 DDNE to 4A-rgpB ter, respectively. The lower KpnI-NotI DNA fragments of those plasmids were inserted into the same region of the pTCB vectors53 to yield pTCB-Mfa1, pTCB-Mfa1 (Δ563), pTCB-Mfa1 (Δ562-563), pTCB-Mfa1 (Δ561-563), or pTCB-Mfa1 DDNE/A, respectively.

Construction of strains for complementation

The pTCB vector containing each mfa13 gene was introduced into E. coli S17-128 by chemical transformation to generate complemented strains. The resulting E. coli transformants were mated with the mfa1::cepA fimX-E::ermF mutant (KDP1105). Transconjugants were selected on blood agar plates containing gentamycin (Gm, 50 mg/ml) and tetracyclin (Tc, 0.7 mg/ml), yielding mfa1::cepA fimX-E::ermF /pTCB-Mfa1 (KDP1112), mfa1::cepA fimX-E::ermF/pTCB-Mfa1(Δ563) (KDP1113), mfa1::cepA fimX-E::ermF/pTCB-Mfa1 (Δ562-563) (KDP1114), mfa1::cepA fimX-E::ermF /pTCB-Mfa1 (Δ561-563) (KDP1115), or mfa1::cepA fimX-E::ermF /pTCB-Mfa1 DDNE/A (KDP1116), respectively.

Gel electrophoresis and immunoblot analysis

SDS-PAGE and immunoblot analysis were performed3 with minor modifications: For whole-cell SDS-PAGE analysis, protease inhibitors N-α-p-tosyl-L-lysine-chloromethyl ketone hydrochloride (TLCK) and leupeptin were added to the solution at 0.1 mM concentration to the SDS sample buffer. For immunoblot analysis, proteins separated by SDS-PAGE were transferred onto a PVDF membrane. The membranes were blocked with 5% skim milk in PBST (PBS containing 0.01% Tween 20) for 1 h at room temperature and incubated with anti-Mfa1 rabbit polyclonal antiserum3. (1:8,000 dilution) in PBST for 1 h at room temperature. After washing three times with PBST, the membranes were incubated with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG secondary antibodies (Sigma-Aldrich) (1:5,000 dilution) in PBST for 1 h at room temperature, followed by three washes with PBST. Immunoreactive bands were detected using enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) reagents and visualized with a ChemiDoc imaging system (Bio-Rad, CA, USA).

In vitro assembly of recombinant Mfa pili

rMfa1 monomers at 1.5 mg/mL concentration were mixed with 1 mg/mL RgpB and incubated at 37 °C for 4 h. Protease inhibitors TLCK and leupeptin were added to the solution at 0.1 mM concentration to stop assembly. Polymerized rMfa1 filaments were precipitated with 4% (w/vol) polyethylene glycol (PEG-6000), 0.2 M NaCl at 4 °C, and collected by centrifugation at 21,000 × g for 30 min at 4 °C. Pellets were resuspended with 20 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.5, and dialyzed against 20 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.5.

Cryo-EM sample preparation and data collection

Three µL of sample solution were applied to holey carbon grids (Quantifoil 2/1 Cu 400-mesh) after plasma cleaning with a Solarus II (Gatan, Pleasanton, USA) for 30 s at 25 °C in a 23% H2, 77% O2 gas mixture. Grids were blotted for 3 s at 4 °C and 100% relative humidity, then immediately plunge-frozen in liquid ethane with a Vitrobot Mark IV (Thermo Fisher Scientific (TFS), MA, USA). Image data were collected on a Titan Krios TEM (TFS) operating at 300 kV in nanoprobe mode using parallel illumination and coma-free alignment, on a Falcon-3 direct electron detector (TFS) in counting mode at a calibrated magnification of 77,142x (corresponding to a pixel size of 1.08 Å/pixel at the specimen level) with a defocus range from −1.5 to −2.25 µm. The microscope and camera were controlled using EPU software (TFS). Images were recorded as dose-fractionated movies in counting mode at a dose rate of 0.5 el/Å2/sec, with a 62-second exposure, for an accumulated total dose of 32 el/Å2.

Cryo-EM image processing

Dose fractions from 2503 movies were aligned using motioncor254, dose-weighted, and summed. All subsequent image processing steps were performed in CryoSparc55. After patch-CTF estimation and manual particle curation, 1,049,307 particles were picked with the filament tracer (diameter 45 Å, separation distance 2 diameters). 2D classification of the 4x-binned extracted particles yielded 710,598 selected filament fragments. Ab initio reconstruction without symmetry and subsequent homogeneous refinement yielded a 3D reference structure at 10 Å resolution. Re-extraction at 2x binning and homogeneous refinement yielded a 4.4 Å structure. Further unbinned refinement within a 400 × 400 pixel box reached 2.89 Å resolution according to the FSC with independent half-sets. 3D classification provided a more homogeneous class with 267,383 particle fragments that were refined to 2.99 Å resolution. This map was chosen over the previous one because it showed clearer structural features for model building. Neither non-uniform refinement nor CTF refinement improved the resolution estimate. Cryo-EM data collection, refinement and validation statistics

Cryo-EM model building and refinement

The crystal structure of Mfa1 pilin (PDB 5NF2) was docked as a rigid body into the cryo-EM density map using UCSF Chimera56. The C-terminal residues in the donor strand were built de novo with an inverted sequence using Coot57. The model containing three Mfa1 molecules was refined using PHENIX_real_space_refine58 (Table 3). The hydrophobicity map was colored according to the Kyte-Doolittle scale implemented in UCSF Chimera31.

Metal ion detection by ICP-AES

Polymerized rMfa1 and DDNE/A rMfa1 filament samples were dialyzed against LC-MS-grade ultrapure water (FUJIFILM Wako, Osaka, Japan) to remove free trace metal ions. Each sample was analyzed at 1 mg/ml concentration on a SPECTRO CIROS CCD spectrometer (SPECTRO, Kleve, Germany).

Quantification of IL-6 production from cultured HUVEC

Human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs) were purchased from Lonza (Morristown, NJ, USA). They were maintained in EBM-2 medium (Lonza) supplemented with FBS, fibroblast growth factor, epidermal endothelial growth factor, hydrocortisone, insulin-like growth factor, ascorbic acid, vascular epithelial growth factor, and amphotericin B. IL-6 production by HUVECs was stimulated by adding purified polymerized rMfa1 filaments to the cell culture at 10 µg/ml and incubating for 48 h. IL-6 amount in the culture supernatant was measured using a human IL-6 enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kit (ELISA MAX™ Deluxe Set Human IL-6, 430504, BioLegend, Inc., San Diego, CA, USA), following the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, an anti-IL-6 monoclonal antibody (diluted in Coating Buffer) was coated onto a 96-well ELISA plate (BD Falcon, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA) to capture the target human IL-6. After blocking each well with Assay Diluent A for 1 h, the sample or standard (diluted in Assay Diluent A from 500 pg/ml to zero) was applied to each well. After application of the detection antibody (diluted in Assay Diluent A), horseradish peroxidase (HRP) conjugated with avidin (1000-fold dilution in Assay Diluent A) was applied to the wells. Colorimetric reactions were developed with o-phenylenediamine (Sigma-Aldrich) in 0.02% H2O2. Color development was stopped with H2SO4 (2N) and measured using an ELISA reader (OD405, Varioskan LUX). The actual concentration of the target was determined by calibration against a standard curve prepared from serial dilutions. Each sample was examined in triplicate wells of a 96-well ELISA plate. The limit of detection for IL-6 was 4 pg/mL.

Statistics and reproducibility

Biochemical experiments were independently repeated at least three times with similar results unless otherwise indicated. Representative data are shown unless otherwise stated. For ELISA assays, data are presented as mean ± SD, and individual data points are plotted as circles. Differences between groups were evaluated using one-way ANOVA followed by appropriate post hoc tests, as indicated in the figure legends. A P value of < 0.01 was considered statistically significant. Each ELISA sample was analyzed in triplicate wells.
