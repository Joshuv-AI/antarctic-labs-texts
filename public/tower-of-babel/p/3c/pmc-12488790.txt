Response to the Letter by Mehta and Sah Regarding Article, Cholesterol and Cardiovascular Risk in Type 2 Diabetes: The Role of Kidney Function
Authors: Mee Kyoung Kim
Published: 2025

We appreciate the letter from Drs. Rachana Mehta and Ranjana Sah1 regarding our recently published study.2 Their insightful comments raise important considerations, and we are grateful for the opportunity to respond.

We agree that relying solely on baseline low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) levels may not fully capture the dynamic nature of lipid control, particularly in chronic disease settings. However, due to limitations in the Korean National Health Insurance Service database, we were unable to obtain repeated LDL-C measurements or longitudinal medication data.3 Nevertheless, we attempted to minimize confounding by excluding participants who developed cardiovascular diseases (CVDs) or died within the first year and adjusted for baseline statin use in our multivariable models. Although reverse causality may still contribute, the consistent and graded association between LDL-C and CVD risk across kidney function strata strengthens the internal validity of our findings.2

We acknowledge the absence of markers such as serum albumin or inflammatory biomarkers, which may influence CVD risk in advanced chronic kidney disease (CKD). To address this limitation, we implemented a 1-year lag period excluding early events and deaths. We also emphasized in our discussion that non-lipid-related mechanisms, including inflammation and uremic burden, may dominate in advanced CKD. Importantly, we incorporated proteinuria as a proxy indicator of renal damage, and our stratified analyses demonstrated markedly higher CVD risk even at LDL-C <70 mg/dL in patients with proteinuria. Nonetheless, we recognize that residual confounding factors in advanced CKD may elevate CVD risk independently of LDL-C.4

We appreciate the suggestion regarding non-proportional hazards. We tested the proportional hazards assumption using Schoenfeld residuals and detected no significant violations. That said, we agree that approaches such as time-dependent Cox modeling or landmark analysis could provide more nuanced insights and intend to explore these in future research.

Our analysis using <55 mg/dL as a reference was exploratory and not intended to imply a universally optimal target. As noted in our discussion, the benefit of such aggressive lipid lowering in CKD populations remains uncertain, and we concur that randomized controlled trials are needed to confirm this threshold. Notwithstanding potential confounding, our data demonstrated a stepwise increase in CVD risk—particularly for myocardial infarction—across higher LDL-C categories, even among those with advanced CKD.

We thank Drs. Mehta and Sah for their critical review and constructive suggestions. Their comments highlight essential directions for future research and underscore the complexity of lipid management in patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus and CKD.
