Research Progress on Dietary Intervention in Radiation Protection
Authors: Jia Du, Xudong Ding, Bingbing Song, Lu Lu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe application of nuclear technology in energy, healthcare, and aerospace sectors is increasingly widespread, causing radiation protection to be an important topic for public health and biomedical research. Ionizing radiation causes harm by damaging cells directly or indirectly. Traditional radiation protection adopts physical barriers and pharmacological methods, which have proved to be effective but have certain limitations. The Mediterranean diet, fasting or calorie restriction, ketogenic diet, plant-based diet, and high-fiber diet are common and reliable dietary patterns. Dietary patterns contribute to regulating key molecular pathways including oxidative stress, inflammatory responses, metabolism, and gut microbiota. Enhancing the body′s resistance to radiation damage through dietary intervention represents a highly promising nutritional strategy. Understanding the mechanisms and research behind dietary interventions for radiation protection facilitates the integration of radiation protection into daily life, enabling the development of personalized, precision-based dietary intervention strategies. This review focuses exclusively on research evidence and mechanism analysis to assess the impact of dietary intervention on ionizing radiation protection and look forward to their potential and challenges for clinical radiotherapy support and radiation protection for special occupational groups.Keywords: dietary patterns, radiation protection, oxidative stress, inflammation, DNA damage repair, gut microbiota

1. Introduction

Nuclear technology finds extensive application across medicine, industry, and nuclear energy development, creating societal benefits. However, the health risks associated with radiation exposure cannot be overlooked. In clinical applications, radiotherapy damages surrounding healthy tissue inevitably while killing tumor cells. 1 Exploration of deep space is constrained by cosmic radiation. Traditional radiation protection primarily adopts physical shielding or chemical agents. Although effective in spacecraft design or medical equipment, physical shielding is often constrained by weight, cost, technical limitations, and practicality. 2 Classic synthetic chemoprotective agents such as Amifostine provide radiation protection but cause adverse effects including hypotension, nausea, vomiting, and drowsiness simultaneously. Its administration timing is critical, requiring intravenous injection within a narrow window (typically 15-30 minutes) before radiation exposure. 3 Cytokine-based protective agents such as G-CSF and GM-CSF may cause myelodysplastic syndromes and promote the proliferation of residual tumor cells, and they are slow to take effect. 4 Other synthetic compounds, such as serotonin receptor agonists and nitric oxide modulators, present issues including narrow specificity of target action, adverse toxic effects, complex administration regimens, or unknown long-term safety profiles. 5 Consequently, there is a need to identify radiation protection strategies that are highly effective, low in toxicity, easy to use, and suitable for long-term application.

Dietary intervention, as a readily accessible and adjustable daily strategy, holds promising applications in radiation damage protection. Long-term dietary modifications can systematically regulate the body′s internal environment by enhancing inherent resistance and resilience. Compared to conventional radiation protectants, dietary pattern interventions offer advantages including systemic effects, low toxicity, long-term sustainability, high acceptability, and cost-effectiveness. While abundant research has examined the radiation-protective effects of individual nutrients or food extracts, systematic reviews focusing on holistic dietary patterns remain relatively lacking. This review systematically elaborates on the characteristics of dietary patterns and their physiological underpinnings, summarizes preclinical and clinical evidence, and proposes clinical translation pathways and cutting-edge application scenarios based on this foundation.

2. Methods

This narrative review involved a structured literature search to identify peer-reviewed articles, conference papers, and relevant reports published up to June 2026, drawing on sources including PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, and Google Scholar. Keywords compliant with the Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) system were used during the search to ensure the relevance of the collected literature. Specifically, the following terms were used: Mediterranean diet, fasting or calorie restriction, ketogenic diet, plant-based diet, high-fibre diet, nutritional interventions, dietary supplementation, ionizing radiation, radiation protection, radiation damage, gut microbiota, antioxidant therapy, oxidative stress, inflammation, energy metabolism, lipid peroxidation, and free radicals. The authors considered studies meeting the following inclusion criteria: (1) focusing on the effects of dietary interventions or dietary components in relation to radiation; (2) examining the impact of dietary interventions on radiation damage (e.g., radiation-induced damage, inflammation, apoptosis, lipid peroxidation, and oxidative stress); (3) involving human subjects or animal models relevant to ionizing radiation; (4) published between 2020 and April 2026; (5) covering theoretical studies and biological foundations in the field of radiation damage and protection. This approach was designed to retrieve studies that addressed the specific objectives of this review. To determine the suitability of studies for inclusion in the analysis, the authors carefully reviewed the titles and abstracts of all retrieved literature, whilst applying the following exclusion criteria: (1) studies using outdated data beyond the specified time frame; (2) studies with irrelevant exposure conditions; (3) studies lacking a mechanistic basis; (4) case reports, editorials, and conference abstracts without complete data. Once relevant studies had been identified, the reviewers independently extracted information from the selected articles to synthesize the findings and present a narrative review. By synthesizing the findings based on their respective expertise and perspectives, the authors ensured the comprehensiveness of the literature analysis.

3. Overview of Dietary Patterns

Animal studies indicate that they affect cancer risk, treatment toxicity and recurrence rates. In radiation protection, unique dietary patterns exert a systemic influence on the human body's radiation response capacity through their special nutrient ratios, micronutrient density, and feeding rhythms. A Summary of the Mechanisms of Action of Foods With Potential for Radiation Protection

3.1. The Mediterranean Diet

Mediterranean diet (MD) is a traditional dietary pattern originating from the Mediterranean region during the 1960s (particularly Greece, southern Italy and Spain). Its pyramid structure emphasizes: based on numerous plant-based foods, including fruits, vegetables, whole grains, legumes, nuts and seeds; extra virgin olive oil (EVOO) as the primary fat source; moderate consumption of fish and seafood (rich in omega-3 fatty acids), poultry, eggs, and dairy products (particularly yoghurt and cheese); infrequent consumption of red meat and processed meats; regular moderate wine consumption with meals (usually as part of the meal); regular physical activity, social dining, and adequate water intake. 52 MD is rich in antioxidants, dietary fiber and resistant starch, which are the primary fuel of beneficial gut bacteria (such as Bifidobacterium, Lactobacillus, and butyrate-producing bacteria). These fuels produce short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) through fermentation,especially butyric acid. Butyric acid provides energy to colonic epithelial cells and maintains tight junctions between cells, as well as functioning as a histone deacetylase (HDAC) inhibitor and G protein-coupled receptor (GPCR) ligand, capable of manipulating the intestinal permeability, cellular growth and proliferation, and the gastrointestinal immune system. 53 It also downregulates the expression of p53-dependent apoptotic signal in hematopoietic stem cells and improves hematopoietic function. 54 Animal studies indicate that MD enhances the body′s endogenous defense against oxidative stress, suppresses inflammatory responses, and regulates apoptotic signal pathways and ageing-related pathways.6-11,55 Clinical application of MD also confirmed its efficacy in mitigating acute toxic reactions to radiotherapy.12,13

3.2. Calorie Restriction

Caloric restriction (CR) means reducing total caloric intake (usually reducing 20%-40%) but making sure of enough consumption of protein, vitamins and essential nutrients. 56 Fasting involves alternating between periods of fasting or extremely low-calorie intake and unrestricted free eating within defined time cycles, including alternate day fasting (ADF), the 5:2 diet and time-restricted fasting (TRF). 57 The body shifts its primary energy source from glucose metabolism to fat oxidation due to the depletion of liver glycogen induced by CR and fasting, with the liver converting fatty acids into ketone bodies such as β-hydroxybutyrate (BHB) and acetoacetic acid. 58 Ketone bodies serve as highly efficient alternative energy sources, and BHB itself functions as a signal molecule. It inhibits HDAC and upregulates antioxidant genes (such as FOXO3a and Mt2), playing a powerful role in potent anti-inflammatory effects in conjunction with blocking the activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome directly. 59 BHB has been confirmed to could reduce inflammation and promote Treg differentiation in a dose-dependent manner. 60 Research indicates that CR and fasting pre-treatment reduce radiation-induced damage to the gastrointestinal tract, bone marrow, hematopoietic system, and nervous system.14-24 This effect relies on the activation of autophagy by CR and fasting, the maintenance of intestinal stem cell function, the alleviation of local inflammation, and the regulation of the gut microbiota, thereby improving radiation damage to the body via the gut-organ axis. However, post-radiation fasting exacerbates oxidative stress and delays recovery from radiation injury. 61

3.3. The Ketogenic Diet

Ketogenic diet (KD) is a dietary pattern characterized by an extremely high proportion of fat about 70-80% of total calories, a low proportion of carbohydrates usually <50g/day or <5-10% of total calories, and a moderate proportion of protein. 62 KD strictly controls carbohydrate intake, imitating the metabolic state of starvation. This forces the body to switch to and sustain long-term reliance on fat metabolism, maintaining relatively high and long-term stable ketone levels in the bloodstream. This is named nutritional ketosis (NK). In recent years, the modified KD has gained attention in the auxiliary treatment of metabolic disorders and tumors. Research indicates that the ketogenic diet takes significant neuroprotective effects, but may induce oxidative stress damage in other tissues.25,26,63 Application of KD needs strict proportional control in clinical practice.

3.4. Plant-Based Diets

Plant-based diets emphasize foods originating from plants, including strictly plant-based diets that exclude all animal products, lacto-ovo vegetarian diets that include eggs and dairy, and flexible approaches centered on plant foods with occasional small amounts of animal products. The core principle is substantial consumption of vegetables, fruit, whole grains, pulses, nuts, and seeds, while avoiding or strictly limiting meat. 64 Plant-based diets are rich in phytochemicals that can influence the body′s antioxidant capacity through multiple mechanisms.27-33,65

3.5. A High-Fiber Diet

A high-fiber diet functions as MD and plant-based diets/vegetarianism. It maintains gut microbiota homeostasis, promotes the production of SCFAs such as butyric acid, and strengthens the physical and immune barriers of the intestinal epithelium because of fermentable fiber and resistant starch.34-37

4. Molecular Mechanisms of Dietary Interventions for Radiation Protection

Dietary patterns protect against radiation damage by regulating complex cellular signaling pathway networks and physiological homeostasis through multi-component, multi-target, and multi-level mechanisms. This chapter will describe the key molecular mechanisms underlying the regulation of different dietary patterns.

4.1. Regulation of Oxidative Stress and Enhancement of Endogenous Antioxidant Defences

Radiation causes indirect damage by generating large quantities of reactive oxygen species(ROS) through the ionization of water molecules. 66 Therefore, enhancing the body′s antioxidant capacity is an important method of radiation protection. Dietary intake directly supplies abundant compounds possessing clear radical scavenging capabilities. Polyphenolic compounds in MD contribute electrons or hydrogen atoms via their phenolic hydroxyl groups to neutralize ROS; carotenoids (such as β-carotene, lycopene, and lutein) quench singlet oxygen through their conjugated double bond structures, interrupting lipid peroxidation chain reactions.7,32 Dietary intake of multiple phytochemicals activates the Nrf2/ARE signal pathway.8,10,33 CR itself and ketone bodies produced by ketosis constitute oxidative stressors capable of mildly activating Nrf2. Nuclear factor E2-related factor 2 (Nrf2) is a key regulator of cellular oxidative stress responses. Under steady-state conditions, Nrf2 binds to the oxidative stress sensor Keap1, subsequently undergoing ubiquitination and degradation. Oxidative stress or certain electrophilic agents (such as dietary polyphenols) may induce conformational changes in Keap1, inducing Nrf2 dissociation. Nrf2 translocates into the nucleus and binds to the small Maf protein to form a heterodimer. This complex activates the antioxidant response element (ARE), thereby initiating the transcription of a series of cell-protective genes such as heme oxygenase-1 (HO-1), NAD(P)H: quinone oxidoreductase 1 (NQO1), glutamate-cysteine ligase catalytic subunit (GCLC) (a key enzyme in glutathione synthesis), superoxide dismutase (SOD), and catalase (CAT). These phase II detoxifying enzymes and antioxidant enzymes enhance cellular detoxification and antioxidant capacity. 67 This means that dietary patterns exert their effects by supplying exogenous antioxidants and activating endogenous antioxidant defense systems.

4.2. Suppression of Excessive Inflammatory Responses

Radiation-induced persistent inflammation is a key cause of tissue damage, fibrosis, and secondary functional impairment. Radiation-induced ROS and DNA damage activate the IKK complex, leading to IκB degradation. This allows NF-κB (p65/p50) nuclear translocation, inducing the gene transcription of TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6, COX-2, and others. 68 Radiation and the resulting ROS and K+ efflux can activate the NLRP3 inflammasome, leading to caspase-1 cleavage and the mature release of IL-1β and IL-18. 69 The NF-κB signal pathway plays an important role in cell proliferation, growth, differentiation, survival, and apoptosis.70-72 Certain phytochemicals block this pathway by inhibiting IKK activity, preventing IκB phosphorylation or NF-κB nuclear translocation,thereby reducing levels of TNF-α, IL-6, IL-1β, and others in post-radiation tissues (such as the intestine, lungs, and blood). 33 BHB is a known NLRP3-specific inhibitor. 26 Butyric acid produced by dietary fiber fermentation also reduces NLRP3 activation. 9 At the same time, certain dietary patterns such as high-fiber diets promote the production of anti-inflammatory factors like IL-10, regulating the pro-inflammatory/anti-inflammatory balance. 37 Dietary patterns systematically regulate immune and inflammatory signaling pathways.

4.3. Promotion of DNA Damage Repair

Dietary patterns influence DNA damage response at multiple stages, reducing radiation-induced DNA damage including activation of the DNA damage response and enhancing DNA repair efficiency. Radiation-induced DNA double-strand breaks (DSBs) rapidly activate ataxia-telangiectasia mutated protein kinase (ATM) and Rad3-related kinase (ATR). ATM and ATR phosphorylate downstream targets including histone H2AX, checkpoint kinases (Chk1/2), and the tumor suppressor protein p53. 73 Certain dietary patterns can upregulate the expression of p53 and its target genes, promoting G1/G2 arrest at the cell cycle checkpoint to afford time for DNA repair.22,63 Activation of SIRT1 induced by phytochemicals and fasting can deacetylate and stabilize p53 and repair proteins. 55 Moreover, DSBs are primarily repaired via non-homologous end joining (NHEJ) and homologous recombination (HR). 74 Research indicates that certain polyphenols influence the activity or recruitment of core NHEJ proteins (such as Ku70/80 and DNA-PKcs).11,75 Fasting and energy restriction in mouse models confirm the ability to upregulate the expression of NHEJ-associated genes (such as DNA-PK and SIRT6), while SIRT6 has been certificated to recruit proteins involved in HR. This suggests that fasting and energy restriction may regulate the HR pathway at the same time.18,76 Fasting enhanced central metabolism, especially the pentose phosphate pathway (PPP), which is a key metabolic pathway providing the reducing power (NADPH) and nucleotide precursors required for DNA repair. 21 However, after radiation exposure, the opposite phenomenon may occur. 61

4.4. Regulation of Cell Fate: Apoptosis, Autophagy, Senescence and Pyroptosis

Radiation induces cell apoptosis, aging, and pyroptosis mediated by Gasdermin family proteins. Phytochemicals (such as genistein and apigenin) may upregulate anti-apoptotic proteins Bcl-2 and Bcl-xL, downregulate pro-apoptotic proteins Bax and Bak, stabilizing mitochondrial membrane potential and inhibiting cytochrome C release.28,65 Autophagy induced by fasting induces the mitochondrial pseudo-membrane potential (PMMP) and removes radiation-damaged mitochondria through autophagy, preventing the release of pro-apoptotic factors and excessive ROS. The mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR) hyperactivation is associated with protein synthesis errors, autophagy suppression and ageing. 77 CR, fasting, and certain polyphenols inhibit mTOR pathway, facilitating cellular entry into a repair state and delaying cellular aging.16,22,78 BHB effectively blocks caspase-1-dependent GSDMD cleavage and pyroptosis by inhibiting the NLRP3 inflammasome. 26 Therefore, appropriate dietary patterns can mitigate radiation-induced cell death.

4.5. Maintenance of Intestinal Barrier Function and Regulation of the Microbiota

The gastrointestinal tract is one of the systems most sensitive to radiation, whose health is closely linked to dietary intake. Radiation damages influence intestinal integrity and barrier function and change the composition and abundance of the gut microbiota. 79 Diet is a significant factor influencing the structure of the gut microbiota,which regulates metabolic status of the organism. 21 High-fiber, high-polyphenol diet selectively promotes the growth of probiotics such as butyric acid-producing bacteria, Lactobacillus, and Bifidobacterium, while reducing the abundance of potentially pathogenic bacteria.80-83 These foods undergo decomposition and fermentation by the gut microbiota to produce short-chain fatty acids, which not only supply energy to intestinal cells but also upregulate the expression of tight junction proteins such as ZO-1 and Occludin, thereby ameliorating radiation-induced damage to the intestinal barrier. Moreover, butyrate modulates the differentiation of regulatory T cells (Tregs), enhancing intestinal immune tolerance; it also inhibits the expression of p53-dependent apoptotic signals in hematopoietic stem cells, thereby improving hematopoietic function.54,84-86 A healthy intestinal barrier and microbiota reduce the entry of endotoxins (LPS) into the bloodstream, thereby alleviating the inflammatory burden and oxidative stress on the liver and promoting systemic regulation of the body′s internal environment via the gut-organ axis. 87

4.6. Promotion of Genomic Stability and Influence of Epigenetic Regulation

Dietary components influence epigenetic regulation by serving as substrates, cofactors, or direct regulators for epigenetic modifying enzymes, regulating the chromatin accessibility of genes involved in radiation responses jointly.

In summary, different dietary patterns exert radioprotective effects through their own unique molecular pathways, and these mechanisms are not simultaneously activated by a single intervention. The Mediterranean diet and polyphenol-rich plant-based diets feature polyphenolic compounds (such as resveratrol, curcumin, caesalpinine, apigenin, and epigallocatechin gallate) as their core active components. They primarily clear radiation-induced ROS by activating the Keap1-Nrf2/ARE antioxidant pathway while simultaneously inhibiting NF-κB nuclear translocation and NLRP3 inflammasome activation, thereby exerting synergistic antioxidant and anti-inflammatory effects. Furthermore, certain polyphenolic components can directly enhance the efficiency of DNA damage sensing and repair through the ATM-p53-p21 signalling axis. A high-fibre diet, on the other hand, primarily works by reshaping the gut microbiota structure. By enriching beneficial bacterial genera such as Bifidobacterium and Lactobacillus, it increases the production of SCFAs—particularly butyrate—thereby inhibiting HDAC activity to attenuate NF-κB-driven inflammatory output, upregulating the expression of the tight junction protein ZO-1 to maintain intestinal barrier function, and reducing the systemic low-grade inflammation triggered by LPS translocation. Calorie restriction and intermittent fasting, in contrast, rely primarily on the regulation of energy-sensing pathways: by inhibiting the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway, they relieve the suppression of autophagy, promoting the clearance of damaged organelles and oxidised proteins; they simultaneously activate NAD+-dependent deacetylases such as SIRT1/6, which, by deacetylating p53 and members of the FOXO family, achieve an adaptive balance between the DNA damage response and cell survival. They can also indirectly regulate the pentose phosphate pathway to influence NADPH production, thereby providing cofactor support for the glutathione (GSH) reduction system. The mechanism modules activated by the aforementioned dietary patterns each have their own specific focus; what they have in common is that they all intervene in core processes such as oxidative stress, inflammatory responses, or metabolic reprogramming via their respective molecular targets, thereby providing protection against radiation damage (Figure 1).

Radiation-induced damage pathways are modulated by diet and dietary components. Following exposure to ionizing radiation, cells produce reactive oxygen species and other free radicals, which cause direct or indirect damage to DNA. The polyphenolic components in the Mediterranean diet and polyphenol-rich plant-based diets exert their protective effects primarily by activating the Nrf2 antioxidant pathway (upregulating HO-1), inhibiting NF-κB inflammatory signaling, and enhancing ATM-p53-mediated DNA repair; high-fibre diets increase SCFA production through gut microbiota remodeling, inhibit HDAC activity, and upregulate ZO-1 to maintain the intestinal barrier; calorie restriction/fasting provides protection by inhibiting mTOR to induce autophagy, activating SIRT1/6 to deacetylate p53, and regulating NADPH metabolism.

5. Analysis of the Applicability of Dietary Interventions in Different Radiation Scenarios

5.1. Acute High-Dose Exposure

For acute, high-dose exposure scenarios such as nuclear accidents or radiation conditioning before bone marrow transplantation, there is an urgent need to prevent or reduce acute radiation syndrome to improve survival rates, particularly hematopoietic and gastrointestinal failure.

Fasting pretreatment represents the most promising strategy. Animal studies have clearly demonstrated that administering IF pretreatment for 24–72 hours before irradiation substantially enhances survival rates. 24 IF rapidly activates endogenous protective program, inducing a state of pre-adaptation in the organism, enabling it to respond more quickly to acute high-dose radiation exposure.

After radiation exposure, patients enter a state of heightened metabolism and increased energy expenditure, usually accompanied with gastrointestinal damage.88,89 A 2026 study cautioned that dietary restriction administered post-irradiation rather than pre-irradiation persistently upregulates DNA damage response signals in hematopoietic cells and delays DNA repair. 61 Given the KD-induced cellular ageing observed in male mice cardiac and renal tissues during animal studies, 63 it is inadvisable to continue with fasting or KD at this stage. Instead, enteral or parenteral nutritional support including easily digestible, high-protein formulations enriched with specific antioxidant nutrients and probiotics should be provided to promote tissue repair and immune recovery. This method may be integrated with MD, plant-based diets and targeted nutritional supplements.

5.2. Chronic Low-Dose Exposure

In scenarios of chronic, low-dose radiation exposure such as occupational exposure or living in areas with high natural background radiation, the primary focus is to reduce long-term risks of cancer, cardiovascular disease, and other conditions, while reducing the random effects of radiation.

Adherence to MD or a balanced diet rich in plant-based foods and dietary fiber is an ideal approach. This dietary pattern maintains lower baseline oxidative stress and inflammation levels, enhances DNA repair capacity, and shapes a healthy gut and systemic microenvironment, reducing the risk of cumulative radiation damage and its progression. During this period, it is essential to ensure adequate intake of folate, vitamins B12 and D, antioxidant vitamins, and dietary fiber to guarantee nutritional balance.

5.3. Medical Fractionated Radiotherapy

Patients undergoing radiotherapy for tumors are our primary concern. As an adjuvant approach, dietary interventions should aim to protect healthy tissues, reduce acute toxic side effects (such as mucositis, dermatitis, and enteritis), and enhance quality of life, while minimizing interference with radiotherapy efficacy.

Adhere to MD during and after radiotherapy ensuring adequate protein and micronutrient intake to support tissue repair and immune function. For head and neck cancer radiotherapy patients, evidence-based nutritional measures such as oral glutamine supplements may be added to the basic diet. For radiotherapy of brain tumors, adjuvant KD may be considered, but only under strict clinical supervision. Patients undergoing pelvic radiotherapy (with risk of radiation enteritis) should prioritize soluble dietary fiber and probiotic intake to maintain gut health and reduce intestinal burden, though fiber types may require adjustment during acute phases.

However, it is worth noting that both chemotherapy drugs and radiation generate reactive oxygen species (ROS), which kill tumor cells through cytotoxic mechanisms; antioxidants, however, weaken this killing mechanism and thus impair the efficacy of cancer treatment. 90 Therefore, during clinical use, it is still essential to closely monitor tumor control and the efficacy of radiotherapy.

6. Conclusion and Prospects

An increasing body of scientific research indicates that ionizing radiation may affect anatomical structures, metabolism, and genes, whether through long-term or short-term exposure. From a fundamental mechanism perspective, radiation exposure can directly or indirectly cause detrimental effects on biomolecules such as DNA, RNA, proteins, and membrane lipids, leading to cellular dysfunction and death. 91 Furthermore, radiation-induced chronic inflammation and oxidative stress will further exacerbate tissue damage and functional impairment. 92 Radiation exposure further induces the production of ROS and cytokines, complicating the pathological mechanisms. Particularly in highly radiation-sensitive tissues such as the central nervous system, hematopoietic system, and gastrointestinal tract, radiation can induce oxidative stress, inflammation, and apoptosis. These mechanisms may act synergistically, leading to irreversible pathological alterations and disruption of normal function.93,94

Dietary patterns show promising applications in the field of radiation damage protection as readily accessible and adjustable intervention strategies. This paper primarily elucidates the physiological basis of dietary patterns with potential for radiation protection, analyzing the applicable scenarios for various dietary approaches based on existing experimental evidence. MD, characterized by its intake of diverse plant-derived natural chemicals and unique fat sources, demonstrates potent anti-inflammatory and antioxidant effects. Moreover, its relatively balanced nutritional profile may foster superior treatment adherence. CR and fasting-induced ketosis enhance stress resilience, enabling swifter and more efficient responses to radiation injury. However, post-exposure CR or fasting may exacerbate radiation-induced oxidative stress. Nutrient-induced ketosis offers neuroprotection, though its high fat content may impact cardiovascular and renal health, with long-term safety yet to be confirmed. Plant-based diets exert protective effects through high intake of natural phytochemicals while reducing consumption of pro-inflammatory compounds generated during meat cooking. Plant-based and high-fiber diets alter gut microbiota composition and abundance, potentially modulating radiation resistance via systemic pathways such as the gut-brain and gut-liver axes. These underlying mechanisms encompass the modulation of a variety of molecular pathways, including Nrf2/ARE, NF-κB, SIRT1, PI3K/AKT/mTOR, PPP, and apoptosis-related pathways. Moreover, dietary interventions reshape the gut microbiota structure, thereby improving metabolism. Therefore, tailored dietary interventions are required for different radiation exposure scenarios. For acute high-dose exposure scenarios, fasting pre-treatment and targeted post-exposure nutritional support demonstrate favorable protective effects. Chronic low-dose exposure, conversely, can be managed through Mediterranean-style diets and plant-rich, high-fiber dietary patterns, which offer sustained and effective long-term risk reduction and mitigation of radiation's stochastic effects.

Despite the substantial theoretical potential and preliminary evidence for dietary interventions in radiation protection, translating basic research into clinical practice and public health applications presents significant challenges. Most evidence of protective effects originates from rodent models, which exhibit pathophysiological differences compared to clinically common scenarios such as fractionated radiotherapy, local radiotherapy, or chronic low-dose exposure. Furthermore, existing clinical studies are predominantly small-sample, single-center, observational, or Phase I/II feasibility investigations, resulting in limited clinical trial evidence. High-quality randomized controlled trials regarding dietary interventions for radiation protection require further development. Furthermore, due to individual heterogeneity originating from genetic background, baseline metabolic status, gut microbiota composition, age, gender, and psychosocial factors, the optimal timing, duration, and specific implementation of dietary interventions require precision-tailored adjustments. It is important to note that routine supplementation with high-dose antioxidants during radiotherapy carries a potential risk of reducing treatment efficacy that may outweigh the benefits of alleviating side effects; therefore, cancer patients are advised not to take high-dose single or combination antioxidant supplements on their own initiative during radiotherapy.

The limitations of this study include the fact that the dietary patterns covered were not comprehensive enough, with no discussion of diets such as the DASH diet or low-protein/amino acid-restricted diets. Furthermore, there was a lack of systematic comparison and meta-analysis among the various diets, and the analysis of clinical application scenarios lacked specific recommendations. However, due to individual heterogeneity, radiation protection strategies for patients require precise customization and rigorous clinical monitoring. Future radiation protection nutrition will evolve towards precision medicine, establishing biomarker systems capable of predicting protective efficacy and guiding personalized protocols to adjust and optimize individual intervention plans. This will have profound and positive influences on cancer treatment, occupational health, space medicine, and public health.
