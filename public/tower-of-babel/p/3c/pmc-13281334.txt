Integrated holistic chronic wound care package for skin-NTDs and other conditions for the primary healthcare setting in Ethiopia
Authors: Tigest Ajeme, Oumer Ali, Agumasie Semahegn, Asrat Mengiste, Eiman Siddig Ahmed, Mersha Kinfe, Selamawit G Kebede, Stephen Bremner, Vasso Anagnostopoulou, Malcolm Brewster, Gail Davey, Abebaw Fekadu, Maya Semrau
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundGlobally, chronic wounds resulting from diverse etiologies impose a significant physical, psychosocial, and economic burden and have remained a highly neglected public health challenge. Yet, people in rural Ethiopia have limited access to quality and comprehensive wound care. There is a marked paucity for an integrated, holistic care model in the primary healthcare settings. Hence, this study aimed to develop a context-tailored, integrated, holistic, chronic wound care package in Ethiopia.MethodsA multi-method approach was applied, including a scoping review, formative assessments, Theory of Change workshops and a qualitative validation study. Purposive sampling was employed to recruit participants. Collected data were transcribed, coded, and thematically analysed to generate insights for refining the intervention package.ResultsA total of 49 stakeholders participated in the Theory of Change (ToC) workshops, while 36 participants were included in the qualitative study. The intervention package is structured around six core thematic components: awareness-raising and stigma reduction, capacity-building, active case detection and follow-up, program and supply chain management, Institutional and community-based rehabilitation, monitoring, and evaluation. Implementation and scale-up of these components are designed to cascade across three levels of the existing primary healthcare system, specifically the health organization, the health facility level, and the community level.ConclusionThis study introduced a context-driven, integrated, and holistic wound care package aimed at managing chronic wounds alongside their associated mental health and psychosocial challenges. The package encompasses interventions that address the physical, psychological, and social impacts of chronic wounds in individuals affected by neglected tropical diseases of the skin and related conditions. It employs multilevel implementation strategies targeting individuals, communities, and the health system to reduce morbidity, disability, and the economic and psychosocial burdens linked to chronic wounds. Background

Globally, chronic wounds pose a substantial burden, representing a significant public health challenge and a major contributor to health inequities. They disproportionately affect individuals and societies in Low- and Middle-Income Countries (LMICs) [1, 2]. The global prevalence of chronic wounds from multiple etiologies is estimated at 1.67 per 1,000 population, with the majority presenting as chronic leg ulcers [3].

Chronic wounds arise from multiple causes, including diabetes, trauma, burns, and infections [4], and skin-related Neglected Tropical Diseases (NTDs) [5]. They also encompass pressure ulcers, vascular ulcers, and complicated surgical ulcers [4, 6, 7]. While chronic wounds are global issue, their burden is particularly pronounced in Africa and Ethiopia, largely due to limited access to comprehensive healthcare services [5, 8, 9]. Evidence indicates that the prevalence of pressure ulcers in general hospital settings is estimated at 11.6% [10]. Whereas the prevalence of diabetic foot ulcers amongst diabetic patients was 12.8% [11], though rates vary widely, reflecting regional disparities [12, 13].

In Ethiopia, chronic wounds resulting from skin-related neglected tropical diseases (skin-NTDs) remain a significant public health challenge. The country bears the greatest burden of podoconiosis and cutaneous leishmaniasis in sub-Saharan Africa [14]. Skin-NTDs account for 17.2% of the overall burden of skin diseases [15]. These statistics underscore stark global inequities in wound care. In high-income regions, chronic wounds are typically managed in specialized clinics using advanced technologies. By contrast, individuals in LMICs, particularly in Africa, often face prolonged physical disability and psychosocial distress [9, 16], and reduced productivity [17], due to limited resources and delayed access to care.

A multidisciplinary, community-centred, evidence-based approach improves early detection, referral, and outcomes of chronic wounds [18, 19], as well as the quality of life for patients [20, 21]. Integrating such programmes into existing primary healthcare units, particularly for NTDs, remains a global health priority. Integrated services can advance disease control in key areas, including disease mapping, diagnosis, clinical management, and prevention.

In Ethiopia, individuals with chronic wounds receive care in primary healthcare settings, regardless of the wound’s cause or duration [22]. However, several critical challenges persist. Delays in diagnosis, absence of standardised protocols, limited provider knowledge and skills, and an inadequately trained workforce [22–24], and poor infrastructure [19]. Furthermore, the mental health and psychosocial dimensions of wound care are often neglected, even though they can perpetuate a cycle that hinders healing [25]. As the burden of chronic wounds grows and remains unknown, their impact on patients, caregivers, and the health system underscores the urgent need for holistic and integrated wound care services in Ethiopia.

The main objective of this study was to develop and implement a comprehensive care package for individuals suffering from chronic wounds caused by skin-NTDs and other conditions. This care package was tailored for integration within the primary healthcare system in Ethiopia. The central research question guiding the study was: What are the essential components of optimal physical, mental and psychosocial care for people with chronic wounds? The study also aimed to standardise wound management practices and facilitate the integration of chronic wound care services into government-operated healthcare systems. This study was conducted under the ‘Excellence in Disability Prevention Integrated across NTDs’ (EnDPoINT) Phase 2 initiative.

Methods

Study setting

The study was conducted from October 2023 to December 2024 in the Sodo District, situated approximately 100 km south of the capital city within the Central Ethiopia Regional State. Sodo District encompasses 43 kebeles (sub-districts), with agriculture and trade serving as the primary economic activities. The district was specifically chosen as the study site due to the burden of the condition and the co-endemic presence of skin-NTDs, including podoconiosis, Lymphatic Filariasis (LF), and cutaneous leishmaniasis. Additionally, Sodo District reflects the climatic diversity characteristic of Ethiopia. The development of the integrated, holistic chronic wound care package constituted the initial phase of the Excellence in Disability Prevention Integrated across NTDs (EnDPoINT) Phase II project.

Study design

This study applied an embedded implementation research (EIR) principle and employed a multi-method approach to develop an integrated, holistic chronic wound care package. Embedded Implementation Research (EIR) is an approach used to improve programs by enhancing the generation and use of evidence [26]. The principle is characterised by four main features: central involvement of policy decision-makers, collaborative research partnerships, positioning research within program processes, and research focused on implementation [26].

A scoping review, formative assessments, and observation of the study setting were conducted to establish the foundation for care package development. Nine key informant interviews and three focus group discussions were carried out using purposive sampling. The objective of the qualitative study was to assess the feasibility, acceptability, and appropriateness of the integrated holistic wound care package in a primary healthcare setting. Additionally, three Theory of Change (ToC) workshops and a consultative meeting were employed to contextualise and validate the intervention packages. Scoping review

A scoping review was conducted to generate evidence related to the management of chronic wounds. This study sought to identify the physical and psychosocial interventions and implementation practices available in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), including Ethiopia, and to draw lessons learnt from these experiences. Specifically, it aimed to examine studies on the integrated implementation of primary health care and community-based physical and psychosocial interventions for individuals living with chronic wounds in LMICs. Challenges and lessons learned captured from the scoping review of wound care practices and strategic plans in LMICs were used as input for the intervention.

The review included published research articles and unpublished documents, program documents, and relevant WHO and Ethiopian national guideline documents. Search terms used include chronic wounds, non-healing wounds, hard-to-heal wounds, mental health, psychosocial support, and skin-NTDs.

Six major databases, including CINAHL, Embase, MEDLINE, PsycINFO, Scopus, and Web of Science, were systematically searched from February 2024 to March 2024 for relevant studies. Studies with a range of designs were considered, both with and without comparators, if they related to existing physical and psychosocial primary healthcare and community-based wound care interventions in LMICs. Eligible studies included those addressing complex, chronic, non-healing, or hard-to-heal wounds among individuals with skin-NTDs and any other relevant conditions, excluding those related to acute injuries or burns. Only studies involving adults aged 18 years or older were considered.

We also included studies that highlighted existing chronic wound management practices, explored the implementation and/or evaluation of interventions for the management of chronic wounds. This included pilot studies in LMICS and research identifying challenges or gaps in wound care. In addition, interventions delivered in clinical or non-clinical settings, including outpatient care, by healthcare professionals and/or community health workers were also eligible. There were no specific racial or gender-based considerations. We excluded studies published before the year 2000, those not in English, and those not based on primary data, to remain focused on recent and relevant research. Additional exclusions applied to several types of studies. We excluded studies that reported solely on chronic wound outcomes and those limited to inpatient populations. Research conducted in high-income countries (HICs) and basic science studies were also not considered. Disease mapping studies without relevance to chronic wound management were excluded, as were publications focusing exclusively on specific dressings or antibiotics rather than implementation strategies.

Finally, studies for which the full text was unavailable were omitted from this review. Grey literature was reviewed, and the screening and selection of retrieved relevant documents were conducted using two phases and guided by the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-analyses Extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) flow diagram [27]. The completed PRISMA-ScR checklist is provided as a supplementary file. Phase one involved screening titles and abstracts, and the second phase comprised full-text screening. The search strategy was entered with filters into each database. Identified citations and potentially relevant sources were collated and uploaded into EndNote [28]. After which duplicates were removed, followed by screening of titles/abstracts and then full-text review, according to the inclusion and exclusion criteria. Data charted from the retrieved documents were organised in an Excel sheet for reference. From 2,824 records screened, 10 peer-reviewed articles, 15 citation-identified studies, and 33 grey literature documents were included, covering skin NTDs and chronic wounds. Key findings were synthesised narratively and presented thematically to guide intervention design.

Formative assessment

The formative assessment was conducted from November 2023 to December 2023, using a cross-sectional study design to understand chronic wound burden in the local context, existing wound care practice, and challenges in the management of chronic wounds. Pilot site observations, including review of the wound care unit and registry book, were carried out to assess infrastructure, patient needs, and training gaps. The assessment focused on three main domains: health services and health coordination centres, planning for chronic wound care, and the availability of health records and reporting systems.

Theory of change workshops

The development of an integrated, holistic wound care package was guided by the Medical Research Council’s (MRC’s) framework for complex interventions [29]. We used a ‘Theory of Change’ approach within this [30]. The MRC framework has been widely used for the development and evaluation of complex interventions (Fig. 2) through four phases: (i) development, (ii) feasibility/piloting, (iii) evaluation, and (iv) implementation [29]. A protocol demonstrates how the MRC framework guided the design of an integrated, holistic wound care package within the EnDPoINT-II project has been published elsewhere [31].

Framework for developing and evaluating complex interventions [32]

During the intervention development phase of the MRC framework, evidence from the scoping review, formative assessment, and ToC workshops informed the initial intervention content, structure, and theoretical foundation. In the feasibility phase, the care package’s acceptability, practicality, and appropriateness were evaluated within real-world health system settings. Findings highlighted both strengths and areas requiring adaptation. Refinements included adjusting intervention delivery and referral pathways, simplifying complex components, prioritising capacity-building measures, and integrating mental health support. The revised, validated, and operational ToC map incorporated realistic assumptions, stronger linkages between community, facility, and system-level actions, and updated causal pathways.

Three ToC workshops, involving 49 participants in total, were conducted between October 2023 and March 2024 to gather stakeholder insights for the development of an integrated, holistic wound care package. The process visualizes intervention outcomes and illustrates causal pathways and intervention functions [30, 33], and defines implementation approaches and components applicable at multiple levels.

The ToC workshops involved one internal workshop with the research team and two external workshops with the research team and key stakeholders over three months. To set plans for regular documentation, community advisory board meetings that included patients, representatives from religious institutions, law enforcement offices, health officials, healthcare providers, and zonal administration were conducted. Participants were selected purposively based on their involvement in planning and implementing health policies, their expertise in chronic wounds, skin-NTDs, and mental health programs, and their involvement in the community.

Each of the ToC workshops was started by participant introduction, a welcoming note, an overview of the project, and an introduction to the workshop. Then, participants were engaged in the process of building the framework. Defining the intended impact was the first step in building the framework. This was followed by determining the intermediate and short-term outcomes necessary and sufficient to achieve the intended impact in the backward approach. Indicators for each outcome were identified, the rationale of how one outcome leads to the next was articulated, and required interventions to achieve the outcomes were identified and agreed upon amongst stakeholders.

Qualitative research and validation workshop

To test the feasibility, acceptability, and appropriateness of the integrated holistic wound care package, a qualitative study was conducted; its implementation was validated through participatory approaches with relevant stakeholders. Data collection for this study was carried out over two months, from February to March 2024. The process began at the national level with the Ministry of Health and was subsequently extended to the pilot zone and district levels. In total, 36 participants were included in the study.

Three focus group discussions (FGDs) were conducted. Each FGD consisted of 8–10 participants and included affected persons, community representatives, health professionals, and zonal decision makers. In addition, nine key informant interviews (KIIs) were carried out with officials from the Zonal Health Department, Woreda Health Office, and Primary Health Care Unit. A validation workshop was also held with stakeholders such as project members, decision makers, health officials, and health workers. The workshop aimed to gather feedback on the draft care package and accompanying training materials from local, national, and international perspectives. This process helped refine the package and ensure its contextual relevance. Participants were purposively selected, and the workshop was conducted at the study site. All qualitative data were audio recorded. Transcriptions were completed in Amharic and translated into English before coding. Data were also managed using qualitative data analysis software (INVIVO). Data management and analysis

Data collection for the situational analysis was facilitated by a structured instrument comprising three sections and twenty-three subsections, adopted from a previously used EnDPoINT phase one project [16]. Quantitative data from the situational assessment were analysed descriptively. For the ToC workshops, data rigor was maintained through dual-investigator minute-taking, with final records cross-verified against audio recordings for accuracy and completeness.

All qualitative data were audio-recorded and underwent a systematic process of transcription in Amharic, followed by forward translation into English before coding. To ensure reliability, professional bilingual translators with subject-matter expertise were employed. To preserve accuracy and contextual richness, the translated data was cross-checked against audio recordings and contextual observations. Memos explaining cultural references or terms that has no direct English equivalents were included. Furthermore, to maintain authenticity and clarity, especially for culturally loaded expressions such as proverbs, the original Amharic term was retained in brackets within the English text, followed by an explanatory note.

Three researchers independently participated in the coding process to enhance analytical rigour and reflexivity. Formal intercoder agreement statistics (e.g., kappa coefficients) were not calculated. This decision aligns with reflexive qualitative methodologies, where the aim is not to achieve quantifiable agreement but to deepen interpretive understanding [34]. Qualitative data analysis followed a hybrid inductive–deductive thematic approach. A preliminary codebook was developed after data familiarization. Inductive codes emerged directly from the interview transcripts, while deductive codes were derived from the interview topic guide and pre-specified areas of the study. The codes were then compared, discussed, and refined through iterative proofreading and consensus meetings. This process ensured that coding remained both systematic and flexible, allowing for the integration of emergent insights while maintaining alignment with the study objectives. The thematic analysis framework was utilized to identify key conceptual patterns.

Gaps identified in the provision of wound care services

While foundational services for common mental health conditions are established, the situational assessment revealed limitations in medical and logistical supplies. Financial barriers were prominent; although 50% of the patient population is enrolled in Community-Based Health Insurance (CBHI), chronic wound management remains an excluded service. Consequently, patients pay out-of-pocket (OOP) expenditures averaging 0.5 USD (25 Birr) per episode of care. The budget for wound care services is primarily sourced from the health facility’s internal resources, with limited external support from governmental and non-governmental organizations.

Existing Skin-NTD and non-communicable disease (NCD) plans demonstrate integration but lack essential frameworks for health equity. Specifically, these policies fail to address gender, residential (rural vs. urban), or socioeconomic disparities, providing no clear strategy for vulnerable populations. Furthermore, the health system lacks holistic management guidelines and integrated mental health and psychosocial support. Clinical data integrity was found to be poor. A three-year retrospective review of the wound care service registration book documented 193 wound cases, but revealed substantial data gaps regarding etiology, duration, and clinical outcomes. Operational challenges were further compounded by human resource and supply chain deficits, mainly the absence of standardised operating procedures (SOPs), wound care guidelines, specialised training for health workers, and a consistent supply of medical consumables.

Development of an integrated holistic care package

We developed an integrated, holistic wound care package for people with chronic wounds, targeting the primary healthcare setting in rural parts of Ethiopia. The care package comprises awareness-raising, stigma reduction, capacity-building, program and supply management, active case detection and follow-up, clinical mentoring, mental health and psychosocial support, community engagement and mobilization, morbidity management and disability prevention, establishment of referral links, institution-based rehabilitation, supportive supervision, documentation and recording, and monitoring and evaluation. The ToC map, which illustrates the intervention components along with expected outcomes and impact identified by stakeholders for the development of an integrated, holistic wound care package across different healthcare levels (Fig. 3).

Theory of Change map

This figure illustrates the sequential logic from inputs through activities, outputs, outcomes, and impact, highlighting the pathways by which the intervention package is expected to improve chronic wound care. The diagram uses standardized symbols and color codes: rectangles represent programmatic elements (Inputs, Activities, Outputs, Outcomes, Impact); alphabets denote underlying assumptions; Arabic numbers indicate interventions; and Roman numbers mark indicators. Solid arrows show direct causal links, dashed arrows indicate indirect influence or feedback, and double-headed arrows represent a bidirectional relationship.

Participants were asked to list assumptions, interventions, and indicators, which were linked using numbers and letters to connect preconditions. Interventions and indicators for measuring implementation success were jointly defined with workshop participants. To achieve the long-term outcome, improved quality of life, enhanced physical, psychosocial, and economic functioning of affected persons and caregivers, and greater community awareness of chronic wound management, these elements were identified as prerequisites. Ensuring the integration of chronic wound management within supportive supervision, maintaining consistent delivery of high-quality care, and building sustainable capacity were further emphasized as essential conditions for success. The current situation revealed that health workers lacked the skills to provide holistic care for people with chronic wounds and related mental health issues. Strengthening their capacity to diagnose, manage, and deliver comprehensive wound care was therefore recommended as crucial. Community sensitization and broad stakeholder engagement were also highlighted as key requirements.

Care package components

Component 1: awareness-raising and stigma-reduction

The awareness-raising and stigma-reduction care package is developed in response to the lack of awareness and persistent stigma, the widespread misconceptions about the causes of chronic wounds, healthcare-seeking, access to care, protection of human rights, and inclusion of patients in communal activities. It has been designed to include robust awareness-raising, community mobilization, and stigma-reduction strategies. These interventions are intended to operate at multiple levels, including health facilities, the broader health system, and within communities. Planned delivery strategies include facility-based health education sessions, community awareness-raising workshops, and widespread information dissemination through leaflets, billboards, and posters. Health extension workers and the health development army conduct community-based awareness-raising events.

Component 2: capacity-building

Provision of standardized and quality wound care services at the primary healthcare level is essential to reduce morbidity and prevent chronic wound-related complications. This can, in part, be achieved by building the capacity of health professionals with the necessary knowledge and skills. Accordingly, the care package incorporates capacity-building for trainers and end-users on the detection and management of chronic wounds and comorbid mental health disorders as a central component. The package also integrates on-site and remote clinical mentorship, supply chain management, supportive supervision, documentation, and recording. To ensure sustainability, Training of Trainers (ToT) is provided within the district. This approach enables continuous training for new staff members and fosters an institutional culture of knowledge transfer.

Component 3: program and supply chain management

Ensuring the regular and appropriate availability of medical and logistical supplies is crucial for the effective implementation of interventions. The care package was developed to help healthcare workers understand the foundational supply chain concepts of the Ethiopian Pharmaceutical Supply Agency (EPSA). It equips them to apply this knowledge to acquire stock, prevent stock-outs, and manage supplies efficiently. During the situational analysis, both required and available supplies were identified. Additional recommendations were provided to minimize out-of-pocket wound care expenses for patients. To promote sustainability, the care package also aims to improve access to wound care services and facilitate medical expense coverage through the Community-Based Health Insurance (CBHI) scheme.

Component 4: active case detection and follow-up

The care package included an intervention to equip health extension workers, who serve as frontline providers in the primary healthcare system, particularly in rural settings. These workers are trained to detect and refer cases of chronic wounds and related mental health conditions within the community. Their involvement in case detection and follow-up strengthens the practical implementation of their assigned roles and responsibilities, while also contributing to sustainability. The Health Development Army (HDA), a network of community volunteers, supports health extension workers by engaging in community mobilization and awareness activities. Healthcare workers in health facilities further conduct chronic wound assessment, diagnosis, treatment, patient counselling, and co-morbid mental healthcare in the primary healthcare unit. The mental health and psychosocial support are achieved using the Mental Health Gap Action Programme (mhGAP) guide and by leveraging existing self-help and social support groups. Patients will be reminded of their appointment dates and encouraged to adhere to their treatment plans. Home visits will be conducted for those unable to attend health facilities. These measures will support the early detection of complications and help prevent further disability.

Component 5: institutional and community-based rehabilitation

The physical disability caused by chronic wounds, along with their mental health and psychosocial impact, is often overlooked. As a result, individuals with chronic wounds are frequently excluded from community gatherings, income-generating activities, and employment opportunities. To address these challenges, the institutional and community-based rehabilitation care package was designed to support patients living with debilitating physical disability, mental health concerns, and psychosocial difficulties. The package promotes the establishment of self-help practices and caregiver support groups within both the community and health facilities. These groups aim to strengthen patient and caregiver involvement in the care process, enhance social integration, and ensure equal access to resources and opportunities. Healthcare providers at facilities, together with health extension workers and members of the Health Development Army (HDA), facilitate the formation and operation of self-help and support groups. This component of the care package seeks to prevent disability among chronic wound patients and improve access to healthcare services for people already living with disabilities.

Component 6: monitoring and evaluation

The care package aimed to strengthen the application of existing health system mechanisms for Monitoring and Evaluation (M&E). It will support primary healthcare workers in incorporating integrated holistic care into the supervision checklist, ensuring that chronic wound care service availability and quality are routinely monitored. Key components include the development of strategic implementation plans, the setting and monitoring of performance indicators, continuous supportive supervision, and human resource coordination at all levels. The provision of additional training and the inclusion of relevant indicators will further benefit the health system by addressing gaps in both technical and logistical areas.

Feasibility, acceptability, and appropriateness of integrating a holistic chronic wound care package

In broad terms, participants considered the integration of a holistic chronic wound care package into primary care to be both feasible and acceptable. However, several preconditions were identified as essential for the effective provision of integrated care. These included the availability of medical and logistical supplies, capacity-building training, continuous supportive supervision, and the active engagement of health extension workers.

By integrating services, we can raise awareness, provide necessary training for healthcare professionals, and ensure proper resource allocation. Additionally, local coordination can improve accessibility and prevent further complications. [KII-participant-8,48-years-old-microbiologist]

This approach will empower individuals to care for themselves at the family level and know when to visit health centers, thereby improving community awareness. If we can provide this service, we will be able to treat individuals at the early stages of their condition, preventing it from reaching a chronic level. [FGD participant-7, 40-year-old male Health official]

Participants generally viewed integrated, holistic care as a feasible approach to chronic wound management. Beyond feasibility, they emphasized its potential to reduce inequities by increasing the visibility of underserved populations, while simultaneously improving care quality and reducing morbidity. Integration of this model into existing healthcare structures was considered essential, both for effective implementation and for ensuring long-term sustainability.

We are not creating new services or facilities; instead, we are strengthening our existing health centers and posts by incorporating this program into the system. This will enhance surveillance and improve the system’s ability to treat such cases [FGD-participant, 32-year-old male, emergency surgeon].

On the other hand, full commitment to implementing the care package was indicated, beginning with the allocation of separate wound care units within health facilities. The high burden of chronic wounds, coupled with the lack of existing community-based wound care services, was identified as a potential driver for the successful implementation of community engagement activities. However, misconceptions among health workers regarding increased workload were noted as potential barriers that could affect full acceptability within health facilities.

Most primary healthcare units are established to serve the community, so I don’t believe they will refuse to provide this service [FGD participant-1, 34- year- old-female, medical doctor].

Regarding health professionals, I don’t think they will immediately accept this new responsibility without reservations. We shouldn’t assume they will accept it straightforwardly. One reason for potential resistance is that they may perceive this as additional work beyond their routine duties, especially since chronic cases require long-term care, which can lead to burnout. [FGD participant-1, 32- year- old-male, emergency surgeon]

Holistic wound care should be patient-centered, ensuring the active involvement of patients in the care process. It must also incorporate palliative and rehabilitative care, alongside psychological and social support. In addition, services should meet specific standards that are applied consistently across health facilities. A distinct registration and reporting format is required to strengthen monitoring and accountability. Despite potential barriers, such as budget constraints, limited resource allocation, and political instability, sustainability is achievable through continuous advocacy, successful implementation, and effective stakeholder engagement. However, the need for conducting resource assessments and aligning the integration of holistic care within existing services was strongly emphasised.

Integrated chronic wound care is an approach that requires the involvement of multiple stakeholders, not just one organization…. For integrated wound care to gain attention, it requires proper advocacy, not just advertising…This includes meticulous planning and evaluation [FGD-participant 7, 40-years-old, male, health officer].

Discussion

The integrated holistic care package developed in the EnDPoINT-II project marks a transition from fragmented clinical encounters to comprehensive, coordinated management of chronic wounds. This package offers valuable support to healthcare providers, decision-makers, community health workers, and program managers engaged in the design and implementation of health programs. In this context, key findings are discussed: the significance of the intervention, gaps in current services, evidence-based solutions, strengths and limitations, and potential implications.

Chronic wounds place a complex burden on individuals and society, encompassing physical, psychological, social, and economic challenges [17, 35, 36]. The interaction of these factors contributes to delayed wound healing and the emergence of comorbid mental health conditions [2, 37]. Despite strong evidence highlighting that global guidelines recommend routine mental health screening, it is frequently neglected in clinical practice [38, 39].The holistic care package addresses these barriers by integrating proactive case identification, awareness-raising, and stigma reduction along with institutional and community-based rehabilitation. By using the WHO Mental Health Gap Action Programme (mhGAP) guide with existing self-help and social support networks, it is possible to reduce morbidity and improve quality of life. Core competencies such as effective communication, counselling, and the promotion of self-help and social support have proven benefits for individuals with hard-to-heal wounds [40].

Furthermore, inadequate clinical services, compounded by the limited adoption of evidence-based practices, highlight persistent gaps in wound care knowledge and practice among HWs [18, 22, 24]. Additionally, comprehensive wound care data remain scarce across many settings, a challenge particularly pronounced in LMICs [19] as well as in Ethiopia. Such poor documentation is directly associated with substandard wound care and adverse health outcomes [19]. In contrast, systematic documentation has been shown to improve the quality of care [24]. Regular documentation, monitoring, and evaluation, included in the care package, allows accurate disease burden estimation and evidence-based planning.

Moreover, the holistic care package helps overcome service gaps and improve coverage and quality by integrating multiple intervention components designed to identify chronic wounds and co-morbid mental health and psychosocial issues. Harnessing existing community conversation (CC) programs and health extension workers (HEWs) presents a viable path to sustainability by leveraging locally established health delivery systems. Evidence also demonstrates that community conversations reduce stigma and improve access to specialized care, such as in lymphoedema programs [41]. What is more, HEWs not only play a critical role in case detection but also empower community members to manage wounds at home [42].

Capacity building is also emphasized in case detection, program and supply management, and supportive supervision, focusing on integrating chronic wound-related data into the national Health Management Information System (HMIS). A thorough understanding of the Ethiopian Pharmaceutical Supply Service (EPSS) logistics framework, together with enhanced supply chain literacy and effective program implementation [42], is essential to ensure continuous stock availability across all levels. What is more, intervention components, such as supportive supervision, clinical mentorship, and tele-health, will counter outdated wound care practices. This components, particularly, telehealth is found to be as safe and effective as traditional protocols for wound management [43]. This could also enable real-time expert consultation, support quality assurance, and expand access to scarce expertise in resource-constrained rural areas.

The holistic wound care package is aligned with Ethiopia’s Primary HealthCare (PHC) model under the Integrated People-Centered Health Services (IPCHS) program [44]. Sustainability is embedded through the active involvement of decision makers, stakeholders, and community members. This alignment builds on Ethiopia’s ongoing efforts to integrate PHC into policies and programs, improving community health outcomes and expanding service coverage [45]. The EnDPoINT-II holistic wound care package offers a standardized pathway to ensure that all individuals with chronic wounds receive comprehensive care within the state health system. While integration can improve accessibility and quality, it also requires significant organizational and cultural change.

Implications

The findings of this study underscore the substantial burden of chronic wounds in LMICs, particularly Ethiopia, and reveal that this issue remains insufficiently addressed. Qualitative results suggest that integrating a holistic care package is both feasible and acceptable, with participants considering it appropriate for the local health system. The active involvement of district health officials, health workers, and community representatives demonstrated a strong commitment to successful implementation. However, persistent substandard clinical practices and the lack of standardized, holistic wound care approaches continue to pose significant barriers to effective service delivery. These findings highlight the urgent need for policymakers and health stakeholders to move beyond fragmented wound management. Institutionalizing integrated, multi-dimensional care models that address both the physiological and psychosocial needs of affected individuals is crucial for achieving sustainable health outcomes.

Strengths and limitations

This study employed a comprehensive methodological triangulation to review existing evidence. By actively engaging a diverse range of stakeholders, the care package was developed to be both evidence-based and contextually grounded within the Ethiopian primary healthcare system. Its components address not only the physical impact of chronic wounds but also the associated mental health and psychosocial challenges. The use of a multi-phase mixed-methods design, combined with expert validation, further enhanced the content validity of the care package.

Although the care package is comprehensive, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the use of convenience and purposive sampling may limit representativeness and introduce selection bias. Second, qualitative interviews are subject to social desirability bias, with participants potentially providing favourable rather than candid responses. Third, the transferability of findings to other regions or countries may be constrained by differences in health systems, resources, and sociocultural contexts. For instance, despite the substantial economic burden posed by chronic wounds, strategies for economic empowerment were not incorporated. Fourth, further evaluation is needed to determine which intervention components are most feasible, acceptable, and cost-effective.

Conclusion

This study outlines the development of an evidence-based, context-driven, and holistic care package for managing chronic wounds caused by skin NTDs and other conditions in Ethiopia. The package integrates interventions designed to address not only the physical but also the mental and psychosocial impacts of chronic wounds on affected individuals. Intended for implementation across healthcare organizations, facilities, and community settings at the primary healthcare level in the Sodo District, Ethiopia, the package’s integration into existing health programs demonstrates its feasibility, acceptability, and appropriateness in resource-limited environments. Employing multi-level implementation strategies, the package targets individuals, communities, health facilities, and various tiers of the health system. This approach has the potential to reduce morbidity, disability, and the associated economic and psychosocial burdens. The package provides a standardized, scalable, and sustainable model of care for Ethiopia and other low- and middle-income countries (LMIC) settings.
