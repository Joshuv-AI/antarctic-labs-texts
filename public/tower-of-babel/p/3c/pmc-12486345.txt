Unsupervised Clustering Reveals Intestine‐Adapted Eosinophil Subsets Shaped by Local Inflammation
Authors: Leigha D Larsen, Arman Kian, Natalie A Falta, Barbara Fox, Lisa A Spencer
Published: 2025

To the Editor,

Steady‐state resident intestinal eosinophils sustain a healthy epithelial barrier, mediate anti‐microbial immunity, and repair and resolve tissue injury. Juxtaposed to these homeostatic functions, activated eosinophils prone to degranulation contribute to tissue pathology in inflamed settings.S1 Extra‐physiologic infiltration of eosinophils is a diagnostic criterion of eosinophilic gastrointestinal disorders (EGIDs; eosinophilic esophagitis (EoE), enteritis (EoN), and colitis (EoC)) and a common characteristic of inflammatory bowel disease (IBD; Crohn's disease and ulcerative colitis). Although generally considered pathologic in EGIDs and IBD, contributions of eosinophils to the pathophysiology of either condition remain incompletely understood. Despite their post‐mitotic state, tissue‐immigrated eosinophils exhibit incompletely understood organ‐specific functional adaptationsS2,S3 shaped in part by nutrient‐, microbiome‐, and tissue‐derived factors and mediated in part via eosinophil‐intrinsic aryl hydrocarbon receptor‐ and notch 2 receptor‐dependent signaling in steady‐state [1, 2, 3]. Separate prior studies identified CD22 expressing homeostatic [1], CLEC4a4 expressing immunoregulatory [4], and PD‐L1+CD80+ expressing anti‐microbial [5], murine intestinal eosinophil subsets that likely contribute to their dichotomous homeostatic and pathologic functions; however, a unified reconciliation of these reported subsets is lacking. Therefore, despite recognition of functional heterogeneity, clinical histopathological assessments remain agnostic to eosinophil phenotypes.

Here we applied targeted multidimensional flow cytometry and unsupervised clustering to discriminate intestinal eosinophil phenotypes within and across inflammatory states (Figure 1A). Single cell suspensions were recovered from digested whole small intestines of mice under conditions of health, an allergen‐induced model of eosinophilic enteritis (EoN), or TNF‐α overexpression (TNFΔARE/+) resulting in spontaneous ileitis (Supporting Information). Eosinophils were gated as described (Figure S1A), and t‐distributed stochastic neighbor embedding (t‐SNE) plots were generated from data concatenated across all experimental conditions. Eosinophils from allergen‐challenged mice clustered closely with those from healthy mice, while a notable divergence appeared within the context of ileitis (Figure 1B,Bi). Receptor expression thresholds were defined manually for each surface receptor (Figure S1B) and expression profiles were overlayed. We previously demonstrated intestinal eosinophils upregulate CD11c along a continuum of expression over time in parallel with their villus migration [6]; we therefore utilized CD11c as a primary indicator of intestinal adaptation. A CD11c− population was observed that also resembled blood eosinophils (e.g., β7 integrin+), and we interpreted these cells to be newly recruited (pre‐adapted). Across all conditions, expression profile alignments revealed strong overlap between CD11c, programmed death ligand 1 (PD‐L1), and CD80 (Figure 1C). This population likely represents the PD‐L1+CD80+ intestine‐restricted “active” subset previously reported by Gurtner et al. in steady‐state, which was further expanded in bacterial infections and IBD [5]. Frequencies of PD‐L1+CD80+ cells among CD11c+ eosinophils were highest in CD11c intermediate to high expressing eosinophils (Figure 1D). Approximately 23% of eosinophils from the concatenated pool expressed low levels of CD11c in the absence of PD‐L1 or CD80, ostensibly representing an intermediate population.

CD11c+PDL1+CD80+ intestine‐adapted eosinophils were further partitioned by expression of the C‐type lectin CLEC4a4 (Figure 1E), with CLEC4a4+ cells enriched among intestine‐adapted eosinophils from healthy mice (90% overlay) but nearly absent within the context of ileitis (12% overlay). 18%–32% of intestinal eosinophils from healthy mice (compared to 3%–9% from TNFΔARE/+) expressed CLEC4a4 in the absence of PD‐L1/CD80. These cells exhibited lower CD11c relative to CLEC4a4+ eosinophils co‐expressing PD‐L1+CD80+ (Supplemental Figure S2A), which may suggest CLEC4a4 is induced earlier than PD‐L1/CD80. CLEC4a4 binds glycan ligands, including N‐glycans expressed on CLEC4a4 itself, and contains a cytosolic immunoreceptor tyrosine‐based inhibitory motif (ITIM), recruiting SH2 domain‐containing phosphatases that suppress activation and cellular responses (Figure S2B). In dendritic cells (DCs) CLEC4a4 homotypic interactions maintain basal ITIM phosphorylation that suppresses activation [7]. Since CLEC4a4+ (relative to CLEC4a4−) intestinal eosinophils were found to express an immunoregulatory transcriptome [2, 4], it is tempting to speculate a similar suppressive function in eosinophils that may restrain activation‐induced processes such as cytolytic degranulation. Although CD22, another ITIM‐containing lectin, did not emerge among the strongest discriminators in our clustering approach, it was expressed on a subset of CLEC4a4+PD‐L1+CD80+ eosinophils expressing high CD11c (Supplemental Figure S2C), and like CLEC4a4, it was enriched in health and diminished in ileitis (Supplemental Figure S2D). Its preferential association with CD11chigh expression places CD22 at a later stage of intestinal adaptation, a finding in line with BrdU tracing studies in Kutyavin et al. [1] Published transcriptomic datasets confirm expression of CLEC4a4 mRNA and protein by CD22+ intestinal eosinophils [1, 4] and support CD22 induction downstream of CD11c (Itgax), CLEC4a4, PD‐L1 (CD274), and CD80 (Supplemental Figure S2E). Altogether, these findings suggest CD11c+PD‐L1+CD80+ intestine‐adapted eosinophils are further characterized by the presence (enriched in health) or absence (enriched in inflammation) of CLEC4a4 (+/− CD22). Further studies are needed to determine whether these ITIM‐containing lectins provide functional restraint to intestinal eosinophils that limit tissue‐destructive activation processes, such as cytolytic degranulation.

Having thus established a minimal immunophenotyping panel that discriminates small intestinal eosinophil phenotypes (Figure 1F,G), we confirmed the panel in separate test cohorts of mice. Among small intestinal PD‐L1+CD80+ eosinophils, > 95% co‐expressed CLEC4a4 in steady‐state. Spatial patterning was evident, with a striking near absence of the CD11c+PD‐L1+CD80+ phenotype on colonic eosinophils, which also exhibited a reduced frequency of CLEC4a4 expression (Figure 1H,I). These data conflict with a prior report that demonstrated a robust population of PD‐L1+CD80+ colonic eosinophils [5], the discrepancy likely reflecting environmental, dietary, and/or microbiome‐related variables between our vivariums affecting intestinal eosinophil phenotypes. Of note, Kutyavin et al. found a similar diminution of CD22+ eosinophils within colonic, relative to small intestinal, tissues [1].

To explore the role of local tissue inflammation, we evaluated small intestinal eosinophils from WT mice following 2 weeks of intragastric food allergen challenges, and in WT and TNFΔARE/+ littermates. Intragastric allergen challenge elicited a non‐significant shift favoring higher frequencies of intestine‐adapted eosinophils lacking CLEC4a4 (Figure S3). It should be noted that a longer chronic exposure model may be needed to affect measurable shifts in eosinophil phenotypes, as Wang et al. reported a decreased frequency of total CLEC4a4+ intestinal eosinophils following 14 intragastric allergen challenges over 6 weeks [4]. TNFΔARE/+ mice exhibited ileal inflammation, including increased eosinophilic infiltration (Figure 2A,B), accompanied by enrichment for PD‐L1+CD80+ eosinophils lacking CLEC4a4 (Figure 2C,D). BrdU pulse‐labeling revealed increased infiltration of bone marrow‐derived eosinophils into inflamed ilea (Figure 2E,F). Rapid local adoption of the CD11c+PD‐L1+CD80+ adapted phenotype was observed in BrdU+ eosinophils in both WT and inflamed mice (Figure 2G). However, while PD‐+CD80+ BrdU+ ileal eosinophils were uniformly CLEC4a4+ in WT mice, approximately 30% failed to express CLEC4a4 in ileitis (Figure 2H). Likewise, conditioned media (CM) from either WT or inflamed intestinal explants upregulated PD‐L1 and CD80 on blood eosinophils in vitro, while CLEC4a4 was preferentially induced by CM from healthy tissue explants (Supplemental Figure S4). These data directly implicate the local tissue environment in regulating CLEC4a4 expression on the intestine‐adapted CD11c+PD‐L1+CD80+ eosinophil subset.

In summary, we provide surface receptor signatures of small intestinal eosinophils across disease states that both reconcile and contextualize prior studies that separately reported a continuum of CD11c expression [6], and anti‐microbial (PD‐L1+CD80+) [5], immunoregulatory (CLEC4a4+) [4], and resident‐homeostatic (CD22+) [1] eosinophil subsets by demonstrating these are the same intestine‐adapted population in steady state but diverge within the context of inflammation. Further studies are needed to fully define functional competencies of eosinophil subsets, determine potential for plasticity between CLEC4a4− (putative unrestrained) and CLEC4a4+ (putative restrained) phenotypes of CD11c+PD‐L1+CD80+ intestine‐adapted eosinophils, and explore translatability of surface receptor repertoires to human tissue. These data have important implications for EGIDs and other eosinophil‐associated intestinal diseases, including opening the door to more nuanced histopathologic assessments of tissue eosinophil subsets to guide therapy, and consideration of novel therapeutic interventions that seek to modulate eosinophil phenotypes.
