Linking Laboratory and Field Observations of Carbon Dioxide Transport at the Nevada National Security Site
Authors: Chelsea W. Neil, John P. Ortiz, Daniel L. Eldridge, Hayden Miller, Philip H. Stauffer, Garrett G. Euler
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Subsurface gas transport is critical for containment performance following underground nuclear detonations or high-explosive tests. Such gases can provide fingerprinting needed to identify whether detected seismic signals have a nuclear or chemical source, provided that their subsurface transport can be accurately interpreted. In this study, we examine carbon dioxide (CO2) diffusion through dry core samples taken from representative lithologies at the Nevada National Security Site (NNSS) and compare laboratory-measured transport with field observations after a subsurface high-explosive experiment. Controlled laboratory breakthrough tests quantified CO2 migration across lithologies with varying zeolite content. Results show pronounced lithologic dependence, with zeolite-rich samples exhibiting marked retardation of CO2 attributed to adsorption within the zeolite frameworks. Field measurements of post-detonation gas composition and concentration gradients similarly revealed delayed CO2 migration within more zeolitized horizons, which were distinct from dissolution into pore water. This corresponding behavior across laboratory and field scales indicates that zeolite content exerts a dominant control on CO2 subsurface mobility and retention at the NNSS. These findings highlight the need to account for mineralogically controlled sorptive processes when modeling gas transport in zeolitized formations and underscore the value of linking laboratory diffusion data with field-scale observations to improve predictions of post-detonation gas behavior at the NNSS and similar sites.

Introduction

Subsurface containment has the goal of preventing the release of radioactive gases and particulates into the atmosphere, which has been a central concern since underground nuclear testing began in 1951. Signing of the Limited Test Ban Treaty in 1963, which prohibited all testing except those conducted underground, increased the frequency of underground testing, with more than 800 underground nuclear tests at the Nevada National Security Site (NNSS) until 1992. These tests provided critical data for weapons science and national defense programs, while also generating complex challenges in understanding and verifying containment, including monitoring post-detonation gas migration over long timeframes (i.e., late-time seepage). − The NNSS contains heterogeneous lithologies and has hosted underground tests conducted in a variety of rock types including granite, alluvium, and volcanic tuffs with varying degrees of welding and zeolite content. , Historic testing data thus represents a natural laboratory for studying how gases evolve and move after underground detonations. In this context, numerous studies have examined the movement of radioactive detonation products including noble gases (e.g., xenon) and other volatile radionuclide species to assess leakage potential, particularly as it relates to environmental and human health − and treaty verification. , However, these radionuclides pose two challenges to verifying containment. First, many of these radionuclides have short half-lives, meaning they could decay into immobile species before reaching the atmosphere. Second, they are often produced at low abundances, making them difficult to detect. There is thus growing interest in exploring the migration of other produced gases, especially those generated in greater quantities such as carbon dioxide (CO2).

CO2 can have multiple sources during an underground nuclear test including high explosive combustion, thermal decomposition of carbonate-bearing lithologies, oxidation of organic material, cavity gas-water-rock reactions, and secondary postshot geochemical reactions. , The relative contribution from each source depends strongly on host rock composition, explosive design, temperature evolution, and redox conditions, which can influence both the total quantity of CO2 generated and its transport behavior. While short-lived radionuclides and other reactive species decay or sorb rapidly, CO2 can persist at high concentrations over time scales suitable for assessing long-term containment behavior. For example, Project Gasbuggy, which aimed to leverage a nuclear explosion for methane production from tight rock, found that cavity gas contained 36% CO2, 17% H2, and 4% CO after six months, highly modified from the original 99% natural gas. Understanding the movement of CO2 through host rocks thus provides valuable insight into post-detonation gas evolution and could offer a new strategy for containment monitoring.

Gas transport in the subsurface is primarily governed by a combination of diffusive and advective processes, modulated by the rock’s pore structure, mineralogy, and saturation. − In the case of tighter rock formations, advective transport can occur through preferential pathways including fractures, faults, and fissures, particularly immediately following pressure transients generated by a detonation event. Within a porous medium, on the other hand, the cross-sectional area available for diffusive transport is reduced by the solid components. Transport must take place within interconnected pores inside the rock, which adds varying degrees of tortuosity to the travel path of the gas molecules. The presence of water in the pores additionally complicates transport, as this further reduces the available gas-filled volume in which vapor-phase diffusion can occur. In addition to increased tortuosity, gas transport through porous geomedia can be hindered through gas interactions with both aqueous (e.g., water partitioning via dissolution) and solid phases (e.g., adsorption). In particular, zeolites, which are abundant in NNSS tuff formations, can exert a significant influence on CO2 mobility through adsorption processes. − Their high surface area and affinity for polarizable molecules (e.g., CO2) can both retard gas diffusion and create transient storage domains, complicating simple transport models. Quantifying these effects is crucial for extrapolating laboratory-measured diffusivities to heterogeneous field systems.

The recent Physics Experiment 1 Shot A (PE1-A) at the NNSS provides a rare opportunity to test these relationships under field conditions. The PE1-A test bed is located within zeolitized volcanic tuffs at the NNSS, where variable welding, alteration, fracture density, and permeability generate strong heterogeneity in the gas transport properties. These units are dominated by vitric nonwelded tuffs with localized alteration to zeolitic assemblages rich in clinoptilolite and heulandite. , Variations in zeolite abundance, pore connectivity, and partial saturation are therefore expected to exert strong controls on gas retardation and diffusion behavior.

Conducted as a high-explosive experiment, PE1-A generated significant concentrations of CO2 and other gas species that migrated through the surrounding tuff units and were monitored using a series of spatially distributed gas-sampling boreholes. These measurements captured the spatial and temporal evolution of gas concentrations and provide a rare field data set for evaluating gas transport in NNSS-relevant lithologies. While laboratory diffusion experiments can quantify gas transport properties under controlled conditions, it remains unclear whether these measurements adequately represent field-scale transport, where lithologic heterogeneity, mineral sorption, and in situ water saturation collectively influence gas migration. Consequently, the extent to which laboratory-derived transport behavior can explain the observed field-scale gas distributions remains largely unresolved.

To address this knowledge gap, this study directly compares laboratory diffusion measurements performed on core samples from relevant PE1-A stratigraphic units to field observations of CO2 transport following the PE1-A experiment. Specifically, we evaluate whether laboratory-derived diffusion behavior and zeolite-controlled CO2 sorption are consistent with the spatial patterns of CO2 observed in the field and assess the role of lithology in governing CO2 transport under realistic subsurface conditions. Establishing this laboratory-to-field relationship improves confidence in interpreting post-detonation gas migration and supports predictive assessments of gas transport and containment at the NNSS and other sites.

Materials and Methods

PE1-A Experiment Description and Sample Acquisition

The PE1-A experiment was designed to explore how physical and chemical signatures produced by an underground explosion behave within a well-characterized geologic setting. Conducted as part of the Low-Yield Nuclear Monitoring (LYNM) program, its goal was to recreate the conditions that occur during a small underground nuclear event with a well-instrumented chemical explosive test to facilitate comparison of the multiphysics signals that are utilized in nuclear explosion monitoring. Before detonation, the PE1-A team invested significant effort in characterizing the host formation: a sequence of porous, nonwelded volcanic tuffs with variable permeability and fracture connectivity. A central shot cavity was excavated and instrumented with sensors to monitor pressure, temperature, and gas composition. Around this central emplacement, eight dedicated gas sampling boreholes were drilled at different radial distances and azimuths (Figure a). Each borehole was sealed with grout to define an isolated sampling interval within the formation.

A key component of the experimental design was the development of a closed-loop gas circulation system linking each borehole to a central measurement station inside the tunnel. This system was engineered to continuously pull gases from the borehole, measure their composition, and return them to the subsurface, maintaining pressure balance and minimizing disturbance. Parallel loops were also installed to monitor background gases in the tunnel atmosphere and vent pathways, providing pre-event baselines for comparison. This infrastructure established a controlled framework for observing the generation and movement of explosive gases post-detonation, enabling new observations of gas transport behavior under realistic containment conditions.

Projection of the eight gas-sampling (GS) boreholes onto a single plane (Figure b) gives a visual representation of both their relative distances from the detonation cavity and their locations within the different lithologic units. All GS boreholes are in vitric nonwelded tuff (VNT), but this unit is extremely heterogeneous as it serves as the boundary for nonwelded vitric tuff and altered zeolitic tuff. Both permeability and zeolite content thus vary substantially (over 5 orders of magnitude in permeability and more than 60% difference by weight, respectively) across the VNT unit, leading to subcategories of VNTa and VNT1–10, with VNT1 being the shallowest, VNT10 being the deepest, and VNTa overlying VNT1. GS sampling boreholes were present in VNT1 (GS-2), VNT5 (GS-1 and 3), VNT8 (GS-5), and VNTa (GS-4, 6, and 8) (Figure c).

To explore gas transport across these units, intact rock core samples were collected from the deepest portions of the core collected from drilling the GS-1, GS-2, GS-4, and GS-5 boreholes for use in diffusion experiments. Rock core composition was measured using quantitative X-ray diffraction (QXRD), while porosity and permeability have been measured and reported in previous publications. , QXRD analyses were performed using a Siemens D-500 diffractometer equipped with CuKα radiation (Bruker AXS, Madison, WI, USA). Alumina powder (Al2O3) was used as an internal standard and mineral phases were identified using the search–match function in JADE software (Materials Data, Inc., Livermore, CA, USA). Quantification of mineral phases was performed using internal standard normalization and whole-pattern fitting within JADE. Relative phase abundances were calculated from integrated peak intensities.

Diffusion Experiments

Before diffusion experiments, rock cores were cut into 5 cm sections and trimmed to create flat, uniform faces for contact with the diffusion cell (Figure ). The radial edges were sealed with a UV-curable polymer (UV15X-6NONMED-2, Master Bond, Hackensack, NJ, USA) to prevent edge gas leakage, then coated with a silicone primer while the top and bottom faces were taped off. Each core was placed in a mold and encapsulated in Room Temperature Vulcanization (RTV) silicone (SILPAK, Pomona, CA, USA), which cured overnight to form a gastight gasket as confirmed in prior studies. ,, The silicone gasket sealed two plexiglass cylindrical reservoirs that formed the diffusion cell: a 75 mL chamber and a 500 mL chamber, each with two sampling ports and an O-ring seal. Gas concentrations were analyzed using a Pfeiffer Vacuum OmniStar GSD 320 mass spectrometer (Pfeiffer Vacuum, Aßlar, Germany) connected through a Valco selector valve. Sampling occurred intermittently at a sampling rate of ∼0.4 mL/min to minimize dilution effects, as sampled gas volumes were continually replaced by air to prevent vacuum formation. , All experiments were conducted at room temperature.

Experimental diffusion cell schematic.

Between runs, cores were evacuated in a vacuum oven at a temperature of <40 °C until mass stabilized, minimizing alteration while removing any free water. Some bound water likely remained, but mass consistency and standardized drying ensured reproducibility. Replicate tests verified consistent diffusion behavior before and after drying. Following each experiment, cores were vacuumed for at least 4 days to fully degas the pore space, as confirmed by prior degassing experiments. For comparison with field measurements, diffusion was measured for CO2 gas and H2 gas using starting spiked concentrations of 5% v/v. In addition to being present as a gaseous explosion byproduct, H2 is known to interact minimally with zeolites, providing a basis for nonsorptive gas transport. A similar gas mixture was used in our previous publication. The concentration in the spiked and breakthrough chambers was measured every 5 min for a total of 5 h, which was enough time to measure some breakthrough for all samples. Previous diffusion measurements for a different zeolite-rich rock sample from the NNSS demonstrated substantial impacts of zeolite on gas transport due to CO2 adsorption.

Diffusion-Adsorption Modeling

To parametrize diffusion and adsorption in our system, we simulated gas tracer transport through the rock core with a diffusion-adsorption model using a 1-D axially symmetric geometry. We then used a parameter optimization routine to determine the range of transport parameters that minimize the error of the calculated breakthrough concentrations compared with experimentally measured values.

Kinetic Langmuir Adsorption

The Langmuir adsorption model has been used to describe adsorption for a variety of tracers. , In the simplest caseone adsorbing species (adsorbate) onto a series of equivalent sites on the surface of the solid (adsorbent)the model is valid given the following assumptions: (1) the surface with the adsorbing sites is perfectly flat and homogeneous, (2) the adsorbing gas adsorbs into an immobile state, (3) all sites are energetically equivalent, (4) each site can hold at most one molecule of the adsorbing species (i.e., monolayer coverage only), and (5) no interactions occur between adsorbate molecules at adjacent sites.

Initial and Boundary Conditions

The inlet chamber is spiked with an initial concentration of 5% v/v of CO2 or H2 as in the experiment. The initial gas tracer concentration is 0 ppmv elsewhere. The initial pressure in the diffusion cell is equal to the ambient atmospheric pressure everywhere (P = 0.08 MPa, atmospheric pressure at LANL’s elevation). Exterior boundaries are all prescribed as no-flux boundary conditions with regard to both fluid flow and tracer transport. The exception is at the sampling and inlet nodes, for which one of each is located in the inlet and outlet chambers (Figure ). The sampling node removes air and tracer mass from the chamber at a constant rate commensurate with the average sampling rate being pulled to the mass spectrometer within both chambers (0.8 mL/h, equivalent to two one-minute samples per hour). The inlet node represents the needle valve in the experiment that prevents a vacuum from forming , and replaces air in the chamber at a rate equivalent to the average sampling rate in the experimental procedure. As in the experimental setup, the sampling procedure removes tracer mass and air and replaces the mass with fresh air. The chambers thus become diluted with air as the experiment proceeds. Simulated concentrations are recorded at the sampling nodes.

Numerical Model Domain

The laboratory experiments were simulated using the Finite Element Heat and Mass (FEHM) transport simulator. − A simple 1-dimensional axially symmetric mesh was generated, assuming radial symmetry about the x-axis such that the 3-D volumes are preserved. A 1-D axially symmetric geometry is ideal for representing the experimental setup because it preserves the cylindrical shape of the rock core sample and is dimensionally consistent in terms of volume and length. In the numerical domain, the spiked (inlet) chamber, rock core, and breakthrough (outlet) chamber dimensions have the same radius, and the lengths of each chamber are adjusted such that the total volume is identical to the experimental setup. The mesh is uniformly discretized such that Δx = Δr = 3.175 mm (Figure ). The numerical mesh was applied only to the rock core, where spatial concentration gradients were resolved. The inlet and breakthrough reservoirs were represented as well-mixed volumes, such that tracer concentrations within each chamber were assumed to remain spatially uniform while varying in time.

Parameter Estimation

We use the differential evolution algorithm to find the transport parameters that produce a global minimum in root mean squared error (RMSE) when comparing the modeled breakthrough to the experimental results. The optimization routine is constrained using the following bounds for the transport parameters: 10−8≤D*≤10−4⁡m2/s 10−10≤KL≤106kgg/mol 10−4≤km≤104h−1

The global parameter space is large and involves three free parameters. The differential evolution algorithm does a reasonable job of arriving at a single (although not unique) solution for the combination of parameters, resulting in the lowest error.

Sample Characterization

Table presents the QXRD composition for GS samples. QXRD spectra with fitting results are presented in Figures S1–S4 in the Supporting Information. It is important to note the broad range of zeolite contents present in these samples (12.8–74.6 wt %). The higher percentages are on par with previously analyzed samples taken from the Pahute Mesa at NNSS and analyzed for their interactions with potential proliferation signature gases. These natural zeolites can play a critical role in controlling subsurface gas transport due to their unique structural and chemical properties, which enable strong interactions with a variety of gas molecules including CO2 and xenon. Their high surface area and abundance of exchangeable cations promote selective adsorption and molecular sieving, allowing zeolites to effectively trap gases within their microporous framework. , These interactions can significantly alter gas migration behavior by enhancing adsorption and delaying the breakthrough of some signatures relative to nonsorbing gases such as H2.

Breakthrough Data

Figure A presents diffusion cell data for CO2 through the four rock cores. C/C0, plotted on the y-axis, is calculated as the ratio of the measured concentration divided by the initial concentration in the spiked chamber. Thus, the spiked chamber concentration decreases from an initial ratio of 1, and the breakthrough chamber concentration increases from an initial value of 0. As the system equilibrates, these concentration ratios approach one another. For the PE1-A core samples, GS-1 displayed rapid depletion of CO2 from the spiked chamber and very little breakthrough by 5 h. GS-2 had the highest breakthrough concentration, though C/C0 was still well below the expected equilibrium of ∼0.8 C/C0, calculated based on equilibrated concentrations across both diffusion cell chambers and the total pore volume. GS-4 had less breakthrough than GS-2 but more than GS-1. Finally, GS-5 had very little breakthrough, though a slower rate of spiked chamber depletion than that of GS-1. Based on previous experience with xenon and CO2 diffusion in zeolitic tuff, we hypothesize that transport trends are related to the zeolite content in subsurface lithologies accessed by the different GS boreholes, as observed using QXRD.

(A) Carbon dioxide diffusion cell measurements for PE1-A rock cores. (B) Carbon dioxide distribution in the diffusion cell at 5 h compared with the rock core zeolite percentage.

Zeolite contents of the rock cores are presented in Figure B along with the distribution of CO2 across the different components of the diffusion cell (spiked chamber, breakthrough chamber, and rock core). We found that the sample with the lowest zeolite content (GS-2) had the highest breakthrough fraction, while GS-1, which had the highest zeolite content, had the most CO2 trapped in the core. Interestingly, GS-5 had significant zeolite content but the least core uptake over the time period observed. To explain this phenomenon, we examined the rock core’s structural properties, which can also greatly influence transport. Reported average permeabilities for these formations are 1.48 × 10–13 m2, 4.79 × 10–13 m2, 3.51 × 10–13 m2, and 1.46 × 10–15 m2 for GS-1, GS-2, GS-4, and GS-5, respectively. Although permeability does not directly affect the diffusive flux of gas through the rock, a lower permeability can imply reduced pore connectivity, smaller pore throats, or highly tortuous pathways, all of which could be responsible for limiting diffusive gas transport in the GS-5 core. These findings indicate the importance of incorporating both mineralogical and structural differences between lithologic layers into field-scale transport models to capture field-scale PE1-A observations.

Diffusion Coefficient Calculation Using FEHM Simulations

Effective CO2 diffusivities through the tuff cores are reported in Table and a comparison of fitted diffusion curves with experimental data is shown in Figure . Effective diffusivity values span more than an order of magnitude and appear somewhat correlated with substantial differences in mineralogical composition. Zeolite contents range from 12.8% (GS-2/VNT-1) to 74.6% (GS-1/VNT-5) and apparently exert a strong influence on transport properties. Cores with higher zeolite abundance generally exhibit lower effective diffusivities D e , and, therefore, higher inferred tortuosities τ, consistent with zeolite-rich matrices providing more complex and/or restrictive pore pathways. This interpretation is supported by the independently measured H2 diffusion data, described in the subsequent section, which reproduce the same relative tortuosity trends despite negligible sorption. The second-most zeolitized sample (GS-5, 61.2% zeolite) shows the lowest D e (0.9 × 10–7 m2/s) and highest τ (66.3), indicating strongly hindered gas mobility. In contrast, GS-4 (39.3% zeolite) and GS-2 (12.8% zeolite) display higher diffusivities and lower tortuosities. Langmuir coefficients K L also vary widely (105–1148 kgg/mol), with the highest value in GS-1 (74.6% zeolite), suggesting enhanced sorptive capacity in the most zeolite-rich material. Because the primary objective of this work was to quantify diffusive transport rather than adsorption kinetics, Langmuir parameters were estimated through calibration to breakthrough experiments. Independent equilibrium adsorption measurements would provide additional constraints on these parameters and represent an important direction for future study.

We note that CO2 breakthrough in GS-1 and GS-5 cores was very limited during the experiments, so the transport parameters derived are less reliable than those for GS-2 and GS-4. If we consider just the GS-2 and GS-4 cores, the estimated transport parameters appear to tell a consistent story. These cores are also more directly comparable because their porosities are nearly identical (36%), though they were collected from different stratigraphic units. Their estimated effective diffusivities are similar (8.2 × 10–7 and 9.9 × 10–7 m2/s), as are the inferred tortuosities (8.4 and 7.0). The primary factor related to slower breakthrough in GS-4 is apparently adsorption: the Langmuir distribution coefficient (K L ) is approximately four times greater in GS-4 than in GS-2, indicating a greater affinity of CO2 to the rock. This finding is corroborated by the nearly four-times-greater zeolite content in GS-4 compared to GS-2, though this is not to imply that the relationship is one-to-one. Taken together, these results highlight a strong mineralogical control on both diffusive transport efficiency and CO2 sorption behavior within the sampled tuff units.

Corroborating Tortuosity Using H2 Diffusion Data

Diffusion experiments using H2 gas reinforce the tortuosity patterns inferred from CO2 measurements (Figure ). Because H2 is essentially nonsorbing and has a much higher free-air diffusivity than CO2, variations in its effective diffusivity predominantly reflect physical restrictions on pore geometry rather than gas–solid interactions. The derived H2 tortuosities in Table reproduce the same relative ranking observed for CO2: GS-4 exhibits the lowest tortuosity (τ = 2.3), followed by GS-2 (τ = 3.2) and GS-1 (τ = 5.8), consistent with relatively well-connected pore networks in these cores. In contrast, GS-5 shows a large increase in tortuosity (τ = 89.2), indicating a highly constricted or discontinuous pore structure that strongly resists diffusive transport. The close correspondence between the CO2- and H2-derived tortuosities, despite having different molecular sizes and sorption behaviors, implies that the observed variations are controlled primarily by intrinsic differences in pore connectivity and zeolite-rich microstructure among the tuff units. Additionally, the fact that GS-1 has rapid breakthrough for H2 but extremely low breakthrough for CO2 implies that adsorption is responsible for limiting transport of CO2 in this lithology, while reduced pore connectivity in GS-5 is primarily responsible for reduced transport of both gases.

Comparison with PE1-A Field Test

PE1-A was detonated on October 18, 2023, as a fully coupled source with no airgap between the explosive and surrounding rock. The high explosive (HE) used was Comp B (59.5% RDX, 39.5% TNT, 1% wax), which was assembled from eight shaved cubes (∼2.8 m3 and ∼1,698 kg each) fitted together into a single package weighing roughly 13,580 kg. The package was detonated using 16 detonators (two at each of the eight shaved edges), triggering an implosion-style firing to ensure full HE burn. HE byproducts were monitored in GS boreholes in the near field (Figure ) for 4 weeks before and after the explosion through a combination of real-time monitoring using commercial sensors (e.g., electrochemical and GasCard NG analyzers) and grab sampling. These boreholes were grout sealed, providing an isolated sampling zone within the geologic formation.

Real-time CO2 measurements from GS-1, GS-2, GS-4, and GS-5 are presented in Figure . It is important to note that concentrations from GS-1 and GS-5 are presented on a secondary axis due to these being orders of magnitude lower than those in GS-2 and GS-4. Interestingly, despite GS-1 and GS-2 having similar permeabilities (1.48 × 10–13 m2 vs 4.79 × 10–13 m2 for GS-1 and GS-2, respectively) and porosities (37% vs 36% for GS-1 and GS-2, respectively), and existing at similar distances from the cavity (Figure ), the concentration of CO2 at GS-2 was more than 100 times greater than at GS-1. Structural features alone thus cannot explain this difference. However, GS-2 has both the highest measured CO2 diffusivity and the lowest zeolite content. Zeolite interactions thus appear to be a controlling factor in determining field-scale CO2 transport behavior. Additionally, there was an initial measured spike in CO2 at GS-1, which quickly dissipated. This spike may be capturing the fast kinetics of zeolite adsorption relative to the slower kinetics of diffusion. However, it is also important to caveat that unlike in our experimental system, field experiments produced an initial pressure pulse with advective transport occurring in addition to diffusion.

Field sensor data for CO2 concentrations at GS boreholes following the PE1-A explosive test. CO2 concentrations maxed out the real-time sensor for GS-2 over an approximately 1.5-day period immediately post shot. The maximum CO2 concentration over this interval from grab sample QMS analysis was 27.2% at 4:57 p.m. on 10/18.

Impacts of zeolite content can also be observed at further GS boreholes, GS-4 and GS-5. For instance, although GS-1 was much closer to the cavity and reached its peak concentration faster, concentrations in GS-4 were more than 30 times higher and still increasing at the end of the two-day real-time monitoring period. GS-5 concentrations were much lower than those in GS-2 and GS-4 and comparable with those in GS-1, although an initial spike was not observed likely due to the further distance from the cavity. GS-5 had a slightly lower zeolite content than GS-1 (61.2% vs 74.6%), but its permeability was also 2 orders of magnitude lower. Thus, both zeolite content and permeability may contribute to slower transport in GS-5; however, given the similarities in both arrival time and peak concentration magnitude between GS-1 and GS-5, zeolite impacts appear to be the controlling factor. It is important to note that within the PE1-A testbed, rocks contain in situ water, making gas transport a function of both lithology and fluid saturation. Lithology governs the abundance of sorptive minerals such as zeolites, pore structure, and permeability, while water saturation modifies the accessibility of the pore space and sorption sites available for gas transport. Laboratory studies have shown that water competes with gases for adsorption sites within zeolite pores, reducing sorption to varying extents depending on the gas identity and the degree of saturation. ,, However, our previous work demonstrated that partially saturated zeolitic tuff still retains sufficient accessible pore space for significant CO2 sorption, indicating that sorptive interactions remain important under realistic subsurface saturations.

Water also influences transport through additional mechanisms, including dissolution and partitioning of CO2 into the aqueous phase, reductions in effective gas-filled porosity, and increased tortuosity of gas migration pathways. ,, Consequently, CO2 behavior in PE1-A likely reflects the combined influence of zeolite-rich lithologies that preferentially sorb CO2 and partially saturated pore networks that simultaneously moderate both the sorption efficiency and gas mobility. These coupled controls suggest that lithologic heterogeneity and spatial variations in water saturation should both be considered when interpreting gas transport and the compositional evolution.

Conclusions

Containment monitoring for underground nuclear explosions has traditionally relied on atmospheric sampling of noble gases, particularly radioxenon. However, there are challenges associated with differentiating these nuclear signatures from the background and accounting for radioactive decay. To this end, we explored the migration of CO2 generated by high-explosive detonation through subsurface geomaterials. Through this study, we demonstrate a clear connection between laboratory-scale investigations of CO2 gas transport and field-scale measurements taken following the PE1-A high-explosive experiment. These findings support the notion that zeolite content is a major factor impacting post-detonation gas transport at the NNSS. While previous studies have shown that high temperatures and increased water content will decrease adsorption effects, field-scale observations from PE1-A clearly show that for rocks with high zeolite content, adsorption continues to have a significant effect on transport even in the near-field. As we begin to consider longer-term transport and make predictions about plume migration, it is critical to incorporate these effects into field-scale modeling, particularly within zeolite-rich lithologies in the NNSS.
