Ethical reporting and oversight in outbreak investigations conducted in India, 2015–2025: a systematic review
Authors: Praveen Kumar S, Rajeev Aravindakshan, Mohammed Shoyaib Khazi, Chandru R
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundOutbreak investigations generate actionable evidence to identify sources of infection and guide effective control measures. In outbreak settings, ethical considerations become even more significant because such investigations often involve vulnerable populations, the collection of identifiable data, and interventions under conditions of uncertainty. The Indian Council of Medical Research states that while there may be a need to undertake research quickly, this should not compromise scientific validity or the need to uphold ethical requirements. However, the extent to which outbreak investigations in India comply with ethics oversight reporting has not been systematically examined. The objective was to evaluate ethical considerations in outbreak investigations conducted in India, published between 2015 and 2025 that reported ethical statements, obtained ethical approval, and participant informed consent.MethodsThe review was registered in PROSPERO 2025 CRD420251140176. Human outbreak investigations conducted in India, published from 1 January 2015 to 25 August 2025, were searched in databases viz., Embase, PubMed, Scopus, and Web of Science. Analysis was conducted using R software.ResultsA total of 76 outbreak investigations were included. Overall, 14 (18%) did not report an ethics statement, while 31 (41%) reported an ethics statement without Institutional Ethics Committee approval, and 31 (41%) reported both an ethics statement and approval. Participant consent was not obtained in 41 (54%) publications, written consent was obtained in 26 (34%), and 9 (12%) reported verbal consent. Investigations published in later years demonstrated significantly higher levels of ethics oversight (p < 0.001), and ethics oversight varied significantly across disease groups (p = 0.04); no association was observed with study setting or methodological completeness.DiscussionEthics reporting in outbreak investigations in India remains inconsistent. Routine ethics preparedness requires IEC readiness for rapid review and integration with outbreak investigation initiation.ConclusionLess than half documented formal Institutional Ethics Committee approval. The findings highlight the need to strengthen ethical preparedness within outbreak response systems. Introduction

Outbreak investigations generate actionable evidence to identify sources of infection and guide effective control measures [1]. They are defined as a systematic, field-based epidemiological process aimed at identifying the source, transmission dynamics, and populations at risk to implement timely control and preventive measures [2]. In such contexts, research is necessary to enable efficient and appropriate health and humanitarian responses during the ongoing emergency, while also informing the preparedness for similar health events in future [3].

In outbreak settings, ethical considerations are of critical significance, as such investigations often involve vulnerable populations, the collection of identifiable data, and interventions are carried out under conditions of uncertainty [4]. Ethics committee review upholds core principles such as autonomy, beneficence, non-maleficence, and justice, which are essential in safeguarding participant dignity, minimising harm, and ensuring equitable distribution of risks and benefits [5, 6]. The World Health Organisation recommends that ethical considerations are maintained during emergencies through expedited review processes rather than being bypassed [7]. Further emphasis is given on the ideology of “core ethical standards must remain intact, even if procedures are expedited” [8].

In India, the Indian Council of Medical Research has progressively strengthened ethics oversight frameworks, culminating in the National Ethical Guidelines for Biomedical and Health Research Involving Human Participants (2017), which explicitly address public health research and emergency settings (Sect. 12 – pg.137). The guidelines state that the urgency of research should not compromise scientific validity or adherence to the requirements of ethical conduct of research [9]. However, Outbreak investigations are often conducted under the Epidemic Diseases Act of 1897, which facilitates rapid public health response but does not explicitly address ethical safeguards for research participants [10]. This creates a structural gap between legal authority and ethical governance.

Outbreak investigations, therefore, operate at a critical interface between public health practice and research, where the urgency of response must be balanced with ethical standards. This urgency may lead to the assumption that ethics review and informed consent procedures can be deferred or bypassed, which creates a fundamental opposition between operational efficiency and ethical accountability. In the Indian context, it is further shaped by the coexistence of legal mandates for outbreak control and ethics guidelines that emphasise the protection of participant rights and scientific integrity.

The extent to which outbreak investigations in India adheres to ethics oversight reporting has not been systematically examined. This systematic review was undertaken to assess human outbreak investigations published in India between 2015 and 2025, with a specific focus on the reporting of ethical approval, informed consent, and adherence to recommended investigation practices. By examining these dimensions, the study aims to identify gaps in ethical compliance and highlight the need for stronger alignment with established ethical guidelines in outbreak research.

Methodology

Review protocol

This systematic review was conducted and reported in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines (Table S3). The review protocol was developed after careful discussion with all the authors and was registered in PROSPERO (Registration Number: CRD420251140176). The registered protocol specified the research question, eligibility criteria, data extraction procedures, and planned methods of analysis. The review was conducted in accordance with this protocol, and no major deviations were made during the study.

Search strategy

The search strategy was developed in accordance with the components of the PRESS (Peer Review of Electronic Search Strategies) 2015 guideline. The search strategy aimed to identify all relevant published literature without any restrictions on language. The search algorithm (Table S1) was finalised in consultation with all authors and was based on a previous systematic review of outbreak investigations [11]. A systematic search was conducted in the following databases: PubMed, Embase, Scopus, and Web of Science. The results were downloaded in RIS format.

Selection criteria

The study selection process was conducted in two stages. Duplicate detection was performed in Excel using DOIs (digital object identifiers); studies with duplicate DOIs were removed. Two independent reviewers (P.K.S. & C.R.) screened titles and abstracts of records retrieved from the database searches against the inclusion and exclusion criteria. The full texts of potentially relevant studies were assessed for final inclusion by the reviewers (P.K.S. & C.R.) independently. Inter-reviewer agreement at this stage was 97.8%, with a Cohen’s kappa of 0.91 (n = 90), indicating almost perfect agreement. Any discrepancies between the reviewers were resolved by third reviewers (R.A. and M.S.K.) to reach consensus.

Inclusion criteria

Studies were included if they met the following criteria:

Population: Investigations involving human populations affected by infectious disease outbreaks, with explicit reporting of an outbreak investigation.

Population: Investigations involving human populations affected by infectious disease outbreaks, with explicit reporting of an outbreak investigation.

Setting: Studies conducted within India, including community, hospital, or institutional settings.

Study Type: Original outbreak investigation reports, including descriptive or analytical epidemiological studies (e.g., case-control or cross-sectional investigations conducted as part of outbreak response).

Time Frame: Published between 1 January 2015 and 25 August 2025, to capture ethical practices in the period surrounding and following the release of the Indian Council of Medical Research (ICMR) National Ethical Guidelines (2017).

Studies were excluded if they met any of the following criteria:

Reports with only laboratory analysis, genomic studies and secondary data analysis.

Data extraction

A standard data extraction form (Table S2) was prepared in consultation with all authors and was pilot tested on four outbreak investigation publications. Data extraction was conducted by author PKS and it was verified for accuracy and completeness by author CR independently, with blinding. Study authors were not contacted for further information.

The data extraction form captured the year of publication, year of outbreak, disease involved, agency conducting the investigation, place of outbreak (area/setting), ethical considerations statement, ethics approval, informed consent processes, details of each step of the outbreak investigation, type of analytical study, and environmental investigations conducted. Studies were included based on the year of publication (1 January 2015 to 25 August 2025), irrespective of the year in which the outbreak investigation was carried out. The year of outbreak investigation was extracted separately as a study characteristic for analysis, as some studies described outbreaks that occurred before 2015 but were published within the eligible time frame. To assess methodological completeness, we adapted the outbreak investigation checklist for the 10-step approach used by the Field Epidemiology Training Programme (FETP) at ICMR-NIE for data extraction [12]. Each of the ten FETP outbreak investigation steps comprises multiple predefined subcomponents (e.g., step 1 “confirm diagnosis” includes excess cases, background rates checked, surveillance system changes, and population changes). During data extraction, each subcomponent was noted as “reported” or “not reported” based on explicit mention in the publication.

A step was considered completed if at least one of its subcomponents was reported. This approach was adopted to account for variability in reporting practices across outbreak investigations. Notably, the absence of detailed documentation does not necessarily indicate that all elements of a step were not performed.

Risk of bias assessment

Given the descriptive and methodological focus of this review on mapping reported ethical practices rather than quantifying intervention effects, a risk of bias assessment of individual studies was not conducted. The objective was to document the presence or absence of ethical reporting, not to judge the internal validity of therapeutic or observational findings.

Synthesis methods

The unit of analysis was the individual outbreak investigation publication, with each included study considered as one observation. Data synthesis was primarily quantitative, with studies grouped by disease category and ethical reporting characteristics. Descriptive and inferential analyses were conducted using R software (version 4.5.1).

Results are presented using descriptive statistics, including frequencies and percentages. Inferential analyses were performed to examine associations between ethics oversight and study characteristics. Associations between categorical variables were assessed using the Pearson’s Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. Fisher’s exact test was applied when more than 20% of cells had expected counts < 5 or when any expected cell count was < 1. A p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

A total of 1483 articles were identified through the initial search. After removing duplicates & conference abstracts, 871 articles were screened based on titles and abstracts. Descriptive summary of ethical consideration

Among 76 publications included, 14 (18%) did not report an ethics statement, while 31 (41%) reported an ethics statement without Institutional Ethics Committee (IEC) approval, and 31 (41%) reported both an ethics statement and IEC approval. Among 27 publications on outbreak investigation that documented the timing of IEC review, 20 (74%) were prospectively conducted, while the remaining reported retrospective approvals. Regarding the annual distribution of outbreak investigation publications, there is an overall increase in reports over time, with a marked increase after 2020. The number of publications increased gradually since 2015, and a sudden surge is observed during the year 2020. Socio-demographic distribution of included reports

Among the 76 outbreak investigations, 41 (54%) of the investigations were conducted in the community setting, 25 (34%) were in hospital settings, while 5 (6.6%) were in mixed community and hospital settings, and the rest were conducted in institutional settings (viz., colleges, military camps). Most investigations were conducted in urban areas 39 (51%), and the rest in rural regions 35 (46%). With respect to outbreak investigations that involved collaborations between multiple institutions, 34 (45%) investigations were done with collaboration among 2–3 agencies, and 23 (30%) single investigations were carried out by a single agency. The remaining investigations were done in collaboration among 4 agencies. The most frequently involved agencies included State Health Institutes 25 (33%), followed by the Government medical colleges 23 (30%), and Other Government Agencies in 23 (30%). The number of published outbreak investigations was higher in the period during or after 2018, i.e., 41 (54%), than in the period before 2018. Food and waterborne diseases constituted the largest category of diseases that warranted an outbreak investigation at 29 (38%). It was followed by airborne/respiratory diseases at 15 (20%) and vector-borne 8 (11%), respectively. (Table 2). State-wise distribution of outbreak investigation showed substantial variation across India (Figure S6).

Ethics oversight and study characteristics association

Ethics oversight was analysed as a three-category variable: no ethics statement, ethics statement without approval, and ethics statement with approval. Discussion

This systematic review of outbreak investigations published in India between 2015 and 2025 reveals substantial gaps in the reporting of ethics oversight. Only 41% of the included investigations documented both an ethics statement and Institutional Ethics Committee (IEC) approval; a further 41% reported an ethics statement without formal approval, and 18% provided no ethics statement at all. Taken together, these findings suggest a persistent assumption that outbreak investigations conducted during public health emergencies are exempt from formal ethics oversight. This assumption is at odds with international guidance, which holds that emergency contexts do not waive ethical obligations but instead require adapted mechanisms, such as expedited review processes, to uphold them [13]. In several reports, waivers were granted by the conducting institute rather than by an IEC, with the activities classified as standard public health response. This indicates a structural gap in ethical governance and reflects ambiguities between public health practice and research oversight that have been documented globally. During the West African Ebola outbreak, for instance, unclear regulatory boundaries led to inconsistent ethics review and consequent delays in implementing critical research [14]. The spatial heterogeneity observed in our review further suggests that institutional maturity, research culture, and the presence of functional IECs together influence adherence to ethical reporting practices. Outbreak investigations occupy a regulatory grey zone between public health practice and research governance, a tension that has been recognised in global bioethics deliberations on health-emergency research [15].

Consent practices in our sample reflect the operational and ethical complexities of emergency fieldwork. Nearly half of the investigations did not report obtaining participant consent; among those that did, written consent was documented in roughly one-third of studies and verbal consent in a smaller proportion. This pattern likely reflects both genuine practical constraints and variability in how field teams recognise their ethical obligations. International guidance endorses context-sensitive approaches, including verbal consent or formal waivers when justified, but it consistently emphasises the need for clear documentation and oversight. Because outbreaks frequently affect socioeconomically vulnerable populations, inconsistent or undocumented consent practices risk exacerbating existing inequities and eroding the community trust on which an effective public health response depends [16].

The significant association between ethics oversight and publication year suggests that temporal trends, including evolving editorial expectations, influence ethical reporting. The association with disease group similarly suggests that the nature of the outbreak shapes the likelihood of obtaining or reporting ethics approval, plausibly reflecting differences in perceived risk, regulatory scrutiny, and the standardisation of investigation protocols. Evidence from the global governance of COVID-19 research reinforces this interpretation: strengthened regulatory oversight and explicit publication requirements during the pandemic were associated with improved reporting of ethics approvals [17]. By contrast, the lack of significant association between ethics oversight and outbreak year, study setting, FETP-step completeness, or the number of collaborating agencies indicates that ethical reporting is not consistently linked to the structural or methodological characteristics of investigations. This pattern implies that current compliance is driven more by external reporting expectations than by embedded institutional processes.

Around 43% of the included investigations incorporated analytical study designs, a modest improvement over earlier reviews [11]. Structural barriers nonetheless persist. India currently lacks a formal mechanism for expedited ethics review of public health research conducted during outbreaks arising from public health emergencies [18]. International guidance recommends rapid-review systems capable of reviewing and approving protocols within 24–72 h so that critical research is not delayed [13, 19]. Although the Integrated Disease Surveillance Programme (IDSP) issues weekly outbreak bulletins that support the timely initiation of investigations [20], the absence of a parallel rapid-review pathway means that timeliness in surveillance is not matched by timeliness in ethics review.

Disease-specific patterns add further nuance. Food- and waterborne outbreaks showed the highest methodological completeness, with 31% completing all ten FETP steps. This likely reflects better operational standardisation in food-safety investigations, mature investigation protocols, and intersectoral coordination between food-safety regulators and public health teams. Comparable trends have been observed elsewhere, where structured surveillance and standardised investigation protocols are associated with improved methodological rigour and ethical compliance [21]. The increase in outbreak-investigation publications and reported ethics oversight observed in 2020 is most plausibly attributable to the COVID-19 pandemic and the heightened scrutiny it brought to research conducted during emergencies [15].

These findings carry several implications for strengthening the preparedness of IECs to support outbreak response. First, standardised ethics-reporting templates should be developed so that authors and reviewers can document oversight in a consistent and verifiable manner. Second, mechanisms for expedited review within 24–72 h should be formally established; such mechanisms are widely endorsed as a means of preserving ethical safeguards without compromising the timeliness of public health action [15]. Third, training in ethical decision-making for field-epidemiology teams should be strengthened, in line with global capacity-building initiatives [22, 23]. Finally, integrating ethics oversight into existing surveillance platforms such as IDSP would enhance both compliance and accountability and would align national practice with evolving global standards for ethical governance of public health emergencies [16]. Collectively, these measures would help ensure that the integrity of India’s epidemic response is matched by the integrity of its ethical oversight.

Strengths and limitations

This review offers a system-level ethical perspective grounded in a policy-relevant research question. Its use of quantifiable indicators, namely the reporting of ethics statements, IEC approvals, and informed-consent procedures as outcomes, allows for an objective assessment of ethical compliance across the published literature.

Methodological rigor was reinforced by prospective protocol registration, standardised data-extraction procedures, and pre-specified eligibility criteria. Independent screening and verification further support the internal consistency and reproducibility of the findings.

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting these results. First, the analysis was restricted to peer-reviewed outbreak investigations; grey literature, including IDSP field reports and internal surveillance documents, could not be comprehensively retrieved, and ethically conducted investigations that went unpublished because they lacked IEC approval are necessarily excluded. The findings should therefore be interpreted in light of both publication and reporting bias. Second, ethical assessment was based solely on information reported in the published articles and was not verified through correspondence with authors. The absence of documented approval or consent should accordingly be read as a limitation in ethical reporting, transparency, and journal disclosure norms rather than as direct evidence of unethical conduct. Third, the FETP ten-step framework was applied retrospectively to published reports, so steps that are routinely performed in practice but not explicitly described in publications (for example, peer discussions under Step 8) may be underrepresented, introducing a potential for misclassification bias.

Conclusion

This systematic review shows that although most outbreak investigations in India include an ethical statement at the time of publication, less than half document formal IEC approval, indicating a persistent gap between ethical principles and their implementation. In recent years, hospital-based investigations and experiments were more likely to provide an ethical statement, indicating growing awareness in institutional preparedness for ethical compliance. Investigator-driven decisions to waive ethical review, which is supposed to be made by the IEC, point to a continued lack of clarity in ethical governance during public health emergencies.

These findings highlight the need for strengthening ethical preparedness within outbreak response systems. Incorporating ethical governance into outbreak surveillance frameworks, standardising ethics reporting practices, and establishing institutional procedures for expedited IEC review might improve compliance during emergencies. Capacity building of field epidemiology teams in ethical decision-making, along with robust oversight mechanisms, is essential for maintaining scientific integrity.

Future research should examine barriers to ethical compliance in outbreak settings, assess the effectiveness of expedited review mechanisms, and incorporate grey literature such as IDSP reports to provide a more comprehensive understanding of ethical practices. Strengthening ethical governance during public health emergencies is essential to safeguard participant rights, maintain public trust, and enhance the credibility of outbreak response systems.
