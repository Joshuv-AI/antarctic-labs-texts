Incidental parathyroidectomy risk in thyroid carcinoma patients undergoing thyroidectomy: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Binzhu Wang, XiaoYi Long, Jianguo Chen, Shu Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIncidental parathyroidectomy (IP) is an important complication of thyroidectomy, and its risk may differ between malignant and benign thyroid pathology. This study aimed to evaluate the association between malignant thyroid pathology and the risk of IP in patients undergoing thyroidectomy.MethodsA systematic review and meta-analysis was conducted in accordance with PRISMA guidelines. Scopus, PubMed/MEDLINE, Web of Science, Embase, and Google Scholar were searched from database inception to November 15, 2025. Eligible studies included cross-sectional, cohort, or case-control studies that compared the incidence of IP between patients with malignant and benign thyroid pathology after thyroidectomy. Pooled odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated using a random-effects model. Sensitivity analysis and assessment of publication bias were also performed.ResultsThirty-three studies comprising 8,062 patients with IP and 25,134 controls without IP were included. Malignant thyroid pathology was associated with significantly higher odds of IP than benign thyroid pathology (OR 1.58, 95% CI 1.30–1.92). In subgroup analyses, no statistically significant associations were observed for Hashimoto’s thyroiditis (OR 1.29, 95% CI 0.95–1.74), papillary thyroid carcinoma (OR 1.29, 95% CI 0.90–1.83), or follicular carcinoma (OR 0.70, 95% CI 0.41–1.19). Sensitivity analysis showed that the overall findings were robust, and Egger’s test did not indicate significant publication bias.ConclusionMalignant thyroid pathology was modestly associated with IP; however, substantial heterogeneity, residual confounding, and reliance on crude observational data suggest malignancy may primarily reflect operative complexity rather than an independent causal risk factor. Findings should therefore be interpreted as exploratory associative evidence.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s00423-026-04138-8.

Introduction

Thyroidectomy is one of the most frequently performed endocrine surgical procedures and is commonly used in the management of both benign and malignant thyroid disease [1, 2]. Although generally safe, thyroidectomy may be associated with several important postoperative complications, including cervical hematoma, recurrent laryngeal nerve injury, hypocalcemia, and hypoparathyroidism [2–4]. Among these, incidental parathyroidectomy (IP), defined as the unintentional removal of one or more parathyroid glands during thyroid surgery, is a clinically relevant complication because it may contribute to postoperative calcium imbalance and parathyroid dysfunction [5, 6].

Preservation of the parathyroid glands during thyroidectomy is essential for maintaining calcium and phosphate homeostasis [7]. Damage to or inadvertent excision of these glands may result in transient or permanent hypoparathyroidism, which can impair quality of life and, in some patients, require long-term calcium and vitamin D supplementation [8]. Because of these consequences, identifying factors associated with IP is important for surgical planning, risk stratification, and the development of preventive operative strategies [5].

Several clinicopathological and surgical factors have been proposed as possible determinants of IP, including the extent of thyroidectomy, central neck dissection, reoperation, inflammatory thyroid disease, and malignant pathology [5, 6, 9]. In particular, thyroid malignancy may be associated with a higher risk of IP because cancer surgery often requires a wider operative field and, in selected cases, more extensive resection or lymph node dissection [5, 10, 11]. However, the available evidence remains inconsistent. While some studies have reported a significantly higher incidence of IP in patients with malignant thyroid lesions [12–14], others have found no significant difference between malignant and benign disease [15–17]. In contrast, Marian et al. reported a higher likelihood of IP in benign lesions [18], and Melot et al. observed an association in univariate analysis that was no longer significant after multivariable adjustment [19].

Previous meta-analyses have evaluated IP mainly as one of several operative risk factors during thyroidectomy and have provided limited assessment of specific benign and malignant pathological subtypes [5, 11]. In addition, several recent primary studies have been published since the latest reviews, potentially expanding the current evidence base [14, 19]. Therefore, the present systematic review and meta-analysis aimed to provide an updated and more pathology-focused evaluation of the association between malignant thyroid disease and IP, including subgroup and meta-regression analyses to further explore heterogeneity across studies.

Methods

Study design and search strategy

This systematic review and meta-analysis was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines [20]. The review protocol was not prospectively registered in PROSPERO before study initiation; therefore, no registration number is available. A comprehensive literature search was performed in Scopus, PubMed/MEDLINE, Web of Science, and Embase from database inception to November 15, 2025. The search strategy was developed according to the indexing system and search algorithm of each database and included both MeSH and free-text terms related to thyroid neoplasms, thyroidectomy, parathyroidectomy, pathology, and thyroid surgery. To improve reproducibility and capture terminology commonly used in the thyroid surgery literature, additional free-text terms specific to unintended parathyroid removal were included, such as ‘incidental parathyroidectomy’ ‘inadvertent parathyroidectomy’ ‘accidental parathyroidectomy’ and ‘unintentional parathyroidectomy’. To maximize the completeness of the search, Google Scholar and gray literature sources were additionally screened to identify potentially relevant studies not indexed in the major electronic databases. The detailed search strategy for each database is provided in Table S1.

Eligibility criteria

Studies were eligible for inclusion if they employed cross-sectional, cohort, or case-control designs, focusing on patients who underwent thyroid surgery and provided sufficient data regarding IP. In most included studies, IP was defined as the unintended removal of one or more parathyroid glands identified on postoperative histopathological examination of the thyroidectomy specimen. However, pathological assessment and reporting practices were not fully standardized across studies. Also, because observational study designs were expected, methodological heterogeneity among studies was considered during interpretation of the pooled findings. The included studies must have categorized participants into two groups: cases (patients who underwent IP) and controls (patients who did not undergo IP). Furthermore, the studies should have compared these groups based on the pathological classification of thyroid tissue as either benign or malignant. Studies that were reviews, meta-analyses, case reports, case series, or in vivo and in vitro studies were excluded from this analysis. Additionally, studies that examined only IP in one of the benign or malignant thyroid tissue groups, or studies that focused solely on one of the case or control groups, were also excluded. It is important to note that studies which only assessed the benign or malignant nature of the thyroid preoperatively, or those that lacked a definitive postoperative pathological diagnosis, were also excluded. This approach ensured the inclusion of studies with confirmed pathology. Studies published in languages other than English or as conference papers were also excluded from this study.

Quality assessment

The potential for bias in the studies included in this analysis was evaluated using the critical appraisal tools provided by the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) [21]. Based on this assessment, the studies were classified into categories of low, moderate, or high risk of bias. Two independent authors individually assessed the quality of each study. In cases where discrepancies arose regarding the quality assessment of the studies, a third author provided the final judgment.

Study screening and data extraction

The studies retrieved through the search process were screened and stored in EndNote version 2020. Two authors independently performed the screening, and in cases of disagreement regarding the inclusion or exclusion of a study, the final decision was made by a third author. Duplicate records were removed prior to title and abstract screening. Full-text assessment was subsequently performed independently by the reviewers according to the predefined eligibility criteria. Subsequently, for the extraction of data from the included studies, Microsoft Excel 2020 was utilized. Two authors independently organized the data extraction sheets in Excel, and in cases of discrepancies in any data points, a third author provided the final judgment. The Excel sheet contained demographic information of the studies, including publication year, data entry period, country, as well as age and gender of the case and control groups. Additionally, the data for both case and control groups were extracted in terms of frequency based on the reported pathology.

It is important to note that countries are categorized into six regions based on the World Health Organization (WHO) classification [22]. Data from these regions were extracted separately. Additionally, in cases where the type of pathology was reported, benign (such as Graves’ disease, goiter, and Hashimoto’s thyroiditis) and malignant (such as papillary and follicular carcinoma) conditions were also extracted separately.

Statistical analysis

The data from this study were analyzed using Stata software, version 18.0. Given the heterogeneity observed across the studies, a random-effects model was employed. To evaluate the consistency of the results, both Cochran’s Q test and Higgins’ I² test were utilized [23]. The pooled effect estimates were primarily calculated using crude event data extracted from the included observational studies because adjusted multivariable estimates were inconsistently reported and were not sufficiently comparable across studies to allow a unified quantitative synthesis. Therefore, the pooled associations mainly reflect unadjusted observational relationships and may be influenced by residual confounding factors. Further investigation into the heterogeneity of the studies was conducted through subgroup analyses, which were categorized by factors such as country, WHO region, risk of bias, publication year, and the period when data collection began. Additionally, the types of benign (including Graves’ disease, goiter, and Hashimoto’s thyroiditis) and malignant (including papillary and follicular carcinoma) pathologies were assessed separately. When necessary, the “Meta Converter” tool was used to adjust for variations in quantitative variables [24]. The findings were visualized in a forest plot. Pooled estimates were calculated using the odds ratios (ORs) along with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). To assess the influence of individual studies on the overall results, sensitivity analysis was performed. To further explore potential sources of heterogeneity, univariable and multivariable meta-regression analyses were conducted using predefined study-level variables. In addition, publication bias was evaluated using Egger’s test, with the results visually depicted through a funnel plot. A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered indicative of statistical significance.

Results

Study selection and characteristics

A total of 1857 records were initially retrieved from the selected databases. After eliminating duplicate entries, 1172 articles underwent an initial screening based on their titles and abstracts. Subsequently, 50 full-text articles were retrieved for further evaluation. Upon reviewing the full texts, 17 studies were excluded as they failed to meet the predetermined inclusion criteria. After a thorough assessment, 33 studies [10, 12, 14, 15, 19, 25–52] encompassing 8,062 cases and 25,134 controls were ultimately included in the meta-analysis, as illustrated in Fig. 1. The publication dates of the studies spanned from 2001 to 2024, with data collection periods ranging from 1995 to 2023. Most studies were conducted within the last decade. The participants’ ages varied from 12 to 70 years, with the mean ages ranging between 44.9 and 55.4 years. Regarding gender distribution, both male and female participants were included in all studies, with a higher proportion of females in the majority of the studies. The studies were conducted across various countries, including the USA, Turkey, Greece, Saudi Arabia, Italy, and other regions. The risk of bias was assessed for each study, with 11 studies classified as low risk, 17 studies as moderate risk, and 5 studies identified as high risk, indicating varying levels of quality across the studies included in the analysis (Table S2). A detailed summary of the study characteristics can be found in Table 1.

Results of the meta-analysis

Risk of IP based on malignant vs. benign thyroid pathology

The pooled analysis demonstrated an OR of 1.58 (95% CI: 1.30–1.92), indicating that patients with malignant thyroid pathology had higher odds of IP than those with benign thyroid conditions (Fig. 2). However, substantial between-study heterogeneity was observed in the overall analysis (I² = 84.70%), indicating that the pooled estimate should be interpreted cautiously and may be influenced by differences in operative extent, central neck dissection practices, surgeon experience, institutional surgical approaches, and pathological reporting standards across studies.

IP Risk by primary variables

For studies published between 2011 and 2020, the OR was 1.746 (95% CI: 1.347, 2.264), indicating a significant association between thyroid malignancy and IP. Similarly, studies with a low risk of bias reported an OR of 2.033 (95% CI: 1.598, 2.586), highlighting a stronger association in these studies. When analyzing by region, studies from the European region showed an OR of 1.337 (95% CI: 1.067, 1.676), while studies from the Americas demonstrated a higher OR of 2.195 (95% CI: 1.352, 3.564) (Table 2).

Malignant and benign types and IP risk

For benign conditions, Hashimoto’s thyroiditis showed an OR of 1.286 (95% CI: 0.949, 1.742), while both goiter and Graves’ disease had ORs close to one, indicating no significant association with IP. Similarly, among malignant diseases, papillary thyroid carcinoma and follicular thyroid carcinoma showed no significant association with IP (papillary thyroid carcinoma; OR = 1.287 (95% CI: 0.904, 1.833), follicular thyroid carcinoma; OR = 0.695 (95% CI: 0.406, 1.188)) (Table 3).

Univariable and multivariable meta-regression analyses to explore the factors contributing to heterogeneity

To explore potential sources of heterogeneity, univariable and multivariable meta-regression analyses were performed using study-level variables, including publication year, implementation-year start, risk of bias, and WHO region classification. In the univariable analysis, only risk of bias showed significant associations with the pooled effect size, whereas publication year, implementation-year start, and region were not statistically significant. In the multivariable model, studies from the Region of the Americas showed significantly higher pooled estimates compared with the European region (p = 0.024). The final model explained 30.04% of the between-study heterogeneity, although substantial residual heterogeneity remained (I² = 77.26%) (Table S3).

Sensitivity analysis and publication bias

The sensitivity analysis showed that the exclusion of any individual study did not substantially alter the overall effect size, confirming the robustness of the findings (Figure S1). Also, as shown in Figure S2, the funnel plot appeared visually symmetrical, suggesting no substantial publication bias. In similar, to further quantify the bias, we conducted Egger’s test. The results of Egger’s test for the overall analysis indicated no significant publication bias, with a bias coefficient (BE) of 0.36 (SE = 0.765, P-value = 0.6339).

Discussion

The present meta-analysis of 33 studies found a modest association between malignant thyroid pathology and IP compared with benign thyroid disease. However, this finding should be interpreted cautiously because substantial between-study heterogeneity was present (overall I² >80%), and most pooled estimates were derived from crude observational data. Differences in operative extent, central neck dissection practices, surgeon experience, institutional approaches, and pathological reporting standards may have influenced the observed association. Subgroup analyses showed non-significant trends toward higher odds of IP for papillary thyroid carcinoma and Hashimoto’s thyroiditis, and toward lower odds for follicular carcinoma. These subgroup findings should be considered exploratory because they were based on fewer studies and events.

Various studies support an increased observed risk of IP in patients with malignant thyroid pathology compared with benign disease. Consistent with our findings, Bai et al. reported that malignancy increased the risk of IP by approximately 60% and also showed that central neck dissection increased the risk by 2.35-fold [5]. Similarly, Ahmed et al. identified malignancy, total thyroidectomy, central neck dissection, and reoperation as significant risk factors for IP [11]. Granata et al. also reported a significant association between malignancy and IP in multivariable analysis, with an approximately 40% increased risk [14]. Malignant thyroid tumors may extend beyond the thyroid gland and adhere to adjacent structures, including the trachea, esophagus, strap muscles, and major vessels [53, 54]. Such local extension can require wider operative exposure, more extensive resection, and central neck lymph node dissection, thereby increasing the risk of inadvertent parathyroid excision or devascularization and related complications such as hypoparathyroidism and calcium imbalance [5, 11, 41–55].

Nevertheless, the relationship between malignant thyroid pathology and IP should not be interpreted as a direct causal effect of malignancy itself. Instead, the association may be mediated largely by the greater operative complexity commonly encountered in thyroid cancer surgery. Malignant lesions frequently require more extensive resections, central neck lymph node dissection, and wider operative exposure due to local invasion or nodal involvement, all of which may increase the risk of inadvertent parathyroid excision or devascularization. In particular, central neck dissection is more commonly performed in malignant thyroid disease and may substantially increase the risk of IP because of the closer anatomical relationship between lymphatic dissection planes and the parathyroid glands [56]. This interpretation is supported by Melot et al., who found that malignancy was not independently associated with IP after multivariable adjustment, whereas central neck lymph node dissection and inadvertent thymus removal remained important predictors [19]. Similarly, Turhan et al. reported that malignancy was significant in univariate analysis but not in the multivariable model, in which female sex, absence of multinodular goiter, smaller thyroid volume, and central neck lymph node dissection were identified as major predictors of IP [52]. Moreover, the pooled estimates in the present meta-analysis were derived predominantly from crude observational data rather than uniformly adjusted multivariable effect estimates, because adjusted analyses were inconsistently reported across the included studies. Consequently, the observed associations may partly reflect uncontrolled operative and clinical confounding rather than an independent effect of malignant thyroid pathology itself. Collectively, the available evidence suggests that malignant pathology may function mainly as a surrogate marker for more extensive or technically complex surgery rather than as an independent causal risk factor for IP.

In the subgroup analyses, papillary thyroid carcinoma demonstrated a non-significant trend toward higher odds of IP, whereas follicular carcinoma showed a non-significant trend toward lower odds. This pattern should not be interpreted as contradicting the statistically significant overall malignant-versus-benign analysis because the two analyses address different comparisons. The overall analysis evaluated malignant thyroid pathology as a broad category compared with benign disease, whereas the subtype analyses examined specific malignant histopathological groups and were based on fewer studies and events. Consequently, the papillary and follicular carcinoma estimates had lower statistical power, wider confidence intervals, and greater potential instability. In addition, malignant histopathological subtypes may differ in tumor behavior, extent of surgery, frequency of central neck dissection, and pathological reporting practices, all of which may contribute to heterogeneity in IP risk. These findings are not entirely consistent with previous studies. For example, Sippel et al. and several subsequent studies reported lower rates of IP in papillary carcinoma compared with follicular carcinoma [30, 40, 51], whereas Turhan et al. observed a higher prevalence of IP among patients with papillary carcinoma [52]. Therefore, the subtype-specific findings should be considered exploratory and interpreted cautiously.

Furthermore, the subgroup analysis for Hashimoto’s thyroiditis demonstrated a non-significant trend toward higher odds of IP compared with other benign thyroid lesions. Additionally, the results showed that Graves’ disease and goiter decreased the likelihood of IP compared to other benign lesions, although none of these results were statistically significant. The study by Granata et al. demonstrated that the incidence of IP in Hashimoto’s thyroiditis was higher than in multinodular goiter [14]. Similarly, the study by Zhou et al. showed that the incidence of IP in Hashimoto’s thyroiditis was higher than in Graves’ disease [37]. The study by Turhan et al. revealed that among benign cases, the prevalence of IP was significantly lower in patients with multinodular goiter; however, in contrast to our findings, the difference observed in this study was statistically significant. Additionally, similar to our results, this study showed that there were no significant differences in IP occurrence among other benign lesions [52]. However, the study by Manouras et al. demonstrated that the incidence of IP in Hashimoto’s thyroiditis was lower than in goiter and Graves’ disease [31]. Although our results were not statistically significant for either benign or malignant lesions in terms of IP occurrence, the observed patterns may hold clinical relevance. Given the limited number of primary studies for subgroup analyses, the quality of the included studies, and the potential for bias, future research with larger sample sizes and more precise methodological designs may help clarify the relationship between the type of benign and malignant lesions and the risk of IP.

This systematic review and meta-analysis, including subgroup analyses based on thyroid pathology, provides additional insight into factors associated with IP. Nevertheless, several limitations should be considered. First, the protocol was not prospectively registered in PROSPERO or another public registry. Although the methods were predefined before data extraction and analysis, the absence of prospective registration may reduce transparency and allow potential selective methodological decisions. Second, substantial heterogeneity was observed across studies, as expected in observational thyroid surgery literature. Variations in study design, extent of thyroidectomy, central neck dissection practices, surgeon experience, institutional approaches, and pathological assessment of IP likely contributed to this heterogeneity. Although subgroup analyses and meta-regression partially explained some variability, considerable residual heterogeneity remained. Third, most included studies were retrospective observational investigations with variable methodological quality, which may have affected the precision and reliability of the pooled estimates. Differences in risk of bias across studies also contributed to effect size variability in the univariable meta-regression model. Fourth, important operative and surgeon-related confounders, including reoperative surgery, lymph node dissection, intraoperative technique, surgical difficulty, and surgeon experience, were inconsistently reported or adjusted for. Therefore, the independent effect of malignant thyroid pathology could not be fully separated from operative complexity. Finally, definitions and pathological reporting of IP were not fully standardized across studies. Although most studies relied on postoperative histopathological confirmation, variation in diagnostic criteria and institutional reporting practices may have reduced comparability and introduced misclassification bias. Accordingly, the pooled estimates should be interpreted cautiously as exploratory associative findings derived from heterogeneous observational data rather than evidence of causality.

Conclusion

Based on the findings of this study, malignant thyroid pathology was associated with a modest but statistically significant increase in the likelihood of IP compared with benign thyroid disease. However, the substantial residual heterogeneity across studies, observational design of the available evidence, and potential influence of operative and methodological confounding factors—including differences in surgical extent, central neck dissection practices, surgeon experience, and pathological reporting standards—limit the certainty and interpretability of the pooled estimates. Therefore, these findings should primarily be interpreted as exploratory associative observations rather than definitive evidence that malignant pathology is an independent causal risk factor. Malignancy may instead serve as a marker of more extensive or complex operative management, and further well-designed prospective studies with appropriate adjustment for operative variables are warranted to better clarify these associations.
