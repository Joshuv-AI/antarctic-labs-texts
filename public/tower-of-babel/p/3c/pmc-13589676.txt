Outcomes for Frail Dialysis Patients Not Referred for Transplantation: A Cohort Study
Authors: Joshua Goodwin, Jordan Thorne, Amanda Vinson, David Clark, Bryce Kiberd, Micheal Sun, Karthik Tennankore
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundFrailty is highly prevalent in kidney failure, but it is unclear whether frailty impacts health outcomes for those initiating dialysis who are not referred for transplantation. Our objective was to determine if frailty is associated with death or development of a permanent contraindication to transplantation among eligible non‐referred patients following dialysis initiation.MethodsWe analyzed all incident adult dialysis patients from January 2009 to December 2018 (last follow‐up December 2020) who lacked an identifiable absolute contraindication to transplant, but were not referred to our institution. Frailty was captured using the Clinical Frailty Scale (CFS) and categorized by severity. Time to death or development of a permanent contraindication to transplantation was analyzed using an adjusted Cox Proportional Hazards Regression model. Associations with the composite outcome were reported using hazard ratios (HR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI).ResultsA total of 401 patients were included, 14% had a CFS of 6–8; 90% of patients with CFS 6–8 either died or developed a permanent contraindication to transplant and none received a transplant during follow‐up. Among patients with CFS 6–8, 27% died or developed a permanent contraindication at 1 year; increasing to 81% by 5 years. After adjusting for patient characteristics, frailty was associated with the composite outcome (CFS 4–5: HR 1.71, 95% CI 1.17–2.21; CFS 6–8: HR 2.34, 95% CI 1.42–3.37).ConclusionsAmong patients not referred for transplantation, a higher frailty severity was associated with death or development of a contraindication, particularly after the first year following dialysis initiation. Future studies should explore whether alternative approaches to the timing of transplant referral in frail patients are associated with improved outcomes and focus on novel strategies to reduce frailty severity or better support frail patients who may otherwise be eligible for transplantation.

Introduction

Kidney transplant is associated with improved quality of life, longer survival, and lower cost, compared with dialysis [1, 2]. There is also evidence that kidney transplant offers increased life expectancy for patients older than 60 years, compared with dialysis [1]. However, not all patients are able to benefit from a transplant. Some patients with limited life expectancy (many of whom have contraindications that are established through national and international guidelines [3, 4]) would not be expected to survive the waiting period for a transplant and would be even less likely to survive to net benefit [5]. While patients with contraindications are often identified prior to or at the time of dialysis initiation, we previously showed that some non‐referred patients experience death or develop a contraindication soon after dialysis initiation [6].

Frailty is a state of increased vulnerability to poor health outcomes due to accumulated deficits across multiple domains. Frailty is common among patients with chronic kidney disease, particularly those receiving dialysis. A 2016 systematic review of frailty among CKD patients identified that 7% to 42.6% of pre‐dialysis populations and 14% to 73% of hemodialysis patients are frail [7, 8]. Frailty is also associated with decreased likelihood of receiving a kidney transplant [9], increased risk of delayed graft function [10], increased risk of post‐operative complications [11], and higher mortality after receiving a transplant [12]. Developing an understanding of the association between frailty and death/development of a contraindication to transplant has implications for care and resource planning. Frail patients who initiate dialysis without a contraindication to transplant may benefit from strategies to expedite transplantation (i.e., medical high‐priority status) or efforts aimed at optimizing access to transplantation (i.e., through use of organs from older or higher Kidney Donor Profile Index (KDPI) donors that might otherwise not have been utilized) [13]. Alternatively, above a threshold of severity, frailty may be a contraindication to transplant based on its association with poor health outcomes, particularly if the time to development of a poor outcome is shorter than the anticipated waiting time for kidney transplantation or the point of net benefit following transplantation [5].

Understanding the association between frailty at dialysis initiation and the probability of developing a contraindication, waitlist referral, waitlisting, and subsequent transplantation is a recognized priority area of improvement for kidney transplantation [14, 15]. Therefore, in a cohort of patients initiating chronic dialysis, our primary objective for this study was to determine if frailty is associated with death or development of a permanent contraindication to transplantation among patients who did not have a permanent contraindication to transplantation at the time of dialysis initiation. We also sought to describe survival times for patients across categories of frailty, to better ascertain whether they would survive anticipated waiting periods for transplantation. We hypothesized that patients with higher degrees of frailty at the time of dialysis initiation are less likely to receive a transplant and more likely to develop contraindications to kidney transplant compared to their non‐frail counterparts.

Methods

Study Design and Participants

We analyzed a cohort of all incident adult (greater than 18 years of age) chronic dialysis patients affiliated with the Nova Scotia Health (NSH) Central Zone Renal Program between 2009 and the end of 2018, with last follow‐up to the end of 2020. The NSH program follows a prevalent population of greater than 500 in‐center hemodialysis patients with an incident rate of 100 to 120 patients per year, from a catchment area of 700,000 to 750,000 patients. We chose to start the analysis in 2009, which corresponded to the year where our dialysis program began documenting clinical frailty scores for each patient initiating dialysis [7].

Prior to conducting the study, ethics approval was acquired from the NSHA Research Ethics Board (REB #1026025). Study data collected from the MyNephrology (dialysis electronic medical record) database was linked with data from our institution's Multi‐Organ Transplant Program database to capture demographics, comorbidities, laboratory values, and transplant referral information for this cohort [16].

Outcome Definitions

Patients were followed until death, loss to follow‐up, transfer to an outside facility, or transplantation. Permanent contraindications (including comorbid conditions) were identified through electronic chart review and based on the Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes and Canadian Society of Transplantation consensus guidelines on eligibility for kidney transplantation [3, 4]. These contraindications (Table S1) included active malignancy, severe heart disease, severe chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, severe pulmonary hypertension, chronic or recurrent infection, active gastrointestinal disease, dementia (based on objective documentation of cognitive testing with a Montreal Cognitive Assessment or Mini‐Mental State Examination), refusal of transplant, morbid obesity (BMI greater than 40 kg/m2), chronic wounds, and non‐adherence to nephrology care. Two reviewers independently reviewed patient diagnostic records to identify contraindications to transplantation. In the event of a discrepancy between reviewers' impressions of comorbidities, consensus was reached with the help of a third reviewer who was blinded to the opinions of the initial two reviewers.

Comorbidities that fit the definition of a permanent contraindication and identified prior to dialysis initiation were used to define exclusion criteria for our cohort of interest. Contraindications identified after dialysis initiation were outcomes of interest, and in all cases, the date of the identified contraindication was also documented. Reasons behind non‐referral were documented as follows: permanent contraindication, no contraindication identified, and perceived contraindication (in cases where the identified reason was potentially subjective, or not explicitly stated or identifiable).

Frailty was the primary exposure of interest for this study, defined using the Clinical Frailty Scale (CFS). For our primary analysis, frailty was categorized as “not frail” (CFS < 4), “vulnerable or mildly frail” (CFS 4–5), or “frail” (CFS 6–8). Frailty was captured and categorized in a similar fashion to prior studies involving this cohort [17]. Briefly, CFS scores were prospectively ascertained based on the clinician's judgment and informed by the provided information for each level of frailty [18]. For both inpatient and outpatient dialysis initiation, a copy of the CFS scoring tool including descriptors of each frailty level was provided to the primary nephrology practitioner, who evaluated CFS scores for each patient in the week preceding or day of dialysis. Physicians and nurse practitioners at our institution have been trained to use the CFS and are experienced with its implementation from prior analyses [7].

Our primary outcome of interest was time to development of a permanent contraindication or death, with follow‐up time censored at the date of transplantation, study end date (December 31, 2020), or loss to follow‐up. We also reported on time to referral following dialysis initiation for all categories of CFS.

Statistical Analysis

Descriptive statistics were reported as counts and percentages for categorical variables, means ± SDs for normally distributed continuous variables, and medians and interquartile ranges for non‐normally distributed continuous variables. Time to each outcome was defined from the day of dialysis initiation to each outcome of interest and was graphically displayed using the Kaplan–Meier product limit method. Time to each event was compared for each CFS score using a multivariable Cox survival analysis reported using adjusted hazard ratios (HR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI). Variables that were included in the model aside from CFS were those that have previously been shown to associate with either frailty or mortality and included basic demographic data (age [19], sex [20], body mass index [21], self‐reported race [22]), comorbidities [23] (dementia, cardiovascular disease, diabetes, hypertension, respiratory disease, malignancy, liver cirrhosis), cause of kidney failure [24] (diabetes, polycystic kidney disease, glomerulonephritis, other), inpatient status at time of dialysis start, dialysis modality [25] (hemodialysis or peritoneal dialysis), and baseline laboratory data [25] (albumin, hemoglobin, and eGFR).

For the primary analysis, transplant was treated as a censoring event. In a prespecified, secondary descriptive analysis, we reported the time to development of a contraindication/death for our cohort (using life table data from Kaplan–Meier survival curves) and compared this to potential estimated time to transplantation including the National median waiting time (3.5 years) [26]. For this analysis, we also reported on the proportion of patients that would have survived various thresholds of waiting time based on their categorical frailty score, including 6 months, 1 year, 2 years, 4 years, and 5 years. For all analyses, a p < 0.05 was the threshold for statistical significance. All statistical analyses were conducted using Stata/MP version [27].

Results

Baseline Characteristics

A total of 1092 incident dialysis patients were identified. As outlined Figure S1, once removing patients referred for transplant prior to dialysis start, those with a permanent contraindication to transplant, and those without a documented CFS, our final cohort of interest contained 401 patients. Patient characteristics in this cohort stratified by categorized CFS are outlined in Table 1. The mean age increased with each CFS category, ranging from 56.5 ± 15.5 to 70.7 ± 10.9 years. The proportion of female patients was comparable across CFS groups, ranging from 36% to 48%. The mean BMI was highest in the CFS 4–5 group (29.5 ± 9.3 kg/m2) and lowest in the CFS 6–8 group (28.9 ± 2.0 kg/m2). Cardiovascular disease and diabetes were prevalent across all groups, with the CFS 4–5 and CFS 6–8 groups showing higher rates of these conditions compared to the non‐frail group. Respiratory disease and malignancy were also more frequently observed in the higher CFS categories 4–5 and 6–8. Patients in the CFS 6–8 group were most likely to be admitted to hospital at the time of dialysis start. The cause of kidney disease was similar across all categories of CFS. Mean albumin levels trended lower with higher CFS, while mean hemoglobin and eGFR upon dialysis start were similar.

Referral and Transplant

A total of 89 patients were referred for transplant after dialysis initiation, of whom 61 (69%) had CFS scores of 1–3. Initial outcomes of interest for the final cohort are outlined in Figure 1. Overall, 31% and 9% of patients in CFS categories 1–3 and 4–5, respectively, received a transplant during study follow‐up. No patients with a CFS score of 6–8 received a transplant during follow‐up, and only one patient was referred (and later withdrawn). Median time to transplant was 1.7 years for patients with CFS 1–3, and 2.2 years for patients with CFS 4–5 (Figure S2). Patients with higher CFS scores were less likely to receive a transplant during follow‐up after multivariable adjustment (HR 0.45, 95% CI 0.29–0.68 for each 1‐point increase in CFS).

Outcomes

Death/development of a permanent contraindication to transplant occurred in 40%, 69% and 92% of patients with CFS 1–3, 4–5 and 6–8, respectively. Categories of transplant contraindications are outlined in Table S2. The most common contraindication in patients with higher CFS was severe cardiac disease, while active malignancy was the most common contraindication for those in CFS category 1–3. Kaplan–Meier survival curves are displayed in Figure 2. For those in the highest category of frailty, the probability of remaining free of the primary outcome (death/development of a contraindication) was 84% at 6 months, 41% at 3.5 years (median national wait time for kidney transplant), 32% at 4 years, and fell to 19% at 5 years after dialysis initiation. In contrast, 51% of patients with CFS 1–3 were alive without a contraindication to transplant at 5 years following dialysis initiation (Table S3).

Time to death/development of a permanent contraindication for patients without a contraindication to transplantation (CFS, clinical frailty scale).

Patients in the highest category of CFS at the time of dialysis initiation were at a significantly higher risk of death or development of a contraindication (CFS 4–5: unadjusted HR 1.93, 95% CI 1.43–2.61; CFS 6–8: unadjusted HR 2.85, 95% CI 1.97–4.11). This association was maintained even after adjusting for patient demographics, comorbidities, and dialysis modality, as demonstrated in Table 2 (adjusted CFS 4–5: HR 1.71, 95% CI 1.17–2.21; adjusted CFS 6–8: HR 2.34, 95% CI 1.42–3.37). Older age was also associated with death/development of a contraindication (HR 1.12 per year, 95% CI 1.21–1.34), as was being admitted to hospital for dialysis start (HR 1.41, 95% CI 1.09–1.99). Sensitivity analyses are found in Figure 3. When treating transplant as a competing event, the association between frailty severity and the composite outcome was maintained (Figure 3; CFS 4–5: HR 1.71, 95% CI 1.21–2.44; CFS 6–8: HR 2.42, 95% CI 1.51–3.85). Point estimates were comparable across each sensitivity analysis (Figure 3, Figures S3 and S4); however, for outcomes in the first year following dialysis initiation, confidence intervals crossed 1.

Discussion

In this cohort study, patients with higher frailty severity were at increased risk of development of a permanent contraindication to transplant or death after dialysis initiation. While reasons for contraindications varied, the majority were based on severe cardiac disease or the development of malignancy following dialysis initiation. Of those who received a kidney transplant, most had a lower frailty severity score and were transplanted within the first 4 years following dialysis initiation. In contrast, no patient with a CFS of 6–8 at dialysis initiation received a transplant during follow‐up.

This analysis suggests that the CFS can offer additional prognostic information on patient outcomes following dialysis initiation. When patients and their care teams are making decisions about transplantation referral, an individual's expected wait time is typically considered. This is especially important in patients of older age, with the understanding that the longer one waits for transplant, the more likely they may develop a contraindication to proceeding and that even those who receive a transplant may not survive to net benefit [5]. As a result, previous analyses have attempted to create prediction models that individualize estimated kidney transplant wait time. Jalbert and colleagues [28] developed a model to create personalized estimates of wait time to assist with patient counseling on refusing or accepting deceased donor offers. There have also been efforts to use machine learning to more precisely predict transplant wait time, with one study using panel reactive antibody result, blood type, and age to individualize time predictions [29]. Our work suggests that frailty may be an important prognostic consideration when evaluating the probability of receiving a transplant when faced with prolonged waiting time, acknowledging its strong association with death/development of a permanent contraindication.

More specifically in this study, higher CFS scores are associated with increased risk of developing a cardiac contraindication to transplantation. Frailty in adults with CKD has been shown to be associated with cardiovascular events and mortality [30]. The same is likely true for patients following dialysis initiation, acknowledging that cardiovascular disease remains the most common cause of death in patients with kidney failure [31]. Our analysis suggests that an individualized approach for pre‐transplant workup could also consider patient CFS. For example, recognizing the higher risk for developing a cardiac contraindication to transplant and the narrower window of opportunity, frail but otherwise eligible patients might be prioritized in local programs for expedited pre‐operative cardiac assessment. However, the potential benefit of an individualized approach remains an area for future study.

While frailty is associated with adverse outcomes after kidney transplantation [32], it can also change over time due to fluctuations in one's health and, in part, clinicians' subjective impressions [33]. Notably, the assessment of clinical frailty can be limited due to the complications of advanced CKD [33]. In kidney failure, frailty severity may worsen with progression of CKD; however, it may also transiently improve following dialysis initiation due to the resolution of uremic symptoms. Furthermore, frailty has also been shown to improve post‐transplantation for some patients [15]. While a single assessment of frailty at the time of dialysis initiation does not capture changes over time, the lack of patients proceeding to transplant for those with CFS scores of 6–8 in our study suggests that most patients, particularly those with CFS scores of 6–8, did not experience an improvement in frailty severity following dialysis initiation.

How might one utilize the findings of this study to approach frail patients without an apparent contraindication to transplant who are not referred? We previously showed that existing contraindications to transplant are generally those conditions where the probability of waitlist survival is less than 50%, noting that the probability of reaching net benefit is even lower [5]. In the current study, only 41% of severely frail patients survived the median national waiting time of 3.5 years, and this fell to 19% at 5 years. Thus, one could surmise that patients with CFS scores of 6 or greater who are anticipated to wait prolonged periods of time would not benefit from a transplant workup given the chance of success is very low. This is further supported by the fact that waitlisting patients with little chance of success is burdensome to patients [34], prolonged workups may lead to patient despair, and committing extensive resources where there may be futility is costly for the health care system [35].

We also identified that 73% of patients in the highest category of frailty were free of the composite outcome at 1 year after dialysis initiation. Further research focusing on this “complication‐free window” would be of value. Potential areas that would benefit from further study would include an evaluation of the benefits of an expedited transplant workup or preemptive transplantation prior to dialysis initiation (particularly where waiting times are anticipated to be short). Identifying potential windows of opportunity for transplantation is crucial, especially since dialysis can accelerate the development of some transplant contraindications, for example, vascular calcification [36].

Given a short interval of time where frail patients may be eligible for kidney transplantation, there are other areas that may be of value for future study, including the use of higher‐risk donor allografts to avoid prolonged waiting times and development of a contraindication. The allocation of high‐KDPI kidneys to preemptive transplant recipients, for example, has been demonstrated to have comparable outcomes to non‐preemptive lower‐KDPI kidney transplants [37]. This has led to allocation strategies that prioritize higher‐KDPI kidneys for preemptive kidney transplantation in elderly patients [38]. Further research is also required on the best approach to kidney replacement therapy for patients who are severely frail. In our analysis, these patients were highly unlikely to proceed to kidney transplant and died more quickly after starting dialysis. An early/preemptive approach to referral and kidney transplantation in these patients may not prevent rapid decline post‐kidney failure. Rather, frailty screening in the pre‐dialysis CKD period with targeted frailty reduction strategies might help some more highly frail patients become good surgical candidates. Patients with adequately improved frailty could then be considered for referral for transplant, perhaps even before dialysis onset. Whereas frailty that is predominantly a result of accumulated advanced chronic diseases is unlikely to be reversible, other aspects of the frailty syndrome, such as muscle weakness, are being investigated as potentially reversible [39]. There is some evidence that high‐intensity resistance exercise can improve frailty, but studies have shown mixed results overall among a variety of intervention strategies in reversing frailty [39]. A trial of “prehabilitation” could help distinguish reversible frailty from irreversible frailty before further consideration of transplant [40].

Our analysis had several strengths. We included two independent assessors to confirm the presence of a contraindication to transplant, improving the accuracy of our classification. Frailty was collected in a prospective fashion, minimizing the risk of misclassification bias. Finally, we demonstrated that our findings were robust across a variety of sensitivity analyses. There were some limitations to consider. Documentation of contraindications was reliant on provider or chart documentation, and both relative and absolute contraindications, for example, are not always explicitly captured in clinical documentation. While our center is well‐versed in capturing frailty using the CFS, there is the possibility of subjectivity in ascertainment of frailty scores and the results of our study may not be generalizable to other centers. In this study, we only had a one‐time measurement of frailty at dialysis initiation, and thus did not have follow‐up scores to better understand the trajectory of frailty following dialysis initiation for those without a contraindication. In addition, patients pre‐emptively referred for transplant prior to dialysis start were excluded in this analysis. This group of patients is almost certainly less frail than the cohort included in our analysis, limiting the applicability of our analysis to a pre‐emptive population. A high CFS may bias providers to not refer patients for transplant, also potentially limiting our study reliability. However, in our center, patients are followed and can be referred by a number of different providers who would be blinded to the initial CFS assessment.

Conclusion

In conclusion, we identified that higher CFS scores were associated with an increased risk of developing a contraindication for kidney transplantation or death in patients who were not already referred for and did not have a contraindication to transplantation at the time of dialysis initiation. Patient frailty may better inform individualized discussions on the timing of transplant referral and potential benefits in the context of estimated wait times. Crucially, this work reinforces the need for further research into a targeted approach to transplant evaluation in patients with more severe frailty and kidney failure.
