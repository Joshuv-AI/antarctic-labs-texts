Long-Term Trends of Distal Radius Fractures in Adults Aged ≥ 50 Years in Korea Using Nationwide Claims Database (2006–2022)
Authors: Jae-Hwi Nho, Seong-Jun Kim, Tae-Young Kim, Kyungjun Lim, Jaiyong Kim, Jihye Kim, Hoyeon Jang, Yong-Chan Ha
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis study aimed to evaluate the long-term trends in the incidence, mortality, and subsequent fractures following distal radius fractures among South Koreans aged ≥ 50 years based on a nationwide claims database from 2006 to 2022.MethodsUsing the National Health Insurance database, we identified individuals aged ≥ 50 years who received treatment for distal radius fractures from 2006 to 2022. Distal radius fractures were defined using diagnostic codes (S525 and S526) and treatment codes (N1601, N1611, N1603, N1613, N0996, N0998, N0983, N0643). Annual incidence rates, 1-year mortality rates, and rates of subsequent fractures were estimated after adjustment for age and sex.ResultsOver the 16-year study period, the total number of distal radius fractures increased steadily (from 51,152 cases in 2006 to 100,729 in 2022). The incidence rate increased slightly from 426 per 100,000 in 2006 to 442 per 100,000 in 2022. The 1-year mortality rates after a distal radius fracture declined from 2.73% in 2006 to 1.79% in 2021 (incidence rate ratio per year, 0.985; P < 0.001), representing an annual decrease of 1.52%. The 1-year mortality rate in 2021 for men (3.41%) was higher than that for women (1.32%) (incidence rate ratio, 2.740; P < 0.001). The rate of subsequent distal radius fractures within 1 year following a distal radius fracture rose from 0.82% in 2007 to 7.44% in 2021 (annual increase of 17.21% [incidence rate ratio, 1.172; 95% confidence interval, 1.152–1.192; P < 0.001]). The rate of subsequent osteoporotic fractures within 1 year after a distal radius fracture also increased from 2.33% in 2007 to 8.96% in 2021, with an annual increase of 10.81% (incidence rate ratio, 1.108; P < 0.001).ConclusionThe long-term trends in this study—an increased number and incidence rate but decreased mortality after distal radius fractures in South Korea—highlight the need to initiate preventive strategies for subsequent osteoporotic fractures to reduce the associated socioeconomic burden.Keywords: Incidence, Mortality, Osteoporotic Fractures, Trends, Wrist Fractures

INTRODUCTION

Osteoporosis and its associated fractures represent a growing health concern among aging populations.1,2,3 These fractures not only diminish the overall quality of life but also contribute substantially to hospitalization rates and healthcare costs among older individuals.4 Previous studies have extensively analyzed the incidence, morbidity, and mortality associated with osteoporotic fractures of the hip and spine.5,6,7,8,9

Distal radius fractures constitute the second most common type of fragility fracture,10,11,12 after spinal fractures, and precede humerus and hip fractures. Distal radius fractures are a relevant concern in middle-aged women and men13 and are widely recognized as sentinel events that indicate an elevated risk of subsequent osteoporotic fractures.14,15 A comprehensive understanding of the epidemiology, mortality, and risk of subsequent fractures following distal radius fractures is crucial for developing preventive strategies against subsequent osteoporotic fractures.

Our previous nationwide study investigated the incidence and mortality following distal radius fractures among Koreans aged ≥ 50 years using National Health Insurance Service (NHIS) data from 2008 to 2012.16 That analysis demonstrated an increase in distal radius fracture incidence from 367.4 per 100,000 in 2008 to 461.5 per 100,000 in 2012 and a decline in mortality from 2.0% (968/48,145) in 2008 to 1.4% (978/72,263) in 2012. However, comprehensive assessments of long-term trends in distal radius fractures in South Korea remain limited. Long-term nationwide data are rare and highly valuable compared with short-term nationwide data, as they not only allow more accurate prediction of future trends but also provide reliable comparisons with international reports using long-term datasets. We recently extended the study period using NHIS data from 2006 to 2022.

This study aimed to evaluate the long-term trends in distal radius fractures with respect to the incidence, mortality rates, and subsequent fractures following distal radius fractures in patients aged ≥ 50 years in South Korea, utilizing this extensive 16-year nationwide database.

METHODS

Data source and identification of distal radius fractures

We extracted data from the Korean NHIS claims database to identify patients aged ≥ 50 years who sustained distal radius fractures between 2006 and 2022. The NHIS is a wage-based universal insurance program covering nearly the entire South Korean population (approximately 50 million people) who use medical services, excluding services related to cosmetic procedures and injuries from traffic or industrial accidents.7,16,17,18 This form of data is widely used in several epidemiological studies and is particularly relevant to osteoporosis research in South Korea because high-energy trauma fractures are spontaneously excluded, as traffic and industrial accidents are covered by different insurance systems.

Distal radius fractures are classified using the 10th revision of the International Classification of Disease Diagnostic Codes (ICD-10), also known as ICD-10, to diagnostic codes S525 (fracture of lower end of radius) and S526 (fracture of lower end of both ulna and radius). Procedures related to these diagnosis include: N1601 (simple radius open reduction of fractured extremity), N1611 (complex radius open reduction of fractured extremity), N1603 (simple radius and ulnar open reduction of fractured extremity), N1613 (complex radius and ulnar open reduction of fractured extremity), N0996 (closed pinning [radius], open reduction of fractured extremity), N0998 (closed pinning [radius and ulnar], open reduction of fractured extremity), N0983 (external fixation [forearm bone]), N0643 (closed reduction of fractured extremity [forearm bone]), T6020 (long arm cast), T6030 (short arm cast), T6151 (long arm splint), T6152 (short arm splint).19 The operational definition of a first distal radius fracture required either 1) hospitalization with one of the above diagnostic codes within 1 month of the associated procedures, or 2) an emergency department visit accompanied by the above procedure codes. This operational definition has been previously validated with a high specificity, accuracy, and positive predictive value (98.6%, 93.8%, and 98.2%, respectively).19

Study design

The annual number and incidence rates of distal radius fractures from 2006 to 2022 were analyzed. Incidence rates were calculated using the NHIS database and refined by episodes of care. The values of each indicator were directly standardized based on the national population structure by age and sex. Incidence rates per 100,000 individuals and annual distal radius fracture events were compared by age and sex.

Mortality rates within 1 year after distal radius fractures were identified from 2006 to 2021, allowing for a 1-year follow-up to evaluate mortality rates. The NHIS database was linked to national mortality records from the Korean National Statistical Office to determine the survival of individuals 12 months following distal radius fractures. One-year mortality rates of distal radius fractures during the study period were compared by age and sex and presented in 10-year intervals.

We also analyzed subsequent fractures occurring within 1 year after the first distal radius fracture between 2006 and 2022. To avoid counting duplicates or follow-up claims, a 6-month washout period after the first fracture was applied. A 1-year follow-up period was required to assess subsequent fracture rates, therefore, analyses were conducted between 2007 and 2021. Subsequent fractures were classified as follows: 1) recurrent fractures occurring at the distal radius, and 2) subsequent fractures occurring at any osteoporosis-related site among the 6 major anatomical locations commonly affected in older adults (hip, spine, distal radius, humerus, ankle, and pelvis).20,21,22,23

Statistical analysis

Incidence and mortality rates were determined using the NHIS database, focusing on distinct “episodes of care,” defined as continuous periods of healthcare utilization within a 35-day period starting from each medical claim date. The 35-day period was selected to capture continuous care related to a single distal radius fracture and to prevent counting follow-up visits or treatments as separate fracture cases. This threshold was established based on patterns of care observed in the national claims data, reflecting the typical distal radius fracture management in South Korea. To account for potential intermittency in care, consecutive episodes separated by fewer than 182 days were combined into single, extended episodes.22 Standardization of incidence estimates was applied to adjust for potential biases due to age and sex distribution. This involved aligning the observed data of the 2020 South Korean population as the reference, classified by sex and 10-year age groups.

Negative Binomial regression with a log-link function and offset terms for the at-risk population was used to analyze mortality and subsequent fracture rates rather than Poisson regression, due to overdispersion in the data. The independent effect of sex was evaluated in this model, with calendar year included as a covariate. Subsequent fracture rates were analyzed using univariable negative binomial regression with calendar year. All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.5.1; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Ethics statement

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of Seoul Bumin Hospital and the requirement for informed consent was waived (IRB No. BMH 2023–04-009).

RESULTS

Incidence of distal radius fractures

Over the 16-year study period, the total number of distal radius fractures increased steadily, rising from 51,152 cases in 2006 to100,729 in 2022 (Fig. 1A). The incidence rate per 100,000 persons also increased from 426 in 2006 to 442 in 2022 (Fig. 1B). Although the annual number of distal radius fractures consistently increased, the incidence rate increased slowly over the past 16 years, with fluctuations.

Regarding the sex-specific distribution of distal radius fractures between 2006 and 2022, the incidence rates were 164 per 100,000 for men and 633 per 100,000 for women in 2006, and 178 per 100,000 for men and 689 per 100,000 for women in 2022 (Fig. 1B). The incidence rates of distal radius fractures slightly rose by 9% for both men and women during the study period. Age-specific analyses showed that the incidence of distal radius fractures consistently increased with advancing age throughout the study period (Fig. 1C).

The mean age at which distal radius fractures occur consistently increased from 65.63 years in 2006 to 67.93 years in 2022 (Fig. 2). The average age increased by 2.52 years in men and by 2.29 years in women during the study period. The increase in the mean age of patients with distal radius fractures was more pronounced in men than in women.

Secular trends in mean age at distal radius fracture in adults aged ≥ 50 years.

Mortality after distal radius fractures

The 1-year mortality rate following distal radius fractures decreased from 2.73% in 2006 to 1.79% in 2021. Mortality rates showed a significant annual decline (incidence rate ratio [IRR] per year, 0.985; 95% confidence interval [CI], 0.976–0.994; P < 0.001), representing a 1.52% annual reduction. Regarding sex-specific distribution, the 1-year mortality rate following distal radius fractures was 4.57% for men and 2.09% for women in 2006, and 3.41% for men and 1.32% for women in 2021 (Fig. 3). Men had higher mortality rates than women (IRR, 2.740; 95% CI, 2.538–2.958; P < 0.001).

Secular trends in mortality rates after distal radius fractures in adults ≥ 50 years.

Subsequent fractures after distal radius fractures

The 1-year subsequent fracture rate at the distal radius following the first distal radius fracture was 0.82% in 2007 and consistently increased to 7.44% in 2021, with an annual increase of 17.21% (IRR, 1.172; 95% CI, 1.152–1.192; P < 0.001). The 1-year subsequent fracture rate at any osteoporotic fracture site following the first distal radius fracture also increased from 2.33% in 2007 to 8.96% in 2021, with an annual increase of 10.81% (IRR, 1.108; 95% CI, 1.099–1.118; P < 0.001). The 1-year subsequent fracture rates following the first distal radius fracture were consistently higher at any osteoporotic fracture site than at the distal radius alone (Fig. 4).

DISCUSSION

This study is the first to evaluate long-term trends in the incidence and mortality of distal radius fractures over a 16-year period in South Korea. We demonstrated that the incidence rate of distal radius fractures increased slowly over the past 16 years, with fluctuations (from 426 per 100,000 in 2006 to 442 per 100,000 in 2022), although the total number of distal radius fractures nearly doubled in the same period (from 51,152 in 2006 to 100,729 in 2022). However, the 1-year mortality rate following distal radius fractures continuously declined from 2.73% in 2006 to 1.79% in 2021.

The gradual increase in distal radius fracture incidence observed in both sexes aligns with findings from other epidemiologic studies.24,25,26,27,28,29 A study from Taiwan on the incidence of distal radius fractures from 2000 to 2007 reported increasing trends in both sexes.28 In developed countries where population aging is slow or stagnant, such as Japan, Germany, and the Netherlands, distal radius fracture incidence tends to stagnate or decrease in recent decades.24,25,27,29,30 Variations in distal radius fracture incidence among nations might be related to study designs such as population-based or claims-based data, the proportion of the older population, age differences in inclusion criteria, and study periods. Additionally, the incidence of subsequent fractures after the first distal radius fracture consistently increased over the long term period of this study, with an annual increase of 17.21% for distal radius fractures and 10.81% for subsequent osteoporotic fractures at any site. These reflect the rapidly aging demographic structure in South Korea, where the proportion of the older population continues to grow at one of the highest rates worldwide (Table 1).31

The 1-year mortality rate following distal radius fractures has been reported to range from 2.8% to 6% in older populations.13,32 The reasons for the differences in 1-year mortality observed worldwide may be related to the proportion of the older population, the healthcare system, and preventive programs for osteoporotic fractures. However, few studies have explored long-term trends in mortality rates after distal radius fractures. In this study, the annual mortality rate declined from 2.73% in 2006 to 1.79% in 2021, representing an annual reduction of 1.52%. This study demonstrated that although the distal radius fracture incidence is increasing, the mortality rate shows a decreasing trend. Several factors, including an enhanced healthcare system and preventive programs against osteoporotic fractures, may explain this improvement. Recently, the introduction of new strong therapeutic agents and updated clinical practice guidelines for high-risk osteoporotic patients may also have contributed to improved survival after distal radius fractures. Furthermore, consistent with previous studies, men demonstrated higher mortality than women. For example, Øyen et al.33 reported a 1-year mortality rate of 3.4% in both sexes; however, the 1-year mortality rate in men (5.4%) was 1.9 times higher than that in women (2.9%).

In this study, the increasing trend of distal radius fractures in the older population indicated that additional subsequent fractures are inevitable. Epidemiological studies on postmenopausal women have shown that distal radius fractures are associated with a 1.4- to 4.4-fold greater risk of subsequent osteoporotic fractures. Therefore, these cascades of osteoporotic fractures necessitate effective secondary fracture prevention strategies. For example, a fracture liaison service is a representative program to prevent subsequent fractures and should be introduced and established to minimize osteoporosis-related complications, such as subsequent fractures, increasing mortality, and socioeconomic burdens. In Korea, such a system can be implemented through the existing NHIS framework, with coordinated care among orthopedic surgeons, primary care physicians, and rehabilitation specialists to ensure post-fracture evaluation and osteoporosis management.

Despite its strengths, this study has some limitations. First, the incidence and mortality rates may be underrepresented owing to patients with asymptomatic or mild osteoporotic fractures who did not visit the hospital and thus were not captured in our database. Second, although the operational definition excluded pathologic fractures that may affect mortality, detecting fractures associated with the underlying disease was not possible. Additionally, comorbidities or medication histories affecting mortality could not be evaluated because such information was unavailable in the claims dataset. Third, the secular trends of subsequent fractures observed in this study may not be generalizable to other countries. Further multinational comparisons are needed to confirm external validity. Finally, the diagnoses of distal radius fractures were made using diagnostic and procedural codes. However, the operational definitions of distal radius fractures used in this study were previously validated for reliability and accuracy. The validation study reported a specificity of 98.6%, an accuracy of 93.8%, and a positive predictive value of 98.2%.19

In conclusion, this nationwide claims database study highlights a rising trend in distal radius fractures in South Korea, coinciding with a decline in associated mortality rates over a recent long-term period. Given the increasing incidence of distal radius fractures, a heightened risk of concurrent subsequent fractures is expected at major osteoporotic fracture sites and at the distal radius. Consequently, implementing a public health policy aimed at preventing distal radius fractures is a prudent approach to reduce the burden of subsequent osteoporotic fractures, alleviate economic strain, and improve overall public health.
