Dosimetric predictors of erectile dysfunction in Moroccan men treated with volumetric modulated arc therapy for prostate cancer
Authors: Ihsane Skitioui, Karima Nouni, Salma Ichou, Rachida Laraichi, Amine Lachgar, Hanan Elkacemi, Tayeb Kebdani, Khalid Hassouni
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundErectile dysfunction (ED) is a frequent and impactful side effect of prostate cancer radiotherapy. Data on the relationship between radiation dose to the penile bulb (PB) and ED severity in North African populations remain limited. This study aimed to assess ED prevalence and severity in Moroccan men treated with volumetric modulated arc therapy (VMAT) and identify associated clinical and dosimetric predictors.Materials and methodsA retrospective analysis was performed on 52 Moroccan patients treated with VMAT for localized or locally advanced prostate cancer (2021–2024). Erectile function was evaluated using the Sexual Health Inventory for Men (SHIM) score. Analyzed dosimetric variables included PB mean dose, PB V30, PB V50, PB V60, and doses to 25–90% of the PB volume (PB D25, PB D50, PB D75, PB D90). Statistical methods included ANOVA, Pearson correlation, ROC curve analysis, and multivariate logistic regression.ResultsModerate to severe ED was found in 65.4% of patients. Strongest correlations with SHIM score were seen for PB D25 (r = −0.47), PB mean dose (r = −0.45), PB V30 (r = −0.41), and PB D50 (r = −0.41). ROC analysis identified PB V30 > 64.9% and PB D50 > 38.7 Gy as optimal cutoffs for predicting ED. ISUP grade was significantly associated with ED severity (p = 0.007), while TNM stage and D’Amico classification were not. No significant association was observed with hypertension, diabetes, or smoking.ConclusionsThis study highlights the importance of PB dosimetry in predicting post-radiotherapy ED. PB V30 and PB D50 emerged as reliable predictors, underscoring the need to integrate PB constraints in VMAT planning to preserve erectile function in prostate cancer survivors.

Introduction

Prostate cancer is one of the most common malignancies in men and represents a major public health concern. The introduction of advanced radiotherapy techniques such as volumetric modulated arc therapy (VMAT) has significantly improved tumor control while reducing toxicity to surrounding tissues. However, erectile dysfunction (ED) remains a prevalent and distressing side effect of prostate cancer radiotherapy, negatively impacting long-term quality of life.

Radiation-induced ED is multifactorial, involving vascular, neural, and structural damage to erectile tissues. Key structures at risk include the penile bulb (PB), neurovascular bundles, internal pudendal arteries, and corpora cavernosa. Endothelial injury can cause fibrosis and reduced blood flow, while radiation-induced neuropathy and impaired nitric oxide signaling further exacerbate dysfunction. These effects are dose-dependent, with higher PB radiation exposure correlating with increased ED risk.

Although several studies in Western populations have demonstrated a clear dose-response relationship, data in North African populations remain limited. Genetic, lifestyle, and healthcare differences may influence baseline erectile function and treatment response. This study aims to assess the prevalence and severity of ED in Moroccan men treated with VMAT and identify clinical and dosimetric predictors, particularly PB exposure, to support individualized treatment planning and functional outcome preservation.

Materials and methods

This retrospective single-center study was conducted at the Radiotherapy Department of the National Institute of Oncology in Rabat, Morocco. It included Moroccan men with localized or locally advanced prostate cancer treated with VMAT between 2021 and 2024. Radiation dose prescriptions varied according to disease stage: most patients received 76 Gy to the prostate, with or without additional dose to the seminal vesicles. Androgen deprivation therapy (ADT) was administered to all except one low-risk patient. Clinical data included age, prostate-specific antigen (PSA) level, prostate volume, tumor–node–metastasis (TNM) stage, Gleason score, International Society of Urological Pathology (ISUP) grade, D’Amico risk group, andcomorbidities(hypertension, diabetes, smoking). Dosimetric analysis focused on the PB, including mean dose (Dmean), volumes receiving 30–70 Gy (V30–V70), and doses to 25%, 50%, 75%, and 90% of the PB volume (D25–D90), with contours validated by two radiation oncologists.

Post-treatment erectile function was assessed using the Sexual Health Inventory for Men (SHIM) questionnaire [1]. All SHIM questionnaires were administered after completion of radiotherapy during follow-up consultations. Statistical analysis was performed using JASP and Jamovi. Pearson correlation and one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Welch’s correction assessed associations between dosimetric variables and SHIM scores. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis identified predictive dose thresholds. Logistic regression was used to evaluate clinical predictors of moderate to severe ED. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

The study cohort included 52 Moroccan men treated with normofractionated radiotherapy using VMAT for prostate cancer. The results are reported in Table 1. The median age was 69 years (range: 56–91) and 96.15% of patients were married, providing context regarding sexual activity. Most patients were classified as having T3 (61.5%) or T2 (32.7%) disease. Node-negative disease (N0) was found in 71.2% of patients, whereas 28.8% were N1. Based on the TNM stage grouping, 44.2% were classified as stage II, 30.8% as stage III, and 25.0% as stage I. Regarding tumor aggressiveness, 51.9% had a Gleason score greater than 7, 40.4% had a score of 7, and only 7.7% had a score below 7. According to the ISUP grading system, the majority of patients were in group 4 (38.5%) or group 2 (25.0%), with 19.2% in group 3, 11.5% in group 5, and 5.8% in group 1. The D’Amico risk stratification revealed that 92.3% of patients were high risk, while only 5.8% were intermediate risk and 1.9% low risk. Comorbidities included hypertension (28.8%), diabetes (7.7%), ischemic heart disease (3.8%), and asthma (3.8%), with 55.8% having no significant comorbidity. A history of smoking was noted in 28.8% of patients. In terms of biological and anatomical parameters, the median PSA level was 25.0 ng/mL (range: 5.97–293.00), and the median prostate volume was 45.5 mL (range: 8.71–119.00), confirming the presence of a wide variability in baseline disease burden among patients.

Erectile function was assessed using the SHIM questionnaire. The mean SHIM score across the cohort was 12.0 ± 3.88, with scores ranging from 6 to 21. The median interval between radiotherapy completion and SHIM assessment was 6.8 months (range: 2–21 months). At the time of SHIM assessment, patients had received a median of 6–10 months of ADT. Based on validated severity categories, 7.7% of patients were classified as having mild ED, 26.9% as mild to moderate, 53.8% as moderate, and 11.5% as severe. Overall, 65.4% of patients experienced moderate to severe ED, underscoring the substantial impact of prostate cancer treatment on post-therapy sexual quality of life.

A Pearson correlation analysis between the SHIM score and various clinical and dosimetric variables was performed (Fig. 1). Correlation analysis revealed a strong inverse relationship between erectile function and PB dosimetry. The SHIM score was most negatively correlated with PB D25 (r = −0.47), followed by the mean PB dose (r = −0.45), PB V30 (r = −0.41), and PB D50 (r = −0.41). Additional moderate correlations were observed with PB V50, PB D75, PB V60, and PB D90 (r ranging from −0.35 to −0.39). Among clinical factors, age showed a modest negative correlation with SHIM score (r = −0.27), whereas PSA level, Gleason score, ISUP grade, and prostate volume showed weak or no meaningful association. These findings suggest that higher radiation exposure to the PB is strongly associated with more severe post-treatment ED.

A multivariate logistic regression analysis was performed to investigate the association between clinical factors and the risk of developing moderate to severe ED in patients treated with VMAT. The model included age, baseline PSA level, diabetes, and smoking status. None of the variables were significantly associated with ED severity. Age [odds ratio (OR) = 1.01, 95% confidence interval (CI): 0.96–1.06, p = 0.748], PSA level (OR = 1.00, 95% CI: 0.99–1.01, p = 0.465), diabetes (OR = 2.20, 95% CI: 0.20–23.87, p = 0.522), and smoking (OR = 0.47, 95% CI: 0.13–1.65, p = 0.245) did not show statistically significant correlations. The model’s overall explanatory power was low (pseudo R2 = 0.034). These results indicate that, in this cohort, traditional clinical variables such as age, comorbidities, and PSA levels do not appear to independently predict the development of radiation-induced ED. A forest plot illustrating the ORs and their 95% CIs confirmed that all CIs crossed the null value (OR = 1) (Fig. 2), reinforcing the lack of significance.

In the current cohort, a progressive increase in ED severity was observed across higher tumor risk classifications (Tab. 2). Regarding the ISUP group, patients with ISUP 1 and 5 were predominantly represented in the severe ED group. Specifically, 46.4% of patients with ISUP 4 and 66.7% of those with ISUP 5 had severe ED. In contrast, lower ISUP scores were more distributed across mild to moderate categories, with 40% of ISUP 2 patients and 25% of ISUP 3 patients experiencing only mild ED. This distribution demonstrated a statistically significant correlation between ISUP grade and ED severity (p = 0.007). For the TNM stage, the majority of patients with stage III disease experienced moderate or severe ED: 50% had moderate ED and 21.4% had severe ED. Conversely, stage I patients were more evenly distributed, with 25% presenting with mild ED and 25% with moderate ED. Although a trend toward more severe ED with higher TNM stage was apparent, the association did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.161). When classified according to the D’Amico risk group, a high proportion of patients with high-risk disease experienced moderate to severe ED. Specifically, 96.4% of patients classified as high-risk presented with moderate ED, and 83.3% of those in this group experienced severe ED. Meanwhile, all patients with mild or mild-to-moderate ED were in the intermediate-risk group. Although these trends suggest a relationship between oncologic risk and ED severity, the association was not statistically significant (p = 0.086).

Robust one-way ANOVA was used to compare PB dosimetric parameters across ED severity groups (Tab. 3). Statistically significant differences were found for several key parameters. The mean dose to the PB (Dmean) was significantly higher in patients with more severe ED (p = 0.0006). Likewise, the volumes of the PB receiving 30 Gy (PB V30, p = 0.0012), 50 Gy (PB V50, p < 0.0001), 60 Gy (PB V60, p < 0.0001), and 70 Gy (PB V70, p = 0.0257) showed strong associations with ED severity. In addition, the minimum doses received by 25% (D25, p = 0.0066) and 50% (D50, p < 0.0001) of the PB volume were significantly higher in patients with severe dysfunction. In contrast, D75 (p = 0.0784) and D90 (p = 0.1056) did not reach statistical significance. These findings suggest that radiation exposure to central and moderately irradiated regions of the PB plays a greater role in post-treatment ED than lower-dose peripheral exposure.

Receiver operating characteristic analysis was performed to determine the most predictive dosimetric parameters for moderate to severe ED (Fig. 3 and 4). Among the tested variables, PB V30 demonstrated the highest predictive accuracy with an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.711 (CI 95%: 0.55–0.85), and an optimal cut-off of 64.9%, yielding a sensitivity of 65% and a specificity of 72%. Other significant predictors included the mean dose to the PB, with an AUC of 0.703 (CI 95%: 0.54–0.86) and an optimal threshold of 40.74 Gy (sensitivity 62%, specificity 78%), and PB V50 with a threshold of 38.4% (AUC 0.700, CI 95%: 0.55–0.84). These findings suggest that intermediate-range dose volumes to the PB, particularly PB V30 > 64.9%, may serve as a useful dosimetric parameter.

Discussion

Erectile dysfunction is a significant quality-of-life concern for men treated for prostate cancer, making it essential to identify the anatomical structures involved. Previous studies have indicated that radiation dose to the PB is linked to radiotherapy-induced ED [2, 3]. Our study is the first to investigate the dosimetric predictors of ED in Moroccan patients treated with VMAT, revealing a significant association between PB dose exposure and ED severity. These findings align with prior research conducted in Western populations but also highlight potential variations in dosimetric thresholds.

The penis includes several structures essential to sexual function, notably the corpora cavernosa, crura, corpus spongiosum, and PB. Erection depends on arterial inflow, reduced venous outflow, and neural control via the pudendal nerves. Radiation-induced ED may result from impaired veno-occlusive function, particularly affecting the corpora cavernosa. Consequently, several studies have focused on the impact of radiation dose to proximal penile structures, especially the PB, to better understand and prevent treatment-related impotence [4].

Several studies have assessed the prevalence of ED following radiotherapy in prostate cancer patients, with reported rates varying significantly based on treatment modalities, patient populations, and evaluation timing. Incidence rates of moderate to severe ED after radiotherapy have ranged from 40% to over 70% in Western cohorts. For instance, Incrocci et al. reported a prevalence of 64% for ED two years post-radiotherapy using the IIEF questionnaire in a Dutch population [5]. Similarly, the Radiation Therapy Oncology Group (RTOG) 9406 trial observed that up to 68% of men treated with dose-escalated 3D conformal radiotherapy (3DCRT) experienced significant decline in sexual function [6].

Our findings of 65.4% moderate to severe ED are higher than most Western reports, which may reflect differences in baseline sexual health, cultural underreporting, comorbidities, or access to sexual rehabilitation.

Fisch et al. [7] were the first to establish a dose-volume relationship between radiation exposure to the PB and the onset of ED in a retrospective study of 21 patients. Their findings demonstrated that men who received PB D70% < 40 Gy were able to maintain erectile function, whereas higher doses were strongly associated with ED. Subsequent studies using 3DCRT confirmed this correlation, with doses exceeding 50 Gy linked to a twofold increase in ED risk in the RTOG 9406 trial [8]. Additionally, the Medical Research Council (MRC) RT01 trial found a strong association between PB D90% and post-treatment ED [3]. These findings prompted the Quantitative Analyses of Normal Tissue Effects in the Clinic (QUANTEC) review, which recommended limiting the mean PB dose to 50 Gy to mitigate the risk of ED [4, 9].

With the advent of intensity-modulated radiotherapy (IMRT), newer studies have further refined PB dose constraints. Cozzarini et al. [10] demonstrated a significant association between maximum PB dose [maximum equivalent dose in 2 Gy fractions (DmaxEQD2) ≥ 74.2 Gy] and ED at one year post-treatment, with an OR of 4.1 (p = 0.022). Other IMRT studies suggested that preserving erectile function was achievable in up to 90% of men when the mean PB dose was kept below 20 Gy [11, 12], advocating for even stricter dose constraints.

Recent data from Rasmusson et al. [13] in the HYPO-RT-PC trial investigated the impact of dose to the penile base structures (crura and PB) in patients receiving hypofractionated radiotherapy. Their findings revealed relatively low mean PB doses (24.5 GyEQD2 in normofractionated regimens and 18.7 GyEQD2 in hypofractionated schedules) [9]. They also found a strong correlation between PB D2% and ED in younger patients (< 65 years old) and proposed dose constraints of PB D2% < 50 GyEQD2 and PB mean dose < 20 GyEQD2 for prostate cancer treatment planning.

The study by Yıldırım et al. [14] supports the link between PB dose and post-radiotherapy ED. Similar to our findings, they reported a significant association between ED severity and PB mean dose, identifying a threshold of > 37.8 Gy, which closely aligns with our > 40.74 Gy cutoff. Their results also emphasized the predictive value of PB V50, consistent with our threshold of > 38.4%. These findings underscore the importance of enforcing strict PB dose constraints in VMAT and IMRT planning to minimize ED risk.

Given the close anatomical relationship between the PB and other penile structures such as the crura, corpora cavernosa, and corpus spongiosum, the PB dose has long been considered a surrogate for the radiation exposure of other critical erectile structures [11]. Our study corroborates these findings by demonstrating a significant association between PB mean dose, PB V30 and PB V50 with ED severity, reinforcing the need for strict dosimetric constraints on PB exposure in VMAT planning.

Our findings identified PB V50 > 38.4%, PB mean dose > 40.74 Gy, and PB V30 > 64.9% as key predictors of ED, consistent with prior studies recommending PB dose limits below 50 Gy (QUANTEC) or even 20 Gy (IMRT, HYPO-RT-PC). Variability in dose thresholds across studies likely reflects differences in techniques and populations. Importantly, our study provides the first dose-response data specific to Moroccan men, addressing a gap in existing literature.

In our study, ISUP grade showed a significant association with ED severity, while the D’Amico risk classification did not. This discrepancy may stem from the composite nature of the D’Amico system, which groups biologically distinct cases into broad categories, potentially masking associations with functional outcomes like ED. In contrast, ISUP grade provides a more detailed histopathological assessment of tumor aggressiveness, possibly reflecting differences in radiotherapy intensity or tumor proximity to erectile structures.

Several limitations should be noted. As a retrospective analysis, the study relied on available clinical records, and baseline erectile function prior to ADT and radiotherapy was not documented, limiting the ability to quantify functional decline. SHIM questionnaires were completed only after radiotherapy, at a time when patients had already received a median of 6–10 months of ADT, which may have contributed to the observed ED. The follow-up period (mean 11.2 months) may not fully capture late-onset ED. Information on baseline sexual activity was limited, although most patients were married. The sample size remains modest, which may reduce statistical power. Certain factors — such as psychological status, concomitant medications, or detailed hormonal measurements — were not systematically collected. As a single-institution cohort treated with normofractionated VMAT, practice uniformity is ensured, but external generalizability may be limited. Finally, use of the PB as a surrogate for erectile structures may not reflect all anatomical variations. These considerations support the need for larger, prospective studies with standardized and longitudinal sexual function assessments.

Our results highlight the need for personalized PB dose constraints in prostate cancer radiotherapy. Future studies should focus on techniques to reduce PB exposure — such as advanced IMRT, adaptive planning, or hydrogel spacers — while maintaining tumor control. Prospective validation of our dose thresholds in larger cohorts is essential to improve functional outcomes.

Conclusions

This study is the first in Morocco to assess dosimetric predictors of ED after VMAT for prostate cancer. A significant correlation was found between ED severity and radiation dose to the PB, particularly the mean dose, PB V30, and PB V50. Thresholds such as PB mean dose > 40.74 Gy and PB V30 > 64.9% offer practical guidance for treatment planning. ISUP grade was the only clinical factor significantly associated with ED, while PSA, T stage, and comorbidities showed no correlation, highlighting the dominant role of dosimetry. Despite limitations — retrospective design, small sample, and short follow-up — the findings underscore the need to prioritize erectile function preservation in radiotherapy planning. Prospective multicenter studies are needed to confirm and refine these results.
