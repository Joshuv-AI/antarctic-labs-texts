Forgetting the knee after high tibial osteotomy versus unicompartmental knee arthroplasty
Authors: Tural Talıblı, Hilmi Alkan, Yasin Erdoğan, Enejd Veizi, Ahmet Fırat, Vedat Biçici
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHigh tibial osteotomy (HTO) and unicompartmental knee arthroplasty (UKA) are widely used surgical options for the treatment of medial compartment knee osteoarthritis. While conventional outcome measures primarily focus on pain relief and functional improvement, joint awareness–based patient-reported outcome measures may provide a more comprehensive assessment of surgical success. The Forgotten Joint Score (FJS) evaluates the extent to which patients are able to “forget” their operated joint during daily activities and has been rarely used in direct comparisons between HTO and UKA. The aim of this study was to compare clinical and functional outcomes, including joint awareness, between patients treated with HTO and UKA.MethodsThis retrospective study included patients who underwent open-wedge HTO or mobile-bearing UKA for anteromedial knee osteoarthritis between February 2011 and August 2022. Patients with complete clinical and radiological data and a minimum follow-up of one year were included. Clinical outcomes were assessed using the Oxford Knee Score (OKS), Visual Analog Scale (VAS), Knee Society Score (KSS), Knee Society Functional Score (KSS-Function), and Forgotten Joint Score (FJS). Preoperative radiographic severity was evaluated using the Kellgren–Lawrence classification. Statistical comparisons between groups were performed using appropriate parametric and non-parametric tests.ResultsA total of 83 patients were included (52 HTO, 31 UKA). Patients in the UKA group were significantly older and had higher preoperative Kellgren–Lawrence grades than those in the HTO group (p < 0.05). Postoperatively, there was no significant difference between groups in OKS or VAS scores (p > 0.05). However, both the KSS knee score and KSS functional score were significantly higher in the UKA group (p = 0.013 and p = 0.020, respectively). The FJS was also significantly higher in patients treated with UKA compared with HTO (p = 0.006), indicating lower joint awareness.ConclusionsDespite being performed in an older patient population with more advanced radiographic osteoarthritis, UKA demonstrated superior functional and patient-centered outcomes compared with HTO. The significantly higher FJS scores suggest that UKA may provide a more “natural” joint perception during daily activities when appropriate patient selection is applied. Incorporating joint awareness–based measures such as the FJS may enhance the evaluation of surgical success beyond conventional clinical scores.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12891-026-09714-z.Keywords: Knee osteoarthritis, High tibial osteotomy, Unicompartmental knee arthroplasty, Forgotten Joint Score

Introduction

Knee osteoarthritis is a common and clinically significant health problem that frequently affects middle-aged and elderly individuals, leading to chronic pain, functional limitation, and a substantial decline in quality of life [1]. The natural course of the disease is not limited to degeneration of the articular cartilage alone, but rather progresses in conjunction with subchondral bone alterations, synovial inflammation, and disturbances in biomechanical load distribution [2]. This multifactorial nature necessitates that treatment planning should not rely solely on radiographic staging, but also take into account the patient’s pain level, functional capacity, activity expectations, and the extent to which the knee joint is perceived as ‘natural’ during daily activities. Particularly in younger and active patients with high functional expectations from surgical treatment, the alignment between the chosen surgical approach and the patient profile directly influences clinical outcomes.

High tibial osteotomy (HTO) has long been widely performed as a joint-preserving surgical option for medial compartment knee osteoarthritis, particularly in young and active patients. The primary objective of this procedure is to correct the mechanical axis of the lower extremity, thereby reducing load transmission to the medial compartment and consequently alleviating pain while slowing the progression of osteoarthritis. In the literature, HTO is emphasized for its implant survivorship, its ability to delay the need for total knee arthroplasty, and its biomechanical advantages; accordingly, it is commonly described as an alternative or a bridging procedure to joint replacement [3]. Nevertheless, despite preservation of the native joint following HTO, residual pain, limited functional improvement, and persistent symptoms such as patients’ inability to ‘forget’ the operated knee have been reported in a subset of cases [4]. This observation highlights that, particularly in studies evaluating patient-reported outcome measures, the clinical success of HTO should be assessed not solely on the basis of radiographic correction and implant survivorship, but also in conjunction with patient satisfaction and perceived functional outcomes.

Unicompartmental Knee Arthroplasty (UKA) was initially regarded as a surgical option approached with caution in selected patient populations due to limited indications and technical challenges related to early implant designs. However, in recent years, clinical perspectives on UKA have evolved substantially with advances in implant technology, the widespread adoption of minimally invasive surgical techniques, and clearer patient selection criteria. In contemporary literature, UKA is increasingly favored in appropriately selected patients because it provides faster functional recovery, less postoperative pain, preserved proprioception, and a joint sensation that more closely resembles natural knee kinematics [5]. In this context, contemporary perspectives increasingly emphasize that the success of UKA should be evaluated not only in terms of surgical outcomes, but also based on the extent to which patients can use their knee without conscious awareness during daily activities and their overall level of satisfaction [6].

In the current literature, numerous studies have compared the clinical outcomes of HTO and UKA, incorporating patient-reported outcome measures in their evaluations [7, 8]. However, the majority of these studies primarily rely on conventional scoring systems that reflect pain and overall functional status, while placing limited emphasis on joint awareness–based parameters that capture the extent to which patients perceive their knee during daily activities [7]. In particular, studies directly comparing HTO and UKA using joint awareness–based measures, such as the Forgotten joint score (FJS), which assess the extent to which patients are able to ‘forget’ their operated joint, remain scarce in the literature. Consequently, surgical success is most often defined by objective parameters such as radiographic correction, implant survivorship, or revision rates, while more subjective yet clinically meaningful outcomes including patient satisfaction and joint awareness tend to receive secondary consideration.

In this context, the aim of the present study was to compare the clinical and functional outcomes of patients with medial compartment knee osteoarthritis treated with HTO and UKA, using patient-reported outcome measures that incorporate joint awareness, in order to determine which surgical approach provides greater advantages in terms of patient satisfaction and daily functional performance.

Materials and methods

Study design and patient selection

This retrospective study was conducted after obtaining approval from the local ethics committee (approval number: E1-23-4040). Patients who underwent HTO or mobile-bearing UKA for the treatment of anteromedial knee osteoarthritis and medial compartment arthrosis (focal osteonecrosis) between February 2011 and August 2022 at the Departments of Orthopaedics and Traumatology of Ankara Atatürk Training and Research Hospital and Ankara City Hospital were included in the study, provided that complete clinical and radiological data were available and a minimum follow-up of one year was achieved. Although longer follow-up periods are desirable for evaluating long-term outcomes, the mean follow-up duration was 33.8 ± 13.0 months (range, 15–82 months), which allowed for the assessment of early to mid-term clinical and functional outcomes.

A total of 208 patients were identified (98 HTO, 110 UKA). In the HTO group, only patients in whom fixation was performed using the TomoFix locking plate (Stryker, Kalamazoo, MI, USA) were included. Patients who required revision surgery after HTO, had implant removal, or demonstrated nonunion at the osteotomy site, implant failure, or progressive lateral compartment osteoarthritis were excluded. In the HTO group, 46 patients (46.9%) were excluded due to revision surgery, missing or inadequate radiographic data, or loss to follow-up, leaving 52 patients (53.1%) for final analysis.

In the UKA group, patients in whom implants other than the Biomet Oxford cementless mobile-bearing unicompartmental knee prostheses (Zimmer Biomet, Warsaw, IN, USA) were used were included. Patients who received cemented prostheses, or those who developed periprosthetic fractures, insert dislocation, or major implant-related complications were excluded. Additionally, patients with a follow-up period shorter than one year, incomplete clinical and/or radiological data, those who died during the follow-up period, and those who declined to participate in the study were also excluded. In the UKA group, 79 patients (71.8%) were excluded because of different implant use, cemented fixation, insufficient follow-up or imaging, reoperation, death, or loss to follow-up. Consequently, 31 patients (28.2%) were included in the study (Fig. 1).

Study population and baseline characteristics

A total of 83 patients were included in the study. Of these, 52 patients (62.7%) underwent HTO, while 31 patients (37.3%) were treated with UKA. The mean age of the entire cohort was 53.3 ± 8.5 years, with a median age of 55 years (range, 36–77). Of the operated knees, 42.2% were right-sided and 57.8% were left-sided. Sixteen patients (19.3%) were male and 67 (80.7%) were female. The mean follow-up duration was 33.8 ± 13.0 months, with a median follow-up of 33.0 months (range, 15–82). A history of previous knee surgery was present in 30 patients (36.1%), whereas no prior surgical intervention was identified in 53 patients (63.9%). Radiological evaluation

Preoperative knee radiographs of the included patients were evaluated according to the Kellgren–Lawrence classification system in order to determine the radiographic severity of osteoarthritis [9]. In both the preoperative and postoperative periods, standing anteroposterior (AP), lateral (LAT; obtained at approximately 30° of knee flexion), and weight-bearing full-length lower extremity radiographs were reviewed in both surgical groups to assess alignment and to identify potential surgery-related complications. Clinical and functional assessment

At the final follow-up, patients were evaluated using the Oxford Knee Score (OKS), Visual Analog Scale (VAS), Knee Society Score (KSS), Knee Society Functional Score (KSS-Function), and Forgotten Joint Score (FJS).

Pain intensity was quantitatively assessed using the VAS in the postoperative period. In this method, patients were asked to indicate their current level of pain on a 10-cm horizontal line anchored by ‘no pain’ at one end and ‘unbearable pain’ at the other. The position marked by the patient was recorded as a numerical value ranging from 0 to 10 [10].

OKS was calculated based on patients’ responses to 12 questions. The individual item scores were summed to generate a total score ranging from 0 to 48, with higher scores indicating fewer symptoms and lower scores reflecting greater symptom severity [10].

The KSS and the KSS-Function were calculated based on patients’ responses regarding pain status and functional activities such as walking and stair climbing. The scores for each component were calculated and analyzed separately, with higher scores interpreted as indicating better clinical and functional status [10].

The FJS was calculated based on patients’ responses regarding their awareness of the operated knee during daily activities. A total score was derived from the 12-item questionnaire, with higher scores indicating less joint awareness and lower scores reflecting more pronounced joint awareness [11].

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software version 20.0 (Chicago, IL, USA). Categorical variables were presented as numbers and percentages, while descriptive statistics for continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and median (minimum–maximum), as appropriate.

Comparisons of categorical variables between groups were conducted using the Pearson chi-square test. For continuous variables, the Mann–Whitney U test was applied to data sets that did not show a normal distribution, whereas the independent-samples t test was used for normally distributed data. A p value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. In addition, post hoc power analysis was performed to assess the statistical power of the significant findings.

Results

When the surgical subgroups were compared, the mean age of patients in the UKA group was significantly higher than that of the HTO group (59.7 ± 6.7 vs. 49.4 ± 7.1 years; p < 0.001). No significant difference was observed between the groups in terms of the distribution of the operated side (p = 0.179). Similarly, there was no statistically significant difference in sex distribution between the HTO and UKA groups (p = 0.256). The mean follow-up duration was comparable between the two groups (33.3 ± 14.9 months vs. 34.8 ± 9.1 months; p = 0.189). Additionally, no significant difference was found between the groups regarding a history of previous knee surgery (p = 0.396). Discussion

In this study, comparison of patients treated with HTO and UKA demonstrated superior functional and patient-centered clinical outcomes in favor of UKA. The significantly higher Knee Society Score—both the knee and functional subscales—as well as the FJS observed in the UKA group suggest that this surgical approach may offer advantages in terms of functional improvement and patient perception.

In previous studies, the functional outcomes of HTO and UKA have been compared using various clinical scoring systems. In a long-term follow-up study by Krych et al., patients who underwent UKA achieved significantly higher postoperative Tegner activity scores and Lysholm functional scores compared with those treated with HTO (p < 0.01), suggesting that UKA may provide superior functional gains, particularly in younger and more active patient populations [12]. In a study by Bouguennec et al., significant functional improvement was reported after both HTO and UKA; however, patients treated with UKA demonstrated higher WOMAC scores, Tegner activity scores, and patient satisfaction levels. These findings suggest that UKA may offer advantages over HTO in terms of functional outcomes [13]. However, in various meta-analyses and comparative studies, UKA has generally been associated with superior functional outcomes and lower pain scores, whereas HTO has been found to be advantageous in certain functional parameters such as range of motion (ROM). These findings underscore the influence of patient selection and appropriate indications on clinical outcomes [14]. Nevertheless, in a study comparing open-wedge HTO with mobile-bearing medial UKA that evaluated five-year outcomes, as well as in mid-term comparative analyses conducted by Takeuchi et al., superior functional scores and higher activity levels were reported in the HTO group [15, 16]. These findings indicate considerable heterogeneity in the literature regarding functional outcomes between HTO and UKA, highlighting the significant impact of variables such as patient selection, the type of outcome assessment tool used, surgical technique, and rehabilitation protocols on reported results. While most studies assess functional outcomes using clinical measures such as the Knee Society Score, WOMAC, or similar scoring systems, the extent to which these instruments accurately reflect knee function and activities of daily living may vary [17]. In the present study, functional assessment was not limited to conventional clinical scoring systems; the FJS, which has rarely been used in comparisons between HTO and UKA in the literature, was also included in the evaluation. This patient-centered measure, which reflects the extent to which patients are aware of their operated knee during daily activities, allows functional improvement to be assessed from a more comprehensive and real-life–oriented perspective.

HTO is commonly described in the literature as a preferred treatment option for younger and more active patients who are likely to benefit from a joint-preserving surgical approach, whereas UKA is generally performed in relatively older patients with more advanced osteoarthritis confined to the medial compartment [18, 19]. In the present study, the difference in age distribution according to surgical approach is an expected finding and is consistent with the well-established clinical paradigm. Similarly, the higher preoperative Kellgren–Lawrence grade observed in the UKA group reflects routine clinical practice, where unicompartmental knee arthroplasty is often reserved for patients with more advanced medial compartment disease. However, despite the older age of patients in the UKA group—an aspect that would theoretically be expected to negatively influence functional outcomes—higher functional scores were observed in this cohort. This finding should be interpreted with caution, as age-related differences in patient selection between HTO and UKA have been reported in the literature [20]. Moreover, the similarity in postoperative pain levels and follow-up durations between the groups suggests that the observed functional gains cannot be explained solely by differences in age or length of follow-up. Although no causal relationship can be established, these findings are consistent with those reported in the literature and suggest that, in appropriately selected older patients, UKA can provide satisfactory symptom control and functional outcomes [21, 22].

While VAS and OKS primarily assess pain intensity and basic functional limitations, the observed differences in KSS and FJS between the groups may be better explained by fundamental differences in the mechanisms of deformity correction between UKA and HTO. In UKA, deformity correction and elimination of the pain source are achieved directly at the joint level through replacement of the pathological medial compartment, resulting in a more balanced load distribution and more physiological knee kinematics. In contrast, HTO corrects deformity extra-articularly at the metaphyseal level, reducing medial compartment loading without fully eliminating intra-articular pathology. This indirect correction may require greater neuromuscular adaptation to restore functional balance. Consequently, although similar pain control and basic functional outcomes were achieved in both groups, direct intra-articular correction in UKA may lead to a more “natural-feeling” knee during daily activities, resulting in higher FJS and functional scores in the UKA group. The FJS offers insight into patient perception and joint awareness in daily life and may be useful when evaluating different surgical strategies such as HTO and UKA. As a patient-reported outcome measure that reflects joint awareness and perceived ‘naturalness’ beyond pain and function, and as a tool more resistant to ceiling effects, the FJS provides valuable insight into successful surgical outcomes. In the present study, the significantly higher FJS observed in the UKA group at the final follow-up, with a mean follow-up duration of approximately 33 months, suggests that, from a patient-centered perspective, UKA may offer advantages in the short- to mid-term postoperative period.

While appropriate indication remains an essential component of surgical decision-making, clinical outcomes should not be interpreted as being determined by indication alone, as multiple factors such as patient characteristics, surgical technique, and postoperative management contribute to the final outcome. These findings support the view that HTO and UKA should be considered complementary rather than competing surgical options, tailored to different patient profiles. While the joint-preserving and biomechanical benefits of HTO remain relevant in younger, active patients, UKA appears to be a strong option in appropriately selected patients in terms of patient satisfaction and daily functional performance.

It should also be acknowledged that although HTO is a joint-preserving procedure, progression of osteoarthritis over time—particularly in the medial compartment—has been reported in the literature and may influence long-term clinical and functional outcomes. Therefore, the findings of the present study should be interpreted as mid-term results, and longer-term follow-up is required to fully evaluate the durability of outcomes after HTO.

Nevertheless, the retrospective design of the study and the non-randomized selection of surgical procedures should be acknowledged, underscoring the need for future prospective, randomized investigations.

This study has several limitations. First, the retrospective design, together with the absence of preoperative patient-reported outcome measures and the lack of blinding in outcome assessments, may have introduced selection and observer bias. Second, the significant age difference between the HTO and UKA groups represents a potential confounding factor that may have influenced the comparison of clinical outcomes. Nevertheless, all procedures were performed by two experienced senior surgeons, which likely reduced variability related to surgical technique and strengthened internal validity. Finally, although post-hoc power analysis demonstrated adequate statistical power for the FJS outcome, the power for KSS-related outcomes was borderline, likely due to the relatively small sample size, particularly in the UKA group. Therefore, larger prospective studies are warranted to confirm these findings.

Conclusion

The results of this study suggest that when patient perception and functional recovery–oriented outcome measures are considered, UKA may offer advantages over HTO within their respective age groups. Nevertheless, HTO remains an important joint-preserving surgical option in appropriately selected patients.
