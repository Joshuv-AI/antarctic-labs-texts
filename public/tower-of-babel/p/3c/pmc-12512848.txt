Triglyceride–glucose index in the associations between chronic low-grade inflammation and carotid intima-media thickness: mediation and effect-modification across triglyceride–glucose strata
Authors: Xueguang Lin, Shishuai Xie, Bingbing Pu, Zheyu Wang, Hui Zhang, Yunxia Li, Jingdong Tang, Bo Yu, Shuai Jiang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimsAtherosclerosis is established as a chronic inflammatory disease, yet the potential mediating role of glucose and lipid metabolism in inflammation-driven atherogenesis has not been fully elucidated. Herein, we investigated the association between low-grade inflammation and atherosclerosis, with joint effects of the triglyceride–glucose (TyG) index.Methods and resultsWe included data from two cohorts: the UK Biobank (UKB), a prospective study, and the Huinan cohort in Shanghai, a cross-sectional study, both with complete data from duplex ultrasounds for carotid intima-media thickness and circulating blood cell counts We identified the function of TyG as an inflammatory index and compared the risk related to atherosclerosis among different TyG levels. In total, 33,123 UKB participants (mean [SD] age 55.24 [7.58] years; 51.42% women) and 3,440 Huinan participants (mean [SD] age 70.32 [6.63] years; 58.26% women) showed increased cIMT along with higher inflammation markers including INFLA-score 0.76 [8.65], and white blood cell (WBC) count (6.58 [1.76]), after adjusting for covariates. High TyG (over the median) was associated with a higher low-grade circulating inflammation-related estimate for cIMT compared to low TyG. In UKB cohort, mediation analysis revealed that the TyG index mediated 23.36%, 10.25%, 26.30%, and 12.19% of the effects exerted by the INFLA-score, CRP, WBC count, and lymphocyte count on cIMT, respectively.ConclusionsThese findings suggested that circulating inflammation status can exacerbate both subclinical and clinical atherosclerosis, with joint effects from the TyG index. This highlights the role of a balance between metabolic and inflammatory factors in atherosclerosis.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12872-025-05199-1.Keywords: Atherosclerosis, Low-grade inflammation, Triglyceride–glucose (TyG) index, Carotid intima-media thickness

Introduction

Classical hypotheses link disturbances in glucose and lipid metabolism to atherosclerosis, with lipid/glucose-lowering treatment demonstrating significant efficacy in controlling the progression of atherosclerotic lesions [1, 2]. Chronic inflammation has been recognized as a key factor in atherosclerosis [3]. Clinical trials targeting interleukin (IL)−1β and IL-6 with pharmaceutical anti-inflammatory therapies have shown encouraging outcomes [4, 5]. Although statin therapy can effectively control lipoprotein levels, residual inflammatory risk remains closely associated with the occurrence of future cardiovascular events [6]. These findings indicate both the limitations of current treatments and the complex interplay between glucose–lipid metabolism and inflammation.

Residual inflammatory risk affects approximately one-third of patients with myocardial infarction, even those who have received treatments to lower low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL) [7]. Positive associations of circulating immune cell counts, including monocytes and lymphocytes, as well as the neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR), with the development of atherosclerosis have been validated. Recent studies have further confirmed that a higher peripheral neutrophil count is positively associated with both cardiovascular events and an increase in carotid intima-media thickness (cIMT) [8–10]. These findings are increasingly shifting the attention from lipid metabolism disorders as the primary driver of atherosclerosis pathogenesis to inflammation as a key factor [11]. Studies have revealed the potential mechanisms underlying the effects of a low-grade inflammatory response and its interaction with lipid/glucose metabolism during the progression of atherosclerosis [12, 13]. However, these findings have been less conclusive in extensive cohort studies. Furthermore, numerous studies have stressed the role of inflammation in the incidence of cardiovascular events among patients with existing atherosclerotic diseases, as a marker of subclinical atherosclerosis. However, research using larger cohorts on cIMT as an outcome of the interaction between inflammation and lipid/glucose metabolism has been relatively limited [14]. Therefore, analyzing the relationship between chronic inflammation and its joint effects with lipid/glucose metabolism could provide insights into how inflammation and metabolism jointly contribute to the onset of atherosclerosis.

In this study, we verified the positive correlation between low-grade inflammation and subclinical atherosclerosis, as well as atherosclerotic lesion-related events, and the interaction effect of the triglyceride–glucose (TyG) index with inflammatory markers in this context among filtered participants from the UK Biobank (UKB) cohort. We also conducted supplementary validation using another cohort primarily comprising Chinese participants. Additionally, we tested the hypothesis that the TyG index plays a mediating role and identified the mediation proportion.

Methods

Study design and participants

This cross-sectional study was designed to investigate whether there is a positive association between low-grade inflammation status and sub-clinical and clinical atherosclerosis, as well as the joint effects of the TyG index. Participants were selected from the UK Biobank (UKB) cohort (Fig. 1) and the Huinan cohort. The UK Northwest Multi-centre Research Ethics Committee approved the UKB study protocol. The UKB is a prospective cohort that initially enrolled over 500,000 individuals aged 40–69 across the UK from 2006 to 2010. From the 2014 follow-up of the UKB, 33,123 participants were specifically analysed, having complete data on peripheral immune cell counts, C-reactive protein, and carotid intima-media thickness (cIMT). The study design and methods of the UKB have been presented previously [15]. Baseline assessment included a comprehensive touchscreen questionnaire covering socio-demographics, lifestyle factors, and medical history [16]. The Huinan cohort (clinical trial number: ChiCTR2400087791) is conducted between 2023 and 2024 in the Huinan community of the southeast part of Shanghai. Our study utilized data collected through this standardized questionnaire (see supporting information 2). After excluding participants with incomplete cIMT and blood routine examination data, 3440 of the 4090 were included.

Exposure assessment and definition

Baseline circulating blood cell counts and comprehensive metabolic examination results were obtained within 24 h of collection using special instruments, from which ratios such as TyG (triglyceride-glucose index), NLR (neutrophil to lymphocyte ratio), and INFLA-score (inflammation core) were calculated to assess peripheral immunity. TyG index was calculated with an equation of Ln[Triglyceride (mg/dL) × Glucose (mg/dL)/2] [17]. The INFLA-score is calculated by assigning scores from + 1 to + 4 for biomarkers (C-reactive protein, white blood cell count, platelet count, and NLR) in the highest deciles (7th to 10th) and from − 1 to − 4 for those in the lowest deciles (1st to 4th) [18]. The final score is the sum of these values for four biomarkers, ranging from − 16 to + 16, with higher scores indicating greater low-grade inflammation.

Outcome assessment

cIMT measurement

CIMT, a well-established marker of early atherosclerotic diseases, represents subclinical atherosclerosis. In the UKB cohort, the cIMT was measured using high-resolution B-mode ultrasonography, following the protocol presented at https://biobank.ndph.ox.ac.uk/ukb/refer.cgi?id=511, focusing on the far wall of the common carotid artery, 1 centimeter proximal to the bifurcation. In this study, we calculated average and maximum cIMT values at 120°, 150°, 210°, and 240° angles for both sides of the carotid artery, deriving specific metrics: Right cIMTmean (average of mean cIMT values from 120° and 150°); Left cIMTmean (average of mean cIMT values from 210° and 240°); Right cIMTmax (average of maximum cIMT values from 120° and 150°); Left cIMTmax (average of maximum cIMT values from 210° and 240°); IMTmean and IMTmean-max (overall averages of mean and maximum values from all angles, respectively). In the Huinan cohort, carotid intima-media thickness (cIMT) was assessed by certified sonographers using high-resolution B-mode duplex ultrasonography with a standardized protocol. Measurements were taken at the far wall of the common carotid artery, 1 cm proximal to the bifurcation, on both left and right sides. The highest value obtained was used for classification, with cIMT < 1.0 mm considered normal and ≥ 1.0 mm considered abnormal [19].

Covariates

Relevant covariates in our analysis were carefully selected to address potential confounding factors affecting the association between peripheral immunity markers and carotid intima-media thickness (cIMT) and the diagnosis of outcome conditions during follow-up. These covariates included anthropometric measures such as age and body mass index (BMI). Demographic variables included age group (< 45, 45–64, and 65 + years), gender (female and male), and ethnicity (for the UKB cohort), categorized into ‘white’ and ‘non-white’. Lifestyle factors were assessed through structured questionnaires, capturing smoking status across three categories (never, former, and current smoker) and alcohol consumption frequency (never, ≤ 3 times per month, > 3 times per month). Socioeconomic status (for UKB cohort) was measured using the Townsend deprivation index (TDI), which considers factors such as unemployment, overcrowded housing, lack of car ownership, and non-homeownership. Based on diagnosis records (field ID 2443 for diabetes; field ID 2453 for cancer in the UKB cohort), chronic conditions such as diabetes and cancer were identified in two cohorts. To ensure the integrity of our analysis, we tested for multicollinearity among these covariates using a variance inflation factor (VIF) less than 5 as the threshold, confirming negligible multicollinearity and maintaining the robustness of our results (Table S4).

Statistical analysis

Univariate and multivariable linear regression was conducted to investigate the associations between cIMT and inflammation markers, including INFLA-score, CRP, and WBC/neutrophil/monocyte/lymphocyte count. Hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% CI were calculated using Cox proportional hazards models with the follow-up time as the time scale to estimate the associations of the inflammation index with the risk of cardiovascular and cerebrovascular events. In this analysis, the crude model was adjusted for none, and the multivariable model included sex, age, ethnicity, smoking status, alcohol use, BMI, TDI, hypertension, HDL, and LDL. Restricted cubic splines (RCS) were used to examine the nonlinear association between the outcome and inflammation markers, with covariates including those mentioned above adjusted. The number of knots was selected based on the Akaike information criterion.

To assess the interactions between the low-grade inflammation index and TyG index, the interaction term was added to the original multivariable linear regression model, except for the TyG index, and the Wald test was performed. Subgroup analyses were performed based on the categories of covariates using the same multivariable regression model, except for the variable excluded from the current subgroup.

We explored the TyG index as a potential mediator of the association between low-grade inflammation (exposure) and subclinical and clinical atherosclerosis (outcome). Although cross-sectional design limits the interpretation of mediation analyses, considering that the inflammatory markers (such as blood cell count and CRP levels) were obtained prior to the information on cIMT and cardiovascular events and as suggested by previous studies, we hypothesized that low-grade inflammation index may influence TyG index and subsequently atherosclerotic events and set the temporal ordering of variables in the mediation analyses. We used a crude model with no adjustments and 2 models accounting for potential confounders. Model 1 adjusted for sex, age, ethnicity, smoking status, alcohol use, BMI, and TDI at baseline. Model 2 further adjusted for hypertension, TyG index, HDL, and LDL, potentially affecting low-grade inflammation markers and atherosclerosis [19, 20]. All statistical analyses were performed using R software 4.4.2. Statistical significance was defined as 2-sided P < 0.05. Data were analysed from August 2024 to February 2025.

Results

Demographics and baseline characteristics

After applying the inclusion criteria, a total of 33,123 participants from the UKB cohort and 3,440 participants from the Huinan cohort were retained for the cross-sectional analysis. As shown in Table 1, the age distribution across different INFLA-score quartiles exhibited a similar pattern, with approximately 10% of the total population being under 45 years or over 65 years of age. The most significant proportion, approximately 80%, was observed in the age group 45–65 years. The sex distribution differed significantly across the groups, with an opposite trend observed. The proportion of women increased from 47.6% in the Q1 group to 57.3% in the Q4 group (Q1, 4706/9886 participants [47.6%]; Q2, 4243/8433 participants [50.3%]; Q3, 4405/8387 participants [52.5%]; Q4, 3678/6417 participants [57.3%]; P < 0.001). The proportion of men decreased from 52.4 to 42.7% (Q1, 5180/9886 participants [52.4%]; Q2, 4190/8433 participants [49.7%]; Q3, 3982/8387 participants [47.5%]; Q4, 2739/6417 participants [42.7%]; P < 0.001). Participants with higher inflammation indices exhibited significantly different behaviors in smoking and drinking. The proportion of current smokers increased gradually from 2.3% in Q1 to 4.6% in Q4 (P < 0.001), whereas the proportion of regular alcohol users decreased from 74.0% in Q1 to 66.0% in Q4 (P < 0.001). Elevated C-reactive protein (CRP), neutrophil counts, WBC count, platelet count, NLR, and SII, along with greater cIMT, were more common in participants with a high INFLA score than in those with a low INFLA score (Q1: 685.2 ± 123.6; Q2: 686.0 ± 122.0; Q3: 690.8 ± 126.7; Q4: 693.1 ± 129.3; P < 0.001). The proportion of individuals diagnosed with diabetes or hypertension and those with a BMI greater than 25 kg/m2, along with metabolic indicators including total cholesterol, triglycerides, HDL, LDL, glucose, and the TyG index, were significantly increased with increased INFLA-score category.

According to linear regression analysis, Table 2 presents the association between low-grade inflammation biomarkers and cIMT. In the fully adjusted models, neutrophil count, monocyte count, platelet count, NLR, and SII were not associated with cIMT, despite showing significant correlations with cIMT in the unadjusted models (except for NLR). For the INFLA-score, CRP, WBC count, and lymphocyte count, significant associations remained after adjusting for sex, age, race and ethnicity, smoking, alcohol consumption, hypertension, the TyG index, BMI, HDL, LDL, and TDI at baseline. Notably, the effect size for lymphocyte count reached 3.00 (unadjusted β = 4.36 [95% CI, 2.88–5.83]; adjusted β = 3.00 [95% CI, 1.51–4.49]), and the effect sizes for other indicators decreased significantly. Interaction analysis of the INFLA-score and TyG index with cIMT

The association between the TyG index and cIMT had been previously discussed in other cohort studies [19, 21, 22]; we therefore validated this association in the UKB cohort (Table S3). According to the TyG index, we stratified the UKB cohort into low- and high-TyG groups and compared the association between different inflammation index quartiles with cIMT between the two TyG groups (Fig. 3). We found a positive association of quartiles for INFLA-score, WBC count, and neutrophil count with cIMT, which appeared to be increased with higher TyG index values (INFLA-score: P for interaction = 0.030; WBC count: P for interaction = 0.005; neutrophil count: P for interaction = 0.003). In trend analysis, significant associations between the inflammation index and cIMT were more readily observed in the high-TyG group, whereas no significant association was found in the low-TyG group.

Mediation analyses were conducted for inflammation biomarkers, including the INFLA-score, CRP, WBC count, neutrophil count, monocyte count, and lymphocyte count (Table 3).

In the crude model, the TyG index demonstrated a mediating effect on the association between all inflammation markers and cIMT, with mediation effects ranging from 18.80 to 90.90% (monocyte count, 18.80%, P < 2 × 10−16; Neutrophil count, 90.90%, p < 2 × 10−16) (Table 3). After adjusting for sex, age, race, and ethnicity, smoking, alcohol consumption, TyG index, BMI, HDL, LDL, and TDI at baseline in model 1, the TyG index was estimated to account for 23.35% of the observed association between the INFLA-score and cIMT (P = 0.004). When adjusting for hypertension in addition to the variables in model 1, the mediation effect of the TyG index remained unchanged, and the proportion of the mediation effect by the TyG index showed only a small change. The TyG index was estimated to account for 23.36% (P = 0.002), 10.25% (P < 2 × 10−16), 26.30% (P = 0.002), and 12.19% (P < 2 × 10−16) of the respective associations, with INFLA-score, CRP, WBC count, and lymphocyte count serving as inflammation markers associated with increased cIMT.

Subgroup analyses

We further analyzed the data according to age, sex, race and ethnicity, socioeconomic status, smoking status, alcohol use, BMI, and baseline hypertension (Table S4). The association between INFLA-score and cIMT showed significant differences across various groups based on sex, smoking status, alcohol consumption frequency, and BMI categories. In cases with a similar sample size, the results of sex stratification showed that for each 1-unit increase in INFLA-score, the cIMT thickness increased by 1.09 mm (95% CI, 0.85–1.33, P < 0.001) in the male group; this association was not significant in the female group. Cross-group tests between these groups revealed a significant difference. In smoking-stratified analyses, the INFLA-score and cIMT association strengthened with greater smoking exposure, with estimated effect sizes rising from 0.13 (95% CI, − 0.06–0.32, P > 0.05) in never-smokers to 0.70 (95% CI, 0.42–0.97, P < 0.001) in former smokers and 0.73 (95% CI, − 0.22–1.68, P > 0.05) in current smokers. The association between CRP and cIMT also showed significant differences across smoking status and BMI categories. Additionally, CRP exhibited notable variations across different age groups. When stratifying participants into three groups based on age thresholds of 45 and 65 years, the β value decreased gradually from 1.75 (95% CI, 0.77–2.74, P < 0.001) to − 0.17 (95% CI, − 1.17–0.83, P > 0.05). In addition to these two inflammation markers, we also analyzed the performance of other inflammation markers across different subgroups, as presented in the supplemental table.

Discussion

This cross-sectional study investigated the associations among circulating inflammation status, the TyG index, and cIMT. We had three significant findings. First, the inflammation marker CRP, the absolute counts of circulating leucocytes, and their composite index INFLA-score were associated with subclinical atherosclerosis. Second, these associations were found to interact with the individual’s metabolic status. Third, mediation analysis suggests that the metabolic indicator TyG index serves as a crucial mediator in the inflammatory process of atherosclerosis onset, transmitting a significant proportion of the effect from inflammation markers to cIMT. Consistent with previous epidemiological studies [9, 23, 24]these findings confirm that inflammation increases the risk of atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease.

The associations between inflammation and cIMT have previously been assessed based on small-scale cohorts and focusing on specific populations [25–28]. Moreover, elevated circulating leucocyte counts—particularly neutrophil counts and the NLR—are positively associated with increased cIMT [28–30]. In this cross-sectional general population study, we examined the relationship between low-grade inflammation and cIMT in a large sample-size cohort and corroborated our findings in an independent cohort of a different racial and ethnic background. By incorporating the INFLA-score, CRP, detailed leukocyte counts, and composite indices such as the NLR and SII, we aimed to comprehensively characterize inter-individual variation in low-grade inflammation [31–33]. The INFLA-score, calculated from a subset of these inflammatory biomarkers, ranges from − 16 to + 16, with values > 0 indicating the presence of low-grade inflammation [34]. The INFLA-score exhibits superior diagnostic performance for low-grade inflammation compared to CRP [34]. In unadjusted analyses, all examined inflammatory markers were significantly associated with cIMT. However, after controlling for covariates, only the INFLA-score, CRP, WBC count, and lymphocyte count remained independently related to cIMT. This pattern differs from earlier reports identifying neutrophil count and the NLR as significant predictors of cIMT, a discrepancy that may stem from differences in covariate adjustment schemes. Therefore, imposing arbitrary cut-points such as 1.0 mm–0.9 mm risks systematic misclassification bias [8]. Consequently, we did not dichotomize cIMT by applying a threshold in the UKB cohort—a deliberate choice to avoid loss of information and maximize analytic power [35].

We observed that lymphocyte count was the most robust immune-cell metric, maintaining a strong positive association with cIMT even after comprehensive adjustment for covariates. Prior investigations of inflammation have rarely included lymphocyte count as an analytic variable, instead concentrating on innate immune indices such as monocytes and neutrophils. This focus derives from the pivotal contribution of innate immune mediators—most notably the NLRP3 inflammasome and its downstream cytokine IL-1β, both predominantly produced by monocytes or macrophages—to the initiation and propagation of inflammatory responses [12, 36]. Our data demonstrated that, in cIMT—the earliest detectable manifestation of atherogenesis—lymphocyte count is a more influential determinant than either the neutrophil or monocyte count. There is evidence that higher lymphocyte counts are associated with carotid plaque progression [37]. Studies in populations of people living with HIV also offer pertinent evidence: one investigation documented heightened activation of CD4⁺ and CD8⁺ T lymphocytes and demonstrated that specific lymphocyte subsets were significantly associated with greater cIMT [38]. In our cohort, RCS modelling corroborated a significant Linear association between lymphocyte count and cIMT; significantly, each 1-unit increment in lymphocyte count corresponded to an estimated 3-mm increase in cIMT—an effect size larger than that observed for any other inflammatory marker examined. Future large-scale cohort studies incorporating detailed immunophenotyping are warranted to pinpoint the specific lymphocyte subpopulations that drive the increased cIMT.

In the current study setting, we detected a statistically significant interaction between low-grade inflammation and the TyG index. The association between low-grade inflammation and cIMT varied between the two groups stratified according to the median TyG index. A significant relationship between low-grade inflammation and cIMT was observed only in the high-TyG group, but this association was no longer significant in the low-TyG group. Similarly, several small-scale clinical investigations have demonstrated a substantial association between systemic inflammation and increased cIMT in individuals with diabetes or pre-diabetes, a pattern that concurs with our present findings [39–41]. Additionally, a cross-sectional study conducted by Kaplan et al. in 2017 among the indigenous South American Tsimané population found that, despite being exposed to high levels of inflammation-related disease risks, Tsimané people maintain a relatively low incidence of coronary heart disease. This was attributed to their low-sugar, low-fat diet [41]. These findings suggest that glucose and lipid metabolism may directly and significantly affect atherosclerotic lesions more than inflammation alone.

In our study, mediation analyses suggested that the TyG index may partly account for the observed association between low-grade inflammation and cIMT. A deeper understanding of this result suggests that metabolism is a key mediator, facilitating the inflammatory processes that drive atherosclerotic lesions. This diverges from previous views that have considered inflammation an independent pathogenic factor equally as crucial as metabolism. In these models, metabolic dysregulation exacerbates disease progression via inflammation, with inflammation alone having the potential to independently drive pathological changes [42, 43]. Based on the interaction effect analysis discussed earlier, we hypothesized that inflammation influences cellular and tissue metabolic functions through multiple pathways, leading to disease progression. Mediation analysis conducted in support of this hypothesis confirmed its validity. We also performed a reverse mediation analysis, examining low-grade inflammation as a mediator for the effect of the TyG index on atherosclerotic lesions. However, the relatively smaller mediation effect suggests that the forward pathway, wherein inflammation influences metabolic dysregulation directly, is likely a more critical and common mechanism in the pathogenesis of atherosclerosis.

Much research today focuses on the pathway from metabolism to inflammation as a systemic condition. However, inflammation is not only the pathological foundation of atherosclerosis but also the immunological basis for numerous metabolic diseases. An earlier review linked inflammation with the pathogenesis of insulin resistance, highlighting the central role of immune activation in the development of metabolic disorders [44]. We aim to redirect researchers’ attention to this critical aspect. Mechanistically, atherosclerosis begins with endothelial injury, where the accumulation of oxidized LDL activates innate immune cells, leading to foam cell formation [45]. Lymphocytes, particularly T cells and B cells, further enhance the local immune response by secreting cytokines and antibodies, promoting foam cell formation. Inflammatory factors, such as TNF-α and IL-6, exacerbate metabolic dysregulation, driving lipid accumulation and insulin resistance, further accelerating intimal thickening [46, 47]. The interplay between immune activation and metabolic dysregulation accelerates the progression of atherosclerosis. These novel findings have potential implications for future disease prevention and the design of clinical trials focused on metabolic regulation and anti-inflammatory therapies.

Our study has several strengths. To our knowledge, we are the first to use the INFLA-score as an index to analyze the association between low-grade inflammation and cIMT, complemented by additional analyses incorporating CRP and immune cell counts. Moreover, we explored the relationship between inflammation and cIMT in two distinct populations, enhancing the generalizability of our findings. Finally, our results are clinically relevant and align with practical applications. Based on our analysis of the association between inflammation and cIMT in TyG subgroups, clinicians can use easily accessible biomarkers to identify high-risk populations.

Limitations

Several Limitations of the present study merit consideration. First, the cross-sectional design of the Huinan cohort precludes any inference of temporal or causal relationships between inflammation, metabolic dysregulation, and cIMT progression. While our mediation analyses suggested potential pathways Linking low-grade inflammation and the TyG index, existing literature strongly supports bidirectional interactions between inflammatory and metabolic processes; our findings should therefore be interpreted as associations rather than directional effects. Second, we did not have access to serial cIMT measurements, so the dynamic trajectory of arterial thickening and its temporal relationship with changes in inflammatory or metabolic markers could not be assessed. Third, the Huinan cohort lacked detailed CRP values and continuous cIMT measurements, providing only dichotomous groupings based on a 1 mm threshold, which limits comparability and statistical power. Fourth, although mediation analyses indicated statistical interrelationships between low-grade inflammation and the TyG index, the absence of time-resolved data prevented us from modeling their reciprocal dynamics. Finally, despite adjustment for a comprehensive set of covariates, residual confounding by unmeasured factors cannot be entirely ruled out.

Conclusions

In this cross-sectional study, a significant interaction was found between metabolic disorder and inflammation status in the onset and progression of atherosclerosis. The different associations between low-grade inflammation and cIMT across TyG subgroups indicated that metabolic factors are crucial in the inflammation-driven atherosclerosis. Specifically, in the high-TyG group, the positive association between low-grade inflammation and cIMT was more pronounced. This suggests that as a marker of metabolic status, the TyG index may reflect the potential role of metabolic dysregulation in promoting atherosclerotic lesions, particularly in individuals with a high metabolic load. In such individuals, chronic inflammation may play a more significant role in the initiation and progression of atherosclerotic lesions, highlighting its crucial contribution to the development of cIMT thickening.
