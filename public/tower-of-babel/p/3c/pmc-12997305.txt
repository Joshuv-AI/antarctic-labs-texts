Multiscale spatiotemporal evolution and patterns of riverine ecological security in Nanjing Yangtze river basin
Authors: Changran Sun, Gongxian Fang, Yangyang Lu, Zihan Zhu, Mingjiang Deng, Guohua Fang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryRiverine zones possess significant land-use potential; however, rapid socioeconomic development has caused severe ecological security challenges. Focusing on the Nanjing section of the Yangtze River, a thirty-indicator, multi-scale ecological security assessment framework was constructed. The set pair analysis model, spatial distance index model, trapezoidal cloud model, Moran’s index, and cold hotspot analysis method Getis-Ord Gi∗ were used to evaluate the spatial and temporal evolution characteristics of riverine ecological security at micro-, meso-, and macro-scales from 2000 to 2023. Based on the results of the cold-hotspot analysis of ecological security at each scale in 2023, the important areas of riverine ecological security at each scale were extracted to construct a comprehensive pattern.Subject areas: Ecology

Introduction

Riverine zones are within the field of interactive linkage between water, land, and the human environment, involving both land and water natural systems and impacted by human activities in multiple ways. They are a highly complex composite ecosystem and a key resource for maintaining the balance between human social development and ecological environmental protection.1,2 Riverine zones have diverse functions, such as ecological health maintenance,3 ecological risk buffering,4 and ecosystem service support.5 Riverine zones play important roles in the ecosystems at different spatial scales as habitats for amphibious organisms, components of ecological landscapes, and participants in regional ecosystem services.6 Consequently, riverine zones have become a research hotspot for ecologists worldwide.7,8 Since the Industrial Revolution, numerous countries have created world-class industrialization and urbanization clusters based on valuable land resources and convenient waterway transportation in riverine zones. For example, the Ruhr region in Germany, relying on the Rhine River and its tributaries, concentrated on steel, chemical, and machinery manufacturing, and with its waterway transportation and hydroelectric power, promoted heavy industrialization in the early twentieth century, becoming one of the largest industrial clusters in Europe today.9,10 The Yangtze River Delta (YRD), centered on Shanghai, Nanjing, Suzhou, and Hangzhou and underpinned by the Yangtze River and its tributaries, has engendered world-class industrial and urban agglomerations, comprising port networks, inland waterways, and specialized electronics, automotive development, and petrochemical industrial parks, which have served as a principal driver of China’s economy since the commencement of reform and opening-up.11 However, riverine zones are highly sensitive and susceptible to strong impacts from interactions among terrestrial ecosystems, aquatic ecosystems, and human disturbances. Against the backdrop of accelerating industrialization and urbanization, the conflict between the utilization of riparian areas and ecological environmental protection has become more pronounced.12 Efficiently safeguarding the ecological security of the riverine zones and achieving the sustainable development of the riverine economy, society, and environment amid swift economic progress have become pressing challenges for highly developed coastal cities undergoing industrialization and urbanization.

With the increasing significance of environmental degradation caused by economic growth, ecological security is gaining greater attention, and more countries have regarded ecological security as a crucial component of their national security frameworks.13,14 Ecological security can be interpreted in two ways: narrowly, it emphasizes ecosystem health and stability; broadly, it reflects the harmony between ecological systems and human development.15,16 Although the definition of ecological security has not been standardized, and scholars have conducted different studies based on different understandings of the principle of ecological security, they usually highlight the three facets of ecosystem health, ecological risk, and ecosystem service function, and fundamentally focus on the characteristics of ecological security at different scales.17,18,19 Ecosystem health refers to the state in which an ecosystem maintains structural stability and functional effectiveness while possessing the capacity to recover and regulate in response to external disturbances. It directly and positively characterizes the safety status of an ecosystem. Ecosystem risk denotes the probability and consequences of undesirable events occurring within an ecosystem, such as damage to its structure and functions caused by external disturbances or natural disasters. It indirectly and negatively characterizes the safety status of an ecosystem. Ecosystem service functions refer to the natural environmental conditions and benefits formed and maintained by ecosystems and ecological processes that sustain human life. They indirectly characterize the security status of ecosystems from a positive perspective. These three dimensions complement each other, collectively characterizing the overall state of ecological security. As an important component of regional ecological systems, riverine ecological security reflects how human development and hydrological processes jointly influence ecosystem health, regulate ecosystem services, and contribute to ecological risk. Ecological security of the riverbank relates to the health and stability of riverine ecosystems and their ecological function attributes when exploiting riverine resources, the extent to which riverine zones are preserved from ecological degradation and environmental pollutants caused by human development, and the level of ecosystem services that riverine ecosystems can provide under the pressure of socioeconomic development.

Riverine ecological safety assessment is an important tool for evaluating the ecological safety status of riverine development and utilization.20 Riverine ecological security assessment consists of three aspects: ecosystem health assessment, ecological risk assessment, and ecosystem service function assessment. In terms of riverine ecosystem health assessment, Lu et al. examined the meaning of riverine ecological health and investigated the relationship between land use change pressure and riverine ecological health.3 Li et al. conducted an assessment of riverine ecological health based on land use change and examined the spatial heterogeneity of ecological health levels in riverine21; and in terms of riverine ecological risk assessment, Li et al. assessed ecological well-being by investigating land use transformations and explored how ecological health conditions varied across the riverine area.21 Regarding riverine ecological risk evaluation, Lu et al. identified ecological issues and risks at multiple scales within the riverine area and developed a “micro-meso-macro” multiscale ecological risk evaluation index system for riverine development.22 Kantharajan et al. employed the InVEST habitat risk assessment model to evaluate ecological risks posed by nine anthropogenic stressors in the Pranita riverine zone of the Godavari basin in Peninsular India.23 Zhu et al. designed an ecological risk assessment index system for riverine zones considering risk sources and ecosystem vulnerability, and predicted future ecological risk using the Gray Markov Chain and PLUS model,4 regarding the evaluation of riverine ecosystem services, shifts in ecosystem service values within ecologically sensitive riverine zones in northeastern China were investigated, integrating remote sensing data with socio-economic indicators.24 Similarly, Zhou et al. developed a conceptual model to explore the factors influencing changes in riverine ecosystem services. This approach, grounded in ecosystem service valuation theory and tailored to the specific features of riverine environments, was applied to assess sustainable transformations along the Jiangsu stretch of the Beijing-Hangzhou Grand Canal.8 Scholars have extensively studied each of these three aspects. However, in riverine ecosystems, health, risk, and services are interconnected, and research on only one of these aspects cannot adequately characterize the safety status of riverine ecosystems. Some scholars have attempted to link two of the three aspects of riverine: ecosystem health, risks, and services.1,25,26 However, most linkage studies on ecosystem health, risk, and services have been conducted in regions and cities,15,17 and few comprehensive studies have been conducted on the health, risk, and services of riverine zones.

Rapid urbanization and industrial development adversely affect riverine ecosystems and undermine riverine ecological security. The problems of riverine ecological security owing to human development vary according to spatial scale.27 Micro-scale focuses on changes in land use owing to development and utilization activities along rivers, which have a direct impact on riverine ecosystem health. Meso-scale focuses on changes in the ecological landscape configuration of the riverine hinterland due to human activities also exert indirect pressures and threats on the ecosystem. Macro-scale focuses on the human-land interrelationships brought about by the socioeconomic development of cities radiating from the riverine, which will lead to the loss of ecosystem services.22 Ecological security issues exhibit significant differences across various scales, with their manifestations and driving factors varying between riverine zones, riparian hinterlands, and broader watershed or urban scales, reflecting the hierarchical nature of socio-ecological systems. The novelty of this study lies in the research of cross-scale correlations in ecological security. Currently, few studies have assessed the ecological problems caused by riverine development and utilization from a multiscale perspective combined with spatial statistics. Existing studies have mainly focused on ecological problems on a single spatial scale28,29,30,31,32,33 or on single-dimensional ecological issues across scales.34,35 Against this backdrop, the aim of this study was to construct a spatial multiscale security evaluation framework and indicator system for riverine ecosystem health, risks, and services, and to establish a mechanism to enhance the management of ecological security challenges in order to achieve comprehensive and efficient management of riverine ecological security. The results of this study will provide an important supplement to the existing studies by focusing on cross-scale correlation between ecological security at different scales along the riverine.

The Yangtze River Basin is one of the most advanced regions in China regarding agriculture, industry, commerce, culture, education, science, and technology.36 As the capital of Jiangsu Province, Nanjing serves as a key national gateway city in the YRD that influences and promotes the development of China’s central and western areas. It is also a pivotal city at the crossroads of the “One Belt, One Road” initiative and the “Yangtze River Economic Belt (YREB)” strategy. The Nanjing section of the Yangtze River (NSYR) simultaneously functions as the gateway to a megacity and a concentrated belt of national-level ecological conservation areas. Its two riverbanks present a composite landscape of mountains, hills, and plains, with markedly asymmetric functional attributes: the right bank serves as the administrative and economic core, whereas the left bank contains newly developed urban areas and industrial clusters. Within its 280.82 km river shoreline, urban centers, industrial zones, and nature reserves are densely interwoven. This unique spatial configuration makes the NSYR one of the regions within the Yangtze River Economic Belt where the tension between ecological protection and economic activity is most pronounced and ecological issues are exceptionally complex,37 thus providing research value distinct from other river basins. Based on the distinctiveness of the Yangtze River’s Nanjing section, ecological issues manifest across different spatial scales, including micro-, meso-, and macro-scale, as ecological health, ecological risks, and ecosystem services, respectively. Addressing this context, this study focused on the following key scientific questions: understanding how ecological security levels at different scales evolve over space and time; determining how multiscale ecological security assessments collectively form the integrated ecological security pattern of the riverine system. The set pair analysis model, spatial distance index model, trapezoidal cloud model, Moran’s index, and cold hotspot analysis were applied to examine the spatial and temporal evolution patterns of ecological security in riverine regions from a multiscale viewpoint. Using the results of the cold hotspot analysis of ecological security at each scale in 2023, critical areas of riverine ecological security at every scale were identified, and a comprehensive framework of riverine ecological security across multiple scales was developed to offer references and guidance for ecological conservation and restoration in the NSYR Basin.

Results

Study area and data sources

Rising from the Qinghai-Tibet Plateau, the Yangtze River stretches about 6300 km in length and descends nearly 5400 m from source to mouth. It passes through the three major regions of Southwest China, Central China, and East China, flowing across 11 provincial-level regions, and then joins the East China Sea, with a drainage basin covering roughly 1.8 million km2. The NSYR encompasses diverse landforms such as low mountains, hills, plains, and depressions (see Figure 1). With a total shoreline length of 280.82 km, the Yangtze River’s shoreline resources rank highest within Jiangsu Province. The riverine area includes administrative districts characterized by vigorous economic activities, such as Jiangning, Yuhuatai, Jianye, Gulou, Qixia, Pukou, and Liuhe districts. The temperate climate makes the NSYR a vital habitat for endangered species. Its significant ecological value, combined with a highly developed economy, positions Nanjing as the most notable region within the YREB regarding the tension between ecological conservation and economic development.

The definition of the riverine ecological security study scale is a key step in conducting riverine ecological security assessments,38 and there are large differences in ecological safety concerns at various spatial levels in the study area. Pollution caused by factory construction and ecological blockage caused by harbor development along the Yangtze River directly affect the Yangtze River front ecosystem and damage ecosystem health. The fragmentation and fragility of landscape patterns caused by human economic activities in the remote areas of the Yangtze River front indirectly cause ecological pressure and risks to the Yangtze River front ecosystem. The contradiction of the human-land relationship due to the rapid socioeconomic growth of radiating cities bordering the riverine of the Yangtze River will result in a lack of ecosystem function in the region along the Yangtze River. This study fully considered the scale and hierarchical nature of ecological safety assessment in the study area when selecting evaluation units.22 Based on existing research by relevant scholars and the actual conditions of this study area, 5 km is a commonly adopted local zone pressure buffer width in riparian ecological studies, effectively reflecting the direct impact of adjacent land use on river ecosystems.4 Meanwhile, town-level administrative units represent the smallest decision-making units for micro-scale ecological security assessments. Therefore, this study selected town-level administrative units within the 5-km buffer zone as the analytical subjects for micro-scale ecological security assessment. Based on landscape ecology research on the spatial extension of regional ecological processes, the ecological impacts of landscape pattern changes and ecological corridor connectivity typically manifest most significantly within a 10–30 km range. Drawing from existing relevant studies and the actual conditions of this study area, 20 km serves as a representative midpoint within this range, effectively representing mesoscale neighborhood ecological impacts.22 The mesoscale ecological security assessment focuses on three sub-dimensions: human activity intensity, landscape ecological risk, and comprehensive ecological sensitivity. Since the constituent indicators for human activity intensity and comprehensive ecological sensitivity primarily rely on 30-meter-level base grids, the analysis objects for both dimensions are selected from 30-meter raster cells within the 20-km buffer zone. For landscape ecological risk, a grid size that adequately reflects spatial variations within riparian buffer zones is appropriate. Based on the topography, natural environment characteristics, and other actual conditions of the study area, 3 km × 3 km grids within the 20 km buffer zone were selected as the evaluation units for landscape ecological risk in the mesoscale ecological security assessment. The calculated grid data were then interpolated using ordinary Kriging to 30 m raster cells to ensure consistent resolution across all sub-dimensions of the mesoscale ecological security assessment (This does not alter the effective analysis granularity of landscape ecological risks or introduce new spatial details). Cities serve as primary governance units for implementing policies such as territorial spatial planning, ecological protection red line demarcation, and ecological conservation and restoration investments. Research outcomes directly possess policy operability. Moreover, ecosystem services arise from cumulative effects of multiple sources, with their overall supply patterns typically most evident at the urban scale.39 The precision of ecosystem service function assessments relies on high-resolution assessment units. Therefore, 30-meter raster within Nanjing City was selected as the analytical object for macro-scale ecological security evaluation. Based on the aforementioned theoretical rationale and existing relevant research, although different assessment units may yield certain local numerical variations, these differences will not substantially alter the primary spatial patterns and cross-scale correlations revealed in this study. Therefore, the existing scale division is robust and appropriate for this research.

To conduct a multiscale assessment of ecological security along the NSYR from 2000 to 2023, publicly available geospatial datasets were utilized (Table 1). All spatial data were standardized to a 30 m resolution and aligned using the WGS 1984-50N projection.

Methods summary

In this study, the micro-, meso-, and macro-spatial scales of riverine buffer zones are regarded as the starting point, and using the pressure-state-response (PSR) model as the basis, the pressures exerted by social and economic development on riverine ecosystems at different scales, and the ecological security issues caused by these pressures are examined. The multiscale assessment indicator system for riverine ecological security was obtained, as shown in Table 2, and the outcomes of the weight calculation of each scale are presented in Tables 3, 4, and 5, respectively. At the microscale, we employed the set pair analysis model to evaluate the effect of land use change on ecosystem health, the classification standards corresponding to different levels of assessment indicators are shown in Table 6. At the mesoscale level, we used the spatial distance index model to reveal landscape fragmentation and risk distribution, the specific security classification standards are shown in Table 7. At the macroscale, we used the trapezoidal cloud model to quantify changes in ecosystem service functions, the classification standards for different levels of assessment indicators are shown in Table 8. Through cold hotspot analysis and Moran index, we identified the spatiotemporal aggregation properties of ecological security at various scales between 2000 and 2023. Based on the cold-hotspot analysis results for 2023, core protection and restoration areas at various scales were extracted to develop a multiscale integrated ecological security pattern for the riverine zones in the NSYR. Between 2000 and 2023, the micro-scale ecological security of the Yangtze River’s riverine zone initially declined before showing signs of recovery. Notably, the proportions of Level IV and Level V units experienced two periods of significant decline between 2005 and 2010 and between 2015 and 2020, reaching a low point in 2020 and rebounding significantly in 2023. In terms of spatial distribution, the right bank of the Yangtze River has significantly lower ecological security than the left bank because of higher urbanization and land development intensity, and the continued attraction of new construction projects as economic centers. The right bank decreased along the river, with the core areas of Gulou District and Jianye District, whereas the left bank showed a similar distribution to the chemical industrial park in Liuhe District and Getang Street as the core areas. Overall, the extent of ecological safety was inversely associated with the intensity of economic growth. Between 2000 and 2023, the ecological security classification shifted from Level II to Level IV (661 km2) and from Level II to Level III (469 km2). Although early urbanization and expansion severely weakened micro-scale ecological security, under the directive of the policy: “To step up conservation of the Yangtze River and stop its overdevelopment.” Nanjing delivered commendable results in the ecological regulation of the Yangtze River riverine zone. Between 2021 and 2023, the “Yangtze River Protection” Policy was fully implemented, strictly restricting development along the riverine zone, and the city’s riverine ecological security level rebounded significantly. In 2023, the total share of Level IV and V units rose to 47.66%, while the total share of Level I and II units declined to 13.13%, laying a strong basis for advancing the high-quality growth of the YREB.

Between 2000 and 2023, the general ecological security status of the riverine zone in the NSYR followed a variable rising trend. The proportion of Level IV and Level V units has grown steadily since 2000, declined slightly from 2010 to 2015, and then continued to rise, reaching a peak in 2023. In terms of spatial distribution, higher (Level IV) and high (Level V) security zones were concentrated in strictly protected ecological preserves, including Laoshan National Forest Park and Zhongshan Mountain National Park. Level III medium-security areas were scattered in transitional patches between high- and low-security areas; lower (Level II) and low (Level I) security areas were concentrated in urban core areas, such as Gulou District and Jianye District, and in suburban industrial parks, such as Jiangning, Liuhe, and Qixia. These areas exhibit severely hardened shorelines and highly fragmented ecological corridors. Overall, there was a “core-transition-periphery” pattern with high-security areas at the core and decreasing security levels toward the periphery. Between 2000 and 2023, the ecological security level changed from Level IV to Level III (2935 km2) and from Level III to Level II (1388 km2). The high security zone centered on Laoshan and Zhongshan will continue to expand to the surrounding areas, with a significant increase in the areas of Levels IV and V by 2023, indicating the remarkable effectiveness of nature reserve management. The low-security zone centered on suburban industrial parks continued to shrink, with most areas upgraded to Level III, reflecting the initial effectiveness of the ecological restoration measures.

Between 2000 and 2023, the macro-scale ecological security status of the riverine zone in the NSYR exhibited a general downward trend: the proportion of Level IV and Level V units declined between 2000 and 2005, rebounded between 2005 and 2010, and continued to decline from 2010 to 2023. In terms of spatial distribution, high (Level V) security zones were concentrated in nature reserves and radiated outwards, maintaining excellent ecosystem services. Higher (Level IV) security zones were distributed in blocks, mainly in central-south and northwestern Nanjing, owing to differences in year and socioeconomic pressures. Medium (Level III) security zones are scattered between high- and low-security zones, serving as a transition zone from well to poor ecological service functions. Low (Level I) and lower (Level II) security zones extend outwards from highly urbanized centers, such as Gulou, Jianye, and Pukou. Owing to high population density and land shortages caused by the “siphon effect,” these areas suffered the most severe degradation of ecological service functions. Overall, a “central siphon” pattern was evident: intensive development and population concentration in urban centers have led to a decline in ecological service functions, further exacerbating urban expansion and infrastructure construction and causing ecological degradation to spread from the center outwards. By 2023, the area transformed from Level III to Level V is 3781 km2, and the area transformed from Level IV to Level III is 3201 km2. The proportion of low-security zones increased from 44.18% in 2000 to 60.53%, indicating that rapid urbanization has caused significant damage to ecosystem service functions and that there is an urgent need for ecological restoration strategies tailored to local conditions.

Spatial autocorrelation analysis

Spatial autocorrelation analysis of micro-scale ecological security

The global autocorrelation tool in ArcGIS Pro was used to compute Moran’s index for the microscale ecological security of the riverine zone in the NSYR between 2000 and 2023, yielding values of 0.328, 0.355, 0.112, 0.162, 0.103, and 0.137, respectively. All p values were lower than 0.05, signifying statistical relevance. Ecological security exhibited a positive spatial correlation and a distinct clustering pattern.

The Getis-Ord Gi∗ global autocorrelation cold-hotspot analysis tool in ArcGIS Pro was employed to derive the spatial pattern of hotspots and cold spots in the microscale ecological security of the riverine zone within the NSYR (Figure 10). According to the spatial significance test, the average p value for hotspot zones (Z(Gi∗) > 1.9) was 0.0138, whereas the average p value for cold spot zones (Z(Gi∗) < −1.9) was 0.0128. The spatial distribution results indicated that one hotspot and one cold-spot existed within the study region. The hotspot zone was situated in the eastern portion of the riverine zone in the NSYR, encompassing Qixia Street and Longtan Street in Qixia District and Longpao Street in Liuhe District. The land use in this hotspot region is primarily agricultural and forestland, functioning as key bases for farming and forestry with minimal construction land expansion, thereby contributing to a high degree of ecological security. The cold spot area was located in the areas under the jurisdiction of Gulou District, Jianye District, Qixia District (part), and Pukou District, within the middle section of the riverine zone in the NSYR. The right bank of the Yangtze River serves as Nanjing’s administrative and economic hub, characterized by highly concentrated construction land. The left bank is the core area of a new national-level district with a similarly high density of construction land, leading to a markedly reduced level of ecological security. Discussion

A riverine zone is defined as an area in which water, land, and human environments interact and intersect. This intricate ecosystem supports both natural ecology and human activities. It is also a crucial resource for maintaining a balance between human social development and environmental protection. Riverine ecosystems play pivotal roles in ecological functions at diverse spatial scales. They serve as vital habitats for amphibious organisms, represent integral components of the ecological landscape, and are pivotal in the provision of regional ecosystem services. However, the rapid progression of socioeconomic development has resulted in a discordance between limited land resources and rapid population growth, leading to disorderly urban expansion. The excessive utilization of riverine resources and significant degradation of riverine ecosystems at various scales have placed limitations on the sustainable progress of human society.

Positioned as a major urban center spanning the Yangtze River, Nanjing has experienced swift socioeconomic expansion along its riverine zones. This growth has intensified the conflict between environmental conservation and economic advancement, making the city a focal point of ecological tension within the YREB. In this research, a micro-meso-macro multiscale evaluation framework was developed to assess the ecological security of the riverine zones in the NSYR from 2000 to 2023. The key findings are summarized as follows.

1.The ecological stability of the riverine zones along the NSYR exhibits evident spatial heterogeneity across all scales, with notable disparities in ecological security across different areas. Zones with high ecological resilience were primarily found near nature reserves, while regions with low security were concentrated in densely urbanized districts and industrial zones. Across all scales, ecological sustainability was negatively correlated with economic development levels. Additionally, the ecological condition of each evaluated unit was influenced by the status of neighboring units, an important factor to consider when formulating strategies for ecological restoration and conservation.

Methodological innovations

The crux of the discourse surrounding riverine ecological security concerns the discord between the demands of social and economic development, on one end, and the demands of ecological protection, on the other end. This discrepancy manifests in divergent ways across diverse spatial scales and exerts varying effects on ecosystems. The scientific definition of the research scale constitutes a pivotal component in assessing riverine ecological security.38 The methodological innovation of this study lies in proposing a comprehensive assessment approach for riverine ecological security across multiple scales and dimensions. It assesses riverbanks at micro, meso, and macro scales, focusing on three dimensions: ecosystem health, ecological risk, and ecosystem service functions, respectively. Direct disturbances such as environmental pollution caused by industrial facility construction along the Yangtze River and ecological corridor blockages caused by port engineering have harmed the composition and functionality of riverine ecosystems. The fragmentation of landscape patterns and weakening of ecosystems driven by human economic activities in the riverine hinterland have exacerbated the potential risks to ecosystems through indirect channels. Furthermore, the intensification of human-land conflicts, precipitated by the rapid urbanization of cities along the riverine zones, has further contributed to the decline of regional ecological service functions. Given the complex nature of ecological security assessment, drawing on the findings of related researchers,21,22 town-level administrative units within the 5-km buffer zone of the NSYR, grid cells with spatial resolution uniformly standardized to 30 m within the 20-km buffer zone, and 30-m raster cells for the entire Nanjing City area to construct micro, meso, and macro assessment units, respectively. The set-pair analysis model, spatial distance index model, and trapezoidal cloud model were applied to complete the assessments of riparian ecological security at micro, meso, and macro scales.

Policy implications

The results of a multiscale assessment of riverbank ecological security in the NSYR showed that since the formal enforcement of the Yangtze River Protection Law of the People’s Republic of China in 2021, the level of riverbank ecological security at the micro and meso scales has shown a significant upward trend, with a clear agglomeration effect, proving that the comprehensive implementation of the “Yangtze River Protection” policy has been effective. However, at the macroscale, the riverine ecological security has continued to decline, a trend that is spatiotemporally consistent with rapid urban expansion. Current ecological restoration policies have achieved measurable success in restoring riverine ecosystem health and reducing local ecological risks, but they remain inadequate in addressing the deterioration of macroscale ecosystem services driven by increasingly intensified human-land conflicts under rapid urbanization. The degradation of ecosystem services at the macroscale indirectly endangers the ecological security of the Yangtze River riverine zone, and the swift expansion of the urban economy must not occur at the cost of the degradation of ecosystem services. Therefore, establishing relevant policies and mechanisms from the macro perspective of urban development planning, optimizing urban planning and development models, promoting economic and industrial transformation, coordinating ecological protection and industrial development, and aiming to attain a balance between urban economic progress and the preservation of macro-ecosystem services is essential.

Comparison with existing studies

Current research by relevant scholars has primarily focused on ecological assessments at a single scale and within a single dimension.28,29,30,31,33 The novelty of this study lies in the research of cross-scale correlations in ecological security. The results of this study provided an important supplement to the existing studies by focusing on cross-scale correlation between ecological security at different scales along the riverine. The multi-scale integrated ecological security pattern of the NSYR (Figure 12) revealed a certain degree of cross-scale correlation between ecological security at different scales. High consistency exists between mesoscale and macroscale ecological security levels. Key ecological security restoration buffer zones at the mesoscale highly co-occur with macroscale ecosystem service function restoration zones, while key ecological security buffer zones at the mesoscale also co-occur with a significant proportion of macroscale ecosystem service function protection zones. The relationship between micro-scale ecological security and meso- and macro-scale ecological security is more complex: Micro-scale core ecological security restoration belts co-occur highly with meso-scale key ecological security restoration buffer zones and macro-scale ecosystem service function restoration zones. while micro-scale core ecological security protection belts coexist within the overlap zone of meso-scale key ecological security restoration zones and macro-scale ecosystem service function restoration zones. This indicates that across the three dimensions of riverine ecological security, low ecosystem health levels highly correlate with both high ecological risks and low ecosystem service functions. Conversely, high ecosystem health levels do not necessarily prevent the occurrence of high ecological risks and low ecosystem service functions.

Limitations of the study

Opportunities exist to refine the research approach used to examine the ecological security of the NSYR at different scales. The combination weighting method based on Monte Carlo simulation employed in this study enhances the rationality of indicator weights. However, it remained influenced by the uncertainty of expert subjective judgments and the uncertainty of the objective weighting sample distribution. Consequently, this affected the calculation of Monte Carlo expectations, introducing uncertainty into the weight calculation results. The assessment models employed in this study, the set pair analysis model, spatial distance index model, and trapezoidal cloud model, were all related their calculation results to the grading criteria of evaluation indicators. While these grading criteria were determined by referencing existing scholarly research, they may not fully align with the specific context of the NSYR. This discrepancy introduces uncertainty into the calculation outcomes of the assessment models. In addition, owing to data collection limitations, the data from different sources in this study had heterogeneous spatial and temporal resolutions and statistical criteria, and the resulting uncertainty may have influenced the reliability of the assessment outcome. Therefore, future research should incorporate quantitative analysis of uncertainties in the aforementioned aspects to enhance the reliability of assessment results. Ecological security in riverine ecosystems is a complicated socioecological system exhibiting significant variation across spatial scales. Although analytical frameworks at micro, meso, and macro scales were constructed, the interaction mechanisms and hierarchical transmission relationships between ecological processes at different scales were not comprehensively analyzed. Accordingly, future research should entail more in-depth quantitative analysis of the multiscale coupling relationship of riverine ecosystems, reveal the trade-off and coordination mechanisms among ecological elements through the construction of cross-scale dynamic models, and provide a more systematic theoretical basis for revealing the evolutionary laws of riverine ecological security.

Method details

Multiscale ecological security assessment framework

Riverine ecosystems have both natural and social attributes, and their ecological security issues interact and are intertwined at multiple scales, urgently necessitating a scientific and reliable assessment framework. In this study, the micro-, meso-, and macro-spatial scales of riverine buffer zones are regarded as the starting point, and using the pressure-state-response (PSR) model as the basis, the pressures exerted by social and economic development on riverine ecosystems at different scales, and the ecological security issues caused by these pressures are examined. At the microscale, we employed the set pair analysis model to evaluate the effect of land use change on ecosystem health. At the mesoscale level, we used the spatial distance index model to reveal landscape fragmentation and risk distribution. At the macroscale, we used the trapezoidal cloud model to quantify changes in ecosystem service functions. Through cold hotspot analysis and Moran index, we identified the spatiotemporal aggregation properties of ecological security at various scales between 2000 and 2023. Based on the cold-hotspot analysis results for 2023, core protection and restoration areas at various scales were extracted to develop a multiscale integrated ecological security pattern for the riverine zones in the NSYR (Figure 2).

Construction of assessment indicator system

The PSR model is extensively utilized in analyzing complex system assessment indicators and is considered to be one of the most effective methods.40,41 The PSR framework is based on the dynamic interaction between human activity and the ecological environment, where anthropogenic pressures alter environmental conditions, prompting adaptive responses from both society and nature. Through this logical framework, the PSR model comprehensively captures the dynamics between humans and nature, thereby helping decision-makers to track ecological conditions more effectively, identify major ecological and environmental issues, and formulate and evaluate environmental policies. Riverine zones have both natural and social attributes. Under human development and utilization, ecological security has characteristics such as comprehensiveness, spatial heterogeneity, dynamism, and multiscale nature. The multiscale assessment indicators of riverine ecological security cover a wide range of factors involving complex interrelationships. The PSR model can comprehensively reflect the mutual feedback relationship between nature and society in an ecosystem, and has strong systematicity and clear causal relationships. Therefore, the PSR model was selected as the structural model in this study to construct a multiscale assessment indicator system for riverine ecological security.

The assessment of riverine ecological security was divided into three scales: micro-, meso-, and macro-scales, each focusing on different ecological security issues. An indicator system was developed using the PSR model, and relevant metrics were chosen to address the core issues of each scale. Microscale assessments focus on land use and its changes in the riverine zone, assessing the direct impact of human development activities on ecosystem health. Therefore, in this study, the assessment of ecological security at the micro-scale corresponds to the assessment of riverine ecosystem health. Mesoscale assessment focuses on landscape ecological elements (such as patches and landscape systems) in the hinterlands of riverine areas and involves analysis of the ecological risks caused by increased landscape fragmentation and vulnerability triggered by human activities. Therefore, in this study, the assessment of ecological security at the meso-scale corresponds to the assessment of ecological risk within the riverine hinterland. The macroscale assessment focuses on human-land interaction in cities radiating from the riverine zone, identifying the resulting indirect ecological pressures and their impact on ecological service functions and resource-carrying capacity. Therefore, in this study, the assessment of ecological security at the macro-scale corresponds to the assessment of the ecosystem service functions of the urban areas radiated by the riverine zones. Based on the multiscale framework shown in Figure 3 and combining the research results of relevant scholars.15,22 First, the functional structure and security needs of riverine zones were determined, then the principles of scientificity, representativeness, scale correspondence, and feasibility were identified, and 30 indicators that reflect ecosystem health, ecological risk, and ecosystem service functions were selected. Finally, a multiscale assessment indicator system for riverine ecological security was obtained, as shown in Table 2. Determination of combination weights

Subjective weighting methods can reflect decision-makers' preferences but often lack objectivity. Objective weighting methods can reveal the statistical characteristics of indicator data, but are highly dependent on raw data and difficult to integrate with decision-makers' intentions. Single weighting methods have limitations, and their results are prone to being influenced by extreme values or subjective biases. A combined weighting strategy can integrate the advantages of various methods and compensate for their shortcomings, thereby achieving a more reasonable weight allocation. This study employs the Analytic Hierarchy Process (AHP)42 to calculate subjective weights, the Entropy Weighting Method (EWM),43 and the Coefficient of Variation Method (CVM)44 to obtain objective weights. It combines these with existing research19 to introduce a Monte Carlo simulation, integrating the three single-weighting methods into an optimized decision-making model to balance subjective preferences and objective data characteristics, thereby generating an ideal combined weight distribution.

In this study, the uncertainty of different indicator weights was quantified based on the triangular probability function in the Monte Carlo simulation probability distribution function according to the upper, lower, and middle values of each indicator. Spatial autocorrelation analysis

The ArcGIS Moran index and cold hotspot analysis were used to conduct ecological security spatial clustering analysis. The Moran index is the most commonly used global autocorrelation index, which can effectively identify the spatial aggregation or discrete distribution patterns of geographical phenomena.50 Cold hotspot analysis uses the Getis-Ord Gi∗ statistical method to identify spatial aggregation patterns, and then determines the spatial locations of aggregation areas with high or low levels of ecological security.51

Quantification and statistical analysis

In this study, we used ArcGIS Pro 3.4 for data analysis. The Getis-Ord Gi∗ function was used to analyze the cold spots and hotspots of ecological security at different scales. The Global Moran’s I function was used to calculate the Moran index of ecological security at different scales. The Raster Calculator function was used to determine the ecological security level at different scales. We used Fragstats 4.2 to calculate the landscape indicators. We used InVEST 3.1.4 Workbench to calculate the ecosystem service indicators. The Monte Carlo simulation was implemented in Python 3.10 and programmed using PyCharm Community Edition 2024.1.2 to calculate the combination weights of indicators.
