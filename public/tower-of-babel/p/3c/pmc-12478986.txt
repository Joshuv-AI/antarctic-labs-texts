Basidiomycete Yeasts of Wine Grapes and Their Potential Applications in Winemaking
Authors: Adele Bunbury‐Blanchette, Lihua Fan, Allison Walker, Gavin Kernaghan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTVineyards support highly diverse communities of native yeasts, but only a small proportion are fermentative ascomycetes capable of alcoholic fermentation. Many non‐fermentative species are also present, including a range of metabolically active basidiomycete yeasts that can influence wine aromatic profiles, especially in the early stages of fermentation. In some cases, basidiomycete yeasts, such as Filobasidium, Rhodotorula, Sporobolomyces, and Vishniacozyma, are more abundant and diverse than ascomycete yeasts in grape musts, with some persisting throughout fermentation. As the existing information on the role of basidiomycete yeasts in winemaking is fragmented, we synthesize the records of these yeasts in association with wine grapes and musts, as well as the research on their potential applications in winemaking. Basidiomycete yeasts are gaining attention for their unique biochemical contributions to wine flavor, influencing sensory attributes through the production of metabolites such as acids, higher alcohols, aldehydes, ketones, esters, and glycerol, as well as by their enzymatic activities and by the production or utilization of resources used by fermentative yeasts. Basidiomycete yeasts play especially important roles in shaping spontaneously fermented wines (which rely solely on the yeasts present on the grapes) and have the potential to help produce wines with increased aromatic complexity.

Introduction

Vineyards harbor a great diversity of yeasts, which persist from grapes to the must (juice) and influence wine fermentations (Fleet 2003). Although alcoholic fermentation is ultimately completed by one or few ascomycete yeasts that are tolerant of low‐oxygen and high‐ethanol conditions, such as species of Saccharomyces, Lachancea, and Torulaspora, many other yeasts lacking the capacity for alcoholic fermentation are also metabolically active, especially during the early stages of fermentation (Jolly et al. 2006). These include a variety of other ascomycetous yeasts, such as species of Aureobasidium, Hanseniaspora, Candida, and Metschnikowia, that can impact the aromatic profile of the wine through the production of many different compounds other than ethanol (Strauss et al. 2001; Capozzi et al. 2015; Borren and Tian 2021).

The community of non‐fermentative yeasts present in musts and wines also includes a high diversity of basidiomycete yeasts (forming basidia in their sexual states), which may constitute a large proportion of the yeasts present throughout the fermentation process and impact wine flavor and aroma (Englezos et al. 2022; Wei et al. 2022; Gao et al. 2024). Basidiomycete yeasts such as Filobasidium, Rhodotorula, Sporobolomyces, and Vishniacozyma are common on grape skins (Renouf et al. 2005; Gao et al. 2019; Martiniuk et al. 2023), leaves and vines (both on surfaces and as endophytes) (Sampaio et al. 2024; Kernaghan et al. 2017), as well as in vineyard soils (Gobbi et al. 2022). They conduct aerobic catabolism, and although non‐fermentative, they are nonetheless enzymatically active, and able to utilize a variety of substrates, including pentoses and sugar alcohols (Fell et al. 2001; Johnson 2013).

Basidiomycete yeasts often represent large proportions of the total yeast abundance and species richness on grapes and in musts. For example, Sporidiobolus pararoseus represented 40% of the yeasts isolated from Slovakian grapes (Nemcová et al. 2015), and next‐generation sequencing showed that species of Filobasidium and Vishniacozyma together represented 46% of the yeast community in Canadian musts (Bunbury‐Blanchette et al. 2023). The latter study also detected 133 species of basidiomycete yeasts in musts, but only 28 ascomycete yeasts.

Although basidiomycete yeasts are generally replaced by fermentative yeasts such as Saccharomyces spp. and Hanseniaspora uvarum as oxygen levels decrease and ethanol increases during fermentation (Bunbury‐Blanchette et al. 2023), some may be tolerant of late fermentation conditions, such as Sporobolomyces roseus and Vishniacozyma heimaeyensis (Martins et al. 2022). In one case, species of Vishniacozyma still represented over 50% of the yeast community after a 25‐day ice wine fermentation (Chen et al. 2020).

Although they have received some attention in the context of biotechnology (Johnson 2013), basidiomycete yeasts have largely been overlooked in wine research. It is clear that they play an important role in winemaking, but we are just beginning to understand the interactions among the basidiomycete yeasts and other components of wine fermentations. Despite the reduction of yeast populations in conventional winemaking by the addition of sulfites to avoid spoilage organisms, basidiomycete yeasts are still likely to influence the trajectory of all wine fermentations by producing secondary metabolites such as acids, higher alcohols, aldehydes, ketones, esters, and glycerol; enzymatically modifying plant cell wall constituents and aroma precursors; and producing or depleting resources that are in turn required by fermentative yeasts.

Basidiomycete yeasts may have especially important roles in shaping wines made by spontaneous fermentation, which relies solely on the yeasts present on the grape skins (Ciani et al. 2004; Čuš et al. 2017; Lu et al. 2020), and as inoculants in mixed inoculum fermentations (using multiple yeast species) with fermentative yeasts (Rossouw and Bauer 2016; X. C. Wang et al. 2017). Both of these fermentation methods can produce wines with more complex sensory attributes and a different range of aromas than fermentations inoculated with Saccharomyces cerevisiae alone (Varela et al. 2009; Calabretti et al. 2012). However, it is difficult to assess the roles of basidiomycete yeasts due to the fragmented nature of the existing information. Therefore, in this review, we summarize the record of basidiomycete yeasts associated with wine grapes and musts and consolidate research on the roles and potential applications of these yeasts in winemaking into a single resource to facilitate further research.

Literature characterizing the yeast community on wine grape surfaces, grape musts, and wine was searched with Google Scholar, using combinations of terms including basidiomycete yeasts, oxidative yeasts, non‐fermentative yeasts, non‐Saccharomyces yeasts, spontaneous fermentation, wine, grape, vineyard, wine quality and flavors. We also searched for literature based on key basidiomycete yeast genera (Table S1).

Grape‐Associated Basidiomycete Yeasts

Microbial community characterization methods may use culture‐dependent assessments of community composition or directly assess DNA within the sample material (e.g., must). Prior to the availability of next‐generation sequencing, methods such as PCR‐DGGE and cloning were used, but basidiomycete yeasts were often not detected (Mills et al. 2002; Di Maro et al. 2007; Nisiotou et al. 2007; Urso et al. 2008), species resolution was often better using culture‐dependent means (Prakitchaiwattana et al. 2004; Renouf et al. 2005; Brežná et al. 2010; Milanović et al. 2013; David et al. 2014; Ženišová et al. 2014; Escribano‐Viana et al. 2018), and studies using any one of these methods are relatively few in number. Therefore, only studies using culture‐dependent or next‐generation sequencing were considered.

There is a clear record that a wide variety of basidiomycete yeast genera are present on grapes and in musts, despite the shortcomings of identification methods. Culture‐dependent studies often fail to isolate, or at least report, basidiomycete yeasts, and next‐generation sequencing studies often only report species making up some minimum proportion of sequences, or those determined to be significantly correlated with a variable of interest.

Regardless, culture‐dependent and next‐generation sequencing methods tend to reveal similar dominant basidiomycete yeasts on grapes and musts, including Rhodotorula (e.g., R. babjevae, R. glutinis, R. mucilaginosa, and R. minuta), Papiliotrema (e.g., P. flavescens and P. terrestris), Filobasidium (e.g., F. globisporum and F. magnum), Sporobolomyces shibatanus, Curvibasidium cygneicollum, Vishniacozyma carnescens, and Cryptococcus spp. (J. Li et al. 2018, 2019; Rantsiou et al. 2020; Drumonde‐Neves et al. 2021; Bunbury‐Blanchette et al. 2023; Bunbury‐Blanchette et al. 2024). However, given that most wine grape and must associated species of Cryptococcus have been reclassified to other genera, Cryptococcus should no longer be considered as one of the most common constituents of these habitats.

Factors Influencing Basidiomycete Yeast Populations and Communities

Although a comprehensive evaluation of the literature considering wine grape and must yeast communities was conducted, direct comparisons among community analyses are difficult—a challenge that is repeatedly noted in the context of wine grape yeast communities due to differences in study design (Fleet 2003; Barata et al. 2012; Sumby et al. 2021). In this light, we encourage caution in assigning causality based on the presence or abundance of taxonomic groups, as there are many interacting components that affect basidiomycete yeast community composition. These include numerous sub‐factors associated with geography, climate, cultivation practices, sampling regime, grape cultivar, condition of grapes, substrate sampled (e.g., grape skins, fresh pressed must, and fermenting must), and fermentation practices (Pretorius et al. 1999; Barata et al. 2012; Bordet et al. 2020; Sumby et al. 2021). These factors act in synergy to create the unique and dynamic terroir, that is, the character of the wine as influenced by environmental and cultivation conditions of a vineyard, or even areas of micro‐terroir within a vineyard (Pretorius et al. 1999; Barata et al. 2012; Sumby et al. 2021).

Basidiomycete yeast species abundances on grapes, in grape musts, and during fermentation also depend on the presence and proportions of one another and on other microbes. For example, yeasts interact with filamentous fungi on grapes, as each produces metabolites that may influence the growth of the other (Fleet 2003). Several basidiomycete yeasts, including Papiliotrema laurentii, Vishniacozyma victoriae, and Rhodotorula spp., may be antagonistic to mycotoxigenic filamentous fungi in the vineyard via the formation of biofilms (aggregations of adhesive cells) on the fruit surface, mycoparasitism, the secretion of defensive enzymes, or by inducing host resistance (Oztekin et al. 2023). The presence of these yeasts, whether naturally occurring or enhanced as part of a biocontrol strategy, alters the fungal community composition.

The actual yeast community, as influenced by these environmental factors, is represented with varying accuracy and precision by community characterization methods. Researchers target specific functional or taxonomic groups of yeasts, either deliberately or inadvertently, by variation in methods for isolation, sequencing, identification, and analysis, and by incorporating cutoffs below which yeasts are not detected or are not reported. Finally, changes to yeast taxonomy also complicate comparisons among studies, and several common genera have undergone extensive systematic revisions since they were first reported as wine grape associates, with further revisions no doubt to come.

Geography and Climate

Some basidiomycete yeast species have been associated with climate or location, despite confounding factors such as the increased use of pesticides in years with higher rainfall (Barata et al. 2012). For example, the genera Filobasidium and Naganisha, including F. magnum and N. albida, have been recorded in greater abundance in dryer, cooler, sunnier regions in China (Longo et al. 1991; Yanagida et al. 1992; R. Li et al. 2021, 2022). In agreement, a global meta‐analysis of vineyard yeasts suggested that Filobasidium and Dioszegia prefer cooler climate vineyards (Drumonde‐Neves et al. 2021). However, this may not always hold true at finer scales, as Filobasidium was most abundant in a Croatian coastal vineyard with relatively high maximum temperatures (Milanović et al. 2022).

The relative abundance of Rhodotorula has also been linked to geographical regions (Kioroglou et al. 2019), in some cases seeming to prefer cooler, sunnier, and/or more humid environments (Yanagida et al. 1992; Lederer et al. 2013; R. Li et al. 2021, 2022) and higher elevations (Li et al. 2021; Milanović et al. 2022), although in other research these effects are not present (Longo et al. 1991; Jolly et al. 2003). Higher abundances of other genera correlated to climate variables include Curvibasidium cygneicollum with low levels of sun and high elevation (R. Li et al. 2021), Papiliotrema with temperature (although inconsistencies suggest interacting effects) (Yanagida et al. 1992; R. Li et al. 2022), Udeniomyces with higher precipitation (Steenwerth et al. 2021), and Cystobasidium and Sporobolomyces with higher temperatures and precipitation (Milanović et al. 2022). Furthermore, Gao et al. (2019) linked basidiomycete yeast groups at higher taxonomic assignments with geographic areas that differed in number of frost‐free days, dryness, altitude, and average temperature.

Multiple studies have correlated basidiomycete yeast species abundances, including species of Cryptococcus, Cystobasidium, Piskurozyma, Rhodotorula, Papiliotrema, Sporobolomyces, Udeniomyces, and Vishniacozyma, with location at the scale of region (Longo et al. 1991; Taylor et al. 2014; Pinto et al. 2015; Tronchoni et al. 2022), sub‐region or individual vineyard (Raspor et al. 2006; Gayevskiy and Goddard 2012; Liu et al. 2021; X. Wang, Schlatter, et al. 2021; Milanović et al. 2022; Martiniuk et al. 2023), or even location within a single vineyard (Setati et al. 2012; Vaudano et al. 2019). Considered as a whole, research that has connected vineyard basidiomycete yeast abundances to geographic or climatic variables strongly suggests variation according to these variables, but with effects being highly moderated by other factors.

Cultivation Practices

Yeast community composition, including that of basidiomycete yeasts, differs among vineyards employing different cultivation practices (Comitini and Ciani 2008; Setati et al. 2012; Milanović et al. 2013; Martins et al. 2014; Setati et al. 2015; Castañeda et al. 2018; Castrillo et al. 2019; Bunbury‐Blanchette et al. 2024). However, there are few consistencies in species‐level effects, likely due in part to the variation in fungicide regimes within management practices. For example, while organic and biodynamic vineyards may use only copper‐ or sulfur‐based fungicides, conventional vineyards may also use these products, in addition to synthetic fungicides, and may choose to apply them more often and in higher concentrations than some organic vineyards. Therefore, correlations between cultivation practice and yeasts susceptible to copper or sulfur fungicides are unlikely to be observed.

Indeed, depending on species, higher proportions of Rhodotorula have been linked to each of biodynamic (Setati et al. 2012), organic (W. Xu et al. 2020; Castrillo and Blanco 2022), integrated (Setati et al. 2012; Bagheri et al. 2015), and conventional cultivation practices (Milanović et al. 2013; Martins et al. 2014; W. Xu et al. 2020; Castrillo and Blanco 2022; Bunbury‐Blanchette et al. 2024). Associations with cultivation practices are similarly mixed for Filobasidium, Naganishia, Papiliotrema, and Vishniacozyma (Comitini and Ciani 2008; Setati et al. 2012; Milanović et al. 2013; Rantsiou et al. 2020; W. Xu et al. 2020; Castrillo and Blanco 2022; Perpetuini et al. 2022; Bunbury‐Blanchette et al. 2024). However, research to date most often demonstrates that Sporobolomyces is positively associated with conventionally managed vineyards (Martins et al. 2014; Kecskeméti et al. 2016; Rantsiou et al. 2020; Bunbury‐Blanchette et al. 2024), although the opposite result was found by W. Xu et al. (2020), perhaps reflecting differential concentrations of copper in the vineyards (Martins et al. 2014, 2022).

Grape Cultivars

Few grape cultivars have been included in multiple comparisons of grape yeast communities. Members of common basidiomycete yeast genera, such as Rhodotorula and Sporobolomyces, are often present in similar abundances among grape cultivars (S. S. Li et al. 2010; Brysch‐Herzberg and Seidel 2015), while cultivars that host differing abundances of rarer yeasts cannot confidently be assigned as causal. Although some authors suggest preferences for red versus white cultivars (Raspor et al. 2006; Li et al. 2010), the larger body of evidence is inconsistent and insufficient to support claims either way. Overall, more research is needed to better understand connections between cultivars and yeast abundances.

Nevertheless, taxa including Filobasidium and the closely related Naganisha (Cureau, Threlfall, Marasini, et al. 2021; R. Li et al. 2021; Tronchoni et al. 2022), Dioszegia, C. cygneicollum, Papiliotrema, and Rhodotorula (Cureau, Threlfall, Marasini, et al. 2021; Tronchoni et al. 2022) have been reported to be more abundant in Cabernet Sauvignon musts than those of other cultivars. However, associations with cultivars likely interact with geographical factors, demonstrated by higher abundances of P. flavescens on Cabernet Sauvignon in only two of four regions sampled (S. S. Li et al. 2010). Other possible associations between basidiomycete yeasts and cultivars include the genera Cryptococcus, Curvibasidium, Cystofilobasidium, Entyloma, Filobasidium, Papiliotrema, and species C. nothofagi, Cystobasidium minutum, Dioszegia hungarica, Microstroma bacarum, N. albida, R. glutinis, R. mucilaginosa, Sporobolomyces phaffii, and S. roseus with various cultivars including Chardonnay, Green Veltliner, Leon Millot, Pinor Noir, Sauvignon Blanc, Syrah, and Zinfandel (Yanagida et al. 1992; Raspor et al. 2006; Lederer et al. 2013; Nemcová et al. 2015; S. Zhang et al. 2019; Cureau, Threlfall, Marasini, et al. 2021; Liu et al. 2021). However, the strengths of these associations were dependent on the year of sampling (Nemcová et al. 2015) and region (Yanagida et al. 1992), highlighting the fact that cultivar effects interact with several other variables, including climate.

Cultivar preferences may be due to variation in skin thickness, bunch tightness, pH, sugar level, and nutrient availability, which may be influenced in turn by yearly variation in climate (Lederer et al. 2013; Cioch‐Skoneczny et al. 2018). For example, higher relative abundances of Sporobolomyces and Cystobasidium were negatively correlated with glucose concentration in Maraština musts (Milanović et al. 2022), indicating that these yeast genera may generally be more abundant in grape cultivars that are less sweet at the time of harvest.

Grape Ripeness and Damage

Regardless of cultivar, grape ripeness and degree of damage affect the yeast community composition. Differences are likely due in part to biochemical and physiological differences in grapes that are intrinsic to ripening, and differences in visitation by insects as the plants transition from flowering to fruiting and then as the grapes ripen (Zhu et al. 2021). Basidiomycete yeasts are generally thought to be more abundant on sound berries that offer fewer available nutrients, while ripe berries release juices, even if they remain visually intact, that better support ascomycete yeasts (Barata et al. 2012). Indeed, in many cases, basidiomycete yeasts are more abundant at earlier stages of ripening (Renouf et al. 2007b; Mateo et al. 2020; Liu and Howell 2021; X. Wang, Schlatter, et al. 2021; Costantini et al. 2022) or reach peak abundance at an intermediate stage prior to harvest (Renouf et al. 2005; Zhu et al. 2021). Some basidiomycete yeast species have been directly linked to berry damage, such as the greater abundance of S. shibatanus found on intact rather than damaged grapes (Nemcová et al. 2015). Conversely, other studies have found the relative abundance of basidiomycete yeasts to increase from earlier stages of ripening to harvest, generally led by increased populations of Vishniacozyma (Zhu et al. 2021; Liu and Howell 2021; X. Wang, Schlatter, et al. 2021), Papiliotrema (Renouf et al. 2005), Filobasidium (Ding et al. 2021; Liu and Howell 2021; X. Wang, Schlatter, et al. 2021), and/or Naganishia (Renouf et al. 2005; Ding et al. 2021), although results may be vineyard dependent.

Fermentation Dynamics

The nature of wine fermentation is such that sugars, nutrients, and oxygen are abundant at the start, supporting a high diversity of microorganisms, but these resources become depleted and replaced by secondary metabolites, creating a less hospitable environment (Fleet 2003), and basidiomycete yeasts do not usually persist to the end of fermentation. In many instances, a wide variety of genera are not detected by early to mid‐ferment (Fleet et al. 1984; Combina et al. 2005; Renouf, Claisse, et al. 2007; Milanović et al. 2013; Ultee et al. 2013; Bagheri et al. 2015; Nemcová et al. 2015; Pinto et al. 2015; Sternes et al. 2017; J. Li et al. 2018; Bougreau et al. 2019; Chen et al. 2020; Liu et al. 2020; Lu et al. 2020; W. Xu et al. 2020; Kamilari et al. 2021; R. Li et al. 2021; Englezos et al. 2022; Martiniuk et al. 2023). For example, Bunbury‐Blanchette et al. (2023) found that V. carnescens represented 20%, and F. globisporum and F. magnum together accounted for up to 25%, of the yeasts in white wine musts, but each fell to below 5% following fermentation.

In some cases, the decrease in basidiomycete yeast abundance occurs in the early stages of fermentation. For example, J. Li et al. (2019) found that Cryptococcus flavescens (now P. flavescens) and C. magnus (now F. magnus) together represented 39% of yeasts in must, but only 8.0% at day 4, after which they became undetectable. Filobasidium floriforme was also only detected up to day 4, with a maximum abundance (22%) on day 2. Sporidiobolus pararoseus, R. babjevae, and R. glutinis were also only detectable in the early stages of fermentation.

However, some basidiomycete yeasts may be more persistent, decreasing in abundance later in the fermentation. This has most often been reported for Filobasidium, including species F. magnum and F. chernovii (David et al. 2014; Chen et al. 2020; W. Xu et al. 2020; Kamilari et al. 2021), but also S. roseus and Bullera alba (David et al. 2014), Cryptococcus (De Filippis et al. 2017), Symmetrospora, Naganishia, and Piskurozyma (Chen et al. 2020), and Udeniomyces puniceus, C. macerans, and C. cygneicollum (Kamilari et al. 2021). J. Li et al. (2018) found that C. flavescens (now P. flavescens), Cryptococcus sp. (BEA 2010) and Rhodotorula sp. (RhSo W01) increased from between 40% to close to 400% of their original abundance in must by day 15 of fermentation but then decreased to between 8% and 18% of their original levels by the final sampling time (day 30), indicating that for these species, conditions were conducive to growth for at least the first half of the fermentation process. These yeasts may have some tolerance to reduced oxygen and increased ethanol, and they can utilize compounds produced earlier in fermentation, but are unable to survive late‐fermentation conditions (Fleet 2003).

On the other hand, a few basidiomycete yeasts may maintain a relatively high, or at least a consistent, abundance until the end of fermentation, as demonstrated for Cystofilobasidium (Bokulich et al. 2016; Chen et al. 2020), Curvibasidium pallidicorallinum (J. Li et al. 2018), P. flavescens (J. Li et al. 2018; R. Li et al. 2021), Rhodotorula spp. (Díaz et al. 2013; J. Li et al. 2018; R. Li et al. 2021; Liu et al. 2021), Vishniacozyma spp. (Chen et al. 2020; Liu et al. 2020), and Filobasidium and Sporobolomyces (H. L. Wang, Hopfer, et al. 2021). An unclassified Sporidiobolaceae, most likely Rhodotorula or Sporobolomyces, also persisted to the completion of fermentation and even increased in abundance in some treatments (Cureau, Threlfall, Carbonero, et al. 2021). In fact, Martins et al. (2022) demonstrated that R. glutinis, B. alba, S. roseus, and V. heimaeyensis exhibited tolerance to osmotic stress and maintained active growth in ethanol concentrations of up to 10%. However, tolerance to pH, temperature, ethanol, SO2, and osmotic pressure during fermentation is strain dependent (Ge et al. 2023).

However, it must be stressed that the population dynamics of any basidiomycete yeast should be considered in the context of the specific parameters found within each individual fermentation (e.g., grape cultivar, sugar and tannin contents, additives, temperature, pH, and other yeasts), and care should be taken against overly broad generalizations.

Methods of Yeast Community Characterization

Studies that use identification methods based on the isolation of pure cultures impact the assemblage of yeasts, including basidiomycetes, depending on culturing and colony selection methods (Beuchat 1992). Media commonly used in grape community research were generally developed for fermentative ascomycete yeasts (Morris and Eddy 1957; Hall 1971), and the detected diversity of basidiomycete yeasts on grapes and in grape musts may be limited by authors choosing to identify or report only isolates adhering to certain properties, such as fermentative activities (Granchi et al. 1999; Fernández et al. 2000). Once yeasts have been isolated, further variation occurs among identification methods, which may be based on physiology, morphology, PCR‐RFLP, Sanger sequencing, or other techniques (Barata et al. 2012). Preliminary screening of basidiomycete yeast cultures by physical traits or physiological tests may be problematic, as different yeast species display different phenotypic ranges, phenotypic variation overlaps among closely related species, yeasts generally have few taxonomically distinguishing traits (Cao et al. 2021), and mating tests are unsuitable to delineate species of basidiomycete yeasts that may hybridize, such as Cryptococcus and Malassezia (G. Wu et al. 2015; Boekhout et al. 2021).

Culture‐independent methods such as next‐generation sequencing generally produce alternate assortments of identified yeast species, and differences in sequencing platform (Kumar et al. 2019; Bunbury‐Blanchette et al. 2023) and data analysis pipeline further impact the reported yeast community composition (McKnight et al. 2019; Halwachs et al. 2017; Lücking et al. 2020). Variation in DNA extraction methods and choices of barcoding gene region(s) and primer pair, even for the same gene, may also have substantial impacts on the detection of different taxa, regardless of the sequencing method (Op De Beeck et al. 2014; Stielow et al. 2015). For example, some common ITS rDNA gene region primers preferentially amplify ascomycete fungi, while others are biased toward basidiomycetes (Bellemain et al. 2010), although newer primers designed for next‐generation sequencing may mitigate these issues (Toju et al. 2012; Bokulich and Mills 2013; Usyk et al. 2017).

Studies that use more than one method to detect and identify yeasts demonstrate variation in basidiomycete yeast community composition results. For example, Rantsiou et al. (2020) isolated Papiliotrema flavescens, P. terrestris, and R. graminis from musts by culturing, but none of these species were detected at an incidence > 0.2% by next‐generation sequencing from the same samples, suggesting that their abundance in culture may be due to successful competition. J. Li et al. (2019), using a culture‐dependent approach, detected Filobasidium floriforme during early fermentation only, while next‐generation sequencing of the same samples detected F. floriforme throughout the fermentation. Costantini et al. (2022) also described different yeast communities, including basidiomycete yeasts, on wine grapes using culture‐dependent and next‐generation sequencing techniques, and although the differences were broadly attributed to the different methods, there were undoubtedly additional effects resulting from different DNA extraction protocols, targeting different gene regions depending on method, and using different databases for sequence identification.

Mechanisms by Which Basidiomycete Yeast May Influence Wine Quality

Basidiomycete yeasts can potentially impact wine flavor through three general, non‐mutually exclusive pathways: (1) the direct influence of yeast metabolites such as acids, alcohols, aldehydes, esters, and glycerol; (2) the enzymatic release of aromatic compounds from non‐volatile precursors; and (3) indirect effects through influences on fermentative (ascomycete) yeasts (Figure 1; Table 1). Basidiomycete yeasts can also potentially influence the overall “body” and mouthfeel of a wine, primarily due to interactions among the concentrations of sugar, ethanol, tannins, and glycerol (Pagliarini et al. 2013; K. Wang, Chen, et al. 2023).

Yeast Metabolites

Acids

Sufficient acidity is an essential aspect of wine, although high concentrations of acids are undesirable. Moderating acidity is therefore a significant aspect of winemaking that may be influenced by basidiomycete yeasts. S. shibatanus, F. magnum, P. flavescens, and V. victoriae can be associated with the production of acetic acid (Verginer et al. 2010; Rossouw and Bauer 2016; Englezos et al. 2022; Wu et al. 2023), which makes up most of the volatile acid component of wines (Swiegers et al. 2005). The concentrations of a wide range of volatile fatty acids, including propionic, hexanoic, octanoic, nonanoic, decanoic, and tetradecanoic acids, may also be affected by basidiomycete yeasts, including R. mucilaginosa, R. glutinis, and S. oryzicola, as well as species of the genera Filobasidium, Naganishia, Hannaella, Udeniomyces, and Malassezia, as determined by analyses of aromatic compounds formed during mixed inoculum fermentations (Calabretti et al. 2011; X. C. Wang et al. 2017; N. Ma, Wang, et al. 2021; K. Wang, Chen, et al. 2023) and correlated with species abundances during fermentations (Chen et al. 2020; Englezos et al. 2022; Wei et al. 2022; Gao et al. 2024).

Higher Alcohols

Basidiomycete yeasts are also commonly correlated with the production of a variety of higher alcohols, which generally contribute positive aromas, provided levels remain within acceptable thresholds (X. Wang, Fan, et al. 2023). For example, nutty and buttery aromas may be heightened by the increased levels of benzyl alcohol associated with Vishniacozyma and Udeniomyces (Y. Ma, Li, et al. 2021; Englezos et al. 2022), while R. mucilaginosa and S. roseus may impart rose‐like floral and sweet aromas via an increased production of phenethyl alcohol (Verginer et al. 2010; X. C. Wang et al. 2017; N. Ma, Wang, et al. 2021; K. Wang, Chen, et al. 2023). Levels of propanol, isobutyl alcohol, and isoamyl alcohol, which may impart fruitiness at low levels (but otherwise carry distinctive alcohol aromas), have been linked to many basidiomycete yeasts, including R. mucilaginosa (X. C. Wang et al. 2017; N. Ma, Wang, et al. 2021; K. Wang, Chen, et al. 2023), R. glutinis (Englezos et al. 2022), S. roseus (Verginer et al. 2010), and P. flavescens (Rossouw and Bauer 2016; X. Wang, Fan, et al. 2023). On the other hand, 2‐ and 3‐methyl‐1‐butanol are often associated with more negative aromas and are also produced by S. roseus in culture (Verginer et al. 2010), although production during fermentation may differ. Chemical variants of hexanol, heptanol, octanol, and nonanol may impart a variety of positive or negative herbal, floral, citrus, or even mushroom‐like aromas to wine, and again, depending on interactions with other factors, most basidiomycete yeasts examined (e.g., Rhodotorula, Rhodosporidiobolus, Hannaella, Papiliotrema, and Vishniacozyma) have some impact on levels of these alcohols (Y. Ma, Li, et al. 2021; Wei et al. 2022; Gao et al. 2024).

Aldehydes and Ketones

In addition, increased production of a variety of aldehydes has been linked to basidiomycete yeasts. The sweet, nutty, or cherry aromas of benzaldehyde with Filobasidium; the orange citrus aroma of dodecanal and herbal or fruity aromas of hexen‐2‐al with Hanaella, and all three, as well as hexanal, which may impart grassy, herbal, or apple aromas, with Udeniomyces (Y. Ma, Li, et al. 2021). S. roseus was also noted to produce 2‐methylbutanal in culture, which may increase cocoa, malty, or nutty aromas (Verginer et al. 2010). In some cases, however, decreased production is associated with a yeast, such as reductions of benzaldehyde correlated with higher abundances of Vishniacozyma and the closely related Hanaella (Y. Ma, Li, et al. 2021). Fewer correlations between basidiomycete yeasts and ketones are known, but the associations of F. magnum with 5‐ethyl‐6‐methyl‐3E‐hepten‐2‐one (X. Wu et al. 2023), Filobasidium, Hanaella, and Udeniomyces with 2‐octanone (Y. Ma, Li, et al. 2021), and P. flavescens with acetoin (Rossouw and Bauer 2016) suggest that further research is likely to reveal additional associations.

Esters

A vast diversity of esters is known from wines, but some of the most investigated groups are the acetates and ethyl esters, due to their relative ubiquity. The fruity, floral, and sweet aromas of ethyl acetate, ethyl phenylacetate, isoamyl acetate, and 2‐phenylethyl acetate are associated with R. mucilaginosa (X. C. Wang et al. 2017; N. Ma, Wang, et al. 2021; Wang et al. 2023a), S. shibatanus and Symmetrospora (Verginer et al. 2010; Englezos et al. 2022), Filobasidium and Hannaella (Wei et al. 2022), P. flavescens (Rossouw and Bauer 2016), and Udeniomyces (Chen et al. 2020). Ethyl esters are also widely associated with basidiomycete yeasts, known from production in culture (Verginer et al. 2010), via the detection of increased levels during mixed inoculum fermentations (Rossouw and Bauer 2016; X. C. Wang et al. 2017; N. Ma, Wang, et al. 2021; K. Wang, Chen, et al. 2023; X. Wu et al. 2023), and from correlations with yeast populations during fermentations (Englezos et al. 2022; Gao et al. 2024). Given that basidiomycete yeasts seem to be universally capable of ester production in wine fermentations, and the vast array of esters that are known to contribute distinguishing aromas to different wines, basidiomycete yeasts may be a largely untapped reservoir for unexpected and unique aroma formation in wine via the production of esters.

However, the use of basidiomycete yeasts carries the risk of an overproduction of aromatic compounds. In addition to levels of those previously listed rising above acceptable thresholds, increased concentrations of pyrazines, phenols, and sulfur compounds generally impart negative qualities, although at low concentrations they may contribute aromas distinctive to some wines. Phenolic off‐flavors may result from an abundance of Rhodotorula or Papiliotrema yeasts (Shinohara et al. 2000), while F. magnum has been linked to increased production of 2‐methoxy‐4‐vinylphenol, which conveys an unpleasant pharmaceutical aroma (X. Wu et al. 2023). S. roseus and R. glutinis may increase levels of the sulfur compound 1,3‐benzothiazole, giving aromas of rubber or cabbage, and S. roseus may also increase 2,5‐dimethylpyrazine and 3‐ethyl‐2,5‐dimethylpyrazine, which impart roasted aromas that may be nutty or chocolatey or may be unpleasantly reminiscent of meat or vegetables (Verginer et al. 2010; Englezos et al. 2022). Vishniacozyma carnescens and Malassezia are also associated with increases in sulfur compounds such as methionol, which imparts aromas of cabbage or potato (Englezos et al. 2022; Wei et al. 2022).

Glycerol

Glycerol is a substantial component of wine, and although it is generally considered to increase both sweetness and viscosity, numerous studies indicate that glycerol actually has negligible effects on viscosity, and its impact is mainly on sweetness (Noble and Bursick 1984; Yanniotis et al. 2007; Goold et al. 2017). Nevertheless, increased glycerol production is often favored in winemaking, and basidiomycete yeasts, including P. flavescens, V. victoriae, Symmetrospora oryzicola, and Tremella globispora, may produce high glycerol levels during wine fermentation, in concert with other desirable compounds that may distinguish the wine aroma (Rossouw and Bauer 2016; Englezos et al. 2022).

Glycerol metabolism may enable yeast growth in later stages of fermentation by initially protecting cells from the high osmotic stress associated with high sugar concentrations, maintaining cell redox balance, and providing precursor molecules for the synthesis of phospholipids in cell membranes (Swiegers et al. 2005). Therefore, high glycerol‐producing basidiomycete yeasts like P. flavescens may survive longer in fermentations and subsequently influence wine aroma more than yeasts that die off at earlier stages. Many glycerol producing yeast strains concurrently produce acetic acid, but there is evidence that mixed inoculation with S. cerevisiae can reduce this effect to avoid excessive acidity (Swiegers et al. 2005; Rossouw and Bauer 2016).

Higher levels of glycerol may also support glycerol metabolism in some yeasts as glucose and nitrogen are depleted (Kot et al. 2016; Klein et al. 2017). For example, some strains of R. glutinis and S. shibatanus can use glycerol as a carbon source, and their growth in individual culture suggests that glycerol concentration could impact their production of lipids during wine fermentation, which may in turn enable the production of aromatic compounds or provide nutrients to other yeasts upon their death (Kot et al. 2016; Kot et al. 2021). Saccharomyces cerevisiae and other fermentative ascomycetes may also utilize glycerol as glucose becomes scare near the end of fermentation, although the ability varies among strains and has not been explored in the context of wine fermentation (Klein et al. 2017).

Ethanol

Although some consumers seek out the “full‐bodied, fruit‐forward” wines produced by sweeter grapes with higher Brix, others prefer “lighter” wines that lack the higher alcohol content that often accompanies these fruitier wines (Goold et al. 2017). The growth of the wine industries in warmer climates, as well as the warming of established wine regions and a desire to harvest fully ripened grapes that best express fruity and varietal aromas, are creating a demand for fermentation methods that maintain these positive aspects but restrict ethanol levels (Swiegers et al. 2005; Gonzalez et al. 2021). Non‐Saccharomyces yeasts generally produce less ethanol than Saccharomyces yeasts, and mixed inoculations using ascomycete yeasts have been explored to reduce ethanol in wine fermentations (Contreras et al. 2014; Quirós et al. 2014).

Given their non‐fermentative metabolism, a higher proportion of basidiomycete yeasts should also correspond with a reduction in ethanol production, but the mechanisms by which these yeasts moderate ethanol levels are not well understood. Species of Udeniomyces and Rhodotorula (including R. mucilaginosa and R. glutinis), as well as F. magnum and P. flavescens, may either increase or decrease ethanol concentration, depending on fermentation conditions (Rossouw and Bauer 2016; Chen et al. 2020; Englezos et al. 2022; R. Li et al. 2021, 2022). As expected, a reduction in ethanol is generally correlated with a higher concentration of final residual sugars and reduced fermentation rate (Sternes et al. 2017; Englezos et al. 2022; R. Li et al. 2021). We are unaware of any research that has explicitly tested basidiomycete yeasts to reduce ethanol in wine beyond inclusion in preliminary isolate screening, e.g. Contreras et al. (2014).

Release of Aromatics From Non‐Volatile Precursors

Enzymatic activity during wine fermentation may act on precursor compounds that may then be converted to pleasant fruity or floral varietal aromatic compounds (Fia et al. 2014). For example, β‐glucosidase partially breaks down a variety of non‐volatile glycosides and oligosaccharides to produce volatile terpenoid compounds, in addition to glucose, which may be utilized by ascomycete yeasts like S. cerevisiae (Swiegers et al. 2005; Maicas and Mateo 2015; Belda et al. 2016). Basidiomycete yeasts of the genus Rhodotorula, including species R. glutinis, R. mucilaginosa, and Rhodotorula toruloides (Belda et al. 2016; X. C. Wang et al. 2017), and others, including Sporobolomyces shibatanus (Baffi et al. 2011; Baffi et al. 2013) and Cryptococcus amylolentus (Belda et al. 2016) produce β‐glucosidase in wine fermentations, although enzyme activities are variable among yeast strains (Ge et al. 2023).

Mixed inoculum fermentations using S. cerevisiae together with R. mucilaginosa or S. shibatanus with high β‐glucosidase activity demonstrate the possible benefits of β‐glucosidase‐producing yeasts to wine aroma, although the interacting effects of grape cultivar and inoculation parameters are not well understood (X. C. Wang et al. 2017; Vilela 2020). In some cases, a pattern emerges in which the addition of R. mucilaginosa results in increased levels of hexanoic, octanoic, and decanoic acids and the corresponding esters ethyl hexanoate, ethyl octanoate, ethyl decanoate, and ethyl hexadecanoate, along with increases of benzyl alcohol, phenethyl alcohol, isoamyl alcohol, 1‐hexanol, ethyl acetate, 2‐phenethyl acetate, ethyl isobutyrate, diethyl succinate, and vitispirane (X. C. Wang et al. 2017; N. Ma, Wang, et al. 2021). The resulting wines have more intense fruit and floral aromas, although they may also possess a faint animal aroma and undesirable sulfur compounds at higher concentrations. These negative effects can seemingly be mitigated, however, by adjusting inoculation parameters, as K. Wang, Chen, et al. (2023) found increased acids following mixed inoculation with R. mucilaginosa, but they remained below acceptable thresholds and so contributed to an overall balanced aroma in the wine and increased the complexity and richness of the mouthfeel. The levels of volatile compounds were also elevated, and the wines received high scores in attributes of green fruit, tropical fruit, aroma intensity, and floral notes (K. Wang, Chen, et al. 2023).

The second pattern of effects resulting from inoculation with β‐glucosidase‐producing strains of R. mucilaginosa or S. shibatanus is characterized by an increase in terpenoid compounds, which is expected as glucosidases convert non‐aromatic terpenoid precursors to their aromatic form (Swiegers et al. 2005; Belda et al. 2017). Higher concentrations of a greater variety of terpenoids, including nerol, β‐damascenone, geraniol, citronellol, linalool, and terpineol, as well as phenethyl acetate, were produced following sequential inoculations with lower ratios of R. mucilaginosa to S. cerevisiae (N. Ma, Wang, et al. 2021), simultaneous inoculation of R. mucilaginosa and S. cerevisiae with different grape cultivars (Calabretti et al. 2011; K. Wang, Chen, et al. 2023), and when using S. shibatanus (Baffi et al. 2013, 2011). The resulting wines had increased astringent and acidic properties (Calabretti et al. 2011) but also had stronger fruity and floral aromas and high scores in general impression and intensity of flavor (Calabretti et al. 2012; N. Ma, Wang, et al. 2021).

α‐L‐arabinofuranosidase, which is produced by strains of Rhodotorula toruloides (Martínez et al. 2006), facilitates β‐glucosidase activity by cleaving the 1,6‐glycosidic linkage in the first step of monoterpene hydrolysis, allowing β‐glucosidase to then liberate monoterpenols (Gunata et al. 1988). Udeniomyces, P. flavescens, Tilletiopsis washingtonensis, and T. globispora have also been linked to higher production of terpenes such as citronellol, farnesol, geraniol, and nerolidol (Rossouw and Bauer 2016; Y. Ma, Li, et al. 2021; Englezos et al. 2022; X. Wang, Fan, et al. 2023), and the genus Filobasidium, including F. magnum, may moderate levels of the norisoprenoids (an alternate class of terpenoid compounds that similarly contribute fruity or floral aromas) safranal and 1,1,5‐trimethyl‐1,2‐dihydronaphthalene (TDN) (X. Wu et al. 2023; Gao et al. 2024), suggesting that these groups may also have desirable β‐glucosidase or α‐L‐arabinofuranosidase activity.

Strains of F. magnum, closely related to N. albida and Piskurozyma capsuligena, may have high lipoxygenase activity, which increases the degradation of astringent higher fatty acid esters and promotes the production of terpenoids such as β‐damascenone and safranal (X. Wu et al. 2023). While associations between F. magnum and positive aroma compounds may in some cases be absent or negative (Englezos et al. 2022; Y. Ma, Li, et al. 2021), effects are likely dependent not only on strain but also on interactions with other microorganisms (X. Wu et al. 2023).

Future research endeavors in this area could include the screening of large numbers of basidiomycete yeast isolates for the production of high levels of β‐glucosidase, proteases, and pectinases using a high‐throughput microtiter plate‐based assay (Beneyton et al. 2017). Selected isolates could then be used to inoculate wine fermentations with the goal of increased liberation of distinctive varietal aromatics.

Indirect Influence of Basidiomycete Yeasts on Fermentative Yeasts

Enzymatic Breakdown of Plant Cell Wall Components

Enzymes, such as pectinases, are beneficial during early stages of winemaking to improve juice extraction, as well as at later stages for improved clarification, extraction of phenols and pigments, and release of aromatic compounds (Louw et al. 2006; Fleet 2008). Pectinases, along with xylanases and α‐L‐arabinofuranosidase, play roles in the degradation of plant cell walls, aiding in the hydrolysis of pectin, xylan, and cellulose, by which precursor compounds may be made available for fermentation and aroma development (Belda et al. 2016; Tolosa and Prieto 2019). Commercial preparations of enzymes like pectinases are often added to wine fermentations but may have undesirable side activities, and there is interest in pectinolytic yeasts that may be used as inoculants (Alimardani‐Theuil et al. 2011).

Basidiomycete yeasts, including Rhodotorula (Vaughn et al. 1969; Merín et al. 2015), Naganishia (Federici 1985; Merín et al. 2015), and P. capsuligena (Merín et al. 2014), may produce pectinases. Strains of P. capsuligena and Rhodotorula dairenensis isolated from wineries produce pectinases under appropriate wine fermentation conditions, and strains of both species have been characterized as having additional favorable cellulase and xylanase activities (Merín et al. 2015, 2014). P. capsuligena is also known to produce α‐amylase and glucoamylase (De Mot and Verachtert 1985), which may be utilized for similar beneficial effects in grape wine fermentation, considering their applications to improve fermentations of banana wine, Chinese yellow wine, and rice wine (Ueda et al. 1991; Cheirsilp and Umsakul 2008; Yang et al. 2013; Bian et al. 2022).

Few studies examine esterase production by basidiomycete yeasts in the context of wine production. However, strains of Rhodotorula may produce acetyl xylan esterase (Lee et al. 1987), which functions in concert with xylanases to complete xylan degradation (Tolosa and Prieto 2019). In general, esterase activity must be balanced by ester synthesis during fermentation to allow a net increase in ester accumulation (Swiegers et al. 2005). However, esterases are also active in the formation of acetate esters, which contribute pleasant wine aromas, and esterase activity alters the overall balance of different esters (X. Wang, Fan, et al. 2023). Further research might capitalize on the purported higher occurrence of esterases associated with basidiomycete rather than ascomycete yeasts (Buzzini and Martini 2002).

Some strains of Sporobolomyces and Rhodotorula produce proteases, but, like esterases, their activity has not been thoroughly explored in wine fermentations (Chomsri 2008; Kot et al. 2021). Proteolytic activity can decrease the haze‐causing protein content of wine and generate usable nitrogen for other microorganisms (i.e., by degrading polypeptides and proteins to amino acids and peptide residues) that may also be used to synthesize aromatic compounds (Chomsri 2008; Tolosa and Prieto 2019). Notably, S. cerevisiae and other fermentative yeasts require sufficient nitrogen to complete fermentation (Maicas and Mateo 2015; Tolosa and Prieto 2019).

Other basidiomycete yeasts, such as R. glutinis and Sporidiobolus ruineniae (Taskin 2013; Kanpiengjai et al. 2020), produce tannases, which catalyze the degradation of tannins and could therefore reduce wine astringency. A product of this hydrolysis is glucose, which is then available to the basidiomycete yeasts and potentially fermentative yeasts as well.

Other Interspecific Yeast Interactions

Individual microorganisms do not influence the characteristics of a wine independently from the rest of the microbial community. Interactions among species influence the dynamic nature of the yeast community during fermentation and therefore influence the production of aromatic compounds. For example, an overabundance of basidiomycete yeasts early in the fermentation may limit the subsequent and necessary growth of fermentative yeasts like S. cerevisiae by depleting essential nutrients or producing toxic compounds (Fleet 2003). Conversely, the death of non‐Saccharomyces yeasts during the early stages of fermentation also provides nutrients to support the growth of S. cerevisiae (Fleet 2003), and basidiomycete yeast enzymatic activity may support the metabolism of other yeasts via the increased production of nutritional compounds (Belda et al. 2016).

Basidiomycete yeasts are again less well researched than their ascomycete counterparts with respect to yeast‐yeast interactions during wine fermentations, but the same relationships that occur among ascomycete yeasts may be presumed to exist in some capacity for basidiomycete yeasts. Interactions may be positive, such as the overproduction of acetaldehyde by a hybrid strain of Saccharomyces in mixed inoculum fermentations, promoting the growth and fermentation rate of an S. cerevisiae strain when compared to monoculture fermentations (Cheraiti et al. 2005). Oluwa (2020) proposed that metabolites produced by P. flavescens may serve a similar function in fermentation to support the later growth of other yeasts. Conversely, some yeasts may deplete nitrogen or other nutrient sources before S. cerevisiae becomes competitive, altering the progression of fermentation and formation of aromatic compounds (Bordet et al. 2020; Zilelidou and Nisiotou 2021). For example, the consumption of nitrogen sources during fermentations that experienced sequential inoculation using Metschnikowia pulcherrima (followed by inoculation with S. cerevisiae) was connected to the expression of varietal thiols, fatty acids, and ethyl esters (Seguinot et al. 2020), and competition for nitrogen and nutrients between different yeasts and S. cerevisiae was similarly linked to the production of higher alcohols and esters (Rollero et al. 2018).

These effects are due to the successive nature by which aromatic compounds are formed as fermentation unfolds, which in turn depends on the assemblage of microorganisms present. As fermentation proceeds, the changing proportions and types of carbon, nitrogen, and other compounds determine the final composition of the wine (Carrau et al. 2008; Jiang et al. 2019; X. Wang, Fan, et al. 2023). Higher alcohols are derived from amino acids and are in turn used along with additional amino acids and ethanol as precursors in acetate ester formation, while ethyl ester synthesis requires medium‐chain fatty acids such as hexanoic or octanoic acid (X. Wang, Fan, et al. 2023). Therefore, basidiomycete yeasts that produce higher alcohols or fatty acids may influence wine aroma either by the specific aromatic effects of those compounds or by facilitating other yeasts to produce aromatic esters.

Basidiomycete yeasts such as P. laurentii that may be present in wine musts and during fermentations may also produce toxins that inhibit the growth of other yeasts (Yurkov and Golubev 2013). “Killer toxins,” or proteins that are lethal to other sensitive yeast strains, are generally associated with ascomycete yeasts in the context of winemaking (“killer yeasts” belong to strains ascribed to 100+ species, including S. cerevisiae, Kluyveromyces lactis, Pichia membranifaciens, Wickerhamomyces anomalus, Cyberlindnera mrakii, and Tetrapisispora phaffi) (Mannazzu et al. 2019). However, strains of P. capsuligena secrete a killer toxin active against species of Cryptococcus and Filobasidium (Keszthelyi et al. 2006), which may alter the overall yeast community during fermentation. Physical contact between yeasts may also be antagonistic, as demonstrated by reduced cell viability of S. cerevisiae in fermentations inoculated with Lachancea thermotolerans allowing for cell‐cell contact, versus fermentations in which L. thermotolerans was restricted by a dialysis membrane, resulting in wines with different concentrations of higher alcohols, fatty acids, and esters (Petitgonnet et al. 2019).

Some next‐generation sequencing studies of wine grapes and fermenting musts include correlation analyses among species that capitalize on their large, quantitative datasets. For example, during spontaneous wine fermentations, larger populations of Papiliotrema, Vishniacozyma, and Filobasidium were negatively correlated with the abundance of S. cerevisiae (Wei et al. 2022; Liang et al. 2023), while Vishniacozyma and Filobasidium exhibited a co‐occurrence effect with Pichia and Hanseniaspora (Y. Ma, Li, et al. 2021). Similar negative correlations were modeled between Vishniacozyma heimaeyensis and S. roseus with Pichia spp. based on metabolic activity (Martins et al. 2022). Abundances of common vineyard constituents Metschnikowia and Naganishia were negatively correlated with each other on grape surfaces (Zhu et al. 2021), and during spontaneous fermentation, Naganishia and Hannaella were negatively correlated with Pichia (Y. Ma, Li, et al. 2021). More broadly, Vishniacozyma and Udeniomyces were negatively correlated with higher abundances of most other fungal genera on grapes, while Rhodotorula was generally positively correlated with other fungal genera (Zhu et al. 2021). Martins et al. (2022) further suggest a possible association between R. glutinis and B. alba.

Challenges in Using Basidiomycete Yeasts in Winemaking

Basidiomycete yeasts cannot complete fermentations on their own, and so their use as inoculants should be in conjunction with a method that incorporates fermentative yeast(s), such as mixed inoculum or a method such as pied de cuve, which uses must that has already begun to ferment (Morgan et al. 2019). Alternatively, steps could be taken to increase their occurrence in spontaneous fermentations (e.g., reducing damage to grapes prior to crushing and pressing). Regardless of the winemaking method, basidiomycete yeasts need to be evaluated for their effects on fermentations in concert with one or more fermentative ascomycete yeasts, adding a layer of complexity to the analysis. However, experimentation using mixed inoculum in wine fermentations is already common in the context non‐Saccharomyces ascomycete yeasts, which also exhibit a wide range of variation among strains (Vilela 2020).

SO2 addition early in fermentation to reduce unwanted spoilage microorganisms, including non‐Saccharomyces yeasts, may impact the results of basidiomycete yeast inoculation (Giacosa et al. 2019). SO2 does not universally inhibit indigenous yeast populations but instead alters the yeast community composition (Raspor et al. 2002; Lange et al. 2014; Morgan et al. 2019; Windholtz et al. 2021) and may reduce the influence of non‐Saccharomyces yeasts on wine aroma (Sumby et al. 2021). The SO2 resistance of individual basidiomycete yeasts during wine fermentation is not known, and therefore they should be used in fermentations without added SO2 or be screened for SO2 resistance.

The metabolisms of basidiomycete yeasts are less well known than in fermentative yeasts, but modern genomic, transcriptomic, and proteomic approaches may help to better understand the influence of basidiomycete yeasts on wine fermentations. Of all the basidiomycete yeasts, the genus Rhodotorula has so far received the most attention in terms of multi‐omics research, as it produces several economically valuable products, including fatty acids and carotenoids (X. Zheng et al. 2025). Rhodotorula therefore seems to be a likely candidate for a model basidiomycete yeast genus to facilitate the elucidation and manipulation of the metabolic pathways involved in the production of wine‐associated metabolites and enzymes such as higher alcohols, ß‐glucosidase, pectinase, and proteases.

Finally, basidiomycete yeasts identified from grape musts and wine fermentations based on next‐generation sequencing studies may not have corresponding physical cultures and may be difficult to isolate and grow in culture, requiring specific isolation, proliferation, and maintenance protocols. Therefore, despite the reported correlations between basidiomycete yeast populations with the concentrations of aromatic compounds or improved perception of wines, it may not be a simple matter of isolating these yeasts and using them to deliberately inoculate fermentations. More research is needed to determine the conditions required to grow basidiomycete yeasts for applications in winemaking.

Conclusions

There is an extensive diversity of basidiomycete yeasts associated with wine grapes and musts that can significantly influence wine quality and flavor. Although largely overlooked in wine making, these yeasts are abundant in the early stages and are likely important in setting the biochemical trajectories of most wine fermentations.

Although basidiomycete yeast species composition is influenced by a variety of interacting factors, including geography, climate, management practice, and grape variety, a core group of vineyard basidiomycete yeasts appears common in vineyards, including species of Rhodotorula, Filobasidium, Sporobolomyces, and Vishniacozyma.

The effects of basidiomycete yeasts on wine quality can be direct, through the production or utilization of metabolites such as glycerol, organic acids, alcohols, acetaldehydes, ketones, esters, and aromatic compounds. They may also have less direct effects through the production of enzymes that liberate varietal aromatic compounds from non‐volatile precursors. However, more consequential impacts may stem from their modification of resources, such as plant cell wall components and nitrogenous compounds, that subsequently inhibit or facilitate the action of fermentative yeasts. Each of these pathways is likely to play an important role in wine making, regardless of whether the fermentation is spontaneous or inoculated with a commercial yeast.

Although challenges exist regarding the incorporation of basidiomycete yeasts into wine production (e.g., the common practice of adding SO2 to reduce the abundance of indigenous yeasts), many avenues of research are available. For example, a species of Rhodotorula could be selected as a model basidiomycete yeast with which to characterize important wine associated metabolic pathways. Also, next‐generation sequencing should be used in combination with pure culture isolation to identify strains to be tested in mixed inocula together with a fermentative yeast such as S. cerevisiae. These objectives should lead to a better understanding of basidiomycete yeast activities and interactions, allowing for increased control over wine fermentation dynamics and the harnessing of basidiomycete yeasts to broaden the currently available palette of wine flavors.
