Burnout and Depression of Medical Faculty: After Mass Resignation of Junior Doctors in Korea
Authors: Su Jeong Seong, Jae Yeon Hwang, Joo Yun Song, Kee Jeong Park, Young Tak Jo, Ju-Hun Lee, Dae-Gyun Park
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe mass resignation of residents and interns in protest against the proposed increase in medical school admissions triggered a healthcare crisis, significantly increasing the workload of the remaining medical faculty. This study examines burnout and depression among faculty members and explores the contributing factors during this crisis.MethodsFaculty from the University School of Medicine and its affiliated hospitals participated in an online survey distributed via group chat in May 2024. Burnout, depression, and stress levels were assessed using the Oldenburg Burnout Inventory, the Patient Health Questionnaire-9, and the Perceived Stress Scale, respectively. Factors associated with depression, burnout (disengagement and exhaustion) were identified. Causal mediation analysis was conducted to investigate whether depressive symptoms mediate the relationship between stress and burnout (disengagement and exhaustion).ResultsDepression was reported by 68.9% of participants, while 99.3% exhibited signs of disengagement or exhaustion. Depression was linked to working more than 52 hours per week and intensive care unit (ICU) duties. Disengagement was associated with specialty and ICU duties, and exhaustion was associated with gender, age, specialty, working hours, and ICU duties. Depressive symptoms mediated the relationship between stress and disengagement but not between stress and exhaustion.ConclusionBurnout and depression were highly prevalent among medical faculty during the healthcare crisis. Abrupt policy change and the mass resignation of residents and interns may have exacerbated burnout and depression among the remaining faculty. The distinct components of burnout appear to have unique risk factors and relationships with depression. Targeted interventions are urgently needed to address burnout and depression among medical faculty members.

INTRODUCTION

Physicians are known to be vulnerable to burnout. Studies involving over a thousand physicians have reported that 32%1 to 54.4%2 experience at least one symptom of burnout. While these rates vary, they are significantly higher than those among general workers, which range from 28.1% to 28.6%.3 After adjusting for age and gender, physicians were 1.41 times more likely to experience burnout than general workers.4 Burnout among medical faculty is also concerning. In Korea, 37.1% of medical faculty exhibited at least one symptom of burnout in 2014.5 Some studies reported even higher rates: 25.0%6 to 70.3%7 for emotional exhaustion, 5.6%6 to 72.3%7 for depersonalization, and 36.4%6 to 100%7 for low personal achievement.

Physicians, including medical faculty, also face an increased risk of depression. A 2022 meta-analysis on the global prevalence of depression among doctors during the first year of the coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) pandemic reported a pooled prevalence of 20.5%.3 Studies involving over 1,000 physicians have reported depression screening rates ranging from 23%8 to 65.3%.9 Additionally, the medical profession is known to encounter several risk factors for depression,10 including inadequate sleep,11 frequent night shifts,12 work-family conflict,13 long working hours,1214 and a lack of regular physical exercise.12 Medical faculty members appear to be similarly vulnerable, with significant symptoms observed in 20%15 to 40.6%16 of medical faculty.

The impacts of physician burnout and depression on personal life can be severe. A systematic review revealed that burnout and depression are associated with anxiety and suicidality.1718 More critically, these conditions compromise patient care and transform personal struggles into societal burdens. Burnout and depression increase the risk of major medical errors19 and unsafe practices, resulting in diminished care quality and lower patient satisfaction.19202122 Physicians with burnout are also more likely to work fewer hours or leave clinical practice,23 exacerbating staff shortages in certain medical fields. Among medical faculty, the impact extends further, affecting not only clinical care but also research, student education, and mentorship of junior doctors.

Although the strong correlation between burnout and depression is well-established,172425 the exact nature of this relationship remains debated.1726 Some studies suggest that burnout increases the risk of depression,272829 while others claim the reverse.303132 Stress, a known risk factor for both conditions,33 further complicates this dynamic. Some studies indicate that burnout mediates the impact of stress on depression,2734 while others suggest that depression mediates the effect of stress on burnout.35

In February 2024, the South Korean government declared a severe healthcare crisis following the nationwide mass resignation of residents and interns. This resignation was a protest against the government’s plan to increase annual medical school admissions by 2,000, from the current 3,058,36 without consulting the medical sector. By March 8, over 10,000 resignation letters had been submitted,37 and as of July 8, only 1,104 (8.0%) of the 13,756 residents at 211 training hospitals remained at work.38 Residents and interns, who constituted 39% of the workforce at the five largest university hospitals,39 were crucial for hospital operations. Their absence left the remaining faculty struggling to sustain medical services, with workloads becoming increasingly unmanageable. A survey of 1,654 faculty members from seven medical schools revealed that 40.4% to 59%, depending on the university, worked over 72 hours per week, and 6.4% to 16% reported working more than 100 hours per week.40

An excessive workload is likely to heighten stress levels and increase the risk of mental health problems. Given the increased demands caused by mass resignations, medical faculty are likely at greater risk of depression and burnout. However, limited research exists on these issues among faculty during healthcare crises. This study aims to investigate burnout and depression among faculty at university hospitals during this crisis and identify the contributing factors. Additionally, it will explore the relationships between burnout, stress, and depression.

METHODS

Participants

In Korea, medical faculty contracts vary in terms of salary sources. This study included all board-certified faculty physicians working in the hospital, regardless of contract type. Participants were faculty members affiliated with Hallym University School of Medicine, Hallym University Hospital, or both. A semi-structured online questionnaire was developed using Google Forms in May 2024, and the link was shared via the Professors’ Association group chats, which included all faculty members. Age was categorized into three groups: 25–34 years, 35–49 years, and 50 years or older. Clinical specialties were grouped into three categories: internal medicine (family medicine, internal medicine, pediatrics, neurology, emergency medicine, psychiatry, rehabilitation medicine, and dermatology), surgical group (urology, obstetrics and gynecology, plastic surgery, neurosurgery, ophthalmology, general surgery, otorhinolaryngology, orthopedic surgery, and thoracic surgery), and clinical support (anesthesiology, radiation oncology, pathology, radiology, laboratory medicine, and nuclear medicine).

Measurement

Burnout was assessed using the Korean version41 of the Oldenburg Burnout Inventory (OBI),4243 which includes 16 items: 8 addressing disengagement and 8 addressing emotional exhaustion. Disengagement refers to distancing oneself from job content and tasks due to negative attitudes toward the job.41 All items were rated on a 4-point Likert scale (1 = strongly agree to 4 = strongly disagree), with total scores ranging from 16 to 64. Higher scores indicate greater burnout. Burnout is defined as an average disengagement score exceeding 2.1 and an average emotional exhaustion score exceeding 2.25, as proposed by Peterson et al.44

Depression was measured using the Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9),4546 a self-report questionnaire aligned with the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders-IV diagnostic criteria for major depressive episodes. Each item was scored from 0 to 3 based on symptom severity, yielding a total score between 0 and 27. The Korean version of the PHQ-9 has demonstrated good reliability and validity.4547 A cutoff score of 10 was used to identify depressive symptoms.48

We used the 10-item version49 of the Perceived Stress Scale (PSS), a questionnaire originally developed by Cohen et al.50 in 1983, to assess perceived stress over the past month using a 5-point Likert scale. Total scores ranged from 0 to 40, with higher scores indicating greater stress. Since the PSS is not designed for diagnostic purposes, no specific cutoff point was provided.

Statistical analysis

We used t-tests and one-way analysis of variances to analyze the associations between sociodemographic and clinical variables and levels of disengagement, exhaustion, and depression. The analysis included the total scores of PSS and PHQ-9, along with the average scores for disengagement and exhaustion from OBI. Correlations between stress, disengagement, exhaustion, and depression were analyzed using simple regression analysis. Additionally, causal mediation analysis was conducted to investigate whether depressive symptoms mediate the relationship between stress and burnout (disengagement and exhaustion). Fig. 1 outlines the hypothetical causal model. This analysis quantified the total effect of perceived stress on burnout, dividing it into the direct effect (natural direct effect) and the indirect effect mediated by depressive symptoms (natural indirect effect). Separate models were developed for disengagement and exhaustion, with stress as the predictor and depression as the mediator. All models were adjusted for confounders, including gender, age, specialty, working more than 52 hours per week, emergency room (ER) duties, and intensive care unit (ICU) duties. Bootstrapping with 500 replications was performed to derive the 95% confidence intervals. A P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, version 24 (IBM Corporation, Armonk, NY, USA) and R: A Language and Environment for Statistical Computing (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Ethics statement

The Institutional Review Board (IRB) of Kangdong Sacred Heart Hospital approved the waiver of written consent (IRB No. 2023-09-015) because the survey was fully anonymous and did not include any identifying information.

RESULTS

Participants

Of the 491 members in the Professors’ Association group chat, 135 responded (27.5% response rate). Among the participants, 54.8% were male. The most common age group was 35–49 years (n = 78, 57.8%), followed by ≥ 50 years (n = 50, 37.0%) and ≤ 34 years (n = 7, 5.2%). Most participants specialized in internal medicine (n = 79, 58.4%), whereas clinical support fields were the least represented (n = 15, 11.1%). Overall, 91.9% worked more than 52 hours per week. ICU duties were performed by 8.1% (n = 11), and ER duties by 4.4% (n = 6) (Tables 1 and 2).

The OBI revealed that 96.3% (n = 130) experienced disengagement, 98.5% (n = 133) experienced emotional exhaustion, and 99.3% (n = 134) experienced at least one burnout symptom. In the PHQ-9 assessment, 68.9% (n = 93) of the participants screened positive for depressive symptoms.

Associated factors for stress, depression, and burnout

Disengagement was associated with specialty and ICU duties. Internal medicine specialists scored significantly higher than those in other specialties. Physicians with ICU duties reported greater disengagement.

Emotional exhaustion was influenced by gender, age, specialty, working hours, and ICU duties. Women, younger individuals, and internal medicine specialists reported higher exhaustion levels compared to their counterparts. Those working more than 52 hours per week and those performing ICU duties also had higher exhaustion scores.

The severity of depressive symptoms varied based on working hours and ICU duties. Physicians working more than 52 hours per week had higher PHQ-9 scores than those working fewer hours, and those performing ICU duties scored higher than those without ICU responsibilities.

Perceived stress levels were linked to age, specialty, and ICU duties. The average PSS score was highest among younger staff and decreased with age. Among specialties, internal medicine reported the highest stress levels compared to surgery and clinical support fields. Elevated stress levels were also associated with ICU duties (Tables 1 and 2).

Causal mediation analysis

The mediating effect of depression on the relationship between stress and burnout (emotional exhaustion and disengagement) was analyzed. For the relationship between stress and disengagement, the P value of 0.004 indicated a significant causal mediation effect of depressive symptoms. However, in the relationship between stress and exhaustion, the P value for the indirect effect was 0.28, suggesting no significant mediation effect (Table 5, Fig. 2). In summary, depressive symptoms significantly mediated the relationship between stress and disengagement but did not mediate the relationship between stress and exhaustion.

DISCUSSION

We found that 99.3% of faculty members experienced at least one symptom of burnout, with 98.5% reporting emotional exhaustion and 96.3% reporting disengagement. These rates were higher than previously reported burnout rates among medical faculty: emotional exhaustion (25.0%5–70.3%6), depersonalization (5.6%5–72.3%6), and reduced personal accomplishment (36.4%5–100%6). A 2014 Korean study found that 37.1% of faculty members exhibited at least one burnout symptom.4 Similarly, the prevalence of depression in our study was 68.9%, exceeding the previously reported rate of 20% in the U.S.15 and 40.6% in Kazakhstan16 among medical faculty. Our findings indicate higher rates of burnout and depression, although comparisons are complicated by differing definitions and assessment tools.

In this study, women and younger staff members were more emotionally exhausted. Research on physicians has often associated higher burnout risk with women,15152 although some studies report no gender differences53 or higher exhaustion in men.16 We observed greater exhaustion in women but no significant gender differences in disengagement. Female faculty have reported more emotional exhaustion, which may be attributed to the dual burden of professional and caregiving roles,52 as well as frequent experiences of bias and discrimination.5455 Concerning disengagement, since burnout is a multifaceted concept, its relationship with gender may vary depending on the dimensions assessed. Lackritz56 reported higher emotional exhaustion in female faculty and higher depersonalization in male faculty. Regarding depressive symptoms, we found no significant gender-based differences. Previous studies on university hospital staff have yielded inconsistent results, with some studies suggesting a higher risk of depression in women,16 others reporting marginal significance,15 and some finding no differences.57 Among medical faculty, factors beyond gender may play a more significant role.

Younger age and extended working hours are established risk factors for burnout and depression,24121415165152585960 although contrary findings have been reported.4 Consistent with prior research, we found that staff under the age of 35 experienced greater exhaustion. At this early career stage, they would face simultaneous demands of clinical care, research, and teaching,52 often with less developed professional skills,61 limited control, and extended working hours.6263 Family responsibilities, such as raising children,64 may further exacerbate exhaustion.

We also found that those working more than 52 hours per week had higher levels of exhaustion and depression. During the COVID-19 pandemic, burnout risk increased by 1.11 times per additional five weekly hours of patient care.51 A 2020 study reported that 69.3% of medical faculty worked more than 52 hours weekly,65 rising to 91.9% in our 2024 study. In our study, 11.9% of participants worked more than 100 hours per week, further increasing their vulnerability.

We found that internal medicine specialists exhibited higher levels of exhaustion and disengagement compared to other specialties. To maintain anonymity, we categorized the specialties into internal medicine, surgery, and clinical support. The internal medicine group showed greater burnout due to the inclusion of high-risk fields such as emergency medicine266 and oncology.66 Loiselle et al.66 reported the highest burnout rates among emergency physicians, followed by medical and surgical oncologists. Burnout was particularly prevalent in frontline specialties such as family medicine, internal medicine, and emergency medicine.67

Our finding that ICU duties increase exhaustion, disengagement, and depression aligns with previous studies. Burnout and depression are prevalent among ICU workers.68697071 A study of 3,461 individuals found that ICU staff experienced significantly higher levels of depression compared to non-ICU staff.72

Healthcare systems also influence burnout. For example, excessive regulation52 and loss of autonomy59 are significant contributors. Recent government actions, such as increasing medical school admissions without consensus, threatening punitive measures against striking residents,73 and proposing legislation to exclude ERs from strikes,74 have likely amplified feelings of regulatory pressure and diminished autonomy.

Stress is another key factor contributing to burnout. The World Health Organization defines burnout as “a syndrome resulting from chronic workplace stress that has not been successfully managed.”75 Higher perceived stress levels were associated with burnout among healthcare professionals during the COVID-19 pandemic.327677 Similarly, in our study, exhaustion and disengagement showed a positive correlation with perceived stress levels.

We also found that depression was significantly correlated with exhaustion and disengagement, consistent with numerous studies reporting an association between depression and burnout.17242530787980 However, the relationship between depression and burnout remains complex. Some studies suggest that burnout predicts or contributes to depression,28293081 while others indicate that depression can influence burnout.30 Longitudinal studies suggest a bidirectional relationship between burnout and depression.2482

Furthermore, stress does not necessarily lead to burnout. Excessive stress or insufficient coping ability can result in negative consequences.42 By impairing coping ability, depression could mediate the pathway from stress to burnout. Although there is no prior research on this among physicians, similar findings have been reported in other cohorts. Mental strain has been shown to affect athletes’ burnout both directly and indirectly through depression.35 Similarly, in a sample of 2,555 dentists, job strain led to burnout through depression.83 In our study, depressive symptoms significantly mediated the relationship between stress and disengagement but not between stress and exhaustion. This result may reflect the multifaceted nature of burnout, complicating its associations with other variables.26 The various components of burnout may have distinct relationships with depression. For example, van Dam84 identified two subgroups of burnout, mild and severe symptom groups, and suggested that their relationships with other psychopathologies can differ.84

Our findings show that depression mediates the pathway from stress to disengagement. Combined with prior research indicating that exhaustion mediates the pathway from stress to depression,34 this suggest mutual reinforcement between the two processes. Stress and exhaustion increase the risk of depression, while stress and depression together elevate the risk of disengagement. Thus, under stressful conditions, exhaustion would heighten the likelihood of developing both depression and disengagement over time.

This study has several limitations. First, the response rate was 27.5%, suggesting possible potential selection bias. The survey’s title, “Evaluation of Burnout and Depression,” may have attracted individuals already affected or more aware of these issues, leading to overestimated prevalence. Second, reliance on self-reported surveys limited the assessment to subjective measures and may have introduced biases such as social desirability or recall bias. Objective assessments should be considered in future research. Third, the study was conducted within a single university hospital system, limiting generalizability. Caution is needed when applying these findings to broader populations of medical faculty across institutions or countries. Lastly, the cross-sectional design of this study limits causal inference. Longitudinal studies are needed to establish causality and temporal precedence.

Despite these limitations, this study provides valuable insights by identifying the highly prevalent mental health problems and associated risk factors among medical faculty during a public health crisis. This illustrates the impact of abrupt, unilateral policy changes and mass resignations on healthcare workers. These findings emphasize the importance of thoughtful policy planning and implementation, as health policies should prioritize improving healthcare quality and efficiency rather than exacerbating burnout and depression among healthcare providers, which could ultimately harm public health outcomes. Our study emphasizes the urgent need for targeted interventions such as gender equity education, mentorship for younger staff, and workforce expansion to reduce long working hours. As organizational interventions have proven more effective than individual-focused approaches,85 institutions must take a more proactive role. Without effective measures, these conditions will remain unsustainable, leading to the departure of depressed and burnt-out faculty, increased workloads for the remaining staff, and further deterioration in their mental health and quality of life. Furthermore, this study revealed the mediating role of depressive symptoms in the relationship between stress and burnout. This insight supports the development of targeted interventions to address burnout effectively. Treating depression can help reduce disengagement, but it is less effective in addressing exhaustion.

In conclusion, burnout and depression were highly prevalent among medical faculty during the healthcare crises, although selection bias may have contributed. Burnout and depression were associated with gender, age, specialty, working hours, and ICU duties. Depressive symptoms significantly mediated the relationship between stress and disengagement but not between stress and exhaustion. Interventions for burnout and depression among medical faculty are urgently needed.
