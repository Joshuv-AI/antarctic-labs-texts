Beyond ‘low salt, low fat’: Reimagining nutrition advice in the cardiac discharge summary
Authors: Suvetha Kannappan, Rajendiran Gopalan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Despite strong evidence on the role of nutrition in secondary prevention of cardiovascular diseases, most cardiac discharge advises are generic and vague. This commentary advocates for a paradigm shift in post-discharge dietary counseling, positioning it as a critical, evidence-based tool for secondary prevention. Gathering evidence from global and interventional studies, we discuss the need for specific, prescriptive nutritional advice during discharge when patients are in the most receptive period. This commentary also briefs about the challenges that prevent clinicians from delivering meaningful dietary advice. We propose a multi-level strategy: integrating nutrition and behavior change counseling into medical education and licensing standards; building physician competence through experiential methods like culinary medicine; embedding structured dietary prescriptions into electronic health records; and ensuring consistent discharge summary quality through institutional policies and training. Furthermore, aligning policy reforms—including insurance coverage for nutrition counseling and food-is-medicine initiatives—will be essential to scale these interventions equitably. By redefining the role of nutrition in discharge planning, health systems can improve patient engagement, reduce readmissions, and contribute meaningfully to cardiovascular disease prevention and health system sustainability.

Introduction

The immediate period following hospitalization is a critical window for optimal recovery. During this time, patients tend to be most receptive to guidance and interventions [1]. The discharge advice given by the treating physician plays a crucial role in this process. Discharge summaries serve as one of the vital documents that not only provide information regarding the course of treatment provided during hospitalization, but also the care plan following discharge [2]. Patients with chronic health problems, especially cardiac conditions like post-myocardial infarction, and heart failure require specific discharge advice, which, if unclear, may lead to frequent readmissions [1]. The American Heart Association (AHA) and American College of Cardiology Foundation (ACCF) guidelines clearly state the need for including non-pharmacological therapies in the care plan for patients with chronic cardiac conditions [3]. Yet, the nutritional components of discharge summaries are vague, failing to empower patients with a powerful tool for secondary prevention [4]. This review seeks to underscore the importance of providing precise, evidence-based nutritional guidance to cardiac patients at the time of discharge, and to examine the scientific rationale supporting this practice.

Why ‘low salt, low fat’ is not enough

It is common to encounter discharge advice restricted to vague terms like ‘low salt, low fat diet’ which are often overlooked by patients or, when acknowledged, lack the specificity needed to facilitate meaningful dietary changes [[5], [6], [7]]. Oversimplification in the discharge summary may not only misinform patients, but may also be a missed opportunity for a teachable moment [1]. Accordingly, the global burden of disease (GBD) study 2019 highlights that the leading dietary risk factors for cardiovascular disease (CVD) are not just high sodium intake but low intake of whole grains, fruits, vegetables, nuts and seeds, and omega-3 fatty acids [8].

Beyond restriction: the power of plant-predominant diets in reducing cardiovascular risk

Dietary factors contribute to 56 % of Disability Adjusted Life Years (DALYs), making them the most important modifiable risk factors to be addressed [8]. Along with pharmacotherapy and other lifestyle modification measures, dietary intervention remains a key component to address the residual risk of CVD recurrence [[9], [10], [11]].While it is important to reduce sodium and saturated fat, a transformative dietary pattern requires inclusion of nutrient-rich foods particularly plant-predominant whole foods [12]. There is a growing body of evidence on the role of plant-predominant dietary patterns with minimal intake of animal foods in reducing the risk as well as recurrence of cardiovascular disease (CVD) [13]. According to the AHA/ACC clinical practice guidelines, there is a strong (class1) recommendation for inclusion of vegetables, fruits, legumes, nuts, whole grains, and lean protein to reduce the risk of CVD events [3].The Mediterranean diet, and Dietary Approach to Stop Hypertension (DASH) represent some of the most effective evidence-based dietary interventions that could meet the AHA/ACC recommendations [12,14].While evidence underscores the transformative potential of plant-predominant diets in mitigating cardiovascular risk, maximizing their impact requires clear communication and reinforcement during critical care transitions—particularly at the time of discharge, where physicians can act as powerful catalysts for nutritional behavior change [15].

Physician-led nutritional guidance at hospital discharge: opportunities and barriers

When physicians actively engage and take ownership of delivering key health messages—particularly those related to diet, and behavior change—they not only enhance patient trust and motivation but also significantly improve adherence, and long-term health outcomes [16,17]. Discharge after a hospitalization is a teachable window that can be well utilized by physicians. Optimizing discharge instructions may be particularly impactful in the window of time between discharge and rehabilitation or discharge and follow-up [1,15,16]. During this window of time, patients may be psychologically receptive, and more inclined to reflect on their health, reassess their lifestyle choices and adopt healthy behavior [1,16].

Yet an important question is “Are physicians adequately equipped to provide such nutrition guidance at discharge”? Despite patient trust in physicians, several barriers limit their effectiveness in this domain, including time constraints, and low self-efficacy in nutritional counseling [18]. Recent frameworks on medical nutrition education recommend that physicians achieve core competencies in nutrition—including knowledge of dietary guidelines, skills in nutrition assessment, application of dietary interventions in chronic disease management, effective patient-centered counseling, and collaboration with other members of the team [3,15,17].

A global systematic review by Crowley, Ball, and Hiddink [19] confirmed that nutrition training remains inadequate across medical schools worldwide, regardless of setting or stage of training. This deficit undermines physicians’ knowledge, skills, and confidence to deliver effective nutrition care—now recognized as an essential component of clinical practice, as emphasized in the EAT–Lancet Commission report [19,20]. As previously discussed, with mounting evidence supporting plant-predominant diets in cardiovascular care, it is increasingly important to strengthen physicians’ competence in counseling patients on these dietary approaches [[21], [22], [23]].

Another critical aspect that influences counseling is how physicians perceive their self-care, and health behavior. Analysis of a group of physicians in the SUN study revealed that physicians who were following a healthy diet counseled their patients on healthy nutrition than their not so healthy counterparts [24].

To overcome the gaps in physician preparedness and patient guidance, it is necessary to translate nutrition science into clear, structured discharge practices that deliver practical, and personalized dietary advice [14].

From concept to practice: a framework for reimagining dietary advice at discharge

The transformation of nutrition advice from being vague to being structured, prescriptive, and patient-centered requires a multi-pronged approach. To have a sustainable change, we have to intervene at various levels –from medical education to health policy reforms. This section highlights strategies that may help bridge the gap between theory and practice.

Building competence in nutrition education and physician readiness

Improving the quality of nutrition education requires an innovative, inter-professional, and socio-culturally relevant approach that combines formal training with personal wellness, and team-based practice [25,26]. Many countries have already developed curricular frameworks that define nutritional competencies for medical students, residents, and practicing physicians [17,27]. Some medical schools have successfully integrated nutrition training into their curricula, demonstrating the feasibility of incorporating nutrition education across different stages of training [17]. Equally important is physician readiness. Physicians who adopt healthy lifestyles themselves experience dual benefits in patient care: they act as credible role models, thereby strengthening patient trust, and they gain experiential insight into the challenges of behaviour change, which enhances the quality of their counselling [19]. To further build competence, immersive learning strategies such as culinary medicine training, hands-on cooking workshops, and lifestyle immersion retreats could be effective educational models. These experiential methods allow physicians to internalize the principles of healthy eating, overcome practical barriers, and better empathize with the challenges their patients face in making dietary changes [17,25].

Physician-led, team-supported approach to dietary assessment and counseling

While physicians should lay the foundation by initiating and reinforcing the importance of dietary change, a multidisciplinary team including registered dietitians, nurses, physician assistants, and trainees are essential to translate these recommendations into practice [1,3,15]. Registered dietitians, in particular, play a crucial role in conducting detailed dietary assessments, translate evidence-based guidelines into individualized meal plans, and provide ongoing support to patients. Their expertise ensures that nutrition recommendations are not only accurate and practical but also culturally appropriate and sustainable, thereby complementing and reinforcing the physician’s initial counseling [1,28,29].

Strengthening nutrition guidance through institutional discharge practices

Institutional support is imperative to convert evidence-based dietary guidelines to practice through the creation of standards for providing structured and actionable discharge practices. Some strategies provided below outline how health care organizations can integrate these guidelines into the regular workflow.

Customized electronic health record (EHR) templates

Screening tools, and dietary recommendations must be integrated into the EHR templates. This not only ensures consistency but also avoids variability between physicians. Some templates, like prompts for specific cardiac conditions like heart failure, model food plates tailored to national guidelines, and cultural variations, will be helpful and time-saving [30,31]. Use of regional language, illustrations, and info graphics has been shown to improve patient adherence, especially among those with low literacy [32].

To enable physicians to approach nutrition prescriptions with the same vigor as pharmacological prescriptions, customized, validated diet prescriptions have to be integrated into the EHR. Using structured models such as TAF (type, amount, frequency) or FITT (frequency, intensity, type, time), clinicians can provide specific, actionable dietary recommendations [33]. A sample prescription template based on evidence-based recommendations for a plant predominant diet is provided in supplement 1 [3,11,14].

Building competence and accountability in discharge nutrition practices

In most institutions, residents and junior doctors are the primary authors of discharge summary, but most of them lack training in crafting it. Hospitals need to include periodic training for all physicians on discharge summary writing as part of their orientation and/or as continuous professional development with a focus on the importance of highlighting lifestyle change. Training programs should be complemented with periodic audits using standardized checklists for discharge summaries, thereby ensuring quality documentation, minimizing omissions, and promoting timely completion. Institutional adoption of standardized templates with structured rubrics can further enhance completeness [30,34,35].

Ensuring effective communication with follow-up providers

A significant challenge to follow up and sustainability is due to the communication gap between secondary, and primary care physicians (PCP) as well as registered dietitians. This challenge can be overcome by establishing a good communication channel between them. Providing access to discharge summaries, establishing systems for direct provider communication with PCPs, and registered dietitians will ensure continued care, thereby paving the way for reduced readmissions, and improved quality of care [29,32,36,37].

Policy-level strategies: elevating nutrition as a clinical priority

Nutrition counseling should be covered in insurance reimbursements to ensure adherence, and sustainability [29,[38], [39], [40]]. Policy initiatives should prioritize programs such as Food is Medicine, which provide produce prescriptions and tailored groceries to support individuals from low-income populations. In parallel, fostering strong partnerships between public health agencies, and clinical care systems is essential to scale up community-based interventions, including subsidized food programs and urban farming initiatives [41].

Conclusion

Redefining dietary advice at the point of discharge is not merely aspirational but a practical, evidence-based strategy to improve patient outcomes, and reduce long-term healthcare burden. Achieving this requires structured discharge processes, alignment with evidence-based nutrition guidance, physician competence and accountability, and supportive institutional policies. By strengthening the quality and clarity of discharge advice, we can transform this critical transition point into a powerful opportunity for sustained cardiovascular risk reduction.
