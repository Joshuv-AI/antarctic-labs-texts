Medicaid prescription cap policies and acute care use and mortality among enrollees with opioid use disorder
Authors: Patience M. Dow, Christopher M. Santostefano, Landon D. Hughes, Elizabeth G. Stettenbauer, Theresa I. Shireman, Julie M. Donohue, Lisa Peterson, Jaclyn M.W. Hughto
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background:Twelve state Medicaid programs limit the monthly number of covered prescriptions. Such cap policies may force enrollees to forego essential medications with important health consequences. We aimed to determine the impact of cap policies on acute care use and all-cause mortality among enrollees with opioid use disorder (OUD).Methods:Using 2016–2019 T-MSIS Analytical Files, we propensity-score matched enrollees with OUD in 12 states with cap policies and 26 states without cap policies. Outcomes measured over 12 months included emergency department (ED) visits, hospitalization, and all-cause mortality and were analyzed via generalized linear regression models. We conducted subgroup analyses by use of medications for OUD (MOUD) and comorbidity level and sensitivity analyses to examine the role of cap policy characteristics.Results:Unadjusted risks were 64.0% vs. 62.5% for ED visits, 27.6% vs. 27.5% for hospitalizations, and 3.2% vs. 2.7% for mortality in cap states and non-cap states, respectively. After adjustment, hospitalization risk was higher (RR=1.89, 99.5%CI:1.13,3.16) in cap states than non-cap states whereas ED visits and mortality did not differ. There were largely no outcome differences by cap status in subgroups. Strict prescription limits allowing 3–4 prescriptions monthly (RR=1.90, 95%CI:1.09,3.30) and lack of MOUD exemptions (RR=2.23, 95% CI:1.32,3.78) were associated with increased hospitalization risk relative to non-cap states.Conclusions:Medicaid prescription cap policies were associated with increased hospitalization risk, but there were no differences in ED use or all-cause mortality. Cap policies may undermine the health of individuals with OUD and could be counterproductive to state efforts to curb Medicaid spending.

Background

Approximately 2.1 million people in the U.S. have opioid use disorder (OUD) (Dydyk et al., 2025) and Medicaid insures about 40% of these individuals - more than any other payer (Donohue et al., 2021; McCarty et al., 2019). Amid large and growing Medicaid enrollments combined with perennial budget constraints, 12 state Medicaid programs currently operate prescription cap policies as a cost-containment strategy (Hughto et al., 2022). Prescription caps limit the monthly number of Medicaid-covered brand-name drugs (Alabama, Illinois, Mississippi, Oklahoma, Tennessee), total number of prescriptions (Arkansas, Kansas, Louisiana, Texas), or controlled substance prescriptions (Florida, Georgia, Wisconsin) (Kaiser Family Foundation. State Medicaid Prescription Limits, 2019). While prior research has associated cap policies with reduced prescription drug access, and morbidity and mortality in the general Medicaid population (Geiger et al., 2021; Lieberman et al., 2016; Martin and McMillan, 1996; Soumerai et al., 1994, 1991), limited research has explored the impact of caps on Medicaid beneficiaries with OUD (Besaw and Fry, 2024; Dow et al., 2025).

People with OUD are at high risk of overdoses and several chronic conditions, including HIV, hepatitis, heart disease, and other comorbidities, raising the importance of mitigating harm in this population (Dawson, K. 2018; Donohue et al., 2021; Frontera and Gradon, 2000; Haider et al., 2020; Hodder et al., 2021; Hughto et al., 2022; McCarty et al., 2019; National Institute on Drug Abuse., 2018; Nelson et al., 2011). The Food and Drug Administration has approved three medications for OUD (MOUD) – buprenorphine, methadone, and naltrexone (Kampman and Jarvis, 2015; National Institute on Drug Abuse, 2018) - which have been shown to reduce the risk of overdose mortality and OUD-related chronic conditions (American Society of Addiction Medicine (ASAM), 2020; Gowing et al., 2008; Larochelle et al., 2018; National Academies of Sciences et al., 2019; Puzhko et al., 2022; Tsui et al., 2014). Given that OUD and related conditions are often best treated with prescription medications; cap policies may contribute to adverse health outcomes by forcing Medicaid beneficiaries with OUD who are subject to the cap to make tradeoffs between which medications to fill. A recent study using national Medicaid State Drug Utilization Data found that enrollees with OUD in states with cap policies filled nearly 15,000 fewer MOUD prescriptions per 100,000 enrollees – a finding that is particularly problematic because MOUD reduces the risk of morbidity and mortality (Besaw and Fry, 2024). Another study found that Medicaid enrollees with OUD in states with cap policies had a greater likelihood of early discontinuation and other trajectories of inconsistent buprenorphine use relative to their counterparts in states without cap policies (Dow et al., 2025). While these emerging studies highlight the impact of cap policies on MOUD utilization, no study, to our knowledge, has explored the relationship between cap policies and health outcomes among enrollees with OUD (Burns et al., 2022).

To address these gaps, we identified Medicaid beneficiaries with OUD living in cap and non-cap states between 2016 and 2018 and assessed the occurrence of emergency department (ED) visits, hospitalizations, and all-cause mortality during a 12-month follow-up period. We also evaluated the impacts of cap policies within subgroups of baseline MOUD use and comorbid chronic conditions and examined the influence of specific attributes of cap policies. Findings can be used to inform Medicaid policy decision-making.

Methods

This study used secondary de-identified data and was deemed exempt by the Brown University Institutional Review Board. Informed consent was not required. This study followed the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) reporting guidelines.

Data sources

We used person-level annual Medicaid T-MSIS Analytic Files (TAF) from 2016 to 2019. TAF data included demographics, eligibility and enrollment, prescription drug claims, inpatient hospital claims, long-term care claims, and outpatient service claims. We conducted a retrospective cohort study given the availability of complete data and stability of cap policies over the study period precluding quasiexperimental designs. We constructed a cohort of eligible individuals with OUD in 12 states with a cap policy during the study period (AL, AR, CA, FL, GA, KS, LA, MS, OK, TN, TX, WI) and propensity-score matched them to individuals with OUD in 26 states, including the District of Columbia, without a cap policy (AK, AZ, CO, CT, DC, DE, HI, KY, IA, IN, MA, MD, ME, MN, MO, MT, NC, NJ, NM, NV, NY, OH, OR, PA, WA, WV). We excluded the remaining 12 states (eTable 1 in the Supplement) for TAF data quality concerns involving cohort or outcome identification.

Participants

We first identified all non-elderly, non-dual eligible, community-dwelling adults (ages 22–64 years) with an ICD-9 or ICD-10 diagnosis code (eTable 2) for OUD between 2016 and 2018. We excluded older adults (age 65+) and those dually eligible for Medicare, whose linked Medicare data was unavailable for this study, as well as adults up to age 21 and residents of long-term care facilities, who were exempt from cap policies in these states. The index date was the earliest OUD diagnosis date after a six-month period during which a given beneficiary was continuously enrolled in Medicaid in a single state and eligible for full/ comprehensive benefits. Eligible individuals were followed for twelve months, or until death, whichever was sooner. Individuals who lost full/ comprehensive Medicaid coverage, became eligible for Medicare, or moved out of their baseline state before the end of the 12-month follow-up window were excluded.

Exposure

In the primary analysis, our binary exposure was the presence of any state Medicaid prescription cap policy, which was constant for all states in our sample between 2016 and 2019. Cap policies are heterogeneous. Thus, we also conducted two sensitivity analyses where we specified the cap exposure as a categorical variable based on the number of prescriptions allowed under the cap or whether MOUD were explicitly exempt from the cap. These three-level exposure categories were defined as: a) no cap vs. cap with 3–4 drug limit vs. cap with 5–8 drug limit and b) no cap vs. cap with MOUD exemption vs. cap without MOUD exemption.

Outcomes

We analyzed three outcomes as potential proxies for adverse health consequences: 1) any ED visits, 2) any hospitalization, and 3) all-cause mortality. Each binary outcome was coded as present if observed in the 12 months following the index OUD diagnosis date. ED utilization was determined by inpatient and outpatient revenue center codes 0450, 0451, 0452, 0456, 0459, and 0981. Hospitalization was identified as unique admission records with bill values corresponding to inpatient or critical access hospital services using the inpatient header file. Dates of death came from the demographic and eligibility file.

Propensity score variables

Individuals with OUD in cap states were matched 1:1 to their counterparts in non-cap states based on 17 baseline covariates identified in the six months before the index observation date (eTable 3). Nearest neighbor matching within a 0.2 caliper was implemented without replacement, in descending order, with random selection of one neighbor in the case of ties. The covariates were calendar year of index OUD diagnosis, age (+/−5 years), sex as recorded, enrollment in a comprehensive Medicaid managed care organization (MCO), receipt of Supplemental Security Income (SSI), residence in a metropolitan county per the 2013 US Department of Agriculture rural-urban continuum code, (US Department of Agriculture E.R.S., 2025) and the raw count of comorbid conditions identified using the Elixhauser comorbidity algorithm (Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality, 2025) applied to inpatient and outpatient claims. We examined the distribution of comorbidity scores in our sample and categorized the cut-points for the comorbidity counts to represent no (0), low (1), moderate (2−3), and high (4+) levels of comorbidity counts. We also included binary indicators for diagnostic conditions or care encounters coded in inpatient or outpatient claims during the six-month baseline, including serious mental illness, chronic pain, respiratory disease, cardiovascular disease, any hospitalization, any ED visit, any surgical procedure, any Evaluation & Management (E&M) outpatient visit, and any prescription drug utilization. A comprehensive list of diagnostic and procedure codes is available in eTable 2.

Regression variables

After individual-level propensity score matching, we conducted multivariable regression analyses adjusting for five state-level policy measures (eTable 4) defined as of each individual’s index OUD diagnosis date. Each of the following policies may be correlated with the exposure and outcome of interest: 1) Medicaid expansion status under the Affordable Care Act, 2) comprehensiveness (low vs. medium/high) of state addiction treatment benefits based on Medicaid coverage of the ASAM levels of care continuum (Andrews et al., 2018), 3) Medicaid coverage of all three or fewer types of MOUD (Grogan et al., 2016), 4) prior authorization requirement for at least one formulation of buprenorphine (Substance Abuse and Mental Health Services Administration, 2018), and 5) fentanyl spread in the state’s unregulated opioid supply defined by whether each individual’s OUD index date occurred before or after the point in time where there was a profound change in state-level fentanyl spread as previously characterized in a prior study (Zoorob et al., 2024).

Stratification variables

In addition to calculating aggregate effects, we stratified the sample by individual baseline comorbidity burden (categorized as zero conditions, 1 condition, 2–3 conditions, or 4 or more conditions) and MOUD use, defined as any prescription drug claim or procedure code for methadone, buprenorphine, or naltrexone (eTable 5) in the six months prior to their index OUD diagnosis. We relied on procedure codes to identify methadone for OUD treatment rather than using pharmacy drug claims which would capture methadone used for pain treatment. Buprenorphine formulations were limited to those indicated for OUD treatment instead of pain treatment. Only injectable naltrexone was considered given its specific indication for OUD treatment unlike oral naltrexone which is used to treat alcohol use disorder.

Statistical analyses

We calculated standardized mean differences (SMDs) to check for covariate balance after propensity score matching. We then calculated the absolute risk difference between the cap and matched non-cap comparator cohorts and the relative risk of cap status for each of the three outcomes. Relative risks were calculated using generalized estimating equations with a log link and binomial distribution, controlling for the five additional state indicators, with cluster-robust bias-corrected standard errors aggregated to the state level. To correct for multiple comparisons across our strata, we applied a Bonferroni adjustment to our hypothesis tests, using a more conservative significance level of 0.005. This was achieved by dividing the standard alpha level of 0.05 by 9, reflecting three outcomes assessed across three analytic contexts: overall, stratified, and sensitivity analyses.

Subgroup and sensitivity analyses

We performed several additional analyses including subgroup analyses by baseline comorbidity level and type of MOUD utilization and sensitivity analyses that defined the exposure variable as a categorical three-level indicator separately for MOUD cap-exemptions and the level of the prescription limit. eTable 6 provides state-level details on prescription limits which ranged from 3 (Texas) to 6 (Arkansas, California, Mississippi, Oklahoma) and explicit MOUD exemptions which were present in Arkansas, Tennessee, and Wisconsin. We also repeated the main analysis excluding California, which accounted for nearly half of the observations among cap states.

Analyses were performed using SAS version 9.4(Inc., S. I. 2023) and Stata version 18.(LLC., 2023) More information about the data and methods is available from Brown University’s Digital Repository (https://doi.org/10.26300/ztzy-8q61).

Results

Sample characteristics

All eligible individuals with OUD from the 12 cap states matched to their counterparts residing in 26 non-cap states (eFig. 1, eTable 7). The analytic sample included 221,310 Medicaid beneficiaries diagnosed with OUD residing in states with prescription cap policies who were matched to 221,310 beneficiaries with OUD from non-cap states (Table 1). SMDs were all less than 0.1 indicating adequate covariate balance post-matching. After matching, the mean (standard deviation [SD]) of age was 41.0 (11.7) years and 56.7% were female in both cap and non-cap states. Almost one-third of individuals were eligible for SSI: 30.4% (cap states) versus 29.8% (non-cap states). Less than one-half of individuals in cap (48.0%) and non-cap (47.7%) states had at least one co-occurring chronic condition. Baseline health service use was similar between cap and non-cap states. Most individuals had baseline E&M visits (cap: 81.1% vs. non-cap: 81.4%) and filled Medicaid-paid prescriptions (cap: 82.4% vs. non-cap: 82.7%). One-half of individuals had ED use (cap: 49.8% vs. non-cap: 49.6%); whereas 16.9% (cap) and 16.0% (non-cap) were hospitalized at baseline. Baseline methadone use was more prevalent in cap versus non-cap states (18.0% vs. 12.0%) while buprenorphine (9.1% vs. 13.3%) and naltrexone (0.4% vs. 0.9%) were more prevalent in non-cap states. Cap and non-cap states also differed by comprehensiveness of Medicaid coverage for addiction treatment, buprenorphine prior authorization requirements, status of Medicaid expansion, and timing fentanyl spread in the state.

Distributions of outcomes

At 12 months, unadjusted risks for ED visits were 64.0% (cap) vs. 62.5% (non-cap) P = 0.362; for hospitalization, 27.6% (cap) vs. 27.5% (non-cap) P = 0.989; and for all-cause mortality 3.2% (cap) vs. 2.7% (non-cap) P = 0.204 (Table 2). The state-level outcome distributions are in eTable 8. ED visits ranged from 53.5% (Florida) to 73.9% (Ohio), hospitalizations ranged from 17.4% (Maine) to 44.4% (Nevada), and mortality ranged from 0.8% (Alaska) to 7.2% (Nevada).

Within subgroups, individuals without MOUD at baseline had higher risks for all outcomes compared with those treated with MOUD. There were apparent differences in the levels of risk for each outcome based on the type of MOUD received with recipients of naltrexone experiencing greater risks than buprenorphine or methadone recipients regardless of cap status. In comorbidity subgroups, the risks of all outcomes increased with higher counts of baseline comorbidities.

Regression results

Compared with non-cap states, individuals in states with cap policies did not differ in the adjusted risks for ED visits (RR=1.01, 99.5%CI: 0.92,1.12) and mortality (RR=1.27, 99.5%CI: 0.62,2.62) whereas hospitalizations were higher among individuals in cap states relative to those in non-cap states (RR=1.89, 99.5%CI: 1.13,3.16), Fig. 1. Risks of outcomes were also similar by cap status within most subgroups of baseline MOUD use and comorbidity levels except that individuals with no comorbidities in cap states experienced higher risks of hospitalization relative to those in non-cap states (RR=1.93, 95%CI: 1.11,3.36). None of the covariates were significantly associated with ED visits and mortality overall and in subgroups (Table 3); however, state fentanyl spread was associated with significantly increased relative risk of hospitalization in the overall sample (RR=1.16, 99.5%CI: 1.02,1.32), eTable 9.

Sensitivity analyses

Results from the main analyses were substantively similar with alternate exposure specifications in sensitivity analyses for the ED and mortality outcomes. However, there was evidence of increased hospitalization risk among individuals in states without MOUD exemptions (RR=2.23, 99.5%CI: 1.32,3.78) or states with strict caps allowing up to 4 Medicaid-covered prescriptions per month (RR=1.90, 99.5%CI: 1.09,3.30) relative to states without cap policies (Table 3). Lastly, excluding California yielded similar results as observed in the main analysis (eTable 10).

Discussion

This analysis of Medicaid enrollees with OUD from 38 states found that prescription cap policies were associated with increased hospitalization risk but no differences in ED visits and all-cause mortality from 2016 to 2019. We also found that enrollees with OUD in states whose cap policies lacked MOUD exemptions or implemented strict prescription limits had significantly greater hospitalization risk relative to enrollees in states without cap policies. Additionally, the findings indicated that cap policies were associated with increased risk of hospitalization among enrollees without comorbidities. The statistically significant association between cap policies and hospitalization was evident in adjusted but not unadjusted analyses with the results indicating that not accounting for fentanyl spread in the state drug supply concealed this association. Notably, we observed that states with cap policies were much less affected by fentanyl spread relative to non-cap states and fentanyl spread was positively associated with hospitalization which could explain the differences between unadjusted and adjusted results.

Prior research conducted in other populations found that cap policies restrict access to essential medications and are associated with to poor health outcomes (Geiger et al., 2021; Martin and McMillan, 1996; Soumerai et al., 1994, 1991). Geiger et al. examined prescription use before and after the transition to age 21 when Medicaid enrollees become subject to monthly prescription caps, and found a 20% reduction in prescription fills and a 13% increase in hospitalizations for Medicaid recipients with disabilities who became subject to the caps for the first time - a finding that aligns with the elevated risk of hospitalization among enrollees with OUD in our study. Furthermore, our finding that strict cap policies were associated with increased risk of hospitalization is consistent with Besaw et al. who found that higher restriction in prescription caps was associated with lower volumes of MOUD prescriptions filled (Besaw and Fry, 2024). Our study extends the literature by elucidating how cap policies influence health outcomes among Medicaid enrollees with OUD.

Although not a direct focus of this analysis, our study suggests that by imposing caps, Medicaid programs potentially influence the selection and utilization of MOUD. While buprenorphine and methadone use were similarly prevalent within non-cap states, 12.0% and 13.3% respectively; the prevalence of methadone was double that of buprenorphine within cap states (18.0% vs. 9.1%). The finding that buprenorphine and naltrexone utilization are lower in cap than non-cap states is consistent with prior research (Besaw and Fry, 2024); however, we observed similar levels of overall MOUD use between cap states and the matched sample of non-cap states. These differences could be explained by variations in study design including that we assessed the prevalence of all three forms of MOUD among enrollees with OUD while Besaw and Fry assessed quarterly rates of buprenorphine and naltrexone prescriptions per 100,000 of all Medicaid enrollees in the state. Furthermore, our findings underscore that higher methadone use in cap states may offset reductions in Medicaid-covered MOUD prescription fills that are potentially imposed by cap policies. The prominence of methadone’s share of MOUD in cap states could reflect efforts to circumvent restrictions that affect pharmacy fills (for buprenorphine or naltrexone) but not methadone delivered in opioid treatment programs (OTPs). These differences may also reflect state variations in the coverage of methadone and buprenorphine MOUD in Medicaid programs (Christine et al., 2023). Therefore, cap policies possibly exacerbate existing structural barriers to access and utilization of MOUD, contributing to negative health consequences for individuals with OUD. Simultaneously, our finding that cap policies were associated with higher rates of hospitalization suggests that cap policies may not be achieving their intended goals of cost containment in state Medicaid budgets.

This study provides important insights for states that may be considering expanding or adding new cost-containment strategies such as prescription caps. Our findings caution that such policies may undermine the health of vulnerable populations and be counterproductive toward the goal of limiting spending to the extent that cost-savings on prescription drugs are outweighed by much more costly inpatient utilization and administrative workload for states operating cap policies (Madden, 2021). For example, many states allow pharmacists and prescribers to submit prior authorization requests to override or waive prescription limits when medically necessary or in other specific situations (Medicaid and CHIP Payment and Access Commission, 2024). State Medicaid programs operating cap policies therefore need to allocate time and resources to process such requests on an ongoing basis.

The finding of greater hospitalization risk among individuals without comorbidities was surprising because we anticipated that cap policies would disproportionately impact those with more comorbidities and thus require more medications to manage these conditions. A potential explanation is that individuals with multimorbidity could be more exposed to patient-oriented (e.g., patient self-management), professional (e.g., clinician training), and organizational interventions (e.g., case/care management) associated with positive health-related outcomes such as reducing avoidable hospitalizations relative to individuals without multimorbidity.(Poitras et al., 2018; van Loenen et al., 2014) Since we considered the total comorbidity count, the impact of cap policies on subgroups with specific chronic conditions (e.g., diabetes, serious mental illness, hypertension) that typically require maintenance medication is unknown. Further research is warranted.

This study has some limitations. First, we did not employ a natural experiment study design because there were no changes in cap status among the analyzed states during our study period. Therefore, we relied on the first observed date of OUD diagnosis that provided a sufficient look-back period as the index date yielding a study cohort comprising incident and prevalent OUD diagnoses and potentially differential risks for the outcomes. Second, we cannot rule out unmeasured confounding although we took steps to mitigate against confounding through propensity score matching combined with covariate adjustment in regression models. Third, to the extent that enrollees with OUD may not be engaging with the healthcare system, it is less likely that diagnoses will be documented thereby increasing the likelihood of misclassifying comorbidity status and counts. Fourth, we assessed the changepoint for fentanyl spread in each state; however, this covariate may not fully represent the prevalence of fentanyl in the drug supply during the study period. In the absence of well-documented and complete historical national data on fentanyl prevalence in the drug supply, we found that Zoorob et al.’s changepoint approach was reasonable for uniformly estimating the severity of fentanyl spread across the states in our analysis. Fifth, qualitative content analyses of cap policies and additional quantitative research are needed to elucidate the impact of cap policies in fee-for-service versus MCO plans. Finally, our findings may be conservative because applying a stringent level of statistical significance (P = 0.005) to account for multiple testing yielded greater likelihood of null findings.

In conclusion, this study of enrollees with OUD found that the risks for ED visits and all-cause mortality did not differ by state Medicaid prescription cap policy status. However, cap policies still employed by 12 state Medicaid programs accounting for over one-quarter (27%) of U.S. Medicaid enrollment were associated with increased risk of inpatient hospitalization. Barring elimination of cap policies, safeguards such as cap exemptions for MOUD and less strict prescription allowances may be warranted to ensure cap policies do not undermine the health of Medicaid enrollees with OUD.
