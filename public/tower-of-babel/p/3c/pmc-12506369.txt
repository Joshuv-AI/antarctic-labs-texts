High fat diet exacerbated glycolipid metabolism disorder in kidney Yang deficiency rats by interfering with IRS 1-PI3K (p85) -Akt-GLUT 4 pathway
Authors: Han Li, Yunqiao Wang, Wei Zheng, Jing Lu, Cuiyao Tang, Mengyang Long, Bo Liu, Bin Chen, Weihong Li, Zihui Xu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundKidney Yang deficiency (KYD) is associated with disturbances in glucose and lipid metabolism. This study examined whether KYD rats on a high-fat diet (HFD) exhibit aggravated metabolic dysfunction and sought to elucidate the underlying mechanisms.MethodsThree-week-old SD rats were assigned to control (Con), KYD, HFD, and KYD + HFD groups. KYD models were induced through cold exposure and gastric perfusion of Cortex Phellodendri. The HFD groups were provided high-fat diets. After 10 weeks, metabolic parameters, organ weights, blood glucose/lipid profiles, and protein levels (PGC1α, PPARγ, IRS1, Akt, PI3K(p85), GLUT4) were assessed.ResultsKYD rats exhibited lower body temperature, weight, and weight gain (P< 0.01) compared to controls. Liver weight was reduced in the KYD group but increased in the HFD group (P< 0.01). KYD + HFD rats demonstrated increased visceral fat (P < 0.01) and decreased muscle mass. The KYD + HFD group showed a higher liver-to-body weight ratio (P < 0.05) and significantly elevated cholesterol and LDL levels (P< 0.01 vs. other groups). Muscle PPARγ was lower in the KYD group, while the AUC of glucose tolerance was elevated in the KYD + HFD group (P< 0.05). GLUT4 and PI3K(p85) levels were reduced in both KYD and HFD groups (P < 0.01，P<0.01). IRS1 expression in skeletal muscle was lower in both KYD and HFD groups (P < 0.01) but was higher in KYD compared to HFD (P < 0.01). Akt expression was marginally increased in KYD and significantly higher in HFD groups.ConclusionsKYD predisposes rats to visceral fat accumulation, dyslipidemia, and fatty liver despite lower weight gain. Mechanistically, the impairment of the IRS1-Akt-PI3K(p85)-GLUT4 signaling pathway in KYD compromises insulin action, exacerbating lipid metabolic disturbances under HFD. These findings highlight the dietary risks for individuals with KYD and emphasize the need for targeted interventions aimed at specific metabolic pathways.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12944-025-02721-4.Keywords: Kidney yang deficiency, IRS1-PI3K(p85)-Akt-GLUT4, High-fat diet, Low metabolism, Hypometabolism, Metabolic disorders

Introduction

A high-fat diet is a primary contributor to type 2 diabetes, cardiovascular and cerebrovascular diseases, malignant tumors, and other health conditions [1–4] and can also adversely affect the health of offspring [5, 6]. With the global trend toward higher lipid consumption, the prevalence of diabetes rose from approximately 333 million cases in 2005 to 435 million in 2015, marking a 30.6% increase [7]. Globally, the count of individuals with diabetes reached 529 million by 2021, corresponding to an age-standardized prevalence of 6.1%. Estimates indicate that this trajectory is projected to continue by 2050, over 1.31 billion people (ranging from 1.22 billion to 1.39 billion) will have diabetes [8], accompanied by a host of complications and substantial economic and social burdens [9, 10]. As such, a comprehensive understanding of the underlying mechanisms and optimization of clinical management for individuals with glucose and lipid metabolism disorders is critical.

The insulin signaling pathway plays a central role in maintaining nutrient absorption and utilization, which is essential for energy metabolism. The insulin receptor substrate-1 (IRS-1)/phosphatidylinositol-3 kinase (PI3K)/protein kinase B (Akt)/glucose transporter type 4 (GLUT4) pathway is pivotal for insulin regulation of glucose homeostasis. Dysregulation of this pathway is closely linked to the onset of metabolic diseases and remains a global focal point of metabolic research [11, 12].

Kidney Yang deficiency (KYD) is a common pattern in Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM), characterized by sensitivity to cold, a preference for warmth, and reduced metabolic function. Typical symptoms include cold intolerance, fatigue, lower back and knee pain, as well as diminished libido and fertility. Kidney Yang governs the body’s overall Yang energy, and adequate Kidney Yang is essential for the normal function of internal organs. When KYD fails to properly warm and activate the Spleen Yang, it disrupts the Spleen’s transport and transformation functions, leading to impaired nutrient absorption and the accumulation of dampness and phlegm in the middle Jiao. Prolonged heat accumulation may eventually deplete Yin, giving rise to conditions such as wasting and thirst disorders, and obesity.

The adenine - induced KYD model, a commonly used experimental paradigm, can lead to a more severe form of KYD. This is manifested as abnormal spermatogenesis and infertility. Notably, these pathological alterations primarily manifest as pathological changes in the testes, epididymides, and seminal vesicles, accompanied by impaired sperm motility [13]. Professor Li Weihong developed a distinctive KYD model based on the Chinese medicine constitution theory [14, 15]. This model incorporates the combined effects of “internal cold” and “external cold” as etiological factors to simulate the influence of both innate constitution and environmental factors on the physical development of individuals. Offspring mice were used for model evaluation and experimentation. Drawing inspiration from the concepts of variety breeding in agronomy and zoology, this model aligns more closely with the TCM understanding of etiology and pathogenesis. The model rats mainly showed apparent changes rather than obvious physiological, biochemical and pathological changes (Appendix1), which is more consistent with the concept of KYD in TCM. The methodology involved administering Huangbai to simulate an internal cold environment, because according to TCM, it belongs to the Kidney meridian and the lower Jiao. External exposure was set at 4 °C for several hours per day to mimic an external cold environment, consistent with the TCM belief that cold evil can harm the Kidney.

Individuals with KYD are prone to metabolic dysfunction, which manifests as reduced sugar, fat, and protein metabolism; but the underlying mechanisms was still unclear [16]. Based on this, two hypotheses have been put forward: (1) Individuals with KYD are more susceptible to metabolic dysfunction, and (2) KYD disrupts key metabolic molecular pathways, leading to metabolic diseases. To explore these hypotheses, this study examined whether rats with KYD are more prone to abnormalities in glucose and lipid metabolism under the influence of a high-fat diet (HFD), or whether any metabolic corrections occur. Given the low energy state associated with KYD, an HFD was selected for energy supply, while a normal diet provided a comparative baseline. Additionally, the impact on the IRS1-PI3K (p85)-Akt-GLUT4 signaling pathway was assessed.

Materials and methods

Animal and experimental design

This study was approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Chengdu Dashuo Experimental Animal Co., Ltd. (SCXK (Sichuan) 2015-030). The study process is outlined in Fig. 1.

Study flowchart

Sprague-Dawley (SD) rats were exposed to a cold environment (4 °C, 2 h/day), and Huang Bo (Cortex Phellodendri, sourced from the pharmacy of Sichuan Hospital) was added to the diet of pregnant mice (0.108 g per 100 g chow) to establish the KYD rat model. The KYD model was constructed based on the KYD macroscale trait score scale (Attachment 1), and the fourth-generation KYD rats were selected as experimental subjects. The rats were randomly divided into four groups: Control group (n = 10, Con), KYD group (n = 10, KYD), HFD group (n = 10, HFD), and KYD high-fat feeding group (n = 10, KYD + HFD). Body weight and food intake were measured every two weeks. All rats used in the experiment were 3-week-old males. The Con and KYD groups were fed a standard rodent diet (14% fat), while the HFD and KYD + HFD groups received a high-fat pellet diet (43% fat, Jiangsu Sutong Biological Co., Ltd., China). The HFD formula was provided by Professor Hui Chen (University of Technology, Sydney) [17].

After 9 weeks of HFD feeding, the rats were fasted for 5 h before undergoing the intraperitoneal glucose tolerance test (IPGTT). All rats were injected with D-glucose (2 g/kg, intraperitoneally) using a 26G syringe needle. Blood samples were collected from the tail tip before glucose injection (baseline 0) and at 15, 30, 60, and 120 min after injection. Blood glucose levels were measured using an AccuCheck® Performa glucose meter (Roche, Branchburg, NJ, USA). The area under the glucose-time curve (AUC) for each animal was calculated using the trapezoidal method, and the Homeostasis Model Assessment (HOMA) index, representing insulin resistance (fasting glucose level × fasting insulin level/22.5), was calculated. At week 13, the rats were fasted overnight, and rectal temperature was measured following anesthesia with 2% pentobarbital (40 mg/kg). Blood was collected via cardiac puncture, and liver, kidney, scapular brown fat, perihepatic white fat, and gastrocnemius muscle were dissected and weighed.

Blood chemical analysis

Blood glucose and glucose tolerance were measured using a glucose meter with Accu-Chek Perform strips (Roche Diabetes Care GmbH, Germany). Plasma insulin levels were assessed using a commercial kit (Crystal Chem, Corporate Headquarters 1536 Brook Drive, Suite A, Downers Grove, IL 60515 USA). Plasma low-density lipoprotein (LDL) and cholesterol were measured using a commercial kit (Abcam, Room 102, Unit A and Unit B, Building 1, No.998 Harley Road, China (Shanghai) Pilot Free Trade Zone).

Western blot

IRS1, PI3K (p85), Akt, and GLUT4 were quantified using Western blot analysis. Total and mitochondrial proteins were extracted from skeletal muscle following a previously published method [18]. Differential extraction was used to obtain whole tissue lysates and mitochondrial fraction proteins. Tissues were homogenized in 200 µl of lysis buffer (Shanghai Xitang Biotechnology Co., Ltd., China). Protein concentrations for both whole-cell and mitochondrial fractions were determined using the Bio-Rad DC Protein Assay (Bio-Rad Laboratories, California, USA) as per the manufacturer’s instructions. Protein samples (2 µg/µl) were loaded onto NuPAGE® Novex 4–12% Bis-Tris protein gels (Life Technology, CA, USA) and subjected to electrophoresis. After separation, the proteins were transferred to a PVDF membrane using either a semi-dry or wet transfer method (Thermo Scientific, Illinois, USA). The membrane was blocked with 5% nonfat milk for one hour at room temperature. Primary antibodies (OXPHOS 1:2500, MnSOD 1:5000, GPx-3 1:1000, GAPDH 1:1000, Abcam, Cambridge, UK; COX IV 1:1000, Life Technology, CA, USA) were incubated overnight with the membrane at 4 °C. Subsequently, the membranes were incubated with secondary antibodies (peroxidase-conjugated AffiniPure goat anti-rabbit IgG and peroxidase-conjugated AffiniPure goat anti-mouse IgG, 1:10,000, Jackson Immuno Research Laboratories, PA, USA) and SuperSignal™ West Pico Chemiluminescent substrates (ThermoFisher Scientific, NSW, Australia). Bands were detected using the LAS-3000 imaging system (Fujifilm, Tokyo, Japan). Protein expression levels were quantified by calculating the ratio of target protein intensity to GAPDH or cytochrome c oxidase subunit (COX) IV band intensity.

Quantitative real-time PCR

mRNA expression levels of the mitochondrial biogenesis marker peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor γ coactivator 1-α (PGC-1α) and the insulin-sensing marker peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor γ (PPARγ) in skeletal muscle were assessed using quantitative real-time PCR. Tissue samples (10–100 mg) were processed using the mirVana™ miRNA isolation kit (Life Technologies) according to the manufacturer’s protocol.

Quantification involved a two-step reaction process: reverse transcription (RT) and PCR. cDNA was synthesized using the GeneAmp® PCR System 9700 (Applied Biosystems, USA) with HiScript II Q RT SuperMix IIa (Vazyme Biotech Co. Ltd, Jiangsu, China). Quantification of target genes was performed using SYBR green primers and the LightCycler® 480 Real-time PCR Instrument (Roche, Switzerland). The primer sequences were designed in-house and synthesized by Generay Biotech (Generay, China) based on mRNA sequences obtained from the NCBI database (Table 1).

At the end of the PCR cycle, an unwinding curve analysis was performed to confirm the specific amplification of the expected PCR product. Statistical method

Data are presented as SD ± SEM. Statistical significance was assessed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by the Fisher-LSD test. A P-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Data analysis and graphical representation were performed using GraphPad Prism 6.0.

Results

Body weight and organ weights of the KYD rats

Compared to normal rats, KYD rats exhibited significantly lower body temperature (Control vs. KYD and KYD + HFD, HFD vs. KYD and KYD + HFD, P < 0.01, Fig. 2a). KYD rats also showed lower body weight and weight gain rates than the other three groups. Compared to the HFD group, the KYD + HFD group had less weight gain, with a slower rate of weight increase (Fig. 2b). No significant differences in daily caloric intake were observed between KYD and normal rats, regardless of whether they were fed a standard or HFD (Control vs. KYD, HFD vs. KYD + HFD, P > 0.05, Fig. 2c). Liver weight in the KYD group was lower compared to the control group, while the HFD and KYD + HFD groups showed significant increases in liver weight (Control < HFD and KYD + HFD, P < 0.01, Fig. 2d1). No significant differences in kidney weight were found across the four groups. However, an HFD notably increased the amount of white adipose tissue (HFD > Control, KYD + HFD > KYD, P < 0.01, Fig. 2d1). The muscle mass in the KYD group was reduced compared to the control group, but it was higher than in the HFD group, with no significant difference in the KYD + HFD group (HFD > Control and KYD, P < 0.05, Fig. 2d1). After weight normalization, the percentage of liver weight was slightly higher in the KYD group compared to the control group, and significantly higher in the KYD + HFD group compared to the HFD group (KYD + HFD > HFD, P < 0.01; KYD + HFD > Con, P < 0.05, Fig. 2d2). White fat content in high-fat-fed rats was significantly higher than in normal diet-fed rats, and the muscle mass percentage remained similar to that of the net body weight (HFD > Control and KYD, P < 0.05, Fig. 2d2). After normalization, the proportion of kidney weight to body weight in the HFD group was lower than in both the KYD and KYD + HFD groups (HFD < KYD and KYD + HFD, P < 0.05, Fig. 2d2), possibly due to the overall higher body weight in the HFD group.

Glycolipid levels in KYD rats

During the glucose tolerance test, only the HFD group displayed a trend toward glucose intolerance, indicated by slightly higher insulin levels(Fig. 2e1) and HOMA values(Fig. 2e3), although these variations were not statistically significant. The area under the curve (AUC) for glucose was significantly increased in the KYD + HFD group compared to the Control group (KYD + HFD vs. Control, P < 0.01), with a modest increase in the KYD group compared to the Control group (KYD vs. Control, P < 0.05, Fig. 2e2). Compared to the HFD group, the KYD + HFD group showed a higher AUC, though the difference was not statistically significant. Regarding cholesterol levels, KYD + HFD rats exhibited higher cholesterol levels than the other groups when compared to the Control group (KYD + HFD vs. Control and KYD, P < 0.01; KYD + HFD vs. HFD, P < 0.05, Fig. 2f1). Similarly, the LDL levels in the KYD + HFD group were significantly higher than in the other three groups (KYD + HFD vs. KYD, HFD, and Control, P < 0.01, Fig. 2f2). The expression of PPARγ in the skeletal muscle of KYD rats showed a decreasing trend compared to the Control group, though the difference was not statistically significant. There was no significant difference in the KYD + HFD group when compared to the HFD group. The HFD group exhibited significantly higher PPARγ levels than the KYD group (HFD > KYD, P < 0.05, Fig. 2g), while no significant changes were observed in PGC-1α expression across the four groups

IRS1-AKt-PI3K(p85)-GLUT4 pathway protein expression in KYD rats

IRS1 expression was reduced in the skeletal muscle of both HFD and KYD + HFD rats compared to the Con group, with the most pronounced reduction observed in the KYD + HFD group (Con vs. KYD + HFD, P < 0.01, Fig. 3d). Expression was higher in the KYD group compared to the HFD group (P < 0.01, HFD vs. KYD). No significant difference in P−p85 expression was observed among the four groups (P > 0.05, Fig. 3f). AKT expression showed no significant difference between the Con and HFD groups (P > 0.05, Fig. 3h), whereas expression in the KYD and KYD + HFD groups was significantly higher than in the Con group (P < 0.01). For GLUT4, the KYD + HFD group had significantly lower expression compared to the Con group (Control vs. KYD + HFD, P < 0.01, Fig. 3b), while the other two groups exhibited a trend towards reduced expression. In terms of phosphorylation, IRS1 levels were significantly higher in the skeletal muscle of the KYD, HFD, and KYD + HFD groups compared to the Con group (Control vs. KYD, P < 0.05; Control vs. HFD, P < 0.01, Fig. 3c). Compared to the HFD group, IRS1 expression was significantly lower in the KYD + HFD group (P < 0.01, HFD vs. KYD + HFD). For PI3K (p85), both the KYD and HFD groups showed reduced expression compared to the Con group (Control vs. KYD, P < 0.05; Control vs. HFD, P < 0.05, Fig. 3e). However, the KYD + HFD group exhibited significantly higher expression compared to the KYD group (P < 0.05, KYD vs. KYD + HFD). For AKT, expression was slightly higher in the KYD group compared to the Con group, though this difference was not statistically significant. The HFD and KYD + HFD groups showed significantly higher AKT expression compared to the Con group (Control vs. HFD, P < 0.05; Control vs. KYD + HFD, P < 0.01, Fig. 3g).

Discussions

Historically, animal models of KYD were created using pathological methods, which provided simple and stable results. However, disease models should primarily focus on the etiology as the causative factor, rather than the pathological changes resulting from it. In this study, a composite etiological modeling approach was employed to simulate the combined effects of innate heredity and acquired environmental factors on the body, aiming to replicate the pathogenesis of KYD rats as accurately as possible. This approach aligns more closely with the pathogenesis theory of TCM. Our previous research demonstrated that this modeling method yielded a high success rate and stability.

The HFD used in this experiment (average 15.33 kJ/g, with 34% of energy from fat, 19% from protein, and 47% from carbohydrates) consisted of granulated high-fat foods prepared from a mix of four standard laboratory foods. Based on previous research conducted by our team, this diet formulation proved both reasonable and effective, producing consistent and reliable experimental outcomes [17, 19]. These model preparation techniques provided a solid and robust foundation for the current experiment.

In this study, the total daily calorie intake of the KYD rat model did not differ significantly from that of normal rats, but the rate of weight gain was slower, and the overall weight was lower, which is consistent with previous findings [20]. However, the proportion of liver weight in KYD rats on an HFD was higher than that in the high-fat rats, suggesting that KYD rats are less prone to obesity but more susceptible to low-weight fatty liver. Their fat distribution does not significantly increase on the body surface but is notably concentrated in the liver. The HFD increased fat content in both KYD and normal rats and also boosted muscle content in normal rats, but did not affect muscle content in KYD rats. This observation aligns with the typical manifestations of KYD, which often includes reduced mobility and muscle mass. It is hypothesized that the muscle loss in KYD rats is due to insufficient activity. The AUC for the time-glucose curve provides a more comprehensive analysis of blood glucose fluctuations over time compared to a single glucose measurement [21]. Serum insulin levels and HOMA are well-established indicators of insulin resistance [22, 23]. An HFD increases fat content and impairs fat metabolism, leading to elevated peripheral blood insulin levels and reduced insulin sensitivity [24–26], leading to obesity [27, 28]. In this experiment, an unexpected finding was that the time-glucose curve in KYD + HFD rats exhibited a significantly greater area under the curve (AUC) compared with the control and KYD groups, even exceeding that of HFD rats. However, both insulin levels and HOMA values in the KYD + HFD group were lower(though not statistically significant) and closer to those of normal diet rats. This suggests that KYD rats may have increased insulin sensitivity, possibly due to their lower body weight or other yet-to-be-identified mechanisms. The small sample size of this experiment may have contributed to some errors in the data. Additionally, the LDL level in KYD rats fed an HFD was significantly higher than that in normal HFD rats, while cholesterol levels were lower compared to normal rats. Previous studies have shown that Yougui Pill can reduce triglyceride synthesis and cholesterol deposition in plasma by inhibiting the expression of JAK2, STAT3, LXRα, SREBP-1c, ABCA1, CETP, and FAS, thereby correcting lipid disorders. Combining these findings with the results from our experiment, individuals with KYD are more likely to develop dyslipidemia-related diseases. Thus, correcting KYD by warming kidney yang may help reduce the incidence of metabolic diseases.

PGC-1α is a critical metabolic regulator that controls lipid metabolism and long-chain fatty acid oxidation by enhancing the expression of several genes involved in the mitochondrial fatty acid oxidation and tricarboxylic acid cycle pathways [29]. Under pathological conditions, the downregulated expression of PGC-1α in muscle tissue impairs glucose transport capacity, which manifests as elevated postprandial blood glucose levels in individuals with diabetes [30, 31]. PPARγ, which belongs to the nuclear hormone receptor superfamily, is mainly found in fat tissue and macrophages. It promotes adipocyte differentiation, regulates the expression of adipocyte-specific genes, and plays a significant role in adipogenesis, glycolipid metabolism, and immune system function [32, 33]. Reduced PPARγ expression can contribute to insulin resistance [33]. In glucose-tolerant rats, PGC-1α expression in skeletal muscle and liver is significantly reduced [34, 35]. In contrast, in this study, the PGC-1α level in KYD rats did not differ significantly from the other groups, and the PPARγ content in the skeletal muscle of KYD rats was only lower than in the HFD group, with no significant difference observed between KYD rats on an HFD and the other groups. These results suggest that neither the kidney-yang deficiency constitution nor the HFD had a significant impact on PPARγ and PGC-1α expression in the skeletal muscle of rats.

Insulin, a key anabolic hormone, is crucial for regulating metabolism and energy balance [36]. When insulin binds to its receptor, it activates the receptor’s intrinsic tyrosine kinase activity (IRK), which then phosphorylates tyrosine residues on target proteins, including insulin receptor substrates (IRS-1 to 6) [37]. Phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase (PI3K) is a major signaling pathway activated in response to IRK activation and is well-studied as a downstream effector of IRS proteins [38]. PI3K interacts with the phosphorylated IRS proteins, enhancing the activity of phosphoinositide-dependent kinase (PDK-1) and subsequently activating protein kinase B (Akt) [39–41]. Phosphorylation of Akt facilitates the translocation of GLUT4 from the cytoplasm to the cell membrane, where it fuses with the membrane and transports glucose into the cell via facilitated diffusion, thus regulating glucose uptake, protein synthesis, adipogenesis, and inhibiting hepatic gluconeogenesis [42, 43]. A reduction in the IRS1-PI3K(p85)-Akt-GLUT4 pathway impairs insulin signaling, reducing glucose uptake and promoting insulin resistance, which ultimately leads to metabolic disorders [44]. In this study, KYD rats showed a decrease in p85 protein expression regardless of diet, but protein levels of AKT, IRS1, and GLUT4 were elevated. After an HFD, these protein expressions approached those of the normal group. These findings suggest that the key insulin signaling pathway in KYD rats is impaired, making them more prone to glucose and lipid metabolism abnormalities. Moreover, the HFD had a corrective effect, which provides valuable insights into the dietary preferences of individuals with KYD, particularly regarding fatty and sweet diets.

TCM constitution provides a comprehensive framework for understanding both the current and future health trends of individuals, considering factors such as individual differences, physiological outcomes, psychological states, and adaptability to environmental and social conditions. This theory has been effectively incorporated into various levels of public health management in China, yielding positive outcomes [45]. Recent research has increasingly focused on TCM constitution, with some scholars noting that individuals with KYD exhibit symptoms resembling those of mitochondrial diseases, including fatigue, reduced physical endurance, cold intolerance, decreased libido, and diminished fertility [46]. This has led to the suggestion that KYD is closely linked to mitochondrial dysfunction. Moreover, numerous studies have established mitochondrial dysfunction as a critical factor in the development of metabolic diseases, such as diabetes and insulin resistance [47, 48]. Therefore, it is hypothesized that individuals with KYD are more susceptible to mitochondrial dysfunction and, upon exposure to an HFD, are predisposed to metabolic disorders.

Despite these insights, no studies have yet explored the relationship between KYD and type 2 diabetes, and the mechanisms underlying KYD are still under investigation. It is anticipated that further high-quality research will provide clearer explanations in the future.

Study strengths and limitations

The unique modeling approach used in this study combines the etiological factors of “internal cold” and “external cold” to simulate the effects of both genetic predisposition and environmental influences on physical development. Offspring mice were selected for model evaluation and experimentation. This approach aligns closely with the core principles of KYD modeling, effectively reflecting both its etiology and pathogenesis. However, this study has certain limitations, particularly the focus on sugar and fat metabolism indices, with fewer energy metabolism parameters specifically related to KYD. In addition, the total duration of the study is too short to fully explain the stability and repeatability of the results.

Conclusions

The findings indicate that KYD rats tend to gain weight more slowly than their normal counterparts and are less likely to develop obesity. Instead, they are more prone to low body weight, elevated blood lipids, and fatty liver disease. Dysfunction in the IRS1-Akt-PI3K (p85)-GLUT4 pathway was observed in KYD rats, making them more vulnerable to disorders in glucose and lipid metabolism. Therefore, individuals with KYD should be particularly mindful of their calorie intake and expenditure. These results offer a new reference point for treating clinically low-weight fatty liver disease.
