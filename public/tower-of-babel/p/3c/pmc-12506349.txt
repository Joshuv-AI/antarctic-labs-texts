Pan-S-locus analysis reveals insights into the origin and evolution of self-incompatibility in the orange subfamily
Authors: Jianbing Hu, Zezhen Du, Chenchen Liu, Hao Wen, Cong Liu, Peng Chen, Chunmei Shi, Junli Ye, QianHua Ji, Xiuxin Deng, Maurice Bosch, Zongcheng Lin, Wen-Biao Jiao, Lijun Chai
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSelf-incompatibility is controlled by a highly polymorphic supergene complex, the S-locus, which is structurally complex, rich in repetitive sequences, and varies in length from hundreds of kilobases to tens of megabases across different plant families. Due to these challenges, the S-locus has been fully reconstructed in only a few species, limiting our understanding of its evolutionary dynamics.ResultsThis study systematically explores the evolutionary mechanisms and structural characteristics of the self-incompatibility system mediated by S-RNase in the Aurantioideae (orange subfamily) of Rutaceae. We construct a pan-S-locus framework spanning 11 genera within the orange subfamily from 14 newly assembled genomes from representative accessions and the analysis of 41 published citrus genomes. Our analyses reveal significant structural variations and transposable element (TE)-driven pseudogenization of SLF genes. By constructing a comprehensive library of S-RNases and developing a novel genotyping pipeline, we reveal divergent frequencies of S-RNases between self-incompatible and self-compatible populations. Comparative analyses demonstrate a unique evolutionary trajectory marked by asynchronous core nonS-genes duplication and TE-mediated structural diversification, distinct from other families. Innovatively, we identified TE-induced self-incompatibility loss as the primary driver of self-compatibility transition, which implies the uniqueness of the origin and evolution of self-incompatibility in the orange subfamily.ConclusionsThrough the construction and characterization of the pan-S-locus, this study provides profound insight into the origin and evolution of the S-locus in the orange subfamily. These findings not only advance our understanding of self-incompatibility mechanisms, but also establish a foundational framework for investigating the evolution of the gametophytic self-incompatibility systems in other families.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13059-025-03817-x.

Background

Self-incompatibility (SI) represents an evolutionary reproductive strategy in angiosperms that prevents inbreeding and promotes outcrossing, thereby maintaining genetic diversity within species [1, 2]. To date, all characterized SI systems rely on sophisticated genetic mechanisms, with their evolutionary significance primarily attributed to the avoidance of inbreeding depression [3]. In most angiosperms, SI is controlled by a single highly polymorphic supergene complex known as the S-locus, which contains two tightly linked S-determinant genes: one specifically expressed in pistil tissues and the other in pollen [4, 5]. Based on the variations in S-determinant genes and their inheritance patterns, SI systems are primarily categorized into sporophytic self-incompatibility (SSI) and gametophytic self-incompatibility (GSI) [4]. Among the GSI systems, the S-RNase-mediated system represents the most extensively studied and widely distributed mechanism [6, 7]. This system comprises a pistil-specific S-RNase gene and multiple pollen-specific S-locus F-box (SLF) genes at the S-locus [4]. However, the high polymorphism, abundance of repetitive sequences, structural complexity, and variable length (ranging from hundreds of kilobases to tens of megabases across different families) of the S-locus have hindered its complete reconstruction in most species [6, 8–12]. On the other hand, due to the high polymorphism, rich TE content, and low recombination rate of the S-locus, comprehensive and accurate annotation of the S-locus is challenging. Therefore, self-incompatibility related loci have rarely been comprehensively described. Furthermore, the annotation of these loci in published genomes has often been incomplete or incorrect, with many genes either unannotated or misidentified. All of these factors have significantly limited research into the evolutionary mechanisms of this supergene complex.

At present, the S-RNase-mediated SI system has been identified in several core eudicot families including Solanaceae, Plantaginaceae, Rosaceae, Rutaceae, Rubiaceae, and Cactaceae, covering two major clades of core eudicots, superasterids and superrosids [11, 13–22]. The phylogenetic analysis of S-RNase genes in the T2 ribonuclease family reveals that those from superasterids and superrosids plants are clustered within a single monophyletic group, suggesting that the S-RNase-mediated SI system in core eudicots may have a single origin [6, 23, 24]. In contrast, evolutionary transitions from obligate outcrossing to self-fertilization have recurrently emerged through convergent evolution [25, 26]. In higher plants, the evolution of selfing typically coincides with the loss of SI [27]. Comparative genomic studies demonstrate that the ancestral S-RNase-mediated SI system underwent multiple independent losses during species diversification and self-compatibility evolution in superasterids and superrosids [1, 6]. While molecular mechanisms underlying the loss of SI have been thoroughly investigated in the Solanaceae, particularly regarding functional alterations of S-genes and their recognition mechanisms [9, 28–31], analyses of evolutionary mechanisms at the S-locus level remain limited in other families.

Systematic phylogenetic analyses of individual genes indicate that the S-RNase-mediated SI system likely originated from a single evolutionary origin [6, 24]. A growing body of research highlights the critical role of the S-locus in shaping the evolution of SI systems [12, 32]. Advances in long-read sequencing technologies have facilitated the characterization of SI-associated loci across diverse plant families, revealing that the S-locus harbors numerous genes, exhibits recombination events, and displays extreme polymorphism across haplotypes [8, 9, 28]. However, due to limitations in research materials and sample size, studies on SI evolution remain largely confined to single-gene analyses, lacking a comprehensive understanding of the genomic architecture and evolutionary mechanisms governing the origin and diversification of the S-locus. The Aurantioideae (Citrus and related genera; also known as the orange subfamily) represents an ideal model system for investigating SI evolution. This group is characterized by abundant germplasm resources, high genetic diversity, and the absence of significant reproductive isolation barriers between genera [33, 34]. The orange subfamily species exhibit diverse reproductive strategies, including outcrossing, selfing, and apomixis, and frequently display transitions between outcrossing and selfing as dominant reproductive modes [11, 35, 36]. The relatively compact genomes of orange subfamily species further enhance their potential for detailed genomic and evolutionary analyses. These features collectively render the orange subfamily as a unique system suited for elucidating the evolutionary dynamics of the S-RNase-mediated SI system.

Our previous work established that citrus species possess an S-RNase-mediated SI system and reconstructed the sequences of the S-locus of two S-haplotypes using Bacterial Artificial Chromosome (BAC) libraries [11]. Subsequent genetic mapping in hybrid populations delineated conserved S-locus boundaries across Aurantioideae, encompassing the two previously identified supergene S-loci [37]. The application of new technologies, such as third-generation sequencing, iterative updates of haplotype assembly algorithms, and phylogenetic resolution of orange subfamily origins [34, 36], has provided the opportunity to move beyond single-gene analyses, to reconstruct S-locus sequences of more S-haplotypes, and to comprehensively investigate the mechanism and evolution of SI in the orange subfamily at the S-locus level. Here, based on the newly assembled 14 genomes from representative accessions (9 Citrus maxima and 5 related species) and the analysis of 41 published citrus genomes, we successfully constructed a pan-S-locus framework covering 11 genera within the orange subfamily. Based on gene and transposable element (TE) annotations, we comprehensively characterized the pan-S-locus at the gene level. We also present the most complete S-RNase library for the orange subfamily and developed a short-read-based genotyping tool for systematic characterization of S-RNase distribution and frequency patterns. Finally, by employing two outgroup genomes, we investigated the origin and evolutionary trajectories of the S-locus within the orange subfamily, proposing an integrative model for S-locus evolution that establishes a foundation for future research on the evolutionary mechanism of this supergene complex.

Results

Construction of pan-S-locus in the orange subfamily

In order to investigate the origin and evolution of the S-locus, we sequenced and assembled 12 genomes of different genera in the orange subfamily (including 9 Citrus maxima, one Murraya paniculata, one Clausena lansium, and one Glycosmis pentaphylla) and 2 outgroup species in the other subfamilies (Ruta graveolens and Toddalia asiatica). The median N50 value for the newly assembled genomes was 22.2 Mb, and the median BUSCO score was 97.1% (Additional file 1: Table S1). We also collected 41 published genomes (with a median N50 value of 29.9 Mb and a median BUSCO score of 98.8%; Additional file 1: Table S2) from the orange subfamily and obtained the sequence information of S-haplotype. After removing redundancies and trimming, a pan-S-locus consisting of 88 S-loci covering 11 genera (27 species) of the orange subfamily was obtained (Fig. 1A; Additional file 1: Table S3). In order to address the problem of the difficulty in achieving comprehensive and accurate annotation of the S-locus, we developed a robust workflow optimized to integrate RNA-seq data (by extracting RNA from styles and anthers of materials carrying specific S-haplotypes for transcriptomic sequencing) for the accurate annotation of S-locus sequences and applied this workflow to comprehensively annotate the pan-S-locus of the orange subfamily (Additional file 2: Fig. S1). Using this analysis, a total of 3134 genes were annotated, including 87 S-RNases, 1136 S-locus F-box (SLF) genes, 1289 core conserved nonS-genes shared by all S-loci of the orange family, 462 dispensable genes, and 160 pseudogenes (Fig. 1B; Additional file 1: Table S4; Additional file 2: Fig. S2).

Our analysis revealed significant variation in the S-loci length across the orange subfamily, ranging from 240 to 690 kb, with an average length of 359 kb. On average, each S-locus contained about 34 genes, including about 13 SLF genes (Fig. 1C; Additional file 1: Table S4; Additional file 2: Fig. S2). In order to further investigate the structural characteristics of the S-locus in the orange subfamily, we selected 15 representative high-quality genomes from different genera (this subset refers to genomes selected specifically to provide broad taxonomic coverage of essential orange subfamily diversity, 4 are derived from newly assembled and 11 are from published genomes), 13 of which covered 11 genera of the orange subfamily and the other 2 were outgroups (Additional file 1: Table S5). Gene collinearity analysis across these 11 genera (13 species) shows that core conserved nonS-genes on the S-loci are highly collinear. While the boundary genes of the S-locus are relatively conserved, the central region containing the S-genes was highly polymorphic (Fig. 1D). The results of whole-sequence alignment also confirmed that the boundary regions of S-loci (internal sequences at the termini of the S-locus) from different genera of the orange subfamily were highly consistent; nevertheless, the sequence collinearity in the middle region was relatively poor, suggesting that complex structural variations (SVs) may have occurred (Fig. 1E).

The polymorphism of sequences often implies significant differences in protein-coding sequences. To further characterize the protein-coding sequences at the pan-S-locus of the orange subfamily, we analyzed 25 non-redundant inter-species (25 different representative species, covering 11 genera of the orange subfamily, excluding duplicate CsiSm-locus and incomplete LscS49-locus) genomic sequences of the S-locus and their flanking regions. The flanking regions were defined as a 200 kb window, extending to a 1 Mb interval on both sides of the S-locus (Additional file 1: Table S6). Our analysis revealed that the density of protein-coding sequences at the S-locus was significantly lower than that in the adjacent flanking regions (Fig. 1F). In addition, we calculated the non-synonymous (Ka) and synonymous (Ks) substitution rates of nonS-genes and SLF genes at the pan-S-locus. The results showed that SLF genes had higher Ka and Ks values compared to nonS-genes, and their Ka/Ks values were also significantly higher than those of nonS-genes, but the values of Ka/Ks were all less than 1 (Fig. 1G, H; Additional file 2: Fig. S3). These results indicate that nonS-genes at the S-locus are more evolutionarily conserved, while SLF genes are driven by both a high rate of intrinsic mutations and weak purifying selection, resulting in rapid evolution under weak constraints, leading to the accumulation of more variations at the evolutionary scale.

Structural diversification of core genes at the pan-S-locus

To understand the characteristics of the most abundant gene family at the pan-S-locus (F-box genes), we conducted a comprehensive analysis of them. Phylogenetic analysis of the deduced amino acid sequences of 1136 SLF sequences showed that they clustered into 15 distinct types, 14 of which were consistent with previous classification [37]. Notably, we identified a novel SLF type, designated as type x, forming a distinct and independent lineage in the phylogenetic tree. This new type was found at the S-locus of only 5 S-haplotypes (MpaS45-locus, ClaS36-locus, ClinS36-locus, CgrS5-locus, and CgrS36-locus) (Fig. 2A; Additional file 1: Table S7). Like the other SLFs, type x SLFs contain F-box and F-box associated domains (Additional file 2: Fig. S4). However, they exhibit higher Ka and Ks substitution rates (Additional file 2: Fig. S5), suggesting that type x SLFs may represent an ancient lineage of SLFs that was gradually lost during the evolution and reorganization of new S-haplotypes. In addition, the Ka and Ks of different types of SLFs varied significantly. Specifically, the frequency of Ka and Ks of type 2–9 SLFs was significantly higher than that of type 10–14 SLFs (Additional file 2: Fig. S5). Previous studies have shown that regions with low gene density usually contain a large number of repeats and TEs. Due to negative frequency-dependent selection, the maintenance of self-incompatibility systems requires a very high level of polymorphism of S-genes, and TEs play an important role in the evolution of polymorphism at the S-locus. Based on this, we conducted a comprehensive analysis of the transposons at the S-locus of the orange subfamily. The results showed that the S-locus exhibited a higher density of TEs than the flanking regions, mainly Copia, Gypsy, and DNA transposons, and that the content of long terminal repeat transposons (LTRs) at the S-locus was significantly higher than that in the flanking regions on both sides (Fig. 2B; Additional file 1: Table S8, S9).

Among the 3134 genes annotated at the pan-S-locus, 160 pseudogenes were identified, 158 of which were F-box pseudogenes, qualifying about 14% of full-length SLF genes (Fig. 2C; Additional file 1: Table S7). It is clear that pseudogenization of SLFs is not a rare event that happens by chance. In order to explore the causes and potential driving forces of pseudogenization of SLFs, we divided the 158 SLF pseudogenes into four types based on their underlying mutations: SNPs and Indels, SNPs, Indels, and SVs. Among these types, SNPs and Indels accounted for the highest proportion (Fig. 2C; Additional file 1: Table S10). Of the 39 pseudogenes caused by SVs, 19 resulted from deletions. Additionally, 15 pseudogenes were caused by TE insertions, of which 13 are Long Terminal Repeat (LTR)-type insertions (Fig. 2C; Additional file 1: Table S10). A genome-wide TE analysis confirmed that pseudogenization of SLFs was significantly associated with TEs. The distance between SLF pseudogenes and their nearest TEs was significantly shorter than the average distance between TEs and nonS-genes (Additional file 2: Fig. S6A), and the TE content near the SLF pseudogenes (within 2 kb upstream and downstream) was significantly higher than that of nonS-genes at the S-loci and the normal SLF (intact/non-pseudogenized) genes (Additional file 2: Fig. S6B). In addition, we found that pseudogenization has an obvious preference among certain types of SLFs, with more than 75% of all SLF pseudogenes belonging to types 10–14 (Additional file 1: Table S10). This pattern may be related to their greater distances from the S-RNase and lower selection pressure. This might also be the “release strategy” chosen by the SI system to optimize its S-locus efficiency, unlock innovative potential, and for the evolutionary innovation of new S-haplotypes.

In addition to the pseudogenization of SLFs, there are significant differences in the number of different types of SLF genes among the 1136 functional SLF sequences annotated. Excluding the newly discovered type x SLFs, the number of type-14 SLFs is only 17, while the number of type-9 SLFs is the largest, reaching 111. The number of other types of SLF genes ranges from 68 to 96 (Fig. 2A). The differences in the number of different types of SLF genes suggest frequent gene duplications and gene losses of SLF genes at the pan-S-locus. Based on this, we used 22 S-haplotypes of a self-incompatible population of pummelo (Citrus maxima) to further explore the dynamic changes of SLF genes at the self-incompatible S-locus. The collinearity results of the core genes (S-genes and the common core conserved nonS-genes) at the 22 pummelo S-locus regions show that the core conserved nonS-genes are very conserved in gene direction and relative arrangement order. In addition, the Ks values were significantly lower in the core conserved nonS-genes compared to SLFs, with type 1–10 SLFs exhibiting the highest Ks values. This suggests that compared to the core conserved nonS-genes, the evolutionary pressure of the SLF genes was more relaxed, allowing for the accumulation of more mutations (Additional file 2: Fig. S7), consistent with our pan-S-locus analysis of the orange subfamily.

To visualize the S-genes dynamics across different S-haplotypes, we extracted the coding sequences of the core genes from 22 pummelo (Citrus maxima) S-loci. The analysis confirmed that gene duplication, gene loss, and pseudogenization frequently occurred among SLF genes on different S-loci within the same population. For instance, a large-scale duplication event was found on the left side of the CgrS19-locus, involving four SLF genes; three copies of type 9 SLF were present at the CgrS10-locus; gene loss occurred in type 12 SLF of the CgrS4-locus, type 5 SLF of the CgrS5-locus, type 6 SLF of the CgrS7-locus and CgrS22a-locus, and type 2 SLF of the CgrS20-locus; and all type 14 SLFs at all the pummelo S-locus regions were pseudogenized (Fig. 2D; Additional file 2: Fig. S8). These results suggest that SLF genes expanded through gene duplication and gradually differentiated into new types during evolution, accompanied by frequent gene loss and pseudogenization. The occurrence of these events might be the result of extensive structural variations at the S-locus, as evidenced by the large-scale duplication event we detected at the CgrS19-locus. Other types of structural variations, due to the high polymorphism of the sequences, are often difficult to detect directly. The significant differences in the gene arrangement and gene collinearity of the coding sequences at the S-locus further imply that complex and extensive structural variations have indeed occurred (Additional file 2: Fig. S8).

Beyond intra-species variation, the evolution of the same S-haplotypes across different genera (haplogroups) provides valuable insights. We identified seven S2-haplotypes from four genera in the pan-S-locus of the orange subfamily. Comprehensive characterization of the S2-haplogroup revealed significant differences in sequence length, which might be caused by repetitive sequences or transposons (Additional file 1: Table S8). However, despite differences in length, core conserved nonS-genes on the S2-haplogroup remained relatively conserved in their collinear arrangement (Fig. 2E). In addition, the evolution of SLF genes within the S2-haplogroup showed frequent patterns of pseudogenization and gene loss. For example, AbuS2-locus type 10 and 13 SLFs were pseudogenes; PtrS2-locus type 11 SLF was a pseudogene, and type 14 SLF was lost; while CgrS2-locus, ClimS2-locus, CmaS2-locus, and CpoS2-locus type 14 SLFs all underwent pseudogenization (Additional file 1: Table S7). Similarly, the type 4–9 SLF genes surrounding the S-RNases, including S2-RNase, had higher Ks values compared to other SLF genes and core conserved nonS-genes (Fig. 2E). A similar pattern was observed in the S8-haplogroup (Additional file 2: Fig. S9). However, unlike the S2-haplogroup, the duplication differences of SLF genes were detected in the S8-haplogroup, in which CauS8-locus and CmeS8-locus, type 7, 8, and 9 SLFs underwent duplication, while AbuS12-locus (also known as AbuS8-locus), CgrS8-locus, and FhiS8-locus only had type 9 SLFs duplication (Additional file 2: Fig. S9). Genome-wide TE analysis showed that the TEs density in the S2-locus between different genera was significantly higher than that in the surrounding genomic regions (Additional file 2: Fig. S10), which might explain why the S2-haplogroup could still maintain its relatively independent polymorphic evolution after differentiation among genera. In summary, our results indicate that the S-haplotypes inherited from the ancestors of the orange subfamily continued to undergo relatively independent polymorphic evolution after differentiation among genera, and the dynamic changes of S-genes played an important role in this process, especially the replication, loss, and pseudogenization of SLF genes.

Divergent distribution of S-RNases alleles in citrus populations

The identification of female and male S-determinants is critical for studying the complex genetic mechanisms, as well as the origin and evolution of SI systems in plants. The SI system exhibits extremely high S-allelic diversity, with strong negative frequency-dependent selection leading to extensive nucleotide sequence divergence among alleles of both pollen and pistil functional components. This leads to high degrees of trans-specific and even trans-generic polymorphisms, which have largely prevented a comprehensive analysis of complete allelic series in natural populations and also hindered evolutionary studies of SI systems. In order to fully characterize the S-RNase alleles in the orange subfamily, we conducted a wide range of analyses of SI individuals with unknown S-RNase genotype using a de novo discovery workflow for the identification of S-RNase allelic variants. This workflow mainly involved reference-free assembly based on tissue-specific transcriptome data of the style, and prediction using a hidden Markov model. Finally, further screening was carried out based on the characteristics of S-RNase in the orange subfamily and experiments (Fig. 3A). In order to comprehensively characterize the S-RNase allele distribution and genotype frequency of different populations of the orange subfamily, we developed a rapid and accurate S-RNase allele genotyping pipeline based on a non-redundant S-RNase allele pool and short-read resequencing data. Using k-mer-based classification of S-RNase sequences, coupled with a Bloom filter and hidden Markov model, we accurately assembled and classified short-read sequencing data from different accessions (Fig. 3B). Testing on 46 kumquat accessions showed that our genotyping pipeline accuracy (for heterozygous diploid SI individuals, the genotyping accuracy rate can reach 100%) was similar to those achieved with PCR-based validation [37], and the efficiency was higher (Additional file 1: Table S12). Applying this method, we successfully genotyped the S-RNase alleles of 579 accessions from a set of 598 accessions from published data of the orange subfamily, identifying two S-RNase alleles per accession (as expected for a diploid SI system; but the trifoliate orange accessions generally contain 3 S-RNase alleles), while only information for one S-RNase allele was obtained from the other 19 accessions (Additional file 1: Table S12). Comparison of S-RNase allele diversity between SI and SC populations showed that SI populations maintained a higher degree of S-RNase allele polymorphism, with the top five S-RNase alleles fairly evenly distributed in the SI population (Fig. 3C). This is consistent with the conclusion that SI systems are maintained by negative frequency-dependent selection. However, in SC populations, the S-RNase alleles usually show low polymorphism, with a few dominant S-RNase alleles associated with SC haplotypes, such as the Sm-allele reported previously [11]; as well as the S10-, S30-, and S31-alleles in trifoliate orange [32]; and the S2-allele in the kumquat population [37] (Fig. 3D; Additional file 2: Fig. S11A).

Analysis of the distribution of S-genotypes in the orange subfamily showed that S-genotypes were shared across different genera, indicating that the polymorphic divergence of the S-RNase genotype predates the inter-generic differentiation of the orange subfamily (Fig. 3E). Based on the 15 representative high-quality genomic information of 11 genera and two outgroups of the orange subfamily (Additional file 1: Table S5), a total of 94 S-like-RNase (T2 ribonuclease family proteins located outside the S-locus), 835 nonS-locus F-box (contained F-box and F-box associated domains and located outside the S-locus; nonSLFs) genes, and 2908 single copy orthologue (SCO) gene pairs were identified (Additional file 1: Table S13). The rates of Ka, Ks, as well as the Ka/Ks ratio, were calculated to assess the evolutionary pressures acting on the S-RNase gene pairs and the S-like-RNase homologous gene pairs in the orange subfamily. The results showed that the Ka and Ks values of the S-RNase gene pairs were significantly higher than those of the S-like-RNase gene pairs, indicating that the evolutionary pressure of S-RNase was more relaxed, allowing for the accumulation of more non-synonymous mutations (Fig. 3F). Moreover, the S-RNase gene pairs exhibited a higher Ka/Ks ratio (less than 1) than the S-like-RNase gene pairs, indicating that it was subjected to weaker purification selection (Additional file 2: Fig. S11B, C). In summary, S-RNase was driven by both a high rate of intrinsic mutations and weak purification selection, resulting in the accumulation of more mutations at the evolutionary scale to meet the requirement of maintaining high polymorphism evolution of the S-gene in trans-specific S-haplotypes.

It possesses all the core conserved nonS-genes on the S-locus of the orange subfamily, and the direction and arrangement of its core genes are completely consistent with the functional S-locus. Over time, different genera within the orange subfamily have undergone varying degrees of S-locus evolution, including length differences of S2- and S8-haplogroups, Ka and Ks substitution rates of SLF genes, gene loss, duplication, and pseudogenization. However, the most impactful evolutionary event has been the loss of SI. Our previous studies have shown that TE-mediated SI loss is most prevalent in the orange subfamily, such as TEs insertion on the S-RNase promoter (Fig. 6B). In this study, we found a new type of SI loss through analysis of the pan-S-locus. We identified two S22-loci, with a size difference of about 5.5 kb, named S22a-locus and S22b-locus in pummelo. Sequence collinearity analysis showed that the S22b-locus contained a 5483 bp insertion in the first exon of the S22b-RNase gene. This insertion was identified as a Copia LTR retrotransposon (Additional file 2: Fig. S14A), and expression analysis showed that it significantly inhibited the expression of the S22b-RNase in the stylar tissue, preventing the ability to reject self-pollen tubes and leading to the breakdown of SI (Additional file 2: Fig. S14B–E). These findings demonstrate that TEs have played a pivotal role in the origin and evolution of the SI system in the orange subfamily. TEs not only facilitated the pseudogenization of SLFs but also mediated the loss of SI through the inactivation of S-RNase genes, a pathway that appears to be broadly conserved in the evolutionary trajectory of SI within the orange subfamily.

Discussion

In the S-RNase-mediated self-incompatibility systems, the maintenance of SI requires tight linkage of functional S-genes within the S-locus [1]. The discovery, identification, and evolutionary origins of these S-genes have long been focal points of research [7]. Recent studies support a single-origin hypothesis for S-genes, as phylogenetic analyses reveal that S-RNase alleles from distinct gametophytic self-incompatibility (GSI) plant lineages cluster into a monophyletic clade [6, 23, 24, 41]. Furthermore, evidence demonstrates that S-genes retain cross-species functionality, reinforcing the hypothesis of their shared ancestral origin [6]. In this study, we systematically identified S-like-RNase and nonS-locus F-box genes (nonSLFs) in the orange subfamily. Phylogenetic analyses revealed that certain S-like-RNases from different genera within the subfamily cluster with true S-RNases. A similar pattern was observed for nonSLF genes, suggesting that S-like-RNases and S-RNases, as well as nonSLF and SLF genes, may share a common evolutionary origin. Interestingly, the evolutionary trajectory of the S-locus supergene cluster in the orange subfamily indicates that the core conserved nonS-genes predated the assembly of S-genes into a proto-S-locus cluster. This suggests that genomic RNase and F-box genes were recruited as ancestral candidates for S-genes, followed by functional divergence driven by position effects and linkage selection. This is supported by the significantly higher Ka and Ks substitution rates in S-locus RNases/F-box genes compared to their nonS-locus counterparts and interspecific single copy orthologue genes. Prior studies also demonstrate that NaS-like-RNases play a critical role in mediating intraspecific mate selection in Nicotiana attenuate [42], while the Sli gene (a nonS-locus F-box gene) acts as a universal key to disrupt SI in potato [43, 44]. These robust examples confirm that certain nonS-locus RNases and F-box genes can mimic S-gene functionality in SI-like responses, aligning with the hypothesis of their shared evolutionary origin.

The structural elucidation of the plant self-incompatibility S-locus has long been obscured by recombination suppression, transposable elements enrichment, and exceptionally high sequence polymorphism [8, 9], thereby severely limiting investigations into the evolutionary origins of supergene clusters at the S-locus. Since the initial identification of S-RNase-mediated SI systems [14], characterization and evolutionary analyses of S-locus architecture have persisted, yet the origin of SI systems remains contentious. Two prevailing hypotheses dominate: one positing a single origin of SI systems and the other advocating multiple independent origins [6, 45, 46]. In this study, we constructed a pan-S-locus for the orange subfamily and comprehensively characterized its structural features at the gene level, revealing remarkable conservation of core nonS-genes within the supergene cluster during evolution. Furthermore, by analyzing homologous fragments of these core nonS-genes in two outgroup genomes, we demonstrated a stepwise duplication-driven origin of the core conserved nonS-supergene cluster in the orange subfamily, a structural pattern distinct from other plant lineages. This diverse pattern of cluster presence and absence, even fragmentation, across two distinct lineages strongly supports a model of gradual aggregation of core nonS-genes within the orange subfamily. Potential gene or fragment loss events cannot readily explain this widespread variation and are unlikely to undermine the core evidence for stepwise assembly. Moreover, the S-locus of the orange subfamily exhibits significant divergence in both genomic span and gene organization compared to other S-RNase-mediated families (e.g., Solanaceae, Rosaceae, and Plantaginaceae) [8–10, 26, 28]. Previous studies have highlighted poor synteny among S-loci across these families, with substantial variation in sequence composition, position, length, and gene number [6, 24, 47]. Additionally, while S-RNase alleles phylogenetically cluster within the same clade, they form lineage-specific subclades across different plant families. Collectively, these findings suggest that the origin of the S-RNase-mediated SI systems may be the result of convergent evolution, with multiple independent origins in divergent plant lineages.

Convergent evolution is frequently mentioned in the evolution of SI systems. Previous studies have confirmed that heterostylous plants show convergent evolutionary characteristics at the morphological and molecular levels [46, 48]. Notably, distantly related heterostylous lineages have independently recruited the brassinosteroid (BR) inactivation pathway to control style length [48]. However, the specific genes involved in BR modulation differ significantly: Primula utilizes CFB, GLO1, CYP734A51, KFB1, and CCM1 [49], while Turnera employs TsSPH1, TsYUC6, and TsBAHD [50, 51]. Within the Rosaceae, phylogenetic analyses reveal monophyletic clustering of both S-RNases and their respective pollen-specific F-box determinants (SLFs in Maleae and SFBs in Amygdaleae) from the Maleae and Amygdaleae [6, 23]. This strongly supports a monophyletic origin for the S-genes in this family. In contrast, GSI systems manifest distinctly in these two lineages. Divergent pollen recognition mechanisms, evidenced by differing copy numbers of SLF genes [52], indicate that GSI evolved independently in the Maleae and Amygdaleae [53]. The convergent selection of the S-determinant, which exhibits monophyletic ancestral characteristics, as the male and female specificity factors in both distinct GSI recognition architectures likely represents a case of convergent evolution. Similarly, in the Brassicaceae, studies show that the S-locus in Leavenworthia employs paralogous of the canonical S-determinants, Lal2 (SRK paralogs) and SCRL (SCR paralogs), but at a genomic location non-syntenic with the ancestral Brassicaceae S-locus [54]. Crucially, the region collinear with the Arabidopsis S-locus in Leavenworthia lacks paralogs of SRK/SCR [55]. These findings demonstrate that neo-functionalization of Lal2/SCRL drove the independent evolution of a functional S-locus enabling SI within Leavenworthia [54, 55]. Our study further extends this pattern, revealing unique S-locus supergene cluster features in the orange subfamily absent in other S-RNase-mediated systems. This implies that supergene origins may be independent across divergent families. Collectively, these cases demonstrate parallelism where distinct lineages convergently adopted S-RNase-based SI, highlighting the recurring role of molecular convergence in reproductive strategy evolution.

In summary, this study established the most comprehensive S-RNase alleles library for the orange subfamily to date and developed a short-read sequencing-based genotyping tool to systematically characterize the distribution and frequency of S-RNase alleles. This analysis revealed significant differences in S-RNase allele genotype distribution and frequency between self-incompatible and self-compatible populations. Furthermore, we constructed a pan-S-locus covering 11 genera within the orange subfamily, providing the most exhaustive annotation of genes and TEs at the S-locus, revealing the genome-wide characterization of the evolutionary dynamics governing S-gene polymorphism, and demonstrating the conservation and polymorphism of the S-locus across the orange subfamily. Finally, through comparative analysis of core conserved nonS-gene fragments in outgroup homologs of the S-locus, we investigated the origin of the S-locus in the orange subfamily. Our results suggest that the S-locus supergene likely originated through multiple asynchronous duplication events: first forming a precursor S-locus via assembly of core conserved nonS-gene clusters, followed by mobilization or duplication of S-genes to establish a prototypical S-locus, and culminating in the emergence of a functional S-locus through complex structural variations, rearrangements, and recombination suppression. Crucially, TEs were instrumental in driving S-gene evolution, often coinciding with pseudogenization events and SI loss. Nevertheless, the comprehensive characterization of the pan-S-locus in the subfamily provides a critical foundation for unraveling unresolved complexities in SI systems, paving the way for mechanistic and evolutionary insights into plant reproductive strategies.

Conclusions

This study integrates genomics and evolutionary analyses to reveal the evolutionary dynamics of the S-RNase-mediated SI system within the orange subfamily. We demonstrate three key insights: Firstly, the S-locus exhibits unique structural characteristics, including a restricted repertoire of only 15 SLF types, distinct TE density, and specific structural variation, markedly differentiating it from other families and suggesting its independent evolution. Secondly, while phylogenetic analysis confirms the monophyletic origin of the S-RNase gene, the assembly mechanism of the S-locus supergene cluster fundamentally differs; asynchronous duplication of core conserved nonS-genes and the recruitment of S-genes occur in a manner distinct from that observed in other families, strongly supporting the hypothesis of convergent evolution and multiple independent origins of functional SI systems. Thirdly, we identify TE insertion leading to suppression of S-RNase expression as the primary, widely conserved mechanism for SI loss across the subfamily. These findings have significant theoretical and applied implications: the constructed pan-S-locus framework and S-RNase allele library provide crucial resources for investigating the evolution of plant reproductive strategies, and the developed genotyping tool offers direct utility for hybrid parent screening in crop breeding. Future research should investigate the recognition mechanism governing the limited SLF types and diverse S-RNase alleles, and validate core assumptions of the convergent evolution model through functional experiments.

Methods

Plant materials and sample extractions

The 9 accessions of Citrus maxima that were used in this study were collected from the Institute of Horticultural Research of the Academy of Agricultural Science of Hunan. The other 5 accessions (Murraya paniculata, Clausena lansium, Glycosmis pentaphylla, Ruta graveolens, and Toddalia asiatica) were planted in the germplasm garden at the National Citrus Breeding Center at Huazhong Agricultural University. Floral organs from SI Citrus maxima were collected [56]. Their styles were removed, immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at −80 °C for further analysis. Fresh leaf tissues of 14 accessions were used for genomic sequencing.

Genomic and transcriptomic sequencing

The sequencing of 10 accessions (6 accessions of Citrus maxima, Murraya paniculata, Clausena lansium, Ruta graveolens, and Toddalia asiatica) was performed using the PacBio Revio platform (Pacific Biosciences, USA) at Wuhan Grandomics Biosciences Co. Ltd. (Wuhan, China). A SMRTbell library with a size of 15 kb was constructed for genomic sequencing following the standard protocol provided by Pacific Biosciences. The sequencing of the other 4 accessions (3 accessions of Citrus maxima and Glycosmis pentaphylla) was performed using the PacBio Sequel II platform (Pacific Biosciences, USA) at Beijing Biomarker Technologies Co. Ltd. (Beijing, China) (Additional file 1: Table S1). Two 300 bp libraries were constructed to survey the genome information using the BGISEQ-500 platform (BGI-Shenzhen, China). Total RNA was extracted from the style tissues of SI Citrus maxima. Paired-end libraries were constructed and sequenced on the BGISEQ-500 platform (BGI-Shenzhen, China).

For RNA-seq analysis, clean reads were mapped to the genetic region of S-RNase using HISAT2 v2.2.1 [57] with default parameters. The TPM (transcripts per kilobase per million mapped reads) of each gene between styles were calculate. The sequence read clusters are from the RNA-Seq data generated from the styles of Citrus maxima and are shown in the Integrative Genomics Viewer v2.18.0 [58].

Genome assembly and S-haplotype reconstruction

The HiFi reads were assembled using Hifiasm v0.20.0-r639 [59] with default parameters. The CLR reads were assembled using Canu v2.2 [60] with the following parameters: – pacbio (minReadLength = 2000, minOverlapLength = 500, corOutCoverage = 150, corMinCoverage = 2 “batOptions = -dg 3 -db 3 -dr 1 -ca 500 -cp 50”, corrected error rate = 0.035). The reads were polished iteratively using two rounds of Pilon v1.24 [61] with WGS BGISEQ data to generate the draft genome. The sequences of S-haplotypes were identified from genomes using the S-alleles as queries. The S-locus were obtained using 200 bp sequences from two non-recombinant markers (K961397 and K1247997; Additional file 1: Table S3). The sequences of the other intergeneric S-locus were obtained from published genomes (the collection of genomic data was shown in Additional file 1: Table S2).

Pan-S-locus annotation

Gene annotations for each S-locus were made using MAKER v3.01.03 [62] and InterProScan v5.62 [63], respectively. Post-quality control, reads (RNA-Seq data from the anther and style tissues) are aligned to the pan-S-locus sequence. Aligned short reads are then de novo assembled to create an S-locus-specific transcript library, which is critical for accurate de novo annotation. Using Maker3, we perform four rounds of model training, two for SNAP and two for Augustus. The first SNAP round is strictly guided by the transcript library, generating initial “True transcripts” annotations and the SNAP_rnd1 model. A second iteration refines this into the mature SNAP_rnd2 model. For Augustus, training uses True transcripts annotations: the first round employs a small gene subset (~ 200 genes), while the second uses the full set, evaluating model performance through cross-comparison. Finally, annotations are generated by integrating results from SNAP_rnd2, Augustus, and True transcripts, validated using EvidenceModeler and transcriptome-aligned read visualizations in IGV. The detailed annotation workflow is shown in Additional file 2: Fig. S1.

Repeat sequences were predicted using RepeatModeler v2.0.4 [64] with default parameters and EDTA v2.1.0 [65] with the parameters “–step all –anno 1 –evaluate 1”. The results from both tools were merged and de-redundantized using CD-HIT to construct a custom repeat library. RepeatMasker v4.1.7 [66] was then used with default parameters to annotate repetitive elements in the genome based on this library.

Identification and isolation of the S-RNase alleles

The style tissues of SI plants were collected before anthesis. Total RNA was extracted using RNAiso Plus (TaKaRa, Japan), and paired-end libraries were constructed and sequenced on the BGISEQ-500 platform (BGI-Shenzhen, China). After the obtained sequencing data was filtered for quality control with fastp v0.23.4 [67], Trinity v2.13.2 [68] was used for reference-free assembly, respectively. Then BUSCO v5.0 [69] was used to evaluate the assembly results (with integrity > 80% as the threshold). Predicted the open reading frame with TransDecoder v5.5.0, and take the longest transcription build protein sequence data set (https://github.com/TransDecoder/TransDecoder). Hmmer v3.4 [70] was used to construct hidden Markov models (with the seed file of T2 Ribonuclease family protein conserved domain from the Pfam database, PF00445) and to perform prediction and search in transcriptional protein sequence data sets, getting the candidate target sequences. The S-RNase of the orange family usually has the following characteristics: single copy, style-specific expression, the sequence is highly polymorphic, belongs to the Class III Ribonuclease T2 family, located in S-locus, two exons and one intron, 200–240 amino acids, 22–27 kDa. False positive results were gradually eliminated, and S-RNase-like genes were excluded. According to Liang’s method [71], it has been modified and improved. Finally, the new S-RNase alleles were obtained by sequencing and pollination experiments in different citrus. The detailed workflow is shown in Fig. 3A.

Algorithm workflow of the S-RNase allele genotyping pipeline

The S-RNase allele genotyping pipeline takes as input a non-redundant S-RNase allele sequence library and short-read sequencing from accessions. First, the pipeline splits the S-RNase allele sequences into k-mers. These k-mers are then converted into hash values using 11 hash functions, and the information is stored in the corresponding bits of a Bloom filter. Simultaneously, the pipeline constructs a k-mer index for each S-RNase allele sequence to facilitate downstream genotyping. After constructing the k-mer index, the pipeline uses the Bloom filter to filter sequencing reads from the accessions. During this step, the pipeline calculates the ratio of S-RNase allele-related k-mers to the total k-mers in a read. Reads with a ratio above the threshold are retained (default is 0.85, but this value can be adjusted by the user as needed). The filtered reads are saved to disk for subsequent analysis. In the next step, users can utilize the filtered reads to perform genotyping based on the Viterbi algorithm of the Hidden Markov Model (HMM). Finally, the pipeline outputs the genotype probabilities for the accession’s S-RNase allele.

Phylogenetic analyses

The deduced amino acid sequences of F-box and RNase were aligned with the ClustalW algorithm, using MUSCLE v3.8.1551 [72]. The ML phylogenetic tree was constructed with deduced amino acid sequences using IQ-TREE v2.3.6 [73] with the maximum likelihood method (1000 rapid bootstraps; bootstrap confidence values > 50%). And based on the Bayesian Information Criterion, the best model (JTT + F + R8) recommended by ModelFinder implemented in IQ-TREE v2.3.6 [73] was used.

For genomic collinearity, phylogenetic relationships, and the whole-genome duplication analysis among different genera, OrthoFinder v2.5.4 [39] was used to identify single-copy orthologous genes, and phylogenetic trees were constructed with RAxML-NG v1.2.2 [74]. MCscanX v1.0.0 [75] was used to identify homologous gene pairs and construct genomic collinearity; among different genera, Ruta graveolens and Toddalia asiatica were used as outgroups. The 4DTv and Ks of homologous gene pairs were calculated using the Yang-Nielsen method [76] implemented in KaKs_Calculator 3.0 [77].

Identification of S-like-RNase and nonS-locus F-box genes

We obtained S-like-RNase genes and nonS-locus F-box genes by performing hmmsearch with hmmer v3.4 [70] against the non-redundant protein databases of 15 species (Additional file 1: Table S5). The seed alignment file of the T2 Ribonuclease family protein conserved domain (PF00445), F-box domain (PF00646), and F-box associated domain (PF07734 and PF08268) obtained from the Pfam database was used for constructing hidden Markov models to identify candidate S-like-RNase genes and nonS-locus F-box genes with P-value < 1e −10 using hmmer v3.4 [70]. After the candidate genes located on S-locus are excluded, the selected genes can be used for subsequent analysis.

Duplicated gene pairs identification and Ka and Ks calculation

Paralogous gene pairs were identified using the DupGen_finder pipeline [78]. The Ka and Ks of syntenic gene pairs were calculated using the Yang-Nielsen method [76] implemented in KaKs_Calculator 3.0 [77]. Homologous gene pairs of RNase and F-box genes used for Ka/Ks calculation were obtained from BLASTP results with more than 80% identity. Data analysis and visualization were performed using the ggstatsplot R package (https://CRAN.R-project.org/package=ggstatsplot). Welch's test was used to analyze the significance of differences between groups.

Microsynteny analysis of S-locus

S-locus from different combinations of the orange subfamily were selected for specific analysis. Based on the core conserved nonS-genes, the precursors of the S-locus were isolated and identified on the genome sequences of two outgroups (Ruta graveolens and Toddalia asiatica). Macrosynteny and microsynteny analyses focused on the S-locus between inter-generic and inter-haplogroup were performed at the gene level (core conserved nonS-genes and S-genes) using MCscan (Python version) incorporated in the jcvi package [75]. MUMmer v4.0.0 [79] was used for whole sequence alignment analysis, and the gggenes R package (https://CRAN.R-project.org/package=gggenes) was used for visualization of S-locus gene annotation and collinearity.

Analysis of S-locus flanking regions

According to the boundary position information of S-locus, 25 non-redundant species accessions were selected, and bedtools v2.31.1 [80] was used to extract the flank region of S-locus. The flanking regions were defined as a 200 kb window, extending to a 1 Mb interval on both sides of the S-locus. Due to the quality of genomic assembly, not all S-locus flanking regions were complete. The flanking sections were named up_F200 to up_F1000 and down_F200 to down_F1000 respectively (see Additional file 1: Table S6 for details). The density analysis of CDS in the S-locus flanking interval was based on genome annotation results, and SeqKit v2.8.2 [81] was used to extract the CDS information of the corresponding interval. Based on the same process as the S-locus, the TEs annotation was carried out for all flank intervals.

Identification and analysis of pseudogenes

According to gene annotation and TEs annotation of the panS-locus of the orange subfamily, PseudogenePipeline [82] was used to identify pseudogenes. Blastn v2.16.0 [83] was used to classify the identified pseudogenes, and the variant types of the pseudogenes were determined artificially based on sequence alignment. Finally, the insertion sequences of structural variant pseudogenes were identified and classified based on the annotation results of TEs. Detailed identification results of SLFs pseudogenes are shown in Additional file 1: Table S10.

Evaluation of self-compatibility

Nearly opened flowers from each individual in the pummelo population and representative Citrus maxima accessions were collected. Pollen was obtained by manually separating and drying the anthers at 28 °C for 12 h. At the flowering stage, approximately 10 flowers (1 day before anthesis) were self-pollinated on each individual plant using a fine paint brush and covered with a paper pollination bag. The pollinated styles were collected after 96 h. Aniline blue staining was performed as described by Liang’s method [71]. Selected styles were imaged using an Inverted Microscope for Industry (Leica DMi8 M). The images were acquired and processed using the associated software package.
