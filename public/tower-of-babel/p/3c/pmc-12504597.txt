Transcriptomic analysis of long non coding RNAs and their association with TET family genes in Sus scrofa embryo
Authors: Itishree Jali, Shailesh Sharma
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Noncoding RNAs play diverse and crucial roles across various cell types, with many long noncoding RNAs (lncRNAs) implicated in germ cell development. Although lncRNAs remain largely uncharacterized, they play essential roles in key biological processes, including X-inactivation, pluripotency, genomic imprinting, and cell differentiation. In this study, we conducted a comprehensive bioinformatics analysis using publicly accessible single-cell RNA sequencing data (scRNA-seq) from Gene Expression Omnibus repository. The dataset includes four distinct cell types from different stages of porcine embryonic development: E11 derived epiblast cells, E14 derived somatic and primordial germ cells, E31 derived primordial germ cells. Our analysis identified a large number of lncRNAs and assessed their expression patterns, highlighting their critical roles in embryonic development. We also explored the relationship between lncRNAs and protein-coding genes, particularly focusing on the ten eleven translocation (TET) family genes, which are known for their role in DNA demethylation during early embryogenesis. We identified approximately 0.15 million lncRNA transcripts in porcine early embryos. Additionally, we investigated the differential expression profiles of both lncRNAs and protein-coding genes across different cell types, observing both similarities and differences in gene expression as the embryo differentiates. Finally, we used LncTar to predict potential interactions between co-expressed TET family genes and differentially expressed lncRNAs, providing further insight into their functional relationships in early embryonic development.

Introduction

Long noncoding RNAs (lncRNAs) are defined as RNA transcripts exceeding 200 nucleotides in length, characterized by their lack of protein-coding potential, yet they play important regulatory roles in diverse cellular processes. This diverse group of transcripts is highly heterogeneous, varying in their genomic origins and mechanisms of biogenesis. Most lncRNAs are transcribed by RNA polymerase II, although other RNA polymerases may also contribute. These RNAs are often spliced, 5'-capped, and polyadenylated. LncRNAs modulate regulatory control over gene expression through molecular interactions at multiple levels with biomolecules such as DNA, RNA, and proteins, influencing processes such as transcription, translation, and chromatin modification1.

Primordial germ cells (PGCs) are embryonic precursors that differentiate into functional gametes sperm and egg. Specification of the PGC is the crucial step where Epiblast cells are subjected to somatic and germline cell fate decisions2. In mice, PGCs are specified from posterior Epiblast cells; gradually, PGC cells are formed, and these PGC cells migrate to the genital ridges and undergo sex-specific development in the gonads3. Similarly, in pig embryo development, four preimplantation stages occur morula (E ~ 4 to 5), early blastocyst (E ~ 5 to 6), late blastocyst (E ~ 7 to 8), and spherical embryo (E ~ 10 to 11)4. In mammals, PGCs are specified from the Epiblast cells5,6. In pigs, PGC cells are specified from Epiblast cells from E12-E14 embryonic days, and then PGCs migrate to the gonadal ridges and undergo proliferation and extensive epigenetic modification7. In mammals, DNA methylation and maintenance is established in the CG sequences. During embryonic development, there are stages of development where demethylation occurs, including after oocyte fertilization, during preimplantation embryo development, and PGC specification8,9. Chromatin remodelling is an essential phenomenon for the epigenetic reprogramming of PGC cells10. DNA methylation one of the earliest discovered epigenetic mechanism, a chemical modification of the genomic DNA by addition of methyl group (-CH3) to the 5th carbon of the cytosine. DNA methylation a crucial epigenetic modification that regulates cellular differentiation, X-chromosome inactivation, and imprinting by silencing one of the paternal or maternal genome alleles. The loss of DNA methylation can cause gene expression dysregulation, gene dosage, and genomic stability problems11. Despite this, genome-wide demethylation was reported during germ cell development in mammals3. The TET (ten-eleven translocation) gene family, consisting of genes TET1, TET2, TET3, oxidize epigenetically crucial DNA base 5mC (5-methylcytosine) to 5hmC (5-hydroxymethylcytosine); this catalysis acts as an intermediate in active demethylation process12. Previous works revealed the critical role of TET1 and TET2 in the oxidation of 5mC to 5hmC during demethylation in PGCs. Studies on mouse embryos reported that, during PGC development, TET1 is specifically expressed in primordial germ cells, TET2 is present in both somatic and germ cells, while TET3 expression is limited to somatic cells13. However, the role of differentially expressed lncRNAs associated with TET family genes during PGC specification of pig embryos has yet to be addressed.

To understand the significance of LncRNAs in pig PGC cell specification and their association with the TET family genes during gonadal ridge migration, we analyzed the RNAseq dataset that covers porcine Epiblast cells, Somatic cells, and PGC cells. The gene expression profile in the early stages of embryogenesis is intricate and difficult to understand. The vast majority of the genome undergoes transcription, giving rise to both coding and noncoding RNAs. These transcripts are localized in the nucleus and cytoplasm14.

Although efforts have been made to understand the role of TET family genes in DNA demethylation in mouse, pig, and human PGC specification, differential expression of long noncoding RNAs associated with TET family genes during PGC specification in pig early embryo still needs to be addressed. However, the expression patterns of differentially expressed lncRNAs, along with their potential interactions and associations with differentially expressed genes during primordial germ cell (PGC) specification, are not yet fully understood. To achieve a thorough understanding of lncRNA involvement in porcine germline development, it is essential to identify germline-specific lncRNAs and characterize their expression profiles throughout the process of PGC specification.

Materials and methods

Single cell RNA-seq dataset collection

The scRNA-seq dataset was sourced from the GEO repository with accession ID (GSE155136)15 submitted by the University of Nottingham, United Kingdom. The data comprised of 47 pig E11 Epiblast cell samples, 89 neighbouring somatic cell samples from E14 embryos, 17 pig PGC cell samples collected from E14 embryos and 22 pig PGC cells collected from E31 pig embryos. The embryos used in this study were obtained from crossbred Large White and Landrace sows between 11 and 35 days after artificial insemination7. The chromosome level genome assembly of the Sus scrofa (Sscrofa11.1) and respective genome annotation was downloaded from NCBI as the reference genome. The genome in Fasta format and genome annotation in GFF format were downloaded for downstream analysis. The Sscrofa11.1 assembly version consists of 18 autosomal chromosomes, one X, Y, and one mitochondrial chromosome.

Quality control, trimming, mapping and assembly of raw reads

Before analyzing the data, the raw sequencing data were underwent quality control and assessment independently for each data with FastQC-v0.11.916 and MultiQC-v1.2017 software and using in-house Python script to automate the analysis. The raw FASTQ files with paired end sequence reads were trimmed with trimmomatic software v0.3918 to remove any adaptors present in the raw reads with a quality score of 33. After quality trimming reads were employed for additional quality evaluation with FastQC-v0.11.9 and MultiQC-v1.20. Then, the trimmed reads were mapped against pig genome assembly Sscrofa11.1 with HISAT2-v2.2.1 (Hierarchical Indexing for Spliced Alignment of Transcripts) aligner software19. Then, the aligned reads were obtained in SAM (Sequence Alignment/Map) format, with the help of SAMTools v-1.920 the resulting SAM files were converted to binary forms as BAM file (Binary Alignment Map) format and sorted using samtools. The mapped reads were assembled by employing StringTie-v2.1.521 to find potentially novel transcripts using the reference genome Sscrofa11.1.gtf file as a guide by clustering full-length reads and removing redundancy. Individual samples of each cell type of a particular time point were merged to obtain merged annotation files of particular cell types at their respective time points. Stringtie assembles and estimates the expression level of the transcripts. The assembled reads reproduce the intron–exon structures of the transcripts. To quantify transcript expression, paired-end sequencing data were utilized, capturing either one or both ends of each fragment. The resulting expression values were normalized by accounting for the total number of aligned fragments (expressed in millions) and the respective transcript lengths (in kilobases), producing standardized measurements reported as fragments per kilobase of transcript per million mapped reads (FPKM)21. With StringTie, a gene count matrix was generated using GTF files containing fully covered transcripts of each sample concerning the reference genome. GffCompare is a standalone tool for merging and comparing multiple samples to examine the differences and similarities between isoforms and reference genome annotations22. This method adapted from our previous work23.

Computational prediction of pig early embryo developmental stage lncRNAs

For each cell type in our study, we merged the respective assembly gtf files of the E11 Epiblast cell, E14 Somatic cell, E14 PGC cell, and E31 PGC cell samples using the Stringtie merge function, resulting in a unified transcriptome. To identify novel transcripts, the generated transcript annotations were evaluated against the existing genome annotations for each cell type using gffcompare. Next, from the gffcompare output, for downstream analysis, we selected three biotypes: intergenic (u), intronic (i), and antisense (x) transcripts to identify noncoding RNAs for further downstream analysis. The transcripts were further filtered using two criteria: sequence length and ORF length. Reads over 200 nucleotides were selected, and ORFs (Open Reading Frames) greater than 100 amino acids were discarded. After the length and ORF filter, the transcripts were searched for the presence of any protein domains within the transcript. Reverse Position Specific BLAST24 was performed to map the filtered transcripts with protein families of the Pfam25 database to find if any protein domains were present within the transcripts. An evaluation was performed to determine whether the predicted transcripts possess protein-coding potential with CPC226 (Coding potential calculator) software. Those transcripts with zero coding potential and no similarity with the Pfam database were considered as predicted lncRNAs. These steps remove all the transcripts capable of protein-coding regions23. This method adapted from our previous work23

Differential expression of genes and lncRNAs

To compare the overall differential expression patterns among E11 Epiblast cells, E14 PGC cells, E14 Somatic cells, and E31 PGC cells, we grouped the samples in to E11 Epiblast cells versus E14 PGC cells, E11 Epiblast cells versus E14 Somatic cells, E11 Epiblast cells versus E31 PGC cells, E14 PGC cells versus E14 Somatic cells, E14 PGC cells versus E31 PGC cells, E14 Somatic cells versus E31 PGC cells. We ran differential expression analysis for DEGs using edgeR, a Bioconductor R package27 for each group. As input, we used quantile normalized expression values as counts for each group. We compared pairwise comparisons between the groups by adding a pseudocount of 1 to the used function calcNormFactors to estimate log-transformed gene expression. Subsequently, negative binomial dispersion parameters were estimated using the functions estimateTagwiseDisp and estimateCommonDisp. Differential expression analysis was then performed using exactTest to identify features exhibiting significant variation between specific cell types in the early porcine embryo.

Similarly, to assess the overall expression of lncRNAs, DE-lncRNAs were estimated for the grouped samples E11 Epiblast cells versus E14 Somatic cells, E11 Epiblast cells versus E14 PGC cells, E11 Epiblast cells versus E31 PGC cells, E14 PGC cells versus E14 Somatic cells, E14 PGC cells versus E31 PGC cells, E14 Somatic cells versus E31 PGC cells. The lncRNA transcripts of the above groups were subjected to alignment, mapping, and assembly, and then the transcript count matrix was prepared and normalized by quantile normalization. NOISeq28, a Bioconductor R package, analyzed the normalized count matrix. This method adapted from our previous work23.

Co-expression analysis of DEGs and DE-lncRNAs

For co-expression analysis, we used an expression matrix of four different cell types of pig embryo at different time points and grouped them as E11 Epiblast versus E14 PGC, E11 Epiblast versus E14 Somatic, E11 Epiblast versus E31 PGC, Hence, a comprehensive understanding of cellular processes is impossible without, E14 PGC versus E14 Somatic, E14 PGC versus E31 PGC, E14 Somatic versus E31 PGC where rows correspond to DEGS and DE-lncRNAs and columns representing individual samples as inputs for the analysis. We assessed gene co-expression patterns across the DE-lncRNA transcripts using WGCNA29, a method for weighted correlation network analysis. The β power values for each group were determined using the ‘pickSoftThreshold’ function, assuming an unsigned network. Modules within the WGCNA networks were inferred using the Topological Overlap Matrix (TOM), and corresponding eigengenes were identified.

GO-enrichment analysis of the co-expressed genes

The co-expressed DEGs sequences were extracted from their coordinates using bedtools and then analyzed for homology and functional annotation using Omixbox software30. Then, DIAMOND BLAST was carried out in BLASTX mode against the NR protein database, using an E-value cutoff of ≤ 1e−6. InterProScan was performed after BLAST to obtain domain/motif information. Then, mapping was conducted to get the Gene Ontology terms. Gene annotation was performed by applying the (AR) annotation rule. Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) was performed for enrichment analysis.

Sequence analysis of the DEGs and DE-lncRNAs

We searched for simple sequence repeats (SSRs) in DE-lncRNAs and DEG using MISA Tool (Microsatellite Identification Tool)31. The sequences of the co-expressed DEGS and DE-lncRNAs were extracted using bedtools32, and then these sequences were used as input for the MISA tool. MISA gives the output as a .gff file containing all the information about monomeric repeat regions, repeat regions, and microsatellites. Further, this information was plotted using Circos software33.

Chromosome-wide distribution of lncRNAs and their separate distribution

The distribution of lncRNAs classes (intronic “I”, intergenic (“u”), and antisense (“x”) across the chromosomes, were plotted with complot library34 a R package. To visualize the distribution of lncRNA transcripts identified in E11 Epiblast cells, E14 Somatic cells, E14 PGC cells, and E31 PGC cells. Chromplot uses the bed files of the corresponding lncRNAs, and a separate genome file with information about the sizes of the chromosomes was given as input. The data plotted across the chromosomes were shown as coloured bands on chromosomal bodies.

DE-lncRNAs and TET family genes binding prediction with LncTar

The co-expression of DE-lncRNAs and TET family genes determined by WGCNA were further checked for their interactions. LncTar is a software35 for predicting the lncRNA-RNA interactions with free energy minimization used to predict the interactions. The presence of interactions between coding RNAs and noncoding lncRNAs was determined using normalized deltaG (ndG) values as the threshold. One hundred sixteen differentially expressed lncRNAs were subjected to LncTar to predict their interaction with TET family genes. Out of 116 DE-lncRNAs 45 were differentially expressed between E11 Epiblast cells versus E14 Somatic cells, 18 were differentially expressed between E11 Epiblast cells versus E31 PGC cells, 25 were differentially expressed between E14 PGC cells versus E14 Somatic cells, 12 were differentially expressed between E14 PGC cells versus E31 PGC cells and 16 were differentially expressed between E14 Somatic cells versus E31 PGC cells. Out of 116 DE-lncRNAs, 56 showed interactions with the TET family genes. A cutoff of − 0.08 ndG was used to validate the interactions. TET3 co-expressed with the lncRNAs differentially expressed between E11 Epiblast cells versus E14 Somatic cells showed 20 interactions, While TET1 co-expressed with DE-lncRNAs between E11 Epiblast cells versus E31 PGC cells showed 10 interactions, between E14 PGC cells versus E14 Somatic cells showed 13 interactions, between E14 PGC cells versus E31 PGC cells showed 6 interactions, between E14 Somatic cells and E31 PGC cells showed 7 interactions.

Results

Characterization and inference of lncRNAs in Pig embryonic cells

To characterize and infer lncRNAs in three major pig early embryonic cells, Epiblast cells, Somatic cells, and Primordial germ cells, we used a total of 175 RNAseq single-cell RNA-seq samples publically available in the GEO database. The first quality evaluation was performed by analyzing the raw reads (Supplementary Figure 1A). Adapter sequences were trimmed. Subsequently, the trimmed reads were reassessed (Supplementary Figure 1B). We performed read mapping and genome-guided transcriptome assembly of the paired-end RNAseq data. The minimum and maximum percentage mapped reads obtained from samples were 88.57% to 96.10% (Supplementary Table 1), with an average mapping percentage of 94.32%.

Based on genomic coordinates, lncRNAs are classified as intergenic (“u”), i.e. not overlapping with the protein coding genes transcribed from intergenic regions, intronic (“i”), i.e. transcribed from introns of the protein-coding genes of the sense or antisense strand and antisense (“x”), i.e. overlapping with coding genes on the opposite strand36,37. In our study, we identified three classes (“i”, “u”, and “x”) of class codes in E11 Epiblast cells, E14 PGC cells, E14 Somatic cells and E31 PGC cells. RPSBLAST was performed on these transcripts with the Pfam database. The transcripts found no similarity with Pfam database were checked for coding potential. CPC2 was performed for noncoding potential estimation of these transcripts (Table1). We analyzed the genomic loci range of lncRNA transcripts, and we found that though the antisense lncRNAs (“x”) are less in number, their genomic loci range is larger as compared to intronic (“i”) and intergenic (“u”) lncRNA transcripts. Longer genomic loci of intergenic lncRNAs enables them to regulate the gene expression over long genomic distances. Shorter genomic loci of intronic and antisense lncRNAs enables them to act in a cell specific manner. As the intronic lncRNAs are positioned within the protein coding genes they might be responsible for influencing stability, splicing or transcription of host genes. Antisense lncRNAs recruit chromatin modifiers with sense transcript by forming RNA–DNA duplexes.

Differentially expressed lncRNAs

The transcriptome-wide study on pig lncRNAs showed cell-specific and developmental stage-specific expressions; studies suggest lncRNAs show lower expression than mRNA expression38. LncRNA expression profiles exhibit a correlation with mRNA expression patterns and are co-regulated in the expression network. Moreover, the lncRNA expression patterns are significant molecular markers for development, cancer, and disease states. The specific expression patterns of lncRNAs result from the function of the cell state. That is, different cell state may establish their characteristic chromatin states of the developmental state, so, therefore, at these stages, we can see different transcriptomes. Certain lncRNAs or groups of lncRNAs experience distinct regulatory control during different phases of their biogenesis, maturation, and degradation. Our results depict that up- and down-regulated lncRNAs play a role in the differentiation of early pig embryos from Epiblast cells to somatic cells and somatic to early and pre-migratory PGC cells. When comparing these four cell types in six separate pairwise comparisons, we found significantly up and down-regulated lncRNAs between different cell types (Supplementary Table 6). We considered DE-lncRNAs significantly expressed within the range of highest log2FC value “+4.0” to lowest log2FC value of “− 4.0”. Functional annotation of the co-expressed DEGs in porcine embryo

Using Gene Ontology analysis based on omics box software for functional enrichment analysis, we found enriched GO terms. In the comparisons between E11 Epiblast cells and E14 Somatic cells (Fig. 6A), and between PGCs from E14 and E31 stages (Fig. 6E), several cellular component Gene Ontology (GO) terms were found to be commonly enriched and upregulated. These include GO:0043233 (organelle), GO:0005654 (nucleoplasm), GO:0005634 (nucleus), and GO:0031981 (nuclear lumen). The enrichment of these GO terms suggests that key intracellular structures, particularly membrane-bound organelles and nuclear components, are actively involved during these developmental transitions. Membrane-bound organelles play a vital role in epigenetic modifications that drive the early stages of primordial germ cell (PGC) differentiation, while the nucleus and nuclear envelope are essential for initiating and maintaining transcriptional regulation. This pattern of enrichment highlights the importance of tightly regulated nuclear and epigenetic processes in controlling gene expression dynamics during early pig embryonic development. In the comparison between E11 Epiblast and E31 PGC cells (Fig. 6B), several biological processes GO terms were found to be significantly enriched, including GO:0051234 (establishment of localization), GO:0051641 (cellular localization), GO:0006810 (transport) and GO:0051179 (localization). The enrichment of these terms reflects a dynamic shift in cellular activity during the transition from pluripotent epiblast cells to specified PGCs. This increased activity suggests that the precise positioning and movement of molecules and cellular components, along with active ion and molecular transport, are crucial during PGC formation. In the comparison between E14 PGC and E14 somatic cells (Fig. 6C), The upregulated GO terms suggest that E14 PGCs are very active in processes related to cell communication, stress response, and molecule movement within the cell. Specifically, the increased activity of oxidoreductase enzymes and the response to oxidative stress highlight the importance of keeping a balance in the cell’s internal environment, which is crucial for the early progression of germ cell formation. The enrichment of terms related to cell adhesion and junction organization shows that PGCs are adjusting their interactions with nearby cells, which might be necessary for migration or integrating into their surrounding environment. Additionally, the processes that regulate how cells respond to internal signals and move molecules around indicate that PGCs are tightly controlling their internal machinery to maintain their specialized role. On the other hand, the downregulated GO terms suggest that E14 PGCs are less involved in structural and cytoskeletal organization compared to somatic cells (cells that make up the body). For example, there is a decrease in processes like microtubule activity, cell projections, and overall organelle organization, which are typically more active in somatic cells as they grow, divide, and specialize. The reduction in these activities might indicate that PGCs are in a more resting or less active state as they prepare for later stages of development, such as migration or changes in their genetic material. It may also suggest that somatic cells are more focused on tissue formation and differentiation at this point in development. In the comparison between E14 somatic cells and E31 PGC cells (Fig. 6D), the upregulated GO terms show active differentiation and functional specialization of E31 PGCs, with increased processes such as protein synthesis, RNA binding, and cell survival that are crucial for germ cell development. The downregulated terms show that E31 PGCs are less involved in immune functions, sensory activities reflecting their focus on germline development rather than roles typical of somatic cells. The downregulated terms reflect a reduced involvement of E31 PGCs in processes like immune responses, sensory perception, and environmental detection. These are typical of somatic cells, while PGCs prioritize processes specific to germline development and differentiation.

Discussion

A significant portion of the genome is pervasively transcribed into long noncoding RNAs, these molecules constitute a diverse group of transcripts that vary in their genomic origin, orientation, and mechanisms of biogenesis38. Recent studies have shown that despite their emergence from heterogenous genomic locations, lncRNAs play critical roles during PGC specification by modulating the gene expressions in chicken40 and zebrafish41. Embryo development in mammals begins from the individual cell level, albeit cells that possess some plasticity progressively acquire a stable cell fate by undergoing epigenetic modification, and the embryo as a whole move towards a developmental scale through developmental progression by undergoing a series of differentiation steps42. This progressive change is studied by transcriptome analysis driven by long noncoding RNAs and DEGs.

Another interesting feature about genomic locations of lncRNAs reveals that intronic lncRNAs (Fig. 1) are widely distributed in the genome during pig Epiblast to PGC cell differentiation, followed by intergenic lncRNAs and antisense lncRNAs. A subset of intronic lncRNAs and their corresponding protein-coding genes, transcribed from the same genomic loci, exhibit correlated expression profiles, implying that intronic lncRNAs may play a regulatory role in modulating the transcription of these protein-coding genes43. The intronic differentially expressed lncRNA TCONS_00151670 and its co-expressed gene TET1 from distinct cells, E11 Epiblast, and E31 PGC cells at the same genomic location suggest potential cis-regulatory role in gene expression modulation.

During the early stages of embryo development and differentiation, chromatic alterations occur by Alterations in histone modifications and DNA methylation changes44. However, the interplay between chromatin modifications and their role in orchestrating epigenetic regulation of gene expression remains largely unknown. DNA methylation is mainly responsible for gene inactivation and chromatin compaction45. TET enzymes, Fe2+, catalyze DNA demethylation, and α-ketoglutarate-dependent dioxygenases is a reversible process12. The expression of TET family genes diminishes during differentiation and is maintained at high levels in embryonic stem cells. The TET family gene products (TET1, TET2, and TET3 enzymes) initiates DNA demethylation by oxidation of 5mC to 5mhC, an intermediate of the methylation mechanism. Previous work showed that promigratory pig PGCs initiate TET activities, activate BER pathway components and mediate demethylation7. Our work demonstrates the interplay of TET1 and TET3 genes with lncRNAs expression profiles during early pig embryo differentiation when compared between Epiblast cells, Somatic cells, and PGC cells. our previous work on bovine showed the interplay between lncRNAs and XIST during E35 embryonic days between males and females23. Expression profiles of lncRNAs in four cell types in six separate pairwise comparisons during PGC specification shows lncRNA expressions are low compared to the protein-coding genes.

Furthermore, previous work reported that premigratory pig PGCs (E14 PGCs) initiate the TET activities and mediate active and passive DNA demethylation in migratory and gonadal PGCs7. The biological significance and molecular mechanism associated with TET-mediated 5mC oxidation remain poorly understood during epigenetic regulation. However, specific target molecules associated with 5hmC and their specific expression are unknown and difficult to decipher. Multiple studies identified several factors regulating the TET function. Transcription regulators, microRNAs mediated mechanisms, post translational protein modifications shows regulation of TET function46. For instance, SIN3A positively regulates TET1 and TET2 gene expression in pulmonary artial smooth muscle cells47. Whereas, TLX represses the expression of TET3 expression in glioblastoma48. Our work predicted the interactions of DE-lncRNAs and TET family genes involved in embryo cell fate. This suggests these DE-lncRNAs might act as a facilitator or epigenetic determinant during demethylation. Therefore, these findings suggest that the identified lncRNAs may play a pivotal role in epigenetic regulation, particularly in DNA demethylation during porcine PGC specification, thereby influencing their differentiation and developmental progression.

Previous studies suggest that lncRNAs are more nuclear localized than the mRNAs49. The functional enrichment patterns during primordial germ cell (PGC) specification suggest an epigenetic regulation, by co-expressed long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs). The consistent upregulation of nuclear components such as the nucleoplasm, nuclear lumen, and organelle-related GO terms indicates an active nuclear environment where chromatin remodelling, transcriptional control, and RNA-mediated regulation are occurring. During the transition from E11 epiblast cells to E31 PGCs, enriched biological processes like localization and transport not only imply intracellular reorganization but also suggest the directed movement of lncRNAs and chromatin modifiers within the cell. The increased activity of signalling and oxidative stress response pathways in E14 PGCs paired with a reduction in cytoskeletal and structural processes further suggests a shift toward epigenetic stabilization. Co-expressed lncRNAs may be helping to maintain redox balance and regulate stress-responsive genes epigenetically, supporting a cellular environment. By E31, PGCs display enhanced protein synthesis, RNA binding, and cell survival functions hallmarks of cells undergoing epigenetic reprogramming and germline commitment. The downregulation of immune and sensory-related processes underscores the specialization away from somatic lineages. Here, lncRNAs likely play a dual role, acting as scaffolds for chromatin-modifying complexes and guiding transcriptional silencing of somatic programs while enabling the activation of germline-specific genes. The enriched GO terms reflect a regulatory landscape where lncRNAs function as epigenetic modulators, guiding chromatin dynamics, nuclear organization, and gene expression programs that are essential for the successful specification and maturation of PGCs.

The functional significance of lncRNAs and its dynamic expression in embryo development have gained more attentions in recent years50. The large number of lncRNA expression suggests that regulation of gene expression at transcriptional level. Understanding the biological functions of lncRNAs and their dynamic association with the other biological macromolecules will provide a comprehensive understanding of the developmental trajectory of the early embryo and cellular processes. This study provides transcriptional insights in to porcine early embryo cells. Understanding the key non-coding molecules of porcine PGC specification benefit from studying genes and gene regulatory mechanisms. Although the lncRNA-TET interactions predicted by LncTar are based solely on thermodynamic stability, the tool does not account for stacked pair and loop energies, which are critical for accurately modeling RNA-RNA binding. Moreover, the use of a high cutoff value increases the likelihood of false-positive predictions. Therefore, these computational results should be validated using experimental approaches such as RNA pull-down assays, dual-luciferase reporter assays, or CLIP-seq to confirm direct interactions between lncRNAs and TET transcripts. Further studies are necessary to uncover the molecular mechanisms underlying pig lncRNA-associated TET-mediated demethylation. Specifically, future investigations will be required to characterize the functional significance of these lncRNAs in the context of porcine PGC specification and epigenetic reprogramming.
