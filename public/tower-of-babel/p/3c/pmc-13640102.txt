Genome-wide analysis of the GDSL esterase/lipase (GELP) family in banana (Musa acuminata) and functional validation of MaGELP81 as a candidate positive regulator associated with resistance to Fusarium oxysporum f. sp. cubense tropical race 4
Authors: Dandan Tian, Zhangfei He, Jiahui Chen, Liping Wei, Baoshen Li, Quyan Huang, Di Wei, Wei Zhou, Shaolong Wei, Sumei Huang, Jialin Li, Liuyan Qin, Chaosheng Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundFusarium wilt is among the most destructive diseases threatening global banana production and severely undermines the industry’s sustainability. GDSL-type esterase/lipase proteins (GELPs) are lipid-hydrolyzing enzymes with diverse substrate specificities that catalyze the generation and transduction of lipid-derived signals, playing key roles in plant defense responses.ResultsHere, we systematically identified members of the GELP gene family in the banana genome and conducted an integrated analysis of their phylogeny, gene structures, and promoter cis-elements. After manual correction of incorrectly annotated gene structures, a total of 92 MaGELP genes were identified and found to be unevenly distributed across 11 chromosomes, with most located near chromosome ends. Phylogenetic analysis combined with collinearity evidence indicated that the family is evolutionarily conserved. Promoter analysis revealed an enrichment of cis-elements associated with light responsiveness and MeJA-mediated signaling. Expression profiling showed that MaGELP81 is strongly up-regulated (fold change > 1.5) by infection with Fusarium oxysporum f. sp. cubense tropical race 4 (Foc TR4) and by SA, JA, and ET treatments, with pronounced differential expression between resistant and susceptible cultivars (fold change > 1.6). Subcellular localization assays demonstrated that MaGELP81 resides at the vacuolar membrane. When MaGELP81 was overexpressed in Arabidopsis thaliana, three independent high-expression lines exhibited better growth after Foc TR4 inoculation and significantly reduced disease incidence, severity scores, and disease index compared with the wild type, suggesting that MaGELP81 may be associated with enhanced resistance to Foc TR4.ConclusionCollectively, this study provides a comprehensive view of the composition and evolutionary features of the banana GELP family, elucidates their potential roles in responses to Foc TR4 stress, and pinpoints MaGELP81 as a key candidate gene. These findings offer foundational insight into the molecular interplay between lipid metabolism and plant immune signaling and provide valuable genetic resources for the molecular breeding of Foc TR4-resistant banana.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12864-026-13114-x.

Introduction

Banana (Musa acuminata) is one of the most important fruit crops worldwide and serves as both a staple food and cash crop in tropical and subtropical regions [1]. Among the many biotic threats to banana production, Fusarium wilt is the most severe and destructive disease [2]. It is caused by the soil-borne fungus Fusarium oxysporum f. sp. cubense (Foc), which infects roots, colonizes and occludes xylem vessels, and ultimately induces wilt, necrosis, and root rot [3]. Fusarium oxysporum f. sp. cubense tropical race 4 (Foc TR4) can infect many commercially important banana cultivars and is now globally widespread, making it the most devastating of the four physiological races of Foc [4]. Effective field control remains elusive; consequently, breeding and deploying resistant cultivars is considered the most promising strategy [5]. Tolerant Cavendish somaclones, such as Formosana (GCTCV-218), are used in commercial fields affected by the pathogen in Taiwan, the Philippines, and Mozambique [6]. However, the molecular mechanisms underlying resistance to Foc TR4 in banana are still incompletely understood, underscoring the importance of dissecting resistance pathways to guide molecular breeding.

GDSL-type esterase/lipase proteins (GELPs) are lipid hydrolases defined by an N-terminal GDSL motif [7]. GELPs contain five conserved blocks (I–V); the catalytic Ser in block I together with Asp and His residues (typically in block V) form the Ser–Asp–His catalytic triad, whereas additional conserved Gly/Asn residues contribute to catalysis and the oxyanion-stabilizing environment [8]. Owing to their broad substrate specificity, GELPs are involved not only in the hydrolysis and remodeling of triacylglycerols, membrane lipids, phospholipids, and galactolipids, but also in the metabolic regulation of cutin, waxes, suberin, secondary metabolites, and extracellular lipid barrier-associated compounds [9–11]. Recent studies have shown that GELPs function not merely as lipid-metabolic enzymes, but also as important regulatory nodes in plant lipid-signaling networks [12]. Upon pathogen infection, membrane lipid remodeling and the accumulation of lipid-derived signaling molecules constitute early events in plant immune responses [13]. By modulating the production and turnover of fatty acids, phosphatidic acid (PA), diacylglycerol (DAG), monogalactosyldiacylglycerol (MGDG), digalactosyldiacylglycerol (DGDG), oxylipins, and jasmonate-related signaling molecules, GELPs may further influence reactive oxygen species accumulation, cell wall reinforcement, antimicrobial compound biosynthesis, and crosstalk among salicylic acid, jasmonic acid, and ethylene signaling pathways [14]. In addition, some secreted GELPs may directly contribute to extracellular lipid barrier formation, cuticle integrity, and restriction of pathogen invasion [11]. Thus, GELPs play important roles in plant growth and development, organ morphogenesis, lipid metabolism, secondary metabolism, and responses to both biotic and abiotic stresses.

Studies in different plant species have further demonstrated marked functional diversification of GELP family members in plant immunity [15–19]. In Arabidopsis thaliana, AtGLIP1 enhances resistance to multiple pathogens by modulating ethylene signaling and also exhibits antimicrobial activity, whereas AtGLIP2 is associated with pathogen defense and auxin signaling, indicating that GELP members can participate in immune responses through distinct hormone-related pathways [16, 17]. In rice, OsGLIP1 and OsGLIP2 negatively regulate resistance to bacterial leaf streak, bacterial blight, and rice blast, mainly through modulation of lipid metabolism [15]. Altered expression of these genes affects multiple lipid classes, including MGDG, DGDG, TAG, and PA, suggesting a close connection between GELP-mediated lipid homeostasis and disease resistance in rice. Conversely, other rice GELP members have been reported to positively regulate broad-spectrum disease resistance and yield-related traits, indicating that different GELP members within the same species may exert opposite or functionally divergent roles in immune regulation [18]. In pepper, CaGLIP1 is induced by pathogen infection, high salinity, and exogenous salicylic acid, ethylene, and methyl jasmonate treatments, suggesting its involvement in both biotic and abiotic stress responses [9]. In cotton, several upland cotton GELP genes are downregulated after PEG treatment, salt stress, or inoculation with Verticillium dahliae, implying that this family may broadly participate in stress adaptation and responses to Verticillium wilt [19, 20]. In tomato, SlGELP49 and SlGELP77 are upregulated after inoculation with Stemphylium lycopersici, whereas SlGELP48, SlGELP46, and SlGELP33 are downregulated, indicating functional divergence among tomato GELP members in the regulation of leaf spot disease resistance [21]. Collectively, these studies suggest that GELP family members are widely involved in pathogen responses across plant species, but their functional roles, regulatory pathways, and biochemical substrates are highly species- and member-specific.

Although GELP family members have been functionally characterized to some extent in several plant species, including A. thaliana, rice, pepper, cotton, and tomato, systematic studies of this family in banana remain very limited [15–21]. This knowledge gap is particularly important because banana is a perennial, clonally propagated crop with a complex genomic background, and Foc TR4 primarily infects roots and subsequently spreads through the vascular system. This infection process is likely accompanied by membrane lipid remodeling, alterations in cell-wall and extracellular lipid barriers, activation of lipid-derived signaling, and crosstalk among defense-related hormone pathways. However, whether the banana GELP family participates in Foc TR4 infection responses, whether individual MaGELP members exhibit expression and functional diversification, and whether they regulate banana disease resistance through lipid metabolism or lipid-mediated signaling remain largely unknown. Therefore, systematic identification of banana GELP family members and characterization of their evolutionary features, expression patterns, and potential functions are essential for improving our understanding of the molecular mechanisms underlying banana resistance to Foc TR4. Recent advances in banana genomics now enable genome-wide surveys of defense-related gene families [22, 23]. Here, we present the first genome-wide characterization of the banana GELP family, analyzing chromosomal distribution, evolutionary relationships, gene structures, and promoter cis-acting elements, alongside expression profiles under Fusarium wilt (Foc TR4) infection and hormone treatments. We further performed subcellular localization and heterologous overexpression in A. thaliana to probe biological functions. These results broaden our understanding of GELP-mediated lipid-associated signaling in plant defense and provide useful clues and genetic resources for unraveling the roles of banana GELPs particularly in the context of Foc TR4 resistance and for advancing molecular breeding against Fusarium wilt.

Results

Identification of the GELP gene family in bananas

To identify and characterize GELP genes in banana, we performed a comprehensive search using HMMER using hmmsearch in the genomes of the triploid Cavendish banana (Cavendish) and the doubled haploid DH-Pahang. GELP genes with abnormal gene structures and lengths were re-annotated and corrected (Fig. S1, Table S1 and Table S2). To ensure the accuracy of the results, the final annotations were validated against the SMART, CDD, and Pfam databases. In the Cavendish genome, the three haplotypes (Hap1: M. acuminata ssp. banksii, Hap2: M. acuminata ssp. malaccensis, Hap3: M. acuminata ssp. zebrina) contained 83, 80, and 96 GELP gene sequences, respectively, while the DH-Pahang genome contained 92 GELP gene sequences. The differences between the haplotypes in the Cavendish genome may be closely related to its polyploid origin and the complex gene recombination and chromosomal variations that occurred during long-term domestication and evolution. Additionally, the significant difference in the number of GELP gene sequences between DH-Pahang and M. acuminata ssp. malaccensis further highlights the diversity in the GELP gene family composition across different genetic resources. These results suggest that the GELP gene family is broadly represented in banana and may have undergone lineage-specific expansion or diversification.

Based on the chromosomal positions of the 92 sequences, we named these genes MaGELP1 to MaGELP92 (Table S1). The open reading frames (ORFs) of the banana GELP family genes range from 714 to 2043 bp, encoding proteins containing 237 to 680 amino acid residues, with relative molecular weights ranging from 26.11 to 74.64 kDa and theoretical isoelectric points between 4.30 and 9.75. Subcellular localization predictions indicated that 37 MaGELP proteins are localized to the chloroplast, 19 to the extracellular space, 11 to the vacuole, 8 to the endoplasmic reticulum, 7 to the plasma membrane, 6 to the cytoplasm, 2 to the nucleus, 1 to the mitochondrion, and 1 to the peroxisome. The 92 banana GELP genes are distributed across 11 chromosomes (Chr01, Chr02, Chr03, Chr04, Chr05, Chr06, Chr07, Chr08, Chr09, Chr10, and Chr11) in the banana genome (Fig. 1). Among them, Chr08 contains the most MaGELP genes, with 14, while Chr09 and Chr10 each contain the fewest, with 5 MaGELP genes. Collectively, these results establish a curated catalog of 92 MaGELPs with wide variation in length, pI and predicted subcellular residence, and reveal an uneven genomic distribution that likely underpins functional diversification within the family.Fig. 1Chromosomal mapping of the GELP gene family in bananas

Phylogenetic analysis and evolution of the MaGELP genes

To better understand the evolutionary relationships of the GELP gene family in banana, we constructed a phylogenetic tree based on the GELP protein sequences from banana, A. thaliana, and rice. Phylogenetic analysis showed that GELP proteins from banana, rice, and A. thaliana could be classified into three major groups (Fig. 2, Table S3). Group 1 consists of 9 GELP genes, including 4 banana GELP genes, 4 rice GELP genes, and 1 A. thaliana GELP gene. These genes are primarily annotated in the Lipase activity and Plant defense and seed development pathways (Figure S2). Group 2 includes 85 GELP genes, with 25 from banana, 46 from rice, and 14 from A. thaliana. These genes are mainly associated with Lipase activity and Lipid Metabolism pathways. Group 3 is further subdivided into 7 subgroups, comprising a total of 216 GELP genes, with 63 from banana, 64 from rice, and 89 from A. thaliana. These genes are predominantly involved in Xylan biosynthetic process, Pollen coat formation, Rhamnose catabolic process, Inhibition of auxin signaling pathway to defend against pathogens, Systemic acquired resistance, and Lipid Metabolism pathways. These results indicate that the banana GELP gene family exhibits significant evolutionary diversity across different species, while also showing a high degree of conservation in certain functional pathways. This conservation and diversity may be closely related to their multiple roles in crucial physiological processes, such as plant defense, lipid metabolism, and seed development. Further, we constructed a phylogenetic tree for Cavendish and DH-Pahang (Figure S3, Table S4). The analysis showed that the number of GELP genes in each branch, representing the three Cavendish haplotypes and DH-Pahang, is very similar, suggesting that both have a highly similar composition in the GELP gene family. Additionally, the phylogenetic relationship between DH-Pahang and M. acuminata ssp. malaccensis is closer, further supporting the idea that DH-Pahang is evolutionarily more closely related to M. acuminata ssp. malaccensis. This finding is consistent with the current understanding of banana evolution, indicating that DH-Pahang, as one of the banana cultivars, has a genomic relationship that is more closely aligned with the ancestral species of M. acuminata ssp. malaccensis.Fig. 2Phylogenetic tree of GELP genes for bananas, A. thaliana, and rice, with different branches of color indicating different groupings, five-pointed star representing banana GELP genes, circle representing rice GELP genes, and triangle representing A. thaliana GELP genes

Evolution, gene structure, and motif analysis of the GELP gene family in bananas

Based on the full-length sequences, coding sequences (CDS), and protein sequences of the banana GELP genes, we conducted phylogenetic tree, gene structure, and motif analyses. According to the phylogenetic tree, all banana GELP gene family members were classified into three subgroups (Fig. 4). Group 1 consists of genes that all have 6 exons, with the exception of MaGELP18, and all but one contain a single motif. Group 2 contains genes with 5 to 8 exons, and with the exception of MaGELP92, each gene contains at least 6 motifs (motif 1, motif 3, motif 5, motif 6, motif 8, and motif 10). Group 3 members have at least 5 exons and contain at least 6 motifs (motif 1, motif 5, motif 6, motif 7, motif 8, and motif 9). Five motifs (motif 2, motif 3, motif 5, motif 6, and motif 10) are shared between Group 2 and Group 3. MaGELP92 has the fewest exons, with only 2, but still contains 7 motifs. These results suggest that there is no direct correlation between the number of exons and the number of motifs. Although the GELP genes in different subgroups vary in terms of motif types and numbers, the conserved nature of their gene structures indicates that their functions have remained stable throughout the evolutionary process. This highlights the evolutionary stability of the GELP gene family, suggesting that despite structural variations, its core functions have been preserved across evolutionary history.Fig. 4Phylogeny, gene structure, and conserved motifs of banana GELPs. Analysis of promoter cis-acting elements of the GELP gene family in bananas

To investigate the potential regulatory logic of banana GELPs, we analyzed the 1,500 bp promoter region upstream of each MaGELP start codon and catalogued cis-acting elements related to light, stress, and hormone responses (Fig. 5). We identified seven classes of light-responsive elements, including G-Box, GATA-motif, Box 4, GT1-motif, MRE, AE-box, and ACE distributed heterogeneously among promoters. Hormone-related elements included MeJA response motifs (TGACG-motif and CGTCA-motif), abscisic acid response elements (ABREs), gibberellin response elements (P-box, GARE-motif, and TATC-box), SA response elements (TCA-elements), and auxin response elements (TGA-elements). Among these, MeJA-responsive elements were the most abundant (310 instances), consistent with a potential role for lipid-derived JA signaling in regulating GELP activity. We also detected four stress-associated elements-MBS (drought), LTR (low temperature), TC-rich repeats, and the CCAAT box implicating MaGELPs in broader abiotic and biotic stress pathways. Each MaGELP promoter harbors a distinct combination and number of cis-acting elements, suggesting that individual genes are differentially wired to environmental and hormonal cues and may contribute to growth, development, and stress adaptation via divergent signaling cascades. Notably, the strong enrichment of MeJA-responsive elements suggests that JA-mediated defense signaling may be an important regulatory layer for MaGELP genes. This observation provided a rationale for subsequent hormone-induction assays and for exploring the possible connection between MaGELP expression and lipid-associated immune responses.Fig. 5Distribution of cis-acting elements in the promoter regions of banana GELP genes. Analysis of the expression pattern of the banana GELP gene family in response to Foc TR4

Because expression dynamics are often indicative of gene function, we analyzed RNA-seq profiles of banana GELPs following Foc TR4 infection (Fig. 6). To identify MaGELP genes showing marked transcriptional changes after infection for subsequent qRT-PCR validation, we first screened 32 MaGELP genes whose transcript levels were altered following Foc TR4 inoculation. These genes were further filtered using the following criteria: detectable expression in at least one sample with an FPKM value ≥ 1; a fold change ≥ 1.5 or ≤ 0.67 relative to the corresponding uninoculated control; and a relatively consistent expression trend across different infection time points. Based on these criteria, 10 candidate genes, namely MaGELP10, MaGELP11, MaGELP37, MaGELP44, MaGELP55, MaGELP60, MaGELP63, MaGELP76, MaGELP81, and MaGELP91, were selected for qRT-PCR validation. Except for MaGELP63, which belongs to Group II, the remaining candidates were assigned to different subgroups within the major phylogenetic Group III, suggesting that these MaGELP members are relatively close in evolutionary relationship and may participate in similar or related biological processes (Table S5).Fig. 6RNA-seq expression analysis of the banana GELP gene under Foc TR4 infection

Subcellular localization and overexpression in A. thaliana

MaGELP81 was selected for further functional characterization not solely because of its induction magnitude in RNA-seq, but because it showed the most consistent defense-associated expression pattern across multiple analyses. Although MaGELP81 was not the most highly induced MaGELP in the RNA-seq dataset, its expression was induced by Foc TR4 infection, confirmed by qRT-PCR, and was markedly higher in the resistant cultivar G9 than in the susceptible cultivar G1. Moreover, MaGELP81 was consistently induced by SA, JA, and ET treatments, suggesting that it may participate in hormone-mediated defense signaling. Therefore, MaGELP81 was selected as a promising candidate for subsequent analyses. Using the genome-annotated CDS as a template, primers were designed to amplify the full-length coding sequence of MaGELP81, which was subsequently fused in-frame with eGFP in the plant expression vector pBI221-eGFP and introduced into Agrobacterium tumefaciens. Following Agrobacterium-mediated transient expression, confocal laser scanning microscopy showed that the MaGELP81–eGFP fluorescence signal largely overlapped with the tonoplast marker Tip-mCherry, indicating that MaGELP81 predominantly localizes to the vacuolar membrane (Fig. 11a). This result is consistent with the predicted vacuolar localization of MaGELP81 based on computational analysis, and further supports its association with the vacuolar membrane (Table S2). We then overexpressed MaGELP81 in A. thaliana and selected three independent overexpression lines (OE) with the highest transcript accumulation for disease‑resistance assays (Figure S4). At 21 dpi with Foc TR4, wild type (WT) plants exhibited marked chlorosis and wilting, whereas the three transgenic lines showed normal growth (Fig. 11b). Compared to the WT, the disease incidence in these three transgenic lines was reduced by 50.00%−68.75%, with disease severity rating decreasing by 86.21%−89.66%, and disease severity index values dropping by 79.17%−83.25% (Fig. 11c, d and e). Collectively, these results indicate that MaGELP81 predominantly localizes to the vacuolar membrane and that heterologous overexpression of MaGELP81 in A. thaliana is associated with reduced Foc TR4-induced disease symptoms. Discussion

In recent years, with advances in sequencing technologies and decreasing sequencing costs, banana genome assemblies have been steadily improved and updated, laying the foundation for genome-wide functional studies [22, 23]. This progress further deepens our understanding of banana genomics and genetics and makes it possible to explore gene families and their phylogenetic relationships. The GELP gene family has been systematically analyzed in plants such as A. thaliana, rice and soybean [24–26]. In this study, the GELP gene family was identified using a high-quality banana genome assembly, and was systematically analyzed with respect to phylogeny, chromosomal distribution, and sequence characteristics. A search of the banana genome identified 92 GELP members. Chromosome mapping showed that most of these 92 genes are distributed near the chromosome ends. Subcellular localization predictions indicated that these proteins may localize to the cytoplasm, endoplasmic reticulum, Golgi apparatus, mitochondria, nucleus, vacuole, and extracellular space, consistent with the complex and diverse functions reported for this family [27, 28]. Based on phylogenetic relationships, the MaGELP members were divided into three subfamilies. All 92 MaGELP genes were assigned to clades that also contain A. thaliana and rice genes, suggesting that their functions may be similar to those of GELPs in A. thaliana and rice. Protein and gene structures are closely related to gene function; within each subfamily, members share similar gene structures and motif compositions, with nearly identical numbers of introns, exons, and motifs [29]. A five-exon/four-intron organization is a notable feature of plant GELP genes, and 76.09% (70) of banana GELPs contain five exons [8–10, 24–26]. This systematic analysis represents a first step toward understanding the roles of GELPs in banana and provides a solid foundation for predicting their potential functions. However, we recognize that genome-wide identification and structural characterization alone provide limited mechanistic insight. Therefore, in this study, we further combined family-level analyses with pathogen-responsive expression profiling, hormone treatments, subcellular localization, and functional validation to move beyond a purely descriptive characterization.

Cis-acting elements associated with plant hormone and abiotic-stress responses are widely present in the promoter regions of banana GELP genes, suggesting that these factors may interact to shape the regulatory mechanisms of the GELP family. In recent years, many studies have shown that cross-talk among plant hormones regulates the balance between disease resistance and growth and development [30, 31]. Plant immune signaling comprises a complex network involving the defense hormones SA, JA and ET, with ET often coordinating with JA to regulate immunity [32, 33]. For example, increased JA signaling in rice confers broad-spectrum resistance to leaf blight and rice blast [34]. JA is a key plant hormone that regulates several processes, including fertility, seedling emergence, wound responses, and the growth defense trade off [35]. Promoter analysis of the MaGELP family identified numerous hormone related cis-acting elements, among which MeJA-responsive elements were the most abundant (310). We also analyzed hormone-induced expression patterns: among the ten candidate genes, seven were significantly regulated by JA. These results suggest that hormone signals may interact to influence the regulatory mechanisms governing banana GELP genes. The enrichment of MeJA-responsive cis-elements, together with the JA inducibility of multiple MaGELP genes, supports a potential link between GELP-mediated lipid metabolism and JA-associated immune signaling. Because GELPs can participate in membrane lipid remodeling and the generation of lipid-derived signaling molecules, these results suggest that Foc TR4-responsive MaGELPs may contribute to defense regulation through lipid-associated signaling pathways.

Lipases, particularly GDSL esterases/lipases (GELPs/GLIPs) not only participate in lipid synthesis and degradation but also mediate the production and transduction of multiple lipid‑derived signals (e.g., oxylipins, phosphatidic acid and lysophospholipids). These signals are tightly integrated with defense‑hormone networks, including SA, JA and ET, thereby playing pivotal roles in plant growth, development, and responses to environmental stimuli [18, 36]. In rice, pathogen infection and SA treatment suppress the expression of OsGLIP1 and OsGLIP2. downregulation of these genes markedly enhances resistance to bacterial and fungal pathogens, whereas overexpression compromises resistance, indicating that both function as negative regulators of immunity [15]. By contrast, AtGDSL1 in A. thaliana positively regulates resistance to Sclerotinia sclerotiorum, loss of function mutants are more susceptible, and overexpression in Brassica napus enhances resistance, suggesting that the GELP family encompasses both positive and negative regulators across species [37, 38]. Against this backdrop, we profiled the transcriptional responses of the banana GELP family to Foc TR4. MaGELP91 showed sustained downregulation during infection, with significantly lower transcript levels in the resistant cultivar than in the susceptible one, pointing to a potential negative regulatory role that echoes the functional pattern of OsGLIP1/2 in rice [12]. In contrast, MaGELP81 was strongly induced by Foc TR4 infection as well as SA, JA, and ET treatments. Importantly, MaGELP81 was not selected solely based on its RNA-seq induction magnitude, but rather on the integration of multiple lines of evidence, including pathogen responsiveness, hormone inducibility, stronger induction in the resistant cultivar, predicted/validated membrane-associated localization, and the disease-resistance phenotype of overexpression lines. Overexpression of MaGELP81 in A. thaliana produced three independent high-expression lines that, at 21 dpi with Foc TR4, exhibited better growth and significantly reduced disease incidence, disease severity scores, and disease index relative to the wild type, suggesting that MaGELP81 is associated with enhanced resistance to Foc TR4 in a heterologous A. thaliana system. However, because lipidomic profiling and enzymatic assays were not performed in the present study, the direct biochemical function of MaGELP81 and its precise role in lipid-mediated immune signaling remain unresolved. Taken together, our family level analysis reveals extensive transcriptional reprogramming of banana GELP genes during Foc TR4 infection and identifies MaGELP81 as a key candidate gene. These findings provide mechanistic entry points into the coupling between lipid metabolism and immune signaling and furnish actionable gene resources and targets for molecular breeding of Foc TR4-resistant banana. Future studies dissecting the enzymatic activities, substrate spectra, and lipidomic signatures of MaGELP81, combined with gene editing and precision expression in banana, will help establish a mechanism-based framework for GELP mediated resistance and accelerate trait improvement.

Conclusions

In this study, we identified 92 banana GELP genes and resolved them into three highly conserved phylogenetic subgroups. Promoter landscapes were enriched for MeJA-responsive cis-elements, consistent with defense-related functions. Expression profiling highlighted MaGELP81 as a leading candidate in Foc TR4 responses, and subcellular localization placed MaGELP81 at the vacuolar membrane. Heterologous overexpression of MaGELP81 in A. thaliana (three independent high-expression lines) improved post-inoculation growth and significantly reduced disease incidence, severity scores, and disease index after Foc TR4 challenge. These findings provide a genome-scale framework for understanding banana GELPs. MaGELP81 represents a promising defense-associated candidate gene for future functional and biochemical validation. Although heterologous overexpression supports its association with enhanced Foc TR4 resistance, lipidomic profiling, enzymatic assays, and banana-based genetic validation will be required to determine whether it directly regulates lipid-mediated immune responses.

Materials and methods

Plant materials

Two Musa acuminata Cavendish cultivars were used for Foc TR4 and hormone-induction expression analyses: Guijiao 1 (G1), which is susceptible to Foc TR4, and Guijiao 9 (G9), which is resistant to Foc TR4 [33, 39]. Seedlings were carefully removed from nutrient cups, and the substrate was rinsed 3–5 times with tap water, followed by 3–4 washes with distilled water and 3–4 washes with ultrapure water. The seedlings were then soaked in a thymol solution (5 mg/mL) for 3 min and rinsed three times with ultrapure water. The plants were transferred to pre-sterilized containers, with their exposed roots covered with an appropriate amount of expanded clay (ceramsite) and supplied with deionized water. Seedlings were maintained in a greenhouse under natural light. After 3 days, 500 mL of half-strength Hoagland solution (1/2 Hoagland) was added. The seedlings were divided into two groups, with no fewer than 60 seedlings per group. For Foc TR4 inoculation, one group was cultured for 4 days in 1/2 Hoagland nutrient solution, and then transferred to 800 mL of Foc TR4 spore suspension (1 × 107 spores/mL) for 4 h. The inoculated seedlings were then returned to 1/2 Hoagland nutrient solution, with their roots covered by ceramsite. Root tissues (1 cm white tips) were sampled at 3 and 7 days post-inoculation, with pre-inoculation samples serving as the controls. The root tips were briefly washed in Phosphate Buffered Saline (PBS, 137 mM NaCl, 2.7 mM KCl, 10 mM Na2HPO4, and 1.8 mM KH2PO4, pH 7.4) to remove surface debris. For the hormone treatments, the second group was cultured for 4 days in 1/2 Hoagland nutrient solution, the leaves were sprayed with 50 μmol L−1 salicylic acid (SA), jasmonic acid (JA), or ethephon as an ethylene (ET) donor. Root tissues were harvested at 0 h (pre-treatment) and 24 h after hormone application for subsequent qRT-PCR analysis. Foliar hormone application was used to induce systemic defense signaling responses throughout the plant, while root tissues were analyzed because Foc TR4 is a root-infecting vascular pathogen. For each treatment, three biological replicates were collected, with each replicate comprising pooled tissues of the same type from 10 independent banana seedlings. All samples were immediately snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at − 80 °C until further analysis.

Identification and physicochemical analysis of banana GELP genes

Genome assemblies and annotations for banana, A. thaliana and rice were downloaded from Ensembl Plants (https://plants.ensembl.org). Candidate GELPs were identified using HMMER v3.2.1 (https://hmmer.org) with the Pfam Hidden Markov Model (HMM) profile PF00657. GELP proteins from A. thaliana and rice with E-values < 0.001 were retained. For the GELP genes with abnormal gene structures and lengths, Genome Sequence Annotation Manager (GSAman) was used to identify and correct incomplete or incorrect splicing issues in the gene structures, ensuring the accurate splicing of exons and introns for each gene. Candidate sequences were validated for the presence of complete GELP domains using SMART (http://smart.embl.de/), CDD (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/cdd), and Pfam (https://pfam.xfam.org/) databases. The final GELP protein sets for banana, A. thaliana and rice were compiled, and physicochemical properties of banana GELPs were calculated using BioPerl.

Phylogenetic analysis

Amino-acid sequences of GELPs from banana, A. thaliana and rice were aligned with ClustalW (default settings) in MEGA X. Phylogenetic trees were constructed using the Maximum Likelihood (ML) method with 1,000 bootstrap replicates [40]. Trees were visualized with EvolView (https://www.evolgenius.info/evolview/).

Collinearity analysis

Interspecific collinearity analysis was performed to compare the gene order and synteny between the banana genome, A. thaliana, and rice. This analysis aimed to identify conserved chromosomal regions and gene families across species. Whole-genome duplications (WGDs) play a significant role in disrupting collinearity, as they result in gene duplications and rearrangements. A. thaliana underwent one 3 × and two 2 × dicot-specific genome duplications, whereas both Musa and rice share a common 2 × duplication event followed by two additional lineage-specific 2 × duplications [41–43]. These duplications can affect gene order and synteny across species, so we focused on identifying conserved gene regions that are still collinear despite these duplication events. The analysis was performed using MCScanX to detect syntenic blocks and interspecific gene alignments [44].

Gene structure and motif analysis

Conserved motifs in banana GELPs were identified using MEME (http://alternate.meme-suite.org/tools/meme) with the maximum number of motifs set to 10. Gene structure information was extracted from the genome GFF3 annotation. MEME XML outputs, phylogenetic trees (NWK), and GFF3 files were jointly visualized in TBtools [45].

Analysis of promoter cis-acting elements

The Extract Upstream Sequences tool in TBtools was used to extract the promoter sequences of genes based on the gene's GFF3 file and the genome file. Promoter regions (1,500 bp upstream of the start codon) of banana GELPs were submitted to PlantCARE (http://bioinformatics.psb.ugent.be/webtools/plantcare/html/) to predict cis-acting regulatory elements. The distribution and abundance of elements were visualized as heatmaps using TBtools [45].

RNA-seq analysis

RNA-seq datasets for G1 and G9 at 0, 3, and 7 days after Foc TR4 inoculation were generated by Beijing Biomics Tech Co., Ltd., and have been deposited in NCBI database (BioProject PRJNA916078). For each treatment and time point, three biological replicates were collected, with each replicate consisting of pooled root tissues from 10 independent banana seedlings. Raw reads were filtered and quality-controlled with fastp [46], aligned to the banana reference genome using HISAT2 [47], and quantified with StringTie [48]. Gene expression was calculated as Fragments Per Kilobase of transcript per Million mapped reads (FPKM), and expression heatmaps were generated with TBtools [45].

qRT-PCR analysis

Total RNA was isolated with the TIANGEN kit (Tiangen, China). First-strand cDNA was synthesized using M-MLV reverse transcriptase (TaKaRa, Japan). Primers (Table S6) were designed in Primer Premier 5.0; MaActin served as the internal reference. qRT-PCR was performed using ChamQ Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Novozan Biotechnology, Nanjing) on the following program: 95 °C for 30 s; then 40 cycles of 95 °C for 10 s, 60 °C for 30 s, and 72 °C for 20 s. For each treatment, three independent biological replicates were analyzed, with three technical replicates performed for each biological replicate. Relative expression levels were calculated using the 2⁻ΔΔCT method [49].

Subcellular localization

The full-length MaGELP81 CDS was cloned into pBI221-eGFP to generate a C-terminal eGFP fusion and introduced into Agrobacterium tumefaciens GV3101. Transient expression was performed in rice (Nipponbare) tissues, and fluorescence was observed on an Olympus FV3000 confocal microscope (excitation 488 nm; emission 505–530 nm for eGFP; chlorophyll autofluorescence recorded accordingly). Subcellular localization was determined based on the overlap between the MaGELP81–eGFP signal and the Tip-mCherry tonoplast marker.

Overexpression in A. thaliana

To generate overexpression lines in A. thaliana (Col-0), the MaGELP81 coding sequence was cloned into the pHB vector under the CaMV 35S promoter (35S::MaGELP81). The recombinant plasmid was transformed into A. tumefaciens GV3101 and introduced into Col-0 using the floral-dip method [50]. T1 plants were selected on the appropriate antibiotic-containing medium and verified for transgene insertion by PCR. Homozygous T2 lines were identified by segregation analysis. Transgene expression was quantified by qRT-PCR, and three independent high-expression T2 lines were selected for functional assays. Plants were challenged with Foc TR4 using a root-wounding inoculation, and at 21 days post-inoculation (dpi), disease incidence and severity were scored according to predefined criteria to calculate the disease index [51]. Disease symptoms were scored using a 0–4 scale: 0, normal plant growth with no visible symptoms; 1, slight chlorosis or wilting of a few leaves; 2, approximately 25%–50% of leaves showing chlorosis or wilting; 3, more than 50% of leaves showing chlorosis, wilting, or necrosis; and 4, severe wilting or plant death [3]. At least 30 A. thaliana plants were evaluated for each treatment, with three biological replicates. Disease incidence was calculated as the percentage of diseased plants among the total number of investigated plants, and plants with disease severity rating (DSR) ≥ 1 were considered diseased. The Disease severity index (DSI) was calculated using the following formula: DSI = [Σ(number of plants in each disease class × corresponding disease score)/(total number of investigated plants × maximum disease score)] × 100.

Statistical analysis

All data were obtained from three independent biological replicates and are shown as the mean ± SE. Statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism 9.0. Differences between two groups were evaluated using a two-tailed Student’s t-test. For qRT-PCR analysis, statistical significance between G1 and G9 at the same time point or under the same treatment condition was determined using Student’s t-test. For comparisons involving more than two groups, one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s multiple-comparison test was performed. For disease-resistance assays comparing wild-type and MaGELP81-overexpressing lines, one-way ANOVA followed by Dunnett’s test was used, with the wild type as the control. Percentage data, including disease incidence, were arcsine square-root transformed before analysis when appropriate.
