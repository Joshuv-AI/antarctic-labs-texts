Cost-Consequences of Adding Gliclazide Modified Release to Metformin in Patients with Uncontrolled Type 2 Diabetes in the United Arab Emirates
Authors: Mohamed Farghaly, Olivier Cristeau, Fatheya Al Awadi, Sara Al Dallal
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionType 2 diabetes (T2D) is a chronic metabolic disease that is highly prevalent in the United Arab Emirates (UAE). This study aimed to assess the cost-consequences of adding gliclazide modified release (MR) to metformin in patients with inadequate glycaemic control on metformin alone and of switching patients currently receiving a combination of sitagliptin and metformin to a combination of gliclazide MR and metformin.MethodsWe developed a cost-consequences decision-tree model comparing gliclazide MR and sitagliptin as second-line add-ons to metformin. Based on 2014–2023 Dubai Real World Claims Database data, the model estimated health outcomes (number of patients reaching HbA1c targets of < 7% and ≤ 6.5% and cardiovascular [CV] events, i.e. myocardial infarction [MI] and hospitalisation for heart failure [HHF]), resource use (inpatient, emergency room, and outpatient visits, and hospital bed days), and costs (drug acquisition and medical costs) associated with the two therapies over a 1-year time horizon from the payer perspective.ResultsAdding gliclazide MR to metformin in a cohort of 126,074 patients with inadequate glycaemic control on metformin alone was estimated to result in 535 CV events and 35 deaths avoided per year at a cost of USD 12,250 per CV event avoided or USD 189,415 per death avoided. Switching patients currently treated with sitagliptin as an add-on to metformin to gliclazide MR was estimated to avert 26 cardiovascular events (5 MI and 21 HHF) and 2 deaths per year while providing annual savings of USD 5.11 million, including USD 4.77 million in drug acquisition costs and USD 330,837 in medical costs.ConclusionInitiating gliclazide MR as an add-on treatment to metformin could help to reduce the clinical and economic burden of poorly controlled T2D among patients in the UAE. Switching patients from sitagliptin to gliclazide MR as a second-line treatment option could generate substantial cost savings.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12325-025-03334-0.Keywords: Cardiovascular outcomes, Cost-consequences analysis, Gliclazide, Glucose-lowering therapy, Glycaemic control, Healthcare cost analysis, Real-world evidence, Sitagliptin, Type 2 diabetes, United Arab Emirates

Introduction

Type 2 diabetes (T2D) is a chronic metabolic disorder characterized by insulin resistance and/or impaired insulin secretion, which can lead to a range of long-term micro- and macrovascular complications [1, 2]. Macrovascular complications of T2D include coronary heart disease, stroke, and peripheral arterial disease, while microvascular conditions include diabetic kidney disease, retinopathy, and peripheral neuropathy [1]. Additional complications associated with diabetes have also been described, including various cancers [3, 4], physical and functional disability [5], dementia [6], liver disease [7], depression [8], and sleep apnoea [9, 10].

In 2021, an estimated 529 million people were living with diabetes worldwide, of which 96% had T2D [11]. The prevalence of T2D is particularly high in the Middle East and North Africa (MENA) region, with age-standardized prevalence rates of 9.3–16.2% reported in 2021 [11, 12]. In the United Arab Emirates (UAE), the age-adjusted diabetes prevalence standardised to the United Nations world population has been estimated at 16.4% (12.3% unadjusted) [12].

Strict control of blood glucose levels reduces the incidence and progression of microvascular complications, including retinopathy and kidney disease [13, 14], although many regimens carry a risk of hypoglycaemia [15]. Glucose-lowering therapies therefore aim to provide glycaemic control while minimising the risk of hypoglycaemia. Effectiveness and safety of treatment are frequently monitored on the basis of glycated haemoglobin (HbA1c) levels, with a target of 7% (corresponding to 53 mmol/mol) generally considered suitable for most adult patients, although a lower target such as 6.5% may be considered if it can be achieved without significant adverse effects of treatment [16–18].

Metformin, a biguanide, has traditionally been recommended as a first-line treatment for T2D; however, when used as monotherapy it is often insufficient to provide adequate glycaemic control. In patients who do not achieve adequate glycaemic control on metformin alone, the joint consensus report from the American Diabetes Association (ADA) and the European Association for the Study of Diabetes (EASD) recommends that other classes of glucose-lowering agents should be considered, and their selection should be individualised on the basis of the balance between glucose-lowering efficacy and adverse event profile [16]. Similarly, the Emirates Diabetes Society (EDES) consensus guidelines for the management of T2D recommend intensification of glucose-lowering therapy in patients who do not achieve adequate glycaemic control on metformin alone [19]. The choice of additional therapy should consider efficacy, risk of hypoglycaemia, impact on weight, and cost [19]. Dipeptidyl peptidase 4 inhibitors (DPP4i) and sulfonylureas are among the add-on therapy classes indicated for a broad population of patients with inadequate glycaemic control on metformin alone [19]. The two medication classes differ in their efficacy and safety profiles—while sulfonylureas provide somewhat improved glycaemic control compared with DPP4i, especially in the short term, they are associated with a greater risk of hypoglycaemia and weight gain [20, 21]. However, weight gain is less of an issue with gliclazide MR compared with older-generation sulfonylureas [22]. Recent real-world studies from the UAE and Saudi Arabia reported that biguanides combined with DPP4i or sulfonylureas were the most commonly prescribed dual therapies for T2D [23, 24].

While the number of individuals with undiagnosed diabetes in the UAE has been estimated at 634,200 and the country has the lowest diabetes diagnosis rate in the peninsula (36%) [12], the rate of glycaemic control in patients who are diagnosed is 31–41%, and is amongst the highest in the region [25, 26]. Nevertheless, crossing data data from International Diabetes Federation atlas [12] and IQVIA MIDAS® pharmaceutical sales [27], we were able to estimate that only approximately 46% of patients with T2D and inadequate glycaemic control receive the indicated second-line treatments, translating to approximately 139,000 patients in the UAE having uncontrolled T2D due to poor optimisation of treatment, which calls for increased efforts to optimise glycaemic management in these patients.

The aim of this study was to assess the cost-consequences of therapy intensification with the sulfonylurea gliclazide modified release (MR) (Diamicron®, Servier Laboratories) in UAE patients with inadequately controlled T2D (HbA1c ≥ 7%). The specific objectives of the study were twofold: (1) assess the cost-consequences of intensifying glucose-lowering therapy by adding gliclazide MR to metformin, (2) assess the cost-consequences of switching to gliclazide MR the patients who receive sitagliptin, a DDP4i, in addition to metformin for therapy intensification.

Methods

We developed a cost-consequences decision-tree model comparing two second-line treatments used as an add-on to metformin. The model estimated health outcomes and costs associated with the two modelled therapies at 1-, 3-, and 5-year time horizons. The base case analysis applied the payer perspective accounting for direct medical costs only, including treatment costs and costs related to cardiovascular events, including myocardial infarction (MI) and hospitalisation for heart failure (HHF). An additional analysis applied the patient perspective to estimate out-of-pocket costs.

This article is based on previously conducted studies and does not contain any new studies with human participants or animals performed by any of the authors.

Model Overview

The population entering the model intended to reflect the target population of gliclazide MR as previously studied in the real-world setting by Zaccardi et al. [28] i.e. adult patients with T2D with inadequate glycaemic control, i.e. HbA1c ≥ 7.0% (53 mmol/mol). Two sub-populations were considered: (1) patients on metformin monotherapy who initiated add-on therapy with gliclazide MR and (2) patients treated with a combination of sitagliptin and metformin who were switched to gliclazide MR and metformin.

The intervention of interest was gliclazide MR used in second line as an add-on therapy to metformin. Comparators were metformin alone (first study objective) and sitagliptin used in second line as an add-on to metformin (second study objective). Sitagliptin was selected as the comparator in the model as a result of DPP4i being recommended in the same place as sulfonylureas within the treatment pathway described in the EDES consensus guidelines [19], and the fact that metformin is commonly combined with a DPP4i in local clinical practice [24].

The structure of the model is presented in Fig. 1. The model was designed as a decision tree with two add-on treatment branches, each stratifying the population into three categories defined on the basis of HbA1c level after 1 year of add-on treatment. The model also included a simplified branch for patients not receiving add-on treatment, in which patients were conservatively assumed to remain at their baseline HbA1c level. The HbA1c categories were HbA1c ≤ 6.5%, 6.5% < HbA1c < 7%, and HbA1c ≥ 7%, based on the endpoints used by Zaccardi et al. and World Health Organization (WHO) recommendations for the use of HbA1c in the diagnosis of T2D [28, 29]. For patients still above the target HbA1c threshold of 7% after 1 year of add-on treatment, the risk of adverse clinical outcomes was estimated considering the proportion of patients who had a ≥ 1% reduction in HbA1c. Each HbA1c category was associated with a risk of MI and HHF. Optionally, each treatment arm could be associated with a risk of severe hypoglycaemic episodes (sHE). Each event was associated with 1-month case fatality rate to estimate the number of related deaths.

Model structure. HbA1c glycated haemoglobin, sHE severe hypoglycaemic event, MI myocardial infarction, MR modified release, HHF hospitalisation for heart failure

Clinical outcomes were estimated for each intervention arm and included the number of patients reaching the HbA1c < 7% and ≤ 6.5% targets, number of MI, HHF, sHE, and related deaths, costs (drug acquisition, event costs, and total costs), number of hospital bed days, and number of visits (inpatient, emergency room [ER], and outpatient). Incremental results were computed for gliclazide MR as an add-on to metformin vs. metformin alone and for gliclazide MR as an add-on to metformin vs. sitagliptin as an add-on to metformin.

Population Inputs

Population sizes were estimated separately for the analyses addressing the two study objectives. Details of the calculations estimating the sizes of both populations are available in Supplementary Methods S1. An additional analysis was conducted on a hypothetical cohort of 10,000 patients who would be switched from sitagliptin to gliclazide, to provide results that are easy to interpret and to transpose to other settings.

The UAE has a unique population structure, with Emiratis comprising only around 12% of the total, while the majority are expatriate workers. The gender distribution is skewed, with men making up approximately 64% of the population [30]. Notably, men are at higher risk of T2D and cardiovascular complications [12, 31]. The gender distribution in the model was therefore adjusted to better reflect UAE population demographics. Calculations are presented in Supplementary Methods S2.

Population Distribution and Clinical Effectiveness

Distribution of patients across the different HbA1c categories after 1 year of add-on treatment were retrieved from the study by Zaccardi et al., a retrospective analysis of the UK Clinical Practice Research Datalink aiming to compare the effectiveness and safety of gliclazide MR to sitagliptin as a second-line add-on treatment to metformin in patients with T2D [28]. This study used high-dimensional propensity score matching to facilitate comparisons between sitagliptin and gliclazide MR. Following matching, the populations treated with both medications were closely aligned in terms of demographics, baseline HbA1c, socioeconomic deprivation, comorbidities and T2DM complications (e.g. neuropathy, nephropathy, retinopathy), concomitant therapies, risk factors for cardiometabolic disease (smoking and alcohol consumption), and key laboratory parameters [28]. The model inputs sourced from the Zaccardi et al. study were based on this matched population. For sitagliptin, data were extracted from the Kaplan–Meier curves for each endpoint of the study (HbA1c ≤ 6.5%, HbA1c < 7%, HbA1c reduction ≥ 1%). For gliclazide MR, hazard ratios (HRs) were applied to sitagliptin data for each of the three endpoints.

To estimate the proportion of patients who did not reach the HbA1c < 7% target but who had a HbA1c reduction ≥ 1%, we conservatively assumed that all patients who reached the HbA1c < 7% clinical target had a HbA1c reduction ≥ 1% and subtracted the percentage of patients with HbA1c < 7% from the percentage of patients with ≥ 1% reduction in HbA1c. A scenario analysis was conducted with a less conservative assumption where patients who had a reduction ≥ 1% were considered to be equally distributed across the different HbA1c categories.

Clinical Data

For each HbA1c category, the risks of MI and HHF were estimated at midpoint HbA1c value, assuming it is representative of the entire category. The baseline HbA1c level of 8.6%, used to estimate the event risks in patients with HbA1c ≥ 7%, was obtained by averaging baseline HbA1c of both treatment arms in the study by Zaccardi et al. [28].

Risks of MI for women and men are available in Table 1 and were estimated for each HbA1c category on the basis of second-order polynomial functions fitted to data from a large UK-based prospective study by de Jong et al. [32]. Further details of the calculations are available in Supplementary Methods S3.

The risk of HHF was estimated for each HbA1c category on the basis of second-order polynomial functions fitted to data from the meta-analysis by Erqou et al. [33]. The estimated risks for each HbA1c category are presented in Table 1 and further details of the calculations are available in Supplementary Methods S3.

Risk of sHE was considered only in scenario analyses. The rates are presented in Table 1.

The model estimated the number of MI and HF-related deaths in each treatment arm. One-month case fatality rates were retrieved from the PEACE MENA registry and applied for both types of events (3.5% due to MI and 7.1% due to HF, Table 1) [34].

Overview of Cost Inputs

Unit costs were estimated in USD using January 2014–March 2023 data from the Dubai Real World Claims Database (DRWCD), which is the largest claims database of private insurers in the Emirate of Dubai capturing nearly all patients covered by private insurance, which corresponds to 89% of the Dubai population. The database contains information pertaining to patients’ demographics, diagnoses, medical, surgical, and diagnostic procedures, prescriptions, and other related services. Cost data are presented in Table 1, while more details on the database analysis are provided in the Supplementary Methods S4.

The DRWCD analysis followed all local legal and regulatory guidelines. Institutional review board approval or informed consent forms were not required for the database analysis, as it utilised anonymised patient level data. The permissions to access and to use the data from the DRWCD were obtained from IQVIA by the funder of this study (Servier).

Drug Acquisition Costs

Average 1-year treatment acquisition costs were estimated for patients with T2D treated with gliclazide MR or sitagliptin as an add-on to metformin on the basis of real-world data from the IQVIA MIDAS® database [27] and the DRWCD database (Supplementary Methods S4). The costs were estimated for branded drugs only. It should also be noted that no generic sitagliptin was sold in the UAE in past 2 years [27]. A scenario analysis was conducted comparing branded gliclazide MR to generic sitagliptin using costs estimated in the DRWCD.

Event Costs

One-year costs of events were included in the model; these comprised acute costs associated with the event and follow-up costs up to 1 year. Acute costs were the hospitalisation costs estimated for each type of event as the average inpatient costs of the index claim in the DRWCD database. The index claim was defined as the first claim in the studied period with a primary diagnosis of MI, HF, or sHE that was associated with inpatient costs. Follow-up costs included all inpatient, outpatient, and ER costs with a primary or secondary diagnosis of MI, HF, or sHE occurring in the year following the event of interest (i.e. index claim). Outpatient costs included pharmacy costs except for the costs of gliclazide MR, sitagliptin, and metformin, which were accounted for separately.

The DRWCD database did not allow one to estimate the costs of palliative care associated with fatal events, and no other UAE data could be identified. Therefore, as a conservative assumption, the costs of event-related deaths were not included.

Healthcare Resource Use

Healthcare resource use was estimated from an analysis of the DRWCD database and included the average numbers of bed days per year for patients experiencing MI, HHF, or sHE and the number of inpatient, ER, and outpatient visits related to these events.

Sensitivity Analyses

One-way deterministic sensitivity analysis was conducted to assess the key drivers of incremental total costs and numbers of MI and HF events avoided. It involved running the model using the high and low values from a plausible range for each clinical or economic parameter. The plausible ranges (Table 1) were determined using the observed variability of model parameters (95% confidence interval [CI] or interquartile range [IQR]). When only standard error or standard deviation was available, 95% CIs were estimated using beta (for probability and percentages) or gamma (for costs) distributions. Parameter variability was sourced from the literature for clinical data and estimated from the DRWCD analysis for costs.

Scenario and Additional Analyses

Additional analyses including severe hypoglycaemic events were conducted for cohorts of 10,000 patients. For the analysis conducted on the population switching from sitagliptin to gliclazide MR, the risk of sHE was estimated using crude incidence rates from Zaccardi et al. for both add-on treatments (i.e. sitagliptin and gliclazide MR) [28]. It should, however, be noted that although Zaccardi et al. observed slightly more severe and non-severe episodes of hypoglycaemia in patients treated with gliclazide MR than sitagliptin, the events were overall rare and the difference was not statistically significant [28]. Therefore, using the numerical sHE incidences from Zaccardi et al. in the model can be considered conservative. For the analysis on the population of patients inadequately controlled on metformin alone and initiating gliclazide MR as an add-on therapy, the sHE rate for metformin was estimated from Zaccardi et al. and Bodmer et al. [28, 35]. As there was no statistically significant difference in the proportion of hypoglycaemia that was severe between patients treated with metformin alone (1.99%) and those treated with a sulfonylurea and metformin (2.09%), we used the inverse of the odds ratio (OR) for hypoglycaemia in patients treated with a sulfonylurea and metformin vs. those treated with metformin alone (OR 1/4.04 = 0.248). This OR was applied to the event rates observed in Zaccardi et al. for patients treated with gliclazide MR and metformin presented in Table 1.

Scenario 1 considered an alternative approach for estimating the risk of cardiovascular events. Patients who had a HbA1c reduction ≥ 1% were assumed to be equally distributed across the different HbA1c categories. This slightly reduced the risk of cardiovascular events in patients with HbA1c > 7% in both treatment groups (although this was more pronounced for gliclazide than sitagliptin), due to the average HbA1c in this category being lower as more patients achieved HbA1c reduction. The analysis was based on a hypothetical cohort of 10,000 patients switching from sitagliptin to gliclazide MR as an add-on to metformin.

Scenario 2 was conducted considering the annual average cost of generic, as opposed to branded, sitagliptin to estimate the cost-consequences of switching patients treated with a combination of generic sitagliptin and metformin to a combination of branded gliclazide MR with metformin.

We also applied the patient perspective in additional scenarios (scenario 3 conducted in the population initiating gliclazide MR and scenario 4 in the population switching their add-on) where out-of-pocket costs were estimated on the basis of expert opinion for patients with basic (60% of the cohort) and premium (40% of the cohort) insurance plans and copayment rates (i.e. 30% and 20%, respectively, for basic and premium plans). Results were estimated as out-of-pocket costs per patient-year for both types of insurance plan.

Results

Initiating Gliclazide MR in Patients Who Do Not Achieve Adequate Glycaemic Control on Metformin Monotherapy

If patients who do not achieve adequate glycaemic control on metformin alone initiated treatment with gliclazide MR, 48,941 patients could achieve glycaemic control (HbA1c < 7%), corresponding to a 38.8% reduction in the proportion of patients with uncontrolled T2D, and 21,723 could achieve HbA1c ≤ 6.5% (Table 2). Approximately 535 cardiovascular events (93 MI and 442 HHF) and 35 related deaths could be avoided each year (Table 2). Healthcare resource use would reduce, with 3447 bed days, 920 inpatient/ER visits, and 1399 outpatient visits avoided per year.

Add-on therapy with gliclazide MR would result in annual savings of USD 6.9 million in costs of cardiovascular events, including USD 3.8 million in acute hospitalisation costs (Table 2). Switching Add-On Treatment to Gliclazide MR in Patients Who Do Not Achieve Adequate Glycaemic Control on Sitagliptin and Metformin

If all patients with uncontrolled T2D currently treated with sitagliptin as an add-on to metformin would be switched to gliclazide MR and metformin, after 1 year of treatment 16,332 of these patients could achieve the HbA1c target of < 7% and 7249 the more stringent target of ≤ 6.5% (Table 3). Relative to sitagliptin, this represented 4234 additional patients achieving the HbA1c target of < 7%, of which 2448 would have HbA1c ≤ 6.5%. Extrapolating the model results to 5 years, more than 21,000 additional patients could achieve glycaemic control if switched from sitagliptin to gliclazide MR (Fig. 2).

Total cost savings associated with the switch to gliclazide MR were estimated at USD 5.11 million per year, including savings of USD 4.77 million in drug acquisition costs and of USD 330,837 in medical costs. Each cardiovascular event avoided was associated with savings of USD 198,639 and each related death avoided with savings of USD 3.1 million.

In a hypothetical cohort of 10,000 patients treated for 1 year, 2876 would reach the HbA1c < 7% clinical target when treated with sitagliptin and 3882 when treated with gliclazide MR. Therefore, when treated with gliclazide MR, 35% more patients reached the HbA1c < 7% clinical target. For a population of 10,000 patients, this would represent 1006 additional patients reducing their HbA1c below 7% every year (Table S1). In terms of cardiovascular events, 6.1 events (1.1 MIs and 5 HHF) and 0.4 deaths could be avoided each year (Table S2). Annual resource use savings were estimated at 39 bed days, 10 inpatient/ER visits, and 16 outpatient visits avoided (Table S2).

The switch from sitagliptin to gliclazide MR was projected to provide substantial costs savings in a hypothetical cohort of 10,000 patients (Table S3). Drug acquisition costs were estimated to be reduced by 42%, generating USD 1.13 million of savings per year. The savings in medical costs were estimated at USD 78,638 per year. Switching patients from sitagliptin to gliclazide MR could therefore generate annual savings of USD 1.21 million, reducing the costs of T2D management by 20%.

Deterministic Sensitivity Analysis

Tornado plots for the number of cardiovascular events avoided and total incremental costs are presented in Fig. S1. Baseline HbA1c was the most important determinant of the number of MI and HHF events avoided. The higher the baseline HbA1c, the more events were avoided. In the order of decreasing importance, subsequent drivers of the number of events were event rates, followed by HRs representing the effectiveness of gliclazide MR for reducing HbA1c at 1 year. Total incremental costs were impacted the most by the annual treatment costs of both therapies, followed by the cost related to HHF events.

Scenario and Additional Analysis

Additional Analysis: Severe Hypoglycaemic Episodes

In 10,000 patients treated with a combination of gliclazide MR and metformin, about 9 additional sHE could occur compared to metformin alone or a combination sitagliptin and metformin. This could represent 11 additional inpatient/ER visits and 45 additional bed days per year. Associated additional medical costs were estimated at a maximum of USD 34,910, including USD 30,833 in acute hospitalisation costs incurred by patients initiating gliclazide MR (Table S4).

Scenario 1: Alternative Distribution of Patients with a HbA1c Reduction ≥ 1%

In scenario 1, which assumed that patients with a HbA1c reduction ≥ 1% at 1 year are equally distributed between those who reached the HbA1c < 7% target and those who did not, the results were slightly more favourable for gliclazide MR compared to sitagliptin than in the base case. Using gliclazide MR resulted in fewer events (7.5 events avoided vs. 6.1 in the base case) and greater cost savings compared to sitagliptin than in base case (USD 53,461 saved in acute hospitalisation costs vs. USD 43,341 in the base case; USD 97,265 saved in medical costs vs. USD 78,638 in the base case) (Table 4).

A patient treated with sitagliptin and metformin who would change their add-on treatment to gliclazide MR (scenario 4) could save around USD 42.6 per year (respectively USD 49.2 and USD 32.8 for basic and premium plans).

Discussion

Initiating gliclazide MR in patients who are currently not receiving recommended second-line treatment could substantially reduce the burden of T2D in the UAE, avoiding a significant number of cardiovascular events and saving lives. With a cost-effectiveness ratio of USD 189,415 per death avoided, our results suggest that this strategy could be cost-effective, corresponding to approximately 3.5 times the 2024 nominal gross domestic product (GDP) per capita (USD 53,916) for each death avoided [36]. For a 45-year-old patient, about 28 years of life expectancy could be lost in the event of diabetes-related death [23, 24, 37]. This would lead to an incremental cost-effectiveness ratio (ICER) of USD 6765 per life year gained (equivalent to disability-adjusted life years [DALYs] due to premature death averted). Using the approach promoted by the WHO’s Choosing Interventions that are Cost-Effective (WHO-CHOICE) project [38], add-on therapy with gliclazide MR would be considered highly cost-effective with an ICER of less than onefold the GDP per capita per DALY avoided. Using a more conservative cost-effectiveness threshold estimated for the UAE by Pichon-Riviere et al. (USD 13,447 per life year gained) [39], the addition of gliclazide MR to metformin would still be considered cost-effective.

Assuming a utility index of 0.7, as estimated for patients with T2D in Iran [40], 19.6 quality-adjusted life years (QALYs) are lost per T2D-related death. Considering a cost of USD 189,415 per death avoided, the incremental cost utility ratio can be calculated as USD 9664/QALY gained. This would be considered cost-effective at the cost-effectiveness threshold of USD 15,889/QALY estimated for the UAE by Pichon-Riviere et al. [39]. It should be noted that this is a very conservative estimate of the ICER, as it does not capture the reduced quality of life associated with uncontrolled T2D and related complications.

When compared with sitagliptin as an add-on therapy to metformin, gliclazide MR was more effective in reducing HbA1c, with 35% more patients reaching the HbA1c < 7% target and 51% more patients controlling their T2D well with a HbA1c ≤ 6.5%. Patients treated with gliclazide MR experienced fewer MIs and HHFs, generating substantial savings in healthcare costs and resources used.

The larger proportion of patients achieving HbA1c targets with gliclazide MR compared with DPP4i is consistent with previously published studies. In a head-to-head trial comparing gliclazide and vildagliptin as add-on treatment to metformin, gliclazide resulted in a significantly larger proportion of patients achieving the HbA1c ≤ 6.5% target (21.1% vs. 15.4%) [41]. The numerical trend was similar for the < 7.0% target, with a slightly higher proportion of patients in the gliclazide arm achieving the target (31.9% vs. 29.6%), although the difference was not statistically significant [41]. Importantly, real-world studies reporting on safety suggest that gliclazide and sitagliptin are associated with a similar incidence of symptomatic hypoglycaemia, even in the setting of fasting during Ramadan [42, 43], which increases the risk of hypoglycaemic events [44]. Nevertheless, when considering a significant difference in the occurrence of sHE between gliclazide MR and its comparators, we showed that the impact on the disease burden was relatively limited, with about nine additional events and less than USD 35,000 of additional costs.

A previously published analysis also supports our conclusion regarding the cost-saving nature of gliclazide treatment compared with sitagliptin. Ethgen and Beillat analysed the cost-consequences of using gliclazide MR versus sitagliptin as an add-on to metformin for patients with T2D in the UK and found gliclazide MR to provide savings in drug acquisition costs of €11,173 (approximately USD 12,085 [45]) per additional patient reaching the HbA1c target of < 7% [46]. In the UAE setting, we estimated total savings at USD 1206. For patients initiating gliclazide MR, additional costs would represent USD 134 per patient reaching the HbA1c < 7% target.

Our results contradict those of Lorenzoni et al. who conducted a cost-consequences analysis comparing sulfonylureas with sitagliptin as an add-on to metformin in patients with T2D in Italy [47]. The study considered sulfonylureas collectively as a class without stratifying the results by individual drug and concluded that the additional drug acquisition costs of sitagliptin could be offset by savings associated with lower need for self-monitoring, reduced incidence of hypoglycaemic events and major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE), and fewer patients switching to insulin-based regimens [47]. However, the findings of Lorenzoni et al. are unlikely to reflect the specific comparison between gliclazide MR and sitagliptin well, as the key clinical inputs of that model were informed by studies on other sulfonylureas. Specifically, the risk of MACE was retrieved from a meta-analysis of clinical trials comparing sulfonylureas with placebo or other antidiabetic therapies, including DPP4i [48]. However, although this meta-analysis found sulfonylureas to be associated with a higher risk of MACE compared with DPP4i, detailed examination revealed that these results appeared driven by the trials assessing glimepiride [48]. With regards to the risk of hypoglycaemic events, Lorenzoni et al. sourced this from a clinical trial comparing sitagliptin to glimepiride [49]. However, meta-analyses of studies assessing gliclazide as a monotherapy or add-on therapy to metformin or insulin demonstrated that gliclazide was associated with an incidence of hypoglycaemia that was lower than with other sulfonylureas and similar to other insulinotropic agents, including DPP4i and glinides [50, 51]. A consensus statement from a group of experts from the Gulf Cooperation Council (GCC) countries stated that newer-generation sulfonylureas, including gliclazide MR, are cost-effective, providing efficient glycaemic control, cardiovascular and renal safety, and are the preferred sulfonylureas for use during Ramadan [52].

Our analysis has several strengths. The modelled population was based on real-world data local to the UAE. Comparative effectiveness data for gliclazide MR vs. sitagliptin was sourced from a real-world study that utilised propensity score matching to enhance comparability between treatment arms [28], increasing the generalisability of the results to everyday clinical practice and ensuring that model inputs were sourced from a balanced population. Cost inputs were sourced from the largest database available in the UAE, enhancing the external validity of the study. The link between HbA1c level and the incidence of cardiovascular events is well grounded in real-world data [53] and modelling this relationship allowed us to explore the effectiveness of glucose-lowering treatments not only in managing glycaemia but also potentially reducing the risk of cardiovascular complications associated with T2D. The analysis used several highly conservative assumptions, for example by considering baseline HbA1c of 8.6% from Zaccardi et al. [28] as opposed to HbA1c of 9.4% observed in the DRWCD for patients not meeting the HbA1c < 7% threshold, and by applying mortality data from the local PACE MENA registry [34], which are lower than those reported in Western Europe [54, 55].

Our study is also subject to several limitations. Although the model assumptions were validated by UAE clinical experts, UK real-world data used to model effectiveness of treatment may not fully reflect the local setting as a result of differences in healthcare systems, patient profiles, and treatment protocols. The DRWCD may not be fully representative of the UAE population as it only provides data for privately insured individuals without including the public sector and the out-of-pocket costs for patients. Furthermore, cost inputs obtained from this database may be underestimated, because 10 years of data (2014–2023) were used and inflation was not considered. The simple structure of the model did not allow disease progression and its impact on the prevalence of cardiovascular events to be explicitly modelled. Many of the complications related to T2D, such as retinopathy and kidney disease, were not considered in this study. Additionally, the model assumed that the prevalence of T2D remained stable over the time horizon and did not consider the increasing trends and alarming projections of T2D prevalence in the UAE [12]. Our study can therefore be considered to provide conservative estimates of the burden of T2D in the UAE and of the potential benefits of gliclazide MR.

Conclusion

Initiating gliclazide MR as add-on treatment to metformin could help to reduce the clinical and economic burden of poorly controlled T2D among patients in the UAE. Gliclazide MR was estimated to provide improved glycaemic control compared with sitagliptin, which could translate to improved prevention of cardiovascular events, as well as savings in healthcare resources and costs. In addition to the direct benefits to patients, this could improve the allocation of limited healthcare resources and budget.
