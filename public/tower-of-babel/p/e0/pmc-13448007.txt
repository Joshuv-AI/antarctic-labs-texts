Perioperative management of direct oral anticoagulant-treated patients who need urgent surgery
Authors: Stephanie Carlin, Alejandro Godoy, James Douketis
Published: 2026

With the expanding use of direct oral anticoagulants (DOACs), it is estimated that 1 to 2 million DOAC-treated patients per year will require perioperative management for surgery or invasive procedures worldwide [1], of which about 8% will need urgent surgery [2]. Although large prospective studies have informed perioperative DOAC management for patients who require an elective surgery or procedure [3], evidence guiding management for urgent surgery is limited. In particular, the role of preoperative hemostatic and reversal therapies, including prothrombin complex concentrates (PCCs), idarucizumab, and andexanet alfa, have been evaluated mainly in observational studies without comparator groups of no hemostatic therapy or an alternative hemostatic therapy. Consequently, clinical practice guidelines provide weak recommendations with resultant variability in practice [4,5].

In this issue of Research and Practice in Thrombosis and Haemostasis, the HaemSTAR Collaborators [6] report the results of a multicenter retrospective cohort study evaluating outcomes in DOAC-treated patients receiving hemostatic and/or reversal agents prior to urgent surgery. They reviewed 198 patients from 38 hospitals in the UK and assessed time from reversal agent administration to procedure, adherence to recommended dosing, perioperative bleeding outcomes, thromboembolic complications, and mortality. Nearly half (48%) of patients studied had procedures categorized as high-bleed-risk, and 22% had prior arterial thrombosis. Apixaban was the most frequent DOAC used (54%), followed by rivaroxaban (24%), edoxaban (13%), and dabigatran (9%); reduced-dose regimens were used in 29% of patients. Preoperative DOAC levels were measured in 13% of cases, with a median level of 108 ng/mL (IQR, 61.5-192 ng/mL), which is expectedly high given the typically short interval between a patient’s last DOAC dose and their development of an urgent surgical situation.

In terms of patient management, the median interval from hemostatic or reversal agent administration to surgery was 1.6 hours, and 76% of patients had their procedure within 24 hours of treatment. Four-factor PCC (4F-PCC) was used in 87% of patients, with a median dose of 2010 units, whereas idarucizumab and andexanet alfa were given in 8% and 5% of patients, respectively. Tranexamic acid was coadministered in 20% of cases. Transfusion of ≥2 units red blood cells was more frequent in patients having high-bleed-risk than low/moderate-bleed-risk surgery (12.6% vs 1.6%). Mortality was 24% and thromboembolic events occurred in 3% of patients, all of whom had received 4F-PCC.

These findings are consistent with previous observational data evaluating perioperative hemostatic strategies in anticoagulated patients requiring urgent surgery. In a multicenter prospective cohort study conducted between 2013 and 2015, 478 DOAC-treated patients having urgent surgery were evaluated, of whom 67% underwent high-bleed-risk procedures and 16% received nonspecific hemostatic agents [7]. Importantly, excessive bleeding occurred in 13% of patients regardless of whether a hemostatic agent was administered. The similar bleeding rates observed between treated and untreated patients highlight the uncertainty regarding the clinical benefit of preoperative reversal or hemostatic therapy in this setting. Although confounding by patient characteristics, surgery type, and DOAC dose may limit data interpretation and conclusions, these findings raise the possibility that current reversal practices may not meaningfully reduce perioperative bleeding. Additionally, 90-day mortality was substantially higher among patients who received hemostatic therapy compared with those who did not (17% vs 4%), although this difference may reflect greater baseline illness severity, bleeding risk, and procedural urgency among patients who received hemostatic or reversal therapies.

Additional smaller studies reported similar findings. In a prospective cohort study of 20 patients receiving apixaban or rivaroxaban who had urgent surgery after PCC administration, moderately or severely abnormal hemostasis was observed in 20% of patients, although half of these cases were attributed to local or technical procedural factors rather than systemic coagulopathy [8]. Similarly, retrospective cohort studies evaluating PCC administration in patients receiving oral factor Xa (FXa) inhibitors prior to urgent surgery found abnormal hemostasis rates of 5% to 14%, thromboembolic event rates of 0% to 5%, and mortality rates approaching 30% [[9], [10], [11]]. Collectively, these studies highlight the substantial morbidity and mortality associated with this patient population, while underscoring the limited evidence supporting current perioperative DOAC management practices.

The study by the HaemSTAR Collaborators represents the largest multicenter cohort study to date, evaluating patient outcomes associated with hemostatic and reversal therapies prior to urgent procedures in oral FXa inhibitor-treated patients. The inclusion of patients undergoing a broad range of procedures with varying DOAC regimens across multiple centers enhances the generalizability of the findings to clinical practice. However, the noncomparative design precludes conclusions with respect to whether hemostatic therapies improved outcomes compared with supportive care alone. Additionally, the predominance of 4F-PCC use limits inferences regarding the efficacy and safety of DOAC-specific reversal agents idarucizumab and andexanet alfa, although as noted by the authors, andexanet alfa has been withdrawn from the United States market, and its ongoing availability in other jurisdictions is uncertain.

Another unresolved issue is the role of measuring DOAC levels before urgent procedures to inform the need for hemostatic or reversal therapy. In the study by the HaemSTAR Collaborators, almost all patients who had a DOAC level measured had a level ≥50 ng/mL, which is expected in an urgent surgical setting where there is limited DOAC interruption; this level also represents a threshold that may be considered clinically important [12]. In the Perioperative Anticoagulant Use for Surgery Evaluation (PAUSE) study, which measured DOAC levels before an elective surgery or procedure, a residual DOAC level ≥50 ng/mL occurred in ∼9% of patients after a 1-day (∼36 hour) DOAC interruption interval [13]. However, in the PAUSE and other studies of perioperative DOAC management, there was no apparent association between preoperative DOAC levels in the 30 to 100 ng/mL range and perioperative transfusion requirements or bleeding [[14], [15], [16]], thereby suggesting that residual DOAC activity alone may not reliably identify patients who would benefit from hemostatic or reversal agents.

Further study is needed to determine not only whether DOAC reversal minimizes bleeding and other complications as compared with more conservative management around urgent surgery (eg, supportive care and tranexamic acid), but also whether DOAC reversal that is driven by residual DOAC levels (eg, ≥50 ng/mL) compared with a non-DOAC level-based approach improves clinical, transfusion-related, and healthcare utilization outcomes. The need for such research is heightened by the emergence of novel DOAC reversal strategies that are in clinical development [17]. Moreover, cost consideration of newer and existing DOAC reversal strategies requires further scrutiny.

In the meantime, we suggest that clinicians continue to individualize management in DOAC-treated patients who need urgent surgery, which includes deciding on the putative benefit of DOAC reversal with PCC or DOAC-specific agents. In centers without the capacity to rapidly measure DOAC levels in such patients, factors that include time since the last DOAC dose, surgery type, and urgency of the surgery may inform the need for DOAC reversal. In centers where DOAC level testing is more readily available, measuring a DOAC level may guide management, since a level <50 ng/mL may obviate the need for reversal. A DOAC level in the 50 to 150 ng/mL range, which is more likely in an urgent surgery setting, may warrant DOAC reversal, especially in high-bleed-risk or closed space (eg, cardiac, intracranial) surgery.

We hope the work by the HaemSTAR Collaborators and others will serve as a clarion call for more research in anticoagulated patients who need urgent surgery. The need is urgent.
