Bad Medicine Is What I Need?
Authors: Brian P Quaranta
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis narrative suggests that a proper recognition of “bad medicine” may need to take into consideration the needs of the patient.

Your love is like bad medicine

Bad medicine is what I need!

Bon Jovi, “Bad Medicine,” 1987

I was trained to avoid “bad medicine.” This is medicine that deviates from accepted practice, that misses the point, that fails to help the patient. To this day, I fear it. And yet, whenever I hear the phrase, I can’t help but also recall the above words from the pen of Jon Bon Jovi, the immortal bard of Sayreville, New Jersey, whose singing chiseled itself into my neural networks over hundreds of bus rides to high school.

Bon Jovi, of course, was not trying to offer commentary on medical practice, but the meaning of poetry often exceeds authorial intent. Bad medicine is what I need. Is there any sense in which this can be true? I offer 2 contrasting cases which I believe suggest an answer.

Case 1

“I don’t want to talk about prostate cancer, I want to talk about art!”

That was how I greeted my new patient, a fine art professor, who presented with a low-risk prostate cancer carrying an excellent prognosis. As I usually do, I planned to spend a few minutes getting to know him. The approach seemed straightforward; I really did want to talk about art, a genuine interest of mine.

But it didn’t work. He replied to my greeting with only a brief and awkward smile. I ignored this signal and pressed on. “I understand you’re an art professor.”

“Yes.”

“That is so interesting! What medium do you work in?”

He responded with brief muttering and then tight-lipped silence. And he continued to react in that manner as I stubbornly peppered him with more questions:

“Who are your influences?”

“Which is your favorite era?”

The man would not engage. And yet, I kept going, unwilling to be diverted. Finally, he blurted out, “Can we please get to what I came here to talk about? My son will be out of school soon, and I need to be there.”

I apologized, but my true emotions were plain. I was insulted and angry. We carried on with the substance of the consult, and I presented his treatment options in detail; he then went home and, unsurprisingly, I never saw him again.

This interaction continued to irk me for days. Couldn’t he see that I was trying to meaningfully connect with him? To be the doctor that patients say that they want? To practice good medicine! I had offered not only my knowledge, but myself, and he had rejected my overtures. My anger felt righteous. But as time passed, the hurt eventually transformed into guilt, as it became clear that the problem had been with me.

I’d heard his words, but hadn’t really processed them, and therefore I missed a key double entendre. When he complained about needing to leave soon, the real issue wasn’t time; the appointment was on schedule. His true concern was that he needed to be there for his son. Had I been focused on his needs, I would have heard him telling me that his child was still in school, and he needed to live long enough to see him into adulthood. For him, this conversation was about life and death, and he had no interest in casual chatter about art history.

My ears failed to hear; but why? It is humbling to recall my opening line: “I don’t want to talk about prostate cancer, I want to talk about art!”

I had let the focus settle on myself, and that’s why I failed to hear him. On the surface, it seemed I had done everything according to protocol; taken my time, attempted to relate on a human level, and given clear, full, and medically correct advice. But the patient did not get what he needed. Any medical care not directly focused on the needs of the patient is, by definition, genuinely bad medicine.

Case 2

Several years later, a patient came to my office with an incurable urothelial tumor that had developed in a previously irradiated field. He was now 62 years old, and in his 20s had received radiation therapy as treatment for testicular cancer. He had suffered severe complications, including multiple surgeries for small bowel obstruction. Now, decades later, he had developed this second, likely radiation-induced, cancer in his ureter. Surgical and systemic options had been exhausted, and the lesion had continued to grow.

The patient understood that no cure was available, but the cancer caused him to suffer desperately from pain. He had already seen a radiation oncologist at a different nearby practice, an experienced and wise clinician, who had declined to offer treatment, on the basis that it was likely to do more harm than good. It was well reasoned advice.

And yet …

This patient would not accept inaction, and so he sought a second opinion in my office. When I also recommended against treatment, on the same basis, he said he didn’t care about the risk of side effects, and politely shrugged aside the suggestion of hospice care. Something had to be done! His pain was unbearable, and medication insufficient. He knew that the tumor would be fatal, and felt that the risk of any side effect, even a lethal one, was acceptable if it meant even a chance of some relief from his pain.

The patient and his wife pleaded to go forward. I listened and could not turn him away; I gave in and offered palliative radiation.

The result was worse than I had feared. Each treatment was a trial for him. Getting onto the table was an exhausting and painful chore. And then, after only a handful of sessions, without any benefit in pain control, he stopped coming in; his clinical condition was worsening, and he died shortly afterward of progressive disease.

I had made a mistake! Clearly, by current standards, this was an instance of bad medicine. My patient had been given ineffective therapy right up until the point of his death. I had not helped, and possibly caused harm.

The patient died in the autumn, and I was surprised, as I walked by the waiting room a few months later, to see his wife there, holding a Christmas basket.

We went into a room, and she handed me a card. She cried for a moment, and then told me how incredibly grateful she was, how grateful he had been, for the fact that we had treated him. No one else had really listened, she felt. Despite everything—how difficult the treatment was, and that it did nothing to relieve his pain—he had experienced peace at the end of his life because he’d been heard, and he was able to take comfort that he’d been actively resisting until the end of his days. I still have the card, years later, which concludes:

“Dr. Q, you are a top-notch healer, and I appreciate your compassion and your willingness to let him continue to fight for as long as possible.”

So: was it really bad medicine after all?

If I’m right, in this case, “bad medicine” was, ultimately, what the patient needed. Of course, no patient should receive “bad medicine” if that means medicine that is unskillful, uncaring, or unjust. Nor should we ever practice quackery. But if love and focused attention on the needs of the patient guides us to a practice that sometimes steps outside of current algorithms, medicine that is difficult to justify with high-level evidence, but that a humane doctor believes might serve his patient, then that type of “bad medicine” may sometimes be what the patient truly needs.
