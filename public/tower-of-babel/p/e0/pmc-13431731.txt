A Porous Interfacial Photothermal Layer of Fused Core–Shell Carbon‐Polymer Nanospheres for Directional Salt Crystallization and Zero‐Liquid‐Discharge
Authors: Haokun Shen, Shudi Mao, Xin Stella Zhang, Yihan Shi, Ying Long, Casey Onggowarsito, An Feng, Yuqing Yang, Jiawang Song, Leonard Tijing, Jiangtao Xu, Qiang Fu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTInterfacial solar evaporation offers a sustainable route for freshwater production; however, effective zero‐liquid‐discharge (ZLD) operation under hypersaline conditions remains fundamentally challenging because high salinity suppresses water activity and vapor pressure while intensifying coupled heat, mass, and salt management. Herein, we report a hierarchical hydrogel evaporator featuring a thin, porous interfacial photothermal layer composed of fused core–shell carbon‐polymer nanospheres, fabricated via interfacial precipitation polymerization. The surface‐confined photothermal layer ensures effective thermal confinement and abundant liquid‐vapor interfaces, while the underlying hydrogel matrix sustains continuous water replenishment, delivering an exceptional evaporation rate of 6.2 kg m−2 h−1 under one‐sun irradiation without requiring bulky 3D architectures. During ZLD operation, direction‐dependent transport within the thin porous layer drives lateral advection of concentrated brine away from the active evaporation interface while suppressing through‐thickness salt accumulation, thereby enabling localized crystallization in the distal region. As a result, near‐complete spatial separation of water evaporation and salt crystallization is achieved from 20 wt.% brine. Outdoor demonstration further delivers 26.6 L m−2 day−1 of freshwater and 7.7 kg m−2 day−1 of recovered salt, underscoring the potential of this platform for practical ZLD desalination and mineral recovery.

Introduction

Solar‐driven evaporation from open‐water surfaces is a fundamental component of the natural hydrological cycle. Engineering this passive, energy‐lean phase‐change pathway offers opportunities for decentralized desalination for freshwater supply and brine concentration for mineral recovery. However, evaporation‐driven water‐salt separation remains limited by inherently low evaporation flux because elevated salinity suppresses water activity and vapor pressure, consistent with Raoult's law as a first‐order description. Conventional solar stills typically produce only 2–5 L m−2 day−1 of freshwater [1, 2]. Brine mining by solar evaporation is likewise hindered by low throughput, often demanding land footprints of up to 30 km2 and extraction cycles longer than 18 months [3]. Interfacial solar evaporation (ISE) has emerged as a sustainable evaporation strategy [3, 4] originally developed to enhance solar‐driven phase‐change efficiency by localizing thermal energy at the evaporating interface. Owing to its intrinsically high evaporation flux and engineered controllability, ISE also offers a compelling alternative to the slow and land‐intensive evaporation ponds traditionally used in brine mining [5, 6, 7, 8].

Under hypersaline conditions, high evaporation fluxes inevitably drive progressive brine concentration as continuous water loss enriches salts near the evaporation interface. The resulting salinity buildup suppresses evaporation kinetics and often induces salt crystallization within or on the evaporator, blocking water transport and vapor escape pathways [7, 9, 10], while the generation of hyper‐concentrated residual brine further imposes substantial economic and environmental burdens for downstream management [3]. To mitigate salt‐fouling, three main strategies have been explored in ISE, namely salt back‐diffusion [11, 12, 13, 14, 15], Janus structural design [16, 17, 18, 19, 20, 21, 22], and localized crystallization [9, 23]. While the former two primarily delay or redistribute salt accumulation through continuous brine replenishment, localized crystallization begins to enable spatial decoupling of evaporation and salt precipitation and therefore offers a more realistic route toward zero‐liquid‐discharge (ZLD). From a mass‐transport perspective, interfacial salt accumulation, salt redistribution, and localized crystallization are fundamentally governed by the competition between advective ion transport and diffusive relaxation, which can be quantified by the Péclet number (Pe) [24]. A representative study by Wang and coworkers demonstrated that suppressing advection to reduce the Péclet number below one can promote diffusion‐dominated selective crystallization [25].

Despite substantial progress in localized crystallization via Marangoni‐driven transport [26, 27, 28, 29, 30, 31], regulated ion diffusion [29, 32, 33, 34, 35, 36], parallel water‐supply architectures [37], and surface wettability engineering [16, 38], systematic structure‐guided regulation of salt transport within a unified advection–diffusion framework remains limited. Many reported localized‐crystallization systems still operate at modest evaporation rates, frequently rely on large brine reservoirs that mask solute accumulation, and commonly suffer from salt deposition on active evaporation surfaces under prolonged operation [39, 40, 41] (Table S1). Moreover, laboratory‐scale demonstrations approaching ZLD with finite‐volume brines remain scarce [37]. These limitations call for hypersaline ISE systems in which transport is deliberately programmed in an anisotropic manner: diffusion should be facilitated across the thin evaporation layer to suppress in‐pore crystallization, while advection should be amplified along the lateral direction to rapidly export concentrated brine away from the active evaporation surface toward a designated crystallization zone. Hydrogel evaporators featuring thin photothermal surface layers provide a promising structural basis for this purpose, yet their architectures have rarely been engineered to regulate anisotropic salt transport and spatially localized crystallization under hypersaline conditions.

Here, we report a novel hydrogel evaporator featuring a thin, porous photothermal film of fused core–shell carbon‐polymer nanospheres and a superhydrophilic hydrogel substrate that continuously replenishes water to the evaporation interface. This architecture confines solar‐thermal conversion to the evaporation interface, while the interconnected porous nanocomposite network provides abundant liquid‐vapor interfaces for rapid interfacial evaporation. More importantly, the structural design deliberately programs anisotropic salt transport by decoupling the Péclet number in orthogonal directions: a reduced cross‐plane transport length promotes diffusive relaxation across the thin evaporation layer to suppress in‐pore crystallization, whereas the Péclet number along the radial direction of the top layer and the hydrogel sidewall far exceeds unity, enabling advection‐dominated export of concentrated brine away from the active top interface toward the sidewall and further to the lower edge. In addition, for ZLD device design, we introduced a parallel water‐supply pathway that provides an additional Marangoni‐driven effect to promote localized crystallization. By coupling interface‐confined evaporation with advection‐directed and Marangoni‐assisted salt migration, this work establishes a design framework of photothermal materials and devices for interfacial desalination and mineral recovery toward genuine zero‐liquid‐discharge operation.

Results and Discussion

A Porous Layer of Fused Core–Shell Carbon‐Polymer Nanospheres via Interfacial Precipitation Polymerization

In our previous study, we developed a skin‐inspired hierarchical polymer architecture in which a nanoporous polymer layer was formed in situ on a hydrogel substrate via interfacial precipitation polymerization [42, 43, 44]. Building on this platform, we introduce carbon black (CB) into the interfacial polymerization system in the present study to generate a photothermal surface layer for interfacial solar evaporation (ISE). Specifically, a polyacrylamide (PAM)‐agar hydrogel was first prepared as the soft substrate and preloaded with ammonium persulfate (APS) initiator. The hydrogel was then immersed in a mixed precursor solution containing CB and monomer (2‐hydroxypropyl) methacrylate (HPMA). At the hydrogel surface, interfacial precipitation polymerization of HPMA produced precipitated PHPMA chains that encapsulated CB, forming core–shell carbon‐polymer nanospheres (denoted CB@PHPMA nanospheres). These nanospheres subsequently aggregated and fused, creating a thin, porous coating layer with interconnected pathways (Figure 1b–h and Note S1). This fusion was facilitated by the PHPMA shells on the nanospheres, which coalesced because of their mutual compatibility. Meanwhile, owing to the two‐way diffusion of initiator and monomer, precipitation polymerization also occurred within the near‐surface region of the hydrogel, enabling the growing PHPMA domains to interconnect with the hydrogel network. This interfacial integration firmly anchored the porous CB@PHPMA coating to the soft substrate, yielding a mechanically robust and flexible photothermal architecture. In this design, CB serves not only as the photothermal component but also as the structural core of the fused nanospheres forming the surface evaporation layer.

This process can be repeated with multiple cycles to the designated layer thickness. Through repeated coating cycles, a series of hierarchical hydrogel evaporators with a surface‐localized photothermal evaporation layer was constructed. The resulting hydrogel evaporators, consisting of a CB@PHPMA coating on a hydrogel substrate, were denoted as CBP × 1, CBP × 2, and CBP × 4, where “ × number” indicates the number of repeated coating cycles (1, 2, and 4, respectively). Additionally, a control hydrogel with homogeneously dispersed CB particles throughout the matrix (denoted as CBH) was prepared via a conventional radical gelation method. Fourier‐Transform infrared (FT‐IR) spectroscopy analysis (Figure S2) indicates distinct chemical signatures for the coating layer, showing a PHPMA‐associated C─O stretching band at 1 030 cm−1, whereas the blank PAM‐agar retains the typical primary‐amide N–H stretching doublet and agar polysaccharide fingerprint bands. The presence of CB is confirmed by the Raman spectrum in Figure S6a, which shows the characteristic defect‐rich D (∼1 350 cm−1) and G (∼1 580 cm−1) bands.

Scanning electron microscope (SEM) longitudinal cross‐sections (Figure 1c–e) reveal that increasing coating cycles progressively thickens the top photothermal layer from 14.38 to 112.32 µm, while also producing a more extended buffer region between the coating and the underlying hydrogel. These results demonstrate that the thickness and interfacial integration of the evaporation layer can be readily tuned by the coating cycle number. Meanwhile, the surface SEM images (Figure 1f–h) reveal distinct coverage states of the CB‐based coating. After a single coating cycle, the hydrogel surface exhibits relatively sparse coverage by fused core–shell CB@PHPMA nanospheres, consistent with its lighter appearance and suggesting incomplete photothermal coverage. In contrast, the two‐ and four‐cycle samples display much denser and nearly continuous surface coverage, in which fused core–shell CB@PHPMA nanospheres form an interconnected porous network through interparticle PHPMA bonding. The apparent surface pore‐size distribution of the CB@PHPMA evaporation layer was further estimated from surface SEM images using an ImageJ‐based method, as detailed in Note S2 and Figure S3. Such morphology is expected to enhance light harvesting while also providing interconnected pathways for brine spreading and enlarged effective evaporation interfaces.

Notably, the four‐cycle sample exhibits more extensive polymer encapsulation and stronger interparticle fusion of the surface CB@PHPMA spheres, which likely improves structural stability and corresponds to the formation of a thicker and more developed porous coating layer, as also evidenced in the cross‐sectional SEM images. Together, these tunable structural features establish the morphological basis for efficient thermal localization, continuous water supply, and spatially regulated salt transport during interfacial solar evaporation.

Thermal Confinement and Interfacial Wettability of the Photothermal Layer

Figure 2 illustrates how the engineered surface coating layer regulates thermal localization and interfacial wettability in the hierarchical hydrogel evaporator, underscoring the functional significance of the coating‐layer design.

CB serves as a low‐cost PTM with high solar‐thermal conversion efficiency. Upon one‐sun irradiation, CB rapidly heated up to 40.5°C and further increased to 62.7°C within 15 min of continuous illumination (Figure S4). Infrared (IR) thermography reveals distinctly different thermal responses depending on whether CB is surface‐localized or homogeneously distributed within the hydrogel evaporator. For CBP × 4 shown in Figure 2a, heating remains strictly confined to the thin surface layer, while both the bulk hydrogel and surrounding water remain at low temperature throughout irradiation, indicating effective and sustained interfacial heat confinement. In contrast, the CBH sample with homogeneously dispersed CB in Figure 2b rapidly loses thermal localization, with heat diffusing into the hydrogel interior within 2 min. This difference originates from the distinct CB distributions. In CBP × 4, surface‐confined CB localizes solar absorption and heat generation to a thin interfacial region, thereby maximizing heat utilization at the evaporation surface. By contrast, homogeneous CB distribution in the CBH induces volumetric heating throughout the hydrogel, promoting heat dissipation into the bulk hydrogel and underlying water, and potentially increasing the effective thermal conductivity of the composite network. Collectively, these results highlight the advantage of surface‐localized photothermal coatings in suppressing bulk heat loss and enhancing interfacial heat utilization over conventional bulk‐distributed photothermal systems.

Water contact angle measurements further reveal the wettability evolution across the hierarchical layers from bottom to top (Figure 2d–f). The AM–agar hydrogel substrate (bottom layer, Figure 2d) exhibits superhydrophilicity with a contact angle of 0°, whereas the buffer layer shows a higher contact angle of 65.76° (Figure 2e), indicating that the PHPMA‐containing interfacial layer is intrinsically less hydrophilic than the underlying hydrogel substrate due to its relatively hydrophobic nature. Notably, all coating layers (top layer, Figure 2f,g) display lower contact angles than the buffer layer, and their contact angles progressively decrease from 57.97° to 36.84° with increasing coating cycles. Importantly, all measured contact angles remain below 90°, indicating that all layers preserve hydrophilic character and therefore do not introduce a wettability barrier to water supply. This trend suggests that the apparent wettability of the coating layer is governed not only by surface chemistry, but also by its evolving porous morphology. As repeated coating produces a thicker and more interconnected porous surface layer, water can more readily infiltrate the surface pores and spread across the evaporation interface, thereby lowering the apparent contact angle. Consistent with this trend, the anti‐gravity water‐uptake response time shown in Figure 2g and Figure S5 progressively decreases with increasing coating cycles and shows a positive correlation with contact angle, indicating that samples with improved wettability can draw water upward more rapidly. In particular, the fastest response was achieved within 0.58 s, confirming the rapid wetting and water‐transport capability enabled by the increasingly developed porous coating layer. Such wettability and dynamic water‐uptake characteristics are favorable for rapid brine spreading, enhanced pore wetting, and enlarged liquid–vapor interfacial area during interfacial evaporation.

Together, surface‐localized heating, continuous water supply, and the favorable wettability of the porous coating layer enable rapid and sustained interfacial evaporation.

ISE Performance of the CBP Hydrogel Evaporators

As expected, the CBP hydrogel evaporator exhibits exceptional interfacial evaporation performance, as evaluated using a custom‐built ISE setup (Figure 3a). In this configuration, the hydrogel evaporator was supported by an expanded polyethylene (EPE) foam to provide thermal insulation, while continuous water supply to the bottom hydrogel was maintained via multiple tissue wicks. An additional thick EPE foam layer was placed beneath the container to further suppress parasitic heat transfer to the balance. Infrared thermography was employed to simultaneously monitor the temperature evolution of the evaporation surface and the underlying bulk water during one‐sun irradiation (Figure 3b,c). Upon illumination, the surface temperature of CBP × 4 increased rapidly within the first 20 min, reaching ∼35°C, and continued to rise to a steady value of 40.1°C. In contrast, the CBH control sample exhibited a significantly lower surface temperature of only 32.5°C, which was close to the bulk water temperature (∼30°C), indicative of pronounced heat dissipation into the hydrogel body and surrounding water. This comparison highlights the critical role of surface‐localized photothermal layers in suppressing thermal diffusion and maintaining a high interfacial temperature gradient.

This surface temperature trend closely correlates with the evaporation performance, as the evaporation rate increases systematically from CBP × 1 (4.92 kg m−2 h−1) to CBP × 4 (6.19 kg m−2 h−1) under one‐sun irradiation (Figure 3d,e), while the CBH evaporator gave a much lower rate of 4.48 kg m−2 h−1. Notably, CBP × 4 can maintain comparably high evaporation rates of 5.76 and 5.50 kg m−2 h−1 in lake water (hydraulically connected to the sea, brackish water) and seawater, respectively (Figure 3f), indicating strong tolerance to salinity and dissolved impurities. This performance exceeds that of most reported 2D hydrogel‐based evaporators and approaches the level typically achieved by 3D evaporators, while avoiding complex 3D architectures [7, 45]. These results demonstrate that the surface‐localized porous coating layer effectively integrates thermal confinement, abundant evaporation interfaces, and continuous water supply to sustain ultrafast interfacial evaporation.

Unlike conventional hydrogel evaporators, directly quantifying the activation state of water molecules in our multicomponent hierarchical architecture is nontrivial. In particular, the thin PHPMA‐rich buffer layer cannot be cleanly isolated from the underlying PAM‐agar hydrogel and is present only in limited mass in each sample, rendering independent thermal analysis impractical. Therefore, differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) and Raman analyses were conducted on the outer coating layer, which primarily governs interfacial evaporation, and on the underlying hydrogel substrate, representing the water reservoir. The equivalent evaporation enthalpies derived from DSC are summarized in Table S2. Notably, the coating layer exhibits a markedly reduced apparent evaporation enthalpy of 1 355 J g−1, substantially lower than that of bulk water (2 427 J g−1). This reduction suggests that the aggregated CB@PHPMA nanospheres create abundant confined interfacial microenvironments, which modify water–solid interactions and lower the apparent energetic barrier for interfacial evaporation [46]. The hydrogel substrate also shows a reduced equivalent enthalpy relative to bulk water, indicating modified water–polymer interactions within the hydrogel matrix, albeit to a lesser extent than at the evaporation interface.

Raman spectra of the coating layer are dominated by the characteristic D and G bands of CB, whereas water‐related O─H stretching features are weak and poorly resolved. This is attributed to the strong Raman background from CB as well as partial dehydration of the dark photothermal coating during measurement. In contrast, the hydrogel substrate exhibits clearly detectable O─H stretching from water (Figure S6b). Deconvolution of the O─H stretching region reveals bands at 3 233 and 3 401 cm−1 (light blue), associated with more strongly hydrogen‐bonded water, and components near 3 514 and 3 630 cm−1 (pink), corresponding to weakly bound intermediate water populations [47, 48]. Based on peak fitting, the ratio of the intermediate water to the sum of intermediate water and free water [IW:(IW+FW)] reaches 0.557, indicating a substantial fraction of non‐bulk‐like water within the hydrogel matrix.

Under an identical water‐supply configuration, the four‐layer coated hydrogel achieves the highest evaporation performance by combining efficient thermal confinement at the porous coating layer with favorable interfacial water states and continuous water replenishment from the hydrophilic substrate.

Péclet‐Number‐Guided Advection‐Dominated Salt Transport

While the above results demonstrate the exceptional evaporation performance of the hierarchical evaporator, effective operation under hypersaline conditions additionally requires that concentrated brine be continuously displaced away from the active evaporation interface. In our system, we hypothesize a directional salt‐transport mechanism in which concentrated brine is continuously displaced away from the active top interface. To elucidate the underlying salt‐transport behavior, we analyzed the system using the Péclet number as a guiding framework, which quantifies the competition between advective ion transport and diffusive relaxation.

To determine whether salt redistribution in the hierarchical evaporator is governed predominantly by advection or diffusion, the Péclet number was evaluated along the actual brine‐transport pathways. As shown in Figure 4, the top evaporation layer and the hydrogel sidewall are both hydraulically connected to the underlying hydrogel matrix through an interfacial transition region, yet they play distinct transport roles. The top surface served as the primary evaporation interface and thus experiences stronger evaporation‐driven brine flow, whereas the continuously wetted sidewall mainly acted as a route for subsequent downward migration of concentrated brine. On this basis, the transport process was resolved along three characteristic directions, namely radial transport within the top evaporation layer (Stop ), downward transport along the hydrogel sidewall (Swall ), and transverse transport across the thin evaporation‐layer thickness (Sn ).

(b) Directional Péclet‐number analysis, indicating advection‐dominated salt transport (Pe ≫1) along the top evaporation layer and hydrogel sidewall, but diffusion‐dominated transport (Pe < 1) across the thin thickness. (c) Simulated initial NaCl mass‐fraction distribution with uniform concentration throughout the evaporator. (d) Simulated NaCl mass‐fraction distribution during evaporation, showing overall salt enrichment near the lower edge of the hydrogel sidewall (left) and a zoomed‐in view highlighting the localized concentration buildup at the lower sidewall edge (right).

The Péclet number was defined as: (1) Pe=uLD where u is the characteristic liquid velocity, L is the transport length, and D is the diffusion coefficient of NaCl. Because the measured evaporation flux represents the net water loss, the apparent velocity of the residual brine at ∼27 wt.% NaCl was corrected by mass conservation from the 20 wt.% feed solution, giving u = 3.31 × 10−6 m s−1 (Note S3). Taking the NaCl diffusion coefficient in water as D = 1.61 × 10−9 m2 s−1, and defining the characteristic transport lengths as the radius of the top evaporation layer (R = 7.555 × 10−3 m), the height of the hydrogel sidewall (H = 7.0 × 10−3 m), and the thickness of the thin evaporation layer (n = 1.10 × 10−4 m), the corresponding directional Péclet numbers were estimated as: (2) PeStop=uRD=15.55 (3) PeSwall=uHD=14.4 (4) Pen=unD=0.23

It should be noted that this treatment uses a characteristic advective brine velocity as a first‐order superficial velocity scale and adopts the NaCl diffusion coefficient in free water, thus providing a conservative approximation. In the real rigid porous shell, the effective diffusion coefficient D is expected to be lower than that in bulk water because of confinement, tortuosity, and pore‐scale transport resistance. The estimated Péclet numbers therefore indicate pronounced directional anisotropy in salt transport: PeStop and PeSwall are well above 1, consistent with advection‐dominated salt transport along the top evaporation layer and hydrogel sidewall, whereas Pen < 1 suggests diffusion‐favored transport across the CB@PHPMA film. This anisotropy favors in‐plane brine displacement and downward salt migration while enabling rapid through‐thickness concentration equilibration, thereby helping mitigate excessive salt buildup at the active evaporation surface, consistent with the experimentally observed salt enrichment at the lower edge of the sidewall (Figure S7).

To further visualize the directional salt‐transport behavior predicted by the Péclet‐number analysis, computational fluid dynamics (CFD) simulations were performed to map the spatial distribution of NaCl concentration in the hierarchical evaporator during evaporation. As shown in Figure 4c, the initial salt concentration is nearly uniform throughout the structure. After evaporation (Figure 4d), the concentration field becomes markedly nonuniform, with pronounced salt enrichment near the lower edge of the hydrogel sidewall, where the local NaCl concentration exceeds 27 wt.%, while the active top evaporation interface remains comparatively less concentrated. This simulated concentration pattern agrees well with the experimentally observed edge‐localized salt accumulation and crystallization.

Together, the CFD results and Péclet‐number analysis support a directional salt‐transport mechanism in which concentrated brine is continuously displaced from the active top evaporation interface, redistributed radially within the top evaporation layer, then guided downward along the hydrogel sidewall, and finally enriched at the lower edge, where the local salt concentration reaches crystallization threshold for localized precipitation.

Windmill‐Inspired Marangoni‐Driven ZLD Architecture

The above results demonstrate that the hierarchical evaporator can effectively displace salt away from the active top interface and concentrate it at the lower edge of the hydrogel sidewall. Although such edge‐localized salt accumulation is beneficial for protecting the main evaporation surface, persistent enrichment at the same location may still impose local stress on the hydrogel and hinder continuous salt removal. To avoid hydrogel damage associated with repeated mechanical scraping and to enable continuous salt harvesting, four parallel feed‐brine channels were introduced using robust, wet‐stable kitchen towels (Figure 5a) [37]. These channels are directly connected to the feed brine and continuously replenish liquid to the brine‐enriched edge regions, thereby locally diluting the concentrated brine. To better accommodate the high evaporation rate of the hydrogel, the towel‐based water‐supply channels were designed with a windmill‐shaped geometry rather than a simple straight‐strip configuration. As shown in Figure S8, straight strip‐like channels can support initial capillary water replenishment, but salt crystals tend to accumulate broadly along the strip surface under high‐salinity evaporation, progressively covering the water‐supply pathway. In contrast, the windmill‐like design consists of asymmetric and gradually narrowed blade ends. These tapered terminal regions promote preferential brine enrichment and salt crystallization at the distal tips, while leaving the main channel regions relatively unobstructed for continuous capillary water transport. Therefore, the windmill‐like geometry spatially separates water replenishment from salt crystallization and converts non‐directional salt accumulation into localized terminal crystallization, enabling more stable water supply during prolonged brine evaporation. Additionally, the windmill‐shaped towel termini establish a surface‐tension gradient that induces Marangoni‐driven interfacial transport, guiding the concentrated brine away from the hydrogel edge toward the distal ends of the towels [26, 27, 28, 29, 30, 31]. As a result, salt deposition is displaced from the hydrogel sidewall, enabling sustained evaporation performance and more efficient salt collection.

To distinguish the contribution of the hierarchical evaporator from that of the windmill‐shaped towel configuration, a control experiment was conducted using a conventional CBH hydrogel under identical evaporation conditions. Although the same windmill‐shaped tissue was used, the CBH hydrogel developed visible cracks after approximately 1 h and became severely dried together with the towel after approximately 3 h. After 12 h, extensive salt deposition was observed across the hydrogel surface, accompanied by further cracking. These results demonstrate that the windmill‐shaped towel alone cannot sustain continuous brine replenishment or directional salt transport. Instead, stable ZLD operation requires the coupling of the hierarchical CB@PHPMA hydrogel evaporator with the windmill‐inspired water‐supply configuration (Figure S9).

A continuous ZLD experiment was conducted using 47 g of a 20 wt.% NaCl solution under one‐sun irradiation (Figure 5b). Time‐resolved optical images reveal that salt crystallization initiates at the tips of windmill arms and propagates directionally along these pathways. After approximately 29 h, extensive salt growth began to back‐propagate toward the hydrogel surface, leading to a noticeable decline in evaporation rate (Figure 5c). At this stage, 5.18 g wet salt was harvested (Figure S10), which immediately restored the evaporation performance. Complete depletion of the liquid phase was achieved within 47 h, confirming successful ZLD operation at the laboratory scale.

The collected salt was loosely attached and readily detachable, yielding a wet mass of 9.16 g and a dried mass of 8.3 g (Figures S10, S11). SEM image in Figure 5e shows well‐defined, discrete salt crystals with a non‐compacted morphology, enabling efficient harvesting without disturbing the original configuration. A mass‐balance analysis (Figure 5d) indicates that the initial solution contained 9.4 g of NaCl and 37.6 g of water. During operation, a total vapor mass of 36.7 g was recorded, while 8.3 g of dry NaCl was recovered together with residual water, corresponding to an overall salt recovery efficiency of 88.3%. The unrecovered fraction is attributed to residual moisture and partial salt back‐migration, likely caused by over‐drying during overnight operation.

Compared with conventional solar pond evaporation, the present system achieves a comparable salt recovery efficiency (∼88%) within a drastically shortened time frame, while maintaining similarly low energy input by relying solely on passive solar irradiation. Unlike many existing ZLD and mineral recovery technologies that rely on substantial thermal or electrical energy input [49, 50, 51, 52], this approach offers a favorable balance between recovery efficiency, energy consumption, and processing time. Enabled by advection‐dominated salt transport and Marangoni‐driven brine redistribution, the system demonstrates effective salt management and sustained ZLD operation at the laboratory scale. The markedly accelerated evaporation–crystallization kinetics and compact footprint underscore the potential of this strategy as a complementary or decentralized alternative to traditional evaporation‐based mineral recovery.

Outdoor ZLD Tests for Freshwater Production and Salt Recovery

Building on the successful implementation of the hierarchical hydrogel and windmill‐shaped water pathways for ZLD, we further assessed the system performance for brine desalination and salt recovery under practical operating conditions. To evaluate long‐term durability, a 10‐day cyclic test was conducted (Figure 6a). Throughout the test, the device maintained a stable evaporation rate of approximately 5.8 kg m−2 h−1 (Figure 6b), demonstrating reliable performance over repeated salt crystalizing and harvesting cycles. To further evaluate the practical applicability of the evaporator, a similar 7‐day cyclic test using threefold‐concentrated real seawater with a salinity of approximately ∼10.94 wt.%, which was conducted to simulate concentrated brine discharge from desalination processes. As shown in Figure S12, the ISE hydrogel maintained a stable evaporation rate of 4.09 kg m−2 h−1 over repeated cycles. Although the concentrated seawater possessed a more complex ionic composition than the single‐component NaCl solution, salt deposition remained highly reproducible and was mainly confined to the edges of the tissue water‐supply channels, while the primary water‐transport pathway remained unobstructed. Complete water evaporation and solid salt deposition were achieved over repeated cycles, confirming stable water‐salt separation and cyclic durability under realistic multi‐ion brine conditions.

The stable evaporation performance observed during the cyclic tests can be attributed to the continuous internal water‐transport pathway within the hierarchical evaporator. The PAM‐agar hydrogel substrate, PHPMA‐containing interfacial layer, and CB‐containing photothermal layer constitute an integrated serial water‐transport pathway. Under steady‐state evaporation, the net water flow through these layers must remain continuous according to mass conservation, although each layer may contribute a different hydraulic resistance and capillary‐pressure drop. Consequently, the key determinant of performance is the overall water‐supply capacity of the intact hierarchical evaporator rather than the individual wicking rate of each component layer. The stable, nearly linear mass‐loss curves during long‐term evaporation indicate that water replenishment from the PAM‐agar substrate can match the evaporation demand. A schematic illustration of the resistance‐in‐series model and serial water‐transport pathway is provided in Figure S13.

Following the durability assessment, outdoor ZLD experiments were performed using 20 wt.% NaCl solution to further examine practical viability. Two configurations were tested: one closed, self‐condensing ZLD setup using a sealed glass jar to simulate simultaneous freshwater and salt production, and an open, pond‐analog ZLD setup using an open jar to mimic practical salt‐harvesting conditions (Figure 6c). The outdoor tests were conducted on a sunny, windless day, with an average solar irradiance of 0.60 kW m−2 and a maximum value of just 0.74 kW m−2 (Figure 6d). In the open‐pond‐analog configuration, 5.6 g of water evaporated during the first 8 h of sunlight, increasing to a cumulative 6.7 g after an additional 2 h, likely due to the release of residual water from the hydrogel following brine depletion (Figure S14). A total of 0.91 g of salt was collected, corresponding to a recovery of 91% and a salt production rate of 7.7 kg m−2 day−1 (Figure 6f and Figures S15). In the closed self‐condensing ZLD configuration, complete drying was achieved within 12 h, delivering a salt yield of 6.0 kg m−2 day−1 (Figure 5f and Figures S16), together with a freshwater production rate of 26.6 L m−2 day−1 (Figure 6f). The collected condensate met the World Health Organization (WHO) guideline for drinking water in terms of sodium concentration (Figure 6e) [53], with an overall condensation efficiency of 62.4%. The slightly reduced evaporation and wetter salt in the closed system are primarily attributed to vapor accumulation within the sealed enclosure, suggesting that improved vapor extraction and condensation management would further enhance salt drying and yield.

In addition, an outdoor ISE test using natural seawater was performed on a separate day (Figure S17). After treatment, the concentrations of four major ions were substantially reduced: Na+ from 11 300 to 3.58 mg L−1, Mg2+ from 1 190 to 9.05 × 10−3 mg L−1, K+ from 1 930 to 0.843 mg L−1, and Ca2+ from 217 to 7.39 × 10−2 mg L−1 (Figure S18). These final concentrations all fall below the WHO‐recommended limits for drinking water [53, 54, 55, 56], confirming the high quality of the collected freshwater.

Conclusion

In conclusion, we have developed a hierarchical hydrogel evaporator featuring a porous interfacial layer of fused core–shell carbon‐polymer nanospheres, which enables ultrafast interfacial solar evaporation through the synergistic combination of localized solar‐thermal conversion, enlarged liquid‐vapor evaporation interfaces, and continuous water replenishment. The evaporator is fabricated via a simple and robust in situ precipitation polymerization approach, highlighting its potential for scalable production. More importantly, this work establishes a directional salt‐transport framework for hypersaline interfacial evaporation, in which anisotropic Péclet‐number regulation enables diffusion‐relaxed through‐thickness transport across the thin thickness while promoting advection‐dominated brine export along the top layer and sidewall. Coupled with Marangoni‐assisted migration through parallel feed‐brine channels, this design continuously removes concentrated brine from the active evaporation interface and redirects it to designated crystallization regions, thereby achieving effective water‐salt decoupling and practical zero‐liquid‐discharge operation. These findings provide scalable materials and device strategy for simultaneous freshwater production and mineral recovery from hypersaline brines.

Experimental Section

Chemicals and Materials

Chemicals including acrylamide (AM), agar, potassium persulfate (KPS), N,N’‐methylenebis(acrylamide) (MBA), ammonium persulfate (APS), carbon black (CB), 2‐hydroxypropyl methacrylate (HPMA), N,N,N’,N’‐tetramethylethylene‐diamine (TMEDA), and sodium chloride (NaCl) were all purchased from Sigma–Aldrich (Australia) and used as received. Kitchen towel was purchased from the local market.

Fabrication of Blank Polyacrylamide‐Agar Hydrogels

20.0 wt.% AM and 2.2 wt.% agar were mixed in deionized (DI) water under 600 rpm stirring for 20 min at a 90°C oil bath. KPS solution (0.2 wt.% of monomer) and MBA (0.2 wt.% of monomer) were added to the mixture. The solution was treated in an ultrasonic bath to eliminate air bubbles. 1 mL of the above‐mentioned solution was pipetted into an Agilent 13–425 Finish 4 mL clear vial. The vial was placed in an oven at 70°C for 4 h.

Fabrication of Hydrogels With CB@PHPMA Nanosphere Composite Coating

First, an above‐mentioned PAM‐agar hydrogel was soaked into APS solution (15 mL, 30 mg/mL) for 10 min, followed by taking it out with a quick rinse by DI water, then dried with lint‐free paper. 0.1 wt.% CB was added to a vial and mixed with 14 mL of water under ultrasonication for 15 min. 1 M HPMA (monomer), 0.02 M MBA (cross‐linker), and 0.13 m TMEDA (accelerator) were then added to the CB dispersed water and stirring under an ice bath for 1 min to allow the fully dissolve of monomer, cross‐linker and accelerator. The hydrogel precursor loaded with initiator APS was then soaked into the above solution for 10 min. The resulting gel was rinsed with DI water to remove physically adhered CB and dried with lint‐free paper. The aforementioned process was repeated twice and four times to fabricate samples CBP × 2 and CBP × 4, respectively.

Characterization

The surface and pore morphology of the hydrogel evaporators was characterized via scanning electron microscopy (SEM, Zeiss Evo LS15 and FEI Nova NanoSEM 450) at an acceleration voltage of 5–15 kV, while the structure of salt crystals was characterized via SEM Zeiss Supra 55VP at a voltage of 5–10 kV. Fourier transform infrared (FT‐IR) measurements were conducted on a Shimadzu MIRacle 10 system. Raman spectra were recorded using a Renishaw Raman spectrometer, while surface wettability was evaluated using an Attension contact angle meter. An anti‐gravity water uptake test was further conducted using the same instrument to assess the ability of the hydrogel surface to capture and transport water against gravity. Briefly, a DI water droplet was placed on a hydrophobic nonwoven fabric to avoid rapid spreading or absorption by the substrate. The hydrogel sample was then positioned above the droplet and gently brought into contact with the droplet surface. The time required for the droplet to detach from the fabric and be lifted by the hydrogel surface was recorded as the anti‐gravity water uptake time. A shorter uptake time indicates stronger water affinity and more efficient capillary‐assisted water transport within the hydrogel. Water phase transitions in the hydrogels were investigated from room temperature to 200°C using a NETZSCH DSC300 Supreme at a constant heating rate of 5°C min−1. Additionally, ion concentrations in brine, seawater, and desalinated water were quantified using an Agilent 7900 ICP‐MS. Infrared (IR) images were captured using a Fluke PTi120 pocket thermal imager.

Interfacial Solar Evaporation (ISE) and Zero Liquid Discharge (ZLD) Test

For ISE tests, the hydrogel evaporators were supported by an EPE foam floating on a beaker containing various solutions, including DI water, lake water, and seawater. Water transport to the hydrogel base was maintained via several tissue wicks passing through a central hole in the EPE foam. The ZLD performance was evaluated using a sealed sample container with 20 wt.% NaCl solution. In this setup, six tissue wicks were threaded through a central hole in the lid: two provided continuous brine supply, while the remaining four were cutted into windmill‐shaped blades and flattened across the lid surface to facilitate salt crystallization and separation.

A solar simulator (NBeT HSX‐F3000 xenon light source) provided the required irradiation. The solar intensity at the hydrogel interface was precisely calibrated to 1 sun (1 kW m−2) using a thermal power sensor and a portable power meter (Thorlabs PM100D and S405C). Water mass loss was continuously monitored using an electronic mass balance (OHAUS Pioneer IC‐PX 124). To minimize parasitic heat transfer, an additional EPE foam layer was inserted between the balance and the beaker. The steady‐state evaporation rates were determined from the slope of the mass‐time curves via linear regression. Furthermore, the surface temperature of the hydrogel and the temperature of the bulk water were recorded at 5‐min intervals using a Fluke PTi120 pocket thermal imager.

CFD Simulation of Salt Distribution

Salt redistribution in the hierarchical evaporator was simulated using ANSYS Fluent 2024 R2. A transient finite‐volume model was established to solve the coupled laminar‐flow and species‐transport processes during evaporation. The liquid flow was governed by the continuity equation and the Navier–Stokes equation: (5) ∂ρ∂t+∇·ρu=0 (6) ρ∂u∂t+u·∇u=−∇p+μ∇2u+F where ρ is the fluid density, u is the velocity vector, t is time, p is pressure, µ is the dynamic viscosity, and F is the body‐force term. Gravity was included in the simulation.

The NaCl concentration field was solved using the advection–diffusion equation: (7) ∂c∂t+∇·−D∇c+uc=R where c is the NaCl concentration, D is the diffusion coefficient of NaCl, and R is the source term, which was set to 0 in the present model. The diffusion coefficient of NaCl was taken as 1.6 × 10−9 m2 s−1.

The model geometry was constructed according to the experimental dimensions of the evaporator. The simulation was initialized with 20 wt.% NaCl brine, and a pressure inlet with the same solution was imposed along the inner wall of the shell to represent continuous liquid supply. Evaporation was prescribed on both the top surface and the sidewall using outward water‐flux boundary conditions, with a larger flux applied on the top surface and a smaller one on the sidewall, consistent with their different evaporation contributions observed experimentally. The brine was treated as an incompressible Newtonian fluid, while its density was defined as concentration‐dependent. The simulated NaCl concentration field was used to visualize directional salt redistribution and salt enrichment within the evaporator during evaporation.
