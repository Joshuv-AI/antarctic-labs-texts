Effect of intermittent folic acid supplementation on production performance and hatchability of Ross 308 broiler breeder flocks
Authors: Marcin Wegner, Dariusz Kokoszyński, Michał Gesek
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe present study evaluated the effect of intermittent folic acid supplementation administered via drinking water on the productive and reproductive performance of Ross 308 breeder flocks. The experiment was conducted in two houses (A and B). Birds from house A received folic acid supplementation (10 mg/L for 5 consecutive days at selected ages: 27, 31, 35, 39, 43, 47, 51, and 55 weeks), while the birds from control group (house B) received no supplementation. Egg production, body weight of hens and roosters, egg weight, fertility, hatchability, egg quality traits, and early embryonic mortality were analyzed during the laying period (24–56 weeks of age). The results showed a typical breeder pattern of decreasing egg production and hatchability with advancing age, accompanied by an increase in body weight of birds and egg weight in both groups. Folic acid supplementation resulted in variable and inconsistent effects depending on bird age and the analyzed trait. At specific production weeks, improvements in fertility, hatchability, and early embryonic survival were observed in the supplemented group; however, these effects were not consistently maintained throughout the entire experimental period. Moreover, some egg quality traits, including cracked and deformed eggs, showed an unfavorable or variable response to supplementation in certain weeks. Overall, no uniform effect of intermittent folic acid supplementation was observed across all reproductive and production parameters. While some beneficial effects on selected early embryonic development traits were identified, these responses were age-dependent and inconsistent. However, these effects are age-dependent and not consistently expressed throughout the laying period, indicating the need for further research to determine the optimal dose and supplementation scheme under commercial production conditions.Keywords: Breeder flock, Chick hatchability, Fertility, Folic acid

Introduction

Egg biological value is closely associated with fertility, which in turn depends on multiple factors, among which particular importance is attributed to the body weight of roosters and the sex ratio in the flock (Gonçalves et al., 2015; Rachwał, 2011). The body weight of roosters influences their mating ability and mating efficiency, as both underweight and overweight males may exhibit reduced reproductive activity and lower fertilization success (Rachwał, 2011). In turn, an appropriate sex ratio in the flock ensures a sufficient number of matings per hen, which directly translates into a higher percentage of fertilized eggs, whereas an in sufficient number of males reduces fertilization efficiency (Gonçalves et al., 2015). Hatchability of chicks is primarily determined by egg characteristics, including weight, shape, structure, shell quality, and storage conditions (Banaszewska et al., 2017; Bergoug et al., 2013; Ekmay et al., 2013; Iqbal et al., 2014; Kucharska-Gaca and Adamski, 2016; Mejia et al., 2012; Zakaria et al., 2005). The literature also highlights the significant role of genetic, environmental, and nutritional factors that substantially modify both fertility and hatchability. Numerous studies indicate that the nutritional status of hens has a significant impact on egg biological value (Ekmay et al., 2013; Koppenol et al., 2014; Mejia et al., 2012; Silveira et al., 2014). Therefore, particular attention is given to nutrients with key metabolic functions, such as folic acid (vitamin B9), which is involved in one-carbon metabolism, nucleotide synthesis, and DNA methylation processes that regulate proper cell proliferation and embryonic development (Liu et al., 2016; Tombarkiewicz et al., 2020). In poultry, as oviparous organisms, embryonic development depends entirely on nutrients deposited in the egg; therefore, breeder nutrition plays a fundamental role in determining embryo survival and chick quality (Zhao et al., 2025). Folates play a key role in regulating reproductive and epigenetic processes, including gene expression (Liu et al., 2016). Zhao et al. (2025) demonstrated that folic acid supplementation improves ovarian function, folate status, and embryonic development in broiler breeder hens. Similarly, Pertiwi et al. (2022) confirmed the beneficial effects of folates on reproductive system development, folate content in eggs, and hatchability. Moreover, folic acid is involved in epigenetic regulation, including DNA methylation and histone modification, thereby influencing the expression of genes associated with immune response and developmental processes (Li et al., 2016). Numerous studies in poultry have shown that folic acid supplementation may improve production and health parameters, including body weight, feed efficiency, antioxidant status, and immune response (Gouda et al., 2020; Pertiwi et al., 2022). Experimental studies involving in ovo folic acid administration have shown that it may affect hatchability and chick weight; however, the effects are dose-dependent and may be both positive and in conclusive (Abdel-Halim et al., 2020; Li et al., 2016; Oladokun et al., 2023). Nevertheless, not all studies are consistent. In some experiments, folic acid supplementation did not significantly affect fertility or hatchability, despite an observed increase in chick body weight. This suggests that the effects of folates depend on dosage, environmental conditions, and baseline dietary levels (Abdel-Halim et al., 2020; Oladokun et al., 2023; Tercic et al., 2014). The route of administration and interactions with other dietary components are also important factors (Al-Jumaili et al., 2025; Li et al., 2016; Pertiwi et al., 2022). Embryonic mortality in poultry production represents a significant economic and biological issue, and its level depends on multiple factors, including breeder nutrition, egg quality, and incubation conditions (Al-Jumaili et al., 2025; Decuypere and Bruggeman, 2007). Early embryonic mortality (up to 48 h of incubation) is of particular importance and may be associated with disturbances in cell division and DNA synthesis, which are directly dependent on folate availability (Liu et al., 2016). Parameters such as blood ring and early embryo arrest are widely used indicators of early embryonic mortality and may reflect metabolic disturbances and reduced biological quality of eggs (Elibol et al., 2006; Lourens et al., 2006; Reijrink et al., 2009). The intermittent supplementation schedule was selected as a practical management strategy for commercial broiler breeder production. This approach was intended to periodically increase folate availability during the laying cycle while reducing vitamin use and minimizing potential degradation of folic acid during prolonged exposure in drinking water systems. Despite increasing interest in the role of folic acid in poultry nutrition, there is still a lack of studies evaluating its effects under commercial broiler breeder conditions, particularly in relation to detailed embryological parameters throughout the entire laying cycle (Kidd, 2020; Pertiwi et al., 2022). Most available data originate from short-term or experimental studies, which limits their direct applicability to production systems (Liu et al., 2016; Zhang et al., 2020). It was hypothesized that folic acid supplementation would improve production and hatchability parameters and reduce early embryonic mortality.

The aim of the present study was to evaluate the effect of folic acid supplementation in broiler breeder hens during the laying period (24–56 weeks of age) on production and reproductive performance, including laying rate, fertility, hatchability, and detailed embryonic mortality profile, as well as selected performance traits such as body weight and egg weight.

Materials and methods

According to Polish law, the present study did not require approval from the Local Ethics Committee for Experiments in Bydgoszcz, as no invasive procedures or interventions causing pain or suffering were performed. All analyses on eggs were conducted during routine hatchery quality control procedures. The applicable regulations concerning the protection of animals used for experimental purposes therefore did not apply to standard agricultural production or commercial animal husbandry.

The experiment was conducted under commercial farm conditions, and animal rearing and management were carried out by the farm personnel in accordance with standard husbandry practices and applicable local animal welfare regulations. The researchers did not perform any invasive handling or procedures on the animals.

Birds and environment

The experimental material consisted of broiler breeder flocks of Ross 308. The study was conducted on 13,000 hens. The rearing period lasted until 18 weeks of age, during which environmental parameters (temperature, air humidity, ventilation) and feeding were managed in accordance with the recommendations. Subsequently, the birds were transported to the production farm, where they were divided into two houses: A – 6,500 hens and B – 6,500 hens. Hens were paired with roosters at a ratio of 10:1, at a stocking density of 6 birds/m². The production houses were closed poultry houses equipped with mechanical ventilation operating under negative-pressure conditions. Environmental conditions were controlled using automatically regulated ventilation fans and air inlets, allowing continuous air exchange. During periods of high ambient temperatures, the houses were additionally equipped with an evaporative cooling system (pad cooling), which reduced heat stress and stabilized internal temperature and humidity. Microclimate parameters were recorded, monitored, and controlled using a Viper climate and production computer (Big Dutchman AG, Vechta, Germany) and were similar in both buildings during the egg production period. The same management system was implemented in both houses. During the reproductive period, the temperature in the poultry houses was approximately 20°C, and humidity was 65%. The birds were kept on a straw litter system, and the houses were equipped with automatic nesting systems (55 hens per nest) from Big Dutchman. During the laying period, birds (males and females separately) were individually weighed (100 males and 200 females randomly selected from each house from 23 to 56 weeks of age) using a WGJ-R electronic hook scale (Jotafan, Kraków, Poland) with an accuracy of 10 g. The average body weights of hens (g) and roosters (g) were calculated for individual weeks of age.

Feeding program

Hens and roosters were fed separately. Feeders for roosters (5 feeders per unit) were suspended at a height that prevented hens from consuming feed intended for males, whereas hens were fed in trough chain feeders equipped with grills preventing roosters from accessing the feed, at a density of 18 cm per bird. The amount of feed provided was in accordance with Aviagen (2016). Feed intake was recorded using the Viper system (Big Dutchman AG, Vechta, Germany), a poultry house management system. Three different feeding phases were applied, using mash diets: pre-laying period up to 21 weeks of age, laying phase I from 22 to 34 weeks of age, and laying phase II from 35 weeks to the end of the reproductive period. The basic declared feed composition is presented in Table 1. Throughout the reproductive period, the birds were fed according to the recommendations (Aviagen, 2016) with unlimited access to water. The poultry house was equipped with nipple drinkers at a density of 10 birds per drinker. Drinking water was supplied through the farm’s standard nipple drinking system, which was managed according to routine farm procedures. No specific modifications of the water system were introduced during the experimental period. The birds were kept under a 13-hour photo period with a light intensity of 30–60 lux.

Experimental design and dietary treatments

At 27, 31, 35, 39, 43, 47, 51, and 55 weeks of age, birds in house A received folic acid supplementation at a dose of 10 mg/L of drinking water for 5 consecutive days. Birds in house B served as the control group and received no supplementation. The supplementation solution was prepared by dissolving folic acid in water to achieve a final concentration of 10 mg/L. The solution was prepared fresh on a daily basis during each supplementation period. The prepared solution was introduced into the drinking water system using the farm’s water dosing equipment, ensuring its distribution to the nipple drinkers in house A. Supplementation was provided continuously for 5 consecutive days during each supplementation period. Throughout the supplementation period, birds had unrestricted access to water. The selected folic acid dose was based on previous studies demonstrating beneficial effects of folic acid supplementation on reproductive performance, fertility, hatchability, and embryonic development in poultry (Kidd, 2020; Pertiwi et al., 2022; Terčič and Pestotnik, 2014; Zhao et al., 2025).

Incubation eggs

Eggs were collected twice daily (09:00 a.m. and 14:00 p.m.). After collection, eggs were classified into hatching eggs and non-hatching eggs (double-yolk, cracked, and deformed eggs). Laying rate (%) per house was calculated using the formula: number of all laid eggs divided by the current number of hens × 100. The percentage of double-yolk, cracked, and deformed eggs in relation to all eggs laid per day was calculated using the formula: cracked eggs or double-yolk eggs or deformed eggs/total number of eggs x 100. At the end of each week, laying rate (%) for the given experimental period was calculated as the weekly average. Each week, 200 eggs per house were collected at the end of the laying week for egg weight determination. At the end of each laying week, eggs (n = 200) were weighed using a Medicat 160 M balance (Medicat Ltd., Zürich, Switzerland). Eggs meeting hatching egg quality standards were subjected to fumigation with formaldehyde vapour (5 g/m³) for approximately 20 min at 24°C and 75% relative humidity. Subsequently, eggs were stored for 3–5 days at 15–16°C and 70–75% relative humidity. After storage, eggs were incubated in a Petersime X-Streamer™ incubator (Petersime NV, Zulte, Belgium). During incubation, the temperature was maintained at 37.6–37.8°C, and relative humidity ranged from 50 to 52%. Eggs were automatically turned every hour at a 90° angle. On days 10 and 19 of incubation, eggs were candled from the air cell side using a candling device (ovoscope; Fermo, Piotrków, Poland) to remove infertile eggs or those containing dead embryos. On day 19 of incubation (after candling), eggs were transferred to Petersime H192 Cleanway hatchers. During hatching, the temperature in the hatchers ranged from 36.5 to 37.2°C, and relative humidity ranged from 55 to 70%, depending on the stage of hatch. Statistical analysis

The collected numerical data on production traits and hatchability were analyzed using standard methods of mathematical statistics. Since the study involved two experimental groups (house A vs. house B), differences between groups were assessed using Student’s t-test for independent samples at a significance level of P < 0.05. Results are presented as means ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical analyses were performed using SAS software (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA), version 9.4. The experimental unit was a single poultry house for egg production, an individual bird for body weight, and individual eggs for egg weight and hatching characteristics.

Results

The analysis of production parameters (Table 2) showed a significant (P < 0.05) effect of the applied supplementation on egg production, body weight of hens and roosters, and egg weight. Egg production exhibited a general decreasing trend with advancing age in both experimental groups. In house A, it declined from 88.4% at 32 weeks to 68.3% at 56 weeks, whereas in house B it decreased from 87.7% to 70.6% over the same period. Significant differences between groups were observed at selected time points, with higher values recorded in house A at 32, 36, 44, and 56 weeks, while higher egg production was noted in house B at 48 weeks (P < 0.05). At 28 weeks of age, significantly higher body weight of hens was observed in house B (3540 g) compared to house A (3500 g). At later stages, significantly higher values in house B were recorded at 48 and 52 weeks, whereas at 56 weeks hens from house A showed lower body weight. Significant differences at body weight of rosters was observed at several time points. At 28 weeks, higher body weight was recorded in house A, whereas at 48 weeks significantly higher values were found in house B. At 52 and 56 weeks, roosters from house A achieved higher body weight than those in the control group. Significant differences in egg weight between the groups were observed at 28, 48, and 56 weeks. Higher egg weight was recorded in house B at 28 and 56 weeks, while in house A higher values were observed at 48 weeks.

Discussion

In the present study, it was demonstrated that intermittent folic acid supplementation administered via drinking water had a variable effect on the productive and reproductive performance of Ross 308 breeder flocks, depending on bird age and the analyzed trait. The observed age related decline in egg production confirms a physiological pattern typical of broiler breeder flocks (Ekmay et al., 2013; Kidd, 2020). Aging of the herd leads to hormonal changes and a decline in the quality of ovarian follicles, which directly limits productivity (Decuypere and Bruggeman, 2007; Kidd, 2020). Higher egg production values observed at selected time points in the supplemented group (house A) may suggest a temporary beneficial effect of folic acid on reproductive system function. This effect may be associated with the role of folates in nucleotide synthesis and cell proliferation, which are crucial for proper ovarian follicle development (Liu et al., 2016; Zhao et al., 2025). However, the lack of a consistent effect throughout the entire experimental period indicates that the response to supplementation depends on the physiological stage and the basal dietary nutrient level. The increase in body weight of hens and roosters with age is consistent with the typical growth pattern observed in breeder flocks, as reported in previous studies (Aviagen, 2016; Rayan et al., 2015). The observed differences between groups were inconsistent and do not clearly indicate a beneficial effect of supplementation. This suggests that folic acid at the applied dose and administration scheme does not significantly affect growth performance parameters, which is also supported by previous studies (Gouda et al., 2020; Pertiwi et al., 2022). The increase in egg weight with hen age is consistent with previous reports, which document a progressive rise in egg size as birds age (Nowaczewski et al., 2022). Despite significant differences at selected weeks, no clear trend indicating a supplementation effect was observed. This may suggest that folic acid does not directly influence egg size but may potentially affect internal egg quality or embryonic development (Koppenol et al., 2014). Fertility remained at a high level (>90%) in both groups, indicating proper flock management, including an appropriate sex ratio (Gonçalves et al., 2015; Rachwał, 2011). Higher fertility values in the supplemented group at selected time points may result from the influence of folates on metabolic processes related to DNA synthesis and methylation, which play a key role in the function of reproductive cells (Liu et al., 2016). Hatchability of set eggs decreased with age, which is consistent with previous reports describing deterioration in egg quality and embryo viability in older breeder flocks (Abdel-Halim et al., 2020; Oladokun et al., 2023; Yang et al., 2020). This is a typical effect observed in breeder hens, associated, among other things, with changes in yolk composition, poorer synchronization of embryonic development, and a decline in the quality of egg membranes and shell (Decuypere and Bruggeman, 2007). Higher hatchability in the supplemented group at several time points suggests a potentially beneficial effect of folic acid on embryonic development. This may be related to its role in one carbon metabolism, which is essential for proper DNA replication and cell division during early embryogenesis (Li et al., 2016; Liu et al., 2016). Experimental and review studies confirm that folic acid supplementation may improve hatchability parameters by increasing folate availability for the developing embryo and enhancing its energy and antioxidant metabolism (Oladokun et al., 2023; Pertiwi et al., 2022). It has also been shown that both dietary supplementation in breeder flocks and in ovo administration can increase chick body weight and, in some cases, improve hatchability indices, although these effects are strongly dependent on the dose and timing of administration (Abdel-Halim et al., 2020; Al-Jumaili et al., 2025). At the same time, the literature indicates that an excess of folates or a suboptimal method of administration may fail to improve, and may even worsen, hatchability, suggesting a narrow range of effective action for this component (Elibol and Brake, 2006; Oladokun et al., 2023). Consequently, the variable effects of supplementation observed in the present study may result from interactions between the age of the birds, their metabolic status, and folate requirements at different stages of embryonic development. At the same time, a higher proportion of rejected, cracked, and deformed eggs was observed in the supplemented group during several production weeks. These findings suggest that the effect of folic acid supplementation on egg quality traits may not be entirely beneficial and should be interpreted with caution. One possible explanation is that periods of higher reproductive activity and improved fertility may have increased physiological demands associated with egg formation, potentially affecting nutrient allocation between embryo-supporting components and eggshell formation. Changes in nutrient partitioning, particularly involving calcium metabolism and shell deposition, could have contributed to the increased incidence of cracked and deformed eggs. Although egg weight was not consistently increased by supplementation, temporary differences in egg size and production intensity may also have influenced shell quality. Similar inconsistencies in egg quality responses to folate supplementation have been reported in previous studies, suggesting that the effects of folic acid may depend on dosage, administration method, nutritional background, and interactions with other dietary components (Oladokun et al., 2023; Terčič and Pestotnik, 2014). Therefore, while folic acid supplementation may improve selected reproductive parameters, its potential impact on egg quality traits should also be considered when evaluating its overall usefulness in commercial breeder flocks. Early embryo mortality was low in both groups, particularly within the first 24 hours of incubation, indicating appropriate incubation conditions and good egg quality (Elibol and Brake, 2006; Reijrink et al., 2009). Significant differences between groups observed at selected time points, especially for 48-hour mortality, blood ring incidence, and total early embryo mortality, may indicate a protective effect of folic acid during early embryogenesis. Folates play a key role in cell division and DNA synthesis, and their deficiency may lead to arrested embryo development (Liu et al., 2016). The variability observed in parameters such as blood ring and “black eye” confirms the high sensitivity of early embryonic development to nutritional and metabolic factors (Lourens et al., 2006). However, the inconsistent differences between groups suggest that the effect of folic acid depends on multiple factors, including bird age, physiological condition, and environmental conditions.

However, some limitations of the present study should be acknowledged. Since the experiment was conducted under commercial farm conditions, water quality parameters were not analyzed during the supplementation period. Although birds received water from the standard farm drinking water system, variations in water characteristics, such as pH, hardness, mineral composition, or microbiological quality, may influence nutrient stability and absorption. In particular, factors such as biofilm formation within the drinking water lines, water temperature, and potential chemical contaminants may affect folic acid stability and its uniform distribution through the nipple drinking system. Future studies should include detailed water quality analyses to better evaluate possible interactions between drinking water characteristics and folic acid supplementation. Conclusions

In summary, intermittent folic acid supplementation administered via drinking water may exert age dependent and trait specific effects in Ross 308 breeder flocks. The results indicate a potential beneficial influence on selected reproductive parameters, particularly fertility, hatchability, and early embryo survival; however, these effects are not consistent over time. The observed variability suggests a narrow range of effective action, likely influenced by the physiological status of the birds, supplementation strategy, and production conditions. These findings are in line with previous reports indicating that the efficacy of folic acid depends on dose, method of administration, and dietary context. Further research should focus on optimizing supplementation protocols and elucidating interactions between folates and other nutrients to enable more effective application in poultry production.
