Biaxially oriented polyethylene film preserves nutritional quality and extends the shelf life of postharvest peaches
Authors: Zhen Wang, Wan-Ken Chen, Kai-Ye Shi, Le-Ying Yan, Song-Li Han, Hui Zhou, Gai-Fang Yao, Hong Wang, Pedro Garcia-Caparros, Hua Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Biaxially oriented polyethylene (BOPE) is a high-strength, lightweight polyethylene material with superior barrier properties. In this study, peaches were packaged using either polyethylene (PE) or BOPE film. The peaches packaged in BOPE film maintained better firmness, color retention, antioxidant content, and nutritional quality than control peaches and those packaged in PE film. Transcriptome sequencing revealed 1041 differentially expressed genes in BOPE-packaged fruit compared with that in the control, and these were enriched in the pyruvate metabolism, flavonoid biosynthesis, and glutathione metabolism pathways. Physiological analyses demonstrated that BOPE packaging reduced malondialdehyde accumulation and increased peroxidase and phenylalanine ammonia-lyase activity, potentially limiting membrane peroxidation and senescence. BOPE-packaged fruit also retained higher levels of soluble sugars, reducing sugars, free amino acids, and proline, which are critical for fruit flavor and stress adaptation. Together, these results underscore the potential of BOPE as a novel packaging material for the preservation of fruits and vegetables.

Introduction

Peaches (Prunus persica L.) are among the most widely cultivated and consumed fruits worldwide, valued for their sweet flavor and rich nutritional content. China accounts for approximately 60 % of the world's peach cultivation area and production (Ezzat et al., 2024). Despite the widespread popularity of peaches, their postharvest preservation remains a significant challenge, and their shelf life and market distribution are severely limited by rapid physiological deterioration. The peach harvest occurs predominantly in the summer months and is characterized by a short duration and high seasonality. During the postharvest phase, peaches are highly susceptible to rapid softening, microbial decay, and physiological deterioration, creating substantial difficulties for storage and long-distance transportation (Minas et al., 2018). These undesirable changes manifest primarily as color changes, loss of firmness, and degradation of key nutritional components, all of which ultimately reduce fruit quality (Zhao et al., 2024). Consequently, the development and implementation of effective preservation technologies are essential for extending shelf life, maintaining postharvest quality, and ensuring the commercial value of peaches.

The selection of packaging materials plays a crucial role in sustaining the postharvest quality of fruits (Nian et al., 2024). Traditional polyethylene (PE) materials are derived from the polymerization of ethylene monomers (Siracusa & Blanco, 2020). PE is widely used in fruit preservation and packaging because it is non-toxic, odorless, highly transparent, relatively inexpensive, and easy to process. Its semi-permeable characteristics facilitate the passive regulation of internal gas composition within the packaging, thereby extending fruit storage life (Zeng et al., 2022). However, PE has only a limited ability to retain moisture and serve as an oxygen barrier, reducing its effectiveness in long-term preservation for higher-end markets, and it is also prone to damage. In recent years, biaxially oriented polyethylene (BOPE) has emerged as a novel packaging material and a promising alternative to PE for fruit-packaging applications. BOPE is produced from a combination of linear low-density polyethylene and high-density polyethylene through a sequential stretching process, it holds cost and sustainability advantages over traditional multi-layer films (Zhang et al., 2024). This innovative material is regarded as one of the most valuable recyclable single-material packaging options because of its superior mechanical properties and enhanced barrier performance. Compared with conventional polyethylene, BOPE has higher transparency, toughness, and puncture resistance, all of which contribute to more effective preservation of peach freshness (Chen et al., 2019). BOPE films thus show significant potential to enhance fruit quality and extend shelf life.

In this study, the effects of BOPE and PE films on the postharvest storage performance of peach fruit were compared. Particular attention was given to examining the molecular mechanisms responsible for differences in fruit quality associated with BOPE packaging. Key quality parameters were evaluated, including color, firmness, antioxidant content, and nutrient composition of peach fruit under different packaging conditions. Transcriptome analysis was then performed to identify differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between BOPE-packaged and control fruit. Functional annotation and pathway enrichment analyses of the DEGs shed light on the molecular pathways associated with postharvest preservation in BOPE-packaged fruit. This work provides novel insights into the mechanisms that govern fruit quality retention and demonstrates the potential of advanced packaging materials to extend shelf life and overall fruit quality.

Materials and methods

Plant material and treatments

Peach fruits (cv. Shuanghongxiali) were harvested at commercial maturity, approximately 90 days after full bloom, from the Peach Germplasm Resource Bank of the Institute of Horticulture, Anhui Academy of Agricultural Sciences (31°53′49″ N, 117°15′11″ E), Anhui Province, China. Fruits of uniform ripeness, size, and color, without visible damage or disease, were selected for the study. The harvested fruits were immediately transferred to the laboratory for postharvest storage experiments. A total of 180 peach fruits were randomly assigned across all treatment groups. Fruits were wrapped in PE film (10 μm thickness) or BOPE film (30 μm thickness) according to previously established methods and the manufacturer's guidelines (Anhui GETTEL High-tech Materials, Anqing, China). Control fruits were left unwrapped and stored under identical conditions. All fruits were stored at room temperature (20 ± 1 °C) in a ventilated environment. Sampling was performed at 0, 3, 6, and 9 days of storage (DAS), and 15 fruits were randomly selected from each treatment group at each time point.

Determination of weight loss and firmness

The weight of peaches in each treatment group was recorded daily during storage using an analytical balance (Beijing Yong Guangming Medical Instrument Co., Beijing, China) with a precision of ±0.001 g. Weight loss was calculated as a percentage using the following equation:Weight loss%=W0−Wn/W0×100%where W0 is the initial weight of the peaches at 0 DAS and Wn is the weight of the peaches at n DAS.

Fruit firmness was measured on the mesocarp of peeled peach flesh at the equatorial region using a handheld hardness tester (GYJ-4, Quzhou Aipu Measuring Instrument Co., Quzhou, China). Before measurement, the peel was carefully removed to a depth of approximately 0.5 mm using a fruit-specific scalpel, exposing a circular area of mesocarp tissue with a 10-mm diameter. Subsequently, a cylindrical testing device with a diameter of 5 mm was employed to pierce the middle of the exposed region. It was inserted to a depth of 3 mm at a steady velocity of 2 mm/s. Three replicate measurements were performed on each fruit. The mean firmness value, expressed in Newtons (N), was calculated from 15 fruits per treatment group at each time point.

Color measurements

Surface color measurements were performed on intact peach fruits at the equatorial region using a chroma meter (ZE7700, Nippon Denshoku, Japan), calibrated against a standard white tile, as described by Hu et al. (2012). Five equidistant points were measured on each fruit, avoiding blemishes and the suture line to ensure consistency. Color parameters, including L* (lightness), a* (red-green), and b* (yellow-blue), were recorded using the CIE system under a D65 illuminant with a 10° observer angle. Mean values were calculated from 15 fruits per treatment group at each sampling point.

After color measurements, fruit peels and pits were carefully removed, and the remaining mesocarp tissue was promptly flash - frozen in liquid nitrogen and then kept at a temperature of −40 °C for the upcoming biochemical examinations.

Measurement of flavonoids, total phenolics, anthocyanins, and carotenoids

Contents of flavonoids, total phenolics, and anthocyanins were measured as described by Kähkönen et al. (1999), with minor modifications. Two grams of peach tissue was homogenized in 10 mL of ice-cold 1 % HCl–methanol (freshly prepared by diluting 37 % hydrochloric acid [Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd. (SCRC) Shanghai, China] in methanol [SCRC, Shanghai, China] to 1 % v/v final concentration) in an ice bath. The homogenate was transferred to a 20-mL volumetric flask, vortexed for 30 s, and extracted at 4 °C in the dark for 20 min with intermittent shaking (5-min intervals, 30 s each time). The resulting mixture was then centrifuged at 8000×g for 10 min at 4 °C, and the supernatant was collected for analysis. A 1 % HCl–methanol solution was used as the blank reference. Spectrophotometric analysis was performed by recording absorbance at 280 nm for total phenolics (using gallic acid as the standard, Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, USA, ≥98 %, R2 = 0.9990), 325 nm for flavonoids (using rutin as the standard, Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, USA, ≥98 %, R2 = 0.9985), and 530/600 nm for anthocyanins (using cyanidin-3-O-glucoside as the standard, Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, USA, ≥98 %, R2 = 0.9968). Anthocyanin content was calculated as the difference in absorbance between 530 and 600 nm (OD530 − OD600). Concentrations were calculated using the corresponding standard curves for each compound and expressed as g·kg−1 on a fresh weight (FW) basis. All measurements were performed in triplicate.

Carotenoid content was measured as described in Wellburn (1994), with slight modifications. In brief, 1.0 g of peach mesocarp tissue was homogenized in 10 mL of 95 % ethanol (SCRC, Shanghai, China) in an ice bath. To remove cellular debris, the homogenate was subjected to centrifugation at 4 °C and 8000×g for 10 min, after which the supernatant was harvested. Using a spectrophotometer, the absorbance of the harvested supernatant was determined at a wavelength of 470 nm, with 95 % ethanol employed as the blank control. For subsequent quantification, a standard curve was constructed using β-carotene as the reference standard (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, USA, ≥98 %), yielding a coefficient of determination (R2) of 0.9995. Carotenoid concentrations in peach samples were calculated from the standard curve and expressed as g kg−1 FW. All measurements were performed in triplicate to ensure reproducibility.

Measurement of POD activity, PAL activity, and MDA content

POD and PAL activities, as well as MDA content, were measured as described by Beaudoin-Eagan and Thorpe (1985), respectively. For the POD assay, fresh peach mesocarp tissue (4.0 g) was homogenized in 8.0 mL of ice-cold 50 mM sodium phosphate buffer (Macklin, Shanghai, China, pH 6.5, containing 1 % [w/v] polyvinylpolypyrrolidone). The homogenate was centrifuged at 15,000×g for 20 min at 4 °C, and the supernatant was used as the crude enzyme extract. The reaction mixture (final volume 3.4 mL) consisted of 2.5 mL of 20 mM guaiacol (SCRC, Shanghai, China) in phosphate buffer (Macklin, Shanghai, China), 0.75 mL of enzyme extract, and 0.15 mL of 400 mM H2O2 (SCRC, Shanghai, China). The reaction was initiated by rapid mixing, and the solution was immediately transferred to a spectrophotometer cuvette. The blank was prepared using heat-inactivated enzyme extract (boiled for 5 min) following the same procedure used for the active samples. Absorbance at 460 nm was continuously monitored in kinetic mode immediately after reaction initiation, and data points were recorded every 15 s for 5 min to monitor the linear phase of the reaction. The assay was performed in triplicate. Time was used as the x-axis and absorbance values as the y-axis for plotting, after which the linear segment of the resulting curve was identified. Subsequently, the rate of absorbance variation per minute (expressed as ΔOD/min) was computed. One unit (U) of POD activity was defined as the quantity of enzyme needed to oxidize 1 μmol of guaiacol per minute under the specified assay conditions, which were maintained at room temperature (20 ± 1 °C). POD activity was expressed as U kg−1 FW.

PAL activity was measured by monitoring the production of trans-cinnamic acid at 290 nm. Fresh peach mesocarp tissue (2.0 g) was homogenized in 6.0 mL of ice-cold borate buffer (0.1 M, pH 8.8, Macklin, Shanghai, China), then centrifuged at 10,000×g for 15 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was incubated with 0.05 M L-phenylalanine (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, USA) at 37 °C for 60 min. The reaction was stopped by adding 6 M HCl, and the mixture was then subjected to centrifugation at 8000×g for 5 min. The absorbance of the supernatant obtained after centrifugation was measured at 290 nm, and the rate of absorbance variation per minute (ΔOD/min) was computed. One unit (U) of PAL activity was defined as a 0.01 increase in absorbance per minute under the established assay conditions. Activity was normalized to sample mass and extraction volume and expressed as U kg−1 FW.

MDA content was determined using a modified thiobarbituric acid (TBA) method. Homogenized peach mesocarp tissue (5.0 g) was extracted with 5.0 mL of 5 % (w/v) trichloroacetic acid (SCRC, Shanghai, China) under ice-cold conditions. The homogenate was centrifuged at 10,000×g for 20 min at 4 °C, and 2.0 mL of the supernatant was mixed with 2.0 mL of 0.67 % (w/v) TBA solution. The mixture was incubated in a boiling water bath at 95 °C for 30 min. After cooling in an ice bath, the mixture was centrifuged at 10,000×g for 10 min to remove precipitates, and the supernatant was transferred to a cuvette. Absorbance of the clear supernatant was measured at 532 nm and 600 nm (for subtraction of non-specific background), using distilled water as the reference. MDA concentrations were calculated using a standard curve generated from 1,1,3,3-tetramethoxypropane (Aladdin, Shanghai, China, R2 = 0.9985) and expressed as 10−3 mol kg−1 FW.

Measurement of soluble sugar and reducing sugar contents

Soluble sugar content was quantified using a phenol–sulfuric acid method with minor modifications. Homogenized mesocarp tissue (5.0 g) was extracted with 10 mL of 80 % (v/v) ethanol in a boiling water bath at 80 °C for 30 min. The mixture was centrifuged at 8000×g for 15 min at 4 °C, and the supernatant was collected. An aliquot (0.5 mL) of the extract was mixed with 0.5 mL of 5 % (w/v) phenol solution (SCRC, Shanghai, China), followed by rapid addition of 2.5 mL concentrated sulfuric acid (SCRC, Shanghai, China). The mixture was vortexed for 30 s and incubated at 25 °C for 20 min in the dark. Absorbance of the orange-yellow chromophore was measured at 620 nm against a reagent blank using a spectrophotometer. Glucose (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, USA) standards (0–200 μg mL−1, R2 = 0.9980) were processed similarly to construct the standard calibration curve. Results were expressed as a percentage of fresh weight.

Reducing sugar content was determined using an adapted 3,5-dinitrosalicylic acid (DNS) colorimetric assay (Miller, 1959) with modifications. Mesocarp tissue (2.0 g) was homogenized and extracted with 10 mL of ultrapure water at 80 °C for 30 min. The extract was centrifuged at 8000×g for 15 min at 4 °C, and the supernatant was collected. An aliquot (1.0 mL) of the supernatant was mixed with 1.0 mL of DNS reagent (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, USA) in a test tube and heated in a boiling water bath for 5 min to develop the red-brown chromogen. The reaction mixture was immediately cooled in an ice bath. Absorbance of the reaction mixture was measured at 540 nm and compared to that of a reagent blank. Glucose standards (0–2.0 g L−1, R2 = 0.9975) were processed similarly to generate a standard curve. Results were expressed as a percentage of fresh weight.

Measurement of amino acid and proline contents

The contents of free amino acids and proline were determined using ninhydrin-based colorimetric assays as described by Jones et al. (2002) and Abrahám et al. (2010), respectively, with slight modifications. For measurement of free amino acids, homogenized peach mesocarp tissue (2.0 g) was extracted with 10 mL of 80 % ethanol (v/v) in water bath at 70 °C for 1 h. The homogenate was centrifuged at 10,000×g for 15 min at 4 °C, and 0.5 mL of the supernatant was mixed with 2.0 mL of 2 % ninhydrin reagent (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, USA, prepared in 0.1 M citrate buffer, pH 5.0). The reaction mixture was heated at 100 °C for 15 min, rapidly cooled, and diluted with 5 mL of 60 % ethanol. Absorbance of the resulting blue-purple complex was measured at 570 nm and compared with that of a reagent blank. For proline measurement, peach mesocarp tissue (1.0 g) was homogenized with 5 mL of 3 % sulfosalicylic acid (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, USA) and centrifuged at 12,000×g for 10 min at 4 °C. An aliquot of the supernatant (2.0 mL) was combined with 2.0 mL of acidic ninhydrin reagent (1.25 % ninhydrin in 6 M phosphoric acid and glacial acetic acid, 1:3 v/v, SCRC, Shanghai, China) at 100 °C for 30 min. The reaction was terminated in an ice bath, and the yellow chromogen was extracted with 4.0 mL of toluene (SCRC, Shanghai, China). Absorbance of the toluene layer was measured at 520 nm. Both analytes were quantified using standard curves. For free amino acids, serial leucine (Phygene, Fuzhou, China) standard solutions were prepared by stepwise dilution with 80 % ethanol (v/v); for proline, serial proline (Phygene, Fuzhou, China) standard solutions were prepared via dilution with 3 % sulfosalicylic acid. For each standard solution, absorbance was measured following the same colorimetric procedures as the samples, and linear regression analysis was conducted between standard concentrations and absorbance values to generate the curves (R2 = 0.9968 for leucine, R2 = 0.9989 for proline). The quantification results were expressed as 10−3 g kg−1. All extractions and measurements were performed in triplicate.

Transcriptome sequencing

Peach samples, preserved on dry ice to maintain RNA integrity, were dispatched to Wuhan Feisha Gene Information Company for transcriptome sequencing and preliminary data analysis. Upon arrival, stranded mRNA sequencing libraries were constructed using the TruSeq Stranded mRNA Library Prep Kit (TruSeq® Stranded mRNA Library Prep, Illumina, Inc., USA), which facilitates the preservation of strand-specific information vital for accurate transcriptome analysis. The libraries were sequenced on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform, generating 150-bp paired-end reads. The raw sequencing data were subjected to stringent quality control measures to ensure that only high-quality reads were retained, and the resulting clean reads were aligned to the Prunus persica reference genome (v2.0.a1, https://www.rosaceae.org) using HISAT2. Transcript abundance was quantified as fragments per kilobase of transcript per million mapped reads (FPKM) using StringTie v2.2.1. Principal component analysis (PCA) and gene expression plots were generated with the DESeq2 R package to assess the consistency among biological replicates (n = 3). All omics data in this study were analyzed with 3 biological replicates to ensure experimental reliability. Pearson correlation coefficients were computed between fruit quality parameters using SPSS Statistics 22.0 (IBM, Armonk, NY) to quantify linear associations, and correlations with a false discovery rate (FDR)-adjusted p value < 0.05 were considered significant. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified using DESeq2, with thresholds of FDR-adjusted P value < 0.05 and |log2(fold change)| ≥ 1 (Love et al., 2014). To examine the biological functions of the DEGs, KEGG pathway enrichment analysis was performed using a hypergeometric test (FDR ≤ 0.05), and Gene Ontology (GO) terms were assigned to genes to categorize their functions. The results were visualized using various plotting techniques in R Studio (v3.2.0).

Assessment of packaging material performance

The gas permeability of packaging materials was determined using the differential pressure method (Carullo et al., 2023). Each specimen was mounted between two chambers with a constant pressure differential of 0.1 MPa applied across it. The volume of gas (cm3) transmitted per unit area over 24 h was measured to characterize barrier performance. Results were expressed as cm3/(m2·24 h·0.1 MPa).

Moisture permeability was assessed by the gravimetric cup method (Khokhar, 2024). Each specimen was sealed over a cup containing desiccant and placed in an environment with controlled temperature and humidity (20 ± 1 °C, 50 ± 5 % relative humidity, RH). The mass of water vapor transmitted per square meter over 24 h (g/m2) was measured to evaluate moisture permeability. Results were expressed as g/(m2·24 h).

Light transmittance was quantified using a spectrophotometric method (Carullo et al., 2023). A UV–Vis spectrophotometer equipped with an integrating sphere was used to measure the ratio (%) of transmitted light intensity to incident light intensity in the visible spectrum (380–780 nm).

Statistical analysis

All measurements were performed with three independent biological replicates (n = 3). Statistically significant differences between treatment groups were identified using t-tests in SPSS Statistics (version 22.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). PCA was performed on autoscaled data (mean-centered and unit-variance scaled) using the FactoMineR package (v2.8; https://CRAN.R-project.org/package=FactoMineR) in R 4.2.1 (R Core Team, 2022; https://www.R-project.org/). Prior to eigenvalue decomposition of the covariance matrix, all variables were standardized by Z-score transformation (μ = 0, σ = 1) to eliminate scale-dependent bias inherent in multidimensional datasets. Quantitative results are presented as mean ± standard deviation (n = 3).

Results and discussion

BOPE is superior to PE film in improving postharvest weight retention rate, firmness retention rate, color stability of peach fruits, and reducing pathogen infection rate

The short postharvest lifespan of peach fruits is due largely to decay, stemming from their high vulnerability to pathogen infection (Aaqil et al., 2024). By 9 DAS, pathogen infection was observed in both the PE-packaged and control fruits, manifested as extensive visible decay and microbial colonization (Fig. 1A). Similarly, in a strawberry preservation experiment, strawberries packaged with PE showed obvious signs of decay and mold growth at 4 DAS. By the 6th day, the decay severity had become more pronounced (Zhang et al., 2023). In order to evaluate the severity of peach rot caused by pathogenic bacteria, the infection rate of peach infected by pathogenic bacteria was calculated (Fig. S1). The percentage of lesion area to the total surface area of peaches was measured as the disease index (Fig. 1B). Changes in fruit color and firmness are reliable indicators of ripening and postharvest quality. Peach, a climacteric fruit, exhibits high respiration rates and rapid ripening; its short post-harvest lifespan is characterized by reduced firmness, weight loss, color degradation, and declines in nutritional quality (Aaqil et al., 2024; Kluge & Jacomino, 2002). Here, fruit weight declined with increasing storage duration, and this decline was much greater in control fruit than in BOPE- and PE-packaged fruit on multiple storage days (Fig. 1C). Firmness also declined progressively over the storage period, but the firmness of BOPE-packaged fruits was 134 % and 113 % higher than that of control fruits and PE-packaged fruits at 6 DAS (Fig. 1D). Decreases in firmness during storage are primarily attributed to the enzymatic breakdown of cell-wall components mediated by polygalacturonase, pectin methylesterase, and β-galactosidase (Goulao & Oliveira, 2008). The hydrolysis of starch into simpler sugars also contributes to softening. The slower softening rate of BOPE-packaged fruits may result from the ability of BOPE film to reduce respiration rates, delay the ripening process, and minimize moisture loss.

Color preservation during storage is significantly influenced by the type of packaging film used (Aaqil et al., 2024). Here, BOPE-packaged fruit maintained higher L* values, which represent lightness, throughout the storage period. The L* value of control fruits had decreased significantly by 3 DAS, whereas that of BOPE- and PE-packaged fruits declined more slowly. At 9 DAS, L* values were higher in BOPE-packaged fruits than in PE-packaged and control fruits (Fig. 1E), demonstrating that BOPE can better preserve the light color of peach fruits. Values of a* also declined more slowly and were always higher in BOPE-packaged fruits than in PE-packaged and control fruits. At 9 DAS, the a* value of BOPE-packaged fruits was 1.34 and 1.26 times higher than that of control fruits and PE-packaged fruits, respectively (Fig. 1F). Similarly, b* values were higher in BOPE- and PE-packaged fruits than in control fruits before 6 DAS, and at 9 DAS, the b* value of BOPE-packaged fruits was 1.67 and 1.35 times higher than that of control fruits and PE-packaged fruits, respectively (Fig. 1G).

BOPE film had approximately 83 % lower gas permeability and 78 % lower water vapor permeability than PE film at both 0 d and 9 d (Fig. 1H–I), demonstrating the stability of its gas-barrier properties. Light transmittance of both materials remained >90 % throughout storage (Fig. 1J). BOPE thus showed superior barrier performance (the ability to block gas and water vapor) while maintaining a transparency comparable to that of PE, effectively preserving freshness without compromising product visibility. The tensile strength of BOPE was significantly higher than that of PE in both the machine direction (MD) and the transverse direction (TD) (Fig. S2), and both films showed minimal declines in performance over the 9-d period. The performance of BOPE is attributed to its excellent barrier properties against oxygen and water vapor (Mellinas et al., 2024), which create a favorable modified atmosphere while preventing water condensation (Fig. S3). Together, these results demonstrate that BOPE packaging can maintain the firmness, pigmentation, and gloss of peach fruit, all of which enhance its sensory appeal and promote consumer interest in its purchase.

BOPE is superior to PE film in maintaining phenolic, flavonoid, anthocyanin, and carotenoid contents in peach fruits during storage

The antioxidant capacity of fruits is influenced by their phenolic content, which is important for oxidative protection, color development, and nutritional quality (Fu et al., 2011). In this study, total phenolic content increased from 0 to 6 DAS, then decreased at 9 DAS. Phenolic content was consistently highest in BOPE-packaged fruits at all time points. Color changes in stone fruits during ripening reflect the accumulation of carotenoids, anthocyanins, and flavonoids, which enhance fruit color and offer antioxidant health benefits (Kapoor et al., 2022). Flavonoid content declined markedly at 3 DAS, then stabilized, and it was higher in BOPE-packaged fruits than in control and PE-packaged fruits throughout the storage period (Fig. 2B). Anthocyanin (Fig. 2C) and carotenoid (Fig. 2D) contents decreased progressively throughout storage. On 9 DAS, the carotenoid content of BOPE-packaged fruits was 1.91 and 1.45 times higher than that of control and PE-packaged fruits, respectively (Fig. 2D). These results suggest that both the appearance and nutritional properties of peach fruits are effectively preserved by BOPE packaging during postharvest storage. Correlations were observed between chromaticity indices (L*, a*, b*) and contents of phenolics, flavonoids, anthocyanins, and carotenoids, indicating that the degradation processes affecting both color and nutritional quality were slowed by BOPE packaging.

BOPE outperforms PE film in maintaining POD/PAL activities and reducing MDA content in peach fruits during storage

Peroxidase (POD), a key enzyme in reactive oxygen species (ROS) scavenging, plays a vital role in maintaining cellular redox balance and limiting the accumulation of peroxides, including H2O2, during fruit maturation (Meitha et al., 2020). Here, POD activity in peach fruit decreased gradually during storage but was significantly higher in BOPE-packaged fruit than in control and PE-packaged fruit (P < 0.05). At 9 DAS, POD activity was 1.66 and 1.32 times higher in BOPE-packaged fruit than in control and PE-packaged fruit, respectively (Fig. 3A). The sustained activity of POD in BOPE-packaged fruit may have helped to mitigate oxidative stress, reducing the accumulation of harmful peroxides (Meitha et al., 2020; Song et al., 2021) and preserving cellular integrity, thus maintaining the postharvest quality of BOPE-packaged peaches.Fig. 3Effects of different packaging treatments and storage durations on POD activity (A), PAL activity (B), and MDA content (C) in postharvest peaches. Phenylalanine ammonia-lyase (PAL), which catalyzes the first step in the phenylpropanoid pathway, is a key rate-limiting enzyme in phenolic metabolism (Barros & Dixon, 2020). PAL activity initially increased and then decreased as storage progressed, and it was consistently higher in BOPE-packaged fruit (Fig. 3B), suggesting that phenolic biosynthesis was enhanced in this fruit, consistent with their higher total phenolic contents (Fig. 2A).

Large amounts of superoxide radicals are generated by plants under environmental stress, leading to lipid peroxidation and malondialdehyde (MDA) production. MDA, a reliable marker of lipid peroxidation, reflects the degree of oxidative damage to cell membranes. Here, MDA content increased gradually during storage and was lower in BOPE-packaged fruit than in control and PE-packaged fruit at 3, 6, and 9 DAS (Fig. 3C). The lower MDA content in BOPE-packaged fruit across all time points indicates that the peroxidation of membrane lipids was effectively reduced. This protective effect likely reflects the lower oxidative stress achieved through higher POD activity and greater PAL-mediated phenolic synthesis, as reported in previous studies of ROS scavenging and phenylpropanoid metabolism (Jia et al., 2023).

BOPE packaging preserves higher soluble and reducing sugars in peach fruits during storage compared to PE film

Carbohydrates not only provide cellular energy and help to maintain cell turgor but also act as signaling molecules to regulate fruit development and contribute to distinctive fruit flavors. Here, soluble sugar content fluctuated during storage in all treatments, but it tended to increase over time, particularly between 3 and 6 DAS. From 6 to 9 DAS, soluble sugar content slightly increased in BOPE-packaged fruit but declined in PE-packaged and control fruit. By 9 DAS, soluble sugar content in BOPE-packaged fruit was 1.16 and 1.10 times higher than that in control and PE-packaged fruit (Fig. 4A). Trends in reducing sugar content varied among the treatments. Reducing sugar content decreased between 0 and 3 DAS in control and PE-packaged fruit but remained stable in BOPE-packaged fruit over the same period. By 9 DAS, reducing sugar content in BOPE-packaged fruit was 1.90 and 1.37 times higher than that in control and PE-packaged fruit, respectively (Fig. 4B). A previous study reported that total soluble solids (TSS) and total sugar content were higher in peaches packaged in shrink film than in those packaged in LDPE film or in unpackaged controls (Mahajan et al., 2015). Similarly, a delayed but sustained increase in TSS and sugar content was reported for pears stored in shrink-film packaging (Kaur et al., 2023). This gradual increase in TSS and sugar levels during storage has been attributed to the enzymatic conversion of starch into sugars and to the delayed ripening of fruits due to a reduced respiration rate (Aaqil et al., 2024). The results of the present study indicate that BOPE-packaged fruit maintained higher soluble sugar and reducing sugar contents during storage, likely due to reduced depletion of sugars via respiration and to a delayed ripening process.Fig. 4Effects of different packaging treatments and storage durations on the contents of soluble sugars (A), reducing sugars (B), free amino acids (C), and proline (D) in postharvest peaches. BOPE maintains higher free amino acid and proline contents in peach fruits during storage compared to PE film

Amino acids can serve as antioxidants, inhibiting oxidative processes through various mechanisms. Several free amino acids, including γ-aminobutyric acid (GABA), aspartate, and phenylalanine, have been reported to play roles in managing abiotic and biotic stress during postharvest storage. Some amino acids act as flavoring agents or contribute to the formation of flavor compounds (Pott et al., 2020). Here, the content of free amino acids increased over time, and BOPE-packaged fruit showed significantly higher levels than PE-packaged and control fruit. At 6 DAS, this content in BOPE-packaged fruit was 1.55 and 1.36 times higher than that in control and PE-packaged fruit, respectively (Fig. 4C). Proline, a well-known stress-related amino acid, enhances stress tolerance through osmotic adjustment, membrane stabilization, and ROS scavenging (Renzetti et al., 2025). The proline content of peaches increased with storage time and was highest in BOPE-packaged fruit. At 6 and 9 DAS, the proline content of BOPE-packaged fruit was 1.70 and 1.47 times higher than that in control fruit and 1.25 and 1.22 times higher than that in PE-packaged fruit (Fig. 4D). The higher levels of free amino acids and proline observed in BOPE-packaged fruit were consistent with findings in grapes stored in CO₂-enriched atmospheres (Vazquez-Hernandez et al., 2018). Higher amino acid levels are likely to have contributed to reduced fungal decay and greater resistance to storage-related stresses, contributing to the overall postharvest quality of BOPE-packaged peaches.

PCA and correlation analysis show BOPE regulates key metabolites and preserves postharvest peach quality better than PE film

PCA was performed on the contents of metabolites and bioactive compounds in postharvest peach fruit from the three treatment groups. The results are summarized in Table S1 and presented visually in Fig. 5A. The first two principal components, PC1 and PC2, accounted for 43.8 % and 38.2 % of the total variance, respectively. Collectively explaining 82 % of total metabolomic variation, which covers the major biological processes affected by postharvest treatments. PC1 was primarily influenced by MDA levels and soluble sugar contents, whereas PC2 was primarily influenced by phenylalanine (Phe) and MDA contents, hinting at strong correlations between MDA content and the contents of other measured metabolites. The strong influence of MDA on both PC1 and PC2 suggested that oxidative stress, as reflected in MDA levels, might be a major driver of variation in the dataset. Indeed, previous studies have demonstrated its relevance as a biomarker of lipid peroxidation and oxidative stress during storage (Morales & Munné-Bosch, 2019). The influence of MDA and soluble sugars on PC1 is also as key indicators of postharvest treatment effects. Similarly, the significant weight of Phe in PC2 highlights its importance as a metabolic marker linked to phenolic biosynthesis and oxidative responses. To examine the relationships among fruit quality parameters during postharvest storage, Pearson correlation coefficients were calculated between all pairs of measured indicators (Fig. 5B). Fruit firmness had a strong positive correlation with L* value (r = 0.95), anthocyanin content (r = 0.98), carotenoid content (r = 0.87), and flavonoid content (r = 0.89). Fruit softening during storage was thus accompanied by concurrent declines in color-related parameters, reflecting the physiological and biochemical changes that occur during postharvest and lead to a decline in appearance quality. POD activity had a strong negative correlation with MDA content (r = −0.89), suggesting that higher POD activity helped to mitigate oxidative stress and thus reduce lipid peroxidation. By contrast, POD activity had a strong positive correlation with the contents of plant pigments such as anthocyanins (r = 0.80). Free amino acid content was strongly correlated with reducing sugar content (r = 0.86). These results highlight the interdependent relationships among key indicators of fruit softness, senescence, and metabolic activity. As fruit senescence occurs during storage, the breakdown of complex carbohydrates like starch leads to an increase in reducing sugars such as glucose and fructose. Simultaneously, protein degradation releases free amino acids. These processes are interconnected through central metabolic pathways like glycolysis and the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle. For instance, glycolytic intermediates serve as precursors for amino acid biosynthesis, and amino acid catabolism can feed into the TCA cycle (Pott et al., 2020), influencing sugar metabolism. This interdependence can result in correlated changes in sugar and amino acid levels during storage.

Several enriched KEGG pathways were identified among the DEGs, including glutathione metabolism (ko00480) and flavonoid biosynthesis (ko00941). The expression levels of DEGs from these pathways were plotted as heatmaps to visualize their transcriptional responses to packaging treatments (Fig. 8). Genes encoding 7-deoxyloganetin glucosyltransferase and a CASP-like protein (Prupe.6G190100 and Prupe.5G013700) showed more than 4-fold upregulation only in BOPE-packaged fruit compared with control fruit. The 7-deoxyloganetin glucosyltransferase enzyme is involved in the biosynthesis of loganin and secologanin, monoterpenoid indole alkaloids that play an important role in delaying senescence (Asada et al., 2013). Casparian strip membrane domain proteins (CASPs) are localized to the Casparian strip and are thought to promote localized lignin polymerization while preventing ROS leakage into the cytoplasm or adjacent tissues (Emonet & Hay, 2022). A CASP gene was strongly upregulated in BOPE-packaged fruit, suggesting that CASP-mediated ROS homeostasis and cell-wall reinforcement might enhance stress resistance and delay fruit softening, though this mechanism remains to be experimentally validated. Future studies of known CASP interactors (e.g., Rab GTPases, EXO70 subunits) might reveal novel targets for the improvement of fruit shelf life CYP75B2 (Prupe.5G203600), a gene involved in flavonoid biosynthesis, was upregulated in BOPE-packaged fruit. Flavonoids are crucial secondary metabolites with essential roles in abiotic and biotic stress tolerance (Shomali et al., 2022). The upregulation of CYP75B2 may therefore influence the fruit's ability to respond to environmental conditions, further supporting the notion that BOPE packaging can enhance fruit stress resistance. GSTU9 (Prupe.5G192700) and GSTU10 (Prupe.5G192600), which encode glutathione S-transferases (GSTs), and HST (Prupe.3G101800), which encodes shikimate O-hydroxycinnamoyltransferase, were upregulated in both BOPE- and PE-packaged fruit. GSTs are well-known for their ability to scavenge ROS (Chan & Lam, 2014), and their upregulation suggests that both BOPE and PE packaging may enhance the fruit's capacity to alleviate oxidative stress. Upregulation of shikimate O-hydroxycinnamoyltransferase in BOPE- and PE-packaged fruit is consistent with greater cell-wall rigidity and increased firmness during storage. LAR (Prupe.1G305100), which encodes leucoanthocyanidin reductase, was upregulated only in PE-packaged fruit. This enzyme catalyzes the conversion of leucocyanidin to (+)-catechin, a key step in the biosynthesis of proanthocyanidins (Liu et al., 2016). The specific upregulation of LAR in PE-packaged fruits may lead to increased proanthocyanidin accumulation, which could influence the fruit's astringency and overall quality. Conclusion

BOPE film has been shown to provide a novel packaging solution that outperforms conventional PE film in maintaining peach firmness, color attributes, antioxidant content, and nutritional components during postharvest storage. Future research should investigate the broader application of BOPE materials to diverse fruit species and explore the optimization of their manufacturing and application processes to enable commercial scalability and meet evolving market demands.
