Health care discrimination and self‐reported health in transgender, gender nonconforming, and nonbinary individuals with cancer
Authors: Daniel Sabater Minarim, Kaitlyn Lew, Suraj Rajan, Kylie M. Morgan, Sakshith Reddy Chintala, Jennifer T. Anger, Carol Y. Ochoa‐Dominguez, Matthew P. Banegas, Brent S. Rose, Paul Riviere
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThere is limited information about care experiences and health outcomes among transgender, gender‐nonconforming, and nonbinary (TGNCNB) individuals with cancer. This study quantifies experienced health care discrimination among TGNCNB individuals living with cancer and its impact on their health.MethodsThis cross‐sectional analysis used data from the All of Us Research Program on individuals with cancer. The authors performed propensity score matching (1:5) to balance TGNCNB and cisgender individuals by sociodemographic factors and cancer site. Health care discrimination and health were assessed using the Discrimination in Medical Settings Scale and the Overall Health survey. They used multivariable logistic regression models to adjust for sociodemographic characteristics.ResultsThe cohort included 1476 participants, of which 246 (17%) identified as TGNCNB. TGNCNB participants had greater odds of reporting feeling unheard by providers (odds ratio [OR], 2.38; 95% confidence interval [CI], 1.79–3.17), treated with less respect (OR, 2.61; 95% CI, 1.91–3.57), receiving poorer service (OR, 2.39; 95% CI, 1.73–3.31), and providers acting afraid (OR, 2.32; 95% CI, 1.37–3.93) compared to their cisgender counterparts. In adjusted models, TGNCNB identity was associated with increased odds of experiencing any health care discrimination (OR, 2.42; 95% CI, 1.81–3.25), and discrimination was associated with self‐reported poor health (OR, 3.24; 95% CI, 2.58–4.07).DiscussionThe findings of this study suggest that in the TGNCNB population, increased rates of health care discrimination are associated with poorer health outcomes, which may perpetuate medical mistrust and decrease patient‐centric quality of care overall. Future research and policy efforts should identify actionable interventions to advance equitable cancer care for TGNCNB individuals.

INTRODUCTION

Limited data exist on the care experiences and health outcomes of transgender, gender‐nonconforming, and nonbinary (TGNCNB) cancer survivors. 1 Approximately 0.8% of US adults (over 2.1 million individuals) identify as transgender, with roughly one‐third identifying as nonbinary. 2 Among the 17 million cancer survivors in the United States, an estimated 62,530 are transgender, yet their clinical experiences and outcomes remain largely undocumented. 3 Between 2012 and 2022, approximately 0.8% of all projects funded by the National Institutes of Health (NIH) were designated for sexual and gender minority (SGM) health projects, and most of these NIH‐funded SGM projects focused on HIV/AIDS (65.5%). 4 Thus, there is an unmet need to investigate and better understand how TGNCNB individuals may have different experiences in cancer care and long‐term survivorship support, especially in the context of implicit bias and discrimination against sexual and gender minorities.

Studies have shown that lesbian, gay, bisexual, transgender, queer, and more (LGBTQ+) cancer survivors experience worse health‐related quality of life, greater distress, and are underserved in supportive services compared to cisgender and heterosexual survivors. 5 , 6 In a national study of LGBTQ+ cancer survivors, a majority had reported unmet needs, with over half reporting unaddressed psychological and sexuality needs. 7

Within health care, TGNCNB patients often face discrimination, and there is a lack of accurate, complete gender documentation and coordination with gender‐affirming care. 8 , 9 For these reasons, many TGNCNB individuals are hesitant to approach health care providers and perceive medical environments as unwelcoming, forgoing disclosing their gender identity to avoid inherent bias, potential discrimination, and even violence. 10 , 11

Compounded with hesitations of TGNCNB individuals to approach health care environments, many oncologists feel ill‐equipped and unprepared to care for LGBTQ+ survivors. 12 Formal, standardized training in LGBTQ+ oncology care, especially for TGNCNB individuals, is lacking for health care providers. 13 In a National Cancer Institute survey study in 2019, one in five oncologists across the nation reported confidence in their knowledge and in providing TGNCNB cancer survivors and the majority (70.4%) were interested in receiving tailored training for LGBTQ+ cancer care. 14 Oncology clinicians who feel unprepared to care for TGNCNB patients are unlikely to provide quality, affirming care or shared decision‐making that privileges patient values and preferences. 9 , 15 This gap in training represents an unmet need, as they may contribute to experiences of health care discrimination among TGNCNB patients, experiences that have yet to be systematically quantified in the oncology setting.

Structural social determinants of health, including area‐level socioeconomic deprivation, may further exacerbate disparities experienced by TGNCNB individuals in cancer care. 16 TGNCNB populations are disproportionately affected by poverty and housing instability and have an increased likelihood of residing in socioeconomically deprived areas. 17 Area deprivation has been independently associated with reduced access to quality health care and worse health outcomes. 18 , 19 Together, gender‐based discrimination and socioeconomic disadvantage may compound barriers to equitable cancer care and survivorship.

This study sought to quantify the gaps in perceived health care discrimination among cancer survivors, hypothesizing that TGNCNB individuals would report higher rates of discrimination, which would contribute to poorer self‐reported health outcomes. Drawing on intersectionality theory, which proposes that social identities such as gender identity, race/ethnicity, and socioeconomic status interact to create unique experiences of discrimination and health outcomes, we controlled multiple factors that may compound disadvantages in health care settings. 20 For this, we used a matched‐cohort design, comparing TGNCNB patients to cisgender patients while matching on socioeconomic factors, including race, ethnicity, income, education, and insurance status.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Data source and research platform

Our cross‐sectional analysis used data from the All of Us Research Program, a nationwide initiative that aims to enroll a diverse group of at least 1 million individuals in the United States to help accelerate biomedical research and improve health outcomes. 21 Since May 2018, this program has collected extensive biomedical information, including patient demographics, electronic health records (EHRs), health questionnaires, social determinants of health surveys, physical measurements, digital health technology, and biospecimen analysis. 17 , 18 Data for the current study were extracted from the curated data repository version 8 of the All of Us Researcher Workbench with a data cutoff date of October 1, 2023. The program protocol follows the Declaration of Helsinki and is approved by the NIH institutional review board. Informed consent is received from all participants, and their data are coded for de‐identification.

Participants

The All of Us Research Program enrolls individuals residing in the United States who can provide informed consent. We included data on All of Us participants who reported a cancer diagnosis in the Personal and Family History questionnaire and completed the Basics and Overall Health Survey questionnaire; individuals missing data on their self‐reported health, discrimination in medical settings, and marital status were excluded (Figure S1). Individuals who specified cancers of reproductive organs (cervix, uterus, ovary, prostate, and testis) and breast tissue were categorized into “cancers of reproductive organs and breast tissue.”

Demographic measures

Demographic information was collected from the All of Us The Basics and Overall Health surveys. Variables included age at the time of the survey, gender identity, sexual orientation, race/ethnicity, marital status, annual income level, level of education, and health insurance status.

Gender identity was assessed using the question “What is your current gender identity?” with options including Man, Woman, Nonbinary, Transgender, and additional gender‐diverse identities. Participants selecting Nonbinary, Transgender, or any nonbinary gender option were classified as TGNCNB; those selecting only Man or Woman were classified as cisgender. Sexual orientation was categorized as heterosexual (selecting Straight only) or nonheterosexual (all other responses). Race/ethnicity was dichotomized as White or Black, Indigenous, and People of Color (BIPOC), which included all participants who did not identify as White only. Marital status was categorized as married or not married. Complete survey questions, response options, and variable derivation procedures are provided in Table S1.

Household annual income was self‐reported and grouped into four categories: <$25,000, $25,000–$50,000, $50,000–$100,000, and >$100,000. Level of education was categorized into advanced degree, high school degree, and less than high school, based on participants' responses.

Participants' health insurance was self‐reported and classified into three categories: private (employer‐based or directly purchased insurance), public (Medicare, Medicaid, TRICARE, VA, or Indian Health Service), or uninsured.

To incorporate participants’ social adversity, we included their area deprivation index (ADI). This index is derived from a principal components analysis of six census tract‐level socioeconomic measures from the 2023 American Community Survey 5‐year estimates, including median household income, poverty level, educational attainment, health insurance coverage, public assistance receipt, and housing vacancy. 22 The scores range from 0 to 1, with higher values indicating greater community deprivation. We categorized participants as high ADI (above the cohort’s median) or low ADI (below the median).

Cohort matching

For this study, the gender minority cohort was defined as TGNCNB individuals. To ensure comparable groups and minimize potential confounding factors, we performed propensity score matching using logistic regression to balance TGNCNB and cisgender individuals. This matching process incorporated covariates including age, education, income, race, insurance, and cancer site. We applied K‐nearest neighbor (KNN) matching at a 1:5 ratio to match each TGNCNB individual with five cisgender individuals based on the calculated propensity scores. All subsequent analyses to assess group differences were conducted using this matched data set.

Exposure—health care discrimination

Health care discrimination was assessed using the Discrimination in Medical Settings Scale (DMS) from the All of Us Social Determinants of Health survey. 23 This scale included seven questions asking participants how often they experienced specific discriminatory behaviors when visiting a doctor's office or other health care providers. These questions assessed general health care experiences and were not specific to a particular provider or care setting. These questions included: being treated with less courtesy, less respect, receiving poorer service than others, having a physician or nurse act as if they thought the participant was not smart, having a physician or nurse act as if they are afraid of the participant, having a physician or nurse act as if they were better than the participant, and feeling like a physician or nurse was not listening them.

Participants could respond to these questions with: “Never,” “Rarely,” “Sometimes,” “Most of the time,” and “Always.” For each of the seven questions, respondents who answered “Sometimes,” “Most of the time,” or “Always” were considered to have experienced health care discrimination for that specific question. A binary variable was also created for experiencing health care discrimination called “Overall Discriminated.” Participants who reported experiencing discrimination (responding “Sometimes,” “Most of the time,” or “Always”) on at least one item of the DMS were classified as having experienced overall discrimination (Table S1).

Outcome—self‐reported health status

Self‐reported health status was gauged using the Overall Health survey. The survey included three questions asking participants to rate their physical, mental, and general health (Table S1).

Responses were on a 5‐point scale ranging from “Poor” (1) to “Excellent” (5). Participants who reported their physical, mental, or general health as “Fair” or “Poor” to any of the categories were categorized into an encompassing Overall Health category as “poor health.” As a sensitivity analysis, we also examined mental health and general health outcomes separately using the same hierarchical regression approach (Tables S2 and S3).

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were calculated for participant demographic, socioeconomic, discrimination, and health status characteristics. For the descriptive statistics (Table 1; Figure 1), we used three gender identity categories (TGNCNB, Man, Woman) to demonstrate the composition of our matched cohort. Analysis of variance and χ2 tests were conducted to assess differences in median age (used due to non‐normal distribution) and in categorical variables across gender identity groups. For all multivariable regression models, cisgender men and women were combined into a single reference group for binary comparisons with TGNCNB individuals. After controlling for demographic and socioeconomic factors, multivariable logistic regression models were used to explore the association between health care discrimination and self‐reported poor health. We also reported point estimates as odds ratios (ORs) and their 95% confidence intervals (CIs). All the statistical tests were two‐sided, at the significance level p < .05. All analyses were performed using Python version 3.8.1 in Jupyter Notebook environment within the All of Us Researcher Workbench.

RESULTS

Demographic characteristics

The cohort totaled 1476 patients, of which 246 (17%) identified as TGNCNB. Among the 246 TGNCNB cancer survivors, 63 (25.6%) identified as transgender and 183 (74.4%) identified gender nonconforming and nonbinary. The median (interquartile range [IQR]) age for TGNCNB participants was 55 (38–67) years. Overall, 73% identified as White, and 27% identified as BIPOC. Among our cohort, health insurance coverage was distributed as follows: 48% private, 48% public, and 4% uninsured. Over two‐fifths (42%) were married. Twenty‐one percent reported an annual income below $25,000, and 54% resided in areas of high socioeconomic deprivation. Close to 30% of TGNCNB individuals had cancers of reproductive organs and breast tissue, and 28% identified as heterosexual (Table 1).

Health care discrimination

TGNCNB participants had a higher prevalence of self‐reported overall health care discrimination (63.8%), compared to cisgender women (48.5%) and cisgender men (29.8%) (χ2: p < .001). Across the specific types of discrimination, “not being listened to by a provider” was reported by 55.0% of TGNCNB participants, more than double the rate among cisgender men (21.2%). The proportion of TGNCNB participants who reported that “providers acted superior” (40.3%), “treated them with less respect” (35.9%), and “assumed they were not smart” (34.5%) was higher compared to their cisgender counterparts (Figure 1).

In separate multivariable models adjusted for ADI and marital status for each discrimination question, TGNCNB individuals reported consistently elevated odds of experiencing health care discrimination. Compared to cisgender individuals, TGNCNB participants had greater odds of being treated with less courtesy (OR, 2.46; 95% CI, 1.79–3.37), treated with less respect (OR, 2.61; 95% CI, 1.91–3.57), perceiving a provider acted afraid (OR, 2.32; 95% CI, 1.37–3.93), or acted superior (OR, 2.51; 95% CI, 1.85–3.40). TGNCNB participants also had greater odds of being perceived as not smart (OR, 2.24; 95% CI, 1.64–3.06), receiving poorer service (OR, 2.40; 95% CI, 1.74–3.30), and not being listened to by providers (OR, 2.38; 95% CI, 1.79–3.17) compared to cisgender individuals (Figure 2).

Predictors of health care discrimination

Table S2 shows the associations between included predictors and overall health care discrimination in a multivariable model adjusted for ADI and marital status. TGNCNB identity was the strongest predictor of overall health care discrimination, showing about double the odds compared to cisgender identity (OR, 2.42; 95% CI, 1.81–3.25, p < .01). Participants who lived in a high‐ADI region had 41% higher odds of reporting overall health care discrimination (OR, 1.41; 95% CI, 1.13–1.75, p < .01) compared to those in a low‐ADI region. Being married was protective against discrimination (OR, 0.56; 95% CI, 0.48–0.74, p < .01).

Predictors of self‐reported poor health

Table 2 presents the results from the hierarchical regression model, which evaluates predictors of self‐reported poor health. In the unadjusted model, TGNCNB individuals had more than twice the odds of reporting poor health compared to cisgender individuals (OR, 2.13; 95% CI, 1.60–2.83, p < .01). The association remained significant in the fully adjusted model. Any health care discrimination (OR, 1.73; 95% CI, 1.29–2.33, p < .01), living in areas with high deprivation (OR, 1.72; 95% CI, 1.28–2.33, p < .01), and being married (OR, 1.71; 95% CI, 1.27–2.32, p < .01) were all associated with TGNCNB having increased odds of reporting poor health in the hierarchical model. We also performed a multivariable model for poor health, adjusted for health care discrimination, ADI, and marital status (Table S3). Experiencing health care discrimination had the largest effect (3.24 OR; 95% CI, 2.58–4.07, p < .01). TGNCNB identity was linked to higher odds of poor health (OR, 1.71; 95% CI, 1.27–2.32, p < .01). Living in high‐ADI areas was also associated with higher odds of poor health (OR, 1.31; 95% CI, 1.03–1.63, p < .01). Being married was associated with lower odds of reporting poor health (OR, 0.51; 95% CI, 0.41‐0.64, p < .01). In the sensitivity analysis that matched on sex assigned at birth, in addition to the other covariates, results were consistent with the primary analysis. TGNCNB identity remained significantly associated with poor health outcomes in the fully adjusted model (OR, 2.08; 95% CI, 1.54–2.80, p < .01), and health care discrimination remained the strongest predictor of poor health (OR, 3.21; 95% CI, 2.57–4.02, p < .01) (Tables S6 and S7).

DISCUSSION

Our study identifies experiences of health care discrimination among cancer survivors, with TGNCNB survivors reporting notably higher rates than their cisgender peers. These findings underscore the higher levels of overall health care discrimination reported by TGNCNB cancer survivors, including specific experiences such as being ignored, receiving poorer service, provider acting afraid, and perceiving provider superiority. We found a strong association between health care discrimination and poor health outcomes, with TGNCNB identity independently linked to worse self‐reported health. This persistence suggests that disparities in health among TGNCNB cancer survivors are not fully explained by health care discrimination alone and reflect additional structural or social factors. Overall, our findings indicate that health care discrimination is a significant and independent predictor of poor self‐reported health among TGNCNB cancer survivors.

These findings align with prior research, highlighting the systemic barriers faced by TGNCNB individuals during their cancer care continuum. In our study, 63.8% of TGNCNB cancer survivors experienced any form of health care discrimination. This is particularly critical, given that previous literature has shown that 23% of transgender individuals avoid health care due to the fear of discrimination and stigma from the medical staff. 17 , 24 We found that experiencing health care discrimination within our cohort of cancer survivors was strongly associated with an individual reporting poor health (OR, 3.24; 95% CI, 2.58–4.07, p < .01). TGNCNB individuals also had higher odds of poor health (OR, 1.71; 95% CI, 1.27–2.32, p < .01), which could be attributed to other clinical factors or other stressors/comorbidities, as stated in the gender minority stress theory. 25 , 26 Consistent with prior literature demonstrating that mental health may be more impacted among TGNCNB individuals with cancer, our sensitivity analysis showed that TGNCNB identity was more associated with poor mental health (OR, 1.98; 95% CI,1.44–2.72) than poor general health (OR, 1.5; 95% CI, 1.1–2.04) in fully adjusted models (Tables S4 and S5). 27 , 28

The health care discrimination and poor health outcomes documented in our study are compounded by several systemic barriers in cancer care delivery for TGNCNB individuals. Because of the limited cancer screening guidelines for the TGNCNB individuals, they are frequently diagnosed at later cancer stages, and are less likely to receive timely treatments, consequently leading to worse survival outcomes. 29 Developing and implementing inclusive screening protocols tailored to this population could address this critical gap in early detection.

Additionally, limited collection of gender identity and the underrepresentation of TGNCNB individuals in clinical trials and national cancer databases, contribute to gaps in representation and evidence generation. 30 Systematic collection of gender identity data and increased trial enrollment would support evidence‐based care and help providers deliver more informed, affirming treatment. At the institutional level, structural inclusion may also involve ensuring electronic health records accommodate chosen names and pronouns, creating inclusive intake forms and clinical signage, and establishing clear policies that protect TGNCNB patients from discrimination. 31

Furthermore, stressors and comorbidities that disproportionately affect minoritized communities, outlined by the gender minority stress theory, such as high rates of HIV infection, substance use, and psychological distress, further complicate cancer care and survivorship among TGNCNB individuals. 32 , 33 , 34 , 35 , 36 , 37 Addressing these interconnected barriers through standardized gender identity documentation, TGNCNB‐inclusive screening protocols, and expanded provider education represents an essential step toward reducing discrimination. However, our findings suggest that interventions targeting discrimination alone may be insufficient to eliminate self‐reported health disparities among TGNCNB cancer survivors. Comprehensive approaches that address the broader social determinants of health and the systemic barriers faced by this population are needed.

There are several limitations to consider. The cross‐sectional nature of the data limits our ability to establish causal relationships between health care discrimination and self‐reported health. This exploratory study aimed to characterize health care experiences among TGNCNB cancer survivors; formal causal mediation analysis would require stronger assumptions and, ideally, longitudinal data with treatment and adherence information. In the All of Us database, health care discrimination and health status were self‐reported, which may be subject to social desirability bias and recall bias, as participants over‐ or underreport their experiences during their cancer care continuum. Additionally, the study data were drawn only from the All of Us Research Program and may not represent the TGNCNB population in the United States. Despite these limitations, our findings provide critical insights into the health care experiences of TGNCNB cancer survivors in a large database, showing the urgency of addressing discrimination to improve health equity and quality of life for this underserved population.

Building on these findings, policy and clinical practice changes must focus on structural inclusion and clinician preparedness to reduce the disproportionate amount of health care discrimination experienced by TGNCNB cancer survivors. 30 , 38 Given the reports of clinicians lacking the knowledge or comfort in managing transgender health, including evidence that over one quarter of transgender individuals report their clinician knows little or almost nothing about transgender care, providing TGNCNB cultural humility trainings is urgently needed. 39 , 40 , 41 Although National Comprehensive Cancer Network guidelines should reflect greater awareness of the medical and psychosocial needs of sexual and gender minority individuals, dedicated guidelines for this population have not yet been developed. 42 Emerging resources, however, provide frameworks for culturally affirming care, including practical recommendations from the Multinational Association of Supportive Care in Cancer, guidance on cancer risk reduction for transgender individuals with hereditary cancer, and educational materials from the American Cancer Society. 43 , 44 , 45 In addition, the representation of TGNCNB patients in clinical trials and the establishment of protocols for routine sexual orientation and gender identity data collection would enable more comprehensive, tailored cancer treatment. 30 This policy shift will enable oncology providers to create a safer and affirming environment that prompts shared decision‐making and timely care. Future research should prioritize using retrospective or prospective cohorts that implement gender identity collection to investigate cancer screening, treatment efficacy, and outcomes for TGNCNB individuals to create inclusive guidelines and treatment. Additionally, studies with larger samples of TGNCNB individuals from diverse racial/ethnic backgrounds should employ formal intersectional analysis examining the association between gender minority status and health care discrimination or health outcomes.
