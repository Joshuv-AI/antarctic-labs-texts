KAT5-mediated acetylation of PC4 facilitates DNA repair by promoting chromatin reorganization
Authors: Aayushi Agrawal, Sweta Sikder, Siddharth Singh, Rakesh Kumar Sharma, Nikhil Pallaprolu, Kalyan Mitra, Sourav Mondal, Rupa Mukhopadhyay, Jayanta Sarkar, Ramalingam Peraman, V Ravichandiran, Tapas K Kundu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractHuman positive coactivator 4 (PC4) is a highly abundant non-histone chromatin protein involved in diverse cellular processes, including transcription regulation, genome organization, autophagy, B-cell differentiation, neurogenesis, DNA repair, etc. Most PC4 is phosphorylated in cells, which interacts with core histones and the linker histone H1 to confer the compact heterochromatin state of the genome. Knocking down PC4 at both cellular and organismal levels leads to significant chromatin decondensation, altered epigenetic landscape, enhanced autophagy, and increased DNA damage susceptibility. Here, we report that besides p300, PC4 also gets acetylated by DNA repair, facilitating lysine acetyltransferase KAT5 (Tip60) at a specific lysine residue (PC4K80) when the cells are subjected to DNA damage. The vulnerability of DNA in PC4 devoid cells was substantially reduced by reintroducing wild-type PC4 to the cells but not the mutant PC4 (PC4K80R), defective in KAT5-mediated acetylation. High-resolution microscopy techniques, including transmission electron microscopy and atomic force microscopy, are employed to visualize chromatin structural changes in response to DNA damage and repair in a Tip60-mediated PC4 acetylation-dependent manner. Presumably, KAT5-mediated acetylation of PC4 at K80 residue facilitates access to the damaged DNA by altering chromatin structures at damage sites, thus promoting DNA repair. This process could be highly significant both in cancer and in aging.

Introduction

The human genome is constantly threatened by various genotoxic agents that can cause severe damage to our genetic material, DNA. DNA double-strand break is the most detrimental form of DNA damage. Erroneous or inefficient repairing of the genome upon such genotoxic insults can cause changes in the DNA sequence itself, which, if not corrected, can trigger genomic instability and thereby lead to a harmful cellular environment and, ultimately, cell death [1]. However, as a safeguard mechanism, cells counteract these adverse effects by activating the DNA damage response (DDR), which causes a synchronized series of events that regulate cell cycle progression and repair of DNA lesions. The tight packaging of the genomic DNA very often poses a threat to the DNA repair pathway in living cells. Therefore, cells modulate the chromatin structure at the site of damage with the help of various factors, such as post-translational histone modifications and adenosine triphosphate-dependent chromatin remodellers. These factors, in concert, help the DNA repair machinery to modulate the chromatin structure, thereby increasing the accessibility of the repair machinery to lesions embedded in chromatin [2]. Histone post-translational modifications (PTMs) constitute an essential layer of epigenomic regulation in the process of DNA damage repair. One such modification is H3K9me3, which is enriched in constitutive heterochromatin regions and is shown to increase at damaged sites, both in heterochromatin and euchromatin regions [3–5]. The players involved in the H3K9me3 pathway, such as writers (SUV39H1/2, SETDB1) and readers (HP1, Tip60), were shown to promote DSB repair by the homologous recombination (HR) pathway.

About 10% of all nucleosomes in eukaryotic chromatin consist of histone H2A variant H2AX instead of canonical H2A. Previous studies have shown that, following the induction of DSBs, Poly(ADP-ribose) (PAR) polymerase-1 (PARP-1) acts as a key DNA damage sensor. In response to DSBs, PARP1 is rapidly activated and catalyses the synthesis of PAR chains on itself and other target proteins. This acts as a signalling mechanism that facilitates the recruitment of DNA repair factors at the site of damage, like the MRN complex (MRE11–RAD50–NBS1). MRN complex binds to the broken DNA ends and recruits ATM, ATR, and/or DNA protein kinase (the PIK-family protein kinases), resulting in the initial phosphorylation of H2AX [6, 7]. The adaptor protein MDC1 then associates with phosphorylated H2AX (γ.H2AX) and recruits additional activated ATMs to the sites of DSBs [8]. This positive feedback loop leads to the expansion of the γ.H2AX region surrounding DSBs to up to 2 Mb and provides docking sites for other DNA damage and repair proteins, including 53BP1 and BRCA1.

Tip60 (KAT5) plays a crucial role in DSB repair, and its inactivation leads to increased sensitivity to ionizing radiation and levels of chromosomal aberrations [9–13]. Tip60 is also required for the activation of the ATM kinase, which phosphorylates multiple DDR proteins, including nbs1, p53, chk2, and SMC1, in response to DSB detection [14]. Further, Tip60 is a haploinsufficient tumor suppressor, and its aberrant expression has been detected in various cancers [15–17]. These studies demonstrate that a critical function of Tip60 is to protect cells from genomic instability and suppress potentially transforming events that can lead to cancer.

In addition to the above-mentioned factors, various non-histone chromatin-associated proteins also play an essential role either by directly interacting with the DNA repair machinery proteins or by regulating the chromatin structure [18]. Human positive coactivator 4, PC4, has been reported to interact with the DNA repair protein XPG and gets recruited early at the DNA damage site [19]. PC4 also prevents mutagenesis and killing by oxidative DNA damage repair by its ssDNA binding ability [20]. Delving more into the molecular repair pathway, it has been shown to enhance the joining of non-homologous end-joining (NHEJ) repair and double-strand break repair activity and also in homologous recombination repair [21, 22]. Interestingly, PC4 interacts with the heterochromatin protein HP1α, in vivo [23]. In this study, we pose the question of whether the DNA repair activity of PC4 is being modulated by any post-translational modifications. Interestingly, we found that the DNA damage-responsive acetyltransferase, KAT5/Tip60, acetylates PC4 at specific sites upon DNA damage. The Tip60-mediated acetylation of PC4 was found to be critical for DNA damage repair, which is presumably involved in regulating the chromatin dynamics and genome stability upon DNA damage [24].

Materials and methods

Filter binding assay

About 1 μg of Histone H4 or Histone H3 was incubated with equivalent activity of baculovirus expressed full-length human Tip60 and p300 at 30°C for 30 min in 2X KAT buffer (1X composition: 50 mM Tris-HCl pH 8.0, 10% glycerol, 1 mM DTT, 1 mM PMSF, 0.1 mM EDTA), 10 mM Na-butyrate, and 1 μl of 2.1 Ci/mmol of [3H]acetyl-CoA. The reaction mixture was then spotted on a Phosphocellulose P-81 filter paper. The radioactive counts were recorded on a Wallac 1409 Liquid scintillation counter.

Gel assay

KAT assays were performed using 1 μg of Histone H3 or 1 μg of recombinant PC4, or 1 μg of p53 incubated in HAT assay buffer at 30°C for 30 min with or without baculovirus expressed recombinant Tip60, 1 μl of 3.3 Ci/mmol of [3H]acetyl-CoA and 10 mM Na-butyrate. To visualize the radiolabelled acetylated protein, the reaction products were resolved electrophoretically on 12% SDS-polyacrylamide gel and subjected to fluorography. The gel was stained with Coomassie to ascertain the presence of protein in equal amounts in each of the reactions and was later dehydrated in DMSO for 1 h. Later, the gel was incubated in scintillation fluid (PPO solution in DMSO) for 30 min and then rehydrated in water for 2 h. The gel was dried using a gel drier and exposed in an X-ray cassette using a film for 7 days in a −80°C cooler. The film was later developed to get intensity profiles for each reaction.

Acetylation of PC4

Mass acetylation of PC4 was carried out using 1 μg of His-PC4. The reaction mixture containing recombinant Tip60 (∼30 000 Counts/μl), 2X KAT buffer, 10mM Na-Butyrate and 1.68 mM acetyl-CoA was incubated at 30°C for 30 min, followed by replenishment with the enzyme and acetyl-CoA every 1 h and then kept for 6–8 h after the final replenishment. A mock acetylation reaction was also set up without the enzyme.

Preparation of the sample

About 1 μg of PC4 was mass acetylated as given in the previous section. The acetylated sample was electrophoresed in a 12% SDS PAGE and was silver-stained by fixing in 40% methanol and 10% glacial acetic acid in water overnight. The gel was then rinsed thrice in 50% ethanol in water. The proteins were sensitized with 0.02% sodium thiosulfate for 2 min and then rinsed in deionized water for a minute. The gel was then incubated with 0.1% silver nitrate for 20 min at room temperature. The gels were then rinsed thrice with deionized water. The gel was developed in freshly prepared 0.04% formalin/2% sodium carbonate (∼4–20 min). The reaction was stopped in 1% glacial acetic acid. The gel was imaged and then stored in 1% glacial acetic acid. The specific band for PC4 was cut, dried and given for mass spectrometric analysis.

Mass spectrometric analysis of in vitro acetylated PC4

The mass spectrometric service of ITSI Biosciences, USA was availed to carry out the PTM analysis. In-gel digestion of the PC4 band was performed using trypsin as the enzyme. After trypsinization, peptides were transferred to another tube, and two more extractions of peptides were done using 1:2 (v/v) 5% formic acid and acetonitrile. Eluates were dried using a speed vac and reconstituted in 2% acetonitrile with 0.1% formic acid. Samples were then loaded onto a PicoFrit C18 nanospray column (New Objective) using a Thermo Scientific Surveyor Autosampler operated in the no-waste injection mode. Peptides were eluted from the column using a linear acetonitrile gradient from 2% to 30% acetonitrile over 90 min, followed by high and low organic washes for another 20 min into an LTQ XL mass spectrometer (Thermo Scientific) via a nanospray source with the spray voltage set to 1.8kV and the ion transfer capillary set at 180°C. A data-dependent Top 5 method was used where a full MS scan from m/z 400–1500 was followed by MS/MS scans on the five most abundant ions, and multistage activation was turned on for detecting acetylated peptides. Raw data files were searched using Proteome Discoverer 1.3 (Thermoscientific) and the SEQUEST algorithm against the most recent species-specific database for Homo sapiens and a custom database with the protein of interest from UniProt. Trypsin was the selected enzyme, allowing for up to three missed cleavages per peptide; Carbamidomethyl Cysteine was used as a static modification, Acetylation on lysine, and Oxidation of Methionine as variable modifications. Proteins were identified when two or more unique peptides had X-correlation scores greater than 1.5, 2.0, and 2.5 for respective charge states of + 1, +2, and + 3 (Supplementary Fig. S1).

Pull-down assay

HEK 293 Cells were harvested, washed with 10 ml of pre-chilled phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), and lysed for 60 min in 1 ml RIPA lysis buffer (150 mM NaCl, 60 mM Tris–HCl, 0.5 mM EDTA, 10% NP-40, pH 7.4) with 1X of protease inhibitor cocktail (Sigma). Next, 1 μg of antibody or IgG was used to bind to the bait proteins for overnight binding at 4 °C on the rotor and then incubated with Protein G Sepharose beads (ab193259) for an additional 5–6 h. Finally, the protein G was washed with 1 ml of RIPA lysis buffer three times at 4°C at 1500 rpm for 2 min and then heated for 10 min at 95°C with 30 μL of 2 × SDS loading buffer. Samples were analysed using SDS-PAGE and western blotting.

Mass spectrometric analysis of PC4 and acetylated-PC4 interacting proteins

The pull-down complexes were then resolved on a 12% SDS PAGE. Each lane of the SDS-PAGE gel was excised into five equally sized segments. Gel pieces were processed using the following protocol: [1] Destained with 50% methanol containing 0.1% acetic acid and washed with 50 mM ammonium bicarbonate followed by acetonitrile; [2] Reduced with 10 mM dithiothreitol at 37°C for an hour followed by alkylation with 55 mM iodoacetamide at RT for 45 min; [3] Digested with trypsin (Thermoscientific) at 37°C for 4 h; [4] Quenched with formic acid and the supernatant was analysed directly without further processing. Each gel digest was analysed by nano LC/MS/MS with a RSLC Nano system (Thermoscientific Ultimate Dionex 3000), MS system (Thermoscientific Orbitrap Exploris 240). Peptides were loaded on a trapping column and eluted over a 75 μm X 25 cm analytical column. Data interpretation was done using Proteome Discoverer, Version 2.5.

Generation of polyclonal antibody (PC4 K80Ac)

In rabbits, polyclonal antibodies were generated by injecting the Keyhole Limpet Hemocyanin (KLH) coupled peptides. New Zealand strains of rabbits were primed with an emulsion of the peptide-KLH conjugate in complete adjuvant. Before the priming, a few ml of blood was collected from the rabbit (control serum). Booster doses were given every 2–3 weeks till the serum showed sufficient strength and specificity against the target protein or modification. A major bleed of 15–20 ml was collected twice while raising the antibody. The serum was separated from the collected blood, and IgG was purified using Protein G Sepharose beads. The IgG was eluted from the beads using 100 mM glycine, pH 3.0, and collected into tubes containing Tris-Cl buffer to neutralize the acidic pH of the elution buffer. The IgG purified was then dialyzed against 50% glycerol in PBS. A peptide was generated spanning the K80Ac site in PC4 and was used to raise K80Ac specific polyclonal Ab of PC4. The peptide was KLH-conjugated KGK (ac) VLIDIREYWMDC (Supplementary Fig. S4).

Comet assay

The comet assay, or single-cell gel electrophoresis assay (SCGE), is a common technique for measuring DNA damage in individual cells. Under an electrophoretic field, damaged cellular DNA (containing fragments and strand breaks) is separated from intact DNA, yielding a classic “comet tail” shape under the microscope. The extent of DNA damage is usually visually estimated by comet tail measurement. Briefly, 0.8% low melting point (LMP) agarose was prepared in saline and was maintained at 37 °C to prevent solidification. Subsequently, individual cells (around 20,000 cells after 48 h of transfection with Flag constructs of PC4 and mutant PC4) were mixed with molten agarose in a 1:10 (v/v) ratio. The resulting suspension was layered onto the frosted slides. The slides were placed on ice for approximately 5 min to solidify the agarose. Subsequently, slides were immersed in lysis solution (2.5 M NaCl, 100 mM EDTA with fresh 1% Triton-X-100 and 10% DMSO) for 1 h to eliminate non-nuclear components. The slides were further immersed in alkaline buffer (300 mM NaOH, 1 mM EDTA, pH 13) for 20 min to allow the DNA to unwind and to convert alkali-labile sites to single-strand breaks. Electrophoresis was conducted for 30 min at 15V and 200 mA (at a rate of 0.6V/cm) using a compact power supply. The slides were gently washed with 0.4 M Tris (pH 7.5) to remove alkali and detergents. The cells were stained with propidium iodide (PI 10mg/ml) and were visualized by confocal scanning microscope, LSM510 META, and ZEN LSM810. Under these conditions, the damaged DNA (containing cleavage and strand breaks) migrates further than intact DNA and produces a “comet tail” shape. Approximately 100–150 images per slide were captured from different imaging fields and were analysed with the appropriate software.

Immunofluorescence microscopy

Cells grown on poly-L-lysine-coated coverslips were fixed with 4% PFA, permeabilized by 1% Triton X-100 in PBS for 10 min, followed by blocking in 5% fetal bovine serum for 45 min. Probing was done with anti-PC4 (ab154852), anti-K80ac PC4 (lab-raised), anti-γ.H2AX (CST-2577S), anti-Flag (Sigma-F3165), and anti-Tip60 (lab-raised) antibodies for 1 h at room temperature, followed by secondary antibodies conjugated to Alexa488 and Alexa 568 (Invitrogen). The cells were stained with 0.1 μg/ml of DAPI (Sigma) in PBS for 5 min to visualize the nuclei. Mounting was done with 70% glycerol. Fluorescence images were taken through a confocal scanning microscope, LSM510 META, and ZEN LSM810.

Cell culture and transfection

HEK 293 and ZR-75–1 cells were obtained from ATCC. HEK293 and ZR-75–1 cells were cultured in DMEM and RPMI 1640 medium, respectively, supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum at 37°C with 5% CO2. According to the manufacturer’s instructions, HEK293 cells were transfected using Lipofectamine 2000 (Invitrogen).

Stable cell line generation

HEK 293 cell lines stably expressing shRNAs targeting the open reading frames (ORFs) of Tip60 (Clone ID: V2LHS_282 664, Dharmacon pGIPZ shRNA; Mature sequence: TTCCATCAGAGCTGTCCTG) and PC4 (Clone ID: V3LHS_331 786, Dharmacon pGIPZ shRNA; Mature sequence: TTTTCTGGAGCAACTTGCT) mRNAs were generated to achieve stable knockdown of Tip60 and PC4, respectively. To control for potential off-target effects related to shRNA transduction, a control cell line expressing a non-silencing shRNA was also established. The non-silencing control shRNA contains a 22-nucleotide sense sequence (5′-ATCTCGCTTGGGCGAGAGTAAG-3′) and a corresponding 22-nucleotide antisense sequence (5′-CTTACTCTCGCCCAAGCGAGAG-3′). This sequence does not match any known mammalian genes (had at least three or more mismatches against any gene as determined via nucleotide alignment/BLAST of 22mer sense sequence). These cell lines were generated using 10 μg of pGIPZ lentiviral shRNAs (targeting PC4/Tip60) co-transfected with helper plasmids (5 μg of psPAX2, 1.5 μg of pRSV-Rev, and 3.5 μg of pCMV-VSV-G) into HEK293T cells using the calcium phosphate method. Forty-eight hours post-transfection, media containing the assembled virus was collected, and its titre was estimated. The desired cell line (here HEK 293) was infected with 105 IU/ml virus. Infected cells were subjected to selection pressure 72 h post-transfection. Cells were first sorted for positive GFP signals, and the GFP-sorted cells were grown in the presence of 3 μg.ml−1 puromycin for five passages to establish the stable cell line. PC4 knockdown cells were then transfected with pFlag-CMV-10PC4 and pFlag-CMV-10K80RPC4 plasmids using lipofectamine, and media change was given within 24 h of transfection and cultured under 1mg/ml dose of G418 selection.

Chromatin isolation

HEK 293 cells were harvested and washed with cold PBST twice. Cells were first treated with TM2 solution (20 mM Tris–HCl pH 8.0, 2 mM MgCl2) containing 0.5% NP40 once for 5 min on ice and then washed with TM2 solution without NP40. 0.1M TE buffer (10 mM Tris–HCl pH 8.0, 0.2 mM EDTA, 100 mM NaCl) was added to loosen up the nuclear pellet and incubated at 37 °C for 5 min. 0.4U of MNase enzyme was added along with 1.5mM CaCl2 for 1 minute at RT, and the reaction was stopped by adding 10mM EGTA. The supernatant was discarded, and the pellet was dissolved in 0.5X PBS and used directly for AFM.

Micrococcal nuclease (MNase) assay

Cells were grown in DMEM medium supplemented with 10% FBS. The nuclei were prepared from cells suspended in hypotonic buffer (10 Tris–HCl, 10 mM KCl and 15 mM MgCl2) followed by 10 min incubation at 4°C. The nuclei were digested with MNase (0.2U) (M0247S) for different time points at 37°C in nuclei digestion buffer (10% glycerol, 10 mM Tris HCl (pH8), 3 mM CaCl2, 150 mM NaCl, 0.2 mM PMSF). The reaction was stopped by adding 10 mM EDTA and proteinase K at 37°C for 30 min, followed by RNaseA treatment for 30 min at 37°C. The DNA was then extracted by phenol-chloroform extraction and ethanol precipitation. About 1 ug of each of the samples was loaded on 1% agarose gel and run for 4–5 h in an Ethidium Bromide free agarose gel electrophoresis system at 50 V for better resolution of the MNase bands. The gels were then stained in Ethidium bromide staining solution for 10 min before visualization in the Chemi-Doc system (Biorad).

AFM imaging

The sample and the buffer solutions were prepared using filtered autoclaved ultrapure Milli-Q water (Millipore) of resistivity 18.2 MΩ.cm at 25°C. The chromatin solution was diluted 200-fold in low salt extraction buffer and kept at 4°C. Subsequently, a 20 μl droplet of the chromatin solution was deposited onto a mica surface that had been pre-treated with (3-aminopropyl) triethoxysilane (APTES) solution in water (APTES: water = 1:10 000 v/v) for 1 min. The chromatin solution was kept in contact with the mica surface for 15 min in a covered petri dish. To remove any unbound chromatin molecules, the substrate was washed with 3 ml (500 μl × 6) of Milli-Q water and subsequently dried using a gentle nitrogen stream. The chromatin-modified mica samples were imaged using intermittent contact or tapping mode at a controlled temperature of 25 ± 1°C and humidity level of 40% using an Asylum Research MFP-3D AFM (Oxford Instruments). The AFM cantilever-tip assembly was cleaned immediately before imaging using a UV-ozone cleaner (Bioforce, Nanosciences). The images were acquired using a μ-masch cantilever with resonance frequency ranging within 250–265 kHz and spring constant ranging within 3.5–27.5 N/m. The range of Z values for all the AFM images is 0–2 nm.

Data acquisition and data analysis

All the AFM images were processed using Igor Pro 6 software. Processing was done minimally and limited to plane-fitting with a linear fit to the background of each line. The cross-section profiles were obtained across the chromatin fibre to estimate the dimensions of the distinguishable nucleosomes. The dimensions of approximately one thousand nucleosomes were analysed, spanning over the eight types of samples. The dimensions were measured as full width at half maximum (FWHM) in the cross-sectional diagrams. Given that tapping mode was employed for imaging and non-damaging imaging settings were utilized, the effect of “tip-induced broadening” is expected to be minimal. To address the “tip shape convolution” factor, the tip-deconvoluted dimension of the nucleosomes was estimated using the formula dt = dm2/8rtip. Here, dt is the true dimension, dm is the measured dimension, and rtip is the radius of curvature of the AFM probe (which is 10 nm as provided by the AFM probe manufacturer).

Transmission electron microscopy

Cells in a T-75 flask were washed with 1X PBS and fixed in 2.5% glutaraldehyde in 0.1 M Phosphate buffer (PB), pH 7.4, at 4°C overnight. Following this, the cells were washed three times with PB and then fixed in 1% osmium tetroxide for 1 h. After post-fixation, the cells were encapsulated in 2% low-melting agarose. The cells were dehydrated in ascending ethanol concentrations, embedded in Spurr resin, and polymerized at 60°C for 24 h. Thin sectioning of the cells was performed using a Leica Ultracut UC7 ultramicrotome to obtain ∼60 nm sections. The ribbon of sections was placed on copper grids and double-stained with uranyl acetate and lead citrate. The grids were analysed under a Jeol JEM-1400 transmission electron microscopy (TEM) (Japan, Tokyo) at 100 kV, and images were acquired using a Gatan Orius CCD camera. The images were analysed using Gatan Micrograph Suite software.

Statistical analysis

The GraphPad Prism software (California, USA) was used to conduct all statistical analysis. All statistical results are presented as the mean ± SD or mean ± SEM, as indicated. P values (* P < 0.05, ** P < 0.01, *** P < 0.001, **** P < 0.0001) were obtained using Student’s t-test (two-tailed) or one/two-way ANOVA from multiple independent biological replicates.

Results

PC4 is a substrate of KAT5 (Tip60) acetyltransferase

Owing to its role in DNA damage, we wanted to investigate PC4 as a potential substrate of the DNA damage-responsive acetyltransferase, KAT5 (Tip60). The bacterially expressed untagged recombinant human PC4 was subjected to in vitro acetylation by both p300 and Tip60 (baculovirus-expressed full-length recombinant His-tagged). p53, a known substrate of Tip60 [25], was taken as a non-histone protein positive control apart from the recombinant histone H3. The reaction mixtures containing equivalent activities of either p300 or Tip60 (as normalized by filter binding assay using Histone H3 and H4, respectively) and the indicated amount of proteins (Fig. 1A) were incubated with [3H]acetyl-CoA for 30 min at 30°C. The reaction mixtures were analysed by autoradiography upon resolving on an SDS-PAGE gel. It was observed that PC4 was not only acetylated by p300 but also with equivalent activity of Tip60 (Fig. 1A, middle panel). Thus, p300 is not an exclusive acetyltransferase that can acetylate PC4 in vitro. We found that, like p53 (Fig. 1A, right panel), PC4 might also be a substrate of the acetyltransferase Tip60. Once PC4 was established as a substrate of acetyltransferase KAT5 (Tip60) by in vitro acetyltransferase assay, we next wanted to identify the specific acetylation sites through mass spectrometric analysis (as described in Materials and Methods). PC4 was mass acetylated and then subjected to mass spectrometric analysis to identify the residues in PC4 that could be acetylated. Our mass spectrometric PTM analysis revealed confident acetylation sites in PC4 (Fig. 1B and Supplementary Fig. S1). We narrowed down to 4 high-scoring acetylated residues (represented in green) in the protein PC4, as indicated in Fig. 1B. The table shows the change in the mass of the peptides due to an acetylated lysine residue obtained by mass spectrometric analysis.

Acetylation of PC4 by Tip60 is abrogated by mutation at K80 residue

Taking a cue from the mass spectrometric data, we wanted to further validate the acetylation sites in PC4 by generating acetylation-defective mutants. Single-site mutants were created for each lysine site (changed to arginine), shown by site-directed mutagenesis (Supplementary Fig. S2). In vitro, an acetylation assay was carried out with these acetylation-defective mutants. Acetylation of PC4 K5R, K80R, and K101R was highly compromised compared to PC4 Wild type (WT) (Fig. 1C, lanes 6 versus 7, 9, and 10, respectively). PC4 K53R showed no change in acetylation with Tip60 compared to the wild-type PC4 (Fig. 1C, lane 8). These data suggest that the K53 residue might not be a significant site of acetylation in PC4 mediated by Tip60. Mock acetylation controls were used (Fig. 1C, lanes 1–5), where, except for the enzyme, all components, including protein, were taken. These data further establish that the acetylation observed is specifically mediated by the enzyme Tip60.

In vitro KAT assay with PC4 acetylation-defective mutants was carried out in a concentration-dependent manner to negate the effects of protein saturation and also to further confirm the mutants that were defective in acetylation by Tip60. A single mutation of lysine to arginine at the 5th amino acid position of PC4 (PC4K5R) completely abolished its ability to get acetylated. Even at higher concentrations, no band was observed. (Supplementary Fig. S3A, lanes 6 and 7). PC4K80R and PC4K101R also showed compromised acetylation ability, which was not affected by the different concentrations of the protein used (Supplementary Fig. S3B, lanes 6–9). Mutation at K53 to arginine did not affect the acetylation ability of PC4 by Tip60 (Supplementary Fig. S3A, lanes 8 and 9). Thus, lysine residues at 5, 80, and 101 appeared to be critically important for the acetylation of PC4 by KAT5.

PC4 is also a substrate of acetyltransferase p300 (as shown in Fig. 1A). To further validate the lysine residues in PC4, identified as substrates of acetyltransferase Tip60 and not p300, KAT assay was performed with each acetylation site mutant and p300. Comparing Fig. 1C and 1D, PC4 K5R showed reduction in acetylation by both p300 (Fig. 1C, lane 7) as well as Tip60 (Fig. 1D, lane 7) whereas PC4 K53R showed no drastic change in acetylation by either p300 (Fig. 1D, lane 8) or Tip60 (Fig. 1C, lane 8). PC4 K101R also showed compromised acetylation ability both by p300 (Fig. 1D, lane 10) and Tip60 (Fig. 1C, lane 10). However, PC4 K80R shows a drastic reduction in Tip60-mediated acetylation (Fig. 1C, lane 9), but when subjected to acetylation by p300 (Fig. 1D, lane 9), it showed a considerable amount of acetylation. Thus, these findings suggest PC4 K80 might be a putative site of acetylation, specific for Tip60. The highly conserved K80 residue of PC4 dwells in its C-terminal domain, which is essential for its single-strand DNA binding activity, which in turn is critical for its DNA repair function [21, 22].

PC4 gets acetylated at K80 upon DNA damage condition

To understand the physiological role of KAT5/Tip60 mediated acetylation of PC4, we raised a site-specific (K80Ac) polyclonal antibody to detect the in vivo acetylated PC4. The polyclonal antibody was tested for its specificity and sensitivity for Tip60-mediated acetylation of PC4 at the K80 site through an in vitro immunoblotting assay (Supplementary Fig. S4). Considering the role of Tip60 in DNA damage and repair that has been well-characterized [26, 27], we investigated PC4 acetylation at K80 sites under cisplatin and actinomycin D treatment conditions. Actinomycin D is a DNA damage-inducing agent that mostly forms DNA intercalation, producing radical oxygen species, and thereby causing genotoxic insult to the DNA. It has also been shown to induce the accumulation of γ.H2AX, which forms a complex with DNA repair proteins in the cells [26]. Cisplatin interacts with DNA and stalls cell proliferation, thereby activating the DDR. Thus, both small molecules were administered to mimic a DNA damage state in the cells. γ.H2AX level was checked by immunofluorescence to measure the extent of DNA damage upon the cisplatin and actinomycin D treatment (Fig. 2). As expected, we observed elevated intensity of γ.H2AX upon the compound administration (Fig. 2A versus 2B and 2C, γ.H2AX panel; and Fig. 2E). The cellular PC4K80ac mark was found to be enhanced upon treatment with cisplatin (Fig. 2A versus 2B, PC4K80ac panel; and Fig. 2D) and also with actinomycin D (Fig. 2A versus 2C, PC4K80ac panel; and Fig. 2D) as revealed by immunofluorescence images using PC4K80ac antibody. However, no significant change in PC4 expression was observed upon DNA damage (Fig. 2F). Since actinomycin D and cisplatin are not only known to induce DNA damage but also to interfere with transcriptional processes, we validated our finding using bleomycin, a DNA-damaging agent that does not directly inhibit transcription. Consistent with our earlier results, bleomycin treatment led to a dose-dependent increase in PC4K80 acetylation, which correlated with elevated levels of γ.H2AX (Supplementary Fig. S5A–D).

To further confirm that PC4 is acetylated at K80 residue in response to DNA damage, we generated stable cell lines expressing wild-type and mutant PC4, defective in PC4 acetylation at K80 in the PC4 knockdown background (Supplementary Fig. S5E and F). Using both immunoprecipitation and immunofluorescence assays, we confirmed that damage-induced acetylation occurred specifically in cells expressing wild-type PC4 but not in those expressing the acetylation mutant PC4 (PC4K80R) (Supplementary Fig. S5G–J). Taken together, these data demonstrate that upon DNA damage, PC4 gets acetylated at the K80 site.

Tip60 is the bonafide acetyltransferase to acetylate PC4 at K80

Although in vitro acetylation assays revealed that Tip60 acetylates PC4 at the K80 site, it does not ensure that in the cellular context, Tip60 is involved in the acetylation of PC4 at K80. To establish the role of Tip60 in the acetylation of PC4 in cells, we generated a stable knockdown of Tip60 in the HEK 293 cell line (sh-Tip60) using a lentiviral-mediated short hairpin RNA (shRNA) delivery system. The sh-Tip60 cells showed Tip60 downregulation both at transcript and protein levels (Supplementary Fig. S6 and S3A, respectively). We observed no change in PC4 expression upon Tip60 knockdown (Fig. 3G). In contrast, when cells were treated with actinomycin D, PC4 got significantly acetylated at the K80 site; however, in the absence of Tip60 (in Tip60 knockdown cells), there was a drastic decrease in the acetylation level (Fig. 3B and F). Interestingly, it was found that upon DNA damage by actinomycin D, the γ.H2AX level is substantially increased in the Tip60 knockdown cells (Fig. 3D and E), suggesting the accumulation of damage, but not the acetylated PC4, in the absence of Tip60 (Fig. 3B and F).

The knocking down of Tip60 drastically reduces the acetylation of PC4, which indicates that the acetyltransferase could be critical for the acetylation of PC4. However, to ascertain whether the catalytic activity of Tip60 is responsible for the acetylation, cells were treated with a Tip60-specific inhibitor, NU9056. The inhibition of Tip60 acetyltransferase activity upon NU9056 treatment was verified by the H4K8ac levels, which is a known target of Tip60. We found that upon treatment with the increasing concentration of NU9056, there is a dose-dependent decrease in the acetylation of histone H4K8 (Fig. 3H, panel 3, lanes 3–6; and Fig. 3J), with the concomitant reduction in the level of PC4K80 acetylation (Fig. 3H, panel 1, lanes 3–6; and Fig. 3I). However, H3K9ac levels remain unchanged upon dose-dependent inhibition of Tip60 catalytic activity, which was used as a negative control (Fig. 3K). Collectively, these data suggest that, indeed, Tip60 acetylates PC4 at the K80 site in vivo.

To further establish the enzyme specificity of PC4K80 acetylation, we treated HEK293 cells with an increasing dose of p300-specific inhibitor LTK14, a highly selective inhibitor of p300 HAT activity [27], and analysed PC4K80ac levels by immunoblotting (Supplementary Fig. S6B–F). The results showed no significant reduction in K80ac signal upon p300 inhibition, suggesting that p300 is not the primary acetyltransferase responsible for this site-specific modification. Hence, collectively our findings demonstrate Tip60 as a specific acetyltransferase mediating acetylation of PC4 at K80 residue in response to DNA damage.

Acetylation of PC4 by Tip60 at K80 site is crucial for its DNA repair ability

PC4 plays a critical role in DNA damage repair. In the absence of PC4, that is, upon knockdown of PC4, γ.H2AX levels increase dramatically (Fig. 4A). This was expected as PC4 is an important nonhistone chromatin protein and its depletion results in substantial chromatin decompaction [28], rendering the genome more susceptible to spontaneous or endogenous DNA lesions. Reintroduction of the wildtype PC4 in the knockdown cells significantly reduced the damage signal, suggesting that PC4 is critical for DNA repair. Interestingly, the transfection of acetylation mutant PC4 could not reverse the γ.H2AX level, indicating that PC4 acetylation at K80 is essential for DNA repair and hence could not rescue the damage already caused due to the absence of the acetylation site responsible for DNA repair (Fig. 4A and 4B). To elucidate further the role of PC4K80ac in the context of DNA repair, we treated the cells with actinomycin D, followed by an additional 12-h recovery period in drug-free media. We found enhanced γ.H2AX levels in control cells compared to the untreated condition, which was further enhanced upon PC4 knockdown. However, upon overexpression of Flag-WTPC4 in the knockdown cells, the DNA damage could be rescued as revealed by the decreased γ.H2AX levels, which was not the case in cells transfected with the acetylation mutant PC4 construct (Fig. 4A and C).

To visualize the essentiality of PC4K80ac in cellular DNA repair, we performed the comet assay. The SCGE, or Comet assay, is a technique that is used to measure DNA damage at the level of the individual eukaryotic cell. As observed by fluorescence microscopy, agarose gel electrophoresis at high pH results in a comet-like structure. The intensity of the comet tail relative to the head suggests the number of DNA breaks. As depicted in Fig. 5A, HEK293 cells with PC4 show negligible comet formation, whereas knockdown of PC4 leads to huge comet formation involving more than half of the nuclear DNA (Fig. 5A, top panel I versus panel II; and Fig. 5B and C). Significantly, transfection of wild-type PC4 to the PC4 knockdown cells (Fig. 5A, top panel, III) nearly completely reversed the comet formation. However, the transfection of the acetylation-defective mutant, PC4K80R, could not alter the degree of comet formation (Fig. 5A, top panel IV; and Fig. 5B and C). Similarly, we performed comet assay in cells treated with actinomycin D, followed by an additional 12-h recovery period in drug-free media, and we observed the highest rescue in PC4 knockdown cells transfected with wild-type PC4 (Fig. 5A, bottom panel, III), which is even more efficient than the control cells with endogenous PC4 expression upon actinomycin D treatment (Fig. 5A, bottom panel I; and Fig. 5B and C). This enhanced repair is likely due to the increased availability of PC4, which facilitates access to more acetylated PC4 (by Tip60), allowing better DNA repair. These data suggest the critical role of PC4 in DNA damage repair, which is mechanistically regulated by the Tip60-mediated acetylation.

We reported earlier that PC4 is downregulated in a substantial number of breast cancer patient samples. In agreement with this observation, it was observed that there are a few patient-derived highly malignant breast cancer cell lines where PC4 expression is significantly downregulated [29]. One such cell line is ZR-75–1, in which PC4 expression is dramatically repressed due to the overexpression of one of the oncomir miR29a. In agreement with our initial data in HEK 293 cells, where γ.H2AX levels were enhanced upon PC4 knockdown, in ZR-75–1 cells, we also found γ.H2AX levels to be endogenously high due to low expression of PC4 (Supplementary Fig. S7A). This reduced PC4 level likely contributes to unresolved DNA damage in these cells, hence contributing to its increased tumorigenicity by promoting genomic instability. Upon transfection with wild-type PC4, the phosphorylated H2AX levels decreased significantly in the ZR-75–1 cells (Supplementary Fig. S7A), while the acetylation-defective mutant (PCK80R) failed to reduce the phosphorylation of H2AX. The comet assay could further demonstrate the ability of the acetylated PC4 to assist in DNA repair. The ZR-75–1, in which PC4 is endogenously downregulated, shows nucleoids with densely stained tails, which drastically reduce upon the expression of wild-type flag-tagged PC4 (Fig. 5D, panel II; and Fig. 5E and F). Such a tailed nucleoid structure could be prominently visualized when the ZR-75–1 cells were transfected with the acetylation-defective mutant (PC4K80R) construct (Fig. 5D, panel III). These data thus signify the pathophysiological implications of the acetylated PC4-mediated DNA repair.

Tip60 mediated PC4 acetylation promotes its interaction with PARP1

To investigate the molecular mechanisms behind the acetylated PC4-mediated DNA damage repair, we performed immunoprecipitation followed by mass spectrometry in HEK293 cells, with and without treatment with actinomycin D. The results revealed that DNA damage specifically induces the interaction of several repair proteins with PC4 and its acetylated form (AcPC4). In particular, we identified 14 DNA repair-related proteins that were found to interact with PC4 exclusively following DNA damage. Notably, among these 14 proteins, five—PARP1, USP14, RPA1, EPRS1, and PSD5A were also found to interact with the K80 acetylated form of PC4 (Supplementary Fig. S8), suggesting that acetylation at this specific residue may play a critical role in mediating these protein-protein interactions in the context of DDR.

Out of the five proteins identified, PARP1 is a highly conserved DNA repair protein essential for catalyzing the poly (ADP)-ribosylation (PARylation) of itself and numerous acceptor proteins at the sites of DNA double-strand breaks (DSBs). It has also been known to interact with PC4, facilitating NHEJ efficiency [30]. Hence, we performed a co-immunoprecipitation assay followed by immunoblotting to elucidate the potential regulatory role of Tip60-mediated acetylation on PC4-PARP1 interaction during DNA damage. The results revealed a significant increase in the PC4-PARP1 interaction in cells treated with actinomycin D (Fig. 6). To determine whether acetylation regulates the interaction between PC4 and PARP1, we performed co-immunoprecipitation (co-IP) experiments both in cells expressing wild-type and K80R mutant PC4. In cells expressing wild-type PC4, actinomycin D treatment led to a robust increase in PC4-PARP1 interaction compared to the untreated control (Fig. 6A, PARP1 panel, lane 2 versus lane 3 and Fig. 6C). This enhanced interaction is proportionate to the degree of PC4 acetylation following damage (Fig. 6A, PC4K80ac panel, lane 2 versus lane 3 and Fig. 6D), suggesting that DNA damage promotes association of acetylated PC4 with PARP1. However, in cells expressing mutant PC4, we did not observe any increase in PC4-PARP1 interaction upon DNA damage (Fig. 6A, PARP1 panel, lanes 4 versus 5 and Fig. 6C), indicating that acetylation of PC4 potentiates its interaction with PARP1. This finding suggests that Tip60-mediated acetylation of PC4 contributes to PARP1-mediated DDR.

Chromatin reorganization upon DNA damage and role of acetylated-PC4

To investigate the alteration of chromatin organization upon DNA damage, we performed Micrococcal nuclease (MNase) accessibility assays using the HEK293 cells treated with different doses of actinomycin D. We found that DNA damage indeed results in chromatin decompaction, as revealed by dose-dependent digestion by MNase (Supplementary Fig. S9A, lane 4 versus lanes 8 and 12) and generation of a higher amount of mononucleosomes compared to the untreated cells (Supplementary Fig. S9B). The chromatin opening at the molecular level was further confirmed by the co-immunoprecipitation assay. The cells expressing wild-type and K80R mutant PC4 were subjected to the assay using FLAG antibody, followed by immunoblotting to assess its interaction with linker histone H1 and heterochromatin protein HP1α, the key structural components of heterochromatin. It was observed that H1 interactions with both the wild-type and acetylation mutant PC4 were significantly decreased upon DNA damage (Supplementary Fig. S9C, H1 panel, lanes 2 versus 3, lanes 4 versus 5; and Supplementary Fig. S9F).

We next evaluate if PC4 regulates the recruitment/removal of heterochromatin protein HP1α to the chromatin upon DNA damage. HP1α is a critical factor in regulating chromatin organization, and its dissociation from heterochromatin is essential in facilitating DNA repair [31]. We observed a substantial decrease in the PC4 interactions with HP1α upon damage (Supplementary Fig. S9C, HP1α panel, lanes 2 versus 3, and lanes 4 versus 5; and Supplementary Fig. S9E), in both wild-type and acetylation-defective mutant expressing cells. Interestingly, this reduction in interaction with H1 and HP1α upon DNA damage occurred regardless of PC4 acetylation status. Collectively, these data suggest that acetylation of PC4 is not primarily involved in chromatin opening upon DNA damage, as reflected by the interaction intensity between PC4 and other heterochromatin components.

Considering the limitations of protein-protein interactions assay to assess the role of a protein in chromatin organization, we performed micrococcal nuclease (MNase) assay of the cellular chromatin following stable transfection of 3X-flag tagged mammalian constructs of wild-type PC4, and K80RPC4 (acetylation-defective mutant) in PC4 knockdown cells with and without treatment of actinomycin D. In agreement with our previous report, we observed that PC4 knockdown led to chromatin decompaction, as evidenced by increased mononucleosomes intensity (Fig. 7B) and more efficient MNase digestion compared to control cells (Fig. 7A, lanes 4 versus 8). This decompaction was rescued by the reintroduction of both WTPC4 and K80RPC4, suggesting that acetylation at K80 does not contribute to chromatin opening under normal conditions i.e. without genotoxic insult (Fig. 7A, lane 8 versus lanes 12 and 16; and Fig. 7B). However, in actinomycin D treated cells, a significant increase in mononucleosome intensity was observed in both control and PC4 knockdown cells compared to untreated cells (Fig. 7C, lanes 4 and 8 versus Fig. 7A, lanes 4 and 8), indicative of DNA damage-induced chromatin relaxation. Interestingly, neither Flag-WTPC4 nor Flag-K80RPC4 reintroduction rescued the chromatin compaction in these cells, as seen in untreated conditions cells (Fig. 7C, lane 8 versus lanes 12 and 16; and Fig. 7D). While no complete rescue was observed, a modest increase in mononucleosome intensity was evident in PC4 knockdown cells upon overexpression of wild-type PC4 (Fig. 7C, lane 8 versus lane 12; and Fig. 7D), indicating that the wild type PC4, which could be acetylated by Tip60 upon DNA damage facilitate the chromatin opening. This suggests that PC4 acetylation at K80 enhances its ability to remodel the local chromatin environment, likely as a part of the dynamic DDR, to provide access to the repair factors to the damage sites.

PC4 acetylation at K80 is crucial for maintaining nuclear architecture dynamics upon DNA damage

To investigate the role of PC4K80ac in maintaining nuclear architecture following DNA damage, we performed high-resolution TEM on cells subjected to DNA damage by actinomycin D (Fig. 8). We used cells expressing wild-type PC4 and a mutant form where K80 was mutated explicitly to arginine (K80R), mimicking a non-acetylated state. In untreated control cells, the nuclear architecture was well-preserved, showing a visible nuclear envelope, uniformly distributed chromatin with patches of heterochromatin, and well-organized nucleolar structures (Fig. 8A, Supplementary Fig. S10A). Upon knockdown of PC4, we observed dramatic changes in nuclear architecture. The distinctive heterochromatin patches were significantly lost, and the general nuclear structure was slightly disrupted (Fig. 8B, Supplementary Fig. S10B). These findings suggest that, indeed, PC4 is essential for preserving nuclear integrity and chromatin organization, even without external stress. Reintroducing wild-type PC4 into the knockdown background led to a remarkable restoration of nuclear architecture and the heterochromatin patches, confirming that wild-type PC4 effectively rescues the structural integrity of the nucleus (Fig. 8C, Supplementary Fig. S10C). These results highlight the critical role of PC4 in maintaining nuclear architecture. In contrast, reintroducing the K80R mutant PC4 did not restore nuclear architecture to its original state (Fig. 8D, Supplementary Fig. S10D). Although there was some recovery of nuclear membrane structure and heterochromatin, it was not as thorough as the PC4 rescue in the wild type.

Upon DNA damage, in control HEK293 cells, there was a noticeable loss of heterochromatin and nuclear membrane disruption (Fig. 8E, Supplementary Fig. S10E). These changes were more pronounced in cells that had PC4 knockdown. Following DNA damage, PC4 knockdown cells showed noticeably increased nuclear architectural abnormalities. There was a significant loss of heterochromatin and disturbances to the nuclear membrane, such as invagination and asymmetric folding. Furthermore, the nucleolus was nearly completely gone, indicating severe damage to the organization and structure of the nucleus (Fig. 8F, Supplementary Fig. S10F). In cells where wild-type PC4 was reintroduced into the PC4 knockdown background, the nucleolar structures and nuclear membrane were largely rescued, indicating that wild-type PC4 can successfully repair certain features of nuclear integrity. The reintroduced wild-type PC4 did not entirely restore the heterochromatin structure; rather, the chromatin looked even more euchromatinized in comparison to the control and knockdown conditions, upon DNA damage (Fig. 8G, Supplementary Fig. S10G). This result is consistent with our MNase data, which demonstrate that after DNA damage, chromatin is significantly open in cells overexpressing wild-type PC4 due to acetylation of the overexpressed protein. In cells expressing the K80R mutant PC4, there was no significant rescue of nuclear architecture following DNA damage. The nucleolus was partially restored, but the nuclear membrane remained disrupted (Fig. 8H, Supplementary Fig. S10H). Furthermore, the chromatin structure remained largely in the euchromatin state, similar to that of the PC4 knockdown cells. These findings suggest that Tip60-mediated acetylation of PC4 at K80 is crucial for the effective restoration of nuclear architecture following DNA damage and that the K80R mutant cannot compensate for the loss of acetylation.

To further study the chromatin level changes in response to DNA damage, we performed AFM imaging in chromatin isolated from cells treated with actinomycin D. In control HEK293 cells, we observed an increase in the chromatin opening compared to the untreated control with chromatin fibre dimension of 50.3 ± 1.5. The chromatin was observed to be more open in actinomycin-D treated cells (Fig. 9A.e and B.e) than in untreated cells (Fig. 9A.a and B.a), which is aligned with our MNase data. As a result, the chromatin fibre in damaged cells was expected to exhibit lower dimensions than in untreated cells. When HEK293-damaged cells were compared to control cells, the chromatin fibres appeared thicker despite the increased openness of the chromatin structure, because actinomycin-D can intercalate into DNA and make the chromatin fibre thicker [32]. Hence, in the damaged background, the dimension of the chromatin fibre might be the dimension of the actinomycin-D-intercalated chromatin fibre, reflecting the changes induced by the drug. The chromatin appeared even more open and dispersed upon PC4 knockdown, with a chromatin fibre dimension of 15.9 ± 1.0. Since the chromatin from the PC4 knockdown cells is already open, they are more vulnerable to DNA damage. Hence, open chromatin with broken threads indicating DNA breakage upon drug treatment was observed (Fig. 9A.f and B.f). In cells overexpressing wild-type PC4 in the knockdown background, highly open chromatin with long interconnected mesh-like threads (Fig. 9A.g and B.g) was observed with a fibre dimension of ∼10.6 ± 0.1 nm (Supplementary Fig. S11G). Due to overexpression, an increased amount of PC4 is available for Tip60 to acetylate, potentially leading to excessive acetylation of PC4 in response to actinomycin D treatment. The long chromatin threads protruding from the compacted chromatin globule signify chromatin relaxation, which is crucial for enabling the repair process. This is consistent with our MNase and electron microscopy (EM) analyses. Chromatin isolated from cells defective in K80 acetylation gets compacted without repair due to the absence of wild-type PC4. Hence, upon DNA damage, it gets decondensed and long broken threads of chromatin, similar to the knockdown condition (Fig. 9A.h and B.h), with a chromatin fibre dimension of ∼34.4 ± 2.7 nm (Supplementary Fig. S11H) could be observed. Consistent with our earlier findings, these data further highlight the role of PC4 acetylation in reshaping chromatin during DDR, thereby ensuring structural integrity by providing more efficient excess for the repair machinery to the damage sites (Table 1).

Discussion

The chromatinization of the eukaryotic genome introduces additional layers of complexity and regulation in the highly complex orchestra of DNA damage repair. Efficient repair of DNA double-strand breaks in cells requires coordinated crosstalk between DNA repair machinery, chromatin remodelling/modifications complexes, and dynamic chromatin components (histones and nonhistone proteins) [33]. Following DNA damage, chromatin undergoes three different phases of structural organization: repressive (compact), transition, and relaxed (open). In the early repressive phase of the DDR, PARP1 initiates chromatin compaction by recruiting the NuA4-Tip60 complex, along with H3K9me2/3 methyltransferases [34, 35]. The nonhistone chromatin protein PC4 interacts with PARP1, which is reported to be enhanced following DNA damage, suggesting a critical role of PC4 in facilitating PARP1 recruitment to damaged chromatin [30]. However, at the damage site, Tip60 (KAT5) acetylates histone H4 tails and ATM [36, 37], triggering the transition from a compact to a more relaxed chromatin state [38]. Activated ATM subsequently binds to H3K9me3-marked chromatin regions and mediates the displacement of HP1α [39], further promoting chromatin relaxation. This stepwise chromatin remodelling is essential for the efficient recruitment of DNA repair machinery to the site of damage. We have found that indeed, upon DNA damage, the interaction between PC4 and HP1α is dramatically reduced, suggesting a disruption of their association during the early chromatin response.

Mechanistically, it is not well understood how the damaged chromatin goes into the open conformation. One of the key events in this process is the Tip60-mediated acetylation of histone H4 at the site of damage [9]. The p400 subunit of the NuA4-Tip60 complex plays a critical role in this context by facilitating the exchange of the histone variant H2A.Z at double-strand breaks (DSBs) [40, 41]. Linker histone H1 plays a key role in facilitating higher-order chromatin folding. For effective repair of DNA double-strand breaks, it is essential to loosen this compact organization by weakening H1 interaction [42]. This chromatin decompaction is facilitated by different PTMs of H1, such as ubiquitylation, phosphorylation, and PARylation, which significantly contribute to the recruitment of DNA repair machinery at the damage sites [43, 44]. Recently, it has been reported that, upon DNA damage, histone H1 undergoes deamidation at asparagine residues Asn76 and Asn77, a modification catalyzed by CTP synthase 1 (CTPS1) [45]. The deamidated histone H1 is shown to be preferentially acetylated by the lysine acetyltransferase p300, a modification that promotes chromatin relaxation during DDR. In contrast, non-histone chromatin protein PC4, when phosphorylated, interacts with histone H1 and induces chromatin compaction [46]. Interestingly, we have observed a significant reduction in PC4-H1 interaction upon DNA damage (Fig. 6), which presumably induces the opening of the chromatin. However, we do not know yet which modification(s) of H1 or PC4 are responsible for these reduced interactions in response to DNA damage.

Previous studies have implicated PC4 in DNA repair and genome stability [19, 21, 30, 47, 48], and more recently, various evidence has demonstrated the involvement of histone and non-histone proteins in licensing chromatin dynamics in response to DNA damage cues. In this work, we provide new mechanistic insights into the role of PC4 in DNA damage-induced chromatin remodelling. Herein, our data revealed the essential role of PC4 acetylation in DSB repair. PC4 is rapidly recruited to DSB sites following Tip60 activation, consistent with the central role of Tip60 in recruiting the wide range of chromatin modifier proteins at the DNA break sites, including BRCA1, 53BP1, ATM, and p53 [9, 49]. Recent evidence shows that the transcriptional coactivator, Spindlin1 (SPIN1), is recruited to DNA lesions, where it interacts with poly-ADP-ribose (PAR) chains of PARP1 and facilitates the recruitment of Tip60 by strengthening the interaction between Tip60 and histone mark H3K9me3 [50]. We demonstrate that during the activation of the DNA repair process, Tip60 acetylates non-histone chromatin protein PC4 at lysine 80 (K80), a modification that is essential for its role in mediating its interaction with DDR components such as PARP1, highlighting the novel functional axis between PC4 and Tip60. Functionally, the K80 acetylation of PC4 is important for its role in facilitating DSB repair. Mutation of PC4 at the K80 site (K80R) impairs DNA repair, as indicated by increased γ.H2AX expression and inefficient resolution of DNA lesions (Fig. 4A). In addition to this, our EM analysis revealed striking structural abnormalities with marked distortion of nuclear integrity in cells expressing the acetylation-deficient PC4 mutant (K80R) upon DNA damage (Fig. 8H). Chromatin isolated from K80R overexpressing cells exhibited increased compaction and reduced plasticity as compared to the chromatin from wild-type overexpressing cells (Fig. 9). Since, the acetylated PC4 is located at the same site of γ.H2AX, and the acetylation-defective mutant PC4 expressing cells fail to repair DNA double-strand breaks, it suggests that Tip60 mediated acetylation of PC4 likely facilitates the recruitment and assembly of DNA repair machinery at damage sites. These data strongly support the model that PC4 acetylation, likely mediated by KAT5 (Tip60), modifies PC4 in a way that increases its affinity for repair factors facilitating chromatin decompaction, a prerequisite for efficient damage recognition and repair factor recruitment (Fig. 10). In the absence of this modification, chromatin remains in a repressive state, physically obstructing access to lesions and contributing to repair failure.

The proposed model of Tip60 mediated PC4 acetylation in response to DNA damage and activation of DNA damage repair. Presumably, upon DNA damage, HP1α gets displaced from the damage site along with ribosylated histone H1, allowing Tip60 recruitment and subsequent acetylation of PC4 at K80. The present study underscores the importance of PC4 in regulating the DDR, particularly through its acetylation and interaction with repair proteins. However, it also points to the broader regulatory complexity of PC4 function through its post-translational modifications. Acetylation and phosphorylation are two well-known modifications that govern PC4’s activity, but the potential crosstalk between these modifications remains largely unexplored. Investigating how acetylation and phosphorylation work together to regulate PC4’s diverse cellular functions would provide valuable insights into the intricate mechanisms of chromatin dynamics, gene expression, and DNA repair. Furthermore, both PC4 and Tip60 are known tumour suppressors and have been reported to be dysregulated in various cancers [29, 51]. Therefore, the functional interplay between KAT5 and PC4 has significant pathophysiological implications, particularly in diseases driven by genomic instability such as cancer, neurodegeneration, and premature ageing syndromes.
