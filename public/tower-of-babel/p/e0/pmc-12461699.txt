A robust protocol for proteomic profiling of secreted proteins in conditioned culture medium
Authors: Takayoshi Otsuka, Atsushi Hatano, Masaki Matsumoto, Hideaki Matsui
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractReliable secretome analysis is crucial for understanding cellular communication and developing therapeutic strategies. However, conventional protein quantification methods, such as the bicinchoninic acid (BCA) assay, can overestimate protein concentrations in concentrated culture media, leading to inconsistent protein loading and compromised quantitative accuracy in mass spectrometry–based proteomics. To address this methodological challenge, we developed an improved sample preparation method for secretome analysis. Our approach introduces a concentration rate–based normalization method that adjusts sample volumes according to the ultrafiltration concentration ratio, ensuring more consistent protein loading across samples. This method enabled reliable identification of 3468 secreted proteins with high reproducibility (r > 0.93) in a model system of nuclear DNA (nucDNA)-induced inflammation in HeLa cells. Secretome profiles were distinctly altered by nucDNA transfection, with 89 proteins showing significant differential release between control and nucDNA-transfected wild-type HeLa cells. Furthermore, we identified a subset of proteins, including chaperone and proteasome complexes, that were consistently released across all conditions, suggesting their potential utility as internal controls for secretome analysis. This study presents a practical solution to the methodological challenge in secretome analysis, enabling more reliable and reproducible secretome profiling. This improved methodology represents an important step toward establishing standardized protocols for secretome analysis, ultimately enhancing the quality and comparability of research in this rapidly growing field.Keywords: DIA proteomics, silver stain, conditioned medium, secreted proteins

Introduction

The analysis of secreted protein profiles is important for understanding cellular communication and function in various biological processes. The secretome, comprising proteins released by cells into their extracellular environment, plays a vital role in intercellular signaling, tissue homeostasis, and disease progression [1]. Mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) secretome has shown considerable therapeutic potential in regenerative medicine. MSCs secrete various autocrine/paracrine factors that support tissue regeneration, induce angiogenesis, protect cells from apoptosis, and exhibit immunomodulatory effects [2, 3]. In aging research, the senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP) has gained significant attention [4–6]. Cellular senescence was originally reported as a positive phenomenon to suppress tumor growth via cell cycle arrest [7–9]. On the other hand, the senescent cells increase secretion of various pro-inflammatory proteins, such as inflammatory cytokines, chemokines, and growth factors are involved in local and distant cell signaling leading to pleiotropic effects such as tumor progression, chronic inflammation, paracrine senescence, and strong link to age-related diseases [10–12]. The cancer secretome plays a crucial role in tumor growth, progression, and communication with other cells in the tumor microenvironment [13, 14]. Several studies have revealed a potential link between cancer and neurodegenerative disorders through the secretome [15–18]. Therefore, analyzing secreted protein profiles may offer potential therapeutic strategies for regenerative medicine and age-related disorders, such as elucidating disease mechanisms, discovering new biomarkers, and developing cell-free treatments [3, 19–21].

Data-independent acquisition (DIA) mass spectrometry has significantly improved secretome analysis by offering increased proteome coverage, improved reproducibility, and enhanced quantification accuracy. This method has been employed for comprehensive secretome analysis with greater confidence [22–24]. Since the secreted protein profile lacks an internal control, it is critical to prepare equal amounts of proteins among samples for appropriate proteome analysis. Different amounts of protein among samples change the ratio of enzyme to protein used for digestion and the amount introduced into the mass spectrometer, affecting the accuracy and reproducibility of the experiment. The bicinchoninic acid (BCA) assay is the gold standard for protein quantification. However, we noticed that the BCA assay presented higher values than the actual amount of protein, possibly because of extraneous substances in the concentrated culture medium.

Herein, we propose that sample preparation for proteomic analysis requires a correction based on the concentration rate. This method provided relatively stable silver staining results, enabling us to use an equalized amount of protein for DIA proteome measurement. In this study, purified nuclear DNA (nucDNA) transfected into HeLa cells was used to induce an inflammatory response. Genomic instability is one of the hallmarks of aging and cytosolic DNA leakage induces inflammation and cytotoxicity through the cGAS-STING pathway [25, 26]. Based on quantified data analysis, the secreted protein profile recapitulated the inflammatory response following nucDNA transfection. This method enables reliable evaluation of secreted protein profiles from the culture medium and is useful for proteomic analysis in various fields.

Materials and methods

Cell line

HEK293 cells were derived from fetal kidneys, and HEK293T cells expressed the SV40 large T antigen (RIKEN Cell Bank, Tsukuba, Japan). HeLa cells were derived from cervical cancer cells obtained from a female patient (ATCC, CCL-2). cGAS-deficient HeLa cells (164-6) were generated using the CRISPR–Cas9 system as previously described [27]. The cGAS gRNA sequence was cloned into pSpCas9 (BB)-2A-Puro (PX495) v.2.0 (plasmid 62988; Addgene, Cambridge, MA, USA). Depletion of cGAS in the stable cell line was verified and monitored by Western blotting and DNA sequencing (Fig. 1c, Supplementary Fig. 1, see online supplementary material for a color version of this figure). IFI16-deficient HeLa cells were established as described previously [27]. All cells described above were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) (Nacalai Tesque, Kyoto, Japan) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) (Biowest, Nuaillé, France) and penicillin–streptomycin solution (100 units/mL penicillin G and 100 µg/mL streptomycin sulfate; Wako, Osaka, Japan) at 37°C under 5% CO2.

nucDNA purification

Chromatin fragments were extracted from HEK293T cells. The cells were lysed using RSB buffer containing 0.1% NP-40 and 0.01% digitonin. The lysates were treated with benzonase for 15 min on ice for chromatin fragmentation. DNA was purified by phenol-chloroform and ethanol precipitation.

Transfection

Cells were seeded on the plate one day before transfection. For nucDNA transfection, Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) was used, according to the manufacturer’s protocol. In brief, 2.0 μg of nucDNA was added to 100 μL of Opti-MEM (Invitrogen), followed by the addition of 4.0 µL of P3000. Meanwhile, 3.0 μL of Lipofectamine 3000 was diluted in 100 μL of Opti-MEM and then mixed with the nucDNA sample. The mixture was incubated for 15 min prior to addition to the culture medium.

Western blotting

Total protein extracts were prepared in lysis solution (50 mM Tris, pH 8.0, 150 mM NaCl, 1% (v/v) NP-40, 0.5% (w/v) deoxycholate, and 0.1% (w/v) SDS). Protein concentrations were determined using a BCA protein assay kit (TaKaRa Bio, Otsu, Japan), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. For the detection of ISG15 in culture medium, same volume was used for Western blotting that were mixed with sample buffer. Protein extracts were separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred to polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes (GE Healthcare, Chicago, IL, USA). The membranes were blocked with 2% bovine serum albumin (BSA) in Tris-buffered saline with Tween 20 (TBST) and probed with primary antibodies at 4°C overnight, followed by incubation with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies at room temperature for 1 h. Anti-cGAS (1:2500, NBP1-86761, Novus Biologicals, Littleton, Colorado), anti-IFI16 (1:2500, ab169788, Abcam, Cambridge, MA, USA), anti-ISG15 (1:7000, 15981-1-AP, ProteinTech, China), and anti-GAPDH (1:14000, 016-25523, Wako) antibodies were used as primary antibodies. Horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG H&L (1:5000, ab6721, Abcam) and goat anti-mouse IgG H&L (1:5000, ab6789, Abcam) were used as secondary antibodies. Western BLoT Quant or Hyper HRP Substrate (TaKaRa Bio) was used for signal detection, and Multi Imager II Chemi Box (BioTools, Gunma, Japan) was used to expose the membranes. Full blot images were shown in Supplementary Fig. 2 (see online supplementary material for a color version of this figure).

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay

The amount of soluble human IL6 protein in culture medium was measured using a commercially available LBIS™ Human IL-6 enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) Kit (294-87401, Wako) according to the manufacturer’s protocol (n = 3 per group).

Immunofluorescence staining

For subcellular localization of dsDNA, immunostaining was performed as previously described [28]. Cells were washed twice with phosphate buffer solution (PBS) and fixed with ice-cold methanol for 10 min at 4°C. The cells were washed three times with PBST for 5 min each and permeabilized with PBS containing 0.2% (w/v) Triton X-100 for 10 min. The cells were washed three times with PBST for 5 min each and incubated with 2% (w/v) BSA in PBST for 30 min. The cells were incubated with a single primary antibody, anti-histone-H2B antibody (1/400, 606302; BioLegend, San Diego, CA, USA), diluted in 2% (w/v) BSA in PBST at 4°C overnight. After washing three times with PBST for 5 min each, the cells were incubated with a highly cross-adsorbed Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated donkey anti-Rat IgG (H + L) (1/500, A21208; Invitrogen) diluted in 2% (w/v) BSA in PBST for 1 h at room temperature (RT). After washing three times for 5 min each with PBST, the cells were incubated with primary antibodies, anti-double-stranded DNA antibody (35I9 DNA) (1/1000, ab27156; Abcam), and anti-Hsp60 antibody (1/400, ab46798; Abcam) diluted in 2% (w/v) BSA in PBST at 4°C overnight. After washing three times with PBST for 5 min each, the cells were incubated with highly cross-adsorbed Alexa Fluor 680-conjugated donkey anti-rabbit IgG (H + L) (1/500, A10043; Invitrogen) and donkey anti-mouse IgG H&L (Alexa Fluor® 594) (1/500, ab150108; Abcam) diluted in 2% (w/v) BSA in PBST for 1 h at RT. The cells were washed three times with PBST for 5 min each, and the specimens were analyzed using an A1R+ confocal microscope (Nikon, Tokyo, Japan).

Quantitative PCR

Total RNA was isolated using TRIzol reagent, according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). cDNA templates were synthesized from purified RNA using the ProtoScript II First Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (New England Biolabs, Ipswich, MA, USA) with oligo (dT)20 primers. Quantitative PCR (qPCR) was performed using TB Green Premix Ex Taq II (Takara Bio) and analyzed in a Thermal Cycler Dice Real-Time System Lite (TaKaRa Bio). The PCR primers used in the present study were IL6 forward (CACCGGGAACGAAAGAGAAG), IL6 reverse (TCATAGCTGGGCTCCTGGAG), IL8 forward (ACATGACTTCCAAGCTGGCC), IL8 reverse (CAGAAATCAGGAAGGCTGCC), ISG15 forward (TGGCGGGCAACGAATT), ISG15 reverse (GGGTGATCTGCGCCTTCA), Glyceraldehyde-3-Phosphate Dehydrogenase (GAPDH) forward (CAGCCTCAAGATCATCAGCA), and GAPDH reverse (TGTGGTCATGAGTCCTTCCA). Statistical analyses were performed using the GraphPad Prism software version 9.3.1 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) and post-hoc analysis with Tukey’s post-hoc test were used to compare more than two groups. Data are presented as the means and ± standard errors of the means.

DIA proteomics analysis of proteins in the cell culture supernatant

Cells were seeded in six-well plate (2.0 × 105 cells/well) and then transfected with or without nucDNA (n = 3 per group). Forty-eight hours post transfection, the cells were washed three times with PBS and placed in serum-free and phenol red–free DMEM supplemented with 2 mM glutamate, 100 µM sodium pyruvate, and penicillin–streptomycin solution for 6 h. The conditioned medium was centrifuged at 1000 × g at 4°C for 5 min to remove cell debris and filtered through a 0.22 µm filter. The samples were concentrated using a Vivaspin 20 with a 3000 MWCO (Sartorius Stedim Biotech GmbH, Göttingen, Germany) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The volume of culture medium was measured before and after ultrafiltration to calculate the concentration rate. Sample volumes were adjusted to equalize concentration levels, and the final buffer conditions were set to 1% SDS and 50 mM Tris (pH 8.8). To confirm the amount of protein, silver staining was performed using the Pierce™ Silver Stain Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Protein concentrations were estimated using a BCA protein assay kit (TaKaRa Bio). Semi-quantitative analysis based on the silver staining image was performed by ImageJ (version 1.54 g, National Institutes of Health, USA).

The concentrated conditioned medium, equivalent to 4.1 mL, were subjected to reduction and alkylation with 2.5 mM tris(2-carboxyethyl)phosphine hydrochloride (Thermo Fisher Scientific), and 12.5 mM iodoacetamide (Sigma-Aldrich, St Louis, MO, USA), respectively. After acetone precipitation of proteins, the protein precipitate was resuspended by sonication in 100 mM HEPES pH8.0 and digested with 0.2 µg of trypsin at 37°C for 16 h. The resulting protein digests were desalted using StageTip (SDB-XC, 3M, St Paul, MN) [29]. The desalted peptides equivalent to 0.16 mL of conditioned medium were injected into a pre-column (L-column micro, CERI, Japan) and fractionated on an in-house–fabricated 20-cm column packed with 2-µm octadecyl silane particles (CERI). Elution was performed with a linear gradient of 5% to 30% solvent B over 70 min at a flow rate of 200 nL/min (solvent A = 0.1% formic acid; solvent B = 0.1% formic acid in acetonitrile) with the use of a Dionex Ultimate 3000 HPLC System (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Eluted peptides were sprayed with a nano-electrospray source and with a column oven set at 42°C (AMR, Japan). The Q Exactive Hybrid Quadrupole-Orbitrap mass spectrometer plus was operated in data-independent acquisition mode. All data were acquired in profile mode using positive polarity. MS1 spectra were collected in the range of 430–860 m/z at a resolution of 35 000 using an AGC target value of 1 × 106 with a maximum injection time for 50 ms. MS2 spectra were collected in the range of >200 m/z at a resolution of 17 500 using an Automated Gain Control target value of 1 × 106 and the automatic maximum ion injection times. Twenty-one DIA windows of 20 unit were ranged from 430 to 850 m/z with an overlap of 1 unit. The normalized collision energy was set to 25. All DIA raw data was processed with DIA-NN (version 1.8) using library-free search mode against human UniProt sequences (https://www.uniprot.org/; accessed 27 April 2021) [30]. Enzyme specificity was set to “trypsin.” Precursor m/z range was set from m/z 430 to 850. Carbamidomethylation of cysteine was set as a fixed modification.

Data analysis

The MaxLFQ intensities of proteins from the DIA-NN searches were imported into Perseus software version 2.0.10.0 (The Max Planck Institute of Biochemistry, Munich, Germany) [31]. The rate of missing values was 7.5%. For quantification, these values were excluded from the analysis according to the published decision chart for dealing with missing values in proteomic data [32]. Pearson correlations were calculated using the multiscatter plot function in the Perseus software. Principal component analysis (PCA) was performed using the resulting dataset. Hierarchical clustering was performed based on Euclidean distance. Volcano plots were generated in Perseus software with default parameters (FDR = 0.05, S0 = 0). Gene ontology (GO term) analysis was performed using ShinyGo (version 0.82; https://bioinformatics.sdstate.edu/go/) [33], with a focus on biological process or cellular component annotations. Functional interactions between the proteins were visualized using STRING (version 12.0; https://string-db.org/) [34].

Results

Inflammatory response was evoked by nucDNA transfection

We aimed to mimic the inflammatory response induced by nucDNA leakage by transfecting the purified nucDNA. The nucDNA was purified from the HEK293T cell line with a size range of 100 bp to 10 kbp (Fig. 1a). Transfected nucDNA was visualized by immunocytochemistry of double-stranded DNA (dsDNA) (Fig. 1b, yellow arrowheads). Confocal microscopy revealed discrete cytoplasmic puncta of the dsDNA signal, indicating the successful delivery of nucDNA into the cytosol. The localization pattern was distinct from nuclear DNA (stained with Histone H2B) and mitochondrial region (stained with HSP60), confirming that the observed signal represented ectopic cytosolic DNA rather than endogenous cellular DNA.

Three cell lines (wild-type (WT) HeLa, 164-6: cGAS-deficient HeLa, 162-5: IFI16-deficient HeLa) were utilized to evaluate the effect of nucDNA transfection (Fig. 1c). Cytosolic introduction of nucDNA led to IL6 and IL8 mRNA expression in WT, whereas these expressions showed a trend of downregulation under cGAS or IFI16-deficient conditions (Fig. 1d). The expression of ISG15, which is downstream of the cGAS pathway, was specifically induced in WT and 162-5 but suppressed in cGAS-deficient cells (164-6), demonstrating the pathway specificity of the response (Fig. 1d). Next, we evaluated secreted protein profiles under ectopic nucDNA conditions.

Improved sample preparation was achieved by concentration rate–based volume adjustment

The sample preparation scheme is illustrated in Fig. 2a. Traditional approaches for secretome analysis rely on protein quantification methods, such as the BCA assay, to normalize sample loading for mass spectrometry analysis [35, 36]. However, we encountered significant challenges with this conventional approach when analyzing concentrated culture medium samples.

(c) Representative images of silver staining of cell lysates and secreted proteins adjusted by the BCA assay. Silver staining revealed that the actual protein amount was substantially lower than the amount measured by the BCA assay based on the comparison with equal amounts of cell lysate (Fig. 2b, Supplementary Fig. 3a, see online supplementary material for a color version of this figure). While cell lysate samples showed a good correlation between BCA measurements and silver stain intensity, culture medium samples consistently showed weaker silver stain signals despite equivalent BCA readings, indicating a systematic bias in BCA measurements for concentrated culture medium. Furthermore, the BCA assay successfully equalized protein amounts when applied to cell lysate samples, as evidenced by uniform silver staining intensities, whereas it failed to achieve consistent protein loading for secreted proteins from the culture medium (Fig. 2c, Supplementary Fig. 3b, see online supplementary material for a color version of this figure).

To address this methodological challenge, we developed an alternative approach based on sample volume normalization according to the concentration rate. The concentration rate was calculated as the ratio of the initial culture medium volume to the final concentrated volume after ultrafiltration (concentration rate = initial volume/final volume). This approach assumes that the protein concentration is proportional to the overall sample concentration during ultrafiltration. After implementing sample volume adjustment based on the concentration rate, silver staining results showed remarkably improved consistency in protein amounts across samples (Fig. 2d), suggesting that protein amounts were effectively normalized using this volume-based approach.

Validation of proteome data

Using the optimized sample preparation method, we identified 4771 proteins in concentrated culture medium. Missing values were omitted, and 3468 proteins were identified. To further validate the secretome dataset, we focused on proteins annotated as “extracellular” in the UniProt database after filtering out missing values from the analysis (Fig. 3a). This approach ensured that our analysis specifically captured secreted proteins, rather than intracellular contaminants. The multiscatter plot displaying Pearson’s correlations showed high reproducibility (r > 0.93) across all samples and even greater consistency within the same experimental groups (r > 0.977) (Fig. 3b, Supplementary Table 1), indicating the reliability of the downstream comparative analyses. Principal component analysis (PCA) and hierarchical clustering revealed a clear separation between control and nucDNA-transfected samples, demonstrating that the secretome profiles were distinctly altered by nucDNA transfection (Fig. 3c and d).

To assess the reliability of the proteomics data, we compared the proteomics results with the qPCR data obtained under the same experimental conditions. A total of 89 proteins showed significant differential release between control and nucDNA-transfected WT HeLa cells (Fig. 3e, Supplementary Table 2). Among these differentially released proteins (DRPs), 39 were upregulated and 50 were downregulated following nucDNA transfection. The correlation between transcript levels (IL-6 and ISG15) and their corresponding protein secretion levels provided strong validation of our methodology (Fig. 3e). Furthermore, the profile plots showed that the secretion levels of these two proteins were consistent with the qPCR results among cGAS and IFI16-deficient cell lines, suggesting that our sample preparation method accurately captured the cellular response to nucDNA transfection (Fig. 3f). In addition, the proteomic results were validated at the protein level. Both ELISA quantification of IL6 secretion and Western blot analysis of ISG15 were consistent with the proteome-derived abundance changes across experimental groups (Fig. 3g and h). These results further support that our sample preparation method reliably reflects protein-level changes in response to nucDNA transfection. To gain insight into the biological processes affected by nucDNA transfection, gene ontology (GO) analysis was performed on the identified DRPs (Fig. 3i). The analysis revealed a significant upregulation of the response to stimuli, such as “Response to wounding,” “Reg. of response to external stimulus,” and “Response to stress.” Additionally, downregulation of “extracellular matrix organization” suggested that nucDNA transfection not only triggers inflammatory responses but also affects cellular interactions with the extracellular environment (Fig. 3i).

Exploration of potential internal control in the secretome

Our analysis revealed several proteins that were consistently released under all conditions, regardless of the cell line or nucDNA transfection status. The coefficient of variation (CV) was calculated for all proteins detected in our assay (3468 proteins) (Supplementary Table 3). Housekeeping genes (proteins) used as internal control for qPCR/Western blot showed relatively stable release such as PGK1 (CV = 0.005160579), TUBB (CV = 0.005493413), and GAPDH (0.009405379). GO analysis was performed using a protein list with minimal variation (CV < 0.005). Among these consistently released proteins, the chaperone and proteasome complexes were enriched (Fig. 4a). Interestingly, these proteins interacted tightly with each other (Fig. 4b). Published proteomic data of HeLa cell lysates were retrieved to investigate the intracellular abundance of these proteins [37]. The rank plot showed that most proteins with minimal variation were highly abundant intracellular proteins (Fig. 4c, Supplementary Table 4). Discussion

In this study, we developed an improved sample preparation method for reliable secretome analysis by using DIA mass spectrometry. Our key finding demonstrates that the conventional BCA assay significantly overestimates protein concentrations in concentrated culture media, leading to inconsistent protein loading and compromised quantitative accuracy. To address this critical methodological challenge, we propose a concentration rate–based volume adjustment approach that normalizes samples according to the ultrafiltration concentration ratio rather than relying solely on protein quantification assays. The improved sample preparation enabled the reliable identification of 3468 secreted proteins with high reproducibility (r > 0.93), demonstrating the practical utility of our approach for comprehensive secretome profiling.

Secretome analysis using mass spectrometry–based proteomics presents several analytical challenges, including contamination by serum proteins, cell death, and intracellular protein leakage [38]. To avoid serum contaminants, the standard approach for secretome analysis is to use serum-free cell culture. However, longer incubation under serum starvation may cause a stress condition and increase intracellular protein leakage caused by cell death, leading to changes in secretome composition [39]. Since the amount of protein in culture media is typically low, ultrafiltration with low-molecular-weight cut-off filters is often used for concentration. Following this process, protein concentration is usually determined by the BCA assay prior to LC-MS analysis; however, our results demonstrated that the BCA assay overestimates protein concentrations (Fig. 2b).

The systematic bias observed in BCA measurements of the concentrated culture medium can be attributed to extraneous substances in the concentrated culture medium. Culture medium contains various interfering compounds, including salts, metabolites, amino acids, and residual culture medium components. Although small molecules passed through the filter by ultrafiltration, some extraneous substances may have remained and artificially elevated the protein concentration. Repeated ultrafiltration with buffer exchange can reduce the interference, but the use of low-molecular-weight cut-off filter can cause protein loss due to selective retention of larger proteins [38]. This is particularly problematic for low-abundance proteins with low molecular weight. Our findings highlight the need for alternative approaches when dealing with concentrated extracellular samples, in which traditional protein quantification methods may not provide reliable results for downstream proteomic analyses.

The concentration rate–based normalization method is based on the principle that the protein concentration should be proportional to the overall sample concentration during ultrafiltration. This approach assumes that proteins are retained by the molecular weight cutoff filter, whereas smaller molecules pass through, resulting in a direct relationship between the concentration factor and protein enrichment. The concentration rate (initial volume/final volume) provides a more accurate measure of protein concentration than chemical assays in the presence of interfering substances. Our silver staining validation strongly supports this theoretical framework, showing remarkably improved consistency in protein loading when samples were normalized by the concentration rate rather than BCA measurements (Fig. 2c and d). Although a direct comparison with the BCA assay was not performed in this study, our proteomic analysis demonstrated high reproducibility across all samples (r > 0.93) with even higher reproducibility within the same experimental groups (r > 0.977). These results suggest that our normalization approach provides robust and consistent protein quantification. This method offers significant practical advantages, including simplicity, cost-effectiveness, and broad applicability across different experimental conditions. The concentration rate calculation requires only volume measurements, making it easy to implement in any laboratory setting. It is also critical to validate the samples by silver staining before performing spectrometry analysis. BSA can be used as a positive control to estimate protein amounts and to check for excessive serum-derived albumin contamination in samples.

The concentration rate–based normalization method should be broadly applicable to various secretome analysis scenarios, including different cell types, culture conditions, and experimental treatments. This approach is particularly valuable for studies involving primary cells, stem cells, and cancer cells, where secretome profiling is critical for understanding cellular communication and therapeutic applications. However, several limitations should be considered when implementing this method. This approach assumes uniform protein retention during ultrafiltration, which may be affected by the protein size distribution and filter characteristics. Importantly, this concentration rate–based method relies on the assumption that cells are seeded at equal densities and that protein secretion rates are comparable between the control conditions. Therefore, this approach is most suitable for comparative studies in which primary intervention is not expected to dramatically alter the overall protein secretion rates or cell viability. For instance, the induction of senescence may affect cell proliferation, resulting in a lower cell number over time, which could influence the total amount of secreted proteins. This represents a technical limitation of our approach, particularly when comparing the conditions associated with substantial changes in cell viability or proliferation. Although our method offers advantages in terms of reproducibility and minimal sample handling, it is important to be cautious when interpreting results from conditions that involve notable differences in cell number. Although our validation used HeLa cells as a model system, it should be noted that HeLa cells have been reported to undergo changes in immune response due to long-term in vitro culture, which may limit the generalizability of our findings to other cell types with different secretory profiles [40]. This limitation is reflected in our GO analysis, where traditional cytokine-related terms were not prominently featured among differentially released proteins, even though responses to stimuli and disturbed extracellular matrix could be captured (Fig. 3i). To ensure a wider applicability, future research should confirm the effectiveness of this method across various cell types and culture conditions.

Our analysis identified a subset of proteins that were consistently released under all experimental conditions, regardless of cell line or treatment status. Among these stable proteins, traditional housekeeping genes, such as PGK1, TUBB, and GAPDH, showed relatively low variation, suggesting their potential utility as internal controls for secretome analysis. Interestingly, GO analysis revealed that proteins with the lowest variation (CV < 0.005) were enriched in chaperone and proteasome complexes, which showed tight functional interactions in STRING analysis (Fig. 4a and b). The consistent release of these protein complexes may reflect constitutive cellular maintenance processes, which are less affected by experimental perturbations. These findings suggest that, unlike intracellular proteomics, where housekeeping proteins serve as loading controls, secretome analysis may benefit from a different set of stably released proteins as normalization standards. It should be noted that some of the intracellular proteins detected in the culture supernatant, such as chaperones and proteasome subunits, are among the most prevalent proteins in HeLa cells (Fig. 4c) [37]. Consequently, even minimal cell damage or leakage induced by lipofectamine treatment could potentially lead to their release into the extracellular space. Although the experimental conditions were designed to minimize cytotoxicity, we acknowledge that this possibility cannot be entirely dismissed and should be considered when interpreting data. Further validation across diverse cell types and experimental conditions is necessary to establish these proteins as reliable internal controls. The identification of internal controls would significantly advance the standardization of secretome analysis protocols and improve the comparability of results across different studies.

This study presents a practical solution to a significant methodological challenge in secretome analysis by introducing concentration rate–based sample normalization. Accurate secretome analysis is crucial for understanding cellular communication and for developing therapeutic strategies. Our approach addresses the limitations of conventional protein quantification methods in concentrated culture media and enables more reliable and reproducible secretome profiling using DIA mass spectrometry. Although limitations exist regarding cell type–specific secretory profiles and differences in cell density among samples, the fundamental principles of our approach should facilitate more robust and comparable secretome studies across the research community. This methodological advancement represents an important step toward establishing standardized protocols for reliable secretome analysis, ultimately enhancing the quality and reproducibility of research in this rapidly growing field.
