DNA and RNA-based amplicon sequencing of paired supragingival and dentin lesion plaque in children with severe early childhood caries
Authors: Allison A Naumann, Elissa M Elmorr, Eleanor I Lamont, Erik L Hendrickson, Tessa Holmes, Travis M Nelson, Jeffrey S McLean, Kristopher A Kerns
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractEarly childhood caries is a form of tooth decay in children under the age of 6 and impacts more than 514 million children worldwide. Severe early childhood caries (S-ECC) represents the most advanced and aggressive form of early childhood caries, defined by an age-dependent number of cavitated, missing, or filled smooth surfaces in the primary maxillary anterior teeth, or an overall count of decayed, missing, or filled tooth surfaces. To advance our understanding of the microbiological differences between different types of dental caries, we sought to investigate deep dentin caries isolated from 13 children with S-ECC and compare it with supragingival plaque isolated from the same site to identify differentially abundant species. Co-extraction methods allowed species identification by both the 16S gene and transcript sequences. By sequencing and analyzing reverse transcribed 16S rRNA in parallel to DNA, we were able to identify bacteria with higher metabolic potential within dentin plaque, allowing the detection of potentially active oral bacterial species that may be driving and/or contributing to this most severe form of dental caries. Here, we highlight that Lactobacillus casei and Oribacterium sp. HMT-078 are significantly differentially enriched in dentin plaque and has an increased RNA/DNA ratio, indicating activity within dentin plaque samples from S-ECC patients. These species may therefore play a significant role in the progression and severity of S-ECC within deep dentin plaque, in addition to other well-established cariogenic species associated with S-ECC, like Prevotella denticola, Dialister invicus, and Streptococcus mutans.Subject terms: Diseases, Oral diseases

Introduction

Dental caries, also known as tooth decay, is one of the most prevalent oral diseases in humans and is estimated to affect nearly 43.8% of the global population and more than 514 million children despite being largely preventable.1–4 Dental caries is a multifactorial disease made up of microbiological, diet, and environmental risk factors like socioeconomic status, parental education, and access to care.5–9 Severe early childhood caries (S-ECC), also known as rampant caries, is the most severe form of dental caries affecting children under the age of 6 years old. S-ECC, unlike other forms of tooth decay, is rapid, can spread to multiple teeth in the mouth, and often affects areas of the tooth that are normally less prone to decay.5,10 If left untreated, S-ECC often results in complete destruction of the tooth crown, abscess surrounding the tooth root, and severe pain and sensitivity that impact the development and behavior of young children, with lasting impacts that can affect oral health-related quality of life throughout their lifespans.11,12 Standard of care for S-ECC cases often requires highly invasive whole mouth treatment, often administered under general anesthesia, resulting in an additional risk to this vulnerable population and increased cost to caregivers.13

Due to the destructive nature of S-ECC, surface lesions can progress rapidly into the dentin, resulting in dentin caries, which occurs when bacteria infiltrate and infect the dentin layer of the tooth beneath the enamel surface—essentially eroding the tooth from the inside out.14 Dentin caries often result in a niche with low levels of oxygen and limited exposure to saliva that allows for aciduric and acidogenic bacteria to thrive, as well as persistently maintain a low pH that exacerbates the demineralization of the tooth’s enamel and dentin.13,15 Several studies have sought to characterize the unique microenvironments within the different types of dental caries in both children, including S-ECC,16,17 and adults.18 These studies and others have shown significant differences in microbial diversity and composition between dental caries lesions and supragingival plaque (SP) within the same individual and have shown a consistent enrichment of aciduric and acidogenic species within the Lactobacillus, Oribacterium, Prevotella, and Streptococcus genera.19–23 However, few studies have sought to identify and characterize bacterial species isolated from deep dentin lesions compared with the SP collected from the same tooth sites in S-ECC patients.24

To investigate the relationship between active bacterial strains within deep dentin lesions and SP within S-ECC patients, we designed and implemented a cross-sectional study that recruited 13 children under the age of 6 years old diagnosed with S-ECC being treated under general anesthesia at the University of Washington Center for Pediatric Dentistry. Each child provided matched SP samples isolated from non-salvageable teeth that had severe active caries lesions with significant tooth crown loss and were undergoing planned extraction under general anesthesia. Dentin plaque (DP) was obtained from teeth immediately after extraction and flash frozen. Using total nucleic acid co-extraction methods, we were able to perform 16S rRNA gene (DNA) and 16S rRNA transcript (RNA) amplicon sequencing in parallel to distinguish total bacterial community composition from transcriptionally active bacterial populations. Due to RNA’s rapid degradation rate when compared to DNA, its presence indicates recent transcriptional activity, making RNA-based sequencing a powerful tool for identifying metabolically active bacteria. This cost-effective paralleled approach facilitates the characterization and assessment of differentially abundant and transcriptionally active bacterial species between dentin caries plaque and SP in S-ECC patients at the strain level using exact amplicon sequence variants (ASVs).

Results

Sequence depth and taxonomic assignment were consistent between the DNA and RNA datasets

16S rRNA gene (DNA) and transcript (RNA) amplicon datasets were filtered for unassigned reads and sequence contaminants. The final comprehensive dataset included 3 562 ASVs represented by 10 phyla, 112 genera, and 436 species. The comprehensive dataset was then split by nucleic acid source and assessed respectively. DNA samples (n = 26) resulted in 1 984 297 paired-end reads with an average of 62 009 reads/sample, and RNA samples (n = 26) resulted in 2 279 153 paired-end reads with an average of 71 223 reads/sample. When stratified by DNA and RNA we observed similar phylogenetic representation classified using the expanded Human Oral Microbiome Database (eHOMD, v 15.1) (DNA: RNA, 10:10 phyla, 100:106 Genera, and 379:398 Species).

DNA and RNA capitulate microbial diversity between dentin and supragingival plaque in S-ECC patients

We observed significantly lower alpha diversity in deep DP compared to SP isolated from S-ECC patients using both 16S gene (DNA) and transcript (RNA) datasets either by the Observed (Padj = 0.035; Padj = 0.019) or Shannon (Padj = 0.010; Padj = 0.012) indices (Fig. 1b). A significant difference between DNA and RNA within SP was also observed (Padj = 0.027, Shannon). We also identified significant differences in beta diversity using weighted Unifrac distances between DP and SP (DNA: P < 0.001, F = 3.851 3; RNA: P < 0.001, F = 3.731 3) with a tighter clustering in DP samples compared to the higher alpha diversity SP samples (Fig. 1c, d). No observed differences in alpha diversity between anterior and posterior tooth sites were observed (Supplementary Fig. 2).

Here we identified 7 differentially abundant genera enriched in DP compared to SP within the DNA dataset and 6 genera within the RNA datasets (Padj < 0.05) (Fig. 2). Several genera, including Atopobium, Dialister, and Oribacterium, showed consistent differential enrichment across DNA and RNA datasets (Supplementary Table 2). The relative abundance of genus-level agglomerated data for differentially abundant genera was assessed (Fig. 3). The RNA/DNA ratio between dentin and supragingival plaque shows Lactobacillus has the highest metabolic potential within dentin plaque in subjects with S-ECC

To gain a better understanding of the association between species detection and inferred biological activity/metabolic potential, we examined the relationship between RNA and DNA for key differentially detected species within the Streptococcus, Lactobacillus, and Prevotella genera (Fig. 6). Total 16S rRNA transcript counts divided by the total 16S rRNA gene counts were used to calculate the RNA/DNA ratio for each genus and species (Supplementary Tables 5 and 6). Here we observed that several species showing enrichment in DP compared to SP, including Streptococcus mutans (DP = 0.75 and SP = 0.46) and Prevotella denticola (DP = 0.58 and SP = 0.85), had RNA/DNA ratios <1. In contrast, most species within the Lactobacillus genus exhibited RNA/DNA ratios >1, including Lactobacillus casei (DP = 1.21 and SP = 0.58), indicating higher metabolic potential and biological activity in the DP samples (Supplementary Table 3). Most Lactobacillus species showed little detection in SP samples, and even those that did tended to show lower RNA/DNA ratios in SP than DP, implying not only an enriched presence but heightened activity within the deep DP in S-ECC subjects, including several Lactobacillus species. Oribacterium sp. HMT-078 was also differentially enriched within DP compared to SP (Supplementary Table 2 and Figs. 2, 3) with an increased RNA/DNA ratio within DP (DP = 1.21 and SP = 0.70) (Supplementary Table 3).

The core (See “Methods”) exact ASVs present in all DP and all SP samples were assessed by both DNA and RNA (Fig. 6d). The DNA dataset resulted in 15 core ASVs within DP and 38 in SP samples, while the RNA dataset showed 13 core ASVs within DP and 36 in SP samples. Notably, 7 ASVs represented by 5 species overlapped between DP and SP for DNA samples, while 5 ASVs represented by 4 species overlapped between DP and SP for RNA samples. 11 core ASVs were identified within both DP DNA and RNA datasets and were represented by 7 species, including Streptococcus mutans, Streptococcus oralis, Rothia dentocariosa, Veillonella dispar, Oribacterium sp. HMT-078, and Lactobacillus casei (Fig. 6d).

Discussion

Despite advances in surveillance and treatment strategies, S-ECC remains a significant public health burden, highlighting the need to understand how the activity of different cariogenic species may contribute to this most severe form of dental caries disease in children.1,2,25 This study aimed to characterize the taxonomic composition of deep DP using 16S rRNA gene analysis while simultaneously evaluating co-extracted 16S rRNA transcripts to better resolve metabolically active species. Additionally, due to our study’s parallel sample collection, processing, and analysis for DP and SP collected from the same tooth sites, we could not only investigate enriched and overlapping species in the deep dentin compared to SP but were also able to compare exact ASVs within individuals and identify dominant ASVs across individuals at different taxonomic levels.

Simultaneous detection of microbes and identifying their metabolic states (activity) has been a longstanding goal in oral microbial ecology.26 With reductions in costs and increases in access, 16S rRNA gene (DNA) amplicon sequencing has seen a dramatic increase in use in recent decades and has been widely used in oral microbiome research to characterize variable states of oral health and disease, including dental caries and S-ECC.1,16,24,25,27–32 However, a major limitation of this technique that is often overlooked is that DNA does not infer the activity, nor viability, of a detected bacterium.26 This is even more problematic as DNA has been shown to persist in dental plaque long after a cell has died, resulting in an increased risk of inflated or false detection of a bacterial species within a particular sample or state.33 As RNA is more conserved and sensitive to degradation, due to its single-strandedness, it does not often persist in the environment long after a bacterium has died. Thus, one of the most informative approaches for characterizing active bacteria associated with dental caries relies on the characterization of their RNA.34–38 While RNA-seq is a powerful technique, it is much more expensive than 16S rRNA amplicon sequencing. Furthermore, unlike eukaryotes, bacterial mRNA can’t be easily enriched by targeting its polyadenylated tail and bulk RNA-seq datasets are often dominated by ribosomal RNA (rRNA), constituting 80%–90% of the total RNA signal, potentially requiring additional methods to enrich the desired mRNA signal along with the associated costs.39 An alternative approach to full metatranscriptomic sequencing that has been widely used in the field is to amplify the highly abundant rRNA, convert it to cDNA through reverse transcription and then amplify the V3-V4 region of the 16S gene for sequencing. Although this approach has its limitations,26,33 our results show the power of this technique, highlighting specific bacterial strains that are enriched and have a high metabolic potential within deep dentin using exact ASVs.

As expected, and consistent with the literature, we observed a reduced alpha diversity within DP samples compared to SP for both DNA and RNA datasets (Fig. 1b) as well as a more similar composition across DP samples (Fig. 1c, d). While both posterior and anterior teeth were included, no significant difference in diversity between sites was observed (Supplementary Table 1 and Supplementary Fig. 2). Bacteria with low variability across samples but differentially abundant between DP and SP samples indicated consistent enrichment in DP (Figs. 2 and 4). These differences were detected at both the genus (Fig. 2) and species (Fig. 4) levels. At the genus level, we observed a consistent differential enrichment of abundant genera Atopobium, Lactobacillus, Dialister, and Oribacterium between DP and SP samples within both DNA and RNA datasets (Fig. 2), a result consistent with our comparative analysis of relative abundance (Fig. 3). Although Anaeroglobus and Megasphaera genera were determined to be differentially abundant by our model (Fig. 2), they both had low relative abundance across our samples.

The use of 16S RNA also allowed us to assess these organisms’ metabolic potential. Here, we define bacteria with increased rRNA transcript to rRNA gene counts to be active within the community in comparison to other less active or potentially dormant species with a lower rRNA transcript/gene (RNA/DNA) ratio. While many organisms showed enriched presence in DP, only Lactobacillus showed a significant increase in RNA compared to DNA within DP, indicating that Lactobacillus species may be highly active within DP.

This increase in metabolically active Lactobacillus species is consistent with in vitro metatranscriptomic RNA-seq studies of mixed oral bacterial communities that have shown Lactobacillus has a very high RNA/DNA ratio when the environmental pH is at its lowest (pH 4.2), which is far below the critical pH required to decay tooth enamel and dentin.40,41 Notably, all the other differentially abundant genera we identified within DP samples showed different patterns. Atopobium and Oribacterium had similar levels between the DNA and RNA datasets. However, Dialister had a significant increase in DNA compared to RNA. Meanwhile, the Dialister, Leptotrichia, Prevotella, and Streptococcus genera showed significant increases in DNA compared to RNA within DP samples may indicate that these genera, which have been previously associated with S-ECC,21–23,32,34–36,38,42 may be overrepresented in comparison to their activity and or viability within DP samples in children with S-ECC.

The analysis of differentially enriched species between DP and SP samples and consistency across DNA and RNA datasets was also performed at the species level (Fig. 4). Here, we observed a consistent differential enrichment of Atopobium parvulum, Oribacterium sp. HMT-078 and Lactobacillus casei within DP samples across DNA and RNA datasets. At the species level, differential abundance was generally in agreement between DNA and RNA datasets (Fig. 5). However, RNA was generally maintained at a higher amount compared to DNA for the most represented Lactobacillus species: L. casei, L. fermentum, L. gasseri, and L. reuteri. As similar enrichment and activity were observed for Oribacterium sp. HMT-078 (Supplementary Table 2, Supplementary Fig. 3), future studies may aim to resolve the acidogenic or aciduric properties of Oribacterium sp. HMT-078 and potential synergistic interactions with other aciduric species like Lactobacillus in order to resolve its potential role in S-ECC. In contrast, Atopobium parvulum showed a consistent ratio of RNA and DNA, while species such as Dialister invisus, Prevotella denticola, and Streptococcus mutans showed higher amounts of DNA, indicating their enriched abundance within the community, yet reduced metabolic potential compared to other cariogenic species.

The 16S rRNA transcript counts, 16S rRNA gene counts, and 16S rRNA transcript/gene ratio for DP and SP samples are highlighted in Fig. 6. These results highlighted that most Lactobacillus species were differentially enriched in DP samples compared to SP and had increased transcript/gene ratios, implying higher activity/viability for Lactobacillus species within DP samples. Only Lactobacillus reuteri was observed to have a higher RNA/DNA ratio within SP samples. Consistent observations of Streptococcus mutans and Prevotella denticola indicate similar enrichments within DP samples; however, these species had lower RNA/DNA ratios, which may indicate that these bacteria were less likely to be the major metabolically active/viable taxa within DP samples (Supplementary Table 3).

Lastly, to identify shared bacterial strains between samples, we investigated core bacterial species using exact ASVs (See “Methods”) (Fig. 6d). Here, we observed only 5 species-level ASVs shared between DP and SP samples within the DNA dataset and 4 species-level ASVs within the RNA dataset with 80% similarity of species identified, including one shared ASV of Streptococcus mutans. Furthermore, we identified 7 core species-level ASVs (>60% prevalence) within DP DNA and RNA datasets, including core shared ASVs of Streptococcus mutans, Oribacterium sp. HMT-078, and Lactobacillus casei.

Together, our study provides additional evidence that Lactobacillus species, including Lactobacillus casei, may be major contributors to the progression and or severity of dentin caries within S-ECC patients. Our study shows differential enrichment and increased transcriptional activity of Lactobacillus casei as well as other Lactobacillus spp. within S-ECC DP using the 16S RNA/DNA ratio (Supplementary Table 3). This is consistent with several studies mimicking acid production and acid tolerance associated with dental caries in vitro40,41 and provides additional evidence in prior literature19–22 that Lactobacillus species may be active contributors and/or drivers of dentin demineralization associated with S-ECC patients. Lastly, future studies may need to look at the synergistic effects of the species involved in S-ECC, including Lactobacillus and other cariogenic-associated Oribacterium, Streptococcus, and Prevotella species, as well as ascertain their abundance and activity in age-matched healthy controls while accounting for potential confounding variables such as diet. Despite these limitations, we consistently detected differential enrichment and activity of multiple aciduric species within DP samples in S-ECC patients. While the role of these species and their potential interactions within DP sites in S-ECC patients remains unresolved, future studies are needed to determine their impact on this rampant, debilitating, and most severe form of dental caries disease.

Methods

Study population

Children diagnosed with S-ECC scheduled for extraction and dental rehabilitation under general anesthesia were recruited from the University of Washington Center for Pediatric Dentistry (Fig. 1a). Written informed consent was obtained from all caregivers under a study approved IRB (STUDY00007221). 13 Children (7 Female, 6 Male) aged 1–5 years old provided both SP and deep dentin lesion plaque from a matched tooth site providing a total of 26 unique plaque samples. Chi-squared test of association was utilized to examine the descriptive statistics of the participants and the sample demographic information. The level of significance was predetermined at P < 0.05 (Supplementary Table 1).

Sample collection

Children diagnosed with S-ECC using current American Academy of Pediatric Dentistry definitions2, undergoing extraction and dental rehabilitation, followed pre-treatment guidelines for treatment under general anesthesia and did not eat or drink fluids except water from the night before, as well as refrained from oral hygiene the morning of surgery. After general anesthesia was administered, SP was collected using a sterile curette from matched sextants of planned extraction sites and placed into a sterile 2 mL screw top microcentrifuge tube filled with anaerobic transfer media (ATM) with glycerol (80%). Teeth planned for extraction were removed and immediately harvested for DP. For extracted tooth samples, the top layer of decay was removed and discarded, and DP was removed using a sterile curette and placed into a sterile 2 mL screw top microcentrifuge tube filled with ATM with glycerol (80%). Samples were flash frozen and preserved in liquid nitrogen (–196 °C) using a mobile dewar that was located within the operatory until they were transferred to the lab on dry ice and stored at –80 °C until sample processing.

Sample processing and library preparation

Supragingival (SP) and dentin plaque (DP) samples were removed from –80 °C and thawed on ice. DNA and RNA were extracted with the AllPrep DNA/RNA/miRNA Universal Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) following the protocol for purification from tissues and with Lysing Matrix E beads (MP Biomedicals, Santa Ana, CA, USA) for cell lysis. DNA and RNA samples were further purified and concentrated using DNA Clean and Concentrator Kit-5 (Zymo Research, Irvine, CA, USA) and RNA Clean and Concentrator Kit-5 (Zymo Research), respectively. The Ambion TURBO DNA-free Kit (ThermoFisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) was used to deplete DNA from RNA samples. cDNA was generated from RNA samples with the iScript Select cDNA Synthesis Kit (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA) with random primers.

Both DNA and cDNA libraries were generated with a negative kit control, a no-template library preparation control, and a ZymoBIOMICS bacterial community standard (Zymo Research) as a positive control, as done previously.43–45 Library preparation was performed as follows: The V3-V4 hypervariable region of the 16S rRNA gene was amplified using gene-specific primers with Illumina adapter overhang sequences (5′-TCGTCGGCAGCGTCAGATGTGTATAAGAGACAGCCTACGGGNGGCWGCAG-3′ and 5′-GTCTCGTGGGCTCGGAGATGTGTATAAGAGACAGGACTACHVGGGTATCTAATCC-3′). Each reaction mixture contained 2.5 µl of template DNA or cDNA, respectively, 5 μL of each 1 μmol/L primer, and 12.5 μL of HiFi HotStart ReadyMix (Roche, Basel, Switzerland). Amplicon PCR was carried out with the following parameters: 95 °C for 3 min, 35 cycles at [95 °C for 30 s, 55 °C for 30 s, 72 °C for 30 s], and 72 °C for 5 min. PCR products were verified by gel electrophoresis (1% agarose gel) and cleaned with AMPure XP beads (Beckman Coulter, Brea, CA, USA). Amplicons were indexed using the Nextera XT Index Kit V2 set A (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA) and purified again with AMPure XP beads. Libraries were normalized to a concentration of 1–2 ng/µL using the SequalPrep Normalization kit according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA). Samples were pooled into a single library and checked for quality and quantity using a TapeStation 4200 High Sensitivity D1000 assay (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA) and Qubit High Sensitivity dsDNA assay (Thermo Fisher Scientific), respectively. A final pooled library was loaded onto an Illumina MiSeq platform (Illumina) with a 15% PhiX spike and run for 602 cycles (2 × 301 bp). A total of 23.5 M paired-end sequencing reads were generated. 20.7 M paired-end reads passed quality filtering, resulting in an average of 370 K raw reads per sample.

Read processing

Demultiplexed paired-end sequences were downloaded from the Illumina MiSeq platform, imported to QIIME2 (v2019.10), and trimmed and denoised using the DADA2 package. Forward reads were trimmed by 12 nucleotides (nt) from the 5′ end and truncated to 290 nt on the 3′ end; reverse reads were trimmed by 12 nt from the 5′ end and truncated to 250 nt from the 3′ end. Denoising and filtering chimeric sequences resulted in 5.7 M paired-end reads with an average of 66 616 reads per sample, with a minimum sampling depth of 22 639 reads. Taxonomic classification was assigned to ASVs using the feature-classifier suite trained on the expanded Human Oral Microbiome Database (eHOMD v15.1)46 using Qiime2.47 The .biom file generated in QIIME2 was imported to RStudio (v4.3.2)48,49 for further processing. Data were then merged with metadata and analyzed using Phyloseq (v1.46.0).50

Alpha, beta, and core diversity were assessed using the microbiome (1.24.0)51 and vegan (2.6–8)52 packages with a detection cutoff of 0.001 with >60% prevalence. Taxonomic differences were assessed using agglomerated data at the phylum, genus, and species levels and were compared within and between DNA vs RNA datasets and SP and DP sample types using the Wilcoxon signed rank and Wilcoxon ranked sum tests, respectively, with FDR correction. Differential abundance and variability using singleton filtered count data were performed at the genus and species level using the corncob (0.4.1) package.53

Data Availability Statement

This project is indexed at GenBank under BioProject accession number PRJNA124183. Sample raw reads are available in the Sequence Read Archive (SRA) linked to BioProject accession number PRJNA124183. All data and code used to generate the analysis presented in this manuscript are publicly available through the Figshare project: https://figshare.com/projects/DNA_and_RNA-Based_Amplicon_Sequencing_of_Paired_Supragingival_and_Dentin_Lesion_Plaque_in_Children_with_Severe_Early_Childhood_Caries/243326.
