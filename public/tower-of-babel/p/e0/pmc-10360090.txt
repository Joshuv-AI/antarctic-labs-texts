Utility of droplet digital polymerase chain reaction for studying somatic mosaicism: brain malformations and beyond
Authors: Wei Shern Lee, Paul J. Lockhart
Published: 2023

Somatic variants and how to find them: One major challenge in studying somatic mosaicism is the low abundance of potentially pathogenic somatic variant(s). Unlike inherited germline variants, which are essentially present in all cells, pathogenic somatic variants only exist in a subset of cells and may be present at very low (< 1%) variant allele frequency (VAF). Despite the low VAF, these somatic variants are disease-relevant and considered pathogenic. In the context of focal brain malformations, which constitute a frequent cause of drug-resistant focal epilepsy, a pathogenic somatic mammalian target of rapamycin (MTOR) variant with a VAF of 0.3% has been described (Baldassari et al., 2019). The identification of pathogenic somatic variants has important implications both clinically and biologically. Clinically, a positive genetic diagnosis can provide diagnostic certainty and inform genetic counseling. A diagnosis can also improve patient care by informing treatment strategies, for example, justifying the use of an mTOR inhibitor such as sirolimus or everolimus. Biologically, identifying pathogenic somatic variants can provide insights into the origin of somatic mosaicism and the biological pathways that are involved in the condition of interest. As a result, somatic variants and the techniques to identify them are an area of interest to clinicians and the scientific community more broadly.

Prior to the era of next generation sequencing (NGS), traditional Sanger sequencing was one method utilized to detect somatic variants, but with an estimated limit of detection of ~20% VAF. This is in part due to the reduced signal amplitude associated with low VAF variants, which makes it difficult to distinguish between the actual somatic variant and sequencing noise. As a result, early studies relied on different techniques to enrich for low VAF variants prior to sequencing, such as using laser capture microdissection (Crino et al., 2010) and co-amplification at lower denaturation temperature-polymerase chain reaction (Li et al., 2009). However, these enrichment methods were not sufficiently robust for larger scale studies, and the use of Sanger sequencing meant that the list of target genes was limited and biased.

NGS has become the method of choice for studying somatic mosaicism, allowing high throughput genetic analysis in a relatively unbiased manner. Profiling somatic variants requires sequencing at a high read depth (at least 300–500 ×) to allow the detection of low VAF somatic variants. Given this prerequisite, the most frequently utilized NGS techniques to identify somatic variants are targeted panel sequencing and exome sequencing. Targeted panel sequencing interrogates only a defined number of genes (typically less than 300 genes), but each gene is sequenced at a high depth. More recently, high depth exome sequencing is becoming more common (Lopez-Rivera et al., 2022), enabled by the advance of sequencing technologies. Together, targeted panel sequencing and exome sequencing have driven the genetic studies of focal brain malformations in the last 10 years. Despite the successes of NGS, it is limited by two major factors: (1) relatively high costs due to the requirement of high read depth for the identification of somatic variants, and (2) long turnaround time due to sequencing library preparation and downstream bioinformatic analyses. As a result, cheaper and quicker assays are required to complement the NGS methods.

A study in droplet: Droplet digital polymerase chain reaction (ddPCR) has emerged as a useful genetic analysis tool that allows the detection of low abundance targets through the means of sample partitioning (Figure 1). In ddPCR, each sample is partitioned into up to 20,000 droplets through microfluidics and water-oil emulsion droplet technology. Each nanolitre-sized droplet functions as a separate PCR reaction and contains only one or a few template molecules. Following PCR, the signal from each droplet is analyzed individually, thereby allowing the detection of very rare somatic variants. The detection power of ddPCR compared to traditional PCR is considerable. In conventional PCR, a sample containing one mutant copy and 99 wildtype copies (i.e., 1% VAF) will have an undetectable mutant signal due to the exponential amplification of the already abundant wildtype copies. However, in ddPCR, the partitioning process generates one droplet containing the mutant copy and 99 droplets containing the wildtype copy, and because each droplet undergoes separate PCR amplification and is analyzed independently, signals from one mutant droplet and 99 wildtype droplets are acquired, resulting in a reported 1% VAF. With up to 20,000 droplets generated per sample, the ddPCR system offers high accuracy and efficiency.

The main histopathological feature of FCDII is the presence of “dysmorphic neurons”. Pathogenic somatic variants are known to be most enriched in the dysmorphic neurons, but the detection of these variants can be difficult due to the low variant allele frequency (VAF). When studying somatic mosaicism, ddPCR is a suitable tool for the independent validation of somatic variants detected through NGS. This is an important step because NGS has its inherent flaws and sequencing artefacts can arise in different forms such as misalignment, poor base calling and duplications. In ddPCR, variant-specific probes (typically based on minor groove binder technology or locked nucleic acid technology) are able to detect single nucleotide polymorphism, insertions and deletions with high specificity. Importantly, studies have established that ddPCR has a limit of detection of ~0.008% (Milbury et al., 2014; Kliman et al., 2018), making it a highly reliable tool for the detection of low VAF variants. Somatic variants confirmed by both NGS and ddPCR are considered true positives, and this combination of technologies has enabled the identification of somatic variants causing focal brain malformations (Baldassari et al., 2019).

In addition to being a validation tool, ddPCR can also be used directly as a genetic diagnostic tool to detect recurrent variants. This type of assay requires prior knowledge about the genetic basis of the disease of interest. For example, pathogenic somatic variants have been identified in up to 53% of cases affected by focal cortical dysplasia type II, which is a common subtype of brain malformations (Baldassari et al., 2019). The majority of these cases can be explained by recurrent variants in genes associated with the mTOR pathway, including MTOR, PIK3CA and AKT3. Given the high prevalence of these recurrent variants, it is feasible to design ddPCR assays to screen for these variants without the need for NGS. Indeed, we and others have used ddPCR assays to rapidly identify recurrent somatic variants in resected brain specimens, with a favorable diagnostic rate of ~26–82% depending on the type of brain malformation (Lee et al., 2022; Pirozzi et al., 2022). With sufficient knowledge about the genetic basis of a disease type, this genetic screening strategy can be utilized in other contexts more generally.

Using ddPCR as a genetic diagnostic tool has several advantages. Compared to NGS, ddPCR has significantly lower cost and faster turnaround. Furthermore, due to the low limit of detection and high accuracy, ddPCR can reliably detect somatic variants at 0.1% VAF or less. Identifying a 0.1% VAF variant with NGS will require a high read depth, which increases the costs for sequencing. In addition, most NGS methods require high quality input genomic DNA (gDNA) of at least 50 ng, but only a very small amount of gDNA is required for ddPCR. For example, a recent study demonstrated that one nanogram of gDNA from formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded tissue was sufficient for ddPCR analysis (Wolter et al., 2022). However, designing an effective ddPCR assay requires prior knowledge about the pathogenic variant(s) underlying a disease. Therefore, NGS remains the method of choice for the identification of novel causative genes and non-recurrent pathogenic variants. Nevertheless, ddPCR is a highly effective genetic analysis tool that can be incorporated in the early stages of a genetic testing pipeline. Successful identification of recurrent somatic variants using ddPCR means that fewer samples need to subsequently be analyzed using NGS, which can significantly reduce the time and costs associated with a large-scale genetic study.

Probe and precision: The specificity and accuracy of ddPCR means the technology has potential applications in precision medicine. The variant-specific probes used in ddPCR can be tailored to detect genetic changes that are directly relevant to a patient’s condition. As a result, ddPCR has been widely used in the context of cancer screening and monitoring. For example, multiplex ddPCR assays are now being used to detect copy number variations in BRCA1 for routine clinical screening of breast and ovarian cancer, with an accuracy that is comparable to the previously utilized multiplex ligation-dependent probe amplification assay (Oscorbin et al., 2019). It is also possible to use ddPCR to monitor circulating tumor DNA in individuals undergoing cancer therapy. Moreover, the detected levels of cancer-specific somatic variants can be used as a quantifiable measure to track the effectiveness of cancer therapy in each individual (Tsao et al., 2015).

The applications of ddPCR may also be extended to the surgical theatre. Given that incomplete resection of tissue carrying a pathogenic somatic variant may be associated with poor surgical outcomes, it is important to identify the margin of affected tissue and perform a complete resection with high precision. In epilepsy surgery, the extent of resection is often determined by imaging and electrophysiological data. In the future, it may be possible to use ddPCR to measure the VAF within tissue biopsies in real-time, thereby informing surgical decisions with molecular-level precision. Indeed, a similar technique is under development in the context of glioma surgery (Shankar et al., 2015). Since somatic variants of as low as 0.3% VAF can cause seizures in brain malformations (Baldassari et al., 2019), real-time genetic data about low VAF somatic variants may be beneficial during surgery. The high accuracy and low limit of detection offered by ddPCR make it a suitable tool for intraoperative assessment of the completeness of surgical resection. In cases where a brain lesion overlaps with the eloquent cortex, maximal safe surgical resection rather than complete resection should be considered.

An additional developing application of ddPCR is in the field of transplant rejection monitoring. In individuals who received a transplanted organ, high levels of graft-derived cell-free DNA (gdcfDNA) in plasma samples can serve as an indication of transplant rejection, triggering further medical attention. However, the detection of gdcfDNA typically requires the use of NGS to identify graft-specific genetic markers that are present in the transplant recipient (Dengu, 2020). Although NGS allows the detection of gdcfDNA with high accuracy, the slow turnaround time may cause a delay in much-needed medical attention for transplant recipients. To address this issue, a recent study described a rapid methylation-based ddPCR assay to monitor the possibility of liver transplant rejection (Cox et al., 2022). By designing probes that can detect methylation patterns only found in hepatocytes, the authors were able to quantify the levels of gdcfDNA released by the transplanted liver with high specificity. This ddPCR assay offers practical and accurate prediction of transplant rejection in a non-invasive manner, with the potential to reduce the need for invasive liver biopsy in some transplant recipients.

In conclusion, ddPCR is a powerful genetic analysis tool that has a wide range of applications in brain malformations, cancer and increasingly precision medicine. Notably, a new system was released in 2022 that is capable of detecting up to six different fluorophores, which significantly increases the throughput and multiplexing capabilities of ddPCR assays. Previously, multiplexing of ddPCR was typically done by adjusting the concentration of different probes in a reaction (Watanabe et al., 2017). With the ability to detect up to six colors in one sample well, investigators will now be able to acquire significantly larger datasets from a small amount of sample gDNA. We anticipate that future advances of technologies will continue to expand the potential applications and efficiency of ddPCR assays.
