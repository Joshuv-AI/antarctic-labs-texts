Uncertainty-calibrated adaptation of clinical transformer foundation models enhances in-hospital mortality and hospital readmission prediction
Authors: Pei-Hung Chung, Byung-Jun Yoon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAccurate prediction of in-hospital mortality and 30-day readmission is important for clinical decision support, yet existing transformer approaches focus narrowly on ICU cohorts, rely on multimodal inputs that few institutions can harmonize, and rarely provide calibrated uncertainty suitable for triage. We address these gaps with a portability-first design, studying the broad general hospital admission population—spanning both ICU and general ward patients—using only ICD procedure code sequences combined with age and sex. This minimal, universally recorded input is itself a contribution, yielding a model substrate that is interoperable, auditable, and applicable to resource-constrained settings. On this foundation, we conduct the first systematic comparison of uncertainty-aware parameter-efficient adaptation strategies for clinical transformer foundation models on general admissions, evaluating full-parameter fine-tuning, Single LoRA, LoRA Ensemble, Bayesian LoRA, and Monte Carlo Dropout against classical baselines, with a leakage-aware mortality protocol on MIMIC-IV and external validation on MIMIC-III. Our analysis shows that no single uncertainty mechanism dominates: stochastic averaging on a fully fine-tuned backbone delivers the strongest discrimination, Bayesian adaptation is best calibrated on the highly imbalanced mortality outcome, and ensemble averaging is best calibrated on the more heterogeneous readmission outcome, while the clinical transformer generalizes more gracefully than classical baselines under domain shift. Together, these findings establish that procedure-code sequences alone, paired with uncertainty-calibrated parameter-efficient adaptation, can support reliable, deployment-ready risk estimates across the general hospital admission population, and clarify how to match the uncertainty mechanism to outcome prevalence and clinical objective.Subject terms: Computational biology and bioinformatics, Diseases, Health care, Mathematics and computing, Medical research

Introduction

Accurate prediction of in-hospital mortality (IHM) and 30-day readmission is vital for clinical decision support, especially in emergency room (ER) settings where clinicians must make rapid decisions with limited information1. Early identification of high-risk patients enables timely escalation of care, more efficient allocation of beds and monitoring capacity, and targeted interventions that reduce delays and avert deterioration2. Beyond discrimination, well-calibrated probabilities are crucial for risk communication: they support admission and intra-hospital transfer decisions and inform discharge planning and follow-up care3,4. However, reliable prediction remains challenging due to incomplete data, evolving patient states, and heterogeneous clinical practice patterns5,6.

Most prior work has focused on ICU cohorts, leveraging rich multimodal monitoring and high-frequency signals, but this narrow scope limits generalizability. In contrast, general hospital admissions capture a broader and more representative population, including patients who appear stable at admission yet later deteriorate or are readmitted7,8. Modeling this setting is clinically important because it supports earlier risk identification and hospital-wide resource planning, and may reduce costly, avoidable readmissions. To our knowledge, this study is the first to systematically study transformer-based clinical prediction with explicit uncertainty and calibration evaluation on the general admission population.

Recent progress in clinical foundation models pretrained on large-scale EHR and biomedical text corpora has advanced clinical prediction by capturing complex sequential and contextual patterns. Representative clinical NLP transformers include ClinicalBERT (and publicly released clinical BERT embeddings) for clinical notes9,10, GatorTron for large-scale clinical language modeling11, ClinicalT5 for clinical text-to-text transfer12, and BioGPT for biomedical text generation13. For structured EHR coding data, Med-BERT demonstrates effective pretraining on ICD-coded records14, while earlier EHR transformer models such as BEHRT introduced transformer-based patient representations for longitudinal structured records15. Related work also explores generative modeling of clinical narratives for downstream prediction, e.g., MedGPT16. More recent foundation-model efforts further extend representation learning for structured EHR and time-to-event modeling, aiming for scalability and generalizability across patient populations, including MOTOR17, EHRMamba18, and transformer-based patient embeddings for stratification and progression analysis19. Despite these advances, many applications still report point estimates without quantifying predictive uncertainty. In high-stakes care, calibrated uncertainty is essential: overconfident models may mislead, whereas uncertainty estimates can support selective escalation or deferral when evidence is insufficient. Classical approaches such as deep ensembles, Bayesian inference, and post-hoc calibration improve reliability20,21, while parameter-efficient methods such as Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA) make it feasible to adapt large transformers in resource-constrained clinical settings22.

In clinical deployment, calibration and uncertainty enable actionable decision policies—for example, escalating uncertain cases for clinician review or additional testing rather than treating overconfident predictions as ground truth21. Importantly, recent work has begun to directly integrate Bayesian uncertainty into parameter-efficient fine-tuning by placing probabilistic structure on LoRA adapters and/or learning dynamics. BLoB formulates Bayesian low-rank adaptation via variational inference to learn distributions over adapter parameters23. MonteCLoRA and IVON-LoRA further improve the stability and calibration of Bayesian LoRA fine-tuning using Monte Carlo estimation or variational optimization, respectively24,25. Complementary approaches such as Laplace-LoRA apply a Laplace approximation over LoRA parameters to obtain lightweight posterior uncertainty estimates26, and C-LoRA introduces a contextualized (data-dependent) Bayesian LoRA formulation to capture sample-level uncertainty while retaining PEFT efficiency27. While these methods are conceptually close, they are largely validated on general NLP benchmarks; less is known about how uncertainty-aware PEFT behaves for clinical risk prediction with structured coding data, where temporal leakage and deployment shift are central concerns.

In this work, we make five contributions. First, we develop an uncertainty-aware adaptation of clinical transformers by integrating Single LoRA, LoRA Ensemble, and Bayesian LoRA into ClinicalT5, and additionally evaluate Monte Carlo Dropout applied at inference to a fully fine-tuned backbone. Together, these yield four complementary mechanisms for calibrated risk estimation that span explicit posterior modeling (Bayesian LoRA), ensemble averaging (LoRA Ensemble), inference-time stochastic averaging (MC Dropout), and a deterministic single-adapter baseline (Single LoRA). Second, we move beyond the ICU-centric settings that dominate prior work and evaluate both in-hospital mortality (IHM) and 30-day readmission on the broader general-admission population. Third, we systematically study ICD procedure code sequences as predictors for these outcomes, demonstrating that these universally recorded codes carry clinically informative signal for risk stratification. Fourth, to mitigate temporal leakage in IHM, we adopt a leakage-aware evaluation protocol that restricts prediction to first-day procedures only, whereas for 30-day readmission we model the full procedure sequence over the index admission. Finally, we assess robustness by evaluating performance on both internal and external test sets, providing evidence of generalization despite differences in dataset characteristics and cohort composition.

Evaluated on MIMIC-IV under a leakage-aware first-day-procedures protocol, ClinicalT5-Large with MC Dropout achieves the strongest minority-class detection on in-hospital mortality (highest AUPRC, lowest NLL, highest MCC and F1), while ClinicalT5-Large with Bayesian LoRA delivers the best probability calibration (lowest ECE). For 30-day readmission, MC Dropout on ClinicalT5-Large again yields the strongest discrimination (best AUROC and AUPRC), whereas LoRA Ensemble produces the best-calibrated probabilities. Across both tasks, the dominant pattern is that inference-time stochastic averaging on a fully fine-tuned backbone (MC Dropout) improves rank ordering and likelihood-based fit; explicit Bayesian regularization on LoRA adapters yields the best calibration for highly imbalanced mortality but degrades on the more heterogeneous readmission task; and ensemble averaging of independently initialized LoRA adapters provides the most reliable calibration improvement on readmission. No single method dominates all metrics, motivating method selection by clinical objective.

Results

In this study, we fine-tuned ClinicalT5 to predict two clinically important outcomes: in-hospital mortality and 30-day hospital readmission. We chose ClinicalT5 as our primary backbone because, under full-parameter fine-tuning (i.e., without LoRA), it consistently outperformed other representative clinical foundation models on the highly imbalanced mortality task—achieving the highest AUPRC (base: 0.4140; large: 0.4321), best NLL (large: 0.0713), and best AUROC (large: 0.9474) on the comparison set in Table 1. Accordingly, we conducted experiments with both the base and large variants.

The model leveraged structured clinical data—ICD procedure code sequences—together with patient demographics (age and gender). Inputs were formatted as textual prompts of the form: "procedures: 2345 4325 5333; age: 65; gender: M", providing a structured yet natural representation. Prediction targets were encoded as single-word labels: "death" versus "discharge" for mortality, and "readmission" versus "no_readmission" for readmission.

For evaluation, we enforced patient-level splitting to prevent leakage across multiple admissions from the same individual. Rather than using a fixed split ratio across tasks, we adopted task-specific splits while preserving class prevalence within each partition. For in-hospital mortality, to mitigate temporal leakage and to align with the external MIMIC-III evaluation protocol, we restricted inputs to ICD-9 procedure codes and used a 72%/14%/14% train/validation/test split. For 30-day readmission, temporal leakage is not induced by the index-admission procedure sequence in the same way, so we included all MIMIC-IV admissions with both ICD-9 and ICD-10 procedure codes and used a 72%/13%/15% split. Following standard practice, we excluded admissions resulting in in-hospital death from the readmission cohort, as mortality precludes subsequent readmission.

We explored three distinct LoRA-based fine-tuning strategies: Single LoRA, LoRA Ensemble, and Bayesian LoRA. Single LoRA and LoRA Ensemble use a LoRA rank of r = 16, while Bayesian LoRA uses r = 8 with mean and variance parameters, yielding a comparable trainable-parameter count (9.44 M) to a standard r = 16 adapter. All three methods share scaling factor α = 32 and dropout rate of 0.05. LoRA adapters were specifically applied to the query (q), key (k), value (v), and output (o) attention projection matrices within ClinicalT5. Each method was trained for a total of three epochs using the AdamW optimizer, with a learning rate of 5 × 10−5, gradient accumulation steps of 4 (batch size of 4, resulting in an effective batch size of 16), and gradient clipping norm of 1.0 for stability.

To enhance model robustness, the LoRA Ensemble approach extended the Single LoRA baseline by training three independently initialized adapters, each initialized with a unique random seed (42, 44, 46). During inference, ensemble predictions were obtained by averaging the outputs of these three adapters, aiming to reduce predictive variance and improve generalization.

To assess seed stability, all ClinicalT5 results reported with mean ± standard deviation in Tables 2 and 4 were obtained by training each configuration with three independent random seeds and aggregating the resulting test-set metrics. This applies uniformly to full-parameter fine-tuning, MC Dropout, Single LoRA, and Bayesian LoRA. For LoRA Ensemble, each of the three reported runs is itself an ensemble of three independently initialized adapters, so the reported ± reflects variability across three independent ensembles rather than across the three within-ensemble adapters. XGBoost results are likewise reported as mean ± standard deviation across three seeds. Logistic regression is deterministic given the data and is reported as a single point estimate.

Performance among clinical foundation models

Before adopting the leakage-aware first-day-procedure protocol used in the rest of this paper, we first compared candidate foundation models under the full-procedure-sequence input regime, in order to characterize each model’s maximum representational capacity for in-hospital mortality prediction (Table 1). Our comparative evaluation among representative clinical foundation models (Table 1) shows that no single model dominates every criterion, but ClinicalT5 consistently leads on the metrics that matter most under severe class imbalance. ClinicalT5-Large attains the highest AUPRC (0.4660), the lowest NLL (0.0660), and the highest AUROC (0.9631), while ClinicalT5-Base attains the highest MCC (0.3533) and F1 (0.3415) and the second-highest AUPRC (0.4140). The structured-EHR baselines remain competitive but trail on AUPRC: DiagBERT (0.4066) and MedBert-MIMIC (0.3936) approach but do not reach ClinicalT5 performance, despite having far fewer parameters (110M vs. 220M/770M). Among classical baselines, XGBoost is a strong reference point (AUPRC = 0.4075, AUROC = 0.9425) but lags both ClinicalT5 variants on AUPRC and ClinicalT5-Large on AUROC and NLL.

Calibration tells a different story. The lowest ECE on this comparison is achieved by MedGPT (0.0021), followed by DiagBERT (0.0025) and XGBoost (0.0030). ClinicalT5-Base (ECE = 0.0069) and ClinicalT5-Large (ECE = 0.0079) are well-calibrated in absolute terms but not the best of the cohort. This pattern—ClinicalT5 leading on rank-based and likelihood-based metrics while smaller or simpler models match or exceed it on raw ECE—motivates the uncertainty-aware adaptations evaluated in Section 2.2: the question is whether parameter-efficient or stochastic-inference variants of ClinicalT5 can preserve its strong AUPRC/AUROC while improving probability calibration. The pronounced gap between ClinicalT5 and the bag-of-codes XGBoost baseline on AUPRC (6% relative) and on NLL is plausibly attributable to ClinicalT5’s subword tokenization of ICD codes, which permits implicit sharing of representation across structurally related procedures, and to its sequence-aware encoder, which captures co-occurrence patterns across the procedure trajectory. These factors together translate into a more discriminative ranking on the rare-event minority class without inflating ECE.

In-hospital mortality

Having selected ClinicalT5 as the primary backbone in Section 2.1, we now switch to the leakage-aware protocol for all subsequent analyses, restricting model inputs to ICD-9 procedure codes recorded on the first hospital day (Section 4.2). The reduction in available context relative to the full-sequence regime of Table 1 modestly lowers ClinicalT5’s AUPRC (e.g., from 0.4321 to 0.3952 ± 0.0069 for ClinicalT5-Large under full-parameter fine-tuning), confirming that most—though not all—of the procedure-sequence signal for mortality prediction is concentrated in the first day. This drop is small enough that ClinicalT5 retains its lead over the structured-EHR and biomedical-LLM baselines from Table 1, supporting the backbone choice under the deployment-relevant input regime.

All ClinicalT5-based results are reported as mean ± standard deviation across multiple seeds. Logistic regression illustrates that AUROC alone is insufficient under severe class imbalance: although AUROC is high (0.9367), minority-class detection is poor (AUPRC = 0.3853) and probabilities are badly miscalibrated (ECE = 0.1606). XGBoost improves AUPRC modestly (0.3940 ± 0.0026) and AUROC (0.9373 ± 0.0009) but, on this leakage-restricted input, also exhibits substantial calibration error (ECE = 0.1437 ± 0.0004). Both classical baselines are notably worse calibrated here than the values reported in Table 1, reflecting the loss of full-sequence discriminative signal once inputs are restricted to first-day procedures.

Among ClinicalT5 variants, three patterns emerge that map cleanly onto the underlying mechanisms of each adaptation strategy.

Inference-time stochastic averaging (MC Dropout) on the fully fine-tuned backbone is the strongest configuration overall. ClinicalT5-Large with MC Dropout achieves the best AUPRC (0.4016 ± 0.0014), best NLL (0.0728 ± 0.0004), best MCC (0.4469 ± 0.0063), and best F1 (0.4598 ± 0.0048), while remaining within noise of the best AUROC (0.9443 ± 0.0019 vs. 0.9445 ± 0.0002 for full-parameter fine-tuning). This is consistent with the nature of MC Dropout: by averaging 20 stochastic forward passes through a model that has already absorbed the full discriminative capacity of full-parameter fine-tuning, it reduces predictive variance without sacrificing representational power. The result is improved expected likelihood (NLL) and improved threshold-dependent performance (MCC, F1) on the rare positive class, where individual deterministic predictions are most sensitive to per-sample noise.

Explicit Bayesian regularization on LoRA adapters yields the best calibration but trades off rank ordering. ClinicalT5-Large Bayesian LoRA achieves the lowest ECE (0.0053 ± 0.0010), substantially better than every other configuration, yet has the lowest AUPRC and AUROC among the ClinicalT5 variants (0.3532 ± 0.0052 and 0.9307 ± 0.0008). This is the expected behavior of a variational LoRA: the KL term in the ELBO regularizes the adapter posterior toward the prior, smoothing the predicted-probability distribution into well-calibrated bins, but at the cost of suppressing the sharp, high-confidence minority-class predictions that drive rank-based metrics. In other words, Bayesian LoRA buys probability reliability with discrimination, and is therefore most appropriate when calibrated triage thresholds are the operational priority.

Parameter-efficient LoRA variants without explicit uncertainty regularization underperform full fine-tuning on this task. Single LoRA (AUPRC = 0.3587 ± 0.0019) and LoRA Ensemble (AUPRC = 0.3640 ± 0.0040) on ClinicalT5-Large both fall below full-parameter fine-tuning (AUPRC = 0.3952 ± 0.0069) and below the same-backbone MC Dropout configuration. This outcome is mechanistically interpretable: at rank 16, LoRA adapters add only a narrow low-rank update to the attention projections, which appears to be insufficient to model the long-tailed, sparse signal of mortality on first-day procedure codes (positive prevalence 2.68%). Ensembling three rank-16 adapters (LoRA Ensemble) recovers some of the lost performance through variance reduction—it improves MCC (0.4390 ± 0.0134) and F1 (0.4477 ± 0.0132) over Single LoRA—but does not close the AUPRC/AUROC gap to full fine-tuning. The takeaway is that for a rare-event task whose signal is distributed across many low-frequency codes, adapter capacity itself becomes the binding constraint.

A final observation concerns model scale. ClinicalT5-Base full-parameter fine-tuning (AUPRC = 0.3931 ± 0.0061, MCC = 0.4064 ± 0.0252, F1 = 0.4091 ± 0.0333) is competitive with its larger counterpart on AUPRC and threshold-dependent metrics, while ClinicalT5-Large full-parameter fine-tuning pulls ahead on AUROC, precision, and specificity. This suggests that for first-day mortality prediction the additional capacity of the large model is most useful for refining the high-precision tail of the score distribution rather than for raising minority-class recall.

To characterize which clinical signals drive predictions in this setting, we examined the structure of predictive features in the XGBoost baseline. SHAP feature importances (Fig. 1) show that signal is distributed across more than 40 features with no single code dominating: emergency intervention codes such as cardiopulmonary resuscitation, ECMO, and therapeutic hypothermia contribute positive mortality signal, whereas elective procedure codes (e.g., total hip replacement, coronary stent insertion) contribute protective signal. Frequency-lift analysis across all 2,194 unique first-day ICD-9 codes28(Table 3) confirms that even the highest-lift codes with meaningful frequency are non-deterministic—for example, only 67.9% of patients receiving therapeutic hypothermia and 57.4% of patients receiving CPR die in hospital—so no individual code provides a ground-truth mortality label. This bidirectional, distributed signal structure is consistent with the model performing genuine acuity stratification on first-day procedure trajectories.

External validation on MIMIC-III

To assess robustness under real-world domain shift, we additionally evaluate full-parameter ClinicalT5 mortality models on an external test cohort from MIMIC-III. MIMIC-III (2001–2012) and MIMIC-IV (2008–2019) reflect different eras of care delivery and patient mix and therefore differ in acuity and outcome prevalence; in our cohorts, in-hospital mortality is substantially higher in MIMIC-III than in MIMIC-IV (11.9% vs. 2.7%). Rather than forcing prevalence matching, we treat this as a natural domain-shift evaluation. For comparability, we harmonize inputs by using ICD-9 procedure codes only and enforcing patient-level separation (one admission per patient). Because MIMIC-III lacks reliable per-procedure timestamps, we use the full ordered procedure sequence per admission rather than a first-day window; this asymmetry between training inputs (first-day) and external testing inputs (full admission) is a methodological constraint imposed by the external dataset, and we quantify its effect explicitly later in this section by decomposing the cross-dataset gap into an input-regime component and a domain-shift component.

Compared with internal MIMIC-IV evaluation (Table 2, internal panel), external performance on MIMIC-III shows the expected degradation under domain shift, but ClinicalT5 generalizes more gracefully than the classical baselines. ClinicalT5-Large full-parameter fine-tuning achieves the best external AUPRC (0.4080 ± 0.0204), best AUROC (0.8373 ± 0.0002), best ECE (0.0108 ± 0.0021), and best NLL (0.2829 ± 0.0008), uniformly outperforming XGBoost (AUPRC = 0.3849 ± 0.0053, AUROC = 0.8263 ± 0.0004, ECE = 0.0283 ± 0.0036, NLL = 0.2958 ± 0.0016) and logistic regression (AUPRC = 0.3654, AUROC = 0.8293, ECE = 0.0445, NLL = 0.3159). ClinicalT5-Base also generalizes well (AUPRC = 0.3989 ± 0.0007, AUROC = 0.8316 ± 0.0010), though with somewhat worse calibration (ECE = 0.0366 ± 0.0014) than the large variant.

A notable behavioral asymmetry appears at the threshold-dependent metrics. ClinicalT5-Large achieves the highest external precision (0.5943 ± 0.0346) and specificity (0.9911 ± 0.0058) but the lowest external F1 (0.1591 ± 0.0938) and MCC (0.1957 ± 0.0777) of any full-parameter variant. The large standard deviations on these metrics, combined with the precision/specificity dominance, indicate that under domain shift the large model becomes highly conservative—predicting positive only when very confident—which preserves AUPRC and ECE (which are threshold-independent) but collapses recall at the default decision threshold. ClinicalT5-Base, with implicitly less aggressive confidence behavior, retains a more balanced operating point (F1 = 0.3271 ± 0.0665, MCC = 0.2919 ± 0.0388). This pattern is consistent with the well-documented phenomenon that higher-capacity models trained to convergence tend to amplify confidence under covariate shift; for clinical deployment across institutions, it implies that probabilistic recalibration of the large model on a small target-domain validation set may be necessary to recover balanced operating points, even though the underlying ranking and probability quality remain strong. External MIMIC-III numbers should therefore be interpreted as upper-bound estimates under a more favorable input regime than the first-day training setup, not as direct comparisons to the internal panel.

On their face, ClinicalT5-Large achieves slightly higher AUPRC on the external MIMIC-III cohort than on the internal MIMIC-IV first-day test set (0.408 vs. 0.395), which would seem to suggest robust transfer. This comparison, however, conflates two effects with opposite signs. Re-evaluating ClinicalT5-Large on the same MIMIC-IV cohort with full-admission procedure sequences (Table 1) raises AUPRC to 0.466, an input-regime gain of + 0.071 over the first-day baseline. The external MIMIC-III evaluation also uses full-admission inputs (because MIMIC-III lacks reliable per-procedure timestamps), and therefore inherits this gain. After controlling for input regime, the MIMIC-III AUPRC of 0.408 sits 0.058 below the matched full-admission MIMIC-IV reference, revealing a genuine domain-shift penalty of similar magnitude that the raw cross-dataset comparison entirely masks. The same pattern holds for AUROC (input-regime gain + 0.019, domain-shift penalty − 0.126) and NLL (input-regime gain − 0.007, domain-shift penalty + 0.217).

30-day readmission rate

Table 4 summarizes 30-day readmission performance on MIMIC-IV. The picture differs substantially from in-hospital mortality, both in the calibration behavior of classical baselines and in the relative ranking of uncertainty-aware adaptations. Classical baselines achieve only modest discrimination and are poorly calibrated on this task: logistic regression yields AUROC = 0.7375 with ECE = 0.2398, while XGBoost yields AUROC = 0.7390 ± 0.0004 with ECE = 0.2561 ± 0.0001. Unlike the mortality task, where XGBoost was relatively well-calibrated, here both baselines exhibit large miscalibration—likely reflecting the higher event prevalence (18.9%) combined with substantial label heterogeneity, since 30-day readmission depends on post-discharge factors only partially observable from index-admission procedure sequences. Every ClinicalT5 variant improves substantially on calibration relative to these baselines (best ECE = 0.0283 ± 0.0218 from LoRA Ensemble on the large model, more than an order of magnitude better than XGBoost) while delivering small but consistent gains in AUROC and AUPRC.

Three behavioral patterns are notable, and each is interpretable in light of the underlying method.

First, MC Dropout on the fully fine-tuned large model again achieves the best discrimination. ClinicalT5-Large with MC Dropout obtains the best AUROC (0.7441 ± 0.0005) and best AUPRC (0.4290 ± 0.0019). As in the mortality task, this is consistent with MC Dropout combining full-capacity fine-tuning with inference-time variance reduction: stochastic averaging reduces sensitivity to per-sample noise without restricting representational capacity, and this benefit transfers across both prediction tasks.

Secondly, LoRA Ensemble achieves the best calibration on this task, reversing its mortality-task behavior. ClinicalT5-Large LoRA Ensemble yields the lowest ECE (0.0283 ± 0.0218), with the next-best calibration coming from ClinicalT5-Base full-parameter fine-tuning (ECE = 0.0364 ± 0.0079). The mechanism is straightforward: averaging the predictions of three independently initialized LoRA adapters reduces the variance of the predicted probability for each sample, which in turn smooths the reliability diagram and lowers ECE. In the mortality task, this smoothing was insufficient to overcome the dominant Bayesian-LoRA effect on a low-prevalence outcome; in readmission, where the positive class is more frequent (18.9% vs. 2.7%), simple averaging is sufficient to dominate calibration. The wide standard deviation on this estimate ( ± 0.0218), however, indicates seed sensitivity, and the calibration advantage should be interpreted with that caveat.

Thirdly, Bayesian LoRA performs worst among ClinicalT5 variants on readmission. Bayesian LoRA on ClinicalT5-Large yields the lowest AUROC (0.7056 ± 0.0009), lowest AUPRC (0.3829 ± 0.0001), and worst NLL (0.4717 ± 0.0057) among all transformer variants, while only achieving moderate ECE (0.0994 ± 0.0089). This reversal relative to its strong performance on mortality is again interpretable from the method’s nature. The variational ELBO objective uses a KL penalty (β = 1 × 10−4) to regularize the LoRA posterior toward the prior, which in the mortality setting helped suppress overconfident predictions on a rare class. In the readmission setting, where the predictive signal is genuinely weaker and more diffuse, the same KL regularization appears to over-suppress the adapter from moving toward the data-dependent solution—the posterior is pulled excessively toward an uninformative prior, degrading both rank ordering and likelihood. This suggests that the optimal β for Bayesian LoRA is task-dependent, and that uncalibrated KL weighting can be actively harmful when the signal-to-noise ratio of the task is unfavorable.

For full-parameter fine-tuning, the base model unexpectedly outperforms the large model on most calibration- and likelihood-related metrics: ClinicalT5-Base achieves the best NLL (0.4299 ± 0.0040), best precision (0.5168 ± 0.0240), best specificity (0.9409 ± 0.0135), and best accuracy (0.8138 ± 0.0046), while ClinicalT5-Large full-parameter has substantially worse ECE (0.1252 ± 0.0062). This is the readmission-task analog of the external-validation finding in Section 2.3: at higher capacity, full-parameter fine-tuning produces overconfident probabilities, which is acceptable when the task signal is strong (mortality on first-day procedures) but counterproductive when the task is harder and more noise-dominated (readmission). The implication for practitioners is that on tasks with weaker available signal, smaller backbones or explicit calibration mechanisms may offer better deployment behavior than scaling up the model. Two complementary takeaways emerge. First, classical baselines are no longer competitive on calibration for this task—every ClinicalT5 variant improves ECE by roughly an order of magnitude over XGBoost—and the AUROC gap, while modest in absolute terms, consistently favors the transformer family. Second, the choice of uncertainty mechanism matters: MC Dropout maximizes discrimination, LoRA Ensemble maximizes calibration, and full-parameter fine-tuning maximizes likelihood and precision-oriented operating-point performance. In practice, we view MC Dropout on the fully fine-tuned backbone as the most robust default choice—it ranks first or near-first on the largest set of metrics across both tasks—while LoRA Ensemble and Bayesian LoRA can be preferred when calibration is the operational priority, with the caveat that Bayesian LoRA is sensitive to the underlying signal strength and may underperform on harder tasks without β tuning.

Discussion

This study shows that structured ICD procedure code sequences, augmented only by age and gender, carry sufficient clinical context to support accurate prediction of in-hospital mortality and 30-day readmission in hospital-wide populations. Procedure sequences implicitly encode the timing and intensity of care—reflecting disease severity, intervention patterns, and resource utilization—and thus serve as a compact proxy for patient trajectories during admission28. The discrimination and calibration achieved across tasks suggest that actionable operational risk can be extracted from universally recorded and portable inputs, even without dense multimodal signals.

A key finding is that the preferred adaptation strategy depends on both the task and the evaluation objective, and that no single uncertainty mechanism dominates across all clinical metrics20,21,29. For in-hospital mortality on first-day procedure inputs (Table 2, internal panel), MC Dropout on the fully fine-tuned ClinicalT5-large is the strongest configuration overall, achieving the best AUPRC, NLL, MCC, and F1, while Bayesian LoRA achieves the lowest ECE. For 30-day readmission (Table 4), the same MC Dropout configuration again yields the best discrimination (best AUROC and AUPRC), while LoRA Ensemble achieves the best calibration. Across both tasks, this division reflects the underlying mechanisms of each method: stochastic inference reduces predictive variance without sacrificing representational capacity, and so consistently improves rank ordering and likelihood-based fit; explicit Bayesian regularization shapes the posterior toward smoother, better-calibrated probabilities, and so wins on ECE in the imbalanced mortality setting; and ensemble averaging trades a small amount of representational capacity for variance reduction across independently initialized adapters, which is most beneficial for calibration on the more heterogeneous readmission task.

Readmission prediction presents a different operating regime than mortality. Because 30-day readmission is less imbalanced ( ~ 18.9% positive) yet more heterogeneous, AUROC differences between methods are modest (0.71–0.74 across all transformer variants), and absolute ECE values are an order of magnitude larger than for mortality across every method. The most clinically actionable result is that ClinicalT5 variants improve calibration over XGBoost by roughly a factor of ten on this task, even as the AUROC improvement is small. This separation is consistent with the underlying nature of readmission risk, which depends on post-discharge factors that procedure sequences only partially capture—making it a setting where the marginal gain from a transformer is concentrated in probability quality rather than rank ordering. Within the ClinicalT5 family, Bayesian LoRA notably underperforms on this task (worst AUROC, AUPRC, and NLL among transformer variants), in contrast to its strong calibration behavior on mortality. We attribute this reversal to the fixed KL regularization weight (β = 1 × 10−4): when the underlying task signal is weak and diffuse, the same KL term that productively suppresses overconfidence on rare-event mortality can over-pull the adapter posterior toward an uninformative prior, degrading both rank ordering and likelihood. This suggests that the optimal β for Bayesian LoRA is task-dependent and motivates per-task tuning of the KL weight when deploying variational adapters across heterogeneous clinical prediction targets.

Model capacity interacts with adaptation choice in a task-dependent way. For mortality on first-day procedures, scaling from ClinicalT5-base to ClinicalT5-large under full-parameter fine-tuning generally improves AUROC and precision-oriented metrics, consistent with increased capacity to represent longer-range dependencies and subtle interactions in procedure trajectories. For readmission, however, the base model unexpectedly outperforms the large model on ECE, NLL, and several operating-point metrics under full-parameter fine-tuning, suggesting that at lower task signal-to-noise ratios, additional capacity primarily amplifies overconfidence rather than improving discrimination. This pattern reinforces the practical value of uncertainty-aware adaptations: MC Dropout and LoRA Ensemble both narrow the calibration gap between base and large models on readmission, while Bayesian LoRA is most beneficial precisely on the task (mortality) where capacity-induced overconfidence is the dominant calibration risk.

The interpretability analysis presented in Section 2.2 supports a clinically coherent picture of model behavior: predictive signal for first-day mortality is distributed across procedures that mark illness severity (CPR, ECMO, therapeutic hypothermia, mechanical ventilation) and elective procedures that mark relative wellness (joint replacement, elective stenting), with no single first-day code functioning as a near-deterministic mortality label. This distributed structure is consistent with the model performing acuity stratification across a range of clinically plausible signals rather than relying on a small number of high-leverage features.

External validation further highlights the deployment-relevant behavior of the model. When evaluated on MIMIC-III (Table 2, external panel), ClinicalT5 generalizes more gracefully than the classical baselines: ClinicalT5-large full-parameter fine-tuning achieves the best external AUPRC (0.4080), AUROC (0.8373), ECE (0.0108), and NLL (0.2829), in every case outperforming XGBoost. The principal degradation under domain shift is concentrated in operating-point metrics: F1 and MCC of the large model collapse on external testing because the model becomes very conservative (high precision, very high specificity, low recall at the default 0.5 threshold), even though the underlying ranking and probability quality remain strong. This pattern—rank-based and calibration metrics that travel well, threshold-dependent metrics that do not—argues for probabilistic recalibration on a small target-domain validation set as a standard part of cross-institution deployment, even when the underlying transformer model generalizes well at the distribution level30. We note that the MIMIC-III external evaluation uses the full ordered procedure sequence rather than a first-day window, because MIMIC-III lacks reliable per-procedure timestamps; external numbers should therefore be interpreted as upper-bound estimates under a more favorable input regime than the leakage-restricted training setup.

Practically, relying on standardized procedure codes and basic demographics enhances portability, interpretability, and integration feasibility. Many institutions lack harmonized access to continuous vitals or laboratory streams, whereas procedure coding is ubiquitous; models operating on this substrate may therefore be easier to deploy and audit at scale5. Future work should extend external validation across diverse hospitals and coding practices, characterize domain shift more systematically, and explore dynamic updating of risk and uncertainty as new procedures occur during hospitalization. While procedure-only modeling performs strongly, selectively incorporating a small set of consistently available modalities (e.g., key labs or summarized vitals) may further improve performance for complex cases when data quality permits6. Overall, our results suggest that procedure-code–driven models, paired with uncertainty-calibrated adaptation, can provide operationally useful and scalable risk estimates for general-admission decision support.

MIMIC-IV contains detailed EHR data for admissions to Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center (BIDMC) between 2008 and 201931, and was used to construct the training, validation, and internal test cohorts for both prediction tasks. Consistent with these differences, the external MIMIC-III cohort exhibits substantially higher in-hospital mortality than MIMIC-IV (11.9% vs. 2.7%), which we treat as a natural domain-shift evaluation rather than attempting prevalence matching.

Cohort extraction and split construction

Unlike prior work that focuses primarily on ICU cohorts, we include both ICU and general ward admissions to reflect a broader hospital-wide population. From each admission, we extracted demographic information (age and sex) and sequences of ICD procedure codes. Adult admissions were included by restricting age to 18–120 years. Procedure codes were ordered chronologically within each admission to form a procedure sequence representation.

Because U.S. clinical coding transitioned from ICD-9 to ICD-10 on October 1, 2015, MIMIC-IV contains admissions coded under both systems. For the in-hospital mortality task, we restrict model inputs to ICD-9 procedure codes only. This design choice aligns the MIMIC-IV training data with MIMIC-III, which contains exclusively ICD-9 procedures, thereby supporting a consistent internal–external evaluation framework. In addition, to mitigate temporal leakage for mortality prediction, we operationalize an early prediction setting by restricting inputs to procedures recorded on the first hospital day, reflecting information plausibly available at the time of initial clinical decision-making. To support the interpretability analysis reported in Section 2.2, we additionally computed frequency-specific mortality rates and lift scores across all 2,194 unique first-day ICD-9 procedure codes in the training set, and derived SHAP feature importances from the XGBoost baseline using the shap32 library with TreeExplainer33.

We deliberately restrict the in-hospital mortality task to ICD-9 procedure codes for three reasons. First, internal–external alignment: MIMIC-III is coded exclusively in ICD-9, so harmonizing MIMIC-IV training inputs to ICD-9 enables a like-for-like cross-dataset evaluation in which any observed performance gap reflects genuine domain shift rather than coding-system mismatch. Second, vocabulary consistency: ICD-9 and ICD-10 procedure codes use different granularities ( ≈ 4,000 vs. ≈ 87,000 codes), different hierarchical structures, and substantially different surface forms; a model trained on a mixed corpus must implicitly learn a cross-system mapping from the data, which on the smaller mortality cohort would conflate code-system effects with predictive signal. Third, deployment portability: many institutions and registries still operate primarily in ICD-9 for retrospective analyses, and an ICD-9-trained model can be applied directly to such cohorts without code translation. We retain the mixed ICD-9 + ICD-10 design only for the 30-day readmission task, where the larger cohort size ( ~ 222k admissions) makes cross-system learning tractable and where no external ICD-9-only readmission test set is used.

For the 30-day readmission task, the prediction target is by construction a post-discharge event, so index-admission procedures are temporally upstream of the outcome and the form of intra-admission leakage that motivates the first-day restriction for mortality does not directly apply. We assume an at-discharge prediction scenario, in which the full index-admission procedure sequence is observable before the 30-day window begins. We note that procedures coded near discharge may still correlate with discharge-planning decisions that themselves shape readmission risk; this reflects clinical decision-making encoded in the procedure record rather than future-outcome leakage, but readers interpreting the model under earlier-in-stay deployment scenarios should weight late-stay codes accordingly.

Data were split at the patient level to prevent information leakage across admissions from the same individual. For mortality prediction, MIMIC-IV admissions were divided into training, validation, and internal test sets in approximately 72%/14%/14% proportions while preserving outcome prevalence across splits. For readmission prediction, admissions were divided into approximately 72%/13%/15% splits. Readmission labels were defined using a forward-looking formulation: an index admission was labeled positive if a subsequent hospital admission for the same patient occurred within 30 days after discharge. Admissions ending in in-hospital death were excluded from the readmission cohort, as subsequent readmission is not possible.

For external validation, we constructed a test cohort from MIMIC-III. Unlike MIMIC-IV, MIMIC-III does not provide reliable procedure timestamps, which precludes restricting procedures to a same-day window. Therefore, for the MIMIC-III test set, we used the entire procedure sequence per admission, ordered by the recorded sequence number. This cohort was used exclusively for testing and not for model training or validation, enabling an assessment of cross-dataset generalization under dataset-specific structural constraints. Evaluation metrics

We evaluated model performance using a comprehensive set of metrics designed to capture classification accuracy, discrimination ability, and probabilistic reliability, which are all critical for clinical prediction tasks. Accuracy (Acc) quantified the proportion of correctly classified instances, offering an intuitive but coarse measure of overall correctness. To assess the quality of probabilistic predictions, we reported the Negative Log-Likelihood (NLL), which jointly penalizes incorrect predictions and overconfident probability estimates; lower NLL values indicate more accurate and well-calibrated probability outputs34. Expected Calibration Error (ECE) further quantified prediction reliability by measuring the alignment between predicted probabilities and observed outcome frequencies, with lower values reflecting superior calibration21.

Given the pronounced class imbalance commonly observed in clinical outcomes, particularly for adverse events, we incorporated additional metrics to provide a more robust and nuanced evaluation. The Matthews Correlation Coefficient (MCC) offered a balanced summary of classification performance by jointly accounting for true positives, false positives, true negatives, and false negatives, making it especially informative under imbalanced conditions35. Specificity (Spec.) measured the model’s ability to correctly identify negative cases, thereby reflecting the risk of false-positive predictions that may lead to unnecessary clinical interventions. Precision (Prec.) assessed the proportion of predicted positive cases that were truly positive, a key consideration when positive predictions trigger downstream clinical actions. The F1-score summarized the trade-off between precision and recall, providing a threshold-dependent measure of classification balance. Finally, the Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic Curve (AUROC) evaluated the model’s overall discriminative ability across all decision thresholds, capturing how well positive cases were ranked ahead of negative ones.

The prioritization and interpretation of these metrics were guided by the distinct clinical characteristics of in-hospital mortality and 30-day readmission prediction. In-hospital mortality represents a rare but irreversible outcome, where failing to identify high-risk patients carries substantial clinical consequences and reliable risk estimation is essential for decision support. Under this severe class imbalance, we emphasize metrics that better reflect performance on the positive class and the quality of predicted probabilities, including the Area Under the Precision–Recall Curve (AUPRC), AUROC, and NLL. In particular, AUPRC is especially informative in low-prevalence settings, as it directly captures the trade-off between precision and recall for high-risk patients. Sensitivity (recall) is additionally reported to quantify the risk of missed mortality cases, while ECE assesses whether predicted risk estimates are sufficiently calibrated to be clinically trustworthy. In contrast, accuracy and threshold-dependent summary metrics such as the F1-score are de-emphasized due to their limited interpretability in highly imbalanced mortality prediction tasks.

In contrast, 30-day readmission prediction is a more operational and resource-oriented task, characterized by a higher event prevalence and a stronger emphasis on avoiding unnecessary interventions. In this setting, false-positive predictions may lead to inefficient allocation of care management resources, making precision-oriented metrics particularly relevant. Accordingly, we focus on AUPRC and AUROC to evaluate overall discrimination, while prioritizing precision and specificity to reflect the practical utility of identifying patients who truly warrant post-discharge follow-up. NLL and ECE are reported to assess whether predicted readmission risks can be meaningfully used for risk stratification, whereas sensitivity-based metrics are considered secondary given the comparatively lower cost of missed cases relative to mortality prediction.

Baseline models

We adopted logistic regression and XGBoost as our baseline methods due to their well-established roles and widespread acceptance within clinical predictive modeling and biomedical research. Logistic regression is a straightforward linear model widely favored in clinical studies due to its interpretability and transparent structure, making it easy for clinicians to understand and validate36,37. The intercept term β0 represents the baseline log-odds when all predictors are zero, while the coefficients β1, …, βn quantify the influence of each predictor on the outcome. The logistic transformation ensures the predicted probabilities remain bounded between 0 and 1, directly facilitating clinical interpretation: positive coefficients indicate an increased probability, whereas negative coefficients suggest a reduced probability. Logistic regression’s simplicity and clarity have made it a cornerstone in clinical predictive analytics.

XGBoost (Extreme Gradient Boosting), on the other hand, is highly valued in clinical and biomedical research due to its robust predictive performance and ability to handle complex, nonlinear interactions among variables. It employs an ensemble method that sequentially builds decision trees by iteratively minimizing prediction errors through optimized loss functions. XGBoost consistently demonstrates strong predictive accuracy across a broad spectrum of biomedical datasets and clinical applications, including risk stratification, disease prognosis, and outcome prediction38,39.

Together, logistic regression and XGBoost serve as essential benchmarks within clinical predictive modeling. Their combined interpretability and predictive strength provide critical reference points, allowing researchers and clinicians to evaluate the incremental value and practical clinical relevance of advanced models, such as transformer-based foundation models like ClinicalT5.

Fine-tune clinical foundation models

We investigated three categories of clinical foundation models to evaluate their effectiveness in predicting critical outcomes such as in-hospital mortality and readmission rates. These categories included Structured-EHR sequence models, Clinical-note encoders, and Biomedical Large Language Models (LLMs).

Structured-EHR sequence models, represented by MedBERT and MedGPT, leverage structured clinical data, particularly ICD codes and procedural records, to effectively model patients’ temporal trajectories. Their strength lies in capturing sequential patterns within structured medical datasets, enabling precise and reliable outcome predictions14. Clinical-note encoders such as Clinical BERT and ClinicalT5, specialize in interpreting unstructured clinical notes, including physician documentation, discharge summaries, and narrative records. These models excel at extracting detailed and nuanced information from textual data, providing crucial insights necessary for accurate clinical predictions10,40. Biomedical LLMs, exemplified by BioGPT, are known for their broad applicability and robust generalization across various biomedical and clinical contexts. Their proficiency in clinical reasoning and generating coherent medical text makes them versatile tools within diverse healthcare applications13.

Among these, we chose ClinicalT5 as our primary foundation model due to its unique combination of analytical and generative capabilities specifically tailored for clinical applications. Unlike other clinical models that typically focus either on encoding structured data or decoding sequences, ClinicalT5 employs a sequence-to-sequence transformer architecture40. This enables the model to effectively comprehend complex clinical information and generate coherent, contextually relevant outputs. Such dual capability is particularly valuable in scenarios requiring detailed interpretation and generation of clinical narratives.

A critical factor influencing our selection of ClinicalT5 was the rich contextual information contained within ICD procedure code sequences.41 These sequences reflect the comprehensive clinical trajectory of a patient, encompassing temporal disease progression, severity, treatment decisions, and management patterns. Additionally, procedure sequences can highlight variations in clinical practices, resource utilization, and potential outcomes such as complications or hospital readmissions. Such detailed contextual information significantly enhances the predictive capability of models designed for clinical decision support.

ClinicalT5’s extensive pre-training on large-scale clinical textual data further strengthens its ability to interpret medical terminology and contextual subtleties effectively. Empirical assessments have consistently demonstrated that ClinicalT5-large delivers strong performance across essential evaluation metrics, including F1-score, Matthews Correlation Coefficient (MCC), and Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic Curve (AUROC)34,35. These metrics are particularly relevant for clinical predictive tasks where balanced sensitivity and specificity are crucial. Therefore, ClinicalT5’s capacity to leverage detailed contextual insights from ICD procedure sequences made it an optimal choice for foundational modeling in this research.

Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA) for fine-tuning ClinicalT5

In this study, we employed Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA) to fine-tune the ClinicalT5 model efficiently. LoRA introduces trainable low-rank matrices into existing transformer weights, significantly reducing computational and memory demands compared to traditional fine-tuning methods. These matrices effectively capture task-specific nuances, enabling rapid adaptation to specialized clinical datasets while updating fewer parameters. Due to its efficiency and effectiveness, LoRA is particularly suitable for clinical predictive modeling tasks, such as forecasting in-hospital mortality and hospital readmission rates, where rapid adaptation to new data is essential.

Computational considerations

Computational cost varies substantially across the evaluated adaptations (Table 6). Full-parameter fine-tuning and MC Dropout share the same training profile (39.4 min/epoch, 11.4 GB peak GPU memory), since MC Dropout is purely an inference-time procedure applied to the fully fine-tuned backbone; its overhead is concentrated at deployment, where T = 20 stochastic forward passes incur a 20 × latency cost (1,888 ms per batch of 32 vs. 94 ms for a single deterministic pass). Single LoRA reduces trainable parameters to 9.44 M (1.3% of the full backbone) and lowers peak training memory from 11.4 GB to 3.0 GB, yet wall-clock training time (42.2 min/epoch) remains comparable to full fine-tuning because the backward pass must still traverse the frozen backbone to propagate gradients to the adapter matrices; the efficiency benefit of LoRA is therefore in memory footprint and trainable-parameter count rather than training speed. LoRA Ensemble inherits the per-adapter memory profile but multiplies total training cost linearly with the ensemble size (3 × 42.2 = 126.6 min total on a single GPU) and incurs sequential inference latency proportional to the number of adapters (319 ms); on hardware capable of parallel adapter execution, ensemble inference approaches the latency of a single Single LoRA forward pass. Bayesian LoRA is instantiated at rank r = 8, with each LoRA weight parameterized by a learnable mean (μ) and log-variance (ρ), yielding the same trainable-parameter count (9.44 M) as a standard r = 16 adapter; this roughly doubles training time to 82.2 min/epoch due to per-step stochastic weight sampling and the KL-divergence term in the ELBO objective, and at inference incurs the same 20 × Monte Carlo overhead as MC Dropout (2,346 ms per batch of 32). For deployment scenarios requiring sub-second per-patient latency, Single LoRA and LoRA Ensemble with parallelised adapters are the most practical choices; for offline or batch risk-stratification workflows where calibrated uncertainty outweighs latency, MC Dropout and Bayesian LoRA offer richer predictive distributions at proportionally higher compute cost.
