Pharmacodynamic, prognostic, and predictive biomarkers in severe and critical COVID-19 patients treated with sirukumab
Authors: Kim Thys, Matthew J. Loza, Linghua Lynn, Katleen Callewaert, Lisa Varma, Marjolein Crabbe, Liesbeth Van Wesenbeeck, Erika Van Landuyt, Sandra De Meyer, Jeroen Aerssens, Inge Verbrugge
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
We examined candidate biomarkers for efficacy outcomes in hospitalized COVID-19 patients who were treated with sirukumab, an IL-6 neutralizing antibody, in a randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled, phase 2 trial. Between May 2020 and March 2021, 209 patients were randomized (sirukumab, n = 139; placebo, n = 70); 112 had critical COVID-19. Serum biomarkers were evaluated for the pharmacodynamic effect of sirukumab and for their potential prognostic and predictive effect on time to sustained clinical improvement up to Day 28, clinical improvement at Day 28, and mortality at Day 28. The absence of detectable IL-4 increase and smaller increases in CCL13 post-baseline were most significantly associated with better response to sirukumab (versus placebo) treatment for all clinical efficacy outcomes tested, especially in patients with critical COVID-19. These data suggest that patients with critical COVID-19 without detectable sirukumab-induced IL-4 levels are more likely to benefit from sirukumab treatment. Introduction

Coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) can result in life-threatening, severe respiratory illness. This is due in part to an increase in proinflammatory cytokines, including interleukin (IL)-6, which may be involved in immune dysregulation and more severe disease1,2. Accordingly, IL-6 pathway–blocking drugs have been investigated for efficacy in COVID-19 since the start of the pandemic. A recent meta-analysis of clinical trials using IL-6 pathway–blocking drugs in 10,930 patients hospitalized with COVID-19 revealed that IL-6 pathway blockade reduced mortality by approximately 14%3; however, treatment response was heterogenous and not all patients benefited. The IL-6 receptor inhibitor tocilizumab was granted emergency use authorization for the treatment of patients with COVID-19 in June 2021 and was approved for the treatment of COVID-19 in December 20224,5.

CNTO136COV2001 was a US-based, double-blind, placebo-controlled, phase 2 trial examining the efficacy and safety of sirukumab, an IL-6 neutralizing antibody, on top of standard of care (SOC) in patients hospitalized with severe or critical COVID-19. Between May 2020 and March 2021, 209 patients were randomized and treated. The primary endpoint of time to sustained clinical improvement (on a 6-point ordinal scale) up to Day 28 was not met. Median time to sustained clinical improvement was 17 days in the sirukumab group and 23 days in the placebo group (hazard ratio [HR], 1.1; 95% confidence interval [CI], 0.66–1.88; P> 0.05), and no significant difference in mortality was observed at Day 286.

Patients with COVID-19 have diverse symptoms and complications, and this heterogeneity presents challenges in investigating effective treatments7. A need remains to identify patients at risk for poor outcomes from COVID-19 and those who are likely to benefit from new interventions. Studies have begun to investigate whether biomarkers could serve as potential prognostic (indicating the likelihood of achieving a certain clinical event) and/or predictive (indicating the likelihood of response to treatment) measures of the efficacy of IL-6 pathway–blocking drugs. For example, ferritin has been identified as a predictor of response to tocilizumab treatment8, but has not yet been confirmed in other studies.

The present study of biomarkers was a planned exploratory objective of the clinical trial, with the main clinical outcomes reported elsewhere6. The aim of this exploratory study was to identify potential biomarkers associated with sirukumab pharmacodynamics and investigate biomarker associations with disease progression and response to sirukumab treatment. Specifically, serum analytes measured at a central laboratory for the trial were evaluated for whether baseline levels or early changes after study intervention are predictive (sirukumab treatment-specific) or prognostic (treatment-agnostic) of clinical response.

Methods

Study design and patients

This was a randomized, placebo-controlled, double-blind, multicenter, phase 2 trial conducted at 16 hospitals in the United States (Supplemental Table 1) that investigated the efficacy and safety of sirukumab plus SOC versus placebo plus SOC in patients hospitalized with severe or critical COVID-19 (ClinicalTrials.gov Identifier: NCT04380961)6. Patients were enrolled between May 2020 and March 2021.

Key eligibility criteria and endpoints have been described6. Briefly, eligible patients were adults (≥ 18 to < 85 years of age) hospitalized with severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2) infection confirmed by real-time polymerase chain reaction (PCR) and severe or critical disease. Severe disease was defined as requiring a low level of supplemental oxygen administration above pre–COVID-19 baseline oxygen requirement, if any. Critical disease was defined as requiring high levels of oxygen supplementation to sustain a blood oxygen saturation of > 93%. Patients were randomized 2:1 to receive a single intravenous (IV) administration of sirukumab 5 mg/kg plus SOC or placebo plus SOC. Randomization was stratified by age (< 65 and ≥ 65 years) and invasive mechanical ventilation use (yes/no) at the time of randomization.

A 6-point ordinal scale was used to categorize patients: 1, not hospitalized; 2, hospitalized but not requiring supplemental oxygen; 3, hospitalized requiring low-flow supplemental oxygen (severe disease); 4, hospitalized requiring noninvasive pressure ventilation, non-rebreather mask, or high-flow oxygen devices (critical disease); 5, hospitalized, on invasive mechanical ventilation (IMV) or extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO; critical disease); 6, death. Following the observation of trends for better responses in Critical Patients at the planned interim analysis, the protocol was amended to continue enrollment of Critical Patients (baseline 6-point ordinal scale category 4 or 5) only.

The primary endpoint was the time to sustained clinical improvement (i.e., an improvement of ≥ 2 categories relative to baseline on the 6-point ordinal scale up to Day 28). Key secondary endpoints were clinical improvement at Day 28 and all-cause mortality at Day 286.

The study was conducted according to the Declarations of Helsinki and Good Clinical Practice Guidelines. All patients or their legally acceptable representatives provided written informed consent.

The exploratory biomarker analyses reported here focus on both critical and severe COVID-19 patients who received study drug (sirukumab or placebo) and had ≥ 1 valid blood sample taken (Supplemental Table 2).

Blood sampling

Blood samples used for the analyses described here were collected at baseline (Day 1, before administration) and on Day 5. Follow-up analysis for IL-4 included data from additional time points during the first 28 days after administration. Serum or plasma was used for subsequent biomarker analysis. At least 1 biomarker sample was available for each patient; details are presented in Supplemental Table 2.

Biomarker analyses

Serum cytokines measured at baseline and approximately days 1, 5, 7, 14, and 21 (depending on when patients exited the study), included: IL-1β, IL-2, IL-4, IL-6 (baseline only), IL-8, IL-10, IL-12p70, IL-13, interferon γ (IFNγ), and tumor necrosis factor α (TNFα); chemokines assessed included: C-C motif chemokine ligand (CCL) 2 (monocyte chemoattractant protein [MCP]-1), CCL3 (macrophage inflammatory protein [MIP]-1α), CCL4 (MIP-1β), CCL13 (MCP-4), CCL17 (thymus and activation-regulated cytokine [TARC]), CCL22 (macrophage-derived chemokine [MDC]), CCL26 (Eotaxin-3), and C-X-C motif chemokine ligand 10 (CXCL10; IFNγ-induced protein 10 kDa [IP-10]). Levels of cytokines and chemokines were determined by MesoScale Discovery (MSD) assays at a central clinical laboratory (LabCorp) as they arrived. Note that IL-6 reagents were removed from postbaseline MSD cytokine assays because of anticipated interference of sirukumab-bound IL-6 in the assay, thus only baseline data are available. Free serum IL-6 was measured using a modified MSD immunoassay at Smithers (Gaithersburg, MD, USA), with postbaseline changes reported elsewhere6. Serum C-reactive protein (CRP), ferritin, plasma procalcitonin, and D-dimer were assessed using standard clinical chemistry methods at a central clinical laboratory (LabCorp) as they arrived.

Subgroup analyses in patients with versus without detectable postbaseline IL-4

Exploratory subgroup analyses were conducted for patients with versus without detectable IL-4 postbaseline (Day 5 or at any visit during the study up to Day 28). Clinical outcomes (percent of patients with sustained clinical improvement up to Day 28, time to sustained clinical improvement up to Day 28, and percent of patients with all-cause mortality at Day 28) were compared between patients with versus without detectable postbaseline IL-4.

Statistical analyses

Populations analyzed

The All Patients Analysis Set included all patients who were randomized and treated in the study. The Critical Patients Analysis Set consisted of all participants in the All Patients Analysis Set with confirmed critical COVID-19, defined as a score of 4 or 5 at baseline on the 6-point ordinal scale. In a post hoc analysis, comparisons were between the subgroups of patients without versus with detectable IL-4 postbaseline.

Available data and imputations

All biomarker values below the lower limit of quantification (LLOQ) of the assays were imputed as LLOQ/2, and values above the upper limit of quantification (ULOQ) were imputed as ULOQ. In addition, values were normalized by log2 transformation before analysis. Biomarkers for which all (or the vast majority) of the reported values were < LLOQ were excluded from respective analyses. Visit windows were imposed, so for a Day 5 sample, collection between Days 4 to 6 was allowed. In case of multiple observations, the measurement closest to the target day was used.

Baseline associations

Associations of baseline patient and disease characteristics with clinical endpoints were assessed using the Wald chi-squared test. Pearson correlation coefficients (r) were calculated between baseline characteristics and baseline biomarker values and between individual biomarkers. Significance was assessed using P values.

Dynamic and pharmacodynamic changes

Changes in biomarker expression levels were calculated for individual patients as the log2 transformation of the ratio of concentration at the indicated time point over the concentration at baseline (i.e., log2[fold/baseline]). Differences in log2(fold/baseline) datasets were evaluated using 1-sample t tests, testing differences from a hypothesized mean of 0. Differences in log2(fold/baseline) values between treatment groups were evaluated using general linear models. Percent change summary statistics were calculated as: 100% × [2estimate – 1], where estimate was the average of log2(fold/baseline) values for changes from baseline within a treatment group or the estimate of the average difference between groups in log2(fold/baseline) values for change from baseline. Fold statistics were calculated as 2estimate, with values < 1 transformed to the opposite of the reciprocal, representing the ratio of the geometric means of the test over the reference group of non–log-transformed data.

Prognostic and predictive models

Potential prognostic biomarkers were assessed in the placebo and sirukumab arms separately. Logistic regression was used for binary endpoints (reached primary endpoint [Y/N] and mortality at Day 28 [Y/N]), with biomarkers as independent variables. Cox proportional hazards models were fitted for time to reach the primary endpoint, with biomarkers as independent variables. Potential predictive biomarkers were modeled in the same way as prognostic biomarkers, with the addition of an interaction term between biomarker values and treatment arm. The same assessments for prognostic and predictive biomarkers were repeated for baseline values and change from baseline values at Day 5.

P values for both analyses were reported as significance levels. No adjustments were made for multiple comparisons. Associations with P values < 0.01 were generally selected for descriptions in the text.

Results

Patients

A total of 209 COVID-19 patients were randomized; 139 received sirukumab plus SOC and 70 received placebo plus SOC. All clinical centers randomized at least one patient for each treatment group except for two, which each randomized one patient only to either the sirukumab or placebo arm, plus 3 centers that did not enroll any patients (Supplemental Table 1). Among these, 112 (53.6%) patients had critical disease (6-point ordinal scale category 4 or 5) at baseline. Among patients with critical disease, 72 received sirukumab plus SOC and 40 received placebo plus SOC. Overall patient demographic and baseline disease characteristics were generally well balanced between the sirukumab and placebo arms6. Key characteristics relevant to this exploratory biomarker study are presented in Table 1. Three patients not requiring supplemental oxygen at baseline were inadvertently enrolled into the study and retained in the study as intent-to-treat population. Correlations among baseline biomarkers and between baseline biomarkers and demographic and disease characteristics are reported in Supplemental Tables 3 and Supplement Fig. 1. The most significant biomarkers associated with indicators of more severe disease at baseline included high or higher levels of procalcitonin, D-dimer, free IL-6, IL-6, IL-8, and CCL2 and low or lower levels of CCL22.

Biomarker changes from baseline to day 5

Changes in biomarker concentrations between baseline and Day 5 posttreatment were evaluated in sirukumab- and placebo-treated patients. CRP was significantly (P < 0.01) reduced at Day 5 compared to baseline in both arms, although to a greater extent in the sirukumab arm compared with the placebo arm (P < 0.01), while procalcitonin was only significantly reduced in the sirukumab arm (P < 0.01). At baseline, IL-4 was < LLOQ in all patients in both treatment groups but was significantly increased in the sirukumab arm at Day 5 (P < 0.01) and remained < LLOQ in all patients in the placebo arm on Day 5. CCL17 was significantly increased at Day 5 in both arms, although to a greater extent in the sirukumab arm (Table 2). CCL22 was significantly decreased in the placebo arm but not in the sirukumab arm (P < 0.01 between treatment groups). IFNγ showed a similar trend as CCL22 (P = 0.026 between treatment groups). CXCL10 was significantly decreased in the sirukumab and placebo arms, with a trend for a lesser decrease in the sirukumab arm. Conversely, CCL4 (MIP-1β) and CCL13 (MCP-4) were significantly increased in the sirukumab and placebo arms, with a trend for a greater increase in the sirukumab arm (P = 0.038 and P = 0.011 between treatment groups, respectively). CCL2 and TNFα were increased in both treatment groups; the increases were numerically higher (but not significantly different) in the sirukumab arm compared with the placebo arm. IL-10 and ferritin were significantly decreased by a similar amount in the sirukumab and placebo arms at Day 5. These results show that sirukumab treatment is associated with significant changes from baseline in some biomarker levels compared with placebo at Day 5, namely, decreased CRP and increased IL-4 and CCL17 compared with placebo.

Biomarkers associated with COVID-19 outcomes

Prognostic biomarkers indicate the likelihood of achieving a certain clinical event; in this study, they indicate the likelihood of achieving the primary and/or key secondary clinical endpoints. Predictive biomarkers indicate the likelihood of response to treatment with sirukumab. Associations between biomarkers and clinical outcomes or treatment response were assessed at baseline and at Day 5 (change from baseline). Because baseline disease severity (itself) was the variable most significantly associated with all primary and key secondary endpoints evaluated, these associations were evaluated separately in the All Patients and Critical Patients populations.

Baseline pretreatment assessments

Baseline biomarkers that were significantly associated with clinical outcomes (i.e., prognostic biomarkers) in the All Patients population included CRP, D-dimer, IL-6, IL-8, IL-10, CCL2, CXCL10, and TNFα (Fig. 1A). Higher levels of these biomarkers were associated with a worse outcome in ≥ 1 of the endpoints assessed. This prognostic effect was tested in the placebo arm but was also observed in the sirukumab arm. Among the biomarkers listed above, levels of IL-6, IL-8, and CCL2 had significant correlation with all 3 clinical endpoints tested. The patient mortality rate numerically increased with increasing IL-6, IL-8, and CCL2 levels (Fig. 2A).

Similar to the All Patients population, for Critical Patients, IL-6 and IL-8 were significantly associated with ≥ 1 of the clinical endpoints tested in the placebo arm (Fig. 1C).

With respect to predictive biomarkers, no baseline biomarkers were predictive of the treatment effect of sirukumab. Although CRP, procalcitonin, IL-6, IL-8, IL-10, TNFα, CCL2, CXCL10, CCL4, and CCL22 were associated with worse outcomes in ≥ 1 endpoint in the sirukumab arm in the All Patients and/or Critical Patients populations, no significantly different associations were observed compared to placebo (Fig. 1A and C). In Critical Patients, lower baseline IL-6 levels showed the strongest predictive sirukumab treatment–specific trend for association with decreased mortality (interaction P value = 0.06; data not shown) in the sirukumab treatment group.

On-treatment changes and their association with outcome

Changes in biomarker levels from baseline to Day 5 that were associated with improved outcomes in ≥ 1 clinical endpoint in the placebo arm of the All Patients population included CRP, D-dimer, ferritin, procalcitonin, IL-8, IFNγ, CCL2, and CXCL10 (Fig. 1B). Except for CRP and ferritin, similar associations were observed in the sirukumab arm. Greater reductions in CRP and CXCL10 were significantly associated with a better outcome for all 3 endpoints tested (Fig. 1B and Fig. 2B). In patients who received placebo and died by Day 28, procalcitonin and IL-8 showed significantly greater increases from baseline while CRP and CXCL10 showed significantly smaller decreases from baseline compared with patients in the placebo arm who survived (Fig. 2B). Similar trends were observed within the placebo arm of the Critical Patients population (Fig. 1D).

At Day 5, changes in D-dimer, procalcitonin, IL-2, IL-4, IL-8, IFNγ, TNFα, CCL2, CCL3, CCL13, and CXCL10 were significantly associated with improved outcomes in ≥ 1 endpoint in the sirukumab arm of the All Patients population, with similar trends observed in Critical Patients (Fig. 1B and D). However, only CCL13, which showed opposite effects in the placebo and sirukumab arms, had a significant interaction between treatment and clinical outcomes for changes in these biomarker levels, and thus was the only sirukumab-specific effect identified (Fig. 1). CCL13 significantly increased at Day 5 in sirukumab-treated patients (Table 2), but the increase was significantly smaller in patients who survived by Day 28 (Fig. 2C). CCL13 also significantly increased at Day 5 in placebo-treated patients (Table 2); however, there was no association of the change in CCL13 levels with survival by Day 28 (Fig. 2C). Notably, IL-4 was not detected at Day 5 in any of the placebo-treated patients (Table 2), and smaller increases in IL-4, presenting as a lack of upregulation > LLOQ, were significantly associated with better outcomes in sirukumab-treated patients (Fig. 1B and D, and Fig. 3). In summary, higher baseline levels of IL-6, IL-8, CCL2 were significantly associated with worse clinical outcomes, whereas smaller changes from baseline in IL-4 and smaller increases in CCL13 at Day 5 posttreatment were associated with better clinical outcomes. No baseline biomarkers were identified that predicted response to sirukumab.

Postbaseline IL-4: A sirukumab-specific marker associated with treatment outcomes

Because a smaller change from baseline in IL-4 at Day 5 was the most significant biomarker associated with better outcomes in all 3 clinical endpoints in sirukumab-treated patients with COVID-19 and these effects were different between the placebo and sirukumab arms (Fig. 3A), the associations between IL-4 levels postbaseline and treatment outcomes with sirukumab were assessed further.

The prevalence of detectable postbaseline IL-4 was assessed in patient subgroups. On Day 5, 0% (0 of 58) of placebo- and 29.5% (31 of 105) sirukumab-treated patients had detectable IL-4 levels (Fig. 3B). Among sirukumab-treated patients, detectable IL-4 was more common in patients with critical disease at baseline (6-point ordinal scale category 4–5; 64.2% [43 of 67]) than in patients with (at most) severe disease at baseline (6-point ordinal scale category 2–3; 12.7% [7 of 55]). In 21 patients (placebo, n = 2; sirukumab, n = 19), IL-4 was not detectable on Day 5 but was detectable at a later time point posttreatment. In total, 3.1% (2 of 65) of placebo-treated patients and 41.0% (50 of 122) of sirukumab-treated patients had detectable postbaseline IL-4.

Clinical outcomes following sirukumab treatment were investigated by detection of IL-4 postbaseline. In the All Patients population treated with sirukumab (either severe or critical disease at baseline), a trend for favorable outcomes was observed in those without detectable IL-4 postbaseline (Fig. 3C). This effect was even more pronounced in the Critical Patients population. Specifically, in sirukumab-treated patients with COVID-19 in the Critical Patients population without (n = 43) versus with (n = 24) detectable IL-4 postbaseline, the median time to sustained clinical improvement was 8.5 days versus > 28 days. Similarly, for Critical Patients without versus with detectable IL-4 postbaseline, the proportion of patients who achieved sustained clinical improvement at Day 28 was 91.7% versus 41.9% and the rate of mortality at Day 28 was 8.3% versus 30.2%, respectively (Fig. 3C and D). Additionally, clinical outcomes for Critical Patients treated with sirukumab with detectable IL-4 postbaseline were similar to clinical outcomes for Critical Patients treated with placebo (n = 40): the median time to sustained clinical improvement was > 28 days versus 23 days, the proportion of patients with sustained clinical improvement at Day 28 was 41.9% versus 55.0%, and mortality at Day 28 was 30.2% versus 30.0%, respectively (Fig. 3C).

Baseline and postbaseline biomarkers associated with detectable IL-4 postbaseline in the sirukumab arm were assessed. Baseline biomarkers elevated to the greatest degree in patients with detectable IL-4 postbaseline included IL-6, CRP, CXCL10, CCL2, IL-8, and procalcitonin (Figs. 3E and 4; and Supplemental Table 4). In the Critical Patients population, only baseline IL-6 was significantly different between patients with versus without detectable IL-4 following sirukumab treatment, with a similar trend (P = 0.039) observed for CRP.

Detectable IL-4 postbaseline was significantly associated with greater upregulation of D-dimer, CCL2, CCL4, CCL13, IFNγ, and TNFα on Day 5 (Fig. 3F and Supplemental Table 4). In addition, levels of CXCL10 showed little or no change in sirukumab-treated patients with detectable IL-4 postbaseline, whereas it was generally decreased in patients without detectable postbaseline IL-4. CRP was downregulated less in patients with versus without detectable IL-4 following sirukumab treatment. Among Critical Patients, the profile was generally similar; significantly greater upregulation of CCL2, CCL4, CCL13, IFNγ, IL-8, and CXCL10 was observed in patients with versus without detectable IL-4 following sirukumab treatment. Taken together, these results demonstrate that a lack of detectable IL-4 postbaseline in sirukumab-treated patients with critical COVID-19 was associated with favorable clinical outcomes.

Discussion

COVID-19 patients were treated with IL-6 pathway inhibitors early in the pandemic. Because responses have been variable, there is a need to determine whether patients who might benefit from such drugs can be identified prior to treatment. In this exploratory study, several biomarkers associated with COVID-19 disease characteristics and biomarkers prognostic for clinical outcomes were identified, including IL-6, IL-8, and CCL2. Furthermore, early changes in CCL13 and IL-4 were associated with clinical outcomes in the sirukumab arm.

Similar to previous studies, several biomarkers tested at baseline in the current study were associated with disease severity at baseline, as measured by a 6-point ordinal scale, and were associated with poor outcomes. Procalcitonin and D-dimer were elevated in the most severe patients and associated with higher mortality or need for intensive care unit care, similar to findings from previous studies9. IL-6, IL-8, IL-10, CCL2, CXCL10, and TNFα have been proposed as prognostic biomarkers for COVID-19 outcomes10–12. CCL22 was the only biomarker in our panel with a significant negative correlation with COVID-19 disease severity at baseline, which is in agreement with a recent report by Ling et al13. However, no association was observed between CCL22 levels and clinical outcomes. High CRP levels were also associated with indicators of more severe disease, although correlations with the biomarkers described previously showed a higher level of significance (P < 0.001).

Because CRP is directly induced by IL-6, it was expected that sirukumab would strongly decrease CRP levels, as observed in patients with rheumatoid arthritis who are treated with sirukumab14. Although CRP levels were decreased in the sirukumab treatment group, CRP levels were also decreased in the placebo group to a lesser extent. In the placebo group, greater decreases in CRP were associated with more favorable outcomes, similar to findings from previous studies15. This could be a consequence of an acute phase response diminishing as the infection resolves with SOC. Those who do not improve would maintain the ongoing acute phase response with smaller decreases in CRP. However, the extent of decrease in CRP levels with sirukumab treatment was not related to clinical outcomes, suggesting that CRP per se is not causing COVID-19 morbidity and mortality. Decreases in procalcitonin, another acute phase reactant, showed a similar prognostic association with outcomes for the placebo group and lack of prognostic association for the sirukumab group.

CXCL10, an IFN-induced lymphocyte chemoattractant, was decreased in both the sirukumab and placebo groups, with greater decreases associated with favorable outcomes. Similar to CRP and procalcitonin, decreases in CXCL10 may be a consequence of resolution of COVID-19, but whether CXCL10 is at least in part causative in the resolution of COVID-19 cannot be determined. On average, levels of IL-8 were not significantly changed in either treatment group. However, there was a significant difference in changes in IL-8 levels associated with clinical outcomes, with trends for increased IL-8 levels in patients with a poor prognosis. This observation suggests that clinical worsening or lack of improvement may lead to IL-8 upregulation independent of IL-6 or that IL-8 upregulation contributes to clinical worsening.

The Th2-cell and regulatory T-cell chemoattractant CCL17 increased by Day 5 in the placebo treatment group; however, considerably greater increases were observed in the sirukumab treatment group. Similar trends were observed for the chemokines CCL2, CCL4, and CCL13. However, the increases were not always associated with worse clinical outcomes in each of the treatment groups, thus the relevance of the relative chemokine increases should be further defined.

Although there was no overall significant clinical benefit of sirukumab6, these data suggest that a subgroup of patients with critical COVID-19 could benefit from treatment with sirukumab. This subgroup of patients with critical disease was characterized by an absence of IL-4 upregulation post–sirukumab treatment. No single baseline biomarker tested in the current analysis could predict sirukumab treatment outcome; therefore, at present, we cannot identify patients at baseline to inform treatment decisions. However, higher baseline IL-6 levels were associated with sirukumab-associated IL-4 upregulation and showed the strongest trend for association with treatment effect on mortality in Critical Patients. This finding is supported by a recent study that found a numerical association between lower upregulation of IL-4 in COVID-19 patients and favorable clinical outcomes in patients treated with tocilizumab16. Collectively, these data suggest that the association between IL-4 upregulation and poor clinical outcomes is a general characteristic of IL-6 pathway blockade in COVID-19. Additional studies will be required to further develop a predictive panel for response to IL-6 pathway blockade in COVID-19 patients.

How could IL-6 pathway blockade lead to IL-4 upregulation, especially in critically ill patients with COVID-19 with higher systemic baseline IL-6 levels? One hypothesis is that systemic IL-6 in critically ill patients with COVID-19 results in a feedback mechanism that inhibits IL-4 upregulation. When the IL-6 pathway is blocked in those patients, this feedback mechanism would be inhibited and IL-4 subsequently upregulated. Sirukumab-induced IL-4 upregulation was associated with IFNγ, TNFα, and CCL2 upregulation as well as upregulation of several other cytokines and chemokines (Fig. 4), which is consistent with observations of IL-6 blockade in other studies16. Thus, it is possible that these cytokines and chemokines may be secreted from the same cells. Candidate cell types include natural killer T cells17and “early” CD4 T-cell subsets18; however, further studies are needed.

Several publications have implicated IL-4 and downstream signaling pathways in pathogenic processes, such as pulmonary fibrosis and fungal infections19–24. Thus, we cannot rule out that induction of IL-4 (and/or associated cytokines or chemokines) contributes to pathogenicity in COVID-19 patients. However, because this study was not designed to assess causal relationships between changes in biomarker levels and clinical outcomes, this should be explored in future studies.

Limitations of this study include the limited sample sizes available in a phase 2 clinical trial coupled with the number of statistical tests performed, with a consequence of nominal significance levels reported without adjustment for multiple testing. Although strict multiple testing corrections may be too strict given the strong intercorrelations both within the 3 major clinical outcomes considered and within many of the serum analytes measured, the associations with p < 0.01 that we generally highlighted would require validation in an independent study prior to practical, clinical application of the reported findings. Although critical patients were analyzed in addition to all patients with similar results, there would have been limited statistical power to directly compare associations with clinical outcomes between severe and critical patients. Mechanistic understanding of the IL-4 pharmacodynamic associations are limited by the lack of pulmonary tissue data sources.

In conclusion, our data suggest that critically ill patients with COVID-19 without detectable postbaseline IL-4 up to Day 28 after sirukumab treatment are a potential group of responders to sirukumab treatment. Next steps could include assessment of these findings in other IL-6 pathway trials and for other immunomodulators.
