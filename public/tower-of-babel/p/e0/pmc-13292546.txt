Protocol to quantify desmoglein 3 tension at cell-cell adhesions using FRET-based tension sensors
Authors: Bahareh Tajvidi Safa, Timothy Goldsmith, Zachary Kezhaya, Sarah Seman, Daniel E. Conway, Ruiguo Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryDesmoglein 3 (Dsg3) is a crucial component of cell-cell junctions, contributing to mechanotransduction and maintaining tissue integrity. Here, we present a protocol to quantify tension at keratinocyte cell-cell adhesion sites using a Dsg3 fluorescence resonance energy transfer (FRET)-based tension sensor. We describe steps for increasing viral titer by amplifying adenovirus in HEK293A cells and optimizing infection conditions to ensure uniform expression of FRET signals. We detail procedures for live-cell imaging of keratinocytes and image analysis to quantify the FRET ratio.For complete details on the use and execution of this protocol, please refer to Jin et al.1

Before you begin

Cell-cell junctions are integral structures that couple neighboring cells together to form tissues. In addition to serving as mechanical linkages to the cytoskeleton and maintaining tissue mechanical integrity, these adhesion complexes regulate cell sorting and migration, and control chemical signals that further direct cell behavior.2 In epithelial cells, these anchoring junctions include adherens junctions, which attach to actin filaments, and desmosomes, which tether to intermediate filaments.3 While functions of adherens junctions have been intensely studied, with abundant reports showing their ability to transduce mechanical stress at the adhesion sites via mechano-sensitive molecules, such as E-cadherin4 and α-catenin,5 the potential of the desmosome as a mechano-signaling hub is less explored. Indeed, it is unclear whether desmosomes are under tension at rest and how tension is transmitted to the desmosome. Recent reports have shown that the desmosome linker molecule, desmoplakin, sustains tension under externally applied load but not under homeostatic conditions.6 Desmosomal cadherin desmoglein 2 (Dsg2), however, has been shown to sustain tension in homeostasis in epithelial cells.7

Using a newly developed Dsg3 FRET sensor, we reported that the desmosomal cadherin Dsg3 is under tension during homeostasis and this tension is released when the desmosome is disrupted in pemphigus vulgaris (PV),1 an autoimmune skin blistering disorder that is characterized by the presence of autoantibodies-directed against desmosomal proteins, primarily Dsg3.8 While PV serves as an excellent model to investigate the mechanisms of organ-specific autoimmunity, the precise mechanisms of antibody-directed keratinocyte acantholysis and blister formation are ill-defined.9 There is now accumulating evidence that the mere binding of autoantibody to epidermal targets may be insufficient in itself to affect lesional pathology, and that mechanical stress and mechanotransduction additionally play a critical role in dictating the fate of cell-cell attachment subsequent to autoantibody attachment in the skin.10

To study these biophysical transformations after antibody binding and their impact on cell mechanics and mechanotransduction responses, we used a FRET sensor engineered to Dsg3, the target of the PV antibody. Our new FRET sensor captures tension loss in Dsg3 after antibody binding, and this tension loss also led to changes in cell-cell junction mechanics and cell contractility pathways. This protocol describes the specific steps from sensor preparation and transfecting the sensors into keratinocytes to using the sensors to quantify tension losses at Dsg3 under PV antibody treatment. The protocol also contains detailed steps for imaging FRET sensors and image analysis.

Innovation

This is a new FRET sensor on desmosomal cadherin Dsg3, capable of measuring tensional change at the desmosome cell-cell junctions. The use of an engineered FRET sensor allows for the measurement of internal desmosomal tension, different from conventional cell mechanical quantification tools such as atomic force microscopy and optical tweezers, which probe externally. The Dsg3 FRET sensor shows that desmosomal cadherin is under homeostatic tension and that this tension balance may be disturbed in disease, leading to mechanotransduction processes that alter disease pathology. This new sensor will allow researchers to examine the impact of mechanical compensation and mechanotransduction on cell-cell adhesions.

Expected outcomes

The Dsg3 FRET tension sensor module (TSMod) has two fluorophores, an mTFP donor and a Venus acceptor. The FRET ratio is measured by averaging the fluorescent intensities of the acceptor (yellow) divided by that of the donor (cyan), representing the energy transfer between the donor/acceptor pair. It is used as an indicator of tensile force at desmosomal cadherin junctions, specifically Dsg3. A low FRET ratio corresponds to an increase in the distance between the two fluorophores, pointing to high tension at the desmosomal adhesion sites. Whereas a high FRET ratio indicates low tension, i.e., the fluorophores are close together. This is demonstrated with HaCaT cells treated with EGTA, a calcium chelator that destabilizes cell-cell junctions. It is expected that EGTA treatment increases the FRET ratio compared with controls, as shown in Figure 4A, demonstrating the sensor’s effectiveness for intercellular tension detection. Additionally, we examined HaCaT cells treated with PV antibodies and reported that the FRET ratio increased by approximately 10% with AK23 at 10 μg/mL for 4 h (Figure 4B), displaying tension loss at the cell-cell junctions. Quantification and statistical analysis

Statistical data was analyzed using GraphPad Prism 9. All results were presented as mean ± standard deviation. Quantile-quantile (Q-Q) plot was used for the normality test. One-way ANOVA with Tukey multiple comparisons test was performed to compare the significance among groups. To compare the difference between several different timepoints with three or more groups, two-way ANOVA with Tukey multiple comparisons test was used. A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant and is shown by a single asterisk (∗). Data sets with p < 0.01 and p < 0.001 were highly significantly different and were denoted by double (∗∗) and triple (∗∗∗) asterisks, respectively.

Limitations

As is common with many TSMod FRET sensors, this Dsg3 FRET sensor has limited range of detectable forces. In addition, FRET images are often low in signal to noise ratio, and the fluorescence signals from the donor and acceptor can be influenced by environmental conditions. These limitations require the rigorous use of properly designed control samples, such as the tailless FRET sensor that was used in this study. Aside from the FRET sensor, the quantification process of the FRET images is also limited by the time-consuming process of drawing manual masks to outline the cell-cell contact region. An automated algorithm is better suited for this purpose.

Troubleshooting

Problem 1

Low cell viability after adenovirus transfection.

Potential solution

Transfect cells when they are approximately 50-70% confluent to prevent overconfluency. Transfected HEK293A cells can be easily detached by mechanical force; thus, it is recommended to handle the flasks gently (Related to step 2 and 12).

Problem 2

Variation in the FRET intensity signal based on the batch of viruses used.

Potential solution

The experiment is suggested to be performed using the same batch of viruses. Thus, it is advised to ensure the amplification amount is adequate when planning experiments. Practice consistency in handling methods and protocols. It is also recommended to have control samples for each experiment (Related to optimizing virus lysate concentration for infection).

Problem 3

Choice of the Z-plane and different imaging areas during confocal imaging.

Potential solution

Potential bias during imaging can be minimized by first conducting a Z-stack to evaluate the thickness of the cell monolayer and identify the focal plane with highest Dsg3 intensity. It may help to refer to brightfield images to check the morphology of the cell monolayer. Always ensure high sample quality and capture multiple images from different locations of each sample. Maintain a clean cell monolayer by minimizing the accumulation of dead cells and avoiding overconfluency within the experiment timeline (Related to step 16 and step 24).

Problem 4

During quantification, low signal to noise ratio and potential bias in drawing manual masks.

Potential solution

Before masking out the junction, subtract the background first to remove the noise signal. Perform rolling ball background subtraction on all raw images via ImageJ before running image analysis. Exclude pixels with saturated fluorescent intensity before analysis and avoid saturation during confocal image acquisition to ensure optimal raw data quality. Ideal masks should include junctions and their region of interaction with other cell boundaries, excluding all signals in the cytoplasm region and the external background (Related to step 33).

Problem 5

Photobleaching of donor and/or acceptor fluorophores during repeated or extended confocal imaging.

Potential solution

Photobleaching can introduce systematic errors into the FRET ratio because the donor (mTFP) and acceptor (Venus) often bleach at different rates. To minimize photobleaching, (i) reduce laser power to the lowest level that still provides sufficient signal above background, (ii) avoid repeated exposure of the same field unless necessary, and (iii) apply the acquisition parameters defined in the optimization step consistently across all conditions and imaging sessions.

Problem 6

Low signal-to-noise ratio in donor and/or acceptor channels during confocal image acquisition.

Potential solution

Low signal-to-noise ratio often arises from suboptimal acquisition settings. Increase the dwell time or apply line averaging to improve photon collection. Do not compensate for weak signal by raising detector gain alone, as high gain without sufficient true photon signal amplifies noise rather than the signal of interest. First confirm that laser power, dwell time, and pinhole size are appropriate, and only then adjust gain.

To rule out spectral crosstalk between the mTFP donor (emission ∼492 nm) and Venus acceptor (emission ∼528 nm) channels, acquire donor-only and acceptor-only control samples and verify that signal in the cross-channel is negligible; if bleed-through is observed, narrow the emission detection windows or switch from simultaneous to sequential excitation. Maintain identical acquisition settings across all samples within an experiment to allow direct comparison of FRET ratios.
