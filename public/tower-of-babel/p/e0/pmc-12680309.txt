Oral food challenges in food protein-induced enterocolitis syndrome: Practical considerations
Authors: Rory Nicolaides, Anna Cheng, J Andrew Bird
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground:Food protein–induced enterocolitis syndrome (FPIES) is a non–immunoglobulin E mediated food hypersensitivity disorder that primarily affects children in the first few years of life but may affect all ages. An FPIES diagnosis remains challenging due to the lack of specific biomarkers and is typically based on the clinical history and oral food challenges (OFC).Methods:This review examines the current literature on diagnosing primarily pediatric FPIES, indications and timing for OFCs, specific considerations for adult and atypical FPIES OFCs, management of co-reactive or cross-reactive foods, and factors that influence the location and approach for conducting OFCs.Results:An FPIES diagnosis includes meeting specific clinical criteria, with OFCs being the diagnostic standard in cases of diagnostic uncertainty. In the United States, OFCs are generally performed 12 to 18 months after the last reaction to a suspected trigger to assess for tolerance. Regional variations in trigger food prevalence and OFC timing were noted. Nutritional and cultural importance of the offending food, severity of previous reactions, and parental comfort are factors in deciding the timing and setting of OFCs. Management of potential co-reactive and cross-reactive foods remains variable, requiring cautious, supervised reintroduction.Conclusion:FPIES requires a careful diagnosis and management. OFC timing and approach should be tailored to individual patient needs and factor in past reaction severity and the importance of the food trigger in the regional diet and nutritional value. More research is needed to standardize OFC protocols. Enhanced guidelines and increased awareness among health-care providers can lead to more accurate diagnoses and better FPIES management, ultimately improving patient outcomes.Keywords: food allergy, Food protein induced enterocolitis syndrome, FPIES, Non-IgE mediated, OFC, oral food challenge, protocol

DEFINITION AND DIAGNOSIS

Food protein–induced enterocolitis syndrome (FPIES) is a non–immunoglobulin E (IgE) mediated food hypersensitivity that typically manifests in infancy after the introduction of first foods (e.g., cow’s milk formula, oat cereal). Acute FPIES is the most prevalent subtype, which affects 90% of patients.1 This condition often presents with persistent, intractable vomiting within 1 to 4 hours after exposure to the food allergen. Initial reactions have been reported to occur on first or second exposure to the food but have also been reported to occur after several previous exposures. Associated symptoms include lethargy, pallor, and diarrhea that occur within 24 hours of consuming the offending food. Chronic FPIES, which is much less common than the acute form, usually appears in early infancy due to exposure to cow’s milk–based formula. In patients in chronic cases, symptoms include intermittent vomiting, chronic diarrhea, failure to thrive, and, in more severe instances, metabolic acidosis.

Historically, FPIES was linked primarily to cow’s milk or soy proteins; however, the range of food triggers has expanded to include solid foods such as rice and oats, hen’s egg, peanuts, tree nuts, and seafood.2 In the United States, eggs, peanuts, and tree nuts have become prominent solid food triggers.1–3 Similar trends are observed globally, with cow’s milk, soy, rice, and seafood being common triggers.3 The prevalence of trigger foods varies regionally and culturally, with seafood, for example, being a more common trigger in countries such as Spain and Italy.4,5 For further information, please reference the other article in this issue.6

Establishing the diagnosis of FPIES is challenging due to the absence of diagnostic biomarkers and a limited understanding of its pathogenesis. Therefore, the diagnosis of FPIES is typically made based on a detailed patient history and clinical response to the suspected trigger food. Given the lack of specific tests, oral food challenges (OFC) are considered the diagnostic standard to confirm the diagnosis of FPIES.

Because there are no reliable surrogate biomarkers to establish the diagnosis, acute FPIES is a clinical diagnosis based on recognition of a collection of characteristic symptoms, with OFCs used to help confirm the diagnosis if it remains unclear. Proposed consensus guidelines recommend diagnosis reliant on one major criterion and at least three minor criteria (Table 1). Chronic FPIES is less delineated; however, there are certain characteristics that may establish diagnosis (Table 1). Despite these clinical criteria, it is still difficult to establish a timely diagnosis of FPIES because some of the symptoms may reflect other differential diagnoses, including infections, e.g., gastroenteritis, other types of food allergies, metabolic and/or genetic disorders, or anatomic obstruction (Table 2). In patients in whom the history is unclear, OFCs can be used to confirm the diagnosis and, more often, can be used to assess tolerance to a suspected food trigger.

INDICATIONS AND TIMING OF FPIES OFCs

In the United States, FPIES diagnostic OFCs are typically conducted no earlier than 12 to 18 months after the most recent reaction to a suspected food trigger.1 Consider the following scenario that illustrates the factors that may influence when an FPIES OFC is offered.

Uncertain Diagnosis

A parent reports that his or her 7-month-old infant experienced several episodes of vomiting 2 hours after hard-boiled egg ingestion. This was the third time that the child had eaten a hard-boiled egg over the course of 6 weeks. The parent is unsure how much was eaten with the first two exposures but, with the third exposure, the parents felt that she ingested at least a fourth of a mashed hard-boiled egg. After the third exposure, the parent notes that the child was sitting in her car seat when the vomiting started. She vomited several times and her stools were “different” for the following 24 hours. There were no known sick contacts, although the child is in daycare. The child appeared sleepy, but the child’s parents were unable to clearly communicate if the child was “lethargic” or “pale appearing.”

In this scenario, the differential diagnosis of the most likely etiologies may include viral gastroenteritis, motion sickness, or FPIES. Because of the value of having allergenic foods, e.g., egg, in the infant diet to promote prevention of IgE-mediated egg allergy, the physician would likely be more inclined to offer OFC to verify the diagnosis rather than delaying introduction or readdressing at a future time. If avoidance of the food were less likely to lead to an IgE-mediated reaction if not introduced and the food is not a regular part of the family’s diet (e.g., sweet potato), then it might be reasonable to reconsider a challenge when the child is older. It would also be prudent to gauge the effect of avoidance of an uncertain diagnosis on the family’s quality of life. Regardless of the food, if the parents experienced significant distress because of the uncertainty, then it would be prudent to confirm or refute the diagnosis expeditiously.

As demonstrated, when to perform an OFC to assess tolerance may vary based on several factors, including the nature of the food trigger, the severity of the reaction, the food's importance in the regional diet or for nutritional needs, and geographic variations in the rates of resolution for specific foods. For instance, in the United States, FPIES related to cow's milk shows a resolution rate of 60% by 1 year of age and 75% by 2 years of age.7 Thus, it is advisable to perform OFC between the ages of 1 and 2 years to assess tolerance, especially because cow's milk and cow’s milk–derived products are staples in the Western diet.8 Conversely, in Korea, where resolution rates are reported at 63% for cow's milk by 10 months old and 90% for soy by 8 months old, OFCs may be considered at younger ages.9

In contrast, solid food–related FPIES generally follows a more-extended course, with resolution typically occurring at older ages. Oat tolerance may develop between 2 and 3 years of age,8,9 and resolution of fish and peanut FPIES may be more persistent, occurring after 4 years of age for the majority of those affected,2–5,7–10 although peanut allergy resolution is not clearly defined and may resolve in the first 2 to 3 years of life for some.11 Given the significant variability in natural tolerance acquisition across different countries, cultures, and food types, no universal ideal timing for OFCs to test for tolerance has been established. Considerations that may influence timing of the challenge include the nutritional consequence of dietary avoidance, its importance in the family’s cultural dietary preferences, and the effects of avoidance on the quality of life for the family. From a practical standpoint, it is the clinical practice of the authors to strongly encourage families who are otherwise hesitant to introduce the food earlier in life to consider a challenge in all children before starting primary school to minimize unnecessary avoidance or, if reactive, to set clear dietary expectations in school to keep the child safe.

CO-REACTIVE OR CROSS-REACTIVE FOODS

Once FPIES is diagnosed for a specific food, management primarily involves avoiding the trigger food and considering potential co-reactive or cross-reactive foods.12 Current evidence on cross-reactivity is limited and inconsistent due to most being single-center studies with varying study populations; however, there have been instances of co-reactivity between milk and soy as well as among cereal grains. In addition, analysis of data suggests possible co-reactivity among fish, shellfish, and other solid foods in older children and adults.1,2,12 Current guidelines leave the management of potential co-reactivity or cross-reactivity open to interpretation, often suggesting cautious, supervised introduction as a viable approach.2 Foods that may be cross-reactive can be carefully reintroduced by OFC under medical supervision as part of a shared decision-making process that involves the patient and his or her family. Other considerations when performing OFC with a co-reactive or high-risk food in a patient already diagnosed with FPIES include the amount of food offered during the OFC, preemptive placement of a peripheral intravenous (IV) line, and extended post-challenge observation duration.

CONSIDERATIONS IN ADULTS

When conducting OFCs for diagnosing or assessing tolerance in adult-onset and atypical FPIES, several unique considerations must be taken into account. Adult-onset FPIES is believed to be less common than presentation in infancy and is less extensively documented in the medical literature. Most cases in adults are triggered by seafood, and the symptoms can differ slightly from those observed in children, with severe abdominal pain being the predominant symptom and vomiting being less common than its occurrence in children. Given the rarity and distinct presentation of adult-onset FPIES, there is no established standard of care for OFCs in these cases, which highlights the need for a tailored and cautious approach during the diagnostic and management process.13 For further information, please reference the other article in this issue .14

CONSIDERATION IN ATYPICAL FPIES

Atypical FPIES is characterized by evidence of IgE sensitization, as indicated by skin-prick tests or serum IgE testing and occurs in 2–12% of patients. In some cases of patients with cow's milk–related atypical FPIES, symptoms can persist beyond the age of 3 years, unlike in those without IgE sensitization.2,12 In addition, cow’s milk–related atypical FPIES may also increase the risk of developing an IgE-mediated allergy. Therefore, it is advisable to test for IgE sensitization before conducting OFC for cow's milk FPIES concerns. When performing OFC, it is recommended to follow protocols designed for both IgE-mediated food allergies and FPIES, which include a graduated approach to food dosing and an extended post-challenge observation period to ensure safety.2

LOCATION OF FPIES OFCs

Most reports in the literature reflect an academic medical center experience with OFCs conducted either in a hospital-based clinic or in an inpatient setting. A recent survey demonstrated that 60% of respondents perform OFC in the regular office area (including outpatient clinics attached to a hospital), whereas ∼37% reported hospital locations (which included infusion centers in a hospital, inpatient procedure units, and medical day units).15 The severity of the past reaction and the patient’s age influenced the chosen location. IV fluid rehydration is not often needed,16 but it is recommended that a plan for IV access be established before initiating challenge.2

A more common clinical dilemma is to decide whether a potential co-reacting food or high-risk allergen may be given at home or should be given under supervision in the clinical setting. Although there are no established criteria that differentiate which patients “should” or “should not” have the food provided under observation, it would be reasonable to consider factors such as the severity of past reactions, parental comfort with dietary expansion, and the patient’s relative access to emergency care when deciding whether to allow dietary expansion at home. During the coronavirus disease 2019 pandemic, expert guidance suggested cautious introduction of lower-risk foods over a 5-to-10–day period at home, starting with a very small amount and doubling the amount twice daily until the full serving is reached.17 Experience in centers outside of the United States suggests that gradual home introduction of high-risk foods in some patients may be safe; however, larger studies are needed to validate this approach.16

APPROACHES TO FPIES OFCs

There is significant heterogeneity in FPIES OFC approaches that have yet to be validated by large studies.8 A practical approach based on the authors’ experiences and best available evidence is outlined below. Because there are differing approaches globally and there is no consensus on a standardized FPIES OFC protocol, it is important for physicians to continue to exercise astute clinical judgment with regard to the practical considerations provided in this publication.

PREPARING FOR FPIES OFC: PATIENT INSTRUCTIONS BEFORE CHALLENGE

It is recommended to fast for at least 4 hours before FPIES OFC or to ingest a light meal 2 hours before (e.g., low fat and half of the typical serving size).19 Similar to IgE-mediated OFCs, it is important to ensure that patients do not have any concomitant illness and that allergic diseases are optimally controlled at the time of OFC. It is not absolutely necessary, however, for patients to discontinue antihistamines if there is no concern for IgE-mediated symptoms.20

PERFORMING FPIES OFC: IV ACCESS

There is no universal agreement with regard to obtaining IV access before FPIES OFC, although international guidelines currently state that some experts recommend securing peripheral IV access before OFC.2,8 However, a recent 2023 single-center retrospective study suggests that FPIES OFCs infrequently require IV rehydration (3.2% of total OFCs performed, 6 of 185 challenges). No patients experienced hypotension, and IV access was secured after the reaction in three of six reactions.16 All the patients were successfully managed in the clinic and did not require emergency department transfer. This suggests that an IV does not necessarily need to be placed before FPIES OFC for all patients, but, ultimately, this decision is up to the clinical judgment of the supervising medical professional and the patient’s family.

It is reasonable to consider a risk-stratified approach. This was demonstrated in a survey of American Academy of Allergy, Asthma and Immunology members with regard to placement of the peripheral IV access before starting FPIES OFCs, in which only 27% reported always securing a peripheral IV line and 41.3% decide based on the severity of the past reactions.13 In the previously mentioned study, 80% in whom IV rehydration was administered had a previous emergency department visit for FPIES reaction (four of five patients).16 Thus, the supervising medical provider may consider securing peripheral IV access before OFC for patients with previous severe reactions to the challenge food, including those who required IV fluid therapy or an emergency department visit.

DOSING

FPIES OFC dosing should aim to minimize reaction severity while assessing for tolerance. There remains a lack of a universally accepted standardized protocol for FPIES OFCs. Current international consensus FPIES guidelines recommend a total dose of 0.06 to 0.6 g of food protein per kilogram body weight (g protein/kg body weight), a maximum 3 g of protein or 30% of the age-appropriate serving size, administered in a single portion or gradually in three equal portions over 30 minutes.2 Additional OFC doses to confirm tolerance are likely unnecessary.21,22 Lower doses and a longer observation time between doses can be considered in cases of patients with a history of severe reaction, reaction soon after ingestion, or concern for atypical FPIES. Wang, et al.23 reported their experience performing FPIES OFCs giving one-third of a serving size with a 4-hour observation period, followed by home titration every 3 days over 9 to 12 days to the full dose. Of 169 OFCs performed, 30 (18%) were positive, of which 13 (7.7%) passed the OFC in clinic but subsequently reacted at home. Symptoms at home were generally mild consisting of diarrhea or vomiting later in the day after the clinic OFC or within the first few days of home dosing. Only one patient with vomiting presented to the emergency department but did not require additional interventions. This, overall, suggested that this protocol seemed to be safe and minimized challenge time.23

OUTCOME, TREATMENT OF REACTIONS, AND OBSERVATION TIME IN FPIES OFCs

Many mild and moderate FPIES reactions may be successfully managed with oral rehydration and oral or intramuscular ondansetron. Mild reactions are characterized by one to two episodes of vomiting with normal activity levels. Moderate reactions may have more than three episodes of vomiting with abdominal pain or cramping that interferes with normal behavior, in which case, parenteral ondansetron, oral antiemetic, and oral rehydration are suggested (IV rehydration can be considered). Hypotension indicates a severe reaction, regardless of other symptoms, and IV rehydration is suggested with or without overnight observation.1 Rates of reactions during OFC that requires treatment with IV fluids are variable across studies, ranging from 3.2% to 8.3%,16,23–25 the frequency of which may be overestimated by reports from the large, academic referral centers with patients of a more severe phenotype that usually secure IV access before OFC.8 As stated, because much of our literature comes from these tertiary-care centers with patients at potentially higher risk, it has been suggested that this may not accurately reflect the majority of FPIES patients, many who have experienced mild reactions not requiring IV rehydration or an emergency room visit for evaluation. Patients who have experienced mild reactions and who are likely to have developed tolerance may be able to safely introduce the concerning food at home with gradual daily dose advancement. Following an objective reaction consistent with FPIES during an OFC, the decision to rechallenge should involve a shared decision-making process. This approach takes into account the nutritional and cultural importance of the food, while recognizing that delayed introduction or prolonged avoidance may increase the risk of IgE sensitization.

OBSERVATION TIME, ANTICIPATORY GUIDANCE, AND COUNSELING AFTER FPIES OFCs

Guidance after FPIES OFC is like guidance provided for patients completing OFC for IgE-mediated reactions. It has been recommended to observe a patient for 4 hours after ingestion of the allergenic food.2 Because FPIES reaction onset is unpredictable and reactions after 4 hours are possible, although rare, it is advisable to educate families on the possibility of a delayed reaction after leaving the clinic. Education should include signs and symptoms of an FPIES reaction and recommended emergency treatment.

Although rare, it is possible for patients to react with subsequent ingestion at home after passing the challenge in the office.8 Centers that administered 25% to 33% of the challenged food under observation and allowing gradual escalation to a full serving at home over 9 to 12 days reported few reactions in those who did not react on day 1 in the clinic, and, in those patients who did react, emergency department evaluation was very rare, occurring in only one patient, whose reaction was reported as mild and did not require IV fluid rehydration.23

Patients reacting during challenge should be observed until they have had complete resolution of symptoms and are tolerating oral fluids. It is typical to advise minimal activity for the rest of the day. It is normal for patients who have reacted and their families to experience worry, disappointment, and increased anxiety after a reaction in the clinic. Active management strategies may include validation of their concerns, reinforcing the safety plan, and telephone follow-up within 1 week of challenge.19 If patients or their families experience significant prolonged distress, it is recommended to consider referral to a mental health professional.
