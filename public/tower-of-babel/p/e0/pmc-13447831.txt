FTO-mediated NR1D1 m6A demethylation contributes to hyperandrogenemia during continuous light–induced circadian disruption
Authors: Tingting Zhou, Yong Tan, Jie Chen, Jiayu Shi, Yuhan Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractN6-methyladenosine (m6A) is a key epigenetic modification closely associated with reproductive physiology; however, its involvement in circadian-related ovarian dysfunction remains unclear. Female Sprague Dawley rats were exposed continuously to light for 10 weeks (LL group) to induce circadian desynchrony, with controls maintained under standard cycles (DL group). Transcriptomic analysis was performed to identify markedly dysregulated pathways, which were validated via qRT‒PCR, immunoblotting, and KGN cell models. Continuous light exposure induced PCOS-like phenotypes in female rats, including disrupted estrous cycles, polycystic ovarian morphology, and hyperandrogenemia. At the molecular level, constant light disrupted circadian regulation of steroidogenic enzymes, increasing and phase-shifting steroidogenic acute regulatory protein (StAR) and Cytochrome P450 Family 11 Subfamily A Member 1 (Cyp11a1), and elevating but arrhythmically expressing 3β-hydroxysteroid dehydrogenase type 2 (Hsd3β2). Mechanistically, continuous light upregulated the m6A demethylase fat mass and obesity-associated protein (FTO), which promoted m6A erasure on Nuclear receptor subfamily 1 group D member 1 (NR1D1) mRNA, reducing NR1D1 expression and relieving NR1D1-mediated repression of StAR, thereby promoting androgen biosynthesis and potentially contributing to hyperandrogenemia. Our study demonstrated that FTO-mediated regulation of NR1D1 is associated with alterations in StAR expression and androgen synthesis, providing a mechanistic link between environmental circadian disruption and ovarian steroidogenic imbalance and suggesting a potential molecular target for circadian rhythm-associated ovarian dysfunction.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-55012-y.Keywords: FTO, NR1D1, Light exposure, Circadian disruption, HyperandrogenemiaSubject terms: Biochemistry, Cell biology, Diseases, Endocrinology, Genetics, Molecular biology, Physiology

Introduction

Circadian rhythms are endogenous 24 h cycles driven by molecular clocks that synchronize physiology with environmental light–dark cues. In modern societies, the use of artificial light at night and irregular sleep–wake schedules have become pervasive, fueling an epidemic of circadian disruption. In addition to metabolic and cardiovascular diseases, circadian misalignment profoundly affects female reproduction. Epidemiological studies have linked night shift work to menstrual irregularities, abnormal sex hormone profiles, infertility, pregnancy loss, and preterm birth1–4. Experimental models have confirmed that altered levels of light exposure compromise oocyte quality and luteal function5,6. Although circadian disruption is clearly associated with ovarian dysfunction, the molecular cascade linking environmental light exposure to reproductive endocrine disruption remains to be fully elucidated.

Steroidogenic enzyme expression is tightly regulated by the circadian clock via a transcriptional–translational feedback loop, in which core components such as circadian locomotor output cycles kaput (CLOCK), brain and muscle arnt-like protein-1 (BMAL1), and period circadian proteins (PER) coordinate rhythmic steroid hormone production7–9. Nuclear receptor subfamily 1 group D member 1 (NR1D1), also known as REV-ERBα, is transcriptionally activated by CLOCK:BMAL1 heterodimers while reciprocally repressing BMAL1 expression via competitive binding to ROR response elements (ROREs)10. As a transcriptional repressor of the circadian clock, NR1D1 integrates clock output with ovarian steroidogenesis through StAR and CYP19A1 expression, thereby limiting progesterone and estradiol production11,12. These observations identify NR1D1 as a potential molecular nexus linking circadian misalignment to impaired ovarian endocrine function. However, the mechanisms by which environmental light exposure dynamically regulate the expression of circadian clock genes remain incompletely understood.

Epitranscriptomic studies have shown that N6-methyladenosine (m6A) modification is crucial for controlling mRNA stability and translation in reproductive endocrinology. In granulosa cells, dynamic m6A patterns are linked to pathways hat control proliferation, follicle maturation, and steroidogenesis13. Fat mass and obesity-associated protein (FTO), a key m6A demethylase, has been implicated in the susceptibility to polycystic ovarian syndrome (PCOS)14,15; elevated expression of m6A has been detected in the granulosa cells of patients with PCOS16. Although direct mechanistic studies under these conditions are limited, FTO has been shown in other contexts to preserve mRNA stability by erasing m6A modifications. For example, in granulosa cells, it demethylates the 3′UTR of FOS mRNA, stabilizing the transcript and restraining FOS‑driven granulosa cell senescence17, and enhances MFN2 mRNA stability through a YTHDF2-dependent pathway, ameliorating mitochondrial dysfunction18. m6A modification of the circadian clock gene also results in significant rhythmic fluctuations19. However, there is no experimental evidence to support whether m6A modification mediates the molecular association between continuous light exposure and ovarian dysfunction.

Therefore, we designed this study to elucidate the role of m6A modification in the pathogenesis of circadian-related ovarian dysfunction. We demonstrated that chronic light exposure disrupts FTO-mediated regulation of NR1D1 mRNA stability, sequentially causing dysregulation of androgen-synthesizing enzymes, excessive local androgen production, and ultimately ovarian dysfunction.

Materials and methods

Animals

Sixty female Sprague‒Dawley rats, weighing 193 ± 13 g and aged 8 weeks, were supplied by Beijing Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd. The rats were maintained in an SPF-grade barrier system at the Laboratory Animal Center of Nanjing University of Chinese Medicine (NJUCM). The environmental conditions were maintained at 22–24 °C with 55–70% relative humidity, and unrestricted access to drinking water and a standard rodent diet. After one week of acclimation, the rats were randomly divided into two groups: (1) the normal light group (DL group), which was exposed to a 12-h light/dark cycle (illumination provided from 07:00 to 19:00 h), or (2) the continuous light group (LL group), which was maintained under 24 h of constant illumination. Neutral white LEDs with an intensity of 500 ± 20 lx were used. The photoperiod intervention lasted for 10 weeks (Fig. 1A). This study was approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of NJUCM (Approval ID: 202310A048). All animal experiments were conducted in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations and all methods are reported following the ARRIVE guidelines. Vaginal smears were collected daily at 09:00 and analyzed microscopically to determine estrous cycle stages on the basis of established vaginal cytology criteria20. Persistent estrus was defined as the presence of cornified epithelial cells for at least 10 consecutive days21.

Tissue collection

At the end of the experiment, the rats were deeply anesthetized with 20% urethane at a dose of 0.6 mL/100 g through intraperitoneal injection, after which the tissues were harvested at 6-h intervals over a 24-h period, corresponding to four Zeitgeber time points: ZT0, ZT6, ZT12, and ZT18. Blood samples were collected via abdominal aortic puncture and allowed to stand at room temperature for 15 min. After centrifugation at 3500 rpm for 15 min, peripheral serum was collected and transferred to 2 mL cryovials for storage at − 80 °C. Following euthanasia by cervical dislocation, both ovaries were excised, trimmed of any adherent connective tissue, and weighed. The ovarian samples were either fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde solution or stored at -80 °C for subsequent analyses.

Serum assays

Serum levels of estradiol (E2), luteinizing hormone (LH), follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH), testosterone (T), dehydroepiandrosterone (DHEA), DHEA sulfate (DHEAS), androstenedione (ASD) and anti- Müllerian hormone (AMH) in rats were quantified using ELISA kits (Cusabio, Wuhan, China) in accordance with the manufacturer’s guidelines. The detection limits of the ELISAs were provided by the manufacturers (e.g., E2: 40 pg/mL; LH: 0.15 mIU/mL; FSH: 0.07 mIU/mL; T: 0.06 ng/mL; DHEA: 0.025 ng/mL; DHEAS: 7.81 pg/mL; ASD: 0.1 nmol/L; and AMH: 0.2 ng/mL).

Cell culture and gene transfection

KGN cells (a human granulosa tumor cell line with steroidogenic properties), purchased from Shanghai Biowing Biotechnology Application Co., Ltd, were cultured in DMEM/F12 medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% penicillin‒streptomycin under controlled atmospheric conditions (37 °C, 5% CO₂). KGN cells were transfected with plasmids or siRNAs using Lipofectamine 3000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Gene transfection efficiency was confirmed by qRT‒PCR and immunoblotting. The target gene sequences are provided in Table 1.

Manual follicle counting

Ovaries were fixed, paraffin-embedded, serially sectioned, and every third section was stained with H&E. Follicles were classified as preantral (primordial and primary), antral (secondary and preovulatory), cystic follicles, and corpora lutea according to established morphological criteria. To avoid double counting, only follicles with a visible oocyte nucleus were included. Follicle and corpora lutea numbers were obtained by summing counts across all analyzed sections per ovary.

RNA extraction and real-time PCR

Total RNA from ovarian tissues and KGN cells was isolated using a commercial extraction reagent and reverse-transcribed into cDNA with 5 × SweScript All-in-One SuperMix (Servicebio, Wuhan, China). qPCR was performed using 2 × Universal Blue SYBR Green Master Mix (Servicebio) with gene-specific primers (Table 2). GAPDH was used for normalization. Amplification specificity was verified by melting curve analysis, and relative expression levels were calculated using the 2^−ΔΔCt method.

Western blot analysis

Ovarian tissues and KGN cells were lysed in RIPA buffer (Servicebio), and protein concentrations were determined using a BCA assay after centrifugation. Equal amounts of protein were separated by 10% SDS–PAGE and transferred onto PVDF membranes. Membranes were blocked with 5% skim milk in TBST for 1 h at room temperature and incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against NR1D1 (1:1000), FTO (1:1000), and StAR (1:2000) (Proteintech, Wuhan, China). After washing, membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (1:20,000; Servicebio) for 30 min. Protein signals were detected using ECL, quantified with ImageJ, and normalized to GAPDH.

Immunofluorescence staining

KGN cells were seeded on polylysine–coated confocal dishes and cultured to ~ 70% confluency, then fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min at room temperature. After permeabilization with 0.1% Triton X-100 and blocking with 5% BSA, cells were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against NR1D1 and FTO (1:200; Proteintech), followed by CY3/FITC-conjugated secondary antibodies (1:200; Proteintech) for 1 h at room temperature in the dark. Nuclei were counterstained with Hoechst 33,342. Images were acquired by fluorescence microscopy and quantified using ImageJ.

RNA stability assay

Following treatment with 5 μg/mL actinomycin D to inhibit RNA synthesis, the cells were harvested at the indicated time points (0, 2, 4, 6, and 8 h) for qRT‒PCR analysis as previously described.

Co-IP analysis

KGN cells were lysed in ice-cold RIPA buffer, and cleared lysates were obtained by centrifugation at 4 °C. An aliquot of the supernatant was reserved as input, and the remaining lysate was incubated overnight at 4 ℃ with anti-NR1D1 antibody, followed by protein A/G magnetic beads for 2 h. After washing, bound proteins were eluted in Laemmli buffer at 95 ℃ for 5 min. StAR and NR1D1 were detected by immunoblotting. IgG served as a negative control, and band intensities were quantified using ImageJ.

RIP analysis

KGN cells were lysed on ice using RIP lysis buffer, and clarified lysates were obtained by centrifugation. Anti-FTO antibody was coupled to protein A/G magnetic beads, which were then incubated with cell lysates containing RNase inhibitor at 4 ℃ overnight. After stringent washing, RNA–protein complexes were eluted by heating, and NR1D1 mRNA enrichment was quantified by qRT–PCR. Isotype-matched IgG was processed in parallel as a negative control.

MeRIP

KGN cells were cultured under standard conditions and infected with Lv-puro-FTO lentivirus (MOI = 50) for 48 h. Total RNA was extracted with TRIzol, followed by rRNA depletion and fragmentation. Protein G magnetic beads coupled to anti-m6A antibody or IgG (Merck Millipore, Burlington, MA, USA) were incubated with RNA samples at 4 ℃ overnight. After stringent washing, immunoprecipitated RNA was eluted, purified with TRIzol, and quantified by qRT–PCR.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism 10.1.2. Student’s or Welch’s t tests and the Mann–Whitney U test were used as appropriate. For multiple-endpoint analyses, p values were adjusted using the Benjamini–Hochberg FDR procedure (FDR-adjusted p < 0.05). For prespecified single comparisons, p < 0.05 was considered significant. No animals or samples were excluded. Blinding was not performed; standardized protocols and predefined criteria were used to minimize potential bias.

Results

Continuous light exposure induces polycystic ovary syndrome-like reproductive characteristics in SD rats

The control and experimental groups of rats were designated DL and LL, respectively. Chronic light exposure induced estrous cycle irregularities in the rats beginning between weeks 4 and 6, with a predominant pattern of persistent estrus (Fig. 1B). The serum levels of LH, T, ASD, and DHEA were markedly elevated in the LL group compared with those in the control group, whereas no intergroup differences in AMH, E2, FSH, or DHEAS levels were detected (Fig. 1C). In addition, ovarian histomorphometric analysis revealed typical polycystic ovarian changes in the LL group, characterized by a significant increase in the number of cystically dilated follicles, which exhibited thinning and disorganization of the surrounding granulosa cell layers, along with poorly defined oocyte–corona radiata structures indicative of abnormal or arrested follicular development. Compared with the DL group, the LL group presented a significantly reduced number of corpora lutea. However, no difference variation in preantral or antral follicle numbers was observed between the two groups (Fig. 1D,E).

Given the pronounced alterations in androgen levels observed in LL rats, we next performed time-resolved LC–MS/MS profiling of multiple circulating steroids along the androgen biosynthesis pathway (Fig. 2A). Continuous light exposure increases ovarian FTO expression

Fto was rhythmically expressed in both groups. The mean expression of Fto was significantly elevated in the LL group compared with that in the DL group, although the amplitude and phase of its rhythm were similar across the groups (Fig. 3A). Western blot analysis confirmed the elevated expression pattern, revealing sustained upregulation of FTO protein expression in LL rats across all four Zeitgeber time points (Fig. 3B).

Continuous light exposure affects the androgen biosynthesis process in rats

To explore potential downstream targets associated with FTO activity, We referred to transcriptomic (mRNA-seq) data previously generated in our laboratory using ovarian tissues from control and experimental rats. This analysis revealed a significant overrepresentation of biosynthetic processes and steroid synthesis based on GO term enrichment. KEGG pathway analysis revealed clustered enrichment in steroid biosynthesis and circadian rhythm pathways (Fig. 4A). Given the prominent enrichment of steroid biosynthesis pathways and the pronounced elevation of circulating androgens, we first focused on CYP17A1, a key rate-limiting enzyme in ovarian androgen biosynthesis. Surprisingly, neither Cyp17a1 mRNA abundance nor CYP17A1 protein levels, as assessed by immunohistochemistry, showed significant differences between the DL and LL groups (Fig. 4B,C). These findings prompted us to extend our analysis to additional steroidogenic components along the androgen biosynthesis pathway.

Cosinor analysis revealed that StAR, Cyp11a1 were rhythmically expressed in ovarian tissues. Compared with the DL group, the LL group exhibited elevated mean expression levels of StAR and Cyp11a1. StAR expression peaked at ZT ~ 7.98 in the DL group and at ZT ~ 6.19 in the LL group, corresponding to a phase advance of ~ 1.79 h. In contrast, Cyp11a1 expression peaked at ZT ~ 9.20 under DL conditions and at ZT ~ 11.07 under LL conditions, indicating a phase delay of ~ 1.87 h. No significant differences in amplitude were detected for two genes (Fig. 4D). Nonlinear cosine regression analysis indicated that Hsd3β2 expression was nonrhythmic in both DL and LL groups. Two-factor mixed-effects analysis (light × time) showed significant main effects of time and light, with a significant interaction. Post hoc comparisons revealed higher Hsd3β2 expression in LL rats at ZT0 and ZT18, but not at ZT6 or ZT12 (Fig. 4E).

NR1D1 contributes to StAR regulation under continuous light exposure

The rhythmic expression of StAR, Cyp11a1 and Cyp17a1 suggests that they may be directly regulated by circadian clock genes, thereby influencing androgen synthesis. Therefore, we assessed the expression of ovarian circadian clock genes in both groups. Nr1d1 exhibited rhythmic expression in both groups. Cosinor analysis revealed alterations in LL circadian parameters, including a marked reduction in the mesor and an increased oscillatory amplitude, while phase synchrony was maintained (Fig. 5A). A strong inverse correlation was observed between Nr1d1 and StAR, as indicated by a Pearson’s r value of -0.77 (Fig. 5B). Western blot analysis revealed that StAR protein levels were increased by ~ twofold in the LL group compared with those in the DL control group. In contrast, NR1D1 protein levels were significantly reduced at all examined Zeitgeber times, as determined by densitometric quantification (P < 0.05) (Fig. 5C).

To elucidate mechanism underlying the interaction between NR1D1 and StAR in ovarian steroidogenesis, we first performed a co-IP experiment using KGN cells. When endogenous NR1D1 was precipitated by an antibody, StAR was coprecipitated, while no obvious StAR band was detected in the IgG control group, indicating that NR1D1 and StAR indeed interact (Fig. 5D). Furthermore, KGN cells were transfected with plasmids to overexpress NR1D1, NR1D1 expression was knocked down via siRNA, and negative control groups were prepared. NR1D1 knockdown upregulated StAR expression, whereas NR1D1 overexpression (OE) suppressed StAR expression (Fig. 5E, F).

FTO participates in the regulation of NR1D1 under continuous light exposure

We observed an inverse correlation between Fto and Nr1d1 expression in the ovaries (Fig. 6A). To elucidate the regulatory relationship between FTO and NR1D1, KGN cells were used. RNA immunoprecipitation (RIP) with an FTO-specific antibody revealed specific binding between the endogenous FTO protein and NR1D1 mRNA. This was evidenced by the significant enrichment of NR1D1 transcripts in the immunoprecipitated complexes compared with those in the IgG controls (Fig. 6B). FTO overexpression significantly suppressed NR1D1 expression, whereas FTO knockdown conversely increased NR1D1 expression compared with those in the controls (Fig. 6C, D). Immunofluorescence staining further confirmed the colocalization of FTO and NR1D1 in KGN cells (Fig. 6E).

FTO-mediated m6A demethylation reduces the stability of NR1D1 mRNA

To clarify the mechanism underlying FTO-mediated m6A modification of NR1D1 mRNA, a putative m6A modification site at position 7443 within the coding sequence (CDS) was predicted using the SRAMP database (http://www.cuilab.cn/sramp) (Fig. 7A). Overexpression of FTO in KGN cells via lentiviral transduction followed by MeRIP–qPCR revealed that FTO overexpression significantly reduced m6A methylation of NR1D1 transcripts (Fig. 7B). Subsequently, actinomycin D was used to treat the control and FTO-overexpressing (FTO-OE) groups at various time points to inhibit transcription. RNA was extracted, and NR1D1 mRNA expression was quantified by qRT‒PCR. Compared with control cells, FTO-OE cells exhibited accelerated decay of NR1D1 mRNA, indicating that FTO overexpression destabilizes NR1D1 transcripts (Fig. 7C).

Discussion

While driven by endogenous rhythms, the circadian clock synchronizes with external cues such as light, temperature, and feeding schedules25. Modern artificial lighting disrupts this alignment by desynchronizing the suprachiasmatic nucleus (SCN), the central pacemaker. This perturbation propagates to peripheral oscillators, including the ovaries, potentially exacerbating reproductive pathologies. In a cross-sectional study of 9335 nurses, 21% of women had menstrual disorders26. Night shift work is significantly positively associated with PCOS, and the same association has been reported for rotating shift work27. Nighttime outdoor exposure to artificial light is related to elevated testosterone and fasting insulin levels and the prevalence of PCOS, and the estimated impact of cumulative exposure to artificial light is greater than that of transient exposure28.

In the present study, 10 weeks of continuous light exposure induced estrous cycle irregularities, polycystic ovarian morphology, and hyperandrogenism in rats, recapitulating several key features of PCOS. In addition to having elevated testosterone levels, the serum levels of DHEA and ASD were increased in rats in the LL group, indicating excessive androgen levels. Consistent with these findings, previous rodent studies have shown that disruption of normal light–dark cycles induces ovarian dysfunction through circadian rhythm desynchronization, implicating photoperiod disturbance in PCOS-related pathophysiology29,30. Clinical observations further support this association, as elevated circulating DHEA and ASD levels have been reported in women who are engaged in night or rotating shift work31. Despite pronounced hyperandrogenism, AMH levels and preantral/antral follicle numbers did not significantly increase in the LL group. Similarly, human studies have shown no significant differences in AMH levels or antral follicle count between night/rotating shift workers and day workers; however, shift work was associated with reduced ovarian responsiveness, including retrieval of fewer oocytes32–34. Moreover, constant light exposure has been shown to induce meiotic defects and compromise oocyte quality in mice, supporting a role for circadian disruption in impairing oocyte competence and follicular quality35. In contrast to PCOS models induced by pharmacological or dietary interventions, such as DHEA or letrozole combined with a high-fat diet, the present study employed continuous light exposure alone to isolate the pathogenic effects of light pollution, a prevalent environmental factor in modern society. This approach minimizes confounding hormonal or metabolic influences and may better reflect environmentally driven, gradual reproductive dysfunction. Beyond ovarian steroid hormones, aberrant light exposure disrupts systemic circadian endocrine cues. In rodent models, continuous light exposure suppresses melatonin secretion, with nocturnal melatonin peaks being abolished or markedly attenuated36. Additionally, in human studies, serum cortisol levels have been reported to be higher in pregnant women exposed to light at night than in those maintained under normal light–dark conditions37. In the setting of disrupted circadian endocrine signaling, we further examined the rhythmicity and overall levels of several key steroid hormones.

Testosterone and progesterone retained circadian rhythmicity in our model and exhibited increased mesor levels as determined by cosinor analysis. The increase in testosterone is consistent with previous reports under continuous light, whereas progesterone responses vary across studies. Higher progesterone levels have been reported in women engaged in rotating night shift work38, while studies in rodents have described unchanged or reduced progesterone levels6,39. In rodent models, these discrepancies are likely related to differences in estrous cycle stage at sampling, as well as the duration and intensity of light exposure. Both serum and salivary profiling studies show pronounced diurnal oscillations of 17-OHP and ASD in humans40,41, matching the rhythmicity observed in our model. Under continuous light, these hormones exhibited increased mesor levels. In women, rapidly rotating shift work has been linked to higher serum androstenedione levels31, whereas direct evidence for changes in 17-OHP following circadian disruption remains limited.

Given these pronounced alterations in ovarian function under continuous light exposure, we next examined potential molecular mediators. We found that expression of FTO was markedly upregulated in the ovaries of LL rats. Clinically, elevated FTO levels are also observed in both the peripheral blood and ovarian tissues of patients with PCOS, where they are significantly correlated with hyperandrogenism and metabolic disturbances42–44. Mechanistic studies further demonstrated that FTO overexpression disrupts granulosa cell function, driving insulin resistance and apoptosis while inhibiting proliferation through the stabilization of flotillin‑2 (FLOT2) mRNA45. Moreover, FTO inhibition reduces androgen biosynthesis by suppressing steroidogenic enzymes and attenuates androgenic activity through the downregulation of androgen receptor (AR) expression46. Combining these clinical and mechanistic observations, we hypothesize that continuous light‑induced FTO upregulation promotes hyperandrogenism via m6A‑dependent modulation of steroidogenesis, ultimately contributing to ovarian dysfunction.

In women, androgens are synthesized in the adrenal cortices, ovaries, and peripheral steroidogenic compartments through sequential enzymatic catalysis of cholesterol by steroidogenic machinery, including StAR, cytochrome P450 (CYP), and hydroxysteroid dehydrogenase (HSD). In this study, we observed that ovarian Star and Cyp11a1 expression exhibit circadian rhythmicity, which aligns with prior findings on the circadian regulation of steroidogenic enzymes47,48. Following light pollution exposure, the expression of key upstream steroidogenesis genes, including StAR and Cyp11a1, significantly increased, accompanied by alterations in the rhythmic phase. Hsd3β2 expression was significantly increased at specific Zeitgeber times, despite the absence of a significant circadian rhythm49. No significant differences in CYP17A1 expression levels were observed between the DL and LL groups across the circadian cycle at either the mRNA or protein level. Although CYP17A1 is widely considered a key enzyme in androgen biosynthesis, steroidogenic alterations may vary across models, and hyperandrogenism can occur without significant changes in CYP17A1 expression50,51. StAR and CYP11A1 have been identified as key drivers of androgen excess, and targeting this mitochondrial initiation step reduces androgen production52,53. Consistently, our LC–MS/MS analysis revealed increased progesterone and 17-OHP, supporting enhanced upstream substrate availability that may drive androgen excess independently of CYP17A1 expression. Moreover, although CYP17A1 expression was unchanged, its enzymatic activity may be regulated independently of expression, potentially further contributing to androgen synthesis. These findings suggest that light-induced circadian disruption may affect ovarian steroidogenesis, although the underlying mechanisms remain to be elucidated.

Our study revealed NR1D1 as a pivotal hub linking circadian inputs to androgen output. Continuous light exposure not only decreases CLOCK expression54 but also dramatically decreases NR1D1 expression and disrupts its amplitude, exacerbating circadian rhythm disorders. Although no studies to date have directly examined the effect of light exposure on ovarian NR1D1 expression, evidence from other tissues supports its circadian sensitivity. In a continuous-darkness model of circadian rhythm disruption, hepatic NR1D1 expression was significantly reduced in rats with PCOS, leading to lipid accumulation through the upregulation of sterol regulatory element-binding protein 1 (SREBP1) expression55. Consistent with these observations, NR1D1 expression is also lower in granulosa cells from PCOS patients than in those from healthy controls. Pharmacological activation of NR1D1 has been reported to ameliorate follicular dysplasia in animal models of PCOS by restoring mitochondrial function and inhibiting autophagy, highlighting its potential as a therapeutic target56.

In addition to its role in lipid metabolic dysregulation and ovarian follicle maturation, NR1D1 transcriptionally regulates rate-limiting steps in steroidogenesis, positioning it as a critical node linking circadian disruption to ovarian endocrine dysfunction. In vivo, an inverse correlation between NR1D1 and StAR expression was observed under continuous light conditions, indicating an association between these two factors in the context of circadian disruption. In vitro mechanistic evidence from KGN granulosa cells further supports a regulatory role of NR1D1 in StAR expression, as NR1D1 knockdown led to increased StAR levels. A similar mechanism has been reported in male interstitial cells57. Together with altered expression levels and/or circadian rhythmicity of Cyp11a1and Hsd3b2, these changes may collectively increase the catalytic capacity of the steroidogenic cascade, thereby potentially promoting androgen synthesis and contributing to hyperandrogenemia.

NR1D1 participates in coordinating circadian rhythms to regulate steroid hormone synthesis; however, its dynamic regulation under continuous light exposure remains unclear. Most previous studies have addressed NR1D1 at the transcriptional level, whereas posttranscriptional mechanisms remain underexplored. Numerous studies have shown that m6A-dependent posttranscriptional regulation directly shapes circadian clock dynamics58–60, and NR1D1 mRNA has been identified as an m6A-modified transcript , and its stability can be altered under circadian disruption61. In vivo, we observed an inverse correlation between Fto and Nr1d1 expression in the ovaries. In vitro, studies in KGN cells demonstrated that FTO directly binds to NR1D1 mRNA and modulates its m6A status and stability. Taken together, the inverse association observed in vivo, combined with cell-based mechanistic evidence, is consistent with a role for FTO-mediated post-transcriptional regulation of NR1D1 in ovarian dysfunction in the context of light-induced circadian disruption. Endocrine phenotypes induced by circadian disruption may be partially reversible. In circadian misalignment models with PCOS-like features, restoration of a normal light–dark cycle has been reported to alleviate hyperandrogenism50. Whether the ovarian FTO–NR1D1 axis is similarly reversible upon restoration of photoperiodic cues warrants further investigation.

Under conditions of light exposure, melatonin is often considered a key endocrine factor, given its suppression under continuous light and its role in regulating circadian rhythms and ovarian function. Melatonin has been implicated in m6A-related regulation62. Considering the sensitivity of FTO to metabolic and redox states63, melatonin may influence its activity by modulating mitochondrial function and redox homeostasis64. Thus, continuous light exposure may affect the FTO–NR1D1 axis through alterations in melatonin signaling, which warrants further experimental validation.

Among the limitations of the present study are reliance on a single animal model, which may limit cross-species extrapolation. In addition, the absence of in vivo rescue experiments precludes definitive causal inference. Furthermore, we were unable to disentangle suprachiasmatic nucleus versus ovarian contributions to FTO–NR1D1 dysregulation under circadian disruption. Finally, we did not systematically interrogate m6A reader proteins (e.g., the IGF2BP family) that may mediate NR1D1 destabilization upon FTO-dependent demethylation, which represents an important direction for future mechanistic studies.

Conclusions

In summary, our study identified a possible epigenetic mechanism underlying hyperandrogenism induced by continuous light exposure-mediated circadian disruption. In this context, prolonged light exposure increases the expression of FTO, which appears to facilitate m6A demethylation of NR1D1 mRNA and reduce its stability and protein abundance. Diminished NR1D1 levels are associated with increased StAR transcription, thereby influencing androgen synthesis and correlating with hyperandrogenemia.
