Exploration of real-time fluorescence quantitative PCR by analyzing effective reference genes in Galleria mellonella (Lepidoptera: Pyralidae)
Authors: Xu Yu Ran, Yan Hong Li, Xiao Lan Cheng, Kai Lang Zhang, Yang Yang Liu, Igor Sharakhov
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Abstract Galleria mellonella Linnaeus (Lepidoptera: Pyralidae) is a major pest of the Chinese honey bees. Real-time quantitative Polymerase Chain Reaction (RT-qPCR) is a stable method for quantifying gene expression, and the selection of appropriate reference genes is necessary for standardizing gene expression analysis. In this study, the expression profiles of 12 candidate reference genes (GAPDH2, TUBB2B, TBP, RPL7, RPL13, RPL22, SOD, AK1, RPS7, RPS20, RPS25, and PKG21D) were analyzed via 5 different treatments. The stability of candidate reference gene expression was assessed using geNorm, NormFinder, BestKeeper, ΔCt, and RefFinder. Significant differences in optimal reference gene combinations were observed under diverse experimental conditions. Based on different developmental stages, larval tissues, UV irradiation of males, low- and high-temperature treatments of females, high-temperature treatments of males, and insecticide applications, the most suitable reference gene combinations were RPL13 + RPS20, RPL7 + RPL13, RPL22 + RPL7, RPS7 + SOD, RPS25 + RPL22, RPL22 + RPS20, and TBP + RPS25. For UV irradiation to females and low-temperature treatment to males, the best reference gene combination was consistently identified as RPL22 + RPL13. These results laid the foundation for the standardization of RT-qPCR analysis and provided crucial support for developing targeted control strategies for the greater wax moth, such as RNA interference and gene editing, for future studies.Keywords: Greater wax moth, Galleria mellonella, reference gene, gene expression, RT-qPCR

Introduction

Galleria mellonella Linnaeus (Lepidoptera: Pyralidae), commonly known as the greater wax moth, poses a major threat to bee colonies worldwide and causes considerable economic losses to beekeepers (Cao et al. 2024). Honey bees are essential pollinators that are crucial for crop pollination (Patel et al. 2021). Bees significantly enhance plant growth and reproduction through their efficient pollination activities, maintaining plant diversity and indirectly supporting the diversity of animals that depend on plants, as well as the stability and functioning of entire ecosystems (Han et al. 2023b, Paramanik et al. 2024). G. mellonella progresses through 4 distinct life stages (ie egg, larva, pupa, and adult) and poses a significant threat to the Apis cerana (Kwadha et al. 2017, Wojda et al. 2020). Once hatched within the brood area, the greater wax moth larvae begin foraging, further destroying honeybee brood and disrupting normal pupal development. This infestation damages the brood and results in the formation of “white-headed pupae” within the colony. Newly emerged bees may get trapped in their nesting chambers made of silk secreted by G. mellonella, restricting their movement (Su et al. 2021b). Current pest management relies primarily on 3 strategies: biological, chemical, and physical control. Nevertheless, chemical control is the most effective method. Chlorantraniliprole is a commonly used chemical insecticide to control G. mellonella (Li et al. 2024b). Although some chemical insecticides are relatively safe, mounting evidence indicates that they may actually have negative effects on bees (Chmiel et al. 2020, Cappa and Baracchi 2024). For example, sublethal pesticides harm European honey bee (A. mellifera) by disrupting digestion, suppressing immunity, and interfering with navigation, while also indirectly affecting colony reproduction (Chmiel et al. 2020).

Previous studies on G. mellonella have focused on transcriptomics, genomics, and proteomics, with an emphasis on internal reference gene selection, which is often represented by generic actin genes (Li et al. 2024). Current research on G. mellonella’s environmental adaptation has significant gaps. Gene expression analysis is key for studying adaptation, as its levels are influenced by both biotic (eg developmental stage, tissues) and abiotic (eg temperature, ultraviolet radiation, insecticides) factors (Zhou et al. 2021, Yang et al. 2022 Xue et al. 2024). Thus, examining gene expression patterns under varied conditions is crucial for understanding insect physiological and biochemical mechanisms. Most studies have used temperature, pesticides, and various treatments to screen insects for reference genes. In contrast, UV light is infrequently utilized in the screening process for reference genes. Crucially, the ideal reference genes should exhibit stable expression under diverse experimental conditions. Significant differences in the stability of gene expression exist among different insect species (Shi et al. 2015, Shakeel et al. 2018). Commonly used reference genes in insects include glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH), tubulin (TUB), elongation factor (EF), ribosomal protein large (RPL), ribosomal protein small (RPS), and adenylate kinase (AK) (Lü et al. 2018).

RT-qPCR is a robust technique that enables the quantification of a PCR product generated at each amplification step based on fluorescent dye intercalation. Shen et al. (2024) reported that this method has high sensitivity, high reproducibility, rapid processing, a high degree of automation, and reduced risk of contamination. Selecting a stable reference gene is important in RT-qPCR experiments to minimize sample quality variability and ensure the reliability of the results, as indicated by Xie et al. (2021) and Guo et al. (2021). Numerous studies have been conducted to identify reference genes in Lepidopterans; however, there is currently no universal reference gene that exhibits consistent expression under various experimental conditions. For example, 18S ribosomal RNA (18S rRNA), 28S ribosomal RNA (28S rRNA), and α-tubulin (α-TUB) of Athetis dissimilis are suitable for various experimental conditions (Tang et al. 2022). In Mythimna loreyi, ribosomal protein 27 (RPL27) and ribosomal protein 10 (RPL10) are appropriate reference genes for assessing developmental stage, tissue type, and adult age. EF and ribosomal protein S3 (RPS3) are acceptable for mating status, AK and RPL10 for temperature treatment, RPL27 for larval diet, and EF and RPL27 for adult diet treatment (Wang et al. 2024a). In Spodoptera frugiperda, superoxide dismutase (SOD), RPL10, and ribosomal protein S24 (RPS24) were considered the most suitable reference genes for developmental stages, whereas α-TUB and RPL10 were stable across various tissues (Han et al. 2021). For Diaphania caesalis, actin (ACT), ribosomal protein 13a (RPL13a), β-tubulin (β-TUB), ribosomal protein S3a (RPS3a), and elongation factor 1α (EF1α) were the most suitable reference genes for various developmental stages, with ACT being the most appropriate for different tissues and ACT, EF1α, and RPS3a for different sexes (Wang et al. 2020). In Hyphantria cunea, ribosomal protein S26 (RPS26) and ribosomal protein 13 (RPL13) are recommended for various developmental stages, and RPS26 and ribosomal protein S15 (RPS15) are recommended for larval tissues (Zhao et al. 2022a). EF1, ribosomal protein 32 (RPL32), and ribosomal protein S23 (RPS23) were stable reference genes for Plutella xylostella under specific conditions (Fu et al. 2013). In Bombyx mori, ribosomal protein S7 (RPS7) can serve as an effective marker for the different developmental stages of the silkworm’s central nervous system (Dalai and Jagota 2024). In Helicoverpa armigera, ribosomal protein 28 (RPL28) and RPS15 were the most stable reference genes across starvation larvae, temperature-stressed larvae, and at various developmental stages (Shakeel et al. 2015).

Despite extensive studies on reference genes in other insects, there is a significant lack of comprehensive reports on reference genes in G. mellonella. Analyzing the transcriptional stability of the reference genes is necessary to identify suitable genes for elucidating gene expression mechanisms in G. mellonella. Consequently, relying on available transcriptomic data for G. mellonella, we analyzed 12 candidate reference genes, including glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase 2 (GAPDH2), tubulin beta chain 2B (TUBB2B), tata-box-binding protein (TBP), ribosomal protein 7 (RPL7), ribosomal protein 13 (RPL13), ribosomal protein 22 (RPL22), superoxide dismutase (SOD), adenylate kinase (AK1), ribosomal protein S7 (RPS7), ribosomal protein S20 (RPS20), ribosomal protein S25 (RPS25), and cGMP-dependent protein kinase 21D (PKG21D). Next, we assessed the expression stability of these genes at various developmental stages and in multiple tissues under specific conditions, such as ultraviolet exposure, temperature fluctuations, and pesticide treatment, using NormFinder, BestKeeper, ΔCt method, and RefFinder software. RT-qPCR was used to assess the expression of these 12 candidate reference genes across various samples of G. mellonella, with the cytochrome P450 gene CYP113520138 used for validation. The results provide a theoretical foundation and reference for future gene expression studies in G. mellonella.

Materials and Methods

Insect Rearing

Galleria mellonella was collected from the Miaojiang Apiary bee breeding base in Huangping County (107°66′E, 26°91′N), Guizhou Province, China, in August 2023. The insects were reared on an artificial diet in a growth chamber (RXZ-380A, Jiangnan Instrument Factory, Ningbo) and fed to a stable experimental population of G. mellonella at the Guizhou Key Laboratory of Biosafety. The growth chamber was maintained at a temperature of 30 ± 1 °C, with a relative humidity of 65% ± 10%, and the photoperiod was set to L0:D24.

Experimental Treatment Preparation

Development Stages and Sex

Collect samples of G. mellonella at different developmental stages, including the egg phase, larval phase from instar 1 to instar 7, pupal phase, and adult phase. Each stage included 3 biological replicates with 1 replicate per trial. One replicate included 50 eggs, 20 first‐instar larvae, 20 second‐instar larvae, 20 third‐instar larvae, 10 fourth‐instar larvae, 10 fifth‐instar larvae, 5 sixth‐instar larvae, 5 seventh‐instar larvae, 3 pupae, and 3 adults (Zhou et al. 2021).

Tissues

Healthy fourth-instar larvae of G. mellonella of the same size (n = 15) were dissected separately, including the head, foregut, midgut, hindgut, Malpighian duct, fat granules, and epidermis (Yan et al. 2021).

UV‐A Irradiation

Healthy and newly feathered adult males and females of G. mellonella were selected and acclimatized to the dark condition for 1 h. The adults of the greater wax moth were irradiated with UV-A (Ultraviolet-A radiation) lamps at an intensity of 300 μW/cm2 for 0 (control group), 60, 90, 120, and 150 min (Zhou et al. 2021). For each treatment, 3 independent and biological replicates were established, and 3 insects were collected from each replicate.

Temperature Treatment

Healthy and newly developed male and female G. mellonella adults were exposed to either low‐temperature (4 °C) or high‐temperature (36 °C) conditions for 0 (control group), 30, 60, 90, 120, and 150 min (Zhou et al. 2021). For each condition, 3 independent and biological replicates were established, and 3 insects were collected from each replicate.

Insecticide Treatments

Chlorantraniliprole (CAP, FMC Corporation, China), an effective insecticide against G. mellonella, was diluted to sublethal concentrations corresponding to LC10, LC30, and LC50 (Wan et al. 2025) values in distilled water. Distilled water was used as the control. The experiment used the larval dip method, in which the fourth-instar larvae of G. mellonella were immersed in a solution containing sublethal concentrations LC10, LC30, and LC50 (Wan et al. 2025) of chlorantraniliprole for 10 s (Xia et al. 2024). After 24 h (Wan et al. 2025), 9 larvae were randomly selected sampled, and the surviving larvae were treated. Three independent and biological replicates were set-up for each treatment condition.

Selection of Reference Genes and Design of RT-qPCR Primers

The transcriptome database of G. mellonella was screened for the Lepidopteran reference genes “GAPDH,” “RPL,” and “TUB” (He et al. 2024). Twelve candidate reference gene sequences were evaluated, including GAPDH2, TUBB2B, TBP, RPL7, RPL13, RPL22, SOD, AK1, RPS7, RPS20, RPS25, and PKG21D. RT-qPCR primers were designed using Primer Premier 5.0 software (Table 1) and synthesized by Sangon Biotech Co., Ltd (Shanghai, China).

Total RNA Extraction and cDNA Synthesis

RNA extraction and cDNA synthesis were performed as described by Su et al. (2021), with minor modifications. Total RNA was extracted from the samples using TRNzol reagent (Tiangen Biotech, Beijing) by grinding. Total RNA (1 µg) was used to synthesize first-strand cDNA using HiScript II Q RT SuperMix Kit (Vazyme, Nanjing, China) with gDNA Eraser to eliminate DNA contamination. The concentration of the cDNA samples was adjusted to be within the range of 460 to 490 ng/µl, and the cDNA was stored at −20 °C for subsequent RT-qPCR experiments.

RT‐qPCR Analysis

RT‐qPCR was used to measure the expression of candidate reference genes in cDNA samples derived from various developmental stages, including emerging adults and different larval tissues, as well as emerging adults under diverse treatment conditions. RT-qPCR was performed using ChamQ Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme, Nanjing, China) with a CFX96 Real-Time System (Bio-Rad, Analytik Jena, Germany). The reaction (20 µl) consisted of 0.4 µl each of the forward and reverse primers at a concentration of 10 µM, 10 µl of qPCR SuperMix, and 8.2 µl of ddH2O. The samples were pre-denatured at 95 °C for 3 min, followed by 45 cycles of 95 °C for 10 s, 55 °C for 15 s, and 72 °C for 10 s. A melting curve analysis was performed and each sample was run in triplicate. Relative standard curves were generated for each primer pair with 5 serial dilutions of G. mellonella cDNA (1, 1/10, 1/100, 1/1,000, and 1/10,000) (Zhou et al. 2021). A correlation coefficient (R2) was determined for each gene under different treatments using a regression equation (Pfaffl 2001).

Expression Stability Analysis

The reference gene evaluation programs, geNorm, BestKeeper, NormFinder, and ΔCt (Xie et al. 2011, Tang et al. 2022), along with the online platform RefFinder (https://www.heartcure.com.au/reffinder), were used to assess the expression stability of candidate reference genes across various developmental stages, tissues, and conditions. Based on the results of RT-qPCR, the data were imported into Excel 2016. After obtaining the 2−ΔCt values, they were separately imported into geNorm and NormFinder for the calculation of corresponding reference gene expression values. For geNorm analysis, an expression stability value (M) of less than 1.5 indicates that the gene exhibits stable expression under the current experimental conditions compared with other candidate reference genes (Vandesompele et al. 2002). The smaller the value of M, the greater the stability of gene expression. When Vn/(n + 1) was less than 0.15, the optimal number of reference genes was determined to be n. When the paired difference value Vn/(n + 1) is less than 0.15, it indicates that the optimal number of reference genes is n, and no additional new genes need to be added; if Vn/(n + 1) is greater than or equal to 0.15, new genes should be included and the calculation process repeated until the V value is less than 0.15 (Vandesompele et al. 2002). NormFinder assesses the stability value (SV) of candidate reference genes by analyzing their variation within and between groups across different samples. A lower SV indicates a more stable gene (Andersen et al. 2004). BestKeeper was used to calculate Ct values and assess the stability of the candidate reference genes, primarily using the standard deviation (SD) (Pfaffl et al. 2004). For SD <1, a smaller SD indicates greater stability in the expression of the reference gene (Pfaffl et al. 2004). Finally, RefFinder utilizes its built-in algorithm to comprehensively integrate and weight the data from the aforementioned tools, thereby producing a unified, standardized stability ranking of candidate housekeeping genes (Xie et al. 2012). A smaller weighted geometric mean indicates greater stability (Silver et al. 2006, Xie et al. 2012).

Validation of Candidate Reference Genes

The cytochrome P450 gene CYP113520138 was selected from the transcriptome for verification. The sequences adjacent to CYP113520138 were as follows: The upstream sequence was 5′-GGTGACACTATCCGGTGGTG-3′, and the downstream sequence was 5′-ATGGTCGACAACGCAGTCTT-3′. Changes in expression levels were evaluated at various developmental stages and in the larval tissue sites.

Statistical Analysis

The data were statistically analyzed using SPSS 27.0 software (SPSS Inc., Chicago, Illinois, United States). The relative expression of the target genes and validation of candidate reference genes were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCT method (Livak and Schmittgen 2001). All data were assessed for homogeneity of variance and normality using Fisher’s exact test (Kim 2017) and the Shapiro–Wilk test, respectively (Starbuck 2023). Welch’s ANOVA (analysis of variance) followed by Tukey’s multiple comparisons test was used to assess the effect of the reference genes (Jeanmougin et al. 2010). The results were visualized using GraphPad Prism 10 (GraphPad, San Diego, California, United States) (Zhang et al. 2024).

Results

Total RNA Quality and Amplification Efficiencies

The A260/280 ratio of RNA ranged from 1.8 to 2.1, indicating high purity across all samples. To assess the stability of the 12 candidate reference genes, specific primers were designed for each gene, and their amplification specificity and efficiency were determined. RT-qPCR products were amplified with all primers producing a single band, as determined by 1% agarose gel electrophoresis and ranging from 120 to 414 base pairs (Supplementary Fig. S1). The amplification efficiencies varied from 90.73% for RPS20 to 112.2% for PKG21D, whereas the regression coefficients ranged from 0.9022 for RPS25 to 0.9906 for TBP (Table 1). In male G. mellonella, a melt-curve analysis revealed that all amplification products exhibited a single peak, indicating high specificity (Supplementary Fig. S2).

Expression Profiles of 12 Candidate Reference Genes

The Ct values of each candidate reference gene are presented in Fig. 1 for various developmental stages; larval tissue parts; and male and female adults subjected to high temperature, low temperature, and UV exposure for different durations as well as insecticide-treated fourth-instar larvae samples. The candidate reference genes exhibited expression levels ranging from 15.1 to 35.8, indicating that all 12 genes were highly expressed across the various test conditions, albeit with varying degrees of expression among them. The expression of reference genes varied significantly across different developmental stages and tissues of G. mellonella larvae (Fig. 1A and B). The differential expression of RPL7 was significantly increased in females following UV exposure (Fig. 1C), whereas AK1 expression exhibited a marked increase in males (Fig. 1D). Significant variations in TBP expression were detected in females subjected to low-temperature treatment (Fig. 1E), whereas SOD expression was notably increased in males exposed to low temperatures (Fig. 1F). The differential expression of GAPDH2 was significantly increased in the high-temperature-treated female samples (Fig. 1G), whereas RPL13 expression was markedly increased in high-temperature-treated male samples (Fig. 1H). In addition, the differential expression of RPL7 was notably pronounced following insecticide treatment (Fig. 1I).

Stability of the Reference Genes under Varied Experimental Conditions

The mRNA expression levels of the 12 candidate reference genes were analyzed based on their Ct values. To assess the stability of these genes across the 4 experimental conditions, we used 4 analytical methods, including NormFinder, BestKeeper, geNorm, and ΔCt. The stability was ranked using the RefFinder tool.

Developmental Stages

GeNorm, BestKeeper, and ΔCt analyses revealed comparable overall stability among the reference genes. Overall, RPL13 and RPS20 were the most stable genes, while GAPDH2 and RPS7 were the least stable. Except for RPS20, RPL13, and RPS25, the SD of the remaining 9 genes based on BestKeeper exceeded 1. The NormFinder results indicated that RPL13 and RPL22 were the most stable genes, whereas GAPDH2 and RPS7 were the least stable (Table 2). The RefFinder analysis revealed the expression stability of the reference genes ranked from high to low as follows: RPL13 > RPS20 > RPL22 > TUBB2B > RPS25 > SOD > PKG21D > AK1 > TBP > RPL7 > GAPDH2 > RPS7 (Fig. 2A). The optimal number of reference genes was determined using geNorm, with all variation coefficients remaining below 0.15 (Fig. 3A). Two reference genes, RPL13 and RPS20, were selected for data normalization (Table 5).

Larval Tissues

In larval tissues, geNorm and ΔCt analyses showed a consistent stability ranking. RPS7 and RPL22 were the most stable genes, whereas SOD and PKG21D were the least stable. The NormFinder results indicated that RPL7 and RPL13 were the most stable genes, whereas SOD and PKG21D were the least stable. The BestKeeper ranking results indicated that GAPDH2 and RPL7 were the most stable genes, whereas AK1 and PKG21D exhibited the least stability. The SD of the remaining 8 genes, excluding GAPDH2, RPL7, RPL22, and RPS7, exceeded 1 (Table 2). The RefFinder analysis revealed the expression stabilities of the reference genes ranked from high to low as follows: RPL7 > RPL13 > RPL22 > RPS7 > GAPDH2 > TBP > RPS20 > RPS25 > TUBB2B > AK1 > SOD > PKG21D (Fig. 2B). The optimal number of reference genes was determined using geNorm, with all coefficients of variation remaining below 0.15 (Fig. 3B). Two reference genes, RPL13 and RPL7, were selected for data normalization (Table 5).

UV-A Irradiation of Females

In females exposed to UV irradiation, ΔCt and BestKeeper analyses revealed a similar stability ranking. RPL13 and RPL22 were the most stable genes, whereas PKG21D and RPL7 were the least stable ones. Except for RPS7, PKG21D, and RPL7, the SD of the remaining 9 genes in BestKeeper was <1. The geNorm analysis revealed that TBP and SOD had the highest stability among the genes assessed, whereas RPS7 and RPL7 exhibited the lowest stability. The NormFinder results showed that RPL22 and TBP were the most stable genes, whereas RPS7 and RPL7 were the least stable (Table 2). The RefFinder analysis revealed the expression stability of the reference genes ranked from high to low as follows: RPL22 > RPL13 > RPS25 > TUBB2B > TBP > SOD > RPS20 > GAPDH2 > AK1 > RPS7 > PKG21D > RPL7 (Fig. 2C). The optimal number of reference genes was determined using geNorm, with all coefficients of variation remaining below 0.15 (Fig. 3C). Two reference genes, RPL22 and RPL13, were selected for data normalization (Table 5).

UV-A Irradiation of Males

In males, geNorm and ΔCt analyses revealed a similar stability ranking, with RPL7 and RPL22 as the most stable genes, whereas SOD and AK1 were the least stable. BestKeeper identified RPS7 and RPL13 as the most stable reference genes, except for TBP, GAPDH2, and RPS25, which were <1 in BestKeeper. The NormFinder results indicated that TUBB2B and RPL22 were the most stable genes, whereas AK1 and SOD were the least stable (Table 2). The RefFinder analysis revealed the expression stability of the reference genes ranked from high to low as follows: RPL22 > RPL7 > TUBB2B > RPL13 > RPS7 > TBP > RPS20 > RPS25 > PKG21D > SOD > GAPDH2 > AK1 (Fig. 2D). The optimal number of reference genes was determined using geNorm, with all variation coefficients below 0.15 (Fig. 3D). Two reference genes, RPL22 and RPL7, were selected for data normalization (Table 5).

Low-Temperature Treatment of Females

In females, geNorm and BestKeeper analyses indicated a similar stability ranking, with RPL22 and RPS7 as the most stable genes, whereas RPL7 and TBP were the least stable. The BestKeeper analysis revealed that all genes, except RPL13, AK1, GAPDH2, RPL7, and TBP, exhibited an SD of <1. The NormFinder results indicated that RPL13 and SOD were the most stable genes. The ΔCt analysis indicated that SOD and RPL22 were the most stable genes (Table 3). Discussion

Real-time fluorescence quantitative PCR is a robust technique for the study of gene expression. This method incorporates a reference gene to normalize and accurately quantify the expression of a target gene (Yin et al. 2020); however, currently, there are no reference genes that demonstrate stable expression in G. mellonella under diverse circumstances. Consequently, a detailed analysis of gene expression and screening for suitable reference genes are essential to provide insight into gene function and mechanisms. In the present study, we identified stable reference genes of G. mellonella at various developmental stages and in multiple tissues under specific conditions, such as UV exposure, temperature fluctuations, and pesticide concentrations.

RPL13 and RPS20 represent the optimal combination of reference genes for G. mellonella across various developmental stages, both of which are members of the ribosomal protein family. Ribosomal protein genes exhibit stable behavior and are often considered suitable reference genes in various contexts (Lü et al. 2018). The results show correlations and differences with other Lepidopterans. For example, GAPDH and RPS11 have been proposed as reference genes for the quantitative analysis of various developmental stages of Tuta absoluta (Yan et al. 2021). RPL13, EF1α, and RPL27 were identified as reference genes for various developmental stages of Phthorimaea operculella (Shen et al. 2022). The various developmental stages of Sesamia inferens were analyzed using 18S rRNA, EF1, and GAPDH (Sun et al. 2015). For Cnaphalocrocis medinalis, EF1β, PGK, and RPL18 were identified as the most stable reference genes during the developmental stage (Zhao et al. 2022); however, other Lepidopteras have similar families of reference genes at different developmental stages. For example, RPS26 and RPL13 were suggested as reference genes at different developmental stages of Hyphantria cunea (Zhao et al. 2022). RPL7 and RPS11 were recommended as reference genes for measuring the expression of target genes at various developmental stages of Illiberis pruni (He et al. 2024). There were also differences in Coleopteras, such as Callosobruchus maculatus, in which the optimal combination of reference genes in different developmental stages was GST and TBP (Brar et al. 2022). It is possible that the reference genes determined in each study may not be consistent, leading to variations in the most stable reference genes. Thus, significant differences may exist in the reference genes selected by various insect species throughout their developmental timelines, which suggests that homologous reference genes are not universally applicable.

In G. mellonella larvae, the most suitable combination of reference genes in various tissues belonged to the ribosomal protein family. For larvae, RPL7 and RPL13 emerged as the preferred reference genes. The ribosomal protein genes (eg RPL, RPS), belonging to the ribosomal protein family, are often regarded as one of the most suitable reference gene families among most insect species (Gershman et al. 2020, Sun et al. 2022). For example, in P. xylostella, RPL32 and RPS23 have been recommended for specific tissues (Fu et al. 2013). RPS3 and RPL18 were identified as the most stable reference genes in the larval tissues of C. medinalis (Zhao et al. 2022). RPL7 and RPL18 were identified as suitable reference genes for various tissues of I. pruni, whereas, RPL7 and RPS11 are specifically recommended as reference genes for female insects (He et al. 2024). There are also reports of varying outcomes, such as the suggested use of VATPase and GST as reference genes in different tissues of adult C. maculatus (Brar et al. 2022). The 18S rRNA and GAPDH genes were considered the most stably expressed reference genes across various tissues of Acyrthosiphon pisum in the order Hemiptera (Gao et al. 2023). Studies examining target gene expression across various tissues in the adult Grapholita molesta suggest that the combination of UBC7, β-tubulin, and β-actin serves as a suitable reference (Han et al. 2023). Stable reference genes are not the same at different insect tissue sites and for larvae of the same insect. This variation is likely attributable to the distinct physiological functions and metabolic activities inherent to these tissues, which in turn, influence both the magnitude and the stability of the gene expression analysis. Therefore, it is important to consider tissue specificity when selecting endogenous genes.

In the present study, we observed that under UV environmental stress, the optimal UV irradiation for G. mellonella females, representing the maximum tolerance without induction of mutations, was associated with RPL22 and RPL13. Conversely, in males, the optimal UV irradiation corresponded to RPL22 and RPL7. Most studies have used temperature, pesticides, and various treatments to screen insects for reference genes. In contrast, UV light is infrequently utilized in the screening process for reference genes (Yang et al. 2022). Nevertheless, the survival and behavior of insects are affected by influences from UV radiation. For instance, it elevates the mortality rate of Musca domestica, while simultaneously reducing their antioxidant activity (Cui et al. 2021). In addition, it affects the antioxidant and detoxification enzymes in the caterpillar of Dendrolimus tabulaeformis (Wang et al. 2019). Therefore, further exploration is needed to investigate the application of UV for screening internal reference genes across various insect species.

The optimal reference genes for male and female adults of G. mellonella vary with temperature and treatment duration. For female adults at low temperatures, the most suitable reference genes are RPS7 and SOD. In contrast, the optimal reference genes for males at low temperatures are RPL13 and RPL22. At high temperatures, suitable reference genes for females are RPS25 and RPL22, whereas for males, RPL22 and RPS20 are appropriate. Insects exhibit adaptive responses to environmental stress by modifying biochemical processes, cellular functions, and gene expression. These adaptations enhance their survival under adverse conditions (Teets and Denlinger 2013, Renault et al. 2018). SOD is involved in reactive oxygen metabolism and is widely distributed across various organisms (Kobayashi et al. 2019). Glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) was established as a stable and reliable reference gene in biological studies, and has been widely used as a reference gene in expression analyses (De Jonge et al. 2007, Lourenco et al. 2008). In contrast to the findings of the present study, GAPDH was identified as the least stable reference gene among females subjected to increased temperatures. Most studies have used various temperatures to identify the most appropriate internal control, few studies have specifically analyzed reference genes for various treatment durations (Ebrahimi et al. 2024). For example, for P. xylostella, ACTB and GAPDH were identified as reference genes at varying temperatures (Fu et al. 2013). In addition, GAPDH and EF1 from Spodoptera litura were used for larvae subjected to temperature stress (Lu et al. 2013). Furthermore, the combination of β-ACT and RPL7 from Chilo sacchariphagus was considered optimal for gene expression analysis across different temperatures (Wang et al. 2024). These studies demonstrate that the degree of expression of the same “housekeeping genes” in different insect species ranged from stable expression to significantly increased abundance following temperature stress. This study incorporating treatment time into the screening of reference genes may facilitate the identification of stably expressed candidates, thereby providing a reliable tool for gene expression analysis.

The optimal reference genes combination for the fourth-instar larvae of G. mellonella under chlorantraniliprole treatment is TBP and RPS25. This finding contrasts with the optimal reference genes identified for Lymantria dispar under insecticide stress, namely RPS3 and GAPDH (Yin et al. 2020), and under Rhopalosiphum padi insecticide treatment, β-Actin and GAPDH were the most stable (Li et al. 2021). In insecticide stress research, the selection and validation of stable and reliable reference genes are crucial prerequisites for ensuring the accuracy, reliability, and comparability of gene expression data. This is essential for elucidating the mechanisms of insecticide action, insect resistance, as well as the development of novel, highly efficient, and low-toxicity pesticides. For example, the different pesticides targeting Nilaparvata lugens in the order Hemiptera are recommended to be used in conjunction with insect-based markers, such as RPS11, in a similar manner (Yuan et al. 2014). The use of insecticides is linked to the stability of reference genes. This relationship arises because the selectivity of insecticides, along with issues related to pest resistance, directly affects the constitutive expression of reference genes across various experimental conditions.

If the selected internal reference gene is not stably expressed, it may result in inaccurate normalization of the gene expression data (Tang et al. 2023). This may lead to the misinterpretation of gene expression patterns. Therefore, meticulous selection and validation of reference genes exhibiting stable expression are necessary to ensure the reliability of results obtained from the gene expression analysis (Tang et al. 2022). The present study indicated that varying the reference gene used for assessing gene expression levels leads to different results. We evaluated the stability of gene expression in G. mellonella during developmental stages and in larval tissues. This was achieved by standardizing gene expression levels using the 2 most stable genes and 1 least stable gene (Yu et al. 2025). The validation results were not consistent with those obtained for G. molesta (Han et al. 2023) and A. dissimilis (Tang et al. 2022), indicating that the use of different internal reference genes may yield varying outcomes across different insect species. The results of this study provide insights into the mRNA expression levels of G. mellonella genes and lay the foundation for subsequent studies, such as RNA interference.

In summary, in this study, we used 5 common tools: geNorm, NormFinder, BestKeeper, ΔCt, and ReFinder. We analyzed the expression stability levels of 12 candidate reference genes (GAPDH2, TUBB2B, TBP, RPL7, RPL13, RPL22, SOD, AK1, RPS7, RPS20, RPS25, and PKG21D) under various treatment conditions in G. mellonella. The results indicated that the optimal reference genes markedly varied under different experimental conditions. The best suitable reference gene combinations for different developmental stages of G. mellonella, different larval tissues, UV irradiation treatments of males, low-temperature treatment of females, high-temperature treatment of females, high-temperature treatment of males, and insecticide treatments were RPL13 + RPS20, RPL7 + RPL13, RPL22 + RPL7, RPS7 + SOD, RPS25 + RPL22, RPL22 + RPS20, and TBP + RPS25, respectively. In contrast, the best reference gene combination for UV irradiation treatment in females and low-temperature treatment in males was RPL22 + RPL13. The findings from the verification process can be integrated with studies on RNA interference to examine its associated functions.
