Patients’ perspectives in German general practice on discussing the environmental impact of medications: A qualitative study
Authors: Dana Neumann, Christiane Müller, Frank Müller
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPharmaceuticals represent a major source of carbon emissions in primary care. General practitioners could avoid prescribing climate-harmful medications and consider eco-friendly alternatives in shared decision-making processes. However, evidence on patients’ perceptions of discussing medication-specific environmental impacts in primary care consultations in Germany remains limited.ObjectivesTo explore patients’ perceptions of discussing medications’ environmental and climate impact during family medicine encounters and their willingness to switch to more eco-friendly options.MethodsThis qualitative study employed semi-structured interviews with patients recruited through GP practices in Northern Germany. Inclusion criteria were long-term use of at least one medication, legal age, and informed consent. Twenty-five interviews were conducted and analysed using structured content analysis according to Kuckartz.ResultsRespondents expressed surprise when environment/climate topics were to be discussed in medical encounters but demonstrated openness to these topics and desired more information about their medications’ environmental impact. Many interviewees showed willingness to switch to eco-friendly medications despite potential disadvantages including more frequent intake, increased side effect risk, or co-payments. Patients exhibited high trust in provider recommendations and sought greater information and transparency.ConclusionResults suggest opportunities to incorporate climate/environmental aspects into shared decision-making. Understanding patient perspectives enables GPs to address environmental/climate-friendly medication topics in treatment discussions. Patients are receptive to these discussions and willing to accept eco-friendly alternatives despite potential drawbacks. The high level of trust in provider recommendations positions family physicians as key actors in promoting environmental stewardship.

Introduction

Climate change poses a significant threat to human health worldwide [1–3]. Paradoxically, the healthcare sector, whose primary mission is to prevent harm, is a substantial contributor to this crisis. While in Germany the healthcare sector accounts for approximately 5% of national greenhouse gas emissions this proportion is much higher in other countries (i.e. United States 8-10%) [4,5]. Within healthcare, pharmaceuticals have been identified as a major and increasing source of greenhouse gas emissions: The global footprint of pharmaceuticals has grown from 124 Mt CO2-equivalents in 1995 to 220 Mt CO2-equivalents in 2019, with an average of 73 kg CO2-equivalents per capita and year in high-income countries and approximately 8 kg CO2-equivalents per capita in low-incoming countries [6]. Beyond greenhouse gas emissions, pharmaceutical residues entering the environment through human excretion, improper disposal, and manufacturing contribute to aquatic contamination, antimicrobial resistance, endocrine disruption in wildlife, and persistent soil and water pollution from per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances [7]. Most of medications are prescribed in primary care settings [8], suggesting that modifications to prescribing practices could significantly reduce the sector’s carbon footprint [9]. For example, reducing prescriptions of metered-dose inhalers containing hydrofluoroalkane as propeller gases in favour of more climate-friendly alternatives (such as dry powder inhalers) could considerably decrease carbon emissions in outpatient care [10,11]. In hospital care, change from inhalational anaesthetics to intravenous anaesthetics have been shown to decrease greenhouse gases considerably [12,13].

Recent research has shown that while the majority of primary care providers acknowledge the reality of climate change and express willingness to incorporate climate-related aspects into shared decision-making with patients, they also voice concerns that addressing climate change during consultations might challenge patients’ political and personal beliefs or their trust in the physician and potentially jeopardise the physician-patient relationship [14,15].

However, interviews revealed that German general practice (GP) patients who had already experienced climate-sensitive health counselling highly accepted it [16]. A study from the Netherlands comparing hypothetical vignettes indicated that GP patients responded neutral to positively towards eco-sustainable treatment advice [17]. Similarly, in a pragmatic survey among German hospital patients 62.2% of respondents expressed interest in knowing about the environmental impact of their medications and 65% reporting this information would affect their medication choices [18]. Research among Dutch outpatient gynecological patients demonstrated that 62% were willing to choose climate-friendly treatment alternatives when equally effective, though acceptance varied by medical condition and patients prioritised maintaining individual choice in treatment decisions [19]. First recommendations on how to generally discuss climate change in primary care consultations highlight the core principles of shared decision making [20] or patient centred communication [16], with transparent communication and maintaining patients’ individual decision-making autonomy, without specifically outlining how medication choices should be discussed [21,22].

Despite these findings, patients’ qualitative perspectives, feelings, and considerations on discussing the environmental impact of their medications during specifically within primary care consultations remains limited. One study about climate-friendly inhaler prescription showed very mixed perspectives of patients [15]. In consequence, this qualitative study aims to explore how patients perceive the discussion of ecological impact of their medications, how patients want to be informed about environmental aspects of medications, what specific information they desire, how they react to such discussions, and eventually providing insights on how shared decision-making conversations about medication environmental impact should be conducted. Understanding these patient perspective is essential for developing patient-centred approaches that integrate environmental considerations into prescribing practices without compromising the therapeutic relationship.

Methods

Study design and theoretical framework

This qualitative study used semi-structured in-depth interviews to explore patients’ perspectives on climate or environment damaging medication as well as their attitudes towards switching to more climate-/environmental-friendly medications. Interview data were analysed using content analysis, according to Kuckartz [23]. Interviews were conducted between April and August 2024 at the Department of General Practice, University Medical Centre Göttingen, Germany, with a parallel study being conducted at Michigan State University in the United States. This paper reports on the German part of the study. Due to unforeseen circumstances, data collection in the US has not yet been successful. In order to make the data from Germany publicly available, we have chosen to include reports on the German part of the study in this article. The study received approval by the institutional review board at University Medical Centre Göttingen (Approval: 16/10/23). Study results are reported following the consolidated criteria for reporting qualitative research (COREQ) [24].

Research team and reflexivity

The research team consisted of the medical student and doctoral candidate (DN) and two experienced health services researchers with one focus in qualitative methodology (FM: sociologist, MD and urban designer, CM: MD and MPH) supervised the project. DN had received training in qualitative interviewing techniques by CM and in analysing the interviews by FM. The entire team was involved in the discursive development of the interview guide, and DN was managing recruitment, data collection, analysis, and interpretation. In regular meetings with CM/FM, the process and the results of the analyses were discussed and adapted. The interviewers had no prior relationship with any of the interviewed patients.

The research team acknowledges the reality of climate change and its health impacts. Some of us engage in research on changing urban microclimate and health. However, none are affiliated with environmental or political organisations. We do not consider individual consumer behaviour the primary lever for reducing (pharmaceutical) environmental impacts - rather, we view systemic and regulatory change as central. We are aware that these perspectives may have influenced how this project has been conducted, especially how findings were interpreted, and we have discussed this during coding sessions.

Participant selection and recruitment

Study participants were recruited in nine family medicine practices in Southern Lower Saxony, and one family medicine practice in Hesse, Germany, using two strategies: either medical assistants approached patients at the practice’s front desk (36% of participants) or DN asked potential participants directly in the practices’ waiting room (64%) to participate in a 30-minute interview. Potential participants received study information on a study about ‘which role nature, environment, and climate can play in discussions between family medicine providers and patients’. The main topic about medications’ impact on the climate was not revealed at this time as it might have biased the sample. Recruitment was conducted consecutively. To capture a broad range of perspectives, we sought to approach people of different ages, genders, and place of residences. Interviews were not conducted on the spot but scheduled subsequently, allowing participants sufficient time to consider participation.

Patients were eligible to participate, when they were (a) adults (≥18 years) with (b) at least one ongoing prescribed medication for a chronic condition. Patients were excluded if they were (a) unable to provide informed consent or (b) had limited German language proficiency.

All participants provided written informed consent prior to participation.

Interview guide development and structure

The interview guide was developed by FM and CM based on Helfferich’s methodology [25]. It was structured to balance open-ended exploration with targeted inquiry, using anchor questions as orientation points. The interview began with broad questions about participants’ general views on environmental protection, gradually moving to more specific topics about environmental considerations in medical consultations. Narrative-generating questions were incorporated to encourage in-depth responses. Key areas covered included: (1) participants’ perspective on environment and environmental protection, (2) their awareness of medications’ environmental impact, (3) their emotional response to learning about potential environmental harm from their medications, (4) their willingness to switch to more environmentally friendly alternatives, and (5) their trust in their family physician regarding environmental information about medications. Before starting data collection, the interview guide was discussed with experienced qualitative researchers from the department to ensure comprehensibility and logical flow. The guide was then piloted in test interviews conducted by CM and DM under supervision, allowing minor adjustments to question wording and sequence. The interview guideline is provided as a supplemental resource in Table S1.

Data collection

Semi-structured interviews were conducted between April and August 2024 by DN (n = 22) and CM (n = 3), either by telephone (n = 19) or face-to-face (n = 6). Face-to-face interviews took place either at the Department of General Practice (n = 4) or in the participating family practices (n = 2). Interviews lasted between 10 and 35 min (mean: 15 min) and were audio-recorded. After each interview, participants completed a sociodemographic questionnaire that included questions about age, gender, residence, occupation, chronic conditions, medications, and three items assessing attitudes towards climate change. All audio recordings were transcribed verbatim using f4x software (audiotranscription dr. dresing & pehl GmbH, Marburg, Germany) and anonymised. Audio files were deleted following transcription and quality check.

Data analysis

Interviews were analysed using qualitative content analysis following Kuckartz’s methodology [23] using the software MAXQDA 24 (VERBI GmbH, Berlin, Germany). The analysis process began with the development of main categories, which were deductively derived from the interview guide, followed by inductive coding of subcategories that emerged from the data. Initial coding was performed independently by two researchers (DN and FM) on four randomly selected interviews, before discussing a preliminary code structure. After reaching consensus on the coding framework, DN coded the remaining interviews (axial coding). Throughout the analysis, codes and emerging themes were regularly discussed within the research team to ensure analytical rigour. We continued this iterative process of coding and category refinement until no new themes emerged from the data. Representative quotes were selected to illustrate the findings and were translated from German to English using forward translation.

Environmental/climate consciousness

All interviewees showed some level of environmental awareness, reflected in eco-friendly habits like waste separation, conscious consumption, sustainable mobility, and nature-friendly gardening. While many felt their individual actions had limited impact, they emphasised the importance of collective efforts and shared responsibility, especially for future generations.

“Whatever we do has a tiny impact, but it does have an impact. We can all do something and should not hold back … I firmly believe that every little bit we contribute has an impact. … Of course, we can also influence the people around us by making our stance very clear.” (P14, pos.6)

Environmental protection was seen as a moral duty tied to human survival. Most participants did not distinguish strongly between climate protection and nature conservation, viewing them as closely connected, though a few noted subtle differences or mentioned natural versus human-driven climate change.

Environment/climate as a topic in the consultation

Participants reported little to no prior experience discussing environmental aspects during medical consultations. While some were surprised by the idea, many showed openness to having such conversations. A number of participants recognised the link between environmental and personal health. However, time constraints and concerns about overburdening physicians emerged as key barriers to integrating environmental topics into medical care.

“Well, I think you have to look at it holistically. (…) Of course, that would fit in well there, because ultimately, climate, nature, and the environment also play a part in our health or illness.” (P15, pos. 18)

Environmental/climate harm caused by medications

Existing knowledge

The participants’ responses reveal varying levels of awareness about medications’ environmental impact. Despite this overall lack of awareness, some participants demonstrated some knowledge about medication disposal pathways and raised concern about the long-term impact of these drugs on the environment and the resulting consequences.

“I could well imagine that certain degradation products, which are just in the wastewater […], that they can cause a certain amount of damage to nature.” (P5, pos. 28)

Attitudes towards medication intake

To explore participants’ perspectives on medication-related environmental impacts, participants were confronted with a hypothetical scenario in which their physician mentions that their medication may have negative environmental impacts. Interviewees reacted differently:

Many participants prioritised their health over environmental concerns, with some accepting potential environmental harm as a necessary compromise.

“Well, then. I mean, I would think about it, but I cannot change anything because I’m taking the medication and I need it. […] I do not think I would think about it at all. I would continue to take the medication.” (P3, pos. 28)

Others expressed guilt but still placed health needs first.

“I would swallow it even if it wasn’t good for the environment. I say it quite simply, I say it quite honestly. My health is more important to me.” (P16, pos. 28)

Some also recognised links between environmental and personal health, suggesting that reducing environmental impact could benefit their own well-being.

“As a rule, what doesn’t affect the environment that much probably doesn’t affect me that much either, I think.” (P19, pos. 30)

Considerations for a medication change

General willingness for a change

When asked whether there would be a basic consideration for a change to a more environmentally/climate-friendly medication, it became clear that there is a willingness to change. Some participants expressed environmental motivation as a positive factor in their decision-making.

“Because I would simply feel like I was doing something good for the environment. Why should I pollute the environment if I don’t have to?” (P21, pos. 46)

Weighting in of risks emerged as an important consideration, with some interviewees indicating willingness to try alternatives under preconditions.

“Yes, I would. Just on the condition that I try it out and can potentially switch back if it doesn’t work for me, […].” (P18, pos. 36)

Despite this general openness, a consistent pattern emerged across responses: the clear prioritisation of personal health outcomes over environmental considerations.

“But that also has its limits; I have to tell you honestly. I’m a cancer patient and my first priority is survival, my survival.” (P1, pos. 20)

Side effects

Responses revealed a nuanced assessment of side effect tolerance that varied based on type, severity, and impact on daily functioning. Some participants expressed a categorical rejection of any new side effects. Most participants, however, differentiated between minor side effects that would not affect daily functioning and can be tolerated and ones that are more significant: […] well, the small side effect is something that you don’t necessarily notice. I do not know. If the toenails grow faster or something, […]. (P2, pos. 34)

Side effects that would disrupt daily activities or work capacity represented a clear boundary for many participants:

[…] if I get diarrhea or an allergic rash …, then of course I would say, then not, […]. (P21, pos. 48)

More frequent intakes

For many patients, the quantity of medication intakes is of secondary importance; they could easily agree to it without any concerns. Some assess frequent dosing as challenging, especially with multiple medications, but would accept it if needed. Additional intakes are seen as problematic if it affects quality of life or adds inconvenience.

“[…] because, if I had to take it at night, for example, and couldn’t sleep through the night anymore. Then I wouldn’t do it. If it didn’t really change anything, taking it twice instead of once a day for example. That wouldn’t be a problem […].” (P2, pos. 38)

Price increase

It becomes apparent that the influence of the medication price depends on various factors. A willingness to make co-payments can be detected by many interviewees, especially in connection with environmental protection. Other patients expressed financial constraints (e.g. due to a low pension, income) as very important and were less open to additional payments. The form of health insurance plays a key role in decision-making. Many interviewees with statutory insurance noted their choice would depend on whether the co-payment is covered and only the prescription fee applies.

“If I had to pay for it myself that would be more difficult. I don’t get that big pension. In addition, I’m a health insurance patient. So you have to rely on what the health insurance companies covers.” (P22, pos. 53)

Expectations and roles of patients and physicians

Information needs

Most of the interviewees expressed a fundamental interest in receiving information about the environmental impact of their medication.

“Yes, I think it would be good in itself, because I actually think it’s generally good if people take care of it and I’m also quite happy if there is somehow input from outside.” (P20, pos. 56)

The participants had a clear desire for more transparency and openness from their doctor, especially regarding side effects, treatment options, and environmentally friendly alternatives.

“And I think it would be good if someone could inform me, also about. […] the poor environmental compatibility of a product. And of course I would then need an alternative, that’s clear.” (P17, pos. 36)

Trust in physicians

For many of the interviewees, the long-standing relationship with their doctor forms the basis for a strong relationship of trust.

“Absolutely trustworthy, because I’ve been a patient there for almost 25 years. […] There is one of them who has been with me consistently over all these years and I trust her absolutely.” (P14, pos. 44)

Trust is often based on positive experiences and the conviction that doctors are acting in the best interests of their patients. However, in some instance, prior health experiences foster a more critical attitude towards physicians, which can have a negative impact on the relationship between patient and doctor.

“Due to my medical history, I have developed a general distrust of doctors.” (P5, pos. 54)

Preferred communication approaches

Communication needs to be effective and understandable, so that patients are able to make a decision. Most participants expressed the wish for a clearer and more easily understandable language. In particular, specific information is easier to understand if it is provided in the patient’s native language.

“Well, it’s about explaining things in a way that’s easy to understand, so that patients—like me—can really grasp it. That is actually the most important part: making sure the information reaches the patient. […] If doctors use technical terms that only they are familiar with, it becomes difficult.” (P6, pos. 74)

Some participants valued detailed explanations of how medication works in relation to their illness. They also showed strong interest in take-home materials like brochures and saw posters in medical practices as helpful for raising awareness.

“I think education is a huge topic and an important one. […] I think that education should also be provided in different ways, from online to flyers somehow. Maybe even in the waiting room of a doctor’s surgery. […] Providing information is the basis for everything else.” (P21, pos. 74)

Time and resource considerations

Interviewees consider time constraints in medical consultations as a common problem. GPs often only have a time slot of five to ten minutes for each individual patient. Participants emphasised that timing is crucial for discussing environmental impacts of medications. They preferred such communication during routine check-ups or regular appointments; acute illness consultations were not considered suitable.

“There are different reasons for going to the doctor. If I have a current illness, i.e. at a general doctor’s surgery, it’s a cold in my case, then I’m not open to a conversation about environmental protection. Then I would do it. Then I want relief from my symptoms, maybe a certificate of incapacity for work, then I want to go home again.” (P14, pos. 48)

Discussion

The analysis of the 25 interviews showed that patients expressed a principal desire to be informed about the environmental and climate impact of the medications they were taking, despite having limited existing knowledge about pharmaceutical environmental effects. While many expressed an openness to consider environmentally friendly alternatives, personal health served as a reference point in their reasoning - not as an upfront rejection, but as a boundary that emerged when trade-offs became concrete. Some were open to accepting disadvantages (co-payments, light side effects, more frequent intakes) when changing to a more environmentally sensible medication. Interestingly, one participant also took the opposite view, indicating that environmentally harmful medication would also harm them personally. Generally, patients indicated high levels of trust in their physicians, with a strong preference for clear, patient-centred communication and the integration of environmental topics during routine check-ups rather than acute visits.

Many of our findings confirm those of studies in other countries. For example, similar to other studies, the patients in our study demonstrated high levels of climate and environmental awareness and expressed openness and often preference towards sustainable healthcare [16–19,22,26]. Despite the common preferences for sustainable healthcare, our study reveals varying levels of awareness about medications’ environmental impact, with an overall lack of comprehensive knowledge. While some participants demonstrated basic understanding of medication disposal pathways and expressed concern about long-term environmental consequences, most had limited knowledge of how pharmaceuticals might affect ecosystems and climate. This finding aligns with international research across multiple countries showing that only a minority of patients report good knowledge about pharmaceutical environmental effects, with most information restricted to basic disposal practices [27–29].

In line with this finding, most participants in our study had no prior experience with environmental or climate-related counselling during GP consultations and were surprised, though not opposed, to the idea that such topics could be integrated into medical encounters. Cohen et al. similarly found that Dutch gynecological patients had no previous exposure to environmental information in clinical settings but expressed interest in receiving such information [19].

Studies from the US and the Netherlands suggest that primary care clinicians were generally open to discussing climate-change and environmental aspects of health care during encounters, but also expressed hesitation regarding potential negative patient reactions and concerns about compromising the physician-patient relationship [14,15]. Our findings directly challenge these physician assumptions. Contrary to clinicians’ concerns about negative patient reactions, participants not only expressed openness to environmental discussions but actively desired such information. Most welcomed being informed about their medications’ environmental impact, viewing such discussions as enhancing rather than compromising transparency and trust. However, Oosterveld’s qualitative study conducted in The Netherlands also revealed more polarised patient reactions when climate-friendly prescribing policies were discussed in focus groups, ranging from positive responses (‘good move’) to strongly negative ones (‘worthless,’ ‘ridiculous’) [15].

Interestingly, the interviewed patients in our study considered their GPs as a reliable and trustworthy source of information on environmental aspects of medications, with many viewing their long-standing physician relationships as the foundation for this confidence. However, this trust may be misplaced, as research suggests that healthcare professionals themselves often feel unprepared or lack knowledge to discuss pharmaceutical environmental impacts [14,30], and – despite some considerable exceptions [31] – pharmaceutical environmental aspects are in many countries not systematically integrated into medical training programs [32,33].

We found a general willingness of the interviewees, to change to a more climate/environmentally friendly medication if suitable alternatives are available. This finding is consistent with other research, such as a UK study demonstrating that 70% of inhaler users were willing to switch to an inhaler with a lower carbon footprint [34]. However, patient responses varied considerably in their willingness to accept potential disadvantages when switching to environmentally friendly medications, ranging from absolute health prioritisation to openness for meaningful compromises. This heterogeneity aligns with recent research identifying distinct patient preference groups and underscores the need for tailored rather than standardised approaches to environmental medication counselling [29].

Strengths and limitations

This study comes with limitations: First, participant recruitment likely introduced selection bias. Since the study was introduced as exploring ‘the role of nature, environment, and climate in GP consultations’, patients with negative attitudes towards environmental and climate protection may have been reluctant to participate, likely reducing representation of sceptical perspectives in our sample. Consequently, our results cannot be generalised to all patients in GPs practices in Germany or anywhere. Additionally, most participants were recruited from Northern Germany, limiting transferability to other regions. Mixed data collection methods (telephone vs. face-to-face interviews) represented another potential limitation, e.g. difference in depths, though interview quality appeared unaffected by method [35]. Additionally, social desirability bias and the hypothetical nature of the presented scenarios during the interviews may limit the transferability of findings to actual clinical practice.

One strength of this study lies in the diversity of interviewees; there was broad variability in terms of gender, age, place of residence, chronic conditions, and medications. The sociodemographic composition of our sample reflects typical GP utilisation patterns in Germany, where consultation rates are highest among older age groups and higher in women than men [36]. The high level of environmental awareness observed is consistent with general population trends, with 88% of German adults rating environmental protection as important or very important [37].

Implications for practice and research

As the impacts of climate change increasingly affect all areas of life, including health, there is a growing need to consider environmental sustainability within the healthcare system itself. The heterogeneity in patient preferences for information underscores the need for shared decision-making frameworks specifically designed for environmental medication counselling. While first communication models and existing climate-sensitive consultation guides for general practice provide initial frameworks in Germany [38], research should develop and test structured guides and decision support tools that can be integrated into routine clinical workflows. A promising approach would be the development of comprehensive medication lists that systematically compare environmentally harmful medications with more sustainable alternatives for common primary care conditions, similar to the Swedish ‘Kloka Listan’ [39], but enhanced with specific alternative recommendations. Such tools would provide physicians with actionable, evidence-based guidance to inform prescribing decisions and could be integrated into electronic health record systems and clinical decision support platforms. Alongside structural interventions, postgraduate physician education on pharmaceutical environmental impacts are essential to promote environmentally conscious prescribing into practice.

Conclusion

Participants of this qualitative study in German general practice were generally open to discussing the environmental impact of their medications and willing to consider eco-friendly alternatives, even when this involves trade-offs. Personal health remained the primary reference point in decision-making, but high levels of trust in providers position family practitioners as relevant actors in promoting environmentally conscious prescribing. Future studies should adopt broader recruitment strategies to better include participants with hesitant or sceptical views towards climate change.
