Real-world efficacy of postoperative adjuvant EGFR-TKI therapy for stage IA2-IA3 lung adenocarcinoma with high-risk pathological factors
Authors: Sida Lu, Chenxu Liu, Heng Tao, Penglong Zhao, Jing Luo, Zhuangzhuang Cong, Yi Shen
Published: 2025

Background

Lung cancer remains a major global health burden, ranking as the second most frequently diagnosed malignancy worldwide, with an estimated 2.2 million new cancer cases and 1.8 million deaths per year [1, 2]. Non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) accounts for approximately 85% of lung cancer cases, with lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) being the most prevalent histological subtype [3, 4].

The ADAURA study demonstrated that adjuvant osimertinib significantly improved 5-year overall survival (OS) in patients with stage IB–IIIA lung adenocarcinoma following surgical resection [5]. Both the National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) and the European Society for Medical Oncology (ESMO) guidelines recommended adjuvant osimertinib after chemotherapy for stage IB patients with high-risk pathological factors to improve prognosis [6, 7]. However, neither guideline currently recommends postoperative adjuvant therapy for stage IA LUAD.

Stage IA patients generally have favorable outcomes, with a 5-year OS rate of 88% [8], while 5-year disease-free survival (DFS) rates among IA1, IA2, and IA3 subgroups varies a lot, 88.2%, 73.6% and 64.5%, respectively [9]. In selected stage IA LUAD patients, a high risk of recurrence and metastasis still remains. Invasive adenocarcinoma was commonly classified into acinar, papillary, micropapillary, lepidic and solid according to the predominant histologic subtype [10]. In 2015, Spread through air space (STAS) was recognized as an additional pattern of invasion by the World Health Organization (WHO) [11]. In 2020, International Association for the Study of Lung Cancer (IASLC) proposed a new grading system for invasive pulmonary adenocarcinomas, which recognized solid growth pattern, micropapillary growth pattern and complex glandular pattern as high-grade patterns. Within this system, any tumors with 20% or more of high-grade patterns were classified into poorly differentiated tumors [12]. These high-grade patterns along with lymphovascular invasion(LVI) and STAS are all high-risk pathological factors, which are associate with poor prognosis [13–17]. Consequently, some clinicians recommend adjuvant Epidermal Growth Factor Receptor Tyrosine Kinase Inhibitor (EGFR-TKI) therapy for stage IA LUAD patients with such high-risk features, although its clinical benefit requires further validation through high-quality clinical trials.

Evidence on adjuvant EGFR-TKI therapy in stage IA LUAD remains limited. Zhao et al. reported that adjuvant therapy, including targeted therapy, significantly reduced the risk of recurrence and metastasis in stage I patients with high-risk pathological factors [18]. Similarly, Liu et al. included stage IA patients in their study and concluded that EGFR-TKIs could serve as postoperative adjuvant therapy for stage II–IIIA and selected high-risk stage I EGFR mutation-positive NSCLC patients [19]. Jiang et al. further suggested that adjuvant EGFR-TKI therapy may improve both DFS and OS in stage IA and IB EGFR-mutant NSCLC [20]. Nevertheless, few studies have specifically evaluated the efficacy of adjuvant EGFR-TKI therapy in stage IA LUAD with high-risk pathological factors. To address this gap, we conducted this retrospective study.

Methods

Patients selection and exclusion

This retrospective study was conducted at Jinling Hospital, Nanjing University. Electronic medical records of patients with lung adenocarcinoma discharged between January 1, 2017, and December 31, 2021 were reviewed to identify eligible stage IA patients with pathological high-risk factors. During case collection, none of the stage IA1 patients had received EGFR-TKI therapy. Therefore, the study population was limited to stage IA2–IA3 adenocarcinoma patients with pathological high-risk features.

Staging was performed according to the IASLC 9th edition TNM grading and staging system [8]. In this study, micropapillary growth pattern, solid growth pattern, complex glandular pattern, along with STAS, and LVI were defined as pathological high-risk factors for tumor recurrence and metastasis. The proportion of each histologic pattern was recorded in 5% to 10% increments to identify the predominant subtype [21]. A high-grade pattern was considered present when it exceeded 5% of the tumor, and IASLC grade was then assigned based on the IASLC grading system [12].

In this study, stage IA LUAD patients with any of these high-risk pathological factors were considered eligible for inclusion. Exclusion criteria were as follows: [1] presence of malignancies in other organs; [2] diagnosis of mucinous adenocarcinoma; and [3] incomplete clinical or follow-up data. The inclusion and exclusion criteria are illustrated in Fig. 1. Patients harboring driver oncogenes other than EGFR mutation were pre-excluded.

Flow chart of the study

Patients were ultimately categorized into EGFR-TKI therapy group and non-EGFR-TKI therapy group. Patients who received EGFR-TKI therapy for less than six months were considered to have received inadequate treatment and were therefore classified into the non-EGFR-TKI group.

Data collection and radiological evaluation

The collected Clinical data included gender, age, smoking history, tumor size, tumor location, surgical method, patients’ pathological report, radiological evaluation on CT, IASLC grade, adjuvant therapy and disease recurrence or metastasis.

Patients who received at least 4 cycles of adjuvant chemotherapy were recognized as having received postoperative chemotherapy. Adjuvant EGFR-TKI therapy was initiated within 4 to 16 weeks after surgery, either with or without prior chemotherapy.

For radiological evaluation, the solid component on CT was defined as an area of increased opacification that completely obscured the underlying vascular markings. GGO was defined as an area of a slight, homogenous increase in density that did not obscure the underlying vascular markings [22]. Patients were categorized into the pure-ground glass opacity (pGGO), mixed-ground glass opacity (mGGO), and solid groups based on consolidation to tumor ratio (CTR).

Follow up

Chest computed tomography (CT) scans were performed every six months following surgery to monitor disease progression. Positron emission tomography (PET)/CT and brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) were additionally used to assess recurrence and metastasis. Patient follow-up was performed in the outpatient department of cardiothoracic surgery, respiratory or oncology, as well as by telephone. Disease-free survival (DFS) was defined as the interval from the date of surgery to the detection of disease progression. Tumor recurrence or metastasis was considered evidence of disease progression and served as the primary endpoint of this study.

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables were described using frequencies and proportions, continuous variables using means and standard deviations. For continuous variables not conforming to a normal distribution, medians and interquartile ranges were used. Differences in categorical variables were assessed using the Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. The independent t-test was used to compare means of continuous variables. Disease-free survival (DFS) for the entire cohort was estimated using the Kaplan–Meier method, and differences were assessed with the log-rank test. Univariate and Multivariate analyses were performed using the Cox regression model. Statistical analyses and plotting were performed using Stata 18.5 and R4.3.3 software. All statistical tests were two-sided, with P < 0.05 as the threshold of significance.

Results

Patient characteristics

Following the screening process, a total of 221 patients were included in the final analysis, of whom 42 received postoperative adjuvant EGFR-TKI therapy. Baseline characteristics of the study population are summarized in Table 1. Patients in the adjuvant EGFR-TKI therapy group had larger tumor diameters compared to those in the non-EGFR-TKI therapy group (22.26 ± 0.70 vs. 20.09 ± 0.41, P = 0.018). No other significant differences were observed between the two groups.

Survival data

The median follow-up time for all included patients was 49 months (IQR:38–63). Kaplan–Meier analysis showed no statistically significant difference in disease-free survival (DFS) between the adjuvant EGFR-TKI therapy group and the non-EGFR-TKI group (P = 0.080) (Fig. 2A). The 3-year DFS rate was 84.4% (95% CI: 79.2–90.0%) in the non–EGFR-TKI group, whereas no recurrence events were observed in the EGFR-TKI group during the same period. Univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses were then performed for the entire cohort (Table 2). Multivariable COX proportional hazards model included tumor size and adjuvant EGFR-TKI therapy. Tumor size was identified as an independent prognostic factor for DFS (HR = 1.069; 95% CI, 1.012–1.128; P = 0.017), whereas adjuvant EGFR-TKI therapy showed no statistically significant effect (HR = 0.321; 95% CI, 0.098–1.046; P = 0.060).

Subgroup analysis of adjuvant EGFR-TKI therapy for DFS

As no DFS benefit from EGFR-TKI was observed in the overall cohort, we conducted subgroup analyses to identify patient groups that might benefit. Univariable Cox regression was performed for each subgroup, and the results are presented in a forest plot (Fig. 3). Subgroup analysis revealed that in the lobectomy subgroup, adjuvant EGFR-TKI therapy was marginally associated with improved DFS (HR = 0.14, 95%CI:0.02–1.02, P = 0.053), suggesting a potential trend that warrants further investigation.

Forest plot of subgroup analyses for the effect of adjuvant EGFR-TKI on DFS based on univariable Cox regression

Therefore, we performed a further analysis focusing on the lobectomy subgroup. Among these patients, EGFR-TKI therapy significantly improved the DFS (P = 0.024) (Fig. 2B). Univariable and multivariable Cox regression analyses were conducted. In the multivariable Cox proportional hazards model, both tumor size (HR = 1.071; 95% CI, 1.010–1.136; P = 0.024) and adjuvant EGFR-TKI therapy (HR = 0.124; 95% CI, 0.017–0.912; P = 0.040) were independent prognostic factors for DFS (Table 3).

Since EGFR mutation testing was not routinely performed in stage IA patients, to strengthen the causal inference regarding treatment efficacy, we compared DFS between the EGFR-TKI and non–EGFR-TKI groups among patients confirmed to be EGFR mutation-positive. No significant difference was observed between the sub-cohorts (P = 0.19) (Supplementary Fig. 1 A). However, among EGFR-positive patients who underwent lobectomy, EGFR-TKI therapy group exhibited significantly better DFS (P = 0.039) (Supplementary Fig. 1B). This result was consistent with our previous findings in the lobectomy subgroup analysis.

Patients undergoing sublobar resection and those receiving monotherapy were further analyzed. In the sublobar resection subgroup, no DFS benefit was conferred by EGFR-TKI therapy (P = 0.42; Fig. 2C). Similarly, no statistically significant differences in DFS were observed among the monotherapy groups (Fig. 2D).

Discussion

Evidence on the efficacy of postoperative EGFR-TKI therapy in stage IA lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) patients with high-risk pathological factors remains limited. A large randomized controlled trial, ADAURA, was conducted in which stage IB-IIIA LUAD patients with EGFR 19del mutation or 21L858R mutation were grouped in a 1:1 ratio and received either placebo or osimertinib targeted therapy for 3 years. The trial demonstrated a significant improvement in disease-free survival (DFS) in the osimertinib group, irrespective of prior adjuvant chemotherapy [5]. Only a few retrospective studies have explored the efficacy of EGFR-TKI therapy for stage I LUAD patients. Zhao et al. reported that adjuvant therapy, including EGFR-TKIs, significantly reduced recurrence and metastasis in completely resected stage I LUAD patients with high-risk pathological factors [18]. The results of Liu et al. suggested that EGFR-TKIs could serve as adjuvant postoperative treatment for selected high-risk stage I EGFR mutation-positive NSCLC patients [19]. The study of Jiang et al. also suggested that adjuvant EGFR-TKI might improve DFS and OS of stage IA and IB EGFR mutated NSCLC [20]. In these retrospective studies, stage IA and IB patients were analyzed together, with stage IB patients comprising the majority. Thus, whether genetic testing and EGFR-TKI therapy are necessary for stage IA patients remains insufficiently clarified. Regarding the efficacy of EGFR-TKIs in stage IA patients remains scarce, we specifically focused our study on this subgroup to address the gap.

Pathological risk factors such as solid growth pattern, micropapillary growth pattern, complex glandular pattern, LVI and STAS have been associated with poor prognosis in LUAD [21]. Therefore, this retrospective study specifically enrolled stage IA2-IA3 LUAD patients with high-risk pathological factors.

Our results indicated no significant difference in DFS between patients who received postoperative EGFR-TKI therapy and those who did not (P = 0.080), consistent with NCCN guidelines, which do not recommend EGFR-TKI therapy for stage IA patients [6]. In the subgroup analyses, EGFR-TKI appeared to have a potential impact on the prognosis of patients undergoing lobectomy. Further investigation of this subgroup showed that patients receiving postoperative EGFR-TKI therapy had significantly improved DFS (P = 0.024). Multivariate Cox regression analysis identified EGFR-TKI as an independent prognostic factor (HR = 0.124; 95% CI, 0.017–0.912; P = 0.040). Given the limited sample size, these results should be interpreted cautiously and considered hypothesis-generating, warranting confirmation in larger prospective studies.

In our study, patients who underwent sublobar resection did not benefit from adjuvant EGFR-TKI therapy, contrary to our expectations. This finding may be attributable to the limited sample size within this subgroup. Previous trials, including JCOG0802 and CALGB140503, demonstrated that sublobar resection was non-inferior to lobectomy for primary tumors measuring 2 cm or less in size [23, 24]. However, for tumors larger than 2 cm, sublobar resection may be inadequate. We initially anticipated that adjuvant EGFR-TKI therapy might serve as a valuable supplemental treatment for patients undergoing sublobar resection. Nevertheless, our data suggest that the adequacy of surgical resection may be a more critical determinant of prognosis, with the effect of postoperative EGFR-TKI therapy being relatively limited.

We also performed survival analyses for patients receiving monotherapy after lobectomy. No statistically significant differences in DFS were observed among the monotherapy groups. Notably, marginal effects were detected in comparisons involving EGFR-TKI (vs. chemotherapy, P = 0.066; vs. non-treatment, P = 0.056). Given the small sample size, these findings should be regarded as exploratory signals rather than conclusive evidence, highlighting the need for further investigation in adequately powered studies.

There are several limitations in this study. Firstly, given the nature of the study, which focuses on stage IA2-IA3 LUAD patients with high-risk pathological factors receiving adjuvant EGFR-TKI therapy as an off-label treatment, the sample size is limited. This limited cohort reduced the statistical power to detect significant differences, particularly in subgroup analyses, and increased the risk of type II error. Additionally, the potential impact of some confounding factors, including postoperative chemotherapy, was therefore not adequately investigated. Secondly, for stage IA patients, postoperative EGFR mutation testing is not routinely performed, leaving the EGFR mutation status unknown for part of individuals in non-EGFR-TKI group. Although the prognostic significance of EGFR mutation status remains controversial [23–27], the imbalance in mutation status between groups might weaken the causal inference regarding treatment efficacy. Additional analysis of EGFR mutation–positive patients were performed, which suggested that our main conclusions might be relatively independent of EGFR mutation status. Thirdly, since the follow-up time is limited, overall survival was not evaluated due to favorable prognosis in Stage IA patients. Longer follow-up remains necessary to provide a more complete assessment of long-term outcomes. Finally, our study is a single-center, retrospective analysis. The retrospective nature of the study may have led to unobserved confounding and selection bias between the two groups. Moreover, our study was limited to a Chinese population, thus the generalizability of these findings to global populations remains uncertain and requires validation in larger, multicenter studies.

In recent years, the efficacy of EGFR-TKIs in advanced NSCLC has been well established, and their application is increasingly extending to early-stage disease as adjuvant or neoadjuvant therapy [5]. Beyond EGFR, other driver alterations—such as KRAS, ALK, BRAF, MET, and HER2—as well as biomarkers reflecting the tumor immune microenvironment are increasingly recognized for their prognostic and predictive value [25–31]. Further studies are anticipated to advance personalized treatment and improve outcomes in NSCLC.

Conclusions

Generally, postoperative EGFR-TKI therapy might provide no significant clinical benefits for stage IA2-IA3 lung adenocarcinoma patients with high-risk pathological factors. However, in specific subgroup, such as patients receiving lobectomy, EGFR-TKI therapy shows potential benefit which requires further exploration in the future.
