Lived experiences of patients with First Episode Psychosis: A qualitative study from India
Authors: Neha Sharma, Swapnajeet Sahoo, Sandeep Grover
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Individuals with first episode psychosis (FEP) encounter both positive and negative life experiences during the early course of illness, yet these remain underexplored in the Indian context.Aim:To explore the lived experiences of patients with FEP by using qualitative methods.Methodology:Twenty patients with FEP in clinical remission were purposively sampled from a tertiary-care hospital in North India. Semi-structured in-depth interviews were audio-recorded, transcribed, translated, and thematically analyzed.Results:Analysis revealed eight themes and 28 sub-themes, including limited illness knowledge, causal attributions, acute episode experiences, treatment barriers, family impact, doctor–patient trust, stigma, and illness consequences.Conclusion:The study highlights early lived experiences of FEP patients in India, showing challenges of stigma, knowledge gaps, and treatment barriers, but also positive experiences of family and doctor support. These findings underscore the need for culturally tailored psycho-education and stigma-reduction strategies.

INTRODUCTION

Patients with first episode psychosis (FEP), defined as having a duration of illness less than 5 years,[1] have unique clinical and psychosocial needs.[23] However, qualitative studies exploring their lived experiences are limited. Existing research has focused on social processes such as identity formation, life perspectives, and difficulties in daily activities and relationships.[456] These studies have identified unique challenges faced by individuals with FEP and highlight the importance of qualitative methods in understanding the illness from the patient’s perspective.[7]

In phenomenological research, lived experiences refer to the subjective meanings people assign to their personal experiences and choices.[8] The goal is to understand these meanings rather than treat experiences as objective facts. Such experiences, whether positive or negative, influence both patients and caregivers and can affect treatment adherence. They also serve as important components of patient-reported outcome measures,[9] offering insights often missed by structured instruments.

Studies have reported various negative experiences among FEP patients and caregivers.[10] Some hospitalized individuals described their experience as distressing, with enforced treatment, loss of autonomy, and significant stigma.[11] Others, particularly young people with psychosis, have shared mixed experiences—positive aspects like access to services and peer support, and negative aspects such as hospitalization, insensitive staff, and medication side effects.[5]

Although several qualitative studies on FEP have been conducted in Western countries, findings may not be transferable to India, where cultural beliefs, stigma, and strong family involvement significantly influence illness perception and recovery. Only one recent Indian study included 10 patients and 10 caregivers, but with limited representation of non-affective psychosis.[12] Considering the paucity of research from India, this study aimed to generate in-depth insights into the lived experiences of patients with FEP in remission, with particular attention to causal explanations, experiences during acute episodes, treatment barriers, stigma, family relationships, and doctor–patient interactions.

By understanding how patients perceive their illness, its causes, the treatment process, stigma, and its impact on relationships and daily life, the study seeks to provide insights that can inform more culturally sensitive, and patient-centered care. The objectives were: (1) to explore patients’ knowledge and explanatory models of their illness, (2) to understand experiences during the acute episode, (3) to examine barriers to treatment and doctor–patient interactions, (4) to explore perceptions of stigma and family relationships, and (5) to assess the perceived consequences of the illness.

METHODOLOGY

It was a cross-sectional study and all the patients were assessed once after taking written informed consent. The Institute’s Ethics committee approved the study. The sample comprised of 20 patients with FEP, diagnosed with schizophrenia with total duration of illness less than 5 years. The study participants were recruited by purposive sampling from the outpatient services of the Department of Psychiatry at a tertiary–care, public-funded, multidisciplinary hospital in North India. The study sample was recruited during the time of January 2023 to December 2023.

To be included in the study, the patients were required to be aged between 18 and 50 years, diagnosed with schizophrenia as per Diagnostic and Statistical Manual criteria, fifth revision (DSM-5 criteria),[13] with duration of illness less than five years and currently in clinical remission as per the scale for assessment of positive symptoms (SAPS)[14] and scale for assessment of negative symptoms (SANS)[15] (item scores ≤2 on selected items), as defined by Andreasen et al., except for the 6 months duration criteria.[16] The upper age cut-off of 50 years was chosen to minimize recall inaccuracies. An illness duration ≤5 year is consistent with widely accepted definitions of FEP.[1] Patients were included only in remission to avoid distortion from active symptoms, and schizophrenia was selected to ensure homogeneity of diagnostic profile.

Patients with treatment resistance were excluded to ensure reflections were not confounded by ongoing severe psychopathology. This allowed the study to focus on experiences in remission. Those who were not in clinical remission, refusing consent to participate, and those with comorbid organic brain syndrome or intellectual disability were also excluded.

We recorded socio-demographic details and clinical details of each subject using a self-designed socio-demographic profile sheet and clinical profile sheet. The residual psychopathology was assessed on SAPS, SANS, and depressive symptoms (if any) using the Calgary depression scale for schizophrenia (CDSS).[17] To ascertain adherence to medications, we used the Medication Adherence Questionnaire (MAQ).[18]

For the qualitative part, in-depth interviews were conducted. A semi-structured interview guide, developed after literature review and expert consultation, was used and pilot tested on two patients. The guide covered domains such as illness perceptions, causal explanations, acute experiences, barriers to care, stigma, family relationships, and coping [Supplementary File 1]. The interview was conducted in the patient’s preferred language. The questions covered various areas (as per the study objectives): subject’s beliefs about their illness, access, and barriers to treatment, any culture-specific influences/explanations of experiences, perceived and experienced stigma, changes in their life (positive and negative), treatment perspectives, medication adherence, relationship with family, and so on. Additionally, the subjects were encouraged to talk/discuss their experiences freely apart from any of the above topics. Based on the responses, further questions were used to understand the lived experiences. All responses were audio recorded and transcribed.

Procedure: After taking written informed consent, the first author conducted all in-depth interviews, each lasting 45–60 min, face-to-face in a private consultation room. Participants were usually interviewed alone, although three preferred a caregiver’s presence. Interviews were conducted in Hindi or Punjabi, according to participant preference. All interviews were digitally audio-recorded, transcribed verbatim, and translated into English. Transcripts were cross-checked against recordings by the second and third authors to ensure accuracy. Data files were stored on password-protected devices with identifiers removed to maintain confidentiality. The first author carried out preliminary identification of themes, after that the second and third authors independently generated themes. Only themes agreed upon by all three investigators were retained for further analysis.

The first author (a psychiatrist) conducted all interviews but was not the primary treating clinician of any participant. Potential reflexivity related to the interviewer’s professional role was managed through independent coding, triangulation across investigators, and peer debriefing.

Data-analysis

For qualitative part

We employed Braun and Clarke’s thematic analysis framework, following established qualitative research standards. Coding was performed manually, without software, and investigator triangulation was employed to enhance reliability. Units of analysis included phrases, sentences, or paragraphs sufficient to derive a theme. Both explicit (manifest) and implicit (latent) meanings were identified. Codes were grouped into subcategories and then into broader themes, with rules for coding documented in memos. Coding proceeded iteratively from open to higher-order coding, and new themes were sought across the dataset until thematic saturation was reached, that is, till no new codes/themes emerged and the entire material was covered. Generation of themes were, thus, based on the grounded theory (constant comparative analysis). Twelve transcripts were independently coded by all three investigators, and inter-rater reliability was assessed using kappa statistics. Although member checking was not feasible, validity was supported through triangulation and peer debriefing.

For quantitative part

Data were analyzed using the IBM Statistical Package for Social Sciences (SPSS) for Windows, Version 20.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, N.Y., USA). Descriptive analysis was carried out for continuous variables and discontinuous socio-demographic and clinical variables.

RESULTS

As all participants had schizophrenia with illness duration <5 years and were in remission, the findings reflect experiences specific to the early phase of psychosis.

Socio-demographic profile of patients with first episode psychosis

The patient population comprised 20 20 FEP with mean duration of illness 29.65 (range: 6–50) months. The mean age of the study sample was 25.75 (standard deviation [SD]: 5.99) years. There were 7 males and 13 females participants. The average educational attainment among the participants was 14.65 (SD: 2.05) years. Occupational data revealed a diverse range of employment statuses among the participants. There were five students, eight individuals who were unemployed, and two homemakers. The remaining participants were engaged in various professions, including one postman, one analyst, one engineer, one lawyer, and a clerk. Majority of the study participants were currently single, unemployed, from middle socio-economic status, nuclear families, urban background, and Hindu by religion. Clinical profile of patients with first episode psychosis

The mean age of onset of illness was 23.25 (SD: 5.7) years and the mean duration of illness was 29.65 (SD: 14.38) months. About one-third (35%) of the patients had psychiatric co-morbidity in the lifetime, with depressive disorder being the most common, followed by anxiety disorder. More than one-third (40%) of the patients had family history of mental illness. More than half (55%) of the patients were on olanzapine and this was followed by risperidone (25%) and aripiprazole (20%). The mean MAQ score for the study sample was 3.85 (0.36), indicating good medication adherence as per the study design all the patients were in clinical remission and a few depressive symptoms, with none of the patients fulfilling the criteria of syndromal depression [Table 1].

All the patients had diagnosis of Schizophrenia. None of patients had treatment resistant schizophrenia and were in remission as per the SANS and SAPS global and composite scores [Table 1]. The mean CDSS was 0.20 (SD: 0.89), with none of the study participants having current depression as per the CDSS. The mean MAQ score for the study sample was 3.85 (0.36), with all the patients reporting good adherence. Analysis of the patients’ transcripts

Preliminary evaluation of all the transcripts showed eight themes and 29 sub-themes or codes [Figure 1].

Themes of lived experiences of patients with first episode schizophrenia

Theme 1: Illness knowledge/lack of knowledge

Theme 1 incorporated 3 sub-themes (a) limited knowledge about diagnosis, (b) symptom awareness and (c) understanding the role of medication. About the awareness about their diagnosis, only 6 (30%) patients were able to say they are suffering from schizophrenia, and the rest were either unable to say the name of the illness, said they did not know, or named it to be depression or nervous illness. When asked further to those who were able to say they had “Schizophrenia,” what they understood by it? only a few patients (n = 3) were able to say clearly that it is a psychotic illness in their own words, which suggested that they had understood the nature of the illness too. When asked about symptoms, 16 (80%) patients were able to tell their symptoms (cross-checked with the family members and the available treatment records), and the rest could not recall. Most of the patients said they used to feel stressed, had anxiety, unwanted thoughts, fearfulness, suspiciousness, disturbed sleep, and heard voices [Table 3]. About role of medications, only four patients were clearly able to articulate the role of medications.

Theme 2: Understanding of causes/precipitating factors

In this theme, the transcripts of the patients’ interviews reporting about causes of their illness were analyzed. Majority of the patients mentioned about “stress”/“tension” being the reason for their illness, others mentioned variety of causes such as excessive phone use, fever, and head injury. When inquired into more details, patients reported many factors which were categorized into two major sub-themes (a) stress-related causes and (b) environmental triggers. When asked to explain the stress-related causes, “what was the cause of the stress?” many patients reported stress due to work, examinations, studies, medical illness, and so on. Few patients also mentioned about environmental factors such as death of mother (patient 8), excessive phone usage (Patient 10, 18), fever (Patient 16), and disturbed family environment (Patient 17, Patient 20) [Table 3].

Theme 3: Acute episode experiences

This theme incorporated the transcripts of the patients’ interviews reporting about experiences during the acute episode. Majority of the patients (n = 17) were able to tell their experiences during the acute episode. Most of the patients reported their experience to be bad and traumatic; would have emotional breakdown and would cry or become angry; felt confused. The sub-themes incorporated to document the acute episode experiences were (a) Severe Anxiety (b) Paranoia Episodes (c) Mood Swings (d) Fear and Confusion, and (e) Hallucinations [Table 3].

Theme 4: Treatment barriers

Many patients reported that they visited faith healers, temples, and exorcists. Others reported about lack of support and medication discontinuation/adherence issues as the main barriers to treatment. Based on these experiences, this theme was divided into four sub-themes namely (a) Financial concerns, (b) Faith healing, (c) Lack of support and (d) Treatment discontinuation. About the financial concerns sub-theme, majority of the patients reported the cost of the treatment to be considerable. However, a few said they had no idea as their family members used to take care of the expenses and some said they had to face financial constraints for treatment. Few patients reported their family members visited faith-healers and practiced several rituals prior to seeking medical help. When asked about reasons for treatment discontinuation, most of the patients reported that they were not aware that they had to continue the medications or said they felt they have recovered or became normal, hence there was no need to continue treatment. Few patients also reported lack of support from family and relatives that led to treatment delay [Table 3].

Theme 5: Impact on family from patient’s perspective

With respect to impact of illness on the family, three sub-themes emerged, which were (a) Increased care and support from family members (b) Change in behavior of family and relatives and (c) Caregiver worry. Majority of the patients (80%) reported positive support and care from their family members. They reported extra care and concern, significant change in the behavior of their parents that is, less critical and more supportive and tolerating their aggressive/bad behavior. However, few felt the behavior of their relatives and family members to be less supportive and patients suffered physical abuse during the acute episode. When asked about if their family members worry about them, few patients reported that their parents worry excessively about future outcome such as marriage perspectives, and staying independently [Table 3].

Theme 6: Doctor-patient relationship

This theme incorporated the excerpts from the patients’ transcripts that have reported about behavior of healthcare professionals. When asked about behavior of health care professionals, two patients reported about trust in doctors, six patients reported about the effective communication during follow-up visits, 13 patients reported about satisfaction from treatment, and six patients talked about the support they received from the doctors. These were categorized into three sub-themes that is: (a) Trust and support from doctors, (b) Effective communication, and (c) Treatment satisfaction.

Theme 7: Encountering stigma

The transcripts of the patient’s interviews reporting about stigma were analyzed, and the stigma faced due to illness was explored in detail by asking leading questions. About the stigma faced due to the illness, six patients denied facing any stigma, 12 patients reported facing self-stigma, 11 patients reported of facing public stigma and three patients reported of facing family stigma. These were categorized into three sub-themes namely (a) Self-Stigma—feeling shame and embarrassment, (b) Public stigma, and (c) Family stigma. Patients reported feeling stigmatized and embarrassed due to their illness. Many reported they had worries regarding future relapse(s), marriage prospects and were not able to tolerate the fact that they had a psychotic breakdown and were out of their control. About public stigma, one–third of the patients reported their family had kept their illness as private and had not disclosed to anyone. Another one-third of the patients reported of feeling discriminated at work place or being mocked at work place due to their illness. Few also reported that they felt demotivated and stigmatized by the words/conversation of their friends and neighbors who distanced them after knowing the nature of the illness. One-fourth of the patients also reported about feeling stigmatized due to the behavior of their family members. Concerns about marriage and future family life were expressed more frequently by female participants.

Theme 8: Consequences of the illness from patient’s perspectives

This theme incorporated all the excerpts where the patients reported about the consequences of illness or how the illness had affected/impacted them. This theme had four sub-themes namely (a) Impact on Education (b) Career disruption (c) Strain in relationships and (d) practicing religious coping. Half of the patients (n = 10) reported that their studies were affected significantly due to the illness, one-third reported that the illness affected their career, as some lost their job, or has disruption in delivering their duties at workplace leading to problems at work place, one-fourth of patients reported effect on their social relations and few patients (n = 3) reported of practicing religious methods to cope with the distress due to illness [Table 3].

DISCUSSION

The present study aimed to explore the lived experiences of patients with FEP, using a qualitative research methodology. The study sample was limited to patients in clinical remission. Most of the earlier studies mention the diagnosis of the subjects to be psychosis or first episode schizophrenia, and hence we can say that our study sample was similar to previous studies.[1920] Further, clinical remission was usually not mentioned in the previous studies and most of the studies had not provided any information about current severity of psychopathology on any standardized rating scale. Therefore, the present study benefit from the fact that the study subjects were in clinical remission.

Themes regarding lived experiences of patients with first episode psychosis

The semi-structured interviews and thematic analysis of transcripts from patients with FEP revealed eight major themes and 28 sub-themes. Several themes, including career disruption, impact on studies, relationship strain, and use of religious coping, reflect aspects of personal recovery in FEP, consistent with existing recovery literature.

The first two themes—knowledge about diagnosis and understanding of causality—highlighted patients’ limited awareness. Many did not know their diagnosis or had fragmented understanding of their illness and symptoms. Only a few recognized the role of medications. These findings are consistent with prior research, which found that patients with FEP often fail to make sense of their experience, affecting treatment engagement and illness management.[51021]

Stress, anxiety, and environmental triggers (e.g. loneliness, bereavement, insomnia, physical illnesses, and excessive mobile phone use) were commonly cited causes. These findings align with prior studies where patients offered diverse explanations for their illness, ranging from substance use to spiritual factors.[2223] Cultural context played a key role in shaping these beliefs, as seen in Indian studies where many patients described their condition vaguely as a “rare illness” needing long-term treatment.[12]

During acute episodes, patients reported fear, confusion, paranoia, anxiety, and auditory hallucinations, describing the experience as traumatic. These results reflect previous studies that reported intense emotional disturbances and altered perceptions during psychosis.[212224] This suggests that clinicians should provide emotional support and psycho-education about the transient nature of symptoms, and monitor for post-traumatic responses after remission.

Barriers to treatment included financial difficulties, treatment discontinuation, lack of awareness, and reliance on faith healing. These are well-documented issues in both Indian and international studies.[4512252627] Patients often sought psychiatric care only after exhausting informal options. Medication non-adherence, due to perceived recovery or lack of awareness, was also noted—an issue highlighted in other FEP research.[28] Early intervention programs with psychoeducation and emotive listening are essential to address these barriers.

Family dynamics were significantly impacted. While most patients reported improved relationships post-illness, some experienced overconcern and negative expressed emotions. These findings echo earlier research on the caregiving burden and emotional toll on families.[1029] Clinicians should recognize and address caregivers’ needs during treatment.

The study emphasized the importance of trust and communication in doctor-patient relationships. Most patients expressed satisfaction due to feeling heard and experiencing symptom relief. These results support earlier findings that strong therapeutic relationships enhance treatment adherence and outcomes.[2229]

Stigma was another key theme. Patients experienced self-stigma (shame, social withdrawal), public stigma (discrimination, jokes), and family stigma (negative attitudes). These forms of stigma affected help-seeking, social integration, and self-esteem. Concerns about marriage and future family life were expressed more frequently by female participants. Multiple studies corroborate these effects, stressing that stigma is a major barrier in FEP care.[24122430] Public education and stigma-reduction campaigns should be integral to early intervention services. FEP also disrupted patients’ studies, employment, and social relationships. Many felt they had lost out on age-normative roles, consistent with prior findings. Some patients used religious coping strategies—also reported in previous quantitative studies.[3132] Early diagnosis and social reintegration must be central goals in FEP management.

Compared to studies in chronic schizophrenia, participants with FEP placed greater emphasis on acute traumatic experiences, recovery hope, and heightened family over-concern. Chronic cases, in contrast, often highlight long-term disability, enduring stigma, and caregiver burnout. Key findings of the present study include poor illness knowledge, stress-related causal attributions, traumatic acute experiences, reliance on faith healing, significant stigma, but also positive family support and trust in doctors. These findings suggest various practical implications such as the need for culturally adapted psychoeducation, stigma-reduction interventions, and family-inclusive care in early psychosis programs. Mental health professionals should be trained in patient-centered care, empathy, and culturally competent communication to enhance satisfaction and outcomes.

Strengths of this study include its in-depth qualitative design, systematic analysis, and context-specific insights into lived experiences of patients with FEP in remission. Limitations include the small, cross-sectional sample and the predominance of urban, relatively educated participants, reflecting the catchment of a tertiary-care hospital; hence, perspectives from rural, or less literate populations may be underrepresented. Exclusion of treatment-resistant patients as well as non-remitters also limits the range of experiences captured. Translation may have led to subtle loss of meaning, and reflexivity remains a consideration given the first author’s role as interviewer. In addition, the absence of member checking may affect credibility. These factors may restrict transferability of findings.

CONCLUSION

In conclusion, this study provides a comprehensive understanding of the early lived experiences of patients with FEP in India. It highlights challenges like poor illness awareness, stigma, and treatment barriers, alongside positive aspects such as family support and therapeutic relationships. Future research should focus on longitudinal studies and culturally tailored interventions to support patients through their recovery journey.
