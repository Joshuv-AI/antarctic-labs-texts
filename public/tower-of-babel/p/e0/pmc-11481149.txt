Status and influencing factors of knowledge, attitudes and practices relating to screening for breast and cervical cancer among rural women aged 40–65 years in China: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Yuexia Liao, Linfei Ye, Qian Cai, Huizi Song, Yuqiu Zhao, Xingchen Shang, Ting Tian
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesThe objectives are to investigate the status of knowledge, attitudes and practices (KAP) and to identify factors related to KAP towards breast and cervical cancer screening among rural Chinese women aged 40–65 years.Design and settingThis cross-sectional study was conducted from July to September 2020 in rural areas of eastern China.ParticipantsThis study involved 301 rural women aged 40–65 years.Variables and outcomesThe questionnaire included demographic information and the KAP related to breast and cervical cancer screening. Multivariate linear regression analysis was used for the multifactor analysis.ResultsA total of 301 rural women aged 40–65 years participated in the survey. The overall score of KAP was (62.41±11.24), and the scores of each KAP domain relating to screening for breast and cervical cancer among participants in rural areas were (1.82±0.97), (44.52±7.20) and (16.06±4.56), respectively. The following factors were significantly associated with the total KAP Score relating to breast and cervical cancer screening among rural women aged 40–65 years, explaining 35% of the variance (p<0.001): aged 50–54 (β=−0.160, 95% CI −7.403 to −0.794), aged 55–59 (β=−0.266, 95% CI −10.763 to −3.757), aged 60–65 (β=−0.243, 95% CI −12.504 to −4.331), junior high school (β=0.186, 95% CI 1.287 to 7.064), housewife (β=0.172, 95% CI 0.849 to 6.868), other work (β=0.171, 95% CI 3.818 to 15.044), annual household income ranging from 50 000 to 79 999 (β=0.281, 95% CI 3.887 to 10.458) and annual household income≥80 000 (β=0.330, 95% CI 4.735 to 11.554).ConclusionsThe status of KAP related to breast and cervical cancer screening still requires improvement. Measures to enhance the participation of the two-cancer screening should be provided to rural women aged between 50 and 65 years with lower education level and lower annual household income, especially women who are farmers.

Introduction

Breast and cervical cancer are among the most common cancers and leading causes of death in women.1 In China, the incidence rates of breast and cervical cancer are 79 per 100 000 and 11.78 per 100 000, respectively.2 3 Breast cancer ranks first in incidence among female malignancies in China, while cervical cancer ranks sixth.4 5 The mortality rates for breast and cervical cancer are on an upward trend, particularly in women aged 40–65 years, which places a heavy burden on public health.6 However, when breast and cervical cancer are diagnosed at an early stage, the incidence and mortality of these cancers can be reduced.7 Therefore, early detection and timely treatment are essential.8

The key to early detection is screening.8 Previous studies have confirmed that early screening, as effective secondary prevention, plays a crucial role in the early detection, diagnosis and treatment of breast and cervical cancer, thereby reducing cancer-related deaths.911 The WHO has recommended promoting early screening worldwide, a suggestion many countries have positively embraced.3 Due to the increased risk of breast and cervical cancer in women aged 40–65 years, China has included screening for these cancers in the national public health services programme since 2009.12 Unfortunately, despite the government’s emphasis on the importance of screening for breast and cervical cancer, participation in early screening in China remains relatively low, especially in rural regions, with participation rates varying from 33.2% to 63.7%.1315 In contrast, some developed countries have achieved relatively high coverage of early screening.16 17 Thanks to screening, the incidence and mortality of breast and cervical cancer in these countries have significantly decreased.16 17 Therefore, more attention should be paid to improving breast and cervical cancer screening among high-risk groups in China.

Evidence indicates that a lack of knowledge about screening for breast and cervical cancer can affect attitudes towards screening and attendance for these cancers.18 A previous study conducted in Jining reported that approximately half of the participants had a low level of knowledge about screening for breast and cervical cancer.13 Women with inadequate knowledge believed there was no need for early screening without signs and symptoms, resulting in low participation in screening for these cancers.18 19 Moreover, attitudes towards screening for breast and cervical cancer play a crucial role in participation.13 Therefore, identifying factors related to knowledge, attitudes and practices (KAP) towards screening for breast and cervical cancer should be the focus of current studies, as this may contribute to increasing the participation rate in screening.

Previous research indicated that KAP regarding screening for breast and cervical cancer is affected by multiple factors such as age, level of education and income.13 The findings of these studies are limited. The factors related to KAP for screening are complex, involving multiple aspects such as the physical and social environment.20 Hence, it is essential to explore more factors associated with KAP for breast and cervical cancer screening to provide more targeted guidance on increasing participation. Moreover, there is a shortage of substantial evidence on factors that influence the screening of KAP in rural women aged 40–65 years, especially in regions of eastern China. This study aimed to investigate the status of KAP and identify factors related to KAP towards breast and cervical cancer screening among rural Chinese women aged 40–65 years to provide theoretical support to increase adherence to screening for breast and cervical cancer in rural regions.

Methods

Study design and participants

This cross-sectional study was conducted from July 2020 to September 2020 in rural areas of eastern China. Participants were eligible if they met the following inclusion criteria: (1) married women aged 40–65 years; (2) permanent residents of rural areas (defined by the National Bureau of Statistics,21 with their registered household of origin in rural areas and having lived there for >12 months); (3) clear consciousness and the ability to communicate with the investigators; and (4) informed consent and participation in the study. Women were excluded if they were pregnant, lactating or had malignant tumours or life-threatening diseases.

Sampling method and sample size

A multistage cluster sampling method was used to recruit participants for this study. In stage 1, Jiangsu province was selected as the first-level unit and divided into three regions: Northern Jiangsu, Central Jiangsu and Southern Jiangsu. In stage 2, a county/district was randomly sampled from each selected region. In stage 3, two villages were randomly sampled from each selected county/district, resulting in six villages.

Based on the required sample size, we contacted the local village committee and, with their guidance, entered each household. Investigators invited eligible women to participate in the study. Before the investigation, participants were informed of the study’s objective and significance. They were given a questionnaire only after providing written informed consent (online supplemental material 1). Participation was entirely voluntary, with no incentives involved. Four nursing undergraduates and two nursing postgraduates trained by the first author were responsible for data collection. The questionnaires were in paper form, and the investigators instructed participants face-to-face using uniform guidelines. After completion, all questionnaires were immediately inspected to identify any missing data. These surveys were anonymous, and no personal privacy information was collected.

According to the rough estimation method proposed by Fang,22 the minimum sample size should be 5–10 times the number of variables in the study. This study includes 22 variables. Considering a 20% dropout rate, the sample size should be between 132 and 264.

Outcome measures

The survey consisted of two sections: individual characteristics and KAP of breast and cervical cancer screening. The questionnaires are provided in online supplemental material 2 .

The individual characteristics questionnaire included demographic information (eg, age, level of education, place of residence, occupation, income and mode of medical payment) and cancer-related information (eg, family history and access to knowledge).

The KAP questionnaire was designed based on a literature review on breast and cervical cancer screening.23 24 The reliability and validity of the questionnaire were evaluated by two chief gynaecologists, two associate chief nurses in gynaecological nursing and one associate professor of nursing. These experts assessed the accuracy and appropriateness of the items and domains related to KAP for breast and cervical cancer screening. They rated the relevance of each item from 1 to 4, with higher scores indicating better relevance. When narrative comments were provided, necessary modifications were made. Quantitative analysis determined content validity, and the items were revised until the final content validity index reached 0.8, indicating good reliability. Subsequently, a pilot study was conducted with 30 married women in rural areas of Yangzhou to test the flexibility and practicality of the questionnaire. The participants in the pilot study were not included in the primary study sample. The knowledge domain included four items: information related to breast and cervical cancer, the best time for screening, how to perform breast self-examination and effective ways to detect cervical cancer. One point was awarded for each correct answer, and 0 was for the wrong answer, resulting in a score range of 0–4, with higher scores indicating better knowledge. The attitude domain included 11 items, such as awareness of the importance of screening and recognition of the benefits of early screening. This domain used a 5-point Likert scale, with scores ranging from 5 to 55. Items 1, 4, 5 and 6 were reverse scored, while the others were scored in the forward direction. The practice domain included five items using a 5-point Likert scale, with scores ranging from 5 to 25. The total KAP Score ranged from 10 to 84, with higher scores indicating better KAP levels. Cronbach’s alpha coefficients for the total questionnaire and the KAP domains were 0.889, 0.834, 0.841 and 0.828, respectively. The overall Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin measure of sampling adequacy was 0.863,25 and Bartlett’s Test for Sphericity was significant (p<0.001), indicating interrelated variables.26

Statistical analysis

The data were analysed using SPSS V.26.0. Continuous variables were summarised as the mean±SD if normally distributed and as the median if not. Categorical variables were expressed as frequencies and percentages. An independent sample t-test or one-way analysis of variance was used to compare differences in KAP and total KAP scores. Pearson’s correlation analysis was performed to analyse correlations between continuous variables. Independent variables with p<0.05 in the univariate analysis were then entered into a multivariate linear regression model. Multivariate linear regression analysis explored significant factors influencing KAP and total KAP scores. P value<0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Patient and public involvement

No patients were involved in setting the research question or the outcome measures, nor were they involved in developing plans for design or implementation of the study.

Discussion

Screening for breast and cervical cancer is an effective measure to reduce mortality from these cancers and improve women’s health. Consistent with the study conducted by Zheng et al,27 our study found that the overall KAP Score was low and needed improvement. Although rural women aged between 40 and 65 years held a positive attitude towards screening, they lacked knowledge about the procedures and showed inactive participation in early screening. A similar study in Zimbabwe by Makurirofa et al also found that while respondents had an optimistic attitude towards cancer screening, their knowledge and participation were generally low.28 In our study, respondents considered annual screening for breast and cervical cancer essential and believed it could effectively aid early detection, diagnosis and treatment. However, they primarily lacked knowledge about performing breast self-examinations, the specifics of screening programmes and the appropriate frequency for these screenings. These findings suggest that medical professionals should provide sufficient and detailed information on self-examination methods and the benefits and frequency of cancer-related screenings to rural women aged 40–65 years.

Our results showed a significant positive correlation between KAP, indicating that increased knowledge about screening led to more engagement and a more positive attitude towards screening. This aligns with studies from Tanzania and Britain.29 30 Previous studies have shown that an adequate explanation of breast and cervical cancer screening is crucial for women, helping them decide to undergo early screening.8 Therefore, enhancing knowledge about screening is necessary, particularly for rural women aged 40–65 years with positive attitudes, to increase their participation in early screenings. However, the information rural women received about screening was insufficient due to the inadequate capability of health institutions to provide screenings and a lack of emphasis on early screening publicity.8 A systematic review of cancer screening interventions reported that a combination of written information and one-to-one discussions had a more significant impact on women’s awareness of early screening than receiving booklets alone.31 Thus, it is urgent to train medical professionals on screening and to use various methods to enhance screening education among rural women aged 40–65 years, such as WeChat, television, brochures and health lectures. Increasing face-to-face communication between rural women and healthcare professionals is also crucial for enhancing knowledge about early screening. New technologies such as the internet and artificial intelligence can also optimise screening services.

As indicated by this study’s results, age was a factor related to KAP in screening the two cancers. Older women were more unwilling to participate in the screening, consistent with previous studies.32 33 Influenced by Chinese culture, older rural women often feel invulnerable to breast and cervical cancer, leading them to avoid screening in the absence of signs and symptoms, which contrasts with other cultural groups.18 Modesty and embarrassment during screening were also key barriers to older rural women’s engagement.34 Additionally, the lack of energy, limited transportation options and greater travel distances to healthcare sites faced by older rural women contribute to the underutilisation of early screening.35 Hence, the government should focus on enhancing the construction of clinics in rural areas, particularly facilities for screening the two cancers. Moreover, flexible screening organisation methods such as mobile service vans can facilitate nearby screenings for rural women. Psychological barriers such as embarrassment can be improved by changing rural women’s perceptions of the screening.36 A systematic review by Musa et al reported that implementing theory-based cancer educational interventions could enhance women’s participation in cancer screening programs.37 Thus, it is necessary to focus on education about screening for the two cancers. Additionally, increasing the proportion of female physicians can reduce the embarrassment of the eligible population during the screening procedure to some extent.

Education level was also considered a factor influencing KAP screening for the two cancers, aligning with the results reported by Wong.38 Rural women aged 40–65 years with lower education levels had insufficient knowledge of screening.38 This population had difficulties understanding the knowledge about screening for the two cancers and required additional support to grasp information about early screening. Moreover, some evidence has confirmed that a lack of knowledge weakens the role of capability, causing negative engagement in early screening.38 39 Inadequate knowledge can be a barrier to identifying the symptoms of the two cancers, leading to a delay in early detection.3 In China, many rural women aged 40–65 years believe they have a low risk of breast and cervical cancer.3 Therefore, it is urgent for authorities, particularly the government, to enhance the publicity of knowledge related to the two cancers among the eligible population. The publicity process should focus on plain explanations rather than technical terms for women with low education levels.

The annual household income was identified as a factor influencing the KAP of rural women aged 40–65 years regarding screening for the two cancers. Women with higher annual household incomes demonstrated better KAP than those with lower incomes, aligning with findings from a study conducted in Indiana.40 Ahmed et al highlighted that women with low incomes often perceive a cancer diagnosis following screening as imposing a significant financial burden on their families, which deters their participation in screening.41 Conversely, higher income levels alleviate financial and psychological burdens associated with screening, resulting in more positive attitudes and practices regarding screening.38 Additionally, women with higher incomes typically have greater access to screening knowledge due to adequate financial support. Therefore, the government should prioritise improving screening-related KAP among rural women with low annual household incomes and provide them with necessary assistance. Encouraging subsidies related to cancer treatment can also alleviate the financial burden of self-payment for this population. Furthermore, enhancing basic public health services and promoting social capital investment can facilitate the implementation of screening for the two cancers.

Employment status was also found to influence the KAP towards early screening. In our study, employed women exhibited higher KAP scores for screening the two cancers than female farmers. Employed women often have access to physical examinations organised by their workplaces42 and more opportunities to obtain screening information through interactions with colleagues,43 leading to increased screening adherence for the two cancers. Therefore, it is necessary to encourage workplaces to organise free medical examinations for employees. Moreover, farmers in China generally belong to a lower socioeconomic level and have lower education levels, resulting in low engagement in screening for the two cancers.38 For this population, it is necessary to develop appropriate publicity and educational materials to help them understand national policies related to screening for the two cancers, thereby encouraging their voluntary participation in screening services.

The findings of our study revealed that knowledge acquired through the internet facilitates engaging in screening practices for breast and cervical cancer. Women who accessed information online demonstrated higher levels of engagement in screening practices. Based on smart terminals, disseminating screening-related information to rural women has opened up a new pathway.44 A previous study by Han et al demonstrated the benefits of internet-based health education in enhancing communication between rural women and healthcare professionals, ultimately improving the quality of life for patients with cancer.45 However, there remains a significant gap in internet access to health-related knowledge among women of different ages from different regions.46 Therefore, to enhance participation in screening among rural women, healthcare providers must use the internet and mobile devices to deliver tailored health-related online materials, considering variations in age and regional disparities. This approach contributes to improving awareness and self-management of breast and cervical cancer among rural women.

Our study offers valuable insights into the current KAP regarding screening for breast and cervical cancer among rural women aged 40–65 years, emphasising the necessity for educational programmes and the utilisation of mobile devices based on the internet for this demographic. Despite the significant contributions of our study, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, we used a self-reported questionnaire to investigate KAP associated with breast and cervical cancer screening, which may be subject to bias and individual interpretation. Future studies could consider using more objective measurement tools. Second, our study design was cross-sectional, precluding the establishment of causational relationships between variables. Third, our participants were recruited from rural regions of eastern China, potentially limiting the generalisability of our findings to other regions. Future research should involve larger sample sizes and more diverse geographic locations to enhance the generalisability of the results. Last, using a multistage cluster sampling method may have resulted in an unbalanced sample distribution, potentially limiting the representativeness of our findings.

Conclusion

In our study, the status of KAP related to screening for breast and cervical cancer still needs improvement. KAP and total KAP were influenced by age, education level, employment status, annual household income and knowledge acquired through the internet. The findings indicate the necessity of enhancing awareness and education on screening for these cancers through brochures and experience sharing, mainly targeting rural women, especially those who are older and have lower education levels. The government should provide adequate financial support for rural women aged 40–65 years who have low incomes and are farmers. Additionally, increasing internet technology and mobile devices to deliver targeted health-related promotional materials could help improve participation in screening programmes. This approach may significantly boost participation among rural women aged 40–65 years.
