Myopia prevalence among primary school students across different districts in Guangzhou: insights from a large-scale school-based screening
Authors: Xiaoxiao Zhang, Junzhe Wang, Yicui Weng, Can Chen, Huixin Jiang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
PurposeTo investigate the prevalence of myopia among primary school students in Guangzhou and examine variations by district, grade level, gender, and parental engagement.MethodsA total of 173,627 students from Grades 1–6 across 151 schools underwent vision screening between January and June 2024 in three districts of Guangzhou—Yuexiu (central urban core), Panyu (southern urban–rural fringe), and Huadu (northern rapidly urbanizing area). Uncorrected visual acuity and non-cycloplegic autorefraction were measured. Myopia was defined as uncorrected visual acuity < 5.0 and spherical equivalent refraction ≤ − 0.50 D.ResultsThe overall prevalence of myopia was 36.97%. Significant regional differences were observed: 37.67% in Yuexiu, 32.56% in Panyu, and 38.92% in Huadu (p < 0.001). Female students consistently showed higher prevalence than males in all districts (p < 0.001). A clear trend of escalating myopia prevalence with advancing grade level, especially around the transition from lower to upper primary grades. Parental query rates were highest in Panyu (62.13%), followed by Yuexiu (53.34%) and Huadu (47.06%) (p < 0.05).ConclusionDistrict-level disparities in myopia prevalence were identified, underscoring the importance of region-specific considerations in future prevention strategies.

Introduction

Myopia has become a major global public health concern, particularly in East Asia, where its prevalence has risen dramatically over the past decades. Recent epidemiological projections estimate that nearly 50% of the world’s population will be affected by myopia by 2050, with approximately 10% experiencing high myopia and its associated complications [1]. In East Asian countries, including China, South Korea, Singapore, and Japan, prevalence among adolescents already exceeds 80%, which is substantially higher than reported in Europe and North America [1–3].

In China, the increasing prevalence of myopia among school-aged children has drawn significant national attention due to its long-term implications for vision health and economic burden. Large-scale surveys indicate that more than half of Chinese children aged 7–18 years are myopic [4, 5]. Multiple environmental and behavioral factors, such as intensive academic demands, prolonged near work, limited outdoor activity, and greater screen exposure, particularly during the COVID-19 pandemic are believed to contribute to this trend [6]. In response, the Chinese government has launched nationwide initiatives to mitigate myopia progression, including integrating prevention into health strategies, encouraging outdoor activity, and promoting school-based vision programs [4]. Significant regional disparities exist in myopia prevalence across China, influenced by variations in socioeconomic development, educational policies, and lifestyle factors. Urban centers such as Beijing, Shanghai, and Shenzhen consistently report higher prevalence compared with less urbanized regions, a pattern often attributed to more intensive academic demands and fewer opportunities for outdoor activity [7–9]. These disparities highlight the need for region-specific interventions tailored to local educational and environmental contexts. Against this background, the present study investigates the prevalence of myopia among primary school students in different districts of Guangzhou, China, with attention to variations by grade level, gender, school type, and parental engagement. Clarifying these differences may inform targeted strategies to slow myopia progression and improve vision health among school-aged children.

Methods

Study population

This cross-sectional study targeted primary school students from Grades 1 to 6 across three districts in Guangzhou, China: Yuexiu (central urban core), Panyu (southern urban–rural fringe), and Huadu (northern rapidly urbanizing area). The selected schools represented diverse socioeconomic backgrounds and varying levels of urbanization to ensure demographic representativeness. The vision screening program was conducted between January and June 2024. To maximize participation, students absent during the initial screening were invited to undergo assessments at affiliated eye care facilities. Inclusion criteria included enrollment in participating schools and completion of both distance visual acuity testing and autorefraction with parental consent. Exclusion criteria were: (1) presence of organic ocular disease or a history of ocular surgery/trauma affecting vision; (2) active ocular inflammation or infection at the time of screening; and (3) incomplete or unreliable data (e.g., uncorrected VA and refraction missing after repeat attempts, or implausible refraction values). Children under ongoing myopia control interventions (atropine therapy, orthokeratology, or defocus lenses) were not excluded, but were documented separately during screening to reflect real-world prevalence.

Vision screening procedures

The vision screenings were conducted within school premises, using large classrooms or auditoriums to provide a standardized testing environment. The screening teams comprised experienced ophthalmologists and certified optometrists trained in pediatric vision assessments. All personnel underwent standardized training before the program to ensure consistency in procedures, equipment handling, and data recording. Visual acuity was assessed monocularly with the Standard Logarithmic Visual Acuity Chart, recorded in decimal notation following standard protocols. Students were instructed to identify the direction of optotypes, beginning with the right eye, while the contralateral eye was occluded. Objective refractive error and corneal curvature measurements were obtained using an autorefractor with an integrated keratometer (Topcon KR-800, Topcon Corporation, Tokyo, Japan). The device provided automated assessments of spherical equivalent refraction (SER) and corneal curvature. Three consecutive measurements were taken for each eye, and the average value was used for analysis. Cycloplegia was not administered in this school-based program due to operational and safety considerations. Objective refraction was therefore measured without cycloplegia. For students identified with abnormal vision or suspected myopia, parents were referred to designated eye care facilities for cycloplegic refraction, and the post-cycloplegia results were reported back to schools for follow-up statistics.

Definition of myopia and data processing

The criteria for defining myopia in this study were based on the guidelines established by the National Health Commission of China. Myopia was classified as an uncorrected visual acuity of less than 5.0 and SER of −0.50D or lower under non-cycloplegic autorefraction. Students confirmed to be wearing orthokeratology (OK) lenses were recorded and included in the “myopic” group for prevalence estimates, since OK lenses are prescribed only for established myopia. However, these students were excluded from analyses requiring numerical SER values (e.g., mean refraction by grade). A sensitivity analysis excluding OK wearers from the prevalence denominator was also performed to test robustness. To ensure the accuracy of the data, students with visual impairment resulting from organic ocular diseases or injuries were excluded from the analysis, as these conditions are not directly related to refractive myopia. Each eye was measured three times; if the SD exceeded 0.50 D, additional attempts were made. Implausible values (SER < − 10.00 D or > + 6.00 D) were flagged and rechecked. If valid data were available from only one eye, the fellow eye was used for classification (person-level rule: myopia present if either eye met criteria). Participants missing both eyes’ data were excluded and documented in the study flow diagram. Only students who met the eligibility criteria following data cleaning and validation were included in the final analysis. To maintain measurement accuracy, all equipment was calibrated daily before use, with automated range checks and regular quality control by senior supervisors to ensure data integrity.

Feedback and follow-up procedures

A structured feedback system was implemented to ensure effective communication of screening results to schools and parents. Individual reports were generated for each student, including visual acuity, refractive status, and recommendations for further care such as referrals for comprehensive eye examinations or optical correction. These reports were distributed through printed documents and electronic platforms accessible via personalized QR codes, allowing parents to access results conveniently. To reduce missed assessments due to absenteeism, free follow-up screenings were offered at designated eye care facilities.

Educational and awareness activities

Following the screenings, educational sessions were conducted to improve awareness of myopia prevention and management among students, parents, and teachers. These sessions were delivered either online via interactive webinars or offline in school classrooms or auditoriums. Content focused on interpreting screening reports, best practices for visual hygiene (e.g., maintaining appropriate reading distances and regulating screen time), the importance of outdoor activity, and correcting common misconceptions about myopia and optical interventions. Interactive Q&A segments were included to actively engage participants and reinforce adherence to recommended eye health practices.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software (version 26.0, IBM Corporation, Armonk, NY, USA). Descriptive statistics were used to summarize demographic characteristics and myopia prevalence rates. Differences in categorical variables, including myopia prevalence across districts, grade levels, and genders, were assessed using chi-square tests. To evaluate the association between parental engagement, measured by access rates to screening reports, and myopia prevalence, Pearson’s correlation analysis was conducted. A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant for all analyses.

Results

Overview of screening coverage

A total of 173,627 primary school students from Grades 1 through 6 were screened across 151 schools in three districts of Guangzhou (Yuexiu, Panyu, and Huadu) between January and June 2024. The distribution of participants was 78,522 students from 53 schools in Yuexiu, 14,933 students from 11 schools in Panyu, and 80,172 students from 87 schools in Huadu. The mean age was 8.9 ± 1.7 years (range: 6–13 years). The overall prevalence of myopia was 36.97%. A progressive myopic shift in SER was observed with increasing grade level. Mean SER (± SD) by grade was: Grade 1, − 0.00 ± 0.82 D; Grade 2, − 0.28 ± 1.06 D; Grade 3, − 0.30 ± 1.41 D; Grade 4, − 0.89 ± 1.37 D; Grade 5, − 1.26 ± 1.61 D; and Grade 6, − 1.39 ± 1.68 D. Of the total participants, 52.5% were male (n = 93,977) and 47.5% were female (n = 79,650). The prevalence of myopia did not differ between key and non-key schools in Yuexiu District (37.71% vs. 37.67%, p = 0.965), but significant differences were observed in Panyu (34.47% vs. 32.17%, p < 0.001) and Huadu (36.24% vs. 38.96%, p < 0.001). Parental query rates also varied by district, being highest in Panyu (62.13%), followed by Yuexiu (53.34%) and Huadu (47.06%) (p < 0.05).

Regional differences in myopia prevalence

The prevalence of myopia differed significantly among the three districts (χ² = 217.80, p < 0.001). Yuexiu District recorded a prevalence of 37.67%, Panyu the lowest at 32.56%, and Huadu the highest at 38.92%. These inter-district differences were statistically significant (p < 0.001). The findings suggest notable geographic variation in myopia burden, which may reflect underlying regional influences. The distribution of prevalence across districts is shown in Fig. 1.

Gender-based differences in myopia prevalence

Significant sex-related differences in myopia prevalence were observed across all three districts (p < 0.001 for all comparisons, Fig. 2). In Yuexiu District, prevalence was 35.53% in males versus 40.10% in females; in Panyu, 30.57% versus 34.83%; and in Huadu, 35.52% versus 43.09%. Across all regions, females consistently exhibited a higher prevalence of myopia than males.

Myopia prevalence by grade level

In Yuexiu District, myopia prevalence increased from 11.33% in Grade 1 to 65.08% in Grade 6; in Panyu, from 8.74% to 60.33%; and in Huadu, from 15.03% to 66.45% (Fig. 3). All inter-grade comparisons were statistically significant (p < 0.001). The most pronounced increases occurred between Grades 3 and 4 in Panyu and Huadu, and between Grades 4 and 5 in Panyu (all p < 0.001). These results demonstrate a consistent age-related escalation of myopia prevalence across districts, with particularly steep increases around the transition from lower to upper primary grades.

Discussion

Comparison of myopia prevalence with other regions

The present study demonstrated significant differences in myopia prevalence among three districts in Guangzhou, with Huadu reporting the highest rate (38.92%), followed by Yuexiu (37.67%) and Panyu (32.56%). These findings are consistent with regional variations documented in other large-scale studies across China. For example, a school-based study in urban Beijing reported a prevalence of 36.7% among primary school children aged 5–14 years [10]. Similarly, a city-wide survey in Guangzhou covering 12 districts found an overall prevalence of 33.8%, with rates increasing from 15.4% in Grade 1 to 52.9% in Grade 6.11 Compared with these benchmarks, Yuexiu and Huadu showed higher-than-average prevalence, whereas Panyu was slightly below the city mean. Comparable disparities have also been observed between urban and rural regions elsewhere in China. A large-scale survey reported a prevalence of 51.1% among primary school children in Shanghai compared with 25.5% in rural Yunnan [11]. Such contrasts highlight the influence of environmental and contextual factors, including urbanization, educational intensity, digital screen exposure, and limited outdoor activity, on childhood myopia. Together, these observations suggest that geographic setting and socioeconomic development level remain important determinants of the myopia burden in metropolitan areas.

When placing our findings in a broader regional context, clear differences emerge between Guangzhou and neighboring countries. In Kazakhstan, a recent school-based study in Almaty reported an overall myopia prevalence of 28.3% among children aged 6–16 years, with rates rising from 17.6% in grade 1 to 40.5% in grade 9.13 In contrast, a study in rural Mongolia found a markedly lower prevalence of 5.8% (95% CI: 4.4–7.2%) among students aged 7–17 years, with girls more frequently affected than boys [12]. Data from the Russian Federation also show wide variation: cycloplegic surveys across regions reported prevalence ranging from 2.4% in grade 1 to 38.6% in grade 11, with significantly higher rates in urban lyceums compared to regular schools (up to 50.7% vs. 30.9%).15 Taken together, these findings suggest that the prevalence observed in Guangzhou primary school students is higher than in Mongolia, similar to or slightly higher than in Kazakhstan, and comparable to urbanized cohorts in Russia [13, 14]. This regional heterogeneity underscores the strong influence of educational environment, urbanization, and parental engagement on myopia burden, over and above shared ethnic or geographic backgrounds.

Geographic and urbanization gradient

The three districts Huadu (north), Yuexiu (central), and Panyu (south) represent a geographic cross-section of Guangzhou’s urban development. Yuexiu, the traditional city center, has the highest population density and academic competitiveness. Panyu, located at the southern urban-rural fringe, is characterized by more balanced development and greater environmental exposure, which may partly explain its lower myopia prevalence. By contrast, Huadu, situated in the northern expansion zone, appears to face the challenges of rapid urbanization without parallel improvements in public health literacy and access, corresponding to its highest recorded prevalence. This spatial stratification highlights the need to adapt public health strategies to geographic and developmental gradients within metropolitan areas.

Gender-based differences in myopia prevalence

Across all three districts, female students consistently exhibited a higher prevalence of myopia than males, with statistically significant differences in each region (p < 0.001). In Yuexiu District, prevalence was 40.10% in females versus 35.53% in males; in Panyu, 34.83% versus 30.57%; and in Huadu, 43.09% versus 35.52%. These results are consistent with previous studies in East Asia reporting a higher risk of myopia onset and faster progression among girls [15]. Several mechanisms have been proposed. Physiologically, biological susceptibility, retinal structural differences, and greater accommodative lag may contribute [16]. Behaviorally, girls are more likely to engage in sustained near work and spend less time outdoors than boys, both of which are established risk factors for myopia [10, 15]. The consistency of gender disparity across three geographically and demographically distinct districts highlights the robustness of this pattern. Targeted interventions addressing modifiable behaviors—such as increasing outdoor activity and regulating near work—may therefore be particularly important for female students.

Grade-level patterns and critical onset window

The grade-stratified analysis showed a progressive increase in myopia prevalence from Grade 1 to Grade 6, with the steepest rise between Grades 3 and 4. This mid-grade acceleration is consistent with national and international observations, where myopia commonly emerges and progresses during the early to middle primary years [1, 4, 15]. This period coincides with greater educational demands, heavier homework load, and reduced outdoor activity, as well as developmental stages when children engage in intensive literacy and screen-based learning. Recognizing Grades 3–4 as a window of heightened vulnerability provides important opportunities for early intervention, including more frequent screening, targeted school-based education, and family-oriented lifestyle guidance.

Region-based implementation strategies and lessons learned

Building on the geographic and socioeconomic differences described above, further analysis of the screening process revealed several operational and strategic factors associated with variation in prevalence. A unified screening protocol was implemented across all districts to ensure methodological consistency, though minor differences in efficiency and communication appeared to influence effectiveness. Huadu District, despite having the highest prevalence, showed the lowest parental query rate (47.2%), suggesting that limited parental engagement may have delayed responses to screening results and reduced the impact of follow-up measures. In contrast, Panyu’s higher query rate (62%) likely facilitated earlier intervention and behavioral adjustment, contributing to its relatively lower prevalence. Huadu also piloted the “Eye Health Corners” initiative, using interactive educational tools to compensate for limited parental engagement, and this model is now being considered for replication in other districts. In addition, cross-sector collaboration with education authorities and township hospitals was strongest in Panyu, where effective coordination improved access, parental participation, and follow-up compliance, potentially explaining the district’s comparatively favorable outcomes.

Despite its large scale and standardized protocol, this study has several limitations that warrant consideration. First, the absence of universal cycloplegic refraction in this large-scale school-based screening may have led to a degree of overestimation of myopia prevalence, particularly among younger children, although a dual definition and referral system were applied. Second, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference. The observed district- and gender-specific differences may be influenced by urbanization, education, or lifestyle, but such associations require longitudinal confirmation. Finally, as the study was limited to three districts within Guangzhou, the findings may not be fully generalizable to other regions with different socioeconomic or educational contexts.

Conclusion

This large-scale, school-based screening revealed significant differences in myopia prevalence across Yuexiu, Panyu, and Huadu districts of Guangzhou, with additional variations by grade level and gender. These disparities may be associated with differences in urbanization, educational environment, and parental engagement, though causality cannot be inferred from the present cross-sectional design. The findings highlight the importance of considering regional characteristics when planning future myopia prevention strategies in Guangzhou and provide a reference for similar urban contexts.
