Yellowness in skin complexion: Analysis of self‐perception of women in China evaluated against clinical parameters of yellowness
Authors: Bee Leng Lua, Julie Robic
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSkin “yellowness” is an abstract and subjective term, without a definitive measurement protocol. Objectives were to analyze Chinese women's self‐perception of skin yellowness and associated parameters and identify objective clinical measurements that correlate with these perceptions.MethodsFollowing focus group discussions, criteria for skin yellowness were defined, and validated by volunteer rankings of facial images. A typology study of 185 women was performed. Participants were grouped into yellow (Color Uniformity, Brightness and Transparency (CUBT) yellow scale grade > 3, chromameter b* value > 16) and non‐yellow (CUBT yellow scale grade < 2, b* value < 14) groups. Participants self‐evaluated their skin on yellowness, transparency, skin uniformity, dullness, radiance, oiliness, and texture. Expert assessments were performed to grade sebaceous pores, ocular area pigmentation, pigmentary spots and CUBT scores. Instrumental analysis of the skin was employed using corneometer, sebumeter, mexameter chromameter, and AGE reader.ResultsWomen in the yellow group self‐evaluated their skin as significantly duller, less uniform, and less radiant than women in the non‐yellow group (P ≤ 0.05). Higher levels of ocular area pigmentation and lower facial skin uniformity and brightness (P < 0.001) were observed in women with yellow skin. CUBT expert grading showed lower pink skin color, but significantly higher beige, yellow, and olive pigmentation (P ≤ 0.05) in women in the yellow skin group. Melanin and b* values were significantly higher in women with yellow skin while L value was significantly lower.ConclusionSelf‐perceived skin yellowness in Chinese women correlates to chromameter and mexameter measurements, as well as expert evaluation of ocular pigmentation and CUBT parameters.Keywords: brightness, cosmetics, melanin, pigmentation, skin yellowness, uniformity

INTRODUCTION

East Asian women can subjectively perceive skin yellowness, an undesirable trait commonly associated with a dull complexion and aging. 1 , 2 , 3 Many Asian women place importance on reducing yellowness in skin complexion. 4 Parameters that may affect the appearance or perception of yellowness include uneven skin tone, oily skin, low skin hydration and skin texture or the presence of dark pigmented spots. 3

Perception of skin color is primarily subjective, and clear definitions, uniform standards, or measurements for the yellowness of facial skin are lacking. 4 Expert evaluation scales are available 5 and objective measurement can be achieved with specialist instruments. 3 , 6 Instrumental methods target factors that determine skin color including melanin pigmentation, skin thickness, advanced glycation end products (AGEs) and photoaging. 1 , 6 An instrumental method used to measure skin color is colorimetry. 6 , 7 Colorimetry devices use the CIE‐L*a*b*color system to detect brightness (L* value), redness (a* value), yellowness (b* value), individual typology angle (ITA), and hue angle, recording subtle differences in skin color. 7

There is a need to understand and further define the parameters that drive the perception of skin in order to monitor and address consumer expectations in cosmetic development. The objective of this study was to analyze self‐perception of skin yellowness and determine objective clinical measurements and expert gradings of skin conditions that correlate with perceptions of skin yellowness in women in China. Facial skin parameters that consumers associate with yellowness in skin complexion were self‐evaluated, graded by trained experts, and assessed by clinical instruments.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study design

This was an observational cohort study that investigated the subjective and objective grading of skin parameters associated with skin yellowing in healthy Chinese women between January to December 2020. A skin yellowing questionnaire was administered in Beijing and Shanghai and subsequent typology study conducted in Shanghai only. Studies were performed according to Good Clinical Practice guidelines and the Declaration of Helsinki. The study was carried out with the approval of the Shanghai Ethnics Committee for Clinical Research (approval number SECCR2020‐165‐01).

Focus groups

Prior to the typology study, focus group discussions facilitated by Nielsen Market Research (Shanghai) were held in four cities across China (Shanghai, Chengdu, Xi'an, and Beijing) to understand the perception of skin yellowness among Chinese women. Seventy‐two women aged 20–50 years who identify with a need to reduce skin yellowness each participated in one of 12 two‐hour small group sessions, divided by age (20ʼs, 30ʼs, or 40ʼs). Perceptions of the prominence, causes, and appearance of yellow skin were explored along with factors that drive expectations when selecting cosmetic products.

Part 1: Defining ‟yellow’’ and ‟non‐yellow’’ groups

To determine objective criteria for the yellow and non‐yellow study groups used in this study, 195 volunteers from Beijing and Shanghai were provided with a series of twenty cloned photographs with differences in skin characteristics and were asked to rank faces from most to least yellow. Within each category of skin characteristics (gloss, transparency, pores, and skin color), researchers constructed an average face image using pre‐existing high‐definition photographs and performed texture cloning to present images in which only one parameter changed. The purpose was to create a scale representing the level of gloss, transparency, pores, and skin color. To complete the photograph ranking questions, volunteers scrolled between the images to select which were most and least yellow in each category. The skin yellowing questionnaire also asked which skin parameters do volunteers think make skin look more yellow. Thereafter the average of the Color Uniformity, Brightness and Transparency (CUBT) yellow scale 5 and skin color chromameter L*, a*, and b* values of the most yellow and least yellow ranked face were identified. These average results from the consumer perceptions were used to validate ‟yellow group’’ and ‟non‐yellow group’’ selection criteria for the subsequent typology study (Part 2, see below). The average of the gloss, transparency and pores measurement of the most yellow and least yellow ranked face were also measured.

Part 2: Typology study

The typology study consisted of three assessments: participant self‐evaluation, expert grading, and clinical instrument measurements (hydration, oiliness, and skin color), as well as high‐definition photography. Measurements commenced 30 min after skin cleansing.

Participants

Female (n = 185) non‐smokers living in Shanghai with body mass index (BMI) ranging between 18.5 and 24 kg/m2, and Fitzpatrick skin type classification II–III 8 were recruited for the typology study. The women were recruited via social media advertisement, were screened and classified into yellow and non‐yellow groups using a questionnaire (Supplementary Appendix 1) and provided written informed consent. Participants were equally divided between age groups (20‐29, 30–39, 40–49 years old). Participants were categorized into the yellow group and the non‐yellow group based on the CUBT yellow scale grade and chromometer b* value cut points established in Part 1, with 30 participants planned per yellowness group within each age group.

Self‐evaluation

Participants completed a self‐evaluation of facial skin parameters that may be related to yellowness while seated at an evaluation table with standardized lighting and wearing a black robe. Each parameter was subjectively ranked on a scale of 1–5 where higher scores are generally favorable, with exception of transparency score. The self‐evaluated yellowness score of 1 indicates very yellow to 5 less yellow; skin uniformity score of 1 is not uniform to 5 very uniform; dullness score of 1 indicates very dull to 5 not dull; radiance score of 1 indicates not radiant to 5 very radiant; texture score of 1 indicates not refined to 5 very refined (smooth), an oiliness score of 1 denotes very oily to 5 non‐oily; a transparency score of 1 indicates very transparent to 5 less transparent.

Expert gradings

Trained experts graded the presence of sebaceous pores on the cheeks (on a scale of 0–5, where 0 is no pores and 5 is large pores), ocular area pigmentation (on a scale of 0–4, where 0 is no pigmentation and 4 is severe pigmentation), density of pigmentary spots (on a scale of 0–7 where 0 is no pigmentation and 7 is severe pigmentation) and number of localized pigmentary spots under standard lighting conditions. Under eye bags were also graded from 0 to 7 (where 0 indicated no eye bags and 7 severe eye bags). Expert gradings were made by trained researchers and were based on photographic scales from the Loreal Skin Aging Atlas (Asian).

Expert grading of CUBT color (yellow, beige, light pink, olive), uniformity, brightness and transparency scores were recorded for each participant similar to previously published methods. 5 , 9 , 10 As luminosity is less relevant to the current population of Asian women, ‘L’ was replaced in the grading by ‘U’, being uniformity.

Instrumental analysis

Pre‐defined morphological facial areas of each participant were assessed using multiple instruments. A corneometer (Courage & Khazaka) was used to determine the hydration level of the stratum corneum by measuring skin dielectric properties. A sebumeter (Courage & Khazaka) was used to determine oiliness by measuring the sebum level of the skin surface. A mexameter (MX18) was applied to the mid‐forehead, cheeks and mid‐chin to measure melanin, and also to compare melanin levels in sun exposed skin of the lower arm and non‐exposed skin of the upper arm. 11 A chromameter (CM‐700D) was used to measure lightness (L* value), redness (a* value), and yellowness (b* value) color parameters of the skin at the mid‐forehead, lower eyelids, middle of lower upper cheeks and mid‐chin as previously described. 4 , 6 The Advanced Glycation Endproducts (AGE) reader was used to measure glycated proteins in tissue in sun exposed and non‐exposed arm skin. This AGE reader is not appropriate to measure facial skin. AGEs accumulate over time and have a role in development of wrinkles and reduction in skin elasticity which may lead to older‐appearing skin. 12 Transludem (Orion Concept) optical index measurements, where higher values represent better transparency, were taken at ten morphological points of each participants face (three points on forehead, three on each cheek and one point on chin). With the exception of sebumeter, all instrument readings were repeated three times to ensure consistency.

Colorface photography

Facial images were created using standardized high‐definition photographs of each participant. Measurements of pores, skin roughness (texture), facial wrinkles, pigmented spots, shininess, and homogeneity were obtained by image analysis with Colorface® technology (Newtone, Lyon, France). Standardized front pictures were used to reconstruct a high‐definition average face using an innovative superposition algorithm developed by Newtone Technologies® (Newtone, Lyon, France). This method uses automatic detection of characteristic morphological points on every individual image. The reconstructed average face generated from high‐definition images from the non‐yellow group (left hand side) and yellow group (right hand side) is provided in Figure 1.

Reconstructed average face generated with Newtone® technologies algorithm from high‐definition images of women in the non‐yellow group (left hand side) and yellow group (right hand side) participants.

Statistical methods

To validate the yellow and non‐yellow group allocations, self‐evaluated skin yellowness was correlated with the CUBT yellowing scale and chromameter b* and L* values. The mean differences of measured parameters between the yellow and non‐yellow groups within each age subgroup were assessed using nonparametric Mann‐Whitney rank test. The sample size of 30 per group was based on assumption of normal distribution. A significance level of P < 0.05 was set to determine statistical significance. Skin parameters among self‐evaluated, expert graded and instrument measurements that are significantly different between yellow and non‐yellow groups were identified using Pearson correlation.

A recruitment questionnaire (Supplementary Appendix S1) was used to minimize participant selection bias. Statistical analyses were conducted using Stata v17 software.

RESULTS

Focus groups

The key attributes relating to skin yellowness mentioned by Chinese women who took part in focus group discussions were uneven skin tone, oily skin, dry skin, dull skin, texture, coarse pores, and dark spots or pigmentations. These attributes were perceived to enhance the look of yellow skin, hence the study set out to objectively analyze each of these parameters.

Part 1: Defining yellow skin groups

Average skin color chromameter measurements of the perceived most yellow face were L* value: 54.14; a* value: 15.38; and b* value: 22.85. For the perceived least yellow face the average skin color measurements were L* value: 64.78, a* value: 15.81 and b* value: 15.69. The perceived most yellow face had significantly lower L* and higher b* value, as well as larger sebaceous pores (Table S1).

This result validated the criteria for typology study group recruitment being CUBT yellow scale grade > 3, b* value > 16 for the yellow group and CUBT yellow scale grade < 2, b* value < 14 for the non‐yellow group. In addition, of the 195 volunteers who completed the Part 1 questionnaire, 91% indicated that pigmentations have an impact on perception of skin yellowness, 90% indicated that radiance has an impact, and 85% indicated that transparency has an impact.

Part 2: Typology study

Self‐evaluation

Participants in the yellow and non‐yellow skin groups performed a self‐evaluation of the yellowness of their skin (Figure 2). Participants in the yellow skin group across all ages rated themselves as having a significantly more yellow skin tone (lower score) than participants in the non‐yellow skin group (Figure 2A). Overall comparison (all age groups combined) showed that transparency, uniformity, dullness, radiance, and texture were significantly different between the yellow and non‐yellow groups (Figure 2B). The self‐evaluated mean transparency score of women with yellow skin was significantly higher (less transparent and less favorable) than in the non‐yellow skin group, and the self‐evaluated uniformity, dullness, radiance, and texture scores were significantly lower (also less favorable) in women in the yellow skin group than in women in the non‐yellow group. There was no significant difference in the self‐evaluated mean oiliness score between groups.

Yellowness score by age group, where a lower score is indicative of a more yellow skin tone. B Various parameter scores for all age groups combined. * p < 0.05, ** p < 0.01, *** p < 0.001.

Pearson correlation found that self‐evaluation of uniformity, dullness, radiance, and texture correlated with self‐evaluation of yellowness. Participants who perceived themselves to have more yellow skin generally rated their skin higher in dullness and lower in radiance (Table S2).

Expert gradings

According to expert grading (Figure 3), there were significantly higher levels of ocular pigmentation in the yellow group compared to the non‐yellow group. No significant differences in sebaceous pores or pigmentary spots were found between groups on expert examination (Figure 3).

Mean expert gradings of facial skin parameters by participants objectively grouped to yellow skin versus non‐yellow skin. *** p < 0.001.

Pearson correlation found that increased ocular area pigmentation was significantly associated with skin yellowness (Table S3).

According to CUBT expert grading (Figure 4), facial skin uniformity and brightness were significantly lower in the yellow skin group than the non‐yellow skin group (Figure 4A,B). The yellow skin group also exhibited lower light pink pigmentation in some facial areas, but significantly higher beige, yellow, and olive pigmentation based on the CUBT color scale (Figure 4C–F). There was no difference in transparency between groups (Figure 4G).

Mean expert gradings of CUBT domains by participants objectively grouped to yellow skin versus non‐yellow skin. A: Uniformity; B Brightness; C: Beige; D Yellow; E: Light pink; F: Olive; G Transparency. ** p < 0.01, *** p < 0.001.

Instrumental analysis

Instrumental analyses performed to assess skin uniformity, brightness, color, and transparency are shown in Figure 5. Most of the instrumental measurements did not show significant differences between yellow and non‐yellow skin groups. Mean melanin (mexameter MX18) readings were significantly higher in the yellow skin group for each area of the skin assessed, including the sun exposed and non‐exposed areas (Figure 5A). Overall transparency across all age groups was higher in the yellow skin group (Figure 5B). There were no significant differences in oiliness (sebumeter), glycated proteins (AGE reader) or hydration (corneometer) between yellow and non‐yellow skin groups (data not shown).

Mean Instrumental measurements by participants objectively grouped to yellow skin versus non‐yellow skin. A: Mexameter; B: Transludem; C, D and E: Chromameter. ** p < 0.01; *** p < 0.001; ns = not significant.

Chromameter assessment of facial skin color parameters found significant differences between the yellow and non‐yellow groups in the a* value (redness) for forehead and eyelid, but not cheek or chin (Figure 5C). Significant differences in the b* value and L* value demonstrated that the yellow group had higher levels of yellowness and lower levels of lightness, respectively (Figure 5D,E). There were significant differences between the yellow and non‐yellow groups observed in each chromameter parameter for both sun exposed and non‐exposed areas (Figure 5C–E).

There was significant correlation of all mexameter measurements between yellow and non‐yellow groups, where lower readings aligned with less yellowing (Table S4).

Pearson correlation of overall (yellow plus non‐yellow groups), yellow groups and non‐yellow groups with different chromameter readings showed that the L* value at lower eyelid and lower cheek as well as the b* value more generally are related to yellow skin (Table S5).

DISCUSSION

During qualitative focus group sessions, volunteers from four cities associated the visible parameters of uneven skin tone, oiliness, dry and dull texture, coarse pores, and dark spots with the appearance of skin yellowing. There was a general belief that inherent yellow skin tone can become more prominent over time due to aging and can also be influenced by lifestyle factors such as sleep and diet.

This study demonstrates that skin parameters such as transparency, uniformity, dullness, radiance, and texture may be related to perceptions of skin yellowing. The classification of yellow and non‐yellow groups via CUBT yellow scale and colorimetry b* value correlated with volunteers self‐perceived yellowness. These classification criteria could be used as a threshold to determine skin yellowness, but this still needs further validation. Building from self‐perception toward expert evaluation, it was noted that trained examination of ocular pigmentation as well as CUBT grading of color (beige and olive), brightness and uniformity can be used to assess skin yellowing.

In the current study there were significant differences in melanin measures between yellow and non‐yellow groups indicating mexameter will be suitable to measure the yellowing aspect of the skin. Previous research has also identified the mexameter's potential for assessment of skin yellowness. 4 , 6 It is of interest that the difference in melanin between the current yellow and non‐yellow group was observed both in sun‐exposed and in non‐exposed skin areas, suggesting that yellowness could be a result of internal systemic non‐pathological factors such as cellular biological oxidation, leading to the presence of carbonyl or glycated proteins or lipofuscin, rather than a local facial effect. Ogura et al. explored the impact of accumulation of carbonyl protein, with in vitro findings suggesting that carbonyl modification of the dermal protein is involved in the production of a yellowish color change in photo‐aged Japanese facial skin. 13 Although alterations in skin glycated proteins have been associated with skin yellowing, 1 our AGE measurements of glycated proteins did not differ between the yellow and non‐yellow groups, noting that measurements were taken at sites on the arm. Ohshima et al found that AGE measurements could be correlated with age and b* value, but found a lack of correlation between melanin index and AGE, concluding that AGEs are likely to play a role in the yellowish discoloration of skin with aging. 1 A previous study which classified 80 healthy Chinese women aged 20–39 years into bright yellowish, dark yellowish and ideal skin color, found higher cheek AGE levels in women with dark yellowish skin. 14 In this same study, volunteers with bright yellowish skin color had higher melanin and lower facial blood flow perfusion; however grouping into dark yellowish or bright yellowish may not be aligned with our definitions. In addition, levels of hydration or sebum do not appear to be good indicators for skin yellowness. Furthermore, self‐rated oiliness is not related to the perception of skin yellowness. As measurements were taken a short time after skin cleansing this may have affected sebum and oiliness levels.

Typology study results suggested that L* value at lower eyelid and lower cheek, as well as b* value more generally, can be used to assess skin yellowness across age groups. These findings add to as an earlier analysis of factors influencing ITA. 15

In this study, the self‐perception of transparency was not correlated with expert grading and instrumental measurement. Further examination of the methods for measurement and grading of transparency may be required before inclusion of this parameter in assessment of perceived skin yellowness.

This study identified consumer expectations on the reduction of skin yellowness and found key skin parameters that may influence or enhance the effect of skin yellowness. To improve the yellowness aspect of the skin, aesthetic or skin care could be targeted to parameters such as pigmentations, wrinkles and pores to have optimal effect in reducing perceived skin yellowness. Addressing skin yellowness in this context is different from the medical condition of yellow skin, jaundice.

Collectively, this study adds to the understanding of an important determinator of perceived skin yellowness in East Asian women. Colorface analysis or photography provides a useful tool in demonstrating an average appearance based on an objective measurement, with similarities to the 3D Skin Chroma diagram described by Han et al. 1 , 4

Strengths and limitations

Strengths of this study include the breadth of instruments employed for analysis. Skin yellowness is abstract and can be subjective; in this study there was a focus on relating perceived parameters beyond merely the skin tone. The study also had limitations. Participants of the typology study were drawn from a single location, and results may not be generalizable. Due to the subjective nature of ranking of images and self‐evaluation of parameters it is possible that participant responses had uncontrolled variability. While yellowing can be a feature of aging, 1 , 3 this study was not of sufficient size or powered to detect any influence of age on the perceptions of yellowing.

CONCLUSION

The term “yellowness” has been highly abstract with no clear definition in consumer perception. Furthermore, there has been no clear measurement method to determine yellowness. This study demonstrates that skin yellowness, as perceived by Chinese women, objectively correlates to chromameter and mexameter measurements, as well as expert evaluation of ocular pigmentation and CUBT parameters. There is potential for application of the results in determining whether the usage of cosmetic products can effectively reduce skin yellowness.
