Limited Sex-Based Differences in Viral Outcomes of ART-Treated SIV/SHIV Infection
Authors: Kedan Endrias, Soham Sonawane, Kirk A Easley, Benedicth Ukhueduan, Michelle Lee, Vijayakumar Velu, Rama R Amara, Steven E Bosinger, Mirko Paiardini, Guido Silvestri, Maud Mavigner, Vidisha Singh, Ann Chahroudi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractLentiviral infection of rhesus macaques represents a robust model for HIV persistence and a key system to evaluate HIV cure-directed interventions. How sex impacts viral reservoirs in macaques is unknown. We sought to uncover potential sex differences in viral measurements during antiretroviral therapy using data from 19 studies conducted at Emory Primate Center. Sex differences in total and intact SIV/SHIV-DNA levels in blood and lymph node CD4+ T cells were not observed, although subgroup analysis of adult macaques with SIV revealed higher reservoir levels in females. Age (adult versus infant) was a significant effect for all viral outcomes studied.Keywords: HIV, SIV, sex, reservoir, macaque

Women comprise 53% of the 40.8 million individuals living with HIV-1 worldwide yet make up <40% of participants in HIV therapy and vaccine research studies, limiting knowledge of sex-specific treatment and immune responses [1]. Women generally exhibit lower set point viral loads during untreated chronic HIV infection and have a higher likelihood of spontaneous control of viremia [1]. Higher type I interferon responses during acute HIV infection and decreased T-cell activation and exhaustion later in infection are seen in women versus men [1]. The impact of sex on the HIV reservoir and the potential for cure are debated with inconsistent conclusions stemming from sample size differences in cohort studies and methodologic factors, such as the specific reservoir assay used and source of cell input for reservoir measurement [2–7].

Infection of rhesus macaques (Macaca mulatta) with Simian immunodeficiency virus (SIV) and Simian-human immunodeficiency virus (SHIV) has been widely used to model HIV pathogenesis and persistence given the similarities in viral and immune dynamics, disease progression, and response to antiretroviral therapy (ART). Numerous preclinical studies using ART-suppressed macaques have been conducted yet small group sizes often preclude stratifying results by sex. Here, we sought to establish the impact of sex on measurements of virus persistence in SIV/SHIV-infected, ART-treated adult and infant macaques studied at the Emory Primate Center. We found a limited effect of sex; the greatest differences in viral outcomes were driven by age, with viral strain also influencing total viral DNA (vDNA) levels and time on ART predictive of intact proviral DNA levels.

METHODS

We conducted a meta-analysis of 16 published and 3 unpublished studies using rhesus macaques at the Emory Primate Center (Table 1) [8–23]. Analysis was blinded to publication status and potential bias related to nonpeer reviewed data were mitigated by being transparent about their inclusion. Inclusion criteria were studies conducted in the past 7 years using macaques treated with daily ART started at least 1 week after infection and with available data on viral dynamics in absence of any additional experimental treatment. Raw data were extracted using the following parameters: demographics (sex and age), virus strain, ART drugs, highest (peak) viral load prior to ART, reservoir data from last available time point, and duration on ART at the time of reservoir measurement. Results for total vDNA, intact proviral DNA, and replication competent virus were obtained using isolated CD4+ T cells; for one study where intact proviruses were measured in mononuclear cells, we corrected for CD4+ T-cell frequencies determined by flow cytometry. Reservoir measurements with undetectable values were assigned an arbitrary value of 1 copy/million CD4+ T cells for graphical and statistical purposes. Initial univariable analyses were performed using nonparametric Mann–Whitney U-test. Statistical significance was defined as a two-sided P value of <.05. To account for the clustered nature of data arising from 19 distinct studies, we employed linear mixed-effects models. Individual animal data meta-analyses were used to assess the effect of sex, age group (adult vs. infant macaques), viral strain (SIV vs. SHIV), and their two-way interactions (sex by age group; sex by viral strain; age group by viral strain) as fixed effects modeled against peak viral load, total vDNA, and intact proviral DNA as continuous dependent variables. For reservoir models, “weeks on ART” was included as a fixed effect, categorized into tertiles (≤52 weeks, >52–60 weeks, and >60 weeks). To adjust for the meta-analytic structure and account for heterogeneity between studies, study number was included as a random intercept. This random effect allows the dependent variable to vary across different studies, effectively adjusting standard errors and estimates for the nonindependence of animals within the same study. This likelihood-based approach ensures that model-based means are unbiased given unbalanced or missing data, provided that data are missing at random and the missingness is noninformative, allowing for inclusion of all available data points without the need for manual imputation. Model parameters were estimated using SAS Proc Mixed.

RESULTS

A total of 297 (91 female and 206 male) rhesus macaques were included (Table 1). We first assessed significant predictors of viral reservoir size during ART using a linear mixed-effects model. Macaques were started on ART between 1 and 21 weeks post infection. Analysis of CD4+ T-cell associated total vDNA in blood indicated that age group (P = .02) and viral strain (P = .04) had significant effects (Figure 1A). Mean total vDNA was higher in adults (2.35 log10, n = 219) compared with infants (1.49 log10, n = 39) and higher in SIV-infected (2.39 log10, n = 226) versus animals with SHIV (1.45 log10, n = 32). Differences based on sex (n = 69 female, n = 189 male) or ART tertile were not observed (Figure 1A). In lymph nodes, age group was a significant predictor of CD4+ T-cell associated total vDNA (P < .001), with adults maintaining a higher mean (2.39 log10, n = 137) than infants (0.91 log10, n = 36) (Figure 1B). Macaques with SIV also showed higher levels than macaques with SHIV (n = 141 vs. n = 32; 2.03 vs. 1.27 log10; P = .05). Total vDNA levels did not differ significantly by sex (n = 50 female, n = 123 male) or ART tertile. Adults had higher levels of total vDNA in rectal CD4+ T-cells compared with infants (n = 70 vs. n = 39; 1.72 vs. 0.68 log10; P = .05) (Figure 1C). Differences were not found based on sex (n = 33 female, n = 76 male), viral strain (n = 80 SIV, n = 29 SHIV), or ART tertile.

Analysis of intact proviral DNA in blood CD4+ T-cells revealed significant effects of both age (P < .001) and duration of ART (P < .001) (Figure 1D). Adult macaques had a larger intact reservoir (3.26 log10, n = 93) than infants (0.71 log10, n = 31). Mean intact proviral DNA levels in the blood showed a clear decline across ART duration tertiles, decreasing from 2.46 log10 in the first tertile to 1.66 log10 in the third (Figure 1D). Sex (n = 30 female, n = 94 male) and viral strain (n = 100 SIV, n = 24 SHIV) did not impact intact proviral DNA levels in blood CD4+ T cells. Consistent with these findings, the intact reservoir in lymph node CD4+ T cells was significantly influenced by age group (P < .001) and weeks on ART (P < .001) (Figure 1E). Adults exhibited higher mean intact proviral DNA levels (3.15 log10, n = 133) compared with infants (0.64 log10, n = 29). ART duration also drove a reduction in the lymph node reservoir, with mean levels falling from 2.23 log10 in the shortest duration tertile to 1.82 log10 in the third tertile (Figure 1E). Sex (n = 35 female, n = 127 male) and viral strain (n = 138 SIV, n = 24 SHIV) were not significant predictors for this outcome.

A sex difference was seen in pre-ART peak viral loads, with higher mean levels in males (6.58 log10, n = 206) compared with females (6.30 log10, n = 83) (Figure 1F). The model indicated a trend toward a sex-by-age interaction (P = .06, Supplementary Table 1) that was driven by a more pronounced sex difference in infants versus adults. Specifically, peak viral load in infant males (6.38 log10; n = 25) was higher than infant females (5.85 log10, n = 18), whereas the difference between adult males (6.79 log10, n = 181) and adult females (6.76 log10, n = 65) was negligible (Supplementary Table 1). The model also confirmed a higher mean log10 peak viral load for SIV (6.88 log10, n = 247) compared with SHIV (6.01 log10, n = 42). By adjusting for these variables and their interaction effects, sex (P = .02), age group (P = .03), and viral strain (P = .005) were identified as statistically significant fixed effects impacting viral loads (Figure 1F).

Most of the data came from studies in adult macaques with SIV initiated on ART during early chronic infection and treated with ART for >52 weeks; we thus assessed potential sex differences in reservoir measurements for this subgroup. Total SIV-DNA levels in blood CD4+ T cells were higher in females (n = 24 females, n = 132 males; P = .045; Supplementary Fig. 1A) and a similar trend was seen for intact SIV proviral DNA levels (n = 18 females, n = 75 males; P = .061; Supplementary Fig. 1B). An effect of sex was not seen for total or intact SIV-DNA levels in CD4+ T cells from lymph nodes (n = 18 female, n = 90 male for total and n = 24 female, n = 109 male for intact; Supplementary Fig. 1A, B). Results of quantitative viral outgrowth assays using lymph node CD4+ T cells were available from only nine adult female macaques and were not different from adult male (n = 29) macaques, all infected with SIV and treated with ART for 29–81 weeks (Supplementary Fig. 1C).

DISCUSSION

Sex-based differences in HIV infection influence viral dynamics and may result in differential responses to cure-directed interventions in people with HIV. Many HIV cure strategies are tested in macaques with SIV or SHIV treated with ART, but viral outcomes by sex are not routinely described. We endeavored to establish a working knowledge of viral reservoir measurements in rhesus macaques stratified by sex. Given that most monkey studies typically utilize small group sizes, we combined data from 19 studies comprising close to 300 macaques treated at the Emory Primate Center. While age was a significant predictor across most outcomes, sex did not significantly influence total or intact vDNA in blood or lymph nodes in the overall cohort.

We did find a sex difference in peak viral loads, although small, mostly driven by a 0.5 log higher mean in infant males vs. infant females. When only SIV (and not SHIV)-infected adult (and not infant) macaques were considered, blood CD4+ T-cell associated SIV-DNA levels were higher in females vs. males and intact proviral SIV-DNA trended in the same direction. This difference was not replicated in the lymph nodes, however. Incorporating sex into the analysis plan could thus be important when studying the impact of a given cure-directed intervention on the peripheral blood reservoir in SIV-infected, ART-suppressed adult macaques. Translating preclinical findings to inform clinical trial design depends on robust interrogation of both positive and negative results, including consideration of age, sex, viral strain, duration of ART, reservoir assay used, source of cells for the assay, and other potential factors.

Reservoir measurements in people with HIV mostly come from small cohort studies and interrogation of peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs). Some of these studies reported similar levels of total and integrated HIV-DNA in women and men on suppressive ART [5, 7]. However, two different studies from France encompassing 765 people (220 female) on ART showed that women were more likely to have lower HIV-DNA levels than men [2, 4]. In a study of US-based women and men, levels of intact (n = 17 each sex) and replication competent (n = 22 each sex) reservoirs were found to be similar across the sexes [3]. By contrast, replication competent reservoirs were higher in women in a Ugandan cohort while total HIV-DNA was similar [6]. These latter two studies used isolated resting CD4+ T cells rather than PBMCs and, given this difference and the conflicting conclusions regarding reservoir measurements in different cohorts, it is fair to say that the impact of sex on HIV persistence remains unclear. Since our results were generated using bulk CD4+ T cells (not resting), we cannot directly compare our findings with any of this prior human research.

There were limitations to this study. While we had a large overall sample size, some individual comparisons had fewer data points and certain comparisons were not possible due to missing data. We also recognize that the time on ART for these macaque studies was shorter than in many studies of virus persistence in ART-suppressed humans and thus our data may not reflect the stable reservoir. As we were primarily focused on ART-suppressed infection, we did not include any studies with macaques left untreated through late chronic infection and thus could not evaluate set point viremia or spontaneous control. We also did not measure immune responses or sex hormone levels; the lack of such correlates and measurements limits mechanistic interpretation and should be the basis for future research.

These findings suggest that some sex differences exist in viral outcomes of SIV/SHIV infection, but that age, viral strain, and duration of ART may have greater influence. While sex is an important consideration in data analysis, our results do not indicate that all nonhuman primate studies of virus persistence and cure must be powered to include equal numbers of both sexes. Additional research is necessary to determine whether there are sex-specific sequelae following ART interruption or therapeutic interventions.
