Spermidine targets GGA-rich small RNAs for virulence regulation
Authors: Chao Wang, Xiaoling Liu, Changqing Chang, Yu Deng, Lian-Hui Zhang, Sujoy Ghosh, Yong-Gui Gao, Kanaga Sabapathy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummarySpermidine (SPD), a polyamine essential for various biological processes, has long been recognized as a signaling molecule. Yet, its specific molecular targets and mechanisms of action remain elusive. In this study, we uncover a previously unknown mechanism whereby SPD selectively binds to small regulatory RNAs, driving their degradation and subsequently modulating virulence in Pseudomonas aeruginosa. We identified GGA-rich SPD-binding RNA motifs in RsmZ and RsmY, two small RNA regulators of the type III secretion system (T3SS) and quorum sensing pathways and demonstrated a motif-dependent interaction with SPD. SPD binding alters the structural properties of these RNAs in a manner associated with reduced RNA stability, promoting their degradation, at least in part, through the CafA endonuclease. In vivo, this process enhances T3SS activity and suppresses quorum sensing, thereby facilitating the acute infection of P. aeruginosa. These findings highlight a previously unrecognized regulatory role of polyamine-small RNA interactions in bacterial virulence, offering potential targets for therapeutic interventions.

Introduction

Spermidine (SPD) is a polyamine compound essential for cellular viability across all living organisms, from bacteria and protozoa to human cells.1 In bacteria, SPD is crucial for cell growth and division, via protecting cellular components from oxidative damage; contribute to biofilm formation; and regulate virulence gene expression.2,3 In protozoa, SPD is vital for the synthesis of macromolecules such as DNA and RNA, playing critical roles in developmental processes and the differentiation of various life cycle stages.4,5 SPD also plays pivotal roles in human cancer, aging, stress tolerance, and infectious diseases.6,7,8,9 Although targeting polyamine homeostasis has been studied as a strategy to inhibit cancer cell growth, success has been limited, partially due to the lack of knowledge about the underlying molecular mechanisms in both normal and neoplastic cells.10,11

Despite their importance, the lack of precise mechanistic understanding of polyamines’ mode of action raises questions such as why polyamines are essential for survival and why inorganic polycations like Ca2+ and Mg2+ cannot replace them. Chemically, a major difference is that inorganic cations have their positive charges associated with the same atom, whereas polyamines have positive charges distributed along a hydrocarbon chain. Theoretically, SPD and other polyamines like putrescine and spermine (SPM) can non-specifically bind to various anionic macromolecules such as DNA and RNA under physiological conditions. However, the uneven distribution of positive charges at fixed positions along the linear backbone of polyamines suggests potential selectivity and specificity in their target binding. This is exemplified by findings that SPD, rather than other analogues such as SPM and putrescine, is the primary signaling molecule involved in human health and diseases.8 Therefore, polyamine-specific interactors, if identified, might unravel the key missing links in our understanding of polyamine-mediated mechanisms and could provide valuable information for designing polyamine-related drugs, though such targets remain elusive after decades of research.11

Pseudomonas aeruginosa is a significant opportunistic bacterial pathogen in humans, known to cause pneumonia, bloodstream infections, and urinary tract infections.12 It is the third-leading cause of nosocomial infections, and the predominant pathogen associated with morbidity and mortality in cystic fibrosis patients.12,13 Notoriously resistant to antibiotics, this pathogen utilizes its type III secretion system (T3SS) to subvert host defenses and promote infection.14,15 The T3SS expression is complex and highly regulated by the master regulator ExsA.14,16 Polyamines, particularly SPD, have been reported to modulate T3SS expression, although the mechanistic basis is unclear.17,18 In this study, we aimed to characterize how SPD functions as a crucial signaling molecule in health and disease. Using P. aeruginosa as a working model, we uncovered that SPD specifically targets GGA-rich small RNAs for regulating virulence gene expression and the bacterial infectivity.

To determine whether SPD directly binds to RsmZ via its GGA-rich loops, we firstly synthesized RsmZ RNA in vitro and performed isothermal titration calorimetry (ITC) assays using SPD, and other polyamine analogues such as putrescine (PUT) and 1,6-diaminohexane (1,6-DAH), with structural similarity to SPD (Figure 1C). The results demonstrated a direct interaction between SPD and RsmZ, with an association constant (Ka) of 1.29 μM, indicating a strong binding affinity (Figure 1D). The lack of interaction of this small RNA with other analogues underscores the specificity of SPD binding to RsmZ. SPM, which structurally contains functional units of SPD (Figure 1C), was also found to interact with RsmZ, with a comparable Ka of 1.16 μM. In contrast, no binding was observed between SPD and RyhB, which is a structurally different 90-nucleotide small noncoding RNA in P. aeruginosa, or with total RNAs purified from this organism, where the abundance of GGA-rich RNAs within the total RNA preparation may not have been sufficient to detect the interactions with SPD. Collectively, these findings support a preferential interaction between SPD and RsmZ under the experimental conditions tested.

We further characterized the interaction between SPD and RsmZ using UV absorbance spectroscopy. The addition of SPD significantly decreased the UV absorbance of native RsmZ at 260 nm (Figure 1E), indicating binding-induced conformation changes. However, no change was observed when RsmZ was denatured. Similarly, no change for the double-stranded DNA version of RsmZ. These results suggested that SPD specifically binds to the RNA form of RsmZ and that this interaction is dependent on RNA secondary structure beyond than its primary sequence.

To identify the specific region of RsmZ essential for SPD binding, we synthesized a truncated version of RsmZ lacking the last 22 nucleotides (RsmZp90), retaining the first 90 nucleotides containing the GGA triplets. UV absorbance analysis showed that RsmZp90 maintained the ability to bind SPD (Figure 1F). Consistent with the full-length RNA, no binding-induced change was observed when RsmZp90 was denatured, confirming the importance of RNA secondary structure in SPD interaction. Next, we synthesized a shorter truncation, RsmZp60, containing only the first 60 nucleotides and lacking the last two GGA triplets. SPD did not affect the UV absorbance of RsmZp60 (Figure 1F), suggesting that the region spanning nucleotides 60–90, which contains two GGA motifs likely contributes to SPD recognition, or could also perturb the higher-order structure of RsmZ and its association with RsmA.

We next synthesized an RNA oligonucleotide corresponding to nucleotides 65–87 of RsmZ rich in GGA motifs: 5′-rCrArGrGrArUrGrArUrGrArCrGrArGrGrGrArCrUrGrArA-3′, and UV absorbance analysis showed that SPD significantly altered the absorbance profile of this GGA-rich RNA oligo (Figure 1F), similar to the effects observed with full-length RsmZ. Melting curve analyses revealed that SPD increased the melting temperature (Tm) of the GGA-rich RNA oligo in a dose-dependent manner, indicating enhanced stability of the RNA-SPD complex (Figure 1G). In contrast, the SPD analogue 1,6-DAH did not affect the Tm, and while putrescine and 1,3-diaminopropane (1,3-DAP) caused slight increases, which reflects a weak, non-specific stabilization of the RNA by these short polyamines, their effects were significantly less than that of SPD. However, our in-line probing results did not reveal large changes in backbone cleavage patterns upon SPD addition (Figure S1), suggesting SPD may modulate RNA function through subtle local rearrangements, rather than inducing major alterations in secondary structure detectable by in-line probing.19 Although no clear structural effect of spermidine on RsmZ was observed under the conditions tested, such effects may become apparent at higher spermidine concentrations or with more sensitive structural approaches, such as the SHAPE analysis.20,21 Collectively, these data from truncation mapping and minimal-domain testing demonstrate that the GGA-rich 60–90 nt domain constitutes the minimal SPD-responsive region of RsmZ. Upstream GGA motifs alone are insufficient, whereas the GGA-containing structural elements within the 65–87 nt segment are necessary and sufficient for the SPD-RsmZ binding.

To further assess the contribution of the GGA motifs within the identified binding region, we generated targeted substitutions in the RNA oligonucleotide used in Figure 1F. Whereas the wild-type oligonucleotide showed a clear SPD-dependent change in the UV absorbance profile, this response was lost in the mutant RNA, where the two GGAs in the wild-type RNA were changed to UAA and AUA in the mutant respectively (Figure 1H). These data indicate that the GGA-containing nucleotides within the 60–90 nt region of RsmZ are essential for the SPD-responsive behavior of this fragment.

SPD promotes degradation of GGA-rich small RNAs via CafA-mediated mechanism

Similar to RsmZ, RsmY contains multiple GGA triplets located in its hairpin loops. RsmZ and RsmY were reported to directly bind to RsmA and thereby inhibit its function in P. aeruginosa.22,23 RsmA is an RNA-binding protein that generally acts as a translational repressor by directly binding to the 5′-UTR of target mRNAs. ITC experiments showed no direct binding between SPD and RsmA (Figure S2), suggesting that the SPD’s effects on small RNAs are not due to direct interaction with RsmA.

Real-time RT-PCR results showed that SPD treatment significantly reduced the level of RsmZ compared to the untreated control (Figure 2B). Importantly, the promoter activity of rsmZ, assessed using a rsmZ promoter-lacZ fusion, was unchanged by SPD treatment (Figure 2C), indicating that transcription was not affected and suggesting that the decrease in transcript levels might be due to decreased RNA stability.

The stability of RsmZ and RsmY in P. aeruginosa is regulated by CafA, a homolog of RNase G involved in RNA degradation. Mutations in the cafA gene lead to accumulation of RsmZ and RsmY and impaired biofilm development.24 Given that SPD binds directly to RsmZ and RsmY but does not disrupt their complex with RsmA, we hypothesized that SPD may promote the degradation of these small RNAs via CafA. To test this hypothesis, we used recombinant CafA nuclease from E. coli to perform an enzymatic digestion assay and observed a specific degradation of RsmZ and RsmY in the presence of SPD in a dose-dependent manner (Figure 2D). CafA is known to act through sequential internal cleavages, producing transient fragments that can be rapidly degraded.24,25 Consistently, we detected robust loss of full-length RsmZ and RsmY, but no cleavage fragments were recorded under our experimental conditions. These results indicate that SPD facilitates CafA-mediated degradation of RsmZ and RsmY, thereby modulating their abundance.

To assess whether SPD affects the stability of RsmY and RsmZ in vivo, we generated a cafA deletion (ΔcafA) mutant in the P. aeruginosa T3SS reporter strain PCZ and performed a rifampicin chase assay to measure the abundance of both sRNAs after transcriptional arrest in the parental strain (PCZ) and the ΔcafA mutant, in the presence or absence of SPD (Figure 2E). In the parental strain, SPD markedly reduced the levels of both RsmY and RsmZ after rifampicin treatment. By contrast, in the ΔcafA mutant, the abundance of both sRNAs remained substantially higher, and the SPD-dependent reduction was strongly attenuated. These results support a post-transcriptional effect of SPD on RsmY and RsmZ and indicate that CafA contributes, at least in part, to the SPD-dependent decrease of these sRNAs in vivo. PCZ carries a promoterless lacZ gene transcriptionally driven by the exsCEBA promoter, the ExsCEBA being a key regulator of T3SS genes.18 In the parental PCZ strain, SPD treatment significantly increased β-galactosidase activity, reflecting enhanced T3SS expression. However, in the ΔcafA mutant, the response to SPD was significantly attenuated, with only a slight increase in β-galactosidase activity observed (Figure 2F). The combined post-transcriptional decrease in RsmZ, the SPD-enhanced CafA cleavage in vitro, and the loss of SPD responsiveness in the CafA mutant together support that SPD promotes the CafA-mediated degradation of RsmZ and RsmY both in vitro and in vivo. Also, the EMSA assay (Figure S3) with purified RsmA and RsmZ in presence of SPD showed no detectable disruption of the RsmA-RsmZ complex. Although a stronger RsmZ-RsmA signal was observed at higher SPD concentrations,20 our EMSA did not reproduce the previously reported stepwise binding of multiple RsmA dimers to RsmZ, likely because intermediate complexes could not be resolved under our experimental conditions. No detectable effect of SPD on the formation of the RsmA-RsmZ complex suggested that SPD does not displace RsmA from its canonical GGA-binding sites. Under the conditions tested, these findings indicate that SPD does not grossly perturb the association of RsmA with RsmZ. However, because the assay was performed using only a single RsmA concentration, it does not exclude the possibility that SPD influences a particular step in the assembly of the RsmZ/RsmY-RsmA complex. Addressing this question will require further analysis across a broader range of RsmA concentrations.

Next, we investigated the involvement of SPD in the RsmZ/RsmY-RsmA signaling pathway. Previous studies have shown that disruption of the polyamine transporter specifically downregulates T3SS gene expression without affecting other genes.18,26 Given that ExsA, a transcriptional factor in the AraC/XylS family, is the central regulator for T3SS gene expression in P. aeruginosa, we investigated whether ExsA is essential for SPD-mediated T3SS activation. We deleted the exsA gene in the PCZ reporter strain and found that this deletion completely abolished the bacterial response to SPD supplementation, suggesting that SPD-dependent T3SS activation depends on ExsA functionality, and that SPD acts upstream of ExsA (Figure 2G). Similarly, deletion of the rsmA gene in PCZ resulted in minimal, if any, response to SPD addition (Figure 2H). Together, these results indicate that SPD acts upstream of the master regulator ExsA and functionally involves RsmA, consistent with our findings that SPD binding regulates the stability of RsmZ and RsmY.

To further investigate whether SPD targets RsmZ and RsmY to regulate T3SS in P. aeruginosa, we overexpressed these small RNAs in PCZ and examined the strain’s responsiveness to SPD (Figure 2I). Consistent with the previous reports, overexpression of RsmY or RsmZ decreased T3SS gene expression. Moreover, overexpression of these small RNAs abolished the SPD-induced activation of T3SS, likely because the excess small RNAs compensated for the SPD-mediated degradation. Conversely, we deleted both rsmY and rsmZ in the PCZ background to assess SPD responsiveness in the absence of these small RNAs. This double deletion mutant exhibited a significant increase in T3SS gene expression compared to the parental strain. Importantly, the mutant showed reduced sensitivity to SPD treatment, unlike the consistent increase in T3SS expression observed in the wild-type PCZ strain upon SPD addition (Figure 2J).

In P. aeruginosa, the fha1 gene encodes a phage-like protein involved in the regulation of the type VI secretion system (T6SS) and is a direct downstream target of RsmA.27 Free RsmA binds to the 5′-UTR of the fha1 mRNA and inhibits Fha1 translation.28 To determine whether SPD-mediated degradation of RsmZ and RsmY frees RsmA to activate T3SS, we generated two reporter strains using P. aeruginosa PAO1. In PAO1(pME6013-fha1), β-galactosidase activity reflects the translational level of Fha1, while in PAO1(pME6016-fha1), it monitors fha1 transcription. Consistent with the previous findings, overexpression of rsmY significantly enhanced fha1 translation without affecting transcription. Upon SPD treatment, the bacterial cells exhibited a substantial decrease in Fha1 translation without changes in transcription levels (Figure 2K). These results confirm that SPD binding to RsmZ and RsmY leads to their degradation, which is consistent with increased effective RsmA activity as reflected by the downstream reporter responses.

To assess the biological significance of SPD targeting the RsmZ/RsmY-RsmA pathway, we performed real-time RT-PCR to measure changes in virulence gene expression upon SPD treatment. SPD treatment increased the mRNA levels of exsA, the master regulator of the T3SS gene cluster, and ExoS, a TTSS-secreted effector that destabilizes the host actin cytoskeleton through RhoGAP activity during acute infection (Figure 3B).

Additionally, β-galactosidase reporter assays showed that SPD downregulated QS genes, including the transcription of the QS signal synthase gene rhlI and the elastase gene lasB as well, in contrast to the subtle change of the gacS gene (Figure 3C). Since pyocyanin biosynthesis is strictly controlled by QS in P. aeruginosa, we measured pyocyanin production and found it was decreased upon SPD addition. Consistent with RsmA functioning as a repressor of QS, deletion of rsmA significantly increased the baseline pyocyanin production, but completely abolished the SPD-induced decrease in pyocyanin production (Figure 3D). These results demonstrate that SPD inhibits QS-related bacterial behaviors through the RsmZ/RsmY-RsmA signaling pathway.

Beyond T3SS and QS genes, SPD also suppressed the transcription of lasB, which encodes a metalloprotease that initiates biofilm formation through activation of nucleoside diphosphate kinase (NDK) in bacterial cells,32 and correspondingly reduced biofilm production in the wild-type strain (Figure 3E). Deletion of rsmA also decreased biofilm production compared to the wild-type strain, mimicking the phenotype observed in the rsmZ/rsmY double deletion mutant. Furthermore, the rsmA deletion mutant did not respond to SPD in terms of biofilm formation, indicating that RsmA is required for SPD’s effect on biofilm inhibition.

Previous data have shown that mutation of the polyamine transporter downregulates T3SS gene expression without affecting other genes, and addition of exogenous SPD activates the T3SS of P. aeruginosa.18 To further understand SPD-mediated RsmZ/RsmY degradation, we manipulated intracellular SPD levels by knocking out spuE and overexpressing spdH. SpuE is a component of the polyamine transporter, and its knockout significantly decreases intracellular SPD concentration. As shown in Figure 3F, knockout of spuE significantly decreased β-galactosidase activity in PCZ, consistent with previous results.33 SpdH is a cytoplasmic dehydrogenase that cleaves SPD into 1,3-diaminopropane and 4-aminobutyraldehyde. Overexpression of spdH significantly decreased β-galactosidase activity in PCZ, similar to the spuE deletion mutant.

Finally, to assess the functional consequences of altered intracellular SPD levels, we evaluated the virulence of the spuE knockout and spdH-overexpressing strains using a mouse infection model. While infection with PCZ cells resulted in 90% mortality, all mice infected with the spdH-overexpressing cells survived, even more than those infected with the spuE deletion mutant (Figure 3G). These results demonstrate that reducing intracellular SPD attenuates P. aeruginosa virulence. Together with our previous data, this indicates that SPD, whether extracellular or intracellular, activates the T3SS and controls pathogenesis by mediating RsmZ/RsmY degradation.

Discussion

Hitherto, identification of polyamine-specific binding targets has been a great challenge albeit advances in proteomic and genomic technology. The lethality of polyamines depletion limited the application of chemical-genetic approaches. Its polycationic nature also impeded conventional screening methods, such as two-hybrid systems and affinity chromatography, often only identifying non-specific interactions, as SPD’s multiple positive charges facilitate binding to negatively charged macromolecules, including nucleic acids and phospholipids.34 This has led to a lack of specificity in identifying direct SPD-interacting proteins and RNA targets.

In this study, we identified GGA-rich RNA motif as specific SPD-binding site, highlighting a previously unappreciated mechanism of RNA recognition by SPD. Our study was fueled by the observations that several small regulatory RNAs in P. aeruginosa harbor GGA-rich triplets. Thus, the P. aeruginosa system, with its extensively characterized virulence pathways and genetic tractability, provided a robust platform for us to dissect the molecular mechanisms of SPD-induced RNA stability. Importantly, this model allowed us to establish a direct link between SPD-RNA binding and phenotypic outcomes, including activation of the type III secretion system and suppression of QS. While UV absorption spectra reflect changes in RNA base stacking and local environment and do not directly distinguish specific binding from indirect conformational effects, here we provide multiple biophysical and functional assays, including ITC, thermal stability, truncation mapping, and CafA activity, to validate the direct SPD-RsmZ/Y interaction.

In addition, we demonstrated that RsmZ/RsmY exhibit a preferential response to SPD when compared with (1) other polyamines and (2) other tested RNAs under our experimental conditions. Firstly, SPD, but not other analogues, induced conformational changes in these small RNAs. Second, SPD mediated the enzymatic degradation of RsmZ and RsmY. Third, overexpression of rsmZ/rsmY attenuated the effects of SPD treatment on the bacteria. Fourth, deletion of rsmZ/rsmY abolished the bacterial virulence in response to SPD. Fifth, SPD-dependent changes in gene expression and T3SS activity required the presence of RsmZ and RsmY. These results suggest that the GGA-containing stem-loops of RsmZ/RsmY provide a structural context that is particularly responsive to SPD. Interestingly, the recent discovery of SPD riboswitches regulating bacterial polyamine biosynthesis further supports the idea that SPD can preferentially bind to RNAs and directly modulate RNA stability and function, although the broader molecular specificity of SPD-RNA interactions remains to be further investigated.20 In P. aeruginosa, RsmZ and RsmY are well-characterized small RNAs that regulate pathogenicity by modulating the activity of the RsmA protein.35 However, mechanisms governing their turnover are not fully understood. The results reported here indicate that the specific interaction between SPD and RsmZ/Y could induce local conformational changes in these RNAs, thereby promoting their enzymatic degradation. Although SPD enhances CafA-mediated degradation of RsmZ/Y, our in-line probing experiments suggest that this effect does not result from large-scale changes in RNA backbone accessibility. Polyamines are well known to modulate RNA behavior primarily through local stabilization and subtle alterations in base stacking, rather than through major refolding of the RNA backbone.36 Given that CafA has been reported to respond to such local microenvironmental changes,24 these results support a model in which SPD binding to the GGA-rich domain of RsmZ/Y subtly modulates local RNA structure in a way that increases CafA degradation efficiency. Nonetheless, we cannot exclude the possibility that SPD also affects CafA directly or promotes formation of a transient ternary complex. Further work will be required to fully resolve this question. Previous structural and biochemical studies have shown that RsmA binds RsmZ in a sequential manner, engaging individual GGA-containing loops in a defined order and avoiding aggregate formation.22,37 In this model, the bulged GGA loop that we identified as the minimal SPD-responsive region is incorporated late into the RsmZ-RsmA complex and has been proposed as a preferred site for RNase attack. Our data that SPD binds this GGA-rich bulged loop, enhances CafA-dependent degradation, yet does not abolish RsmA-dependent regulation are therefore more consistent with SPD modulating the local structure of this loop to promote CafA cleavage, rather than acting as a simple competitor that displaces RsmA from RsmZ/Y.

Our study also provides evidence that SPD promotes the degradation of RsmZ/RsmY in vivo, representing a previously unappreciated regulatory mechanism for bacterial virulence. Polyamine-sensing riboswitches have recently been identified in several Pseudomonas species, which form structured metabolite-binding pockets distinct from the GGA-containing stem-loops of RsmZ/Y. Consistently, no such element was identified upstream of SpeE, RsmZ, or RsmY in PAO1, suggesting that the SPD responsiveness of RsmZ/Y described here is mechanistically separate from the polyamine riboswitch class. During Pseudomonas infection, host-derived SPD could be imported into bacterial cells, where it specifically binds to RsmZ/RsmY and facilitates their degradation. This process releases RsmA from the RNA-protein complex, allowing it to suppress biofilm formation and activate the T3SS, thereby enhancing the acute infection of Pseudomonas. Together, these findings highlight the concept that SPD acts as a signaling molecule in host-pathogen interactions, playing a critical role in the dynamic communication between P. aeruginosa and its host.38 Understanding this mechanism opens potential avenues for therapeutic interventions targeting SPD-RNA interactions to mitigate bacterial virulence. It is noted that we did not directly quantify RsmA protein levels after SPD treatment. However, both our genetic data on the loss of SPD effects in the rsmZ/rsmY mutant and previous work on the Rsm regulon indicate that RsmA activity could be primarily tuned by changes in RsmZ/Y levels rather than by large shifts in RsmA abundance,35 although minor effects on RsmA expression upon SPD treatment cannot be ruled out. Whether similar mechanisms of SPD-mediated signaling exists in higher order organisms is currently under investigation, which could reveal novel vulnerabilities for therapeutic intervention.

Limitations of this study

Although this study identifies an SPD-binding RNA motif involved in bacterial virulence regulation, the structural basis of SPD recognition remains to be defined. In addition to CafA, other endonucleases also contribute to SPD-induced RsmY/RsmZ degradation, and their identities require further investigation. Whether similar polyamine-small RNA regulatory mechanisms occur in other bacterial species or broader RNA networks remains to be determined.

Experimental model and study participant details

Bacterial strains, media and growth conditions

The bacterial strains and plasmids used in this study are detailed in key resources table. Unless otherwise stated, bacterial strains were grown aerobically at 37 °C in Luria-Bertani (LB) medium or MINS medium, with antibiotics added when necessary. Bacterial growth was monitored by measuring optical density at 600 nm (OD600). To induce TTSS expression, P. aeruginosa was cultured in LB supplemented with 7.5 mM nitrilotriacetic acid (NTA). Spermidine (SPD) was purchased from Sigma-Aldrich and was used at a final concentration of 0.7 mM unless otherwise specified.

Mouse model

Mouse work was conducted according to protocols approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee, Singapore (#100555). Adult female FVB/N mice, 8–10 weeks old, were housed at the Biological Resource Center, Singapore. For infection experiments, bacterial cells were prepared as described under mouse infection assay.

Method details

Molecular cloning and genetic modifications of P. aeruginosa

The plasmids Np19-spdH, Np19-rsmZ, and Np19-rsmY were constructed by inserting PCR-amplified gene-encoding regions into pUCP19. The translational reporter vector pME6013-fha1 and the transcriptional reporter vector pME6016-fha1 were generated by cloning the fha1 promoter region into the pME6013 and pME6016 vectors, respectively, as described previously. Deletion constructs, including NpG3-ΔrsmA, NpG3-ΔexsA, NpG3-ΔrsmY, NpG3-ΔrsmZ, and NpG3-ΔcafA, were generated similarly to the previously published pEX18G-ΔspuE construct. All constructed plasmids were verified by DNA sequencing.

E. coli was transformed by heat shock, and P. aeruginosa was transformed by electroporation unless otherwise specified. Gene deletion in P. aeruginosa was performed as previously described with minor modifications. Briefly, deletion constructs were transformed into E. coli S17-1 and introduced into P. aeruginosa by conjugation. Integrants were isolated on MM agar plates with gentamicin and sucrose selection, followed by PCR screening and validation by DNA sequencing.

For pET14b-rsmA and pET14b-cafA, the coding regions of rsmA and cafA were amplified by PCR from genomic DNA of P. aeruginosa PAO1 and cloned into the corresponding sites of pET14b (Novagen). After verification by DNA sequencing, the expression vectors were transformed into E. coli BL21(DE3), and transformants were grown on LB agar plates containing 150 μg/mL carbenicillin.

β-galactosidase assay

β-galactosidase activity was determined in reporter strains with or without SPD supplementation, normalized against cell number, and plotted as mean ± standard deviation as described previously.

Isothermal titration calorimetry assay

Isothermal titration calorimetry (ITC) analyses were conducted using a Malvern Micro-ITC200 instrument with purified RsmZ and related RNA samples, SPD, and SPD analogues. Experiments were conducted in 50 mM KCl-Tris buffer (pH 8.0) by titrating the indicated small molecules (280 μM) into purified small RNAs (5.6 μM) at 25 °C. Binding isotherms were fitted using a one-site binding model according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

RNA analyses

Bacterial cells were grown to an OD600 of approximately 1.0 and harvested. Total RNA was extracted using the RNeasy Protect Bacteria Mini Kit (Qiagen). Contaminating DNA was removed by DNase I treatment, and purified RNA was quantified using a NanoDrop 1000 spectrophotometer. For real-time RT-PCR, 0.1 μg total RNA was used as template for each reaction, and the SYBR Green RT-PCR kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific) was used to quantify target gene expression on a LightCycler v1.5 system (Roche). The rpoC gene, encoding the β′ subunit of RNA polymerase, was used as the internal control. Results are presented as ratios of target gene expression relative to rpoC.

Real-time RT-PCR primers were as follows: exsA, 5′-CGAGCGGAGAATCCTCTATG-3′ and 5′-CGATGTCGACGATGCTCA-3′; exoS, 5′-CAGCGATGAGCAGGGAGT-3′ and 5′-TGGCTATGGCCACTCTGC-3′; rsmZ, 5′-CGTACAGGGAACACGCAAC-3′ and 5′-ATTACCCCGCCCACTCTTC-3′; rsmY, 5′-AGGACATTGCGCAGGAAG-3′ and 5′-GGGTTTTGCAGACCTCTATCC-3′; and rpoC, 5′-CTGTTCAAGCCGTTCATTTTC-3′ and 5′-CTTGATGGTGGTGGCCATA-3′.

RNA in vitro transcription and UV absorbance assays

The rsmZ, rsmY, and rhyB transcripts were synthesized from PCR products using T7 RNA polymerase. The T7 promoter sequence was engineered into each 5′ primer. PCR products were gel purified and used as templates for in vitro transcription using the TranscriptAid T7 High Yield Transcription Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific). DNA templates were removed by DNase I digestion, and synthesized RNAs were purified using the RNeasy Kit (Qiagen), assessed by electrophoresis, and quantified using a NanoDrop 1000 spectrophotometer.

Unless otherwise specified, purified RsmZ RNA at approximately 10 μM was used for UV absorbance profiling on a NanoDrop 1000 spectrophotometer in the presence of the indicated concentrations of polyamines dissolved in RNase-free Tris-HCl buffer (10 mM, pH 7.5). UV profiling data were extracted from original NanoDrop screen captures, and absorbance values were digitized using WebPlotDigitizer (https://automeris.io/).

RNA melting curve assay

Melting curve assays were performed using SYBR Green on a Bio-Rad CFX96 real-time PCR system. Briefly, synthesized RNA oligonucleotide nc1291 (20 μM) was added to 20 μL of SYBR Green reaction mixture supplemented with the indicated polyamine concentrations. Melting temperature (Tm) was determined according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

RNA degradation assay

In vitro degradation of RNAs by purified CafA was examined in the presence of various concentrations of SPD. Briefly, rsmZ, rsmY, and rhyB RNAs were synthesized in vitro and purified as described above. In total, 500 ng RNA transcript and the indicated concentrations of SPD were mixed with 1 μg purified histidine-tagged CafA in buffer containing 1 mM Tris-HCl, 0.5 mM MgCl2, 10 mM NaCl, and 0.1 mM 2-mercaptoethanol, pH 8.0. The mixture was incubated at 37 °C for 30 min and visualized on a 1% agarose gel by ethidium bromide staining.

Protein expression and purification

Protein expression was induced in bacteria by treatment with 1 mM IPTG at 18 °C overnight. The next morning, cells were centrifuged and disrupted using a French pressure cell, and lysates were cleared by centrifugation. His6-tagged proteins were purified under native conditions using nickel-affinity chromatography according to the manufacturer’s recommendations (Qiagen), followed by dialysis in PBS buffer. Protein purity and integrity were examined by PAGE, and protein concentration was measured using Bradford protein assay solution (Bio-Rad).

Rifampicin chase assay

SPD-depleted P. aeruginosa PAO1 and the ΔcafA strain were grown in MM medium to an OD600 of approximately 1.0, supplemented with 2 mM spermidine, and subsequently treated with rifampicin (500 μg/mL) to block transcription. At 0 and 40 min after rifampicin addition, equal volumes of culture were harvested. Total RNA was purified using TRIzol Reagent, and rsmY and rsmZ transcript levels were measured by RT-qPCR. rpoC was used as the internal control for normalization.

Pyocyanin measurement

Overnight cultures were grown for 24 h in PA medium. After measuring OD600, 7 mL of culture supernatant was mixed with 3 mL chloroform, and 2 mL of 0.2 M HCl was added to the chloroform layer for pyocyanin extraction. Pyocyanin was quantified by measuring absorbance at OD520, and results were normalized against cell number.

Biofilm assay

Biofilm production was assayed as previously described with minor modifications. Overnight cultures were diluted 1:1000 into LB, and triplicate 1 mL aliquots were cultured in polystyrene tubes with SPD supplementation where appropriate. Following static incubation for 18 h, the medium was removed. Tubes were washed gently with PBS, and biofilms were stained with crystal violet and quantified by measuring OD575.

Mouse infection assay

Bacterial cells for mouse infection were prepared as follows. Fresh P. aeruginosa cultures were diluted 1:200 into 10 mL LB medium and grown with shaking at 250 rpm for 4 h at 37 °C. Cells were then harvested and washed three times with PBS. A total of 3 × 105 cells were administered intratracheally into mice for infection.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation unless otherwise indicated. The number of biological replicates is indicated in the figure legends. Statistical significance was assessed using a two-tailed unpaired Student’s t test for comparisons between two groups and one-way ANOVA for comparisons among multiple groups, as indicated in the relevant figure legends. Statistical significance is denoted as follows: ∗, p < 0.05; ∗∗, p < 0.01; ∗∗∗, p < 0.001; ns, not significant.
