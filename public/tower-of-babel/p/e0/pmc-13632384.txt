Preoperative fragmented QRS as a predictor of postoperative left ventricular systolic dysfunction after isolated mitral valve replacement
Authors: İsmail Balaban, Seda Tanyeri Uzel, Ahmet Karaduman, Zeynep Esra Guner, Barkin Kultursay, Cemalettin Yilmaz, Mustafa Ferhat Keten, Kadir Biyikli
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDespite preserved preoperative left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF), many patients develop postoperative left ventricular (LV) systolic dysfunction after mitral valve surgery for chronic mitral regurgitation. Fragmented QRS (fQRS) on surface electrocardiography has been proposed as a simple marker of adverse myocardial substrate. This study evaluated the prognostic value of preoperative fQRS for predicting postoperative LV systolic dysfunction after isolated mitral valve replacement and developed an exploratory perioperative risk model.MethodsThis retrospective study included 279 consecutive patients undergoing elective isolated mitral valve replacement for chronic mitral regurgitation, with or without concomitant tricuspid annuloplasty, between January 2022 and June 2025. Fragmented QRS was assessed on preoperative 12-lead electrocardiography. Postoperative LV systolic dysfunction was defined as LVEF < 50% on early postoperative echocardiography. Multivariable logistic regression analyses, including an exploratory backward stepwise approach, were performed. Model discrimination and calibration were evaluated, and a nomogram was constructed for individualized risk estimation.ResultsFragmented QRS was present in 71 patients (25.4%). Postoperative LV systolic dysfunction occurred in 92 of 279 patients (33.0%) overall. Although preoperative LVEF was similar between groups, patients with fQRS had lower postoperative LVEF (p = 0.036) and a higher incidence of postoperative LV systolic dysfunction compared with those without fQRS (45.1% vs. 28.8%; p = 0.012). In the fully adjusted multivariable model, preoperative fQRS showed a directional association with postoperative LV systolic dysfunction, although this association narrowly missed conventional statistical significance (OR 1.90, 95% CI 0.99–3.64; p = 0.052). The exploratory model demonstrated acceptable discrimination (area under the ROC curve, 0.78). A nomogram was developed to facilitate individualized exploratory risk prediction.ConclusionsPreoperative fQRS showed a directional association with postoperative LV systolic dysfunction after isolated mitral valve replacement, although this association narrowly missed conventional statistical significance in the fully adjusted multivariable model (OR 1.90, 95% CI 0.99–3.64; p = 0.052). When integrated with baseline ventricular function and perioperative variables, fQRS may contribute to exploratory perioperative risk stratification in selected patients. Introduction

Preservation of left ventricular (LV) systolic function after surgical treatment of chronic mitral regurgitation remains a key determinant of both short- and long-term clinical outcomes. Despite substantial advances in surgical techniques, myocardial protection strategies, and perioperative care, a considerable proportion of patients with preoperative left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) within guideline-recommended thresholds develop early or late postoperative LV systolic dysfunction after mitral valve replacement for mitral regurgitation [1, 2]. This apparent dissociation between preserved preoperative systolic function and postoperative ventricular performance suggests that conventional functional parameters may not fully capture the underlying myocardial structural substrate governing postoperative recovery.

In chronic mitral regurgitation, sustained left ventricular volume overload drives progressive myocardial remodelling characterized by cardiomyocyte hypertrophy, extracellular matrix expansion, and interstitial fibrosis. Although surgical correction restores valvular competence, established myocardial fibrosis may persist and limit reverse remodelling, predisposing patients to postoperative LV dysfunction despite preserved preoperative systolic function [3–5]. Cardiac magnetic resonance studies have demonstrated that myocardial fibrosis burden is closely associated with postoperative LV functional trajectory following valvular surgery [6–8]; however, routine CMR use remains constrained by availability and cost, prompting interest in simple surrogate markers. Fragmented QRS (fQRS) on surface electrocardiography reflects heterogeneous ventricular conduction associated with myocardial scar and fibrosis across ischemic and non-ischemic heart diseases [9–12] and has demonstrated prognostic relevance in coronary artery disease, hypertensive heart disease, and non-ischemic cardiomyopathies [13–15]. Although fQRS is best regarded as a marker of adverse myocardial substrate rather than a quantitative fibrosis index [16–18], emerging evidence suggests that preoperative fQRS may identify patients at increased risk of postoperative LV systolic dysfunction after mitral valve surgery [19, 20].

Nevertheless, most available studies have been conducted in relatively small, valve-specific populations with heterogeneous outcome definitions, and the interaction between myocardial structural vulnerability and perioperative surgical stress has not been explored in integrative predictive models. Although contemporary guidelines recommend multimodality imaging and comprehensive risk assessment in the perioperative management of valvular heart disease [1, 2], electrocardiographic markers of myocardial substrate have not yet been systematically incorporated into perioperative risk stratification frameworks. Therefore, the present study aimed to evaluate the prognostic significance of preoperative fQRS for predicting postoperative LV systolic dysfunction in patients undergoing isolated mitral valve replacement and to develop an exploratory predictive model incorporating electrocardiographic, echocardiographic, and surgical variables. Methods

Study design

This study was conducted as a retrospective cohort analysis of consecutive patients who underwent elective isolated mitral valve replacement between January 2022 and June 2025 using data extracted from institutional medical records. The analytical cohort, primary outcome, and covariates were defined at the time of the present analysis.

Because of the retrospective nature of the analysis, the institutional ethics committee waived the requirement for written informed consent. The study was conducted in accordance with the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki.

Study population

Consecutive patients who underwent elective isolated mitral valve replacement for severe mitral regurgitation with either mechanical or bioprosthetic prostheses during the study period were screened for eligibility. Inclusion criteria were as follows: (i) availability of a standard 12-lead surface electrocardiogram (ECG) obtained in the preoperative period; (ii) assessment of LV systolic function by transthoracic echocardiography both before surgery and during the early postoperative period; and (iii) preserved preoperative left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF ≥ 50%).

Patients undergoing mitral valve replacement for predominant mitral stenosis were not eligible for inclusion. Patients with preoperative LVEF < 50%, those who underwent mitral valve repair, concomitant aortic or other left-sided valvular surgery, prior cardiac surgery, or non-elective procedures were excluded; concomitant tricuspid annuloplasty was not considered an exclusion criterion. Additional exclusion criteria included bundle branch block, permanent pacemaker rhythm, congenital intraventricular conduction abnormalities, significant ECG artifacts, inadequate echocardiographic image quality, or missing essential clinical data. These criteria were applied to ensure reliable assessment of fQRS and accurate evaluation of LV systolic function. Of 518 patients screened, 239 were excluded for the above reasons, yielding a final analytical cohort of 279 patients (Supplementary Figure S1).

Clinical and perioperative variables

Demographic characteristics, cardiovascular risk factors, and comorbid conditions (including hypertension, diabetes mellitus, chronic kidney disease, and history of atrial fibrillation) were recorded using standardized data collection forms. Chronic kidney disease was defined in accordance with internationally accepted guidelines [21]. Surgical variables included mitral valve prosthesis type (mechanical or bioprosthetic), the presence of concomitant tricuspid annuloplasty, and aortic cross-clamp duration. Cross-clamp time was incorporated into the analyses as a surrogate marker of perioperative myocardial ischemic burden. Myocardial protection was achieved using Del Nido cardioplegia administered via the antegrade route under moderate systemic hypothermia (32 °C), with repeat doses administered every 45–60 min throughout the procedure.

Electrocardiographic assessment

Preoperative ECGs were obtained using standard settings (paper speed 25 mm/s, calibration 10 mm/mV) and analysed from 12-lead surface recordings. Fragmented QRS was defined as the presence of notching, multiple R waves, or additional R′ deflections within the QRS complex in the absence of bundle branch block, observed in at least two contiguous leads [10–12]. All ECGs were independently evaluated by two experienced investigators who were blinded to clinical, surgical, and echocardiographic data. Interobserver reproducibility was assessed in a randomly selected subset of 60 ECGs (22% of the study cohort). The observed agreement rate was 94.4%, corresponding to a Cohen’s κ coefficient of 0.86 (95% CI 0.74–0.98), indicating excellent interobserver agreement (Supplementary Table S1). In cases of disagreement, discrepant ECGs were jointly re-reviewed until consensus was reached.

Echocardiographic assessment

Transthoracic echocardiography was performed in the preoperative period and during early postoperative follow-up in accordance with current echocardiographic guidelines [22, 23]. Left ventricular ejection fraction was primarily calculated using the biplane Simpson method. Left atrial diameter, left ventricular end-diastolic and end-systolic dimensions, and other standard echocardiographic parameters were recorded concurrently. Postoperative transthoracic echocardiography was performed prior to hospital discharge at a median of 5 (IQR 4–6) postoperative days, following hemodynamic stabilization and completion of intensive care unit stay, in accordance with the institutional practice of routine echocardiographic assessment after transfer to the ward. All echocardiographic measurements were analysed independently of electrocardiographic findings and clinical data, and assessments were conducted under blinded conditions to minimize measurement bias.

Outcome definition

The primary endpoint of the study was the development of postoperative LV systolic dysfunction. Postoperative LV systolic dysfunction was defined as a reduction in LVEF to < 50% at postoperative follow-up. This threshold was selected in accordance with commonly used criteria in the literature for defining clinically relevant LV systolic impairment after mitral valve surgery.

Statistical analysis

The distribution of continuous variables was assessed using visual (histograms and Q–Q plots) and analytical methods. Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation if normally distributed, or as median with interquartile range (IQR) if non-normally distributed. Categorical variables are expressed as counts and percentages.

Missingness was less than 3% for any candidate variable (CRP, monocyte count, and uric acid). No missing values were present among variables retained in the primary multivariable model; therefore, complete-case and multiply imputed analyses yielded numerically identical results (Supplementary Table S2).

Between-group comparisons were performed using the independent samples t test or the Mann–Whitney U test for continuous variables, as appropriate, and the Pearson chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables.

To identify potential determinants of postoperative LV systolic dysfunction, univariable analyses were initially performed (Supplementary Table S3). Candidate covariates were entered into the full multivariable model if they showed p < 0.10 in univariable analyses or were specified a priori as clinically relevant variables (age, sex, preoperative LVEF, fQRS, hypertension, diabetes mellitus, chronic kidney disease, aortic cross-clamp time, and BMI). The fully adjusted model included 9 prespecified predictors. Given the 92 patients who experienced the primary outcome (postoperative LVEF < 50%), the events-per-variable ratio was approximately 10, consistent with the commonly accepted lower threshold for multivariable logistic regression. A backward stepwise selection procedure guided by the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) was subsequently performed to derive a parsimonious exploratory model. Variables were retained if their removal worsened overall model fit according to the AIC, even when individual p-values did not reach conventional statistical significance. Odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were reported. Model discrimination was assessed using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis, and the area under the curve (AUC) was calculated. The discriminative performance of the full and parsimonious models was evaluated using ROC curve analysis. Model calibration was evaluated using calibration plots and the Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test.

Based on the final multivariable model, a nomogram was constructed to enable individualized risk estimation of postoperative LV systolic dysfunction at the patient level. All statistical analyses were performed using appropriate statistical software (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria), and a two-sided p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Study population and baseline characteristics

A total of 279 patients who underwent elective mitral valve replacement, with or without concomitant tricuspid annuloplasty, were included in the study. Fragmented QRS (fQRS) on preoperative electrocardiography was identified in 71 patients (25.4%), whereas 208 patients (74.6%) had no evidence of fQRS.

Baseline demographic, clinical, laboratory, echocardiographic, and operative characteristics according to fQRS status are summarized in Table 1. Patients with fQRS were significantly older than those without fQRS (p = 0.019) and exhibited a markedly higher prevalence of preoperative atrial fibrillation (p < 0.001). A history of smoking was more frequently reported in the fQRS-negative group (p = 0.040). Chronic kidney disease tended to be more common among patients with fQRS; however, this difference did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.083).

Preoperative left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) was similar between groups (p = 0.725). No significant between-group differences were observed in left ventricular dimensions, most laboratory parameters, or cardiopulmonary bypass and aortic cross-clamp durations (Table 1). Concomitant tricuspid annuloplasty was more frequently performed in patients with fQRS compared with those without fQRS (40.8% vs. 25.5%, p = 0.014). Median preoperative QRS duration was 92 ms (IQR 84–100 ms). The fQRS definition was applied uniformly across narrow (≤ 80 ms, 28.3%) and intermediate-width (81–119 ms, 71.7%) QRS morphologies. The distribution of fQRS prevalence and primary outcome events according to QRS-duration category is summarized in Supplementary Table S4.

Postoperative left ventricular systolic function

Despite comparable preoperative LVEF values, postoperative echocardiographic findings differed significantly according to fQRS status (Table 2). Patients with fQRS exhibited a significantly lower postoperative LVEF compared with those without fQRS (p = 0.036). Accordingly, the incidence of postoperative left ventricular systolic dysfunction—defined as an LVEF < 50%—was significantly higher in the fQRS-positive group than in the fQRS-negative group (45.1% vs. 28.8%; p = 0.012).

In addition, the change in LVEF from the preoperative to postoperative assessment (ΔLVEF) was significantly more pronounced in patients with fQRS, indicating a greater postoperative deterioration in LV systolic function (p = 0.038). The full distribution of postoperative LVEF changes across additional thresholds, stratified by fQRS status, is presented in Supplementary Table S5.

In a subset of patients with available 3-month follow-up echocardiography (n = 63, 22.6% of the study cohort), 28 patients had early postoperative LVEF < 50%. Of these, 24 patients (86%) continued to have LVEF < 50% at the 3-month follow-up. Follow-up echocardiography was available as part of routine outpatient clinical care rather than a predefined study protocol. Therefore, these findings are presented as descriptive observations and should be interpreted with caution given the limited size of the follow-up subgroup.

Postoperative rhythm disturbances and early clinical outcomes

Postoperative atrial fibrillation occurred significantly more frequently in patients with fQRS than in those without fQRS (56.3% vs. 40.4%; p = 0.020). In contrast, the incidence of other early postoperative complications—including stroke, need for dialysis, permanent pacemaker implantation, cardiac tamponade, surgical revision, in-hospital mortality, and 30-day mortality—did not differ significantly between the two groups (all p > 0.05) (Table 2).

To explore the potential mediating role of postoperative AF in the association between fQRS and postoperative LV systolic dysfunction, postoperative AF was added to the parsimonious multivariable model. Addition of postoperative AF as a covariate attenuated the magnitude of the fQRS association, whereas postoperative AF itself remained strongly associated with the outcome. Taken together, these findings are compatible with partial mediation and are reported in Supplementary Table S6.

Multivariable logistic regression analyses

To identify independent determinants of postoperative left ventricular systolic dysfunction (LVEF < 50%), a two-step multivariable logistic regression approach was applied.

In the first step, a full model including age, aortic cross-clamp time, preoperative LVEF, body mass index, sex, presence of fragmented QRS (fQRS), hypertension, diabetes mellitus, and chronic kidney disease was constructed (Table 3). In this model, aortic cross-clamp time and preoperative LVEF were independently associated with postoperative left ventricular systolic dysfunction. Each 1-minute increase in aortic cross-clamp time was associated with higher odds of postoperative LVEF < 50% (OR 1.014, 95% CI 1.010–1.020; p < 0.001), whereas higher preoperative LVEF was associated with lower odds of postoperative LV dysfunction (OR 0.868, 95% CI 0.782–0.962; p = 0.007). In the full multivariable model, preoperative fragmented QRS showed a directional association with postoperative LV systolic dysfunction (OR 1.90, 95% CI 0.99–3.64), although this association narrowly missed conventional statistical significance (p = 0.052).

In the second step, backward stepwise variable selection guided by the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) was applied to derive a clinically pragmatic, parsimonious model intended solely to support nomogram development (Table 4). This model should therefore be interpreted as exploratory rather than inferential. In the final stepwise model, aortic cross-clamp time, preoperative LVEF, presence of fQRS, chronic kidney disease, sex, and hypertension were retained. Sex and hypertension remained in the model because their removal increased the AIC. Within this exploratory model, preoperative fQRS was associated with postoperative LVEF < 50% (OR 2.00, 95% CI 1.06–3.81; p = 0.033), together with longer aortic cross-clamp time (OR 1.015, 95% CI 1.010–1.020; p < 0.001) and chronic kidney disease (OR 3.06, 95% CI 1.11–8.56; p = 0.030). Regression coefficients and 95% confidence intervals from the stepwise model are illustrated in Fig. 1. Internal validation using 500 bootstrap replicates demonstrated limited overfitting, with an optimism-corrected AUC of 0.765 compared with an apparent AUC of 0.784 (95% CI 0.727–0.839). At the Youden-optimal threshold, the model demonstrated a negative predictive value of 85.8%, suggesting reasonable ability to identify patients at lower risk for postoperative LV systolic dysfunction (Supplementary Table S7).

Sensitivity multivariable analyses demonstrated generally consistent fQRS effect estimates across alternative model specifications (Supplementary Table S8). Addition of left atrial diameter, bioprosthesis use, or monocyte count resulted in only minor changes in the magnitude of association between fQRS and postoperative LV systolic dysfunction. Inclusion of postoperative atrial fibrillation or concomitant tricuspid annuloplasty attenuated the fQRS estimate to borderline statistical significance (p = 0.062 and p = 0.056, respectively), although effect sizes remained directionally consistent across all models. Model discrimination remained stable, with AUC values ranging from 0.784 to 0.807.

Model performance: discrimination and calibration

Model performance was assessed in terms of both discrimination and calibration. The discriminatory ability of the full multivariable logistic regression model, evaluated using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis, yielded an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.79. In the simplified stepwise model, the AUC was 0.78, indicating that discrimination was largely preserved despite model simplification and supporting the exploratory clinical utility of the parsimonious model (Fig. 2).

Incremental value of fQRS

To formally quantify the incremental contribution of preoperative fQRS beyond established clinical and surgical predictors, fQRS was added to a base model containing aortic cross-clamp time, preoperative LVEF, sex, hypertension, and chronic kidney disease. The addition of fQRS significantly improved overall model fit, as demonstrated by the likelihood-ratio test (χ2(1)= 4.51, p = 0.034). The addition of fQRS increased the model AUC from 0.769 to 0.784 (ΔAUC + 0.015, 95% CI + 0.003 to + 0.023). Complementary measures of incremental predictive performance showed a continuous net reclassification improvement (NRI) of + 0.279 and an integrated discrimination improvement (IDI) of + 0.015. Because continuous NRI has recognized methodological limitations and may overestimate improvements in risk classification, these results should be interpreted as supportive rather than definitive evidence of incremental predictive value. These results are summarized in Supplementary Table S9. Decision curve analysis further demonstrated that incorporation of fQRS into the exploratory prediction model resulted in a modest but consistent improvement in net clinical benefit across clinically relevant threshold probabilities compared with the base model without fQRS (Fig. 4).

Nomogram development

Based on the final stepwise logistic regression model, a nomogram was developed to provide individualized exploratory risk estimation for postoperative LV systolic dysfunction (Supplementary Figure S2). In the nomogram, each variable is assigned a weighted score proportional to its regression coefficient, and the cumulative score corresponds to the estimated probability of postoperative LV dysfunction.

Aortic cross-clamp time, preoperative LVEF, fragmented QRS, and chronic kidney disease contributed the largest proportion of total risk points. This visual tool illustrates the relative contribution of individual predictors identified in the multivariable model and may provide an exploratory framework for perioperative risk stratification.

Discussion

In this study, preoperative fragmented QRS (fQRS) showed a directional association with postoperative left ventricular (LV) systolic dysfunction in patients undergoing mitral valve replacement for mitral regurgitation, with or without concomitant tricuspid annuloplasty, although this association narrowly missed conventional statistical significance in the fully adjusted multivariable model. The direction and magnitude of this association remained generally consistent across multiple adjusted and sensitivity analyses, including the exploratory prediction model. Collectively, these findings support the hypothesis that postoperative LV functional recovery following mitral valve replacement for mitral regurgitation is associated not only with the hemodynamic consequences of valve correction, but also with pre-existing myocardial structural and electrical characteristics. This interpretation aligns with the concept that conventional functional indices alone may not fully capture latent myocardial vulnerability that becomes clinically relevant after surgical intervention [1, 3–5].

Myocardial substrate and postoperative LV functional recovery

Chronic mitral regurgitation is associated with sustained left ventricular volume overload, leading to progressive myocardial remodelling characterized by cardiomyocyte hypertrophy, extracellular matrix expansion, and the development of interstitial and replacement fibrosis. Although mitral valve replacement restores valvular competence and abruptly alters loading conditions, established myocardial fibrosis may persist and be associated with impaired reverse remodelling. Consequently, postoperative LV systolic dysfunction may occur even in patients with preserved preoperative ejection fraction, underscoring the importance of the underlying myocardial substrate in shaping postoperative functional trajectories [4, 5]. Nevertheless, because postoperative LVEF was assessed at a median of 5 postoperative days, transient perioperative factors—including altered loading conditions, myocardial stunning, and temporary inotropic support—may also have contributed to early reductions in LVEF in some patients. Although based on a limited and non-systematic follow-up subgroup, the available 3-month echocardiographic data suggest that early postoperative LV systolic dysfunction frequently persisted beyond the immediate postoperative period.

Cardiac magnetic resonance (CMR) studies using late gadolinium enhancement and quantitative tissue characterization techniques have consistently demonstrated that myocardial fibrosis burden is closely associated with impaired postoperative LV recovery and adverse outcomes after mitral valve surgery for mitral regurgitation [6–8]. However, despite its high diagnostic accuracy, routine use of CMR in the perioperative evaluation of patients with mitral regurgitation remains constrained by availability, cost, and logistical considerations, limiting its widespread application in everyday clinical practice.

Fragmented QRS as a surrogate marker of myocardial vulnerability

Fragmented QRS (fQRS) on surface electrocardiography reflects heterogeneous ventricular conduction resulting from discontinuous myocardial activation. Experimental, histopathological, and cardiac magnetic resonance studies have demonstrated significant associations between fragmented QRS, histologically confirmed myocardial fibrosis, myocardial scar, and late gadolinium enhancement across a wide spectrum of ischemic and non-ischemic heart diseases [9–12, 16–18]. Collectively, these findings support the concept that fragmented QRS reflects adverse myocardial structural remodeling rather than merely representing a prognostic electrocardiographic marker. Importantly, fragmented QRS should not be interpreted as a direct surrogate for imaging-based quantification of myocardial fibrosis. Instead, it may represent a pragmatic and widely available electrocardiographic phenotype associated with an adverse myocardial substrate characterized by electrical heterogeneity and reduced myocardial reserve [12, 15].

Interaction with perioperative stress and surgical factors

A key observation of this study is the interaction between pre-existing myocardial vulnerability, as reflected by fragmented QRS (fQRS), and perioperative ischemic burden, represented by aortic cross-clamp duration. While cross-clamp time consistently emerged as a determinant of postoperative left ventricular (LV) systolic dysfunction, the directionally consistent association observed across the primary, exploratory, and sensitivity analyses may be associated with reduced tolerance to perioperative ischemia–reperfusion stress rather than acting as an isolated determinant of postoperative outcome. This substrate–stress interaction is consistent with contemporary cardiac surgery literature emphasizing that postoperative ventricular performance reflects the combined contributions of intrinsic myocardial characteristics and procedural stress rather than either factor alone [24–26]. Collectively, these observations support the concept that fragmented QRS may identify patients with reduced myocardial tolerance to perioperative ischemia, whereby a pre-existing adverse myocardial substrate becomes more susceptible to the detrimental effects of prolonged aortic cross-clamp time and ischemia–reperfusion injury.

In the present cohort, tricuspid annuloplasty was performed more frequently in patients with fQRS, likely reflecting more advanced atrial and right-sided remodelling rather than a direct cause of postoperative LV systolic dysfunction. Consistent with this interpretation, adjustment for tricuspid annuloplasty attenuated the fQRS estimate to borderline significance, suggesting partial collinearity rather than independent confounding (Supplementary Table S8).

The median aortic cross-clamp time of 189 min (IQR 144–224) exceeded values typically reported in contemporary mitral valve replacement series, reflecting the complex case-mix of a tertiary referral centre with advanced rheumatic mitral regurgitation, frequent subvalvular debridement, and concomitant procedures including tricuspid annuloplasty (29.4%) and Maze surgery (10.0%). The independent association between cross-clamp time and postoperative LV dysfunction further supports the contribution of intraoperative ischemic burden to early postoperative ventricular performance.

The higher prevalence of postoperative atrial fibrillation (AF) among patients with fQRS suggests that AF may partially mediate the association between fQRS and postoperative LV dysfunction. Adjustment for postoperative AF attenuated the fQRS estimate, whereas analyses restricted to patients remaining in sinus rhythm demonstrated directionally consistent effect estimates, indicating that a direct ventricular pathway cannot be excluded. Overall, these findings support the concept that fQRS reflects an adverse electromechanical substrate associated with both postoperative AF and ventricular dysfunction rather than acting solely through an AF-mediated mechanism (Supplementary Table S6).

Taken together, these findings suggest that fQRS represents an accessible electrocardiographic marker of diffuse electromechanical vulnerability rather than merely a ventricular conduction abnormality.

Relation to previous studies

Evidence linking fQRS to postoperative LV systolic dysfunction in valvular heart disease remains limited but increasingly consistent across different surgical settings. Yılmaz et al. [19] demonstrated an association between preoperative fQRS and early postoperative LV dysfunction in a smaller cohort of 85 patients undergoing mitral valve repair, defining the outcome as LVEF < 55% at postoperative day 7. In contrast, the present study included a larger cohort (n = 279), focused exclusively on mitral valve replacement, and used a more stringent definition of postoperative LV systolic dysfunction (LVEF < 50%) assessed on predischarge echocardiography at a median of 5 postoperative days.

Celik et al. [20] extended these observations to patients undergoing aortic valve replacement for severe aortic regurgitation. Importantly, this population represents a pressure-overload remodelling phenotype distinct from the predominantly volume-overload physiology of mitral valve disease. Moreover, postoperative LV dysfunction was assessed at 3 months, thus reflecting mid-term ventricular recovery rather than the immediate postoperative phase evaluated in the present study.

Importantly, the biological basis of fragmented QRS in chronic mitral regurgitation differs from that in ischemic heart disease or pressure-overload conditions. Whereas coronary artery disease predominantly results in replacement fibrosis secondary to myocardial infarction and aortic valve disease is characterized by pressure overload–induced concentric remodelling, chronic mitral regurgitation leads to sustained volume overload, eccentric left ventricular remodelling, and progressive interstitial fibrosis. Despite these distinct remodeling pathways, all ultimately promote heterogeneous myocardial conduction and electromechanical dysfunction, providing a plausible mechanistic explanation for the prognostic value of fragmented QRS across different cardiac diseases, including chronic mitral regurgitation.

Predictive modelling and clinical applicability

Beyond identifying associations, the present study provides an exploratory approach to individualized perioperative risk estimation by integrating fragmented QRS with baseline ventricular function and surgical variables. Despite simplification, the exploratory parsimonious model maintained acceptable discrimination and calibration, supporting its potential use for exploratory risk estimation.

From a practical perspective, identification of fragmented QRS on standard preoperative electrocardiography may offer incremental insight into myocardial vulnerability in patients undergoing mitral valve surgery. In particular, fQRS may help identify patients at increased risk for postoperative left ventricular systolic dysfunction despite preserved preoperative ejection fraction, supporting heightened perioperative awareness, earlier postoperative echocardiographic surveillance, and closer clinical follow-up. However, because fQRS was absent in the majority of patients, it should not be considered a standalone screening tool, and the absence of fQRS should not be interpreted as evidence of low perioperative risk. Rather, patients without fQRS should continue to be risk-stratified using established clinical, echocardiographic, and procedural variables. These implications remain hypothesis-generating and require prospective validation before routine clinical application. Given its limited sensitivity for myocardial fibrosis, fQRS should not be interpreted in isolation but rather integrated within a comprehensive clinical, echocardiographic, and procedural risk assessment.

Although the absolute increase in discrimination after adding fQRS to the base clinico-surgical model was numerically modest, the statistically significant likelihood-ratio test (χ² = 4.51, p = 0.034) provides the primary evidence supporting the incremental value of adding fQRS to the prediction model. Complementary measures of model improvement (IDI and continuous NRI) also suggested modest incremental predictive performance beyond conventional predictors. However, because continuous NRI has recognized methodological limitations and may overestimate improvements in risk classification, these findings should be interpreted as supportive rather than definitive evidence of incremental predictive value. Furthermore, decision curve analysis demonstrated a modest but consistent improvement in net clinical benefit across clinically relevant threshold probabilities after incorporation of fQRS into the exploratory prediction model, providing additional support for its potential clinical utility. This pattern is not unexpected for a single biomarker added to an already moderately performing clinical model.

Limitations

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting these findings. First, the retrospective, single-center design limits causal inference and may affect the generalizability of the results. Second, myocardial fibrosis was not directly quantified using cardiac magnetic resonance imaging or histopathology; instead, fragmented QRS was used as a surrogate marker of myocardial structural disease, which may be prone to misclassification and has limited sensitivity for diffuse fibrosis.

In addition, the analysis focused on early postoperative left ventricular systolic dysfunction defined by an LVEF threshold and did not capture more subtle changes in myocardial mechanics or long-term left ventricular remodelling trajectories. Baseline speckle-tracking echocardiographic parameters, particularly global longitudinal strain (GLS), were also not systematically available. Because GLS is a more sensitive marker of subclinical myocardial dysfunction than LVEF, the absence of baseline strain imaging limited our ability to validate fragmented QRS against contemporary echocardiographic measures of myocardial mechanics and to detect subtle preoperative myocardial dysfunction. Furthermore, early postoperative LVEF measurements, obtained at a median of 5 postoperative days, may be influenced by residual loading conditions and inotropic support, and may not fully reflect the longer-term trajectory of left ventricular functional recovery.

Although concomitant tricuspid annuloplasty was included as a perioperative variable, residual confounding related to disease severity and right-sided involvement cannot be fully excluded. Detailed mitral valve morphological characteristics, including leaflet morphology and the severity of mitral regurgitation, were not systematically incorporated into the multivariable models. Accordingly, their potential interaction with fQRS as a marker of myocardial substrate remains unexplored. Preoperative symptomatic status, functional class, and baseline natriuretic peptide measurements (BNP or NT-proBNP) were also not systematically available or adjusted for. Consequently, we were unable to determine whether fragmented QRS provides incremental prognostic value beyond these well-established biomarkers of myocardial wall stress, which may represent an additional source of residual confounding.

The parsimonious stepwise model was derived using backward elimination guided by AIC, a method known to produce potentially unstable coefficient estimates. Accordingly, this model should be interpreted as clinically exploratory and used solely for nomogram-based risk estimation rather than for formal inference. Moreover, although the exploratory stepwise model demonstrated a statistically significant association between fQRS and postoperative LV systolic dysfunction, the primary fully adjusted multivariable model demonstrated a directional association that narrowly missed conventional statistical significance. Therefore, these findings should be interpreted with appropriate caution pending external validation.

Furthermore, in-hospital mortality was higher than typically reported in contemporary Western MVR series, likely reflecting the advanced mitral regurgitation burden, frequent concomitant procedures, and the complex case-mix of a tertiary referral centre. Importantly, a sensitivity analysis excluding in-hospital deaths yielded consistent effect estimates, supporting the overall robustness of the primary findings. Several sensitivity and exploratory analyses were performed, increasing the possibility of type I error and findings requiring confirmation in independent datasets. Because this cohort represents a tertiary referral population undergoing mitral valve replacement for advanced chronic mitral regurgitation, with a substantial proportion of patients requiring concomitant procedures, the observed event rates may not be directly generalizable to lower-risk contemporary mitral valve replacement populations. Accordingly, external validation of the predictive model in independent cohorts is required before broader clinical application.

Conclusions

In the primary fully adjusted multivariable analysis, preoperative fragmented QRS showed a directional association with early postoperative left ventricular systolic dysfunction following isolated mitral valve replacement, although the association narrowly missed conventional statistical significance. Taken together, the overall findings suggest that fragmented QRS may reflect an adverse electromechanical substrate associated with increased vulnerability to perioperative stress. When interpreted alongside established clinical, echocardiographic, and procedural variables, fragmented QRS may provide complementary information for exploratory perioperative risk assessment. However, these findings should be considered hypothesis-generating and require external validation and prospective confirmation before routine clinical use.
