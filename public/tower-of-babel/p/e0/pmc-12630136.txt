Molecular basis of tRNA aminocarboxypropyl-transferase TapT for substrate recognition
Authors: Wei-Yan Wang, Heng-Rui Liang, Yu-Cong Wu, Ping-Wei Liu, Zhu Wang, Yi-Yang Jiang, Hai-Xue Pan, Gong-Li Tang, Jin-Yue Pu, Ai-Ai Sun
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Abstract3-(3-amino-3-carboxypropyl) uridine (acp3U) is a highly conserved modification found in variable- and D-loops of transfer RNAs (tRNAs) in bacteria and eukarya. TapT has been identified as the enzyme responsible for acp3U modification at position 47 of Escherichia coli tRNAs in the presence of S-adenosylmethionine (SAM), while the specific substrate binding details and catalytic mechanism of TapT remain unknown. Here, we determined the three-dimensional structure of TapT in a SAM-bound state. The overall structure adopts SPOUT folding, with SAM located in an adaptive pocket. The cofactor recognition mode was further evaluated by additional structures of S-methyl-5′-thioadenosine or sinefungin to TapT, together with isothermal titration calorimetry to explore binding affinity and liquid chromatography–mass spectrometry experiments to detect enzymatic activity. Furthermore, we performed fluorescence polarization assays to explore the tRNA recognition mechanism of TapT. It thus provides a thorough molecular basis for TapT substrate recognition and may promote further application in biochemistry, molecular biology, and tumor diagnostics.

Introduction

As an adaptor molecule, transfer RNA (tRNA) plays a crucial role during protein synthesis [1]. Most tRNAs undergo numerous posttranscriptional modifications. Till now, among the up to 150 identified RNA modifications, ~80% are present in tRNA [2]. These modifications are significant for decoding the genetic code and stabilizing the three-dimensional structure of tRNAs [3], which ensures the proper function of tRNAs.

3-(3-amino-3-carboxypropyl) uridine (acp3U) is a hypermodified base found in both bacterial and eukaryotic tRNAs [4]. The acp3U is predominantly located within the V-loop or D-loop of the tRNA molecules. In Escherichia coli, acp3U was identified at position 47 in the V-loop of eight tRNAs [5]. While in vertebrates, it is present at position 20 or 20a in the D-loop of tRNAs [6]. For some organisms, a dihydrouridine derivative acp3D has also been observed at position 47 or 20/20a [7]. As an elongated amino acid chain at the uridine N3 position, the acp moiety had no severe impact on translation capability [4] but conferred thermal stability to tRNA [8], indicating an influence of this modification on the proper folding of tRNA. The level of acp3U is significantly increased in tumor, indicating it may serve as a tumor marker [9]. In addition, acp3U can be further modified. An acetylated acp3U (acacp3U) was identified in Vibrio cholerae [10]. Acp3U has been recognized as a primary attachment site for N-glycans in glycoRNAs in mammalian cells [11, 12], and recent studies show that acp3U was exposed upon de-N-glycosylation, rendering RNA immunostimulatory [13].

The biosynthetic pathway of acp3U has been thoroughly employed [8, 14]. Via comparative genomics, biochemical and cell experiments, a novel enzyme TapT (also known as TuaA or YfiP) was identified as the catalyst for acp3U47 formation in E. coli (Fig. 1A), while its human homologs DTWD1 and DTWD2 were confirmed to be responsible for acp3U20 and acp3U20a formation, respectively [8, 14]. As the last tRNA-modifying enzyme discovered in E. coli, TapT transfers the acp moiety from S-adenosylmethionine (SAM) to specific tRNA molecules [8, 14]. Two other enzymes, Tyw2/Trm12 and Tsr3, also mediate acp transfer but are involved in distinct RNA modification processes. Tyw2/Trm12 contributes to the biogenesis of wybutosine (yW) in archaeal and eukaryotic tRNAs with a Rossmann-fold class-I methyltransferase tertiary structure [15–17]. The other enzyme Tsr3 catalyzes acp transfer during the formation of m1acp3Ψ1191 in yeast 18S ribosomal RNA (rRNA) [18]. It belongs to the TDD superfamily, which shares a DTW domain containing a D/E-X-T/S-W motif. Further structural analysis revealed that Tsr3 folds as a SPOUT-class RNA methyltransferase [18].

TapT also belongs to the TDD family of proteins, indicating it shares a similar core structure with Tsr3 [8, 14]. However, further sequence analysis has revealed that the basic amino acids between TapT and Tsr3 are not conserved [8, 14]. These residues are supposed to be important for RNA binding, implying TapT employs a distinct RNA recognition mode [8, 14]. Therefore, further structural research is necessary to explore the molecular basis for substrate recognition and acp3U formation of TapT. Here, we describe the tertiary structure of TapT in a SAM-bound state, complemented by the structure of TapT in complex with its product S-methyl-5′-thioadenosine (MTA) or a SAM analog sinefungin (SFG). Structural comparison, mutational analysis, isothermal titration calorimetry (ITC), and tRNA modification assays collectively provide the molecular basis for TapT substrate recognition mechanism. Furthermore, fluorescence polarization (FP) assays were performed based on an AlphaFold model to explore the tRNA recognition of TapT. Our study thus paves the way for future developments toward potential applications in biochemistry, molecular biology, and tumor diagnostics.

Materials and methods

Materials

Tris, HEPES, KCl, NaCl, β-mercaptoethanol, dithiothreitol (DTT), imidazole, kanamycin, and ZnSO4 were all purchased from Sangon Biotech (Shanghai, China). SAM and MTA were purchased from Sigma–Aldrich (StLouis, MO, USA). SFG was purchased from TargetMol (Shanghai, China).

Cloning, mutagenesis, and protein expression and purification

The gene encoding full-length TapT or a truncated variant was amplified from the genomic DNA of E. coli (strain B/BL21-DE3) and subsequently cloned into a modified pRSFDuet-1 vector, which encodes an N-terminal His6-SUMO-tag that can be specifically cleaved by SUMO protease (Ulp1 peptidase). For mutants, the plasmids were generated from the pRSFDuet-His6-SUMO-TapT vectors via site-directed mutagenesis polymerase chain reaction (PCR). DNA sequencing was employed to confirm all the recombinant constructs.

All TapT constructs used in this work were expressed in E. coli BL21(DE3) cells. The cells were cultured in LB medium containing kanamycin (12.5 mg/l) and ZnSO4 (160 mg/l) at 37°C until an OD600 of ~0.8 was reached. Then TapT expression was induced by the addition of 0.2 mM isopropyl β-D-1-thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG) followed a 24-h growth period at 18°C. After harvested by centrifugation, cells were resuspended and lysed by an ultrahigh pressure homogenizer (FB-110X-PLUS; Shanghai Litu Machinery Equipment Engineering Co. Ltd.) in lysis buffer (25 mM Tris, pH 8.0, 1 M NaCl, 20 mM imidazole, and 1 mM β-mercaptoethanol) with an additive of 0.25 mM PMSF. Proteins were first purified by affinity chromatography with an Ni-IDA Beads column (Smart-Lifesciences) and followed by cation-exchange chromatography with a 5 ml lexCap SP 6FF column (Smart-Lifesciences) equilibrated with Buffer A (40 mM HEPES, pH 7.4, 50 mM NaCl, and 2 mM β-mercaptoethanol). Proteins were eluted with a linear gradient of Buffer B (40 mM HEPES, pH 7.4, 1 M NaCl, and 2 mM β-mercaptoethanol) from 0% to 100%. Then, proteins were further purified by SEC using a Superdex 16/600 75 pg column (Cytiva) in a storage buffer (40 mM HEPES, pH 7.4, 200 mM NaCl, and 2 mM DTT). The purified TapT protein was frozen and store at −80°C for further use.

Crystallization and X-ray structure determination

Concentrated TapT (10 mg/ml) was incubated with SAM at a molar ratio of 1:12.5. Crystallization screening was conducted using a sitting-drop vapor diffusion setup. High-quality crystals formed under the conditions of 0.2 M lithium sulfate monohydrate, 0.1 M Tris hydrochloride (pH 8.5), and 30% (w/v) polyethylene glycol 4000 (Hampton Research). Crystals were carefully transferred to reservoir solutions containing 20% (v/v) glycerol and then looped and rapidly frozen in liquid nitrogen for data collection. The crystals of TapT with SFG or MTA were obtained under the aforementioned conditions with a crystal optimization procedure. The crystal diffraction data were collected at the National Center for Protein Sciences Shanghai (NCPSS) or the Shanghai Synchrotron Radiation Facility (SSRF) on beamlines BL18U1, BL19U1, BL02U1, and BL10U2. The data were further processed and scaled using the XDS package [19].

The structure of TapT–SAM complex was solved by molecular replacement with a model generated by alphafold2 via Phaser [20]. The model was further refined using phenix.refine [21] and manually adjusted in COOT [22]. The structure and restraints of SFG were generated in eLBOW from the PHENIX suite [23]. All structural figures were generated with PyMOL (www.pymol.org) or Chimera X [24]. The X-ray statistics of all crystals are listed in Table 1.

Data collection and refinement statistics

Isothermal titration calorimetry

ITC experiments were performed using a MicroCal PEAQ ITC microcalorimeter at 20°C. Both proteins and ligands were in buffer (40 mM HEPES, pH 7.4, 200 mM NaCl, and 2 mM β-mercaptoethanol). Either wild-type or mutated TapT with a concentration ranging from 50–80 μM was titrated with SAM or MTA, respectively. The first injection was 0.4 μl, followed by 19 injections of 2 μl each with 120 s intervals. The data were analyzed using a one-site binding model via the MicroCal PEAQ-ITC analysis software package (provided by the manufacturer).

Surface plasmon resonance

Surface plasmon resonance (SPR) experiments were performed using a BIAcore 1K system equipped with a CM5 sensor chip at 25°C. The chip surface was activated with 1-ethyl-3-(3-dimethylaminopropyl) carbodiimide and N-hydroxysuccinimide, followed by immobilization of protein samples in buffer (10 mM sodium acetate, pH 4.5) at 10 μl/min for 180 s. After blocked with 1 M ethanolamine (10 μl/min, 420 s) and equilibrated in running buffer (40 mM HEPES, pH 7.4, 200 mM NaCl), SAM or SFG was flowed over the sensor chip at 30 μl/min using either a 120 s association/120 s dissociation or a 180 s association/180 s dissociation kinetics protocol. The surface was regenerated with 50 mM NaOH between different cycles. The data were analyzed with BIAcore 1K Evaluation software (Cytiva) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Multiangle light scattering analysis

For multiangle light scattering (MALS) analysis, samples including TapTFL D137N, E. coli tRNAMet-CAU, or their complex were injected into an AKTA FPLC system (GE Healthcare) equipped with a Superdex 200 Increase 10/300 GL column (GE Healthcare) in the buffer containing 40 mM HEPES (pH 7.4), 50 mM KCl, 2 mM MgCl2, and 1 mM DTT. The chromatography system was integrated with a static light scattering detector (miniDAWN, Wyatt Technology) and a differential refractive index detector (Optilab, Wyatt Technology). Data were acquired every 0.5 s at a flow rate of 0.5 ml/min and processed using ASTRA 6 software (Wyatt Technology).

In vitro transcription of tRNA

The DNA sequence for tRNA transcription was cloned into a pUC19 vector with a hepatitis delta virus ribozyme sequence followed. The DNA template was amplified using PCR and verified by sequencing. The tRNA sample was prepared by in vitro transcription with a lab-purified bacteriophage T7 RNA polymerase. The transcribed RNA was separated with denaturing urea polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis. The tRNA was annealed at 65°C for 5 min in a refolding buffer containing 50 mM HEPES (pH 7.9), 50 mM KCl, and 2 mM MgCl₂ and cooled at room temperature for half an hour. The tRNA was further purified by SEC with a Superdex 16/600 75 pg column (Cytiva) in a buffer containing 40 mM HEPES (pH 7.4), 200 mM NaCl, and 2 mM MgCl2 and stored at −80°C for further use.

Fluorescence polarization assays

FP assays were performed using a Synergy NEO2 multi-mode plate reader (BioTek) with excitation at 485 nm and emission at 530 nm. The 5′-Fluorescein (FAM)-labeled tRNAMet-CAU was purchased from GENEray Biotechnology and refolded as described above in the refolding buffer. The FP assays were conducted using a black 384-well plate at room temperature. The concentration of 5′-FAM-labeled tRNA was diluted at 20 nM, meanwhile the concentration of TapT was varied from 0 to 15 μM in Buffer (20 mM HEPES, pH 7.4, 50 mM KCl, 2 mM MgCl2, 2mM β-mercaptoethanol) without extra SAM or MTA added. Three independent experimental replicates were carried out. The data were plotted in GraphPad Prism and the dissociation constant Kd was estimated as described [25].

tRNA modification assay in vitro

The reconstitution of acp3U modification was conducted in vitro at 37 °C for 1 h in a reaction mixture containing 40 mM HEPES (pH 7.4), 10 mM MgCl2, 100 mM NaCl, 50 mM KCl, 1 mM DTT, 1 mM SAM, 20 μM tRNAMet-CAU, and 1 μM full-length TapT. The tRNA product was digested with 50U nuclease P1 (NEB) and 2U FastAP Thermosensitive Alkaline Phosphatase (Thermo Fisher) at 37°C for 12 h. The product was further analyzed on a Shimadzu LC-20A HPLC system equipped with a ShimNex CS C18 column (4.6 × 250 mm, 5 μm particle size). The mobile phase consisted of Solvent A (5 mM ammonium acetate in H2O) and Solvent B (CH3OH), with the following gradient: 0–5 min, 5% Solvent B; 5–20 min, linear gradient from 5% to 50% Solvent B; 20–21 min, linear gradient from 50% to 95% Solvent B; 21–23 min, linear gradient from 95% to 5% Solvent B. This process was conducted at a flow rate of 1 ml/min with UV detection at 254 nm. MS analysis was performed on an LCMS-2020 system (Shimadzu) mass spectrometer equipped with an electrospray ionization source.

Results and discussion

The overall structure of TapT–SAM complex

SAM was identified as an acp donor of TapT previously [8, 14]. To illustrate the molecular interaction between TapT and SAM, we initially tried to co-crystallize SAM with the full-length TapT (TapTFL) but unfortunately failed. Subsequently, we analyzed the secondary structure with Jpred website of the protein and generated a truncated TapT (from residue R29 to Q219, termed TapT29–219 hereafter) that lacks the flexible N-terminal region and C-terminal tail. We successfully co-crystallized TapT29–219 with SAM and solved the structure at 2.05 Å resolution. The details of the data collection and refinement statistics were summarized in Table 1. There are two TapT29–219 proteins per asymmetric unit, with an R.M.S.D. around 0.152 Å. Besides, one loop region spanning residues 174–178 was not visible in the electron density.

At the N-terminus of TapT29–219, a zinc ion is coordinated by four cysteine residues C31, C34, C41, and C43, forming a zinc finger motif, in accordance with previous research [14] (Fig. 1B). The motif may contribute to the tRNA binding ability of TapT, which requires further research. Besides the zinc finger motif, the overall structure adopts a globular α/β structure consisting of a four-parallel-stranded β-sheet sandwiched by α-helices on both sides. Notably, the whole structure exhibits a deep trefoil knot, which indicates the classic SPOUT-like RNA-methyltransferase fold of TapT [26].

SAM recognition by TapT

In the complex structure, the acp-donor SAM is clearly visible in the electron density map and located in a deep crevice around at region I (β1–α1), region II (β3–η2), region III (β4–α3), region IV (α4–α5), and the trefoil knot region of TapT (Figs 1B and 2A). The surface representation of the SAM-binding pocket shows that the adenine base and acp tail of SAM are exposed to the solvent, with the sugar ring deeply embedded in TapT (Fig. 2C). The adenine base and sugar ring of SAM are located in a hydrophobic cavity formed by V109, P111, V161, L163, V185, Y180, and W140. Notably, the adenine base engages in a π–π interaction with P111, and its N7 atom forms a hydrogen bond with the amide backbone of Y180. A water molecule intermediates hydrogen bonds between the adenine N1 atom and the amide backbone of V161 (Fig. 2D). The two hydroxyl groups, 2′-OH and 3′-OH, form hydrogen bonds with the main chains of V109 and L136, respectively (Fig. 2D). The sulfonium cation linkage methyl group was buried in the pocket, with the conserved tryptophan residue W140 acting as a cap above it. The acp tail of SAM interacts with TapT via its α-COO− group, forming hydrogen bonds with the side chains of N69 and T70 as well as the main chain amides from T70 and G71 (Fig. 2E). Furthermore, we note that the sulfonium cation of SAM is in close proximity to the oxygen atoms of the side chains of D137 (OD1, 5.4 Å) and T182 (OG1, 3.9 Å), as well as the main chain oxygen of G138 (3.1 Å, Fig. 2E).

The spatial orientation of the oxygen atoms around the sulfonium cation was also observed for other acp transferase structures. In Tsr3, OD1 of D70, OG1 of T112, and the main chain oxygen of C71 point at the sulfonium cation with distances of 4.3 Å, 3.7 Å, and 3.0 Å, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 1A). In Tyw2, the sulfonium cation is surrounded by the main chain oxygen atoms of F133 (4.1 Å), A134 (5.3 Å), and G199 (3.9 Å), respectively (Supplementary Fig. 1B). This conserved spatial distribution suggests a common role for these oxygen atoms in stabilizing the sulfonium cation of SAM. Meanwhile, the residue D137 is within the D/E-X-T/S-W motif and highly conserved among TDD superfamily, which has been thought crucial for its acp transferase activity [14].

Calorimetric analysis and tRNA modification assay

Mutational analysis together with ITC was performed to evaluate the impact of individual key residues discussed above. We found that wild-type TapT29–219 binds SAM with micromolar affinity (Kd of 57.7 μM) under our experimental conditions (Fig. 3A), which is similar to those in the previous study [14]. Mutation of P111 (that engaged in a π–π interaction with the adenine base of SAM) to G resulted in a loss binding to SAM (Fig. 3B). To assess the impact of W140 as its capping role for the methyl group of SAM, we mutated W140 to A140. Not unexpectedly, the W140A mutant no longer binds to SAM (Fig. 3C). Further to test the importance of the oxygen atoms surrounded the sulfonium cation of SAM, we investigated three mutations, D137A, D137N, and T182A. The dissociation constant of the T182A variant is 469 μM, while the dissociation constants of the D137N and D137A variants are 390 μM and 590 μM, respectively (Fig. 3D–F). All three mutations dramatically reduced their binding abilities toward SAM.

ITC assay of TapT29–219 binding with SAM. Raw ITC data (top) and integrated heat signals fitted to a one-site binding model (bottom) for (A) wild type, (B) P111G, (C) W140A, (D) D137N, (E) D137A, and (F) T182A. Abbreviation: ND, no detectable interaction under the experimental conditions.

We further evaluated the enzymatic activity of these mutants using an in vitro tRNA modification assay, with LC–MS to detect acp3U formation in tRNAMet-CAU (Fig. 4 and Supplementary Fig. 2). Wild-type TapTFL efficiently catalyzed acp3U formation, whereas P111G and W140A mutants exhibited <20% of wild-type activity. The D137A, D137N, and T182A mutants showed a more severe reduction in activity (<10% of wild type), consistent with their impaired SAM binding. Collectively, these results confirm that the residues identified in the crystal structure are critical for both SAM recognition and catalytic activity of TapT.

Structure of TapT–MTA complex

TapT can also bind to MTA, the product of SAM for TapT, although the affinity to MTA is reduced in comparison to the cognate cofactor SAM. ITC analysis gave a roughly decreased dissociation constant Kd of 150.0 μM for MTA (Supplementary Fig. 3). To further explore the recognition mode of SAM, we co-crystallized TapT29–219 with MTA and solved the TapT–MTA complex at 2.35 Å resolution.

The SAM-binding pocket of TapT retains its architecture also when bound to MTA (Fig. 5B). The adenine base and sugar ring of MTA are inserted in the hydrophobic cavity formed by V109, P111, V161, L163, V185, Y180, and W140. Likewise, the original SAM acp group is substituted by a water molecule that establishes hydrogen bonds with T70 and T182, thereby stabilizing the conformation of the pocket region. The sulfur atom of MTA is also surrounded by the oxygen atoms from the side chain of D137 (OD1, 5.0 Å) and T182 (OG1, 3.2 Å) and the main chain oxygen of G138 (4.3 Å), with similar distances comparison to those in the original TapT–SAM complex, which indicates the sulfer atom orientation of SAM in TapT.

Structure of TapT–SFG complex

SAM donates methyl, aminocarboxypropyl, adenosyl, and amino moieties in distinct SAM-dependent enzymes [27]. In most cases, sulfonium cation plays a crucial role during the catalysis procedure. In order to test the role of the sulfonium cation of SAM in TapT, we tried to co-crystallize TapT with a C-mustard SAM analog SFG (Fig. 5C). In SFG, besides the S-to-C exchange, the original methyl moiety is substituted with an amino group [28]. The dissociation constant of wild-type TapT29–219 bound to SFG measured by SPR experiment is 795 μM, which is approximately seven-fold weaker compared to SAM (Kd of 116 μM, measured by SPR as well; Supplementary Fig. 4). The structure of the TapT–SFG complex was solved at 1.94 Å resolution.

SFG is also bound within the SAM-binding pocket of TapT in TapT–SFG complex while exhibiting some discernible differences (Fig. 5C). First, within TapT–SFG complex, the 2′-OH of SFG’s sugar ring forms water-mediated hydrogen bonds with L136 and V109. Second, the carbon linkage amino group (originally sulfonium cation linkage methyl group in SAM) points outward and generates a hydrogen bond with the main chain of Y180. Finally and most importantly, the carbon atom in SFG (originally sulfonium cation in SAM) moves away from the oxygen atoms previous discussed. The distances of the carbon atom in SFG toward OD1 of D137, OG1 of T182, and the main chain oxygen of G138 significantly shift from 5.4 Å to 7.6 Å, 3.9 Å to 4.8 Å, and 3.1 Å to 4.0 Å, respectively. The aforementioned phenomenon suggests that SAM serves as a natural substrate molecule for TapT, wherein its sulfonium cation plays a crucial role not only in enzyme activity but also in the recognition and precise localization of SAM by TapT.

Comparison with Tsr3 and Trm10

A DALI search implied two similar folding structures Tsr3 (DALI Z-score 9.4) and Trm10 (DALI Z-score 6.0). Tsr3 has been previously reported as an acp transferase for rRNA m1acp3Ψ1191 biogenesis [18], while Trm10 is a structurally well-studied methyl transferase responsible for N1 methylation of G9/A9 in archaeal and eukaryotic tRNAs [29, 30]. The complex structure of TapT–SAM was compared with that of Tsr3–SAM (PDB ID, 5APG) and Trm10–SAH (PDB ID, 4JWF).

The overall structures of TapT, Tsr3, and Trm10 all exhibit a typical SPOUT folding architecture (Supplementary Fig. 5). However, the number of β-strands among these proteins increases successively, with TapT showing four parallel β-strands, and Tsr3 five and Trm10 six, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 5).

Regarding the SAM-binding pocket, the cofactor molecule is embedded more deeply in either Tsr3–SAM or Trm10–SAH complex (Fig. 2B and Supplementary Fig. 6). In the Tsr3–SAM complex, the adenosine moiety forms hydrogen bonds with the main chain of L93, Y108, and L110, thus not exposing to solvent environment compared with that in TapT–SAM complex. The sulfonium cation linkage methyl group is capped by a conserved tryptophan residue W73, which makes sure it was buried in the SAM-binding pocket. In Trm10–SAH complex, the homocysteine group interacts with D207, N209, L214, and C215 and exhibits a strikingly inward fold compared to the acp group in both TapT–SAM and Tsr3–SAM complexes. This conformation in Trm10–SAH facilitates the embedding of both adenosine and homocysteine groups within the binding pocket, with only sulfur atoms being exposed. The conserved tryptophan residue in TDD superfamily proteins, in conjunction with the subtle difference in the configuration of the cofactors, may explain why TapT and Tsr3 function as acp transferases rather than methyltransferases.

tRNA recognition mechanism of TapT

To illustrate the tRNA recognition mode of TapT, we purified the full-length TapT (TapTFL) and transcribed E. coli tRNAMet-CAU in vitro. MALS analysis of TapTFL and tRNAMet-CAU suggests that the complex formed with an 1:1 molecular ratio (Supplementary Fig. 7). To validate the region that is important for tRNA recognition, a model of TapTFL bound to E. coli tRNAMet-CAU was generated using AlphaFold3 (Fig. 6A). The sequence of tRNAMet-CAU is shown in Fig. 1A. The model predicts that TapT interacts with the V-loop (containing U47, the modification site), T-loop, and D-loop of tRNA. Electrostatic surface analysis of the interface toward tRNA revealed two large positively charged surface patches (Patch1, Patch2) on TapT (Fig. 6A). Patch1 contains R16, R19, R24, and R27, while Patch2 contains R88, R144, K145, R148, and K149. Similar results were observed in other AlphaFold prediction structures with different tRNA molecules. Then, we conducted FP assays to investigate the interaction mode between TapT and tRNA (Fig. 6B and Supplementary Table 1). The wild-type TapTFL interacts with tRNAMet-CAU with a Kd of 0.80 μM, while the Patch1 variant (R16A/R19A/R24A/R27A) dramatically reduced binding affinity by ~50-fold (Kd of 40.04 μM). This finding is in accordance with previous research that the N-terminal sequence influences RNA binding significantly [14]. And the Patch2 variant (R88A/R144A/K145A/R148A/K149A) has also demonstrated a marked reduction in binding affinity, with a Kd of 9.97 μM. Further tRNA modification assay showed that no acp transfer activity was detected of Patch1 or Patch2 variant (Fig. 4 and Supplementary Fig. 8). Meanwhile, D137 of TapT and U47 of tRNAMet-CAU are positioned on opposite sides of SAM, suggesting that TapT may employ a relatively complicated catalytic mechanism, which requires further investigation.

(B) Fluorescence polarization assays to evaluate the influence of the two patches region for tRNA binding. Three independent experiments were performed.

Herein, we reported the complex structure of TapT with its acp-donor SAM, product MTA, or a C-mustard SAM analog SFG. SAM-binding pocket analysis of TapT–SAM complex revealed that the methyl group attached to the sulfonium cation is capped by a tryptophan residue (W140) conserved among the acp transferases of the TDD superfamily but absent in structure-related methyltransferases. This finding indicates the disparate enzymatic abilities of these two enzyme classes with the same SAM donor. The sulfonium cation itself in SAM is in proximity to the oxygen atoms in TapT–SAM and TapT–MTA complexes stated in this work, as well as in previously reported structures, Tsr3–SAM or Tyw2–SAM. However, the substituted carbon atom of SFG (originally sulfonium cation in SAM) in TapT–SFG complex moves far away from the oxygen atoms surrounding the sulfonium cation of SAM mentioned in TapT–SAM complex. All these observations suggest that sulfonium cation of SAM plays a crucial role not only in enzyme activity but also in the recognition and precise localization of the cofactor. Additionally, our study identified two basic patches of TapT that has been validated important for tRNA recognition. In summary, our research thus provides a comprehensive molecular basis for TapT substrate recognition and may promote further application in biochemistry, molecular biology, and tumor diagnostics.
