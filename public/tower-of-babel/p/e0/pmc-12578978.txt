Design of a compact digital to analog converter with optical Kerr effect
Authors: Fariborz Parandin, Mohammadreza Jomour, Babak Karami
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This article presents a novel 2-bit digital-to-analog converter design based on photonic crystals. In this structure, a nonlinear Kerr effect has been considered to transfer the light intensity inside the ring resonator, which can control the optical behavior of the digital-to-analog converter. There are two nonlinear ring resonators on top and bottom of the structure. Also, inside each ring, some nonlinear rods have been shown in black. The rods designed in this structure are based on Silicon, and their refractive index is 3.46. Also, the linear and nonlinear refractive index for black rods in the ring resonators are 1.4 and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$10^{{ - 14{\text{m}}^{2} }} /_{{\text{W}}}$$\end{document} , respectively. This structure has a footprint of 324 µm2, of which there are 31 linear rods in the X direction and 31 linear rods in the Y direction. Also, the sampling rate is 125 GS/s, with the lowest casualties in output power. The analysis of this proposed digital-to-analog converter uses finite difference time domain (FDTD) and Plane Wave Expansion (PWE) methods.

Introduction

Since the development of transistor technology, the transistor has been recognized as one of the main components of an electronic circuit. In an electronic transistor that operates based on electrons, it can be simply figured out that for switching, electrons must be moved from one side of the transistor to the other. By considering transistors in a smaller size, we can figure out that the way electrons are moved and the time to switch will be much lower. Also, increasing the number of transistors on a chip will be possible, which would allow us to have a high-speed chip. However, for an obvious time in the future, miniaturization will finally reach its physical limits, and the physical parameters will not be able to decrease the size of transistors further. So, we will see obviously an ending in the growth of processor speeds.

For this reason, in recent years, interest in replacing electronic processors with photon-based processors has increased. There is also an interest in making information transmission and processing systems entirely optical. One of the structures that has been a special niche for researchers is photonic crystals. These structures have a myriad of arrangements that can be disparate in one-dimensional, two-dimensional, or three-dimensional1–4.

Today, photonic crystal fibers (PCFs) have had great importance in all-optical communication circuits because they propose high processing speed, excellent efficiency with lower power loss, and greater accuracy in data processing compared to electronic circuits5–8. This has led to a lot of extensive research on photonic crystal fibers in various fields over the past few decades, being done by researchers. Applications of photonic crystals include optical fibers, sensors, and filters 9–14.

One of the most important applications of photonic crystals is their use in digital circuits5,15–18. Among the digital circuits that have been designed based on photonic crystals, various logic gates can be mentioned. Additionally, various digital circuits such as decoders, encoders, demultiplexers, comparators, subtractors, and adders have also been designed with the use of this structure19–26.

Most signals in the real world are continuous, and to process them with computers, they must first be converted into discrete signals. Discrete signals can be converted into discrete quantities through analog-to-digital converters (ADCs). Likewise, digital-to-analog converters (DACs) can convert discrete optical digital signals into optical analog signals. One of the new applications of photonic crystals is all-optical ADCs and DACs27–31.

Researchers have shown that the optical behavior of photonic crystals depends on their refractive index. In other words, the refractive index of the dielectric material depends on the intensity of the input light, which is known as the Optical Kerr effect. Photonic crystal structures can be used for designing ADCs because the intensity of light can control their optical behavior. In 2017, Moniem et al.28 presented a 3-bit all-optical digital-to-analog converter (DAC) based on photonic crystals. In their proposed structure, three nonlinear ring resonators have been designed to control the light intensity through the nonlinear Kerr effect. The footprint of this proposed structure is about 1628 µm2 and has a reported error rate of 2.3%. It is considered that this structure was the first research design in the field of digital-to-analog converters.

Indira Gandhi et al. (2019)29 presented a 2-bit all-optical DAC that included two nonlinear ring resonators for converting discrete digital signals into optical analog signals based on photonic crystals. In this proposed structure, the reported error rate and the footprint of the structure are 4.8477% and 358 µm2, respectively.

In 2020, Sridarshini et al.30 proposed a new design for a DAC. Their proposed structure was a 4-bit optical converter that combined two 2-bit digital-to-analog converters. In fact, their proposed structure was an updated version of the structure they presented in 2019 for an optical digital-to-analog converter. There are four nonlinear ring resonators in this proposed structure. Also, the footprint and reported error rate in this structure are 1150 µm2 and 2.79%, respectively.

The proposed structure, which has been examined in this study, has paid profound attention to all the issues mentioned in previous studies. Also, a DAC with two square-shaped nonlinear ring resonators is offered based on a photonic crystal structure. The proposed structure consists of two parts. One part relates to the design and additional explanation of the converter, and the other part involves examining the optical behavior of the converter in different modes.

In addition to the introduction section, the other sections of this paper are as follows: In Section "Initial design for proposed converter", the proposed DAC design is discussed, and the numerical results obtained are reviewed. Section "Design of optical digital to analog converter" contains the conclusion. In this paper, the design and simulation of a digital-to-analog converter have been done manually through the RSoft CAD Simulation in version 2022.

Initial design for proposed converter

As mentioned in the introduction, digital-to-analog converters (DACs) have a great place in optical circuits based on photonic crystals. In fact, these converters are designed to exchange measurable discrete optical signals (binary codes 0 and 1) into continuous signals. Designing a 1-bit DAC requires considering several important parameters. If the purpose of these converters was to convert binary codes into continuous signals, a logical port and a bias input port should be considered at the input of the structure.

As shown in Fig. 1, the logical port in the structure has a duty to apply binary bits 0 and 1 as input power into the structure. The bias input port is also responsible for applying and resonating the main light within the structure. In fact, it can be said that the most important component in designing DACs is the logical port because it has a lot of duty to control, direct, and apply the light inside the structure.Fig. 11-bit digital-to-analog converter structure.

1-bit digital-to-analog converter structure.

When binary bits 0 and 1 are applied as input light power through the logical port to the structure, a drop port is needed for more controllability and to display the best output state in the structure. In the next section, there will be a comprehensive and complete explanation of the mechanism of the mentioned ports.

To guide light within waveguide paths, a photonic bandgap is created using an alternating structure. Common methods for this include square and hexagonal lattices. In both cases, the photonic bandgap is determined by the physical characteristics and lattice type of the structure. This means the bandgap can be adjusted to a desired range by altering these physical parameters. However, the square lattice offers a simpler unit cell, making it less complex to analyze and fabricate. Therefore, a square lattice was chosen for the proposed structure.

In this proposed structure, the Lattice Constant is a = 600 nm. To design the proposed DAC, a photonic crystal with a square arrangement of dielectric rods in an air background has been considered. In this structure, the refractive index of the dielectric rods is 3.46, and the radius of the rods is r = 0.2a. The resonance wavelength in the structure is also 1550 nm. Figure 2 shows the Photonic Band Gap (PBG) diagram for the proposed structure.Fig. 2PBG diagram for TE (red) and TM (blue) in the proposed photonic crystal.

PBG diagram for TE (red) and TM (blue) in the proposed photonic crystal.

The PBG is a region where light cannot pass through the dielectric rods in the structure. This optical diagram is obtained through Plane Wave Expansion (PWE) by using band structure computations. As shown obviously in Fig. 2, the initial structure has two PBGs in the TM mode. The main PBG has a normalized frequency range of 0.29 < a/λ < 0.42. A simple calculation shows that the wavelength range will be from 1.43 µm < λ < 2.07 µm.

Design of optical digital to analog converter

As mentioned in the previous sections, a DAC has a duty to convert binary codes to continuous signals. In the final version of the structure, two square nonlinear ring resonators, PCRR1 and PCRR2, have been designed. To control the light inside the structure, three optical waveguides with the names L1, L2, and L3 are used. There are some black nonlinear dielectric rods above and below each of the ring resonators because of the use of the optical Kerr effect. The main components of a DAC are the logic port, bias input port, and drop port. As shown in Fig. 3, two bias input ports are considered above and below each ring resonator. The operation of these bias input ports has been to intensify the input power with a fixed intensity within the structure. The input power for both bias input ports is 1mW/µm2. These two bias input ports consistently have input power in the four stages of the simulation.Fig. 3Final structure of 2-bit optical DAC based on Photonic crystal.

Final structure of 2-bit optical DAC based on Photonic crystal.

In the middle of each ring resonator, a logic port is designed to apply the binary code, where each logic port represents a binary bit of 0 or 1. In fact, the optical control and switching of the structure in different states in a DAC are performed through the logic ports. When a logic port is turned on and has input power, it means binary code 1. When the logic port does not have input power, it means binary code 0. The operation of the logic ports will be like that: when a logic port is turned off, the bias input power resonating through the respective port is coupled into the ring resonator through the nonlinear dielectric rods and exits through the drop port, in this state binary code will be 0. When the logic port is turned on (it has input power), the input power from the bias port does not enter the ring resonator, and it will couple towards the output port. The power directed by the logic port into the structure will prevent the power from entering the ring resonator by the nonlinear dielectric rods because of the Kerr effect, so that the power will exit from the main output port. In this state, the binary code is 1. The logic port in PCRR 1 is considered bit (MSB) X, and the logic port in PCRR 2 is considered bit (LSB) Y. Bit X has the highest value, and bit Y has the lowest value, as shown in Fig. 3.

Important parameters in the operation of a DAC

In an all-optical digital-to-analog converter, there are some important parameters in the function of the converter. These parameters are Relative Output Power, Desired Relative Output, Insertion Loss, and Absolute Error, respectively. The Output Power is the amount of power emitted to the output that will be obtained from the simulation results by the Finite Difference Time Domain (FDTD) method. Relative Output Power is the ratio of the output power in different digital states to the maximum output power obtained for the highest input power value. The desired Relative Output will calculate the decimal equivalent of the logic input. Simulation and analysis of the final structure result

In the previous sections, an explanation of the proposed structure and its operation was given. In this section, a complete analysis of the structure is provided, and its function will be illustrated precisely. The analysis of the structure will be regarding four different simulation states (binary codes 00, 01, 10, and 11). Both input ports (Input Bias Port 1 and Input Bias Port 2) are always turned on with a fixed power of 1 mW/µm2 for all four states. The logic ports (Logic Port 1 and Logic Port 2) in this converter have a duty to control and switch within the structure, and by switching these two ports (turning on or off), we will have four output states in the structure. In other words, they can allow or prevent light from coupling into the ring resonators; this operation is called the final mechanism of a DAC.

In this structure, there are two paths to exit the power in each ring resonator, and these two paths are named Drop Port 1 and Drop Port 2. These ports are located below each logic port and are activated when the binary code 0 is turned on in the structure. Figure 4 shows how the structure operates for the four different input states. Additionally, Fig. 5 examines the output values obtained for the four different states (00, 01, 10, and 11). It should be noted that the nonlinear state in this structure is manually set according to the software help documentation and obtained through RSOFT CAD Simulation version 2022.Fig. 4Analysis of the power in the Proposed DAC Structure based on Photonic Crystal-Based (a) X = Y = 0, (b) X = 0, Y = 1, (c) X = 1, Y = 0 and (d) X = Y = 1.Fig. 5Output power values in the proposed DAC structure.

Analysis of the power in the Proposed DAC Structure based on Photonic Crystal-Based (a) X = Y = 0, (b) X = 0, Y = 1, (c) X = 1, Y = 0 and (d) X = Y = 1.

Output power values in the proposed DAC structure.

State X = 0, Y = 0: In this state, both logic ports are turned off. Therefore, the power that is transferred inside the structure through the input bias port is coupled into the ring resonators via the nonlinear dielectric rods and finally exits the structure through the drop port.

State X = 0, Y = 1: In this state, the logic port in the ring resonator PCRR 2 has an input power of 1 (100 mW) and prevents the coupling of the input power into the ring resonator. Consequently, the input power will transfer towards the main output and waveguide L3 through waveguide L2. The input power of port 1 also exits the structure through the drop port because the logical port is turned off. The output power obtained in this state is 0.73.

State X = 1, Y = 0: In this state, logic port 1 has an input power of 1 (100 mW). When the input power is transferred inside the structure, the logic port prevents the power from entering the power ring PCRR 1 according to previous explanations, which causes the power to move from waveguide L1 to waveguide L3, eventually exiting through the final output port with a value of 1.1. Meanwhile, logic port 2 transferred the power towards the drop port, which means the gate is turned off and the binary bit is 0 because the logic port has no input power.

State X = 1, Y = 1: In this state, both logic ports (Logic Port 1 and Logic Port 2) have an input power of 1 (100 mW). This causes the input powers at the top and bottom of the structure not to couple into the PCRR 1 and PCRR 2 rings via the nonlinear dielectric rods because the logical ports are turned on, and they will move towards the output waveguide L3 through waveguides L1 and L2. This state represents binary bits 1 and 1. The output power obtained in this state is 1.5.

The input bias power must be progressively increased to enable the switching operation via the logic input and to normalize the output power. This gradual increase also facilitates correct light intensity transfer through the logic input port.

Optimal switching performance, based on logical input, is observed at input bias values of 1, 3, 5, and 40. To facilitate easier analysis of the output power, it is normalized by considering input power values ranging from 1 to 40 mW. This range is narrower than the 70 mW input power range used in some previously published works in this field.

Figure 4 depicts the optical power distribution within the waveguide paths. When the X and Y inputs are in different logic states (0 and 1), the emitted optical signal interferes with the bias input. This interference, dependent on the power value, induces changes in the nonlinear coefficient of the nonlinear rods. Consequently, the power transmitted to the output path varies, resulting in distinct output power levels for each input state, as presented in Fig. 5.

Figure 5 illustrates the optical power output over time. As depicted, various output power levels are generated based on the input conditions. The stabilization time for the output is approximately 1 ps, indicating that this proposed structure is suitable for high-speed digital circuit applications.

Conclusion

In summary, we have presented a novel design for an all-optical digital-to-analog converter (DAC) based on photonic crystals. This structure includes two square-shaped nonlinear ring resonators. Inside these nonlinear ring resonators, several black color nonlinear dielectric rods are utilized which the linear and nonlinear refractive index for the nonlinear material is 1.4 and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$10^{{ - 14{\text{m}}^{2} }} /_{{\text{W}}}$$\end{document}, respectively. The operation of the proposed structure was analyzed using the PWE and FDTD methods at λ = 1550nm. The advantages of the proposed structure include its smaller dimension compared to previous structures with rings. The sampling rate is 125 GS/s, and the overall size of the structure (footprint) is 324 µm2.
