Reversal of neuromuscular blockade with sugammadex based on the residual amount of rocuronium in the body using pharmacokinetic simulation
Authors: Tomotaka Morita, Osamu Uchida, Satoshi Shibuta, Satoshi Hagihira, Yuji Fujino
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUNDThe relationship between the residual amount of rocuronium and the dose of sugammadex required to reverse neuromuscular blockade (NMB) remains unclear.OBJECTIVESTo determine the required dose of sugammadex based on the estimated residual rocuronium in the body using pharmacokinetic simulation and evaluate the reversal of NMB using sugammadex.DESIGNObservational study.SETTINGSingle-centre, randomised, controlled, four-group, single-blind study conducted at Osaka University Hospital, Japan.PATIENTSPatients without cardiac, hepatic, renal or neuromuscular disorders scheduled for elective surgery.MAIN OUTCOME MEASURESFor induction, 0.6 mg kg−1 rocuronium was administered. During maintenance, the effect site concentration of rocuronium was maintained at greater than 1 μg ml−1. The residual amount of rocuronium in the body was calculated postoperatively through pharmacokinetic simulation. Considering the ratio of sugammadex to rocuronium molecules, patients were divided into four groups based on sugammadex dose: control (2 mg kg−1), and four, six and eight times the residual amount of rocuronium. Sugammadex was administered shortly after surgery, and the recovery time from administration of sugammadex to the train-of-four (TOF) ratio of 0.9 was recorded.RESULTSOverall, 123 patients were analysed and successfully extubated, and no signs of recurarisation were observed. Regardless of the sugammadex dose (7.57 ± 2.35 vs. 4.08 ± 0.16 vs. 6.01 ± 0.19 vs. 8.00 ± 0.23 mg kg−1, P < 0.01), no significant differences in recovery times were observed among the groups (142 ± 74 vs. 161 ± 98 vs. 152 ± 82 vs. 120 ± 49 s, P = 0.21).CONCLUSIONBased on pharmacokinetic simulation, all patients successfully recovered from NMB using rocuronium, and excellent recovery profiles were achieved.

Introduction

Sugammadex is a γ-cyclodextrin derivative and a selective relaxant binding agent designed to reverse neuromuscular blockade (NMB) caused by rocuronium bromide.1–3 A sugammadex molecule chemically encapsulates a rocuronium molecule in plasma, which is its unique pharmacological action.1 This encapsulation reverses the effect of rocuronium at the neuromuscular junction through a mechanism of NMB reversal that differs completely from that of acetylcholinesterase inhibitors such as neostigmine.4

According to various clinical studies,4–9 2 mg kg−1 sugammadex is recommended to reverse shallow to moderate NMB. The required dose of sugammadex to encapsulate all rocuronium molecules in the body can be calculated if the residual amount of rocuronium at the time of sugammadex administration is known. Given that the molecular weights of sugammadex and rocuronium are 2178.0 and 609.7, respectively, a sugammadex dose of 3.57 times the residual amount of rocuronium would theoretically contain an equimolar ratio of sugammadex to residual rocuronium molecules in the body and could encapsulate all residual rocuronium molecules, thereby inactivating the effect of rocuronium.10,11 However, to our knowledge, the relationship between the dose of sugammadex required to reverse NMB and the residual rocuronium remains unclear.

Therefore, we hypothesised that the optimal dose of sugammadex required to reverse NMB caused by rocuronium could be estimated based on the residual amount of rocuronium using pharmacokinetic simulation. This study aimed to estimate the appropriate dose of sugammadex by simulating the residual amount of rocuronium in the body using pharmacokinetic modelling, and to assess its effectiveness in reversing NMB.

Methods

Ethics

This single-centre, randomised, controlled, four-group, single-blind study was conducted at Osaka University Hospital, Japan, in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki for human studies. The Ethics Committee of Osaka University Hospital (2-15 Yamadaoka, Suita, Osaka, Japan), approved the study protocol (approval no. 13052) on 26 September 2013, and written informed consent was obtained from all patients before enrolment. The protocol has been registered in the University Hospital Medical Information Network clinical trial registry (registration number: UMIN000017126).

Patient population

Patients with the ASA physical status classes I to III, aged 20 to 79 years, were considered for enrolment in this study. All patients were scheduled for elective surgical procedures under general anaesthesia, after which extubation was planned. Based on several studies,4–9 using a type I error rate (α) of 0.05, 27 patients would be required in the treatment group to obtain a power of 0.8. However, to compensate for dropouts, we decided to include at least 30 patients in each group.

We excluded patients with expected difficult intubations, those who were currently prescribed medications known to interact with rocuronium or sugammadex, and those with cardiac, hepatic, renal and/or neuromuscular disorders, bronchial asthma, history of malignant hyperthermia, allergies and/or contraindications to medications used during the study. Patients who exhibited anaphylactic symptoms after administration of sugammadex were also excluded.

Study design

Patients were randomised into the following four groups based on the administered dose of sugammadex: groups C, R4, R6 and R8. In group C, sugammadex doses recommended to reverse shallow to moderate NMB were determined by patients’ body weights (2 mg kg−1). In the remaining three groups, sugammadex doses were determined based on the residual amounts of rocuronium in the patients. The number after the letter ‘R’ indicates the multiplier for the residual amount of rocuronium. As the sugammadex molecule is 3.57-fold greater in weight than the rocuronium molecule, a sugammadex dose four-fold greater than the residual amount of rocuronium would theoretically contain more sugammadex molecules than residual rocuronium molecules in the body and would encapsulate all residual rocuronium molecules. Two additional groups with larger sugammadex doses were included to address the possibility of an insufficient ratio of sugammadex to rocuronium molecules required to reverse NMB. This could be attributed to the distribution of sugammadex in various tissues. These groups were incorporated to avoid postoperative complications, such as respiratory insufficiency associated with residual NMB.12–15 The residual amount of rocuronium in the body was calculated using pharmacokinetic simulation, based on Wierda's model.16 The predicted plasma concentration (Cp) and effect site concentration (Ce) were also calculated using pharmacokinetic simulation, and the residual amount was defined as the cumulative administered amount minus the amount eliminated from the body (Fig. 1).

Anaesthesia

No premedication was administered before any surgical procedure in any patient. Anaesthesiologists, who were blinded to the study design or protocol, induced and maintained anaesthesia using a target-controlled infusion of propofol, remifentanil and fentanyl. After the patient became unconscious, muscle relaxation was facilitated by 0.6 mg kg−1 rocuronium, and the patient was intubated. An additional 0.2 mg kg−1 rocuronium dose was administered every 20 to 30 min to maintain Ce at greater than 1 μg ml−1, regardless of the train-of-four (TOF) values or ratios. Routine monitoring, including electrocardiography, pulse oximetry, capnography, noninvasive blood pressure monitoring and bladder temperature, was performed using a bedside monitor (Life Scope TR, Nihon Kohden, Tokyo, Japan). Ventilation was adjusted to maintain end-tidal carbon dioxide between 30 and 40 mmHg. Bladder temperature was maintained at higher than 36 °C using a temperature management unit (Bair Hugger Model 750, 3M, Maplewood, Minnesota, USA).

NMB was monitored using an acceleromyography device (TOF-Watch SX, Organon Ireland, Dublin, Ireland). The response of the adductor pollicis muscle to ulnar nerve stimulation was recorded using acceleromyography.17 Calibration was performed before anaesthesia was induced. After calibration, TOF-Watch SX was switched to the repetitive TOF mode of stimulation (2 Hz; 0.2 ms), which was applied at 1 min intervals intra-operatively. The intervals were adjusted to 15 s shortly before the administration of sugammadex, and the stimulation continued until the TOF ratios exceeded 0.9.18–20

Outcomes

Cp, Ce and the residual amount of rocuronium in the body were calculated postoperatively and before the administration of sugammadex using pharmacokinetic simulation (Fig. 1). In all groups, sugammadex doses were calculated based on body weight or residual amount of rocuronium, as previously described. We recorded the recovery time, which was defined as the time from administration of sugammadex to TOF ratios of at least 0.9. The Cp and Ce of rocuronium, dose of sugammadex administered and recovery time from NMB were also recorded.

Statistical analysis

Data are expressed as mean and standard deviation and were compared among the groups using one-way analysis of variance with the posthoc Tukey–Kramer ‘honestly significant difference’ test. Statistical significance was set at P value less than 0.05. JMP Pro version 11.0.0 for Mac (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, North Carolina, USA) was used for statistical analyses.

Results

Participant profiles

Overall, 133 patients were enrolled in this study conducted between 1 November 2013 and 6 March 2015. Ten patients were excluded (Fig. 2). Nine patients were able to flex their arms and/or legs, and their TOF ratios were at least 0.9 when the surgeries were completed and before they were extubated or awake. One patient in group R6 developed a rash shortly after the administration of sugammadex but recovered immediately after the administration of chlorphenamine and was extubated safely. Therefore, 123 patients were finally included in this study. All patients were successfully extubated, and no sign of recurarisation was observed.

Administration of rocuronium

According to pharmacokinetic simulation, no significant differences were observed in the residual amounts of rocuronium among groups C, R4, R6 and R8 (0.29 ± 0.08 vs. 0.28 ± 0.08 vs. 0.27 ± 0.07 vs. 0.28 ± 0.07 mg kg−1, P = 0.71, respectively; Fig. 3 and Table 3). Administration of sugammadex

The average amount of sugammadex administered in group C was significantly greater than that in groups R4 and R6 (2.03 ± 0.10 mg kg−1; 1.14 ± 0.33 mg kg−1, P < 0.0001; 1.62 ± 0.42 mg kg−1, P = 0.0003, respectively; Table 3). No significant difference was observed between groups C and R8 (2.26 ± 0.54 mg kg−1, P = 0.1007; Table 3).

The average ratio between the administered dose of sugammadex and residual rocuronium in group C was 7.57 ± 2.35 mg kg−1, which was significantly different from that in groups R4 and R6 (4.08 ± 0.16 mg kg−1, P < 0.0001; 6.01 ± 0.19 mg kg−1, P < 0.0001; Table 3). However, it did not significantly differ from that in group R8 (8.00 ± 0.23 mg kg−1, P = 0.5226; Table 3).

The recovery times for NMB reversal in groups C, R4, R6 and R8 were 142 ± 74, 161 ± 98, 152 ± 82 and 120 ± 49 s, respectively, indicating no significant differences in recovery times among the groups (P = 0.21, Table 3). Furthermore, no significant differences were observed in the time from administration of sugammadex to extubation among the groups (404 ± 197, 455 ± 293, 527 ± 334 and 441 ± 265 s, respectively; P = 0.38, Table 3).

Discussion

In this study, we estimated the residual amount of rocuronium in the body following reversal of anaesthesia and the dose of sugammadex required to encapsulate the residual rocuronium using pharmacokinetic simulation. Our method is unique and has not been previously used in clinical practice. Moreover, the results provide evidence that NMB could be reversed with sugammadex in doses calculated based on the residual amount of rocuronium in the body. We propose that this theoretical method can be safely applied in clinical practice, as all patients were extubated without complications, even when smaller-than-recommended doses of sugammadex were administered.

Benefits from our study

In standard clinical practice, the recommended dose of sugammadex to reverse shallow to moderate NMB is 2 to 4 mg kg−1.21 The Ce of rocuronium was maintained at at least 1 μg ml−1 in our study, according to Wierda's model.16 However, based on other reports,22,23 a Ce of 1 μg ml−1 might indicate a shallow NMB.

The estimated dose of sugammadex administered to patients in group R4 was significantly smaller than that used in standard clinical practice21; however, no patients exhibited signs of recurarisation in our study. We observed that NMB caused by rocuronium can be completely reversed by administering smaller-than-recommended doses of sugammadex, and the amount of sugammadex required to encapsulate the residual rocuronium can be estimated.

There are additional benefits to estimating residual rocuronium. First, sugammadex doses are determined based on TOF ratios or posttetanic counts in usual clinical practice.24–26 Therefore, administering less-than-necessary doses of sugammadex can lead to recurarisation after patients are transferred from the operating room.27 Recurarisation may occur when the sugammadex dose is rendered insufficient due to redistribution of rocuronium from peripheral to central compartments.28,29 However, by estimating the residual amount of rocuronium, we can calculate the approximate number of unbound rocuronium molecules and the additional dose of sugammadex required to encapsulate the residual rocuronium molecules. Consequently, the possibility of recurarisation after extubation can be decreased, and patients can be safely transferred to the wards.

Second, if re-intubation is required, the appropriate rocuronium dose to counteract sugammadex can be calculated. According to standard recommendations,21 more sugammadex than necessary is often administered to reverse the NMB. Therefore, sometimes, larger doses of rocuronium might be required to achieve sufficient NMB for re-intubation. However, the additional dose of sugammadex after encapsulating the residual rocuronium molecules and the dose of rocuronium required to be encapsulated by the additional sugammadex molecules in the body can be estimated using this method. Furthermore, by adding 0.9 mg kg−1 to the calculated dose of rocuronium for rapid muscle relaxation, we can calculate the dose of rocuronium required for NMB in cases of re-intubation.

Third, several pharmacokinetic programs for computers or tablets are available for calculating the Cp and Ce of rocuronium. However, the residual amount of rocuronium is an extension of these calculations. In this study, we developed a specific application to calculate the residual amount of rocuronium in the body. However, this calculation of residual rocuronium was not as complex as anticipated. We postulate that this method used to determine the correct dose of sugammadex is sufficiently accurate to recommend its adoption in anaesthesia records or other software programs.

Comparison among groups

Our data did not indicate any significant differences in the recovery time among the groups. However, we observed a weak trend indicating that larger doses of sugammadex may be associated with shorter recovery times, which is consistent with that reported in another study.8 Moreover, the recovery time further decreased when additional sugammadex was administered. We speculate that the number of molecules of rocuronium and sugammadex directly influenced these results. When all rocuronium molecules in the plasma are encapsulated, additional rocuronium molecules may enter the plasma from the tissues and be immediately encapsulated by the remaining sugammadex molecules, thereby contributing to faster recovery.

Limitations

Firstly, the residual amount of rocuronium was a calculated value derived from pharmacokinetic simulation, not a directly measured value. We used Wierda's model for this calculation; however, the estimated residual amount of rocuronium might have differed if other models30–34 had been used for simulation. Therefore, the recommended dose of sugammadex in our study may represent an underdose or an overdose.

Secondly, we conducted this study based on pharmacokinetic simulation rather than on the depth of NMB. We primarily used the TOF-Watch SX to measure TOF ratios before extubation; however, we did not refer to TOF counts or ratios during maintenance. The relationships between the Cp or Ce and TOF counts or ratios (the degree of NMB) may differ among patients. Although the groups showed no significant differences in the Cp or Ce of rocuronium, TOF counts varied even among patients within the same group. Furthermore, significant differences might be observed if the same depth of NMB is targeted by observing TOF counts and Cp or Ce.

Thirdly, we administered rocuronium as a single dose. Under this condition, it is difficult to evaluate Cp or Ce accurately. Our method must also be verified when rocuronium is administered continuously, that is, when Cp or Ce are calculated more precisely.

Finally, the target degree of NMB in this study was shallow to moderate. Deep NMB may be required in some patients or surgical procedures. Although our method can theoretically be used to calculate the residual amount of rocuronium and facilitate recovery from deep NMB, further studies are warranted to confidently apply our method to deeper NMB.

Conclusion

In this study, we compared the recovery profiles of patients receiving different doses of sugammadex to reverse shallow to moderate NMB induced by rocuronium. Shallow to moderate NMB can be potentially reversed by estimating the residual amount of rocuronium in the body and administering calculated doses of sugammadex using pharmacokinetic simulation. This method may be sufficiently safe for incorporating requisite improvements in software or applications used to calculate residual rocuronium and could possibly be comparable to TOF, which is the current gold standard for assessing NMB. In a future study, we plan to use our method to safely extubate patients with deeper NMB.
