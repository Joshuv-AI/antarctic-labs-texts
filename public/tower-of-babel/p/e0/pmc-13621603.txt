Triglyceride glucose-weight adjusted waist index as a promising predictive marker for incident diabetes: a large-scale retrospective cohort study
Authors: Longsheng Zhang, Huankai Zhang, Zhiliang Huang, Renzhe Lin, Zitian Luo, Shujun Ye, Duo Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe triglyceride-glucose (TyG) index and weight-adjusted waist index (WWI) are separately associated with diabetes mellitus (DM). However, the longitudinal relationship of their combination (TyG‑WWI) with incident diabetes remains unclear. We aimed to investigate the association between TyG‑WWI and new‑onset DM and to evaluate its predictive performance.MethodsThis retrospective cohort study included 15,453 non‑diabetic Japanese adults. Cox proportional hazards models were used to estimate hazard ratios (HRs) for TyG‑WWI as both continuous and quartile variables. Restricted cubic spline analysis was applied to examine the dose‑response relationship. Time‑dependent receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were constructed to compare the predictive performance of TyG‑WWI, TyG, WWI, and body mass index (BMI) at 5, 10, and 12 years of follow‑up.ResultsOver a median follow‑up of 5.75 years for men and 5.13 years for women, 373 participants developed diabetes. After full adjustment, each one‑unit increase in TyG‑WWI was associated with a 5% higher risk (HR = 1.05, 95% confidence interval (CI): 1.03–1.06). Compared with the lowest quartile, the highest quartile had a 2‑fold increased risk (HR = 2.02, 95% CI: 1.13–3.62). Restricted cubic spline analysis revealed a positive, approximately linear dose‑response relationship between TyG‑WWI and incident diabetes (P for non‑linearity > 0.05). TyG‑WWI showed superior predictive accuracy at 5 years (area under the curve (AUC) = 0.7693), 10 years (AUC = 0.7706), and 12 years (AUC = 0.7793) compared with TyG, WWI, or BMI alone (all P < 0.05 except for BMI at 5 and 10 years, but significant at 12 years, P = 0.002).ConclusionHigher TyG‑WWI is independently associated with an increased risk of incident DM and demonstrates better and more stable predictive performance than TyG, WWI, or BMI over long-term follow-up. This simple, low‑cost index may serve as a practical long‑term risk stratification tool for identifying high‑risk individuals in primary care.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12902-026-02367-y.

Background

Diabetes mellitus (DM) has emerged as one of the most pressing global public health challenges of the 21st century. According to the International Diabetes Federation (IDF) Diabetes Atlas, approximately 589 million adults were living with diabetes in 2024, a figure projected to rise to 853 million by 2050 [1]. This chronic metabolic disorder not only imposes a staggering economic burden on healthcare systems but also significantly increases the risk of cardiovascular disease, renal failure, and premature mortality [2, 3]. More than one million people die from diabetes annually, making it the world’s ninth leading cause of death [4]. While pharmacological interventions and lifestyle modifications remain the cornerstones of diabetes management, identifying high-risk individuals through accessible and accurate predictive markers is essential for primary prevention and early therapeutic intervention [5].

Insulin resistance (IR) is a primary pathophysiological driver of type 2 diabetes. The triglyceride-glucose (TyG) index has been widely recognized as a reliable and cost-effective surrogate marker for IR, showing a strong correlation with the hyperinsulinemic-euglycemic clamp—the gold standard for IR assessment [6]. Recent large-scale prospective studies have further consolidated the predictive value of the TyG index for incident type 2 diabetes across diverse populations. In a multicenter cohort study of 60,103 normolipidemic Chinese adults, a robust positive association was observed between the TyG index and incident diabetes [7]. While TyG reflects biochemical metabolic status, obesity—particularly central adiposity—is another primary driver of DM. Traditional metrics like body mass index (BMI) cannot distinguish between muscle and fat distribution. Consequently, the weight-adjusted waist index (WWI) was developed as a novel anthropometric indicator to better assess adiposity [8]. Unlike BMI, WWI is positively associated with fat mass and negatively associated with muscle mass, making it a more precise indicator of central obesity [9]. Several recent cross-sectional and prospective studies have confirmed the independent association between WWI and type 2 diabetes. In a large cross-sectional analysis of 20,477 US adults, each one-unit increase in WWI was associated with a 67% higher odds of type 2 diabetes after full adjustment [10]. Because IR and central adiposity are critical to the pathogenesis of diabetes, the integrated index termed triglyceride glucose-weight adjusted waist index (TyG-WWI) may provide a synergistic assessment of metabolic risk that surpasses the efficacy of its separate components [11]. To date, the association between TyG-WWI and incident diabetes risk has received limited research attention. While a recent cross-sectional study demonstrated that TyG-WWI was positively associated with prevalent diabetes [12], the longitudinal relationship between this novel combined index and future diabetes incidence has not been thoroughly examined in a large-scale cohort study. Furthermore, whether TyG-WWI provides superior predictive performance compared with the TyG index or WWI alone in a longitudinal setting remains uncertain.

To address these gaps, we conducted a retrospective cohort study using data from the NAGALA (NAfld in the Gifu Area, Longitudinal Analysis) study, a large-scale health screening cohort from Japan. Our primary objective was to investigate the association between baseline TyG-WWI levels and incident diabetes during follow-up. A previous study using the NAGALA cohort has reported a U‑shaped association between the TyG index and incident diabetes [13]. However, to date, no NAGALA-based study has investigated the combined TyG-WWI index, directly compared its predictive performance with its individual components via time-dependent receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis, or assessed its longitudinal association with incident diabetes. Therefore, we hypothesized that higher TyG-WWI would be independently associated with an increased risk of new-onset diabetes and that TyG-WWI would demonstrate superior predictive accuracy for diabetes compared with its individual components (TyG and WWI) as well as BMI.

Methods

Study design and participants

Data for the present study were derived from the NAGALA study. Initiated in 1994, this ongoing cohort has continuously recruited individuals undergoing health checkups at Murakami Memorial Hospital, with the primary objective of prospectively investigating the incidence and associated factors of chronic diseases, including diabetes and nonalcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD). Detailed descriptions of the cohort are available in previous studies by Okamura et al. [14], and the dataset is publicly accessible in the Dryad database (http://www.Datadryad.org/). Because the vast majority of individuals undergoing health screenings at the hospital return for repeat screenings (60% undergo one or two screenings per year), the NAGALA research team conducted a long-term follow-up survey to track future incident DM. For this analysis, we conducted a retrospective cohort study using data from 20,944 subjects enrolled in the NAGALA cohort between 1994 and 2016. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) pre-existing diabetes (including self-reported diabetes or baseline hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c) ≥ 6.5%) or diagnosis of liver disease (excluding fatty liver) at baseline; (2) baseline fasting plasma glucose (FPG) ≥ 6.1 mmol/L; (3) excessive alcohol consumption; (4) use of medication; (5) incomplete data or unexplained withdrawal from the survey; (6) participants with missing covariate data. The detailed flowchart of this study is shown in Supplementary Fig. 1. Ultimately, 15,453 subjects were included in the analysis. The study protocol received ethical approval from the Ethics Committee of Murakami Memorial Hospital, with written informed consent obtained from all participants. The research followed the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki and complied with the guidelines for Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology.

Baseline data collection and measurements

Standardized, trained medical examiners collected basic information—including sex, age, smoking status, drinking status, and exercise habits—via a questionnaire. Smoking status was categorized as never, past, or current. Drinking status was classified as no/minimal, light, moderate, or heavy based on weekly alcohol consumption in the month prior to participation. An exercise habit was defined as engaging in at least one physical activity per week.

Anthropometric measurements including weight, height, waist circumference (WC), systolic blood pressure (SBP), and diastolic blood pressure (DBP) were taken indoors using standard methods, with subjects wearing light clothing and no shoes. Fatty liver was diagnosed by gastroenterologists based on liver contrast and brightness on abdominal ultrasound images.

Venous blood samples were drawn from the forearm after an overnight fast of at least 8 h and sent to a standard laboratory. Concentrations of biochemical parameters, including alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), gamma-glutamyl transferase (GGT), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), total cholesterol (TC), HbA1c, triglycerides (TG), and FPG, were measured using an automated biochemical analyzer.

Exposure variable

TyG-WWI was the exposure variable in our study. The TyG-WWI is obtained by multiplying the TyG index with the WWI. The WWI is calculated as WC / (weight)1/2, where WC is waist circumference in centimeters and weight is measured in kilograms [15]. The TyG index is computed as Ln [1/2 × FPG (mg/dL) × TG (mg/dL)], with FPG representing fasting plasma glucose and TG denoting triglycerides [16]. Consequently, the TyG-WWI is expressed as: TyG-WWI = TyG index × WWI [17]. Both TyG and WWI are treated as continuous variables without absolute physical units that affect relative comparisons; the TyG index is dimensionless, and WWI—though having units of cm/(kg)1/2—is used as a standardized anthropometric score. Their multiplication yields a composite index that is mathematically permissible and has been previously validated [12, 18]. Our study did not define a cutoff value for TyG-WWI, given the variations in cutoff values reported by other research. Instead, the TyG-WWI measurements were categorized into quartiles.

Study endpoint

The primary outcome of this study was the incidence of diabetes during follow-up. Based on the American Diabetes Association criteria, diabetes was defined as any of the following: HbA1c ≥ 6.5%, FPG ≥ 7.0 mmol/L (measured during follow-up), or self-reported diabetes verified through medical records or blood glucose measurements [19].

Statistical analysis

Only 11 participants (0.07%) had missing HDL‑C values; therefore, we performed a complete‑case analysis without imputation. Participant characteristics were analyzed according to TyG-WWI quartiles. Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median (interquartile range [IQR]) and compared using one-way analysis of variance or the Kruskal–Wallis H test. Categorical variables were presented as numbers (percentages) and compared using the chi-square test.

The incidence rate of diabetes was calculated as the number of incident cases divided by person-years of follow-up, expressed per 1,000 person-years with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) based on the Poisson distribution. Person-years and median follow-up time (IQR) were reported overall and by TyG-WWI quartile. These descriptive measures are presented in Supplementary Tables 1 to enhance transparency of the survival data.

To explore the relationship between different levels of the TyG-WWI and the risk of incident DM, we plotted Kaplan–Meier survival function curves corresponding to the TyG-WWI quartiles. The significance of differences between groups was assessed using the log-rank test. Subsequently, univariable and multivariable Cox proportional hazards regression models were built to quantify the association between the TyG-WWI and new-onset diabetes, calculating corresponding hazard ratios (HRs) and their 95% CIs. TyG-WWI was treated as a categorical variable (quartiles) and as a continuous variable. Additionally, to facilitate the interpretation of the association between the exposure variable and the outcome, the continuous exposure variable (TyG-WWI) was standardized by its SD. The variable was entered into the Cox proportional hazards model as a per-SD change, such that the estimated HR corresponds to a one-SD increase in the exposure variable. For multivariable Cox proportional hazards models, candidate covariates were selected based on prior literature and clinical knowledge. We also adjusted for features that, when added to the model, changed the matched HR by at least 10% [20]. To avoid overadjustment, variables that are direct components of the TyG-WWI (i.e., FPG, TG, WC, and body weight) were not included as separate covariates, as doing so would mathematically obscure the independent effect of the composite exposure. BMI was excluded from adjustment due to its overlap with weight and because it served as a comparator in predictive performance analysis. Furthermore, prior to model construction, we assessed multicollinearity among all other candidate continuous covariates using the variance inflation factor (VIF). A VIF threshold of > 4 was considered indicative of significant multicollinearity, and any such covariate was precluded from entering the multivariable model [21]. Analyses were conducted using three sequential models: Model 1 was unadjusted (crude model). Model 2 incorporated adjustments for age and sex. Model 3 further adjusted for ALT, AST, GGT, HDL-C, TC, HbA1c, SBP, DBP, height, fatty liver, habit of exercise, smoking status, and alcohol consumption. The proportional hazards assumption for the Cox models was formally tested. The assumption regarding the primary exposure variable (TyG-WWI) was assessed using Schoenfeld residuals, which revealed no significant violation (P = 0.605). A diagnostic plot of the Schoenfeld residuals against time exhibited no apparent trend, providing visual confirmation of the assumption (Supplementary Fig. 2).

To investigate potential nonlinear relationships, restricted cubic spline analyses with four knots (positioned at the 5th, 35th, 65th, and 95th percentiles) were applied to characterize the dose-response association between TyG-WWI and incident DM, with adjustment for the same covariates as in Model 3. Subgroup analyses stratified by age, sex, fatty liver, exercise habits, smoking status, and alcohol consumption were conducted to assess effect modifications, and likelihood ratio tests were used to evaluate interactions across subgroups.

To evaluate the predictive value of the exposure variable for the outcome, time-dependent ROC analysis was performed. The area under the curve (AUC) was calculated at three landmark time points: 1825 days (5 years), 3650 days (10 years), and 4380 days (12 years). The 12-year time point was selected because the maximum follow-up duration in the cohort was 4732 days (~ 12.96 years), allowing assessment of long-term predictive stability. The predictive performance of different indicators—TyG-WWI, TyG, WWI, and BMI—was compared using the time-dependent AUC at these time points. In addition, AUC trend curves were then plotted against follow-up time to visually assess and compare the dynamic changes in predictive ability of the four indicators.

Sensitivity analyses were conducted to evaluate the robustness of our results. Firstly, given that HbA1c reflects pre-existing or subclinical glycemic status and could act as an intermediate rather than a confounder, we performed an additional sensitivity analysis by removing HbA1c from the fully adjusted model (Model 3) to assess whether adjusting for this downstream marker led to overadjustment and attenuated the association between TyG-WWI and incident diabetes. Secondly, to reduce the potential for reverse causality, we excluded participants whose follow-up time was less than one year.

All statistical analyses were conducted using R software (version 4.2.2; https://www.R-project.org/) and the Free Statistics Analysis Platform (version 2.4.0; https://www.clinicalscientists.cn/freestatistics). Statistical significance was defined as a two-tailed P-value below 0.05. Given that this study was a secondary analysis of previously collected data, no prospective sample size calculation was carried out.

Results

Baseline characteristics of the study population

The baseline characteristics of the participants are presented in Table 1. The included patients were categorized by TyG-WWI quartiles as follows: Quartile 1, < 72.85; Quartile 2, ≥ 72.85 and < 78.83; Quartile 3, ≥ 78.83 and < 85.12; Quartile 4, ≥ 85.12. Across increasing TyG-WWI quartiles, participants were progressively older (mean age from 39.8 ± 7.8 years in Q1 to 47.5 ± 8.8 years in Q4; P < 0.001) and more likely to be male (from 32.9% in Q1 to 71.5% in Q4; P < 0.001). Markers of metabolic risk, including ALT, AST, GGT, TC, TG, FPG, SBP, DBP, and HbA1c, increased significantly across quartiles, whereas HDL-C decreased (all P < 0.001). Similarly, anthropometric indices such as WC, weight, BMI, and WWI rose steadily with higher TyG-WWI quartiles. The prevalence of fatty liver was markedly higher in Q4 (43.1%) than in Q1 (1.3%). Unhealthy lifestyle factors, including lack of exercise, current smoking, and heavier alcohol consumption, were more frequent in higher quartiles (all P < 0.001).

Effects of TyG‑WWI on incident DM

Over a median follow‑up of 5.75 years for men and 5.13 years for women, 373 participants (286 men, 87 women) developed type 2 diabetes. The proportion of incident diabetes increased progressively across TyG-WWI quartiles: 0.4% in Q1, 0.9% in Q2, 2.3% in Q3, and 6.0% in Q4 (Fig. 1). Detailed information on incident cases, person-years of follow-up, incidence rates per 1,000 person-years, and median follow-up time by quartile is provided in Supplementary Table 1. The incidence rate ranged from 0.7 (95% CI: 0.4–1.1) in Q1 to 10.0 (95% CI: 8.8–11.4) in Q4, supporting a graded association between TyG-WWI and diabetes risk. Kaplan–Meier survival curves showed a significant separation among the four quartile groups (log-rank P < 0.0001), with the highest TyG-WWI quartile exhibiting the lowest diabetes-free survival probability (Fig. 2).

Cox proportional hazards regression analyses (Table 2) revealed a strong positive association between TyG‑WWI and incident diabetes. When TyG‑WWI was modeled as a continuous variable, each one‑unit increase was associated with an HR of 1.12 (95% CI: 1.11–1.13) in the unadjusted model (Model 1); this association remained significant after adjustment for age and sex (Model 2: HR 1.11, 95% CI: 1.09–1.12) and after full adjustment (Model 3: HR 1.05, 95% CI: 1.03–1.06). A per‑SD increment in TyG‑WWI yielded an HR of 1.50 (95% CI: 1.30–1.74) in the fully adjusted model. Compared with Quartile 1, the HR for Quartile 4 was 14.19 (95% CI: 8.67–23.22) in Model 1, 9.93 (95% CI: 5.94–16.57) in Model 2, and 2.02 (95% CI: 1.13–3.62) in Model 3 (P for trend = 0.006). Restricted cubic spline analysis further demonstrated a positive, approximately linear dose-response relationship between TyG-WWI and the risk of incident diabetes (P for non-linearity > 0.05, Fig. 3).

Predictive values of TyG-WWI, TyG, WWI, and BMI

To evaluate and compare the predictive ability of TyG-WWI, TyG index, WWI, and BMI for incident diabetes, time-dependent ROC analysis was performed at landmark time points of 5 years (1825 days), 10 years (3650 days), and 12 years (4380 days) of follow-up (Table 3). At 5 years, the AUC for TyG-WWI was 0.7693 (95% CI: 0.7331–0.8055), which was significantly higher than that of TyG (AUC 0.7327, 95% CI: 0.6937–0.7718; P = 0.001) and WWI (AUC 0.6922, 95% CI: 0.6516–0.7328; P < 0.001), while the difference against BMI (AUC 0.743, 95% CI: 0.7003–0.7857) did not reach statistical significance (P = 0.216). At 10 years, the superiority of TyG-WWI was maintained, with an AUC of 0.7706 (95% CI: 0.7418–0.7995), compared with 0.7252 (95% CI: 0.6942–0.7562) for TyG and 0.711 (95% CI: 0.6804–0.7415) for WWI (both P < 0.001); the AUC for BMI was 0.7425 (95% CI: 0.7112–0.7739), which remained non‑significant versus TyG‑WWI (P = 0.075). At 12 years, TyG‑WWI demonstrated a significantly higher AUC than BMI (0.7793 vs. 0.7274, P = 0.002), in addition to maintaining superiority over TyG (0.7195, P < 0.001) and WWI (0.7515, P = 0.026). The optimal cutoff values of TyG‑WWI for predicting incident diabetes were 83.50 at 5 years (Youden index 0.4065), 84.76 at 10 years (Youden index 0.4025), and 81.46 at 12 years (Youden index 0.3969). The longitudinal trends of the time‑dependent AUC over the entire follow‑up period are displayed in Fig. 5. Throughout most of the follow‑up, the AUC curve for TyG‑WWI remained consistently above those of TyG, WWI, and BMI, indicating a persistently superior and more stable predictive performance for incident diabetes. In contrast, the predictive accuracy of TyG and WWI alone was lower and showed greater fluctuations over time; BMI exhibited intermediate performance but was consistently inferior to TyG‑WWI across nearly the entire follow‑up.

Sensitivity analyses

Sensitivity analyses were performed to test the robustness of the results. First, after removing HbA1c from Model 3, the association between TyG-WWI and incident diabetes remained robust and nearly identical to the primary analysis (Supplementary Table 2). The restricted cubic spline analysis also demonstrated a positive linear dose-response relationship (Supplementary Fig. 3). Second, excluding participants with follow-up less than one year to address potential reverse causality yielded consistent findings (Supplementary Table 3; Supplementary Fig. 4).

Discussion

In this large-scale retrospective cohort study of 15,453 initially non-diabetic Japanese adults, we observed a significant positive and linear dose-response association between baseline TyG-WWI and incident type 2 diabetes. After comprehensive adjustment for potential confounders, each one‑unit increase in TyG-WWI was associated with a 5% higher risk of new-onset diabetes, and participants in the highest TyG-WWI quartile had a more than two‑fold increased risk compared with those in the lowest quartile. Notably, time‑dependent ROC analysis demonstrated that TyG-WWI exhibited superior predictive accuracy for incident diabetes at 5, 10, and 12 years of follow‑up, significantly outperforming TyG, WWI, and BMI. These findings remained robust across a series of sensitivity analyses, including the removal of HbA1c from the adjustment model and the exclusion of participants with follow-up less than one year.

To our knowledge, this study is the first to systematically evaluate the longitudinal association between TyG-WWI and the risk of incident diabetes in a large Asian general population cohort, as well as to compare its predictive performance with TyG, WWI, and BMI over long-term follow-up. While previous cross-sectional studies have reported a positive correlation between TyG-WWI and prevalent diabetes [12], the prospective relationship has remained largely unexplored. By leveraging the well‑characterized NAGALA cohort with long‑term repeated health screenings, we provide robust evidence that TyG-WWI is not only independently associated with future diabetes risk but also offers incremental predictive value beyond traditional metabolic indices. The practical implication of this finding is substantial: TyG-WWI integrates four simple, routinely measured clinical parameters (FPG, TG, WC, and weight) into a single composite marker that can be easily calculated without additional cost. Thus, TyG-WWI holds promise as a practical screening tool for identifying high‑risk individuals in primary care settings, facilitating early lifestyle or pharmacological interventions to prevent or delay the onset of diabetes.

The robust association between the TyG index and incident diabetes has been extensively documented across diverse populations. A 12-year longitudinal study involving over 4,000 Korean adults confirms a strong positive correlation between the TyG index and the risk of developing type 2 diabetes. The findings reveal a clear dose-response relationship, where the likelihood of incident diabetes rises significantly with each successive TyG index quartile [22]. Similarly, data from the Tehran lipid and glucose study indicate that the baseline TyG index is an independent and powerful predictor of type 2 diabetes [23]. Beyond Asian populations, the predictive value of the TyG index for incident diabetes has been validated in Western cohorts as well [24]. A meta-analysis of prospective cohort studies further consolidated the TyG index as a reliable predictor of incident diabetes [25]. These findings align with the established role of TyG index as a surrogate marker of IR. However, it is noteworthy that, using the same NAGALA cohort, a previous study reported a U‑shaped association between the TyG index and diabetes risk [13]. This nonlinear pattern highlights the potential for more comprehensive modeling of the TyG index, where exploring the combined influence of metabolic phenotypes and body structure could further enhance its diagnostic precision. The descending limb of that U‑shaped curve—i.e., increased diabetes risk at very low TyG levels—is likely driven by individuals with low TyG but high WWI (a phenotype of sarcopenic obesity). Multiplying TyG by WWI reclassifies such individuals into higher risk strata, thereby removing the paradoxical low‑risk tail and generating a linear dose‑response relationship (Fig. 3). Thus, TyG‑WWI may provide a more biologically coherent risk gradient than TyG alone.

WWI, as a novel anthropometric index reflecting the balance between fat mass and muscle mass, has also been implicated in diabetes risk prediction. In a large Chinese prospective cohort study involving 21,750 participants with nine years of follow-up, each one‑unit increment in WWI was associated with a significantly increased risk of incident diabetes. Additionally, ROC analysis revealed that WWI exhibited superior discrimination and accuracy in predicting cardiovascular disease and non‑accidental mortality compared with conventional adiposity indexes, including BMI and WC [26]. Similarly, a cross‑sectional analysis of 7,973 participants from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey 2017–2020 database reported that each one‑unit increase in WWI was associated with a 111% higher likelihood of diabetes, and the correlation between WWI and diabetes was stronger than that between WC or BMI and diabetes [27]. The predictive performance of WWI for diabetes has also been validated in East Asian populations beyond the Japanese context. A nationwide population-based cohort study from Korea examined multiple body composition indices including WWI and reported that waist‑to‑height ratio was the strongest predictor of new‑onset type 2 diabetes across all age groups in men, whereas the most relevant indices varied by age in women [28]. Although WWI was not the strongest predictor in that Korean study, the findings nonetheless support the utility of WWI as a component of diabetes risk assessment in East Asian populations. Taken together, these studies provide evidence that WWI is independently associated with diabetes risk across diverse populations, although its predictive superiority over traditional anthropometric indices may vary depending on the study population and outcome of interest.

Importantly, the above studies examined the TyG index and WWI as independent predictors of diabetes but did not investigate their combined effect. The synergistic capture of both IR and body composition abnormalities may yield a composite marker with enhanced predictive value beyond either component alone. Indeed, the underlying pathophysiological rationale—that type 2 diabetes arises from the interplay between IR and central obesity—supports the notion that a combined index may more accurately reflect the multifaceted metabolic risk profile of individuals.

Beyond diabetes, emerging evidence has linked TyG-WWI to a range of adverse clinical outcomes, such as cardiovascular mortality, stroke, and heart failure [18, 29, 30]. However, to date, the literature on the relationship between TyG-WWI and incident DM remains limited, with only a few studies having examined this novel composite index [11, 12]. Several important distinctions should be noted between our study and these previous investigations. First, and most critically, the existing TyG-WWI studies are cross-sectional in design. In contrast, our study employed a prospective cohort design with a median follow-up of approximately 5.4 years, enabling us to establish the temporal sequence between baseline TyG-WWI and incident diabetes. Second, previous TyG-WWI studies were conducted exclusively in the U.S. population, whereas our study is the first to evaluate TyG-WWI in a large Asian general population cohort, thereby providing crucial evidence on the generalizability of this index across different ethnic groups. Third, we performed time-dependent ROC analyses to compare the predictive performance of TyG-WWI, TyG index, and WWI at multiple landmark time points and tracked longitudinal AUC trends, demonstrating that the superiority of TyG-WWI is sustained over long-term follow-up.

The strong and independent association between TyG‑WWI and incident type 2 diabetes likely arises from the synergistic interplay of IR and central adiposity with muscle‑fat imbalance, rather than through separate, additive pathways. The TyG component reflects a state of chronic hyperglycaemia and hypertriglyceridaemia, which together exert glucolipotoxicity on insulin‑sensitive tissues: excess free fatty acids accumulate as toxic lipid intermediates, impairing insulin signalling via protein kinase C activation and insulin receptor substrate inhibition [6, 31]. Elevated TyG also correlates with a pro‑inflammatory and pro‑oxidant phenotype, including increased oxidised low-density lipoprotein, nitrotyrosine, C‑reactive protein, and Toll‑like receptor expression, thereby promoting systemic low‑grade inflammation that accelerates the transition to overt diabetes [32]. Meanwhile, the WWI component captures a distinct but complementary burden: a higher fat mass relative to muscle mass, independent of total body weight. Central visceral adipose tissue, when expanded, undergoes adipocyte hypertrophy, apoptosis, and macrophage infiltration, leading to increased secretion of pro‑inflammatory cytokines (tumor necrosis factor-α and interleukin‑6) and reduced adiponectin, which induces both hepatic and peripheral IR [33, 34]. Moreover, the negative association of WWI with skeletal muscle mass reduces the primary site for glucose disposal, directly impairing insulin sensitivity and worsening postprandial glycaemia [9]. Consequently, TyG‑WWI integrates two complementary yet distinct pathways—TyG capturing the metabolic consequences of IR (glucolipotoxicity, oxidative stress, and inflammation) and WWI capturing the structural and functional burden of central adiposity combined with muscle depletion—providing a more comprehensive assessment of the “diabesity” phenotype and explaining its superior predictive performance over either index alone [11].

Interestingly, while TyG‑WWI outperformed BMI at all time points, the difference was not statistically significant at 5 or 10 years but became significant at 12 years. This may reflect the slow accumulation of metabolic risk in initially healthy populations, where the synergistic effect of IR and central obesity requires a longer observation period to translate into measurable differences in diabetes incidence. These findings support the use of TyG‑WWI as a long‑term risk stratification tool rather than a short‑term screening instrument.

The significant sex interaction observed in our study (P for interaction = 0.039) warrants further consideration. Although TyG‑WWI consistently predicted incident diabetes in both men and women, the association appeared slightly stronger in women (HR 1.08 per one‑unit increase) than in men (HR 1.04). This quantitative difference may reflect fundamental biological distinctions in body composition, fat distribution, and sex hormone regulation. Women have a higher proportion of subcutaneous and gluteofemoral adipose tissue, which is metabolically less deleterious, and estrogen enhances insulin sensitivity and lipid metabolism. Conversely, men typically accumulate more visceral adipose tissue, which is strongly linked to IR [35, 36]. However, the TyG‑WWI index might capture visceral adiposity more efficiently in men, potentially leading to a relatively stronger baseline risk signal; the observed HR difference, though modest, could also be influenced by residual confounding or the skewed sex distribution across TyG‑WWI quartiles (from 67.1% female in Q1 to 71.5% male in Q4). Importantly, the direction of association remains positive in both sexes, supporting the overall robustness of TyG‑WWI as a metabolic risk marker. Future studies should explore whether sex‑specific cutoffs improve clinical risk stratification.

This study has several notable strengths. First, to our knowledge, it is the first large-scale, longitudinal cohort study to systematically investigate the association between the TyG-WWI and incident type 2 diabetes in an Asian general population, while also comparing its long-term predictive performance with its individual components. Second, the use of the well-characterized NAGALA cohort, with its standardized data collection, long follow-up duration, and low rates of missing data, enhances the internal validity and reproducibility of our findings. Third, we employed rigorous statistical approaches, including restricted cubic spline analysis, time-dependent ROC curves, and comprehensive sensitivity analyses, which collectively support the robustness of the observed associations. Fourth, the TyG-WWI is derived from routinely collected clinical parameters (FPG, TG, WC, and weight), making it a highly practical, low-cost, and easily implementable screening tool in primary care settings without requiring additional laboratory or imaging resources.

Nevertheless, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, although we adjusted for a wide range of potential confounders, residual or unmeasured confounding—such as dietary patterns, socioeconomic status, family history of diabetes, or medication adherence during follow-up—cannot be fully excluded given the observational design. Second, the study population consisted exclusively of Japanese adults who voluntarily participated in health screening programs, which may limit the generalizability of our findings to other ethnic groups, younger populations, or individuals with limited access to routine health checkups. Moreover, the study population consisted of non‑diabetic adults with FPG < 6.1 mmol/L, not all non‑diabetic adults; our findings may not be directly generalizable to individuals with higher fasting glucose levels (e.g., FPG 6.1–6.9 mmol/L) or established prediabetes. Third, the TyG-WWI was calculated using only baseline measurements; we could not account for dynamic changes in metabolic parameters or body composition over time, which might influence diabetes risk. Fourth, despite the prospective design, the possibility of reverse causality cannot be entirely ruled out, as subclinical metabolic disturbances may have existed at baseline even after excluding participants with prediabetes. Fifth, although the time-dependent ROC analysis showed that TyG-WWI had a statistically significant improvement in discrimination, the absolute increase in AUC was modest. Therefore, the conclusion that TyG-WWI has superior predictive performance should be interpreted with caution from a clinical perspective, as statistical superiority does not automatically translate into large clinical utility. Moreover, we did not perform additional measures such as calibration, net reclassification improvement, integrated discrimination improvement, or decision curve analysis in this study; these approaches could further evaluate the incremental clinical value of TyG-WWI and will be explored in future research. Future research should focus on externally validating the predictive utility of TyG-WWI in diverse ethnic and socioeconomic settings, exploring its longitudinal trajectory and dynamic changes in relation to diabetes risk, and evaluating its added value beyond existing clinical risk scores.

Conclusions

In this large Japanese cohort, higher TyG‑WWI was independently associated with an increased risk of incident type 2 diabetes, showing a linear dose‑response relationship. TyG‑WWI demonstrated superior and more stable predictive performance than TyG index, WWI, or BMI over long‑term follow‑up, with the most pronounced advantage emerging at 12 years. As a simple, low‑cost composite index derived from routine clinical parameters, TyG‑WWI, particularly when used with these suggested thresholds, may serve as a practical long‑term risk stratification tool to identify high‑risk individuals in primary care.
