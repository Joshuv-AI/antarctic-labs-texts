Synergistic effects of zinc and cadmium on phytoremediation potential of Christmas moss (Vesicularia montagnei)
Authors: Puntaree Taeprayoon, Kanwara Pongphontong, Khanitta Somtrakoon, Theerawut Phusantisampan, Weeradej Meeinkuirt
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
The hyperaccumulation potential of zinc (Zn) and cadmium (Cd) and their synergistic effects were examined in relation to Christmas moss (Vesicularia montagnei (Bél) Broth., Hypnaceae), an aquatic and terrestrial moss, dosed with Cd (Cd1 and Cd2), Zn (Zn1 and Zn2) and combined Zn and Cd (Cd1Zn1 and Cd2Zn2). Zinc promoted plant growth and development, particularly in the highest Zn and combined Zn/Cd treatments (Zn2 and Cd2Zn2). The Zn treatment resulted in substantial moss chlorophyll content and highest percentage relative growth rate in biomass value (0.23 mg L−1 and 106.8%, respectively); however, the Cd2Zn2 treatment achieved maximal production of chlorophyll a and total chlorophyll (0.29 and 0.51 mg L−1, respectively) due to synergistic effects. These findings suggest that Christmas moss is a highly metal-tolerant and adaptable bryophyte species. Zinc was essential for reducing the detrimental effects of Cd while simultaneously promoting moss growth and biomass development. Furthermore, Christmas moss exhibited hyperaccumulation potential for Cd and Zn in the Cd2Zn2 and Zn alone treatments, as evidenced by highest Cd and Zn values in gametophores (1002 and 18,596 mg per colony volume, respectively). Using energy dispersive X-ray fluorescence (EDXRF) spectrometry, atomic percentages of element concentrations in moss gametophores in the Zn2, Cd2 and combined Zn/Cd treatments were generally in the order: K > Ca > P > Zn > Cd. When comparing the atomic percentages of Zn and Cd in gametophores, it is likely that the higher atomic percentage of Zn was because this element is essential for plant growth and development.

Introduction

Studies of heavy metal (HM) phytoremediation in contaminated terrestrial and aquatic ecosystems using vascular plants have been conducted extensively in Thailand. Such plants generate substantial biomass, possess strong tolerance and adaptation to polluted environments, and exhibit high uptake of HMs. Eucalyptus camaldulensis Dehnh. and Acacia mangium Willd. are examples of trees and shrubs suitable for HM remediation over large areas including barren mine sites1. These plants have been used to rehabilitate abandoned mine sites contaminated with high concentrations of lead (Pb) at the Song Toh (KEMCO) mine in Kanchanaburi Province and zinc (Zn) and cadmium (Cd) at the Padaeng mine in Tak Province2. The phytoremediation potential of non-vascular plants has also been thoroughly studied; however, this work includes only micro- and macroalgae. These plants are utilized in field settings for treatment of wastewater3,4. The study of other non-vascular plants in Thailand for phytoremediation, especially bryophyte species, is very limited.

Studies are underway on the biology and ecology of Scopelophila cataractae (Mitt.) Broth., also known as ‘rare copper moss,’ in which accumulation and localization of HMs in tissue are being investigated. When compared to other bryophyte species at field sites in Thailand, S. cataractae displayed noticeably higher concentrations of several HMs in its tissue, including iron (Fe), copper (Cu), Cd, and Zn5,6. In tissue culture experiments, S. cataractae produced the greatest number of gametophores and buds (26 per cushion and 255 per 25 mm2, respectively) following addition of copper sulfate (500 μM) to the media. This species accumulated significant quantities of Cu in tissue (≈ 3000 mg kg−1), indicating that it can be classified as a hyperaccumulator of Cu6. Studies on bryophyte communities at Thailand sites where substantial Zn and Cd occur from Zn mining operations have recently been conducted. Bryophyte species identified at the contaminated sites are found throughout Thailand and have medium diversities; however, certain species demonstrated the ability to accumulate certain HMs such as Cd (50 mg kg−1) for Philonotis thwaitessii Mitt. and Zn (300 g mg−1) for Bryum coronatum Schwägr.7. Terricolous moss V. montagnei, widely distributed in terrestrial ecosystems, was identified in streambank soil at the Pha Daeng waterfall; tissue contained medium concentrations of Cd and Zn (8.1 mg kg−1 and 1961.9 mg kg−1, respectively)7,8. Nonetheless, V. montagnei may function as an accumulator of HMs that can be used in field settings in practical situations. This species can be cultivated on HM-contaminated agricultural land in order to produce biomass and accumulate substantial HM concentrations; such work has never been documented elsewhere. This moss may be suitable for use in phytoremediation experiments.

By virtue of substantial HM accumulation in gametophore shoots, several moss species, such as Scopelophila ligulata (Spruce) Spruce and Funaria hygrometrica Hedw. have the potential to function as hyperaccumulators of iron (Fe) and Pb, respectively. Gametophore shoots can absorb more heavy metals than can the rhizoid, produce substantially greater biomass, and both directly and indirectly absorb HMs from soil through rhizoids, followed by transfer to gametophores as well as to the air via phyllidium surfaces9,10. Multi-elemental imaging via fluorescence (XRF) spectrometry is an innovative technique that has facilitated substantial advances in several sub-disciplines of plant science by enabling the investigation of plausible mechanisms underlying HM uptake and metabolism in plants. Application of XRF techniques allows for quantitative measurement of the elemental distribution of a specified area of plant tissue; in the current context, this includes shoots of hyperaccumulators of HMs11. XRF is effectively employed for a variety of purposes, including agronomic study. By providing clarification to research questions in plant physiology, phytopathology, bioremediation, and seed technology, XRF contributes to a better understanding of plant metabolism and development12. Owing to the non-destructive feature of XRF, this instrument may enable time-resolved analyses of plant tissue in vivo conditions. It can additionally be combined with other non-destructive methods13.

Few studies have been conducted on the effects of HMs, including Cd and Zn, on bryophytes, particularly moss species. Owing to scarcity of data, the precise level of adaptability (e.g., tolerance, HM accumulation and distribution) of suitable bryophyte species to serve as bioindicators of HMs in harsh environments is not certain14,15. The primary aim of the reported research was to determine growth and HM accumulation of V. montagnei (Christmas moss) grown in hydroponic culture dosed with varying concentrations of Cd, Zn, and of mixtures of the two metals. Findings from this study may elucidate the synergistic effects of Cd and Zn mixtures on this species. The XRF technique was used to map elemental distribution in surfaces of gametophore shoots of Christmas moss.

Materials and methods

Moss specimen and cultivation on soil

Moss samples were collected from a site in Mae Sot District, Thailand that is co-contaminated with Cd and Zn. Christmas moss was the only species selected at this site following identification using the Moss Flora of China16. Moss samples were labeled, stored in paper bags, and brought to the laboratory. Moss tissue was gently rinsed several times with tap water to remove adhering soil and other debris, followed by gentle rinsing with deionized (DI) water. Moss samples were transferred to the University research farm. Soil at the farm was spread evenly over the cultivation area (approximately 0.5 ha) and covered with nylon nets to provide shade from excessive sunlight. Prior to moss cultivation, sand and gravel were placed over the soil. To maintain moisture levels, tap water was sprayed in the morning and evening. Once per month, 1 kg of slow-release fertilizer (Osmocote; 14-14-14) was diluted with 30 L of DI water for watering moss. Christmas moss was grown for at least one year or until it reached the desired biomass for the experiments. All methods used to determine phytoremediation potential of the Christmas moss (e.g., cultivation methods, HM determination) were carried out according to local guidelines ensuring ethically conducted research.

Experimental design of hydroponic cultures

A healthy sample of Christmas moss was selected from the University research farm and thoroughly rinsed several times with tap water followed by rinsing with DI water to remove attached soil. The moss was then acclimatized to a modified Hoagland’s nutrient solution in PVC containers in an air-conditioned room at Mahidol University, Nakhonsawan Campus, for 14 d. The Hoagland solution had low phosphate content (0.2 mmol KH2PO4), and FeCl3⋅6H2O was included instead of Na(Fe)EDTA at pH 5.5. Environmental conditions for moss cultivation throughout the experiment were as follows: temperature range of 23–27 °C, photoperiod of 12-h, relative humidity of roughly 36.4–38.9%, and average light intensity of 1500 ± 330 lx.

Following plant acclimatization, 300 g (F.W.) of moss was placed into individual 12 L plastic containers (30 cm × 40 cm × 10 cm) containing 400 mL of modified Hoagland’s solution with pH adjusted to 5.5 and having extremely low phosphate (0.01 mmol KH2PO4). A total of 54.4 mL of DI water was added to each container daily to ensure that the entire moss sheet was submerged for the duration of the 3-month experimental period. The containers were placed on a modified propagation bench in a completely randomized design (CRD). In order to maintain optimal nutrient levels for moss during the experimental periods, 600 mL of Hoagland's solution (very low phosphate) was added to the container at 1.5 months. Each of the following solutions—Cd(NO3)2 (Merck, Germany), Zn(NO3)2 (Merck, Germany), and Cd(NO3)2 + Zn(NO3)2 solution—were added with Hoagland's solution and DI water. The Cd solution concentrations were 0.064 and 0.128 mg Cd L−1 (designated as Cd1 and Cd2) and Zn solution concentrations were 0.047 and 0.094 mg Zn L−1 (Zn1 and Zn2). The combined Zn/Cd solution concentrations were 0.064 mg Cd L−1 + 0.047 mg Zn L−1, and 0.128 mg Cd L−1 + 0.094 mg Zn L−1 (designated Cd1Zn1, and Cd1Zn2). These HM solutions were formulated based on Cd and Zn concentrations detected in the Mae Tao stream, which serves as irrigation water for local agricultural fields in contaminated areas of the Mae Tao River Basin. While being suitable for plant growth, the designated HM concentrations were used to mimic naturally contaminated water17,18; greater HM concentrations may impart have phytotoxicity effects. Hoagland’s solution and DI water without addition of Cd and Zn served as the control treatment. At the start of incubation, moss in the three containers, each represented by three replicates and of the same fresh weight, was oven-dried for 3 d. The dry weight of the moss in each container was noted on day 0 of the experiment. Fresh moss was cultured in the container with three replicates of each treatment. Following harvest at 3 months, moss tissue was gently rinsed with DI water, oven-dried for 3 d, and the dry weight of each replicate from each treatment was noted.

Cadmium and zinc determination in plant tissue

Following weighing of the dried moss samples, tissue was ground to a fine powder using a mortar machine (IKA; A11 Basic, Japan). The ground tissue was passed through a 2-mm nylon mesh screen to obtain a fine powder. One-half gram dried weight of moss sample was digested with 70% nitric acid (HNO3) at 100 °C for 10 to 15 min, and 1:1:1 (DI water: HNO3: hydrochloric acid (HCl) at 110 °C for 10 to 15 min in a heating block. The solutions were allowed to cool for approximately 1 h. Impurities were filtered with Whatman No. 42 filter paper and quantitatively transferred to a 25-mL volumetric flask. The volume of the solution was adjusted using 2% HNO3 (TraceMetal, Merck, Germany). Cadmium and Zn concentrations were determined using a flame atomic absorption spectrophotometer (FAAS; AAnalyst 600, Perkin Elmer). Calibration curves were used to determine the concentration of a sample by analyzing a series of Cd and Zn standard solutions (Cd(NO3)2 and Zn(NO3)2; Merck, Germany) in 2% HNO3. A set of six concentrations were used to generate new calibration curves, and newly prepared standard solutions were used each time.

Analysis of chlorophyll content

Chlorophyll was extracted based on the method of Sitthichoptham et al.19 with modifications. A total of 0.1 g dried moss was ground to a fine particle size. Ten mL of 80% acetone was added to a Pyrex test tube containing the finely ground material, and the tube was left overnight in a dark cabinet. The solution was passed through Whatman No. 42 filter paper and centrifuged for 10 min at 8965×g. Using a UV–visible spectrophotometer, 3 mL of supernatant was transferred to a cuvette to measure absorbance at 663 nm for chlorophyll a and 646 nm for chlorophyll b, respectively. Elemental mapping in gametophore shoots

Quantitative analysis of elemental distribution across the entire gametophore surface (roughly 2 cm) was determined using energy dispersive X-ray fluorescence (EDXRF; HORIBA XGT-5200, Indonesia) spectrometry. Following placement of the dried moss on a type-affixed acrylic, the distribution of K, Ca, P, Fe, Zn, and Cd was determined. The Rh X-ray tube was operated at 50 kV and 1 mA, whereas the fluorescence detector was operated at 0–40 keV.

A high index value can also suggest that a plant is more tolerant of a harsh environment (i.e., contaminated media)20. Results and discussion

Growth performance of Christmas moss

Dry biomass production of Christmas moss was in the following order: Zn2 (14 g) > Ctrl (12 g) > Cd1 (11.8 g) ≈ Cd1Zn1 (11.8 g) > Cd2Zn2 (11.5 g) > Zn1 (11.2) > Cd2 (10.8 g) (Fig. 1a). Moss grown in the highest Zn concentration (Zn2) had greatest dry biomass content when compared to other treatments (p < 0.05). The biomass content of the Zn2 treatment was 1.2 times greater than that of the control. Furthermore, the Zn2 treatment resulted in highest percentage relative growth rate when compared to other HM-amended treatments (106.8%, p < 0.05, Fig. 1b). This suggests that Christmas moss in the Zn2 treatment was more resilient. The other treatments had lower percentage relative growth rate in biomass when compared to control (p < 0.05). This result implies that Zn promotes growth of Christmas moss. Zinc is an essential trace element in moss physiology, serving as a structural building block for numerous proteins and enzymes. Both vascular and non-vascular plants require small quantities of this micronutrient; it additionally helps regulate a variety of biological and metabolic processes that enhance plant growth and development. Zinc levels typically range from 15 to 60 mg kg−1 in tissue of all green plants, while they range from 0.10 to 2.0 mg kg−1 in growth media22. However, evidence from previous studies shows that excessive Zn concentrations in the media can result in growth inhibition and toxicity symptoms in bryophytes, though these effects are less acute than those of Cd, Pb, and Cu, respectively15,23. According to Shaw24, a Zn concentration of 60 mg kg−1 in solid media inhibited protonemal development of F. hygrometrica by up to 40 to 90%.Figure 1Growth performance of Christmas moss: (a) total dry biomass; (b) percentage relative growth rate in dry biomass. Moss dry biomass production decreased dramatically when exposed to the highest Cd concentration (p < 0.05) (Fig. 1). Dry biomass production decreased by approximately 1.1 times in the Cd1 treatment (0.064 mg Cd L−1) when compared to the Cd2 treatment (0.128 mg Cd L−1) (p < 0.05). A previous investigation25 revealed the effects of excessive Cd concentrations in solution media on growth of bryophytes. The gemmaling growth of Marchantia and Funaria spores was significantly inhibited by Cd concentrations as high as 10 mg L−1. Conversely, Cd concentrations ranging from 1 to 5 mg L−1 significantly enhanced gemmaling growth of Marchantia and Funaria spores. The respiratory rates of the bryophyte species decreased by roughly two times (1185 mL O2 evolved g−1 D.W.) when exposed to the highest Cd concentration (50 mg L−1) as compared to lowest Cd concentration (1 mg L−1), where respiratory rate was 2404 mL O2 evolved g−1 D.W.26. Previous research has indicated that metal cations disrupt the essential cations in charge of water oxidation, interfering with the O2-evolving complex27. Furthermore, short-term stress is known to induce heat shock protein 70 (HSP70), a biomarker of stress. Maresca et al.28 reported that HSP70 was induced in liverwort Conocephalum conicum (Marchantiales) grown in 6.6 mg L−1 CdCl2-contaminated media. However, expression of this stress-inducible gene was inhibited at excessive Cd concentrations, e.g., 66 mg L−1 CdCl2.

Expression of stress genes in plant tissue is triggered by increased Cd content; cell damage is an additional detrimental consequence. Other related consequences include alteration of photosynthesis and respiration and inhibition of mineral absorption. This is consistent with research showing that increased Cd uptake caused progressive decline of photosynthesis of the moss Rhytidiadelphus squarrosus (Hedw.) Warnst., which in turn resulted in a decline in dry biomass production29. Total dry biomass of the tested moss did not differ significantly between the Cd1 and control treatments (p > 0.05); this indicates that moss development may not be affected by low to medium concentrations of Cd. When cytohistological damage to gametophores was taken into account, the comparison of Cd effects at different concentrations (36 and 360 μM CdCl2) in media revealed only minor injuries: no significant damage occurred in the moss Leptodictyum riparium Hedw. Phylloids. No impact on moss growth and development was noted; nevertheless, HM tolerance and survival rates of individual species may vary30. Shoot segments of R. squarrosus experienced lower gas exchange rates, intracellular K concentrations, dry weight per unit length, and cell wall:protoplast weight ratios when exposed to Cd between 4 to 16 h. Their findings suggest that gametophore tolerance to Cd was dependent on moss age and vitality31.

The majority of phytoremediation research focuses on response effects to a single HM; few studies have evaluated the impact of multiple HMs on the capacity for uptake and accumulation by plants. Furthermore, significantly less research has been conducted on the phytoremediation potential of bryophytes. Mixed HMs in various media (soil, sediment, water) may interact antagonistically, additively, or synergistically to plants32. When Christmas moss was exposed to combined Zn and Cd, total dry biomass was marginally greater as compared to the highest Cd concentration treatment (p < 0.05). Mechanisms underlying the interactions of HMs include their absorption, translocation, accumulation and physiological activity in plants33. Numerous studies have demonstrated that Zn functions as a diluent in Cd-contaminated media17,18. It can alleviate toxic effects, aiding in adaptation of plants, and effectively antagonize the absorption of other HMs, as well as reduce the degree of HM absorption in plants. Zinc furthermore plays an important role in overall plant development and reproduction32,34. If Zn were supplied to soil in the proper quantity, plants grown on Cd-contaminated media might have an improved chance of survival.

Heavy metal-contaminated sites, particularly those associated with mining activities, have the potential to generate HM mixtures, which are consequently distributed throughout the surrounding environment. Moss substrate at the Nancha manganese mine (China) had substantial contamination by multiple HMs, which led to reduction in moss species diversity. This could be a result of substantial species replacement and high species richness variation. Conversely, growth substrates contaminated with low concentrations of HMs exhibited a high diversity of moss species. Such results could be attributed to low species replacement and low variation in species richness35. The effects of individual HMs on response of moss is challenging when multiple HMs coexist in field sites36. Synergistic action of several HMs could result in biphasic, i.e., beneficial or harmful effects, on moss. Total dry biomass at the higher concentration (Cd2Zn2) was marginally less than that of the lower concentration (Cd1Zn1). However, the Cd2Zn2 treatment exhibited higher percentage relative growth rate in dry biomass production when compared to the Cd1Zn1 treatment by about 6.2 × (p < 0.05). Given the higher Zn concentration in the media, the synergistic effects of Cd and Zn may improve moss tolerance and adaptability. In other words, an optimal Zn concentration could improve growth performance even in the presence of a high Cd concentration.

Chlorophyll extracted from Christmas moss

Chlorophyll content is a crucial factor in estimating photosynthetic capacity and vegetative production. Mosses are capable of photosynthetic assimilation in extremely low light—they have low light saturation levels for photosynthesis, or approximately 20% of full sunlight23. Chlorophyll a and b, as well as carotenoids (xanthophyll and carotene), serve as the primary accessory pigments of these bryophytes37. Values for chlorophyll were typically in the following order: total chlorophyll > chlorophyll a > chlorophyll b (Fig. 2). Only the control and Cd1 treatments showed somewhat higher values for chlorophyll b than chlorophyll a; the total chlorophyll value was the highest. Previous research has shown that differences in chlorophyll forms across different moss species are influenced by the photoinhibitory conditions of photosystems I and II38. The Cd1 and Cd2 treatments were the only ones that underperformed compared to the control in terms of total chlorophyll, and chlorophyll a and b values (p < 0.05). The Zn2 treatment greatly increased chlorophyll a, b, and total chlorophyll contents (p < 0.05) (Fig. 2). Optimal levels of HMs such as Zn and Cu could promote moss growth because they increase the quantity of chlorophyll in cells. This has been demonstrated by their rapid recovery from pollution damage and their inherent high growth rate23. Previous reports indicate that excess Cd and Cu in contaminated media are more toxic than is Zn, resulting in moss having a lower tolerance and survival rate. Moss is more susceptible to Cd and Cu toxicity than to Zn-contaminated media (greenhouse or field site), where moss species were shown to have higher chlorophyll contents and greater metabolic rates39,40. Contents of total chlorophyll and chlorophyll a in the combined Zn/Cd treatments, particularly Cd2Zn2, were clearly higher than for other treatments (p < 0.05), which could indicate that synergistic effects benefited moss physiology, growth and development.

Cadmium and Zn accumulation and uptake

Comparable patterns were noted in accumulation and uptake of Cd and Zn, as indicated by the order: Cd2Zn2 > Cd2 > Cd1 > Cd1Zn1 > Ctrl (Fig. 3a,b) and Zn2 > Cd2Zn2 > Zn2 > Cd1Zn1 (Fig. 3c,d), respectively. This finding implies that the quantities and ratios of Cd and Zn in the supplemental solutions affected the capability of moss tissue to take up and accumulate these metals. According to Sricoth et al.41, high metal concentrations in hydroponic experiments led to higher metal uptake and accumulation by aquatic plants. Statistical analysis showed no significant differences (p > 0.05) in Cd or Zn uptake. Several reports indicate that addition of Zn to Cd-contaminated media dilutes Cd while alleviating uptake capability of Cd. Following harvest, Christmas moss accumulated substantial Zn and Cd in the Cd2Zn2 treatment, and Cd2 and Zn2 treatments, even though most HM treatments had lower total dry biomass when compared to the control. A previous study43 reported that Christmas moss removed HMs from contaminated water for seven days. It is categorized as an aquatic moss based due to its robust growth in water. Substantial concentrations of Zn (8 mg kg−1) and Cu (9.9 mg kg−1) accumulated in Christmas moss in the current investigation. Christmas moss accumulated substantial Zn in the Zn2 and Cd2Zn2 treatments (> 1000 mg kg−1) whereas Cd concentrations in Cd2 and Cd2Zn2 treatments exceeded 80 mg kg−1. The aquatic moss L. riparium cultured in media supplemented with 10−3, 10−4, 10−5, and 10−6 M CuSO4, ZnCl2, CdCl2, and Pb(NO3)2 was investigated using transmission electron microscopy. Sub-lethal quantities of HMs (Cd, Pb, Zn, and Cu) caused slight alterations in moss cells; Cd, however, induced the most significant modifications44. The ability of L. riparium to withstand high concentrations of HMs in its medium without experiencing significant harm to cells or subcellular components indicates that it may be suitable as a bioindicator of HM accumulation44,45.

In the current study, Zn was accumulated in moss tissue at higher levels than was Cd. Findings in the current study vary from those in previous studies, i.e., they report HM uptake efficiencies in different orders for different moss species (i.e., Riccia crystalina Kash, Timmiella barbuloides (Brid.) Mönk., Hyophila involuta (Hook.) A. Jaeger, and Physcomitrium repandum Mitt ex Bruehl.): Pb > Co, Cr > Cu, Cd, Mo, Ni, V > Zn > As46,47. Kłos et al.48 reported that individual epigeic moss species exhibited varying sorption capabilities for Cd and Zn solutions (CdCl2 and ZnCl2) – sorption capacities of Cd ranged from 0.0319 to 0.1335 mmol g−1, whereas those for Zn ranged from 0.0491 to 0.1287 mmol g−1. These authors listed sorption capacities of moss species in the order: Sphagnum sp. > Pleurozium schreberi (Brid.) Mitt. ≥ Dicranum scoparium Hedw. ≥ Thuidium tamariscinum (Hedw.) Schimp. (syn. Thuidium tamariscifolium Lindb.) > Eurhynchium praelongum (Hedw.) Bruch & Schimp. > Leucobryum glaucum (Hedw.) Angstr. > Polytrichum commune Hedw. The order of HM accumulation in tissue of two epiphytic mosses found in urban locations, Fabriona ciliaris (Brid.) Brid. and Leskea angustata Taylor., were as follows: Zn > Pb > Cr > Cd49. The varying capacities of plant species as regards HM uptake and accumulation may be associated with plant genotype and source of anthropogenic load50.

Although Christmas moss thrives on moist soil substrate, it is best described as aquatic or aquarium moss as it lives in a submerged state43. The Christmas moss employed in this study may therefore be able to adapt to harsh conditions in hydroponic culture. As shown by the maximal Cd accumulation and uptake values in the Cd2 and Cd2Zn2 treatments (83.7 to 87 mg kg−1 and 903.5 to 1002.1 mg per colony volume, respectively), the Christmas moss demonstrated its potential for Cd accumulation. Furthermore, moss tissue experienced substantial Zn uptake and accumulation in the same treatments (1115.8 to 1328.2 mg kg−1 and 12,835.3 to 18,595.6 mg per colony volume, respectively), indicating its potential for Zn hyperaccumulation. Christmas moss is proposed as a valuable phytoremediation agent in contaminated areas and for biomonitoring heavy metal pollution by virtue of its effectiveness in metal uptake and accumulation, adaptability, and apparent tolerance to Cd and Zn stress.

Many studies have reported that different moss species absorb HMs from rain and aerial deposition via a whole-surface adsorption mechanism. A variety of moss species are therefore employed as bioindicators of HM pollution51,52. A recent study7 determined slightly higher Zn accumulation (1961.9 mg Zn kg−1, respectively) or 1.5 to 1.8 × in Zn/Cd co-contaminated areas of Tak Province, Thailand, compared to the current study, but markedly lower Cd accumulation in Christmas moss (8.1 mg Cd kg−1) or 10.3 to 10.7×. Further investigation is required to confirm the adaptation and tolerance capability regarding cellular/subcellular damage and moss cell response to HMs. If moss species exhibit high endurance to HM stress without experiencing significant cellular or subcellular alterations, they may be employed as bioindicators of HMs44.

A variety of moss species exhibit substantial heavy metal accumulation in their tissue, which may render them suitable candidates for phytoremediation. This is because moss species primarily accumulate HMs in their gametophores, while their rhizoids only function to anchor plantlets to the substrate9. According to the current investigation, Cd and Zn uptake values can shed insight on the phytoremediation capacity of Christmas moss because metal uptake reflects the relationship between metal content and plant biomass. Furthermore, this species can be grown using conventional agricultural practices for phytoremediation of HM-contaminated soil.

Atomic percentages of elemental concentrations in Christmas moss gametophores across selected treatments were generally in the order: K > Ca > P > Zn > Cd (Table 1 and Fig. 4). Zinc and Cd concentrations were undetectable in the Zn2 treatment. Since ZnSO4 was added in small amounts to Hoagland's solution, Zn was present in all treatments. As a macronutrient, phosphorus is essentially required by plants at concentrations greater than 1 mg kg−1, while low concentrations of micronutrients such as Zn, i.e., 0.1 to 100 mg kg−1 D.W., are necessary for plant growth and development52. Phosphorus occurs in organic forms including polyphosphate, phosphate diesters (C-O-P-O-C bond, e.g., nucleotides and phospholipids), and phosphate monoesters (C-O-P bond, e.g., phylate). It is distributed uniformly across gametophores; similar patterns have been observed in tissue of other plant species53. Potassium and calcium are immobile elements; thus, their distribution in moss tissue was less pronounced, but they were nonetheless present in substantial quantities. These elements are essential for cell signaling, ion homeostasis and cell wall strength in plants54. Compared to Cd, zinc had higher percentage concentrations on gametophores; nevertheless, as they are immobile elements, their atomic percentages were determined to be low. According to XRF analysis, Zn was distributed preferentially in the capsula of Polytrichum commune and had also accumulated substantially in stems55. In urban polluted areas, Grimmia pulvinata (Hedw.) Smith had a lower Zn content by 140.3×, 34×, and 11.3× compared with Ca, K, and Al, respectively56. The degree of HM accumulation, including Zn and Cd, in field locations is influenced by moss genotype and point source. Conclusions

Numerous studies have explored the potential of moss species as phytoremediators and bioindicators of heavy metals; however, certain criteria and conditions, such as proper cultivation and practices in growth media, especially soil, cannot be achieved at field sites. According to the current study, Christmas moss (V. montagnei) is a viable species that can be grown in soil and yield the required biomass for effective remediation of contaminated soil and water. Only the highest Zn concentration showed substantial improvement in growth performance when comparing growth rate in relative biomass between HM and control treatments. This may indicate that Christmas moss is capable of adapting to and tolerating polluted conditions when an appropriate quantity of Zn is included in the media. It was found that the effects of HM supplements in the media could be explained by analyzing the amount of chlorophyll present in moss tissue. Chlorophyll content is regarded as a useful tool for measuring degree of HM sensitivity in harsh environments. According to the current study, addition of Cd to the media decreased levels of chlorophyll, while Zn treatments—either alone or in combination with Cd—mitigated detrimental effects on moss, as shown by elevated chlorophyll levels. It must be noted that the presence of a single HM may impart either beneficial or detrimental effects on moss, and a suite of HMs may cause synergistic effects. The current findings imply that Zn promotes growth and development of moss, particularly in cases of significant Cd contamination. Furthermore, Zn in the highest combined Zn/Cd concentration treatment effectively increased the concentration of bioavailable Cd in the media, improved remediation efficiency, and may enhance plant phytoremediation potential for treatment of Cd and Zn co-contaminated soil. EDXRF analysis revealed that Zn content in moss tissue was greater than was Cd content, suggesting that metal uptake and accumulation by moss are influenced by HM concentration and moss genotype.
