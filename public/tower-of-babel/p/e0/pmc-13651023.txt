Factors associated with low social support and participation among Brazilian older adults with disabilities: a cross-sectional study based on the 2019 national health survey
Authors: Érika Giovana Carvalho da Silva, Matheus de Sousa Mata, Damião Ernane de Souza, Saionara M A Câmara
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLack of social connections and limited social participation are associated with an increased risk of adverse health outcomes. Understanding the determinants that influence these social dimensions is essential for the early identification of vulnerable groups.Therefore, this study aimed to analyze factors associated with low social support and low social participation among Brazilian older adults with disabilities.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted with 5,511 older adults (≥ 60 years) with permanent disabilities, based on data from the 2019 Brazilian National Health Survey. Social support from friends and family, as well as the frequency of participation in physical, community, and religious activities, were assessed in relation to sociodemographic, economic, and health-related variables. Pearson’s Chi-square tests and multivariate logistic regression models were applied.ResultsThe prevalence of permanent disability was 24.7%, and the mean age of participants was 73.2 years. Lower levels of social support were observed among older adults aged 60–74 years, individuals with lower educational attainment, those without a partner, residents of urban areas in the North and Southeast regions, those with multiple domains of disability, and those reporting poorer self-rated health. Low social participation was more prevalent among men, older adults of more advanced age, White individuals, those with lower education, urban residents, individuals with poor self-rated health, those not using assistive devices, smokers, those with multiple domains of disability and those with no multimorbidity.ConclusionsThe findings contribute to the identification of groups most vulnerable to low social support and low social participation among older adults with disabilities. Because these patterns vary according to the type of social support and type of participation, this study highlights the importance of targeted strategies to promote social inclusion in aging populations.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12877-026-07910-z.Keywords: Social support, Community participation, Older adults, People with disabilities, Population surveys

Introduction

Population-based studies estimate that, worldwide, 250 million older adults live with moderate to severe disabilities, while in Brazil [1] nearly half (47.2%) of people with disabilities are older adults [2]. This convergence between population aging and disability increases exposure to social vulnerability and reinforces the relevance of social relational determinants in later life. Within this context, social support and social participation have been consistently identified as modifiable determinants of health in aging populations. Social support refers to the resources and processes derived from interpersonal relationships, including emotional, instrumental, and informational assistance, as well as the perceived availability and quality of support, often embedded within close social ties and broader social networks [3, 4]. In contrast, social participation refers to individuals’ engagement in social, civic, and leisure activities within their community, reflecting their active involvement and interaction within social environments [5]. Although conceptually distinct, both constructs represent complementary dimensions of social experience, each showing independent associations with health and functioning outcomes [6, 7].

The World Health Organization [8] identifies social support and social participation as modifiable determinants with protective effects on health and considers them as essential to the care of older persons [9]. The lack of social support and reduced participation in later life have been associated with increased risk of cognitive decline, depressive symptoms, anxiety, social isolation, loneliness [10, 11], and higher morbidity and mortality rates [12]. Therefore, continuous monitoring of these factors is crucial, especially among those most vulnerable to adverse outcomes, such as individuals with disabilities.

Although older adults and people with disabilities face several common barriers and are exposed to a complex accumulation of multiple vulnerabilities, an integrated analysis of these two conditions has not yet been fully clarified. A recent report from the government of the state of São Paulo highlights the importance, and the current insufficiency, of studies addressing the intersection of aging and disability in the Brazilian population, which should inform strategies and the protection of rights for this demographic group [13]. According to the International Classification of Functioning, Disability and Health (ICF) [14] functioning and participation are shaped by the dynamic interaction between personal, environmental, and health-related factors, providing a comprehensive framework for understanding how multiple levels of influence may affect social support and participation among older adults with disabilities. Cultural variations, personal characteristics, and social disparities operate at multiple levels that may directly affect social participation and support among older adults with disabilities [15].

Although factors related to social support and social participation are already known in the general older population, evidence specifically focused on people with disabilities remains more limited, particularly regarding these outcomes. Furthermore, these relationships remain insufficiently explored among older adults with disabilities. Existing studies often examine aging and disability separately, without addressing how their interaction may influence social support networks and opportunities for participation, despite the need for integrated approaches that consider the interplay of multiple individual and contextual factors, as highlighted by the ICF framework [14]. This gap is particularly relevant in low- and middle-income countries, where social inequalities may intensify the barriers experienced by older adults with disabilities. To date, nationally representative evidence in the Brazilian context remains limited, particularly regarding analyses that address the intersection between aging and disability, thereby constraining the understanding of how overlapping vulnerabilities affect social support and participation and hindering the development of targeted strategies to promote inclusion, health, and well-being, as well as limiting the identification of vulnerable groups and the monitoring of population-level risk factors [16, 17].

In this context, the objective of this study is to analyze the factors associated with different dimensions of social support (family and friends) and social participation (physical/recreational, community, and religious activities) among Brazilian older adults with disabilities, focusing on sociodemographic, economic, and health-related variables, using data from the 2019 National Health Survey. This study provides nationally representative evidence to improve the understanding of these associations in this population.

Methods

Study design

This is a cross-sectional, population-based study that used data from the 2019 edition of the Brazilian National Health Survey (PNS, from the Portuguese Pesquisa Nacional de Saúde), a nationwide household survey conducted by the Brazilian Institute of Geography and Statistics. All variables were assessed at the time of the survey data collection, reflecting a single point-in-time measurement. This study followed the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines [18] for cross-sectional studies, and the PNS research project was approved by the National Research Ethics Committee (approval number 3.529.376). All Informed Consent Forms are available on the PNS website (https://www.pns.icict.fiocruz.br/aspectos-eticos/).

PNS sample planning

The PNS [19] uses a three-stage cluster sampling design, including census tracts, households, and residents. Within each selected household, one adult resident was randomly selected from a list of eligible residents to respond to the individual questionnaire. The questionnaire is structured into modules, including household-level information, data on all residents, and an individual module administered only to the selected respondent. Information on social support and social participation was obtained from this individual module and was therefore available exclusively for these respondents. Consequently, the present analysis was restricted to individuals selected for the individual interview.

Population and eligibility criteria

Eligible participants were individuals aged 60 years or older, of both sexes, who reported at least one type of permanent disability – visual, physical, hearing, or intellectual. The analysis was further restricted to participants with available data on social support and social participation, which were collected exclusively through the individual questionnaire.

The final analytical sample consisted of 5,511 individuals. Figure 1 presents the flowchart of sample selection for the present study.

Definition of disability

Permanent disability was defined based on self-reported level of difficulty in performing the following activities: seeing, hearing, walking or climbing steps, lifting a bottle of water, handling small objects, or performing usual activities. For each item, response options were: “no difficulty,” “some difficulty,” “a lot of difficulty,” or “cannot do at all.” Individuals were classified as having a disability if they reported “a lot of difficulty” or “cannot do at all” in at least one activity, in accordance with the recommendations of the Washington Group on Disability Statistics [19].

Outcome variables

The primary outcomes of this study were low social support and low social participation.

Social support was assessed using two items from the PNS questionnaire addressing perceived availability of support from family members and friends [19]: “How many relatives or family members can you count on in good and bad times?” and “How many close friends can you count on in good and bad times?” Response options included “none,” “one,” “two,” or “three or more.” For analytical purposes, responses were grouped into two categories: “none or one” and “two or more,” with low social support defined as reporting “none or one”.

Additionally, sensitivity analyses were conducted using an alternative categorization (none vs. one or more sources) to assess the robustness of the findings.

Social participation was assessed using three PNS items related to engagement in social activities over the previous 12 months [19]: “how often have you met with other people for participation in sports, physical exercise, recreational, or artistic activities?”, “how often have you participated in meetings of groups such as neighborhood or employee associations, social or community movements, student organizations, or similar?” and ” how often have you attended collective religious activities?”. Response options for all items were: “more than once a week,” “once a week,” “2 to 3 times a month,” “a few times a year,” “once a year,” or “never”. For analysis purposes, responses were dichotomized into “never” and “once or more per year” and low social participation was defined as reporting “never” for each activity domain.

Exposure variables

The independent variables were defined based on evidence regarding social support and social participation among older adults [20, 21], as well as the ecological model of aging [22, 23], which highlights the interaction between individual, social, and environmental factors in impacting health and functionality. To provide an organized analytical framework, the variables were organized according to domains consistent with the ICF model [14].

Variables related to body functions and structures included self-reported diagnosis of depression (yes or no), multimorbidity, and the number of disability domains. Multimorbidity was defined based on self-reported medical diagnoses of chronic conditions and categorized as none, one, or two or more conditions. The number of disability domains was constructed by summing the presence of different types of impairments (visual, hearing, physical, and intellectual) and categorized as one, two, or three or more domains, reflecting the extent of disability across multiple functional dimensions.

Environmental factors comprised educational level (no education, elementary, secondary, or higher education), living with a partner (yes or no), per capita income (less than one minimum wage, one to two minimum wages, or more than two minimum wages), area of residence (rural or urban), geographic region (North, Northeast, Southeast, South, or Midwest), and use of assistive devices (yes or no).

Personal factors included sex (female or male), age group (60–74 years or ≥ 75 years), smoking status (yes or no), self-rated health (very good, good, fair, poor, or very poor) and race/skin color (White or non-White). A diagrammatic representation of the hypothesized relationships among study variables, based on the ICF framework, was developed to guide the analytical strategy (Supplementary Figure S1). All variables were considered potential determinants of social support and participation and were included in the analyses as independent variables. Health-related variables, including multimorbidity, smoking status, self-reported diagnosis of depression, and the number of disability domains, were evaluated in the adjusted models as potential confounders, and their inclusion in the final models followed the predefined statistical criteria.

Statistical analysis

Initially, descriptive analyses were performed, and study variables were presented using weighted relative frequencies. Associations between dependent and independent variables were assessed using survey-adjusted Pearson’s Chi-square test, considering a 95% confidence level. Given the distribution of responses and the need to ensure sufficient cell sizes across categories, outcomes were dichotomized to facilitate model stability and interpretability.

Separate binary logistic regression models were fitted for each outcome: support from family or relatives, support from friends, participation in physical or recreational activities, community or group activities, and religious activities. In the first stage (Model 1), variables with p-values < 0.20 in the bivariate analyses were included. A backward stepwise procedure was then applied to obtain parsimonious models. The final adjusted models (Model 2) included variables that remained significantly associated with each outcome (p < 0.05).

Although a backward stepwise procedure was used to derive parsimonious models, variable selection was also guided by theoretical and epidemiological relevance. Results were expressed as odds ratios (OR) with 95% confidence intervals. Multicollinearity among independent variables was assessed in preliminary analyses using variance inflation factors (VIF) and tolerance values, with no evidence of problematic collinearity observed. The distribution of categorical variables was examined through cross-tabulations to identify potential sparse data issues, and all categories presented adequate frequencies to ensure model stability. Given the use of survey-weighted logistic regression, traditional goodness-of-fit tests are limited; therefore, model adequacy was evaluated based on the consistency, direction, and plausibility of the estimated associations, as well as the precision of confidence intervals.

All analyses accounted for the complex sampling design using the Complex Samples module of SPSS. Sampling weights, primary sampling units (clusters), and stratification variables were incorporated through a predefined analysis plan file. Both Pearson’s Chi-square tests and logistic regression models were conducted using Complex Samples procedures, ensuring consistent incorporation of sampling weights, clustering, and stratification across all analyses to obtain nationally representative and unbiased estimates.

Analyses were conducted using all available data from individuals who met the eligibility criteria. No missing data were observed among the variables included in the analyses within the final analytical sample (n = 5,511), and all models were therefore fitted using the same sample. Additional analyses stratified by type of disability (physical, hearing, visual, and intellectual) were conducted as sensitivity analyses to explore potential heterogeneity in the associations. These analyses followed the same modeling strategy as the main models and are presented in the supplementary material. Statistical analyses were performed using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS), version 21.0.

Results

Data from 5,511 Brazilian older adults with permanent disabilities were analyzed. The mean age was 73.24 years (± 9.08), ranging from 60 to 107 years. Among all older adults (≥ 60 years) interviewed in the 2019 PNS (n = 43,554), 10,768 reported at least one permanent disability, corresponding to a prevalence of 24.7%. Details on the distribution of the study population according to disability type and selection status are provided in Supplementary Table S1. Because information on social support and social participation was collected only from the selected household respondent, the analytical sample comprised 5,511 individuals, representing 12.6% of the total older-adult sample in the PNS. In the analytical sample, most participants were women, aged 75 years or older, non-White, had elementary education, had per capita income between one and two minimum wages, lived in urban areas, in the Southeast region of Brazil, did not live with a partner, did not use assistive devices, reported fair self-rated health, were not smokers or diagnosed with depression, had at least one domain of disability and had two or more chronic conditions (Table 1).

Factors associated with social support

In bivariate analyses (Table 1), low social support from family and friends was more frequent among those not living with a partner, those reporting poorer self-rated health, and urban residents. Age was associated with low family support only, with a higher frequency observed among those aged 60–74 years. Low support from friends was more frequent among those with lower educational attainment, per capita income below one minimum wage, residing in the North region of Brazil, and higher number of disability domains.

In the final multivariable logistic regression model (Table 2), not living with a partner remained associated with higher odds of low support from both family (OR = 1.61; 95% CI: 1.26–2.04) and friends (OR = 1.21; 95% CI: 1.01–1.46). Compared with individuals living in rural areas (reference group), those residing in urban areas had higher odds of low support from friends (OR = 1.87; 95% CI: 1.51–2.32). Lower educational attainment was associated with higher odds of low support from friends, particularly among individuals with no formal education (OR = 2.60; 95% CI: 1.61–4.21) and those with primary (OR = 2.47; 95% CI: 1.56–3.90) and secondary education (OR = 2.00; 95% CI: 1.20–3.35), compared with those with higher education. Poorer self-rated health was also associated with higher odds of low support from friends, both among those reporting regular (OR = 1.43; 95% CI: 1.10–1.84) and poor or very poor health (OR = 1.77; 95% CI: 1.34–2.33). For family support, individuals aged ≥ 75 years had lower odds of low support compared with those aged 60–74 years (OR = 0.42; 95% CI: 0.32–0.56). Additionally, living in the North (OR = 1.61; 95% CI: 1.03–2.53) and Southeast (OR = 1.73; 95% CI: 1.13–2.66) regions was associated with higher odds of low family support, whereas living in the South was associated with lower odds of low support from friends (OR = 0.61; 95% CI: 0.45–0.84). Regarding the number of disability domains, individuals with three or more domains had higher odds of low social support from friends (OR = 1.37; 95% CI: 1.07–1.76) compared with those with only one domain. Factors associated with social participation

In bivariate analyses (Table 1), low social participation was more frequent among younger individuals and those with lower education and income. Poorer self-rated health was consistently associated with lower participation in physical and community activities. Lower participation in physical activities was also observed among rural residents, those living in the North and Northeast regions of Brazil, and those not using assistive devices. Lower participation in community activities was more frequent among individuals not living with a partner. In contrast, higher participation in religious activities was observed among younger individuals, non-White individuals, and those living in rural areas.

In the final multivariable logistic regression models (Table 3), those aged ≥ 75 years had higher odds of low participation in physical (OR = 1.55; 95% CI: 1.24–1.93), community (OR = 1.89; 95% CI: 1.36–2.61), and religious activities (OR = 1.76; 95% CI: 1.45–2.13).

Poorer self-rated health remained associated with higher odds of low participation in physical (OR = 2.05; 95% CI: 1.44–2.91) and community activities (OR = 2.15; 95% CI: 1.26–3.69).

Not using assistive devices was associated with higher odds of low participation in physical (OR = 1.40; 95% CI: 1.09–1.79) and religious activities (OR = 1.31; 95% CI: 1.05–1.63).

Compared with individuals living in rural areas, those residing in urban areas had higher odds of low participation in community (OR = 1.74; 95% CI: 1.25–2.42), and religious activities (OR = 1.41; 95% CI: 1.13–1.76). Additionally, individuals with per capita income between one and two minimum wages had higher odds of low participation in community activities (OR = 1.63; 95% CI: 1.09–2.42), but lower odds of low participation in religious activities (OR = 0.71; 95% CI: 0.54–0.93). Men (OR = 1.67; 95% CI: 1.38–2.03) and non-White individuals (OR = 0.68; 95% CI: 0.56–0.82) showed significant differences in religious participation, with men having higher odds of low participation and non-White individuals showing lower odds of low participation. Both smoking and having three or more domains of disability were associated with higher odds of low participation in religious activities (smoking: OR = 1.62; 95% CI: 1.26–2.09; ≥3 domains: OR = 1.89; 95% CI: 1.51–2.36), whereas multimorbidity was associated with lower odds (OR = 0.70; 95% CI: 0.54–0.93).

Sensitivity analyses by type of disability

Additional sensitivity analyses stratified by type of disability (Supplementary Tables S2–S5) showed overall patterns consistent with the main analysis, with some relevant differences highlighted below. Regarding low family support, factors such as poor health and smoking, which did not show a significant association in the analysis of any type of disability, were associated with higher odds of low support only for hearing and visual disabilities, respectively. Similarly, the use of assistive devices reduced the odds of low support only among those with intellectual and hearing disabilities. Living in a rural area, which was also not significant for disability in general, was associated with lower odds of low support for intellectual disability and higher odds for visual disability. Regarding support from friends, lower income was significantly associated with low support only for people with physical and visual disabilities, while educational level was not associated only among those with intellectual and hearing disabilities. Not living with a spouse, a factor associated with low support when considering any type of disability, remained associated only for intellectual disability.

The associations found in the overall sample for older age, associated with lower participation in physical and community activities, and lower income, associated with community activities, showed non-significant results for intellectual and hearing disabilities.

Poor self-rated health was associated with worse participation in community activities for all types of disability, with non-significant results only for hearing disability. The opposite pattern was found for self-rated health and participation in religious activities, with significant results only for hearing disability. Considering participation in religious activities, race and area of residence remained associated only among those with physical disability, while lower income remained associated only for hearing disability.

Discussion

In this study, socioeconomic and demographic factors, health perceptions, and assistive device use were identified as factors associated with low levels of social support and social participation among Brazilian older adults with disabilities. Based on the results of the multivariate analyses, we identified that social support and participation varied according to the specific dimension of social support and the domain of social participation analyzed.

Individuals with lower educational attainment, living without a partner, residing in urban areas, living in the North or Southeast regions of the country, reporting poorer self-rated health, and those with three or more disability domains were associated with less favorable patterns of social support, particularly in relation to support from friends, whereas older adults aged ≥ 75 years showed lower odds of low family support. Additionally, living in the South region of Brazil was associated with lower odds of low support from friends. We also found that men, individuals aged ≥ 75 years, urban residents, those with lower educational attainment, poorer health perceptions, and those not using assistive devices had lower levels of social participation, especially in physical and community activities. Conversely, per capita income between one and two minimum wages and those identifying as non-White showed lower odds of low participation in specific domains, such as religious activities. In addition, smoking and having three or more domains of disability were associated with higher odds of low participation in religious activities, whereas multimorbidity was associated with lower odds in this domain, highlighting factor-specific patterns of association. Sensitivity analyses using alternative categorizations of social support resulted in weaker associations and reduced discriminatory capacity, supporting the adopted approach (data not shown).

These findings highlight that specific characteristics are important markers of vulnerability among older adults with disabilities and should be considered in the design of population-level strategies aimed at strengthening social support and participation. Our results may contribute to the identification of priority groups for outreach, monitoring, and targeted public health interventions. Attention to these characteristics may help inform strategies to strengthen social support and participation among older people with disabilities. To our knowledge, no other study has previously investigated this information. Given the large sample size, our findings may provide valuable insights into the older population with disabilities in Brazil. However, considering that the prevalence observed among respondents was lower than the estimates for the general context, these results should be interpreted with caution. Still, they provide relevant evidence regarding characteristics associated with more vulnerable social profiles, including weaker support networks and lower levels of social participation.

Social support

The present study identified that lower levels of education are associated with weaker networks of support from friends. This finding is consistent with previous research in older populations indicating a relationship between low educational attainment and increased emotional and social loneliness among older adults [24], constructs that, although related to social support, represent distinct dimensions of social experience, as well as the tendency for higher education to be correlated with greater social support [25]. Furthermore, poorer self-rated health was associated with lower levels of support from friends, consistent with studies conducted in older adults, including those with specific health conditions, showing that self-assessed health in older adults is positively associated with stronger support [26]. Illiteracy and poor health perceptions thus emerge as barriers to social integration, underscoring the need for policies aimed at mitigating these obstacles.

In addition, our results revealed that individuals not living with a partner are more likely to experience low social support, both from family and friends. These findings are in line with evidence from studies with older people showing that marriage enhances interaction with other family members and has a protective role against loneliness, although it does not eliminate the risk, as marital quality is critically important [24]. Being married or cohabiting is often associated with higher levels of social support, given that spouses or partners frequently play the primary role as support providers and facilitate the expansion of friendship networks [27]. Living arrangements, whether with a partner or other household members, serve as strong predictors of social support and are protective against mortality [28].

Conversely, living alone has been shown to negatively affect perceived social support [29].

Our results also indicate that older individuals tend to report greater family support. However, the literature on the relationship between age and perceived social support is mixed, with most evidence derived from general older populations, including studies showing a decline among those over 80 years of age [11, 25]. These discrepancies may partly reflect cultural factors, given that social norms and expectations of care in old age vary between collectivist and individualist societies. Although these patterns are not yet fully understood, it is important to note that isolation and loneliness among older adults, while conceptually distinct from social support, increase the risk of multiple health conditions, hospitalizations, and mortality. Moreover, studies focusing specifically on people with disabilities, across different ages, suggest that support networks are often centered on the nuclear family [30]. On the other hand, social support, whether from family, friends, or neighbors, combined with social participation may help mitigate these risks [31].

This study also found that residing in the South region of Brazil was associated with stronger support from friends, whereas living in the Southeast and North regions was linked to weaker family support. In addition, residing in urban areas was associated with higher odds of low social support from friends [32]. These findings align with previous research indicating that rural older adults often rely more on proximal community ties, while urban residents may experience more dispersed social networks [33]. Additionally, studies suggest that the role and strength of social support may vary across these contexts, with relatively stronger patterns observed in rural settings [34]. Furthermore, evidence from Brazilian populations, including individuals with disabilities, indicates that support networks may vary across different regions of Brazil, influencing the challenges faced by older adults with disabilities [35]. These geographic patterns should be interpreted as contextual differences rather than direct explanatory mechanisms. These geographic variations in social connections may reflect broader contextual differences, including cultural, psychosocial, and socioeconomic factors.

Our findings further indicate that the presence of three or more disability domains is associated with lower levels of social support from friends. Although the relationship between disability and social support is complex and not strictly linear, existing evidence suggests that functional limitations can alter the structure and dynamics of social relationships over time [36]. Longitudinal findings indicate that changes in disability status are accompanied by changes in social networks, reflecting progressive restrictions in social participation and interaction [36]. Moreover, research in people with physical disabilities highlights that social support is influenced by multiple contextual factors and may shift toward more proximal or obligatory relationships, rather than broader social ties [37, 38]. Importantly, these findings suggest that disability may be associated with differences in the structure and availability of social support. In this context, the accumulation of disability domains may contribute to a reconfiguration of social networks, with a relative reduction in support from friends. Taken together, these findings suggest that disability in older adults may not only be associated but also intensify existing social inequalities in support, amplifying the effects of socioeconomic and health-related vulnerabilities on social relationships.

Social participation

Our study identified that older age, lower educational attainment, and poorer self-rated health were associated with reduced social participation among older adults with disabilities.

These associations were observed consistently across multiple domains of participation, although the results varied by type of activity. This finding is supported by previous research involving both older populations [39] and individuals with disabilities [40], which also demonstrates that unfavorable socioeconomic conditions and negative health perceptions are linked to reduced social participation. Longitudinal monitoring over 16 years has shown that advanced age, low education, and poor mental health are predictors of faster declines in social participation among older adults [41]. In parallel, increases in physical illness and difficulties with activities of daily living, commonly associated with advanced age, directly affect individuals’ health perceptions [42]. Moreover, living in adverse socioeconomic conditions limits opportunities for social participation, as many activities entail costs that restrict access to both venues and participation [24].

Beyond socioeconomic and health-related factors, variations in social participation were also associated with gender and race. Our results indicated that women and non-White individuals were more engaged in religious activities. Similar findings were reported in a study that analyzed differences in racial and gender determinants of religious participation [43]. Evidence further suggests that engagement in any community activities reduces the risk of disability and mortality [44], and that greater involvement in social activities is associated with longer disability-free life expectancy [45].

Although religious participation may serve as an inclusion factor for certain groups, we found that not using assistive devices was associated with lower social participation, particularly in physical and religious activities, among Brazilian older adults with disabilities. Assistive technologies play a fundamental role as enablers of participation and social inclusion, as their use can reduce functional barriers. However, the relationship between assistive device use and social participation may also be influenced by other factors, such as severity of disability, stigma associated with device use, and environmental inaccessibility [46]. Evidence suggests that willingness to participate may remain high even among individuals with severe mobility limitations, and that living with family may facilitate social participation among people with disabilities [40]. These findings suggest that social participation among people with disabilities can be enhanced by reducing environmental and individual barriers [40].

Regarding habits and health-related characteristics, smoking and having three or more domains of disability were associated with lower participation in religious activities. Smoking has been linked to complex patterns of social participation, with evidence suggesting a bidirectional relationship in which smoking behaviors are shaped by, and embedded within, specific social contexts [47]. In this sense, lower participation in religious activities among smokers may reflect the normative expectations of these environments, where smoking tends to be less socially accepted. Similarly, the accumulation of disability domains is a key determinant of participation restrictions; however, recent evidence highlights that the relationship between disability and social participation is heterogeneous and varies according to the type of activity and socioeconomic context [48].

In contrast, multimorbidity is often associated with constraints on social participation; however, evidence suggests that individuals with multiple chronic conditions may actively seek social participation as a strategy to cope with health-related challenges [49]. This may help explain the inverse association observed in this study, particularly in the context of religious activities, which may represent accessible sources of emotional and social support.

However, residual confounding, particularly related to functional status and disease severity, cannot be ruled out. Taken together, these findings reinforce the domain-specific nature of social participation, indicating that behavioral, functional, and health-related factors may operate differently depending on the social context and type of activity.

Additionally, contextual and regional factors also play a significant role in shaping barriers and facilitators of social participation. National survey data from Canada indicate that distinct characteristics of rural and urban communities must be taken into account when considering aging in place [50]. In this study, individuals residing in urban areas were more likely to report low participation in religious and community activities, suggesting that environmental and social features of urban settings may impose barriers to engagement in certain domains of participation.

Limitations

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, our sample size was reduced because we considered as older adults with disabilities only those who reported higher levels of permanent difficulties. However, these criteria followed the operational recommendations of the PNS [19] based on international parameters from the Washington Group on Disability Statistics. Another limitation concerns the subjective nature of self-reported difficulties, as discrepancies may exist between participants’ responses and objective reality, potentially introducing bias into the data. Nonetheless, it is important to note that the conceptualization of disability, even in population-based surveys, remains under development and requires further refinement of its biopsychosocial dimensions.

Non-response bias must also be considered, since the sample excluded individuals who were not the household respondents selected by the PNS but who might otherwise meet the study criteria. Furthermore, due to the cross-sectional design, causal relationships cannot be established, and findings are limited to identifying explanatory factors associated with support and social participation. Future longitudinal studies are strongly recommended to identify additional environmental and contextual factors influencing support and social participation over time.

Although we adjusted for a range of sociodemographic and health-related variables, including multimorbidity, depressive symptoms, smoking status, and number of disability domains, residual confounding cannot be ruled out. Unmeasured factors such as severity of functional dependence, quality of social relationships, and broader environmental and contextual characteristics may influence both disability and social participation outcomes.

These variables were not available in the National Health Survey dataset, as this study relied on secondary analysis of publicly available data.

The dichotomization of social support and participation outcomes may have reduced variability and limited the ability to capture more nuanced associations. Collapsing ordinal response categories into binary measures may have attenuated differences in frequency and intensity of social participation, given the broad thresholds used to define participation. While this approach facilitates interpretation and model estimation, it may simplify complex and multidimensional social constructs and should be considered when interpreting the findings.

However, sensitivity analyses using alternative categorizations yielded consistent results, supporting the robustness of the findings. In addition, the use of a stepwise model-selection strategy, while useful for identifying parsimonious models, may be sensitive to sample-specific variations and should be considered when interpreting the results.

Despite these limitations, this study offers significant contributions. To date, no other research has analyzed, as comprehensively, the size of support networks and the breadth of social participation activities in a nationally representative sample of Brazilian older adults with disabilities. These findings provide relevant insights and reinforce the importance of further investigations aimed at identifying strategies to strengthen support and expand social inclusion for this population.

Conclusions

Low social support and limited social participation among older adults with disabilities were associated with factors such as low education, absence of a partner, poor self-rated health, non-use of assistive devices, advanced age in relation to social participation, younger age in relation to social support, having multiple domains of disability, and smoking. In contrast, living in rural areas or in the South region of Brazil was associated with lower odds of low social support. Additionally, being female, identifying as non-White and having multimorbidity were associated with lower odds of low participation in religious activities.

These findings also indicate that patterns of social participation vary according to the type of activity and social context. Importantly, disability, particularly when involving multiple domains, emerged as an important marker of social vulnerability associated with less favorable patterns of social support and participation among older adults. These results highlight the relevance of sociodemographic, economic, and health-related characteristics, as well as assistive device use, as markers of vulnerability associated with these outcomes. From a policy and practice perspective, these findings may inform the development of context-sensitive strategies, including approaches such as social prescribing, which aim to address social and health-related needs through community-based resources. Overall, the results contribute to a more nuanced understanding of social inequalities in later life and may support efforts to promote social inclusion among older adults with disabilities.
