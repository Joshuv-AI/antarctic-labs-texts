Perineal hernia repair with synthetic mesh and bivectorial gluteus maximus myocutaneous island flap
Authors: Inês Carreira, Nelson Teixeira, Alexandra Rosa, José Guerreiro, Joana Pedro Marques
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPerineal hernia is a rare but significant complication of pelvic surgery. This report describes a unique reconstructive approach for a complex perineal hernia, combining mesh reinforcement, a modified gluteus maximus myocutaneous flap, and bone anchors – an approach not previously described.We present the case of an 88-year-old male who developed a perineal hernia with small bowel protrusion 14 years after abdominoperineal resection. Surgical repair utilized a combined synthetic mesh and a bivectorial gluteus maximus island rotation-advancement flap. This flap provided dead space obliteration, pelvic floor reinforcement via a sling-like configuration, and improved skin coverage. Bone anchors secured both the mesh and flap, ensuring stable fixation in an anatomically compromised area. The patient had an uneventful postoperative course, with no recurrence or complications during follow-up. This technique offers a promising and novel reconstructive option for challenging perineal hernias.Keywords: Perineal hernia, Synthetic mesh, Modified gluteus flap, Bone anchors

Introduction

Perineal hernias (PH) are a rare but well-recognized complication of pelvic surgery, particularly following abdominoperineal resection (APR) or pelvic exenteration. PH typically presents as a bulging mass associated with discomfort that worsens when standing or sitting. In most cases, however, it remains asymptomatic. When symptomatic, it may be associated with urinary dysfunction, bowel obstruction, or skin erosion.1 Presentation as a protrusion is exceedingly rare.

PH can be repaired using either open or laparoscopic surgical techniques. Among open approaches, both transabdominal and transperineal routes have been described, with similar outcomes.2 The repair technique generally involves reduction of the hernial sac and its contents, followed by closure of the hernia defect using synthetic mesh, biological mesh, or autologous tissue. Currently, there is no clear evidence favoring one technique over another. However, generally, reconstruction using autologous tissue is typically reserved for large or recurrent hernias, particularly in the context of irradiated tissue or infection.3,4

The purpose of this article is to describe the use of synthetic mesh in combination with a bivectorial gluteus maximus island rotation-advancement flap for PH repair. This flap serves a triple function: obliteration of the dead space resulting from hernia reduction, reinforcement of the pelvic floor through a sling-like configuration, and improved skin coverage using a cutaneous island.

Clinical case

An 88-year-old Caucasian man, independent in activities of daily living, and able to ambulate without assistance, with a history of adenocarcinoma of the middle third of the rectum, stage T3N0M0, underwent APR with partial sacre and coccygectomy 14 years ago. He also received adjuvant chemo and radiotherapy. In addition to this condition, the patient has a history of heart failure, arterial hypertension, hypothyroidism, and benign prostatic hyperplasia. The patient also has a history of symptomatic gallstone disease, for which he underwent laparoscopic cholecystectomy 10 years ago, and bilateral inguinal hernia, treated with bilateral inguinal hernioplasty 8 years ago.

From an oncological perspective, he had an adequate evolution, with no evidence of local or distant recurrence.

He presented to the emergency department with protrusion of small intestine loops through a perineal wound (Figure 1). In the emergency department, hernia reduction and primary closure were performed by the general surgery team, and the patient was admitted for inpatient care. After 10 days of antibiotic therapy and regular bowel transit, he was discharged, and correction of the PH was deferred.

Initial presentation in emergency department. Protrusion, complication of pH.

The skin of the pelvic floor was thin and redundant. The dermal atrophy and skin expansion were likely the result of years of insufficient support for the pelvic floor. Adjacent to this central skin redundancy, there were visible signs of radiotherapy sequelae.

The repair of this perineal hernia involved collaboration between general surgery and plastic and reconstructive surgery teams. The procedure was performed under general anesthesia, with the patient in the prone jackknife position (Figure 2).

Initial presentation in the operating room after protrusion and primary closure in the emergency department.

Using a transperineal approach, an elliptical skin incision was made to excise compromised and excessively stretched skin. The hernia sac was carefully dissected and opened to ensure that all small bowel loops were clear. The resulting defect measured approximately 10 cm × 8 cm.

Next, hernioplasty was performed using polypropylene mesh (Optilene® Mesh 30 × 30 cm), which was trimmed to fit the defect. The mesh was secured in the perineum using 2 mm Mitek anchors (Mini QuickAnchor™ Plus 2/0 Suture), with one anchor placed in the sacrum and two in each ischial tuberosity. Anteriorly, sutures from each anchor secured the mesh to the superficial transverse perineal muscles, remnants of the levator ani muscle, and Colles’ fascia (Figure 3A).

(A) Defect after hernioplasty and reconstruction with mesh. Mitek anchors were used to secure the mesh to the pelvic floor. (B) The gluteus maximus bivectorial myocutaneous island rotation-advancement flap was applied two different pivot points of mobilization: cutaneous V-Y advancement and muscular pendular rotation.

After excising the redundant and expanded skin, a gluteal myocutaneous island flap, based on the inferior gluteal artery, was used to cover the sacral and perineal defect.

We present a modification of the classical myocutaneous island flap for this case, in which two different pivot points of mobilization were applied: cutaneous V-Y advancement and muscular pendular rotation.

First, a V-Y island was designed over the gluteus maximus muscle following traditional references. Subsequently, the presence of the inferior gluteal artery was confirmed using a handheld Doppler, with perforators localized in the middle third of the vertical line from the posterior superior iliac spine to the gluteal fold. The flap incision area was infiltrated with 1 % lidocaine with adrenaline to minimize bleeding and postoperative pain. The skin and subcutaneous tissue were incised down to the gluteus maximus and fascia. The posterior femoral cutaneous nerve of the thigh was identified and preserved. Subcutaneous dissection was then performed in the direction of the gluteal tuberosity.

The attachment of the gluteus maximus to the iliotibial tract and the gluteal tuberosity of the femur was identified and released. This resulted in a strong muscle sling capable of supporting the pelvic floor. After direct visualization of the entry point of the inferior gluteal vascular pedicle into the muscle, the distal insertion was reflected proximally and reinserted into the pelvic floor (Figure 3B). This transfer of the muscle sling was performed using a rotational pendulous movement. The same anchors used for mesh fixation were applied to secure the muscle sling.

Finally, a perforator was identified in the central part of the island, and the island was circumferentially freed from the underlying muscle. Perforator dissection was not necessary.

Two active drains were placed: one deep to the muscle and another along the flap in the subcutaneous tissue. Monofilament sutures were used for flap adaptation, and skin closure was performed with staples (Figure 3).

The drains were kept in place for 15 days, and the staples were removed after 12 days. After surgery, the patient was maintained only in a prone or lateral position. Motor rehabilitation was initiated on postoperative day 15, focusing particularly on hip stretching exercises, and he also resumed walking for short periods. He was discharged after 32 days of hospitalization, without complications and walking autonomously, with no complaints of hip instability or rigidity.

The patient continued follow-up in consultation. At 6 months, there were no signs of hernia recurrence or other complications. The patient reported no mobility or gait complications, although this was not formally evaluated.

Discussion

PH are an uncommon complication following abdominoperineal resection for low rectal cancer. Factors such as chemoradiation and sacrococcygectomy contribute to an increased risk of perineal hernia formation. There is no consensus on the optimal surgical treatment, and the best technique for PH repair remains controversial.3 The most common approaches include mesh reconstruction, muscular or myocutaneous flaps, or a combination of both.5

Meshes used for pelvic reconstruction can be broadly divided into synthetic and biologic. Synthetic meshes are advantageous because of their inert nature, greater tensile strength, availability and lower cost, which was the rationale for their use in the present case. However, synthetic materials are more susceptible to infection and may therefore be less suitable in contaminated fields. In such situations, biologic meshes are generally considered a safer alternative.6

Mesh reconstruction reinforces the pelvic floor but does not fill the perineal defect. In irradiated tissue, mesh may be less effective due to fibrosis and is associated with higher recurrence rates.7 In this patient, the absence of the sacrum and coccyx resulted in constant exposure of the skin and subcutaneous tissue to intra-abdominal pressure. Clinically, this led to an initial thinning of the skin and subcutaneous tissue in the perineal and sacral regions, followed by herniation of abdominal contents. Given these considerations, we believe that hernioplasty alone is insufficient to counteract intra-abdominal pressure and gravitational forces, increasing the risk of recurrence.

In cases of extensive pelvic dead space, myocutaneous flaps are generally more effective in achieving adequate obliteration due to the greater bulk of tissue they provide. Traditionally, the preferred flaps for perineal reconstruction are the vertical rectus abdominis myocutaneous (VRAM) flap and the gracilis flap. The VRAM flap is well-established, with good long-term survival outcomes. However, it is associated with significant donor-site morbidity and an increased risk of incisional hernias due to abdominal wall weakening.8,9 Additionally, the need to reposition the patient during surgery and the presence of an ostomy are drawbacks that further discourage this approach. The gracilis muscle flap is another alternative, offering lower donor-site morbidity. However, it is associated with a higher rate of perineal complications compared to the VRAM flap, as it provides insufficient bulk to fill large perineal defects.5

Fasciocutaneous flaps, such as the gluteal rotational flap or the gluteal V-Y advancement flap, and perforator flaps like the inferior gluteal artery perforator (IGAP) and the superior gluteal artery perforator (SGAP) flaps, remain valuable alternatives in situations with limited dead space, such as after newer techniques like ELAPE (extralevator abdominoperineal excision), where pelvic tissue loss is less pronounced.9,10 These flaps have the advantage of being associated with lower donor site morbidity, as the muscle is preserved.

As described, a good alternative for these defects is the gluteus maximus bivectorial myocutaneous island rotation-advancement flap. This design allows for independent movement between the muscle and the skin island. The distal insertion of the gluteus maximus can be tensioned as needed and securely fixed to the bone, providing both structural support and adequate defect coverage. This approach restores the function of the pelvic diaphragm to support the intra-abdominal contents, obliterates the dead space and enables a tension-free repair of the perineal defect.

Normally, only ligation of the visceral branches of the internal iliac artery is performed during an APR. However, it is advisable to confirm the presence of the inferior gluteal artery with a handheld Doppler or Angio-CT, as bilateral internal iliac artery ligation would preclude the use of gluteus maximus flaps. Because the inferior gluteal nerve accompanies the inferior gluteal artery, isolated nerve injury is rare and can be ruled out intraoperatively using electrostimulation. .5 Preservation of inferior gluteal nerve helps prevent muscle atrophy and permits the flap to retain its contractile properties, allowing it to act dynamically and partially reproduce the function of the levator ani. Nevertheless, even in the event of nerve damage, the muscular component would still provide static support.

One of the challenges of this procedure is the lack of surrounding tissue to anchor and support the mesh and muscle sling. To address this, the mesh was secured to the sacrum and ischial tuberosities using bone anchors.11 The same anchors, along with an additional anchor at the ischial tuberosity, were then used to fix the muscle in its new position, creating a muscular sling at the pelvic floor. The use of bone anchors to secure either a mesh or a flap has been previously described in the literature.8,11 However, the simultaneous use of anchors to fix both the mesh and the flap has not been previously reported.

The distinct and independent movement of the skin island allows for optimal adaptation to the perineal skin's requirements. We prefer a V-Y design for closure, as it facilitates easy donor-site closure and allows for re-advancement if needed. Additionally, the minimal dissection required for island advancement results in less seroma formation compared to rotational skin flaps.

Lastly, detachment of the gluteus maximus from its femoral insertion raises the potential concern of impaired hip stability and gait. In the present case, no postoperative limitations in ambulation or hip function were observed, and the patient remained fully independent in daily activities. However, the long-term morbidity of this approach remains uncertain, and further clinical experience with functional assessment is required to clarify its long-term impact on mobility and hip function.

Conclusion

PH is rare, and achieving an effective long-term treatment is challenging. In this case, a combination of two techniques was used: reconstruction with mesh and the use of a flap. For coverage with autologous tissues, a modification of the classic gluteal myocutaneous flap was performed, aiming to strengthen the pelvic floor and reduce the likelihood of recurrence.

The use of anchors to simultaneously fix both the mesh and the flap has not been previously described in the literature. It is particularly useful in cases where there is a lack of tissue to support the mesh, allowing for the strengthening and stabilization of the pelvic floor.

The gluteus maximus bivectorial myocutaneous island rotation-advancement flap is a good option for reconstructing large perineal defects. Our modification of this flap, involving muscular rotation and reinsertion into the pelvic floor, creates a dynamic muscular sling that enhances the support provided by the mesh. Additionally, the island advancement enables a tension-free closure of the defect.
