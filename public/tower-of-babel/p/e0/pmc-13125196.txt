Novel MC1R variants cause red hair and lighter skin color
Authors: Deepak K. Kashyap, Srashti J. Agrawal, Meenakshisundaram Karthikeyan, Disha Biradar, Eerapagula Ramesh, Krithika Subramanian, Lomous Kumar, Urgyan Chorol, Audditiya Bandopadhyay, A. Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryThe melanocortin 1 receptor (MC1R) is one of the fundamental proteins regulating skin and hair color in mammals. In India, the red hair color (RHC) phenotype is extremely rare. We analyzed MC1R and identified an ultra-rare pathogenic variant, c.872C>A (p.Ala291Asp), that is responsible for the RHC phenotype in an Indian child. Further, we screened the complete coding region of MC1R in a total of 11,021 individuals, representing 91 distinct Indian populations across India, and found a total of 21 novel or ultra-rare variants. In silico, in vitro, and zebrafish-based functional analysis showed that c.742G>T (p.Gly248Cys) and p.Ala291Asp variants lead to loss-of-function (LoF) effects. The distribution of some of these variants differed significantly among Indian populations. One of them is MC1R c.−226A>T (rs3212363), which is significantly associated with lighter skin pigmentation in the Bodh population inhabited in Ladakh. On average, TT homozygotes were 8.46 melanin units lighter compared with AA homozygotes (95% confidence interval [CI]: 3.211 to 13.72; adjusted p = 0.0005). Our study identifies and functionally validates ultra-rare MC1R variants as potential causes of RHC phenotype in Indians.

Introduction

Melanocortin 1 receptor (MC1R) is one of the fundamental proteins regulating skin and hair color in mammals.1 MC1R is primarily expressed on the surface of melanocytes, which produce the pigment called melanin.2 MC1R is a highly polymorphic gene with classical evidence of positive selection in European populations.3 Many homozygous or compound heterozygous MC1R variants associated with red hair color (RHC; MIM: 266300) have been identified in Irish, Dutch, and Swedish populations.4,5

Although RHC is found in 1%–2% of the world population, its frequency varies greatly among populations of different ethnicities. However, in India, the RHC phenotype is extremely rare.6,7 Additionally, India has a diverse range of complexion tones, from pale to wheatish, brown, and dark brown, across various ethnic populations. Both genome-wide and candidate gene studies have suggested that variants in SLC24A5, TYR, and SLC45A2 are significantly associated with light skin color in South Asians.8,9,10 Nevertheless, a detailed genetic investigation of functionally relevant MC1R variants associated with the RHC phenotype and variable skin color has not yet been conducted in the Indian population.

In the present study, we identified an ultra-rare MC1R variant in an Indian child with RHC phenotype, which follows an autosomal-recessive mode of inheritance. Additionally, we screened MC1R in 11,021 samples from 91 ethnic populations of India to estimate the distribution of identified variation and to identify novel MC1R variants, if any. Further, we studied the effects of some of these variants on pigmentation using in silico, in vitro, and zebrafish models.

Material and methods

Studies involving human samples (blood) were approved by the institutional ethics committee of CSIR-Centre for Cellular and Molecular Biology, Hyderabad, India. Informed written consent was obtained from all the study participants. We performed whole-exome sequencing (WES) of an Indian child with the RHC phenotype to identify the associated genetic variant. We screened MC1R gene variants in 11,021 individuals, representing 91 diverse Indian communities. Of these, we used the whole-genome sequence data of 9,772 individuals from the GenomeIndia Consortium,11 and for the remaining 1,249 samples from our lab, we performed targeted resequencing. For genotype-phenotype association studies, 277 individuals from the Bodh community in the Himalayan region and 361 from the Mahto community in the Central India region have been included. We used in vitro and zebrafish models to characterize a few MC1R variants identified from this study.

A detailed methodology for WES and various experiments performed in this study is given in the supplemental information.

Results

Identification of the MC1R variant in an Indian child with the RHC phenotype

A 5-year-old Indian girl with the RHC phenotype (IV-2 in Figure 1A and proband in Figure B) exhibited red hair on the scalp, body, and eyebrows while retaining dark black eyes. WES of the child identified an ultra-rare homozygous MC1R variant, c.872C>A (p.Ala291Asp) (Figure 1C). Homology-based modeling of the p.Ala291Asp variant showed that substituting alanine (nonpolar) with aspartate (polar, negatively charged) introduced new intramolecular noncovalent interactions, reducing overall protein flexibility (Figure 1D). Sequence alignment revealed that residue 291 is evolutionarily conserved among vertebrates, with serine present in most species but replaced by alanine in primates—both residues being small and nonpolar (Figure 1D).

Multiple in silico prediction tools classified c.167A>G (p.Asn56Ser), c.742G>T (p.Gly248Cys), and p.Ala291Asp as potentially damaging, and according to ACMG-AMP criteria, they were categorized as variants of uncertain significance (VUSs) (Table S1; Figures S3 and S4).

In vitro functional characterization of p.Ala291Asp and p.Gly248Cys variants

Overexpression of the GFP-tagged wild-type MC1R and p.Ala291Asp variant in A375 cells (human melanocytes) for 24 h did not affect the membrane localization, unlike the previously reported c.880G>C (p.Asp294His) variant (Figure S5). However, the p.Gly248Cys variant in A375 cells showed partial accumulation in the cytosol, indicating that this variant partially impairs membrane localization of the mutant protein (Figure 2A). Overexpression of the p.Ala291Asp variant in HEK293T cells resulted in a significant increase in ERK1/2 phosphorylation at 24 and 48 h compared to the wild-type MC1R (Figures S6A and S6B), and the effect was more pronounced upon treatment with alpha melanocyte-stimulating hormone (α-MSH) ligand (10−6 M) for 1 h (Figure S6E). However, no significant difference in ERK1/2 phosphorylation was observed in cells expressing the p.Gly248Cys variant compared with wild-type MC1R (Figures S6D and S6E). Importantly, overexpression of both p.Ala291Asp and p.Gly248Cys variants led to a significant downregulation of microphthalmia-associated transcription factor (MITF) at 24 h (Figures S6A, S6C, and S6D). This downregulation was even more pronounced when cells were treated with α-MSH ligand (10−6 M) for 60 min (Figures S6F and 2B). In vivo functional characterization of p.Ala291Asp and p.Gly248Cys variants in zebrafish embryos

To further validate the LoF effects of p.Ala291Asp and p.Gly248Cys variants, we utilized zebrafish embryos as a model for the study. To investigate the effects of endogenous mc1r loss, a translation-blocking morpholino (MO) was used to suppress the expression of zebrafish-specific endogenous mc1r transcripts. Injection of the mc1r MO at the one-cell stage resulted in a significant reduction of pigmentation in the embryos at 2 days post-fertilization (2 dpf) compared to those injected with a scrambled control MO (Figure 2C). This pigmentation deficit, caused by MO-induced knockdown of zebrafish-specific endogenous mc1r, was rescued by overexpression of human wild-type MC1R (Figure 2C). However, co-injection of MO with plasmids carrying either p.Ala291Asp or p.Gly248Cys variants resulted in partial or no restoration of pigmentation levels, respectively, when compared to the wild-type MC1R (Figure 2C). To quantify these observations, we measured the mean gray value in the head region of the embryos. Notably, embryos injected with the p.Gly248Cys variant exhibited a less pronounced rescue than those injected with p.Ala291Asp, and their pigmentation levels were similar to those of MO-injected embryos (Figure 2D). These findings suggest that both p.Gly248Cys and p.Ala291Asp variants exhibit LoF effects in vivo, albeit to different extents.

Distribution of MC1R c.−226A>T (rs3212363) variant in Indian population

While screening the MC1R gene in the Indian population, the distribution of MC1R c.−226A>T (GenBank: NM_002386.4; rs3212363) variant revealed pronounced allele frequency differences across northern, northeastern, and southern Indian populations. This variant has previously been proposed as a candidate SNP associated with lighter skin pigmentation in South Asians, though replication studies failed to confirm this association.8 Global allele frequency data from gnomAD v.4.10, dbSNP, and Ensembl show that the T allele occurs at high frequencies in East and Southeast Asians (Japanese: 0.80, Korean: 0.76, Vietnamese: 0.58, and Cambodian: 0.56) and is lowest in Europeans (0.26). Within India, we observed substantial regional variation, with the highest T allele frequency (0.89) in the Bodh population (Ladakh) and the lowest (0.377) in a Dravidian tribe from South India (Figure S7B). Populations with lighter complexions in the north and northeast showed a higher percentage of T homozygotes (45%–67.1%), whereas South Indian groups with darker skin complexion showed much lower values (6.6%–32.5%). Chi-squared (χ2) tests revealed significant associations between allele frequencies and both linguistic (χ2 = 382.004, degrees of freedom [df] = 3, p < 1 × 10−6) and geographic (χ2 = 172.526, df = 5, p < 1 × 10−6) groupings (Tables S3 and S4; Figures S7C and S7D), implying geographic and genetic stratification within India.

Genotype-phenotype association of the rs3212363 variant with skin pigmentation

To investigate whether the rs3212363 variant is significantly associated with lighter skin pigmentation, we performed targeted sequencing-based genotyping among 277 participants (168 females and 109 males) from the Bodh community in Ladakh, with their average melanin index (MI) serving as the phenotype measure. The homozygotes with the derived allele (TT) of rs3212363 were observed to be, on average, 8.46 melanin units lighter compared with AA homozygotes (95% confidence interval [CI]: 3.211 to 13.72; adjusted p = 0.0005; Figure 3A). Regression analysis with AA, AT, and TT genotypes and MI units showed linear correlation with a significant non-zero slope (R2 = 0.057; 95% CI slope: −0.629 to −2.21; p < 0.0001; Figure 3B). For further understanding, we used SNPStats12 (a web tool for SNP analysis; https://www.snpstats.net/start.htm) to assess the effect of genotype on skin pigmentation phenotype. No significant interaction of the rs3212363 variant with gender was observed (interaction p = 0.57; Table S5), but among males, MI units showed linear correlation with AA, AT, and TT genotypes with a significant non-zero slope (R2 = 0.052; 95% CI slope: −6.578 to −0.671; p = 0.01; Figure 3B). In a replication cohort, we performed a genotype-phenotype association study among 361 participants (183 females and 178 males) belonging to the Mahto community in Central India. In the age- and gender-adjusted analysis, the TT genotype did not show a significant association with the observed lower MI (R2 = 0.003, p = 0.63; recessive model, p = 0.23; 95% CI: −0.97 to 3.99) (Figure 3C; Table S6). However, the interaction analysis with sex as a covariate, adjusted for age, showed that TT homozygous males were observed, on average, to be 6.27 MI units lighter compared with AA homozygotes (test for interaction in trend: 0.0063; 95% CI: 2.14 to 10.40; p = 0.01) (Table S6). Linear regression analysis with AA, AT, and TT genotypes and MI units showed a significant linear correlation, with a non-zero slope (R2 = 0.037; 95% CI slope −5.09 to −0.693; p = 0.01) (Figure 3D).

The CTCFBSDB 2.0 database (https://insulatordb.uthsc.edu/) predicted two distinct CTCF binding sites (one on the forward strand with a score of 4.3 and one on the reverse strand with a score of 8.3) situated one nucleotide downstream to the genomic location of rs3212363 (Figure 3C). To assess the functional impact of rs3212363, we performed a dual luciferase reporter assay in HEK293T cells. The firefly luciferase signal was significantly decreased in the presence of rs3212363 as compared to the wild type (p = 0.0001), indirectly suggesting that the TT genotype might decrease the overall expression of MC1R and thus is involved in the regulation of melanin synthesis in melanocytes (Figure 3D).

Discussion

In the present study, through WES of a child with the RHC phenotype and targeted screening of the MC1R gene in a total of 11,021 individuals, representing 91 endogamous populations across India, we identified a total of 21 variants (9 novel and 12 ultra-rare) in the coding region of the MC1R gene (Table 1). Further, we characterized the p.Gly248Cys and p.Ala291Asp variants using in silico, in vitro (A375 and HEK293T cells), and zebrafish models to show that these variants have LoF effects.

A diversity of skin pigmentation is observed among Indians, ranging from light to dark skin complexion. Generally, Dravidian speakers from Southern India (with a higher percentage of the ancestral South Indian genetic component) have a dark complexion with an average MI of 48.4 (range: 30–70).10,13 In contrast, Indo-European speakers from northern India (with a higher percentage of ancestral North Indian genetic components, genetically close to Middle Easterners, Central Asians, and Europeans) have lighter skin pigmentation (average MI: 35.6; range: 18–57) than South Indians.13,14 Similarly, Tibeto-Burman speakers (from Himalayan and Northeastern states of India with higher East Asian ancestry) also have lighter skin pigmentation (average MI: 40.2; range: 17–55; the Bodh community from this study; average MI: 31.8; range: 30-50 in East Asians) than South Indian populations.13,15,16,17 The genetic admixture of two or more distinct groups would have generated the diversity palette of skin pigmentation observed among Indians.13,18 Besides endogamy and consanguinity, geographical and environmental factors play an important role in Indian skin pigmentation diversity.18,19,20

The distribution of rs3212363 varies significantly among different populations globally. A higher percentage of homozygotes with the T allele was found among communities frequently observed to have lighter skin color in the North and Northeastern states in India. The association of rs3212363 with lighter skin pigmentation was further supported by a genotype-phenotype analysis of the Bodh community from Ladakh, in which homozygotes with the derived allele (TT) of rs3212363 were observed, on average, to be 8.46 melanin units lighter than AA homozygotes. However, in a replication cohort from Central India, only homozygous males with the TT genotype were observed to be, on average, 6.27 MI units lighter than AA homozygotes. In females, the TT genotype did not show a significant (R2 = 0.007, p = 0.25; recessive model p = 0.15; 95% CI: −5.47 to 0.85) reduction in the skin melanin content compared to AA homozygotes.

Sexual dimorphism in pigmentation may result from pleiotropy, hormonal modulation, or cultural differences in exposure to sunlight and clothing practices. Estrogen has been shown to directly influence melanogenesis through the canonical melanin synthesis pathway, while sexual selection and ancestry differences further shape phenotypic expression.21,22

The genetic ancestry components of people residing in Ladakh are different from those in Central India.13,18,23 Therefore, the combination of genetic determinants of skin pigmentation would vary between these two populations. The individual effect of the rs3212363 variant on skin pigmentation, being a polygenic trait, would be modified greatly due to different combinations of genetic determinants in these two populations.

Luciferase assays demonstrated that rs3212363 reduces MC1R transcription, possibly by disrupting CTCF binding near promoter regions. CTCF acts as a chromatin organizer and transcriptional regulator, enhancing RNA polymerase II and TAF3 recruitment.24 Alterations within or adjacent to CTCF motifs can reduce gene expression, as seen in IFITM3, where a similar SNP impairs CTCF binding and decreases expression, elevating influenza risk.25,26 Another upstream variant, MC1R c.−445G>A (rs3212361), has been reported to downregulate MC1R expression, contributing to red hair and altered pain sensitivity.27

In conclusion, we found novel MC1R variants in Indian populations. Further, our study confirms that p.Ala291Asp and p.Gly248Cys are LoF variants that affect pigmentation through reduced CREB phosphorylation. We also demonstrated a significant association of variant rs3212363 with lighter skin pigmentation in Indians, with a gender bias toward male individuals. The rs3212363 distribution varied regionally, with the T allele highest in the Bodh population of Ladakh (0.89) and lowest in a South Indian Dravidian tribe (0.377). The missense and frameshift MC1R variants from this study could be further explored for a better understanding of the great diversity in skin pigmentation observed across Indian populations. In the clinical perspective, MC1R variants increase the risk of melanoma and non-melanoma skin cancer either independently or by increasing the penetrance of other causal variants.28,29,30 A detailed cohort study would help in understanding the functional relevance of these ultra-rare MC1R variants in skin cancer among Indians.

Data and code availability

The variants included in the manuscript were identified by screening the MC1R gene in the whole-genome sequencing data of participants included in the GenomeIndia project. The GenomeIndia allele frequency data are publicly available via the Indian Biological Data Center (IBDC; https://ibdc.dbtindia.gov.in/genomeindia/downloadfile?path=9768GI_SummaryStats.tar.gz).
