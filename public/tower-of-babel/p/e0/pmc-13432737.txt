Elucidating the Staphylococcus aureus TSST‐1 Regulatory Network as a Response to Vaginal pH
Authors: Carla S Maduta, Nicholas R Walton, Emily P Barbosa, Erica N DeJong, Karine Dufresne, John K McCormick
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTMenstrual toxic shock syndrome (mTSS) is a life‐threatening disease caused by the Staphylococcus aureus superantigen TSST‐1. At menstruation, the typical acidic vaginal environment rises to near neutral pH, which allows for enhanced TSST‐1 production. However, the regulatory network which alters toxin production in response to pH is unknown, despite the importance of this cue in the vaginal environment. To mimic the vaginal environment, we used Vaginally Defined Medium to assess TSST‐1 promoter activity in the mTSS strain S. aureus MN8 and discovered a significant upregulation of tst expression occurring at pH 4.5 in a low glucose environment, referred to here as the acidic virulence surge. This increased expression was observed in several regulatory mutant backgrounds tested, including in the absence of SaeS, which has previously been thought to be required for TSST‐1 production. Recent studies in non‐mTSS S. aureus strains have identified GraXRS as a pH sensor, in addition to its function in cationic antimicrobial peptide sensing. We therefore hypothesized that GraXRS alters TSST‐1 expression at low pH. Deletion of the sensor‐kinase graS gene resulted in the loss of TSST‐1 surge at pH 4.5, indicating that GraXRS is required for the acidic virulence surge. We also found expression from the SaeRS P1 and SarA promoters to be significantly attenuated in the ∆graS background. At low pH, the absence of GraS resulted in reduced T cell activation from S. aureus supernatants when compared to other regulatory mutants, suggesting that GraXRS is the dominant activator at pH 4.5. Finally, we developed an in vivo murine model to measure tst expression using luciferase expression. Our results demonstrate a complex sequence of events that occur in response to changes in pH and further suggests that GraXRS is the main activator of TSST‐1 expression at low pH.Keywords: GraXRS, menstrual toxic shock syndrome, mTSS, SaeRS, Staphylococcus aureus, TSST‐1, vaginal pH

Introduction

Staphylococcus aureus is a prolific human colonizer and opportunistic pathogen, typically found on mucosal surfaces including the vagina, where it can be part of the commensal microbiota, or take part in dysbiosis and pathogenesis (Maduta, Tuffs, et al. 2024). The most highly researched pathology attributed to vaginal S. aureus is the life‐threatening disease known as menstrual toxic shock syndrome (mTSS). The development of mTSS is caused by the production of the superantigen toxic shock syndrome toxin‐1 (TSST‐1). In essence, TSST‐1, like other superantigens, is able to bypass the peptide specificity required in T cell activation (Tuffs et al. 2024). Superantigens crosslink MHC‐II molecules on antigen presenting cells with T cell receptors, leading to the activation of numerous T cells. This can initiate a pro‐inflammatory cytokine storm that may lead to toxic shock syndrome that can be fatal if untreated.

The production of TSST‐1 is tightly controlled by an array of staphylococcal regulators which respond to environmental cues, including two‐component systems (TCS). S. aureus encodes 16 TCS, each composed of a sensor histidine kinase that signals to its response regulator to initiate changes in transcription (Ali and Abdel Aziz 2024). Several TCS have been implicated in TSST‐1 regulation including Agr, SrrAB and SaeRS. The Agr system responds to cell density and functions as an indirect activator of TSST‐1 by relieving repression by Rot (Tuffs et al. 2019). The SrrAB system is an activator of TSST‐1 when oxygen levels are increased, such as through the insertion of menstrual devices (Yarwood et al. 2001; Tiwari et al. 2020). Finally, SaeRS is believed to be required for TSST‐1 production, as its deletion or inhibition eliminate TSST‐1 production (Baroja et al. 2016; Dufresne et al. 2024). While SaeRS is the dominant TSST‐1 activator, it is unclear what the sensor is detecting, although it is hypothesized to detect membrane perturbations (Sun et al. 2010). Other stand‐alone regulators have been implicated in TSST‐1 regulation, with the most notable being CcpA which represses TSST‐1 transcription in response to high glucose levels that are persistent outside of menstruation (Dufresne et al. 2022). SarA has been previously identified as a repressor in the S. aureus strain MN8 (Baroja et al. 2016), yet in Vaginally Defined Medium (VDM), it can function as an activator (Dufresne et al. 2024). Members of the vaginal microbiota can also alter the expression of TSST‐1 in vitro by manipulating S. aureus regulation systems (Maduta, McCormick, and Dufresne 2024; Carriou et al. 2026).

A critical host defense mechanism within the vagina is acidic pH, primarily via the production of lactic acid by a variety of Lactobacillus species (Mirmonsef et al. 2014, 2016; O'Hanlon et al. 2019). Typically, vaginal pH ranges from 3.5 to 4.5 (Amsel et al. 1983; O'Hanlon et al. 2013), which serves as a protective function by limiting the growth of vaginal pathogens, which may include S. aureus . It has long been postulated that acidic pH represses TSST‐1 expression as initial studies were unable to detect toxin production below a pH of 6.5 (Schlievert and Blomster 1983; Bergdoll 1989; McCormick et al. 2001; Weinrick et al. 2004). This pH effect contributes to the restriction of mTSS to menstruation, which is when vaginal pH rises from acidic to near neutral levels. Despite the importance of pH as an environmental cue in the vagina, the S. aureus signaling pathway which responds to pH and regulates TSST‐1 expression has not been identified. Recently, in non‐mTSS S. aureus strains, the TCS GraXRS was identified to have a role in bacterial survival in acidic environments (Villanueva et al. 2018; Flannagan et al. 2018; Kuiack et al. 2020). Here, we set out to elucidate the TSST‐1 regulatory network as a response to pH changes. Surprisingly, we found that TSST‐1 can be induced at low pH levels ~4.5 and using a series of phenotypic assays we identified GraXRS to be the predominant positive regulator of the toxin's expression at acidic pH.

Results

An Abrupt Increase in TSST‐1 Expression Occurs at pH 4.5

While pH has been considered a critical environmental cue in repressing TSST‐1 expression, most transcriptional studies have been performed in standard laboratory media which may not necessarily represent the vaginal environment. To better mimic mTSS conditions, we used VDM adjusted to 0.7 mM glucose in order to relieve CcpA mediated repression (Geshnizgani and Onderdonk 1992; Anukam and Reid 2008; Dufresne et al. 2022; Maduta, McCormick, and Dufresne 2024). VDM was next adjusted to pHs from 3.0 to 8.0 using inorganic acids in order to control for biological effects of lactic acid isomers (Witkin et al. 2013; Beghini et al. 2015). The medium was also unbuffered, as a different buffer for each pH interval would be required; therefore, affecting the medium composition. To rule out any pH effects on our luciferase reporter system, the assay was performed using the RNA polymerase rpoB promoter as a control (Figure S1). Importantly, the luminescent curves were similar across all the pHs tested, with the exception of pH 8.0 (Figure S1J).

The luciferase reporter assay was then used to measure activity from the tst promoter (Figure 1A–J). In alignment with previous observations (Schlievert and Blomster 1983), optimal tst expression occurred at pH 6.8 (Figure 1H), which is a pH level consistent with menstruation. As pH decreased, tst expression decreased drastically (Figure 1K); although surprisingly, at pH 4.5, there was a large increase in tst expression (Figure 1D,K), herein referred to as the “acidic virulence surge.” This pH level is the most acidic at which S. aureus was able to grow (Figure 1A–D). A ~5 h delay in tst expression was noted at pH 4.5 (Figure 1D), although this was likely due to the delayed growth of S. aureus where the OD600 did not start to increase until ~8 h, which corresponded to the increase in luminescence. The transcriptional reporter data was further confirmed via anti‐TSST‐1 Western blotting, where TSST‐1 production stopped being detected at pH 5.0 and returned at pH 4.5 (Figure 1L). Although TSST‐1 was not detectable, the sample at pH 4.0 needed to be grown in a larger volume to obtain the required amount of supernatant, as growth was very poor at this pH condition. Overall, these data confirm that acidic pH is generally repressive in a vaginal‐like environment yet simultaneously identify a potentially alarming increase in virulence at a growth‐limiting pH.

Acidic Activation Does Not Overcome Glucose Repression

While pH is an environmental cue which fluctuates greatly at menstruation, glucose levels also decrease significantly at menstruation. We have previously demonstrated that TSST‐1 production does not occur in the presence of high glucose due to CcpA repression (Dufresne et al. 2022). However, it is unknown whether the surge in TSST‐1 production at pH 4.5 can be overridden by glucose repression. Therefore, we set out to investigate whether pH or glucose is the dominant regulatory cue at pH 4.5. Luciferase reporter assays for tst expression were performed in high glucose VDM (60 mM); tst expression from wild‐type MN8 did not occur at any pH level, nor was TSST‐1 detected by Western blotting (Figure 2). These data indicate that between acidic driven activation or glucose driven repression, glucose is the dominant cue in the vaginal environment.

Known TSST‐1 Regulators Are Not Responsible for the Acidic Virulence Surge

To investigate the surge in TSST‐1 production occurring in low glucose conditions (Figure 1D,K), we turned to a plethora of regulatory mutants which were previously only tested at pH 6.8 (Dufresne et al. 2024). If any of these regulatory systems were responsible for the increase, we expected to observe no increase in tst expression at pH 4.5. The luciferase assay was repeated in previously constructed tst luminescence reporters in the following mutational backgrounds: ∆ccpA, ∆saeS, ∆sarA, ∆srrAB, ∆agr, ∆rot, and ∆agr∆rot.

In the ∆agr background, no tst promoter activity was observed given that repression from Rot is maintained (Figure 3A; Tuffs et al. 2019); yet, TSST‐1 was still detectable via Western blotting (Figure 3B,C). In the ∆rot and ∆agr∆rot backgrounds, TSST‐1 was expressed to similar levels as the wild‐type. When examining the difference between expression at pH 6.8 and pH 4.5 in these regulatory mutants, the strains phenocopied the wild‐type and demonstrated the acidic virulence surge. Therefore, these data suggest the Agr‐Rot axis is not responsible for the low pH phenotype as elimination of this axis maintained the response.

Given that SaeRS is the major activator of TSST‐1 expression, we next sought to investigate whether this regulatory system was responsible for the spike at pH 4.5. In the ∆saeS background we observed no tst expression (Figure 3A) nor production (Figure 3B) at pH 6.8, which is consistent with SaeRS being required for TSST‐1 expression (Baroja et al. 2016). However, at pH 4.5, activity from the promoter was surprisingly detected, as was the TSST‐1 protein via Western blotting (Figure 3A,C), which suggests that SaeRS is not responsible for this spike.

We next turned to the S. aureus MN8 ∆srrAB background, as SrrAB is another activator of TSST‐1 production (Yarwood et al. 2001). Nevertheless, the surge at pH 4.5 was modestly detected, ruling out this regulatory system (Figure 3A). Similarly, the ∆ccpA strain also displayed increased expression at pH 4.5. Finally, we turned to ∆sarA, as the regulator was recently established to be a TSST‐1 activator in VDM (Dufresne et al. 2024). The ∆sarA strain also demonstrated increased tst expression at pH 4.5, ~3‐fold larger than the wild‐type (Figure 3A). Altogether, these results indicate that previously identified TSST‐1 regulators are not responsible for the increase in tst expression at pH 4.5 and there must be an additional positive regulator which acts in acidic conditions.

GraXRS Expression Is Enhanced at Acidic pH

Previous studies (Flannagan et al. 2018; Kuiack et al. 2020) identified a novel function for the TCS GraXRS in survival and genetic regulation at acidic pH. Tn‐seq experiments using the Nebraska Transposon Library also identified graS as a key gene required for the bacterial stress response in TSB at pH 4.5 vs. 7.3 (Beetham et al. 2024). We therefore hypothesized that GraXRS may play a role in regulating TSST‐1 production in response to pH.

Given that expression of regulatory systems differs among S. aureus strains, as well as across different media, we first aimed to determine whether GraXRS was adequately expressed in S. aureus MN8 when grown in VDM. We constructed a luciferase reporter for the GraXRS promoter (Falord et al. 2011) and found that activity was highest at acidic pH and significantly decreased towards neutral and basic pH ranges (Figure 4). These results indicate that GraXRS expression is altered as a response to acidic pH in S. aureus MN8.

GraXRS Is Required for TSST‐1 Production at pH 4.5

As we established that GraXRS was expressed by S. aureus MN8 in VDM, we next generated a clean deletion of the sensor kinase graS gene to investigate how the deprivation of this TCS altered TSST‐1 expression. A clean deletion of graS was constructed, followed by electroporation with pAmilux::P tst , as well as the reporter pAmilux plasmids for the promoters saeP1, sarA, and agrP3. When testing the ∆graS strain for the production of TSST‐1, the toxin could be detected by Western blot between pH ranges 5.0–8.0 (Figure 5A). Importantly, at a pH of 4.5, TSST‐1 was not detected. Relative to the wild‐type S. aureus MN8, ∆graS expressed significantly less tst between pH ranges 4.5–8.0 (Figure 5B), suggesting that GraXRS is a positive regulator of TSST‐1. To further validate the role of GraXRS, a luciferase assay was performed with wild‐type S. aureus MN8 in the presence of the GraR inhibitor MAC‐545496 at a pH of 4.5 (El‐Halfawy et al. 2020). While we did note some growth inhibition with as little as 0.25 μg/mL of MAC‐545496, inhibitory effects on tst expression were observed beyond 0.5 μg/mL (Figure S2). Despite the effects on growth, inhibition of GraR provides further evidence into the role of this two‐component system at pH 4.5.

Considering that the acidic virulence surge was detected both in wild‐type S. aureus MN8 as well as in the absence of various regulatory systems (Figure 3), we sought to investigate whether the deletion of graS would also reverse the increase in a non‐wild‐type (WT) background. Given the surprising production of TSST‐1 by MN8 ∆saeS (Figures 3C and 5C), we created a double ∆graS ∆saeS mutant. In this strain lacking both graS and saeS, TSST‐1 was no longer detected at a pH 4.5 (Figure 5D). A faint band was detected at pH 8.0; however, this is not a biologically relevant vaginal pH. Taken together, the reversal of the acidic virulence surge indicates that GraXRS is required for activation of TSST‐1 production at pH 4.5.

Given that tst expression was affected at other pH levels (Figure 5B), yet graXRS promoter activity was decreased (Figure 4), this suggests that GraXRS may not be responsible for the decreased expression at pH ranges above 4.5. This led us to hypothesize that other positive regulatory systems may be implicated. Examining the P3 promoter from agr, we saw similar expression in wild‐type S. aureus MN8 and MN8 ∆graS backgrounds, suggesting that differential regulation of agr is not responsible for the changes in tst expression (Figure 6A). While there appears to be a large increase in relative agrP3 expression at pH 4.5 (Figure 6A), this is likely due to a 2‐to‐3‐h lag in growth in the ∆graS background relative to the wild‐type, affecting the area under the OD600 curve, and not due to increased promoter activity (Figure S3). However, when examining activity from the saeP1 promoter (Figure 6B) and the sarA promoter (Figure 6C), there were significant decreases in the ∆graS background, following the trend in tst expression (Figure 5B). Overall, these data indicate that GraXRS affects other regulatory systems, but it is only required for TSST‐1 production at pH 4.5.

Deletion of graS Significantly Decreases T Cell Activation by S. aureus at pH 4.5

To investigate whether the differences in tst expression and TSST‐1 production among the regulatory mutant backgrounds corresponded to a biological difference, a T cell activation assay was performed using human peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs). Briefly, S. aureus strains were grown in VDM at either pH 6.8 or pH 4.5, and the supernatants were then exposed to PBMCs from healthy human blood donors for 18 h, followed by an interleukin‐2 (IL‐2) ELISA to assess T cell activation. A bacterial supernatant eliciting greater IL‐2 production at higher dilution levels is considered to have greater toxin potency (Figure 7A–C). To make quantitative comparisons of superantigen potency among the strains, the supernatant dilution points at which half‐maximal IL‐2 was induced were plotted and a one‐way ANOVA was performed, comparing the means to pH 4.5 (Figure 7D). At pH 6.8, there was no significant difference in IL‐2 production between the wild‐type S. aureus MN8 and ∆graS (Figure 7A), ∆saeS (Figure 7B), or ∆sarA (Figure 7C) mutants. Lowering the growth pH to 4.5 resulted in a significant decrease in potency when comparing ∆graS and WT (Figure 7D). All regulatory mutants triggered higher IL‐2 production at pH 6.8 than at 4.5, despite evidence of equivalent/greater TSST‐1 expression at this pH level, suggesting less activity from the seven other MN8 superantigens in acidic growth conditions.

To identify the activity of these superantigens (SEA and the enterotoxin gene cluster), an additional 3 blood donors were recruited (Figure S4). To isolate these superantigens, we used a strain where we had deleted TSST‐1 (∆tst) grown at pH 4.5 and found that it elicited the least amount of IL‐2 production, highlighting limited activity from the other superantigens at this pH level.

Overall, these data suggest greater superantigen activity from pH 6.8 than pH 4.5 grown S. aureus , with the least amount of activation occurring in the absence of GraS. Overall, these data further indicate that GraXRS is the dominant activator at pH 4.5.

GraXRS and SaeRS Are Activators of tst Expression In Vivo

To date, a plethora of studies have been done in vitro to examine the regulation of TSST‐1 production and identify key regulators. However, there is little to no knowledge regarding the relevance of these regulators in an in vivo model of S. aureus vaginal colonization. The pAmilux luciferase reporter plasmid has been used primarily for in vitro studies, as well as an ex vivo study on srrAB expression in hypoxic skeletal tissues (Wilde et al. 2015). Adapting our laboratory's vaginal colonization workflow (Dufresne et al. 2025), we set out to develop a model for examining tst promoter activity in vivo and to determine the contributions of graS and saeS in the neutral pH mouse vagina.

Female BALB/c mice (7–9 weeks old) were inoculated with 1 × 106 CFU of wild‐type S. aureus MN8, or the ∆graS or ∆saeS mutants, each harboring pAmilux::P tst , and imaged from 6 to 24 h post‐infection (hpi). Generally, tst promoter activity was observed starting from 8 h (Figure 8A). The wild‐type strain resulted in the most tst promoter activity at 12 and 24 hpi among the three strains (Figure 8B,C). The ∆saeS group produced significantly less light than the wild‐type S. aureus MN8 group at both timepoints, while the ∆graS group only trended downwards at 24 h hpi (Figure 8C). No differences were noted in bacteria recovered between WT and ∆graS, although the ∆saeS mutant had small but significantly increased recovery of CFUs (Figure S5), potentially due to a higher initial infectious dose. Taken together, these data suggest that GraXRS and SaeRS are activators of TSST‐1 production in vivo, with SaeRS being required for tst expression in a neutral pH vaginal colonization model.

Discussion

The environmental cues present in the vagina play a critical role in the production of TSST‐1 by S. aureus . It has long been suggested that the optimal environment in which TSST‐1 is produced aligns with the conditions seen at menstruation including decreased glucose, increased oxygen, and near‐neutral pH (McCormick et al. 2001). While the regulation network which alters TSST‐1 production has been extensively studied (Figure 9), the role of pH as a regulatory stimulus has remained elusive. In this study, we demonstrate that acidic pH generally reduces TSST‐1 production, but at the outer boundary of growth, production of TSST‐1 is exaggerated.

Regulation of tst expression in S. aureus MN8. The two‐component regulatory systems SaeRS, Agr, and SrrAB are known to control transcription of the TSST‐1 gene (tst). SaeRS is a dominant and direct activator of tst transcription where the SaeS sensor kinase responds to membrane stress including human neutrophil peptides and unsaturated fatty acids to auto‐phosphorylate and transfer the phosphate to SaeR that directly activates tst. The Agr quorum sensing system, upon high cell density, activates due to the secretion of agrD‐encoded auto‐inducing peptides (AIPs) that are processed and secreted by AgrB and sensed by AgrC. Phosphorylated AgrA functions to activate transcription of the RNAIII signaling molecule that subsequently derepresses expression of the repressor of toxins (Rot) protein to relieve repression of tst transcription. The SrrAB redox sensing system functions to enhance expression of tst in the presence of elevated levels of O2 through the redox sensing kinase SrrA that subsequently controls the SrrB response regulator through a phosphorylation event. The stand‐alone transcription factor SarA functions to repress tst in rich media but can also act as an activator when S. aureus is grown in Vaginally Defined Medium. The CcpA transcription factor directly represses tst transcription in response to elevated glucose concentrations. Shown here, in response to acidic pH, the GraRS functions to induce TSST‐1 production within a low glucose, vaginal environment mimicking medium.

Through testing TSST‐1 production at pHs ranging from 3.0 to 8.0, we determined that optimal expression occurs at ~ pH 6.8 (Figure 1). Interestingly, we characterized a new window of expression at pH 4.5, occurring at the lowest pH at which S. aureus MN8 could grow. This increased expression was evident in all the regulatory mutant backgrounds at varying magnitudes (Figure 3), except for in the absence of graS (Figure 5), suggesting that the TCS GraXRS activates expression of the toxin at this pH level. An additional key finding from this work is the ability for TSST‐1 to be produced in S. aureus MN8 ∆saeS at pH 4.5 (Figure 5C). Our previous work identified SaeRS as required for the production of TSST‐1 (Baroja et al. 2016), which we have now established to be pH‐dependent. SaeRS is well known to be vastly responsible for virulence gene expression (Liu et al. 2016), and without it many key virulence determinants, such as α‐toxin (Goerke et al. 2001), are not expressed. Consequently, this system is a common target in the development of anti‐virulent compounds (Dufresne et al. 2024; Gao et al. 2023). It is unknown whether this acidic virulence surge we have observed occurs with other SaeRS regulated genes. It would be interesting to investigate other toxins in this context, especially as they relate to other niches known to be exposed to acidic conditions, such as the skin. Given that expression from the SaeP1 promoter was decreased in the absence of GraS (Figure 6B) and the double ∆graS ∆saeS mutant did not display the acidic virulence surge (Figure 5D), anti‐virulence strategies should also further consider targeting the GraXRS system when considering SaeRS regulated genes. While our work suggests there could be overlapping signaling between GraXRS, SaeRS, and SarA (Figure 6), we were unable to detect the GraR binding site upstream of these systems (Falord et al. 2011). It is therefore likely that an unknown intermediary is involved.

While the increased virulence we have detected is worrisome, an important finding from this work is that glucose repression overrides pH activation (Figure 2). The vagina is typically an acidic environment with high glucose and glycogen levels. Estrogen is responsible for the maturation of vaginal epithelial cells and the buildup of glycogen (Lindahl 2014), which is then catabolized by amylases into smaller sugars (Spear et al. 2014; Nasioudis et al. 2015; Nunn et al. 2020; Jenkins et al. 2023). These sugars are then used by lactobacilli, such as Lactobacillus crispatus , to form either D‐ or L‐ lactic acid to acidify the environment. With a lactobacilli‐dominated microbiota, a vaginal pH of 4.5 should be associated with abundant glucose levels. The high glucose levels would therefore prevent the acidic virulence surge from occurring (Figure 2). However, as only an abundance of lactobacilli is associated with higher glycogen levels (Mirmonsef et al. 2014; Nunn et al. 2019), their decreased abundance, such as in vaginal dysbiosis, may alter sugar dynamics. Therefore, vaginal dysbiosis may be a setting in which glucose levels are drastically altered, and repression of TSST‐1 could be relieved, allowing for the acidic virulence surge we have observed to occur. This disturbance would be particularly worrisome as Gardnerella vaginalis and Lactobacillus iners are two bacteria associated with dysbiosis which can also interact with S. aureus and exacerbate TSST‐1 production (Maduta, McCormick, and Dufresne 2024). Alternatively, should a S. aureus strain carry a mutation within ccpA or its regulatory cascade, it is possible that glucose‐dependent repression of TSST‐1 could be lost. Therefore, this work highlights the necessity of understanding the dynamics of the vaginal environment as they relate to microbial composition, pH, and glucose.

When examining activation of PBMCs by S. aureus supernatants, we found that S. aureus supernatants grown in acidic conditions resulted in decreased IL‐2 production (Figure 7), despite an increase in TSST‐1 production. While TSST‐1 is the dominant superantigen produced by S. aureus MN8, this strain also encodes 7 additional superantigen genes which also contribute to the IL‐2 profile (i.e., staphylococcal enterotoxin A and the enterotoxin gene cluster, egc). Their superantigenic activity is evidenced by the ∆saeS and ∆tst supernatants from pH 6.8 conditions that still trigger IL‐2 production from PBMCs, despite the lack of TSST‐1 production (Figure 7B and Figure S4). At pH 6.8, the ∆saeS and ∆tst profiles were nearly identical (Figure S1), suggesting that SaeRS does not regulate SEA or the egc under these conditions. In contrast, at pH 4.5 the ∆saeS and ∆tst treatments did not yield similar IL‐2 profiles (Figure S4C), as under these conditions the saeS mutant produces TSST‐1. Indeed, at this pH level, the ∆saeS profile most closely resembled the wild‐type (Figure S4B). Taken together, this suggests less activity of SEA and/or the egc at pH 4.5, resulting in an overall decrease in T cell activation, despite the presence of TSST‐1.

This acidic virulence surge that occurs at pH 4.5 begs the question of what its biological function may be. The ability of S. aureus to survive in an acidic vagina is curious, as this can be considered a defense mechanism aimed at limiting vaginal pathogen survival. The eubiotic vagina ranges from pH 3.0–4.5, while S. aureus demonstrates deficits in growth in vitro below the range of 4.0–4.5 (Figure 1A–D). While TSST‐1 has often been associated with pathogenesis, our laboratory recently demonstrated in a murine vaginal colonization model that TSST‐1 promotes colonization of the vaginal tract (Dufresne et al. 2025). Another factor which promotes vaginal colonization is the S. aureus fibronectin‐binding proteins (Lyon et al. 2023), yet they have been found to be down‐regulated in response to acidity, which may suggest a decrease in colonization potential (Costa et al. 2024). Taken together, we propose that the biological function of TSST‐1 overexpression at pH 4.5 may not necessarily amount to pathogenesis; rather, it may be used as a colonization tactic in a harsh environment, particularly if other colonization factors are limited. Further studies examining pH, colonization versus pathogenesis, and TSST‐1 production are required to elucidate this further.

The use of pAmilux in our vaginal colonization model serves as an important tool for measuring virulence gene expression in vivo. The use of luminescence reporters could be expanded to examine other genes of interest, as well as other sites of S. aureus colonization and infection, and for testing anti‐virulent therapies in vivo. While we were able to identify GraXRS and SaeRS as activators of tst expression in vivo (Figure 8), a limitation in our study is the pH of the mouse vaginal tract. The mouse vagina is not naturally colonized by lactobacilli, which in humans function to acidify the vagina. Moreover, “humanized” mice which are enriched in Lactobacillus‐dominated microbiotas are also unable to attain a highly acidic vaginal pH (Mejia et al. 2023). In a rat vaginal model, acidification could be achieved through the exogenous addition of lactate, acetate, and citrate gels; however, it could not be sustained for longer than 1–2 h (Sun et al. 2017). Consequently, we were unable to investigate the acidic virulence surge in vivo due to natural limitations in existing vaginal colonization models. Further work is required within the field to develop animal models which better mimic the human female reproductive tract. Nevertheless, we have developed a method of monitoring TSST‐1 gene expression during infection/colonization in real time which can be applied to other infection models, as well as using promoter constructs for other staphylococcal genes.

Overall, this work highlights the importance of investigating a wide range of environmental conditions present in S. aureus niches when assessing pathogenesis. Our work highlights the potentially dangerous possibility of TSST‐1 production occurring outside of menstrual conditions, with implications for menstrual health and safety. Altogether, our results demonstrate a complex sequence of events that occur in response to changes in pH and further indicate that environmental alterations may drastically alter the virulence potential of S. aureus.

Experimental Procedures

Ethics Statement

For experiments with human PBMCS, healthy adult blood donors were recruited at the University of Western Ontario (UWO) and written, informed consent was obtained from each volunteer. Blood was anonymized, and no information regarding the identity of the donor, including sex and age, was retained as per the study protocol. The study was reviewed and approved by the UWO Health Sciences Research Ethics Board (HSREB #110859).

Bacterial Growth Conditions

A list of bacterial strains used in this study can be found in Table 1. Wild‐type and deletion mutants of S. aureus MN8 were plated on tryptic soy supplemented with 1.5% agar (TSA). S. aureus strains were routinely grown overnight in tryptic soy broth (TSB) at 37°C and 250 rpm unless otherwise specified. All E. coli strains were grown overnight in Luria‐Bertani broth (LB) at 37°C and 250 rpm unless otherwise specified. Vaginally Defined Medium (VDM) was prepared as previously described (Geshnizgani and Onderdonk 1992; Maduta, McCormick, and Dufresne 2024). To account for potential differences between lactic acid isomers (Witkin et al. 2013; Beghini et al. 2015), pH adjustments were performed with 1 M HCl or 1 M NaOH. Unless otherwise stated, VDM was formulated at 0.7 mM glucose. As appropriate, chloramphenicol selection was used at 10 μg ml−1 for S. aureus , and ampicillin selection was used at 100 μg ml−1 for E. coli .

Western Blotting

Western blots were performed as previously described (Maduta, McCormick, and Dufresne 2024). S. aureus strains were grown overnight in 3 mL TSB, and subcultured 1:100 in 3 mL VDM. After 3 h, the bacteria were standardized to OD600 0.01 and subcultured in 20 mL VDM overnight. The following day, 6% trichloroacetic acid (TCA) precipitations were performed to concentrate exoproteins to 12 OD600 units and stored at −20°C. Samples were diluted 1:4 with Laemmli buffer and run on 12% SDS‐PAGE for 30 min at 85 V followed by 1 h at 150 V. If 12 OD600 units could not be attained at the time of TCA precipitations, the volume loaded to the gel was adjusted by the appropriate factor. Gels were either stained with ReadyBlue (Sigma‐Aldrich) or transferred to a polyvinylidene difluoride membrane and blocked overnight in blocking buffer (Maduta, McCormick, and Dufresne 2024). Western blotting was performed with 1:1000 TSST‐1 rabbit polyclonal anti‐sera, and 1:20,000 IR‐Dye800 goat anti‐rabbit IgG antibody (Rockland). Blots were imaged using an ODYSSEY imager (LI‐COR Biosciences).

S. aureus promoters of interest were PCR amplified from S. aureus MN8 genomic DNA (gDNA) followed by a restriction digest and ligation with pAmilux. The ligated plasmid was then heat‐shock transformed into E. coli XL1 blue, extracted, and then transformed into E. coli SA30B for appropriate methylation. One positive clone for each construct was extracted, sequenced, and then electroporated into competent S. aureus cells.

Luciferase Assays

Luciferase assays were performed as previously described (Maduta, McCormick, and Dufresne 2024). Briefly S. aureus containing pAmilux reporter plasmids were grown overnight in 3 mL TSB with chloramphenicol, followed by a three‐hour subculture in 3 mL VDM at pH 6.8. The cultures were then adjusted to an OD600 of 0.01 in the media of interest and 200 μL plated in technical replicates of three in a 96‐well plate. Each assay was also performed with three biological replicates. The plate was incubated in a BioTek Synergy H4 reader at 37°C with continuous shaking on the medium setting. Data were plotted as the area under the curve (AUC) of the relative luminescence units over the AUC of the OD600 readings. Data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism 10.

Deletion of graS

The pKOR1 plasmid was used to create a markerless deletion of the graS gene as previously described (Bae and Schneewind 2006; Dufresne et al. 2022). Briefly, primers were created for the ~800 bp regions upstream and downstream of graS and PCR amplified from wild‐type MN8 gDNA (Table 2). The upstream and downstream PCR products were then ligated and recombined with pKOR1 (BP Clonase II, Thermo Fisher). The recombined plasmid was transformed into E. coli XL1 blue at 30°C, extracted, and then transformed into E. coli SA30B at 30°C for methylation. The methylated plasmid was extracted, sequenced, and electroporated into competent wild‐type S. aureus MN8 or S. aureus MN8 ∆saeS cells at 30°C. The strains were grown in TSB at 30°C overnight, then subcultured and grown overnight at 42°C. S. aureus clones were screened for integration and subcultured three times at 30°C prior to plating on anhydrotetracycline plates (1 μg ml−1). Colonies were picked and screened for chloramphenicol resistance, where sensitive colonies were then screened for the deletion, and sequenced.

Peripheral Blood Mononuclear Cell (PBMC) Activation Assays

PBMC assays were performed as previously described (Maduta, McCormick, and Dufresne 2024). Briefly, S. aureus strains were grown overnight in VDM at pH 6.8 or pH 4.5. Supernatants were collected and stored at −20°C until use. Blood was collected from healthy blood donors and PBMCs were isolated via density gradient centrifugation. Cells (1 × 106 cells/mL) were seeded and exposed to diluted S. aureus supernatants. Cells were incubated overnight in complete RPMI (Maduta, McCormick, and Dufresne 2024), and the following day cell supernatants were collected and stored at −20°C. ELISAs were performed to detect IL‐2 production (Invitrogen).

Mice

Female BALB/c mice 7–9 weeks of age were used for in vivo experiments. Mice were purchased from Jackson Laboratory (stock 000651). Animals were housed without exceeding four animals per cage and provided water and food ad libitum with appropriate environmental enrichment. All animal experiments were in accordance with the Canadian Council on Animal Care Guide to the Care and Use of Experimental Animals, and the animal protocol was approved by the Animal Use Subcommittee at the University of Western Ontario (Protocol #2024‐082).

IVIS Vaginal Colonization Model

The vaginal colonization model was adapted from Dufresne et al. (2025). Briefly, 7‐ to 9‐week‐old female mice (Jackson Labs) (n = 6) were provided with chloramphenicol water (2.25 mg/mL) ad libitum 24 h prior to infection and refreshed every 24 h, synchronized with β estradiol, and pre‐infection imaging was also performed (IVIS Spectrum CT). Mice were vaginally inoculated with 1 × 106 CFU of wild‐type, ∆graS, or ∆saeS harboring pAmilux::P tst . Following inoculation, mice were imaged at 6, 8, 10, 12, and 24 hpi. At 24 hpi, the mice were sacrificed, and the lower reproductive tracts were excised, homogenized, and plated in the presence or absence of chloramphenicol for enumeration.

Statistical Analysis

All statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism 10. Ordinary one‐way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used without correction for multiple comparisons for luciferase data, IL‐2 ELISAs, and in vivo work. Unpaired t‐tests were also performed for luciferase data where specified.
