Innovation under pressure: bold ideas for a changing malaria landscape
Authors: Adrienne Epstein, Julie-Anne Tangena
Published: 2025

Malaria research is at a rare inflection point. After decades of steady progress, a wave of new tools is moving from the lab into real-world settings. Yet this shift comes at a difficult moment: funding is shrinking, cases are rising, and current interventions are struggling to hold the line.

Innovation has never been more urgent. Encouragingly, the malaria research community is responding. Next-generation insecticide-treated nets (ITNs) [1], new indoor residual sprays (IRS) [2], and spatial emanators are reaching communities [3]. Vaccines such as R21 and RTS,S are moving from trials into wider use [4, 5]. On the treatment front, the pipeline now includes long-lasting antimalarials [6], triple combination therapies (TACTs) [7], and single-dose drugs like tafenoquine [8]. Novel approaches such as ivermectin, recently shown to reduce malaria incidence when deployed in mass drug administration campaigns, are also gaining traction [9]. Looking further ahead, early breakthroughs in gene drive in mosquitoes to bias inheritance of a specific genetic trait (like sterility or resistance to malaria) is passed on to offspring, and monoclonal antibodies, which point to bold new frontiers [10, 11]. Together, these advances represent the most significant broadening of the malaria toolkit in decades.

But innovation alone will not be enough. The momentum is colliding with deep structural challenges. Funding cuts threaten to derail the rollout of new tools. Resistance is mounting: mosquitoes have developed resistance to most insecticides used for vector control, and parasites are showing reduced sensitivity to artemisinin and even lumefantrine, a key partner drug to control artemisinin resistance, in East Africa [12]. Climate change is redrawing the risk map by shifting mosquito habitats. Health systems remain fragile, weakened by COVID-19, extreme weather, and political unrest. Even when technologies are available, their potential can be weakened by regulatory delays, fragmented governance, or inequities in access and limited community trust. The path forward requires more than breakthroughs in science. Sustained progress will depend equally on the strength of delivery systems, stable financing, and governance that can translate innovation into equitable impact.

Even as these challenges mount, opportunities exist to do more with less by harnessing technology. Mobile health applications can extend the reach of limited health personnel, improving diagnostics [13, 14], reporting [15], and case management [16]. As smartphones and access to electricity become more widespread, such tools are increasingly feasible. Furthermore, other digital platforms and geospatial tools can inform decision-makers by tracking outbreaks in real time and allowing for targeted responses [17, 18]. Artificial intelligence can further enhance these systems by supporting predictive modelling and enabling rapid data analysis. Many of these tools have already been developed and implemented. The challenge now lies in scaling them up, sustaining them, and evaluating their utility.

Just as important are approaches that place communities at the center, not as passive recipients of aid or programs but as leaders in the fight against malaria. One such example is integrated campaigns that combine malaria interventions with other health programs such as vaccination or nutrition services, which can reduce costs while building trust and participation [19, 20]. Harnessing the strengths of local health workers, civil society, faith-based networks, and schools can create sustainable infrastructure that benefits malaria control and broader public health. School-based initiatives, for instance, in which children are trained as health messengers, have shown promise in improving outcomes [21]. These community-driven strategies are more likely to be sustained, ensuring impact beyond the duration of individual programs.

Lasting progress also depends on political commitment and regional leadership. National Malaria Programs (NMPs) are increasingly shaping research agendas, regulatory pathways, and financing strategies, fulfilling an essential role as global funding declines. The NMPs are already demonstrating the value of cross-border collaboration by sharing data, exchanging experiences, and driving knowledge transfer to advance both control and elimination efforts. NMPs are also advocating for global partnerships that better reflect subnational realities, while governments are breaking down silos to align policies, financing, and implementation across departments. Innovation can only be accelerated through proactive engagement, with regulatory and policy challenges anticipated and addressed early to enable smoother adoption of new tools. Across regions, governments are showing resilience and creativity in adapting strategies, underscoring that malaria control is a dynamic process rooted in shared knowledge and innovation across borders, institutions, and sectors.

The launch of this collection on Advances in malaria control and elimination (https://link.springer.com/collections/hhbfdbfibj) comes at this critical moment in the world of malaria research. Malaria control requires sustained investment in both research and delivery. Scientific discovery alone is not enough; success depends on interdisciplinary efforts integrating social science, entomology, epidemiology, and implementation science research. Equity must remain a guiding principle, with affected communities and countries leading in defining priorities and shaping solutions. We hope this collection inspires new thinking, sparks debate, and strengthens commitment to ensuring that novel tools reach the people and places where they are most needed.
