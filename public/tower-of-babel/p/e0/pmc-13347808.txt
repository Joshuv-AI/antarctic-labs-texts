Patient-reported outcomes after surgical resection for non-small cell lung cancer and perceptions of postsurgical rehabilitation programmes
Authors: Mette Kaasgaard, Morten Borg, Jens Pers Kaltoft, Gitte Alstrup, Anne Orholm Nielsen, Pernille Kristiansen, Lise Stensen Bhatia, Jens-Ulrik Stæhr Jensen, René Horsleben Petersen, Erik Jakobsen, Ole Hilberg, Uffe Bodtger
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSurgical resection is essential in the treatment of early-stage non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), followed by rehabilitation with physical exercise training to improve physical capacity, quality of life (QoL) and symptom burden. In Denmark, rehabilitation is municipality-based and not disease-specific and without a maintenance model. We aimed to investigate QoL and symptoms 6–12 months after NSCLC surgery, hypothesising impaired QoL and symptom levels. Moreover, we explored rehabilitation attendance.MethodsAn observational, cross-sectional, survey-based study was conducted from March to June 2025 across two Danish regions in patients after surgery for stage I or II NSCLC and without adjuvant oncological treatment (preregistration: osf.io/vmpft). Each site contacted their own eligible patients via digital mail. Registry data from the Danish Lung Cancer Registry and electronic patient registries: socio-demographics, disease-specific characteristics and surgical procedure. Survey data: QoL and symptoms burden (measure: The European Organisation for Research and Treatment of Cancer Quality of Life Questionnaire–Core 30; Reporting: Raw and transformed scores and dichotomised according to defined thresholds and cut-offs for clinical importance) and self-reported information about rehabilitation attendance. Data analyses: descriptive and stratified analyses.ResultsWe identified 168 patients, 94 (56% of 168) were eligible and 67 (71% of 94) responded to the survey. No differences were found in baseline characteristics and overall mean time since surgery was 8.9±1.8 months. Current QoL and symptoms were clinically impaired (eg, physical functioning: 51%; dyspnoea: 70%). Around half (54%) reported having attended a rehabilitation programme and those having attended were more likely to have clinically impaired physical functioning and dyspnoea at present.ConclusionsQoL and symptoms were impaired 6–12 months after NSCLC surgery, and the current level did not seem to be related to having attended a postsurgical rehabilitation programme. Disease-specific challenges should be addressed in future rehabilitation and maintenance models.Keywords: Non-Small Cell Lung Cancer, Patient Outcome Assessment, Pulmonary Rehabilitation, Exercise

Introduction

Lung cancer is the second most prevalent cancer in Denmark (>5000 new cases/year),1 2 with approximately 25% of patients diagnosed at stage I and with increasing 5-year survival rates and prevalence of long-term survivors.2,7 Surgical resection is the cornerstone of curative intent treatment for early-stage non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) (stages I and II), defined as localised disease without metastatic spread.2 8 Besides, other curative intent therapies may include stereotactic radiation therapy (early-stage disease) and concurrent chemoradiotherapy (locally advanced disease).3 8 In the Danish follow-up programme, patients are offered active surveillance for 5 years to detect potential cancer recurrence or new primary lung cancer and reduce treatment-related symptoms.9 More specifically, surveillance includes clinical assessment in local, regional respiratory or oncology outpatient clinics and repeated contrast-enhanced CT scan every 3 months for 2 years, followed by every 6 months during the subsequent 3 years, though, since 2025, specifically stage I surveillance implies repeated CT scan every 6 months for 2 years with annual follow-up for 3 years.8,11

Patients with lung cancer often have persistently reduced quality of life (QoL) compared with age-matched controls12 13 and symptom clusters (eg, dyspnoea, cough, pain, fatigue), besides reduced physical and social activity and risk of deconditioning.3,511 Moreover, due to tobacco smoking as shared aetiology, for example, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) is a common comorbidity.14,16 Accordingly, compared with other cancer patients, lung cancer patients display different and more symptoms, including poorer physical function, more emotional distress and more dyspnoea, which all contribute to poorer QoL levels.17,19

Specifically, physical functioning and QoL are potentially modifiable prognostic factors for survival in NSCLC.20 21 The European Respiratory Society and the American Thoracic Society22 recommend pulmonary rehabilitation to improve physical functioning, QoL and symptoms management, supported by Cochrane reviews23 24 and other systematic reviews.25 26 The rehabilitation programmes for stage I-II lung cancer should include physical exercise training527,31 with Grade A-evidence for combined strength and endurance training, either alone or in combination with respiratory training.27 Additionally, a recent Delphi study on a Core Outcome Set (COS) in lung cancer rehabilitation31 reached consensus for the following critical outcomes: ‘Breathlessness, activities of daily living, physical function, health-related QoL, emotional and mental well-being and pain’.31

However, in Denmark, there is currently no homogeneous rehabilitation programme specifically targeted at lung cancer patients.32 33 Instead, patients are offered rehabilitation in municipal-based healthcare centres, either as cancer rehabilitation (mixed cancer types) or pulmonary rehabilitation (mixed lung diseases), depending on local preferences and resources.11 12 33 In a recent Danish cohort study, conducted 5 months after NSCLC surgery,32 only 25% had received disease-specific rehabilitation. This study also reported on symptoms which were physiologically oriented (fatigue (78%), dyspnoea (65%), pain (47%)),32 as in another Danish cohort study which, however, did not evaluate rehabilitation content.11 Likewise, a Danish randomised controlled trial (RCT) found impaired functional QoL, persistently across 12 months after NSCLC surgery.13 An updated investigation of functional QoL and symptom burden—related to current rehabilitation programmes—is, however, essential to elucidate whether the current short-term and long-term strategies following NSCLC surgery are adequate or need modification.

Study aims and hypotheses

In the present study, we aimed to investigate the status of QoL and symptom burden 6–12 months after curative intent video-assisted thoracic surgery (VATS) for NSCLC. Specifically, we hypothesised that a significant proportion of patients currently demonstrate impaired QoL and symptoms in one or more domains of ‘The European Organisation for Research and Treatment of Cancer (EORTC) Questionnaire to Assess Functional QoL and Symptoms in Cancer Patients (EORTC Quality of Life Questionnaire–Core 30 (QLQ-C30))’.34 35

Secondarily, we aimed to explore patient-reported characteristics (type, content and delivery) and perceptions related to any rehabilitation programme attendance immediately following NSCLC surgery, hypothesising that the programmes had been heterogeneous and not disease-specific but that patients perceived the programmes as beneficial.

Methods

Study design and study oversight

We conducted an observational, cross-sectional, survey-based study between 26 March and 24 June 2025, across two Danish regions. The study was performed in accordance with the Helsinki II Declaration, and the study protocol was preregistered at Open Science Framework (osf.io/vmpft) 1 May 2025.

Participants

We included patients who had undergone VATS procedure for NSCLC (either lobectomy, segmentectomy, wedge resection or bilobectomy) 6–12 months ago, referred from two hospitals:

Site 1

Department of Medicine Roskilde and Naestved, Zealand University Hospital (surgery performed at Department of Oncology, Odense University Hospital or at Department of Thoracic Surgery, Copenhagen University Hospital Rigshospitalet).

Site 2

Department of Medicine, Hospital Lillebaelt, Vejle (surgery performed at Department of Thoracic Surgery, Odense University Hospital).

Patients were screened according to the following criteria: Inclusion criterion: (1) 6 months’ CT follow-up scan showing no signs of recurrence. Exclusion criteria: (1) metastatic disease; (2) need for oncological adjuvant therapy; (3) having had other major surgery within the past 12 months; (4) inability to read and understand Danish and (5) non-adherence to the scheduled CT follow-up programme.

Data collection procedure

Data were obtained from the following sources:

The Danish Lung Cancer Registry (DLCR) and from electronic patient registries.

The Danish Lung Cancer Registry (DLCR) and from electronic patient registries.

Patient-Reported Outcome Measures (PROMs) (ie, validated and standardised questionnaires) and researcher-developed questionnaires (patient-reported questionnaires, developed by the research group for this study).

Patient-Reported Outcome Measures (PROMs) (ie, validated and standardised questionnaires) and researcher-developed questionnaires (patient-reported questionnaires, developed by the research group for this study).

Each site retrieved data from patient registries, related to their own, local patients and screened for eligibility according to study criteria. Subsequently, each site contacted their own patients via digital email (da: ‘e-boks’), comprising written information about the project and its purpose, a link to the questionnaire survey, and a consent declaration. If response was not received within 7 days, the patient was contacted by telephone across two attempts, that is, in total up to 3 contact attempts.

Data were collected in the metadata-driven, secure database (EDC software, Research Electronic Data Capture, Vanderbilt University, USA) with a separate profile configured for each site. After completion of data collection, data were anonymised at each site, transferred as a spreadsheet file via secure email to the primary study investigator, and imported into statistical software StataNow/BE V.19.5 (Stata).

Outcomes and measures

Data from the DLCR and from electronic patient registries

Basic information about: civil registration number (da: CPR), age, sex, municipality, date of surgery, presurgical forced expiratory volume in 1 s, per cent of predicted (FEV1 % predicted), and diffusion capacity for carbon monoxide, per cent of predicted (DLCO % predicted), previous surgery for NSCLC (yes/no), surgery type and classification, pathology (stage, type, localisation), presurgical risk factors (co-morbidities: COPD, cardiac disease, other disease), postoperative complications, any days of intensive care need after surgery (number of days), and smoking status.

Moreover, we collected self-reported data on comorbidities and medicine consumption within the following domains: COPD or other lung disease, cardiovascular disease or kidney disease, hypertension, diabetes or other endocrinological diseases, musculoskeletal disorders, nervous system disorders, cancer other than lung cancer, anxiety or depression, other disease (multiple choice).

The researcher-developed questionnaires were developed based on questionnaires employed in previous studies11 32 and customised for this study. All members of the author group provided their comments for the questions and variables, and the questions were subsequently tested in four patients before onset of data collection to ensure optimal semantic quality and comprehensibility. Minor adjustments were made according to patient feedback before reaching the final version of the questions.

Additionally, we explored compliance of the patient-reported content and elements in the rehabilitation programme with the guidelines on combined components in physical exercise training within stage I–II lung cancer rehabilitation (combined strength and endurance training; combined strength, endurance and respiratory training).27

Analysis

Data were described as mean±SD or as number and percentage. Distribution and between-group differences were tested using Student’s t-test (two-tailed), paired-samples t-test, χ2, or Fisher’s exact test. Statistical significance was reached at p<0.05. Multivariate logistic regression models were used to explore maximum-likelihood in dichotomous variables of interest.

Results

Participants

As depicted in figure 1, 168 patients (across two Danish regions and covering 21 municipalities) were identified 6–12 months after NSCLC surgery, and 101 (60% out of 168) were eligible according to study criteria and were approached. Major exclusion criteria included need for oncological adjuvant treatment and metastatic disease (n=36; 54% out of 67), and seven (11%) had died (figure 1). Seven of the 101 (7%) were excluded, as >12 months had passed between surgery and time of response, leading to a total of 94 eligible. In total, 67 (71% out of 94) responded to the survey (Region Zealand: n=59 (88%); Region of Southern Denmark: n=8 (12%)).

Participant flow diagram. NSCLC, non-small cell lung cancer.

In the study cohort (n=67), mean age was 70.0±8.5, around two-thirds were female (n=58 (62%)), 28 (30%) were current or former smokers and 32 (34%) had COPD and/or other risk factors (29 (31%)) (table 1). Time since surgery was 8.9±1.8 months, and lobectomy was the most common surgical procedure (78 (83%)). No statistically significant differences were observed between responders and non-responders, but numerically, non-responders were more often current smokers than responders (12 (44%) vs 16 (24%)) (table 1).

Physical exercise training was included in all programmes, and the majority perceived the physical exercise training as adequately challenging 32 (89%) (table 4). Overall, 24 (67%) reported to be ‘satisfied’ or ‘very satisfied’ with the programme, and 23 (64%) felt that attendance had helped them get back on top after surgery. Factors related to rehabilitation attendance

No differences or factors were observed between patients who had attended rehabilitation or not (online supplemental table 4). However, those who had attended more often displayed more dyspnoea and worse physical functioning than those who had not attended (see online supplemental table 4). This pattern was also suggested in the regression models of factors associated with self-reported rehabilitation attendance: Attendance implied OR: 7.74 for clinically impaired dyspnoea (p=≤0.01) and a trend for impaired physical functioning (OR: 3.64; p=0.07). See online supplemental table 5 for details.

Factors related to combined physical exercise training within rehabilitation programmes

In total, 20 (30%) reported that the physical exercise training had included combined strength and endurance training, whereas 10 (15%) had received a combined offer with both strength, endurance and respiratory training (double counting for strength and endurance) (table 4). Neither combined training models conferred better QoL or symptoms compared, although the between-group distribution regarding satisfaction varied (online supplemental table 6).

Discussion

In this observational, cross-sectional, survey-based study, conducted 6–12 months after NSCLC surgery, patients had persistently and clinically impaired QoL and symptoms, particularly regarding physical functioning and dyspnoea. QoL and symptoms status did not seem to be related to self-reported attendance in a postsurgical rehabilitation programme.

Our study provides updated information on QoL and symptoms status and prevalence after NSCLC-surgery, and our findings concur with previous findings that functional QoL is impaired after NSCLC surgery3,511 and that lung cancer patients display significant dyspnoea.17 18 Moreover, our study explored current QoL and symptoms status and prevalence related to self-reported postsurgical rehabilitation attendance which, to our knowledge, has not previously been explored in a Danish cohort.

Specifically, our findings on QoL and symptoms status and prevalence are comparable to two recent Danish cohort studies which also found impaired QoL and symptoms11 32 but were conducted 3 months11 and 4–6 months32 after surgery, that is, at a time closer to surgery than in the present study. This supports that impaired QoL and symptoms persist over time and aligns with a previous Danish RCT,13 where EORTC QLQ-C30 did not change significantly from 26 to 52 weeks after NSCLC surgery.13 Taken together, this suggests persistently impaired QoL and symptoms after NSCLC surgery and that, consequently, there is a need for intensifying a focus on short-term and long-term strategies to support this vulnerable patient group, not least as physical functioning and QoL are associated with prognostic factors and survival in NSCLC.20 21

In contrast to the previous Danish studies,11 13 32 we dichotomised data according to defined thresholds for clinical practice and research36 and cut-offs for clinical and severe/serious problems,38 instead of only presenting the raw and transformed EORTC QLQ-C30 scores, as these plain scores are challenging to interpret34,3638 and as values are often related to changes in longitudinal pre–post study designs.3639,41 Importantly, in the development study of the EORTC QLQ-C30 thresholds, lung cancer was the third most common cancer diagnosis among the various included cancer cohorts,36 which strengthens the usability of EORTC QLQ-C30 in lung cancer patients, and may support the credibility of our findings. Moreover, there was overall congruence in our findings across the defined thresholds36 and cut-offs38 frameworks, which further may strengthen overall credibility as well as clinical relevance and transferability of our findings.

Rehabilitation programmes aim to support physical functioning, QoL, and symptoms management, but for example, awareness, uptake, transportation and lack of motivation constitute well-known barriers to rehabilitation attendance.12 42 43 In the present study, only half of the study participants (36 (54%)) reported to have attended a rehabilitation programme (table 4), which had been heterogeneous and not disease-specific. Both the reported low attendance rate and the heterogeneity are in line with previous Danish cohort studies.11 32 Regarding the specific content, physical exercise training was indeed reported included in all programmes—as overall recommended527,31—although the Grade A-recommendations27 for combined strength and endurance training were only met in 20 (30%) cases, and for combined strength, endurance and respiratory training, only in 10 (15%) (table 4; online supplemental table 6), according to the participants’ reportings. Notably, our findings related to rehabilitation programmes and their content are, however, only indicative, as they solely rely on participants’ reportings, as the number in each stratum was small, and as the cross-sectional study design did not include data on QoL and symptoms status presurgery, postrehabilitation or any pre–post changes. Nevertheless, status of QoL and symptoms as suggested in the present study align well with the objective presurgical lung function measures, which may enhance the credibility of the findings on long-term QoL and symptoms status.

Overall, rehabilitation attendance did not seem to lead to better current QoL or symptom burden in the present study, even in the combined interventions (online supplemental table 6). The study obviously cannot point to a clear causality due to lack of power and methodological limitations, including that the study design was not appropriate to investigate the composition and implications of rehabilitation programmes. One aspect that might also have impacted the findings on QoL or symptom burden related to rehabilitation attendance is that presurgical lung function in the cohort was high, and, evidently, it is easier to improve from a poorer starting point.44 45 However, the level of lung function in the study cohort is in line with recent data on NSCLC surgery from the DLCR, and also reflects that Danish patients with poorer lung function more often are referred for stereotaxy.2 Nevertheless, the study generates various interesting hypotheses such as: (1) Those who feel well after surgery may not wish to attend rehabilitation as they just wish to press on with their lives; (2) Current rehabilitation programmes are ineffective in addressing disease-specific needs in NSCLC; (3) Effects of rehabilitation with physical exercise training have vanished at the time of the present study. Further studies with sufficient power and with appropriate controlled pre–post study designs should be conducted to investigate these hypotheses.

Nonetheless, as QoL and symptoms seem to persist over time—as previously demonstrated13—our study suggests unmet disease-specific needs of those with impaired QoL and symptoms. This is supported by the patient-perspectives domains, in which 16 (45% out of 36) did not feel that they had benefitted from rehabilitation attendance and 14 (39%) were not satisfied with the programme (table 4). We speculate that both the low uptake rate and these replies indicate that a proportion does not feel motivated for participating in physical exercise training. This hypothesis would indeed be in line with the previously addressed challenges and barriers to rehabilitation uptake, attendance and drop-out2242 46,48 and would support the ongoing request for developing and investigating novel, motivating and evidence-based strategies as supplement or alternative to physical training within rehabilitation.3 47 48

In light of this, future rehabilitation practice and research should focus on building effective, perceived relevant, and motivating rehabilitation and maintenance models which meet the aspects of the recently defined COS in lung cancer rehabilitation.31 Specifically, inclusion of interventions which target respiratory training and dyspnoea management should be considered a mandatory part, as dyspnoea is heightened in lung cancer.11 17 18 32 Several targeted non-pharmacological interventions have already been suggested as potentially relevant and motivating activities to supplement standard physical exercise training,43 49 such as telerehabilitation,50 inspiratory muscle training,50 Tai Chi,51 yoga52 and singing.4553,57 Notably, our study, moreover, suggests that special attention should be given to those with low presurgical lung function to personalise rehabilitation programmes and support short and long-term QoL and symptom burden. However, further research is warranted to conclude on efficacy, effectiveness, underlying mechanisms, optimal delivery and phenotypes, and which interventions specifically are optimal to target the disease-specific challenges, long-term impaired QoL levels and prominent symptoms after NSCLC surgery.

Strengths and limitations

The present study has several strengths. First, the study provides updated data on QoL and symptoms in patients after NSCLC surgery from a real-life cohort outside a clinical study setting, thus minimising risk of allocation bias. Second, we used predefined thresholds and cut-offs for evaluation of the EORTC QLQ-C30 domains, instead of only presenting the scores, thus improving interpretation and translation into clinical practice and research. Third, the study includes patient-reported perceptions on benefits and satisfaction with rehabilitation programmes postsurgery, which strengthens the patient perspective. Fourth, we found consistency in the study results as well as overall congruence with previous studies, thus strengthening the credibility, transferability and validity of our findings.

However, our study also has some limitations. First, it was an observational and cross-sectional study based on only two Danish regions, which may impact representativeness and generalisability to a broader context, other settings and other countries. Moreover, the fundamental study design (eg, with lack of follow-up) is not appropriate to address causality, and findings should solely be regarded as hypothesis generating. We found no statistically significant baseline differences between responders and non-responders, although non-responders, numerically, were more often smokers and had more comorbidity. Nevertheless, the overall suggested pattern of status and prevalence of QoL and symptoms aligned with earlier studies. Second, we had no predefined sample size calculation regarding the EORTC QLQ-C30, which may have led to a risk of either type I or II error, as the cohort intravariability may have been too wide or too narrow and as there might have been responder bias in terms of who responded and who did not. However, endpoints were predefined, and significant findings were thoroughly explored and challenged in regression models for robustness. Third, all outcomes regarding rehabilitation were based on self-reported data which may have led to a risk of recall, information and responder bias, and the reporting on rehabilitation content may not be in line with perceptions and definitions of health-professionals, potentially impacting credibility of the patient-reporting. Moreover, the basic self-selected choice of attending rehabilitation may have led to selection bias, again reflecting the limitations of the study design. Fourth, the small sample sizes, the numerically (although not statistically significant) uneven distribution in sex, and the high presurgical lung function levels may have led to risk of type I error and, thus, may impact representativeness and generalisability of our findings. Nevertheless, our study includes important patient perspectives and adds to the body of evidence of considerable content heterogeneity concerning rehabilitation in Denmark and to the need for increasingly disease-specific approaches.31 32 58 59

Conclusions

Functional QoL and symptoms were impaired 6–12 months after NSCLC surgery. Current level of physical functioning and dyspnoea did not seem to be related to having attended a rehabilitation programme immediately postsurgery. Future practice and research should address disease-specific challenges and symptoms after NSCLC surgery.
