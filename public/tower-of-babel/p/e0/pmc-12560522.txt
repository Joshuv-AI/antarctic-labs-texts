Associations between reproductive factors and frailty in middle-aged and older women: evidence from the China health and retirement longitudinal study
Authors: Xiaobing Xian, Jie Xiang, Shiwei Cao, Wanting Tang, Yao Wu, Jiaxia Li, Shiqin Ren, Yuanyuan Wang, Kun Shen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAmid global aging, frailty significantly threatens the health of middle-aged and older women. This study is the first to explore the association between reproductive factors (age at first livebirth, number of biological children, reproductive period, age at menopause, age at menarche, and history of abortion) and frailty among middle-aged and elderly Chinese women.MethodsBased on the 2014 life history survey and 2018 cross-sectional data from the China health and retirement longitudinal study (CHARLS), 2,948 female participants aged 45 years and above were included in the analysis. Frailty status was assessed using a Frailty Index (FI), constructed from 50 health deficit indicators. Data on reproductive factors, including age at first livebirth, number of biological children, reproductive period, age at menopause, age at menarche, and history of abortion were collected. Multivariable logistic regression models and restricted cubic spline analyses were used to examine the associations between frailty and reproductive factors.ResultsThe study found that, compared with participants who had two children, those with three or more children had a 26.6% higher risk of frailty. Women with menarche at age 18 or older had a 30.1% higher frailty prevalence than those with menarche at 15 or younger. Similarly, those who reached menopause at age 55 or older had a 57.4% higher prevalence of frailty compared to those who experienced menopause between ages 45 and 55. In addition, a history of abortion was more strongly associated with an increased prevalence of frailty in women, whereas the reproductive period ≤ 33 years appeared to be a protective factor. No significant association was found between age at first livebirth and frailty.ConclusionsThe findings suggest that the risk of developing frailty among middle-aged and older Chinese women is strongly associated with specific reproductive factors, including number of biological children, age at menarche, history of abortion, and age at menopause. These findings provide potential reproductive health indicators for the accurate clinical identification of frailty, offering a scientific foundation for developing targeted prevention and intervention strategies that can help alleviate the disease burden associated with frailty in an aging society.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s41043-025-01077-w.Keywords: Cross-sectional study, Frailty index, Prevalence, Association analysis

Introduction

In addressing the global challenge of population aging, frailty has received increasing attention [1]. It is defined as a clinical condition characterized by reduced physiological reserves, diminished stress resistance, and increased susceptibility to disease and physical vulnerability [2]. In China, frailty affects 4–17% of adults aged 65 and older, with rates rising to 10–45% among those aged 75 and above [3]. Notably, the prevalence of frailty is significantly higher in women [4], potentially due to the fact that women often have lower muscle strength than men, bear more household responsibilities, and have greater symptom tolerance [4, 5]. In addition, female reproductive characteristics may further influence frailty severity. This condition increases the risk of hospitalization, falls, disability, and death, posing a serious threat to quality of life and imposing a heavy financial burden on healthcare systems [6]. Modern geriatric medicine places particular emphasis on the importance of disease prevention. Therefore, the timely identification and adjustment of key risk factors for frailty are essential for the development of effective prevention and intervention strategies [7].

Reproductive history is a unique life experience for women, influencing hormone levels, health status, and lifestyle. Extensive literature has explored the association between reproductive behavior and health in later life, highlighting the potential negative impacts of having more children and giving birth at an early age on both physical and mental health [8, 9]. Studies on middle-aged and older Chinese women reported that an increase in the number of children is significantly associated with a higher risk of co-morbidities and chronic illnesses [10, 11]. Other studies have also shown that women with multiple children (≥ 5) tend to have poorer cardiovascular health [12] and a higher risk of cardiovascular disease [4], while women without children are at a lower risk of cardiovascular disease [5]. A study involving British women further showed that having more pregnancies was significantly associated with a higher risk of frailty, a finding that has also been confirmed in other populations [13].

Menopause marks one of the pivotal transitions in a woman’s life cycle. During this period, ovarian failure causes fluctuations in estrogen, progesterone, and follicle-stimulating hormone levels, which may lead to psychological, physical, and endocrine disorders [14]. A recent UK Biobank study identified a significant association between the age at menopause and frailty in middle-aged and older women [15]. This provides a theoretical basis for the hypothesis that menopause, as a reproductive factor, may increase the risk of frailty among Chinese women. The onset of the menstrual period is a significant marker of puberty, signifying the beginning of female reproductive capacity. The increase in gonadotropin levels during puberty stimulates the ovaries to produce estradiol, triggering a series of profound physical, emotional, cognitive, and social changes in females [16, 17]. A study conducted among British women revealed a significant association between the age of menarche and frailty in middle-aged and older women [15]. It is worth noting that there is currently controversy in the academic community regarding the association between a history of miscarriage and frailty. In a study of elderly Greek women, scholars found that induced abortion may increase the risk of frailty. However, in another two-sample Mendelian randomization study, there was no significant association between pregnancy loss and frailty in older age [18].

In summary, there is a research gap regarding the association between female reproductive factors and frailty: first, most previous studies have been conducted in Western countries, and research data on Asian populations remain limited. This is particularly due to notable racial and ethnic differences in reproductive characteristics [19]. For instance, Chinese women typically experience menarche later and menopause earlier than white women [20]. Furthermore, to the best of our knowledge, the association between reproductive factors and frailty among middle-aged and older women in China remains unclear.

Therefore, based on nationally representative data published by the China health and retirement longitudinal study (CHARLS), this study is the first to explore the association between reproductive factors (age at first livebirth, reproductive period, age at menopause, number of biological children, age at menarche, and history of abortion) and frailty status among middle-aged and older Chinese women. These findings may serve as a scientific foundation for the development of appropriate frailty prevention and intervention strategies, thereby improving health and quality of life for middle-aged and older Chinese women.

Materials and methods

Population

The data analyzed in this study are derived from the China health and retirement longitudinal study (CHARLS) [21]. CHARLS is a Longitudinal survey targeting residents aged 45 and above in mainland China, collecting information on socioeconomic status and health conditions. A stratified multistage probability proportional sampling (PPS) was employed, covering 150 regions and 450 villages or urban communities, and including 17,708 individuals from 10,257 Households. To date, CHARLS has successfully implemented four surveys, conducted in 2011, 2013, 2015, and 2018, and detailed information about the survey is available on its official website (http://www.charls.pku.edu.cn/en) [21]. The survey received ethical approval from the Institutional Review Board of Peking University (IRB00001052-11015), and written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

In this study, we specifically utilized data from a 2014 life history survey and data released in 2018. A total of 19,816 individuals participated in the 2018 follow-up. We established the following exclusion criteria: 1. Respondents were male; 2. Frailty data had missing values; 3. Covariate data had missing values; 4. Reproductive factor data had missing values. A total of 2948 participants were ultimately included in the analysis. The detailed selection process is shown in Fig. 1.

Flowchart for the study design and participants

The minimum sample size of the study was determined according to the formula for calculating sample size in cross-sectional studies [N = (Z2(α/2)-p-q)/δ2]: (1) N denotes the sample size needed for the study; (2) p represents the prevalence rate of frailty among middle-aged and elderly women in China; (3) q = (1-p); (4) Zα/2 is set to 1.96, and α is set to 0.05 for the two-sided test; (5) δ denotes the permissible error, calculated at 0.1p. According to a previous study, the prevalence of frailty among middle-aged and older Chinese women was 16.57% [22], which was calculated to determine that at least 1,934 participants were needed for this study to achieve the required sample size. Therefore, the sample in this study meets the minimum sample size requirement.

Assessment of reproductive factors

Information on female reproductive factors was extracted from the cross-sectional survey data released by CHARLS, including the number of biological children, age at menopause, age at menarche, and history of abortion, and calculated age at first livebirth and reproductive period. Information on the number of biological children was extracted from the cross-sectional survey data published in CHARLS 2018 (Wave 4). Status and age at menopause were collected from Wave 1 to Wave 4 data, using the earliest available records. Age at menarche and history of abortion were extracted from Wave 4 data. Age at first birth was estimated based on the respondent’s age and the age of her eldest child. The duration of the reproductive period was calculated as the period from age at menarche to age at menopause.

Assessment of frailty

The Frailty Index (FI), a quantitative assessment tool designed to measure the degree of frailty in individuals by accumulating age-related Health deficits, can be programmed and flexibly constructed as needed, provided that it adheres to the principles for selecting Health deficits, and must include at least 30 health deficit items [23]. In this study, 50 indicators were selected for the FI assessment scale based on the following criteria: 18 items related to physical restrictions, 10 items related to depressive symptoms, 13 items related to chronic diseases, 2 items related to trauma history, 3 items related to cognitive impairment, and 4 other items. A detailed list of the indicators is provided in eTable 1. Each Health variable within these dimensions is assigned a value ranging from 0 to 1.The FI is calculated by dividing the total score of health deficits by the number of indicators (50 items in this study), yielding a value between 0 and 1, with higher values indicating a greater degree of frailty. The FI has been validated and applied in previous CHARLS-based studies [24, 25]. Based on previous research experience, individuals are classified as frail when the FI value is greater than 0.25 [13, 26].

Assessment of covariates

The covariates were divided into four categories, including basic social characteristics (age, education, marital status, residence), health status (hypertension, diabetes, heart disease, BMI), lifestyle and health behaviors (social activities, physical activities, smoke, drink), and mental status (self-rated health, life satisfaction, depression) (eTable 2).

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables were expressed using frequency counts and frequencies (n (%)). Continuous variables were subjected to the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test, and if P > 0.05, they were expressed as mean plus or minus standard deviation (M ± SD). Comparisons between groups were made using a two-sample independent t-test and a χ2 test. The relationship between reproductive factors and frailty was examined in this study, as the potential continuous FI was converted into a binary outcome variable. Based on previous research experience, an FI value > 0.25 was defined as frail, and ≤ 0.25 as non-frail [13, 26]. Logistic regression is particularly suitable for analyzing the association between such binary outcomes and independent variables [27], so a multivariate logistic regression model was used to estimate the odds ratio (OR) and 95% confidence interval (CI). Model 1 was the unadjusted model. Model 2 was adjusted for age, marital status, residence, education level, social activity, physical activity, hypertension, diabetes, heart disease, self-rated health, smoke, drink, life satisfaction, BMI, and depression. To further investigate the dose–response relationship between reproductive factors and frailty, a restricted cubic spline analysis was performed, with the most appropriate number of nodes determined by the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC).

Subgroup analyses were conducted based on age, residence, education, and BMI. For age, participants were divided into two groups: < 60 years and ≥ 60 years, according to the commonly used age classification standard for middle-aged and older adults from previous CHARLS studies [28]. For BMI, participants were categorized into two groups based on the World Health Organization(WHO) classification standards: normal (18.5–24 kg/m2) and abnormal (< 18.5, 24–28, ≥ 28 kg/m2) [29]. Residential areas were categorized into rural and urban, and educational attainment was categorized into illiterate and literate.

All analyses were performed using R 4.3.0 [30]. Two-sided p-values less than 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

Basic characteristics of the study population

A total of 2948 women were enrolled in the study, with an average age of 70.59 ± 8.72 years. Among them, 595 (20.18%) participants were classified as frail (Table 1), the chi-square test results revealed significant variations in frailty prevalence across different age groups (P < 0.001), educational backgrounds (P < 0.001), marital status (P < 0.001), residential locations (P < 0.001), and the presence or absence of depression (P < 0.001). Specifically, individuals aged 65 years or older, those with lower levels of education, those who were widowed, divorced, or unmarried, rural residents, and those with depression had higher rates of frailty. Additionally, notable differences in frailty prevalence were observed based on the number of biological children (P < 0.001) and the age at menarche (P < 0.001). Among them, people with three or more biological children and those who experienced menarche at age 18 or older had a higher prevalence of frailty.

Association between reproductive factors and frailty

The results of the associations between the six reproductive factors and frailty are presented in Table 2. After adjusting for all covariates, we found that: participants with ≥ 3 children had a 26.6% increased risk of frailty compared to participants with two children(OR = 1.266, 95% CI = 1.008–1.589); participants with ≥ 18 years of age at menarche had a 30.1% increased prevalence of frailty compared to participants with age at menarche ≤ 15 years(OR = 1.301, 95% CI = 1.002–1.688); and participants who experienced menopause at the age of 45–55 years showed a 57.4% higher prevalence of frailty compared to those who reached menopause at or after 55 years of age(OR = 1.574, 95% CI = 1.109–2.233). Women with a history of abortion were found to have a greater association with frailty (OR = 1.271, 95% CI = 1.022–1.580). In addition, having a reproductive period ≤ 33 was a protective factor compared to 33–43(OR = 0.720, 95% CI = 0.580–0.895). No significant association was found between the age at first livebirth and the likelihood of frailty.

Subgroup analysis of reproductive factors and frailty

Table 3 presents the association between various reproductive factors and frailty, categorized by age, residence, education, and BMI status. The findings indicate that women aged 60 years or older who had three or more children exhibited a significantly higher prevalence of frailty (OR = 1.444, 95% CI = 1.084–1.924). A history of abortion was connected to a greater chance of frailty, particularly in individuals with a normal BMI (OR = 1.445, 95% CI = 1.007–2.075). On the other hand, experiencing menopause at an earlier age (≤ 45 years) reduced the risk of frailty in women residing in urban areas(OR = 0.715, 95% CI = 0.520–0.982), whereas menopause at a later age (55 years and older) increased the risk of frailty in older age, especially among those who were 60 years or older, educated, and had a normal BMI(Age ≥ 60 years: OR = 1.690, 95% CI = 1.158–2.466; educated: OR = 2.028, 95% CI = 1.261–3.259; normal BMI: OR = 1.976, 95% CI = 1.202–3.250). In addition, shorter reproductive period (≤ 33 years) were associated with a reduced risk of frailty, particularly among those ≥ 60 years of age, urban-dwelling, illiterate, and a normal BMI(Age ≥ 60 years: OR = 0.721, 95% CI = 0.572–0.910; urban-dwelling: OR = 0.656, 95% CI = 0.517–0.833; illiterate: OR = 0.575, 95% CI = 0.419–0.789; normal BMI: OR = 0.575, 95% CI = 0.419–0.789). These findings highlight the potential influence of reproductive factors on frailty risk in specific populations.

Discussion

It is demonstrated by our findings that middle-aged and older women with three or more biological children are at a higher risk of frailty compared to those with two biological children. Moreover, a higher risk of frailty is observed among individuals who experienced menarche at age 18 or older, have a history of abortion, or underwent menopause at a later age, compared to those who experienced menarche at age 15 or younger, have no history of abortion, and reached menopause at a typical age (45–55 years). In contrast, a shorter reproductive period (≤ 33) reduces the risk of frailty, with the 33–43 years reproductive period serving as a reference.

Notably, the study results confirmed the association between the number of biological children and the prevalence of frailty among middle-aged and older women, which is consistent with previous studies [13, 31, 32]. A larger number of children suggests more pregnancies. The reasons why women who have had more pregnancies are more likely to become frail in old age remain unclear. Previous studies have shown that multiple pregnancies can lead to a range of pathological and physiological changes in the body. For example, a higher number of pregnancies has been associated with increased insulin resistance, weight gain, and reduced glucose tolerance [13], as well as a heightened risk of developing diabetes, certain types of cancer, and stroke [33–35]. Furthermore, during pregnancy, some women encounter complications like gestational diabetes, gestational hypertension, pre-eclampsia, and mental health issues. The occurrence of these complications indicates an elevated risk of future cardiovascular and metabolic diseases [36]. Additionally, pregnancy significantly alters the natural hormonal process. From conception onwards, estrogen and progesterone levels in women continue to rise, then decline after the baby is born [37]. Research indicates that during periods of abnormally elevated estrogen levels, mechanisms such as sustained stimulation of cell proliferation, disruption of endocrine metabolic balance, and impaired vascular function may not only increase the risk of hormone-dependent tumors like breast cancer and endometrial cancer but also potentially trigger menstrual disorders, metabolic syndrome, and cardiovascular diseases, posing a potential and progressive threat to women’s multi-system health [38–41].

In addition to relevant pathological and physiological factors, certain socioeconomic and lifestyle factors also warrant special consideration. Evidence suggests that mothers with a large number of children are inclined to be from rural households characterized by low incomes, low educational attainment, and inadequate health insurance coverage [42]. Most of them are more likely to be engaged in manual labor and to have experienced unemployment and malnutrition, which may help explain the association between the number of biological children and the risk of debilitating illnesses found in this study. Moreover, having more children entails greater responsibilities and increased financial stress, which can lead to heightened anxiety and reduced sleep among mothers. These adverse effects may, in turn, accelerate the onset and progression of frailty in older women [43].

Another important finding of this study is that women who experienced menarche at age ≥ 18 years have a higher risk of developing frailty relative to those whose menarche occurred at age ≤ 15 years. This compares with previous findings in both British women and Chinese women living in Singapore, where younger age at menarche was associated with a higher risk of frailty in later life [11, 44]. A J-shaped relationship between age at menarche and the prevalence of frailty was observed in British women, suggesting that both precocious and delayed menarche are linked to a higher likelihood of frailty [11]. From a physiological perspective, early menarche reflects premature activation of the hypothalamic-pituitary-ovarian axis, which can affect women’s growth and development and long-term hormonal balance. Studies have shown that early menarche is associated with uterine cancer [45]. In addition, hormonal instability may increase the risk of gynecological diseases such as menstrual disorders and polycystic ovary syndrome [46].

According to energetics theory, girls growing up in environments with limited food and nutrition tend to grow more slowly, experience delayed pubertal development (including menarche), and they will reach smaller adult body sizes compared to those raised under more favorable nutritional conditions [47]. Thus, a delayed age at menarche suggests slower female development associated with early nutritional deficiencies [48], which may lead to an elevated risk of frailty later in life. In general, menarche, the onset of the first menstrual period, is considered the central event of female puberty [49], and the onset of menarche is complex and controlled by multiple genetic and environmental factors. Explaining the mechanisms linking age at menarche and the prevalence of frailty is beyond the scope of this study, and we believe more research is necessary to explore this.

In addition, we determined that a significant effect of abortion history on frailty among Chinese women was identified. Inducing an abortion can prove to be a psychologically traumatic experience for women, often resulting in emotional distress. Studies have indicated that pregnancy loss has been shown to be associated with anxiety, depression, or post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) [50]. These psychological issues can have an enduring impact on both physical and mental well-being, potentially increasing the risk of frailty in the long term.

Historically, evidence regarding the relationship between natural age at menopause and frailty has been inconsistent [51]. Nevertheless, research conducted on British women and Chinese females residing in Singapore has revealed an association between an earlier onset of menopause and a heightened vulnerability to frailty [11, 44]. To our knowledge, this research marks the initial exploration of the association between menopause age and frailty in middle-aged and older Chinese women. The results demonstrate that a later age at menopause is associated with an increased risk of frailty. The contradictory results observed across different geographic, cultural, and living contexts may stem from complex underlying factors. We hypothesize that genetic variations between populations may play a key role, potentially influencing both menopause timing and its long-term health consequences. Some genetic traits may predispose specific populations to experience early or late menopause, and these same traits may also be associated with other health indicators.

In addition, Chinese women living in China have significantly different dietary habits, levels of physical activity, living environments, and access to healthcare compared to those living in Singapore. In certain regions of China, historical healthcare constraints mean that women may be at higher risk of complications during childbirth and disease, which can lead to frailty in the elderly. This may explain why our results present a later age of menopause associated with a higher risk of frailty prevalence. Furthermore, environmental factors such as pollution and stress can affect the endocrine system and immune function, thereby indirectly influencing the association between menopausal age and health. Taken together, the association between age at menopause and the risk of frailty is not a simple linear correspondence but rather the outcome of complex interactions among multiple variables. We believe that future studies need to examine the social, economic, and cultural contexts of specific populations in greater detail in order to reveal deeper mechanisms.

Unlike findings in other national populations, our analysis demonstrated that a shorter reproductive period was associated with a reduced risk of frailty development among middle-aged and elderly Chinese women. A separate study of British individuals observed that shorter reproductive periods (< 30 years) were associated with an increased risk of frailty [11]. Research on Korean women revealed that extending the reproductive period by one year was associated with a 4% reduction in frailty risk [52]. It has been shown that the risk of breast cancer increases with age; however, the rate of increase in risk is significantly lower after the loss of ovarian function. This suggests that the hormones associated with ovarian production are key risk factors for human breast cancer. The reproductive period reflects the time of exposure to endogenous estrogen [53]. A shorter reproductive cycle means that estrogen exposure is relatively limited, which may reduce the incidence of breast cancer and other hormone-related diseases, and thus reduce the risk of debilitating diseases.

Conclusion

Based on data from CHARLS, this study systematically analyzed the association between reproductive factors and frailty among 2,948 women aged 45 and older. The results demonstrated that compared with women who had two children, those with three or more children had a 26.6% increased risk of frailty; women who experienced menarche at age 18 or older had a 30.1% higher prevalence of frailty than those who experienced menarche at age 15 or younger; women who reached menopause at age ≥ 55 had a 57.4% higher risk of frailty than those who reached menopause between ages 45 and 55; women with a history of abortion had a significantly higher risk of frailty; and a reproductive period of ≤ 33 years was a protective factor against frailty. These findings have important theoretical and practical implications. From a theoretical perspective, this study is the first to investigate the association between reproductive factors and frailty in Chinese middle-aged and older women, filling a research gap in the field of reproductive health and frailty in Chinese women. From a practical perspective, the study results provide scientific evidence for developing targeted prevention strategies, such as health monitoring and intervention for high-risk populations.

Limitation

The present study has some limitations in exploring the association between reproductive factors and frailty in middle-aged and older women. Firstly, this study used cross-sectional data and was unable to further determine the causal relationship between variables. Future studies should conduct relevant cohort studies or intervention trials to further confirm this. Secondly, most of the data in this study relied on participants’ self-reports. Although previous studies have confirmed the reliability of self-reported data [54, 55], it is inevitable that a certain degree of recall bias and social expectation bias will be introduced. In future studies, more precise measurement methods should be considered to verify this association. In addition, the study did not cover all possible reproductive factors that may affect frailty, such as birth spacing and breastfeeding, which should be further considered in future studies. Finally, while we adjusted for numerous confounding factors from four aspects, namely basic social characteristics, lifestyle, health status, and mental state, there may still be some potential confounding factors that were not considered (such as environmental factors and genes). In future studies, other confounding factors should be widely included.
