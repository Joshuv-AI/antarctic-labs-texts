AI-driven audio-to-video generation for dynamic content creation via stable diffusion and CNN-augmented transformers
Authors: Deepak Dharrao, Madhuri Dharrao, Sneha Padgaonkar, Sneha Kumari, P. S. Sreevaishnavi, Priya Pise, Anupkumar Bongale
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Translating spoken words into emotionally and contextually aligned video content remains an open challenge in generative AI. Subtle vocal patterns—such as pauses and pitch modulations—often obscure emotional cues, resulting in visuals that feel emotionally disconnected or flat. While several models excel at text-to-image generation, they struggle with interpreting speech-based inputs, often misreading paralinguistic cues and contextual intent. To address these limitations, this research introduces EchoVid, an audio-to-video synthesis model designed to prioritize contextual fidelity and emotional alignment. A scalable web interface built with React.js and TypeScript connects to Node.js backend with the MongoDB Atlas for near-real-time generation (≈ 1.2× input-duration latency at 512² frames on RTX A4000) interaction. Using PyAudio input, EchoVid guides Hugging Face’s Stable Diffusion v2.1 via emotion-aware prompts, with CNN-enhanced diffusion transformers supporting the video generation process. Preliminary results show that EchoVid can generate visuals that reflect both emotional tone (e.g., joyful imagery for upbeat speech) and context. The proposed EchoVid model is compared with MoCoGAN and Stable Video Diffusion variants based on metrics like, FVD, FID-VID and CLIPScore. Further this research introduces two novel evaluation metrics namely Temporal Semantic Stability (TSS) and Perceptual Flicker Index (PFI) that scores the semantic consistency and frame-to-frame change in the generated video. The results show that EchoVid outperforms the other models and can generate relatively better videos.

Introduction

Human communication relies on the interplay of sound and imagery, where auditory cues and visual context amplify meaning. Recent advances in generative artificial intelligence (AI) have enabled machines to synthesize dynamic video content from audio inputs, promising transformative applications in education, media, and assistive technologies1. However, generating visuals that align with the emotional nuances and semantic intent of speech remains an unresolved challenge, limiting the practical utility of such systems.

Prior work in generative AI has laid critical groundwork for multimodal synthesis. Early innovations, such as the TiVGAN2, demonstrated the feasibility of text-to-video pipelines but struggled with temporal inconsistencies in generated sequences. Vondrick et al.3 advanced scene dynamics modelling; however, their methods faced difficulties in replicating natural motion patterns. Contemporary surveys highlight semantic misalignment between audio and video as a key bottleneck, exacerbated by the rise of deepfake technologies requiring robust authenticity safeguards4,5. Singh and Reibman4 emphasize how important it is to use quality metrics that are aware of the specific task in assistive applications, as this can influence how well users do. However, there is still no complete framework that addresses emotional accuracy, the context of use, and how well this technology can handle growth or scaling in audio-based video creation.

This paper presents EchoVid, a neural architecture that maps audio inputs to contextually relevant video stories. In contrast to previous approaches, EchoVid combines a three-phase architecture: (a) speech-to-text translation, (b) semantically conscious image generation via stable diffusion, and (c) temporal frame interpolation. The mechanism employs multi-attention mechanisms5 to combine visual outputs with emotional and linguistic features of speech, leading to authenticity and relevance.

In addition to innovation in technology, EchoVid considers social impact. For example, the proposed system can be used for translating podcasts into animated lectures or assisting hearing-impaired individuals through visual storytelling6. EchoVid is efficiently tested via suitable performance evaluation metrics to check the semantic coherence and visual quality of the generated videos. The findings show that contextually informed audio-to-video synthesis is possible. The proposed method can be scalable and can lead to better natural human-AI collaboration.

To achieve this, EchoVid integrates several design components that collectively distinguish it from existing generative frameworks:

Related works

Foundations of video generation

The discipline of video synthesis has undergone a dramatic transformation in the last ten years, fuelled by the requirement of simulating spatial details (e.g., object textures, shapes) and temporal phenomena (e.g., motion, scene changes)9,10. The initial methods focused on generative adversarial networks (GANs), which introduced a new paradigm for generating realistic content by training a generator to deceive a discriminator network11. A basic work by Vondrick et al.3 proved the potential of GANs in video synthesis by splitting the task into two parallel streams: one for static background generation through DCGAN architectures and another for foreground motion prediction through optical flow estimation. While this approach generated promising results on artificial data (resolution of 32 × 32 px, MSE = 0.021), its unrestricted sampling of noise created unrealistic motion patterns for real-world scenery. For example, synthetic outdoor videos had unrealistic motions of objects, such as opposing directions of swaying trees, demonstrating the challenge of achieving a tradeoff between spatial resolution and temporal coherence.

The following work attempted to enhance motion modelling through the addition of a hierarchical architecture. Villegas et al.12,13 presented a human motion forecasting framework based on stacked long short-term memory (LSTM) networks, which handle sequential data by carrying past input memory. Their method, trained on the Human3.6 M dataset, uses residual connections to incrementally refine joint coordinate predictions, reducing the mean per-joint position error (MPJPE) to 55.3 mm on par with early motion-capture technology. The emphasis of skeletal motion neglects finer details such as facial expressions or hand gestures, resulting in stiff-looking animations. For example, as the model correctly predicted movements of the limbs in a walking pattern, minute movements such as eye blinks or finger jerks were missing, highlighting a wider shortcoming of early video synthesis: the compromise between macromotion correctness and microdetail realism.

The advent of transformer-based models created a paradigm that overcomes the temporal coherence limits of recurrent models14. MoCoGAN, a framework for decoupling video generation into a static content vector (i.e., object identities) and a dynamic motion latent space, evolved progressively with the help of gated recurrent units (GRUs) and was proposed by15. When trained on the Sky Timelapse dataset, MoCoGAN results in a 64 × 64 px resolution with seamless transitions in cloud movements. However, rapid scene transitions, such as instant transitions from daytime to sunset, add temporal flickering, measured by a 2.1 dB reduction in the peak signal-to-noise ratio (PSNR), which is a measure of frame-by-frame coherence. To rectify this, Yan et al.16 presented VideoGPT, which is a mixed model that incorporates Vector Quantized Variational Autoencoders (VQ-VAEs) for spatial compression and GPT-2 transformers for autoregressive token forecasting. This strategy quantized frames into 8 × 8 × 3 latent tokens, allowing 128 × 128 px video synthesis on the UCF-101 dataset with an Fréchet Video Distance (FVD) score of 52.8, reflecting high perceptual quality. While these improvements were made, VideoGPT needed four times the amount of training data as GAN-based models did, which capped its usability for low-resource tasks.

A critical review by Zhou et al.1 identified persistent temporal granularity gaps in all prevailing frameworks. According to their work, less than 10% of video models based on a GAN used optical flow regularization, a method that imposes physical motion coherence by maintaining tracking of the pixel movement between frames. This supervision resulted in 68% of the sports videos created from datasets such as NBA2K having artifacts, with objects such as basketballs showing discontinuous trajectories or unnatural acceleration. Additionally, initial models focused more on modelling spatial resolution rather than micromovement modelling—minor details such as wrinkles on cloth, facial spasms, or respiratory patterns that add photorealism. For example, while a GAN can create a high-res face, the absence of micromovements such as lip trembling or eye widening might make the output eerily motionless. These comments point to the need for systems that combine high-res synthesis with fine-grained temporal motion and are currently being served through advancements such as diffusion models and neural radiance fields (NeRFs).

Text-to-video synthesis

The challenge of translating text prompts into temporally coherent videos requires accurate coordination of language semantics and visual dynamics, a challenge that has spurred iterative innovation in generative models. Early frameworks, such as the TiVGAN2, followed a sequential architecture to solve this challenge. The initial phase employs adversarial networks for generation work, generating keyframes by conditioning on word vectors from GloVe embeddings, a technique preserving semantic associations via co-occurrence statistics. Convolutional layers that project feature maps spatially to images generated static visual anchors17. The second phase deployed volumetric convolutions (3D-CNNs) for interpolating intermediate frames, reducing sudden transitions through a loss function that emphasized frame-to-frame differences (ℓ₂-norm penalties). The TiVGAN’s dependence on predefined scene templates resulted in coherent outputs for concrete prompts. For example, the description “festival crowd” tended to produce static or repeatedly animated figures in videos, missing natural interactions such as dancing or vendor exchanges—a shortcoming measured by a 34% semantic inconsistency rate in open-domain tests1,18. Today’s multimodal video generation systems need to coordinate disparate inputs—speech, text, and visual information—together with frame-accurate precision. One key breakthrough area is speech-driven synthesis, in which audio signals control visual output. For example, Mittal et al.19 built Sync-Draw, a system that extracts speech-to-text features (Mel-frequency cepstral coefficients, or MFCCs) and temporally aligns spoken words with hand-drawn strokes. Trained in LSTM networks and dynamic time warping, a method for aligning asynchronous audio‒visual pairs, the model obtained 83% synchronization accuracy in sketch‒animation tasks. However, Sync-Draw relies on spectral audio features such as MFCCs and does not capture prosodic features such as pitch variation and speech rhythm, leading to emotional mismatches. For example, an upbeat voiceover in a “celebratory scene” could be paired with static, affectless animation, eliminating the desired affective effect in 27% of instances. This deficit underscores the demand for end-to-end speech-to-text systems that not only encode lexical meaning but also paralistic information such as tone and rhythm.

Protracted challenges to text-to-video synthesis arise from linguistic vagueness and computational resource limitations. Empirical evidence indicates that 41% of inputs from users hold polysemous verbs (e.g., “draw” may imply sketching or attracting), thereby creating conflicting visual interpretations such as pencils appearing in scenes where magnetized objects are being shown19. In addition, the computational costs of state-of-the-art models remain unaffordable: latent diffusion models require 8× longer processing to generate the same result as adversarial networks do, and training cycles of high-resolution models run for more than 10,000 TPU hours—equal to the lifetime energy usage of 50 refrigerators20,21. They highlight the need for lightweight architectures and context-dependent disambiguation algorithms, such as the utilization of knowledge graphs or multimodal user feedback, to improve practicality and semantic fidelity. Speech-to-text and multimodel integration

Contemporary multimodal video generation models are confronted with the challenge of synchronizing heterogeneous inputs—e.g., speech, text, and visual—at frame-level accuracy. One key development in this area is speech-guided synthesis, wherein audio signals control visual outputs. For example, Mittal et al.19 introduced Sync-Draw, a system that processes speech-to-text features (Mel-frequency cepstral coefficients, or MFCCs) to synchronize spoken words with hand-drawn sketches temporally. With LSTM networks and dynamic time warping—a method of aligning asynchronous audio‒visual pairs—the model attained 83% synchronization accuracy on sketch‒animation tasks. Sync-Draw’s use of spectral audio features (e.g., MFCCs) ignores prosodic aspects such as pitch variation and speech rhythm, leading to emotional mismatches. For example, a bright voiceover on a “celebratory scene” could be accompanied by static, unemotional animations, defeating the effect intended in 27% of instances. This disparity emphasizes the necessity for end-to-end speech-to-text systems to encode not only lexical content but also paralinguistic information such as tone and speed.

To improve alignment between speech, text, and visuals, current research has focused on joint embedding spaces where disparate data are projected into a common latent space. Satapathy et al.22 established a technique through training a model to map transcript keywords (e.g., “climate change”) onto related video scenes (e.g., melting ice caps) via triplet loss—a loss function that combines related concepts and pushes apart unrelated pairs. Their approach lowered semantic mismatches by 41% in learning materials such as TED Talks. Shankar & Surendran23 built on this concept with Graylag reinforcement learning (GRL), an adaptive system that maximizes the temporal alignment of spoken dialogue and on-screen activity24. By rewarding models for aligning verb-noun pairs in speech (e.g., “riding a bike”) with sequences on the screen (e.g., a cyclist), GRL reached a BLEU-4 score of 0.62 on captioning tasks. However, these models rely on annotated audio‒text pairs, which restricts their usability for languages with limited labelled resources, such as Bengali or Swahili. This highlights the need for self-supervised approaches to speech-to-text alignment for multilingual scenarios.

Despite progress, major challenges persist in realizing smooth audiovisual integration. Lip synchronization remains a technical bottleneck: while models achieve 74% accuracy in matching English phonemes (visemes) to lip movements, performance decreases to 52% for tonal languages such as Mandarin, where pitch variations alter word meaning25. For example, the Mandarin syllable “ma” could mean “mother” (high pitch) or “horse” (falling pitch), but incorrect lip rendering disrupts comprehension. Additionally, real-time processing constraints hinder deployment in interactive applications. Autoregressive architectures such as Mittal et al. generate high-resolution (512 × 512 px) videos at 8 frames per second (FPS)—too slow for live scenarios such as video conferencing or augmented reality—where 24 FPS is the baseline for smooth motion. Future work must prioritize lightweight architectures and tonal-language-specific visual models to bridge these gaps.

Advanced conditioning mechanisms

Advanced conditioning techniques have emerged to increase precision in aligning text, speech, and visual modalities during synthesis. Early work by Mansimov et al.26 pioneered token-aware attention layers for image generation, where relevance scores (α values between 0 and 1) were assigned to textual tokens via additive attention mechanisms. This approach, inspired by neural machine translation frameworks, improved the element positioning accuracy by 22% on static image benchmarks such as COCO. However, its applicability has been limited to single-frame outputs, lacking the temporal adaptability required for speech-driven video generation.

To address dynamic alignment, Shankar & Surendran23 devised GRL, a hierarchical reward system tailored for audiovisual coherence. At the granular level, GRL minimizes discrepancies between spoken captions and on-screen actions via ℓ₁-norm penalties a method that quantifies absolute differences between predicted and ground-truth sequences. At the narrative level, a pretrained BERT-based evaluator scores semantic consistency, optimizing for cross-entropy loss to ensure logical progression (e.g., a “car chase” scene maintaining continuity across explosions and tire skids). While effective for scripted content, GRL struggled with unscripted speech inputs, such as live interviews, where disfluencies (“uh,” pauses) disrupted reward calculations.

Cross-modal fusion remains a critical obstacle, particularly for abstract or affective concepts. Pan et al.27 attempted feature fusion through elementwise multiplication of text and video embeddings (Hadamard product layers), achieving a 34% reduction in semantic discrepancies on the MSR-VTT dataset. However, their framework faltered with nonliteral language: prompts such as “a moment of joy” incurred 19% higher error rates than concrete actions such as “a person running”22. This gap underscores the challenge of translating prosodic speech elements (e.g., emotional intonation in “joy”) into visual metaphors without explicit textual anchors. Future advancements may require hybrid architectures that jointly model speech-to-text paralinguistics (pitch, stress) and visuo-semantic abstractions.

Recent advances in robust multimodal fusion and trustworthy generation

Recent advancements in multimodal AI have placed increasing emphasis on robust fusion, semantic consistency, and trustworthy generative modeling, transitioning from early unimodal or loosely coupled approaches. A key development in emotion recognition is RMER-DT, which combines diffusion models with transformers to jointly process speech and text for improved emotional understanding in conversations28,29. RAFT further contributes to this area through adversarial training strategies that fuse heterogeneous modalities for more resilient sentiment prediction30. Expanding on semantic fidelity, the CIME framework models contextual interactions between modalities, while contrastive approaches have been used to filter out misleading or irrelevant emotional signals, thus improving model robustness31,32.

Beyond affective computing, models developed in other domains also offer valuable insights into robust multimodal processing. A notable example is the dynamic global–local spatiotemporal graph framework designed for long-term PM2.5 forecasting across cities, which demonstrates how hierarchical integration of local and global features enhances temporal prediction33. Similarly, knowledge generation and distillation have proven effective for road segmentation in intelligent transport systems, enabling improved generalization through structured learning34. In the healthcare domain, federated domain generalization has been explored for fundus image segmentation across multiple medical centers, ensuring both visual clarity and cross-domain consistency35.

Efforts to ensure trustworthy generation are also evident in work on image enhancement. Symmetrical and recursive neural architectures have been proposed to address issues like hallucination and structural distortion in super-resolution tasks, thus enhancing perceptual reliability36. Accessibility and usability are reflected in client–server recognition frameworks designed for non-contact assessment of emotional and behavioral states, which support real-time, inclusive applications37.

Finally, the field has seen significant progress in authenticity verification within generative AI. Deepfake detection models now employ spatial kernel selection with attention mechanisms38, as well as feature refinement networks that progressively enhance discriminative capabilities39. While these approaches focus on identifying manipulated content, they align with the broader goal of ensuring semantic and emotional fidelity in both recognition and generation contexts40.

While the architecture adopts diffusion models for enhanced text-to-image alignment (Stage 2), the principle of progressive refinement from GAN-based frameworks is inherited. Dataset used

The LibriSpeech ASR Corpus41 was used as the main benchmark to assess speech preprocessing and noise robustness. It consists of more than 1,000 h of 16 kHz English speech from public-domain audiobooks across various speakers, accents, and acoustic conditions. Its clean, large-scale segmented utterances make it well suited for training and testing noise reduction pipelines. In our work, spectral smoothing filters were used to segregate the 80–4000 Hz speech region, suppressing extraneous frequencies via Gaussian-weighted masking. Postprocessing testing on LibriSpeech’s “test-clean” and “test-other” subsets revealed an 18% reduction in the WER over unfiltered audio, highlighting the effectiveness of our preprocessing pipeline under noisy conditions. The corpus’ standardized partitions allowed for direct comparison to state-of-the-art automatic speech recognition (ASR) systems, with reproducibility and stringent benchmarking.

Proposed novel model

The mined keywords and transcribed text are blended into high-fidelity imagery via Stability AI’s Stable Diffusion v2.1 model, a latent diffusion model designed with semantic coherence and computational efficiency in mind42. Text inputs are first projected into a 768-dimensional embedding space via the model’s CLIP text encoder, which preserves syntactic relationships (e.g., “sunset over waves” vs. “waves at sunset”) while resolving ambiguities through contextual attention. Domain-specific terms extracted from MongoDB (such as “forest,” “urban”) are added to the base prompt to bias the generation towards thematic coherence so that the outputs resonate with the speech’s literal meaning.

This CFG formulation enables fine-grained tradeoffs between visual creativity and prompt adherence. For example, “a foggy mountain landscape” enhances both spatial structure and atmospheric realism through guidance-influenced denoising.

The model operates at a resolution of 256 × 256 using Stable Diffusion v2.1 with 20 DDIM steps for low-latency generation (~ 1.2 s per frame). Inference was conducted with mixed-precision (fp16) and a batch size of 1 on an NVIDIA RTX A4000 GPU (16 GB VRAM) provided by the university research laboratory. A lightweight upsampling module is applied post hoc to upscale outputs to 512 × 512, enhancing texture and edge sharpness. An img2img strength schedule linearly decays from 0.35 to 0.20 across frames to maintain temporal consistency. Negative prompts (e.g., “blurry,” “low quality,” “deformed face”) are applied consistently to suppress visual artifacts. Scene coherence is enforced via a keyword-conditioned segmentation mechanism—for instance, when prompted with “a cabin next to a river,” pretrained CLIP-based filters generate spatial attention masks for each object class, which are injected into the denoising loop through spatial modulation layers. Generated outputs are stored as PNG sequences with accompanying metadata (e.g., prompt, step count, latent noise seed) to facilitate frame interpolation in Stage 3.

The stable diffusion-based synthesis stage consistently produced high-quality frames with strong adherence to the semantic content of the refined prompts. The use of domain-specific keywords from MongoDB and motion-related enhancements (e.g., “dynamic poses”, “same faces”, “consistent background”) contributed to strong scene coherence. Outputs preserve spatial relationships and visual consistency across character appearances and scene elements, especially in scenarios such as “fight scenes” or “magical environments.”

However, failure cases were observed in prompts with highly abstract phrasing or conflicting adjectives (e.g., “foggy desert with vibrant colors”), which led to visual artifacts or compositional ambiguity. Additionally, certain human-centric prompts resulted in minor facial inconsistencies across frames owing to limited control over identity preservation in img2img inference.

Dataset used

The pretraining phase relies on the LAION-5B dataset, an enormous collection of 5.8 billion publicly sourced image‒text pairs from various web pages. This dataset enables broad visual‒semantic relations to be captured through foundational training of diffusion models, thus enabling strong generalization over various objects, scenes, and styles. When domain-specific fine-tuning is needed, the model is finetuned on COCO-Captions43, a 120,000-image human-annotated set with five descriptive captions per image. This is to fine tune matching between textual keywords and fine-grained visual textures (e.g., abstract words such as “mountains” to contextually appropriate features such as snow-covered hills or rocks). The combination of the LAION-5B scale and COCO-Captions precision offers adaptability to accommodate both general and task-specific synthesis demands.

Dataset used

The image generation stage in this research leverages a two-tiered dataset strategy to balance generalization with task-specific fidelity. At the foundation level, the LAION-5B dataset is employed for model pretraining. Compared with more than 5.8 billion image–text pairs scraped from publicly available sources, LAION-5B offers expansive visual diversity and semantic richness, allowing the underlying stable diffusion model to learn generalized image–text correlations across various domains, objects, and abstract concepts47. This scale ensures robust performance when generating frames from diverse prompts without overfitting to narrow visual categories.

For domain-specific fine-tuning, the COCO-Captions dataset43 is used. The training data include 120,000 images, all of which are annotated with five human-written textual descriptions emphasizing complex relationships between objects and other entities in the images. Fine-tuning on the COCO dataset refines the model’s ability to map prompt phrases into specific visual items and enhance spatial accuracy while reducing semantic distortion. The two-dataset approach enables the system to learn rapidly from user prompts, spanning from very abstract ideas to very detailed stories.

To ensure visual consistency critical for video synthesis, EchoVid conditionally filters the LAION-synthesized image-text pairs by prompt complexity and visual coherence at the precaching stage. Outputs are ranked on spatial consistency and object faithfulness dimensions, drawing inspiration from benchmark protocols employed in AIGCBench48, which measure the quality of image-to-video generation. Filtering guarantees that only high-quality frames are forwarded to the sequencing stage, limiting artifacts in the output video.

Overall workflow.

In summary, the EchoVid pipeline offers a modular and scalable approach to multimodal generation, integrating speech recognition, semantic image synthesis, and framewise video construction. The system combines spectral filtering for robust audio transcription41, latent diffusion models for high-fidelity visual rendering47, and prompt-guided video assembly mechanisms influenced by temporal dynamics-aware frameworks15,49. Through keyword-enhanced prompts and classifier-free diffusion guidance50, the methodology ensures emotional coherence and semantic alignment between spoken content and generated visuals.

The use of large-scale datasets such as LAION-5B47 and COCO-Captions43 provides a dual advantage: generalization across diverse concepts and fine-grained alignment with natural language inputs. Furthermore, motion-aware prompt engineering and latent frame interpolation enable temporal consistency without the complexity of autoregressive transformers16,51. This design ensures that EchoVid remains extensible and computationally efficient, with the potential for future expansion into dialect-aware language modelling, facial animation, and dynamic lip-sync modules48.

Results and discussion

The proposed EchoVid model is rigorously evaluated along three dimensions: qualitative analysis, quantitative metrics, and system-level performance. The qualitative evaluation focuses on prompt-based visual analysis, where generated images and videos are inspected for their alignment with textual prompts and overall perceptual plausibility.

For quantitative assessment, the model’s outputs are validated using standard video generation metrics, including FVD, Fréchet Inception Distance for Video (FID-VID), and CLIPScore. In addition, we introduce two complementary metrics—Temporal Semantic Stability (TSS) and Perceptual Flicker Index (PFI)—to more precisely capture temporal aspects of video quality. TSS measures the semantic consistency of generated content across frames, while PFI quantifies frame-to-frame perceptual fluctuations, enabling a more fine-grained analysis of temporal smoothness and coherence.

Beyond visual quality, we also compare the proposed EchoVid model against well-established baselines in terms of practical performance indicators: feature availability, cost and latency analysis across devices. Finally, an ablation study is conducted to analyze the contribution of key architectural components and design choices. Collectively, these evaluations validate EchoVid from multiple perspectives to ensure both high perceptual quality and robust practical performance.

Qualitative evaluation

Qualitative comparisons with baseline approaches such as MoCoGAN15, VideoGPT16, and more recent diffusion-based models like Latte52 and Tune-A-Video reveal key strengths and trade-offs of the proposed EchoVid framework. While transformer-based models like VideoGPT and diffusion models such as Latte and Tune-A-Video generate highly fluid and temporally coherent motion, EchoVid achieves superior prompt interpretability, higher visual resolution, and real-time generation without the need for model training.

Unlike MoCoGAN, which often suffers from temporal identity drift and content hallucination, EchoVid’s deterministic generation pipeline and keyword-based prompt conditioning deliver consistent frame coherence. Compared to VideoGPT, which requires significant computational resources and pretraining time, EchoVid operates with significantly lower latency and provides users with greater stylistic control. While Latte52 and Tune-A-Video offer cutting-edge motion smoothness and realistic transitions, they typically come at the cost of longer inference times, higher hardware demands, and limited controllability via text prompts, areas where EchoVid remains strong. As shown in Table 2, EchoVid excels in prompt control and usability, making it well-suited for rapid content generation and interactive creative applications. Although it does not match the motion realism of transformer or newer diffusion models, its advantages in efficiency, output resolution, and semantic alignment provide a compelling trade-off. To ensure a fair comparison, all models were evaluated with identical prompts, fixed clip lengths (5 s), and consistent frame rates (12 FPS).

Language coverage and limitations

Although EchoVid demonstrates strong semantic alignment and visual coherence across diverse speech inputs, several limitations persist. The current ASR–translation pipeline primarily supports English and Hindi, with reduced accuracy for low-resource or code-mixed inputs, yielding an average CLIPScore drop of about 8% in Hindi due to transliteration inconsistencies. Emotion-to-visual mapping can also produce exaggerated gestures under intense emotions (e.g., anger, surprise), indicating the need for finer prosody–gesture calibration. Model generalization is limited in highly dynamic visual domains such as cinematic or sports scenes, which could improve with larger datasets like WebVid-10 M. Finally, CPU inference remains slower than GPU processing despite near–real-time (≈ 22 FPS) generation on RTX A4000/T4 GPUs. Future work will address efficiency through diffusion distillation, mixed-precision inference, and multilingual ASR integration.

Conclusion and future directions

This work presented EchoVid, a modular speech-to-video generation framework integrating ASR, latent diffusion-based image synthesis, and prompt-guided temporal video construction. By decomposing the system into three interpretable stages: speech-to-text conversion, semantic-aware image generation, and image-to-video synthesis, EchoVid enables controllable and emotionally aligned video generation from spoken input. Leveraging pretrained diffusion, CLIP, and ASR models allowed rapid prototyping without retraining, while motion-aware prompts and MongoDB-based context retrieval enhanced semantic fidelity. Experimental results demonstrated superior visual quality, coherence, and efficiency compared with prior frameworks such as MoCoGAN and VideoGPT, achieving near real-time performance (≈ 1.2× input duration) at 512² resolution on an RTX A4000 GPU.

EchoVid employs the LAION-5B and COCO-Captions datasets, with NSFW and PII filtering and keyword-based prompt guards to ensure safe generation. On LibriSpeech41, preprocessing reduced WER from 7.4% to 6.1% (test-clean) and from 13.2% to 10.8% (test-other), validating the noise-reduction pipeline. While limitations remain in multilingual generalization, tonal-language lip-sync, and emotional calibration, future versions aim to integrate dialect-aware ASR, refined emotion conditioning, and diffusion distillation for improved efficiency. Quantitative validation through TSS, PFI, and PEAS metrics further confirms EchoVid’s robustness in maintaining both perceptual and semantic consistency across generated frames.

Future work will focus on two main directions to strengthen EchoVid’s inclusivity and temporal coherence:

Integrating dialect-aware ASR systems for low-resource and regional languages (e.g., Hindi) to improve multilingual performance.Incorporating advanced interpolation models (e.g., RIFE, DAIN) to enhance motion fluidity and visual stability in long-form videos.
