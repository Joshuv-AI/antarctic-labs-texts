Elucidating the growth-promoting factors of Bacillus amyloliquefaciens on rapeseed through metabolomics
Authors: Song Pei, Deng Yue, Yu Yaoying, Hu Zijin, Zhang Zhongmei, Yang Xiaoxiang, Zhang Lei, Liu Yong, Huang Xiaoqin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPlant Growth-Promoting Rhizobacteria (PGPR), such as Bacillus amyloliquefaciens, play vital roles in enhancing plant growth and stress tolerance. This study aims to evaluate the effects of the B. amyloliquefaciens strain Bam22 on the growth and metabolic profile of rapeseed (Brassica napus) seedlings, specifically focusing on uncovering the underlying metabolic mechanisms.ResultsInoculation with Bam22 significantly promoted rapeseed growth. Compared to the control, plant height increased by approximately 17.1% and root length by 14.4% at 35 days post-inoculation (dpi). While leaf number remained no significant changed, fresh weight showed significant increases of approximately 70.3% (shoot, 7 dpi), 39.4% (shoot, 35 dpi), 23.8% (root, 7 dpi), and 26.8% (root, 35 dpi), respectively. Wide-targeted metabolomic analysis using UPLC-MS/MS detected 1,785 metabolites. Statistical analysis revealed significant time-dependent metabolic shifts. After 7 days, 192 metabolites were significantly up-accumulated, with notable enrichment in flavonoids (particularly flavonols, e.g., sophoraflavonoloside, FC = 3.39) and phenolic acids. At 35 dpi, 135 metabolites were up-accumulated, with significant enrichment in lipids (predominantly lysophosphatidylcholines-LPCs and lysophosphatidylethanolamines-LPEs) and flavonoids. Crucially, Bam22 induced a profound and directed reprogramming of flavonoid metabolism. Key changes included enhanced flux towards naringenin chalcone synthesis and the accumulation of specific anthocyanin precursors (e.g., cyanidin 3-O-glucoside, delphinidin 3-O-glucoside) and flavonols (e.g., Kaempferol-O-sophoroside).ConclusionsThis study provides the first report linking a B. amyloliquefaciens strain to systemic reprogramming of the flavonoid metabolome in rapeseed, revealing its role as a key mechanism underlying PGPR-induced growth enhancement. This flavonoid-centric metabolic shift, potentially enhancing stress tolerance (e.g., UV protection) and facilitating beneficial plant-microbe communication, provides crucial mechanistic insights into plant-microbe interactions and highlights the potential of targeting flavonoid metabolism for developing Bam22-based biofertilizers to optimize rapeseed productivity.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-025-07538-y.Keywords: Bacillus amyloliquefaciens, Brassica napus, Plant growth promotion, Metabolomics, Plant-microbe interactions

Introduction

Brassica napus L. (AACC, 2n = 38), commonly known as oilseed rape or rapeseed, is a crucial oilseed crop that plays a significant role in agricultural economies worldwide. As the second-largest oilseed crop globally, the oil extracted from rapeseeds accounts for approximately 13% to 16% of total vegetable oil production worldwide [1, 2]. Microorganisms are essential for the growth and development of rapeseed, particularly beneficial microorganisms known as plant growth-promoting rhizobacteria (PGPR). These microorganisms enhance nitrogen uptake, phosphorus solubilization, and overall nutrient efficiency, thereby playing a critical role in the optimal growth of rapeseed [3–5].

A notable member of the PGPR group is Bacillus amyloliquefaciens, recognized for its multifaceted contributions to enhancing plant growth and disease resistance [6, 7]. For instance, Ramírez demonstrated that the B. amyloliquefaciens strain FZB45 enhances phosphorus uptake through phytase activity, thereby promoting plant growth [8]. In another study, researchers found that the strain GD4a promotes plant growth by producing a variety of beneficial metabolites [9]. B. amyloliquefaciens also exhibits biological control activity. For instance, it can suppress Fusarium diseases by modifying the rhizosphere microbiome, promoting beneficial taxa [10]. In the interaction between rapeseed and B. amyloliquefaciens, the strain AK-12 aids in establishing protection against Brevicoryne brassicae while enhancing the growth of rapeseed seedlings [11]. Additionally, B. amyloliquefaciens has been found to protect rapeseed from four pathogenic fungi with different infection strategies [12].

Although PGPR (including Bacillus) are well-documented to improve plant fitness through nutrient facilitation (e.g., phosphorus solubilization [13]) and induced resistance [14], their impact on host metabolic pathways—particularly secondary metabolites like flavonoids—remains largely unexplored. Metabolomics is an emerging field that focuses on the comprehensive analysis of metabolites—small molecules that play vital roles in various biological processes. Numerous studies have demonstrated the utility of metabolomics in identifying and quantifying metabolites involved in plant responses to diverse environmental factors [15, 16]. An untargeted metabolomics approach is particularly beneficial for elucidating the effects of microbial colonization on plant metabolic networks, enabling the identification of potential metabolites associated with plant-microbe interactions [17, 18]. The advent of ultra-performance liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (UPLC-MS) has significantly advanced metabolomic research, owing to its superior resolution, speed, and sensitivity, which enhance the detection and quantification of metabolites [19]. Primary metabolites—including carbohydrates, proteins, and lipids—are essential for plant growth and development, while secondary metabolites, although generally not directly involved in growth and reproduction, play crucial roles in plant resistance to external stresses [20–22]. Metabolomics has emerged as a powerful tool for elucidating plant-microbial interactions, especially in the context of microbial-induced growth promotion in plants. For example, Bacillus megaterium BT22 has been shown to promote growth in Arabidopsis thaliana by modulating phytohormone homeostasis and inducing metabolomic reprogramming [23]. Another study indicated that Bacillus atrophaeus WZYH01 stimulates ion transport and nutrient uptake in maize by modulating key physiological metabolites, resulting in enhanced growth [24].

Recent studies have underscored the pivotal role of microbial interactions in modulating host metabolism, both in pathogenic and beneficial contexts. Transcriptomic and hormonal profiling of fungal pathogenesis has revealed intricate host responses driven by metabolic reprogramming, highlighting how microbes can profoundly alter host physiology [25]. In the rhizosphere, dynamic interactions among soil nutrients, root exudates, and microbial communities have been shown to shape plant responses to abiotic stress, with secondary metabolites like flavonoids playing central regulatory roles [26]. Moreover, recent work on synthetic Bacillus consortia demonstrates that cooperative bacterial behavior enhances plant growth through systemic metabolite modulation [26]. These findings, along with microbial enzyme applications in agricultural biotechnology [27], support the growing recognition that plant-associated microbes can trigger profound shifts in plant secondary metabolism, reinforcing the rationale for investigating flavonoid pathway reprogramming (metabolic profiling) induced by Bacillus amyloliquefaciens Bam22 in Brassica napus. Building on these advances, we hypothesized that: Bacillus amyloliquefaciens Bam22 promotes rapeseed growth through metabolic reprogramming (metabolic profiling).

In this study, we employed a wide-targeted metabolomics approach to uncover the mechanisms by which the B. amyloliquefaciens Bam22 strain promotes growth in rapeseed. We assessed changes in rapeseed metabolites using UPLC-MS/MS at 7- and 35-days post-inoculation with B. amyloliquefaciens. Metabolite clustering analysis and pathway enrichment analysis were conducted to clarify the effects of the Bam22 strain during the growth and development stages of rapeseed. The findings from this study provide a theoretical basis for understanding the probiotic mechanisms underlying the mutualistic relationship between rapeseed and B. amyloliquefaciens.

Methods

Experimental materials

Bacillus amyloliquefaciens Bam22 is a patented strain isolated and screened from the rhizosphere of rapeseed in our laboratory [28], with proven effects on disease prevention and growth promotion (preservation number: CGMCC No. 11329). ‘Huashuang 5’, a semi-winter-type conventional variety of Brassica napus, was bred by Huazhong Agricultural University.

Seedling Cultivation of Rapeseed (B. napus)

Uniform seeds of B. napus ‘Huashuang 5’ were surface-sterilized with 1% sodium hypochlorite (10 min), followed by five rinses with sterile distilled water. Seeds were placed on moist filter paper and germinated in darkness at 25 ± 1 ℃ until radicle emergence (1–2 mm). Germinated seeds were individually transferred to sterile nursery pots (10 cm in diameter) filled with autoclaved (121 ℃, 20 min) substrate (peat: vermiculite: perlite = 3:1:1, v/v/v), with two seedlings per pot. The substrate moisture was maintained by quantitative spraying with tap water. Seedlings were cultivated in a controlled-environment chamber (Conviron BDW-40) under a 16-h light/8-h dark photoperiod (photosynthetic photon flux density [PPFD]: 150 µmol·m⁻²·s⁻¹) at a constant temperature of 25 ± 0.5℃ and 65% relative humidity.

Preparation of Bam22 suspension

The strains Bam22 was streaked onto LB agar plates (10 g/L tryptone, 5 g/L yeast extract, 10 g/L NaCl, 15 g/L agar, pH 7.2) and incubated at 28℃ for 24 h. A single colony was inoculated into 150 mL of LB broth (comprising the same components as above but without agar) in a 500 mL Erlenmeyer flask, followed by cultivation at 28 °C with continuous shaking (180 rpm) using an incubator shaker until reaching the mid-exponential growth phase (determined by measuring OD600 at 1.0 ± 0.05 with a Shimadzu UV-1800 spectrophotometer). The bacterial suspension was stored at 4 ℃ for immediate use within 24 h.

Application of Bam22 suspension to B. napus Seedlings

At 7 days post-sowing, one vigorously growing seedling per seedling tray was retained for subsequent treatments. The pre-prepared Bam22 suspension (initial concentration: 1.0 × 109 CFU/mL) was diluted 100-fold in sterile distilled water (final concentration: 1.0 × 107 CFU/mL) and administered at a dose of 1 mL per plant via root drenching to the root zone of selected seedlings. Control plants were treated with an equivalent volume (1 mL) of LB medium diluted 100-fold, using the same method. Following treatment, seedlings were maintained under the previously established controlled-environment conditions.

Sample collection and processing

Plant samples were collected at three time points(0 d, 7 d, and 35 d post-Bam22 suspension application). Each treatment group was established with three biological replicates. For each sampling interval, two randomly selected seedlings were uprooted per replicate. Root-adhered soil particles were gently removed by rinsing with tap water, followed by three sequential washes with sterile distilled water to eliminate residual contaminants. Surface moisture was carefully absorbed using sterile filter paper, and morphological parameters (plant height, root length, and fresh weight et al.) were immediately measured.

Root tissues were excised using sterile surgical scissors, sectioned into 0.5 cm fragments via longitudinal cutting, and transferred into 2 mL sterile centrifuge tubes. The samples were flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80 °C for subsequent metabolomic analyses. This protocol ensured the preservation of tissue integrity and metabolic stability for downstream experimental validation.

Dry sample extraction

Using vacuum freeze-drying technology, place the rapeseed samples in a lyophilizer (Scientz-100 F), then grinding (30 Hz, 1.5 min) the samples to powder form by using a grinder (MM 400, Retsch). Next, weigh 50 mg of sample powder using an electronic balance (MS105DΜ) and add 1200 µL of −20 °C pre-cooled 70% methanolic aqueous internal standard extract (less than 50 mg added at the rate of 1200 µL extractant per 50 mg sample). Vortex once every 30 min for 30 s, for a total of 6 times. After centrifugation (rotation speed 12000 rpm, 3 min), the supernatant was aspirated, and the sample was filtered through a microporous membrane (0.22 μm pore size) and stored in the injection vial for UPLC-MS/MS analysis. The subsequent metabolomics testing work was completed by MetWare (http://www.metware.cn/).

UPLC conditions

The sample extracts were analyzed using two complementary LC-MS/MS platforms. For high-resolution qualitative profiling, an ExionLC™ AD UPLC system coupled to a SCIEX TripleTOF 6600 mass spectrometer was used. For highly sensitive quantitative analysis, an ExionLC™ AD UPLC system coupled to a SCIEX QTRAP 6500 mass spectrometer operated in Multiple Reaction Monitoring (MRM) mode was employed. The analytical conditions were as follows, UPLC: column, Agilent SB-C18 (1.8 μm, 2.1 mm * 100 mm); The mobile phase was consisted of solvent A, pure water with 0.1% formic acid, and solvent B, acetonitrile with 0.1% formic acid. Sample measurements were performed with a gradient program that employed the starting conditions of 95% A, 5% B. Within 9 min, a linear gradient to 5% A, 95% B was programmed, and a composition of 5% A, 95% B was kept for 1 min. Subsequently, a composition of 95% A, 5.0% B was adjusted within 1.1 min and kept for 2.9 min. The flow velocity was set as 0.35 mL per minute; The column oven was set to 40 °C; The injection volume was 2 µL. The effluent was alternatively connected to an ESI-triple quadrupole-linear ion trap (QTRAP)-MS.

ESI-Q TRAP-MS/MS

Widely targeted metabolomic analysis was performed using a hybrid approach combining high-resolution tandem mass spectrometry (HR-MS/MS) for metabolite identification and triple quadrupole (QQQ)-based MS/MS in multiple reaction monitoring (MRM) mode for quantification.

For qualitative profiling, samples were analyzed on a SCIEX TripleTOF 6600 system (SCIEX, Framingham, MA, USA) coupled with an ExionLC™ AD UPLC system. Quantitative analysis was conducted using a SCIEX QTRAP 6500 system (SCIEX) operated in MRM mode with an electrospray ionization (ESI) source. Metabolite identification was based on matching four criteria against the MetWare Database (MWDB): (i) accurate molecular mass (mass tolerance ≤ 20 ppm), (ii) MS/MS fragmentation patterns, (iii) isotopic distribution, and (iv) retention time (tolerance ± 0.2 min).

For quantitative analysis, metabolites were analyzed using MRM mode on a triple quadrupole mass spectrometer. In MRM mode, the first quadrupole filters the precursor ion of the target metabolite, thereby excluding ions of unrelated molecular weights and reducing initial background interference. The selected precursor ions were then fragmented in the collision cell, and the resulting product ions were passed through the third quadrupole, where a specific characteristic fragment ion was selected for detection. This two-step selection process significantly improves specificity, sensitivity, and reproducibility of quantification. After obtaining the raw mass spectrometry data, chromatographic peak integration was performed for all detected metabolites. Peak areas were used for quantification, and rigorous alignment and correction were applied to ensure consistent peak integration across different samples.

The ESI source parameters were set as follows: ion source temperature, 500 °C; ion spray voltage, 5500 V (positive mode) or − 4500 V (negative mode); nebulizer gas (GS1), heater gas (GS2), and curtain gas (CUR) at 50, 60, and 25 psi, respectively; collision-activated dissociation (CAD) gas was set to high. Nitrogen was used as the collision gas and was set to medium pressure for both linear ion trap (LIT) and QQQ scans in MRM experiments. Declustering potential (DP) and collision energy (CE) for individual MRM transitions were optimized to maximize sensitivity and specificity. A scheduled MRM algorithm was applied, in which a specific set of MRM transitions was monitored according to the expected elution window of each metabolite.

Metabolite identification was conducted at different confidence levels according to the Metabolomics Standards Initiative (MSI). Compounds identified based on accurate mass, MS/MS spectra matched against the MWDB, and retention time were assigned to level 2 (confident putatively annotated compounds). The identification of most flavonoids, in the absence of authentic chemical standards, is therefore considered tentative. Mass spectrometry data were processed using Analyst 1.6.3 software, and peak integration and calibration were performed with MultiQuant software. All UPLC-MS/MS analyses were performed by Wuhan MetWare Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (www.metware.cn).

Data analysis methods

Unsupervised PCA (principal component analysis) was performed by statistics function prcomp within R (www.r-project.org). The data was unit variance scaled before unsupervised PCA. For two-group analysis, DAMs were determined by VIP (VIP > 1), Student’s t test (FDR-corrected P-values) and absolute Log2FC (|Log2FC| ≥ 1.0). VIP values were extracted from OPLS-DA result, which also contain score plots and permutation plots, was generated using R package MetaboAnalystR. The data was log transform and mean centering before OPLS-DA. In order to avoid overfitting, a permutation test (200 permutations) was performed. Detected metabolites were matched against the KEGG Compound database (http://www.kegg.jp/kegg/compound/, accessed June 9, 2025). Pathways overrepresented by significantly altered metabolites were subjected to Metabolite Set Enrichment Analysis (MSEA), with statistical significance assessed via hypergeometric test P-values.

Results

Growth-promoting effect of Bam22 on B. napus

In this study, we selected rapeseed seedlings of relatively uniform length for inoculation with the Bam22 strain (Fig. 1a) and assessed its growth-promoting effects at 7 days and 35 days post-inoculation (dpi). The results showed that there was a significant difference in plant height between the treatment and the control group in 35 dpi (increased 17.1%), but no significant difference at 7 dpi. (Fig. 1b, d, e). Additionally, the number of leaves did not exhibit a significant difference compared to the control group (Fig. 1f). Root length showed no significant difference at 7 dpi, but was significantly increased 14.4% by Bam22 inoculation at 35 dpi (Fig. 1g). In terms of fresh weight, both the above-ground biomass and root fresh weight of rapeseed seedlings treated with Bam22 showed significant differences compared to the control group after 7 and 35 days of inoculation, approximately 70.3% (root, 7 dpi), 39.4% (root, 35 dpi), 23.8% (shoot, 7 dpi), and 26.8% (shoot, 35 dpi), respectively. Metabolite detection of Bam22 after root drench

Having observed significant growth promotion, we next examined metabolic changes via metabolomics to elucidate the pattern of changes in B. napus following treatment with Bam22. A total of 1,785 metabolites were detected, many of which were tentatively identified (for Level 3, see Supplementary Material Table S1). All detected metabolites were classified into 11 primary categories (Class I), which were further subdivided into secondary subclasses (Class II): Alkaloids (14.44%), Amino acids and derivatives (14.49%), Flavonoids (10.16%), Lignans and Coumarins (5.61%), Lipids (12.33%), Nucleotides and derivatives (4.66%), Organic acids (5.72%), Others (15.44%), Phenolic acids (10.99%), Quinones (1.17%), and Terpenoids (5%) (Fig. 2a).

Overall statistical analysis of dams treated by Bam22

We first analyzed the reliability of the differential metabolite data using OPLS-DA, with results indicating that the Q² values for all subgroup comparisons exceed 0.9 (Fig. S1), which confirms the appropriateness of the constructed model. The accumulation patterns of metabolites following different durations of Bam22 treatment were further examined through clustering heatmaps (Fig. 2b). The analysis revealed significant differences in metabolite profiles across the various groups, with distinct biological replicates clustering closely together, demonstrating strong homogeneity among the biological replicates and high data reliability. To interpret the dispersion of the five sample sets, we performed principal component analysis (Fig. 2c), which illustrated that the samples predominantly segregated into three regions: CK-0d, CK-7d, and Bam-7d, as well as CK-35d and Bam-35d. Consequently, we conducted separate principal component analyses for the treatment and control groups at each time point (Fig. S2), revealing clear separation characteristics at both 7 days and 35 days post-treatment.

Statistical analysis of DAMs after Bam22 treatment

To characterize metabolite and pathway changes at an early treatment stage, we analyzed B. napus 7 days post-Bam22 application. Differential accumulated metabolites (DAMs) were identified using dual criteria: |Fold Change | >2 and Variable Importance in Projection (VIP) > 1. Significantly changed metabolites meeting both thresholds were visualized in volcano plots (Fig. 3a). Compared to the control group, a total of 192 metabolites were found to be up-accumulated, 64 down-accumulated, and 1544 showed no significant change following Bam22 treatment. Metabolite cluster analysis indicated that the DAMs were predominantly enriched in Class I categories, including ‘Others’, ‘Flavonoids’, and ‘Phenolic acids’ (Fig. 3b). Furthermore, multi-group differential volcano plots demonstrated the enrichment of Class II DAMs within the ‘Others’ and ‘Flavonoids’ groups. Notably, the enrichment of up-accumulated metabolites in the ‘Others’ subgroup was highest for ‘Saccharides’ (Fig. 3c), while ‘Flavonols’ emerged as the most enriched category within ‘Flavonoids’ (Fig. 3d). Subsequently, we ranked the up-accumulated metabolites in descending order based on their fold change values and selected the top 50 metabolites for classification and statistical analysis. Our findings revealed that ‘Glucosinolates’, ‘Saccharides’, and ‘Flavonoids’ constituted the largest proportion of the second category of metabolites (Fig. 3e).

Changes in the three metabolite classes in different groups

According to the results of the analysis, we primarily focus on the change patterns of three types of metabolites: ‘Saccharides’, ‘Flavonoids’, and ‘Lipids’. ‘Saccharides’ demonstrate significant accumulation during the initial stage (7 days) of Bam22 treatment, while ‘Lipids’ exhibit significant accumulation in the later stage (35 days) of the same treatment. In contrast, ‘Flavonoids’ are characterized by significant accumulation at both treatment stages. We analyzed the commonalities among these three metabolites across different groups and selected the ten metabolites with the highest up-accumulated FC values, illustrating the changes in their levels across various groups (Fig. 5). Among the ‘Lipids’ metabolites, the comparison between the Bam-35d and CK-35d groups reveals the most distinct DAMs (Fig. 5a). The ‘CK-7d vs. CK-0d’ group ranks second in terms of the number of specific up-accumulated metabolites (18) and shares a greater number of up-accumulated metabolites (11) with the ‘Bam-35d vs. CK-35d’ group. In the top 10 up-accumulated metabolites, two of the substances belong to the LPE category, while eight belong to the LPC category. Three metabolites (Waln010192, pmp001270, and pmp001251) exhibited high levels at CK-0d and CK-7d, but their levels were significantly reduced at 35 d; however, treatment with Bam22 notably increased their content at 35 d (Fig. 5b). Regarding the changes in ‘Saccharides’ metabolites, no specific up-accumulated metabolites were identified in the comparisons between Bam-7d vs. CK-7d and Bam-35d vs. CK-35d (Fig. 5c). The top 10 ranked up-accumulated metabolites were all at low levels at CK-7d, whereas Bam22 treatment significantly elevated the levels of these substances at 7 d (Fig. 5d). In the ‘Flavonoids’ category, the comparison groups CK-7d vs. CK-0d and Bam-7d vs. CK-7d revealed 30 and 25 unique DAMs, respectively (Fig. 5e). However, metabolite commonality analysis indicates that Bam-7d vs. CK-7d shares a greater number of up-accumulated metabolites with the other comparison groups, whereas CK-7d vs. CK-0d shares fewer up-accumulated metabolites. The levels of the top 10 up-accumulated metabolites show a significant up-accumulated following Bam22 treatment, with some substances (pme2960, MWSN0056, Cmxp003975, and Lmsp004301) having a content of zero at 7 d (Fig. 5f).

Bam22 reprograms flavonoid metabolism in rapeseed

Based on metabolite change patterns under different treatments, we focused on Flavonoid biosynthesis pathways. These primarily comprise three interconnected KEGG pathways: Flavonoid Biosynthesis (ko00941), Anthocyanin Biosynthesis (ko00942), and Flavone and Flavonol Biosynthesis (ko00944). Figure 6 presents a partial visualization of this complex network, highlighting key changing metabolites and precursors.

Network diagram of the metabolic pathway ‘Flavonoids biosynthesis’

Within the Flavonoid Biosynthesis pathway (ko00941), naringenin chalcone is synthesized exclusively from p-coumaroyl-CoA by chalcone synthase (CHS). While p-coumaroyl-CoA and its precursors were undetected, naringenin chalcone levels showed significant down-accumulated (content was 0 in CK-7d) in the control group from 0 to 7 dpi. However, Bam22 treatment for 7 days significantly increased naringenin chalcone’s abundance (VIP = 1.68). Conversely, downstream p-coumaroyl-CoA metabolites (p-coumaroyl shikimic acid and 2’,3,4,4’,6’-Pentahydroxychalcone 4’-O-glucoside) significantly up-accumulated in the CK group between 0 and 7 dpi. No significant differences in these compounds were observed between CK and Bam22-treated plants at 7 dpi. This indicates that Bam22 redirects p-coumaroyl-CoA flux toward naringenin chalcone synthesis, contrasting with its natural utilization for other derivatives.

For Anthocyanin Biosynthesis pathway (ko00942), metabolomics detected activity only in the cyanidin and delphinidin branches. For cyanidin, cyanidin 3-O-glucoside levels remained stable in CK (0–7 dpi) but were significantly elevated in Bam22-treated plants at 7 and 35 dpi versus CK. Downstream, cyanidin 3-O-(6-O-p-coumaroyl) glucoside up-accumulated significantly in CK (0–7 dpi) and differed from CK with Bam22 at 35 dpi. Conversely, cyanidin 3-O-sophoroside was unchanged in CK (0–7 dpi), significantly up-accumulated with Bam22 at 7 dpi, but showed no difference at 35 dpi. In the delphinidin pathway, delphinidin 3-O-glucoside (key node) was stable in CK (0–7 dpi) but significantly up-accumulated after 7 days Bam22 treatment. Downstream metabolite delphinidin 3-(6-p-coumaroyl) glucoside showed no significant differences, while petunidin 3-O-glucoside up-accumulated significantly in CK (0–7 dpi) and was significantly higher in Bam22-treated plants at both 7 and 35 dpi versus CK.

In the Flavone and Flavonol Biosynthesis pathway (ko00944), key precursors kaempferol showed divergent metabolite responses. In the kaempferol branch, sophoraflavonoloside was significantly up-accumulated by Bam22 at both time points, while kaempferol 3-sophorotrioside up-accumulated only at 7 dpi (FC = 3.39). Trifolin up-accumulated at 7 dpi but down-accumulated versus CK at 35 dpi. Quercetin was undetected; however, its derivative isoquercitrin up-accumulated significantly at 7 dpi (FC = 2.35, P-value = 0.02). Rutin showed no change at 7 dpi but down-accumulated significantly at 35 dpi, while another derivative, myricetin, was unchanged at 7 dpi but significantly up-accumulated at 35 dpi versus CK.

Discussion

In this study, we investigated the effects of B. amyloliquefaciens Bam22 on the growth and metabolism of rapeseed (Brassica napus). The results showed that Bam22 significantly promoted the growth of rapeseed, especially in terms of plant height and fresh weight, confirming its effectiveness as a potential biofertilizer. These findings are consistent with previous studies and emphasize the importance of microorganisms in plant growth promotion, especially in strengthening the plant root system and improving nutrient uptake efficiency [29, 30]. By evaluating the growth of rapeseed plants, we observed that the height of plants treated with Bam22 was significantly higher than that of the control at both 7 and 35 days (Fig. 1b, e). Meanwhile, the significant increase in root length at 35 days indicated that Bam22 may enhance water and nutrient uptake by promoting root development (Fig. 1c, g). Additionally, a well-developed root system facilitates greater communication with the inter-root microbial community, thereby enhancing the plant’s competitiveness for further growth [31]. Although the number of leaves did not show significant changes, this may indicate that Bam22 prefers to enhance the growth potential of the plant through the root system rather than by increasing the surface area for photosynthesis.

Metabolomics analyses showed that a total of 1,785 metabolites were detected in this study, and a variety of key metabolites were found to exhibit significant changes, including sugars, flavonoids, and lipids (Fig. 2). The changes in these metabolites may be closely related to the adaptive mechanisms of plants in response to environmental stresses and their growth processes [32–34]. The significant accumulation of sugar metabolites in the early stage of treatment (7 days) not only provided the plants with necessary energy and nutritional support, but also may have played an important role in promoting root growth. Notably, sucrose serves dual roles as both an energy source and a signaling molecule in regulating plant root growth and development [35, 36]. Synthesized primarily in aerial tissues, sucrose is translocated from the shoot to the root system. Upon reaching the root tip, sucrose promotes the accumulation of the enzyme TAA1. This enhances the conversion of tryptophan to indole-3-pyruvic acid (IPA) and ultimately to indole-3-acetic acid (IAA, auxin), a key phytohormone required for lateral root initiation and development [37]. In the present study, we examined sucrose content and its key metabolic precursors in multiple experimental groups. The results demonstrated that only sucrose content was significantly elevated in the 7-day Bam22 treatment group compared to the control. In contrast, levels of the other precursors assayed showed no significant differences (Fig. 7a, S4). Our data indicate that IAA levels remain unchanged following Bam22 treatment (Fig. 7b), suggesting that classical auxin signaling is unlikely to mediate Bam22-induced growth promotion. Similarly, elevated sucrose levels do not correlate with activation of its canonical biosynthetic pathway (Fig. 7a). Consequently, Bam22’s growth-promoting effects are primarily attributable to induced metabolic reprogramming-particularly in flavonoid and lipid metabolism – which may activate alternative signaling cascades or directly enhance synthesis of structural components (e.g., cell wall/membrane constituents).

a, Comparative analysis of sucrose and metabolic precursor content. b, Comparative analysis of tryptophan-dependent IAA biosynthesis pathway precursor content. DAMs were determined by VIP (VIP > 1) and absolute Log2FC (|Log2FC|≥1.0), P-value: hypergeometric test’s P-values

Our metabolomic analysis revealed a significant association between B. amyloliquefaciens Bam22 inoculation and profound reprogramming of flavonoid metabolism in rapeseed, concomitant with the observed growth promotion. While the precise causal relationship requires further elucidation, the coordinated accumulation of specific flavonoid classes warrants exploration of their potential functional contributions to the PGPR effect. Flavonoids are plant specialized metabolites characterized by a diphenylpropane (C6-C3-C6) backbone [38, 39]. They play significant roles in regulating plant growth and development. Crucially, they mediate plant defense responses against biotic stresses (such as diseases) and abiotic stresses (including environmental challenges) [40–43]. Within the flavonoid biosynthesis pathway (ko00941), Bam22 redirects metabolic flux away from downstream products of p-coumaroyl-CoA (e.g., p-coumaroyl shikimic acid) towards the synthesis of naringenin chalcone. Naringenin chalcone functions as an essential biosynthetic node and regulatory metabolite in plant systems [44, 45]. As the committed precursor in flavonoid biosynthesis, its isomerization by chalcone isomerase (CHI) yields naringenin—the core scaffold for anthocyanins, flavonols, and proanthocyanidins critical for plant development [46–49]. In the Anthocyanin Biosynthesis pathway (ko00942), Bam22 treatment significantly enhanced accumulation of key precursors (cyanidin 3-O-glucoside and delphinidin 3-O-glucoside) and specific derivatives (e.g., petunidin 3-O-glucoside). Anthocyanins and their precursors are renowned for their roles in mitigating abiotic stresses [50, 51]. Their strong antioxidant capacity can protect cellular components, including membranes and proteins, from ROS damage [52]. In addition, their ability to absorb ultraviolet radiation can protect the photosynthetic apparatus and DNA of seedlings, indirectly providing assistance for plant growth. Regarding Flavone and Flavonol Biosynthesis (ko00944), Bam22 modulated metabolism by dynamically regulating kaempferol derivatives (e.g., initial up-accumulated of sophoraflavonoloside followed by subsequent down-accumulated of trifolin), and altering quercetin derivative profiles (e.g., increased isoquercitrin at 7 dpi and myricetin at 35 dpi). These results indicate that Bam22 exerts a directed regulatory effect on the synthesis and metabolic pathways of flavonoids in rapeseed. Other studies have reported that chalcones, quercetin, dihydroquercetin, and quercetin are involved in lignin formation [53, 54]. Therefore, the accumulation of quercetin chalcone contributes to increased rapeseed biomass, consistent with our phenotypic studies (Fig. 1). On the other hand, the accumulation of anthocyanin precursors also helps plants resist damage from ultraviolet rays and strong light, and helps them withstand stress from cold, drought, and other conditions [51, 55–57], ensuring normal growth and development. An alternative interpretation considers that the observed flavonoid accumulation might be a consequence rather than a cause of the improved growth status. Enhanced photosynthetic capacity resulting from better nutrient uptake or overall plant vigor could lead to increased carbon fixation and allocation towards secondary metabolite pools, including flavonoids.

Beyond their well-established roles in plant defense and development, flavonoids serve as crucial signaling molecules mediating communication between plants and plant growth-promoting rhizobacteria (PGPR). Substantial evidence supports this function: For instance, Bacillus subtilis elevates flavonoid levels (e.g., rutin, myricetin, daidzein) in tobacco as a defensive response against Agrobacterium tumefaciens infection [58]. Notably, specific flavonoids can directly recruit PGPR, enhancing plant growth and tolerance to abiotic stresses like water and nutrient limitation. Root-derived flavonoids, such as apigenin and luteolin, promote the enrichment of beneficial Oxalobacteraceae bacteria in maize, particularly under nitrogen deficiency [59]; Similarly, apigenin and luteolin in rice root exudates stimulate biofilm formation by the nitrogen-fixing bacterium Gluconacetobacter diazotrophicus, enhancing root colonization and biological nitrogen fixation efficiency in nutrient-poor soils [60]. Our metabolomic analysis reveals that Bam22 treatment significantly increases luteolin accumulation while concurrently reducing apigenin levels (Fig. 6). This metabolic shift toward luteolin production may facilitate the recruitment of beneficial microorganisms and enhance biological nitrogen fixation, suggesting a potential mechanism by which Bam22 improves plant performance. Whether the flavonoid changes are a direct driver of the growth promotion or a parallel outcome of Bam22’s interaction with the plant remains unclear. Nonetheless, our results demonstrate a clear association between PGPR treatment and secondary metabolism shifts.

Our metabolomic profiling revealed a striking temporal shift in lipid metabolism following Bam22 inoculation. While significant lipid accumulation was not observed at the early stage (7 dpi), treatment with Bam22 induced a substantial up-accumulated of 57 lipid metabolites by 35 dpi, with LPCs and LPEs representing the predominant up-accumulated subclasses (Figs. 4d and 5a). This delayed yet robust lipid accumulation coincides temporally with the significant enhancement in root length and fresh biomass observed at 35 dpi (Fig. 1g, i), suggesting a functional linkage between lipid metabolic and the progressive growth promotion exerted by Bam22. The accumulation of LPCs and LPEs, key structural components of cellular membranes and signaling molecules, likely underpins multiple physiological adaptations beneficial to plant growth [61, 62]. LPCs and LPEs serve as direct precursors for the synthesis of phosphatidylcholine (PC) and phosphatidylethanolamine (PE), major phospholipids essential for membrane integrity and expansion during cell growth and organ development [63, 64], particularly in rapidly elongating roots and expanding leaves observed at later stages (Fig. 1c-d). Lysophospholipids like LPCs are increasingly recognized as bioactive signaling molecules in plants that can mediate interactions between plants and microorganisms [65, 66]. The specific enrichment of LPCs/LPEs (Figs. 4d and 5a-b) raises the intriguing possibility that Bam22 may modulate root developmental programs or recruiting microorganisms resilience partly through altering lysophospholipid-mediated signaling cascades.

Our findings further demonstrate the temporally coordinated nature of the metabolic reprogramming induced by Bam22. Lipid accumulation exhibited a distinct later onset compared to the early up-accumulated of saccharides and flavonoids. This staged response—characterized by the initial enrichment of signaling- and energy-related metabolites (e.g., saccharides, flavonoids), followed by the subsequent accumulation of structural and storage lipids—suggests that Bam22 orchestrates metabolic shifts aligned with the developmental progression of B. napus. This temporal coordination aligns with the dynamic nature of plant-microbe interactions, where microbial effects often manifest progressively and integrate with host developmental cues, as supported by previous studies [67, 68]. Crucially, the late-stage, pronounced accumulation of LPCs and LPEs highlights a key adaptive shift in lipid metabolism. This shift likely underpins the observed enhancement in root development and biomass production at 35 dpi by facilitating membrane biogenesis, energy storage, and potentially modulating growth signaling pathways. Collectively, the distinct temporal patterns (early saccharides/flavonoids; late lipids) underscore the sophisticated, developmentally synchronized interplay between Bam22 and B. napus physiology.

This study, while revealing novel metabolic reprogramming linked to B. amyloliquefaciens Bam22’s growth promotion in rapeseed, has limitations that highlight future research needs. The data from our metabolomic work shows a strong association between Bam22 and specific changes in flavonoid abundance, such as the accumulation of sophoraflavonoloside, and growth. But, this data does not offer proof of causation. Future work using pharmacological inhibition of important enzymes (e.g. L-AOPP), genetic mutants (e.g. CHS or FLS) and transcriptomics/enzyme assays will show whether these flavonoid changes directly cause the growth. In addition to that, trehalose and sucrose are isomers, and the existing metabonomic technology cannot accurately distinguish the two substances. Therefore, it also brings difficulties to the functional analysis based on substance content to a certain extent. Experiments need to be validated under field conditions with biotic/abiotic stresses (sclerotinia/drought) to see if Bam22 can perform agronomically and whether the metabolic response as a result of Bam22 improves stress tolerance. Moreover, although profiling of metabolites was performed on days 7 and 35 of inoculation, more regular sampling at early developmental metabolisms and more extended studies past day 35 is required to predict the dynamic metabolic reprogramming. It is critical to address such points to move beyond correlation, validate the functional roles of metabolic shifts and translate Bam22’s potential into effective sustainable rapeseed cultivation strategies.

The findings of this study hold significant implications for agricultural practices. Application of Bam22 may enhance rapeseed growth, potentially leading to improved yield and quality. Consequently, the development of Bam22-based biofertilizers represents a promising strategy for future sustainable and eco-friendly agriculture. Future research should focus on elucidating the response of diverse plant species to Bam22 across various developmental stages to further explore its agricultural potential.

Conclusions

In conclusion, our study demonstrates that B. amyloliquefaciens Bam22 can significantly promote B. napus growth and induces specific changes in the plant’s flavonoid profile. This linkage between a PGPR and host secondary metabolism provides new insights into plant-microbe interactions and suggests that manipulating root microbiomes could enhance crop performance.
