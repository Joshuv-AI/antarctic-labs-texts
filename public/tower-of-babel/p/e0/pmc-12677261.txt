Improving the Recovery of Haemato-Oncological Inpatients by Implementing a Multifaceted Noise Control Programme
Authors: LinYing Liao, WenYan Cao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Objective:Investigate the effect of implementing a multidirectional noise control programme on the rehabilitation outcomes of hospitalised haemato-oncological patients.Methods:The clinical data of haemato-oncological patients who received their first chemotherapy treatment in Ganzhou People’s Hospital between March 2022 and February 2024 were retrospectively analysed. The patients were categorised into the noise reduction group and the conventional group in accordance with whether they had received the multidirectional noise control programme during their hospitalisation. A total of 72 cases were included in the final noise reduction group and 73 cases in the conventional group. The measurement of the acoustic environment in the wards of both groups and the monitoring of various serological indicators, such as the 12-item General Health Questionnaire (GHQ-12) and the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI), were used to evaluate the clinical effects of the multidirectional noise control programme. In addition, patient satisfaction with health care services was evaluated using the Inpatient Satisfaction and Experience Monitoring scale.Results:After the implementation of the noise control intervention, the ward noise levels of the noise reduction group were significantly lower than those of the conventional group (P < 0.001). After the intervention, the noise reduction group exhibited significantly lower levels of norepinephrine, adrenocorticotropic hormone and cortisol compared with those of the conventional group (P < 0.05). The GHQ-12 and PSQI scores of the patients in the noise reduction group were significantly lower than those of the patients in the conventional group after the end of the programme (P < 0.001). In addition, the satisfaction scores with the clinical medical services of the patients in the noise reduction group were significantly higher than those of the patients in the conventional group (P < 0.001).Conclusion:Systematic noise management significantly improved the sleep quality and mood and stress levels of haemato-oncological inpatients whilst enhancing medical satisfaction.

INTRODUCTION

A recent study found that the prevalence of haematologic tumours has been increasing since 1990, reaching more than 13 million cases as of 2019.[1] Haemato-oncological diseases are amongst the top 10 malignant tumours in China in terms of morbidity and mortality, and the prognosis tends to be poor; therefore, an aggressive, long-term, inpatient treatment regimen is typically required.[2] A clinical study of haemato-oncological departments found that noise from clinical equipment and professionals causes sleep disturbances.[3]

Sayilan et al.[4] also concluded that excessive noise can exert a detrimental effect on patients’ sleep and recovery from illness. High-decibel noise stimulates sympathetic nerves, leading to increased heart rate (HR) and blood pressure amongst patients. In addition, noise further aggravates physiological stress response amongst haemato-oncological patients who may already be suffering from cardiovascular burden caused by chemotherapy or the disease itself.[5] In addition, prolonged noise disturbance can reduce patients’ sleep quality and lead to a decrease in immunity, increasing the risk of infection and slowing down the treatment process amongst patients with haemato-oncological diseases whose immune function is inherently compromised, necessitating the implementation of proactive preventive strategies.[6]

Single noise control measures can hardly address other major noise sources, such as alarm sounds of medical equipment (e.g., monitors and ventilators) and the movement of treatment vehicles. In addition, relying on missionary work alone frequently lacks binding force, and patients and their families may repeatedly generate noise because of emotions, habits and other factors.[7] Compared with a single-noise management measure, a multidirectional noise control plan exhibits a good synergistic effect. In addition, the noise level in inpatient wards can be reduced as a whole by linking equipment noise reduction, floor sound-absorbing materials and nurse station voice prompting system. This technique is superior to individual noise control programmes, such as the use of sound-insulating doors, the wearing of earplugs and other measures. Therefore, the present study is the first to systematically evaluate such interventions in this specific patient population and to systematically validate the clinical implementation of a multifaceted hospital noise control programme. It focuses on constructing an assessment framework from the dimensions of the patients’ physiological index improvement, psychological experience optimisation and medical quality enhancement, to provide a basis for an innovative practice in hospital environmental management in an evidence-based medical environment.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Sample Size Estimation and Screening

This work is a retrospective study between March 2022 and February 2024, a total of 150 patients underwent eligibility assessment, of which two patients with renal failure, two patients with dementia and one patient with gastric cancer were excluded. Ultimately, 145 patients were included in the final analysis. Upon reviewing the relevant literature,[8] the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI) score of patients who received ward noise management was (9.31 ± 2.47), whilst that of patients who did not receive such management was (10.82 ± 2.61). The required sample size was calculated using PASS 2023 software (NCSS, LLC, UT, USA). Sample size calculation referred to the following formula: n = [2 × σ2(Zα + Zβ)2]/δ2. The Cohen’s d value of 0.59 was calculated, and the significance level α was 0.05. Test efficacy 1−β was 0.80, and the calculation indicated that 45 patients were required for each group. We enrolled 150 participants, ensuring ≥80% power even if the actual effect size was 15% smaller than the estimated value (δ = 1.28) or σ increased to 2.8.[9]

Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) age >18 years; (2) patients with haematologic tumours who have completed an established chemotherapy regimen (i.e., leukaemia, multiple myelomas and lymphomas); (3) normal auditory function and verbal communication; (4) complete medical records and (5) in the acute phase and require hospitalisation.

The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) patients with concomitant severe disease of vital organs (e.g., heart, kidney and liver); (2) comorbidities with other malignant neoplastic diseases; (3) recent (within 1 month) participation in other studies that may affect sleep or mood and (4) severe cognitive dysfunction (e.g., dementia and psychiatric disorders) or inability to cooperate or answer the questionnaire.

All the participants signed a written informed consent form. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Ganzhou People’s Hospital (approval number: 2024-017). It was conducted in accordance with the new version of the World Medical Association Declaration of Helsinki.[10]

Grouping

The patients were categorised into the noise reduction group and the conventional group in accordance with whether they had received the multidirectional noise control programme during their hospitalisation. A total of 72 cases were included in the final noise reduction group, whilst 73 cases were included in the conventional group.

Data Collection

Baseline Data

Relevant data about the patients were collected from the hospital’s electronic medical record system, including their gender; age; body mass index (BMI); whether they were smokers (individuals who had smoked more than 100 cigarettes in their lifetime and who continued to report smoking on a daily basis or on certain days);[11] whether they were hazardous alcohol drinkers (definition: hazardous alcohol consumption was defined as an average daily alcohol intake of ≥61 gram for males and ≥41 gram for females);[12] marital status (unmarried, married, divorced or widow/widower); family genetic history; type of disease (leukaemia, multiple myelomas, lymphoma); length of hospital stay; HR (early morning, i.e. 07:00–8.00, fasting resting state) and temperature.

Ward Noise Measurement

Noise measurement: A decibel metre (model: FKT-ZY, manufacturer: Shenzhen FKT Technology Co., Ltd.) was used to conduct 24 hours dynamic monitoring of the wards, corridors, nurses’ stations and other areas. The average noise level during hospitalisation was recorded for both groups.

Stress Indicator I Tests

In both groups, 3 mL of venous blood was collected in the evening (18:00–19:00) of the day of admission and during management closure night (18:00–19:00). The collected blood was tested using an enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay kit (manufacturer: Guangzhou Orient Bio-technology Co., Ltd.; Guangzhou, China) to detect norepinephrine, adrenocorticotropic hormone and cortisol (cort) levels.

12-Item General Health Questionnaire (GHQ-12) Scale

The mental health problems of the two groups of patients at different moments (upon admission and after the implementation of the noise control intervention) were evaluated using the GHQ-12 scale,[13] which comprises 12 items, with each item scored within the range of 0–3 points, and a full score of 36 points. A higher total score indicates a more serious mental health problem. This questionnaire exhibits good internal consistency (Cronbach’s alpha: 0.87).

PSQI Scale

The PSQI scale uses 18 evaluation indicators to comprehensively assess sleep quality. It covers seven dimensions, including sleep duration, disorder and quality. The total score of the scale is 21, with lower scores indicating better sleep quality. The scale exhibits good reliability, with an overall Cronbach’s α coefficient of 0.876, α coefficients of all dimensions of >0.85 and split-half confidence of 0.856.[14]

Hospitalisation Satisfaction Survey

The Chinese Hospital Patient Experience and Satisfaction Monitor Scale was used to assess the patients’ satisfaction with their hospitalisation and care prior to discharge. The scale consists of 28 closed-ended entries, with 6 dimensions, as follows: accessible admission (5 entries), general inpatient services (5 entries), therapeutic services (7 entries), complaint and opinion management services (2 entries), environmental and logistic services (6 entries) and discharge guidance services (3 entries). This scale is scored on a 5-point Likert scale, with the following options, in order from left to right: ‘strongly disagree’ (score 1), ‘disagree’ (score 2), ‘fair’ (score 3), ‘agree’ (score 4), ‘strongly agree’ (score 5), providing a choice for patients who have not been exposed to a service or who are difficult to assess. This scale is also scored as 0, with scores ranging from 28 to 140. The higher the score, the higher the patient’s satisfaction with the hospital’s medical services. The overall Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of the scale is 0.956.[15]

Statistical Analyses

The collected data were analysed and processed using SPSS (version: 26.0; manufacturer: IBM Corporation; place of origin: Chicago, IL, USA). The data were classified in accordance with type as follows: continuous and categorical variables. Firstly, the Shapiro-Wilk method was used to perform the test of normal distribution for continuous variables. On the contrary, variables that did not conform to the normal distribution were expressed as M [P25, P75], and the Mann–Whitney U-test was used for comparison. Secondly, Pearson’s chi-squared test or Fisher’s precision probability test was used for the categorical variables, denoted by n (%). Statistical significance was considered when P < 0.05.

DISCUSSION

In this study, the implementation of a multifaceted noise control programme can reduce stress indicators in haemato-oncological patients, and the implementation of the programme can improve patients’ mood and sleep, improving their overall evaluation of healthcare services.

Noise is a common environmental pollutant that activates the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis, leading to increased levels of adrenaline and cortisol.[16] An animal study found that a noisy environment at 75 dB can cause learning and memory impairments,[17] and increased interleukin 6 levels in the hippocampus. Noise reduction decreases HPA axis activity and cortisol release.[18] In addition, noise reduction decreases sympathetic nervous system (SNS) activity and reduces stress hormone levels. Idrobo-Ávila et al.[19] found that the use of a silent laboratory optimisation system could reduce salivary cortisol levels in the study participants, and the results obtained corroborated those of our study.[20] Therefore, the implementation of noise control may directly reduce abnormal auditory input, blocking the transmission of noise signals to the central nervous system. When noise levels are reduced, the hyperactivation of the auditory cortex and limbic system (particularly the amygdala) is mitigated, reducing the excitability of HPA and SNS, and stress response.

The multifaceted noise control programme designed in the current study exerts significant advantages over traditional single interventions. Traditional single-phase noise management measures frequently target only one aspect of the noise environment, and thus, they disregard the multiple sources of noise pollution and the totality of patient perception. In the current study, we propose a multifaceted control programme based on the ‘source–path–receptor’ integrated management model. In terms of noise control, noise generation is reduced through the development of a quiet management system in the ward. In terms of transmission pathway control, soundproofing equipment is used to reduce the transmission of noise. In terms of receptor protection, active noise-canceling headphones are provided to patients. This multilevel intervention can produce a synergistic effect because various measures are targeted at different links in the noise transmission chain, complementing each other rather than simply adding on to each other.

In the current study, the patients in the noise reduction group had significantly lower GHQ-12 scores at T1 than those in the conventional group (P < 0.001), indicating that noise control in wards can improve the psychological state of patients. This view is supported by a study conducted by Jaquez et al.,[21] who recruited a number of children who were undergoing surgical procedures from a paediatric dermatology clinic. The children were divided into the conventional and noise-cancelling headphone groups, and the study showed that participants who received noise-cancelling headphones had significantly lower anxiety scores. Therefore, noise management improves cardiovascular function and relieves anxiety and depression by reducing noise exposure and suppressing sympathetic overactivation.

Sleep deprivation and poor sleep quality have become a global public health challenge, with low sleep quality being significantly associated with serious health risks, such as cardiovascular diseases, metabolic dysfunction and cognitive decline.[22] The results of the current study indicated that the PSQI scores of the patients in the noise reduction group were significantly lower than those in the conventional group after the multifaceted noise control programme (P < 0.001), also indicating that the implementation of the multifaceted noise control programme not only improves the quality of sleep, but also alleviates the condition of patients. A prospective study found that the use of noise-cancelling earplugs improves sleep quality in healthcare shift workers, which is similar to our findings.[23] Noise management can make the ward environment of inpatients quieter, and this quieter environment can reestablish healthy sleep habits through positive conditioning. Further noise management reduces the number of sleep interruptions and improves sleep quality by reducing unexpected sound sources (e.g., equipment alarms and talking people).

Patient satisfaction is an important healthcare quality indicator.[24] The inpatient environment is positively associated with satisfaction, and noise during hospitalisation can disrupt sleep and lead to deterioration in health.[2526] The use of noise-cancelling headphones reduced anxiety and improved satisfaction in patients who were undergoing spinal anaesthesia, and similar findings were found in our study, allowing us to confirm that the implementation of noise control measures enhances patients’ clinical satisfaction.[27] The ward noise reduction programme enhances patient satisfaction through the chain mechanism of physiological repair–emotional regulation–environmental optimisation–rehabilitation promotion–humanistic shaping.

Although some results have been achieved, several limitations are still identified. For example, the current study has a small sample size, and it is a single-centre study, which may limit the extrapolation of the results. The current study did not use the propensity score matching method to minimise the effects of data bias and confounding variables on the study, possibly masking the true clinical effect and reducing statistical efficacy. This study also failed to control for the effects of other confounding factors (e.g., individualised treatment differences and escort behaviour) on the outcomes, and it did not track long-term effects (e.g., post-discharge psychosocial status). Factors, such as medication use, disease severity and others, which might affect stress indicators and sleep quality were also not considered, possibly leading to biased results, which is one of the inherent limitations of a retrospective design. These limitations may threaten the internal and external validities of the study and limit the generalisable applicability of the findings in clinical practice. Therefore, sample size should be enlarged in subsequent studies, and a multicentre randomised controlled trial should be conducted to explore the synergistic effects of noise management with other environmental interventions (e.g., light, temperature and humidity). Noise management was incorporated into standardised care processes in oncology wards, and dynamic noise monitoring and feedback mechanisms with patient participation were developed. The importance of noise control in improving the physiological–psychological–social outcomes of haemato-oncological patients was also reiterated, and hospital administrators were asked to give attention to the weight of environmental optimisation in the evaluation system of healthcare service quality. The results of the current study may also be affected by the specific medical environment, such as ward structure and equipment configuration, which will reduce the extrapolation of the conclusions. The conclusions are more applicable to a similar laminar flow ward structure of haematology oncology or equipped with the same type of noise reduction equipment (e.g., white noise machine, acoustic windows) in the medical unit. In the future, we plan to conduct multicentre validation in conjunction with a number of hospitals and further assess the influences of different care procedures on the effect of noise reduction.

CONCLUSION

The results of this study confirm that the implementation of a multifaceted noise control programme can improve the comprehensive healthcare experience of haemato-oncological inpatients. Noise control is not only an environmental optimisation measure, but also a key path to enhancing the quality of care for oncology patients through physiological–psychological synergies, providing a practical basis for improving inpatient experience in a clinic.
