Do robot-assisted percutaneous vascular interventions improve effectiveness and safety compared with manual procedures: protocol for a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Xiaotian Wu, Shuaiwei Guo, Xiao Zhang, Zixuan Xing, Linsen Zhang, Shengyan Cui, Xiangyu Li, Yixin Sun, Jichang Luo, Xinjie Lin, Liqun Jiao, Shiqi Liu, Tao Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundRobot-assisted percutaneous vascular interventions have emerged as a promising technology across cardiovascular, neurovascular, and peripheral vascular procedures. Although robotic systems may offer advantages such as enhanced precision and reduced radiation exposure, their clinical effectiveness and safety compared with conventional manual techniques remain uncertain, and the current level of procedural autonomy has not been systematically evaluated. This study seeks to provide direct and practical clinical evidence for clinicians.Methods and analysisIn this protocol, Preferred Reporting Items for systematic review and meta-analysis Protocols were followed. The primary outcomes are clinical success rates and major adverse cardiovascular/cerebrovascular events (MACE) rates. Secondary outcomes include technical success rates, procedural efficiency metrics, and radiation exposures. Comparative studies will be used for the primary effectiveness analyses, whereas single-arm studies will be analyzed separately as descriptive and supportive evidence. We will assess the quality of evidence, heterogeneity, and reporting bias following the recommendations of the Cochrane Handbook for systematic reviews of interventions.DiscussionThis systematic review will synthesize high-quality evidence on the comparative efficacy and safety of robot-assisted versus manual vascular interventions, with territory-specific analyses as the primary analytical framework. Introduction

Vascular pathologies, spanning cardiovascular, neurovascular, and peripheral vascular territories, remain the leading cause of mortality and a critical global health burden [1–3]. To address this challenge, percutaneous vascular intervention has emerged as the therapeutic cornerstone, endorsed by contemporary clinical guidelines for its minimally invasive nature and proven efficacy [4–8]. Nevertheless, the conventional manual paradigm faces three critical bottlenecks. First, limited accessibility: the steep learning curve and high technical risks restrict the decentralization of care, exacerbating the operator shortage in underserved regions [9]. Second, physiological constraints: inherent limits in manual stability and visual-haptic perception restrict the ultimate precision of maneuvers, even for experts [10]. Third, occupational hazards: the requirement for bedside operation while wearing heavy lead aprons exposes physicians to cumulative radiation and chronic orthopedic strain [11, 12].

Driven by the imperative to mitigate these limitations and enabled by advancements in robotics, interventional surgical robots (ISRs) have emerged as a transformative solution [13, 14]. These systems allow the interventionalist to manipulate devices from a shielded cockpit, thereby effectively decoupling the operator from the radiation source. Furthermore, they translate manual inputs into precise, micrometric robotic maneuvers with enhanced stability, even during prolonged or complex procedures [15]. Reflecting this technological maturity, the clinical landscape has evolved from the pioneering CorPath 200 [13, 16], to be dominated by advanced systems such as the CorPath GRX [15] and the Robocath R-One [14]. Moreover, this field is witnessing a rapid expansion with the influx of novel platforms like the YDHB-NS01 [17], ETcath200 [18, 19], and PANVIS-A [20]. Looking ahead, the integration of artificial intelligence (AI) promises to further revolutionize ISRs, enabling autonomous navigation and optimized path planning [10, 21].

Despite the therapeutic promise of robot-assisted intervention, current evidence regarding its effectiveness and safety relative to standard manual techniques remains fragmented and inconclusive. While some studies highlight enhanced precision and reduced radiation exposure [22, 23], others report prolonged procedure times and variable clinical benefits [18, 20]. These inconsistencies are largely driven by substantial heterogeneity in study designs, small sample sizes, and varying operator experience levels across both cardiovascular and neurovascular domains, necessitating an updated and comprehensive synthesis of the available data. Previous meta-analyses have predominantly focused on isolated vascular territories or relied on non-comparative data, limiting the generalizability of their findings. Furthermore, these reviews lack a granular assessment of key clinical and technical outcomes and fail to incorporate the latest high-quality evidence [17, 18, 20]. Current ISRs exhibit significant heterogeneity in hardware architecture and functional capabilities. Yet, a systematic framework to categorize their autonomy levels remains conspicuously absent. Addressing this void is pivotal for understanding the technological maturity of ISRs and informing their future development. Therefore, an updated systematic review is urgently needed to synthesize the current body of evidence.

This systematic review aims to provide a territory-specific and furthermore holistic assessment of robot-assisted versus manual percutaneous vascular intervention surgeries. We will conduct a stratified analysis of key outcomes, categorized into: clinical and technical success rates, major adverse cardiovascular/cerebrovascular events (MACE) rates, procedural efficiency outcomes, and radiation exposures. Furthermore, this study will systematically benchmark the levels of autonomy across included robotic systems. By synthesizing the most current and comprehensive evidence, this study elucidates the present landscape of robotic interventions and identifies trajectories for future innovation. Ultimately, these findings will empower clinicians, healthcare administrators, and policymakers to make evidence-based decisions concerning the integration and optimization of robotic systems in vascular practice.

Methods

This protocol is reported in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for systematic review and meta-analysis Protocols (PRISMA-P) guidelines (see Supplemental file 1, PRISMA-P checklist) [24]. The systematic review has been registered with PROSPERO database (registration number: CRD420251231572). We will promptly update on the PROSPERO registration if we make revisions to this protocol and ensure the review process is updated promptly. This study will follow the recommendations of the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions [25].

Types of studies

We will include randomized controlled trials (RCTs) and observational studies (including cohort studies, case–control studies, and case series) investigating robot-assisted percutaneous vascular interventions. Given the emerging nature of this technology, both comparative studies (robot-assisted versus manual) and single-arm studies (robot-assisted only) will be eligible for inclusion. To ensure data quality, case reports, small case series (< 10 participants), reviews, editorials, conference abstracts, and animal studies will be excluded. We will consider studies published in any language from database inception to January 31, 2026. Detailed eligibility criteria are provided in Table 1.

Experimental intervention: The experimental group comprises robot-assisted percutaneous vascular interventions. This is defined as procedures wherein the operator utilizes a dedicated robotic system (e.g., CorPath GRX, Robocath R-One) from a remote cockpit or console to execute core interventional maneuvers. Types of comparators

For comparative studies, the comparator is traditional manual vascular intervention, performed entirely by human operators using standard manual techniques at the patient’s bedside. For single-arm studies, the comparator is not applicable.

Search methods for identification of studies

Electronic searches

We will systematically search the following electronic databases from their inception to January 31, 2026: PubMed, EMBASE, Web of Science, and the Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials (CENTRAL). The search strategy will be developed by an experienced medical librarian.

Search strategy

The search strategy will employ a combination of Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) or Emtree terms and free-text keywords related to robotics and vascular interventional procedures. Boolean operators (“OR” and “AND”) will be used to combine terms within and between concepts. The search syntax will be adapted appropriately for the specifications of each database. The specific search terms include, but are not limited to:

Concept 1 (Robotics): “Robotics,” “Robotic Surgical Procedures,” “robot*,” “robot-assisted,” “robotic system,” “robot-enhanced.”

Searching other sources

To ensure a comprehensive literature search, we will manually screen the reference lists of all included studies and relevant reviews. Additionally, we will search clinical trial registries, including ClinicalTrials.gov and the WHO International Clinical Trials Registry Platform (ICTRP), to identify ongoing or unpublished trials. We will also contact the corresponding authors of relevant studies to obtain missing data or identify further unpublished work if necessary.

Assessment of heterogeneity

We will assess statistical heterogeneity using the I2 statistic and the X2 test. An I2 value > 50% will be considered indicative of substantial heterogeneity. In addition, τ2 and prediction intervals will also be considered to estimate the heterogeneity as complements to I2.

Data synthesis

Statistical analyses will be performed using RevMan 5 and R software, including the meta and metafor packages where appropriate. RevMan will be used primarily for standard pairwise meta-analyses of comparative studies and prespecified subgroup analyses. R will be used for analyses requiring greater statistical flexibility, including funnel plots and tests for publication bias, leave-one-out sensitivity analyses, meta-regression, and proportional meta-analyses of single-arm studies. The two platforms will therefore be used for complementary analytical purposes rather than duplicate analyses.

A random-effects model will be used for all meta-analyses because clinical and methodological heterogeneity is anticipated across vascular territories, procedure types, robotic platforms, and study designs.

Given the expected heterogeneity across cardiovascular, neurovascular, and peripheral vascular interventions, territory-specific analyses will serve as the primary analytical framework. Separate meta-analyses will be conducted for cardiovascular, neurovascular, and peripheral vascular interventions whenever sufficient data are available. A pan-vascular pooled estimate, if performed, will be considered secondary and exploratory and will be interpreted cautiously in relation to the territory-specific findings.

Quantitative synthesis will be conducted only when at least two studies report the same outcome and are sufficiently comparable in terms of PICO framework, vascular territory, comparator, and outcome definition. Studies with incompatible comparators or substantial clinical or methodological heterogeneity will not be quantitatively pooled; instead, they will be summarized narratively. Comparative meta-analyses will be restricted to robot-assisted versus conventional manual procedures. For studies comparing two robotic platforms, the robotic arms will be treated as single-arm data and analyzed separately as descriptive or proportional evidence. Studies with incompatible comparators will not be pooled, and narrative synthesis will be provided when quantitative synthesis is not appropriate.

Comparative analyses of robot-assisted versus manual vascular interventions will constitute the primary analytical approach for comparative effectiveness and safety outcomes. Single-arm studies will not be pooled together with comparative studies. Instead, they will be analyzed separately using proportional meta-analysis and reported as supportive and descriptive evidence.

For comparative studies: Dichotomous outcomes with very low or very high event rates, such as clinical success, technical success, MACE, and mortality, will be expressed primarily as risk ratios (RRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). ORs may be considered in sensitivity analyses where appropriate. Continuous outcomes will be expressed as mean differences (MD) or standardized mean differences (SMD) with 95% CI.

If more than 10 studies are included for a specific outcome, we will assess publication bias using funnel plots, Egger’s test, and Begg’s test.

Certainty of evidence

The quality of evidence for key outcomes will be assessed using the GRADE (Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation) approach, classifying evidence as high, moderate, low, or very low certainty.

Discussion

The integration of robotic systems into vascular intervention was initially driven by the imperative to reduce significant occupational hazards, such as ionizing radiation and orthopedic strain, for interventionalists. However, while operator-centric benefits are well recognized, the translation of robotic precision into superior patient outcomes remains equivocal. This clinical uncertainty is compounded by a fragmented evidence landscape and discordant findings regarding procedural effectiveness and safety.

This systematic review is therefore timely and necessary. Unlike previous reviews limited to specific vascular territories or single-arm data, this study will provide a structured synthesis across cardiovascular, neurovascular, and peripheral vascular interventions, with territory-specific analyses as the primary basis for interpretation. Pan-vascular synthesis will also be performed to describe broad trends across the field. Distinctively, this review represents the first systematic effort to characterize and establish a classification framework for the autonomy levels of vascular interventional robots. This contribution is pivotal for defining the current technological maturity of the field and delineating the trajectory for future intelligent innovations. Ultimately, these findings will translate into actionable insights: for clinicians, validating the balance between occupational protection and procedural precision; for patients, clarifying the potential for improved safety outcomes; and for policymakers and hospital administrators, providing the high-quality evidence required to optimize resource allocation, reimbursement strategies, and the strategic integration of robotics into standard practice.
