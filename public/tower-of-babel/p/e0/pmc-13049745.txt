Early risperidone exposure impairs cognitive function by perturbation of the gut microbiome and bile acids/tyrosine-PTP1B axis
Authors: Huaiyu Ye, Xiaoying Yang, Mingxuan Zheng, Wei Dong, Xi Chen, Jiajia Chen, Minmin Hu, Menglu Zhou, Peng Zheng, Lijun Shen, Yuqing Wu, Kuiyang Zheng, Xu-Feng Huang, Yinghua Yu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSecond-generation antipsychotics (SGAs) are increasingly being utilized in children and adolescents. Risperidone, one of the most commonly prescribed SGAs in this population, has been found to adversely affect cognitive function; however, limited knowledge exists regarding the impact of risperidone on the gut microbiome-brain axis. We hypothesized that the cognitive impairment induced by risperidone is mediated by alterations in the gut microbiome and its metabolites.ResultsIn this study, we found that early-life risperidone exposure impaired cognition in mice, including deficits in behavior tests and hippocampal dendritic architecture. The risperidone-exposed mice also exhibited gut microbiota dysbiosis along with damage to the intestinal barrier. Fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) from treated donors to recipients demonstrated the causal role of the gut microbiome in risperidone-induced cognitive deficits. Of note, risperidone increased the abundance of species Escherichia coli, Eggerthella lenta, Ruminococcus gnavus, Clostridium perfringens, Clostridium difficile, and Blautia hydrogenotrophica. These altered species are identified to encode 7α-HSDH, 3β/α-HSDH, TyrB, and porA, the key enzymes in secondary bile acid metabolism and tyrosine metabolism. Furthermore, a significant reduction in tauroursodeoxycholic acid (TUDCA, the metabolite of bile acid metabolism) and accumulation of p-cresol (the metabolite of tyrosine metabolism) were observed in the brains of mice exposed to risperidone. Mechanically, TUDCA prevented cognitive impairment and endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress in the hippocampus induced by risperidone, while p-cresol induced neuronal ER stress. Knockout of protein tyrosine phosphatase 1B (PTP1B, ER stress-associated protein) in neurons ameliorated cognitive impairment and neurological damage induced by risperidone.ConclusionsThis study, for the first time, reveals that early risperidone exposure induces gut microbiome dysbiosis and disturbs the bile acids/tyrosine-PTP1B axis to impair cognitive function. These findings alert the risk of gut and neurological side effects of SGAs treatment and highlight that it is crucial to maintain gut homeostasis during the brain developmental phases of children and adolescents with SGAs exposure. Background

There has been a substantial augmentation in the prescription of second-generation antipsychotics (SGAs) for pediatric patients over the past two decades, driven largely by off-label use for behavioral and mood disorders [1–3]. This trend is particularly prominent in children and adolescents aged 6–17 years. Among SGAs, risperidone has the highest rate of prescription in this age group [3]. Risperidone’s established pharmacological mechanism involves the antagonism of monoaminergic receptors, specifically dopamine D2 and serotonin 5-HT2A receptors, which underlies its primary therapeutic action [4]. It has been primarily utilized in the management of schizophrenia, bipolar disorder, and autism spectrum disorders approved by the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) [5, 6]. Furthermore, risperidone has been found to be highly efficacious (mostly off-label) in treating childhood behavioral disorders such as obsessive–compulsive disorder, anorexia nervosa, and aggression associated with behavioral disorders [7–9]. Previously, it has been reported that the utilization of SGAs has led to deterioration in cognitive domains in children at clinically high risk for psychosis [10]. However, in clinical studies, it is difficult to justify that the progression of cognition decline in these patients is due to the effects of SGAs or the progress of the disease. Rats study has shown that early risperidone treatment leads to deficits in working memory during adulthood [11]; however, the mechanism of risperidone on cognitive decline is unknown.

In recent years, the gut microbiome has gained increasing recognition as a crucial determinant in cognitive function [12, 13]. Specifically, disruptions in the gut microbiome can impact the gut-brain axis, alter brain development, and exacerbate neuropsychiatric symptoms or comorbidities [14]. In previous research, depletion of the gut microbiome in children and adolescent mice leads to cognitive deficits [12]. Notably, it is reported that nearly all antipsychotics exhibited anticommensal activity with a greater inhibitory effect on gut bacteria in vitro in a research screening of more than 1000 marketed drugs against 40 human representative gut bacterial strains [15]. Furthermore, clinical and rodent research showed that SGAs administration altered the composition of the gut microbiome [16–21]. It is reported that the risperidone administration for at least 1 year has increased the Firmicutes to Bacteroidetes ratios and Actinobacteria abundance in children [16]. Together, these findings suggest that risperidone-induced gut microbiome dysbiosis may disrupt the gut-brain axis, potentially contributing to cognitive side effects. However, the effects of early risperidone exposure in disturbance of the gut microbiota-gut-brain axis have not been reported.

In the present study, we modeled a developmental exposure window in mice (postnatal days 20–50, corresponding to human childhood through adolescence) to investigate the impact of risperidone. We found that risperidone treatment leads to cognitive impairment, including deficits in a series of cognitive behavior tests, hippocampal dendritic architecture, and synapse-associated proteins (PSD-95, BDNF) (Experiment 1). Subsequently, our study showed that risperidone caused gut microbiota dysbiosis and impairments in the intestinal barrier, while by fecal microbiota transplantation, we confirmed that cognitive impairment induced by risperidone was largely due to its side effect on gut microbiota (Experiment 2). Concretely, risperidone increased the abundance of species Escherichia coli, Eggerthella lenta, Ruminococcus gnavus, Clostridium perfringens, Clostridium difficile, and Blautia hydrogenotrophica, which were identified to encode the key enzymes in secondary bile acid metabolism and tyrosine metabolism, thus leading to a reduction of TUDCA (the metabolite of bile acid metabolism) and the accumulation of p-cresol (the metabolite of tyrosine metabolism) in the brain of mice exposed to risperidone (Experiment 3). Given that TUDCA and p-cresol exert opposing effects on endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress [22, 23], we hypothesized that risperidone-induced metabolic disturbances trigger neuronal ER stress signaling. Protein tyrosine phosphatase 1B (PTP1B) is a well-established ER-resident phosphatase that is critically involved in synaptic plasticity and cognitive function, particularly under conditions of metabolic and cellular stress [24, 25]. We therefore investigated PTP1B as a key downstream mediator linking gut microbiome-derived metabolic signals to risperidone-induced cognitive side effects (Experiment 4). We found that TUDCA supplementation mitigated cognitive impairment, ER stress, and PTP1B activation induced by risperidone, whereas p-cresol promoted the ER stress and PTP1B expression in neurons. Furthermore, the knockout of PTP1B in neurons ameliorated risperidone-induced cognitive impairment and synaptic protein deficits. Overall, our research provides valuable insights that risperidone exposure in early life disturbed the gut microbiome and the bile acids/tyrosine-PTP1B axis, thereby resulting in cognitive impairment. Moreover maintaining gut homeostasis is important during the brain developmental phases of children and adolescents with SGAs exposure.

Results

Childhood/adolescent exposure to risperidone induces cognitive impairment and hippocampal deficits in mice

To assess the effects of risperidone exposure during childhood/adolescence on cognitive function, the mice (postnatal day 20) were given risperidone for 30 days, and the cognitive behavior tests were performed at PD 55 (Fig. 1A). In the nest test, risperidone impaired the ability to build a nest in both male and female mice, resulting in lower nest scores and increased weight of untorn nestlet (Fig. 1B, C). In the Y-maze test, risperidone decreased spontaneous alternation and recognition memory in both male and female mice (Fig. 1D, E). In the novel object recognition test, the discrimination index was significantly decreased in both male and female mice (Fig. 1F). In addition, the discrimination index in temporal order memory and novel location tests was reduced in female mice with risperidone administration, but not in male mice (Fig. 1G, H). With Golgi silver staining in the hippocampus of mice, we observed that risperidone impaired the neurite outgrowth (Fig. 1I, J, K, L, M, N, O and Fig. S1). In CA1 of the hippocampus, the risperidone-exposed mice displayed a significant reduction in the total neurite length per cell and the number of neurite branches (Fig. 1J, K), but not in the average neurite length per branch (Fig. 1L). Furthermore, risperidone reduced dendritic arborization and complexity in CA1 of the hippocampus, as evidenced by a decrease in the sum and the maximum number of dendritic intersections (Fig. 1M, N, O). In addition, risperidone reduced dendritic arborization and complexity in CA3 of the hippocampus (Fig. S1A, B, C, D, E, F, G), but not in the DG of the hippocampus (Fig. S1H, I, J, K, L, M, N, O). By Nissl staining, we found that the thickness of the hippocampus was not altered in the risperidone mice (Fig. S2A, B, C, D). Synaptic functional plasticity in the CA1 region was evaluated through long-term potentiation (LTP) measurements. Quantitative analysis revealed that risperidone-exposed mice exhibited a significant reduction in the slope of hippocampal field excitatory postsynaptic potentials (fEPSPs) following high-frequency stimulation (HFS) compared to controls (Fig. 1P, Q, R), indicating that synaptic plasticity of neurons in the mice’s CA1 region is significantly impaired. Moreover, synaptic proteins, postsynaptic density protein 95 (PSD-95), and brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) were reduced in the hippocampus of the risperidone group (Fig. 1S, T, U). In addition, the pro-inflammatory cytokines TNF-α and IL-6 mRNA expression were increased in the hippocampus of the risperidone group, whereas IL-1β levels remained unchanged (Fig. 1V, W, X).

Risperidone-induced gut microbiota dysbiosis significantly contributes to cognitive impairment

The gut homeostasis plays an important role in brain function [26]. We next examined the effects of risperidone for 30 days on gut microbiota (PD 55) and intestinal barrier integrity. By using 16S rRNA gene sequencing (PD 55), gut microbiota diversity and composition in response to risperidone were analyzed. At the phylum level, the gut microbiota of both control and risperidone exposure mice was predominantly composed of Firmicutes, Bacteroidetes, Proteobacteria, Actinobacteria, and Patescibacteria (Fig. 2A). The principal component analysis (PCoA) showed relatively separated distribution profiles, indicating a notable difference in β-diversity for the microbial community between the risperidone and control group (Fig. 2B, C). Bacteroidetes were significantly reduced in the risperidone mice compared with the control group, while Actinobacteria and Patescibacteria were significantly increased (Fig. 2D, E, F). Through KEGG annotation and functional enrichment, we identified 13 functional categories that exhibited different enrichment levels, including secondary bile acid metabolism and tyrosine metabolism between the risperidone and control groups (Fig. S3A). Further shotgun metagenomic data revealed a significant reduction in key mucosal barrier-related species, such as Bacteroides fragilis and Bacteroides thetaiotaomicron (Fig. 2G, H). Using Alcian blue staining, the thickness of the colonic mucus was decreased in the risperidone group compared with the control group, which was confirmed by the staining for MUC2, the inner layer of mucus (Fig. 2I, J). The tight junction proteins, ZO-1 and occludin, were reduced in the colon tissue of the risperidone group (Fig. 2K, L, M). Furthermore, the LPS level was increased in the serum of the risperidone group, suggesting intestinal barrier impairment and increased permeability (Fig. 2N). The pro-inflammatory cytokines TNF-α and IL-6 mRNA expression were increased in the colon of the risperidone group but not in IL-1β mRNA expression (Fig. 2O, P, Q). In addition, to establish the temporal dynamics of the disruptions of gut microbiota and behavior, we tracked their progression by including a cohort of mice after 15 days of risperidone exposure (PD 35). Notably, the alterations in gut microbiota composition by 16S rRNA sequencing (PD 35) and the impaired cognitive behavior were already detectable at this early stage (Fig. S3), indicating that the gut-brain axis is affected early in the treatment course.

To investigate whether microbiota dysbiosis plays an important role in risperidone-induced cognitive decline, we transplanted the gut microbiota of the mice in the risperidone and control groups into healthy recipient mice, followed by assessing cognitive behaviors (Fig. 2R). Mice that received gut microbiota from risperidone-exposed donor showed decreased alternation triplets in the Y-maze test and ratio of visits novel arms in the Y-maze spatial memory test compared with mice transplanted with microbiota from the control donor group, while the discrimination index of the temporal order memory test was decreased (Fig. 2S, T, U), without alterations in cognitive indexes in nest behavior, novel object recognition, and novel location tests (Fig. S3K, L, M). Mice transplanted with microbiome from the risperidone group displayed a significant decrease of PSD-95 and lower BDNF (statistic trend, p = 0.0518) in the hippocampus (Fig. 2V, W, X). Notably, 16S rRNA sequencing of the fecal samples from recipient mice demonstrated that the fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) protocol successfully transferred core microbiomes from the donor mice. Similar to the distinct microbiota patterns observed in donor mice (Fig. 2C), β-diversity index was significantly decreased in the FMT-Ris group (Fig. S3N). Key risperidone-associated alterations at the phylum level, specifically, decreased Bacteroidetes and increased Actinobacteria (Fig. 2D, E), which were also recapitulated in the FMT-Ris recipients (Fig. S3O, P). Furthermore, LEfSe analysis demonstrated that the same bacterial genera enriched in risperidone-treated donors (e.g., Escherichia, Eggerthella, Mediterraneibacter, Blautia, and Clostridium) were significantly enriched in FMT-Ris recipient mice (Fig. S3Q, R). Taken together, these results demonstrated that risperidone induced cognitive and synaptic impairment through the gut microbiome.

Alterations in bile acid and tyrosine metabolisms are associated with changes of gut microbiome in risperidone-exposed mice

To further investigate the functional characteristics of gut microbiota and metabolites, we performed metagenomic and metabolomic studies on cecal content samples of risperidone-exposed mice. Shotgun metagenomic analysis revealed a distinct gut microbial community structure in risperidone-treated mice. While within-sample alpha-diversity indices (Simpson, Shannon, Sobs, Ace, Chao) were unchanged (Fig. S4A, B, C, D, E), the overall community composition (beta diversity) differed significantly from controls (analysis of similarities, ANOSIM, R = 0.888, p = 0.01). This separation was visualized by using nonmetric multidimensional scaling (NMDS) and principal component analysis (PCA) plots (Fig. S4F, G, H). LEfSe analysis identified specific bacterial genera and species that were differentially abundant between groups (Fig. S4I, J). The variable importance in projection (VIP) score for the gut microbiota showed that the most differential significantly abundant species of gut microbiota in the risperidone group was enriched in Escherichia coli, Eggerthella lenta, Ruminococcus gnavus, Clostridium perfringens, Clostridium difficile, and Blautia hydrogenotrophica (Fig. 3A). Furthermore, the abundance of above species were significantly increased in risperidone group compared with the control group (Fig. 3B). These altered species are identified as encoding 7α-HSDH, 3β/α-HSDH, TyrB, and porA, the enzymes involved in tyrosine metabolism and secondary bile acid metabolism, by analyzing data from the NCBI and UniProt database. Specifically, function annotation and enzyme scanning based on gene catalogue (Unigenes) indicated that tyrosine metabolism and secondary bile acid metabolism were the key metabolic pathways affected by the gut microbiota altered by risperidone. Changes in the gene abundances are shown in a pathway of secondary bile acid metabolism and tyrosine metabolism (Fig. 3C). Pathway modules, which establish a boundary for bacterial biosynthesis and degradation, were manually constructed by modifying KEGG pathway maps that refer to “Microbial metabolism in diverse environments (map00350, map00121) or the literature [27, 28] (Fig. 3D and E). Genes involved in tyrosine metabolism, which transform tyrosine into p-cresol, exhibited notable upregulation, including aromatic-amino-acid transaminase (TyrB) and pyruvate ferredoxin oxidoreductase alpha subunit (porA) (Fig. 3D). Genes of 7α-HSDH, 3β/α-HSDH, and BaiH, involved in secondary bile acid metabolism, were significantly elevated in the risperidone group (Fig. 3E). Bile acid-targeted metabolomics of cecal content further confirmed that secondary bile acids, chenodeoxycholic acid (CDCA) and ursodeoxycholic acid (UDCA), were significantly reduced in risperidone administrated group compared with the control group (Fig. 3F). Therefore, secondary bile acid and tyrosine metabolism were dysregulated in the gut of the risperidone group, with alterations in the gut microbiome responsible for these two metabolic pathways.

TUDCA decreases and p-cresol increases in the brain of mice exposed to risperidone

With a widely targeted metabolomics analysis of metabolites in the brain tissue, 28 metabolites exhibited statistically significant differences between the risperidone and control groups (Fig. S5A). Orthogonal partial least squares discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA) showed that there was a distinct clustering pattern between metabolites from risperidone and control groups (Fig. S5B). The variable importance in projection (VIP) scores for the different metabolites showed that TUDCA and p-cresol contributed most significantly to the group separation (Fig. 4A). Notably, we observed that the level of TUDCA (a hydrophilic bile acid containing taurine conjugated with the UDCA) was decreased in the brain compared with the control group, while the level of p-cresol (a metabolite produced by gut microbiota after fermentation of tyrosine) was significantly increased (Fig. 4B, C). Furthermore, we confirmed that the serum TUDCA levels were significantly decreased in the Ris mice and the Ris-FMT recipients compared with the control groups (Fig. 4D, E). Spearman’s correlation analysis revealed that there was a positive correlation between TUDCA and cognitive indexes, while Pearson’s correlation analysis revealed that p-cresol was negatively correlated with cognitive indexes (Fig. 4F).

TUDCA improves ER stress and cognitive impairment induced by risperidone, while p-cresol increases neuronal ER stress

The gut microbiota and its metabolites may contribute to cognitive function through their effects on maintaining ER homeostasis and neuronal function. Given that TUDCA is a known suppressor of ER stress and p-cresol is a neurotoxic metabolite linked to ER stress activation [22, 23], we investigated whether their imbalance contributes to risperidone-induced ER stress and cognitive deficits. Since we found that TUDCA decreased in the brain of the risperidone group, we investigated if TUDCA supplementation could rescue risperidone-induced cognitive decline in mice (Fig. 4G). TUDCA improved the ability to build a nest with higher nesting scores in the nest test (Fig. 4H), improved spontaneous alternation with alternation triplet (Fig. 4I) and recognition memory with the number of visit novel arms (Fig. 4J) in the Y-maze test, and improved the discrimination index in the novel object recognition test (Fig. 4K), but did not improve the discrimination index of novel location and temporal order memory tests (Fig. S5C, D). Furthermore, the ER stress sensor, inducer, and mediators, glucose-regulated protein 78 (GRP78), C/EBP-homologous protein (CHOP), and PTP1B, were significantly increased in the hippocampus of risperidone group compared with the control group, while TUDCA ameliorated the elevation of these ER stress markers (Fig. 4L, M, N). We further found that TUDCA increased the levels of synaptogenesis-associated proteins, PSD-95 and BDNF, which were decreased in risperidone groups (Fig. 4O, P, Q). Furthermore, p-cresol may cause mislocalization and abnormal expression of N-methyl-D-aspartate glutamate receptor (NMDAR) subunits [29], which can activate ER stress [30]. In N2A neuronal cells, we found that p-cresol significantly increased ER stress markers GRP78, CHOP, and PTP1B and decreased synaptic-associated proteins PSD-95 and BDNF, while these alterations were effectively inhibited by NMDAR blocker MK-801 (Fig. S6A, B, C, D, E, F).

Neuronal knockout of ER stress-associated protein PTP1B in neurons ameliorates risperidone-induced cognitive impairment

PTP1B is an ER stress-associated phosphatase known to induce synaptic and cognitive deficits [31, 32]. To test whether TUDCA directly suppresses PTP1B-mediated ER stress, we generated a stable PTP1B-overexpressing (OE-PTP1B) HT22 hippocampal neuronal cell line (Fig. 5A). The HT22 cell line was chosen as it is derived from mouse hippocampal neurons and maintains key synaptic signaling properties [33, 34]. TUDCA (200 μM) treatment decreased PTP1B level and increased PSD-95 level (Fig. 5B, C, D) in OE-PTP1B cells, but did not affect BDNF level (Fig. S6G, H). Next, we investigated whether neuronal PTP1B plays an important role in risperidone-induced cognitive impairment (Fig. 5E). We created CaMKIIαCre:PTP1Bfl/fl mice to selectively knockout PTP1B in forebrain neurons (including hippocampus) by crossing PTP1Bfl/fl mice with mice expressing Cre recombinase controlled by the CaMKIIα (expressed in the forebrain neurons). We confirmed significant knockdown of PTP1B mRNA in the primary hippocampal neurons of CaMKIIαCre:PTP1Bfl/fl mice (Fig. S5J). Behaviorally, we found that in the nest test, CaMKIIαCre:PTP1Bfl/fl mice showed increased nest scores compared to the PTP1Bfl/fl mice with risperidone exposure (Fig. 5F). In the Y-maze test, CaMKIIαCre:PTP1Bfl/fl mice with risperidone exposure showed the improvement of spontaneous alternation (Fig. 5G). Furthermore, the discrimination index in the novel object recognition test was significantly increased in CaMKIIαCre:PTP1Bfl/fl mice with risperidone exposure (Fig. 5H). Moreover, the PSD-95 and BDNF levels in the CaMKIIαCre:PTP1Bfl/fl group were improved compared to the PTP1Bfl/fl group with risperidone exposure (Fig. 5I, J, K, R).

Discussion

Previously, a clinical study involving children at high clinical risk for psychosis reported that treatment with second-generation antipsychotic medications for 6 months resulted in a decline in learning and memory, as measured by the California Verbal Learning Test (CVLT) [10]. However, it is unclear if the decline of cognition is due to the effects of SGAs or the progress of the disease. In the present study in children/adolescent mice, we found that the administration of risperidone for 30 days causes a decline in the ability of daily living performance, spatial memory, and recognition memory by using a series of cognitive behavior tests. These findings indicate that the use of SGAs risperidone during children/adolescents can induce cognitive decline. Some longitudinal imaging studies found that brain volume declines after the initiation of antipsychotic medication treatment for 1.5–2 years [35–37]. For example, whole-brain volumes and larger cerebral ventricle volumes were observed in first-episode schizophrenia subjects after antipsychotic medication treatment for up to 2 years [36]. In a normal macaque monkey study, another SGA, the use of olanzapine, for 17 months reduced the gray matter volume in antipsychotic-exposed monkeys [38]. Importantly, in the present study, we found that using risperidone in children/adolescents decreased neurite outgrowth and complexity in the hippocampus of mice. These findings suggest that antipsychotic-induced impairment of synaptogenesis may represent a mechanism contributing to the brain volume reductions observed in schizophrenia patients and antipsychotic-treated monkeys. Therefore, a chronic blockage of neurotransmission by antipsychotic drugs may cause brain structure changes. The maintenance of normal synaptogenesis is important for cognitive function [39]. PSD-95 (postsynaptic protein) plays a crucial role in synaptic functions, and BDNF (secretory growth factor) promotes neurite outgrowth and synaptic plasticity. Deficiency in these synaptic-associated proteins disrupts synaptic morphology and cognitive function [40–42]. Our study found a significant reduction in the levels of PSD-95 and BDNF in the hippocampus of risperidone-exposed mice. Therefore, it is speculated that the impairment of synaptogenesis, decreased synaptic-associated proteins, may contribute to the cognition decline induced by risperidone. While we observed elevated hippocampal TNF-α and IL-6 expression in the risperidone group, these neuroinflammatory changes are correlational. Future studies employing targeted anti-inflammatory interventions (e.g., cytokine-neutralizing antibodies or pharmacologic inhibitors) will be necessary to determine whether TNF-α/IL-6 signaling plays a causal role in mediating the cognitive deficits induced by risperidone.

A study screening over 1000 marketed nonantibiotic drugs against human gut microbiomes reports that antipsychotics have the most extensive impact on gut bacteria [15]. The administration of SGAs has been demonstrated to induce alteration of gut microbiota in clinical and animal studies [16, 19, 20]. For example, by using 16sRNA, a clinical study found that the abundance of phylum Bacteroidetes was notably diminished, while phylum Actinobacteria displayed an increase in psychiatric children treated with administration of risperidone up to 10 months [16]. In the present study of children/adolescent mice, we also found that risperidone decreased the abundance of the phylum Bacteroidetes and increased the abundance of the phylum Actinobacteria, suggesting the observation of alteration of phylum in psychiatric children treated with risperidone may mainly be due to the risperidone effect. Furthermore, specific species within the Bacteroidetes phylum, such as Bacteroides thetaiotaomicron and Bacteroides fragilis, are known to be critical for mucosal barrier integrity [43]. For example, Bacteroides thetaiotaomicron increases colonic gene expression involved in the synthesis of mucosal glycans [44]. Oral administration of Bacteroides fragilis strengthens the intestinal barrier and attenuates gut leakage in the autism mouse model [45]. Our metagenomic analysis revealed a significant decrease in both of these protective species in risperidone-treated mice. Therefore, this specific depletion may contribute to thinning of the mucus layer over the intestinal epithelium, resulting in damage to the epithelial barrier in the risperidone group. It is supported by evidence that intestinal tight junction proteins (occludin and ZO-1) were decreased in the risperidone group along with increased systemic endotoxemia (indicating damage in the integrity of the epithelial barrier and increased translocation of bacterial LPS into the circulation). As a component of the gut-brain axis, the gut microbiota plays a crucial role in cognitive health [12, 13]. In our study, fecal microbiota transplant of donor risperidone mice led to a decline in cognition indexes in normal mice, suggesting that alteration of microbiota induced by risperidone plays an essential role in cognitive impairment. The reduction of synaptic proteins, PSD-95 and BDNF, in the hippocampus of the FMT-risperidone-receiving mice, further verified the important role of microbiota in cognitive impairment induced by risperidone. Importantly, for the recipient mice in our FMT experiments, we deliberately avoided antibiotic pretreatment or germ-free conditions to preserve physiological neurodevelopment and prevent their direct confounding effects on BDNF signaling and neurogenesis in juvenile mice [12]. The ecological validity of this FMT model was maintained through rigorous environmental standardization via bedding transfer. Overall, these findings suggest that risperidone may cause adverse effects on cognition in children and adolescents through the alteration of the microbiota-gut-brain axis (Fig. 6).

Importantly, through metagenomics analysis, we have found that risperidone altered the abundance of species, including Escherichia coli, Eggerthella lenta, Ruminococcus gnavus, Clostridium perfringens, Clostridium difficile, and Blautia hydrogenotrophica, which were responsible for secondary bile acid metabolism and tyrosine metabolism. The Escherichia coli, Eggerthella lenta, Ruminococcus gnavus, and Clostridium perfringens encode the gene for 7α-HSDH, 3β/α-HSDH, and BaiH, the key enzymes in secondary bile acid metabolism [46, 47], while Clostridium difficile and Blautia hydrogenotrophica encode the genes for TyrB and porA, which catalyze tyrosine metabolism [28]. With metabolomics analysis, we have confirmed that the metabolite of secondary bile acid metabolism TUDCA decreased, while the metabolite of tyrosine metabolism p-cresol increased in the brains of risperidone-exposure mice. While our findings demonstrate that the dysbiotic community as a whole mediates the observed metabolic and neurodevelopmental effects, we acknowledge that FMT reflects the integrated effect of a complex microbial ecosystem. This approach links the functional phenotype to the transplanted community but cannot delineate causality to specific members. A more targeted approach, such as colonization with a defined microbial consortium composed of species overexpressing key metabolic genes (e.g., TyrB/porA and HSDHs), would be required to directly test species-specific contributions.

Clinical research has reported that an altered bile acid profile is associated with cognitive impairment in Alzheimer’s disease (AD) [48]. The study observed significantly lower serum concentrations of cholic acid (CA) in individuals with AD compared to cognitively normal older adults. Additionally, the Bai operon was found to be strongly associated with cognitive decline in AD. Moreover, the decrease of UDCA and TUDCA in serum in patients with hepatic encephalopathy is an important cause of neurological damage [49]. Conversely, TUDCA supplementation prevents cognitive impairment and amyloid deposition in APP/PS1 mice [50]. In our present study, the altered bile acid profile may contribute to cognitive decline in risperidone mice, especially the decreased level of TUDCA in the brain and CA in cecum content. Interestingly, oral supply of the TUDCA improved cognitive decline (the ability of daily living performance, spatial memory, and recognition memory deficits) in risperidone mice. TUDCA is already safely used in clinical treatment for cholestasis. Supplementation with TUDCA now is used in treating multiple sclerosis (MS) and AD in clinical trials (ClinicalTrials.gov: NCT03423121, ClinicalTrials.gov: NCT03533257). Therefore, it highlights that TUDCA supplementation may encounter the side effects of risperidone in the reduction of TUDCA-induced cognitive impairment. Notably, heightened levels of p-cresol have been detected in children with autism, a condition that is associated with the neurotoxic properties of p-cresol [51–53], which instigate endoplasmic reticulum stress, ultimately leading to neurological dysfunction [19, 43, 44]. Moreover, the abundance of Clostridium difficile and Blautia hydrogenotrophica, able to push the fermentation of tyrosine up to the formation of p-cresol [28], was significantly increased in risperidone mice leading to an increase in p-cresol in the brain.

TUDCA and p-cresol have the opposite effects on ER stress [22, 23]. TUDCA, as the ER chaperone [54, 55], inhibits ER stress sensor (GRP78, CHOP), further decreasing ER stress responsor PTP1B [55]. We found that a supplement of TUDCA decreased these ER stress markers and PTP1B in the hippocampus of risperidone mice, while specific knockout of PTP1B in the neurons of risperidone mice improved cognition and synaptic-associated proteins. Therefore, reduction of TUDCA may contribute to the failure to suppress ER stress in synapses and cognitive impairment. It is reported that p-cresol (a gut neurotoxin) induced autism-like behavior through N-methyl-D-aspartate glutamate (NMDA) in rats [56], while NMDA receptor activation may increase ER stress [57]. At the neuronal cellular level, we found that p-cresol increased the protein level of the marker of ER stress GRP78 and CHOP and increased the PTP1B, while MK‐801 (the NMDA inhibitor) alleviates the ER stress, inhibits the increase of PTP1B, and rescues the synapse damage induced by p-cresol. Therefore, p-cresol may act on NMDA to induce ER stress and lead to synaptogenesis and cognition impairment in risperidone mice.

Conclusions

This study provides evidence that risperidone negatively impacts synaptogenesis and cognition through microbiota-gut-brain axis alterations, potentially via PTP1B upregulation associated with decreased brain TUDCA levels and increased p-cresol. By integrating FMT and supplementation evidence, the findings establish a causal gut microbiome–metabolite–brain pathway, highlighting the importance of maintaining gut homeostasis during neurodevelopment in pediatric SGA therapy. Overall, these findings indicate that monitoring cognitive function may be warranted when prescribing SGAs to pediatric patients, particularly for off-label uses.

Limitations and future directions

First, the translational scope of this work is confined to the early developmental phase (childhood/adolescence), and the persistence of the observed cognitive and microbiota changes into adulthood remains a critical, open question for future longitudinal research. Second, the use of wild-type mice in this study reflects the effects of risperidone itself, independent of a psychiatric disease state. Future studies in disease-relevant models are needed to confirm these effects in a pathological context. Third, the specific microbial contributions to the neurological effects require further investigation through fecal microbiota transplantation studies using human donors with or without risperidone exposure. Fourth, the translational relevance of these findings needs verification through clinical validation of the identified microbial and metabolic markers in pediatric populations. Finally, it should be noted that our mechanistic validation relies on indirect strategies, pharmacological restoration of TUDCA and NMDA receptor antagonism, due to the current lack of direct inhibitors for TUDCA depletion or p-cresol activity. Although this approach provides robust evidence for the proposed pathway, the development of selective pharmacological tools in the future would enable a more precise dissection of each metabolite’s individual contribution.

Methods

Animals

Wild-type (WT) C57BL/6 J male mice (17 days old) were obtained from the Experimental Animal Center of Xuzhou Medical University (SYXK_Su_2020-0048). The mice were housed in environmentally controlled conditions (temperature 22 °C, 12-h light/dark cycle) and allowed ad libitum access to water and a standard laboratory chow diet (3.9 kcal/g: 10% fat, 74% carbohydrate, 16% protein). The mice were used for experiments approved by the Institutional Animal Care Committee of Xuzhou Medical University following the Chinese Council on Animal Care Guidelines. CaMKIIαCre (B6.Cg-Tg (CaMKIIαCre) T29-1Stl/J, no. 005359) and PTP1Bfl/fl (B6;129S4-Ptpn1tm2Bbk/Mmjax, no. 012679) mice were purchased from the Jackson LABORATORIES (USA), which were identified by nucleic acid electrophoresis technology (Supplementary Fig. 4I). Neuronal-specific PTP1B knockout mice (CaMKIIαCre:PTP1Bfl/fl, PTP1B-cKO) were obtained by intercrossing PTP1Bfl/fl mice and CaMKIIαCre:PTP1Bfl/− mice, which were obtained by intercrossing CaMKIIαCre mice and PTP1Bfl/fl mice.

Experimental design

This study was structured into four experiments to investigate the impact of risperidone on the microbiota-gut-brain axis and cognition (Fig. S7).

Experiment 1: modeling risperidone exposure and characterizing its cognitive and neurobiological phenotypes

C57BL/6 J mice were administered risperidone (0.6 mg/kg/d, oral) or vehicle from postnatal day 20 (PD 20, n = 14). Cognitive behavior tests were assessed at PD 35 and PD 55. Hippocampal morphology, LTP, and synaptic protein levels were assessed at PD 55.

Experiment 2: determining the causal role of risperidone-induced gut microbiota dysbiosis in cognitive impairment

Gut microbiota alterations induced by risperidone were characterized via 16S rRNA sequencing (PD 35 and PD 55). To establish causality, we performed fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) from risperidone-treated or control donors (PD 55) into recipients (n = 12). Recipient cognitive behavior and gut microbiota composition were subsequently assessed.

Experiment 3: identifying key gut-derived metabolites and testing their functional roles in cognitive deficits

We integrated cecal metagenomic and metabolomic data from risperidone-exposed mice with brain-wide targeted metabolomics, which identified significantly depleted TUDCA and elevated p-cresol levels. A rescue experiment was conducted by administering TUDCA to mice (n = 14). Concurrently, the effect of p-cresol on the ER stress-PTP1B pathway was examined in vitro using N2A neuronal cells. TUDCA was selected for in vivo study due to its clinical relevance and safety, allowing direct assessment of neuroprotective potential, whereas the neurotoxin p-cresol was investigated in vitro to safely delineate its mechanistic role.

Experiment 4: validating the central molecular mechanism involving PTP1B as the target of microbial metabolites

We confirmed TUDCA’s ability to rescue deficits induced by the PTP1B overexpression in vitro usingHT22 neuronal cells. The necessity of PTP1B for the observed phenotypes was further verified in vivo using neuronal-specific PTP1B knockout mice (n = 12).

Drug treatment groups

Animals were then assigned randomly to one of two experimental groups per gender on PD 20: (1) Risperidone (Yangsen, Xi’an, China) or (2) control (Con). The drug exposure period from PD 21 in the mice was carried out at the equivalent time of the childhood/adolescent phase in humans [58]. A staggered drug treatment pattern, where lower risperidone dosages are slowly increased to a final dosage amount, was used to mimic a clinical setting [59]. In brief, the risperidone dose was initiated on PD 21 at 0.1 mg/kg/day three times per day and then increased in three steps over the first 7 days, to achieve a final dose on PD28 of 0.6 mg/kg/day, three times per day for risperidone. The cookie dough pellets were fed to the mice three times per day (07:00 h, 14:00 h, and 22:00 h, with 8 ± 1 h intervals) over the 4-week treatment period. Medicated pellets were prepared by homogenizing finely ground risperidone powder (12 mg) with 100 g of nutritionally balanced cookie dough base (composition: 30.9% cornstarch, 30.9% sucrose, 6.3% gelatin, 15.5% casein, 6.4% fiber, 8.4% minerals, 1.6% vitamins) and distilled water. Pellets (0.1 g each) contained risperidone quantities (0.002, 0.006, or 0.012 mg) corresponding to 0.1, 0.3, and 0.6 mg/kg doses respectively [60]. Control pellets matched this composition without risperidone. All mice underwent 3-day acclimation (PD 18–20) with control pellets to establish consistent self-administration behavior. During treatment, direct observation confirmed complete pellet consumption. Serum risperidone levels were not measured in this study. Serial blood sampling in pediatric/adolescent mice would introduce repeated stressful procedures, which could confound behavioral and neurodevelopmental outcomes. The dosage regimen of 0.6 mg/kg (three times daily) [60–62] was selected based on prior pharmacokinetic and receptor occupancy studies, which demonstrate that this protocol achieves 65%–80% dopamine D2 receptor occupancy [63]. Furthermore, the doses are clinically relevant, translating via body surface area to equivalent human pediatric doses used in psychiatric practice [63–65].

Behavioral tests

Nest behavior, Y-maze, novel object recognition test, novel location test, and temporal order memory tests were performed to examine recognition memory and spontaneous rodent behaviors. Tests were conducted similar to previous studies [66–72]. For the nest behavior test, each mouse was individually housed in a clean cage with adequate food and water 1 h before the dark phase. Nesting behavior was assessed 12 h later at the onset of the next light phase. The Deacon nest score and the untorn nest weight were used to evaluate spontaneous rodent behavior (the ability of daily living). The spontaneous alternation in the Y-maze test, a measure of working memory, was evaluated as the percentage of spontaneous alternations: [number of actual alternations/(total arm entries − 2)] × 100. For the recognition memory in the Y-maze test, there were two 5-min trials with a 120-min interval. In the first trial, one arm was blocked. In the second trial, all three arms were accessible. The number of entries into the previously blocked (novel) arm was recorded. In the novel object recognition test, the novel object discrimination index was calculated by using the formula as follows: the time spent with the novel object/the total object exploration time × 100. In the novel location test, the place discrimination index was calculated by using the following formula: the time spent with the object moved to a novel place/the total time spent in exploring both the object moved to a novel place and the object remaining in the familiar place × 100. In the temporal order memory test, the discrimination ratio was calculated as follows: [(old familiar exploration time − recent familiar exploration time)/total exploration time] × 100. A detailed schematic of the behavioral test timeline of Experiments 1–4 is provided in Supplementary Fig. S8. Further methodological details are available in the supplementary methods.

Golgi-Cox staining and image analysis

We utilized Golgi analysis, as previously reported [73]. The Golgi-Cox staining was performed using FD Rapid Golgi Stain Kit (FD Neuro Technologies) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Following deep anesthesia (pentobarbital sodium, 100 mg/kg i.p.) and transcardial perfusion with 0.9% saline, brains were carefully extracted and immersed in Golgi-Cox solution (A + B) for 14 days (light-protected, room temperature). After impregnation, tissues were transferred to Solution C for 72 h with daily solution changes. Using a vibratome (Leica VT1500), 100-μm coronal sections were cut and mounted on gelatin-coated slides and then air-dried overnight in a humidity chamber at room temperature. For quantitative morphometric analysis, two independent investigators blinded to experimental conditions selected eligible pyramidal neurons in hippocampal CA1, CA3, and DG subfields based on stringent criteria: (1) complete silver impregnation throughout all dendritic processes, (2) unambiguous isolation from adjacent stained neurons, and the (3) absence of sectioning artifacts or truncated dendrites. Digital images were acquired at 400 × magnification using an Olympus VS120 virtual slide scanning system. Dendritic reconstruction was performed using the NeuronJ plugin (NIH Fiji ImageJ) with manual verification, quantifying total neuritic length, number of neuritic branches, and average neuritic length. Sholl analysis was then performed on the tracings, and soma-centered concentric circles were drawn automatically. The dendritic complexity was quantified in terms of the number of intersections, the number of neurite branches, and the total dendritic length.

Nissl staining

The Nissl staining was performed by using a Methylene Blue Stain Kit (G1434, Solarbio Life Sciences, China). Frozen brain (hippocampus) sections in 20 μm were rehydrated and then immersed in methylene blue staining solution for 10 min. After being immersed in the Nissl differentiation solution for 3 s, the sections were rinsed in water and then dehydrated in pure alcohol. A microscope (CX23, Olympus, Japan) was used to obtain images.

Cecal microbiome sequencing and analysis (16S rRNA and shotgun metagenomics)

Cecal contents were collected from risperidone- and vehicle-treated mice in Experiment 1. One aliquot was used for 16S rRNA gene sequencing to characterize overall microbial community structure and diversity. Based on the significant alterations observed, a second aliquot from the same animals was subjected to shotgun metagenomic sequencing to achieve species-level taxonomic resolution and to profile functional gene content.

For 16S profiling, DNA was extracted from cecal contents (200 mg per sample) using the QIAamp DNA Isolation Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) following the manufacturer’s instructions. The V3–V4 variable regions of 16S rRNA genes were amplified by PCR (94 °C for 5 min, followed by 26 cycles at 94 °C for 30 s, 56 °C for 30 s, and 72 °C for 20 s; and a final extension at 72 °C for 5 min) with universal primers 341F: CCTACGGGNGGCWGCAG and 806R: GGACTACHVGGGTATCTAAT. High-throughput sequencing was performed on the Illumina sequencing platform by Genedenovo Biotechnology Co. Ltd. (Guangzhou, China). The sequenced reads were quality-filtered using fastp and merged by FLASH. Operational taxonomic units (OTUs) were clustered based on a 97% similarity cutoff using UPARSE. The variation between the experimental groups (β-diversity) was assessed with principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) plots. Based on the KEGG functional pathway, the predicted functional composition of the gut microbiome was inferred for each sample using PICRUSt2. Statistical analyses were conducted with Statistical Analysis of Metagenomic Profiles (STAMP) software, and functional differences in orthologs among groups were assessed by Kruskal–Wallis tests.

For shotgun metagenomic sequencing, total genomic DNA was extracted from cecal contents. Sequencing libraries were constructed using the NEBNext® Ultra™ DNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina (NEB, USA). Paired-end sequencing was performed on Illumina NovaSeq™ X Plus (Illumina Inc., San Diego, CA, USA) at Majorbio Bio-Pharm Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China), using the NovaSeq X Series 25B Reagent Kit according to the manufacturer’s instructions (www.illumina. com). Raw sequencing reads were quality-filtered and adapter-trimmed using fastp (https://github.com/OpenGene/fastp, version 0.20.0) [74] and aligned using BWA (http://bio-bwa.sourceforge.net, version 0.7.17). A nonredundant gene catalog was constructed using CD-HIT, and gene abundance was estimated with SOAPaligner (https://github.com/ShujiaHuang/SOAPaligner, version soap2.21 release). More details are included in the supplementary information: supplementary methods.

Cell viability assay

Cell viability was detected using Cell Counting Kit-8 (YEASEN). Specifically, cells were seeded on 96-well plates, and the cells were subsequently exposed to MK-801 and p-cresol. To investigate the toxic dose of MK-801 and p-cresol to cells, we treated cells with MK-801 and p-cresol in a concentration gradient (PBS group; DMEM group; MK-801 groups: 0, 5, 10, 15, and 20 μM; p-cresol groups: 0, 50, 100, 200, and 300 μM). Following the treatment, 10 µL of CCK-8 was processed to 37 °C for 24 h and 48 h for each incubation, and the absorbance was measured at 450 nm in a plate reader. Cell viability is expressed as a percentage of the control group.

Cell culture and treatment

Mouse N2A neuroblastoma cells were purchased from American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, Manassas, VA, USA). Cells were supplemented in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) containing 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, CLARK) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin at 37 °C in a humidified 5% CO2 incubator. In brief, to investigate the ER stress signaling pathway, N2A cells were divided into four groups: control group, p-cresol groups, MK-801 groups, and MK-801 + p-cresol groups at 37 °C at regular intervals (0, 24, and 48 h). MK-801 possesses a working concentration of 10 μM in the culture medium, whereas p-cresol exhibits a working concentration of 200 μM in the culture medium. Besides, p-cresol is a volatile phenolic toxin with documented systemic and neurotoxic effects, and its prolonged in vivo administration is technically challenging and raises animal-welfare concerns; therefore, it was investigated only in vitro [75].

Fecal microbiome transplantation experiment

The protocol of the fecal microbiome transplantation experiment was modified from previous studies [76, 77]. Disinfected cages were daily prepared to pool feces from the risperidone treatment group and control group mice, while 100-mg feces (about 5–6 fecal pellets) were then put into new sterilized tubes before the subsequent step for the sake of maintaining the vitality of gut microbiome. The mice were randomly divided into two groups (n = 12 per group): (1) FMT-Con group and (2) FMT-Ris group. Consistent with developmental research standards [12, 78], we avoided antibiotic pretreatment or germ-free conditions to maintain physiologically relevant neurodevelopment [79, 80]. To control for microbial variability while preserving natural developmental trajectories, we implemented rigorous environmental standardization: housing all mice in the same controlled environment and systematically mixing bedding between cages three times weekly to ensure consistent microbial exposure across experimental groups. After the fecal microbiome was colonized in extenso for 30 days, the behavioral tests were determined. More details are included in the supplementary information: supplementary methods.

Widely targeted metabolome (including analysis of TUDCA and p-cresol)

Widely targeted metabolome analysis was conducted to profile brain metabolites, including TUDCA and p-cresol, using an established LC–MS/MS platform at Wuhan Metware Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Wuhan, China). Whole-brain tissue was used for the initial metabolomic analysis to capture the global metabolic impact of risperidone, as gut-derived metabolites like TUDCA and p-cresol circulate systemically and can have broad effects on the central nervous system. Brain tissue samples (100 mg) were homogenized and extracted overnight with 1.0 mL 70% methanol solution at 4 °C. The analysis was performed using an ultra-performance liquid chromatography (UPLC) system (Shim-pack UFLC SHIMADZU CBM30A) coupled with tandem mass spectrometry (MS/MS) (Applied Biosystems 6500 QTRAP, AB Sciex, Boston, MA, USA). Chromatographic separation was achieved on an Agilent SB-C18 column (1.8 µm, 2.1 × 100 mm) with a mobile phase consisting of solvent A (pure water with 0.1% formic acid) and solvent B (acetonitrile with 0.1% formic acid). The gradient program started at 95% A and 5% B, linearly changed to 5% A and 95% B over 9 min, maintained for 1 min, returned to initial conditions within 1.1 min, and re-equilibrated for 2.9 min, with a constant flow rate of 0.35 mL/min at 40 °C column temperature and 4-μL injection volume. Metabolite identification and quantification were performed using a customized multi-species database that incorporated retention time alignment and mass spectral matching. Each metabolite was detected using one primary quantitative ion and 2–3 secondary qualitative ions in selected ion monitoring (SIM) mode to ensure analytical specificity. Rigorous quality control measures were implemented, including verification of retention time consistency with authentic standards when available and background subtraction to eliminate interference. Among the comprehensively profiled metabolites, 28 compounds (including TUDCA and p-cresol) demonstrated statistically significant differences (p < 0.05) between the risperidone-treated and control groups.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) of TUDCA in plasma

Mice plasma samples were collected using anticoagulant-treated tubes (EDTA, heparin, or citrate), gently inverted for mixing, and centrifuged at 2–8 °C for 20 min (2000–3000 rpm) to isolate plasma. Samples were aliquoted and stored at ≤ − 20 °C; any precipitates were recentrifuged before analysis. TUDCA concentration was quantified using a competitive ELISA kit (mmbio). Plasma samples (10 μL) were diluted fivefold by adding 40-μL kit-provided sample dilution buffer in anti-TUDCA antibody-coated wells. Standards (0–400 ng/L) and diluted samples were incubated with HRP-conjugated TUDCA antigen (50 μL/well) at 37 °C for 60 min. After five washes with 30 × diluted wash buffer, 50 μL each of substrates A and B were added and incubated at 37 °C for 15 min in the dark. Reactions were stopped with 50-μL stop solution, and absorbance was measured at 450 nm within 15 min. Sample concentrations were interpolated from the standard curve and multiplied by the fivefold dilution factor to report TUDCA in ng/mL. The assay range was 10–500 ng/L, and NaN₃-containing samples were excluded per kit specifications.

Alcian Blue staining

After the methanol-stored colon samples were embedded in paraffin and cut into Sects. (5 μm), the Alcian Blue staining was performed by the protocols described previously [81]. The paraffin sections were deparaffinized in xylene and hydrated with distilled water. Then, sections were incubated with Alcian Blue solution for 30 min and washed in running tap water for 2 min. After being rinsed in distilled water, sections were dehydrated (2 × changes) and treated with absolute alcohol (2 × changes) for 3 min each. At last, they were cleared in xylene (3 × changes) for 3 min each after which the cover glass was mounted. The thickness of the colonic sections was measured using ImageJ.

Immunohistochemistry

Mice colon tissue Sects. (5 μm thick) mounted on slides were deparaffinized in xylene (2 × 10 min) and rehydrated through a graded ethanol series (100%, 95%, 70%—2 min each) followed by distilled water rinses. Antigen retrieval was performed by heating slides in preheated 10-mM sodium citrate buffer (pH 6.0) at 95–100 °C for 20 min, followed by cooling at room temperature for 30 min and three 5-min washes in Tris-buffered saline (TBS, pH 7.4). Sections were permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 in TBS for 10 min, washed three times in TBS, and then encircled with a hydrophobic barrier. Nonspecific binding was blocked with 5% normal goat serum in TBS for 1 h at room temperature in a humidified chamber. Primary antibody incubation used rabbit anti-MUC2 (Abclonal, A14659) diluted 1:500 in TBS containing 1% bovine serum albumin (BSA) and 0.025% Triton X-100 (TBST), applied overnight at 4 °C. After three 10-min TBST washes, sections were incubated with goat anti-rabbit IgG Alexa Fluor 488 secondary antibody (Invitrogen, A32731) diluted 1:1000 in 1% BSA/TBST for 1 h at room temperature protected from light, followed by three 10-min TBST washes. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI (1 μg/mL in TBS) for 10 min at room temperature, rinsed three times in TBS (5 min each), and mounted with ProLong Diamond Antifade Mountant, curing overnight while protected from light. Imaging was performed using an Olympus inverted epifluorescence microscope with consistent exposure settings. Fluorescence images were acquired using Olympus microscope in ImageJ (Fiji) by thresholding MUC2-positive areas and normalizing to the DAPI signal or tissue area. Appropriate controls (no-primary antibody, isotype control) were included in all experiments.

Western blotting

The mouse hippocampus was homogenized in ice-cold RIPA lysis buffer, supplemented with a complete EDTA-free protease inhibitor cocktail and PhosSTOP phosphatase inhibitor. The supernatant was collected, and a BCA assay was used to quantify the protein concentration. Equal amounts of protein were separated by sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) and transferred onto polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membranes. The membrane was blocked with 5% nonfat milk at room temperature for 1 h and then incubated overnight at 4 °C with different primary antibodies. After washing three times with TBST, the protein bands were detected with Clarity™ ECL Western blot substrate (Bio-Rad, 1,705,060) and visualized using the ChemiDoc Touch Imaging System (Bio-Rad). The expression of the protein in each sample was normalized to β-actin.

RNA extraction and quantitative (q) real‑time polymerase chain reaction (qPCR)

RNA extraction and qPCR were performed based on methods previously described [42]. In short, total RNA was extracted with TRIzol (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) from the hippocampus and colon. RNA purity was determined by the absorption ratios (260/280 nm), which were 1.8–2.0 for all samples. One-microgram purified RNA was used to generate cDNA with a High-Capacity cDNA Reverse Transcription Kit (Takara, Dalian, China) and qPCR were performed using the SYBR Green Master Mix (TaKaRa, Japan) and determined on a real-time PCR detection system (Bio-Rad, USA). The CT value of each reaction was provided, and the changes in the transcriptional level of the target gene normalized to β-actin were calculated by the following formula: relative mRNA level of target gene (folds of control) = 2−ΔΔCT.

Lipopolysaccharide determination

LPS levels in sera were detected using a chromogenic endpoint TAL kit (Xiamen Bioendo Technology Co., Ltd., Xiamen, China) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The absorbance was determined at a wavelength of 545 nm using a spectrophotometer (Asuragen ClinBio128, USA). All samples for LPS measurements were performed in duplicate.

Establishment of stable cell lines

PTP1B lentivirus was purchased from Heyuan Biotechnology (Shanghai) Co., Ltd. (Ptpn1 gene 19246, vector number: GL180, vector name: pcSLenti-EF1-EGFP-P2A-Puro-CMV-MCS-3xFLAG-WPRE, and post-constructed name: pcSLenti-EF1-EGFP-P2A-Puro-CMV-Ptpn1-3xFLAG-WPRE; control number: GL180, control name: pcSLenti-EF1-EGFP-P2A-Puro-CMV-MCS-3xFLAG-WPRE). Stable cell lines were generated by infection with lentivirus and selected with 2 mg/ml puromycin (Vicmed, Xuzhou, Jiangsu, China) for approximately 4 weeks.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using the statistical package SPSS (Version 20, Chicago, USA). Two-way ANOVA was used to analyze the effects of two factors, including risperidone, gender, and their interaction. Three-way ANOVA was used to analyze the effects of three factors, including risperidone, gender, PTP1B, and their interaction. After data were tested for normality, the Student’s t-test and Mann–Whitney U-test were used to compare the two groups. For the comparisons of more than two groups, a one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used, followed by the post hoc Tukey–Kramer test for multiple comparisons. A P-value of < 0.05 was considered to be statistically significant. An α level of 0.05 (two-tailed) was set for significance. The value was considered an outlier if it had more than two standard deviations from the mean.
