Determinants of nursing care rationing among nurses employed in surgical and conservative hospital wards
Authors: Stanisław Manulik, Michał Czapla, Ercole Vellone, Heba Aldossary, Raúl Juárez-Vela, Antonio Martínez-Sabater, Izabella Uchmanowicz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimTo identify factors associated with nursing care rationing among nurses employed in surgical and conservative hospital wards.Materials and methodsThis cross-sectional study included 383 nurses employed in surgical (n = 161) and conservative (n = 222) hospital wards. Nursing care rationing was assessed using the Basel Extent of Rationing of Nursing Care-Revised (BERNCA-R), whereas life satisfaction and dispositional optimism were evaluated using the Satisfaction with Life Scale (SWLS) and the Life Orientation Test-Revised (LOT-R), respectively. Group differences were analysed using non-parametric tests, and factors associated with nursing care rationing were identified using univariate and multivariate linear regression analyses.ResultsThe mean nursing care rationing score in the whole group was 1.31 (SD = 0.90), whereas mean life satisfaction and dispositional optimism scores were 21.30 (SD = 6.57) and 14.85 (SD = 3.93), respectively. No significant differences between surgical and conservative ward nurses were observed in sociodemographic characteristics (all p > 0.05). Shift work was independently associated with higher levels of nursing care rationing in both surgical (β = 0.364; p = 0.022) and conservative wards (β = 0.290; p = 0.035). In conservative wards, completion of specialist courses was independently associated with lower levels of care rationing (β = −0.272; p = 0.039).ConclusionsOrganisational determinants appear to be more strongly associated with nursing care rationing than individual psychological resources. Shift work was the factor most strongly associated with missed nursing care irrespective of ward profile, whereas completion of specialist courses was associated with lower levels of care rationing in conservative wards.Keywords: Nursing, Care rationing, Life satisfaction, Dispositional optimism, BERNCA-R, SWLS, LOT-R, Surgical ward, Conservative ward

Introduction

Nursing workforce shortages and increasing clinical demands have intensified pressure on hospital nursing systems worldwide [1]. High workload, staff shortages, and shift work contribute to occupational strain and may compromise care quality and completeness [2–4]. These challenges have become particularly important in the post-pandemic period because healthcare systems continue to face substantial staffing deficits [5–7].

Nursing care rationing refers to the omission or delay of necessary nursing activities because of insufficient organisational resources, including limited staffing and time constraints [8]. Large international studies, including RN4CAST, have shown that higher levels of missed nursing care are associated with adverse patient outcomes, including medication errors, pressure ulcers, falls, and lower patient satisfaction [9]. Previous Polish studies have linked nursing care rationing with occupational burnout, job satisfaction, and organisational factors [10–12]. This phenomenon is increasingly recognised as a marker of healthcare system performance and patient safety [13]. Previous studies have shown that omitted nursing care may contribute to delayed clinical response, reduced continuity of care, and deterioration in patient outcomes, particularly in settings with persistent workforce shortages and high clinical burden [8, 14–16].

Positive psychosocial resources, including life satisfaction and dispositional optimism, may reduce the negative effects of occupational stress among nurses. From a theoretical perspective, these psychological resources may influence nurses’ ability to cope with high workload, prioritise competing clinical demands, and maintain work engagement under conditions of organisational stress, thereby potentially reducing the likelihood of omitting or delaying essential nursing activities [17–19]. Higher levels of optimism and psychological well-being have been associated with lower burnout and reduced care rationing [17–19]. Nevertheless, evidence regarding the independent contribution of psychosocial resources to nursing care rationing remains inconsistent. Some studies suggest that organisational determinants, including staffing levels and work organisation, may have a greater impact on missed nursing care than individual psychological characteristics [20–22].

The organisational context of nursing practice may substantially influence the risk of nursing care rationing. Although previous studies have identified multiple organisational and individual determinants of nursing care rationing, most investigations have analysed heterogeneous hospital populations without examining whether these determinants differ according to ward profile. Surgical and conservative wards represent substantially different clinical environments with respect to patient turnover, procedural complexity, workload distribution, urgency of care, and continuity of nursing interventions. Consequently, it cannot be assumed that factors associated with nursing care rationing are identical across these settings. Clarifying these differences may support the development of ward-specific organisational strategies to reduce missed nursing care, improve patient safety, and optimise workforce organisation.

This issue is particularly relevant in healthcare systems facing persistent nursing shortages, ageing workforces, and increasing complexity of inpatient care. Against this background, this study was based on the assumption that both organisational and psychosocial factors may be associated with nursing care rationing, although their relative importance may differ between surgical and conservative hospital wards. Therefore, the aim of this study was to identify sociodemographic, organisational, and psychosocial factors associated with nursing care rationing among nurses employed in surgical and conservative hospital wards.

Materials and methods

Study design and participants

This cross-sectional exploratory study included 383 nurses employed in surgical (n = 161) and conservative (n = 222) hospital wards in Lower Silesia, Poland. Data collection was conducted between October, 2024 and June, 2025. Participants were recruited using a convenience sampling strategy through hospital-based professional networks and institutional communication channels. The inclusion criteria were current employment in a surgical or conservative ward, at least 1 year of professional experience, provision of informed consent, and completion of the study questionnaire. The exclusion criterion was employment in hospital wards other than surgical or conservative wards. Conservative wards included internal medicine, cardiology, pulmonology, and gastroenterology units, whereas surgical wards included general surgery and orthopaedic units.

Data collection was performed using an anonymous online survey distributed among nurses working in hospital settings. Participants received access to the electronic questionnaire via an individual survey link. Before completing the questionnaire, participants received information regarding the study objectives and study procedures. No personally identifiable information was collected, participation was voluntary, and participants could withdraw from the study at any stage without consequences. Only fully completed questionnaires were included in the final analysis.

The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the Bioethics Committee of Wrocław Medical University, Poland (approval no. KB-647/2024). The study was reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines.

Instruments

The Basel Extent of Rationing of Nursing Care-Revised (BERNCA-R), developed by Schubert et al. [8] and adapted into Polish by Uchmanowicz et al. [23], was used to assess the frequency of omitted or delayed nursing activities resulting from limited organisational resources, including insufficient staffing, time constraints, and lack of equipment. The instrument consists of 32 items rated on a 5-point Likert scale (0–4). The final score is calculated as the mean item value, with higher scores indicating greater intensity of nursing care rationing.

Life satisfaction was assessed using the Satisfaction with Life Scale (SWLS) developed by Diener et al. [24] and adapted into Polish by Juczyński [25]. The scale consists of five items rated on a 7-point Likert scale, with total scores ranging from 5 to 35 points. Higher scores indicate greater life satisfaction.

Dispositional optimism was assessed using the Life Orientation Test-Revised (LOT-R) developed by Scheier, Carver, and Bridges [26] and adapted into Polish by Juczyński [25]. The questionnaire includes six scored items and four filler items rated on a 5-point Likert scale (0–4), with total scores ranging from 0 to 24 points. Higher scores reflect greater dispositional optimism.

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables were compared between groups (surgical vs. conservative wards) using the chi-squared test (with Yates correction for 2 × 2 tables) or Fisher’s exact test, when the chi-squared assumptions regarding expected counts were not satisfied. Quantitative variables in two groups were compared with the Mann-Whitney test. The univariate and multivariate associations between potential explanatory variables and the BERNCA-R score were analysed using linear regression; results are presented as regression coefficients with 95% confidence intervals. For the multivariate analysis, variables were selected that, in the univariate analyses, showed a significant or near-significant effect on the dependent variable, in such a way that the SPV (subjects per variable) index was greater than 15. This approach was adopted to obtain parsimonious exploratory models while minimising the risk of model overfitting through an adequate subjects-per-variable ratio. The significance level was set at α = 0.05; all p values below 0.05 were interpreted as indicating significant relationships. Analyses were performed in R, version 4.5.3 (R Core Team, 2026).

Results

Sociodemographic characteristics of the sample

The analysis included a total of 383 individuals: 161 nurses employed in surgical wards and 222 in conservative wards. The group was strongly feminised (96.61% women overall), predominantly aged 41 to 60 years (65.01%), and in stable relationships (79.90%). Most respondents held a Bachelor’s degree in nursing or midwifery (41.78%) or a Master’s degree (40.73%). Specialisation was the most frequently reported form of postgraduate education (63.45%). Two thirds of the group (65.54%) worked in a shift system; more than half declared dissatisfaction with their remuneration (53.00%). Comparison of the two ward groups did not reveal statistically significant differences in any of the studied sociodemographic variables (all p values > 0.05). Descriptive statistics and reliability of instruments

In the whole group, the mean BERNCA-R score was M = 1.31 (SD = 0.90; median 1.09; range 0 to 4), placing the group in the moderate care-rationing range. The mean SWLS score was M = 21.30 (SD = 6.57; median 22), placing the group in the “above average” range according to the interpretation of Pavot and Diener (1993). The mean LOT-R score was M = 14.85 (SD = 3.93; median 15), indicating moderately high optimism. Cronbach’s alpha values for BERNCA-R and SWLS were very high (0.976 and 0.924, respectively), while for LOT-R it was acceptable (0.633), corresponding to typical values for 6-item scales with a complex measurement structure. Univariate factors associated with care rationing

In both ward types, a series of univariate linear regression models was performed, with the BERNCA-R score as the dependent variable and all analysed sociodemographic and organisational variables, as well as SWLS and LOT-R scores, as explanatory variables (Table 3). In surgical wards, among all the variables considered, only shift work was significantly associated with BERNCA-R. Working in a shift system increased the frequency of care rationing on average by 0.404 points (95% CI: 0.105 to 0.703; p = 0.008) compared with single-shift work. The remaining variables (age, education, place of residence, marital status, number of workplaces, patients per shift, professional experience, working hours per month, form of employment, satisfaction with remuneration, SWLS, and LOT-R) showed no significant association with BERNCA-R. In conservative wards, two variables were significantly associated with BERNCA-R. Completion of a specialist course was associated with a 0.279-point lower frequency of care rationing (95% CI: −0.512 to − 0.046; p = 0.019), whereas shift work was associated with a 0.357-point higher frequency of care rationing (95% CI: 0.096 to 0.618; p = 0.008) compared with single-shift work.

Multivariate factors associated with rationing

In surgical wards, variables with p < 0.08 in the univariate analyses were included in the multivariate analysis; the resulting SPV index was 26.8 (above the threshold of 15), confirming sufficient analytical power. The multivariate linear regression model showed that shift work remained independently associated with BERNCA-R, increasing the frequency of care rationing on average by 0.364 points (95% CI: 0.053 to 0.676; p = 0.022) compared with single-shift work. In conservative wards, variables with p < 0.15 in the univariate analyses were included in the multivariate analysis; the resulting SPV index was 17.1 (above the threshold of 15). The multivariate linear regression model revealed two variables independently associated with BERNCA-R: completion of a specialist course, decreasing the frequency of care rationing on average by 0.272 points (95% CI: −0.530 to − 0.014; p = 0.039), and shift work, increasing the frequency on average by 0.290 points (95% CI: 0.021 to 0.558; p = 0.035) compared with single-shift work. Discussion

The present study identified organisational characteristics of nursing work as the factors most strongly associated with nursing care rationing among nurses employed in surgical and conservative hospital wards. Shift work was consistently associated with higher levels of missed nursing care across both ward profiles, whereas completion of specialist courses was associated with lower levels of nursing care rationing in conservative wards. Although the observed regression coefficients were of moderate magnitude, the consistency of these associations across ward profiles suggests that relatively small organisational changes may have meaningful implications for nursing care delivery at the population level. In contrast, psychosocial variables, including life satisfaction and dispositional optimism, were not independently associated with care rationing after adjustment for organisational factors. These findings suggest that structural and work-related determinants may be more strongly associated with nursing care than individual psychological resources, particularly in healthcare systems affected by persistent staffing shortages and increasing clinical demands.

In the present study, shift work was independently associated with higher levels of nursing care rationing in both surgical and conservative wards, suggesting that organisational workload and temporal characteristics of nursing schedules may substantially associated with the completion of essential nursing activities. Similar observations were reported in large RN4CAST analyses, where the frequency of missed nursing care significantly differed across morning, afternoon, and night shifts, particularly regarding fundamental and relational nursing activities [27]. Furthermore, evidence indicates that prolonged working hours and overtime contribute directly to increased missed nursing care, lower quality of care, and poorer nursing practice environments, likely through mechanisms related to fatigue, cognitive overload, and reduced recovery time between shifts [28]. These findings are additionally supported by scoping review data demonstrating that workload, staffing inadequacy, night shifts, and emergency clinical demands represent some of the most consistent determinants of missed nursing care internationally [29]. From a mechanistic perspective, shift work may impair clinical prioritisation, concentration, and task continuity due to circadian rhythm disruption and accumulated occupational fatigue, particularly in settings characterised by chronic staff shortages and high patient acuity [30–32]. Therefore, the observed association between shift work and care rationing in our study appears to reflect predominantly organisational rather than individual-level determinants of nursing performance.

The observed relationship between shift work and nursing care rationing may additionally be explained by fatigue-related cognitive impairment associated with prolonged working hours and insufficient recovery between shifts. Previous studies have demonstrated that extended working hours, overtime, and high workload are associated with increased risk of adverse events, medication errors, and reduced patient safety [33, 34]. Long and irregular shifts may impair nurses’ attention, vigilance, and decision-making capacity, particularly in high-demand clinical environments [32]. Evidence from ICU settings further suggests that circadian rhythm disruption and night-shift work negatively affect cognitive performance, including working memory and attention processes essential for safe nursing practice [35]. Moreover, recent post-pandemic studies indicate that insufficient inter-shift recovery and occupational fatigue are significantly associated with higher levels of missed nursing care [36]. Collectively, these findings support the assumption that both organisational workload and fatigue-related cognitive impairment may contribute to nursing care rationing, particularly in healthcare systems affected by persistent staffing shortages and increasing clinical burden.

Although previous studies suggested that optimism, resilience, and psychological well-being may protect against burnout, productivity loss, and nursing care rationing, our findings indicate that these factors were not independent determinants after adjustment for organisational and workload-related variables. Jaworski et al. demonstrated that optimistic thinking and greater job and life satisfaction were associated with lower levels of implicit nursing care rationing among Polish nurses, suggesting that positive psychological resources may improve coping capacity and work engagement [37]. Similarly, Hussein et al. reported that poor psychological well-being among nurses was associated with substantial productivity loss, presenteeism, absenteeism, and impaired daily functioning, all of which may negatively affect quality and continuity of care [38]. However, our findings support the growing body of evidence indicating that organisational determinants may be more strongly associated with nursing care rationing than individual psychological characteristics alone. Milecka et al. identified staffing shortages, workload, shift work, and burnout as major factors associated with nursing care rationing, demonstrating that increased patient load substantially intensified the frequency of omitted nursing activities [39]. Likewise, Innab et al. showed that poor patient safety culture was associated with lower resilience and higher turnover intention among nurses, emphasizing the central role of organisational climate in maintaining workforce stability and care quality [40]. Taken together, these findings suggest that individual psychological resources may provide only a partial buffering effect against adverse workplace conditions. In environments characterized by chronic understaffing, excessive workload, and organisational strain, even highly resilient or optimistic nurses may be unable to maintain complete nursing care delivery. Therefore, interventions focused exclusively on improving individual resilience are unlikely to substantially reduce nursing care rationing without simultaneous organisational-level improvements in staffing adequacy, workload distribution, and institutional support systems. The absence of statistically significant associations between dispositional optimism and nursing care rationing should also be interpreted with caution. Although the internal consistency of the LOT-R was within the range commonly reported for this instrument, it was lower than that observed for the other study measures (Cronbach’s α = 0.633), which may have reduced the ability to detect weaker associations.

These findings have important implications for healthcare systems and workforce policy. Evidence from RN4CAST and subsequent longitudinal studies consistently demonstrates that inadequate nurse staffing is associated with increased mortality, adverse events, and poorer patient safety outcomes [27]. Furthermore, recent evidence indicates that rationing and omission of nursing care are strongly linked with medication errors, falls, infections, pressure ulcers, and hospital readmissions [13]. Our findings therefore support the view that missed nursing care should be considered a systemic and organisational problem rather than an individual failure of nurses. Organisational interventions focused on adequate staffing, supportive work environments, leadership engagement, and postgraduate education may represent modifiable determinants of patient safety and workforce sustainability [13, 41]. Importantly, interventions focused exclusively on individual resilience are unlikely to be sufficient without concurrent structural improvements in staffing adequacy and work organisation. Recent qualitative evidence additionally highlights the importance of institutional support, education, and leadership in promoting sustainable nursing practice and long-term workforce retention [42].

Study limitation

The present study has several limitations. First, its cross-sectional design precludes causal inference; therefore, the observed regression associations may be bidirectional. For example, higher levels of nursing care rationing may negatively influence nurses’ perceptions of working conditions and indirectly affect their assessment of the work system. Second, the study relied on self-reported data, which may be subject to recall and response biases, including social desirability bias. Furthermore, the exclusive use of self-reported questionnaires may have increased the risk of common method bias. Third, the classification into surgical and conservative wards, although clinically justified, remains relatively broad. Each category included wards with substantially different clinical profiles and organisational characteristics (e.g. general surgery, orthopaedics, and gynaecology within surgical wards; internal medicine, cardiology, and neurology within conservative wards), which may have introduced residual heterogeneity. Nevertheless, the relatively large sample size and the application of multivariate regression models with statistical control based on the events-per-variable (EPV/SPV) approach strengthen the robustness and reliability of the findings at the level of both ward categories. Additionally, the use of convenience sampling may have introduced selection bias and limits the generalisability of the findings beyond the participating hospitals. Furthermore, the regression models explained only a limited proportion of the variability in nursing care rationing, suggesting that additional organisational and individual factors not assessed in the present study may also contribute to this complex phenomenon. Finally, because multiple statistical comparisons were performed, the possibility of type I error cannot be completely excluded despite the use of multivariable modelling.

Conclusions

Organisational characteristics of nursing work appear to be the factors most strongly associated with nursing care rationing among nurses employed in surgical and conservative hospital wards. Shift work was consistently associated with higher levels of missed nursing care irrespective of ward profile, suggesting that work organisation and scheduling may be associated with the completeness of nursing care delivery. In conservative wards, completion of specialist courses was associated with lower levels of nursing care rationing, indicating that postgraduate education may be associated with lower levels of omitted nursing activities.

Life satisfaction and dispositional optimism were not independently associated with nursing care rationing after adjustment for organisational factors, suggesting that structural and work-related determinants may be more strongly associated with missed nursing care than individual psychological resources alone.

Overall, nursing care rationing should be considered primarily a systemic and organisational challenge related to staffing adequacy, work organisation, and working conditions. Optimisation of shift-work organisation, improvement of staffing conditions, and strengthening postgraduate nursing education may represent important strategies for reducing missed nursing care and improving patient safety in hospital settings.
