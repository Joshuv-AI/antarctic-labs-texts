Dysbiosis of gut microbiota is associated with pathogenesis of peptic ulcer diseases through inflammatory proteins: A Mendelian randomization study
Authors: Jianing Li, Hongbo Cai, Yongsheng Zhang, Jingyi Li, Dianxiang Wang, Haolin Li, Hongyu Cai, Qiuqiu Wang, Tong Fu, Zhonglin Shao, Huili Huang, Haiqiang Wang, Ying Tong
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
The gut microbiota and inflammatory proteins may affect the development of peptic ulcer disease. However, this association remains unclear. We analyzed genome-wide association study data of gut microbiota, inflammatory proteins, and peptic ulcer disease using Mendelian randomization with instrumental variables to assess causal relationships. Various statistical methods, including inverse variance weighting, Mendelian randomization Egger regression, and sensitivity analysis were employed to evaluate the data and calculate mediation ratios. Our findings reveal that the genus Butyriciccus plays a role in mitigating the adverse effects of gastric ulcers by 7.9%, primarily through reducing beta-negative growth factor levels. Additionally, the genus Lachnospiraceae UCG004 can significantly alleviate the negative outcomes of gastric ulcers and reduces hepatocyte growth factor and beta-reserve growth factor levels by 6.39% and 7.45%, respectively. This study highlights the independent and mediating effects of the gut microbiota and inflammatory proteins on peptic ulcers, offering insights on potential pathways and targets for future preventive interventions.

1. Introduction

Peptic ulcer diseases (PUD) are prevalent digestive disorders that affect approximately 4 million individuals globally annually, negatively affecting their quality of life with pain and indigestion. Additionally, PUD may cause severe complications, including bleeding and perforation, which may be fatal. Statistical data reveal a gradual rise in the mortality rate of PUD over the past 15 years.[1,2] Consequently, healthcare providers and the public should focus on preventing and controlling PUD. Recent epidemiological studies report that the risk of developing PUD is associated with diverse genetic, lifestyle, and environmental factors. For example, a comprehensive study involving European and Asian populations identified 25 new genetic markers that are linked to peptic ulcers, suggesting that gastric and duodenal ulcers may share certain genetic risk factors.[3] Nevertheless, these factors do not completely explain the etiology of PUD. The complex interactions among these factors further complicate the causal relationships between them and the PUD risks.

The gut microbiota is a fundamental component of human physiology and regulates the overall health of the host.[4] In PUD, dysbiosis of the gut microbiota may influence PUD’s progression and treatment outcomes. Notably, dysbiosis could disrupt the intestinal mucosal barrier, increasing susceptibility to invasion by pathogenic bacteria and potentially triggering or exacerbating PUD. Intriguingly, intestinal flora may be beneficial to PUD treatment by generating effective metabolites, such as short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs). SCFAs maintain intestinal mucosal health and inhibit inflammation.[5] Therefore, studying the intestinal flora that are associated with PUD development aids early prevention or treatment and enhances our understanding of their interrelationships. However, previous studies have been generally limited by inadequate statistical robustness due to small study cohorts, inadequate mechanistic findings, and other biases inherent to the conventional epidemiological investigations.

Mendelian randomization (MR) is a robust epidemiological and genetics method used to explore the causal relationships between exposures and outcomes. Instrumental variables (IVs) offer a mechanism for delineating causal relationships by leveraging randomly assigned genetic variants, which are typically unaffected by environmental or lifestyle factors. These characteristics provide more dependable evidence for causal inference than those achieved by conventional observational studies. By using genetic variation as an IV, MR can infer causal relationships between the gut microbiota, inflammatory proteins, and PUD. MR may overcome confounding and reverse-causality biases often generated by observational studies, thereby producing stronger and more reliable evidence for understanding how the gut microbiota and inflammatory proteins may interact to affect the genetics and pathogenic mechanisms that underlie PUD. This approach enhances the disease risk assessment and facilitates the development of more effective prevention strategies.

Studies using MR have linked the gut microbiota, with genetically predictive characteristics, to a potential risk of PUD.[6] However, many MR studies focused on a restricted number of intestinal floral species and failed to clarify the mechanisms by which gut microbiota influences PUD. The gut microbiota importantly regulates and modulates the release of some inflammatory factors by producing SCFAs and tryptophan metabolites. SCFAs and tryptophan metabolites potentially modulate the gut barrier function, blood–brain barrier permeability, and neuroinflammatory responses. Moreover, the intestinal barrier integrity can be affected by the gut microbiota, consequently facilitating invasion by pathogenic bacteria and release of inflammatory factors. Finally, intestinal dysbiosis also may cause abnormal release of inflammatory mediators, thereby disturbing the overall inflammatory state and immune regulation.[6]

Inflammation contributes significantly to the development of PUD. PUD commonly arises due to gastric mucosal damage or erosion caused by gastric acid and is exacerbated by the release of inflammatory factors.[7] For example, interleukin-6 (IL-6), interleukin-1β (IL-1β), and tumor necrosis factor-α may promote the formation and progression of PUD. These inflammatory cytokines can impair the mucosal barrier function, stimulate ulcer formation, and impede ulcers’ healing.[8] We hypothesize that inflammatory cytokines may link the gut microbiota to the onset of PUD.

Future research may elucidate the multifaceted etiology of PUD, therapeutic modalities, and pathological complexities, thereby facilitating the discovery of novel interventions and preventive strategies for alleviating patients’ suffering. Understanding the potential contributions of intestinal microbiota and proinflammatory proteins in maintaining or disturbing homeostasis and mitigating or promoting the incidence or progression of PUD and other related ulcers, including duodenal ulcer (DU), gastric ulcer (GU), gastroduodenal ulcer (GASTRODU), esophageal ulcer (OESU), and gastrojejunal ulcer (GJU), is imperative.

2. Methods

2.1. Exposure data

The genome-wide association study (GWAS) of the gut microbiota comprised sequencing of the 16S ribosomal RNA and genotyping of 18,340 participants across 24 cohorts. The cohorts represented diverse countries and ethnicities and were strategically studied to obtain overarching data about the influence of host genetic variations on the composition of the gut microbiota.[9] Comprehensive GWAS data on inflammatory proteins and the corresponding statistics were available online through the University of Cambridge resources, individual genetic and proteomic datasets were retrieved through the European Genome Phenotype Archive, and gene-expression data on inflammation-related diseases were accessed through the GEO database. Additionally, the eQTLGen consortium provides statistical analyses of the cis-expression quantitative trait loci of the whole blood, and the eQTL catalogue provides credible and targeted eQTL sets. Integration and analysis of these datasets potentially unveils the genetic underpinnings between the inflammation-associated proteins and their potential implications in immune-mediated disorders.[10]

2.2. Outcome data

GWAS summary statistics for the 5 types of peptic ulcers were sourced from the FinnGen consortium R10 release, accessible at https://www.finngen.fi/fi. The FinnGen consortium, a significant public–private collaboration, is dedicated to aggregating and examining the genomic and health information of 500,000 participants in the Finnish biobank.[11] The dataset which was used for analysis encompasses information up to December 2022. The specific peptic ulcer conditions that were investigated included OESU, GU, GASTRODU, DU, and GJU.

2.3. Instrumental variables

Selecting IVs is a pivotal component of MR studies. By choosing IVs that are strongly linked to an exposure of interest but not to potential confounding variables, researchers can precisely estimate the causal effect of the exposure on an outcome. Simultaneously, selecting IVs that change outcomes solely through exposure can remove bias when estimating causal effects. We identified the single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) associated with inflammatory proteins at the genome-wide significance threshold of P < 5.0 × 10−6 as potential IVs. To investigate a more comprehensive causal relationship between intestinal microbiota and PUD, we used a higher threshold (P < 1.0 × 10−5) to capture additional gut microbiota IVs.[12]

Linkage disequilibrium (LD), which refers to the genetic association between different loci, can introduce bias or inaccuracies in MR analyses. Clumping procedures are frequently used to assess LD between IVs and mitigate biased effect estimations resulting from genetic linkages. The LD threshold for clumping is typically set at r2 < 0.001 with a window size of 10,000 kb.

The validity of MR analysis hinges on 2 key assumptions: the absence of genetic variants linked to potential confounding factors and the absence of horizontal pleiotropy. To mitigate these concerns, the MR-PRESSO and MR-Egger regression tests were used. The MR-PRESSO outlier test and global test were repeated until the P-value was no longer significant. The F-statistics serves as a common metric for evaluating the strength of the association between instrumental variables and exposures. A high F-statistics indicates a robust instrument. IVs are considered strong when the F-statistics exceeds 10.[6,13] The remaining SNPs were aggregated into the GWAS database for outcome evaluation, with each SNP scrutinized for potential violations of assumptions 1 and 2 using PhenoScanner (www.phenoscanner.medschl.cam.ac.uk).[14]

2.4. MR estimates

Five methods were used to identify causal associations, including the inverse variance weighting (IVW), MR-Egger regression, simple mode, weighted mode, and weighted median methods. The IVW method, commonly used for evaluating MR estimates assuming balanced pleiotropy, was applied.[15] Relevant SNPs and outliers were identified from the dataset, and the primary analysis used the multiplicative random-effects IVW model to tackle the heterogeneity bias.[16] MR-Egger and weighted median approaches were used to enhance the accuracy of IVW estimates, ensuring robust estimations across diverse scenarios. MR-Egger permits pleiotropic effects across all genetic variants but requires independence of effects from the variant–exposure association.[16] The weighted median method allows for the inclusion of invalid instruments, assuming at least 50% of the IVs used in the analysis are valid. The IVW approach is commonly used as the main reliability indicator in MR analyses. A favorable conclusion is typically determined using a significance criterion of P < 0.05. In cases of significant inconsistencies among IVW estimates, a stricter threshold for the P-value of IVs was applied.[17]

2.5. Sensitivity analyses

We used the Cochran Q test to evaluate heterogeneity in investigations, with significance level set at P < .05, indicating heterogeneity. The presence of pleiotropy, suggesting that IV influenced the outcome through factors unrelated to the exposure variables, could compromise the independence and exclusivity of outcomes. To assess pleiotropy and ensure reliability, the MR-Egger intercept test was used. A P-value < .05 indicated the presence of pleiotropy in the data. Sensitivity was analyzed by using the “leave-one-out” method, which involved removing outcomes associated with specific SNPs. The MR-PRESSO test was used to remove the variant in question. We used the R programming language to generate a funnel plot for assessing SNP symmetry and reliability. The research flowchart is depicted in Figure 1. We used the TwoSampleMR (version 0.4.25) and MR-PRESSO (version 1.0) packages available in R (version 4.3.2) to perform MR analysis.[18–20] The study design is illustrated in Figure 1.

3. Results

In total, 2559 SNPs were utilized as IVs for 196 known gut microbiota (see Table S1, Supplemental Digital Content, http://links.lww.com/MD/N627 which illustrates the SNPs associated with gut microbiota). Subsequently, 1817 SNPs were linked to 93 inflammatory protein expression (see Table S2, Supplemental Digital Content, http://links.lww.com/MD/N627 which illustrates the SNPs associated with inflammatory proteins). Thousand eight hundred ninety one, 1492, 1382, 1247, 1620, and 1875 SNPs were used as IVs for PUD, DU, GU, GASTRODU, OESU, and GJU, respectively (see Tables S3–S8, Supplemental Digital Content, http://links.lww.com/MD/N627 which illustrates the SNPs associated with PUD, DU, GU, GASTRODU, OESU, and GJU). The F-statistics for all SNPs were >10, suggesting minimal susceptibility to weak instrumental variable bias in the estimates.

3.1. Causal association of gut microbiota with PUD

Fourteen microbial taxa were shown to be associated with PUD, of which 5 were correlated with an elevated risk of PUD. These included the genera Intestinibacter (OR = 2.01, PIVW = .0459), Coprococcus1 (OR = 2.96, PIVW = .0475), and Holdemanella (OR = 2.17, PIVW = .0094); and the Prevotellaceae (OR = 2.24, PIVW = .0315) and Alcaligenaceae (OR = 3.16, PIVW = .0216) families. Nine gut microbiota were associated with a decreased risk of PUD, including the genera Parabacteroides (OR = 0.22, PIVW = .0270), Odoribacter (OR = 0.29, PIVW = .0281), Family XIII AD3011 group (OR = 0.43, PIVW = .0448), Anaerofilum (OR = 0.54, PIVW = .0316), and Howardella (OR = 0.63, PIVW = .0428); the Actinomycetaceae (OR = 0.30, PIVW = .0176) family; the orders Actinomycetales (OR = 0.30, PIVW = .0177) and Bacteroidales (OR = 0.42, PIVW = .0452); and the class Bacteroidia (OR = 0.42, PIVW = .0452) (Fig. 2).

3.2. Causal association of inflammatory proteins with PUD

Four inflammatory proteins were shown to be associated with PUD, with interleukin-20 receptor subunit alpha (OR = 2.65, PIVW = .0445) and interleukin-7 levels (OR = 2.35, PIVW = .0481) being correlated with an increased risk of PUD. Conversely, interleukin-18 (OR = 0.58, PIVW = .0178) and CUB domain-containing protein 1 levels (OR = 0.62, PIVW = .0248) were linked to a reduced risk of PUD.

Four inflammatory proteins were shown to be associated with DU. Eukaryotic translation initiation factor 4E-binding protein 1 (OR = 1.30, PIVW = .0204) levels were associated with a decreased risk of DU. Conversely, sulfotransferase 1A1 (OR = 0.81, PIVW = .0002), C-X-C motif chemokine 10 (OR = 0.82, PIVW = .0207) and tumor necrosis factor receptor superfamily member 9 (OR = 0.86, PIVW = .0134) levels were associated with an increased risk of DU.

Six inflammatory proteins were identified as having associations with GU. Notably, beta-nerve growth factor (β-NGF) levels (OR = 1.15, PIVW = .0499), HGF levels (OR = 1.16, PIVW = .0472), Eukaryotic translation initiation factor 4E-binding protein 1 levels (OR = 1.21, PIVW = .0212) and artemin levels (OR = 1.22, PIVW = .0029) were linked to an increased risk of GU. Conversely, Interleukin-20 levels (OR = 0.79, PIVW = .0063) and C-C motif chemokine 23 levels (OR = 0.92, PIVW = .0425) were associated with a decreased risk of GU.

Six inflammatory proteins were found to be associated with GASTRODU. Specifically, tumor necrosis factor ligand superfamily member 14 (OR = 1.08, PIVW = .0414), artemin (OR = 1.18, PIVW = .0080), and eukaryotic translation initiation factor 4E-binding protein 1 (OR = 1.22, PIVW = .0109) levels were associated with an increased risk of GASTRODU. Conversely, interleukin-20 (OR = 0.84, PIVW = .0155), neurotrophin-3 (OR = 0.85, PIVW = .0383), and adenosine deaminase (OR = 0.94, PIVW = .0288) levels were correlated with a decreased risk of GASTRODU.

Four inflammatory proteins were identified as being associated with OESU. Notably, leukemia inhibitory factor receptor (OR = 1.19, PIVW = .0375) and tumor necrosis factor ligand superfamily member 12 (OR = 1.19, PIVW = .0424) levels were correlated with an increased risk of OESU. Conversely, interleukin-17C (OR = 0.76, PIVW = .0186) and C-X-C motif chemokine 9 (OR = 0.81, PIVW = .0328) levels were linked to a reduced risk of OESU.

Two inflammatory proteins were identified as being associated with GJU. Specifically, interleukin-5 levels (OR = 2.10, PIVW = .0146) were correlated with an increased risk of GJU. Interferon gamma levels (OR = 0.50, PIVW = .0268) were linked to a reduced risk of GJU.

3.3. Mediation analysis

We also analyzed the inflammatory protein as a mediator of the pathway from the gut microbiota to PUD. The results showed that genus Butyricicoccus mediated 7.9% of the adverse effects of GU by reducing the β-NGF levels. The results also indicated that the genus Lachnospiraceae UCG004 had substantial effects on mitigating the negative consequences of GU by decreasing the hepatocyte growth factor (HGF) levels and β-NGF levels, which accounted for 6.39% and 7.45%, respectively, of the overall effect.

3.4. Sensitivity analysis

Several sensitivity analyses were employed to assess and address potential pleiotropy in the causal estimates. Based on the MR-Egger regression intercept approach, the findings suggested that genetic pleiotropy did not introduce bias into the results. Additionally, the MR-PRESSO analysis indicated the absence of horizontal pleiotropy in the MR study. Cochran Q test and funnel plots revealed no heterogeneity or asymmetry among the SNPs concerning their causal relationships. The impact of each SNP on the overall causal estimates was validated using a leave-one-out analysis. Following the removal of each SNP, we systematically re-conducted MR analyses for the remaining SNPs, which yielded consistent results that underscored the significance of all SNPs in establishing the causal relationship (see Table S9, Supplemental Digital Content, http://links.lww.com/MD/N627 which illustrates the causal effects of gut microbiota on PUD; see Table S10, Supplemental Digital Content, http://links.lww.com/MD/N627 which illustrates the causal effects of inflammatory proteins on PUD; see Figures S1–S6, Supplemental Digital Content, http://links.lww.com/MD/N626 which illustrates the forest, scatter plots, funnel plots and leave-one-out analysis for PUD, OESU, GU, GASTRODU, DU, and GJU).

4. Discussion

The gut microbiota and inflammatory factors play important roles in the occurrence and development of PUD. Imbalances in intestinal flora can promote the release of inflammatory mediators, including tumor necrosis factor-α, IL-1β, and IL-6, thereby exacerbating inflammation within the gastric mucosa. Moreover, alterations in intestinal flora may lead to compromised intestinal barrier integrity, facilitating the translocation of harmful substances and bacteria across the mucosal barrier into the systemic circulation, thereby inciting a systemic inflammatory cascade that impacts gastric mucosal health. Furthermore, aberrant intestinal flora can perturb immune system balance, precipitating dysregulated immune responses that further intensify the inflammatory process and contribute to the initiation and progression of PUD.[21,22] This study employed MR analysis to explore the potential involvement of the gut microbiota and inflammatory proteins in peptic ulcer development susceptibility, with particular emphasis on elucidating the intermediary role of inflammatory proteins in gut microbiota-induced PUD risk.

Numerous studies have elucidated the potential significance of Lachnospiraceae in both the etiology and treatment of GU. Lachnospiraceae UCG004 represents a subgenus within the Lachnospiraceae genus. Studies indicate that Lachnospiraceae potentially mitigate intestinal inflammatory responses, consequently shielding the gastric mucosa from inflammation-related damage. By modulating intestinal microbiome equilibrium, Lachnospiraceae may maintain intestinal health, decrease the incidence of inflammatory reactions, and confer protective effects on gastric mucosal integrity.[23]

Our findings offer novel genetic insights supporting the protective role of Lachnospiraceae on gastric mucosal health. Employing the IVW method, we identified that HGF and β-NGF serve as mediators in the protective mechanism of Lachnospiraceae, particularly UCG004, against gastric ulcer development. HGF has pivotal roles in cell growth, motility, and morphogenesis across various cell types. Research indicates that topical HGF administration notably expedites the healing of gastric ulcers by concurrently enhancing blood flow to the ulcer peripheries and upregulating cyclooxygenase-2 expression within the ulcerous mucosa.[24] Furthermore, topical application of HGF fosters activation of cyclooxygenase-2 gene expression in gastric epithelial cells via phosphorylation of the c-Met/HGF receptor and subsequent activation of the ERK2 signaling pathway. Hence, HGF emerges as a significant contributor in promoting gastric ulcer healing \* MERGEFORMAT.[25] β-NGF is a nerve growth factor (NGF). NGF plays a pivotal role in the healing cascade of gastric ulcers. Beyond its classical functions in promoting neuronal growth and survival, NGF also contributes to angiogenesis. Studies have revealed that NGF expression occurs in gastric endothelial cells and stimulates neovascularization independently of vascular endothelial growth factor levels.[26] Topical administration of NGF in young rat models with gastric ulcers has been demonstrated to enhance neovascularization, expedite ulcer healing, and enhance mucosal regeneration quality.[27] Moreover, in aged rat models (with decreased NGF expression in gastric endothelial cells), topical NGF administration has been shown to augment vascular neogenesis, accelerate ulcer healing, and improve mucosal regeneration quality in ulcer scarring.[28]

Furthermore, our investigation unveiled that β-NGF serves as a mediator in the contribution of the genus Butyricicoccus to GU susceptibility. Butyricoccus is an intestinal bacterium that produces butyrate, which may play a role in inflammation-induced gastrointestinal disorders. It is worth mentioning that butyric acid has an anti-inflammatory effect in the intestinal tract, helping to maintain the integrity of the intestinal mucosa and immune homeostasis. Under inflammatory conditions, reduced numbers or impaired function of Butyricoccus may lead to decreased levels of butyric acid, which in turn affects inflammatory regulation and increases the risk of developing gastrointestinal diseases. Interestingly, imbalances in the intestinal flora are closely associated with the onset and progression of gastrointestinal diseases, and Butyricoccus may be an important contributor to intestinal inflammation as well as gastrointestinal diseases.[29–32]

While we identified only the inflammatory proteins that mediate GU, other ulcerative diseases of the gastrointestinal tract also may be influenced by inflammatory proteins and intestinal microbiota, with inflammatory proteins being crucial for the association between intestinal microbiota and PUD. Multiple biological mechanisms underlie the processes by which intestinal microbiota induces PUD via inflammation. Initially, dysbiosis of intestinal flora can aggravate and perpetuate inflammation, consequently negatively affecting the gastrointestinal mucosal health, by disrupting the mucosal barrier, leading to damaged gastric mucosa which facilitates ulceration. Certain pathogenic bacteria among the intestinal flora can generate harmful substances like ammonia and sulfides. These substances potentially directly harm gastric mucosal cells, prompting and promoting inflammatory responses. Moreover, ammonia and sulfides may damage the gastric mucosal layer, rendering it susceptible to erosion by gastric acid and other digestive enzymes, thereby contributing to ulceration. Moreover, intestinal floral dysbiosis may abnormally activate the immune system, causing excessive inflammation. Inflammatory mediators such as cytokines and chemokines released by immune cells can induce tissue inflammation and damage, exacerbating ulcer formation. Furthermore, such excessive inflammatory responses may lead to vasodilation and increased permeability, exposing the gastric mucosa to additional harmful factors. Gastric mucosal cells typically continuously renew to repair damaged areas and maintain mucosal integrity. Intestinal flora dysbiosis and persistent inflammation may impair these regenerative and reparative mechanisms, thereby hindering ulcer healing.[33–35]

Our study has several limitations. Firstly, although focusing exclusively on individuals of European ancestry minimizes issues arising due to population differences, our results may not be relevant or applicable to all other populations. Secondly, because the GWAS database is publicly available, we could not obtain detailed participants’ information to analyze the subgroups. Thirdly, analyses of intestinal microbiota at superficial taxonomic levels may potentially bias our results. Lastly, eliminating all the potential horizontal pleiotropy is impossible even when consistent results are observed after using multiple and diverse analytical methods.

5. Conclusions

In conclusion, the mechanisms by which intestinal microbiota induces PUD through inflammatory responses involve various factors, including harmful substances produced by pathogenic bacteria, abnormal activation of the immune system, inflammation-induced tissue injury, and impaired mucosal regeneration and repair. Specifically, we conducted MR analyses to assess the effects of microbiota and inflammatory mediators. Therefore, intestinal floral homeostasis is crucial for preventing and treating PUD. Our results may guide future strategies on PUD prevention and treatment. Undertaking comprehensive epidemiological and mechanistic studies is important to elucidate the pathways whereby intestinal microbiota may influence the PUD onset and progression through mediation by inflammatory proteins.
