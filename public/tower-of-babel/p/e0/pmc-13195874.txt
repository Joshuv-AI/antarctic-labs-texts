Epithelial CD155–TIGIT/CD226 signaling links CD8⁺ T-cell exhaustion to frequent exacerbations in COPD
Authors: Dan Wang, Hui Li, Zhijun Zhao, Jiale Zhao, Xiaolan Hai, Yuchun Fan, Weirong Ma, Juan Chen, Xiahui Ge, Xiangguo Duan, Jia Hou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe immune mechanisms underlying chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) remain incompletely understood, particularly regarding immune checkpoint dysregulation. This cross-sectional observational study aimed to determine whether activation of the T-cell immunoglobulin and ITIM domain (TIGIT) pathway contributes to CD8-positive T-cell exhaustion and susceptibility to frequent exacerbations in COPD.MethodsWe integrated publicly available single-cell RNA sequencing data from human lung tissue with analyses of peripheral blood, bronchoalveolar lavage fluid, and lung tissue obtained from patients with COPD and healthy controls. Flow cytometry was used to assess CD8-positive T-cell differentiation and expression of TIGIT and its co-stimulatory counterpart CD226, while immunohistochemistry quantified the TIGIT ligand CD155 in airway and alveolar epithelium. Functional assays, including cytokine production, apoptosis, and lentiviral TIGIT knockdown, were conducted to evaluate the reversibility of TIGIT-mediated exhaustion.ResultsSingle-cell and flow-cytometric analyses revealed an expansion of terminally differentiated effector memory CD8-positive T cells re-expressing CD45RA with high TIGIT expression in COPD, particularly among frequent exacerbators. CD155 was upregulated in airway and alveolar epithelial cells, whereas CD226 expression on CD8-positive T cells was reduced. TIGIT-positive CD8-positive T cells exhibited decreased tumour necrosis factor-alpha and interferon-gamma production and increased apoptosis. TIGIT knockdown restored cytokine secretion and reduced apoptosis, indicating a reversible exhausted phenotype.ConclusionThe TIGIT–CD155/CD226 pathway is associated with epithelial stress, CD8⁺ T-cell exhaustion and frequent exacerbations in COPD. Targeting this immune checkpoint may help restore antiviral immunity and reduce airway damage, providing a promising therapeutic avenue for immune modulation in chronic airway disease.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12931-026-03717-z.

Introduction

Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) is a progressive inflammatory lung disorder characterized by irreversible airflow limitation and recurrent acute exacerbations. Although cigarette smoking remains the primary risk factor, accumulating evidence highlights the pivotal role of dysregulated immune responses in COPD pathogenesis [1]. Among immune cells, T lymphocytes—particularly CD8⁺ T cells—are crucial mediators of airway inflammation, cytotoxicity, and parenchymal destruction in COPD [2–4]. However, the mechanisms underlying their functional impairment and immune exhaustion remain incompletely understood.

T-cell immunoreceptor with immunoglobulin and ITIM domain (TIGIT) is an inhibitory checkpoint receptor increasingly recognized for its role in modulating T-cell responses in chronic infections, cancer, and autoimmune diseases [5, 6]. TIGIT expression is a hallmark of T-cell exhaustion—a state defined by diminished effector function, reduced cytokine secretion, and enhanced susceptibility to apoptosis [3]. While the role of CD4⁺ regulatory T cells has been extensively investigated in COPD, the suppressive and exhausted features of CD8⁺ T cell subsets—particularly those expressing TIGIT—remain largely unexplored.

TIGIT exerts its immunoregulatory effects primarily through binding to CD155 (poliovirus receptor, PVR), which is upregulated on inflamed epithelial and tumor tissues. CD226 (DNAM-1), a co-stimulatory receptor expressed on T cells and natural killer cells, competes with TIGIT for binding to CD155 but delivers activating signals that enhance cytotoxicity and cytokine production. The balance between TIGIT-mediated inhibitory signaling and CD226-mediated co-stimulation is therefore critical for maintaining effective immune responses [4, 7]. Disruption of this balance has been implicated in T-cell dysfunction in cancer and chronic infection, but its role in COPD remains poorly defined.

In this study, we comprehensively characterize CD8⁺TIGIT⁺ T cells in peripheral blood and bronchoalveolar lavage fluid from COPD patients and healthy controls. We further evaluate their differentiation status and functional attributes and explore their association with clinical exacerbation frequency. By integrating molecular and functional analyses, this work aims to clarify the immunoregulatory role of the TIGIT–CD155 axis in COPD and its implications for disease progression and immune modulation.

Materials and methods

Patient enrollment and study design

Patients with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) and healthy controls were recruited from Ningxia Medical University General Hospital (Yinchuan, China) between January 2024 and June 2025. COPD diagnosis and severity were defined according to GOLD 2023 criteria [8]. Participants with asthma, allergic rhinitis, active infection, malignancy, autoimmune disease, or recent (≤ 4 weeks) antibiotic, corticosteroid, chemotherapy, or immunosuppressant use were excluded. Patients were classified by exacerbation frequency: frequent exacerbators (FE, ≥ 2 moderate or ≥ 1 severe exacerbation in the past year) and non-frequent exacerbators (non-FE, ≤ 1). For clinical stratification, moderate exacerbations were operationally defined as episodes requiring systemic corticosteroids and/or antibiotics, whereas severe exacerbations were defined as events resulting in emergency department visits or hospitalization, in accordance with GOLD risk stratification and commonly used research criteria. Exacerbation history over the preceding 12 months was ascertained through structured review of medical records, including outpatient visits, prescription data, emergency encounters, and hospitalizations, supplemented by standardized patient interviews at enrollment. Self-reported exacerbation events were cross validated against available clinical documentation to enhance accuracy and reproducibility. All COPD subjects were clinically stable for ≥ 4 weeks before enrollment.

Three independent cohorts were included: (i) Peripheral-blood cohort, consisting of COPD patients and age- and sex-matched healthy volunteers for immunophenotypic and functional assays; (ii) BALF cohort, including COPD patients and healthy non-smoking volunteers undergoing diagnostic bronchoscopy; and (iii) Lung-tissue cohort, comprising COPD patients undergoing resection for high-risk pulmonary nodules. Lung samples were obtained from macroscopically normal regions > 5 cm from lesions without histologic or radiologic tumor involvement. Control tissues were from individuals with benign nodules, normal lung function, and no chronic lung disease. The study size was determined by the availability of eligible COPD patients and matched healthy volunteers who met inclusion criteria during the study period. Sample numbers were chosen to ensure adequate power for flow cytometric and functional analyses based on prior COPD immunophenotyping studies, rather than a formal power calculation.

The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Ningxia Medical University General Hospital (KYLL-2024-0886) and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. We used the STROBE writing guide when drafting this article, and the STROBE checklist (see supplementary materials research checklist) to demonstrate adherence to the STROBE reporting guideline [9].

Single-cell RNA sequencing data processing and analysis

A publicly available human lung scRNA-seq dataset was obtained from Zenodo (https://zenodo.org/record/8393742) using the keywords “COPD” and “scRNA-seq.” Data were processed in R (v4.1.1) with Seurat (v5.2.1). The preprocessed .RDS file included normalization, scaling, and doublet removal by the original authors. Additional quality control excluded cells with < 200 or > 6,000 genes or > 10% mitochondrial reads. Cells were grouped into epithelial (n = 11,776), endothelial (n = 4,609), lymphoid (n = 58,648), stromal (n = 813), and myeloid (n = 33,515) lineages. Dimensional reduction was performed with PCA and UMAP (resolution = 0.6), and marker genes were identified using “FindAllMarkers.” CD8⁺ T cells were extracted from the lymphoid lineage and reclustered into TN, TCM, TEM, and TEMRA subsets. Differentially expressed genes (logFC > 0.25, adjusted p < 0.05) were analyzed using GO and KEGG enrichment to assess functional heterogeneity.

Antibody-derived tag (ADT) data from CITE-seq were CLR-normalized and integrated with transcriptomic profiles using weighted nearest neighbor (WNN) analysis to jointly capture RNA and protein expression features. UMAP visualizations and feature plots were used for exploratory characterization of cell-type–specific expression patterns without inferential statistics. For group comparisons supporting biological conclusions, CITE-seq protein expression analyses were performed at the donor level to avoid pseudo-replication. Specifically, for each donor, median ADT expression values were calculated within predefined cell populations, including CD3⁺CD8⁺ T cells for CD226 and epithelial cells for CD155. These donor-level summaries were then used for statistical comparisons between healthy controls and COPD patients or across disease stages using appropriate non-parametric tests.

Sample collection and processing

Peripheral blood was collected into EDTA tubes, and peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were isolated by Ficoll-Paque Plus density-gradient centrifugation (400 ×g, 20 min, 22 °C), followed by two PBS washes (300 ×g, 5 min, 4 °C). The resulting PBMCs were resuspended in complete RPMI-1640 medium for downstream assays.

Bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL) was performed as previously described [10]. To minimize smoking-related effects, current smokers abstained for ≥ 12 h before the procedure. Under local anesthesia (lidocaine), four 50-mL aliquots of sterile saline were instilled into the right middle lobe using a flexible bronchoscope (Olympus BF-290, Tokyo, Japan). The first aliquot was discarded to avoid airway contamination. The recovered BAL fluid was filtered, washed, and resuspended in RPMI-1640 medium (10⁶ cells/mL) and kept at 4 °C until immediate processing.

Flow cytometric analysis and apoptosis assessment

Freshly isolated PBMCs were stained with fluorochrome-conjugated antibodies for 30 min at 4 °C in the dark and analyzed on a BD LSR II cytometer (BD Biosciences). For intracellular cytokine assays, surface staining for TIGIT and other markers was performed prior to stimulation. Cells were then stimulated with PMA (50 ng/mL) and ionomycin (1 µg/mL) for 5 h in the presence of a protein transport inhibitor, followed by fixation and permeabilization using the eBioscience Intracellular Fixation & Permeabilization Buffer Kit according to the manufacturer’s protocol. IFN-γ and TNF-α were detected intracellularly, and surface markers (CD8, CCR7, CD95, TIGIT, CD226, CD45RA) were evaluated using antibodies from BD Biosciences, Pharmingen, and eBioscience. Apoptosis was assessed with the PE Annexin V kit (BD Biosciences). Data were analyzed using FlowJo software (Tree Star).

To ensure the quality of flow cytometric analyses, predefined acquisition and viability criteria were applied. For peripheral blood samples, a minimum of 50,000 total lymphocyte events and at least 5,000 CD8⁺ T-cell events were acquired per sample. For bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) samples, which exhibit greater inter-individual variability in cellularity, a minimum threshold of 1,000 CD8⁺ T-cell events was required for inclusion in downstream analyses. Cell viability was assessed using 7-AAD or Annexin V/7-AAD staining, and samples with viability below 90% for PBMCs or 85% for BALF were excluded. Samples that did not meet these acquisition or viability criteria were not included in subset frequency analyses.

Flow cytometry data were analyzed using predefined gating strategies. Quantitative analyses were performed without access to clinical information, including disease group or exacerbation status, to minimize analytical bias.

CFSE staining and T-cell proliferation assay

CD8⁺ T cells were labeled with 1 µM carboxyfluorescein diacetate succinimidyl ester (CFSE; Invitrogen) and seeded at 4 × 10⁴ cells per well. Cells were stimulated on anti-CD3–coated plates (5 µg/mL) in the presence of soluble anti-CD28 (5 µg/mL) and cultured in RPMI-1640 medium for 96 h. Cell proliferation was then evaluated by flow cytometry.

Separation, activation, and lentiviral transduction of CD8⁺ T cells

CD8⁺ T cells were isolated from PBMCs by negative selection using a commercial kit (Stemcell Technologies) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Purified cells were activated for 48 h with anti-CD3/CD28 antibodies (Stemcell, #10971) and recombinant human IL-2 (200 IU/mL). For lentiviral transduction, 96-well plates were coated overnight with Retronectin (TAKARA, T100A) at 4 °C, blocked with 2% BSA for 30 min, and rinsed with PBS. Activated CD8⁺ T cells (1 × 10⁵/well) were seeded and infected with lentiviral particles at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 10 in the presence of Polybrene (5 µg/mL). Plates were centrifuged at 1000 ×g for 90 min and then incubated at 37 °C in 5% CO₂. Transduction efficiency was assessed by fluorescence intensity before subsequent functional assays.

Immunohistochemistry and quantification of CD155 expression

Paraffin-embedded lung tissue sections from COPD patients and controls were stained for CD155 (PVR) using standard immunohistochemistry. After deparaffinization, rehydration, and antigen retrieval in citrate buffer (pH 6.0), endogenous peroxidase was blocked with 3% H₂O₂. Sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with anti-CD155 antibody (1:200; Boster, A00664-2), followed by a biotinylated secondary antibody and DAB detection. Nuclei were counterstained with hematoxylin. Digital images were captured at ×200 magnification under identical settings. For each case, 3–5 random fields from small airways and alveolar regions were analyzed using ImageJ (NIH). DAB-positive areas were quantified by color deconvolution and expressed as a percentage of total tissue area. An H-score was calculated as Σ (Pi × i), where i denotes staining intensity (0–3) and Pi the percentage of cells at each intensity. Mean values from multiple fields were used for statistical analysis (unpaired t-test; p < 0.05). Immunohistochemistry staining was evaluated by an experienced investigator who was blinded to the clinical characteristics and group allocation of the samples at the time of scoring.

Statistical analysis

Data normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Variables with normal distribution are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and were compared using the unpaired two-tailed Student’s t-test. Non-normally distributed variables are expressed as median with interquartile range (IQR) and were compared using the Mann–Whitney U test. Categorical variables were analyzed using Fisher’s exact test. For comparisons among more than two non-normally distributed groups, the Kruskal–Wallis test was performed, followed by post hoc pairwise Mann–Whitney U tests with Bonferroni correction (p < 0.017 considered significant). Unless otherwise specified, a p < 0.05 was regarded as statistically significant. All analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism version 7.0 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA), and all tests were two-tailed. No formal a priori power calculations were performed. Sample sizes were determined by sample availability and feasibility, and statistical analyses were conducted to identify associations rather than to establish definitive effect sizes.

Results

Patient characteristics and sample overview

Three distinct COPD cohorts and corresponding healthy control groups were analyzed. The peripheral blood cohort was used to delineate systemic immune phenotypes of CD8⁺ T cells (Table 1); the BALF cohort provided insight into airway-resident immune populations (Supplementary Table 1); and the lung tissue cohort enabled histological and molecular validation in structurally preserved lung parenchyma (Supplementary Table 2). Control samples were derived from healthy volunteers (for blood and BALF) or from non-COPD individuals undergoing surgery for benign pulmonary nodules (for lung tissue). Baseline variables—including age, sex, smoking history, lung function indices, and exacerbation frequency—were consistent with previous COPD studies.

Exploratory single-cell transcriptomic profiling identifies enrichment of TIGIT⁺ TEMRA CD8⁺ T cells and dysregulated CD226–CD155 signaling in COPD

Analysis of a publicly available human lung scRNA-seq dataset (Zenodo database; see Methods for details), comprising 13 donors (including 7 COPD patients and 6 healthy controls), identified nine lymphocyte subsets, including a terminally differentiated effector memory CD8⁺ population re-expressing CD45RA (TEMRA) (Fig. 1A). These cells expressed high levels of KLRG1, CX3CR1, PRF1, GZMH, and EOMES, consistent with a cytotoxic, late-differentiation phenotype (Fig. 1B). COPD lungs showed an enrichment of TEMRA CD8⁺ T cells with elevated TIGIT expression (p < 0.0001) (Fig. 1C–F), indicating an association between TEMRA expansion, TIGIT upregulation, and immune checkpoint imbalance in COPD.

To complement the transcriptomic analysis and validate immune cell identity at the protein level, CITE-seq data were further analyzed based on CD3 and CD8 surface expression. Cells were classified into CD3⁺CD8⁺, CD3⁺CD8⁻, CD3⁻CD8⁺, and double-negative populations (Fig. 1G), enabling protein-level characterization of CD8⁺ T cells. CITE-seq analysis further demonstrated progressive downregulation of CD226 and increased epithelial CD155 expression with advancing disease—from healthy controls to GOLD III and end-stage COPD (Fig. 1H–I). Within CD8⁺ T cells, CD226 expression was markedly reduced in COPD (Fig. 1J). Together, these results suggest that disregulation of the CD226–CD155 signaling axis between epithelial and CD8⁺ T cells is associated with immune exhaustion in the COPD lung microenvironment.

Circulating CD8⁺ T-cell differentiation is skewed toward TEMRA cells in COPD and accentuated in frequent exacerbators

Flow-cytometric profiling revealed a marked shift toward terminal differentiation in COPD. TEMRA cells were increased versus controls (38.5 ± 16.1% vs. 23.0 ± 7.0%, p < 0.0001), accompanied by fewer naïve (17.6 ± 7.9% vs. 39.1 ± 11.0%, p < 0.0001) and more effector-memory cells (27.4 ± 11.0% vs. 22.1 ± 8.2%, p < 0.05) (Fig. 2A–B). Frequent exacerbators (FE) showed higher TEMRA frequencies than non-FE patients (48.7 ± 12.3% vs. 27.0 ± 10.5%, p < 0.001), indicating accentuated terminal differentiation. TIGIT expression increased along the differentiation continuum, peaking in TEMRA subsets of both FE and non-FE groups (Fig. 2C–G). CD8⁺TIGIT⁺ cells were enriched in COPD (61% vs. 26%, p < 0.0001) and most abundant in FE patients (~ 60% vs. ~ 30%, p < 0.0001). TEMRA cells exhibited the highest granzyme A and perforin levels (87.4%), followed by TEM (55.9%), with lower expression in TN and TCM subsets (Figure S1). These data indicate that frequent exacerbations are associated with enhanced terminal differentiation and TIGIT upregulation in circulating CD8⁺ T cells.

CD8⁺ T-cell differentiation is skewed toward TEMRA subsets with elevated TIGIT expression in the BAL of COPD patients

To assess local immune remodeling, CD8⁺ T-cell subsets in bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL) were classified as TN, TCM, TEM, and TEMRA (Fig. 3A). Consistent with peripheral findings, COPD patients showed increased BAL TEMRA frequencies versus controls, most pronounced in frequent exacerbators (FE) compared with non-FE patients (median 21.9% vs. 9.45%, p < 0.001; Fig. 3B). CD8⁺TIGIT⁺ T cells were also higher in FE patients than in non-FE counterparts (median 19.35% vs. 5.15%, p < 0.001; Fig. 3C–D). These data indicate that the BAL compartment in COPD—particularly in frequent exacerbators—is enriched with terminally differentiated, TIGIT-expressing CD8⁺ T cells, reflecting enhanced local immune exhaustion.

Upregulation of CD155 and reciprocal downregulation of CD226 delineate an imbalanced TIGIT–CD155/CD226 signaling axis in COPD lungs

To determine whether TIGIT signaling is activated in the COPD lung microenvironment, we examined the expression of its ligand CD155 and the co-stimulatory receptor CD226. Immunohistochemistry revealed markedly increased CD155 staining in small airway and alveolar epithelial cells of COPD lungs compared with controls (Fig. 4A). Quantitative analysis confirmed a significant expansion of CD155-positive areas in COPD tissues, indicating local upregulation of the TIGIT ligand. Flow-cytometric analysis of BALF showed that CD226 expression was markedly lower on CD8⁺ TIGIT⁺ T cells than on their TIGIT⁻ counterparts (77.5 ± 8.0% vs. 87.1 ± 8.2%, p < 0.05; Fig. 4B). Moreover, the overall frequency of CD226⁺ CD8⁺ T cells in BALF was significantly reduced in COPD patients compared with healthy controls (66.0 ± 11.9% vs. 75.6 ± 12.0%, p = 0.038; Fig. 4C). These findings indicate that enhanced TIGIT–CD155 inhibitory signaling, together with diminished CD226 co-stimulation, is linked to features of CD8⁺ T-cell exhaustion in COPD.

Consistent with this inhibitory milieu, CD8⁺TIGIT⁺ T cells exhibited a distinct exhausted phenotype characterized by co-expression of multiple inhibitory receptors. Flow-cytometric analysis revealed that PD-1, TIM-3, and LAG-3 were markedly upregulated on TIGIT⁺ CD8⁺ T cells relative to their TIGIT⁻ counterparts (Figure S2). This pattern indicates that TIGIT marks a subset of CD8⁺ T cells with advanced exhaustion and immune-dysregulatory potential.

Functional impairment of CD8⁺TIGIT⁺ T cells from COPD patients can be reversed by TIGIT knockdown

To determine the functional impact of TIGIT, cytokine production, apoptosis, and proliferation were compared between TIGIT⁺ and TIGIT⁻ CD8⁺ T cells from COPD patients. TIGIT⁺ cells showed markedly reduced TNF-α and IFN-γ production and increased Annexin V and CD95 expression (Fig. 5A–B), indicating impaired effector function and enhanced apoptosis. Despite this exhausted phenotype, they retained proliferative potential (Ki-67⁺ and CFSE⁺) (Figure S3A–C). Lentiviral TIGIT knockdown in purified CD8⁺ T cells restored TNF-α and IFN-γ secretion and reduced apoptosis (Fig. 5C–E), confirming TIGIT as a key inhibitory checkpoint mediating CD8⁺ T-cell exhaustion in COPD.

Discussion

This study identifies an immune-inhibitory checkpoint imbalance associated with epithelial stress and CD8⁺ T-cell exhaustion in COPD. Airway epithelial CD155 is induced by cigarette smoke and inflammatory stimuli, while CD8⁺ T cells exhibit increased TIGIT and reduced CD226 expression. The resulting TIGIT–CD155 interaction is associated with functional features of CD8⁺ T-cell exhaustion marked by diminished IFN-γ and TNF-α yet preserved cytotoxic granule release, delineating a pathway that connects impaired antimicrobial defense with tissue injury and chronic inflammation (Fig. 6).

Chronic smoke exposure and airway inflammation reshape the epithelial–immune interface in COPD. Under stress, airway epithelial cells upregulate CD155 (poliovirus receptor, PVR), a nectin-like adhesion molecule serving as a ligand for TIGIT and CD226 [11–13]. Normally expressed at low levels on epithelia and endothelia, CD155 is inducible by IFN-γ, TNF-α, oxidative stress, and viral infection through NF-κB and MAPK signaling [14–17]. Transcriptomic studies of bronchial brushings from smokers likewise reveal immune-gene remodeling with increased PVR expression [13]. Thus, the smoke-injured epithelium provides an immunomodulatory surface enriched in inhibitory ligands, which may enable sustained engagement with infiltrating lymphocytes.

Within this remodeled niche, COPD CD8⁺ T cells display elevated TIGIT and reduced CD226, favoring inhibitory signaling. TIGIT and CD226 compete for CD155 binding but elicit opposite outcomes: TIGIT suppresses activation, whereas CD226 provides co-stimulation [18]. Loss of CD226 therefore amplifies TIGIT dominance. Functionally, CD8⁺TIGIT⁺ T cells secreted less IFN-γ and TNF-α but retained granzyme A and perforin, indicating partial or adaptive exhaustion rather than terminal dysfunction. This phenotype may reconcile the paradoxical coexistence of defective host defense and excessive tissue injury in COPD. Diminished type-1 cytokine responses weaken antiviral and antibacterial protection, predisposing to recurrent infections and exacerbations, while preserved cytotoxic effector capacity may permit ongoing epithelial and alveolar damage [19–22]. Such an exhaustion-like or maladaptive immune state, reminiscent of that seen in chronic viral or autoimmune contexts [23, 24], may reflect the overlap of immunodeficiency and self-injury within the COPD lung.

Notably, the reduced IFN-γ production by TIGIT⁺ CD8⁺ T cells observed here seems at odds with evidence that IFN-γ drives emphysematous destruction [25]. This apparent discrepancy can be reconciled by stage- and context-dependent roles of IFN-γ: in earlier disease or during acute exacerbations, non-exhausted CD8⁺ T cells may deliver IFN-γ that directly contributes to alveolar damage; in advanced/frequent-exacerbator COPD, chronic TIGIT signaling induces selective cytokine exhaustion (impaired IFN-γ/TNF-α) while preserving cytotoxic granule release. This “exhausted yet cytolytic” phenotype likely impairs anti-infective immunity, promoting recurrent exacerbations and secondary injury rather than primary emphysematous progression.

The imbalance of the TIGIT–CD155/CD226 axis likely extends beyond isolated cell interactions. Smoke- or inflammation-induced epithelial CD155 upregulation may promote inhibitory signaling through TIGIT, curtailing CD8⁺ T-cell cytokine output. Impaired antimicrobial immunity then promotes recurrent infections and acute exacerbations that further intensify inflammation and oxidative stress, again driving CD155 induction. This proposed self-perpetuating loop (Fig. 6) may sustain immune exhaustion, tissue injury, and disease progression. Spatial proximity between CD8⁺TIGIT⁺ T cells and CD155⁺ epithelial cells in BALF and tissue sections supports direct ligand–receptor engagement, highlighting epithelial–immune crosstalk as a critical determinant of local tolerance and fibrotic remodeling. Analogous mechanisms operate in tumors, where malignant cells exploit TIGIT–CD155 signaling to evade cytotoxic surveillance, and in chronic viral infections where persistent ligand expression enforces T-cell exhaustion [26–28]. Our findings extend these paradigms to COPD, suggesting that chronic epithelial stress is linked to immune paralysis via the same inhibitory circuitry.

Collectively, these data support a unifying immunopathogenic model in which cigarette smoke and inflammation orchestrate an epithelial–immune inhibitory axis. Upregulated epithelial CD155 engages TIGIT on CD8⁺ T cells, while concomitant CD226 downregulation abolishes co-stimulation, leading to cytokine hyporesponsiveness and partial exhaustion. These “exhausted yet cytolytic” cells are associated with persistent inflammation and structural injury, while recurrent exacerbations further enhance epithelial CD155 expression and TIGIT signaling, reinforcing immune suppression. This vicious cycle may represent a central mechanism linking immune exhaustion, epithelial damage, and progressive COPD.

Therapeutically, modulating this pathway may represent an exploratory strategy to restore immune competence while limiting collateral cytotoxicity. TIGIT blockade or CD226 reinforcement has reversed exhaustion in tumors and chronic infections [28–32]; however, whether analogous strategies can be safely and effectively applied in COPD remains uncertain. While modulation of this axis may theoretically enhance antiviral defense, systemic immune checkpoint intervention may also carry risks of excessive immune activation and tissue injury in the chronically inflamed COPD lung. Importantly, unlike cancer or chronic viral infection, COPD is characterized by ongoing tissue damage and heightened baseline inflammation, raising concerns that immune checkpoint modulation could exacerbate immunopathology if not carefully controlled.

Although the present study is single-center in nature, the multi-layer validation—from transcriptomic analyses to ex vivo functional assays—supports the biological relevance of this pathway. Nevertheless, several limitations should be acknowledged. While epithelial CD155 upregulation and TIGIT expression on CD8⁺ T cells were observed in parallel, direct epithelial–T cell interaction experiments were not performed, and causality cannot be definitively established. In addition, functional assays focused on TIGIT manipulation in CD8⁺ T cells rather than upstream epithelial ligands. Future longitudinal studies and mechanistic experiments, including epithelial–immune co-culture systems or CD155-targeted interventions, will be required to further validate causality and assess the therapeutic potential of targeting this immune checkpoint axis in COPD. Furthermore, downstream signaling events of the TIGIT–CD155/CD226 axis were not directly assessed in this study, which limits mechanistic interpretation of the observed associations.

In conclusion, the TIGIT–CD155/CD226 inhibitory axis orchestrates an imbalance between immune regulation and cytotoxic persistence in COPD. Expansion of CD8⁺TIGIT⁺ TEMRA cells, particularly in frequent exacerbators, is associated with chronic immune exhaustion and epithelial injury. By defining a checkpoint-associated immunophenotype linked to disease severity, this study provides a rationale for further preclinical exploration of TIGIT–CD226 related immune modulation in COPD.
