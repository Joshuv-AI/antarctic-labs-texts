Age-associated changes in major lymphocyte subsets and sex-specific patterns in healthy Chinese adults
Authors: Liqin Xiang, Peng Wu, Chengliang Zhu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
To delineate age-associated changes in major lymphocyte subsets in healthy Chinese adults, identify key inflection points, and explore sex-specific patterns. Flow cytometry data of peripheral blood T, B, and NK cell subsets were collected from 6,550 healthy individuals aged 18–96 years at Hubei Provincial People’s Hospital. Piecewise regression models were used to identify inflection points where the rate of change in immune parameters accelerates. A composite immune index (CII) was constructed via principal component analysis (PCA). Immune characteristics were compared across four age groups (youth, young middle-aged, middle-aged elderly, elderly) and between sexes. Key inflection points were identified: CD8 + T cell percentage and CD4+/CD8 + ratio declined significantly around age 54, while CD4 + T cell and B cell counts showed accelerated decline after ages 63.12 and 62.00, respectively. Total T cell count declined most rapidly after age 67.50; The CII, integrating 11 immune parameters, exhibited a significant inflection point at age 63 (p < 0.001), with the rate of decline accelerating from − 2.64 units/year to − 10.66 units/year thereafter; In individuals aged ≥ 63 years, females maintained higher CD4 + T cell counts and percentages, B cell counts and percentages, CD4+/CD8 + ratio, and total T cell percentage, whereas males had higher NK cell counts and percentages. This study delineates the age-associated trajectories of lymphocyte subsets in healthy Chinese adults, identifying age 63 as a data‑driven inflection point associated with accelerated adaptive immune decline. Pronounced sexual dimorphism in late-life immune profiles underscores the need for sex-specific strategies in immune health assessment and personalized health management.

Introduction

The progressive functional decline and homeostasis imbalance of the immune system with advancing age, collectively referred to as immunosenescence, represent a hallmark of aging1,2. This multifaceted process is prominently manifested in the dynamic changes of peripheral lymphocyte subsets: a sharp reduction in T cell receptor repertoire diversity, a decrease in naïve T cell output due to thymic involution, and an expansion of terminally differentiated memory T cell subsets3–5. It is important to distinguish between the broad functional concept of immunosenescence and the more specific phenotypic observations of age-associated changes in lymphocyte composition, the present study focuses on the latter. These alterations in lymphocyte composition are associated with the aging of the adaptive immune system and have been linked to biological age in previous studies6. Indeed, shifts in the balance between CD4 + and CD8 + T cells, declines in B cell numbers, and compensatory increases in NK cells have all been associated with adverse health outcomes in older adults, including increased susceptibility to infections, diminished vaccine efficacy, and a higher incidence of malignant tumors, autoimmune diseases, and other chronic conditions1,7–14. The age‑related remodeling of lymphocyte subsets is therefore a relevant phenotypic marker of systemic aging15. It should be noted, however, that our study focuses on the phenotypic composition of circulating lymphocyte subsets, which represents only one dimension of the complex immunosenescence process.

Given the accelerating global population aging, understanding the dynamics and mechanisms of immune aging has become a pressing challenge for both basic immunology and public health. As the country with the largest and fastest‑growing elderly population, China faces particularly enormous health pressures16,17. To date, most large‑scale studies on immune aging have been conducted in Western populations, establishing assessment systems and theoretical frameworks that may not be directly applicable to other ethnicities5,18–22. Accumulating evidence indicates that genetic background, early‑life pathogen exposure, epigenetic regulation, and environmental factors collectively shape the baseline characteristics and aging trajectories of the immune system23–26. Consequently, the immune aging patterns of the Chinese population may differ substantially from those of Caucasians, yet systematic, large‑sample investigations in China remain scarce. Directly applying Western‑derived reference models could overlook population‑specific features and hinder the development of precise public health strategies.

In this context, we conducted a cross‑sectional study using high‑resolution multicolor flow cytometry to analyze peripheral blood lymphocyte subsets in 6,550 healthy Chinese adults aged 18–96 years. Our primary objectives were: (1) To determine whether key age‑related inflection points exist in the dynamics of major lymphocyte subsets (T cells, B cells, NK cells, and their key subpopulations); (2) To characterize the continuous age trajectories of parameters such as CD4+/CD8 + ratio and NK cell counts; (3) To explore the influence of sex on these age‑associated changes. We identified multiple inflection points, most notably at age 63, where the rate of change in adaptive immune components (CD4 + T cells, B cells) accelerates, and constructed a composite immune index (CII) that captures the overall age‑related shift in lymphocyte composition. Furthermore, we observed pronounced sex‑specific patterns in individuals aged ≥ 63 years, with females maintaining higher adaptive immune reserves and males exhibiting elevated NK cell levels. These findings provide essential baseline data for establishing immune health assessment standards tailored to the Chinese population and offer a scientific foundation for early warning and targeted interventions at critical life stages, ultimately contributing to the promotion of healthy aging in China.

Research methods

Research subjects and data sources

This study was a cross-sectional observational study. The research subjects were 6,550 healthy adults who underwent routine health check-ups at the Physical Examination Center of Hubei Provincial People’s Hospital from January 2022 to May 2025. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Renmin Hospital of Wuhan University(Approval No. WDRY2025-K145).

Inclusion criteria: (1) Age ≥ 18 years; (2) No history of immune-related diseases such as autoimmune diseases, malignant tumors, or human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) infection; (3) No use of immunomodulatory drugs (including glucocorticoids, immunosuppressants, biologics, etc.) within the past three months; (4) Absence of acute infection symptoms (e.g., fever, cough, sore throat) on the day of physical examination; (5) No significant abnormalities in clinical laboratory tests (including complete blood count, liver and kidney function, blood glucose, blood lipids, etc.). Exclusion criteria: (1) History of the aforementioned immune-related diseases; (2) Acute infection phase; (3) Use of immunomodulatory drugs within the past three months; (4) Chronic infectious diseases (e.g., tuberculosis, chronic viral hepatitis); (5) Pregnant or lactating women.

It should be noted that cytomegalovirus (CMV) serological status was not detected in this study, as this factor may influence the composition of T-cell subsets. We will address this limitation in the discussion section.

We divide the eligible population into four age groups: the youth group (18–25 years old), the young and middle-aged group (26–54 years old), the middle-aged and elderly group (55–75 years old), and the elderly group (76–96 years old). These four age groups cover a wide range of people from youth to old age, aiming to systematically assess the characteristics of immune function changes in different age groups. Each group maintained a balanced distribution in terms of gender and basic health status to ensure comparability among groups. The basic characteristics of each group are shown in Table 1.

Experimental methods

1. BD Multitest IMK four-color kit, containing one vial of each reagent (the concentration values of conjugated antibodies are shown in Table 2):

(1) BD Multitest CD3/CD8/CD45/CD4 reagent was dissolved in 1 mL buffer containing 0.1% sodium azide, comprising FITC-labeled CD3 (clone SK7), PE-labeled CD8 (clone SK1), PerCP-labeled CD45 (clone 2D1 (HLe-1)), and APC-labeled CD4 (clone SK3).

(3) BD Multitest IMK Kit Hemolysin (10x Concentrate).

2. BD Trucount tube: A disposable tube containing lyophilized particles with fluorescent microspheres.

Sample collection and processing

5mL peripheral venous blood was collected from all subjects in the early morning under fasting state, and EDTA was used as anticoagulant. Flow cytometry analysis should be conducted within 2 h after blood collection to prevent a decline in cell activity from affecting the accuracy of the results.

Flow cytometry detection

Detection was performed using the BD FACSCanto flow cytometer, with the specific operational steps as follows:

(1) Take two BD Trucount tubes and label them with letters (A and B) for differentiation.

(2) Add 20 µL of BD Multitest CD3/CD8/CD45/CD4 reagent to the bottom of the tube labeled A; add 20 µL of BD Multitest CD3/CD16 + CD56/CD45/CD19 reagent to the bottom of the tube labeled B.

(3) Add 50 µL of fully mixed anticoagulated whole blood to the bottom of each of the two tubes.

(4) After tightening the tube cap, gently shake to mix thoroughly, and incubate at room temperature (20 ~ 25 °C) protected from light for 15 min.

(5) Add 450 µL of 1X BD Multitest lysing reagent to each tube, tightly cap the tubes and gently vortex to mix. Incubate at room temperature (20–25 °C) protected from light for 15 min.

(6) On-machine testing: Data was collected using BD FACSCanto clinical software, with at least 10,000 lymphocyte events recorded per sample.

Explanation of test results

The antibody panel used in this study targets the following lymphocyte surface antigens, enabling precise identification of each subset through multiparameter flow cytometry:

(1) CD3: Identifies the epsilon chain of the T-cell antigen receptor (TCR) complex, expressed on 61%–85% of normal peripheral blood lymphocytes. CD3 + cells are defined as total T cells.

(2) CD4: The surface antigen of helper T cells with a molecular weight of 55 kDa is recognized, expressed on the CD3 + CD4+ T lymphocyte subset, which accounts for 28%-58% of normal peripheral blood lymphocytes. CD3 + CD4+ cells are defined as helper/inducer T cells.

(3) CD8: Identifies the CD8 molecule α subunit (32 kDa), expressed on T lymphocyte subsets and some NK cells. CD3 + CD8+ cells are defined as cytotoxic T cells.

(4) CD16 + CD56: Co-labeled for identifying NK cell populations. CD16 recognizes the Fcγ receptor III (FcγRIII) with a molecular weight of 50–70 kDa, expressed on NK cells and a subset of granulocytes; CD56 identifies the extracellular immunoglobulin-like domain of the neural cell adhesion molecule (NCAM), expressed on NK cells. CD3 − CD16+CD56 + cells are defined as NK cells.

(5) CD19: The B-cell-specific antigen with a molecular weight of 90 kDa is identified, expressed on approximately 7%-23% of peripheral blood lymphocytes. It is present in B cells at all developmental stages but does not react with T cells, granulocytes, or monocytes. CD3 − CD19+ cells are defined as B cells.

(6) CD45: Identifies the leukocyte common antigen (T200) with a molecular weight of 180–220 kDa, expressed on all leukocytes (lymphocytes, monocytes, granulocytes, etc.), and is used to assist in lymphocyte gating and signal regulation-related analyses.

Based on the aforementioned molecular marker combinations, data analysis was performed using BD FACSCanto clinical software with the following gating strategy: First, the leukocyte population was delineated via a scatter plot of CD45 versus SSC; Within the lymphocyte gate, T cells (CD3+) and non-T cells (CD3−) were distinguished using a scatter plot of CD3 versus SSC; Among the CD3 + cell population, CD4 + T cells and CD8 + T cells were differentiated through a scatter plot of CD4 versus CD8; Within the CD3 − cell population, B cells were identified by CD19, and NK cells were recognized by CD16 + CD56. The detailed gating strategy is illustrated in Fig. 1.

The results are reported as the number of positive cells per microliter of blood (absolute count) or the percentage of positive cells among the total CD45-positive lymphocytes. All ratios mentioned in the results are calculated using absolute counts. During the analysis process, by comparing the number of cells with the number of microspheres, the absolute count of positive cells in the sample (cells/µL) can be determined. We use BD FACSCanto clinical software, and the absolute count of positive cells can be directly obtained through this software. The specific calculation formula is as follows:

(Number of positive cells in the cell population ÷ Number of microspheres in the absolute count region) × Number of microspheres per test ÷ Test volume = Absolute count of the cell population.

The number of microspheres per test can be found on the foil bag label of the BD Trucount tube.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was conducted using R language (version 4.4.1) and SPSS software (version 27.0). The significance level was set at p < 0.05, and all tests were two-sided tests.

Segmented regression model

Using the R package “segmented” to analyze the inflection point positions of immune parameters with age. The model takes age as the independent variable and immune parameters as the dependent variables, estimating one or more inflection points through iterative methods, and comparing the differences in linear slopes before and after the inflection points. A 1000-time bootstrap resampling was performed to calculate the 95% confidence intervals of the inflection point ages. The significance of slope changes was evaluated using Davies’ test.

Comparison between groups

Since the data did not conform to a normal distribution (as determined by the Shapiro-Wilk test), the Kruskal-Wallis test was employed for comparisons among multiple groups. If the overall difference was significant, the Bonferroni correction method was further applied for pairwise comparisons. For comparisons between two groups, the Mann-Whitney U test was used.

Correlation analysis

The Spearman rank correlation coefficient was used to assess the correlation between immune parameters and age.

Principal component analysis (PCA)

To evaluate the overall trend of immune status changes with age from a multi-parameter integration perspective, this study conducted principal component analysis (PCA) on all 11 immune parameters. The data were first standardized, and the Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin (KMO) test and Bartlett’s test of sphericity were employed to assess the suitability of the data for PCA. Principal components with eigenvalues greater than 1 were extracted, and varimax rotation was applied to enhance interpretability. The component score coefficient matrix after rotation was used to calculate the scores for each principal component. Ultimately, the composite immune index (CII) was derived as the weighted sum of the principal component scores, with the weights being the proportion of each principal component’s variance contribution to the cumulative contribution rate.

Gender difference analysis

Based on the piecewise regression model which identified a breakpoint at age 63, we defined a post hoc subgroup of participants aged ≥ 63 years for sex-stratified analysis. This threshold differs from the predefined age groups used earlier (youth, young and middle-aged, middle-aged and elderly, elderly), and the analysis should be considered exploratory. A total of 926 participants aged ≥ 63 years were included in this gender difference analysis. The Mann-Whitney U test was used to compare the differences between males and females across 11 immune parameters, with results presented as median and interquartile range. All analyses were conducted in R language (version 4.4.1), with the significance level set at p < 0.05.

Research results

This study systematically analyzed the age-related changes in peripheral blood lymphocyte subsets of 6,550 healthy Chinese adults aged 18 to 96 through high-resolution flow cytometry. By combining piecewise regression models, principal component analysis and comparisons among multiple groups, it revealed the unique trajectory, key inflection points and gender difference characteristics of immune aging in the Chinese population. The piecewise regression model identified multiple inflection points where significant slope changes occurred in immune parameters at specific ages. Among them, a total of six parameters were identified with inflection points(Table 4): NK cells(cells/µL), CD4 + T cells(cells/µL), CD8 + T cells(%), CD4+/CD8 + T cells, Total B cells(cells/µL), Total T cells(cells/µL). The inflection points of the percentage of CD8 + T cells and the ratio of CD4+/CD8 + T cells both occur at approximately 54 years old, reflecting that the cellular immune balance begins to shift significantly in the later middle age. The inflection point of NK cell quantity was 54.98 years old, but the slope change did not reach a significant level ( p = 0.157), suggesting that its growth trend with age was relatively stable. The inflection points of CD4⁺ T cells and B cells are located at 63.12 years and 62.00 years respectively. The inflection point of the total T cell count is the latest, at 67.50 years old (Fig. 2). These inflection points are data‑derived estimates from the current cohort; their generalizability to other populations should be validated in future longitudinal studies.

The Bartlett sphericity test was significant (p < 0.001), rejecting the null hypothesis that the correlation matrix is an identity matrix (Table 7). The first four principal components were extracted, with a cumulative contribution rate of 97.252%, effectively capturing the vast majority of immune variation information (Table 8). Finally, we obtain a comprehensive indicator, the “composite immune index”, abbreviated as CII. Its value is the sum of the scores of each component multiplied by its load. The calculation formula is ultimately simplified as:

CII = 0.439× CD4+ T cells(cells/µL) + 0.452× CD4+ T cells(%)- 0.008× CD8+ T cells(cells/µL)- 0.198× CD8+ T cells(%)- 0.227× NK cells(cells/µL)- 0.383× NK cells(%) + 0.358×CD4/ CD8 + 0.455× Total B cells(cells/µL) + 0.421× Total B cells(%) + 0.265× Total T cells(cells/µL) + 0.198× Total T cells(%).

The Spearman rank correlation test was used to examine the correlation between this Index and age. It was found that there was a significant negative correlation between CII and age (p = -0.208, p < 0.001), this indicates that the index can reflect the overall trend of immune parameters changing with age. CII showed a significant inflection point at the age of 63 (p < 0.001, 59.47–66.53). The annual rate of decline was 2.64 units/year before the inflection point, and rose sharply to 10.66 units/year after the inflection point. Moreover, the dispersion of CII values increased with age, exhibiting a broader distribution range in elderly individuals (Fig. 4).

There were significant differences in several immune parameters between males and females at age ≥ 63 years (Table 10; Fig. 5). The number and proportion of CD4 + T cells, CD4+/CD8 + ratio, number and proportion of B cells, and total T cell proportion were significantly higher in females than in males, reflecting that females maintain stronger adaptive immune reserve during aging. Males showed a higher number and proportion of NK cells. There was no significant difference in CD8 + T cell parameters between genders, indicating that the senescence changes of this subpopulation were less affected by gender. These differences suggest that gender is an important factor affecting the evolutionary process of lymphocytes, which should be fully considered in clinical evaluation and health intervention.

Discussion

This large‑sample cross‑sectional study systematically delineated the age‑associated trajectories of peripheral blood lymphocyte subsets in healthy Chinese adults. Using piecewise regression, we identified key inflection points where the rate of change in major lymphocyte subsets accelerates, most notably at age 63 for adaptive immune components. We further constructed a composite immune index (CII) that captures the overall shift in lymphocyte composition and revealed significant sex‑specific patterns in individuals aged ≥ 63 years. These findings provide essential reference data for the Chinese population and offer insights into the dynamics of immune aging.

1. Phased changes in lymphocyte subsets and their biological interpretation.

Our results demonstrate that the age‑related evolution of lymphocyte subsets is not a uniform process but occurs in distinct phases marked by specific inflection points. The percentage of CD8 + T cells and the CD4+/CD8 + ratio exhibited significant changes around age 54. This inflection reflects the cumulative effects of thymic involution, which begins in early adulthood and leads to a progressive decline in naïve T cell output27,28. Concomitantly, lifelong exposure to environmental antigens, particularly persistent viruses such as cytomegalovirus (CMV), drives the expansion of oligoclonal memory CD8 + T cells, a phenomenon known as memory inflation29. Although CMV serostatus was not available in our study, previous research has shown that CMV infection can profoundly alter T cell subset composition and may contribute to the inflection point observed in CD8 + T cell parameters3,30. The subsequent acceleration of CD4 + T cell and B cell decline after ages 63.12 and 62.00, respectively, aligns with the concept of a “second wave” of adaptive immune aging, involving reduced bone marrow output, impaired germinal center reactions, and altered cytokine milieus31–35. The latest inflection point for total T cell count (67.50 years) represent a stage of exhaustion of the overall T cell pool34.

Our findings are broadly consistent with recent large‑scale studies in other populations. A German study (CoNAN) analyzing 231 healthy adults aged 19–93 years using mass cytometry also identified a significant decline in CD4 + T cells between 58 and 63 years and a decline in CD8 + T cells at 53–55 years36. Similarly, a Dutch study using delayed exponential modeling reported age‑dependent normalization functions for T lymphocytes, with distinct phases of decline4. These cross‑population concordances suggest that the inflection points we identified may reflect biologically meaningful transitions, although the exact ages may vary across populations and analytical methods. Notably, the age 63 inflection point identified in this study should be considered a data‑derived estimate from the current cohort rather than a fixed clinical or universal biological threshold.

2. Heterogeneity among lymphocyte subsets: adaptive versus innate immunity.

The trajectories of different lymphocyte subsets were clearly distinct. While adaptive immune parameters (CD4 + T cells, CD8 + T cells, B cells) showed pronounced inflection points and progressive declines, NK cell counts increased steadily throughout adulthood without a statistically significant inflection. This divergence supports the notion that innate immune components may undergo compensatory expansion to offset the waning of adaptive immunity37–40. However, it is important to note that NK cell numbers do not necessarily equate to function; age‑related declines in NK cell cytotoxicity and cytokine production have been documented37. Future studies combining phenotypic and functional assessments are needed to fully understand the role of NK cells in immune aging.

3. Composite immune index: capturing multidimensional age‑related changes.

To integrate multiple immune parameters into a single metric, we constructed the CII using principal component analysis. The index showed a strong negative correlation with age and exhibited a sharp inflection at 63 years, with the rate of decline increasing from − 2.64 to − 10.66 units/year thereafter. Notably, the dispersion of CII values increased with age, a pattern consistent with the well‑recognized phenomenon of increasing inter‑individual heterogeneity during aging6. This heterogeneity may arise from differences in genetic background, environmental exposures (CMV, nutrition), and lifestyle factors24,25. Our PCA revealed that the parameters contributing most to the variance were CD4 + T cell count, CD4 + T cell percentage, CD4+/CD8 + ratio, and B cell percentage, underscoring the centrality of these measures in tracking the evolutionary process of lymphocyte subsets. The CII is thus best understood as an exploratory composite metric that summarizes age-associated shifts in lymphocyte subset composition, rather than a validated biological index with established mechanistic meaning. Given the moderate KMO value and the inherent correlations among parameters (e.g., total T cell count is naturally related to CD4 + and CD8 + counts), the CII should be viewed as a compressed composite phenotypic axis rather than an independent biological entity.

4. Sexual dimorphism in late‑life immune profiles.

In participants aged ≥ 63 years, we observed pronounced sex differences: females maintained higher levels of CD4 + T cells, B cells, CD4+/CD8 + ratio, and total T cell percentage, while males exhibited higher NK cell counts and percentages. These findings are in line with the known female advantage in humoral immunity and longevity41,42. Mechanistically, sex hormones and X‑linked immune genes are plausible contributors; estrogen has been shown to enhance B cell survival and antibody production, whereas testosterone may suppress certain immune functions43–46. However, our dataset does not include hormonal or molecular measures, so these remain hypotheses rather than demonstrated mechanisms. Males showed a higher number and proportion of NK cells. This sex difference may be partially explained by Y chromosome-encoded regulators such as UTY, which has been reported to repress NK cell development and maturation40. Additionally, the higher NK cell numbers in elderly males could represent a compensatory response to earlier adaptive decline, a possibility suggested by recent work demonstrating accelerated naive CD8 + T cell decline in males39. This results in reduced lymph node cellularity and impaired antigen recognition capacity in middle-aged and elderly males compared to females. In response to this contraction of the adaptive compartment, the innate immune system may upregulate NK cell numbers as a compensatory mechanism to maintain overall immune surveillance. This compensatory shift could be driven by homeostatic cytokines such as IL‑15, which is known to promote NK cell proliferation and is often elevated in settings of T cell lymphopenia47. However, it remains unclear whether the increased NK cell abundance in males is accompanied by preserved functionality, some evidence suggests altered maturation and reduced cytotoxic capacity40. Thus, while the numerical expansion of NK cells may partially offset adaptive deficits, this interpretation should be considered speculative without functional validation39,40. Importantly, no sex differences were observed in CD8 + T cell parameters, suggesting that this subset is less influenced by sex, due to its strong dependence on CMV‑driven memory inflation, which affect both sexes similarly3,48. These sex‑specific patterns underscore the need for gender‑aware approaches in immune health assessment and intervention.

Limitations and future directions

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the cross‑sectional design precludes inference of individual‑level longitudinal trajectories; future studies with repeated measurements are needed to validate the inflection points. Second, we did not assess CMV serostatus, which is a major confounder in T cell aging studies; inclusion of CMV data in future work will help disentangle its contribution. Third, our study focused on phenotypic quantification and lacked functional assays (T cell proliferation, cytokine secretion) and molecular markers (telomere length, epigenetic clocks), which would provide a more comprehensive picture of immune aging. Finally, the single‑center design may limit generalizability to the entire Chinese population.

Conclusion

In summary, this study provides the first large‑scale description of age‑associated changes in major lymphocyte subsets in healthy Chinese adults, identifying age 63 as a data‑driven inflection point associated with accelerated adaptive immune decline. The composite immune index (CII) provides an exploratory multidimensional summary of lymphocyte subset composition, and the observed sex differences highlight the importance of personalized health strategies. These findings establish a population‑specific reference framework for immune monitoring and early intervention, contributing to the promotion of healthy aging in China.
