Development and validation of radiopathomics models for predicting molecular subtypes and WHO grades in adult-type diffuse gliomas: a multicenter study
Authors: Qian Liang, Xin Duan, Haili Yan, Xuan Li, Zehui Li, Wenju Niu, Xu Liu, Yan Tan, Xiaochun Wang, Guoqiang Yang, Tao Bai, Xiangli Yang, Hui Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundEarly identification of molecular subtypes and WHO grades in adult-type diffuse gliomas (ADGs) provides critical evidence for prognostic evaluation and personalized therapeutic decision-making. This study aims to develop and validate radiopathomics models for the prediction of molecular subtypes and WHO grades in ADGs, addressing the limitations of unimodal approaches.MethodsIn this retrospective multicenter study, 499 consecutive ADG patients from three centers (training set: n = 306, testing set: n = 132, external validation set: n = 61) were included. Radiomics features were extracted from preoperative MRI sequences (T2-FLAIR and CE-T1WI), while pathomics features were derived from whole-slide images (WSIs). Feature selection methods and Multilayer Perceptron (MLP) classifier were performed to construct radiomics, pathomics, and radiopathomics models for molecular subtype classification and ADG grading. The performance of the model was evaluated using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves, area under the curve (AUC), accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, and F1 score. Decision curve analysis (DCA) was performed to assess clinical efficacy. The Shapley Additive Explanation (SHAP) analysis was employed to explore the interpretability of models.ResultsFor discriminating molecular subtypes, the radiopathomics model demonstrated superior performance compared to standalone radiomics or pathomics models, achieving AUCs (macro/micro) of 0.847/0.864 in the testing set, and AUCs (macro/micro) of 0.858/0.867 in the external validation set. For differentiating WHO grades, the radiopathomics model achieved superior performance compared to models based solely on radiomics or pathomics features. The AUCs for the radiopathomics model were 0.849 (95% CI 0.775–0.915) in the testing set and 0.855 (95% CI 0.748–0.945) in the external validation set. DCA confirmed superior net clinical benefit across wider risk thresholds compared to unimodal alternatives. SHAP analysis provided interpretable insights into the predictive significance and contributions of individual features.ConclusionThe proposed radiopathomics models demonstrate robust diagnostic performance by synergizing cross-scale features, offering a clinically actionable tool for ADG stratification.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12967-025-07073-2.

Introduction

Gliomas are the most common malignant tumors of the central nervous system, exhibiting significant heterogeneity in molecular characteristics, histological features, and clinical outcomes [1]. The fifth edition of the WHO Classification of Central Nervous System Tumors (WHO CNS5) [2], published in 2021, redefined adult-type diffuse gliomas (ADGs) into three molecular subtypes: (1) astrocytoma, IDH-mutant, (2) oligodendroglioma, IDH-mutant and 1p/19q-codeleted, (3) glioblastoma, IDH-wildtype. Additionally, the 2021 WHO CNS5 classification specifies that: (1) IDH-mutant astrocytomas with homozygous deletion of cyclin-dependent kinase inhibitor 2A/B (CDKN2A/B) are classified as CNS WHO grade 4. (2) Adult-type IDH-wildtype diffuse astrocytomas harboring one or more of the following molecular alterations are directly defined as CNS WHO grade 4: telomerase reverse transcriptase (TERT) promoter mutation, epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) gene amplification, and chromosome 7 gain combined with chromosome 10 loss (the + 7/− 10 signature). Therefore, IDH-mutant astrocytomas are graded as WHO grades 2, 3, or 4; oligodendrogliomas are classified as grades 2 or 3, and IDH-wildtype glioblastomas are designated as grade 4. Notably, grades 2 or 3 are categorized as lower-grade gliomas (LGGs), whereas grade 4 corresponds to high-grade gliomas (HGGs) [3]. Given that both molecular subtypes and tumor grades critically influence prognosis and treatment selection [4, 5], their accurate preoperative determination is essential for prognostic assessment and personalized therapeutic planning.

Currently, the confirmation of molecular subtypes and tumor grades relies on histopathological assessment combined with gene testing. However, this approach is susceptible to interobserver variability, high financial burden, and time-consuming, thereby facing significant challenges. Moreover, molecular diagnostics have not been widely adopted in routine clinical management. Therefore, a reliable alternative method for early diagnostic stratification of ADGs is urgently needed.

By leveraging high-dimensional imaging feature analysis, radiomics provides comprehensive tumor characterization, offering improved diagnostic stratification and treatment guidance [6, 7]. Previous studies have indicated that radiomics serves as a promising tool for evaluating molecular markers and grades of gliomas [8–14]. Recently, Guo et al. attracted widespread attention for their three-class radiomics model classifying molecular subtypes of ADGs based on conventional magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and diffusion weighted imaging (DWI) [15]. Although radiomics provides robust quantification of tumor characterization, its clinical utility may be limited by the inability to capture cellular/molecular-level microscopic information. Pathomics enables quantitative analysis of digitized whole-slide images (WSIs) derived from histopathological specimens, providing in-depth analysis of microarchitectural tissue alterations at the microscopic level [16]. This approach facilitates molecular subtyping [17, 18], pathological staging [19], and prognostic prediction [20–22] in oncology. The integration of radiomics and pathomics offers an opportunity to characterize tumor heterogeneity across macroscopic and microscopic scales, where these complementary approaches may synergistically generate more robust composite biomarkers [23].

While such multi-scale integration enhances biomarker discovery, the interpretability of these complex models remains challenging. Shapley Additive Explanations (SHAP) is an advanced interpretability technique that effectively deciphers the "black-box" nature of conventional machine learning models by providing both global and local explanations [24]. This approach can identify key predictive features and quantify their sample-level contributions, with interpretable visualizations that elucidate the model’s decision-making process for clinicians [25].

In this study, we aim to develop radiopathomics models integrating multiparameter MRI with WSIs to predict molecular subtypes and WHO grades of ADGs, with external validation in an independent cohort to assess their generalizability and performance consistency. Additionally, The SHAP analysis was employed to explore the interpretation of model.

Materials and methods

Patients

This study retrospectively collected 601 consecutive patients with gliomas from three tertiary hospitals between December 2011 and March 2023: Shanxi Medical University First Hospital (Center 1), Shanxi Provincial People’s Hospital (Center 2), and Shanxi Bethune Hospital (Center 3). The three hospitals are affiliated with Shanxi Medical University. The research protocol was approved by the ethics committee of Shanxi Medical University (approval number: 2021-K-K073). Given the retrospective nature of the study, informed consent was waived. All patients across the three centers were subjected to uniform inclusion/exclusion criteria at enrollment. The inclusion criteria included: (1) age ≥ 18 years; (2) patients with central nervous system tumors who were pathologically diagnosed with gliomas following surgical resection; (3) availability of genetic data for diagnosing ADGs and classifying/grading them based on the 2021 WHO CNS5; (4) availability of preoperative MRI images (including T2-FLAIR and CE-T1WI images) and WSIs. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) age < 18 years; (2) incomplete genetic information and MRI sequences; (3) image segmentation was not feasible due to poor image quality; (4) history of surgery, radiotherapy, or chemotherapy. The flowchart of patient inclusion and exclusion is presented in Fig. 1. Gene testing

To analyze IDH, CDKN2A/B, and TERT promoter status in glioma specimens, genomic DNA was extracted from formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tissues using the Simplex OUP FFPE DNA Extraction Kit (TIB, Shanghai, China). Subsequent PCR amplification was performed using the Applied Biosystems 9700 Thermal Cycler (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), followed by Sanger sequencing analysis on the Applied Biosystems 3500 Genetic Analyzer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). For ATRX and TP53 mutation detection, genomic DNA extracted from paraffin-embedded tissues using the Simplex FFPE DNA Extraction Kit (Tiangen Biotech, Beijing, China) was spectrophotometrically quantified and analyzed by Sanger sequencing. Additionally, fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) was employed to assess 1p/19q deletions, EGFR amplification, and the + 7/− 10 signature using the reagent kit (VividFISHTM FISH CEP, GeneCopoire).

Image acquisition, processing and segmentation

Patients from three centers underwent scanning on the Signa HDxt, GE Healthcare, USA, 3.0T, Discovery 750, GE Healthcare, USA, 3.0T, and Siemens Skyra, 3.0T MRI scanners to acquire preoperative MRI images. The detailed scanning protocol is provided in Supplementary Information I (Table S1). T2-FLAIR images were rigidly registered to the CE-T1WI reference using ITK-SNAP software (version 3.8.0, http://www.itksnap.org). To standardize spatial resolution across datasets from different MRI scanners, all images were resampled to 1 × 1 × 1 mm3 isotropic voxels using B-spline interpolation. Subsequently, intensity normalization was performed on all images using histogram matching. The tumor region was manually delineated on CE-T1WI as the region of interest (ROI), excluding surrounding peritumoral edema. Then, the delineated ROI was registered onto the corresponding T2-FLAIR image. The ROI segmentation was performed by a radiologist with over 10 years of experience and reviewed by another radiologist with more than 20 years of experience.

WSIs were obtained by scanning hematoxylin and eosin (H&E)-stained histopathological slides using the Pannoramic SCAN digital slide scanner (3DHISTECH Ltd., Budapest, Hungary). All images were digitized and saved in MRXS file format. Image processing was performed using Aperio ImageScope software (version 12.4.6, https://www.leicabiosystems.com/digital-pathology/manage/aperio-imagescope). WSIs were magnified 20 ×, and the pathologist selected 10 representative non-overlapping image patches at 1000 × 1000 pixels, containing the most tumor information, as the ROIs. The final results were reviewed by a pathologist with over 20 years of experience. All image tiles underwent color normalization via the Macenko method for stain separation and consistency.

Feature extraction

Radiomics features were extracted by PyRadiomics package. Finally, 2394 radiomics features were extracted from tumor ROIs of T2-FLAIR and CE-T1WI images, including seven types of features: shape features (n = 28), first-order features (n = 468), grey level co-occurrence matrix (GLCM) features (n = 572), grey level run length matrix (GLRLM) features (n = 416), grey level size zone matrix (GLSZM) features (n = 416), grey level difference matrix (GLDM) features (n = 364), and neighboring gray-tone difference matrix (NGTDM) features (n = 130).

CellProfiler software (https://cellprofiler.org, version 4.2.7) [26] was employed to extract pathomics features from image patches. We extracted features from each image patch using two pipelines: Object Recognition Measurement and Whole Image Measurement. The average of the features from 10 tiles per patient was calculated as the final pathomics features. Ultimately, 1110 pathomics features were extracted for each patient. Additional details on the extracted pathomics features are available in Supplementary Information II.

Feature selection

Z-score normalization was applied to standardize both radiomics and pathomics features. The normality of the radiomics and pathomics features was evaluated using the Shapiro–Wilk test. For features associated with molecular subtypes classification, those exhibiting a normal distribution were subjected to analysis of variance, whereas non-normally distributed features were analyzed using the Kruskal–Wallis test. Regarding features associated with WHO grades, normally distributed features were assessed via independent samples t-test, while non-normally distributed features were examined using the Mann–Whitney U test. Features with P values less than 0.05 were retained for further selection. The Minimum Redundancy Maximum Relevance (mRMR) algorithm was then employed to prioritize features by maximizing their relevance to the target variable while minimizing inter-feature redundancy. Subsequently, Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) regression was applied to further refine the feature set, with the optimal value of λ determined via 10 fold cross-validation, and features with non-zero coefficients were selected as the optimal radiomics and pathomics features. Correlation analysis and Recursive Feature Elimination (RFE) were employed to identify the optimal radiopathomics features from selected radiomics and pathomics features.

Development and validation of predictive models

Following feature selection, the final three feature sets were input into six classifiers: XGBoost (XGB), Support Vector Machine (SVM), Logistic regression (LR), k-Nearest Neighbors (KNN), Random Forest (RF), and Multilayer Perceptron (MLP). Hyperparameter tuning was performed using a grid search algorithm with five fold cross-validation to optimize model performance. The optimal classifier for each feature set was then selected based on performance metrics to construct radiomics, pathomics, and radiopathomics models for molecular subtype classification and ADGs grading tasks. The predictive performance was evaluated on both the testing and external validation sets. Figure 2 illustrates the workflow of the predictive model construction in this study. The performance of each model was comprehensively assessed using the following metrics: receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve, area under the curve (AUC), accuracy, sensitivity, specificity and F1-score. For the ADG grading models, the Delong test was used to compare AUC differences between models. Model interpretation

SHAP analysis was performed to enhance the interpretability of the radiopathomics model through both global and local explanations. Features are ranked by their importance, with higher-ranking features indicating greater contributions. Additionally, the summary plots visualize the directional influences of features, distinguishing positive and negative contributions to model output. SHAP force plots visually demonstrate the contribution magnitude of each feature to the prediction outcome for individual samples.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analysis were performed using Jupyter Notebook (version 6.5.4), R (version 4.4.2), and IBM SPSS Statistics (version 26.0). Continuous variables were compared using the independent sample t-test or Mann–Whitney U test, while categorical variables were analyzed using the chi-square test. A two-sided P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Feature selection and model development

A total of 2394 radiomics features and 1110 pathomics features were initially extracted. Following LASSO logistic regression, we retained 22 optimal radiomics features and 18 optimal pathomics features for molecular subtypes classification, as well as 23 optimal radiomics features and 16 optimal pathomics features for ADGs grading. Subsequent correlation analysis and RFE further refined the selection, yielding 20 optimal radiopathomics features for molecular subtypes classification and 18 optimal radiopathomics features for ADGs grading. The optimal radiomics, pathomics, and radiopathomics features for both tasks are illustrated in Fig. 3. For each task, six classifiers were applied to three distinct feature sets. Finally, the MLP classifier delivered the best predictive performance in both tasks and was therefore selected to develop radiomics, pathomics, and radiopathomics models for molecular subtypes classification and ADGs grading. Multilayer Perceptron (MLP) is a feedforward neural network that enables complex pattern recognition through hierarchically transformed nonlinear representations [27]. The ROC curves for the six classifiers in each model can be found in supplementary information III (Figs. S1–S4). Performance of three models for molecular subtypes classification

Performance of ADGs grading models

The performance metrics of the three models for ADGs grading across all datasets are summarized in Table 3. As shown in Fig. 5, the radiopathomics model consistently outperformed both unimodal models, demonstrating superior discriminative ability with AUCs of 0.901 (95% CI 0.864–0.938), 0.849 (95% CI 0.775–0.915), and 0.855 (95% CI 0.748–0.945) in the training, testing, and external validation sets, respectively. Comparative analysis revealed that the radiomics model yielded AUCs of 0.846 (95% CI 0.791–0.895), 0.783 (95% CI 0.682–0.874), and 0.788 (95% CI 0.616–0.925), while the pathomics model showed slightly lower performance with corresponding AUCs of 0.823 (95% CI 0.771–0.866), 0.760 (95% CI 0.676–0.840), and 0.716 (95% CI 0.533–0.887). Statistical comparison using the Delong test confirmed the radiopathomics model’s significantly higher AUC in the testing set compared to both the radiomics (P = 0.035) and pathomics (P = 0.046) models (Fig. 6a and b). Although no statistically significant difference in AUC was observed among the three models in the external validation set, DCA curves demonstrated that the radiopathomics model offers a greater net benefit across a reasonable threshold range. Discussion

Early and accurate identification of molecular subtypes and WHO grades in ADGs is critical for advancing precision medicine in glioma management. In this study, we developed radiomics, pathomics, and radiopathomics models using MRI and WSI data from ADG patients for the prediction of molecular subtypes and WHO grades. The results demonstrate that the radiopathomics models effectively integrate macroscopic and microscopic tumor information, exhibiting superior predictive performance compared to single-modality models. The enhanced classification accuracy of our multimodal approaches highlights their critical value in the stratified early diagnosis of ADGs, which facilitates prognostic assessment and personalized management of ADG patients.

Since molecular subtyping was first introduced into glioma diagnosis in the 2016 WHO classifications of gliomas [28], a growing number of studies have employed radiomic biomarkers to predict molecular subtypes and WHO grades [29–31]. Following the fifth edition update, the tumor type and WHO grade of gliomas have undergone significant changes due to the incorporation of high-risk molecular markers such as TERT, EGFR, CDKN2A/B, and + 7/− 10 signature. Moodi et al. [32] evaluated radiomics-based machine learning (ML) algorithms for glioma grading across various WHO criteria, demonstrating that ML algorithms performed better based on WHO 2021 criteria. Wang et al. [33] employed ultra-high b-value DWI for ADG tumor subtype differentiation, showing excellent performance with ADCb500/b6000 (glioblastoma vs. astrocytoma, AUC = 0.818), ADCb500/b8000 and ADCb500/b10000 (glioblastoma vs. oligodendroglioma, AUC = 0.979), and ADCb500/b10000 (astrocytoma vs. oligodendroglioma, AUC = 0.773). Wu et al. [34] evaluated the utility of amide proton transfer-weighted (APTw) imaging-derived radiomics model in classifying ADGs, achieving AUCs of 0.87 for IDH mutation prediction, 0.83 for glioblastoma-astrocytoma differentiation, 0.95 for glioblastoma-oligodendroglioma distinction, 0.63 for oligodendrogliomas-astrocytomas differentiation, and 0.86 for tumor grading. Guo et al. [15] developed a three-class multiparametric radiomics model for molecular subtype classification, yielded internal/external validation AUCs of 0.8181/0.7571 for IDH-mutant and 1p/19q-noncodeleted, 0.8524/0.7779 for IDH wild-type, and 0.8502/0.7491 for IDH-mutant and 1p/19q-codeleted. Although previous studies have demonstrated encouraging results, limitations such as small sample sizes, lack of external validation, and the cumbersome multi-step binary classification approach for molecular subtype classification may hinder model translation. Additionally, while radiographic imaging captures the spatial macrostructure of tumors, it may overlook in-depth microstructural information regarding cellular properties and microenvironmental characteristics within local tumor lesions [35]. Thus, the standalone radiomics approach may fail to achieve adequate predictive accuracy and stability in certain scenarios.

Pathomics features provide information at different scales compared to radiomics, revealing tumor heterogeneity from a microscopic perspective and reflecting underlying molecular characteristics or genetic patterns [36]. Recent advances in glioma research have demonstrated the potential of pathomics. Luo et al. [37] developed a deep learning model utilizing histopathological images of glioma patients, achieving concordance indices of 0.630 and 0.652 for predicting disease-free survival and overall survival, respectively. Liechty et al. [38] employed a machine learning model to predict IDH mutation status in infiltrative gliomas from WSIs, with an AUC of 0.881. Brindha et al. [39] employed a Shallow Neural Network classifier based on WSIs to distinguish between glioblastoma and low-grade glioma, achieving an accuracy of 97.21%. Therefore, we incorporated pathomics features in this study, which may overcome the limitations of the single-modality approach and improve predictive performance. To our knowledge, studies integrating radiomics and pathomics features for predicting molecular subtypes and WHO grades of ADGs remain extremely limited under the 2021 WHO CNS5 criteria, which constitutes a key innovation of this study.

Through the integration of radiomics and pathomics features, we developed comprehensive radiopathomics models that demonstrated superior accuracy and stability in predicting molecular subtypes and WHO grades of ADGs compared to single-modality approaches. External validation confirmed the generalizability of the radiopathomics models. Specifically, we developed a three-class radiophathomics model providing direct molecular subtype stratification of ADGs. The overall performance was evaluated using macro-AUC, micro-AUC, and overall accuracy. Our approach overcomes single-modality limitations and achieves improved classification accuracy, suggesting significant incremental value from pathomics features. These findings are consistent with the study by Hsu et al. [40], where a fusion method combining multiparameter MRI and WSIs was developed for predicting glioma subtypes according to the 2016 WHO classification criteria. The results by Guo et al. [15] demonstrated the incremental value of DWI in a multiparametric radiomics model for molecular subtype classification. However, this study only focused on conventional MRI sequences. The incorporation of advanced MRI sequences may provide greater incremental value in future studies. Since the 2021 WHO CNS5 update, the WHO grade of ADGs has significantly changed. Previously classified LGGs based on 2016 WHO criteria with high-risk molecular alterations are now categorized as CNS WHO grade 4. Our grading model achieved accurate discrimination between LGG and HGG, demonstrating improved predictive metrics compared to single radiomics and pathomics approaches. DCA revealed consistently higher net benefits across a wide range of probability thresholds, offering more precise support for clinical decision-making. These findings demonstrate the complementary advantages of radiomics and pathomics features in the classification and grading of ADGs, underscoring the critical importance of multimodal data integration.

SHAP analysis provides an intuitive visualization of feature importance. In both tasks of molecular subtypes classification and ADGs grading, the features that contribute most significantly to the model outputs are all derived from the CE-T1WI sequence. Among them, the wavelet-based GLDM texture feature demonstrated the most contribution to molecular subtype prediction, and the wavelet-derived first-order feature exhibited the greatest influence on tumor grading. SHAP force plot visualization for individual samples reveals the magnitude and direction of each feature’s contribution to model predictions, along with their synergistic effects. This interpretability improves model transparency and trustworthiness, enabling clinicians to better comprehend its decision-making process and supporting its integration into clinical practice.

Clinical implementation of the proposed models would enable early identification of molecular subtype and grade of ADGs through preprocessed image analysis, potentially sparing select patients from costly genetic testing while significantly conserving healthcare resources and alleviating financial burdens. Additionally, the model’s high specificity prevents low-risk patients from being misclassified as high-risk, thereby optimizing treatment decisions and enhancing patient management efficiency. Notably, DCA demonstrated significant net clinical benefit across a wide range of probability thresholds, confirming the model’s utility in clinical decision-making. This study delivers a translatable decision-support tool for precision glioma management, with a multicenter validation framework and demonstrated clinical benefit collectively supporting its broader implementation in clinical practice.

Although this study reveals significant findings, several limitations should be acknowledged. Firstly, although data were collected from multiple centers, this retrospective study retains inherent limitations such as selection bias and inadequate confounding control, necessitating prospective validation. Secondly, while SHAP analysis improves the interpretability of our radiopathomics model, the biological mechanisms linking radiopathomics features to immune evasion and tumor microenvironment (TME) alterations remain incompletely defined. Future work will elucidate mechanistic links between these features and TME/immune evasion through integrated multi-omics data, spatial transcriptomics, single-cell profiling, and experimental validation, enhancing biological plausibility. Furthermore, manual ROI segmentation on radiological and histopathological images is inefficient and suffers from inter-observer variability, warranting investigation of AI-based auto-segmentation to optimize workflow efficiency. In addition, the radiomics and pathomics features in this study were based on traditional feature extraction methods. Adopting more advanced feature extraction techniques may capture more comprehensive tumor information, thereby facilitating model improvement. On the other hand, this study advances early stratification of ADGs through integrated radiomics and pathomics features. In future work, we plan to incorporate additional multi-omics data (e.g., genomics) to optimize model performance. Finally, this study only incorporated radiomics features derived from conventional MRI sequences. In the future, it is advisable to integrate advanced MRI sequences (e.g., DWI/ADC, perfusion-weighted imaging, and DKI) to optimize the models.

Conclusion

This study developed radiopathomics models by integrating MRI-derived radiomics features and WSI-based pathomics features to predict molecular subtypes and WHO grades of ADGs. The radiopathomics models demonstrated superior predictive performance compared to single-modality models. These findings support their potential for improving early diagnosis and precision management of ADGs.
