Syk inhibition limits autoimmunity and abnormal B cell phenotype and function in mice with B cell-specific TRAF3 deficiency
Authors: Emma L Hornick, Kyp Oxley, Ivy L Debreceni, Nathaniel Wieting, Tiffany K Ybarra, Bruce S Hostager, Scott M Lieberman, Gail A Bishop
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAutoimmune disorders reduce quality of life and lifespan and can increase B cell lymphoma risk. The adaptor protein TRAF3 regulates B cell survival, activation, and differentiation by restraining signaling through Toll-like receptors, tumor necrosis factor (TNF) receptor superfamily members, and the B cell antigen receptor—pathways linked to autoimmunity and lymphoma. Mice lacking TRAF3 in B cells (B-Traf3−/−) develop both autoimmunity and B cell malignancies. TRAF3 negatively regulates activation of spleen tyrosine kinase (Syk), which is involved in multiple B cell signaling pathways. B-Traf3−/− mice treated with the Food and Drug Administration (FDA)-approved Syk inhibitor Fostamatinib, exhibited reduction in autoantibodies, gland inflammation, and expansion of autoimmune-associated B cell populations. Fostamatinib also corrected enhanced pro-survival proteins and aberrant B cell survival in Traf3−/− B cells, without inhibiting humoral responses to immunization. These findings identify elevated Syk activation as a key driver of B cell dysfunction and autoimmunity in B-Traf3−/− mice, suggesting therapeutic potential for Syk inhibitors in conditions linked to decreased B cell TRAF3 levels.

Introduction

The cytoplasmic and nuclear adapter protein tumor necrosis factor (TNF) receptor-associated factor 3 (TRAF3) is a critical negative regulator of signaling pathways promoting survival and activation in B cells.1 TRAF3 restrains B cell homeostatic survival, glucose uptake and metabolism, as well as signaling through TNFR superfamily receptors (eg CD40 and BAFF receptors), Toll-like receptors (TLRs), the B cell antigen receptor (BCR), and cytokine receptors.2–10 The cumulative effect of these dysregulated pathways in B cell-specific TRAF3 knockout mice (B-Traf3−/−) is hypergammaglobulinemia, autoimmunity, and lymphadenopathy that progresses to B cell lymphoma in mice ≥9mo of age.2,11,12 TRAF3 also exerts negative regulatory functions in human B cells. Human B cell malignancies regularly have loss-of-function somatic mutations in TRAF3,13 but the early lethality of mice with whole-body Traf3 deficiency14 suggested that human germline TRAF3 mutations were unlikely. However, Rae et al. recently described several unrelated families with monoallelic loss-of-function TRAF3 mutations causing TRAF3 haploinsufficiency (TRAF3HI) syndrome.15 The common findings in TRAF3HI syndrome-autoimmunity—enlarged secondary lymphoid organs and hypergammaglobulinemia- are very similar to the phenotype of the B-Traf3−/− mice, emphasizing the major contribution of B cell dysfunction to TRAF3HI syndrome.2,15,16 Inborn errors of immunity affecting TRAF3 are rare, but GWAS data reveal that common genetic variants that reduce TRAF3 expression are associated with increased risk of B cell malignancy and autoimmunity.13,15,17–20 Consistent with these data, our recent work shows that even decrease of 30–40% in the amount of B cell TRAF3 is sufficient to alter TRAF3-regulated signaling pathways and proteins.21 Importantly, this degree of post-translational loss of TRAF3 protein is also seen in B cells from healthy aging humans and mice, so TRAF3 loss can result from multiple processes.

We recently described TRAF3’s participation in BCR and TLR signaling, noting that spleen tyrosine kinase (Syk) is hyperphosphorylated in resting cells and after TLR or BCR activation in TRAF3-deficient B cells compared to TRAF3-sufficient counterparts.8,9 Indeed, Syk and TRAF3 regulate many of the same pathways in B cells. Syk typically acts as a positive regulator by adding activating phosphorylation to its substrates, whereas TRAF3 performs its negative regulatory functions by controlling interactions between signaling effectors and their targets.22 For example, TRAF3 dampens IL-6R signaling by recruiting the inhibitory phosphatase PTPN22 to the IL-6R signaling complex, where PTPN22 dephosphorylates JAK1 and STAT35. TRAF3 restrains B cell TLR signaling by blocking access of the positive regulator TRAF6 to TLR complex-associated proteins, including IRAK1 and Syk.9 Syk promotes BCR signaling by binding to CD79A/B at phosphorylated immunoreceptor tyrosine-based activating motifs and initiating downstream signaling cascades that drive BCR-responsive survival, plasma cell differentiation, and germinal center formation.23,24 Syk’s potentiation of BCR signaling is also linked to its positive regulation of TLR signaling and BAFFR signaling, both of which can synergize with BCR to augment B cell activation and survival.23–26 Unchecked Syk activation—a result of B cell TRAF3 deficiency8,9 - has a similar effect to a loss of B cell TRAF3: mice develop hypergammaglobulinemia, autoantibodies, and B cell malignancies.22,27,28

Fostamatinib (commercial name Tavalisse) is the only Syk inhibitor approved by the Food and Drug Administration (FDA). Fostamatinib is metabolized to its active form (R406), which is an ATP-competitive inhibitor of Syk’s catalytic domain.29 Fostamatinib is used for treatment of chronic Immune Thrombocytopenia (ITP).30 ITP is an autoimmune disorder in which autoantibodies trigger Fc receptor (FcR)-mediated phagocytosis of platelets by macrophages.30 Syk activation is a central event in FcR signaling, thus Fostamatinib halts the destruction of platelets and improves disease symptoms.22,28 B cell-driven autoimmune diseases are a promising target for Syk inhibitors. Fostamatinib improves disease in mouse models of systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) and other autoimmune conditions.22,28,31 Syk inhibitors also show promise as a treatment for B cell malignancies due to their ability to interfere with signaling pathways that promote B cell survival.27 Notably, Syk inhibition reverses BAFFR/BCR-mediated upregulation of the anti-apoptotic protein Mcl1, which is significantly elevated in TRAF3-deficient B cells.6,32 Given the substantial overlap between pathways regulated by Syk and TRAF3 in B cells, we hypothesized that Syk inhibition would restrain some of the signaling pathways that are dysregulated in the absence of TRAF3, and that this could prevent development of spontaneous autoimmunity seen in B-Traf3−/− mice.2 As this autoimmunity precedes development of B cell lymphoma, a link also seen in humans, alleviation of autoimmunity could preclude this enhanced susceptibility to later-life malignancies.33

We show here that sustained Fostamatinib treatment reduced spleen and lymph node size and cellularity, and B cell numbers in B-Traf3−/− mice, with pronounced effects on expanded B cell populations. Molecular analysis revealed that Fostamatinib lowered key TRAF3-regulated signaling protein levels, including those related to enhanced survival. Fostamatinib-treated B-Traf3−/− mice also had lower levels of autoantibodies and immune cell infiltration of salivary and lacrimal glands, as seen in Sjögren’s Disease (SjD). These results reinforce our emerging understanding of Syk as an important player in TRAF3-regulated signaling pathways, and have important implications for managing the consequences of reduced B cell TRAF3 seen in several human clinical conditions.

Materials and methods

Mice

We previously described the generation and screening of B cell-specific TRAF3-deficient mice (B-Traf3−/−).2 Briefly, loxP-flanked Traf3 alleles are deleted from B cells specifically by breeding to a Cd19-driven Cre-expressing mouse (JAX mouse strain #006789). Mice with 2 LoxP-flanked Traf3 alleles and 1 Cd19-Cre allele are referred to as “B-Traf3−/−”. We considered 2 options for control mice with normal B cell TRAF3 expression: littermates of the B-Traf3−/− mice that lack the Cd19-Cre allele or Cd19-Cre+/- mice without a floxed Traf3 allele. Although the latter have been reported to show alterations in the B cell compartment,34 these changes largely differ from the B-Traf3−/− phenotype and can even be opposite to those observed in B-Traf3−/− mice (eg reduced MZ B cells). Given that littermates are born to the same dam and are co-housed, giving them similar microbiomes, we selected the littermates and refer to these as “WT” controls throughout. This design was most appropriate for our study, which focused on differences between drug-treated and untreated mice, rather than on the presence or absence of B cell TRAF3. Upon weaning, cohorts of age- and sex-matched mice were fed irradiated OpenStandard diet (“control chow,” D11112201, ResearchDiets, New Brunswick, New Jersey, USA), or OpenStandard diet D11112201 with3g/kg Fostamatinib added (“Fostamatinib chow,” ResearchDiets and Rigel Pharmaceuticals, San Francisco, California, USA). Control and Fostamatinib chow were provided to us by Rigel Pharmaceuticals. WT and B-Traf3−/− mice within each cohort were housed together. All animals were housed in specific pathogen free facilities and used in accordance with National Institutes of Health (NIH) guidelines. Experiments were carried out as described in animal protocol no. 4052397, which was approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee at the University of Iowa.

Tissue processing, B cell isolation, B cell culture

Spleens, lymph nodes (LNs), femurs, and tibiae were harvested from each cohort of control and Fostamatinib-treated mice. Spleens were weighed, then spleens and LNs were mashed through 70 µm mesh in Hank’s Balanced Salt Solution (Gibco Life Technologies, Waltham, Massachusetts, USA) containing 2% fetal bovine serum (FBS) to generate a single cell suspension. Approximately 5 × 106 splenocytes and all LN cells from each animal were set aside for flow cytometric analysis (described below). B cells were enriched from the remaining splenocytes with a mouse B cell negative selection kit (no. 19854, StemCell Technologies, Vancouver, Canada). For in vitro culture with R406 (active metabolite of Fostamatinib, MedChem Express) or DMSO, B cells were plated at 1x106 cells per well of a 24-well plate with R406 or equal volume of DMSO (vehicle) and counted daily.

Flow cytometry

Single cell suspensions of spleen, lymph node, and bone marrow were plated at 106 cells per well in a round-bottom 96-well plate (Corning, Corning, New York, USA) and washed once in Dulbecco’s Phosphate-Buffered Saline (Gibco). Viability staining with Live/Dead Blue viability dye (L23105, ThermoFisher Scientific, Waltham, Massachusetts, USA) was performed for 15 min at room temperature in PBS. Cells were washed once in PBS and once in FACS Buffer (PBS + 2% FBS + 0.02% NaN3), then stained with fluorescently labeled antibodies in FACS Buffer with 2% rat serum, CellBlox Plus (C001T06F01, ThermoFisher Scientific), and Brilliant Stain Buffer (566349, BD Biosciences, Franklin Lakes, New Jersey, USA) at room temperature for 30 min. Antibodies against the following targets were purchased from BioLegend (San Diego, California, USA): CD19 (clone: 6D5, conjugates: SparkBlue550, Pacific Blue); CD45R (RA3-6B2, SparkNIR 685); IgM (RMM-1, BV421); IgD (11-26c.2a, PE-Cy7); CD43 (S11, APC Fire 750); CD23 (B3B4, Pacific Blue, PE/Dazzle 594); CD21/35 (7E9, Pacific Blue, PE/Dazzle 594); CD11b (M1/70, AlexaFluor700); CD5 (53-7.3, APC, PE); CD138 (281-2, BV605, BV786); GL7 (GL7, PE); CD95 (SA367H8, FITC, APC); CD24 (30-F1, APC); CD3 (500A2, AlexaFluor700); Ly6C/G (RB6-8C5, AlexaFluor700); Ter119 (TER-119, AlexaFluor700). Antibodies recognizing CD93 (AA4.1, PE) and CD249/BP-1 (6C3, FITC) were purchased from ThermoFisher Scientific. After staining, cells were washed twice in FACS Buffer, then resuspended in 1X BD FACS Lysing Solution (BD Biosciences), and incubated at room temperature for 10 min. Cells were washed twice and resuspended in 1X PBS, then kept at 4 °C until data collection on an Aurora 5 L Flow Cytometer (Cytek Biosciences, Fremont, California, USA). Raw spectral flow data were unmixed using SpectroFlo (Cytek Biosciences) and analyzed in FlowJo (BD Biosciences).

Western blotting

Splenic B cells were lysed in 1X Radio Immunoprecipitation Assay Buffer (Cell Signaling Technologies, Danvers, Massachusetts, USA) with EDTA-free cOmplete mini protease inhibitor cocktail (Roche Pharmaceuticals, Indianapolis, Indiana, USA). Lysates were subjected to polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (NuPAGE system, ThermoFisher Scientific) and transferred to polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membranes with the XCell II blotting system (ThermoFisher Scientific), following manufacturer recommendations. Membranes were incubated in a solution of 5% nonfat milk in Tris-buffered saline with 0.01% Tween-20 (TBS-T) for 30 min at room temperature to block nonspecific binding of antibodies. PVDF membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C in primary antibody at the manufacturer’s recommended concentration. The following day, blots were washed in TBS-T and incubated with HRP-conjugated goat anti-mouse or rabbit IgG (Jackson ImmunoResearch Labs, West Grove, Pennsylvania, USA) for 2 h at room temperature. Blots were then visualized with SuperSignal West Pico or Femto substrate (ThermoFisher Scientific) and the LAS400 low-light imaging system (Fuji Medical Imaging, Lexington, Massachusetts. USA). Quantification of bands on Western blot images was performed with MultiGauge imaging software (Fuji Medical Imaging). Density values of bands for proteins of interest were divided by density values of bands from the loading control for the same sample to account for variations in total protein loading. Primary antibodies against the following proteins were purchased from Cell Signaling Technologies: Hxk2 (no. 2867); NFκB2 p100/p52 (no. 4882); c-Myc (no. 13987); and Mcl1 (no. 94296). Anti-Pim2 (PA5-22315) was purchased from ThermoFisher Scientific and anti-β-Actin (sc-47778) was purchased from Santa Cruz Biotechnologies (Santa Cruz, California, USA).

Serum immunoglobulin enzyme-linked immunosorbent assays (ELISAs)

Serum immunoglobulins were measured by ELISA as previously described.2 Unlabeled capture antibodies (rat anti-mouse IgA, goat anti-mouse IgG and IgM), HRP-conjugated detection antibodies (goat anti-mouse IgA, IgG1, IgG2a, IgG2b, IgG3, and IgM), and standards (unlabeled mouse IgA, IgG1, IgG2a, IgG2b, IgG3, and IgM) were purchased from Southern Biotech (Birmingham, AL). TMB Substrate and TMB Stop solution were purchased from Kirkegaard & Perry Laboratories (Gaithersburg, Maryland, USA). For 4-Hydroxy-3-nitrophenylacetyl (NP)-specific antibody ELISAs, plates were coated with 0.5 µg/well NP7-BSA (high-affinity Ab detection) or NP25-BSA (total NP Ab detection) generated as described35 or purchased from LGC, BioSearch Technologies (Hoddesdon, UK). Anti-mouse IgG conjugated to HRP (Southern Biotech) was used for detection, and mouse anti-NP IgG1 (clone B1-8) was used as a standard on each plate. Serum anti-double-stranded DNA antibodies were quantified using an anti-dsDNA ELISA kit (Chondrex, Woodinville, Washington, USA). For all ELISAs, absorbance at 450 nm was quantified on a Spectramax iD5 spectrophotometer.

Quantification of salivary and lacrimal gland inflammation

Tissue processing, staining, and scoring was performed as described.36 Briefly, formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded exorbital lacrimal glands and submandibular salivary glands were sectioned (5 µm) and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E). Two individuals independently quantified inflammation in a blinded manner, using a standard light microscope with a 10X objective. The average of their scores was used as the final focus score. A focus was defined as an aggregate of at least 50 mononuclear cells and the focus score defined as the number of foci per 4 mm2 of tissue. Tissue areas were calculated by ImageJ software using low magnification digital images obtained by scanning H&E-stained sections with the PathScan Enabler IV or PathScan Enabler V (Meyer Instruments, Houston, Texas, USA).37 Representative images from H&E stained sections of salivary and lacrimal glands to demonstrate normal tissue and typical focal lymphocytic inflammation were obtained with an Olympus BX6IVS and Olympus VS-ASW 2.9 software.

Immunization and induction of autoimmunity

To assess the humoral immune response to the hapten 4-Hydroxy-3-nitrophenylacetyl (NP) during inhibitor treatment, mice were fed control or Fostamatinib chow for 2 wk, then immunized via intraperitoneal injection with 20 µg NP conjugated to Keyhole Limpet Hemocyanin (MedChemExpress, Monmouth Junction, New Jersey, USA) in alum (Invivogen, San Diego, California, USA). Mice remained on control or Fostamatinib chow for 4 additional weeks, then tissues were harvested for analysis of NP-specific immune responses. To assess the effect of Fostamatinib treatment on the response of mice to an autoimmune “trigger,” mice received 3×/weekly applications of 5% Resiquimod cream, a TLR7 agonist, to the ear pinna for 4 wk.38 Mice began receiving Fostamatinib or control chow 2 wk prior to the start of treatments and remained on the chow for the duration of the treatments.

Statistical analysis

GraphPad Prism software was used for graphical representations of data and performing statistical tests. All pairwise comparisons performed are annotated in figures. The specific details of statistical tests, number of replicates, and number of individuals are included in figure legends.

Results

Rationale and experimental design

Both the B cell-specific TRAF3 knockout mouse (B-Traf3−/−) and humans with TRAF3 haploinsufficiency have autoimmune manifestations and abnormal expansion of B cell compartments.2,15,16 Mechanistically, TRAF3 deficiency removes a crucial brake on several signaling pathways implicated in pathogenesis of autoimmune and lymphoproliferative disorders, notably Toll-like receptor (TLR) and B cell antigen receptor (BCR) signaling. TRAF3 associates with spleen tyrosine kinase (Syk) early in both BCR and TLR signaling cascades, and Syk itself has increased activating phosphorylation in the absence of TRAF38,9 We thus investigated whether inhibition of Syk in B-Traf3−/− mice could correct the effects of B cell TRAF3 deficiency.

We elected to inhibit Syk by treating with chow containing 3 g/kg of the FDA-approved Syk inhibitor Fostamatinib. The 3 g/kg dose in chow completely inhibits activating phosphorylation of Syk and prevents skin and kidney disease in lupus-prone mouse strains.31,39,40 Abnormalities in the B cell compartment of B-Traf3−/− mice are evident by 8–12 wk of age,2 thus we started Syk inhibitor treatment at weaning and analyzed the effects after 12–16 wk of treatment (Fig. 1A).

The impact of fostamatinib-mediated Syk inhibition on B cell populations in WT and B-Traf3−/− mice

Fostamatinib treatment significantly reduced B-Traf3−/− mouse spleen weight, cellularity and splenic B cells compared to B-Traf3−/− mice fed control chow (Fig. 1B–L). All mature B cell populations are expanded in B-Traf3−/− mice, with particular enrichment of marginal zone (MZ) and germinal center (GC) B cells.2 Fostamatinib-treated B-Traf3−/− mice had significantly fewer splenic MZ B cells, GC B cells, and follicular B cells than control B-Traf3−/− mice (Fig. 1D–L). We found a similar reduction in LN B cells in B-Traf3−/− mice treated with Fostamatinib compared to control-treated mice (Fig. 1M, N). Our findings in WT mice were consistent with previous work showing that long-term Fostamatinib treatment reduces B cell numbers and populations in WT mice. However, strikingly, the magnitude of the decrease between treated and untreated WT mice was smaller than in B-Traf3−/− mice (Fig. 1B–N), suggesting the importance of elevated Syk activation in the expansion of these cellular populations in the B-Traf3−/− mice.

The effect of systemic Syk inhibition on TRAF3-regulated signaling proteins

Organ-level changes in B cell number and function are a consequence of altered signaling in TRAF3-deficient B cells.2 We thus investigated the effect of prolonged Syk inhibition on TRAF3-regulated signaling proteins in B cells from B-Traf3−/− mice and littermate controls. We began by examining constitutive NFκB2 activation, which is the only molecular event that occurs in all types of TRAF3-deficient cells, although it is only associated with increased survival of B cells, not of other cell types. In the absence of TRAF3, NFκB-inducing kinase (NIK) accumulates and initiates signaling events that lead to proteolytic processing of NFκB2 p100 to p52, which forms a transcriptionally active complex with RelB.41 Syk has not been directly implicated in NFκB2 activation events but participates in signaling cascades (eg BAFFR signaling) that can induce NFκB2 activation.24 B-Traf3−/− mice have ligand-independent BAFFR signaling.2 As expected, Traf3−/− B cells had an elevated amount of the NFκB2 subunit p52 compared to B cells from WT littermates in the control chow-fed cohort (Fig. 2A, B, and G). The increase in p52 in TRAF3-deficient B cells was accompanied by a corresponding decrease in p100 protein (Fig. 2A). Traf3−/− B cells from Fostamatinib-treated mice, however, had significantly reduced NFκB2 p52 compared to corresponding untreated Traf3−/− B cells (Fig. 2A, B, and G).

Constitutive NFκB2 activation contributes to the enhanced glucose uptake and metabolism in TRAF3-deficient B cells.7 B cell TRAF3 restrains the amount of key effector proteins, including Hexokinase 2 (Hxk2), which catalyzes the first step of glycolysis.7 We found that Traf3−/− B cells had higher cellular levels of Hxk2 than WT B cells in the control chow-fed male and female cohorts, though the magnitude of the response of B cells from Fostamatinib-treated male and female B-Traf3−/− mice differed by sex (Fig. 2A, C, and H). Traf3−/− B cells from Fostamatinib-treated female mice had significantly less Hxk2 than B cells from untreated female B-Traf3−/− mice, while the difference was not statistically significant in the male cohorts.

TRAF3 deficiency confers a homeostatic survival advantage to B cells partly due to an increase in the prosurvival proteins c-Myc and Pim2, and the anti-apoptotic Bcl2 family member Mcl1.2,6,10 B cells from both male and female B-Traf3−/− mice treated with Fostamatinib had significantly lower amounts of these proteins than B cells from B-Traf3−/− mice fed control chow (Fig. 2D–F and I–K). These results are consistent with and extend the data on the B cell populations shown in Fig. 1.

Our data indicated that Syk inhibition ameliorated abnormalities related to the enhanced homeostatic survival of TRAF3-deficient B cells. Therefore, we next asked whether inhibition of Syk in vitro would reverse the survival benefit conferred by B cell TRAF3 deficiency. Indeed, TRAF3-deficient B cells treated with different concentrations of Fostamatinib’s active metabolite, R406, showed a dose-dependent decrease in homeostatic survival of B cells in vitro (Fig. 2L). At the lowest dose (1.25 µM) survival of Traf3−/− B cells was significantly decreased compared to the vehicle (DMSO)-treated group (Fig. 2L), whereas the same dose caused a trending but not significant decrease in WT B cell survival (Fig. 2L).Treatment of Traf3−/− B cells with 2.5 µM R406 reduced their survival to significantly lower than vehicle-treated WT B cells after 24 hr of treatment, and not significantly different than untreated WT B cells at 48–96hrs, after which no live cells were detected in either group (Fig. 2L, M).Together these data show that Syk inhibition has a much more pronounced effect on the increased Traf3−/− B cell survival than on WT B cell survival, a result that may have promising translational implications.

Baseline and immunization-induced immunoglobulins in WT and B-Traf3−/− mice treated with Fostamatinib

The clinical presentation of patients with TRAF3 haploinsufficiency has substantial overlap with the phenotype of B-Traf3−/− mice, including hypergammaglobulinemia and altered humoral response to immunization.2,15 We thus next sought to determine the effect of Fostamatinib treatment on the level of total and immunization-induced immunoglobulins. As expected based on our previous studies,2 serum concentrations of IgA, IgM, IgG2a IgG2b, and IgG3 were significantly higher in the control chow-fed B-Traf3−/− mice than in WT mice (Fig. 3A–L). Fostamatinib treatment significantly decreased the serum concentration of all immunoglobulin isotypes in B-Traf3−/− mice to levels near control-treated WT levels in both male and female mice, with one exception: IgG1 was significantly decreased in B-Traf3−/− female mice following Fostamatinib treatment (Fig. 3C), but not in male mice (Fig. 3I).

B-Traf3−/− mice generate higher titers of NP-specific IgG than WT littermates when immunized with the T-dependent antigen TNP-KLH2. Thus, we next examined the effect of Fostamatinib treatment on the humoral response to immunization in B-Traf3−/− mice. Mice were weaned onto control or Fostamatinib-containing chow and treated for a total of 6 wk. Two weeks into their treatment, they were immunized with TNP-KLH in alum. Plasma cells and antibody titers were quantified four weeks later. Pre-immunization serum had no detectable NP-specific antibodies. There were significantly more plasma cells in the spleens B-Traf3−/− mice compared to WT mice regardless of treatment (Fig. 3M). There was no significant difference in the number of bone marrow plasma cells in B-Traf3−/− mice fed Fostamatinib or control chow (Fig. 3N), but both total and high-affinity NP-specific IgG titers were lower in the B-Traf3−/− mice treated with Fostamatinib than B-Traf3−/− mice fed control chow (Fig. 3O and P). Together, these data indicated that systemic inhibition of Syk significantly reduced the baseline elevation in serum immunoglobulins in B-Traf3−/− mice but did not prevent the development of a humoral immune response to immunization. This property may be important for the potential of Syk inhibition as a treatment for chronic autoimmune disease, without depleting B cells nor preventing effective responses to immunization.

The effect of systemic Syk inhibition on autoimmune manifestations in B-Traf3−/− mice

Results to this point showed that Syk inhibition was highly effective in reducing how TRAF3 deficiency promotes abnormal B cell survival and activation. We hypothesized that this treatment would alleviate the autoimmune manifestations seen in B-Traf3−/− mice. We previously described the presence of anti-dsDNA antibodies and deposition of immune complexes in the kidneys of B-Traf3−/− mice, though these mice do not show obvious external manifestations of kidney disease by adulthood.2 In contrast, the human patients with TRAF3 haploinsufficiency have autoantibodies and autoimmune diseases. The most common autoimmune disease among human patients was Sjögren Disease (SjD), thus we examined the lymphocytic infiltration of salivary and lacrimal glands of WT and B-Traf3−/− mice in both treatment groups. Blinded evaluators scored H&E-stained sections of lacrimal and salivary glands, assigning a focus score equal to the number of aggregates of ≥50 mononuclear cells (foci) per 4mm2 of tissue (Fig. 4A–C). The results were of different magnitude in males and females, but the overall trend was similar (Fig. 4B and C). B-Traf3−/− mice fed control chow had significantly greater focus scores than WT mice in both salivary and lacrimal glands (Fig. 4B and C). Interestingly, Fostamatinib treatment did not significantly reduce the magnitude of the focus scores in B-Traf3−/− mice at either site (Fig. 4B and C). However, the odds of having a focus score greater than zero in both lacrimal and salivary glands were significantly higher in B-Traf3−/− mice (Fig. 4D), suggesting a strong association between inflammation in both glands and a lack of B cell TRAF3 (Fig. 4D). In the Fostamatinib-treated groups, the association between inflammation in both glands and the B-Traf3−/− genotype was trending but not statistically significant. Together, these data suggest that salivary and lacrimal gland inflammation are elevated in B-Traf3−/− mice without Fostamatinib treatment, and that Fostamatinib treatment may modulate such inflammation in B-Traf3−/− mice.

B-Traf3−/− mice have significantly higher serum anti-dsDNA antibody titers than WT mice at baseline, and we found that Fostamatinib treatment decreased these titers, consistent with its effect on total serum immunoglobulins (Fig. 3 and Fig. 4E). We were interested to determine whether Fostamatinib treatment could protect B-Traf3−/− mice from developing anti-dsDNA antibodies in the presence of an autoimmune trigger. Sustained treatment of mice with epicutaneous TLR7 agonist Resiquimod induces anti-dsDNA antibodies, splenomegaly, and other autoimmune manifestations in WT mice,38 thus we tested this method of inducing autoimmunity in combination with Fostamatinib treatment. WT and B-Traf3−/− mice were fed control or Fostamatinib-containing chow for 2 wk prior to and during a 4-wk course of 3×/weekly topical Resiquimod, then serum anti-dsDNA antibody titers were quantified (Fig. 4F and G). Anti-dsDNA antibodies were elevated after Resiquimod treatment in the control chow-fed group compared to baseline, and the B-Traf3−/− mice had significantly higher titers than WT in the control cohort (Fig. 4F and G). Consistent with the effect on baseline autoantibody titers, the Fostamatinib-treated B-Traf3−/− mice had significantly less serum anti-dsDNA IgG than the corresponding control chow-fed group of B-Traf3−/− mice (Fig. 4F and G). Together, these data indicate that Fostamatinib treatment effectively suppressed anti-dsDNA autoantibodies in B-Traf3−/− mice at baseline and in the presence of an autoimmune “trigger” (Fig. 4F and G).

Discussion

Aberrant B cell expansion and autoimmunity can arise independently from either Syk hyperactivation or TRAF3 deficiency; yet whether and how Syk activity specifically contributes to the molecular and tissue-level effects of B cell TRAF3 deficiency was unclear.2,42 We were particularly interested in the role of Syk in autoimmune manifestations in B-Traf3−/− mice because of their overlap with clinical features seen in TRAF3HI patients.15 Syk and TRAF3 are key regulators of B cell TLR and BCR signaling, both of which are strongly implicated in pathogenesis of many autoimmune conditions.43–47 TRAF3 associates with Syk and restrains its activation in BCR signaling, as TRAF3-deficient B cells stimulated through the BCR show elevated phosphorylation of Syk and its downstream targets.8,12,48 TRAF3 also regulates TLR signaling intensity, limiting access of Syk and TRAF6 to the TLR/MyD88 complex.9 We thus predicted that Syk inhibition would have a substantial corrective effect on the molecular and tissue-level abnormalities in B-Traf3−/− mice because it would counteract dysregulation in both TLR and BCR signaling pathways.8,9 Indeed, our results reveal critical cross-talk between Syk and TRAF3 in B cell biology and highlight abnormal Syk activity as an important contributing factor to B cell dysfunction and autoimmunity in B-Traf3−/− mice.

Pharmacological inhibition of Syk with Fostamatinib resulted in pronounced reductions in spleen and lymph node size and B cell numbers in B-Traf3−/− mice (Fig. 1). Underpinning these tissue-level changes, Fostamatinib treatment altered the amount of key TRAF3-regulated proteins in B cells from B-Traf3−/− mice (Fig. 2). B cell NFκB2 p52, Hexokinase 2, c-Myc, Mcl1, and Pim2—all of which are typically increased in TRAF3-deficient B cells—were significantly reduced in B cells from Fostamatinib-treated B-Traf3−/− mice compared to those from B-Traf3−/− mice in the control group (Fig. 2). Consistent with the effect of Fostamatinib treatment on key survival-related proteins, in vitro treatment of Traf3−/− B cells with Fostamatinib’s active metabolite R406 caused a dose-dependent decrease in survival (Fig. 2). Autoimmune manifestations were also reduced in B-Traf3−/− mice. The baseline elevation in splenic GC B cells, serum immunoglobulins, and autoantibody titers were significantly decreased in Fostamatinib-treated B-Traf3−/− mice compared to B-Traf3−/− control mice (Figs. 1, 3, and 4). Importantly, Fostamatinib treatment was effective at suppressing development of anti-dsDNA antibodies even when mice were given an autoimmune “trigger” and did not prevent mice from developing a humoral immune response following immunization (Figs. 3 and 4). This is of potential clinical importance, as many B cell-suppressing treatments for autoimmunity deplete B cell numbers to well below normal levels and/or reduce normal B cell responses, putting treatment recipients at increased risk of multiple infections.

This is the first report of significantly elevated focal mononuclear cell infiltrates in lacrimal and salivary glands in B-Traf3−/− mice, to our knowledge. By definition, these histopathologic features of SjD are mononuclear cells, which typically comprise B and T lymphocytes. Non-mononuclear cells, such as neutrophils, are not included in the required minimum of 50 mononuclear cells to be counted as a focus. Histologically, these focal inflammatory infiltrates in our study appeared typical for SjD-type features in mice.36 We thus expect that these mononuclear cells were lymphocytes. However, additional studies would be necessary to confirm this histologically or by flow cytometric analysis of lacrimal and salivary glands. Moreover, SjD is characterized by several autoantibodies (including SS-A and SS-B along with others such as anti-muscarinic acetylcholine receptor type 3 antibodies) and development of gland dysfunction (eg decrease in tear and saliva volume).49 Additional studies are needed to more comprehensively characterize these mice as a potential model for SjD. Although not all mice had detectable inflammatory foci, we believe this is likely due to the age and the genetic background of the mice rather than a lack of effect from TRAF3-deficient B cells. There is a notable prevalence of SjD in TRAF3HI patients.15 There are also other indications that reduced B cell TRAF3 could be a contributing factor to B cell dysfunction in SjD pathogenesis. For example, elevated serum BAFF is common in SjD patients.50 BAFFR signaling induces ubiquitination and degradation of TRAF3, and repeated stimulation of BAFFR can cause sustained TRAF3 depletion.21 Finally, SjD is a disease most frequently diagnosed in the 5th or 6th decade of life, an age at which we recently demonstrated that TRAF3 protein level is reduced in human B cells.21 C57Bl/6 mice at the equivalent age (≥16 mos) develop spontaneous salivary gland inflammation and have reduced B cell TRAF3.21,51 Thus, we believe the salivary and lacrimal gland inflammation in B-Traf3−/− mice warrants further study, as it may have important implications for our understanding of the molecular pathogenesis of SjD.

Epstein–Barr virus (EBV) can cause a functional TRAF3 deficiency in B cells, as the EBV-encoded CD40-mimicking protein Latent Membrane Protein 1 tightly binds and sequesters B cell TRAF3, and EBV seropositivity is a risk factor for SjD and other autoimmune diseases.52 As mentioned above, EBV protein LMP1 binds tightly to TRAF3, significantly depleting cellular TRAF3 and causing abnormal signaling through TRAF3-regulated pathways that is reminiscent of genetically TRAF3-deficient B cells.53

The effect of Fostamatinib on autoimmune manifestations in B-Traf3−/− mice supports a small body of work arguing that Syk inhibition may merit further exploration as a treatment for autoimmune diseases beyond ITP, for which Fostamatinib is approved.28 Fostamatinib and other Syk inhibitors have shown promise in preclinical testing and in patients with rheumatoid arthritis and lupus, but larger studies are needed for clear efficacy data.22,28,39 Our work suggests future testing should include autoimmune diseases for which reduced B cell TRAF3 gene expression/protein levels is a risk factor, and could include assessment of individuals’ B cell TRAF3 levels to correlate with responsiveness to Fostamatinib treatment.15 Notably, the ability of Fostamatinib-treated mice to mount some degree of antigen-specific humoral response is a highly desirable feature for a treatment that must be administered long-term.

Viral infections are believed to be among the environmental “triggers” of autoimmune disease. For example, chronic infection with EBV has been linked to systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE), SjD, rheumatoid arthritis, and multiple sclerosis.54 Fostamatinib treatment was extremely effective at restraining development of anti-dsDNA antibodies in B-Traf3−/− mice at baseline (Fig. 4). Fostamatinib-treated mice that received repeated applications of Resiquimod, activating TLR7, developed significantly lower titers of anti-dsDNA antibodies than mice in the control group, raising the possibility that Fostamatinib treatment could be protective even in the face of real-world triggers for autoimmune disease or flares. Retaining ability to respond to infections or immunizations is also an important consideration for any treatment that modulates immune system function. Mice treated with Fostamatinib generated NP-specific IgG following immunization with TNP-KLH, albeit to a lesser extent than control-treated mice (Fig. 4). Interestingly, bone marrow plasma cells after immunization were not significantly higher in both Fostmatinib-treated and control B-Traf3−/− mice than in corresponding WT mice (Fig. 3). Enhanced IL-6R-induced STAT3 activation in B-Traf3−/− mice drives increased long-lived plasma cell accumulation.5 Although there is a report of IL-6-medated Syk activation in squamous cell carcinoma cells,55 there is no evidence that Syk directly regulates STAT3 activation. Syk inhibition likely had little effect on bone marrow plasma cell accumulation because the enhancement in B cell IL-6R signaling in B-Traf3−/− mice was intact, and long-lived plasma cell-driven immunity to previous infections or immunizations could therefore remain relatively intact following Syk inhibitor treatment.

One limitation of this study is that while the B-Traf3−/− mouse model recapitulates key features of TRAF3HI syndrome in humans, mouse models do not perfectly replicate human disease or physiology. It is important to note that humans with TRAF3 deficiency have such loss in all cells, and TRAF3 has cell-type-specific functions.56,57 However, it is striking that the role of TRAF3 in immune cells, particularly lymphocytes and monocytes, appears to be dominant in the disorder. Additionally, the 16-week treatment is too short to assess the effect on the B cell lymphomas that develop in B-Traf3−/− mice at around 9 mo of age.11 This is an important aspect of the phenotype, relevant to the lymphadenopathy in TRAF3HI patients, the increased incidence of lymphoma among individuals with autoimmune diseases, and the coincidence of reduced B cell TRAF3 and elevated rate of B cell malignancies in otherwise healthy adults over age 60. Because C57Bl/6 mice tend to develop multiple health problems at older ages, the large number of very old mice that would be required to study impacts of Syk inhibition of late-life BCL development presents feasibility challenges. Anecdotally, we did not note any gross abnormalities upon necropsy of Fostamatinib-treated animals, while 4 of the B-Traf3−/− mice in the control cohorts showed evidence of malignancy and were therefore excluded from our analysis. A subset of B cell malignancies respond robustly to Fostamatinib treatment due to their reliance on BCR signaling to upregulate pro-survival proteins including TRAF3-regulated c-Myc and Mcl16,10,58 Given this overlap and prevalence of mutations causing a loss or reduction of TRAF3 in B cell malignancies, it would be interesting to know the TRAF3 status of the malignant B cells in Fostamatinib responders.

In summary, our data identify Syk inhibition as a robust approach to counteracting the autoimmune effects and enhanced B cell numbers and functions observed in mouse B cell TRAF3 deficiency. Future studies should assess the durability of these effects, mechanisms of resistance and compensation, and the translational potential of Syk inhibitors for any condition or characteristic that reduces B cell TRAF3: patients with TRAF3HI syndrome, autoimmune diseases such as SjD featuring chronic B cell activation, older individuals, those with mutations that reduce TRAF3 expression, and TRAF3-deficient or EBV+ B cell malignancies.
