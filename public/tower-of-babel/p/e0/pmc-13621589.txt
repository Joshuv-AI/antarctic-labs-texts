Parent–child alienation, peer alienation, and sleep disturbance in Chinese college students: a longitudinal study of bidirectional relationships and gender differences
Authors: Kai Jing, Jingqun Wang, Baojuan Ye, Guorong Wang, Jinxing Wang, Shufang Zhou, Xuhong Song
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSleep disturbance is highly prevalent among college students and poses significant risks to mental health. However, the longitudinal relationships among parent–child alienation, peer alienation, and sleep disturbance remain poorly understood, particularly regarding gender differences.MethodsA three-wave longitudinal study involving 4,852 Chinese college students was conducted. The Random Intercept Cross-Lagged Panel Model (RI-CLPM) was used to examine bidirectional associations and mediating effects, and multigroup analyses were conducted to assess gender differences.ResultsAt the within-person level, parent–child alienation and sleep disturbance reciprocally predicted each other (β = 0.112–0.121 and β = 0.060–0.066, respectively; p < .05). Peer alienation predicted subsequent sleep disturbance in the total sample (β = 0.064–0.066, p < .01), but this association was significant only among girls (β = 0.222–0.274, p < .001). Sleep disturbance, in turn, predicted subsequent peer alienation in both genders. Furthermore, peer alienation mediated the association between parent–child alienation and sleep disturbance in the total sample (indirect effect = 0.006, 95% CI [0.002, 0.013]). Gender-specific analyses revealed partial mediation in both directions among girls (indirect effects = 0.047, 95% CI [0.026, 0.068] and 0.012, 95% CI [0.002, 0.023]), whereas no significant mediating effects were observed among boys. The standardized within-person effects were generally small (most βs < 0.12), suggesting a multifactorial etiology.ConclusionPeer alienation partially mediated the longitudinal association between parent–child alienation and sleep disturbance among girls but not among boys. These findings highlight interpersonal alienation as a meaningful target for gender-sensitive prevention and early-identification efforts aimed at improving sleep health among college students.

Introduction

Sleep disturbance in college students

Sleep disturbance is highly prevalent among university students worldwide. A recent systematic review and meta-analysis reported that the global prevalence of insomnia symptoms among undergraduate students was 46.9% [1]. In China, multi-center studies and campus-based surveys have similarly shown that the prevalence of sleep disorders among college students ranges from 26.4% to 39.3% [2–3]. Poor sleep has been linked to a range of adverse outcomes, including impaired attention and academic performance [4], increased burnout and psychological distress [5–6], and elevated risk of suicidal ideation [2, 7]. These findings indicate that sleep problems are not merely transient inconveniences but represent a significant public health concern in this age group.

Given the high prevalence and broad negative consequences of sleep disturbance, it is important to clarify the key correlates and risk processes that may underlie sleep problems in college students. Although numerous factors contribute to sleep disturbance, including academic stress, technology use, and lifestyle habits, interpersonal processes are increasingly recognized as important for sleep health. In particular, the quality of relationships with parents and peers, which constitute two major sources of emotional support and stress during emerging adulthood, may play a crucial role in the onset and maintenance of sleep difficulties. However, the longitudinal and potentially bidirectional links between interpersonal alienation in these relationships and sleep disturbance, especially in college students, remain insufficiently understood.

Interpersonal alienation and sleep disturbance

Interpersonal alienation refers to weakened emotional bonds, distrust, poor communication, and feelings of distance or rejection in close relationships [8]. During the college years, parent–child and peer relationships are especially salient, and experiences of alienation in these relationships may undermine psychological security and emotion-regulation capacity, increasing vulnerability to sleep disturbance. At the same time, sleep problems may impair emotional and social functioning, which could further exacerbate alienation in family and peer contexts.

Parent–child alienation and sleep disturbance

Attachment theory [9] provides a key framework for understanding associations between parent–child alienation and sleep disturbance. Early interactions with primary caregivers are internalized as “internal working models” that shape beliefs about self and others and influence socioemotional functioning across the lifespan. Secure attachment, fostered by caregivers’ emotional availability and sensitivity, promotes positive beliefs about others’ reliability and one’s own worth, as well as adaptive emotion-regulation abilities [10–11]. In contrast, parent–child alienation reflects a weakened emotional bond with parents, characterized by distrust, poor communication, and emotional distance [8]. Prolonged experiences of alienation may foster negative internal working models, undermine psychological security, and increase attachment anxiety [12–13], which can impair emotion regulation [14] and, in turn, increase vulnerability to sleep disturbances.

Empirical evidence consistently links family relationships to sleep health. Among Chinese college students, positive family relationships and family cohesion contribute to better sleep quality and less bedtime procrastination, partly through more adaptive coping [15–16]. A recent meta-analysis further indicates that positive parenting behaviors—such as warmth and autonomy support—are associated with longer sleep duration, shorter sleep onset latency, and fewer sleep disturbances, whereas negative family relationship factors predict poorer adolescent sleep [17–18]. Longitudinal research also shows that family support can buffer the adverse effects of academic stress on sleep quality [19], and that family cohesion and support may reduce sleep disturbance by increasing security and lowering perceived stress [20–21]. Collectively, these findings suggest that alienated parent–child relationships may constitute an important interpersonal risk factor for sleep disturbance.

Family systems theory adds that family members are interdependent, so changes in one member’s functioning may influence other family relationships through systemic interaction patterns [22]. Sleep disturbance has been linked to heightened emotional reactivity, impaired emotion regulation, and persistent negative affect [23], all of which may undermine positive parent–child interactions. Individuals with persistent sleep problems may respond with withdrawal, irritability, or conflict during daily communication with parents, thereby increasing emotional distance within the family [24–25]. Longitudinal studies have shown that children’s sleep problems predict increases in maternal negative emotions, decreases in parental sensitivity, and greater parent–child conflict [26], and that shorter or more variable sleep predicts subsequent increases in family conflict [27–28]. Despite this growing evidence, most prior work has focused on how family relationships influence sleep, whereas comparatively less attention has been paid to whether sleep disturbance may in turn impair parent–child relationships [18, 29]. Clarifying the potential bidirectional relationship between parent–child alienation and sleep disturbance therefore requires longitudinal designs that can separate temporal directions of effect.

Peer alienation and sleep disturbance

Beyond the family system, peer relationships represent another key interpersonal context during the college years. Peer alienation refers to perceived weakening of emotional connections with peers, reflected in reduced intimacy, diminished trust, limited peer support, and feelings of rejection or isolation [8]. Attachment and social support theories suggest that peer relationships gradually become important sources of emotional security and social connectedness during adolescence and emerging adulthood [19, 30]. High-quality peer relationships help individuals develop adaptive internal working models and effective emotion-regulation abilities [31] and provide emotional buffering during stressful periods [32]. Individuals with secure peer attachment are more likely to regulate stress adaptively and maintain better sleep quality, whereas attachment insecurity may increase vulnerability to sleep disturbance [33]. In contrast, alienated or insecure peer relationships may deprive individuals of social support, leading to prolonged stress activation and increased risk of sleep problems [30].

Consistent with these perspectives, a growing literature documents close associations between peer relationships and sleep. Systematic reviews and meta-analyses indicate that social relationships and sleep mutually influence one another and that negative peer relationships and poor sleep show bidirectional associations over time [34–35]. Adolescents experiencing peer exclusion have been found to have a higher risk of difficulty falling asleep [36], and friends tend to exhibit similar sleep patterns and timing, suggesting interpersonal synchronization in sleep behaviors [37]. Among Chinese college students, better friendship quality has been linked to better sleep quality [38], and higher perceived peer support has been associated with lower risk of sleep complaints and short sleep duration [39]. Longitudinal findings further show that increases in peer victimization predict transitions from good sleep to poor sleep [40]. These results suggest that peer alienation may be an important interpersonal risk factor for sleep disturbance, potentially through heightened stress reactivity and impaired emotion regulation.

Sleep disturbance may also negatively influence peer relationships. Peer relationships in late adolescence and emerging adulthood are largely voluntary and reciprocal and depend on effective emotional expression, social engagement, and interpersonal responsiveness to maintain quality [41]. According to social interaction models and social identity theory [34, 42], sleep problems may impair social functioning by disrupting emotional and cognitive processes necessary for successful interpersonal interactions. Experimental work indicates that sleep deprivation impairs self-regulation and social behavior [43], while individuals with insomnia are more likely to perceive social rejection and show reduced willingness to seek emotional support after negative peer experiences [44]. Longitudinal and daily-process studies suggest that sleep problems predict poorer social ties [45], reduced prosocial behavior and increased social withdrawal [46], and heightened loneliness [47] over time, partly via impaired emotion regulation. Neuroimaging research further shows that sleep loss reduces activity in brain regions involved in social cognition and empathy, leading to decreased prosocial behavior and increased social withdrawal [46]. These findings collectively suggest that sleep disturbance and peer alienation may mutually reinforce one another over time.

Interplay between parent–child and peer alienation

Although parent–child and peer alienation each show independent associations with sleep disturbance, these two relationship systems do not operate in isolation. According to family systems theory, the family is the earliest social system individuals encounter, and family interaction patterns strongly shape later interpersonal functioning in other contexts [22, 48]. Parent–child alienation, as a form of emotional detachment within the family subsystem, may contribute to maladaptive interpersonal and emotional regulation patterns that generalize to peer relationships [49]. From a social learning perspective, communication styles, conflict resolution strategies, and emotional expression patterns learned in parent–child interactions may be carried into peer interactions [50]. Prolonged parent–child alienation may foster avoidant or conflictual interaction tendencies [51], making it more difficult to establish and maintain high-quality peer relationships and thereby increasing the risk of peer alienation [52]. Longitudinal studies support this perspective: maternal rejection has been shown to negatively predict later peer attachment [53], parental phubbing has been positively associated with adolescent peer alienation [52], and early social withdrawal predicts subsequent peer rejection and victimization [54]. These findings suggest that dysfunctional parent–child relationships may undermine adaptive peer functioning over time.

Peer difficulties may, in turn, have downstream implications for parent–child relationships. The developmental cascade model proposes that adjustment problems in one domain may generate cumulative consequences across multiple systems through ongoing emotional, behavioral, and cognitive transactions [55]. Peer alienation may heighten emotional distress, promote social withdrawal, and bias interpersonal cognitions, thereby negatively influencing interactions with parents and contributing to spillover from the peer system to the family system [55]. For example, distress stemming from peer problems may carry over into family interactions, impair emotion regulation during parent–child communication, and foster withdrawn or avoidant interpersonal styles that generalize to the family context. Empirical work provides preliminary support for such spillover processes: peer victimization has been found to predict subsequent increases in parental rejection and decreases in parental warmth [56], and peer attachment prospectively predicts parent–child attachment and lower subsequent parental rejection via perceived social support [53, 57]. Although these studies examine constructs related to, rather than identical with, alienation, they are broadly consistent with reciprocal links between family and peer relationship difficulties.

Current study

Despite this growing body of evidence, integrative longitudinal models that simultaneously examine parent–child alienation, peer alienation, and sleep disturbance remain scarce. This study is grounded in attachment theory [9], family systems theory [22], social learning theory [50], and the developmental cascade model [55]. We integrate these perspectives to propose a framework in which family and peer interpersonal processes are dynamically linked to sleep disturbance. From this perspective, parent–child alienation and peer alienation may function as interconnected interpersonal risk factors that both shape and are shaped by sleep disturbance over time. Attachment- and support-related processes may influence sleep via psychological security and emotion regulation [10, 14, 17], whereas family systems and cascade perspectives highlight bidirectional spillover among parent–child relationships, peer relationships, and sleep‑related functioning [22, 55]. However, most existing studies have examined only one direction of influence (for example, family to sleep [17, 18] or peers to sleep [34, 35]) or have focused on a single interpersonal system. Many rely on cross‑sectional or two‑wave designs that confound between‑person and within‑person effects [18, 35, 58]. Integrative longitudinal models that incorporate both parent–child and peer alienation together with sleep disturbance are still rare [34, 40, 59], and most longitudinal work has focused on children and adolescents during earlier developmental transitions rather than college students [18, 40, 54].

Within this framework, peer alienation may play a key mediating role in the longitudinal association between parent–child alienation and sleep disturbance. The developmental cascade model suggests that difficulties arising in one social system can spread to other systems over time through ongoing emotional, behavioral, and cognitive transactions [55]. In the forward direction, prolonged parent–child alienation may undermine functioning in the peer domain [52–53], thereby increasing the risk of peer alienation; at the same time, peer alienation has been linked to sleep problems [40] and related difficulties such as loneliness [60] and adverse interpersonal experiences [59], suggesting a potential mediating pathway from family difficulties to sleep disturbance via peer relationships. From the reverse perspective, sleep disturbance may impair emotion regulation [23] and social functioning [43, 45], thereby increasing vulnerability to peer alienation, which may then spill over into the family system and contribute to further deterioration in parent–child relationships [53, 56]. Together, these patterns suggest that peer alienation may mediate the longitudinal links between parent–child alienation and sleep disturbance, serving as a conduit through which difficulties in the family and peer systems reinforce each other over time.

The present study addresses these gaps using a three‑wave longitudinal design over one year among Chinese college students and the Random Intercept Cross-Lagged Panel Model (RI-CLPM) [58, 61]. This approach separates stable between‑person differences from within‑person dynamics and provides a more rigorous test of temporal ordering and bidirectional predictive associations among variables. We focus on parent–child alienation, peer alienation, and sleep disturbance within a single longitudinal framework and examine whether peer alienation mediates longitudinal links between parent–child alienation and sleep disturbance, building on prior work on family–sleep and peer–sleep processes [17, 34, 40]. We also extend existing research by concentrating on college students, an understudied group in this literature despite evidence that interpersonal relationships and sleep problems are highly salient during this developmental period [1, 5, 38].

Existing research further suggests that associations among parent–child relationships, peer relationships, and sleep disturbance may vary by gender [21, 40, 62]. For example, large multi‑university samples indicate that sleep problems are more prevalent among female than male college students [62], and some studies have reported gender differences in the effects of peer victimization, family support, and parental behaviors on sleep quality and peer alienation [21, 40, 52]. In the Chinese sociocultural context, traditional gender‑role norms and patterns of parental investment may also shape parent–child and peer relationships [63–65]. Because these cultural factors were not directly measured, the present study cannot empirically distinguish cultural from alternative explanations. Related interpretations should therefore be regarded as tentative and examined in future research. Building on this evidence, the present study additionally examines whether the longitudinal associations and mediating pathways among parent–child alienation, peer alienation, and sleep disturbance differ between male and female college students. Materials and methods

Participants

This study is part of a nationwide project conducted in Mainland China. Using random cluster sampling, six universities in Guangdong, Jiangsu, Jiangxi, Sichuan, Shaanxi, and Xinjiang were selected as survey sites. Three waves of longitudinal data were collected over one year, in September 2024, March 2025, and September 2025. The initial number of participants in each wave was 5,486, 5,377, and 5,235, respectively. After removing invalid responses due to student absence or patterned answering (e.g., selecting the same option for all items), a final sample of 4,852 participants who completed valid questionnaires across all three waves was included in the longitudinal analysis, resulting in an attrition rate of 11.67%. The valid sample comprised 2,362 males (48.68%) and 2,490 females (51.32%), with a mean age of 18.97 years (SD = 0.96). All participants were Chinese citizens. The majority were of Han ethnicity (n = 4,511, 92.99%), with the remaining 7.01% (n = 341) from other ethnic groups. To assess whether sample attrition introduced systematic bias, attrition analyses were performed. Results showed no significant differences between participants who dropped out (N = 634) and those included in the final analysis with respect to gender (χ² (1) = 0.95, p = .33) or age (t = -0.89, p = .37). Additionally, the two groups did not differ significantly in baseline (T1) scores for parent–child alienation (t = -0.59, p = .56), peer alienation (t = -1.37, p = .17), or sleep disturbance (t = -1.53, p = .13). These findings suggest there was no systematic attrition bias in the data.

Procedure

Before data collection, this study received approval from the Ethics Committee of the School of Psychology at Jiangxi Normal University (Approval Number: IRB-JXNU-PSY-2024045). Data were collected online via the Questionnaire Star platform. Assessments were administered by trained mental health educators who received uniform training during class sessions. The entire process was anonymous, and participants could withdraw at any time. Before starting, all participants were required to read an informed consent form and click “Agree” to continue with the survey; those who clicked “Disagree” were automatically directed to exit. All data were stored on encrypted servers and kept strictly confidential. Because the electronic questionnaire required responses to all items, there were no missing data at the item level.

Measurements

Parent–child alienation and peer alienation

Parent–child and peer alienation were measured using the Alienation subscales of the Inventory of Parent and Peer Attachment (IPPA) [8]. For parent–child alienation, the original IPPA includes separate six-item alienation subscales for father and mother, with identical item content differing only in the referent (e.g., “My father doesn’t understand…” vs. “My mother doesn’t understand…”). To create a unified indicator of parent–child alienation while minimizing redundancy between the father and mother subscales, we combined the two original six-item alienation subscales into a single six-item parent–child alienation scale by replacing the parent-specific referent with the unified term “parents” (e.g., “My parents doesn’t understand what I’m going through these days”). All items were rated on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (Strongly Disagree) to 5 (Strongly Agree).

To evaluate the structural validity of the adapted parent–child alienation scale, we conducted a confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) using the T1 data. A single-factor model was specified. The model showed good fit:χ2 (6) = 16.297, CFI = 0.999, TLI = 0.997, RMSEA = 0.017 (90% CI [0.007, 0.027]), and SRMR = 0.006. Standardized factor loadings ranged from 0.640 to 0.788 (all p < .001), indicating that all items loaded adequately on the latent construct. Internal consistency was good (Cronbach’s α = 0.867) in the current study.

Peer alienation was assessed using the seven-item peer alienation subscale from the same IPPA inventory [8], used without modification. A sample item is “My peers doesn’t understand what I’m going through these days,” rated on the same 5-point Likert scale. In the current study, the internal consistency for the peer alienation subscale was 0.811.

Sleep disturbance

Sleep disturbance was measured using the Sleep Disturbance subscale of the Chinese College Student Mental Health Screening Scale [66], a standardized instrument developed and validated for Chinese college students. The subscale assesses common subjective sleep-related complaints, including difficulty initiating or maintaining sleep, non-restorative sleep, and daytime sleepiness. Unlike multidimensional sleep measures such as the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI) [67], this subscale specifically focuses on perceived sleep disturbance symptoms. The subscale consists of four items (e.g., “Shallow sleep, easy to wake up” and “Feeling tired even after waking up”), rated on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (never) to 5 (always). Item scores were averaged, with higher scores indicating greater sleep disturbance. In the current study, the scale demonstrated good internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.837).

Data analysis

SPSS 27.0 was used for data management, descriptive statistics, reliability and validity analyses, common method bias tests [68], and correlation analyses among variables. Preliminary analyses were conducted in Mplus 8.3, including longitudinal measurement invariance testing for the core variables using ΔCFI and ΔRMSEA criteria [69], as well as calculation of intraclass correlation coefficients (ICCs) to assess the stability of individual traits over time.

To distinguish stable between-person differences from within-person dynamic processes, the RI-CLPM was used as the primary analytical framework [58, 61], with gender controlled by regressing it onto the random intercepts. To identify the optimal model specification, a series of nested RI-CLPMs with increasing equality constraints were systematically compared using chi-square difference tests. Specifically, a baseline model with freely estimated autoregressive and cross-lagged paths across time points was compared with models constraining autoregressive paths, cross-lagged paths, or both to equality over time. A non-significant chi-square difference test (p > .05) indicated that the more parsimonious constrained model should be retained.

All RI-CLPM analyses were conducted in Mplus 8.3 using maximum likelihood estimation (ML). In the RI-CLPM, each observed variable was decomposed into a trait-like random intercept and wave-specific within-person latent components. Autoregressive and cross-lagged paths among within-person factors were estimated to capture temporal dynamics, while within-wave covariances among within-person factors and covariances among random intercepts were freely estimated. Gender was specified as a predictor of the random intercepts.

Longitudinal indirect effects were tested using bias-corrected bootstrap confidence intervals based on 5,000 resamples. The indirect effects of peer alienation were operationalized as the products of the relevant cross-lagged paths across adjacent waves (e.g., parent–child alienation T1 → peer alienation T2 → sleep disturbance T3), estimated within the full RI-CLPM framework.

Finally, gender differences were examined using multi-group RI-CLPM analyses. An omnibus chi-square difference test first compared a fully constrained model, in which all cross-lagged paths were constrained to equality across genders, with an unconstrained model. When the omnibus test was significant, Wald tests were conducted to identify the specific cross-lagged paths that differed between males and females.

Results

Common method bias assessment

Potential common method variance (CMV) was evaluated by comparing a single-factor CFA model with a three-factor model (parent–child alienation, peer alienation, and sleep disturbance). At Wave 1, the single-factor model fit poorly: χ2 (119) = 9977.13, RMSEA = 0.119 (90% CI [0.117, 0.121]), CFI = 0.636, TLI = 0.584, SRMR = 0.099; in contrast, the three-factor model showed good fit: χ2 (106) = 1979.99, RMSEA = 0.055 (90% CI [0.053, 0.057]), CFI = 0.931, TLI = 0.911, SRMR = 0.047 (ΔCFI = 0.295, ΔRMSEA = 0.064). Wave 2 replicated this pattern, with the three-factor model again substantially outperforming the single-factor model (ΔCFI = 0.317, ΔRMSEA = 0.073). The consistent superiority of the three-factor model across waves suggests that common method variance was unlikely to pose a serious threat to the study’s validity [68].

The RI-CLPM and gender difference analysis results

Model comparison and selection

Chi‑square difference tests were conducted to compare the nested models (Table 3). Compared with the unconstrained model (M1), constraining the autoregressive paths to be equal across time (M2) produced a significant increase in chi‑square (Δχ²(3) = 25.00, p < .001), indicating a worse fit. Constraining the cross‑lagged paths to be equal (M3) did not significantly worsen fit (Δχ²(6) = 9.06, p = .170). However, further constraining both the autoregressive and cross‑lagged paths (M4) led to a significant deterioration relative to M1 (Δχ²(9) = 36.04, p < .001) and to M3 (Δχ²(3) = 26.99, p < .001). Therefore, M3, which imposes equality constraints only on cross‑lagged paths across time, was retained as the final model (Fig. 1). M3 exhibited good fit indices: χ²/df = 2.07, CFI = 0.999, TLI = 0.998, SRMR = 0.010, RMSEA = 0.015 (90% CI [0.007, 0.022]).

Cross‑lagged associations in the total sample

The RI‑CLPM revealed clear cross‑lagged patterns among parent–child alienation, peer alienation, and sleep disturbance in the total sample.

First, there was a significant bidirectional relationship between parent–child alienation and sleep disturbance, supporting Hypothesis 1. Parent–child alienation positively predicted subsequent sleep disturbance (T1 to T2: β = 0.121, p < .001; T2 to T3: β = 0.112, p < .001), and sleep disturbance positively predicted later parent–child alienation (T1 to T2: β = 0.066, p < .01; T2 to T3: β = 0.060, p < .05).

Second, peer alienation and sleep disturbance also showed reciprocal effects, supporting Hypothesis 2. Peer alienation positively predicted subsequent sleep disturbance (T1 to T2: β = 0.064, p < .01; T2 to T3: β = 0.066, p < .01), and sleep disturbance positively predicted later peer alienation (T1 to T2: β = 0.081, p < .001; T2 to T3: β = 0.067, p < .001).

Third, a unidirectional predictive relationship was observed between parent–child alienation and peer alienation: parent–child alienation significantly and positively predicted peer alienation at subsequent time points (T1 to T2: β = 0.085, p < .01; T2 to T3: β = 0.071, p < .01), whereas peer alienation at earlier time points did not significantly predict parent–child alienation (T1 to T2: β = 0.037, p = .090; T2 to T3: β = 0.038, p = .093). These findings partially support Hypothesis 3, which expected bidirectional effects.

Mediation via peer alienation in the total sample

Mediation analyses indicated that peer alienation significantly mediated the longitudinal association between parent–child alienation and subsequent sleep disturbance, but not the reverse pathway. Specifically, the indirect effect from T1 parent–child alienation to T3 sleep disturbance via T2 peer alienation was significant (indirect effect = 0.006, 95% CI [0.002, 0.013]). In contrast, the reverse indirect pathway from T1 sleep disturbance to T3 parent–child alienation via T2 peer alienation was not significant (indirect effect = 0.003, 95% CI [0.000, 0.008]). Therefore, Hypothesis 4 was partially supported. Notably, subsequent analyses suggested that these overall associations varied by gender.

Gender omnibus test and specific path differences

Using the optimal time-constrained model (M3), in which cross-lagged paths were constrained to be equal across time, multi-group analyses were conducted to examine gender differences. The omnibus chi-square difference test indicated that constraining all six cross-lagged paths to be equal across genders significantly worsened model fit (Δχ² (6) = 164.47, p < .001), suggesting overall gender differences in the cross-lagged associations (Table 4). Follow-up Wald tests were then conducted to identify the specific paths contributing to these differences (Table 5). Significant gender differences were observed for the paths PEA → PCA (Δb = 0.269, p < .001), PEA → SLD (Δb = 0.386, p < .001), and PCA → PEA (Δb = 0.080, p = .020), with stronger effects observed among girls than boys. No significant gender differences were found for the remaining three paths (PCA → SLD, SLD → PCA, and SLD → PEA). Accordingly, the unconstrained multi-group model was retained, and subsequent analyses were conducted separately for boys and girls (Fig. 2). These findings support Hypothesis 5.

Discussion

The present study examined longitudinal associations among parent–child alienation, peer alienation, and sleep disturbance in Chinese college students. Using a three‑wave RI‑CLPM, we found bidirectional associations between parent–child alienation and sleep disturbance, as well as between peer alienation and sleep disturbance, at the within‑person level. Parent–child alienation also prospectively predicted peer alienation, although the reverse path was not significant in the full sample. In addition, peer alienation mediated the longitudinal association from parent–child alienation to sleep disturbance in the total sample. Multi‑group analyses revealed that these dynamic processes varied by gender: peer alienation predicted subsequent sleep disturbance and parent–child alienation only among girls, and peer alienation mediated both directions of the parent–child alienation–sleep disturbance link in girls but not in boys.

Bidirectional relationship between parent–child alienation and sleep disturbance

The present study found a significant bidirectional predictive relationship between parent–child alienation and sleep disturbance among college students. This pattern aligns with attachment theory [9]. When individuals experience long‑term parent–child alienation, the absence of a secure emotional base may increase psychological stress and hyperarousal, thereby interfering with sleep initiation and maintenance [72–73]. In addition, impaired emotion-regulation ability associated with insecure attachment may make it more difficult to manage daily stress effectively, prolonging emotional arousal into the night [14]. Pre‑sleep rumination about interpersonal stressors may further contribute to sleep difficulties [74].

Sleep disturbance significantly predicted later parent–child alienation, consistent with family systems perspectives that emphasize spillover of individuals’ psychological states into family interactions [22]. Sleep disturbance involves daytime fatigue, irritability, impaired cognitive functioning, and emotion dysregulation [23], which can weaken individuals’ ability to sustain positive parent–child interactions. Sleep loss may also impair social‑cognitive processing. As a result, individuals may become more likely to view neutral parental behaviors as critical or rejecting, which can fuel avoidance or conflict. Neuroimaging evidence shows that sleep deprivation reduces activity in brain regions involved in social cognition and empathy (e.g., medial prefrontal cortex, temporoparietal junction), leading to reduced prosocial behavior and emotional understanding in close relationships [46]. In sum, sleep disturbance may gradually widen emotional distance in parent–child relationships.

Although bidirectional associations between parent–child alienation and sleep disturbance were observed in both boys and girls, Wald tests indicated no significant gender differences in these cross‑lagged paths. This suggests that the reciprocal association between parent–child alienation and sleep disturbance may represent a relatively robust developmental process across genders. Descriptively, the associations were somewhat stronger among girls, which may reflect greater sensitivity to interpersonal stress and a higher propensity for rumination in females, as reported in previous research [14, 62]. However, these mechanisms were not directly measured and such interpretations remain tentative.

Overall, these findings extend prior longitudinal evidence documenting reciprocal links between sleep problems and family functioning in children and adolescents [18, 26–28] to a college student population, suggesting that the interplay between parent–child relationship difficulties and sleep disturbance may persist into emerging adulthood.

Bidirectional relationship between peer alienation and sleep disturbance

We also found a significant bidirectional relationship between peer alienation and sleep disturbance in the total sample: peer alienation predicted later sleep disturbance, and sleep disturbance predicted later peer alienation, consistent with previous longitudinal and meta‑analytic findings [35]. Gender‑difference analyses, however, showed that the prospective effect of peer alienation on sleep disturbance was significant only among girls, whereas the reverse path from sleep disturbance to peer alienation was significant in both genders. Wald tests further indicated that the path from peer alienation to sleep disturbance was the strongest gender‑differentiated effect among all cross‑lagged paths.

When individuals cannot obtain effective emotional support during stress, prolonged psychological strain may disrupt sleep initiation and maintenance [30]. The present findings suggest that this interpersonal pathway to sleep disturbance may be particularly pronounced for girls. One possible explanation is that girls tend to place greater emotional importance on peer relationships and rely more heavily on peers for emotional support during adolescence and emerging adulthood [75]. Consequently, experiences of peer alienation may evoke stronger feelings of rejection and emotional distress among girls, increasing the likelihood of rumination and emotion dysregulation, which may in turn contribute to sleep problems [76]. Prior research has also documented stronger emotional and physiological responses to social rejection in females than in males [77], which may further help explain the stronger predictive association between peer alienation and sleep disturbance among girls, although this mechanism awaits direct empirical testing.

Unlike the pattern observed for girls, the association between peer alienation and sleep disturbance was not significant among boys. This may be because boys tend to cope with interpersonal stress through activity‑ or task‑oriented strategies rather than through repetitive emotional processing [78]. Their friendships are often more strongly organized around shared activities and competition than around emotional disclosure [79], which may reduce the direct emotional impact of peer alienation on sleep. Males are also more likely to respond to social stress through externalizing pathways, whereas females are more likely to exhibit internalizing responses such as anxiety and rumination [80]. Taken together, these patterns may contribute to the weaker link between peer alienation and sleep disturbance among boys.

Sleep disturbance significantly predicted later peer alienation for both genders. This finding is consistent with social interaction models suggesting that sleep problems impair social functioning and interpersonal adjustment [34]. The daytime fatigue, cognitive impairment, and emotion-regulation difficulties that accompany sleep loss may all undermine effectiveness in peer interactions [43]. Insufficient sleep may also impair accurate interpretation of social cues, increasing the likelihood of perceiving neutral interactions as rejecting or hostile and thereby contributing to avoidance or conflict [44]. Sleep disturbance may further promote social withdrawal, reducing opportunities to establish and maintain supportive peer relationships. These findings align with longitudinal and daily‑process evidence documenting the multifaceted social consequences of sleep loss, including disruptions to social ties, prosocial behavior, and loneliness [45–47]. Together, these cognitive, emotional, and behavioral consequences of poor sleep may help explain why sleep disturbance predicted subsequent increases in peer alienation across genders.

Bidirectional relationship between parent–child alienation and peer alienation

In the full sample, parent–child alienation significantly predicted subsequent peer alienation, whereas the reverse effect of peer alienation on later parent–child alienation was not significant. Gender‑specific analyses revealed a more nuanced pattern: among girls, parent–child alienation and peer alienation were reciprocally associated over time, whereas neither direction reached significance among boys. Wald tests indicated that both the path from parent–child alienation to peer alienation and the path from peer alienation to parent–child alienation were significantly stronger in girls than in boys.

The longitudinal association between parent–child alienation and later peer alienation is consistent with family systems and social learning perspectives [22, 48, 50]. Individuals experiencing long‑term parent–child alienation may gradually develop avoidant or distant interpersonal styles, undermining the formation and maintenance of supportive peer relationships [51]. Attachment theory further suggests that alienated parent–child relationships contribute to negative internal working models characterized by expectations that others are unreliable or emotionally unavailable [49]. Such expectations may make individuals more vigilant, less trusting, and more socially withdrawn in peer contexts, thereby increasing the risk of peer alienation. Prior longitudinal research documenting links between parental rejection and later peer difficulties [52–53] is broadly consistent with this pattern.

This parent–child‑to‑peer pathway was significant only among girls. As discussed above, girls’ greater sensitivity to relational stress and tendency toward internalizing responses [75] may make experiences of parent–child alienation more likely to spill over into peer interactions and foster interpersonal insecurity and withdrawal [49]. By contrast, boys’ relationships with parents may be more oriented toward autonomy, rules, and shared activities, which could attenuate emotional spillover from the family system to peer relationships [65]. Cultural factors, such as gender‑role expectations and differential parenting practices, may further shape these patterns in the Chinese context [63–65, 81], though these mechanisms remain speculative in the absence of direct assessment.

The reverse pathway from peer alienation to later parent–child alienation, was significant only among girls, broadly consistent with developmental cascade models that posit spillover of adjustment difficulties across domains over time [55]. As noted above, girls’ heightened reactivity to interpersonal stress [75–76] may cause peer alienation to evoke stronger and more persistent distress that spills over into family interactions and impairs parent–child communication. Boys, on the other hand, may be more likely to cope with peer stress through activity‑oriented or externalizing strategies, reducing the likelihood that peer difficulties translate into changes in parent–child relationships.

The mediating role of peer alienation

Peer alienation emerged as a gender‑specific mediator in the longitudinal association between parent–child alienation and sleep disturbance. In the total sample, the indirect pathway from parent–child alienation to later sleep disturbance via peer alienation was significant, consistent with the developmental cascade model [55]. Parent–child alienation may increase the risk of subsequent peer alienation through attachment‑related and interpersonal processes [52–53]. Peer alienation, as a salient interpersonal stressor, may in turn contribute to sleep disturbance by heightening distress and impairing emotion regulation [40, 61]. Thus, difficulties originating in the family system may indirectly affect sleep health by disrupting functioning in the peer system.

Gender‑specific analyses revealed that these mediating pathways were significant only among girls, consistent with the pattern of gender differences observed above. As noted above, girls heightened interpersonal sensitivity and tendency to internalize relational stress [75–76] may render both the forward and reverse indirect pathways more salient for them. Under traditional son‑preference norms in some Chinese families, girls may also be particularly vulnerable to perceived emotional neglect and alienation [81], which may generalize to peer relationships through increased interpersonal insecurity and withdrawal. In turn, peer alienation is especially likely to trigger rumination and emotion dysregulation among girls [75–76], thereby interfering with sleep. These interpretations, while consistent with prior findings, remain speculative and should be directly tested in future studies that include measures of gender‑role attitudes, perceived parental preference, and specific coping styles.

No significant indirect effects were observed among boys. As noted above, boys’ tendency toward activity‑oriented coping [78] and externalizing responses to stress [80] may reduce the role of peer alienation in linking parent–child relationships and sleep disturbance. In addition, the IPPA peer alienation subscale may be less sensitive to activity‑based and less emotionally expressive friendship patterns that are more characteristic of boys’ peer relationships [79]. As a result, peer‑related difficulties among boys may have been underestimated in the present study. Boys are often encouraged to suppress emotional vulnerability and avoid seeking interpersonal support, whereas girls are more likely to express distress through interpersonal and emotional channels [65, 78]. Consequently, parent–child alienation among boys may contribute more directly to internal distress and sleep problems without necessarily operating through peer relationship difficulties [82].

It is also important to interpret these mediation findings in light of the modest within‑person effect sizes. The indirect effects, although statistically significant among girls, were small, suggesting that peer alienation represents only one of multiple pathways linking family relationships and sleep disturbance. These results should therefore be viewed as identifying a specific interpersonal risk process rather than a dominant or primary mechanism.

Strengths and limitations

This study has several methodological and theoretical strengths. First, the three‑wave longitudinal design, combined with the RI‑CLPM, allowed us to separate stable between‑person differences from within‑person fluctuations and to more rigorously test temporal associations among parent–child alienation, peer alienation, and sleep disturbance. Second, multi‑group analyses identified gender‑specific cross‑lagged pathways and mediation patterns, highlighting that peer alienation mediated the longitudinal association between parent–child alienation and sleep disturbance among girls but not among boys. Third, by situating these processes within the Chinese sociocultural context and considering potential gender‑related patterns, the present study extends prior applications of the developmental cascade model. Although the within‑person standardized effects were generally modest, the findings may still inform the development of gender‑sensitive approaches to promoting sleep health and interpersonal adjustment among college students.

At the same time, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, all key constructs were assessed using self‑report questionnaires administered at the same time points. Although the CFA results comparing a single‑factor model with a three‑factor model suggested that common method variance was unlikely to fully account for the observed associations [68], shared method variance and reporting biases (for example, negative affectivity or a general tendency to respond negatively) cannot be ruled out. Participants who were more distressed may have rated their sleep, family relationships, and peer relationships more negatively in a consistent manner, which could inflate correlations and partly bias the estimated cross‑lagged paths, even in a longitudinal design. Future research should incorporate multi‑method and multi‑informant approaches, such as objective sleep indicators (e.g., actigraphy), ecological momentary assessments, and reports from parents or peers, to better distinguish substantive associations from common‑method effects and to provide a more stringent test of the proposed interpersonal processes.

Second, the sample consisted exclusively of Chinese college students, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to other cultural contexts or clinical populations. Future cross‑cultural research should directly assess cultural factors such as gender‑role attitudes, perceived son preference, and family resource allocation to clarify the mechanisms underlying the observed gender differences and to determine whether similar patterns emerge in other sociocultural settings. Third, the one‑year longitudinal design may not fully capture longer‑term developmental cascades across emerging adulthood. Longer follow‑up periods with more than three waves would allow for more fine‑grained modeling of change processes.

Fourth, several potentially important mechanisms, including emotion regulation, rumination, academic stress, and technology use, were not directly examined and should be incorporated into future longitudinal or experience‑sampling studies. Doing so would help clarify how specific cognitive‑emotional and contextual processes link interpersonal alienation with sleep disturbance. Fifth, father and mother alienation were combined into a single parent–child alienation construct. Although CFA results supported the validity of this approach in the present data, paternal and maternal relationships may show distinct developmental associations, and future research should examine them separately. Sixth, participants were recruited from six universities using cluster sampling. Although the large and geographically diverse sample enhances representativeness, the RI‑CLPM analyses did not explicitly model potential clustering effects at the university level. Future studies could use multilevel extensions of RI‑CLPM to examine whether institutional‑level variation influences the observed associations.

Despite these limitations, the present study provides longitudinal evidence of gender‑specific associations among parent–child alienation, peer alienation, and sleep disturbance, and underscores the importance of incorporating both interpersonal and gender‑related perspectives in research on college students’ sleep and mental health.

Conclusion

This three‑wave longitudinal study demonstrated reciprocal associations between parent–child alienation and sleep disturbance, and between peer alienation and sleep disturbance, among Chinese college students. Parent–child alienation also prospectively predicted peer alienation, and peer alienation mediated the pathway from parent–child alienation to subsequent sleep disturbance. Gender‑specific analyses revealed that peer alienation partially mediated both directions of the parent–child alienation–sleep disturbance link among girls but not among boys, underscoring the role of gender in shaping these longitudinal associations. Given the modest within‑person effects, these interpersonal processes should be viewed as one component of a broader multifactorial etiology of sleep disturbance. The findings nevertheless highlight interpersonal alienation as a meaningful target for gender‑sensitive prevention and early‑identification efforts, and point to the need for future research using objective sleep measures, longer follow‑up periods, and direct assessments of cultural and gender‑role constructs across diverse contexts.
