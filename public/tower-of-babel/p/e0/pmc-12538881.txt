Higher graft failure rates following primary ACL reconstruction and conservative MCL treatment compared to simultaneous primary ACL and MCL reconstruction: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Mohammad Razi, Mohammad Soleimani, Haleh Dadgostar, Saeed Razi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCombined anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) and medial collateral ligament (MCL) injury is the most common combination of ligamentous knee injury. Many authors support the conservative treatment of MCL tears. This study aimed to compare the outcomes of simultaneous ACL reconstruction (ACL-R) and MCL reconstruction with ACL-R and conservative MCL treatment in cases of combined ACL and grade III MCL injuries.MethodsIn this retrospective cohort study, we evaluated consecutive patients with combined ACL and MCL injuries who underwent surgical treatment over five years in two private hospitals in Tehran, Iran. We compared postoperative knee range of motion (ROM), pain intensity (visual analog scale [VAS]), functional outcomes (Tegner-Lysholm and International Knee Documentation Committee Subjective Knee Form [IKDC] scores), time to and proportion of return to sports, time to return to work, activity level (Marx activity rating scale), and graft failure rate between ACL-R and conservative MCL treatment (ACL-R + conservative MCL group) and ACL-R and MCL reconstruction (ACL-R + MCL-R group). All patients were followed for at least twelve months.ResultsOverall, 110 patients, comprising 92 men (83.6%) and 18 women (16.4%), were included, consisting of 67 (60.9%) in the ACL-R + conservative MCL group and 43 (39.1%) in the ACL-R + MCL-R group. The mean age of the patients was 27.5 ± 9.0 years. Age, sex, side, and follow-up period were comparable between the groups (P > 0.05 for all). Graft failure occurred in 6 patients (9.0%) in the ACL-R + conservative MCL group, but in none of the patients in the ACL-R + MCL-R group (P = 0.046). Range of extension and flexion of the knee, VAS, Tegner-Lysholm score, IKDC score, time to return to sports, time to return to work, proportion of return to sports, and activity level did not differ significantly between the groups (P > 0.05 for all).ConclusionACL-R and MCL reconstruction resulted in similar knee ROM, pain intensity, functional outcomes, time to return to work and sports, and activity level to ACL-R and conservative MCL treatment in patients with combined ACL and MCL injury. However, ACL-R and MCL reconstruction significantly reduced graft failure rates, suggesting the potential benefit of surgical MCL management in conjunction with ACL reconstruction.Keywords: Anterior cruciate ligament, Medial collateral ligament, ACL reconstruction, Arthroscopy, Graft failure

Background

The anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) is the most commonly injured ligament in the knee, but isolated ACL tears are uncommon. Combined ACL and medial collateral ligament (MCL) injuries are the most frequent pattern of ligamentous knee injury [1, 2]. Recent studies report that the incidence of combined ACL and MCL injuries is more prevalent than previously thought, occurring in up to 67% of ACL injuries [1–3].

Given the high vascularity and extra-articular nature of the MCL, nonoperative management of the MCL is often favored in cases of combined ACL and MCL injuries, largely because surgical repair has been associated with postoperative complications and increased stiffness [3, 4]. Biomechanical studies suggest that medial laxity may increase the strain on reconstructed ACL grafts, potentially elevating the risk of graft failure. Consequently, some authors advocate for simultaneous MCL reconstruction to restore joint stability and reduce postoperative complications [5–8]. Nevertheless, the optimal management of high-grade MCL injuries remains controversial [1].

Despite growing awareness of the limitations of conservative MCL treatment, few studies have directly compared revision rates between nonoperative and surgical management of the MCL when performed concurrently with ACL reconstruction [4]. Moreover, prior large-scale studies have included patients with all grades of MCL injury [9–11], which may obscure the risks associated with laxity. In contrast, our study focuses exclusively on grade III MCL injuries, where the need for surgical intervention is most debated.

Additionally, although multicenter registries offer broad generalizability, they inherently introduce considerable variability in surgical technique, rehabilitation protocols, and outcome assessment. Our study analyzed a homogeneous cohort of patients managed by a single orthopedic surgeon using a consistent surgical technique and follow-up protocol. This enhances the internal validity and allows for more precise interpretation of treatment effects.

We hypothesized that more patients would require revision surgery after ACL reconstruction (ACL-R) and conservative MCL treatment than after simultaneous ACL-R and MCL reconstruction in cases of combined ACL and MCL injuries. In this study, we aimed to compare the short-term outcomes of ACL-R and conservative MCL treatment with those of simultaneous ACL-R and MCL reconstruction in patients with ACL and grade III MCL injury.

Methods

This retrospective cohort study included consecutive patients with combined ACL and MCL injuries treated surgically by a single surgeon at two private hospitals between September 2018 and August 2023. We adhered to the RECORD guidelines to report the data [12]. This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board and the Ethics Committee of the Iran University of Medical Sciences (code: IR.IUMS.FMD.REC.1403.2900). All patients provided written informed consent.

The inclusion criteria were age 15–55 years, combined ACL and grade III MCL injury, and undergoing either primary arthroscopic ACL-R or arthroscopic simultaneous ACL-R and MCL reconstruction. The exclusion criteria were simultaneous ACL-R and anterolateral ligament (ALL) reconstruction, prior knee surgery, ACL repair, revision surgery, or open ligament reconstruction. No patient with MCL repair was included in this study.

All patients underwent standardized physical examination (Lachman, pivot shift, anterior drawer, posterior drawer, valgus stress, and varus stress tests) by the senior author, and they were evaluated for anterior and rotatory instability. Anteromedial laxity was assessed using the anterior drawer test in external rotation. Since posteromedial injuries can lead to external rotation laxity, too, we differentiated these injury patterns using additional clinical and paraclinical findings.

Typically, a valgus stress test is used to evaluate an MCL injury. However, not all cases show a positive valgus stress test despite MCL injury. We evaluated all patients with both the valgus stress test and the “reversed valgus stress test”, which, by applying varus stress, reduces the medial gapping created by the valgus knee.

We used Hughston’s classification system for MCL injuries, with grade I having some torn fibers, localized tenderness, and no laxity, grade II indicating a partial tear with more generalized tenderness, and some laxity, showing a firm endpoint, and grade III representing a complete tear with significant laxity, which further subdivides into grades 1+, 2+, and 3 + based on the amount of medial joint gapping under valgus stress [13].

Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) was performed to evaluate ligament, meniscus, or cartilage injuries, which were subsequently reevaluated via arthroscopy.

In the acute setting, we reconstructed the MCL along with the ACL-R for professional athletes to minimize the time to return to play. For other patients, we preferred a delayed ACL-R and MCL reconstruction in cases of laxity after achieving a full range of motion (ROM) of the knee. In the chronic setting, we performed ACL-R and MCL reconstruction in cases of medial knee laxity.

We included patients with ACL and MCL injuries who underwent either ACL-R and conservative MCL treatment (ACL-R + conservative MCL group) or ACL-R and MCL reconstruction (ACL-R + MCL-R group). The group assignment was based on medial laxity grade and the evolving clinical protocol over time. Initially, patients underwent ACL-R and conservative MCL treatment for grades 1 + and 2+. Patients with grade 3 + injury underwent ACL-R and MCL reconstruction. Over time, based on the senior author’s evolving clinical judgment, MCL reconstruction was extended to all grades of laxity. This shift in treatment approach, from conservative MCL treatment to MCL reconstruction, was implemented gradually over several months.

Outcome measures

The primary outcome was graft failure, defined as laxity due to ACL graft tear requiring revision. Secondary outcomes included pain, postoperative ROM of the knee, medial injury grade, pain intensity, functional outcomes, time and proportion of return to sports, time return to work, and activity level.

Pain was evaluated using a visual analog scale (VAS) [14]. Functional outcomes were evaluated using Tegner-Lysholm and International Knee Documentation Committee Subjective Knee Form (IKDC) scores [15–18]. We also assessed activity levels using the Marx Activity Rating Scale [19]. The MCL injury was assessed using physical examination and MRI, and it was confirmed via arthroscopy. All ligaments were evaluated via arthroscopy, including ACL, MCL, and PCL. For MCL, the medial gap sign was assessed, and probing of the undersurface of the medial meniscus was performed, and the tear in the meniscotibial (coronary) ligament was evaluated [Fig. 2].

A large medial gap sign was observed in arthroscopy

Surgical technique

The senior author (M.R.) performed all surgeries. For the arthroscopic ACL reconstruction, we used bone-patellar tendon-bone (BTB) autograft for all patients. In situ tibialis posterior allograft reconstruction was used for MCL reconstruction.

Two methods were used for MCL reconstruction: arthroscopic [20] (Fig. 3) or mini-incision, without interfering with the injured MCL and exploring the area. Mini-incision MCL reconstruction was performed in all cases with a grade 1 + injury, in some cases with a grade 2 + injury, and in time-sensitive cases. A short incision exposed the posteromedial structures of the knee. Subsequently, a guide pin with an islet was passed and replaced with a No. 2 Ethibond or Vicryl suture. The allograft was initially routed through the tibial tunnel and then subfascially into the femoral tunnel. The graft was secured in the femoral tunnel using a bioabsorbable interference screw or a tendon staple. Following appropriate graft tensioning, the tibial tunnel fixation was performed using another bio-interference screw. The screw size was the same as the tibial or femoral tunnel (with a length of 30 mm and a diameter matching that of the tunnel). Graft motion during flexion and extension was assessed; however, minor displacement (a few millimeters) was considered acceptable, as with ACL grafts.

Medial drive-through sign observed in arthroscopy

Arthroscopic MCL reconstruction was mostly used for patients with extensive injuries who underwent double-bundle reconstruction and also received arthroscopic POL reconstruction. Moreover, since we used a narrow PCL jig, all patients with grade 3 + and some patients with grade 2 + MCL injury underwent arthroscopic reconstruction. In the double-tunnel technique (one femoral, two tibial), the graft was first secured in the femoral tunnel and then guided through both tibial tunnels. On the tibial side, a screw was typically inserted through the anterolateral tunnel orifice; in select cases, the screw was inserted through the posteromedial orifice. The double tunnel technique was employed in cases with grade III (3+) MCL and posteromedial laxity.

Post-op rehabilitation

A simple knee immobilizer was used in the operating room to immobilize the knee for a period of three weeks. Then, it was used only during ambulation for three weeks. Patients ambulated with bilateral crutches for seven weeks, followed by transition to a single cane for an additional four weeks.

Partial weight-bearing (PWB) was recommended for patients undergoing isolated ACL-R. For patients undergoing ACL-R and MCL-R, non-weight-bearing (NWB) was advised for three weeks if no meniscus or cartilage injury was present. Then, PWB was recommended as tolerated for four weeks, with adjustments made individually. For patients with meniscal injuries, NWB was recommended for six weeks. However, a personalized approach was taken for the NWB protocol of these patients based on the condition of the meniscus.

Active knee ROM was encouraged in the first two weeks in cases of ligamentous injuries. If the knee ROM was not satisfactory at two weeks postoperatively, gentle passive ROM of the knee was also requested.

ASA (81 mg) was prescribed for three to six weeks for all patients (according to age, body mass index, and meniscus status). The same analgesics were prescribed for both groups.

Follow-up

All patients were followed for a minimum of 12 months. We visited patients at the 1st, 2nd, 4th, 8th weeks, and 4th, 8th, and 12th months routinely. The patients underwent physical examinations preoperatively and at all follow-up visits, conducted by the senior author. A standardized knee stability examination (including Lachman, pivot-shift, anterior and posterior drawer, and valgus and varus stress tests) was performed beginning at the 4th postoperative month follow-up visit.

Statistical analysis

We used SPSS version 26 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA) and G*Power version 3.1 (Heinrich-Heine-Universität, Düsseldorf, Germany) for data analysis. Mean and standard deviation (SD) were reported for quantitative data, and number and percentage were reported for categorical data. The Kolmogorov-Smirnov test was used to evaluate the normality of data. To compare categorical data between the groups, we used the Chi-square test. For quantitative data comparisons between groups, the independent samples t-test or the Mann-Whitney U test was used. A P value < 0.05 was considered significant.

Results

A total of 110 patients, comprising 92 men (83.6%) and 18 women (16.4%), were included. We included 67 patients (60.9%) in the ACL-R + conservative MCL group and 43 patients (39.1%) in the ACL-R + MCL-R group. Post hoc power analysis showed a power of 0.78 for the study.

The mean age of the patients was 27.5 ± 9.0 years. Table 1 shows the baseline characteristics of patients in each group. Age, sex, side, interval between the injury and surgery, and follow-up were comparable between the two groups. Twelve patients (17.9%) required a revision surgery on the same knee, all of which were in the ACL-R + conservative MCL group, which was significantly higher than the ACL-R + MCL-R group (P = 0.003).

There were six (9.0%) cases of graft failure in the ACL-R + conservative MCL group and none in the ACL-R + MCL-R group, which was significantly higher in the ACL-R + conservative MCL group (P = 0.046). Five of them underwent revision ACL-R and MCL-R, and one declined a revision surgery.

Four revision surgeries were performed to address the rotatory instability of the knee. Two patients underwent an additional MCL reconstruction, and two underwent additional ALL reconstruction, which effectively resolved the knee laxity. One patient underwent revision surgery due to arthrofibrosis. The mean time from surgery to graft failure was 16.86 ± 7.21 months.

The reinjuries occurred in soccer, futsal, basketball, and tennis in 5, 1, 1, and 1 cases, respectively. Additionally, six cases sustained a non-contact injury, and two cases sustained a contact injury.

Discussion

We evaluated postoperative ROM of the knee, pain, functional scores, activity level, time return to work, time to and proportion of return to sports, and graft failure in patients with concomitant ACL and MCL injury who underwent ACL-R and conservative MCL treatment or simultaneous ACL-R and MCL reconstruction. We found that the ACL-R + conservative MCL group had a higher graft failure compared with the ACL-R + MCL-R group.

Residual rotational instability of the knee is a well-established predisposing factor for recurrent ACL injuries [21, 22]. Many surgeons perform ALL reconstruction along with ACL-R to address this rotational instability. While ALL reconstruction may reduce pivot shift, it does not address residual valgus or external rotation laxity. Some studies have reported no significant advantage of combined ACL-R and ALL reconstruction in functional outcomes over isolated ACL-R [23, 24].

Injuries to the medial knee structures can cause anteromedial rotatory instability of the knee [25]. Moreover, MCL not only serves as the primary restraint against valgus instability but also controls anteromedial rotatory instability, in conjunction with the ACL [5].

MCL injury is associated with an increased load on the ACL [6, 7], which compensates for the laxity caused by MCL deficiency [26]. Thus, excessive mechanical stress on a newly reconstructed ACL may increase the risk of graft failure. There is higher anteroposterior and varus-valgus laxity after isolated ACL-R in patients with concomitant ACL and MCL injuries than those with isolated ACL injuries, supporting the need to address the MCL injuries [27]. To improve anteromedial rotatory stability, MCL reconstruction has been suggested by some authors [5, 8]. The reconstruction of MCL has also been demonstrated to be more effective than its repair [4].

Although some authors advocate for early MCL repair [2], multiple studies have reported increased stiffness when MCL repair is performed alongside ACL-R [1, 28–30]. Moreover, MCL reconstruction offers superior stability compared to MCL repair. Prior studies have demonstrated that combined ACL-R and MCL reconstruction results in less residual medial laxity compared to ACL-R with MCL repair [31].

Several studies have reported increased postoperative stiffness following MCL repair [31–33]. A recent systematic review also reported improved outcomes after both MCL repair and reconstruction, although MCL repair resulted in a higher rate of knee stiffness [34]. In our technique, tibial and femoral tunnels are created to route the allograft tendon, avoiding disruption and exploration of native MCL tissue.

Recent studies indicate that the preferred approach for concomitant ACL and MCL injuries is a delayed ACL-R, providing time for self-healing of the MCL, and ACL-R and MCL reconstruction or repair in cases of residual valgus instability [35, 36]. Our clinical experience also supports the superiority of this approach. However, we prefer a delay only until the full ROM of the knee is achieved. Contrary to prior studies suggesting spontaneous healing of MCL, in our clinical experience, functional ligamentous integrity is not often restored [37, 38].

Following an MCL injury, muscle force imbalance, particularly the dominance of the biceps and popliteus muscles, may contribute to a gradual subclinical valgus malalignment, and the conventional valgus stress test may be negative. However, this should not be mistaken for MCL “healing”, as a “reversed valgus stress test” may be positive, indicative of an MCL injury. MCL reconstruction may be considered in all cases of medial laxity, particularly when grading is uncertain.

Moreover, since most ACL injuries are due to the valgus external rotation mechanism [39], residual valgus laxity, regardless of the MCL injury grade, may lead to repeated ACL graft injury.

Svantesson et al. reported that patients who did not undergo MCL reconstruction or repair had higher rates of ACL revision, but also reported a higher quality of life compared to those undergoing simultaneous ACL and MCL surgery [40].

Senorski et al. found that concomitant MCL injury, meniscus injury, female sex, and older age were predictors of a lower rate of return to sports in patients who underwent ACL surgery [41]. Additionally, Svantesson et al. found that patients with MCL repair or reconstruction had lower sports and recreation scores (a subscale of the Knee Injury and Osteoarthritis Outcome Score) compared to patients with isolated ACL injuries and those undergoing non-surgical treatment for MCL injuries [40]. In contrast, our findings showed no significant differences in functional outcomes between the ACL-R + MCL-R and ACL-R + conservative MCL groups. Despite the comparable age, sex, timing of surgery, meniscal injury status, return to sports, and activity level between the two groups, we found that patients in the ACL-R + conservative MCL group sustained second injuries more than patients in the ACL-R + MCL-R group. This fact further emphasizes the importance of MCL treatment in patients with combined ACL and MCL injuries.

Limitations

This study had several limitations. We included patients with meniscal injuries, which may confound outcome interpretation. However, both treatment groups had comparable rates of meniscal injury. We used an evolving protocol, and no distinct two groups were evaluated from the beginning. Additionally, we did not calculate an a priori sample size. Moreover, we included both professional athletes and non-athletes in our cohort, which may introduce bias. Further, although all patients had grade III MCL injuries, different stability grades (1+, 2+, and 3+) were observed, which could have been a source of bias. Moreover, two distinct, although similar, methods for MCL reconstructions were used. We suggest that a randomized controlled trial involving patients without concomitant meniscal injury would more precisely isolate the effects of MCL treatment.

Conclusion

Our results indicated that postoperative ROM of the knee, pain intensity, functional outcomes, i.e., Tegner-Lysholm and IKDC scores, time to return to work, time to and proportion of return to sports, and activity level are not significantly different in patients with combined ACL and MCL injury undergoing primary arthroscopic ACL-R and conservative MCL treatment than those undergoing primary simultaneous arthroscopic ACL-R and MCL reconstruction. However, the rate of graft failure following ACL-R and conservative MCL treatment is significantly higher, suggesting that surgical management of the MCL may reduce the risk of graft failure when performed alongside ACL-R.
