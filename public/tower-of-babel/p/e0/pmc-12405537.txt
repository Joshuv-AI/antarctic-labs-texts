Multispecies view of the effectiveness of the giant panda conservation programme
Authors: Chi Xu, Xing Chen, Qiang Dai, Bing Yang, Ke He, Zhisong Yang, Xiaodong Gu, Xuyu Yang, Biao Yang, Lifeng Zhu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractGiant panda (Ailuropoda melanoleuca) conservation programs have attracted significant funding and helped increase panda numbers. However, their effectiveness in achieving wider conservation goals is often doubted. This study evaluates the impact of giant panda reserves on the habitat suitability of 12 endangered species within 5 km of roads and assesses changes in human disturbances. Using data from the third and fourth national panda surveys and a matching approach, results show that these reserves enhance giant panda population density and habitat suitability, and benefit most wildlife species, particularly ungulates, with spillover effects improving adjacent areas’ habitat suitability, likely due to reduced human disturbances. However, some species like the Chinese red panda (Ailurus styani) still gain limited protection. The findings stress the need for more targeted measures in the Giant Panda National Park’s development. This multispecies study offers solid evidence for the effectiveness of giant panda conservation efforts and useful insights for protecting other flagship species.Subject terms: Ecology, Ecology

Introduction

In the Anthropocene, biodiversity faces unprecedented threats driven by the rapid development of human society and the global economy1,2. Current research indicated that we are amidst the sixth mass extinction event1. Scientists are exploring strategies to combat biodiversity loss, including the Convention on Biological Diversity’s (CBD) proposed 30×30 target3. However, limited conservation funding presents a significant challenge4, making it impractical to comprehensively protect all areas with conservation value5,6. In addition to financial constraints, social considerations—such as land use conflicts, community interests, and environmental justice—also play a crucial role in determining conservation priorities7. In this context, focusing on the conservation of rare and endangered species has been widely recognized as an effective approach to achieving regional biodiversity conservation goals8. Furthermore, the iconic status of such species serves as a powerful symbol of biodiversity, inspiring broader public engagement, attracting organizational support, and securing additional conservation funding9–11. A prime example of this is the Chinese government’s conservation efforts for the giant panda (Ailuropoda melanoleuca). These efforts have not only safeguarded the giant panda but also protected the habitats of tens of thousands of other species12 and preserved critical ecosystem functions11. The success of this initiative has had profound implications for biodiversity conservation, both within China and globally11,13.

The giant panda is a rare and endangered species endemic to China and an iconic symbol of global conservation13. Historically, giant pandas were widely distributed across eastern China14. However, due to factors such as climate change, natural habitat shifts, and human activities, their population and habitat have dramatically declined. Today, giant pandas are confined to six mountain ranges in central and western China—Qinling (QIN), Minshan (MS), Qionglaishan (QIO), Daxiangling (DXL), Xiaoxiangling (XXL), and Liangshan (LS)—with their current geographic range representing less than 1% of their historical distribution15. Over 70% of the giant panda’s habitat is located within the Sichuan province (Fig. 1a)16,17.

To protect this endangered species, the Chinese government began establishing nature reserves for the giant panda in 1963. By 2018, the number of such reserves had increased to 67, forming a comprehensive habitat protection network. In 2021, to ensure the integrity of giant panda habitats, China officially designated the Giant Panda National Park, which encompasses an area of 22,000 km². The park protects over 70% of the panda’s habitat and connects 13 isolated panda populations through ecological corridors18. Since 1974, China has conducted four national surveys on the giant panda, approximately every decade. The two most recent surveys, the third (1999–2003)16 and fourth (2011–2014)17 survey, revealed significant progress in habitat restoration19 and underscored the potential role of the national park in future conservation efforts18,20. In addition to investing substantial resources in establishing reserves and conducting surveys, the Chinese government has implemented large-scale conservation programs across the panda’s range21, such as the Natural Forest Conservation Program (NFCP) and the Grain-to-Green Program (GTGP)22. The GTGP compensates landowners for converting farmland to forest, while the NFCP provides financial support to local governments for forest conservation and management.

Given the substantial investment by the Chinese government in the giant panda conservation program, its effectiveness has been a focal point of academic inquiry, particularly regarding the efficacy of the nature reserve system19,23 Studies have shown that improvements in the habitat suitability of the giant panda are largely attributable to the protection afforded by reserves, with spillover effects observed beyond reserve boundaries24. These positive impacts are also expected to enhance the resilience of giant pandas to climate change25. Moreover, research indicates that the establishment of reserves has successfully curbed increases in human disturbances19 and that forest benefits within reserves are significantly higher than those outside26. While the effectiveness of the reserve system in supporting giant panda populations and habitats has been extensively studied, little is known about its protective impacts on other co-occurring wildlife species.

To date, a limited number of studies have demonstrated that nature reserves effectively protect the habitats of most rare and endangered wildlife species8,23. However, certain species, such as forest musk deer (Moschus berezovskii), Asiatic black bears (Ursus thibetanus)27, and apex predators28, remain inadequately protected. Research on the giant panda nature reserve network in Sichuan has revealed significant increases in populations of herbivores and medium-sized carnivores over the past decade, highlighting the reserves’ positive impacts on these wildlife groups. However, the current reserve system falls short in safeguarding large carnivores8. Additionally, differences in habitat use among wildlife species mean that those with specialized habitat requirements often fail to benefit from the giant panda reserve system29. While these findings suggest limitations in the reserves’ effectiveness for multi-species conservation, many of these studies suffer from a critical issue: they fail to account for environmental differences inside and outside reserves24. Simply comparing conditions within and beyond reserve boundaries cannot reliably capture the reserves’ true impacts on biodiversity27,29. Significant disparities in environmental and accessibility conditions may introduce bias in either direction—underestimating reserve effectiveness when reserves are subject to greater human pressure, or overestimating it when reserves are located in areas with naturally low disturbance (e.g., remote or high-altitude regions)30. This highlights the need for spatially controlled evaluation designs to minimize such biases.

To address the aforementioned issues, we employed a more rigorous analytical method—the matching approach—to evaluate the effectiveness of giant panda nature reserves in conserving multiple species24. Specifically, we utilized this approach (detailed in Methods) to investigate the impact of giant panda reserves on the habitat suitability of various endangered wildlife species between 2003 and 2014. Furthermore, we analyzed the spillover effects of reserves on wildlife habitat suitability and assessed the impact of reserves on changes in human disturbances. Our study provides robust evidence for evaluating the effectiveness of the giant panda conservation program, offering new insights into its broader conservation outcomes and informing future biodiversity protection strategies.

Results

Increased giant panda density from 2003 to 2014

The density of giant pandas increased from approximately 2.9 individuals/100 km2 to 3.5 individuals/100 km2 together (Fig. 1e, f) with an increase in habitat (Fig. 1b, c), between the last two national surveys. The suitable habitat area within the commonly surveyed regions grew from approximately 27,857 km2 to 29,086 km2 during this period (Fig. 1b, c). Using the matching analysis, we found that the increase in giant panda encounter rates within reserves was significantly higher than that outside reserves (average treatment effect (ATE) = 0.0129 ± 0.0008, P < 0.0001, Supplementary Fig. 3A).

Habitat suitability distribution of co-occurring animals

Results from the MaxEnt model indicated that the AUC values for the vast majority of species exceeded 0.9, demonstrating excellent model performance and providing robust characterization of potential suitable habitat distribution across different periods (Supplementary Figs. 1, 2). Compared to the third survey, the suitable habitat areas for most species increased in the fourth survey. For example, the habitat area of the tufted deer (Elaphodus cephalophus) increased by 10,131 km², and that of the leopard cat (Prionailurus bengalensis) increased by 11,771 km² (Fig. 2). These findings preliminarily suggested that the conservation measures implemented for the giant panda have also provided significant protection for co-occurring wildlife. However, we observed decreases in the suitable habitat areas of the Sichuan snub-nosed monkey (Rhinopithecus roxellana) and the Chinese red panda (Ailurus styani), particularly in the LS. Most wildlife species (including Chinese takin (Budorcas tibetana), Chinese serow (Capricornis milneedwardsii), Chinese goral (Naemorhedus goral), forest musk deer (Moschus berezovskii), and Asiatic black bear (Ursus thibetanus)) also exhibited a declining trend in habitat distribution within LS. This decline may be attributed to higher levels of human disturbance in this region (Supplementary Fig. 4C). Additionally, the Chinese red panda’s suitable habitat area experienced a reduction in most mountain ranges with the exception of DXL and the Sichuan snub-nosed monkey showed a similar pattern of habitat contraction across most regions except XXL (Fig. 2).

Impact of nature reserves on habitat suitability

Our results demonstrated that the habitat suitability of most species benefited from the positive effects of nature reserves (Fig. 3). Among the analyzed species, ungulates were the most positively affected group (Supplementary Fig. 5), with three species showing habitat suitability improvements approaching 0.10 under the influence of nature reserves (tufted deer: ATE = 0.0929 ± 0.0045, P < 0.0001; Chinese serow: ATE = 0.0880 ± 0.0033, P < 0.0001; Chinese goral: ATE = 0.1013 ± 0.0046, P < 0.0001). Beyond ungulates, the Sichuan snub-nosed monkey (ATE = 0.0971 ± 0.0038, P < 0.0001), the leopard cat (ATE = 0.0910 ± 0.0042, P < 0.0001), and the Asiatic black bear (ATE = 0.0745 ± 0.0035, P < 0.0001) also showed significant increases in habitat suitability, strongly influenced by the reserves. The results indicated that for most species, habitat suitability within protected areas improved by approximately 3% to 10% compared to areas outside the reserves (Supplementary Fig. 5). In contrast, the Chinese red panda, ATE = -0.0170 ± 0.0029, P < 0.0001) and the hog badger (Arctonyx collari, ATE = −0.0076 ± 0.0038, P = 0.0463) experienced relatively decreases in habitat suitability (Supplementary Fig. 5).

Spillover effect of nature reserves on habitat suitability

Our results reveal that nature reserves have a positive spillover effect on species habitat suitability in surrounding areas (Fig. 4). For most species, habitat suitability in the three regions closest to the reserves was significantly higher than in the farthest region (>20 km). The magnitude of these differences rapidly decreased with increasing distance from the reserves but remained statistically significant.

The tufted deer appears to be the species most positively affected by the reserves’ spillover effects, with a habitat suitability increase of 0.0638 (P < 0.0001) in areas within 5 km of the reserves (Fig. 4). Similarly, other species, including the Sichuan snub-nosed monkey, Temminck’s tragopan, Chinese serow, Chinese goral, forest musk deer, and leopard cat, showed a marked decline in the degree of habitat suitability improvement as the distance from the reserves increased (Fig. 4). For other species, the habitat suitability changes did not consistently decrease with increasing distance from the reserves (Fig. 4), suggesting that the spillover effects for these species were not as pronounced. Notably, the reduction in potential suitable habitat for the Chinese red panda and hog badger diminished with increasing distance from the reserves (Fig. 4).

Impact of nature reserves on human disturbance

The analysis of human disturbance revealed a declining trend in encounter rates, both within and outside the nature reserves, indicating progress in mitigating human disturbance (Supplementary Fig. 6). Furthermore, compared to the third survey, the reduction in encounter rate within the nature reserve was significantly more pronounced than outside (ATE = −0.0334 ± 0.0039, P < 0.0001). Logging was rarely observed in the fourth survey, and the frequency of the majority of other forms of human disturbance (e.g., livestock, shoot gathering, herb gathering, roads, hunting, and farming) decreased (Supplementary Fig. 4).

In the third survey, the top three types of human disturbance were reported to be logging, livestock, and roads (Supplementary Fig. 2B, C). Livestock was the most threatening factor for the giant pandas and their co-occurring animals (Supplementary Fig. 4B). Livestock is the main type of human disturbance when compared to the common reserve region, especially outside the reserve regions19. Here, the same survey grids were evaluated and the differences in human disturbance at the mountain level were compared. We found that MS and LS consistently had the highest human disturbance in the recent surveys among the mountainous regions (Supplementary Fig. 6D), and the decreasing trend was not similar to the trends observed in the QIO and XXL mountains (Supplementary Fig. 4C). This indicated that there are long-term high pressures on the giant pandas and their co-occurring animals from livestock in MS and LS during the ten years being surveyed. Furthermore, an increased frequency of hydropower among all five mountains was observed, especially in QIO, LS, and XXL (Supplementary Fig. 4C), which indicated an increased threat during these ten years.

Discussion

To protect the giant panda, a rare and endangered species, the Chinese government has expanded the nature reserve network and implemented a series of conservation programs11,12. By comparing the results of the third and fourth national giant panda surveys, we observed a significant increase in both the population density (Fig. 1e, f) and encounter rates (ATE = 0.0129 ± 0.0008, P < 0.0001) of giant pandas. This result aligns with the observed changes in habitat suitability24, further demonstrating the effectiveness of reserve establishment in promoting giant panda conservation. This positive trend can be attributed to the establishment of conservation areas and the implementation of related forestry projects22,23. Notably, our findings also support the idea that panda-focused conservation efforts can deliver broader ecological benefits: reductions in human disturbance (Supplementary Fig. 4) and improvements in habitat quality (Figs. 3–4) have likely contributed to better living conditions for other co-occurring animals, reinforcing the role of the giant panda as a potential umbrella species.

In this study, our matching analysis reaffirmed the effectiveness of the giant panda nature reserve system in conserving co-occurring animals. Among these, ungulates appear to benefit the most from the reserves (Figs. 2–4). Historically, ungulates have been one of the primary targets of poachers. The establishment of reserves and the implementation of enforcement measures have provided artificial protection for their survival. This helps explain why many ungulate species rely heavily on nature reserves for shelter and foraging31,32. Moreover, our results reveal positive spillover effects of the giant panda reserves, as indicated by increased habitat suitability in adjacent areas (Fig. 4). This indicated that the giant panda nature reserves not only effectively protect giant pandas themselves24 but also play a crucial role in improving the habitat suitability of co-occurring wildlife. This suggests that the protective influence of nature reserves can extend beyond their formal boundaries, benefiting co-occurring wildlife species. While early literature often anticipated negative spillover—where protection might displace human pressures outward33,34—our findings align with more recent studies reporting positive ecological spillovers under certain conservation regimes24. We hypothesize that this effect may stem from heightened conservation awareness among local communities near the reserves, leading to reduced exploitation of natural resources. Additionally, policies associated with giant panda conservation may have shifted livelihood activities in these areas from agriculture and animal husbandry to ecotourism-related services, thereby reducing disturbances to species habitats35,36. These findings further underscore the effectiveness of giant panda nature reserves in multi-species conservation.

Although earlier studies suggested that giant panda reserves were not effective in protecting co-occurring wildlife27, we argue that these findings were largely influenced by methodological limitations24. Previous studies often relied on direct comparisons between protected and non-nature reserves19, overlooking landscape heterogeneity. This led to the inclusion of remote, undisturbed areas—where human pressures are inherently low—in the evaluation of reserve impacts, potentially underestimating reserve effectiveness24. In contrast, our study applied a matching analysis framework to minimize such biases by ensuring that comparison sites outside reserves were similar in key environmental and anthropogenic factors (Table 1). By matching only areas with comparable levels of human influence, we were able to isolate the actual effects of reserve designation. This refined approach revealed that giant panda reserves contribute significantly to conserving multiple species, confirming their role in protecting rare and endangered wildlife (Figs. 3, 4).

However, for certain species, such as large carnivores8,28, the umbrella effect of giant panda reserves has notable limitations29,37, which must be addressed in practical conservation planning. Although occurrences of large carnivores such as snow leopards38 have been increasingly reported within conservation areas—suggesting that these reserves may provide partial benefits—their extensive habitat requirements cannot be fully met by panda reserves alone. In our study, found evidence that habitats of some species are still declining in certain mountain ranges (Fig. 2). Notably, the red pandas have suffered habitat loss and show no apparent positive spillover benefits from the reserves (Fig. 4). While giant panda reserves play an important role in conserving many rare and endangered species (Fig. 3) and significantly reduce direct human disturbances such as poaching (Supplementary Fig. 4), over-reliance on these reserves may be insufficient for meeting the ecological needs of certain taxa. Therefore, targeted conservation strategies are needed for species with specific and distinct habitat requirements8.

In recent years, efforts to expand the network of panda reserves have led to increases in both the area and quality of protected habitats11,12,18–20. For example, the number of giant panda natural reserves increased from 31 to 46, and the area of the giant panda natural reserves increased from approximately 17,500 km2 to 24,200 km2 during this interval period (Fig. 1d). Despite these gains, our results indicate that the effectiveness of panda reserves in safeguarding co-occurring wildlife still faces challenges in several mountain ranges, particularly DXL, XXL, and LS39–41. Currently, livestock presence ranks as the foremost threat factor, especially in areas like MS, XXL, and LS (Supplementary Fig. 4). In these regions, unrestricted livestock grazing not only competes with wildlife for food resources but also disrupts their habitats42. Thus, the first targeted conservation strategy should focus on regulating livestock activities within the habitats shared by giant pandas and other wildlife. We also observed an increasing trend in the number of hydropower stations in the surveyed areas, notably in QIO, LS, and DXL. Thus, the second targeted strategy should focus on the management of hydropower stations in these mountains, especially in the natural reserve region (Supplementary Fig. 4). Since the fourth survey, increased regulation of hydropower development by local governments has led to improved management of these areas. The HSI of several co-occurring animals decreased in the QIO and LS during these ten years (Fig. 2). We suggest that future conservation should focus on these mountains to improve the population recovery of some co-occurring animals. Our results revealed that habitat suitability improvements were spatially uneven, and certain areas such as DXL, XXL, and LS still suffer from ongoing habitat fragmentation (Fig. 2). In these regions, establishing ecological corridors and considering species translocation strategies may help enhance connectivity and facilitate recovery13,18,39. These measures, combined with continued control of human disturbances, could offer additional benefits for the conservation of both giant pandas and co-occurring wildlife.

One important limitation of our study is that the effects attributed to nature reserves may not be entirely distinguishable from those of other large-scale conservation initiatives, particularly the NFCP and GTGP. Both programs have been extensively implemented in the same regions as the panda nature reserves and are known to influence habitat quality, forest regeneration, and patterns of human activity22. While our analysis is spatially explicit and primarily focuses on reserve boundaries to infer conservation effects, we acknowledge that these broader policy interventions may have contributed to the observed changes in habitat suitability. Future work should aim to integrate the effects of multiple conservation interventions to more fully understand their relative contributions to biodiversity outcomes.

The giant panda conservation program has demonstrated remarkable success in safeguarding not only the giant pandas but also a wide array of co-occurring species. By mitigating severe human disturbances such as poaching and habitat degradation, the program has significantly improved the habitat quality and suitability for numerous rare and endangered species. Looking ahead, future conservation efforts should consider the specific needs of these species to optimize protection strategies. Particularly in the context of the Giant Panda National Park initiative, conservation plans should balance the protection of panda populations and habitats with the needs of other rare and endangered species, such as the Chinese red panda. By building on the achievements of the giant panda conservation program and adopting adaptive, inclusive strategies—such as incorporating local communities’ needs, promoting community-based conservation31, and supporting sustainable livelihoods43—the initiative has the potential to solidify its global reputation as a model for flagship species conservation that benefits entire ecosystems.

Methods

Study area

This study was based in the Sichuan province, which has over 70% of the habitats of the giant panda16,17. Wild giant pandas are distributed in six mountainous regions. Among these, more than 70% of the panda’s habitats are situated within Sichuan Province (Fig. 1)16,17. To protect the giant panda, the Chinese government established four nature reserves in the early 1960s21. Over time, additional reserves were created, and by the end of 2015, 67 reserves dedicated to giant panda conservation had been established. This study primarily focused on these reserves and their surrounding areas, which are located in the transitional zone between the Sichuan Basin and the western Sichuan Plateau. Despite the ongoing expansion of the nature reserve network, habitat degradation for giant pandas persisted into the early 21st century13. To address this issue, the Chinese government implemented a series of conservation initiatives, including enhancing the nature reserve system and launching payments for ecosystem services programs, such as the NFCP and the GTGP. This comprehensive approach reflects China’s commitment to balancing habitat restoration with long-term conservation goals.

Field survey

Since 1974, four national surveys (approximately one every ten years) have been conducted, with the two most recent surveys being the third (1999–2003)16 and fourth (2011–2014)17 survey. In the Sichuan province, the third survey employed over 1000 staff members and surveyed approximately 16,000 km2, and the fourth survey employed over 2000 workers and surveyed approximately 27,400 km2 (Fig. 1a). A detailed field survey can be found in the third and fourth national giant panda surveys16,17. Firstly, 2 km2 surveying grids (square) were established in the digital maps of the mountainous regions and the fourth surveyed region covered most of the third surveyed regions (Fig. 1a). Secondly, in each grid, the surveying line was based on landscape characteristics, plant coverage, bamboo distribution, altitude, and wild field accessibility conditions. The length of the ‘Z’ shape line was approximately 0.75 km and was used to gather information regarding the panda and co-occurring animals living conditions, such as panda activities, feces, hairs, trace, bamboo species, coverages, and landscape information. Finally, to prevent duplications in species distribution points, instances of activity traces for the same species observed within a 300-meter radius were recorded only once. It is noteworthy that in cases where the species cannot be identified from the traces, it is documented as unknown. Through the above survey methods, we obtained activity locations of giant pandas and co-occurring wildlife species (Supplementary Table 1).

Environmental variables

We selected 14 environmental variables for the correlation analysis in this study24,44. Based on relevant research, we categorized these variables into four groups: climate, topography, vegetation, land use and human disturbance (Table 1). Climate variables, derived from CHELSA (http://chelsa-climate.org), included precipitation (PR), monthly average maximum temperature (TMAX), and monthly average minimum temperature (TMIN). Topographic variables consisted of elevation (ELE) and slope (SLP). Vegetation was represented by the maximum normalized difference vegetation index (NDVI), obtained from the Institute of Geographic Sciences and Natural Resources Research, Chinese Academy of Sciences’ Remote Sensing Team for Land Use and Global Change45. Land use data, sourced from the Data Center for Resources and Environment of the Chinese Academy of Sciences (http://www.resdc.cn/), include categories such as forest land, grassland, water bodies, constructed land, and bare land. Human disturbance factors encompass roads and human settlements. Using the Euclidean Distance tool in ArcGIS 10.8, we calculated the distance from each grid cell to these land-use types and disturbance factors, labeling them as distance to forest land (DTF), grassland (DTG), water bodies (DTW), constructed land (DTC), cropland (DTCR), bare land (DTB), roads (DTR), and settlements (DTS). In the species distribution model, all environmental variables except disturbance factors were included, as these were reserved for subsequent matching analysis. It is essential to note that the data obtained from the third and fourth national panda surveys are from different time periods, accounting for temporal variations in environmental variables. In our model analysis, environmental variables such as climate, vegetation, and land use correspond to their respective time periods. To minimize multicollinearity among environmental variables, we performed a spearman correlation analysis and excluded one variable from each pair with a correlation coefficient |r | > 0.75 (Table 2). As a result, DTCR, TMAX and TMIN were removed (Tables 1–2). These procedures are widely applied in MaxEnt modeling to reduce spatial autocorrelation and multicollinearity, which could otherwise introduce bias into model performance and the interpretation of variable importance.

Calculation of giant panda density

Population size data (by bamboo shoot and moving distance methods) of the third and fourth surveys16,17 was used to calculate panda density in these mountain regions. The survey area was divided into several 100 km2 units and a statistical overlay analysis was performed with the individual panda points of the third or fourth surveys. Thus, panda density per unit area was obtained in ArcGIS 10.8 (https://www.arcgis.com/index.html).

Spatiotemporal dynamics of wildlife habitat suitability analysis

MaxEnt 3.3.3k46,47 software was used to model wildlife habitat regions between the third and fourth surveys based on species occurrence points and environmental factors (Table 1). Compared to other species distribution models, MaxEnt has demonstrated superior accuracy46,48. The output of MaxEnt is a probability map, which can be interpreted as the Habitat Suitability Index (HSI). The HSI values range from 0 (unsuitable habitat) to 1 (suitable habitat), providing a detailed spatial representation of habitat suitability for the species under study.

In this study, we utilized wildlife occurrence data collected during the third and fourth surveys to predict the habitat suitability distribution of co-occurring animals across different time periods. To reduce the effects of spatial autocorrelation in the species occurrence data, we filtered the occurrence points for each period based on a 1 km radius criterion: we randomly retained only one point if multiple points were located within a distance of less than 1 km from each other. After filtering based on conservation status and endangerment levels, 12 species were selected for further analysis (Supplementary Table 1). These 12 species represent typical mammal and bird found within panda habitat, with sufficient population sizes and encounter rates (excluding the rare species), making them suitable indicators of regional biodiversity in this study.

We constructed separate MaxEnt models for the third and fourth surveys, utilizing occurrence points and environmental variables corresponding to the respective periods (2000 and 2010). The MaxEnt model parameters were set as follows: random test percentage at 20%, a maximum number of 10,000 background points; 10 replicates, and a regularization multiplier of 1. The final model prediction was generated as an averaged output grid from the 10 replicates, producing a logistic habitat suitability index ranging from 0 (least suitable) to 1 (most suitable). To convert the continuous habitat suitability maps into binary suitable/unsuitable habitat maps, we applied maximum training sensitivity plus specificity logistic threshold. Subsequently, to assess habitat suitability changes for each species between the two periods, we used the Raster Calculator in ArcGIS 10.8 to compute the difference in habitat suitability values for each species across the two time periods.

Estimating the impact of nature reserves on habitat suitability change

Due to systematic differences in biogeographical conditions between areas inside and outside nature reserves—such as initial habitat conditions, elevation, and remoteness—direct comparisons are not feasible24. To address this, we employed a multivariate matching approach49 to evaluate the impact of giant panda nature reserves on habitat suitability changes for different species between 2001 and 2013. The primary goal of the matching method is to control for observable differences between protected pixels (inside nature reserves) and unprotected pixels (outside nature reserves), ensuring an “apples-to-apples” comparison. While a difference-in-differences (DID) approach combined with matching could be ideal for isolating treatment effects over time, its application requires high-resolution temporal data and the fulfillment of the parallel trends assumption. Due to data limitations—specifically, the lack of continuous trend data at the pixel level across multiple time points—we adopted matching as a more feasible and robust alternative under these conditions50. The habitat suitability distributions obtained from the model provided a large sample of data for matching analysis, effectively mitigating the limitations often associated with the matching method24. This ensures a more comprehensive and accurate assessment of the effects of PAs on habitat suitability changes.

In this study, we first screened the pixels included in the analysis based on the following criteria: 1) Exclusion of unlikely habitats. Pixels classified as bare land, built-up areas, or water bodies were excluded, as these areas are unlikely to serve as habitats for the species under study. 2) Exclusion of newly established nature reserves. Given that our study focuses on the impact of nature reserves on habitat suitability changes between 2001 and 2013, PAs established after 2000 (n = 31) were excluded from the analysis. 3) Addressing spillover effects. Previous studies have shown that the effects of PAs can extend to adjacent unprotected lands51. To minimize the influence of such spillover effects on our assessment, we excluded pixels located within 20 km of the boundaries of panda PAs. 4) Focus on accessible areas. Pixels located more than 5 km away from roads were excluded. This distance threshold was selected based on prior knowledge of human accessibility and disturbance in the region: areas beyond 5 km from roads are often extremely remote, with steep terrain and limited accessibility, and are thus rarely affected by direct human activities. Including these inaccessibly remote areas in the analysis would introduce bias, as their intact condition may not be attributable to any conservation intervention, but rather to their physical inaccessibility. Limiting the analysis to areas where human influence is feasible allows a more accurate comparison of conservation effectiveness under similar threat pressures. In these remote areas, the protection offered by PAs provides minimal additional value for habitat suitability due to the negligible human disturbance. Including a substantial number of pixels from such low-disturbance areas could dilute the observed effects of PAs, making their impact harder to detect. Moreover, this could obscure the critical contributions of PAs in areas under greater anthropogenic pressure. To address this limitation, we selected only pixels within 5 km of roads to construct the protected and unprotected pixel groups for impact evaluation. The 5 km threshold was chosen because residents in the study area typically live near roads, and previous research has indicated that human activities, such as agricultural production and fuel collection, predominantly occur within 5 km of roads in this region52.

For each pixel within the nature reserves, our matching method identified a corresponding pixel outside the nature reserves that was similar across nine covariates (Table 1). These covariates included the species-specific initial habitat suitability in 2001 (IHS), elevation (ELE), slope (SLP), distance to roads (DTR), distance to settlements (DTS), distance to constructed land (DTC), distance to water bodies (DTW), NDVI, and precipitation (PR)24. The inclusion of ‘species-specific initial habitat suitability in 2001’ was intended to account for potential temporal changes in habitat suitability that could influence the observed effects of nature reserves. This variable helps to ensure that the comparison between protected and unprotected pixels is robust to variations in baseline habitat conditions. To enhance the quality of matching, a caliper was applied to constrain the difference in each of the eight covariates between protected and unprotected forest pixels to within 0.5 standard deviations. This means that the analysis was designed to force matching pairs from all the regions and reserves included in the study. The matching process was conducted separately for each species to ensure accuracy. The pre-and post-matching environmental variable data distributions are shown in Supplementary Fig. 7.

Post-matching balance assessment used standardized mean differences (SMD) and variance ratios (VR). An SMD threshold of 0.1 was set, with |SMD | < 0.1 indicating good balance. The VR measured the consistency of covariate distributions, with values between 0.5 and 2 being acceptable. Results showed most covariates had SMD values below 0.1 and VR values within the acceptable range (Supplementary Fig. 8; Supplementary Table 2), indicating successful removal of systematic differences between groups. The average treatment effect (ATE) was calculated by subtracting the habitat suitability of unprotected pixels from that of matched protected pixels. To correct for potential biases from residual differences in pixel attributes, a bias-adjusted estimator was used, with the regression coefficient (β) serving as the ATE53. A positive ATE with P < 0.05 indicates a significant positive reserve impact, while a negative ATE indicates a negative impact. It should be noted that similar analyses for giant pandas were conducted by Yang et al.24. Based on kernel density analysis results, we used matching to compare changes in giant panda encounter rates inside and outside reserves (Table 1). Post-matching covariate data showed good balance (Supplementary Fig. 9; Supplementary Table 2). The matching analysis was performed in R 4.3.2 using the MatchIt and cobalt packages.

Evaluating the spillover effect of nature reserves

To assess the spillover effect of nature reserves on habitat suitability in surrounding areas, we analyzed habitat suitability changes across pixels at varying distances from the boundaries of 46 nature reserves, following previous research51. Pixels outside the reserves were grouped into five buffer zones based on their distance from the reserve boundaries: 0–5 km, 5–10 km, 10–15 km, 15–20 km, and >20 km. We employed a matching method to compare habitat suitability changes in the four near-distance groups (0–5 km, 5–10 km, 10–15 km, and 15–20 km) with those in the farthest group (>20 km)24. For these comparisons, we included only pixels within 5 km of roads to control for accessibility-related factors. The matching procedure was identical to that used for comparing protected and unprotected pixels to quantify the effects of nature reserves on habitat suitability changes. For each pixel in the four near-distance groups, the matching method identified a corresponding pixel in the farthest group with similar values for eight covariates, including the species-specific initial habitat suitability in 2001 (IHS), elevation (ELE), slope (SLP), distance to roads (DTR), distance to settlements (DTS), distance to cropland (DTC), NDVI, and precipitation (PR). The matched dataset achieved good covariate balance between treatment and control groups (Supplementary Table 3). If (1) a statistically significant difference in habitat suitability change is observed between the near-distance and farthest groups, and (2) the magnitude of this difference gradually diminishes with increasing distance from the reserve, then the presence of a spillover effect of the nature reserve on habitat suitability can be inferred.

Estimating the impact of nature reserves on human disturbance rate

During the surveys, the frequency of various human disturbance types (e.g., logging, livestock, roads, shoot gathering, herb gathering, hunting, farming, mining, tourism, fire marks, and hydropower stations) was recorded along each surveyed transect16,17. Data on human disturbance points were obtained from the third and fourth surveys16,17. Using the kernel density tool in ArcGIS 10.854, we calculated the human disturbance rate within the overlapping regions between the two survey periods (Fig. 1a: approximately 14,472 km²). To assess whether nature reserves reduced human disturbances, we conducted a matching analysis comparing the change in human disturbance rates inside versus outside protected areas. The matching procedure was conducted using the same method and covariates as for the habitat suitability analysis. This analysis was also restricted to areas within 5 km of roads, consistent with the habitat suitability assessment, to control for spatial accessibility and observation bias. In this matching framework, the outcome variable was the change in human disturbance rate, while the baseline variable used for matching was the initial human disturbance level, replacing the baseline habitat suitability used in the prior analysis (Table 1). The matched dataset achieved good covariate balance between treatment and control groups (Supplementary Fig. 9; Supplementary Table 2), supporting the robustness of the inference. We emphasize that while distance-based variables may broadly proxy for human pressure, direct disturbance observations provide a more proximate and empirically grounded indicator of anthropogenic impact. Therefore, we retained this analysis as a complementary evaluation of reserve effectiveness in mitigating human disturbances.
