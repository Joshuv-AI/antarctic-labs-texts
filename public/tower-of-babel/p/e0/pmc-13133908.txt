Synthetic Biology Approaches to Enzymology in Food and Agriculture Systems
Authors: Sonia Su, Julie M. Goddard
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Global food insecurity remains a challenge, with 2.3 billion people worldwide experiencing food insecurity. Applications of synthetic biology offer a promising way to address this crisis through innovative and sustainable enzyme-mediated solutions. This review explores enzymology with food and agriculture systems and how recent advances are aided by synthetic biology. Focusing on how enzymes can be engineered for the greatest good to promote food safety, improved production, and coproduct valorization, we survey state-of-the-art advances in enzyme engineering to achieve these goals, providing a critical review on how technology from other industries could be adapted to food and agriculture. Key areas discussed include biocatalysis of food ingredients, synthetic biology for yield improvements, and computation design of enzymatic pathways for more resource-efficient food processing. This review concludes with a discussion of current limitations, regulations, and future directions for integrating synthetic biology into global food systems.

Introduction to Enzyme Technologies in Food and Biotechnology

Enzymes are proteins that fold into specific three-dimensional shapes, enabling them to act as biological catalysts that lower activation energy to accelerate chemical reactions within and outside of cells. Intracellular enzymes govern cellular processes and operate within the cell in which they are produced, while extracellular enzymes are secreted outside the cell to catalyze reactions of extracellular substrates. To further categorize enzymes, they are split into seven major enzyme classes (oxidoreductase, transferase, hydrolase, lyase, isomerase, ligase, and translocase), , each with high specificity to their optimal substrate (Table ). Often referred to as a “lock and key” mechanism, the enzyme active site (the lock) binds specifically to a specified substrate (the key) to form the enzyme–substrate complex, facilitating catalysis.

Since the late 19th century, enzymes have been used industrially in food and agriculture, from chymosin coagulation of milk in cheese production to alpha-amylase inhibition of staling in commercially baked bread, for optimization and improvement of process efficiency. − Understanding the diverse sources and technological opportunities of enzymes and the array of potential applications can pave the way for improved enzyme applications in food and agriculture. One such emerging technology is the use of synthetic biology to engineer enzymes for targeted applications. In this review article, we survey the state-of-the-art in enzyme technologies in food and agriculture and the advances made in synthetic biology approaches to engineering enzymes for improved environmental stability and activity, and finally, we discuss challenges and opportunities in the field.

Distinct elements in synthetic biology have cross-sector translational ability. Pharmaceutical and medical applications broadly implement synthetic biology for drug discovery, precision enzyme systems for personalized medicine, biomanufacturing, and advanced therapeutics. The skills and knowledge gained from this higher profit-margin sector can help to improve enzyme technologies in food and agriculture. With hurdles to adoption based on regulatory, consumer acceptance, and matrix optimization, synthetic biology approaches like genetic pathway design, computational design, and cell-free synthesis, we can imagine a new landscape for innovations in enzyme technology in food and agriculture.

The applications of enzymes for food and agriculture discussed in this review only scratch the surface of the extensive potential enabled by synthetic biology. Through engineering enzymes and biological systems, synthetic biology provides opportunities to improve food production, safety, and accessibility. Reviews by Yang et al., Yeşiltoprak and Dayısoylu, James et al., and Kumar et al. provide a deeper look into enzyme applications for food and agriculture. − Synthetic biology outside of enzymology has also been well reviewed and can be found in these reviews by Shi et al., Roell and Zurbriggen, and Goold et al. −

Enzyme Sources

As the biocatalytic driving force for nearly all reactions within all cells, enzymes can be sourced from prokaryotic organisms (bacteria and archaea), eukaryotic organisms (fungi, yeasts, plants, and animals), and acellular origins. Table summarizes each of these enzyme sources, outlining strengths, limitations, opportunities for synthetic biology, and regulatory considerations. Certain bacteria- and archaea-sourced enzymes have been found to thrive in extreme environments and are robust and resilient enzymes well suited for industrial applications. The Bacillus genus is the most prominent group of bacteria used to produce extracellular enzymes in food and agriculture, yielding proteases, amylases, and cellulases. , For research and industry, Escherichia coli is the preferred choice for producing recombinant enzymes because of the extensive knowledge, stability of plasmids when introduced, rapid growth rates, and high expression yields, along with the well-developed tools for genetic modification and purification. The main benefits of enzymes sourced from bacteria are high growth rates, ease of genetic manipulation, and stability under extreme temperatures and pHs. However, some strains produce endotoxins that need to be removed and provide limited post-translational modifications. , Enzymes from thermophilic archaea, such as DNA polymerase used in polymerase chain reactions, enable performance and stability at high temperatures. In another example, thermostable proteases are used to degrade complex proteins at 40–60 °C to predigest baby foods or soft meats. Archaea-sourced enzymes have well-reported stability and activity under high temperature, high salinity, and other extreme conditions but are more challenging to culture than bacteria, and the limited research and knowledge reduce the ease of widespread use.

Eukaryotic enzyme sources include fungi, yeasts, plants, and animals. They are applied in biotechnological applications, food production, and even biofuel because of their diversity and application abilities. , Currently utilized fungi sources include the mold Aspergillus niger to produce enzymes like glucoamylase, pectinase, and phytase for use in food and feed industries. Penicillium species are also prominently used, producing enzymes like cellulases and proteases. , Fungi-sourced enzymes can lead to ease in enzyme purification with their secretion mechanisms but require more complex media and have slower overall growth rates compared to bacteria. Yeasts, such as Saccharomyces cerevisiae, produce invertase and zymase that can be used in the food and bioethanol industries. Yeast-sourced enzymes can perform post-translational modifications like higher eukaryotes. Some yeasts, such as S. cerevisiae, are well studied for recombinant enzyme production but typically require specialized fermentation equipment and have lower secretion levels than bacteria and fungi. ,

Enzymes sourced from plants, such as fruits, vegetables, seeds, and tubers, are cost-effective, sustainable, and less likely to cause an allergic reaction than their animal counterparts, such as egg-derived lysozymes. Papain from papaya is used for meat tenderizing and digestive aids, pectinase from carrots is used to aid in fruit juice extraction, alpha-amylase from barley is used for the production of high fructose corn syrup and brewing, and patatin from potatoes is commonly used for emulsification in sauces and dressings. Plant enzyme sources are highly abundant and renewable with another benefit for fruits and vegetables being the mild extraction conditions; however, the seasonal variation and high concentrations of impurities prove to be difficulties. Seeds and tubers can produce high yields of specific enzymes with high stability but do require complex purification and extraction conditions.

Animals produce enzymes like trypsin and lipases in the pancreas for medical treatments and laboratory research, as well as pepsin and lactase in the stomach and intestines for use in dietary supplements and diagnostics. The greatest advantage of animal-sourced enzymes is that they have the greatest biocompatibility for medical applications and can produce complex enzymes with proper post-translational modifications. Unfortunately, animal-sourced enzymes have a higher associated cost for enzyme extraction and purification while still carrying a high potential for contamination. In addition to the economic and purity challenges presented, animal-derived enzymes raise ethical and regulatory concerns, oftentimes conflicting with religious-based dietary laws.

Acellular enzyme sources are derived from nonliving systems, either through cell-free synthesis or by extraction from natural sources without retaining any intact cellular components. , Consisting of cellular machinery (e.g., ribosomes, tRNA), cell-free synthesis uses a reaction mixture of DNA or mRNA templates, amino acids, nucleotides, and other necessary cellular components in a small-scale reaction vessel to screen many manufactured enzymes for rapid development and use. Although it is not currently being used commercially in food and agriculture, cell-free protein synthesis is being leveraged for drug development, rapid protein therapeutics, and vaccine production. Cell-free protein synthesis has been used to produce protease with high purity for research and industrial applications. The in vitro synthetic biology techniques allow enzyme production without the need for living cells and allow rapid enzyme production without cell culture limitations. Despite recent scientific advances, cell-free protein synthesis is still more expensive and produces lower yields compared with cell-based systems. Extracted proteins are beneficial to industrial processes where cell debris must be minimized. Cell-free protein synthesis produces high-purity enzymes that are highly specific for their applications but can involve a complex and costly extraction process that often requires significant resource dedication.

Enzyme Production and Modification

Another way to define enzymes used in industrial applications is based on their method of production and genetic modification status, namely, nonrecombinant native, recombinant native, and engineered enzymes.

Nonrecombinant native enzymes are enzymes that are extracted and purified directly from their natural source and used without further genetic modifications. Many food applications of nonrecombinant native enzymes are sourced from plants such as papain from papaya as a meat tenderizer or amylases from barley used in brewing and baking. These enzymes provide the most unaltered enzyme that can be extracted with well-defined industrial methods; however, their catalytic activity, yield, and purity can be highly variable. From a sustainability perspective, nonrecombinant enzymes must be harvested from their sources indefinitely, which raises concerns about sustainability and ethical implications. For food labeling in the United States, these enzymes are classified as generally recognized as safe (GRAS) so long as the extracted enzyme follows the purity and quality standards, retains source documentation, and is included in labeling if it could be of allergenic concern.

Recombinant native enzymes are produced using recombinant DNA technology containing the same amino acid sequence as that of the naturally occurring enzyme. In recombinant native enzymes, the naturally occurring enzyme gene sequence is extracted from the source and produced in a new host organism, such as E. coli, to allow increased production speed, yield, and enzyme purity for use in industrial applications. Recombinant native enzymes enable precise control over what enzyme amino acid sequence will be expressed for more consistent enzymes in terms of quality and purity due to the controlled production process. They can also be scaled, depending on individual enzyme application needs. Nevertheless, initial startup and production costs can be high, and regulatory requirements can present a hurdle for food applications. For food labeling in the United States, recombinant native enzymes require extensive regulatory and safety approval before they can be used in food products, resulting in additional labeling requirements to notify consumers of genetically modified organisms (GMOs) or bioengineered ingredients used in the food or food processing. ,

Engineered enzymes are enzymes that have been genetically modified to enhance properties, such as activity, stability, specificity, or ability to self-assemble onto solid supports with precise control over the orientation. There are many benefits to using engineered enzymes for precise control over expressed enzymatic properties, structures, and stability within food processing conditions; however, a portion of the public has shared concerns over the use of genetically modified materials in foods. − Having heard these concerns, food manufacturers and agricultural producers have been sharing information to educate consumers about the benefits that are achieved with these engineered enzymes, including increased food production for our growing population. , In the United States, when engineered enzymes are used as a processing aid, comprehensive safety assessments are used to ensure the enzyme is safe. When used as a food ingredient, engineered enzymes have to undergo comprehensive safety assessments, and final food products must disclose that bioengineering has been used if there is a detectable level of modified DNA.

Strategies in Enzyme Engineering

To implement synthetic biology and create an arsenal of engineered enzymes that have enhanced stability, activity, and specificity compared to the wild-type, engineering techniques commonly applied are rational design, − semirational design, − directed evolution, − and genetic code expansion. − Rational design uses well-characterized amino acid interactions of protein structures and function to predict potential mutations for targeted amino acid changes in an overall protein sequence. A deep understanding of the three-dimensional interactions of the protein sequence is needed to make changes that promote enhanced characteristics. , Molecular modeling software and computational tools, such as PyMOL, FoldX, Rosetta, etc., provide an expansive database to predict the effects of each specific mutation. Rational design produces highly specific and effective modifications that are highly predictable to estimate the enzyme function. The major disadvantage is that detailed structural and mechanistic data are needed, leading to limited accuracy of predictions for enzymes with minimally available structural data.

Semirational design combines the previously discussed elements of rational design with random mutagenesis. This method involves a more extensive use of resources to screen the generated library of enzyme variants. , The goal of this approach is to use rational design principles and focus mutagenesis on specific regions of the enzyme that will affect the enzyme function. This approach aims to create a diverse set of enzyme variants that can provide some beneficial mutations that rational design alone may miss; however, this method still requires significant knowledge of the enzyme structure and function, in addition to the extensive time and resources needed to screen the variants.

Directed evolution is a core tool in synthetic biology, aiming to mimic natural selection and, over iterative cycles, evolve the enzyme with desired traits. Random mutagenesis and recombination techniques are used for creating the diverse library, and high-throughput screening is used to identify the variants that show promise for their intended application. This method is useful when very limited information is known about the enzyme structure, providing a wide range of mutations that can be explored, and is most useful in applications where high-activity enzymes are needed. Directed evolution allows enzyme mutations to start with any enzyme and can explore a large sequence space to produce effective enzymes. The main disadvantages are that this approach is very time-consuming and resource-intensive, with the need for efficient screening methods.

Genetic code expansion is an advanced synthetic biology technique that allows the incorporation of nonstandard amino acids into enzymes and redesigns the fundamental biological system. This expands the use of amino acids beyond the 20 natural amino acids to provide the creation of novel properties and functions that cannot be achieved with more traditional protein engineering methods. Incorporating noncanonical amino acids, amino acids not found in nature that have unique chemical properties such as photoreactivity, metal-binding capacities, and enhanced catalytic activity, into the genetic code of an enzyme will produce enzymes with novel properties. ,,, Studies have shown that noncanonical amino acids can strengthen catalytic activity, , enhance resistance to denaturation, enable new functionalities, , and even regulate activity. , This approach provides unprecedented opportunities for improving enzyme diversity, precision engineering, and broad enzymatic applications; however, there is great technical complexity, requiring sophisticated equipment, low protein yield efficiency, and limited host systems.

There are benefits and drawbacks to each of these approaches, especially when the end cost is considered for industrial applications (Table ). Rational design is fast to apply and low-cost but requires a robust library of known structures and functions. Semirational design is moderate on all accounts, requiring moderate knowledge and only requiring a moderate screening workload. Directed evolution excels when structural data is low but has a high cost and time input, which makes it less optimal for low-margin food and agriculture enzymes. Genetic code expansion requires a low screening workload but has high input requirements in terms of time, cost, and knowledge needed. At this current age of food and agriculture, genetic code expansion would be reserved for high-value, low-dose applications, which are rare in the food industry. Overall, for commodity-applied enzymes like those used in food and agriculture, strategies that minimize screening and can create novel computational strides will be unlikely to scale unless input costs and regulatory hurdles can be overcome without much additional complexity in manufacturing, validation, and consumer acceptance.

Applications in Food and Agriculture

The food industry applies enzymes in a variety of ways, from food coproduct valorization and processing aids ,,,, to even active and intelligent packaging. − The coproducts in the food industry can also be valorized with enzymes to become value-added ingredients. In the winemaking industry, nearly 12 million tons of grape pomace are produced as a byproduct of the winemaking process. Using a variety of cellulolytic enzymes, phenolic compounds can be extracted from the pomace to be used as value-added ingredients in nutritional supplements and as antioxidants in food products. In the food industry, waste potato mass is a sustainable substrate for biofuel production. With the introduction of amylase for saccharification and cellulase for cellulose hydrolysis in the complex biofuel production method, a final energy production of 7.15 × 103 MJ and 390 kg of digestate fertilizer resulted from 1 t of waste. Other food wastes can be enzymatically hydrolyzed for the recovery of the reducing sugars. Zhang et al. reported that the recycling of low-cost waste biomass can use a simple two-step enzymatic hydrolysis, involving the application of α-amylase and then glucoamylase to produce a 204 g/L reducing sugar concentration in comparison to ∼50 g/L from acid hydrolysis and ∼23 g/L from alkaline hydrolysis. This approach also produces high-purity glucose, which could lead to high feasibility for implementation and high profitability.

Although a variety of current applications for coproduct valorization exist and have been studied, increased production values of these valorized ingredients could be met with further optimized enzymes. Current limitations around coproduct valorization are predominately around cost and existing infrastructure. With coproducts in the food industry, there is not much existing infrastructure for some valorization efforts, which rely on high costs for implementation, leading to shortcuts and lacking full evaluation of tolerance or key processing conditions. Higher profit value-added ingredients and nutritional supplements offset the cost of implementation and additionally minimize the cost of waste removal. In terms of the technology readiness level (TRL) of implementation for grape pomace, waste potato mass, and various other food coproducts, the technology is ready for industrial application with a score of 7–9, especially from large volume enzymes that can be created through prokaryotes in fermentation, especially enzymes such as proteases and amylases. The main hurdle remains with having the appropriate processing conditions and capital equipment for these applications, often not to the direct benefit of the producer; however, some food coproduct manufacturers instead sell the coproducts to companies with existing infrastructure for subsequent valorization. , For all potential applications, TRL and life-cycle assessments demand numerous assumptions that are sensitive to dynamic, nonstatic conditions (e.g., supply chains, regulatory environments, and new market opportunities). ,

Incorporation of enzymes into the packaging matrix has two predominant functions: detection or preservation. Lysozyme, glucose oxidase, or lactoperoxidase can function as antimicrobial agents when incorporated in packaging and can thus extend the shelf life of foods. Chang et al. created a novel polymer incorporating lysozyme with Gracilaria chouae polysaccharide and carboxymethylcellulose to create a polymer that increases fresh seafood shelf life by at least 3 days by minimizing the microbial spoilage. Similarly, Hu et al. found that the lysozyme that was effectively released in the film system had inhibitory effects on Staphylococcus aureus and E. coli when the film was tested in application, showing promise of antimicrobial effects on food packaging. Laccase immobilized on a chitosan/poly­(vinyl alcohol)/tetraethylorthosilicate electrospun film has been seen to serve as a time–temperature indicator for milk to visually provide up to counts of 106 colony-forming units/mL of lactic acid bacteria in milk. Lipases have also been used as time–temperature indicators for milk, producing irreversible color changes for up to 16 days as the pH of milk decreased in the presence of lactic acid bacteria production.

The TRL of enzymes incorporated into packaging materials is lower than coproduct valorization, in the 2–4 range, as research is in infancy and there is no established pathway toward regulation. There are vast benefits that can be seen from integrated packaging for detection and preservation, and since they would be considered food contact materials, the testing around these enzymes will be informed by prior case studies and, in the US, the FDA guidance FDA-2016-D-4844 for GRAS status. Around current guidance, much of the extensive testing can be waived, especially if these enzymes are used only as processing aids. This guidance begins with a variety of characterization studies assembled from research around the enzyme planned to be used, and based on the conditions of use or exposure, the toxicity data could be generated from toxicity information that has been established in literature. As each new enzyme would have unique characteristics, it is common that safety data from similar enzyme homologues be translated across, especially when exposure is expected to be negligible.

Prominent technologies utilizing enzymes can be seen in agriculture acting as environmental bioremediation agents, natural biocontrol agents, − genetically engineering crops with specially expressed enzymes, reducing toxicity in compost or accelerating the decomposition process, , and valorizing agricultural waste. Enzymatic bioremediation is a strategy utilized to remove the increasing environmental pollutants from man-made sources. ,, Laccase enzymes have the ability to remove dyes that have been found in the environment from pharmaceuticals, personal care, and other household sources. , According to Uygun et al., free laccase had decolorization efficiencies of 34–55%, whereas an immobilized laccase from Trametes versicolor reached 76–94% decolorization efficiency of azo-dye pollutants in textile wastewater. This improved performance of immobilized laccase is more efficient, environmentally friendly, and cost-effective in removing wastewater dyes. Native hydrolases have been seen to cleave the ester bonds found in certain plastics, namely, polyethylene terephthalate (PET), to degrade the plastic into the monomer components, which can be collected and repolymerized into new PET with properties similar to the virgin plastics. − Many advances have been made after screening native bacterial enzymes, and Ideonella sakaiensis-sourced PETase has been found to be the most active hydrolase in breaking down PET plastics. As environmental plastic pollutants have grown, remediation of this pollutant needs to advance as well, and genetic modifications of PETase have begun to be explored. The focus on mutations in PETase is being widely explored with methods of semirational design being used to improve the affinity of the amino acids around the PET active site up to a 15-fold improvement in activity. Enzymatic advances have also been reported in cold composting applications. When ambient temperatures are low, compost typically does not have the ability to effectively degrade compost to maturity, but as reported by Sun et al., the use of cold-adapted microbial agents, such as engineered β-glucosidase, urease, protease, and invertase, accelerated the composting process, raised composting temperatures, and improved the overall quality of compost. Hydrolysis of municipal waste has also been proven to be an effective way to create a more circular economy. Organic fractions of municipal solid waste can be hydrolyzed by commercially available Viscozyme L, a cocktail of β-glucanases, pectinases, hemicellulases, and xylanases, to produce a liquid sugar concentrate for Bacillus thuringiensis biopesticide production through solid-state fermentation. This method of biopesticide production yielded 50.65 g/L liquid sugar that was implemented in biopesticide fermentation for 3.9 × 108 viable cells g–1 dry matter with a 33% sporulation ratio.

In agriculture, the TRL for bioremediation is 5–7 because extensive lab and pilot work has been done for a variety of enzymatic degradation of pollutants. Challenges around enzyme stability in environmental matrices and optimization for specific applications remain, but the broad category has made significant advances toward commercial launch. , For many agricultural and environmental applications, additional research needs to be done on the long-term effects of enzymes and hydrolyzed components if they cannot be recaptured. Natural biocontrol agents and genetically engineered crops for specially expressed enzymes are closer to 8–9 for the TRL. Biopesticides are a mature market in the US with rapid commercial growth and a continued upward trend for commercial adoption as research increases and consumer preference away from chemical pesticides continues. , Genetically engineered crops have also been heavily commercialized for decades, and enzyme extraction from these crops is greatly accepted by most consumers and regulatory bodies. , Accelerated decomposition of compost and agricultural byproducts has a TRL of 5–8, being heavily pathway-dependent with enzyme-assisted extraction and bioconversions of high-value products needing more research to break out of the lab and pilot scale. Compost decomposition, for application on an industrial scale, is at a more mature market since extensive studies have been conducted on enzymes for targeted hydrolysis; the application of a GRAS enzyme cocktail on compost is easily adopted at scale. , Various environmental matrices need to be evaluated, and optimal conditions could accelerate decomposition, but constraints exist within the infrastructure.

Regulatory and Consumer Acceptance

In the food industry, processing regulatory compliance, enzyme functionality, cost, consumer acceptance, and sustainability all need to be considered when engineering enzymes for food applications. Regulatory compliance as an engineering consideration is important to ensure that enzymes used as a food ingredient or processing aid can remain with GRAS status. If the engineering targets create a new enzyme that needs to be evaluated for toxicity and allergenicity, a significant financial hurdle to application is introduced. The cost associated with full toxicity and allergenicity testing limits the scale-up potential for new engineered enzymes in the food and agriculture industry. Research has shown that for ensuring safety in new food enzymes, integrated biochemical assays that can be done at a small scale can reassure both regulatory bodies and consumers. , Specific testing will depend on the enzyme created for application, but in vitro digestibility assays and modeling represent an opportunity for reducing the need for animal testing and minimizing cost for allergenicity concerns. As more enzymes are being tested, more data will be created to minimize future costs of testing, offering predictive insights that can feed into artificial intelligence or machine learning models to complement traditional assays. When bringing enzymatically processed foods to other countries, regulatory bodies have differing requirements for food safety regulation before they are approved to sell to that market. Beyond governmental regulations, nongovernmental organizations are important to consider as well, for example, agencies that certify adherence to religious dietary laws (e.g., Orthodox Union, Islamic Food and Nutrition Council of America) and those that define different (often more stringent) standards than governmental agencies (e.g., Non-GMO Project). Enzyme functionality in terms of the following food processing conditions, reaction times, and substrate specificity is also important to consider while modifying an enzyme. If the processing conditions need to be drastically changed to employ the enzymatic process, then the likelihood of adoption of that enzyme will be low. An assortment of cold-active enzymes has been developed to negate the need for drastic changes in the processing conditions. Enzyme stability within a food matrix is a critical consideration when engineering enzymes. While thermostability is a desirable trait among food processes, excessive stability will not allow deactivation of the enzyme, potentially compromising the food’s shelf life or quality. Consumer acceptance for recombinant enzymes is a huge factor in using them as it affects the bottom line for a food company, and consumer demand for transparency is increasing. The food industry is driven by consumers and the growing emphasis on sustainability, which dictates the development of environmentally friendly enzyme technologies.

Increasing enzyme use in food, food processing, and agriculture has demanded regulatory definition on the use and labeling of enzymes. In the United States, enzymes used as processing aids in food products are generally exempt from labeling on food products if they are removed from the food before the final packaging, are present in “insignificant amounts” and do not have any functional effect on the food, or are used to facilitate the process without an effect on the final food product. Enzymes in bread baking, juice clarification, and beer production, regardless of their classification, have been deactivated or removed from the food product before final sale to the consumer and are examples of products where this labeling is exempt, keeping in mind that if the enzyme contains a high-priority allergen or is obtained from an animal, there may be additional requirements for labeling to ensure exposure to an allergen or dietary restriction does not happen. Not all enzymes are deactivated, and some are used to preserve shelf life in products like baked goods or breads; thus, enzymes have to be disclosed on the food product label. The European Union has different regulations regarding enzyme labeling, following regulation No. 1332/2008 to allow food manufacturers to use only approved and tested enzymes. The European Union does not require labeling of enzymes used as processing aids so long as they do not remain active in the final product form and requires food manufacturers to declare enzymes prepared with any potential allergens on food labels. The Food and Agriculture Organization does not regulate the enzymes of the food industry directly but does provide general guidelines for countries to integrate into their own regulations, primarily focusing on the safety evaluations of enzymes used in food and good manufacturing practices. Having standardized information across the industry and regulations worldwide would enhance enzyme acceptability and regulation clarity for governing bodies and consumers alike.

Engineering Targets

Building on the broad applications of enzymes, recombinant DNA technology is used to modify enzymes in a way to be more applicable in the chosen application. , For enzymes to be used in the food and agriculture industries, enzymes are designed to retain performance in extreme (pH, temperature, salinity, etc.) environments, have improved performance (growth, availability, mixing, etc.) in bioreactors, and have fusion tags for ease of purification or immobilization.

Enhanced Performance in Extreme Environments

Enzymes can be denatured when their optimal environment is not the environment needed for the food or agriculture process in which they wish to be applied. Recombinant DNA technologies allow enzymes to be modified to become more thermostable, pH stable, or stable in extreme environments like high salinity. There are physical factors that can be manipulated in a processing environment to shift the optimal pH of an enzyme, such as buffer systems, additives, encapsulation, and surface modification. Site-directed mutagenesis can also be used to alter the enzyme active site and the interaction with the microenvironment. Li et al. found that for the phosphofructokinase B family of enzymes, the pH-affecting region could be mutated to increase the optimum pH from 4.0 to 5.0. For thermostability, many engineering modifications begin with evaluating sources of extremophiles or extremozymes as sources of naturally stable enzymes. The addition of noncovalent interactions, such as salt bridges, hydrogen bonds, or aromatic interactions, are some basic principles for thermostabilizing an enzyme. Thermostable enzymes that have been studied in-depth report greater amounts of hydrogen bonds compared to mesophilic enzymes. The introduction of additional hydrogen bonds in T4L lysozyme, as seen in Carlsson et al., shows a ∼3 kcal/mol stabilization without a degradation of activity. Salt bridges have been shown to stabilize tertiary and quaternary structures to help maintain the overall protein shape and assembly of the protein subunits. According to Ban et al., individual mutations on the 1,4-α-glucan branching enzyme to form local “two-residue” salt bridges increased the half-times of these mutants at 60 °C by 51% longer than that of the wild-type. Further studies reported that the introduction of salt bridges at flexible amino acid sites enhanced thermostability by 36.6% at 60 °C, improved substrate specificity, and increased relative activity by 31% over the wild-type enzyme.

Enhanced Production with Bioreactors

Enhanced bioreactor growth modifications are in high demand for recombinant enzymes to use a productive host organism, often E. coli or other bacteria, and maximized bioreactor conditions to produce enzymes at scale for large industrial applications. Recombinant native enzymes are a key target to be scaled up and applied in industrial applications. Shahzadi et al. constructed recombinant endoglucanase in E. coli to be grown in bioreactors. Their efforts resulted in high-efficiency overproduction of recombinant endoglucanase, yielding 6.9 g/L of biomass that retained 1185 U/mg catalytic activity when bioreactor conditions were optimized. Mou et al. produced recombinant lipase with in situ fibrous bed bioreactors to receive a 1.4-fold productivity increase as compared to classical bioreactors. This method not only highlights the capabilities of recombinant protein modifications for use in bioreactors but also shows the physical optimization that can go into the production of enzymes for industrial use.

Enhanced Recovery and Reuse with Fusion Tags

To aid in immobilization, which will promote greater enzyme stability for reuse and recovery, fusion tags can be genetically engineered to encode enzymes. Fusion tags can allow for simultaneous purification and immobilization, thereby streamlining the process. Some fusion tags have also been shown to improve the solubility of proteins by preventing aggregation. Tags have been used for a variety of enzymes: commonly used are His6, HaloTag, and SpyTag for purification, silica-binding domains and cellulose-binding domains for immobilization, and maltose-binding protein for enhanced enzyme solubility.

His6, HaloTag, and SpyTag are peptide sequences that exhibit low toxicity to host cells and can provide a high yield, purity, and activity after purification. These shorter tags are more chemically stable than larger proteins and allow easy adaptability from benchtop to industrial scale. Notably, the SpyTag has been used by Gao et al. to directly immobilize d-allulose 3-epimerase, resulting in overall higher thermal and pH stabilities than the free enzyme and maintaining 80% of relative activity after 7 consecutive cycles. This technique provides directional immobilization that can contribute to the accelerated production of d-allulose for industrial applications. Larger fusion tags and binding domains for immobilization have also been evaluated. According to Wang et al., recombinant lactase with a cellulose-binding domain was able to self-immobilize on cellulose to retain over 30% of initial catalytic activity over nine cycles of reuse without changes to optimum reaction conditions. Silica-binding domains have also been used in various agricultural applications. Zurier et al. reported that once bound to mesoporous silica, silica-immobilized PETase showed a 2.5-fold increased stability and a 6.2-fold increase in catalytic activity as compared to the free enzyme. To promote enzyme solubility of l-amino acid deaminase, Zhang et al. employed the maltose-binding protein tag and was able to not only achieve soluble expressed enzyme but also see an increase in catalytic activity compared to membrane-bound protein.

Immobilization overall provides the ability of the enzyme to be reused and recovered in industrial food and agriculture processes that provide sustainable and cost-saving benefits to manufacturers. Careful consideration must be taken to ensure that the fusion tag does not interfere with the function or stability of the native enzyme, and in some cases, short peptide linker proteins can help retain the natural enzyme folding while adding the fusion tag. ,

Synthetic Biology Frameworks

Synthetic biology frameworks in enzyme deployment in the food and agriculture industries can elevate enzyme functionality beyond that of traditional engineering methods by considering the broader genetic, regulatory, and ecological context for each specific enzyme and its intended application. Some of these approaches include de novo design of genetic circuits that can dynamically regulate enzyme expression; synthetic consortia for coordinated and multistep bioconversions; integrated design, build, test, and learn workflows; and genetic code expansion as previously mentioned. These approaches combine computation, automation, and machine learning that are key characteristics of synthetic biology to offer distinct benefits to end users but face numerous hurdles in scalability, regulatory approval, and cost-effectiveness.

De novo design of genetic circuits can take into consideration dynamic enzyme expression in response to environmental changes. Currently this is being applied in enzymes for high-level design objectives in bioproduction systems by engineering new bypasses within existing pathways, creating hybrid pathways, and creating new pathways for enzymes. , These dynamic regulators can assist during food and agricultural processing but have a fairly low TRL, likely 3–5, as there is much uncertainty around the pathways for food-grade organisms and the metabolic burden that is created. The insufficient experimental data available for genotype-to-phenotype data limits the speed of adoption and true understanding, which will likely continue to hinder commercial adoption until large libraries are created and studied. After greater data generation and collection, these dynamic regulators could be used for maintaining productivity in things like fermentation or agricultural waste degradation.

Synthetic consortia are already being used in preliminary stages in the agriculture industry, whereas food processors tend to take their cascade and coordinated steps by dosing various enzymes individually. , Using multistrain and multispecies systems to mimic natural cooperation and allow for specialized bioprocesses to be controlled brings great value to industry as it would shorten the time to finish a product. Primary challenges involve navigating the GMO landscape for acceptance from the consumer and regulatory bodies, resulting in a TRL of 4–6. While knowledge for specialized applications exists and there is the ability to create these multistep tasks, there is not great understanding for additional uses, and it can be lacking in industrial scalability.

Integrated “design, build, test, learn” workflows have opportunities because of the fast iterations and machine learning optimization but lack a clear application in food and agriculture. This technique excels when applied to bench-scale prototyping, with a TRL of 2–3, for developing enzymes and biochemicals, but industrial-scale adoption faces major hurdles, such as prohibitive costs, limitations of throughput at scale, and regulatory uncertainty. As a result, the major uses in food and agriculture for this workflow will likely remain in research-focused settings for enzyme discovery and model development rather than full-scale production. Integrated “design, build, test, learn” workflows could help reduce screening burden for research and enzyme companies by being able to learn the characteristics and create new enzymes, but that brings them to the main challenge in food and agriculture: toxicity and allergenicity in new food contact materials and ingredients.

Challenges and Opportunities in Synthetic Biology for Enzymology in Food and Agriculture

The greatest challenges that face the food and agriculture industries when applying enzymes are reusability, performance under extreme conditions, catalytic efficiency, production cost, and regulatory challenges. Enzymes, no matter their source, classification, or engineering or whether they are used in free or immobilized form, can suffer denaturation and decreased catalytic activity after multiple reuses. By applying enzymes in their free form, the cost of use increases drastically, as there is no opportunity for recovery and reuse. In some novel processes, especially for agricultural and environmental bioremediation, the environments are more extreme than the enzymes can handle and are not able to perform at high catalytic activity. Production costs for very pure enzymes with minimal microbial contamination are still higher than many starting food and agricultural producers can afford. Adoption of recombinant and engineered enzymes can face criticism from the public similar to the response to genetically modified additives in food or in food processes. Nevertheless, the increasing need for food security will dictate the need for more efficient processing methods and heightened integration of enzymes into technically challenging methods; synthetic biology offers a unique approach to meeting these challenges.

In addition to engineering, specialized techniques are used to aid in immobilization, such as encapsulation, cross-linking, entrapment, or covalent binding. Enzymes show promise for continued sustainability improvements for many food and agriculture processes by being biodegradable, highly specialized, and energy efficient. Not only can these techniques create more stable enzymes but also, if factored into their design, can minimize cost over time for the food and agriculture industries. Given the current knowledge of enzymes, there exists a significant opportunity to screen enzymes across various applications in food and agriculture to forecast their scalability and utility. By identifying and translating novel enzymes from other industries, new opportunities exist to enhance processes, solve emerging challenges, and streamline development time for enzyme applications. Tailored enzymes and rapid screening techniques are pivotal to enable the development of novel food and agriculture applications. Advances in understanding transition-state stabilization and ground-state destabilization have been studied by Chen et al., providing insight on catalytic reactions at a molecular level for practical artificial enzymes designed for drug use that can be later applied to the food and agriculture industries. As discussed by Adegboye et al., metabolic engineering has been a powerful tool used with enzyme engineering in biofuel production to increase the number and type of bioactive compounds that can be biosynthesized by microorganisms. , Metabolic engineering could be applied in food systems with advanced enzyme engineering techniques to create targeted bacteria to produce targeted enzymes, such as lactase-producing bacteria that could produce lactase in higher purity and yield. There is a market space to produce food-safe colorants and dye using enzymes; currently, indigo for the textile industry can be produced in a more sustainable and environmentally friendly way through the use of enzymes, moving away from traditional fossil feedstocks. To maximize profit and production, high-margin products should be developed first, informing further knowledge and information for lower-margin products. In many other biotechnologically focused industries, scaling up recombinant enzyme production has overcome a variety of problems and can find economically feasible ways to produce industrially needed amounts of enzymes from bioreactor design enhancements to novel membrane uses, aiming to keep infrastructure costs down and keep costs competitive for food and agriculture. ,

Cross-Sector Translation

The most beneficial way to implement new technology is to learn from other sectors and take the key findings and successes from one sector to another for implementing synthetic biology strategies. Innovations in pharmaceuticals, biofuels, and biopharmaceuticals provide transferable strategies that can accelerate the adoption of synthetic biology in food and agriculture.

The biggest translation hurdles primarily involve regulation and cost sensitivity. The food and agriculture industries are typically considered low-margin industries, but with value-added ingredients being able to be harnessed from waste streams, they provide value and cost alleviations to industry members who were once paying to remove their coproducts or wastes. In municipalities, the implementation greatly increases in difficulty as new processes and changes to the system typically are not within the budget, and economies of scale from various municipalities may be the best outlook forward.

A key insight from the pharmaceutical industry is the use of genetic code expansion for therapeutic proteins. − This knowledge can inform the creation of food and agricultural enzymes with enhanced stability or even novel catalytic functions. As pharmaceuticals continue to implement and expand this technique, food and agriculture can continue to benefit. Biofuels boast promise from metabolic pathways being refactored for isobutanol. − Building from this knowledge, there is great opportunity to optimize value-added ingredient, vitamin, or amino acid extraction for fortified foods. Biopharmaceuticals have been implementing cell-free protein synthesis for rapid prototyping of vaccines and other drugs. − This quick turnaround could aid in faster prototyping for novel applications in food and agriculture enzymes.

Future Outlook

Enzymes continue to play a crucial role in improving the quality and sensory characteristics of hydrolytically processed foods and are being integrated into food packaging to ensure food safety. Overall, synthetic biology enables unprecedented opportunities in redesigning existing enzymes (and designing new ones) to enhance their performance to improve food quality and safety, driving innovation and improving sustainability across the food and agricultural sectors. Synthetic biology makes advanced enzyme engineering economically viable in food and agriculture by combining biological efficiency with economic pragmatism. By finding better hosts, streamlining pathways, and designing with purpose, there is opportunity to leverage the existing infrastructure to commercially scale production. Over time, AI-driven optimization and economies of scale will drive costs down, making even the most complex techniques economically viable for any and every market.
