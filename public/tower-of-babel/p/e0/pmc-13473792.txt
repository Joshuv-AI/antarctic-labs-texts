Patterns and predictors of transition between multiple sclerosis phenotypes: A longitudinal analysis
Authors: Gauruv Bose, Nupur Greene, Brian C Healy, Howard L Weiner, Lisa Farnett, Keiko Higuchi, Tanuja Chitnis
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMultiple sclerosis (MS) phenotype transitions are challenging to characterize. Our understanding of their predictors is also limited.ObjectiveTo investigate the transition between relapsing-remitting MS (RRMS) and secondary progressive MS (SPMS) within the Comprehensive Longitudinal Investigation of MS at the Brigham and Women's Hospital (CLIMB) study.MethodsThis retrospective study included adult people with MS having ≥10-year follow-up data who were classified as RRMS, primary progressive MS, and SPMS (active SPMS [aSPMS]: with relapse; nonrelapsing SPMS [nrSPMS]: no relapses within 2-year pre-index). Demographics, disability scores, and transition predictors were assessed.ResultsNinety-seven of 565 people with RRMS (17.2%) transitioned to SPMS, of whom 92.8% (n = 90/97) were classified as nrSPMS by the final visit. People with RRMS who transitioned to nrSPMS were older (mean age, 53.1 vs. 45.4 years; P < 0.001) and took longer time to transition (mean, 13.0 vs. 7.8 years; P < 0.001) than those who transitioned to aSPMS. Older age at MS onset and a high number of disease-modifying therapy switches were significantly associated with a shorter time-to-nrSPMS transition (P ≤ 0.001).ConclusionThis study provides a comprehensive characterization of MS phenotype transitions, that may enhance clinical decision-making and therapeutic targeting of underlying pathophysiology driving disability in progressive MS.Keywords: Multiple sclerosis, secondary progressive multiple sclerosis, phenotype transition, disability accumulation, predictors, longitudinal study

Introduction

Multiple sclerosis (MS) is a chronic inflammatory, demyelinating, degenerative disease of the central nervous system, that can result in irreversible disability accumulation.1,2 It is among the most common neurological disorders in adults, with more than 2.9 million cases worldwide. 1 In the United States (U.S.), approximately 1 million people (288 per 100,000 individuals) older than 18 years are diagnosed with MS. 1

The clinical course of MS is highly heterogeneous. Current MS phenotyping is primarily focused on clinical classifications based on the patient's presentation of the disease.3,4 The most common phenotype is relapsing-remitting MS (RRMS), accounting for up to 85% of cases at onset. 5 Although progression may be slowed by disease-modifying therapies (DMTs), 6 some people with RRMS eventually transition to secondary progressive MS (SPMS).7,8 SPMS is clinically defined retrospectively and can be categorized based on the 2013 Lublin criteria distinguishing active and nonactive disease states. 3 Active SPMS (aSPMS) is defined by the presence of clinical relapses and/or radiological activity (new or enlarging T2-hyperintense lesions and/or T1 gadolinium-enhancing lesions on magnetic resonance imaging [MRI]). 3 People with aSPMS might qualify for specific treatments.9,10 However, disability continues to accumulate in some people with SPMS who are no longer experiencing relapses, a phenotype referred to as nonrelapsing SPMS (nrSPMS). Therapeutic options for nrSPMS remain limited, with several investigational agents currently in clinical development.10,11 Approximately 15% of people with MS (PwMS) experience consistent progression of disability from onset and are diagnosed with primary progressive MS (PPMS). 12

The transition from RRMS to SPMS presents a substantial diagnostic challenge, attributable to its gradual nature and lack of established clinical diagnostic criteria.13,14 Clinical trials commonly employ standardized measures of confirmed disability progression, defined as a sustained increase in Expanded Disability Status Scale (EDSS) score over 3 to 6 months, which provides a structural framework for interpreting disability accumulation. 15 However, in routine clinical practice, diagnoses are typically made retrospectively after a period of uncertainty. 13 Furthermore, people who transition from RRMS to SPMS are not well characterized. 16 Consequently, there is insufficient evidence of the predictors of transition, particularly for aSPMS and nrSPMS.

Therefore, this study aimed to (1) characterize demographic and clinical differences across MS phenotypes; (2) compare transition patterns and disability trajectories among individuals transitioning to nrSPMS directly or via aSPMS; (3) assess the rate of disability accumulation using time-to-EDSS milestone analyses; and (4) identify predictors of transition from RRMS to SPMS (including nrSPMS) within the Comprehensive Longitudinal Investigation of MS at the Brigham and Women's Hospital (CLIMB) study.

Materials and methods

Study design

This was a retrospective analysis of PwMS enrolled in the U.S.-based CLIMB study. CLIMB is a single-center prospective study that has been enrolling PwMS since 2000, with an attrition rate of 2% to 5% (www.climbstudy.org). 17 People aged 18 to 65 years and diagnosed with MS per the 2017 McDonald criteria were eligible. 18

The study was approved by the Mass General Brigham Institutional Review Board. Written informed consent was obtained from all PwMS enrolled in the CLIMB study. No identifiable information related to the study participants is presented in this study. A preliminary version of this analysis was presented at the Americal Academy of Neurology (AAN) Annual Meeting 2023. 19 This manuscript provides comprehensive methodological details, complete analyses, and a full interpretation of the study findings.

Study cohorts

Of the 2768 PwMS enrolled by database lock or “index” date (December 22, 2021), the analysis included those who enrolled in the CLIMB study within 3 years of symptom onset (n = 1181), received a diagnosis of MS between January 1, 2000 and December 31, 2010 (n = 795), and had ≥10 years of follow-up data (n = 579; Figure 1). PwMS aged <18 years or with incomplete medical records were excluded. The 2000 to 2010 enrollment window ensured ≥10-year follow-up by database lock date, providing adequate observation time to capture phenotype transitions and enable robust time-to-event analyses. The database lock date represents the most recent data available for analysis, balancing the need for contemporary data with the requirement for sufficient longitudinal follow-up.

People with multiple sclerosis in the CLIMB study transition analysis.

Eligible PwMS were characterized by their final phenotype, i.e., RRMS, SPMS, and PPMS, defined per their specific diagnoses validated by their neurologists. The SPMS population was further grouped based on the presence or absence of relapses, i.e., aSPMS (≥1 relapse in 2 years pre-index) and nrSPMS (no clinical relapse in 2 years pre-index).

Study assessments

All PwMS underwent clinical examinations, 1.5-T MRI scans of the brain and spinal cord with gadolinium using a standardized protocol, and cognitive evaluations, 17 at enrollment and then at different time points during the study. A volumetric analysis was completed to obtain the T2-hyperintense lesion volume (T2LV) of the brain and the brain parenchymal fraction (BPF). Both T2LV and BPF were reported as first observed and closest to the transition date for the descriptive analyses, ensuring all imaging findings were based on complete datasets.

Neurologic examinations were conducted biannually to obtain EDSS and timed 25-foot walk scores. The EDSS scores were categorized as EDSS 3 (moderate disability in one functional system), EDSS 4 (fully ambulatory for ≥500 m with relatively severe disability in ≥1 or a combination of functional systems with moderate disability), and EDSS 6 (requiring a gait aid to walk ≥100 m). Treatment information and relapse history were also recorded at each visit. The annualized relapse rate (ARR) was assessed based on the number of relapses over each 1-year period obtained from medical records.

Phenotype transition assessment

Phenotype transitions were determined at three time points: (1) initial MS diagnosis at CLIMB enrollment; (2) first SPMS transition (defined as the first visit documenting RRMS-to-SPMS conversion); and (3) final phenotype at the last study visit prior to the database lock date. Biannual neurological examinations enabled regular assessments of disease status, facilitating capture of phenotype changes over time. However, given the retrospective nature of SPMS classification, there may be delays in identifying some transitions, which represent an inherent limitation of clinician-based phenotyping.

Statistical analysis

Baseline demographics and clinical characteristics were summarized by the final MS phenotype (RRMS, aSPMS, nrSPMS, or PPMS). To characterize MS transition, the initial RRMS cohort transitioning to SPMS was compared with those remaining RRMS. Furthermore, PwMS who were “ever” aSPMS (experienced clinical relapses within 2 years before or after the date of their SPMS transition) were compared with those who were “never” aSPMS (transitioned directly from RRMS to nrSPMS without an aSPMS phase). It should be noted that this classification is a post hoc construct that does not distinguish between pre- and post-transition disease activity, introducing temporal ambiguity that limits causal interpretation. The data were summarized using descriptive statistics, including means with standard deviations (SDs), interquartile ranges for continuous variables, or counts (n) and proportions (%) for categorical variables.

The Kaplan–Meier (KM) method was used to estimate time-to-transition and to generate event curves from MS onset to transition and age at transition, stratified by PwMS who met or did not meet the criteria for aSPMS. Time to first EDSS scores were also estimated via the KM method to generate event curves, stratified by the final MS phenotypes. The ARR was estimated using a negative binomial model with robust variance estimation.

Predictor analysis was conducted via Cox regression, modeling time-to-SPMS and time-to-nrSPMS as outcomes. Individual predictors were examined separately through univariate analyses, followed by a multivariable model to identify independent factors associated with transition. For each analysis, P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

All statistical analyses were conducted using complete datasets. Of the 2768 PwMS enrolled in the CLIMB cohort, 17 (0.6%) were excluded due to incomplete or missing data (Figure 1). This high rate of data completeness (99.4%) reflects the robustness of the CLIMB cohort and ensures the reliability of all reported findings.

Results

Transition between MS phenotypes

A total of 579 PwMS (RRMS: n = 565; PPMS: n = 12; nrSPMS: n = 2 at initial MS diagnosis) were included in the study (Figure 2). Of the 565 people with RRMS, 95 transitioned to SPMS (aSPMS: n = 30; nrSPMS: n = 65 at initial SPMS diagnosis; Figure 2). The baseline characteristics of PwMS by their final phenotype are provided in Table 1. PwMS who transitioned to SPMS were significantly older (mean age, 39.0 vs. 34.3 years; P < 0.001) and had a longer mean follow-up duration (16.6 vs. 15.2 years; P < 0.001) than those who remained RRMS (Table 2). Moreover, PwMS who transitioned to SPMS had significantly higher first EDSS scores, lower BPF scores, and more DMT switches than those who remained RRMS (all P < 0.001; Table 2).

Considering the full cohort of 579 PwMS, there were 39 PwMS (6.7%) who had relapses within 2 years before or at any point after the date of their transition to SPMS and were classified as “ever” aSPMS. From this group of “ever” aSPMS, 32 of 39 (82.1%) eventually transitioned to nrSPMS at their final visit. Among the 95 people with SPMS, 56 (58.9%) transitioned “directly” from RRMS to nrSPMS without sustaining a clinical relapse during the follow-up period and thus “never” met the criteria for aSPMS. The first transition from RRMS to nrSPMS occurred in a significantly longer period than RRMS to aSPMS (mean [SD], 13.0 [5.0] vs. 7.8 [5.0] years; P < 0.001; Figure 3A; Supplementary Table 1). In addition, those who transitioned directly to nrSPMS were significantly older (mean [SD], 53.1 [10.5] vs. 45.4 [10.7] years; P < 0.001; Figure 3B; Supplementary Table 1) and were less likely to change DMTs post transition (45.0% vs. 74.0%; P = 0.004; Supplementary Table 1) than those who transitioned via aSPMS. However, EDSS scores post transition remained comparable between the 2 cohorts (4.3 vs. 4.4; P = 0.73; Supplementary Table 1).

During the subsequent transitions, when considering the time-to-event analysis, neither disease duration nor age at nrSPMS was significantly different between PwMS who directly transitioned to nrSPMS and those via aSPMS (P = 0.932 and P = 0.302, respectively; Supplementary Figure 1).

Time to reach the EDSS milestone

The median time from disease onset to reach EDSS level 3 was 16.2 years overall, varying by MS phenotype: 19.2 years for RRMS, 3.4 years for aSPMS, 4.5 years for nrSPMS, and 3.4 years for PPMS. In the overall population, 50% did not reach EDSS level 4 or 6 during the follow-up period, which was mainly driven by the RRMS group. The risk of earlier progression to EDSS milestones 3, 4, or 6 was significantly higher for people with aSPMS, nrSPMS, or PPMS relative to those with RRMS (all P < 0.0001; Figure 4).

Annualized relapse rate

A total of 2235 relapses were reported in the overall population. The mean (SD) number of relapses per person was 3.9 (3.3), over a follow-up of 15.5 (3.2) years, resulting in a mean (SD) ARR of 0.3 (0.2) relapses per year (Supplementary Figure 2). Differences in the mean number of relapses per person and the average ARR were observed across phenotypes: RRMS (3.8 relapses per person [ARR, 0.3]), aSPMS (7.0 relapses per person [ARR, 0.4]), and nrSPMS (4.1 relapses per person [ARR, 0.3]).

Predictors of transitions

When assessing time-to-SPMS transition via univariate analysis, older age at MS onset, higher baseline EDSS scores, more DMT switches, greater functional system signs, higher baseline T2LV, and lower baseline percent BPF were significantly associated with shorter time to SPMS (all P < 0.05). After adjusting for all factors in the multivariable analysis, older age at MS onset (hazard ratio [HR] [95% confidence interval; CI], 1.05 [1.02–1.07]), higher baseline EDSS score (1.24 [1.0–1.54]), and more DMT switches within 3 years of onset (1.24 [1.13–1.36]) remained significantly associated with the risk of developing SPMS (all P < 0.05; Figure 5).

Discussion

This retrospective real-world study analyzed 579 PwMS from the U.S.-based CLIMB cohort over ≥10 years of follow-up, assessing patterns of RRMS-to-SPMS transitions. Of the 565 people with RRMS, 95 transitioned to SPMS, with 90 ultimately classified as nrSPMS by the end of follow-up. Notably, 56 PwMS transitioned directly to nrSPMS without experiencing prior relapses, whereas others progressed via aSPMS.

The onset of SPMS is often identified as a “key turning point” in the MS disease course; however, the precise timing and clinical criteria for identifying this transition remain challenging in clinical practice, with diagnoses often made retrospectively.20,21 Although standardized phenotypic definitions are essential for clinical practice, regulatory decisions, and research, they do not fully capture the biological complexity of individual disease trajectories or the gradual nature of transitions. 14 These findings should therefore be interpreted within an evolving conceptual framework, whereby transitions reflect clinician-documented progression along a continuum rather than distinct disease categories.22,23 Consistent with this perspective, we observed that PwMS who transitioned from RRMS to SPMS tended to be older and had greater baseline disability than those who remained RRMS, similar to findings from a cross-sectional study of the US National Health and Wellness Survey. 24

Among the SPMS population, the initial RRMS-to-SPMS transition was dependent on age and disease duration; people transitioning to nrSPMS were significantly older and had longer disease duration than those transitioning to aSPMS. However, these effects diminished in subsequent transitions as age and disease duration at nrSPMS were similar regardless of transition pathway (direct nrSPMS vs. via aSPMS). This suggests that PwMS entered the progressive phase at different ages but exited the relapsing phase within a similar age range, consistent with age-related relapse decline reported in the literature.25,26 Additionally, post-transition EDSS scores were nearly identical among those who transitioned directly to nrSPMS versus via aSPMS. Together, these findings suggest that aSPMS and nrSPMS may not represent biologically distinct pathways but rather reflect varying contributions of inflammatory and neurodegenerative processes along a shared disease continuum.27,28

Time-to-event analyses demonstrated faster attainment of EDSS milestones in progressive phenotypes compared with RRMS. Although EDSS trajectories are partly incorporated into phenotype definitions, these temporal patterns may still provide clinically relevant insights when interpreted alongside other measures of disability progression.29,30 Regarding relapse activity, people with aSPMS reported the highest ARR across phenotypes, underscoring the importance of early diagnosis for optimal patient management.

Predictor analysis demonstrated that older age at MS onset and frequent DMT switches within 3 years of symptom onset were associated with RRMS-to-nrSPMS transition, consistent with previous longitudinal studies on SPMS.22,25,31 Critically, the association between early DMT switching and shorter time-to-nrSPMS transition is likely subject to confounding by indication rather than reflecting a causal relationship. Frequent early treatment changes likely serve as a proxy for inadequate disease control and should be interpreted cautiously rather than as a causal driver of progression.

Overall, these findings underscore an unmet need for novel therapies. Despite multiple available DMTs,32–34 limited post-transition DMT use in nrSPMS reflects significant treatment gaps. 10 Therapies targeting relapse-independent progression beyond focal inflammation, such as Bruton tyrosine kinase (BTK) inhibitors, could reshape therapeutic strategies for progressive MS. 11

Beyond conventional MRI measures used in this study, emerging biomarkers provide additional insights into disease activity and progression. The growing recognition of MS as a biological continuum is driving the field beyond conventional clinical classifications toward biologically informed phenotyping for improved disease characterization. In this context, advanced imaging markers, including brain atrophy, paramagnetic rim lesions, slowly expanding lesions, and cortical pathology, as well as complementary biomarkers such as serum neurofilament light chain, kappa free light chains, and optical coherence tomography–derived peripapillary retinal nerve fiber layer measurements, are increasingly being explored for their ability to capture relapse-independent disease activity and progression.35,36 The absence of these biomarkers in our study represents a limitation, as their integration could enhance the precision of progression assessment beyond clinician-defined phenotypes and may help refine identification of transition patterns in future studies.

This study has several limitations. First, reliance on clinician-based RRMS-to-SPMS classification may introduce misclassification bias, as some people might have already transitioned prior to formal documentation. This is particularly relevant for nrSPMS, where absence of relapse served as the defining feature. Second, the inclusion criteria resulted in the selection of a subset of the CLIMB cohort (n = 579 of 2768; 21%), potentially introducing survivorship bias and limiting generalizability to individuals with more rapidly progressive disease or incomplete follow-up. However, this approach was necessary to ensure adequate observation time for phenotype transitions occurring over extended periods. Third, as a single-center tertiary care study, these results may not be generalizable to the broader MS population in the U.S. However, any sampling bias would likely affect both groups who did or did not transition, minimizing bias in comparative estimates. Finally, the study period (2000 to 2021) encompasses substantial therapeutic evolution, from first-generation injectable therapies to high-efficacy DMTs, introducing temporal heterogeneity in treatment exposure that may influence long-term SPMS transition risk.

Nonetheless, this study had several strengths, including low variability across prescribing practices and physicians at the CLIMB study center. Furthermore, multiple measures were used to assess MS, rather than relying solely on lesion accrual from MRI and EDSS measurements.

Conclusions

This longitudinal study of the CLIMB cohort provides a comprehensive characterization of MS phenotype transitions and their clinical implications. Transitions from RRMS to SPMS occurred via an active phase with relapses or directly into a nonrelapsing progressive phase. PwMS transitioning through aSPMS tended to do so earlier and were more likely to undergo treatment escalation, whereas those transitioning directly to nrSPMS experienced fewer relapses yet reached comparable levels of disability. Disease severity was similar across progressive phenotypes, emphasizing the need for earlier progression detection. Predictive modeling identified age at onset, baseline disability, and treatment history as significant transition factors. Continued research is needed to develop interventions targeting progressive MS pathophysiology, particularly the relapse-independent mechanisms that drive disability accumulation, to effectively mitigate long-term disability.
