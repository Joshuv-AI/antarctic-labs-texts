Hidden risks of treatment: The vital role of patient education in preventing life-threatening adverse drug effects: A case report of toxoplasmosis encephalitis medication mismanagement
Authors: Sedigheh-Sadat Erfani, Mahila Monajati, Rahmat Allah Sharififar, Narges Lashkarbolouk, Hanieh Raghimi, Sina Mohajernoei
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMedication errors remain a leading cause of patient harm, with significant consequences for both health outcomes and healthcare systems. In this case report, we presented a human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) positive patient who was treated with pyrimethamine due to Toxoplasmosis encephalitis. He unaware of the critical role of folinic acid, neglected to take it, resulting in severe hematologic complications, including disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC). This report highlights the importance of effective patient education and clear communication regarding the potential risks of medication regimens. It also underscores the need for healthcare systems to implement robust safety protocols, enhance training for healthcare professionals, and encourage a culture of transparency in error reporting. Preventing medication errors requires both systemic changes and a commitment to continuous education for both patients and providers to ensure patient safety and improve healthcare quality.Keywords: Medical error, HIV, Patient safety, Patient education, Patient harm, Toxoplasmosis encephalitis

Introduction

Medical errors have emerged as a major public health concern, increasingly recognized for their unfortunate effect on the healthcare system and patient outcomes. Recent studies have highlighted that medical errors represent the third leading cause of death in the United States. The incidence and outcomes of medical errors are still challenging to quantify as a result of the diversity of their nature and consequences, ranging from near-miss events to severe harm or fatalities [1].

The World Health Organization (WHO) establishes patient safety as the reduction of unnecessary harm or potential harm related to healthcare to an acceptable minimum [2]. Medication errors are considered preventable events that may cause inappropriate medication consumption or patient harm under the control of a healthcare professional, patient, or consumer. These errors are especially concerning in therapeutic frameworks where complex interactions between professionals, institutions, and technologies inherently increase the risk of mistakes [3].

The safe administration of medications is still a cornerstone of patient safety [2]. As an example, Pyrimethamine is widely prescribed in combination with other medications for the treatment of Toxoplasma gondii infections. Pyrimethamine inhibits dihydrofolate reductase, leading to a blockade of DNA synthesis. Administering high doses of Pyrimethamine can result in severe myelosuppression, which is a side effect that can be reduced by prescribing folinic acid along with it.

Failure to adhere to this precaution has been associated with life-threatening complications, underscoring the critical need for education and adherence to medication regimens [4].

In addition, errors in medication management can also impact healthcare staff, who may experience guilt, shame, or fear in the wake of adverse events [5]. The pervasive culture of blame in medicine has been cited as a barrier to transparent reporting and learning from mistakes [5], [6]. Addressing these cultural and systemic issues is vital for fostering an environment of safety and accountability [6].

This case report illustrates the profound consequences of a medication error involving the omission of folinic acid in a patient treated with Pyrimethamine and sulfadiazine for cerebral toxoplasmosis. The bone marrow suppression and disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC) exemplify the potentially fatal outcomes of these errors. By presenting this case, we aim to emphasize the importance of attentive bonding to medication guidelines, patient education, and systemic improvements in medication safety practices to prevent parallel conditions in the future.

Methods

Data collection

This is a case report study about a 57-year-old male patient who was diagnosed with human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) and treated with pyrimethamine due to Toxoplasmosis encephalitis. All information related to this case report was taken from the patient's clinical record and the hospital's health system. To write this case report, all relevant articles up to May 2025 were used.

Ethics approval and consent to participate

Written informed consent was obtained from the patient for publication of this case report and any accompanying images. A copy of the written consent is available for review by the Editor-in-Chief of this journal. An ethical code is not mandatory for case report study in our medical university. The purpose of this case report was completely explained to the patient and he was assured that his information would be keep confidential by the researchers. This case report was performed in line with principles of the declaration of Helsinki.

Case presentation

A 57-year-old male patient presented to the neurosurgery clinic with numbness in his right limbs that began 10 days prior. Given the symptoms, a brain Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) was ordered, which showed a 23 mm thick, regularly enhancing lesion in the left thalamus (Fig. 1). This lesion was accompanied by significant surrounding vasogenic edema, extending into the deeper regions of the left parieto-temporal lobe and into the left midbrain tegmentum. The findings were indicative of a potential focal brain abscess formation.

Serological tests were positive for both HIV and Toxoplasma antibodies. CSF analysis was normal. The patient was informed of the HIV diagnosis, and a comprehensive risk factor assessment was conducted, including a detailed inquiry about unprotected sexual contact, history of transfusions, intravenous drug use or needle sharing, perinatal transmission, and occupational exposure. The patient consistently and repeatedly denied any identifiable risk factors. No history of surgery, transfusion, tattoos, or substance use was reported.

The patient was initially prescribed Pyrimethamine, folinic acid, and sulfadiazine. Because of the unavailability of pyrimethamine, treatment was started with clindamycin and cotrimoxazole as a temporary alternative until it could be obtained. The patient underwent surgery utilizing the stereotactic biopsy technique to rule out lymphoma. Postoperatively, the patient remained hemodynamically stable with an unchanged overall condition. Based on a positive Western blot and a CD4 count of 64, the patient was treated with a combination of emtricitabine/tenofovir and dolutegravir for antiviral (ART) treatment. Prophylactic regimens of fluconazole, clarithromycin, and isoniazid were initiated based on the CD4 level, along with the original prescription of Pyrimethamine, sulfadiazine, and folinic acid.

The patient's wife and 13-year-old child were unaffected. Biopsy revealed necrotic areas consistent with Toxoplasma encephalitis, not brain lymphoma. Although, as a treatment approach, the patient could receive two weeks of treatment for HIV and toxoplasmosis (with positive IgG for both and imaging consistent with toxoplasma encephalitis), and if there was no adequate response, further evaluation for brain lymphoma could be considered. After several days of monitoring, the patient, in good condition, was discharged.

Three months later, the patient was readmitted to our hospital with hemoptysis, hematuria, and gastrointestinal bleeding. A hemogram revealed pancytopenia with severe thrombocytopenia (Table 1). Upon reviewing the patient’s home medications, it was discovered that folinic acid was not included. The patient's wife admitted that they had personally ensured the procurement and administration of all medications, but mentioned that the medical staff had not emphasized the importance of proper medication usage and the potential side effects of missing folinic acid.

Based on medical history findings, clinical presentation, and initial laboratory results, the possibility of DIC was investigated for him (Table 3). The DIC was established based on the The International Society on Thrombosis and Haemostasis (ISTH) overt DIC scoring criteria, using available parameters [7]: severe thrombocytopenia (platelets 7 × 10⁹/L), prolonged prothrombin time (PT) (15.1 s), elevated d-dimer (1.9 mg/l), hypofibrinogenemia (fibrinogen 167 mg/dL), combined with clinical evidence of multisite bleeding.

Following treatment with packed cells and platelets, the patient stabilized and survived. Two weeks after starting folinic acid and correcting the blood indices, Pyrimethamine was reintroduced along with sulfadiazine. In addition, an MRI was required to monitor the lesion, which has since reduced in size compared to the initial admission (Fig. 1).

At the time of initial discharge and during follow-up, the patient’s cognitive status was formally assessed using the Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE), on which he scored 27/30, indicating normal cognitive function. He was fully oriented to person, place, and time, and showed adequate short-term memory and attention. His wife confirmed that he had no history of cognitive impairment, dementia, or psychiatric illness. However, after the diagnosis of HIV, he became depressed and preferred to be isolated. Therefore, an effort was made to provide all education related to the disease and how to take medications, along with mentioning danger and warning signs, to the patient's spouse, in addition to the patient himself.

Currently, 2.5 years after the last hospitalization, the patient is still alive, with a CD4 count of 89. The patient continues to receive prophylactic cotrimoxazole due to limited access to Pyrimethamine in the region. However, as a result of cachexia and severe weakness, he is unable to walk and remains bedridden at home, supported by their family.

Discussion

Medical errors remain a critical challenge in healthcare systems, as even a single mistake can lead to severe complications or fatal outcomes [8]. By presenting this case, we accentuate an avoidable medical error due to the failure to adequately inform the patient about the necessity of simultaneously administering folinic acid and Pyrimethamine. This omission led to severe hematologic complications, including DIC, pancytopenia, and subsequent hospitalization in a critical state.

A fundamental issue in this case was the lack of sufficient communication regarding the essential role of folinic acid in counteracting pyrimethamine-induced bone marrow suppression. Pyrimethamine is a potent dihydrofolate reductase inhibitor that disrupts folate metabolism, which can lead to tetrahydrofolate deficiency [9]. If this deficiency remains uncorrected, it can result in myelosuppression, leukopenia, thrombocytopenia, and severe anemia. Despite established guidelines recommending concomitant folinic acid supplementation to mitigate these adverse effects, insufficient emphasis on this precaution led the patient to neglect folinic acid intake, ultimately culminating in life-threatening complications [10].

Multiple studies have shown that medication errors are among the most common categories of medical errors. The causative factors are broadly categorized into human factors (e.g., fatigue, stress, and insufficient knowledge) and systemic failures (e.g., poor hospital management, lack of standard protocols, and ineffective monitoring systems) [8], [11]. Notably, in a study conducted by Asghar et al. showed that more than 74% of hospitalized patients experience medication errors, with more than one-third of these errors leading to moderate-to-severe harm [12]. Additionally, a significant proportion of these errors go unreported due to fear of punitive consequences, inadequate error-reporting systems, and an ambiguous definition of what constitutes a medical error [11], [13].

DIC is a serious disorder characterized by widespread activation of coagulation, leading to microvascular thrombosis and concurrent consumption of clotting factors and platelets, resulting in bleeding [7]. In this patient, DIC presented as hemoptysis, hematuria, and gastrointestinal bleeding, with laboratory findings of severe thrombocytopenia, prolonged PT, elevated d-dimer, and hypofibrinogenemia. DIC is a rare complication of severe, untreated pyrimethamine-induced myelosuppression.

To foster a culture that supports error reporting, healthcare institutions should implement a constructive approach. Establish dedicated patient safety committees responsible for reviewing reported errors without punitive action and identifying system-based solutions. Develop standardized, anonymous, and easy-to-use reporting protocols that reduce administrative burden. Provide mandatory, ongoing training for all healthcare staff on recognizing, documenting, and reporting medication errors. Implement a non-punitive, just culture policy that distinguishes between human error, at-risk behavior, and reckless behavior. Conduct regular multidisciplinary morbidity and mortality conferences that focus on system failures rather than individual blame. Provide feedback about actions taken, closing the loop, and reinforcing the value of reporting [14], [15].

In our case report, the healthcare team's negligence in delivering vital information about pyrimethamine-induced folate depletion and its prevention represents a serious lapse in patient education and medical responsibility. Modern healthcare systems must prioritize preventive strategies, including the implementation of electronic prescribing systems, fostering a culture that encourages error reporting, and reinforcing continuous medical education for healthcare providers [8], [11], [16]. Additionally, enhancing direct patient education about their prescribed medications and their potential adverse effects should be an integral component of patient safety initiatives [16], [17].

Conclusion

The results of our study underlined the dreadful consequences of medication errors and the vital role of comprehensive patient education in preventing avoidable complications. The failure to emphasize the necessity of folinic acid supplementation alongside Pyrimethamine resulted in severe hematologic toxicity and increased morbidity. To prevent similar events, healthcare authorities should implement strengthened training programs for medical staff, initiate effective monitoring systems for high-risk medications, and foster an environment where error reporting is encouraged rather than penalized. Ultimately, the prevention of medication errors is not merely a matter of improving individual clinical practice but a fundamental component of healthcare system safety and quality improvement.
