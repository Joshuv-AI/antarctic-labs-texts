Impact of home-based follow-up care on hypertension control among patients attending Thika Level Five Hospital, Kiambu County, Kenya: A randomized control study
Authors: Veronica Njaramba, Peter Mwaura, Samuel Mungai, Bernard N Kanoi, David Kamau, Jesse Gitaka
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHypertension contributes to a large burden of cardiovascular diseases, with a higher prevalence in low- and middle-income countries. Hypertension management is mainly hospital-based, but it faces low retention rates in facilities, non-adherence and subsequent suboptimal blood pressure (BP) control. This study aimed to implement home-based follow-up care and assess its impact on controlling BP.MethodsAn open-label randomized controlled study was conducted on 200 participants with uncontrolled BP selected using a systematic random sampling technique and randomly assigned into home-based follow-up care (intervention group) and hospital-based follow-up care (control group). Data were collected using a structured questionnaire containing the 8-item Morisky Medication Adherence Scale and Hypertension Self-Care Activity Level Effect (H-SCALE). Data were analysed using SPSS version 29.0.ResultsHypertension control was 52.0% and 92.2% in the control and intervention groups, respectively. In the interventional group, drug adherence increased from 38.2% to 94.1% compared with a reduction in the control group from 45.9% to 33.7%. Dietary Approaches to Stop Hypertension diet adherence rose from 2.9% to 76.5% compared with a control group from 1% to 2%.ConclusionsHome-based care follow-up has a significant impact on the control of hypertension.Keywords: adherence, control, home-based care, hypertension

Introduction

Hypertension is a risk factor for cardiovascular diseases (CVDs) that contribute greatly to the increasing burden of non-communicable diseases (NCDs) globally and more so in developing countries, where 80% of the global NCDs are reported.1,2 The prevalence of hypertension is increasing with low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) disproportionately affected. A quarter of the Kenyan adult population is hypertensive, contributing to 28% mortality.3,4 In Kiambu County, hypertension is the number five cause of death and the number six cause of morbidity.5 Low awareness, treatment and control rates have been reported. The 2015 Kenya STEPS survey reported that 56% of Kenyans have never had a blood pressure (BP) measurement, an awareness rate of 15% with 8% being on treatment, of whom 4.6% are controlled.6 Some of the identified patient-related factors for poor control are low knowledge of hypertension, poor adherence to drugs and lifestyle modification strategies, financial constraints, psychosocial stress and inadequate access to healthcare services.7,8 Uncontrolled hypertension results in complications such as stroke, hypertensive heart disease, kidney failure and coronary artery disease.9 Management of the complications is expensive, and more so in LMICs, where there are limited resources.7 Follow-up of hypertensive patients is a major component of their care, with frequent follow-up leading to good BP control due to frequent monitoring and adjusting of medications to achieve control and offering an opportunity for health education, including the importance of lifestyle modification.10,11 Current hypertension care is mainly hospital-based in a nearby facility or of the patient's choice for both diagnosis and follow-up. In Kenya, low rates of linking and retaining patients to follow-up care in hospitals have been reported.10,12 Home-based follow-up care of hypertension has been implemented in other countries and was found beneficial in BP control and adherence to medication.13 In Kenya, it has not been implemented or studied in hypertension follow-up despite being used in the management of other conditions, such as human immunodeficiency virus, and was found beneficial. It has not been integrated into the healthcare delivery system and its structure is not developed.

This study applied an intervention of home-based follow-up care in the interventional group and continued with routine hospital-based follow-up care for the control group. It assessed the impact on BP control, adherence to medication and lifestyle modification.

Methods

Study area

This research was carried out at a Thika Level 5 Hospital in Kiambu County. It was purposively selected because it is in the central Kenya region, which has the highest prevalence of hypertension in the country at 37.2%.14 Kiambu County has been reported to be second in the country in NCD prevalence.15 In Kiambu County, hypertension has been reported to be the number five cause of death and the number six cause of morbidity16 with 453 745 cases reported in 2012.15 The Thika Level 5 Hospital was purposively selected, being one of the three level 5 hospitals in the county and is situated in Thika town, which has been reported to be first in the utilization of healthcare services in the county and so may reflect more on the health status of the county and is also cosmopolitan, so the results can be generalized to a diverse population.

Study design

The research design was an open-label mixed cross-sectional randomized controlled study. Recruitment and training of one team of research assistants comprising one clinical officer (CO) and one community health worker (CHW) was done. A total of 210 participants sampled using a systematic sampling method were recruited and informed consent was obtained. Baseline data were collected using a researcher-administered questionnaire. Participants were randomized into two groups: a control group (hospital-based follow-up) and an interventional group (home-based follow-up). The interventional group received weekly text messages reminding them to take medication and to adhere to lifestyle modifications and were followed up at home once every month with quarterly hospital reviews for 6 months. Each visit had five components: medical history and physical examination, BP measurement, counselling on drug adherence and adverse effects assessment and advice on lifestyle modification. Details were recorded in a follow-up form. The control group continued with the usual standard hospital-based care follow-up at the discretion of the managing physician/medical officer or CO. Post-intervention data were collected after 6 months of intervention implementation. All the study participants were visited at home. The questionnaire used to collect baseline data was administered to collect the post-intervention data, allowing comparison with the pre-intervention data as a means to evaluate the impact of home-based follow-up on control of hypertension.

Study population

The study population was hypertensive patients ≥30 y of age, hypertensive for >2 months and on treatment with uncontrolled BP visiting the Thika Level 5 Hospital medical outpatient clinic and outpatient clinic. Those with comorbidities other than diabetes mellitus, with mental problems, pregnant, with BP >180/110 mmHg, in need of emergency care and temporary residents of Kiambu County likely to leave within 7 months of recruitment were excluded.

Data collection and study tools

Pre-intervention and post-intervention data were collected using a researcher-administered structured questionnaire. The information in the questionnaire was classified into four parts. Part 1 contained the sociodemographic characteristics of the patients. Part 2 contained BP and anthropometric measures including weight, height, body mass index (BMI), waist circumference and waist:hip ratio. Part 3 contained a medication adherence assessment using the 8-item Morisky Medication Adherence Scale (MMAS-8). The response choices for items 1 through 7 are ‘yes’ or ‘no’. For these items a ‘no’ response is scored 1 and a ‘yes’ response is scored 0, except for item 5, where a ‘yes’ response is scored 1 and a ‘no’ response is scored 0. Item 8 uses a 5-point Likert scale. The score for item 8 (ranging from 0 to 4) is standardized by dividing the results by 4 before adding it to the total score. A total possible score of 8 indicated high adherence, a score of 6 or 7 indicated medium adherence and a score <6 indicated low adherence. Part 4 contained a lifestyle modification assessment using the Hypertension Self-Care Activity Level Effect (H-SCALE), which were in five parts. Part 1, medical adherence (three items each with a score of 7), has a response range of 0–21; a score of 21 is considered adherent. Part 2, DASH diet (11 items each with a score of 7), has a response range of 0–77; a score ≤32 is considered low diet quality, 33–51 is medium diet quality and ≥52 is considered adherent. Part 3, engaging in physical activity (two items each with a score of 7), has a response range of 0–14; a score ≥8 is adherent and <7 is non-adherent. Part 4: avoiding smoking (two items each with a score of 7), has a response range of 0–14; a score of 0 is adherent. Part 5, strategies to maintain or lose weight (10 items each on a Likert scale of 1 strongly disagree–5 strongly agree), has a response range of 10–50; a score ≥40 is considered adherent to good weight management practices. Home-based follow-up forms were used to record the clinical information of a patient for every visit and were kept as part of their clinical records. This form had patient identification details including the research number, date of review, medical history (chief complaints with emphasis on risk factors), vital signs (temperature, pulse rate, BP, respiratory rate), medication the patient is on and counselling on adherence to medication and lifestyle modification.

Testing for validity and reliability

A pilot study of the model, questionnaire, tools (BP machine, thermometer, tape measures and weighing scales) and skills was done at the Gatundu Level 5 Hospital on 10% of the sample size (i.e. 22 participants) after which adjustments were made to both the model and the skills. The data collected were analysed to determine whether it helped to meet the study objectives. Due to the use of the MMAS-8 and the H-SCALE in the questionnaire, the pretest data were analysed using Cronbach's α, after which adjustments were made to meet a Cronbach's α of 0.7. The Cronbach’s α reliability coefficient test revealed that the reliability results for the questionnaire as an instrument for sociodemographic characteristics was 0.779, for adherence to antihypertensive was 0.806 and for adherence to dietary and lifestyle modification was 0.750. The researcher and the research assistants administered the questionnaire to the patients so that the questions were explained to the participants so that they understood uniformly. The use of a systematic random sampling technique and randomization of the participants also helped to avoid bias in sample selection. It was also explained to the participants that they were to be enrolled only once. The files of the enrolled participants were assigned a research number and a different coloured sticker for the two study groups: green for the control group and red for the intervention group.

Data analysis

Quantitative information was analysed using SPSS version 29.0 (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA). The z-test was computed using the BP readings obtained before and after the intervention. Correlation analysis was employed to test the relationship between dependent and independent variables. χ2 and odds ratio (OR) at the 95% confidence interval (CI) were used to estimate the strength of association between independent variables and dependent variable. The threshold for statistical significance was set at α=0.05.

Results

A total of 210 respondents participated in the baseline study, with 200 participating in the post-interventional study. The return rate for questionnaires was 95.2% and was thus considered sufficient to provide adequate and reliable information.

Participants’ drug adherence to antihypertensives

At baseline. high adherence using the MMAS-8 was 52.0% for the control group and 33.3% for the intervention group, with the prevalence of adherence using the H-SCALE being 45.9% and 38.2% for the control and intervention groups, respectively. Post-intervention prevalence of high adherence was 18.4% and 93.1% using the MMAS-8 for the control and intervention groups, respectively, and the prevalence of adherence using the H-SCALE was 33.7% and 94.1% for the control and intervention groups, respectively (Table 1).

Participants’ adherence to lifestyle modification using the H-SCALE

Baseline DASH diet adherence was 1.0% and 2.9% for the control and intervention groups, respectively, while the post-intervention adherence was 2.0% and 76.5% for the control and intervention groups, respectively. Adherence to physical activity for the control group was 18.4% at baseline and post-intervention and was 31.4% at baseline for the intervention group and 60.8% at post-intervention. Control of hypertension

All the hospital-based respondents (98 [100.0%]) and home-based respondents (102 [100.0%]) who participated in the post-intervention had uncontrolled hypertension at baseline. After the intervention, slightly more than half (52.0%) of the control group had controlled hypertension, with 92.2% of the intervention group controlling hypertension (Table 3).

Effect of intervention on adherence to antihypertensive

Participants in the intervention group with high adherence using the MMAS-8 increased from 33.3% at baseline to 93.1% post-intervention and was statistically significant (χ2=11.328, p=004), while those adherent using the H-SCALE increased from 38.2% at baseline to 94.1% at post-intervention and was statistically significant (χ2=10.866, p=0.012). The overall level of adherence increased from 56.9% to 93.1% before and after the intervention and the increase was significant (χ2=7.986, df 1, p=0.018) (Table 4).

Effect of intervention on dietary and lifestyle modification

High adherence to DASH diet intake increased from 2.9% to 76.5%. There was an increase in adherence to physical activity from 31.4% to 60.8% and avoidance of smoking from 88.2% to 95.1% at pre- and post-intervention, respectively. Discussion

Our study provides evidence that home-based follow-up intervention for hypertensive patients delivered by healthcare providers and CHWs was efficacious in improving BP control compared with the usual hospital-based care.

After 6 months of follow-up, there was significant control of BP in the intervention group compared with the control group. These findings are consistent with a study on home-based follow-up in Nigeria that showed better BP control in the intervention group (85.9%) than in the control group (76.6%).13 The findings are also consistent with a study in Kenya using CHWs for follow-up, which decreased BP in the intervention group by 19 mmHg.17 Similarly, a meta-analysis of the effects of home-based care on BP control showed a significant reduction in diastolic and systolic BP.18 This could be due to participants receiving personalized health education and solutions, regular BP monitoring and involvement of the family support system, which encouraged adoption of lifestyle modification and adherence to medications. The interventions offered of regular BP monitoring and reminders and counselling on medication and lifestyle modification adherence are also evidence-based.17,18

The study revealed significant post-interventional medication adherence in the intervention group, with 93.1% high adherence and 94.1% adherent as per the MMAS-8 and H-SCALE, respectively. These findings were notably higher than adherence rates in studies conducted in southwest Ethiopia (62.8%) and Pakistan (77%).19,20 This may have been because of differences in the study design, settings, sample size and participants’ characteristics. The findings of high adherence are similar to studies done in the USA (91%) and a study in Kenya (94%).11,21 This may be due to the study design, like use of the MMAS-8, and also with interventions to improve accessibility to care.

The study found that the place of follow-up, age and duration of illness were significantly associated with adherence to antihypertensive medication. This finding aligns with other studies that show adherence to medication increases with advanced age up to 70 y and with a longer duration of illness; after the age of 70 y adherence decreases.22,23 This has been hypothesized to be due to the fact that older people have greater severity of illness than younger ones, leading to increased awareness about their health status, while those >75 y of age have reduced adherence due to cognitive impairment, functional disabilities, transportation problems and financial problems.24

The study showed improved post-intervention adherence to the DASH diet (76.5%), physical activity (60.8%) and smoking (95.1%). Adherence to the DASH diet was similar to that in a study in Ethiopia (76.4%).25 This might be because the two studies used standardized tools to assess adherence. The findings in the current study are higher than in studies in Saudi Arabia (11.8%) and Nigeria (20%).26,27 The difference may be due to gender inclusion, as the study in Saudi Arabia used only males, the use of a larger sample size, sociocultural differences and study design.

Adherence to physical activity was higher than in studies in Ethiopia (37.4%) and the USA (52.2%).28,29 This difference may have been because of differences in the study design and social setting. However, the findings were lower than in a study in India (89%),30 which could be due to demographic differences in that the India study was for males only, and again the India participants demonstrated a high awareness level of the recommended physical activity.

In this study, 95.1% of the participants avoided smoking. The findings of this study are consistent with studies done in Addis Ababa and Kenya, where patients with hypertension who were not smoking or quit smoking were 95.8% and 90.5%, respectively.31,32 However, the findings were greater than in a study in eastern Ethiopia, where adherence to not smoking was 81.5%.33

There were higher adherence rates to lifestyle modification among younger, married, female respondents and those with longer disease duration in the intervention group. These findings of higher adherence with the young are similar to studies in Durame, Ethiopia, China and Nigeria.27 The possible explanations for this are older people are less educated and are unable to obtain information on lifestyle modification than the young and also they have reduced income, which impedes their ability to achieve lifestyle modification. The findings of higher adherence in those with a longer duration of disease were also consistent with findings from Durame, Ethiopia and Nigeria.27 This is thought to be due to long-term counselling and health education about lifestyle modification changes.

Conclusions

Home-based follow-up management of hypertensive patients was effective in achieving BP control and medication and lifestyle modification adherence compared with the usual hospital-based care. In particular, the study showed that integration and implementation of the components of the chronic care model of conducting coordinated, planned and regular follow-up visits and self-management support by empowering patients with health education in the management of hypertension are feasible in the low-income setting of Kiambu County. However, the current findings are only based on a small sample derived from a Kenyan population and therefore future studies with much larger sample sizes and encompassing other geographic areas are recommended to assess the effectiveness of the intervention.
