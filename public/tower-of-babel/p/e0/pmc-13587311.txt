Dynamics of spatiotemporal pharmacological models for hepatitis B virus infection under lamivudine and pegylated interferon alfa-2b therapies
Authors: Boris Rosmes Tchioffo, Alain Mvogo, Patrice Ele Abiama
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractChronic hepatitis B virus (HBV) infection remains a major global health concern due to the limited availability of curative treatment options. In this study, we investigate both analytically and numerically a spatiotemporal mathematical model of HBV infection incorporating pharmacological effects. The model consists of a system of three partial differential equations describing the dynamics and interactions between healthy hepatocytes (S), infected cells (I) and free viral particles (V). The model also considers spatial diffusion and dual-drug treatment of lamivudine and pegylated interferon alfa-2b (PEG-IFN alfa-2b). A general analytical expression is derived to determine the minimal drug dose required to effectively suppress viral replication and eliminate the infection. The basic reproduction number () is calculated as a function of drug effectiveness and model parameters. To illustrate the relevance of the model, we apply it to clinical data from two HBV infected patients undergoing different therapeutic regimens. The first patient received 200 mg daily of lamivudine, while the second was treated with 200 mg of lamivudine daily and 0.2 mg weekly of PEG-IFN alfa-2b. Using nonlinear least squares, we fit the model to clinical data and estimate relevant parameters. In the absence of treatment, we find . Pharmacodynamic results show that after 4 weeks, the first patient experienced a copies/ml viral decline (64% effectiveness), while the second exhibited a copies/ml decline (69% and 64% effectiveness for PEG-IFN alfa-2b and lamivudine, respectively), corresponding to basic reproduction numbers of 185 and 58, respectively. To achieve sustained viral suppression (), we optimize the therapies. Lamivudine alone requires 99% effectiveness, which corresponds to a minimum dose of 565.92 mg daily over 350 days. For combination therapy, a PEG-IFN alfa-2b efficacy of 69% and lamivudine efficacy of 94.36% are sufficient to reach after 315 days. A Turing instability analysis reveals the emergence of diffusion-driven instabilities and allows the identification of a critical Turing wave number . Numerical simulations confirm the analytical predictions and show the formation of circular patterns. These patterns have been identified as having promising diagnostic and therapeutic value. Under treatment, the model predicts a progressive disappearance of infected patterns and a return toward spatial homogeneity. These findings highlight the crucial role of mathematical modeling in refining HBV treatment protocols and provide valuable insights for optimizing antiviral strategies.Keywords: Hepatitis B virus, HBV mathematical model, Pharmacokinetics, Pharmacodynamics, Treatment effectiveness, PEG-IFN alfa-2b, Lamivudine, Basic reproduction number, Turing instability, Pattern formation

Introduction

Hepatitis B is a potentially fatal liver infection caused by the hepatitis B virus (HBV) [1–4]. This infection represents a major global health burden, with the World Health Organization estimating that approximately 257 million people were living with chronic hepatitis B infection in 2022. Additionally, around 1.5 million new infections occur annually [5].

The high prevalence of HBV underscores the urgent need for effective prevention, diagnosis, and treatment strategies to mitigate its impact on public health. One of the main transmission mechanisms is the release of viral particles from infected cells into the extracellular environment [6, 7]. These viral particles can then infect neighboring healthy cells by binding to specific receptors on their surfaces. However, it is important to note that not all healthy cells are susceptible to infection by the hepatitis B virus. Liver cells, in particular, are prime targets for the virus due to the presence of specific receptors that facilitate virus binding and entry. Once inside the host cell, the virus hijacks the cellular machinery to replicate and produce new viral particles, perpetuating the infection cycle. The infection rate can also be influenced by several factors, including an individual’s general state of health, viral load (i.e., the amount of virus present in the blood), the effectiveness of the immune system in controlling infection, and other physiological and environmental conditions. These factors contribute to the complexity of HBV infection dynamics and pose challenges for disease management and treatment. Among the long-term complications of HBV infection, a significant proportion of individuals may develop advanced liver diseases such as cirrhosis and hepatocellular carcinoma [8]. These conditions impose a substantial burden in terms of morbidity and mortality, making hepatitis B a leading cause of liver-related deaths worldwide. In that sense, many studies have focused on the mathematical models to assess the dynamics of hepatitis B [9–14]. The models have used theoretical and computational approaches to better understand viral replication, immune response and disease progression [9–11]. Other models explored the role of time delays in the interaction between healthy cells and free virus particles; and the results revealed that the delayed immune responses or viral production can influence disease dynamics and treatment outcomes [15–19].

However, chronic HBV infections can be managed with antiviral drugs, particularly oral nucleotide/nucleoside analogs, which can suppress viral replication. In that sense, mathematical modeling of drug effects [20–22] allows a better understanding of the complex interactions between antiviral drugs and HBV and helps optimize treatment strategies to improve clinical outcomes. Just to cite a few, Nowak et al. [20] developed a viral dynamics model to explain the biphasic decline of HBV DNA under antiviral therapy, highlighting the importance of drug efficacy in blocking viral production. Perelson et al. [21] proposed a well-known model of HIV infection under antiretroviral treatment, which has since been adapted to HBV to estimate infected cell lifespan and drug effectiveness. More recently, Dahari et al. [22] established a mathematical framework for HBV kinetics during treatment with nucleoside/nucleotide analogs, distinguishing between the blocking of viral production and the prevention of new infections. Although the diffusion effect of drugs has not been taken into account, the above studies have contributed to a deeper understanding of hepatitis B infection and guided the development of improved control and treatment strategies. In reality, antiviral agents administered to patients must diffuse through biological tissues before reaching infected cells, which may lead to spatial heterogeneity in drug concentration and consequently affect treatment efficiency.

In this paper, we focus on the modeling and analysis of the pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics of HBV taking into account the drug diffusion effects. The diffusion process of drugs is fundamental in pharmacokinetics, as it governs the absorption, distribution and elimination of drugs. We then reformulate the pharmacokinetic equations by integrating drug diffusion in the intestine, blood and hepatic tissues. By accounting for spatial heterogeneity and concentration gradients, the proposed mathematical model can provide a better representation of drug transports and interactions in target tissues. We also introduce the Beddington saturation term [23] to capture the nonlinear density-dependent effects on viral spread. As an application of the work, we analyse the effectiveness of two types of therapy: lamivudine monotherapy or combined with PEG-IFN alfa-2b. We discuss on the effects of drugs on viral replication and viral load in the model and provide more precise information on the mechanisms of drug action and therapeutic strategies. The rest of the paper is organized as follows: In section “Mathematical model”, we first present the mathematical model that describes the dynamics of the disease. We introduce the pharmacotherapeutic modeling, which allows us to account for the effects of antiviral treatments. We analyze the steady states of the system and determine the basic reproduction number. We found the optimal drug dose required to eradicate the infection. We also perform the Turing instability analysis and determine the critical Turing wave number for pattern formation. Section “Discussion and conclusion” provides a discussion of the results and concludes the paper.

Mathematical model

In the modeling of within-host viral dynamics, several state variables are commonly used to describe the infection process. These include the density of healthy hepatocytes (S), the density of infected cells (I) and the density of free viral particles (V). Healthy liver cells are the main targets of the HBV. The virus can infect these cells and hijack the host cellular machinery to replicate its genome and produce new viral particles. Infected cells release newly formed viral particles into the extracellular space within the liver. From there, viral particles can diffuse through the interstitial fluid, enter the bloodstream and potentially infect other hepatocytes. Cells infected with the hepatitis B virus can have a variable lifespan. They can be eliminated quickly by the immune system or can persist for months or even years. Virus-infected cells can also migrate to other parts of the liver, contributing to the spread of infection within the organ. From a clinical perspective, the endemic equilibrium state under treatment represents a stable condition where HBV is reduced to lower levels compared to the untreated endemic state. While not achieving full viral clearance, this reduction represents a substantial decrease (approximately 57% lower) that is clinically meaningful. This equilibrium is achieved when the rate of viral replication is sufficiently reduced, allowing the immune system to control the infection and promote the restoration of liver function. In this state, the number of infected hepatocytes and circulating viral particles is significantly decreased, indicating a substantial reduction in viral propagation within the liver. Clinically, this suppression of viral replication leads to reduced hepatic inflammation and lowers the risk of developing severe complications such as cirrhosis and hepatocellular carcinoma. Antiviral treatments that effectively target HBV replication facilitate the attainment and maintenance of this favorable equilibrium, thereby enhancing patients’ quality of life and improving long-term prognosis [8, 49].

Pharmacotherapeutic analysis

This section is devoted on the effects of the pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics of therapeutic agents in the system. Pharmacokinetics is a branch of pharmacology that studies the absorption, distribution, metabolism, and elimination of drugs in the body. Pharmacodynamics focuses on the relationship between drug concentration and its response, as well as the interactions between drugs and biological targets [54]. In the PK-PD process, drugs act by binding to specific receptors in the body. Receptors are proteins present on the surface or inside cells. When a drug binds to a receptor, it can activate or inhibit the receptor’s function, resulting in a biological response. We consider two classes of treatment:

Treatment 1: immunomodulatory therapy This class of antiviral treatments consists of immunomodulatory agents that enhance the body’s natural defense mechanisms against infections. These drugs stimulate the immune system by activating various signaling pathways, promoting the production of antiviral proteins, and enhancing the activity of immune cells such as natural killer (NK) cells and cytotoxic T lymphocytes. As a result, they help inhibit viral replication and facilitate the elimination of infected cells. Immunomodulatory therapies are commonly used in the treatment of chronic viral infections and other immune-related conditions. Examples of such drugs include immunomodulators such as PEG-IFN-2b, Toll-like receptor agonists (e.g., GS-9620) and checkpoint inhibitors (e.g., anti-PD-1 therapies), which regulate immune responses to enhance antiviral activity [55–57].

Treatment 2: Inhibition of viral DNA synthesis [58]: nucleoside/nucleotide analogs inhibit the activity of the viral DNA polymerase enzyme, which is responsible for synthesizing viral DNA from viral RNA. By incorporating themselves into the viral DNA chain, these drugs cause premature termination of DNA synthesis, thereby preventing viral replication and reducing viral load. Examples of such drugs include lamivudine, entecavir, tenofovir, adefovir, and telbivudine [59–61], which are commonly used in the treatment of viral infections such as hepatitis B.

Pharmacokinetics analysis

Pharmacokinetics of immunomodulatory agents

The immunomodulatory antiviral agents are administered by subcutaneous injection. They follow a specific pathway in the body. First, the medication is injected into the subcutaneous tissue layer, forming a depot. From there, the drug is gradually released into the surrounding tissues. It is absorbed by the blood capillaries and transported through the circulatory system to target organs, particularly the liver. Once in the liver, these agents stimulate the immune system to combat viral infections, inhibit viral replication, and promote the elimination of infected cells. This antiviral and immunomodulatory action helps reduce inflammation and improve biological parameters associated with the disease. Previous pharmacokinetic models [62, 63] often overlooked the role of spatial diffusion, assuming a well-mixed system that does not fully capture the heterogeneous distribution of antiviral agents in the body. However, to better represent the spatio-temporal distribution of these immunomodulatory antiviral agents, we extended the pharmacokinetic model by incorporating diffusion terms. These terms explicitly account for the passive movement of drug molecules within bodily fluids and tissues, which is critical to understanding their uniform distribution in target tissues. It should be noted that drug transport in the systemic circulation is primarily governed by advection due to blood flow rather than by molecular diffusion. Therefore, the diffusion terms considered in the present model should be interpreted as an effective representation of local drug dispersion within hepatic tissues and microvascular regions over relatively short spatial scales. While this assumption allows us to investigate the spatial distribution of antiviral agents within the liver microenvironment, a more realistic description of systemic drug transport would require the inclusion of advection–diffusion mechanisms, which is left for future work. By integrating diffusion, the model provides a more realistic representation of drug dispersion, overcoming the limitations of previous approaches that neglected these spatial effects. Pharmacokinetics of nucleoside/nucleotide

Nucleoside and nucleotide analogs are commonly administered orally in the form of tablets. Once ingested, the medication follows a specific pathway in the body. It is first absorbed through the gastrointestinal tract, where it encounters gastric acidity and then traverses the small intestine. The drug is absorbed by the intestinal wall and enters the bloodstream, where it is transported to target organs and tissues, including the liver and immune cells. Nucleoside and nucleotide analogs act by inhibiting the replication of HBV within infected cells, reducing viral load, and slowing the progression of the infection. Unlike standard pharmacokinetic model, which assume uniform distribution, the analysis incorporates spatial diffusion into the bloodstream. This approach accounts for the drug’s gradual distribution across the vascular system, reflecting a more realistic mechanism. The results show a continued decline in viral activity, with a more pronounced degradation of the spatial patterns associated with infected cells and free viral particles. Unlike the observations at 200 days, after 320 days the characteristic circular structures have almost entirely vanished, and the tissue environment appears largely cleared of infection zones. This significant reduction highlights the cumulative impact of prolonged therapy, leading to a near-complete suppression of the infection. Biologically, this suggests that the extended treatment duration allows the immune system and antiviral agents to progressively eliminate even the most persistent viral reservoirs. The near-total disappearance of structured infection zones reflects an advanced stage of viral control, and potentially signals a transition toward viral eradication or long-term remission. These findings emphasize the importance of sustained treatment in chronic hepatitis B management. The combination of PEG-IFN alfa-2b and lamivudine demonstrates a powerful and durable effect when administered over an extended period. Importantly, the lack of resurgence in viral patterns after 320 days suggests a reduced likelihood of relapse, reinforcing the necessity of maintaining antiviral pressure beyond initial improvements. This result also supports clinical strategies that favor longer treatment regimens to achieve robust and lasting therapeutic outcomes.

Discussion and conclusion

In this study, we investigated a spatiotemporal mathematical model of HBV infection under antiviral therapy. The model integrates the pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic effects of lamivudine and pegylated interferon alfa-2b (PEG-IFN-2b). It also considered, along with diffusion terms for healthy cells infected cells, and free viral particles. Biological saturation effects were incorporated to reflect constraints in viral replication. The theoretical analysis was based on linear stability analysis, the Routh Hurwitz stability criterion and Turing instability theory. This allowed to derive the basic reproduction number as a function of key parameters, including drug efficacies and diffusion coefficients. These modeling choices are consistent with established within-host HBV modeling frameworks [20, 32, 33], which have similarly employed compartmental representations of healthy hepatocytes, infected cells, and free viral particles to study infection dynamics. Without treatment, is estimated at 184. Under non-optimized therapy, it drops to 58. Optimization results suggest that a combination of 69% interferon efficacy and 94.36% lamivudine efficacy corresponding to a daily dose of 494.79 mg brings below 1, a critical threshold for viral eradication. Similarly, our pharmacodynamic study showed that monotherapy with lamivudine requires 565.92 mg/day to reduce below 1. While this dosage reduces the viral load to undetectable levels and remains below toxicity thresholds, infected cells and residual viral particles persist, suggesting limited control and potential resistance with monotherapy. This observation aligns with previous modeling and clinical studies reporting that lamivudine monotherapy provides incomplete viral suppression and carries a significant risk of resistance development over prolonged treatment [12, 14, 81]. In contrast, the combination of lamivudine and PEG-IFN-2b proved significantly more effective. Numerical simulations show that a dual therapy with 494.79 mg of lamivudine and 0.2 mg of PEG-IFN-2b administered over approximately 315 days promotes not only viral load suppression but also clearance of infected cells. Under optimized conditions, viral eradication is achieved after 315 days. The combined therapy thus reduces both treatment duration and the risk of toxicity, while limiting viral persistence. The superiority of combination therapy is consistent with findings reported by Liu et al. [74] and Carey and Harrison [81], who demonstrated that dual-drug regimens achieve greater reductions in viral load and infected cell counts compared to monotherapy, owing to the complementary mechanisms of action of nucleoside analogues and immunomodulatory agents.

In addition to temporal dynamics, our model captured essential spatial features of infection. The Turing analysis led to the identification of a critical instability parameter , indicating conditions under which spatial heterogeneities may arise. Simulations confirmed the emergence of circular spatiotemporal patterns, which progressively dissipate under effective treatment. These patterns likely reflect localized viral reservoirs or immune escape zones, critical to understanding treatment failure or success. Their disappearance following optimized therapy suggests spatial homogenization and tissue recovery. The formation of such diffusion-driven spatial patterns in reaction-diffusion biological systems has been studied theoretically by Zheng et al. [77] and Lv et al. [78], and the present results extend this line of work to the specific context of HBV infection under pharmacological treatment. To our knowledge, this is the first study to link the emergence and resolution of Turing patterns to antiviral therapy outcomes in HBV infection, offering a spatially explicit perspective that complements earlier temporal models [10, 25]. In conclusion, this study highlights the importance of combining pharmacological data with spatially explicit mathematical modeling to optimize HBV treatment strategies. While monotherapy shows limitations, combination therapy offers more robust control by promoting the clearance of both viral particles and infected cells. Furthermore, the emergence and resolution of spatial patterns during treatment provide novel insights into infection dynamics, offering potential biomarkers for therapeutic success. Our approach opens avenues for integrating spatial data into treatment design and reinforces the relevance of pattern formation in understanding and managing chronic viral infections. However, the current model is subject to several limitations. First, the model calibration relies on data from only two patients, which limits the generalizability of the findings and may not capture the full spectrum of inter-individual variability in treatment response. In addition, the model assumes an effective diffusive dispersion of healthy and infected hepatocytes, which should not be interpreted as actual cellular motility but rather as a mathematical approximation of local infection spread, tissue remodeling and spatial heterogeneity within the liver. Second, the inclusion of a time delay, while biologically motivated, introduces additional complexity into the model and can affect the stability and accuracy of numerical simulations. Third, the model does not explicitly incorporate the host immune response nor the covalently closed circular DNA (cccDNA) reservoir, both of which play a central role in viral persistence and treatment outcome. Fourth, drug-resistant viral mutants, a well-documented concern under prolonged lamivudine therapy [59], are not accounted for, which may lead to an overoptimistic assessment of long-term monotherapy efficacy. Finally, the spatial domain is represented as a homogeneous two-dimensional square, which does not capture the complex lobular microarchitecture of the liver. Future work should therefore consider extending the model to larger patient cohorts, incorporating immune response dynamics, and validating the spatial patterns against histological data from liver biopsies in order to strengthen the biological and clinical relevance of the proposed framework [10].
