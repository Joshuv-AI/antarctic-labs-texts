Versatile phenolic composites by in situ polymerization of concentrated dispersions of carbon nanotubes
Authors: Zhilong Yu, Chier Zhang, Mingtao Chen, Jiaxing Huang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractUniform dispersion of carbon nanotubes in a polymer matrix is a prerequisite for high-performance nanotube-based composites. Here, we report an in situ polymerization route to synthesize a range of phenolic composites with high loading of single-wall carbon nanotubes (SWCNTs, >40 wt%) and continuously tunable viscoelasticity. SWCNTs can be directly and uniformly dispersed in cresols through noncovalent charge-transfer interactions without the need for surfactants, and further concentrated before in situ polymerization of the solvent molecules, yielding phenolic composites in the forms of conductive pastes, highly stretchy doughs, and hardened solids with high nanotube loading and much enhanced electrical conductivity (up to 2.7 × 104 S m−1). These conducting phenolic composites provide a versatile material foundation for many areas. As a proof of concept, the conductive paste is used to construct ultrasensitive motion sensors that can operate at unprecedently low voltages (e.g. 0.1–10 mV). Moreover, the ease of processing and shaping of phenolic composite solids from the dough state is demonstrated.

Carbon nanotubes (CNTs) enhance the performance of engineering polymers and functional materials, but achieving uniform dispersion while maintaining high CNTs loading remains a challenge. Here, an approach that can achieve very high loadings of uniformly dispersed CNTs in phenolic resins is demonstrated by in situ polymerization of concentrated CNT dispersion in m-cresol. The unique decoupling, where filler interaction and polymerization proceed independently through different molecular sites of cresol monomers, leads to high versatility and tunability of this in situ polymerization strategy. This is an example that resolves the long-standing issue of balancing good dispersion in polymers with loading capacity when using CNT powder, which should lead to the development of a range of high-performance CNT-based composite materials for engineering applications.

Introduction

Carbon nanotube (CNT) has been widely used to enhance the performance of many engineering polymers and fabricate functional materials (1–9). Achieving uniform CNT dispersion is essential to maximize their synergistic effects with macromolecules and unlock their full modification efficacy.

Highly conductive CNT/polymer composites require minimizing both contact resistance and tunneling resistance between CNTs, which depends on high loading, uniform dispersion, and pristine CNT surfaces (10). However, for CNT starting materials in the form of powders, which are now mass producible, a persistent challenge exists in that uniform dispersion and high loading are fundamentally difficult to achieve. On one hand, CNTs act as effective solution thickeners due to their low mass density and high aspect ratio. On the other hand, better dispersion increases viscosity, impeding higher loading. Consequently, relatively good dispersion (e.g. nano-dispersed CNTs) is typically achievable only at low loadings (<1 wt%). While CNTs can be dispersed in common solvents via mechanochemical, physicochemical, or irradiation treatments (11–20), these methods often yield homogeneous dispersions only at low concentrations, damage or contaminate CNT surfaces, and compromise composite properties. Although harsh solvents like superacid can disperse CNTs (21, 22), their extreme corrosiveness limits practical use with polymers.

Alternatively, polymers can act as a solvent-free matrix to disperse CNT. However, directly mixing CNT powder with viscous polymers (e.g. phenolic resin) typically causes CNT aggregation or macro-scale dispersion, where CNT-rich clusters become isolated within the polymer. This occurs due to the significant mixing entropy penalty between high-molecular-weight macromolecules and CNTs. Consequently, even high loadings (e.g. 70 wt% multi-wall CNTs) yield poor electrical conductivity (∼8 S cm⁻¹) (23). To assess CNT efficiency in enhancing conductivity, we define a “weight-normalized conductivity” as the overall conductivity divided by the CNT content. Most reported bulk composites exhibit low normalized conductivities (<20 S m−1 wt%−1), particularly at high CNT loadings (>10 wt%), indicating inadequate dispersion (Table S1, Fig. 1a). A promising approach to achieve both high loading and homogeneous dispersion would be in situ polymerization, where low-molecular-weight monomers premixed with CNTs are polymerized, circumventing the challenges of blending with viscous polymers.

In our earlier studies, phenolic solvents, in particular m-cresol, have been shown to uniformly disperse various types of CNTs through charge-transfer interaction without the need for surface functionalization or any additional dispersing agent (24, 25). Meanwhile, cresols can polymerize with aldehydes (e.g. formaldehyde) to produce phenolic polymers. It is worth mentioning that the phenolic hydroxyl group of m-cresol plays a key role in the charge-transfer interaction, but it does not react in the polymerizing reaction. Therefore, the final polymer retains abundant phenolic hydroxyl groups available to interact with the CNTs, leading to well-dispersed CNTs in the final resin (Fig. 1b).

This work presents a series of phenolic nanocomposites featuring single-walled CNT (SWCNT) loadings as high as 44 wt% and electrical conductivities up to 2.7 × 104 S m−1. In situ polymerization of m-cresol and formaldehyde ensures homogeneous SWCNT dispersion in all cases (Fig. 1b). To further increase SWCNT loading, the precursor solution (SWCNT/m-cresol/formalin) was concentrated via three methods before polymerization. First, concentration by centrifugation produces composites with <1 wt% SWCNTs and conductivity <10 S m−1. The second approach employs solvent dilution to reduce the m-cresol content, thereby increasing SWCNT concentration relative to reactive monomers after centrifugation, yielding composites with ∼5 wt% SWCNTs and conductivity ∼800 S m−1. In the third approach, vacuum-assisted filtration is applied to remove the maximum possible m-cresol, achieving SWCNT loadings in the range of 20–44 wt% and conductivities up to ∼2.7 × 104 S m−1. This performance surpasses earlier CNT/polymer composites derived from powders by 1–2 orders of magnitude (23, 26, 27). The in situ polymerization approach also delivers composites with superior normalized conductivity (up to 690 S m−1 wt%−1) and a wider tunable CNT loading range (Fig. 1a). Control over the polymerization degree readily transforms the material across distinct states: a viscous paste, an intermediate stretchable dough, and a hardened solid. Disruption of the conductive SWCNT network in the paste by external forces produces significant resistance changes. Leveraging this, a proof-of-concept sensor fabricated from the paste detects minute disturbances while functioning at unprecedentedly low operating voltages (0.1–10 mV). The mechanical properties of the solid composites are markedly improved by the homogeneous SWCNT dispersion, highlighting their potential as conductive engineering materials. Furthermore, the unique dough state emerges as an excellent morphing material, enabling the effortless creation of customized and complex architectures.

Results

Commercially available SWCNT powders typically contain significant aggregates and bundles (Fig. S1). To overcome this, a homogeneous SWCNT/m-cresol dispersion (1 mg mL−1) is first prepared employing a sonication-based procedure as reported earlier (24). The aqueous formaldehyde solution (i.e. formalin) is then added as the second monomer and thoroughly mixed with the dispersion at a defined molar ratio relative to m-cresol (Fig. 1). Although m-cresol is normally immiscible with water due to the predominant hydrophobicity of its benzene ring and methyl group, it exhibits surprising miscibility with formalin. This behavior is presumably due to the formation of low-molecular-weight poly(oxymethylene) glycols within the formaldehyde solution (28, 29), resulting in a homogeneous ternary SWCNT/m-cresol/formaldehyde precursor solution. However, the addition of formalin substantially dilutes the SWCNT concentration. Direct polymerization of this diluted mixture would yield final solid composites with very low SWCNT content, estimated at ∼0.07 wt%. To achieve higher SWCNT loadings, the precursor solution is concentrated prior to initiating polymerization. Crucially, the complete miscibility between m-cresol and formalin ensures that the residual solvent in the concentrated precursor retains a monomer molar ratio similar to the initial feed ratio.

Synthesis of different conductive composites

Three methods based on direct centrifugation, dilution-centrifugation, and vacuum-assisted filtration were employed to increase SWCNT concentration before in situ polymerization (Fig. 2a–c). An initial SWCNT/m-cresol/formalin solution (<1 mg mL−1 SWCNTs) was concentrated via centrifugation into a thick slurry to ∼5 mg mL−1 (Fig. 2a). By modulating the degree of polymerization, this concentrated slurry yielded three distinct product forms: viscous paste, soft dough, and hard solid. For simplicity, these SWCNT/phenolic composites are designated SWCNT/PR-x, where x denotes the formaldehyde-to-m-cresol molar ratio. Thermoset intermediates (i.e. products that are crosslinkable to solids upon heating) were typically obtained when x > 1. Raman spectroscopy confirmed charge-transfer interactions between SWCNTs and phenolic hydroxyl groups, evidenced by a blue shift in the G-band (∼1,582 cm−1, attributed to in-plane sp2 carbon vibrations; Fig. S2). This shift indicates reduced SWCNT electron density (30, 31).

Versatile phenolic SWCNT composites with tunable nanotube loadings and viscoelasticity. a–c) Schematic drawings illustrating different concentrating approaches based on a) centrifugation, b) solvent-exchange and centrifugation, and c) vacuum-assisted filtration to prepare three states of SWCNT/PR composites, including (d, e) conductive pastes, (f, g) moldable doughs, and (h, i) hardened solids with controllable SWCNT loading. a) Centrifugation produces composites with relatively low level of SWCNT content of <1 wt% and b) solvent-exchange combined with centrifugation leads to a medium level of SWCNT content of around ∼5 wt%, while (c) filtration yields the highest SWCNT loadings (20–44 wt%). e, g, i) SEM observation of fractured dried samples shows that SWCNTs are well dispersed in the pastes, doughs, and hardened solids after processing steps such as spreading, rolling (∼1 cm in width), kneading (∼1.5 cm in width and ∼3 cm in height), and press molding (∼4 cm in width).

Cresol's phenolic hydroxyl groups are primarily responsible for charge-transfer interactions with SWCNTs during dispersion, yet they do not react during polymerization—a hallmark of phenolic resin chemistry (32). Importantly, since these phenolic hydroxyl groups persist in the final polymer matrix, it enables robust interfacial interaction between the polymer and the CNTs, which directly enhances mechanical and electrical properties. This unique decoupling—where filler interaction and polymerization proceed independently—is the defining strength of cresol monomers, underpinning the versatility and tunability of this in situ polymerization strategy. By controlling the degree of polymerization, a viscous gel-like SWCNT/PR paste can be obtained and the uniform SWCNT dispersion is well preserved, as shown by the scanning electron micrograph (SEM) and microscopic image (Figs. 2d, e and S3a).

When increasing the degree of polymerization, a soft, stretchy, and dough-like material with uniform SWCNT dispersion can be obtained between the paste and the final solid states (Figs. 2f, g and S3b). In contrast to the high stickiness of the conventional phenolic resin, the semisolid SWCNT/m-cresol/formaldehyde dough behaves like the stretchable plasticine. The methyl group acts as an electron donor which increases the electron density of the benzene ring and the hydroxyl group, likely contributing to the low stickiness (33). In addition, the methyl group increases the steric hindrance of phenol and thus retards curing kinetics, ensuring a stable dough at room temperature (34). In the presence of SWCNTs, the composite dough (hereby named SWCNT/PR dough) retains excellent stretching capabilities (Video S1) and processibility. The nonstickiness allows origami or kirigami of its paper form, and makes the composite compatible with various processing methods, such as rolling, kneading, and press molding to create complex geometries and architectures (Fig. 2f).

Solid products can be made by further increasing the degree of polymerization, e.g. intentionally curing the intermediate-state paste by mold-casting, or curing the dough after reshaping into geometrical architectures (Fig. 2h). The thermosetting intermediate products, e.g. SWCNT/PR-1.2 paste or dough, can be fully cured at high temperature (usually ∼200 °C for phenolic resin). The m-cresol-derived resin has been demonstrated as less thermally stable compared to the conventional phenol-derived resin, because the methyl substitution at the meta position decreases the stability of dihydroxy diphenyl methane unit of the phenolic polymer (35). So, in our curing procedure, the curing temperature is set to be no >180 °C to avoid thermal degradation (Fig. S4). The SWCNT dispersion is well preserved in the solid products (Fig. 2i).

Centrifugation yields composites containing <1 wt% SWCNTs across all material states (Fig. 3a), which is insufficient to generate good electrical conductivity (discussed in detail later). Achieving higher conductivity necessitates increased SWCNT loading, which is determined by the SWCNT concentration in the concentrated precursor slurry. Directly increasing the SWCNT concentration in m-cresol significantly elevates viscosity due to SWCNT-induced thickening, making it harder to achieve dispersion homogeneity. An alternative strategy involves reducing the proportion of reactive monomers (acting as solvent) within the slurry, effectively increasing the relative SWCNT concentration. To implement this, a solvent-dilution process was developed: a volatile, compatible secondary solvent dilutes the reactive cresol monomer concentration, enabling higher relative SWCNT concentrations following centrifugation. Previous studies indicate low-dielectric solvents are compatible with SWCNT/m-cresol dispersions (25). This secondary solvent must also be compatible with the phenolic polymer and exhibit sufficient chemical and thermal stability throughout the reaction. 1,4-Dioxane (Dio), a low-dielectric solvent, meets all requirements and was selected as the ideal co-solvent. Adding a defined amount of Dio dilutes the SWCNT/m-cresol/formaldehyde solution; subsequent centrifugation produces a concentrated slurry with a reduced reactive monomer fraction (Fig. 2b). Note that Dio reduces the polymer content and retards polymerization, hindering attainment of the stable dough state. Consequently, only one sample (formaldehyde/m-cresol molar ratio = 1.2), labeled SWCNT/PR-1.2-Dio, is presented. In a typical synthesis, initial m-cresol and formaldehyde concentrations are halved by Dio addition, doubling the relative SWCNT concentration to monomers postcentrifugation, assuming a similar SWCNT recovery ratio in the slurry. Upon heating, the dioxane-containing paste forms a dense, uniform solid coating exhibiting higher SWCNT density and more homogeneous dispersion compared with cured SWCNT/PR-1.2 paste prepared without Dio (Fig. S5).

The paste and dough intermediate forms contain low-molecular-weight substances, including unreacted monomers, due to incomplete polymerization. However, residual safety concerns—particularly regarding toxic chemicals like formaldehyde—are effectively mitigated through posttreatments, such as washing or extraction. For instance, 4-amino-3-hydrazino-5-mercapto-1,2,4-triazol (AHMT) spectrophotometry (see Materials and methods) revealed that the as-made SWCNT/PR-1.2 paste contains ∼0.37 wt% residual formaldehyde, which was reduced to 0.14 wt% after water washing, 0.10 wt% after tert-butanol (TBA) washing, and 0.0003 wt% after ethanediamine (EDA) washing, respectively (Fig. S6). Upon full curing, over 98 wt% of the material becomes insoluble, as confirmed by Soxhlet extraction. Notably, dioxane is not universally required for processing; lower-toxicity solvents such as tetrahydrofuran and chloroform serve as viable alternatives for extraction, further alleviating safety concerns.

Dilution with dioxane could improve SWCNT content only to a certain extent, otherwise, excess dioxane would interfere the SWCNT dispersibility, making it not suitable for the pursuit of ultra-high (e.g. >20 wt%) CNT dispersion. A more powerful concentration technique is necessary to further decrease the residual monomer concentration, for example, vacuum-assisted filtration, which has been widely used as a universal method to make the highly densified and super-strong composites (Fig. 2c) (36). Following the same ideology of decreasing m-cresol resin amount, the high-concentration SWCNTs after filtration thicken the residual liquid into a soft and malleable cake that can be peeled off and easily shaped into a film, pallet, or block by rolling or press molding, followed by subsequent polymerization and solidification to produce final solids (Fig. 2c). In the bulk of the solid, SWCNTs are well dispersed and interwoven into a homogeneous microstructure (Fig. 2i). Tomographic images (micro-CT) also verify the well-dispersed SWCNTs without distinguishing any large clusters or aggregates (Fig. S7). The obtained solids with high SWCNT concentration are named HCNT/PR-x, where x represents the molar ratio.

Conductivity and mechanical properties

All aforementioned methods aim to increase SWCNT content while maintaining homogeneous dispersibility in the final products. Centrifugation-concentrated SWCNT/PR paste exhibits an SWCNT content of 0.3–0.7 wt% (Fig. 3a). This yields an electrical conductivity of 0.2–0.5 S m−1 (Fig. 3a). Further polymerization produces flexible dough with comparable SWCNT content (∼0.4 wt%, Fig. 3a), yet its conductivity (10−3–10−2 S m−1, measured by the two-electrode method) is significantly lower than the paste despite similar SWCNT loading. Upon complete solidification, both paste and dough form solids with ∼0.6 wt% SWCNT content. This structural transition enhances conductivity to ∼5 S m−1 as measured by the four-point method (Fig. 3a, solid).

The incorporation of dioxane to enhance SWCNT loading results in a solidified SWCNT/PR-1.2-Dio composite with an increased SWCNT content of ∼5.5 wt% and a conductivity of ∼800 S m−1. Remarkably, this value is nearly two orders of magnitude higher than that of the SWCNT/PR-1.2 composite (Fig. 3a). Despite operating with a halved precursor concentration, the SWCNT content increases by ∼10-fold, attributed to dioxane's inhibitory effect on phenolic polymerization (37). This inhibition promotes the evaporation of monomers and low-molecular-weight species during synthesis, further concentrating SWCNTs. Vacuum-assisted filtration amplifies this effect, enabling composites with exceptionally high SWCNT loadings (20–44 wt%) and conductivities up to 104 S m−1 (Fig. 3a). Notably, higher formaldehyde-to-m-cresol molar ratios dilute the starting solution with formalin, ultimately yielding composites with elevated SWCNT concentrations after polymerization.

The combination of well-dispersed SWCNTs and tunable loading (0.3–44 wt%) endows SWCNT/PR composites with a broad conductivity range. Their electrical conductivities scale exponentially with SWCNT content, exhibiting a power-law exponent of ∼1.85 (Fig. 3b). This aligns closely with the theoretical percolation threshold (∼2) for three-dimensional (3D) conductive networks within an insulating matrix (38–40). Composites with high SWCNT loadings outperform conventional blends of CNTs and polymers by almost two orders of magnitude in conductivity (Fig. 3b, Table S1). While prior efforts achieved high conductivities (104 S m−1) by infiltrating polymers into preformed CNT frameworks (e.g. films or arrays), such predesigned CNT structures depend on energy-intensive chemical vapor deposition (41, 42). In contrast, our approach provides a scalable, cost-effective route to fabricate highly conductive composites directly from CNT powders, bypassing the need for complex preprocessing or specialized equipment.

CNTs serve as exceptional reinforcing and toughening agents in polymeric systems due to their high aspect ratio, mechanical strength, and flexibility (43, 44). In phenolic resins, the homogeneous integration of an SWCNT network effectively mitigates the material's inherent brittleness. This enhanced toughness and processability enable the fabrication of nearly pore-free composites (∼0.25% porosity, Fig. S7) with exceptional densification. Notably, the resulting HCNT/PR-x films display remarkable flexibility (Video S2), contrasting starkly with conventional rigid phenolic resins. The monomer fraction (x) critically influences both the phenolic polymer's molecular characteristics—promoting its transition from thermoplastic novolac (x ≤ 1) to thermoset resole (x > 1)—and CNT loading via reactant mass modulation. While CNT content remains similar at x ≤ 1, the polymer's molecular weight rises significantly with increasing x. Mechanical improvements in this regime arise predominantly from broader molecular-weight distributions, which promote chain entanglement for enhanced strength and toughness. For instance, HCNT/PR-1 demonstrates a tensile strength of ∼90 MPa, Young's modulus of ∼7 GPa, and elongation at break of ∼4.1%, yielding an exceptional energy dissipation (tensile toughness) of 2.6 MJ m−3 (Fig. 3c). Conversely, in crosslinked thermosets (x > 1), molecular-weight differences diminish due to their rigid 3D networks, rendering CNTs loading the dominant factor in mechanical performance (Fig. 3d). As shown in Fig. 3e, elevated CNT content directly enhances ductility and toughness in these systems. Owing to their pristine SWCNT dispersion and unprecedentedly high loadings (Table S2), these composites surpass previously reported CNT/phenolic systems in mechanical performance, validating the efficacy of our fabrication strategy.

Given the structural applications of thermosets, the fracture toughness of HCNT/PR-1.2 was assessed via three-point bending tests. The composite exhibited a rugged fracture surface (Fig. S8), deviating from the smooth fracture surface typical of brittle phenolic resins. Quantitative analysis via single-edge notch bending (SENB) tests revealed a fracture toughness of ∼1.42 MPa m0.5. Microscopic imaging (Fig. 3f–h) unveiled tortuous crack paths, microcrack proliferation, CNT pull-out, and CNT bridging at crack tips—mechanisms widely recognized for dissipating fracture energy in structural materials (45, 46). These observations confirm that CNTs induce crack deflection, branching, and bridging, synergistically enhancing toughness. Combining high conductivity (as demonstrated earlier) with these superior mechanical properties, HCNT/PR composites emerge as promising candidates for multifunctional, high-performance engineering materials.

Low-voltage pressure sensors

Leveraging the outstanding electrical conductivity of SWCNT/PR composites across formulations with varied SWCNT loadings, we developed a proof-of-concept stress sensor that demonstrates superior sensitivity to previously reported materials, even at ultralow operational voltages (0.1 mV). The working mechanism aligns with established electron-tunneling theory for conductive polymer composites, where internanotube gaps govern charge transport dynamics (10, 47). Resistance modulation arises from mechanical disruption of the 3D SWCNT conductive network during deformation (48). While solid-phase SWCNT/PR composites exhibit constrained mechanical compliance, their paste and dough counterparts enable substantial stress-induced structural reorganization, yielding markedly amplified instant conductivity changes. The liquid-like paste formulation outperforms semisolid dough due to its combination of higher baseline conductivity and easier deformability, enabling ultrasensitive response to microscale mechanical perturbations through pronounced resistance fluctuations.

SWCNT/PR-1 paste displays instantaneous electromechanical coupling under both tensile and compressive stresses. When supplied by 0.1 mV (<1% of typical device operating voltages 5 V (47)), the material generates distinct resistance transients corresponding to each deformation phase (stretching/compression/recovery) across tested frequencies (Fig. S9). Intermittent square-wave mechanical loading induces characteristic resistance behavior: initial path disruption causes abrupt resistance spikes (ΔR > 200%, Fig. S10a), followed by creep-induced SWCNT reconfiguration that restores conduction pathways and reduces resistance until subsequent compressive loading reverses the trend. Continuous triangular-wave deformation produces symmetrical periodic response curves (Fig. S10b), with subsecond hysteresis owing to rapid stress relaxation in the viscoelastic matrix. Voltage amplification to 10 mV enhances signal-to-noise ratio by enabling longer electron-tunneling distances (49)—a feature that simultaneously reduces baseline resistance while amplifying relative resistance shifts through optimized conductive network formation (Fig. S11).

The paste exhibits pronounced sensitivity to localized microdeformations. Under a probing stimulus, it generates a discernible response to displacements as tiny as 50 μm when powered by a minimal voltage of 0.1 mV. Elevating the supply voltage to 10 mV further reduces the lower detection limit to 10 μm (Fig. 4a). The relative resistance change demonstrates a robust linear correlation with displacement, yielding a gauge factor (sensitivity metric) of 187% mm−1 (Fig. 4b). In contrast, the highly flexible and deformable dough shows limited sensitivity to minute deformations due to its low conductivity and high viscosity, achieving only a 100-μm detection limit and a gauge factor of 0.78% mm−1 even under a 10-V bias (Fig. 4c and d).

The ultrasensitivity and strong adhesive properties of the SWCNT/PR-1 paste enable its application as a practical pulse monitoring sensor. Arterial pressure signals, which reflect quasi-periodic heartbeat rhythms and contain critical physiological data related to blood pressure and cardiovascular health (50, 51), are captured effectively using this material. When coated onto a food-wrap substrate and powered by a low voltage (10 mV), the sensor clearly records pulse waveforms, resolving key features such as systolic peaks, dicrotic notches, and dicrotic peaks (Fig. 4e and f). This performance positions the paste as a promising candidate for biomedical sensing applications. Unlike conventional solid elastomer-based stress sensors, which rely on substantial contact area changes for deformation detection (52–54), the SWCNT/PR-1 system leverages the unique electron-tunneling mechanism between dispersed CNTs. This allows detection of subtle disturbances even under ultralow voltages (0.1–10 mV), enabling compatibility with nanogenerators or microbatteries for wearable electronics. Long-term stability, however, depends on matrix integrity, particularly in low-polymerization samples where residual oligomers may undergo auto-polymerization over time, altering mechanical properties.

Discussion

Conventional CNT/polymer composites, typically fabricated by blending CNT powders with polymers, often suffer from inadequate CNT dispersion within the matrix. In this work, m-cresol—a phenolic solvent—serves dual roles: it acts as a potent solvent for directly dispersing SWCNTs while simultaneously functioning as a reactive monomer for phenolic resin synthesis. Polymerizing m-cresol and formaldehyde in the presence of uniformly dispersed SWCNT yields conductive SWCNT/phenolic composites. By modulating concentrating methods and polymerization degrees, materials spanning paste-like, stretchable dough, and solid states with SWCNT loadings of 0.3–44 wt% are achievable. Notably, high-pressure filtration enables SWCNT concentrations up to ∼44 wt% in thermally cured solids, achieving exceptional conductivity of 2.7 × 104 S m−1—surpassing conventional CNT/polymer bulk composites by two orders of magnitude—while retaining mechanical robustness. These composites show broad applicability in conductive coatings and sensors. As a proof of concept, the paste functions as a form-free, ultrasensitive sensor capable of detecting microscale disturbances (e.g. wrist pulse signals) at ultralow operating voltages (0.1–10 mV). Furthermore, the semisolid dough represents a novel state for phenolic composites, offering unprecedented processing flexibility for customizable conductive products. The in situ polymerization strategy presented here may inspire designs for other high-performance conductive composites. Future work could explore blending m-cresol with copolymerizable monomers that preserve its charge-transfer interactions with CNTs or incorporating compatible presynthesized polymers into m-cresol dispersions prior to polymerization, thereby diversifying matrix materials for tailored applications.

Materials and methods

The SWCNT powder (TNSR; 95% purity, approximate diameter of 1–2 nm and length of 5–30 μm) is purchased from Chengdu Organic Chemicals Co. Ltd. Other chemicals such as m-cresol, formaldehyde (37%) solution, standard formaldehyde solution (10 mg mL−1), sodium hydroxide (NaOH), hydrochloric acid (HCl), Dio, AHMT, TBA, or EDA, ethylenediamine tetraacetic acid disodium salt (EDTA-2Na), potassium hydroxide (KOH), potassium periodate (KIO4), ethanol, and epoxy resin were obtained from Sigma-Aldrich.

Dispersing SWCNTs in m-cresol

m-cresol has been shown to uniformly disperse various types of CNTs through charge-transfer interaction without the need for surface functionalization or using any additional dispersing agent, based on our earlier findings (23, 24). The SWCNT powders were first dispersed in m-cresol by tip sonication (2 s on and 2 s off) in an ice bath for 1 h. The SWCNT concentration was 1 mg mL−1.

Synthesis of SWCNT/PR-x paste

Formaldehyde was added into the dispersion following a certain molar ratio. For synthesis of thermoset paste (x > 1), taking SWCNT/PR-1.2 as an example, 10 mg SWCNTs were dispersed in 10 mL m-cresol firstly. Then, 8.62 mL formaldehyde was added. After the thorough mixing, the solution was centrifuged under 10,000 rpm for 10 min. After removing the supernatant, the concentrated paste was mixed with 50 mg NaOH and placed in an 80 °C oil bath under gentle stirring. The paste products can be obtained in ∼30 min. Other SWCNT/PR-x paste can be synthesized using the same as above except for using different formaldehyde solution. When synthesizing thermoplastic paste (x ≤ 1), instead of NaOH, 50 μL HCl (1 M) was used as catalyst, and due to the high polymerization speed under acid catalyst, the reaction was conducted at 60 °C for 30 min. All samples were dried in vacuum at room temperature overnight. The products were weighted and the SWCNT concentration can be calculated.

Synthesis of SWCNT/PR dough

The synthesis process is similar to the paste described above without Dio dilution. Because a higher polymerization degree is needed to reach the dough state, the reaction was conducted in a 90 °C oil bath with gentle stirring for 1 h. After cooling down, the soft and nonsticky dough can be obtained after drying in a vacuum for 24 h. The SWCNT content was calculated based on the mass.

Processing of the dough includes cold-rolling, kneading, folding, and mold-casting. The dough was sandwiched between two Teflon sheets during rolling. For mold-casting, the SWCNT/PR-1.2 dough was pressed into a polydimethylsiloxane mold, and then was taken out for curing. All reshaped thermoset samples (SWCNT/PR-x, x > 1) can be fully cured at low temperature for 1 week. Low temperature was used to avoid polymer creeping and help to maintain the geometry.

Synthesis of SWCNT/PR solid

To solidify the paste samples, the dried paste samples were coated onto a glass slide (2.5 × 2.5 cm2) and heated at 100–120 °C for thermoplastic samples (x ≤ 1) and 180–200 °C for thermoset samples (x > 1). Note that the thermoset samples were totally cured after heating for 2 h, and the thermoplastic samples further polymerized to generate a solid Novolac coating. The weights were recorded during every step, and the SWCNT contents were calculated.

Casting paste into solid

The thermoset SWCNT/PR-1.2 paste was filled into the mold and cured at 80 °C for 1 week. The solid product can also be made by solidifying SWCNT/PR dough. For example, SWCNT/PR-1.2 dough was kneaded into a pyramid structure and fully cured at 180–200 °C for at least 6 h.

Synthesis of SWCNT/PR-1.2-Dio paste

To increase SWCNT content of the paste, Dio is used to dilute the starting solution. The synthesis process is similar to the abovementioned synthesis. After mixing SWCNT/m-cresol/formaldehyde/catalyst solution in a certain ratio, a certain amount of Dio ((m-cresol + F) = 1:1 by volume) was added to dilute the monomers, followed by the concentrating and in situ polymerization under the same condition as the above thermoset paste. The paste can be cast on a microscope glass and then dried at elevated temperature 180–200 °C to get the solidified coating. The mass of the products during every step was recorded for the estimation of SWCNT contents.

Synthesis of HCNT/PR-x products with high SWCNT loading

The synthesis process is similar to above. After thoroughly mixing CNT/m-cresol/formaldehyde/catalyst (HCl or NaOH) solution in a controlled molar ratio of formaldehyde and m-cresol, the mixture was filtered on anodic alumina oxide AAO membrane (pore size 0.45 μm) with assisted vacuum until no liquid flowing down to get a semisolid cake, which was then taken out for reshaping and polymerization. The mass of the products during every step was recorded to calculate the CNT content.

For the film product, the cake was sandwiched in-between two Teflon films and hot-pressed slightly to avoid corrugation with a set temperate of 180–200 °C for thermoset sample (x > 1) and 100–120 °C for thermoplastic sample (x ≤ 1). The in situ polymerization took ∼6 h.

For HCNT/PR products in other shapes, the filtered cake was reshaped by kneading and cold-rolling and then filled into a mold to be reshaped into pallet or block, followed by the polymerization at 100–120 °C for thermoplastic sample (x ≤ 1) and thermal curing at 180–200 °C for the thermoset sample (x > 1).

Conductivity measurement of the conductive paste

The conductivity of the paste was calculated based on Ohm's law. The paste was filled into an epoxy mold and relaxed for 24 h. The paste size was 6 mm × 3 mm × 5.4 mm. The resistance of the paste was calculated based on the current–voltage (I–V) curves. The conductivity was calculated by σ=RL/S, where σ is the conductivity, L is the gap between two electrodes, R is the resistance obtained from the I–V curve, S is the effective cross-sectional area of the paste.

Paste washing by water, TBA, and EDA

The residual monomers (both formaldehyde and m-cresol) can be washed away by using excess water, TBA, or EDA solution in a 40 °C water bath. The solvent was changed regularly until there were no oily substances extracted out. The paste was then re-dried at 40 °C in a vacuum.

AHMT spectrophotometry for residual formaldehyde measurement

Basically, 5 mL formaldehyde-containing solution was mixed with 2 mL EDTA-2Na/KOH (5 M) and 2 mL AHMT (5 g L−1) at room temperature (55). After thorough mixing, the mixture rested for 20 min, followed by adding 0.5 mL KIO4 solution (15 g L−1). After 5 min, the UV-vis absorption of the solution was measured on a UV-vis analyzer. Standard formaldehyde solutions with concentrations of 0.2, 0.4, 0.8, 2, and 4 μg mL−1 were measured first to quantificationally determine the linear fitting of absorption (550 nm) and concentration. The original paste and washed pastes (∼50 mg) were dissolved in 1 mL ethanol, and all the residual formaldehyde is supposed to dissolve in solvent. After adding 4 mL water, the solution was centrifuged (14,000 rpm × 10 min) to precipitate the CNTs. One milliliter of supernatant was then diluted to 10 mL for original paste or to 5 mL for washed pastes before AHMT measurement.

Mechanical tensile test of HCNT/PR products

The tensile test was conducted with HCNT/PR films (with thickness of 40–80 μm). Films were cut into stripes (15 mm × 2 mm) and both sides were glued on a small A4 print paper with epoxy resin. The epoxy-glued sides ensure the tight clamping on the tensile mold. After fully curing epoxy resin at 80 °C, the tensile performance was tested on dynamic mechanical analyzer DMA equipment (2980 DMA, TA Instruments) with a tensile rate of 0.01 mm s−1. At least three samples were tested for each material. The results were plotted in origin, and the tensile toughness was calculated based on the area underneath the stress–strain curves.

The mechanical three-point bending test was conducted with HCNT/PR-1.2 pallet, which were firstly machine-cut into small bars (∼2 mm × 1 mm × 15 mm), which were then tested based on the three-point bending method with a bottom supporting span of 10.5 mm and a loading rate of 1 μm s−1.

SWCNT/PR paste as a shapeless sensor to monitor the movement of machine parts

SWCNT/PR-1 paste was used as an example. The compressive module of the DMA instrument is used to simulate the moving parts. Two copper plates were attached onto both surfaces of the compressive module, serving as electrodes. SWCNT/PR-1 paste (∼0.5 g) was piled up in the center of the bottom plate, and then the top plate was lowered to contact with the paste. The gap between the plates was set as 2 mm. For the cycled tensile movement, the upper plate moves at a tensile strain of 100% in the triangle wave mode and square-wave mode, respectively. The frequency of the cycled tensile movement was set at 0.1 Hz. The resistance change of the paste was recorded at different voltages (0.1 or 10 mV).

For a cycled compressive test with a compressive strain of 50%, the upper plate moved down and up in a triangle wave mode with different frequencies. The resistance change of the paste was monitored at a voltage of 0.1 mV.

Relative resistance changes upon tiny perturbation

A polylactic acid (PLA) mold with an open square cavity was made by 3D printing. SWCNT/PR-1 paste was filled in the mold. A thin wood stick was installed on the moveable plate of DMA, ensuring controlled downward displacements to generate perturbations to the paste. The paste was disturbed by a single perturbation every time. For every displacement (0.01, 0.02, 0.05, 0.1, 0.2, 0.5, 0.8, and 1 mm), the wood moved stepwise at a certain displacement every time and was repeated three times. Then, the wood was reset to the initial place, and the paste was allowed to relax for 30 min before testing another perturbation. The paste resistance was monitored and recorded at the voltages of 0.1 and 10 mV, respectively. The sensitivity was calculated by fitting the maximum resistance change with the displacement. Here, the R0 means the instant original resistance just upon the application of perturbation.

Demonstration of SWCNT/PR-1 paste as a wrist pulse sensor

The wrist was firstly covered by a commercial food wrap. SWCNT/PR-1 paste was used as the sensing material, which was spread on the site where wrist pulse exists. Two copper wires were buried inside the paste and fixed by tap. The resistance of the paste was recorded at the voltage of 10 mV. Note that the sensing performance is independent of the paste mass or shape.

Characterization

Optical microscopy observations were made on a Nikon TE2000-U inverted microscope, and images were taken with a monochrome interline CCD camera (Photometrics, CoolSNAP HQ2). SEM images were taken by a scanning electron microscope (Hitachi S-4800). Raman spectroscopy measurement (WITec Alpha 300 with a 532-nm laser). The conductivity of the coating, solid blocks, and HCNT/PR products was measured by using an in-line four-point probe equipped with a Keithley 2400 source meter. Viscosity and rheological properties were measured by using an Anton Paar MCR 502 rheometer with a cone-on-plate configuration (25-mm diameter and 5° gap angle). Resistance of the CNT/PR pastes in the mold was measured by using cyclic voltammetry scans with a Keithley 2400 source meter. Conductivity can be calculated based on the Ohm's law. Mechanical performances were performed with a dynamic mechanical analyzer (2980 DMA, TA Instruments). The insoluble fraction is measured by Soxhlet extraction. The X-ray 3D construction images were analyzed with ZEISS Xradia 520 Versa X-ray microscope and then reconstructed on Dragonfly-Pro software to obtain the 3D microstructure and analyze porosity.
