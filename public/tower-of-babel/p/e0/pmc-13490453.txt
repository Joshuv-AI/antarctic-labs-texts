Proactive detection of voice phishing networks using call log analysis and machine learning
Authors: Kyungjong Kim, Lanu Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Voice phishing represents a rapidly evolving form of cyber-enabled financial fraud that exploits telecommunications networks to deceive victims. Traditional reactive policing approaches face significant limitations in preventing such crimes in real time. This study introduces a data-driven framework for the proactive detection of phone numbers linked to voice phishing operations using large-scale call log data from South Korea. Behavioral features derived from call metadata were utilized to capture distinctive communication patterns that differentiate fraudulent users from legitimate ones. A stepwise logistic regression model was initially employed to identify key predictors of fraudulent behavior, followed by advanced machine learning models–including random forest, gradient boosting, and Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP)—for classification. The results reveal that voice phishing numbers display unique behavioral characteristics, such as concentrated weekday activity, shorter call durations, higher outgoing call ratios, and a preference for Mobile Virtual Network Operator (MVNO) usage. The proposed models achieved high performance, exceeding 95% accuracy and 97% recall, demonstrating their robustness in detecting suspicious numbers. These findings underscore the potential of integrating artificial intelligence and behavioral analytics into proactive fraud detection systems and contribute to the development of early-warning mechanisms for preventing telecommunication-based financial crimes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-54515-y.

Introduction

Voice phishing, a type of fraud through telephone communication, has become prevalent and brought substantial financial losses and psychological distress on innumerable victims. This form of intelligent crime has constantly evolved, transcending the boundaries of traditional criminal activities by exploiting advanced telecommunications technologies. Rather than being individual or spontaneous, voice phishing is a highly organized crime that follows a clearly defined process consisting of preparation, execution, and post-crime handling1,2. In order to evade law enforcement, perpetrators continuously alter their methods and become increasingly sophisticated.

Researchers have responded to the urgent need to confront the crime. Early research empirically analyzes the organizational structure and operational methods of voice phishing, revealing a structured crime procedure with detailed scripting and division of roles1. Furthermore, cross-national case studies in regions such as Vietnam, Cambodia, and Europe demonstrate that voice phishing is a form of transnational organized crime often involving social media and online financial platforms3,4. Recently, researchers also focus on the sophisticated techniques by which voice phishing leverages artificial intelligence (AI), including call interception via malicious apps, script adaptation based on emotional analysis, and deepfake attacks using deep learning-based voice synthesis technologies5–8.

However, these prior studies are limited in addressing voice phishing because many technological detection and blocking systems focus on the execution stage, not the preparation, which is already after the potential victims are widely expanded9. If the prevention attempt on the execution stage occurs after when the potential victims are decreased, the effect of the prevention would be more than doubled. Although researchers have studied preventing spam or robocalls and developed the detection methods10, they are not fully adequate for effectively classifying the complex and dynamic calling behaviors specific to the preparation stage of voice phishing11,12.

To overcome these limitations, we emphasize the importance of strategies that block call attempts when perpetrators are trying to approach potential victims, before the crime moves to the execution stage. By leveraging call log data from voice phishing call centers, we quantitatively analyze the abnormal call patterns and behavioral characteristics unique to voice phishing phone numbers–such as call distribution by day and time, call duration, or telecom provider preferences. Based on these insights, we develop a customized detection model identifying and blocking fraudulent numbers at an early stage. This approach complements existing technical responses by addressing current blind spots, and substantially contributes to the prevention of voice phishing by blocking crime attempts before they occur. By doing so, we present a novel, data-driven strategy that targets the most vulnerable early stage of voice phishing crimes.

In addition, this study makes the following key contributions. First, we propose a data-driven framework for early-stage detection of voice phishing phone numbers using call log data, focusing on the approach stage before the execution of the crime. Second, we design domain-specific features derived from exploratory data analysis (EDA) of real-world call log data obtained from law enforcement agencies, capturing behavioral characteristics such as temporal call distribution, short call duration patterns, outgoing call dominance, and telecom provider preferences. Third, we conduct empirical validation under imbalanced data conditions using repeated sampling and bootstrap-based statistical analysis to ensure robustness. Finally, by quantifying the influence of each variable through interpretable models, this study provides practical insights that can support organizational responses and policy-level interventions by telecom operators and government agencies.

Our findings demonstrate that specific call patterns and telecom provider information serve as crucial indicators for effectively classifying voice phishing phone numbers. Through the exploratory analysis, we confirm that voice phishing calls predominantly occur during weekday daytime hours, continue only for short durations, have a high outgoing call ratio, involve a large number of unique call recipients—revealing abnormal behavioral patterns compared to normal users. In addition, a strong preference for MVNO (Mobile Virtual Network Operator) lines is an important classification feature. The logistic regression analysis using these characteristics as variables confirms its statistical significance under .05 alpha level in classifying voice phishing numbers. Lastly, machine learning-based classification models—Random Forest, Gradient Boosting, and MLP—show high accuracy and recall, effectively identifying voice phishing numbers.

Additional details on the organizational structure of voice phishing operations, exploratory data analyses, model diagnostics, and the sampling procedure are provided in the Supplementary Information (Appendices A–I). The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. “Literature review” reviews prior studies on voice phishing and fraud detection techniques. “Background” provides background information on the organizational structure and operational stages of voice phishing crimes. “Data and method” describes the dataset and methodology, including feature engineering and model design. “Results” presents the experimental results and evaluation. Finally, “Discussion and conclusion” discusses the implications and limitations of the study and concludes the paper.

Literature review

Research on phishing crimes

Researchers have constantly examined the organizational structure and operational strategies of voice phishing. Choi et al. 1 analyze 182 voice phishing investigation records using a crime script framework to derive a typical offense scenario. According to their findings, unlike ordinary crimes, voice phishing is characterized by a structured and organized system. It follows a three-stage process—preparation → execution → post-crime handling—with each stage systematically carried out based on clear divisions of responsibility. During the preparation stage, the criminals in the main organization detail phone call scripts and recruit call center personnel. Then, during the execution stage, victims are lured through the crafted phone call scripts impersonating financial institutions or law enforcement agencies. Finally, in the post-crime stage, the proceeds of the crime are transferred to the organization through complex account transactions or cash withdrawals to hide from police investigation1. Similarly, phishing crime groups in Europe also operate with systematic collaboration between domestic and international networks2. While these studies effectively illustrate the basic structure of voice phishing, they fall short of devising the method to reduce victims of voice phishing crimes.

Voice phishing has evolved beyond an individual criminal act and become a transnational organized crime. According to Luong and Ngo 3, international fraud rings operating primarily in Vietnam and Cambodia are linked to Chinese criminal networks. These groups exploit social media and online financial platforms to lure victims and use international money laundering systems to conceal illicit procedures. Such findings indicate that voice phishing is not merely a phone-based fraud but rather a global organized crime—highlighting the need for international cooperation and technology-driven detection strategies. Similar patterns have been observed in Europe and the UK. For instance, Button et al. 13 analyze telephone-based financial scams targeting individuals over the age of 75 in the UK and emphasize that emotional vulnerability and lack of digital literacy are key factors that increase victimization risk. This suggests that international and technological countermeasures should be complemented with tailored protection strategies for socially vulnerable populations, thereby bridging global responses with individual-level protection. Nonetheless, even if voice phishing operates as a cross-border organized crime, deceiving the victim ultimately requires cooperation with domestic networks. From the perspective of law enforcement agencies that operate within national boundaries, a practical response must focus on targeting domestic collaborators and the telecommunications infrastructure they utilize. Accordingly, this study precisely analyzes the communication patterns and behavioral traits used by voice phishing organizations, with the goal of identifying and blocking them during the early stage of their operations within domestic boundaries.

At the same time, recent research has explored how AI technologies have been used to refine voice phishing techniques. Kim et al. 5 analyze 1017 Android-based malicious voice phishing apps and reveal that such apps use various techniques to deceive victims, including call interception, spoofed caller ID, and the insertion of pre-recorded audio. They propose a detection framework called HearMeOut, which suggests an automated approach for identifying voice phishing activity. Nyassi et al. 6 emphasize that voice phishing perpetrators analyze a victim’s emotional state in real time using AI and dynamically adapt their scripts accordingly. Yao et al. 7 introduce a backdoor attack called EmoAttack, which leverages emotional voice conversion technology, demonstrating that emotionally manipulated speech can exploit vulnerabilities in AI models. This highlights the increasing potential for voice phishing to manipulate human emotions in more sophisticated ways. Finally, Alali et al. 8 empirically demonstrate that deep learning-based voice synthesis can be weaponized for voice phishing attacks. They introduce the Partial Fake Speech Attack, which blends real and AI-synthesized speech, and find that it can deceive not only humans but also existing voice authentication systems. These findings suggest that perpetrators could impersonate specific individuals using AI-generated voices, enabling highly credible and convincing scams. Taken together, this stream of research demonstrates how perpetrators weaponize AI technologies to enhance the offensive capabilities of voice phishing.

Technological and social responses to voice phishing have also been actively explored. Lee et al. 9 propose a method to detect abnormal behavior in malicious apps and block voice phishing-related calls in real time. Nyassi et al. 6 present a model that distinguishes between normal and fraudulent calls by analyzing the emotional patterns embedded in speech. Such AI detection systems, based on diverse data including voice, call logs, and app usage behaviors, have gained attention for their real-time blocking capabilities and are increasingly being applied in the financial and cybersecurity sectors. However, these systems primarily focus on the execution stage, after criminals have already deceived the victim, limiting their ability to fundamentally prevent voice phishing. In contrast, this study focuses on the importance of strategies that block call attempts during the early stage—prior to the execution of the crime. Detecting and blocking the phone numbers used for deceiving victims beforehand presents an effective preventative approach. When combined with existing technical responses, such a strategy can create a multi-layered defense system that makes a meaningful contribution to reducing voice phishing crimes.

Meanwhile, prior research also emphasizes that technical solutions alone cannot sufficiently mitigate the risk of victimization. Broader societal measures, such as public awareness, education, and cross-sector collaboration, are essential complements to technical defenses. Jensen et al. 14 empirically analyze the effectiveness of a nationwide anti-fraud campaign in the Netherlands, showing that in addition to technical detection systems, public awareness campaigns and education can be effective in preventing voice phishing victimization. This supports the view that the strategy to voice phishing should not rely solely on technology but also incorporate broader societal approaches. Similarly, Button et al. 4 propose a systemic response framework for preventing fraud targeting older adults, emphasizing the need for cross-sector collaboration among social welfare, healthcare, judiciary, and financial institutions. These studies reaffirm that combating organized fraud requires an integrated approach that combines technological tools with broader societal systems.

These perspectives are aligned with the analytical direction of this study. Voice phishing is not simply a technological or individual issue, but a complex crime embedded in social and organizational structures. Accordingly, effective responses should combine the data-driven quantitative analysis with systematic, societal-level interventions—an approach consistently emphasized in existing literature.

Call pattern and network-based detection techniques

Numerous studies have sought to identify criminal phone numbers by analyzing the calling behavior of spam or robocalls (automated mass-calling systems). For example, Swarnkar and Hubballi 11 develop a system called SpamDetector, which models the relationship between callers and receivers as a graph and detects anomalies within this graph to identify spam calls. In their study, call logs are represented using nodes (phone numbers) and edges (call connections), with behavioral attributes such as average call duration, successful connection ratio, and call direction (outgoing/incoming) used as weighted features for each node. The algorithm then compares behavioral similarities among neighboring nodes and flags phone numbers exhibiting outlier patterns as potential spam callers. The results show that the system identifies spam callers with very high accuracy11. Similarly, Azad et al. 12 propose a framework called ROBO-SPOT, which evaluates the trustworthiness of outgoing numbers through large-scale call social network analysis. This system calculates a trust score for each caller based on their call history and inputs these scores into a machine learning classifier to distinguish between legitimate callers and potentially fraudulent robocallers. In an evaluation using over 1 billion call records collected over a 10-day period, the proposed model achieves a detection rate of 97% with a false positive rate of less than 0.01%. These results demonstrate the effectiveness of analyzing caller reputation to identify fraudulent calls before they cause harm to users in the telecommunications network12.

While these prior studies have proven effective in detecting spam callers by identifying anomalous patterns and node relationships within telecommunications networks, their approaches are largely optimized for robocallers or spam callers that exhibit simple, automated calling patterns. For example, robocalls are typically characterized by high-volume, unidirectional call activity over short time periods. In contrast, voice phishing involves complex and dynamic calling behaviors: once a victim is lured, calls often continue for extended periods and may include two-way communication. That is, although voice phishing may resemble robocalls during the initial baiting stage with similar outgoing patterns, the interaction that follows successful contact can closely resemble that of normal or social calls. This makes it difficult to accurately classify phishing calls using conventional spam detection algorithms alone.

Therefore, while it is valuable to reference existing network-based anomaly detection techniques, there is a need to develop a tailored detection model that captures the unique characteristics of voice phishing—such as weekday and daytime call concentration, and telecom provider preferences. This study builds upon that distinction by focusing on voice phishing calls during the early stage, thereby contributing to previous research efforts.

Intelligent detection using machine learning and deep learning

In contrast to the offensive use of AI described in the previous section, another body of research focuses on employing AI and deep learning as defensive strategies for detecting and preventing voice phishing attacks. With recent advancements in artificial intelligence—particularly deep learning—there has been a growing interest in applying these techniques to the detection of voice phishing calls. According to a trend analysis by Nguyet Quang Do et al. 15, vishing (voice-based phishing) has a higher success rate compared to email or web-based phishing because users find it more difficult to make real-time judgments and responses during phone conversations. Thus, deep learning has emerged as a promising approach for next-generation phishing detection, as it automatically learns features from large, complex datasets15.

A representative example is the work of Xing et al. 16, who develop a deep learning-based fraud call classifier using large-scale Call Detail Records (CDR) collected from a telecommunications provider over a 1-year period. Their model constructs a 176-dimensional feature vector based on phone number metadata (e.g., calling patterns) and calling behavior (e.g., frequency, duration, geographic distribution), and feeds this into various deep neural networks such as Convolutional Neural Networks (CNN), LSTM, and Stacked Denoising Autoencoders (SDAE) to classify whether a call is fraudulent. Notably, their model does not rely on manual feature engineering but instead learns patterns automatically, enabling adaptive detection that could respond sensitively even as fraud techniques evolved.

However, the high performance of Xing et al.’s model is largely supported by their access to comprehensive datasets 16. With full CDRs provided by the telecom provider, they fully capture network structures between callers and receivers, repeated calling patterns, and behavioral similarities across calls. In contrast, this study is constrained by the lack of such full-scope data and relies solely on analyzing the call logs of voice phishing call center phone numbers because South Korea’s government and private sectors highly concern about data privacy. With the limited data availability, we seek for the refined data-driven work to detect voice phishing calls in the context of South Korea.

Recent studies have further extended this line of research by addressing the challenges of fraud detection in imbalanced, high-dimensional, and sequential datasets using advanced machine learning techniques. For example, Shukla et al. 17 propose a hybrid deep transformer-based model combined with hyperparameter optimization techniques for fraudulent account detection in social media. Their approach integrates transformer-based representation learning with additional optimization strategies, enabling improved detection performance in complex fraud scenarios. Similarly, Hu et al. 18 propose a cost-sensitive graph neural network (GNN) framework that effectively handles class imbalance in mobile social network fraud detection. Their approach leverages relational structures between users and demonstrates improved performance in identifying fraudulent accounts under highly imbalanced conditions. These advancements highlight the growing importance of advanced learning models in capturing complex and evolving fraudulent behaviors.

In addition, recent studies have explored phishing detection using approaches such as PhiUSIIL19 and StealthPhisher20, which focus on identifying phishing attacks based on URL characteristics, webpage structures, and other content-level features. These methods have shown strong performance in detecting web-based phishing attacks. However, they primarily rely on access to webpage or message content. In contrast, this study focuses on behavioral patterns derived from call log metadata, offering a complementary detection perspective based on communication behavior rather than content analysis.

Despite these advances, existing studies primarily rely on either fully accessible large-scale datasets (e.g., complete CDRs) or focus on complex deep learning architectures and content-based phishing detection approaches. Moreover, much of the prior research focuses on detecting fraudulent behavior at or after the execution stage, or on spam and robocall detection, which does not fully capture the dynamic and interactive nature of voice phishing calls. There remains a lack of research specifically addressing early-stage detection of voice phishing based on call log behavioral patterns under real-world data constraints. Therefore, we fill this gap by proposing a data-driven detection framework that leverages call metadata to identify voice phishing numbers at the early approach stage, while ensuring interpretability and practical applicability in real-world telecommunications and law enforcement settings.

Background

Organizational structure of voice phishing crime in South Korea

Voice phishing crimes in South Korea are conducted in a highly organized and intelligent manner. Fig. 1 summarizes this complicated context. In order to succeed and evade law enforcement surveillance, criminal organizations actively leverage advanced technologies. For instance, although call centers responsible for deceiving victims are often located overseas—in countries such as China or Taiwan—they exploit illegal relay devices or remote call systems to make it appear as if they are calling from a local mobile phone number.Fig. 1Organizational structure of voice phishing crime.

Organizational structure of voice phishing crime.

To maximize their success rate, voice phishing call centers collaborate with various private criminal groups. Their methods of approach are tailored to the characteristics of each victim to increase the likelihood of deception. For example, they target financially struggling individuals with high-interest debts by offering fraudulent refinancing schemes. In the case of socially inexperienced university students, they often impersonate law enforcement or investigative agencies to exploit fear and confusion. They further enhance the illusion of local presence by manipulating caller ID to display a domestic mobile number. Additionally, they collaborate with organizations that develop and distribute malicious apps to gain full control over the victim’s smartphone. To evade financial tracking by authorities, they also work with money laundering networks that handle the illicit proceeds.

Given the decentralized and highly coordinated nature of these operations—where different roles are distributed among multiple, semi-independent units—it is essential to understand the full structure of voice phishing organizations to effectively prevent crimes. Detailed descriptions of each organizational role and its operations are provided in Appendix A.

While Choi et al.1 conceptualize voice phishing as a three-stage process (preparation → execution → post-crime), this framework does not fully capture the division of labor observed in recent cases. In particular, the preparation stage often involves two distinct sets of actors: outsourced criminal groups that specialize in baiting potential victims, and call center operators who carry out the actual approach. Moreover, what Choi broadly categorized as “execution” and “post-crime handling” encompass multiple sub-processes that differ in both actors and techniques. In this study, the “execution” stage is subdivided into three distinct components: the “approach stage,” in which call center operators initiate direct phone contact with victims; the “deception stage,” in which perpetrators deploy malicious apps or psychological manipulation to mislead victims; and the “fraudulent transfer stage,” in which the stolen funds are acquired and laundered. Limitations of traditional police investigations

Although the structure and methods of voice phishing crimes are well understood, practical challenges remain in apprehending offenders or effectively blocking these crimes. These difficulties are closely tied to the structural limitations of traditional policing methods.

First, voice phishing is a highly organized crime composed of multiple complex stages, and perpetrators deploy various countermeasures at each stage to evade law enforcement tracking. Thus, disrupting only one stage is rarely sufficient to prevent the entire crime.

Second, most voice phishing operations are based in overseas call centers, and the stolen funds are often transferred abroad. This limits the jurisdiction of domestic law enforcement, and international investigative cooperation is constrained by time-consuming procedures and legal barriers. Once the crime crosses borders, the ability of local police to intervene declines sharply.

Third, the police individually investigate each incident while the voice phishing crime is systematically coordinated, which slows down the investigation and makes it inefficient. In particular, caller ID spoofing using technologies such as private VoIP converters makes physical tracing extremely difficult. Under such conditions, it is often impossible for conventional investigation systems to identify the origin of calls or block them swiftly.

Fourth, when victims voluntarily initiate fund transfers, investigations are sometimes conducted passively due to the principle of “victim responsibility.” Because law enforcement cannot intervene until the victim becomes aware of the scam, perpetrators delay this realization for as long as possible. This tactic is especially effective during the deception stage, which often involves malicious apps—further complicating the timing of police intervention.

In this way, voice phishing crimes skillfully exploit the blind spots of traditional investigative frameworks at each stage, making it extremely difficult to interrupt the process at any single point. Thus, decreasing the initial attempts by blocking voice phishing calls is the most fundamental and effective response. If voice phishing calls are prevented at the early stage, the subsequent steps of the crime will simply not happen. This is supported by the data shown in Fig. 3, illustrating a correlation between the number of monthly voice phishing call attempts and the amount of financial damage incurred during the same period in 2021. Data and method

Data

Our data set was the 1,442,122 call records from 2692 phone numbers used by voice phishing organizations in South Korea between January and December 2021. These data were obtained from the Voice Phishing Crime Data Analysis Team of the Korean National Police. To comply with Article 28-2 of the Personal Information Protection Act of Korea, all phone numbers were pseudonymized for the purposes of scientific research. Each call log column included the outgoing number (caller), incoming number (recipient), the date and time, and other call-related metadata. The example of the dataset is included in Table C1, Appendix C.

To distinguish abnormal patterns of voice phishing numbers from normal users, an additional dataset of 438,442 call records from 108 non-criminal users’ phone numbers was separately collected. Due to the imbalance of sample size between criminals’ (n = 2692) and non-criminals’ phone numbers (n = 108), we repeatedly random-sampled to balance the data for fair comparison.

This imbalance reflects real-world data collection constraints. While law enforcement agencies accumulate a large number of confirmed voice phishing numbers, access to normal users’ call records is strictly limited due to privacy regulations. As a result, the number of non-criminal samples is relatively small. To mitigate the potential bias arising from this limitation, we conducted additional sensitivity analyses under varying class ratios (1:1, 10:1, and 100:1), as described in “Predicting voice phishing numbers using machine learning models”. Further details of the sampling procedure are provided in “Method”.

Variables

We operationalized seven variables from call records: number of calls per week, day of the week score, daytime call ratio, call duration ratio, outgoing call ratio, number of unique contacts per week, and carrier information. These variables were derived on a per-phone-number basis using the call log data; meaning that all variables used in the subsequent analyses were calculated at the level of individual phone numbers.

Importantly, these variables were not arbitrarily selected but were derived through exploratory data analysis (EDA) of voice phishing call logs, as presented in Appendix D. The exploratory analysis revealed distinctive behavioral patterns of voice phishing phone numbers, such as concentration of calls during weekdays and daytime, high frequency of short-duration calls, and extensive outreach to a large number of unique recipients. Based on these observed patterns, we constructed domain-specific features that capture the operational characteristics of voice phishing call centers.

Furthermore, the selected variables are designed to ensure interpretability and practical applicability. Unlike complex feature representations used in deep learning models, these variables can be readily understood by investigators and policy makers. This interpretability is particularly important in real-world law enforcement settings, where model outputs must be explainable and actionable. In addition, several variables—such as carrier type, call frequency, and temporal calling patterns—can be directly linked to regulatory or operational interventions by telecom providers, enabling the potential integration of the proposed model into policy-driven detection and prevention frameworks.

Here, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$N_{\textrm{unique}}$$\end{document} represents the number of distinct recipients a specific phone number had contacted during the observed period, and D refersr to the number of days in that period. Carrier (telecom provider)

There are four carriers in South Korea: SKT, KT, LGU+, and MVNO. The voice phishing phone numbers are not evenly distributed across four carriers (Fig. D6 in Appendix D), so we used this variable to distinguish the voice phishing phone numbers. Method

Logistic regression

With the variables defined above, we ran the logistic regression model to understand factors that best described the characteristics of voice phishing phone numbers. While our final models included all variables as the formula below, we added them one-by-one to understand how the variables interacted in the same model.12\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \begin{aligned} \ln \left( \frac{P(Y=1)}{1-P(Y=1)}\right)&= \beta _{0} + \beta _{1} \cdot \mathrm {Number\ of\ Calls\ per\ Week}^{(\textrm{standardized})} \\&\quad + \beta _{2} \cdot \mathrm {Day\_score}^{(\textrm{standardized})} \\&\quad + \beta _{3} \cdot \mathrm {Daytime\ Call\ Ratio}^{(\textrm{standardized})} \\&\quad + \beta _{4} \cdot \mathrm {Call\ Duration\ Ratio}^{(\textrm{standardized})} \\&\quad + \beta _{5} \cdot \mathrm {Outgoing\ Call\ Ratio}^{(\textrm{standardized})} \\&\quad + \beta _{6} \cdot \mathrm {Number\ of\ Unique\ Contacts\ per\ Week}^{(\textrm{standardized})} \\&\quad + \beta _{7} \cdot \textrm{Carrier}_{\textrm{KT}} + \beta _{8} \cdot \textrm{Carrier}_{\textrm{LGU}^+} + \beta _{9} \cdot \textrm{Carrier}_{\textrm{MVNO}} + \varepsilon \end{aligned} \end{aligned}$$\end{document}

The equation above summarizes our model. Here, P(Y=1) represented the probability that a given phone number is associated with voice phishing. The left-hand side of the equation \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\ln\left( \frac{P(Y=1)}{1-P(Y=1)}\right)$$\end{document} represents the log-odds of this probability. \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\beta _{0}$$\end{document} is the intercept, while \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\beta _{1}, \beta _{2}, \ldots , \beta _{9}$$\end{document} denote the coefficients of independent variables. Among these, the Carrier variables were dummy variables representing categorical telecom providers. SKT was chosen as the reference group. The error term \varepsilon accounts for the effect of unobserved factors not included in the model. In logistic regression, this term captures the probabilistic nature of the model and compensates for deviations from the predicted values.

All continuous variables in the model were standardized using the Z-score method, with the exception of dummy variables such as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textrm{Carrier}_{\textrm{KT}}$$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textrm{Carrier}_{\textrm{LGU}^+}$$\end{document}, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textrm{Carrier}_{\textrm{MVNO}}$$\end{document}. Each variable was transformed by subtracting the mean and dividing by the standard deviation, to eliminate unit-based differences and ensure comparability across variables. Since most variables followed an approximately normal distribution, the Z-score method was deemed appropriate for standardization.

Furthermore, to address the class imbalance between voice phishing and normal phone numbers, we performed logistic regression with bootstrap resampling 1000 times. Bootstrap is a statistical resampling technique used to estimate the distribution of a statistic by repeatedly sampling with replacement from the original data. We used the bootstrap method to reduce bias arising from class imbalance and to obtain reliable standard errors for the coefficients. For bootstrapping, out of a total of 2692 voice phishing phone numbers, 972 samples (108 × 9) were randomly selected without replacement. Then, 108 normal phone numbers were combined with the sampled 972 voice phishing numbers to create a sampled dataset of 1080 numbers with a 9:1 ratio. We sampled the voice phishing numbers nine times more than normal numbers because the carrier distribution among voice phishing numbers is heavily skewed toward MVNO. There was a risk that certain carriers might not have been included at all in some samples. This process was repeated 1000 times and we calculated the standard error with a thousand coefficients. Coefficients of each variable were calculated based on the average values of the bootstrapped sample. Also, p-values were computed for each variable to determine statistical significance with .05 alpha level (two-tailed test).

In addition, to examine potential interaction effects between key variables, we estimated an extended logistic regression model including interaction terms between the daytime call ratio and carrier type, as well as between the outgoing call ratio and the proportion of short-duration calls. These interaction terms were included to assess whether the effects of temporal calling behavior differ across telecom providers and whether outgoing behavior interacts with short-duration call patterns. The extended model was compared with the main-effects model using AIC and likelihood-based model comparison. The bootstrap distributions of the estimated coefficients are shown in fig. E1,Appendix E.

Application of classifier-based machine learning techniques

Lastly, we trained machine learning models to see how accurately these features can classify voice phishing phone numbers from normal users’ numbers. In addition to three popular machine learning-based classification algorithms [Random Forest, Gradient Boosting, and Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP)], we included logistic regression as a baseline model for comparison. These three machine learning algorithms are widely used due to their high classification accuracy and stable performance, while logistic regression provides an interpretable benchmark. They each adopt distinct learning structures, allowing for complementary performance comparisons.

Random Forest is an ensemble method that combines multiple decision trees to reduce overfitting and demonstrates strong predictive performance even in classification problems involving complex interactions and non-linear relationships among variables21. Gradient Boosting is an ensemble technique that incrementally improves performance by iteratively correcting the errors of previous models, making it effective for fine-grained predictions 22. MLP is an artificial neural network composed of an input layer, one or more hidden layers, and an output layer. It belongs to the deep learning family and is particularly strong in learning complex data patterns 23. Logistic regression is a widely used linear classification model that provides interpretable coefficients and serves as a standard baseline in classification tasks.

We used the same data and the bootstrap approach for model training we did with the logistic regression analysis. For each of the 1000 iterations, the dataset was split into a training set (70%) and a validation set (30%), and stratified sampling was applied to preserve the class ratio.

Each classification model was independently trained under the same dataset and experimental conditions. In each iteration, key evaluation metrics—True Negative (TN), False Positive (FP), False Negative (FN), True Positive (TP), Accuracy, Precision, Recall, and F1-score—were computed based on the validation set. Furthermore, the mean and standard deviation of each evaluation metric were calculated across the 1000 iterations to assess both the quantitative performance and the consistency of predictions. This approach enabled an in-depth comparison between the baseline model (logistic regression) and machine learning models, and a thorough examination of their practical applicability for detecting voice phishing. Detailed algorithmic steps of the sampling and evaluation procedure used in the machine learning experiments are provided in Appendix I. In addition, detailed validation procedures, including stratified five-fold cross-validation and learning curve analysis, are provided in Appendix G.

Logistic regression results

To classify voice phishing phone numbers, a stepwise logistic regression was conducted by sequentially adding explanatory variables. The analysis consists of eight models (Table 3), where each subsequent model includes an additional variable compared to the previous one. Each cell in the table presents the estimated coefficient for the corresponding variable, with the standard errors from 1000 bootstrap iterations reported in parentheses. The results show that the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) generally decreases as variables are added, indicating improved model fit. Model 8 records the lowest AIC value, suggesting the best overall fit. Among the variables, Day of the Week Score and Carrier are found to be the most influential predictors in distinguishing voice phishing numbers. This is evident from the notable drop in AIC, from 703.3 (Model 2) to 440.0 (Model 3), and from 357.4 (Model 7) to 271.9 (Model 8). These findings reflect the pattern that voice phishing calls are typically concentrated on weekdays and associated with specific carriers.

In addition, variables such as the day of the week score (p < 0.001), the daytime call ratio, the ratio of calls shorter than 30 s, the outgoing call ratio, and the number of unique contacts per week also demonstrated strong statistical significance (see Table 3 for p-values). This implies that voice phishing numbers tend to exhibit repeated patterns in terms of timing and call structure.

These findings suggest that voice phishing numbers can be more accurately classified when not only call direction patterns but also a combination of temporal, structural, and carrier-related features are considered. Therefore, incorporating such multidimensional features into the call detection systems is essential for improving accuracy and robustness.

To further examine whether the effects of key variables vary across conditions, we additionally evaluated an extended model including interaction terms. The interaction model showed a slightly improved log-likelihood; however, the overall improvement in model fit was minimal. Specifically, the average AIC decreased only marginally (ΔAIC ≈ 0.74), and the increase in pseudo \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R^2$$\end{document} was small. Furthermore, the likelihood ratio test indicated that the interaction effects were not statistically significant (p > 0.05). These results suggest that the inclusion of interaction terms does not substantially improve the explanatory power of the model. Therefore, for the purpose of interpretability and model parsimony, we retain the main-effects model as the primary specification, and detailed results of the interaction model are provided in Predicting voice phishing numbers using machine learning models

To detect voice phishing phone numbers, we applied four classification algorithms (Random Forest, Gradient Boosting, MLP, and logistic regression) and compared the performance among them. Table 4 summarizes the average performance of each classification model. All machine learning models show high predictive performance, with Random Forest and Gradient Boosting producing almost identical results. Random Forest achieved the most stable classification performance, with an average accuracy of 0.969, precision of 0.983, recall of 0.982, and F1-score of 0.983. The Gradient Boosting model yielded comparably high performance. While the performance of the MLP model was slightly lower, all three models demonstrated very high recall (above 0.97), indicating strong potential to minimize false negatives in detecting voice phishing numbers.

As a baseline model, logistic regression was additionally evaluated. While logistic regression achieved high precision (0.985), its recall was noticeably lower (0.929) compared to the machine learning models. In particular, the number of false negatives was substantially higher, indicating that logistic regression fails to identify a significant portion of voice phishing cases.

To further examine the robustness of the models under realistic class imbalance conditions, we conducted an additional sensitivity analysis by varying the ratio between normal and voice phishing samples (1:1, 10:1, and 100:1). As shown in Fig. 4, Random Forest maintains consistently high performance across all imbalance settings, while Gradient Boosting shows moderate degradation. In contrast, the performance of the MLP model drops substantially under severe imbalance (100:1), indicating its sensitivity to class distribution. These results suggest that tree-based ensemble models are more robust and reliable in highly imbalanced real-world environments such as voice phishing detection. Detailed numerical results of this analysis are provided in Appendix H.

In conclusion, while logistic regression provides an interpretable baseline, the machine learning models (Random Forest, Gradient Boosting, and MLP) demonstrate superior performance, particularly in terms of recall and F1-score. In particular, Random Forest and Gradient Boosting maintained high accuracy and stable performance while also demonstrating a high sensitivity to phishing number detection, making them applicable in practice. These results suggest that machine learning-based classification techniques can serve as practical, data-driven tools for the early detection and proactive response to voice phishing crimes. Discussion and conclusion

While traditional police approaches have primarily focused on post-crime recovery or detection after the crime has occurred, we emphasize the early intervention by blocking call attempts during the baiting and approach stage of voice phishing schemes. To this end, we quantitatively analyzed various characteristics of voice phishing calls—such as call time, day-of-week patterns, call duration, outgoing/incoming call ratio, number of unique weekly contacts, and telecom carrier information—based on the call log data from 2692 phone numbers used by voice phishing organizations in South Korea in 2021.

Using the logistic regression and machine learning-based empirical models such as Random Forest, Gradient Boosting, and Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP), the study found that voice phishing numbers exhibited abnormal patterns compared to normal numbers. These included the concentration of calls during weekday daytime hours, shorter call durations, higher outgoing call ratios, a greater number of unique contacts, and a strong preference for MVNO carriers. Notably, MVNO usage reached 93.3%, indicating that telecom provider information can serve as a key classification feature. All three machine learning models demonstrated outstanding performance in predicting voice phishing calls based on these features with average accuracy above 0.95 and recall exceeding 0.97.

These findings are of both academic and practical significance, as they provide empirical insights into evolving voice phishing crime trends based on call behavior data. Given that voice phishing is neither an individual nor isolated offense but a highly organized crime, law enforcement agencies must adopt strategic and systematic frameworks rather than relying solely on case-by-case responses. By clearly identifying the preferred time windows, call structures, and telecom providers used by perpetrators, this study opens up the possibility of detecting voice phishing attempts during the early approach stage, which has long been overlooked. This analysis highlights the limitations of traditional manpower-based investigative methods and introduces a new paradigm based on quantitative, data-driven approaches–carrying significant policy implications.

However, it is important to note that the behavioral patterns identified in this study are derived from the telecommunications environment of South Korea, which is shaped by country-specific institutional, regulatory, and market conditions. For instance, the distribution of telecom providers, the prevalence of MVNO usage, and regulatory frameworks governing telecommunications may differ substantially across countries.

Therefore, while the proposed data-driven approach of analyzing call log features for proactive voice phishing detection is broadly applicable, the specific model parameters and feature distributions identified in this study may not be directly transferable to other national contexts without adaptation. Instead, the framework should be interpreted as a general methodological approach that requires re-calibration using country-specific data.

Despite these limitations, this research exemplifies how data science can provide practical solutions in the criminal response. By extracting meaningful behavioral patterns from large-scale call log data and designing algorithms for voice phishing number classification, this study demonstrates the applicability and utility of data science. Especially in the context of sophisticated crimes that exceed the capacity of law enforcement alone, this approach provides scientific grounds to promote collaboration with telecom providers and related agencies. For instance, the findings could support requests to telecom companies for early warning systems targeting MVNO usage patterns, or proposals to financial institutions for linking high-risk number alerts—forming the basis for a multi-agency policy framework. This underscores that effective crime response in a complex modern society requires inter-agency cooperation and coordination.

In fact, the Korean National Police Agency has operated a proactive analysis system based on call records since 2021 to counter voice phishing crimes. The effectiveness of this system is evident in the statistics: the number of voice phishing cases decreased from 30,982 in 2021 to 18,902 in 2023, and the total financial damage also dropped from 774.1 billion KRW to 447.1 billion KRW during the same period. These improvements are attributed to efforts such as early analysis of call logs and detection of illegal private relay stations. In this context, our study offers further empirical evidence that can support the police’s strategic response by enabling refined and detailed analysis of call patterns.

This study is not without limitations. First, the data used for analysis was limited to the year of 2021, which restricts the study’s ability to reflect changes in crime patterns or the introduction of new telecommunications technologies. Second, due to privacy concerns, the complete call logs of normal phone numbers could not be obtained, making it difficult to compare all variables in detail. Third, the generalizability of the findings may be constrained by the country-specific nature of the dataset, and caution should be exercised when applying the model to different institutional or telecommunications environments. Fourth, the study’s findings can only be applied directly to real-world investigations if the detection system is automated and capable of real-time processing, which is a significant research topic for future research.

Particularly, future research should focus on enhancing the predictive models proposed in this study, in order to achieve fast and accurate predictions. It is also necessary to redesign the model architecture to encompass real-time data streaming environments. Further comparisons of various deep learning architectures, advanced machine learning models, and ensemble methods are required to improve predictive performance and robustness. In particular, the exploration of more sophisticated model architectures is necessary to better capture complex and evolving behavioral patterns in voice phishing data. These efforts will help advance this work into a fully deployable detection system for field use.
