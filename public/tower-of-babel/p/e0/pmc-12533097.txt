External validation of PreOpNet to predict 30-day mortality after major non-cardiac surgery using digital electrocardiogram
Authors: Arnaud Champetier, Pedro Lopez-Ayala, Christian Puelacher, Koray Durak, Emel Kaplan, Danielle Gualandro, Noemi Glarner, Mirjam Pargger, Gabrielle Huré, Katrin Burri-Winkler, Daniel Bolliger, Luzius A. Steiner, Stefan Schären, Edin Mujagic, Martin Clauss, Andreas M. Mueller, Didier Lardinois, Jasper Boeddinghaus, Felix Mahfoud, Ivo Strebel, Christian Mueller
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
PreOpNet is a novel deep-learning algorithm using 12-lead digital electrocardiogram (ECG) for preoperative risk assessment of all-cause death and major adverse cardiac events (MACE) within 30 days. Its performance in European high-risk patients undergoing major non-cardiac surgery—the target population for guideline-recommended risk assessment—and comparison to high-sensitivity cardiac troponin T (hs-cTnT), is unknown. In a prospective European study (2014–2019), 6098 high-risk patients with available ECGs were enrolled. PreOpNet showed moderate discrimination for death (AUC 0.707) and MACE (0.675), but overestimated risk. It outperformed the revised cardiac risk index (RCRI) for death (AUC 0.644), but not for MACE (0.662). Hs-cTnT remained superior for both outcomes (AUC 0.762 and 0.743). Importantly, PreOpNet provided incremental prognostic value when combined with RCRI and/or hs-cTnT. PreOpNet has limited benefit for preoperative risk stratification in high-risk surgical patients as a stand-alone test. However, it holds promise when used in conjunction with RCRI and hs-cTnT. Clinical Trial Registration: ClinicalTrials.gov number: NCT02573532; https://www.clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT02573532.

Introduction

An estimated 313 million surgical procedures are performed worldwide each year, of which 85% are non-cardiac surgeries1. Postoperative deaths within 30 days following the surgery account for 7.7% of all global deaths, making it the third leading cause of death worldwide1. With increasing rates of cardiovascular comorbidities and the expansion of surgical services, postoperative deaths are projected to increase from 4.2 to 6.1 million annually1. In clinical practice, preoperative risk assessment is routinely employed to estimate the risk of adverse procedural outcomes, such as death and major adverse cardiac events (MACE). These assessments commonly include the use of risk scores, such as the Revised Cardiac Risk Index (RCRI), the 12-lead electrocardiogram (ECG), and, more recently, cardiovascular biomarkers, such as high-sensitivity cardiac troponin (hs-cTn)2–4. Both the 12-lead ECG and hs-cTn are widely available, noninvasive, and inexpensive tools and may offer superior predictive performance compared to traditional clinical risk scores.

Machine learning techniques, including deep learning (DL), have recently shown their great potential in analysing raw ECG data for the diagnosis and prediction of several cardiovascular conditions5–7. In this context, the PreOpNet model was derived and validated based on large retrospective administrative data sets in the United States8 and the first DL model to predict death and MACE at 30 days after surgery using only the 12-lead ECG with high discrimination, outperforming the RCRI. Despite its potential, PreOpNet has several limitations. First, the model was trained and validated in all-comers, including a high proportion of low-risk patients and low-risk operations, for which the latest European and American clinical practice guidelines do not recommend routine preoperative risk assessment, leading to a possible overestimation of its performance3,9–12. Second, PreOpNet has not been directly compared with hs-cTn, which has a class I recommendation for this indication3,9–11. Third, external validation in prospective, high-resolution, geographically distinct cohorts is lacking, limiting its generalizability and transportability9,13.

To address these gaps, we aimed to (1) externally validate the performance of PreOpNet in a large, prospective, single-centre, European cohort of high-risk patients undergoing major non-cardiac surgery and (2) directly compare its predictive performance with the RCRI and preoperative hs-cTnT concentration.

Results

Patient characteristics

Of the 14,294 patients originally enrolled in the Basel-PMI study, 6098 individuals (42.7%) met the eligibility criteria and were included in this analysis (Fig. 1). The median age was 74.0 (69.0–80.0) years, 43.5% were female, 32.7% had known coronary artery disease (CAD), 21.7% peripheral artery disease (PAD), 20.6% atrial fibrillation, 15.1% chronic heart failure, and 28.5% chronic kidney disease (Table 1). A 30-day follow-up was completed in 99.9% of patients. Death within 30 days occurred in 211 (3.5%) patients, and MACE in 426 (7.0%) patients. Discrimination and calibration

The distribution of the risk computed by PreOpNet ranged from 0–90% for death and from 0–95% for MACE (Fig. 2a and d). The prognostic performance of PreOpNet as quantified by the AUC for predicting 30-day death and MACE was 0.707 (95% CI: 0.672–0.742) and 0.675 (95% CI: 0.649–0.701), respectively (Fig. 2b and e). Calibration plots showed overprediction for both 30-day death (intercept −2.04 [95% CI: −2.18 to −1.90]; slope 0.73 [95% CI: 0.59–0.86], Fig. 2c) and MACE (intercept −2.37 [95% CI: −2.48 to −2.27]; slope 0.62 [95% CI: 0.52–0.72], Fig. 2f). Comparison with RCRI and hs-cTnT

Among the 5828 patients with available preoperative hs-cTnT concentrations, 204 (3.5%) died within 30 days, and 413 (7.1%) had a MACE (Supplementary Table 3). The discriminative performance of PreOpNet in this subgroup was similar to the main cohort for death and MACE (AUC 0.703 [95% CI: 0.668–0.738] and 0.668 [95% CI: 0.642–0.695], respectively). PreOpNet’s discriminative performance for predicting death was significantly superior to RCRI (AUC 0.644 [95% CI: 0.607–0.681], p = 0.19) but significantly inferior to hs-cTnT (AUC 0.762 [95% CI: 0.730–0.793]; p = 0.009) (Fig. 4a). PreOpNet’s discrimination for predicting MACE was similar to RCRI (AUC 0.662 [95% CI: 0.637–0.688], p = 0.74) and significantly inferior to hs-cTnT (AUC 0.743 [95% CI: 0.719–0.767], p < 0.001) (Fig. 4b).Fig. 4Discrimination of PreOpNet, hs-cTnT and RCRI.Receiver-operating-characteristic curves showing the discrimination of the PreOpNet model (green), high-sensitivity cardiac troponin T (blue), and the RCRI (red) for predicting a death and b MACE within 30 days. Added value of PreOpNet to RCRI and hs-cTnT

The addition of PreOpNet to the RCRI significantly improved discrimination for predicting 30-day mortality (AUC 0.644 [95% CI: 0.607–0.681] vs AUC 0.730 [95% CI: 0.695–0.764]; p < 0.001 for difference) and MACE (AUC 0.662 [95% CI: 0.637–0.688] vs AUC 0.712 [95% CI: 0.687–0.738]; p < 0.001 for difference). Similarly, combining PreOpNet with hs-cTnT also enhanced discrimination for mortality (AUC 0.762 [95% CI: 0.730–0.793] vs AUC 0.785 [95% CI: 0.756–0.814]; p = 0.003 for difference) and MACE (AUC 0.743 [95% CI: 0.719–0.767] vs AUC 0.755 [95% CI: 0.732–0.777]; p = 0.023 for difference) PreOpNet also showed incremental value when added to RCRI and hs-cTnT together (Table 2). Sensitivity analysis using population-wise normalisation of the ECG

Using the population-wise normalisation preprocessing, PreOpNet’s predictions range remained unchanged from 0–90% for death and 0–95% for MACE. The distribution of the risk was slightly more left-skewed for patients not developing adverse outcomes (Fig. 5a and d) compared to the distributions of the main analysis. Using the cohort with available preoperative hs-cTnT (Supplementary Table 2), the discriminative performance of the PreOpNet increased for death and MACE outcomes (AUC 0.746 [95% CI: 0.712–0.78] and 0.690 [95% CI: 0.664–0.716], respectively) versus those in the main analysis. Calibration showed again overprediction for death and MACE outcomes (intercept −1.96 [95% CI: −2.10 to −1.81]; slope 0.71 [95% CI: 0.61–0.82] and intercept −2.34 [95% CI: −2.44 to −2.23]; slope 0.53 [95% CI: 0.45–0.61], respectively, Fig. 5c and f). PreOpNet’s discriminative performance for predicting death was significantly superior to RCRI (p < 0.001) and similar to hs-cTnT (p = 0.490) (Fig. 5b). Secondary analysis

The prognostic performance of PreOpNet for predicting the combined endpoint of perioperative myocardial infarction/injury (PMI) and MACE was 0.616 (95% CI: 0.597–0.634), similar to RCRI (AUC 0.623 [95% CI: 0.605–0.641], p = 0.540) but lower than hs-cTnT (AUC 0.716 [95% CI: 0.699–0.733], p < 0.001) (Supplementary Fig. 1). The addition of PreOpNet to the RCRI significantly improved discrimination for predicting the combined endpoint at 30 days (p < 0.001), but not when added to hs-cTnT (p = 0.069)(Supplementary Table 4).

Discussion

This study (1) externally validated the performance of PreOpNet in a large, prospective, European cohort of high-risk patients undergoing major non-cardiac surgery and (2) directly compared its performance with the RCRI and preoperative hs-cTnT concentration. We report seven major findings.

First, PreOpNet demonstrated moderate discrimination for 30-day death and MACE, but weak calibration, with a systematic overestimation of risk (i.e., predicted risk consistently exceeded observed event rates). Second, the AUC for both endpoints was lower in this European cohort of high-risk patients than previously reported in the original internal and external validation cohorts comprising all-comers. Third, subgroup analyses revealed consistent findings for both death and MACE. Fourth, PreOpNet’s discrimination for 30-day all-cause death was higher compared to RCRI, a well-established simple reference standard, but lower than hs-cTnT, the guideline-recommended aid in risk stratification for high-risk patients3,11. PreOpNet’s discrimination for 30-day MACE was comparable to RCRI and lower than hs-cTnT. Fifth, PreOpNet demonstrated more incremental value when added to the RCRI than to hs-cTnT for predicting both endpoints. Sixth, applying population-wise normalisation during ECG preprocessing modestly improved PreOpNet’s discriminative performance for death and MACE predictions. However, preoperative hs-cTnT still achieved similar or better performance. Seventh, the use of LIME to identify, visualize, and interpret relevant features in the ECG for better understanding the model’s decision making was unsuccessful, as the highlighted features proved difficult to interpret and did not relate to previous ECG knowledge.

These findings extend and corroborate prior research demonstrating the potential of the 12-lead ECG as a simple, low-cost, widely available clinical tool for risk prediction using CNN modelling5,6,8,14. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first external validation of PreOpNet in a prospective cohort of high-risk patients undergoing major non-cardiac surgery and has important clinical implications. The inclusion criteria and patient characteristics of this study reflect the target population for which preoperative risk stratification is recommended in current clinical practice guidelines3,11.

Several factors could have contributed to the observed drop in performance of PreOpNet. First, PreOpNet was developed using a large retrospective electronic health record dataset including patients undergoing catherization procedures, cardiac surgery and non-cardiac surgery, of which a large proportion were low-risk patients as seen from the baseline characteristics (e.g., 65 years of age, 34% hypertension and 22% history of CAD) and the relatively low outcome prevalence (1.8% death and 2.9% MACE). In contrast, the BASEL-PMI cohort consisted exclusively of patients undergoing major non-cardiac surgery who were considered to be at an increased risk of mortality, defined as ≥65 years of age, or ≥45 years with a history of CAD, PAD, or stroke. This resulted in a cohort with a higher baseline risk and event prevalence (3.5% death and 7% MACE). This shift in baseline risk may partially explain the observed differences in model performance, particularly the model’s calibration8. Second, PreOpNet may be prone to overfitting due to its high number of parameters relative to the training data size and the relative low outcome prevalence15,16. The systematic overprediction seen in the calibration curves points to possible overfitting in model derivation despite having implemented early stopping to mitigate it15. Third, distributional shifts in ECG features due to variations in healthcare settings, recording equipment, or patient characteristics across centres may also have contributed to the drop in performance.

Our study has important clinical implications, suggesting that PreOpNet performance for the prediction of 30-day death and MACE is insufficient to justify its use as a standalone tool in routine clinical care. Furthermore, the explainability of the model overall seems limited, as the identified ECG features are still poorly understood. This external validation showed that PreOpNet is not a replacement for hs-cTnT in clinical practice. However, it may serve as a complementary tool to further enhance preoperative risk stratification. As PreOpNet demonstrated statistically significant incremental value, its clinical utility and relevance deserve to be evaluated in future studies17.

Although the ECG is primarily a cardiac test, the model predicted all-cause mortality (AUC 0.70) better than MACE (AUC 0.67), consistent with the derivation study8. This suggests the model captures broader health indicators (e.g., heart rate, variability) more strongly linked to mortality than to acute myocardial infarction (AMI) or heart failure. In contrast, MACE and PMI depend on acute changes less visible on a preoperative ECG. The performance drop when PMI was added to MACE highlights the challenge of generalising models beyond their intended outcomes.

Several limitations merit consideration when interpreting these findings. First, the Basel-PMI study was conducted exclusively in patients undergoing non-cardiac surgery. The performance of PreOpNet in other clinical settings used for its derivation, such as cardiac surgery and catheter-based interventions, requires further investigation. Second, the performance of PreOpNet was assessed in a cohort where patient care was not guided by PreOpNet. To assess the true impact of embedding PreOpNet into routine clinical practice, prospective implementation studies would be needed. Third, only 42.7% patients in BASEL-PMI met the eligibility criteria, possibly introducing some selection bias in the analysis. Patients included in the study tended to have higher cardiovascular risk factors and a higher prevalence of adverse outcomes compared to those excluded from final analysis. As a result, the final validation cohort had a slightly higher baseline risk than the initial BASEL-PMI cohort. However, it is important to note that this final higher-risk validation cohort is even more clinically relevant, as these patients are the focus of guideline-recommended preoperative risk stratification efforts. Fourth, although LIME was used to identify, visualize and interpret relevant futures in the ECG, other available interpretability algorithms were not explored18.

In conclusion, in high-risk patients undergoing major non-cardiac surgery, the target population for risk stratification tools, the PreOpNet model demonstrated moderate discrimination and limited calibration for predicting 30-day mortality or MACE. Although it outperformed the RCRI Score for predicting 30-day mortality, it did not surpass hs-cTnT, the current guideline-recommended standard. While PreOpNet has limited benefit for enhancing risk stratification in high-risk surgical patients as a stand-alone test when hs-cTnT is available, it holds promise when used in conjunction with RCRI and hs-cTnT.

Methods

Study design and participants

This was a secondary analysis within the prospective Basel-PMI study program (NCT02573532, https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT02573532)19,20. Consecutive adult patients undergoing major non-cardiac surgery at the University Hospital Basel, Switzerland, from October 2014 to September 2019 who were eligible for the institutional routine PMI active surveillance program and provided written general consent to registration in a dedicated prospective database, were enrolled. Patients were screened if they had a planned hospital stay exceeding 24 hours after surgery (hence defined as major surgery) and were considered at increased risk of mortality, defined as ≥65 years of age or ≥45 years with a history of CAD, PAD, or stroke19,20. For this external validation, patients were eligible if a digital ECG was available in the 30 days preceding the surgery. When a patient underwent two surgeries associated with a single ECG, only the time window of the first surgery was retained, and the second surgery was not considered. If multiple ECGs were available within the 30-day window prior to surgery, the one closest to the day of surgery was used. Additionally, for the comparison between PreOpNet and hs-cTnT, all patients without a preoperative hs-cTnT measurement were excluded (Fig. 1).

The study was carried out according to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the local ethics committee (Ethikkommission Nordwest- und Zentralschweiz, project number: PB_2016-02633). Informed consent was obtained from all participants. Reporting is in accordance with the TRIPOD + AI statement (Supplementary Table 1).

Data collection

The RCRI was calculated for all patients. The cardiovascular risk of surgery was classified as proposed by the European Society of Cardiology and the European Society of Anaesthesiology3,21. Hs-cTnT was measured prospectively by using the Elecsys System using the Modular Analytics E170 or the Cobas e602 (Roche Diagnostics) assay with a limit of detection of 5 ng/L, a 10% coefficient of variation at 13 ng/L, and the 99th percentile of a healthy reference population at 14 ng/L22. Hs-cTnT was measured within 30 days before surgery.

During follow-up, patients were contacted after 365 days by mail or telephone, and local death registries were checked. In case of an outcome event, study personnel requested reports from the general practitioners, treating facilities, or death registries. Patients lost to follow-up were censored at the last contact with the study team, a hospital, or their general practitioner.

ECG preprocessing

The 12-lead ECGs were preprocessed according to the open-access code provided by PreOpNet’s authors (https://github.com/ecg-net/PreOpNet), including a lead-wise normalisation. In addition, the powerline interference at 50 Hz and its harmonics were attenuated using a band-stop filter. A sensitivity analysis was performed using cohort-wise rather than lead-wise normalisation due to the concern of lead-wise normalisation potentially obscuring inter-lead relationships critical for patient-level analysis. It also heightens the risk of overfitting to the training data’s lead-specific patterns and, when applied to an external validation cohort, further isolates leads that may be uncorrelated with the initial dataset. In contrast, cohort-wise normalisation mitigates this bias by scaling ECG data across all leads and patients, capturing population variability while preserving inter-lead correlations. As a result, the model could better generalise to the new cohort, with ECG values standardised for consistent interpretation. As cohort-wise normalization is a new preprocessing method not described or used in the derivation manuscript, the source code has been made available online (https://github.com/arnaudMLchamp/ecg_preprocessing).

PreOpNet

PreOpNet is a composite of two convolutional neural network (CNN) models optimised for predicting death and MACE 30 days after surgery from preoperative ECGs8,23. PreOpNet’s weights were available on GitHub (https://github.com/ecg-net/PreOpNet) and used for this analysis. No retraining was performed. PreOpNet’s architecture was designed to mirror the layer structure of EfficientNet, aiming to optimise run-time efficiency while minimising model complexity8,23. After the initial “atrous” layers, PreOpNet utilises convolutional layers with an inverted residual structure24. This structure featured bottleneck layers at both the input and output, with an intermediate expansion layer in between25. In each set of these layers, the number of input channels progressively increased, facilitating the integration of information across the ECG leads. The model takes input in the form of 12 × 5000 matrices, representing 10-s, 12-lead digital ECG recordings sampled at 500 Hz. It outputs a value between 0 and 1, which serves as an estimate of risk. However, this estimate may not be well-calibrated.

Outcomes

The primary outcomes were the occurrence of all-cause death and MACE within 30 days after major non-cardiac surgery. MACE was defined as a composite of AMI, acute heart failure (AHF), life-threatening arrhythmia, and death. AMI was defined according to the 4th universal definition of myocardial infarction26. In brief, AMI was centrally adjudicated when there was evidence of myocardial necrosis in association with a clinical setting consistent with myocardial ischemia. Myocardial necrosis was diagnosed by at least 1 hs-cTn value >99th percentile together with a significant rise and/or fall. AHF was defined as recommended by current guidelines27. The adjudication of AHF in the BASEL-PMI study has been described in detail previously28. Briefly, the presence of AHF was centrally adjudicated according to current guidelines as an episode of AHF within 1 month after surgery (regardless if the patient was still in-hospital) based on symptoms and signs of AHF, chest X-ray, B-type natriuretic peptide (BNP) or N-terminal pro-BNP plasma concentrations, and cardiac imaging including echocardiography. Life-threatening arrhythmia included cardiac arrest, sustained ventricular tachycardia, third-degree atrioventricular block or any arrhythmia requiring cardiopulmonary resuscitation. To evaluate the generalisability of PreOpNet to additional prognostic endpoints in non-cardiac surgery, PMI was incorporated into the MACE endpoint in a secondary analysis. PMI was defined as an hs-cTnT increase ≥14 ng/L above baseline values within 7 days of surgery19.

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables were reported as count (percentage), and continuous variables as median (25th–75th percentiles [Q1–Q3]). The 95% confidence intervals (95% CI) were calculated using Wilson’s method if not specified otherwise29.

To appraise the diagnostic performance of PreOpNet, its discrimination and calibration were evaluated. Model discrimination was assessed by means of the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC)29. The difference between two AUCs was tested according to DeLong et al. when both AUCs were computed in the same cohort, and according to Hanley and McNeil otherwise. Confidence intervals of AUC were calculated according to the method by DeLong and colleagues30. Model calibration, the agreement between observed and predicted risk, was assessed visually using calibration curves with a Loess-smoothing fit31. In addition, calibration intercept and calibration slope were also reported.

The discrimination of PreOpNet for predicting 30-day death and MACE was further evaluated in pre-specified subgroups: age (cutoff at median age), sex, days of ECG collection before surgery, RCRI score (cutoff at 3), renal failure (defined as an eGFR <60 ml/min) and level of hs-cTnT (cutoff at 14 ng/L, the upper reference limit of the assay).

To assess the potential added value provided by PreOpNet to RCRI and hs-cTnT for predicting 30-day mortality or MACE, the difference in the AUC between a base model (including either RCRI, hs-cTnT or both) and an extended model (base model + PreOpNet) was calculated. Models were fitted using logistic regression and hs-cTnT was modelled using restricted cubic splines with 3 knots (10th, 50th and 90th percentile) to relax the linearity assumption, as suggested by Harrell32. In addition to the change in the AUC, the added value provided by PreOpNet was further evaluated by means of the likelihood ratio and the AIC.

We conducted a sample size calculation using the method proposed by Grolleau et al.33. With a mortality prevalence of 3% in our cohort, assuming similar performance to that reported in the external validation of the original publication (AUC 0.79) and targeting a maximum 95% confidence interval width of 0.08, we estimated a minimum required sample size of 4209 patients. Similarly, for the MACE endpoint with a prevalence of 7% and based on the expected performance reported in the original study, we estimated a minimum required sample size of 2033 patients.

PreOpNet evaluation was performed using Python 3.11.734. All statistical analyses were performed using R 4.4.0 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria)35.

Explainability

LIME was used to identify and visualise relevant features in the ECG used for model decision-making (code https://github.com/ecg-net/scd-oregon/tree/main). The three ECGs with the lowest and highest risks for each endpoint and their most relevant features for the model were highlighted by LIME.
