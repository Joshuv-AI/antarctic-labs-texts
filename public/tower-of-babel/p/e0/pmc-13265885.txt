Effects of Astragalus membranaceus stems and leaves on broiler growth, antioxidant and immune status: Associating gut microbiota and serum metabolome
Authors: Lili Guo, Tangxuan Jiang, Yuhang Shi, Xinyuan Gao, Nan Qin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAstragalus membranaceus stems and leaves (AMSL), the aerial parts of the plant Astragalus membranaceus, are produced in large quantities annually but remain underutilized. AMSL contain active components such as flavonoids, saponins, and polysaccharides, as well as nutrients such as amino acids and minerals, indicating potential applications in animal husbandry. However, it remains unclear whether AMSL supplementation in animal feed is accompanied by alterations in endogenous metabolites and the gut microbiota. In this study, broilers were fed a basal diet supplemented with varying concentrations of AMSL (0%, 1%, 3%, and 5%) to evaluate the effects of AMSL on growth performance, antioxidant capacity, immune status, the gut microbiota, and serum metabolomics of broilers. The results showed that moderate AMSL supplementation (1–3%) did not negatively affect broiler growth performance, whereas excessive AMSL (5%) reduced production efficiency. Additionally, AMSL promoted the development of the spleen, increased total serum protein levels, and decreased serum aspartate aminotransferase level. Appropriate AMSL supplementation increased serum antioxidant activity and immune capacity, particularly at concentrations ranging from 3 to 5%. Further analysis of the intestinal microbiota revealed that 3% AMSL significantly modulated the composition of the cecal microbiota by promoting the growth and enrichment of Lactobacillus while inhibiting Pseudomonas, Ruminococcus, and Bacteroides. Moreover, 3% AMSL regulated serum endogenous metabolite levels by influencing purine, amino acid, and lipid metabolisms. This study confirms the potential of using AMSL in broiler farming, and provides a scientific basis for their use as a functional feed or feed additive in the poultry industry, promoting the intensive development and value-added utilization of AMSL.Keywords: Astragalus membranaceus stems and leaves, Broiler, Immunity, Gut microbiota, Metabolomic analysis

Introduction

Astragalus membranaceus, a member of the Fabaceae family, is a perennial medicinal herb widely used in China and Korea. The root of Astragalus membranaceus (known as Astragali Radix) is the traditional medicinal part of this plant and has multiple bioactivities, such as immunomodulatory, antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, neuroprotective and anticancer effects (Ghabeshi et al., 2023). Currently, Astragali Radix is recognized as a food and medicine homologous substance in China, which inevitably leads to increasing demand for this valuable material. However, the extensive harvesting of Astragali Radix will generate substantial amounts of above-ground by-products, such as the Astragalus membranaceus stems and leaves (AMSL). To date, AMSL are often discarded directly along with the annual excavation of roots, leading to significant environmental pollution and a waste of natural resources. The annual output of AMSL can reach as high as 300,000 tons (Wang et al., 2023). Therefore, how to promote the in-depth development and utilization of AMSL has become an important and urgent research topic.

AMSL are a safe natural plant resource and non-toxic, with an oral LD50 value greater than 250 g/kg body weight (Li et al., 2021). Scientific studies have also shown that AMSL contain 15 amino acids and abundant major elements such as calcium and phosphorus, as well as five trace elements including magnesium and iron (Zhang et al., 2016; Wang et al., 2023). With respect to its functional components, AMSL share a similar profile of active ingredients with its roots, including flavonoids, saponins, and polysaccharides; thus, they have been reported to have antioxidant, immune-enhancing, and antibacterial effects (Samuel et al., 2021; Smaali et al., 2025). On the basis of the above, it can be concluded that AMSL represent a high-quality raw material that integrates both nutritional value and biological efficacy, with potential to serve as a functional source and dietary supplement.

The broiler industry, as a vital component of China's animal husbandry sector, plays a key role in ensuring the supply of high-quality animal protein and promoting economic development. However, under intensive management systems, broilers demonstrate relatively low resistance to environmental stressors and diseases, necessitating increased attention to prevention and control measures (Moore, 2024). With the total ban on the use of antibiotics in animal feed, finding effective antibiotic-alternative functional substances is highly important for ensuring the safety of meat products, including poultry products such as chicken. Astragalus membranaceus, particularly its root, has been extensively documented in numerous studies to confer beneficial effects when incorporated into animal feed or as a feed additive. These benefits have been observed not only in broilers (Zhang et al., 2020), but also in dairy cows (Huang et al., 2022), sheep (Wang et al., 2021), and other livestock species (Che et al., 2024). Research has shown that the inclusion of 2% fermented Astragali Radix powder in broiler feed enhances growth performance and promotes the development of immune organs (Han et al., 2024).

Some exploratory work has also been reported regarding the application of AMSL in broiler husbandry, indicating that they can promote the growth rate and enhance immunity in broilers (Xue and Bai, 2014). It is well known that host immunity is closely linked to the gut microbiota, which can influence host metabolism and immune status through the production or modulation of specific metabolites (Rooks and Garrett, 2016). However, the specific role of AMSL in regulating the gut microbiota and modulating endogenous metabolites remains unclear. Therefore, the objective of this study was to elucidate the effects of AMSL on growth performance, antioxidant status and immunity in broilers, and further explore the associations between the gut microbiota and serum metabolites, thus assessing the potential of AMSL as a functional feed resource or phytogenic feed additive in broiler production and promoting their in-depth development and high-value application.

Materials and methods

Animals, diets and experimental design

The study received approval from the Animal Care and Use Committee of Shanxi University of Chinese Medicine, China (Approval No. 2020DW113). A total of 200 one-day-old male white-feathered broiler chicks (Arbor Acre) were used as the experimental animals. After a 7-day pre-feeding period with basal diet, the chicks were randomly divided into four groups with five replicates per group and ten broilers per replicate. The broilers were housed in stainless-steel wire cages (150 cm × 60 cm × 70 cm) and provided with ad libitum access to feed and water. The control group (CG) received a basal diet, whereas the 3 experimental groups (EG) were fed a basal diet supplemented with 1% (EG1), 3% (EG2) or 5% AMSL (EG3) for 42 days (8–49 d). The basal diets were formulated to meet the nutritional requirements of poultry (NRC, 1994, Washington DC, USA), and the composition and nutrient content of the basal diet are detailed in Table 1. The AMSL used in this experiment were harvested in September 2019 from Yingxian County, located in the north of Shanxi Province (China) with geographic coordinates of 39°22′39″ N‌ and 113°18′34″ E. After air-drying, AMSL were ground into powder and sieved through a 40-mesh screen for use. The proximate composition of AMSL was analyzed according to the recommendations of the AOAC (2005), and its determined composition is shown in Table 2. Briefly, dry matter was obtained by oven-drying at 105°C to constant weight (AOAC 934.01), crude protein was measured using the Kjeldahl method (AOAC 984.13), crude fat was analyzed by Soxhlet extraction method (AOAC 920.39), crude fiber was determined by gravimetric analysis (AOAC 978.10), ash content was obtained using the furnace incineration gravimetric method (AOAC 942.05), calcium content was obtained by dry ashing and titration with potassium permanganate (AOAC 927.02), total phosphorus was measured by titration of ammonium phosphomolybdate method (AOAC 964.06), and amino acids were determined using an automatic amino acid analyzer (LA8080, HITACHI, Tokyo, Japan) following AOAC 994.12.

Evaluation of growth performance

On days 8, 21, and 49, the broiler weights were recorded for each replicate, and the average daily gain (ADG) was calculated. The feed intake was recorded on a weekly basis to calculate the average daily feed intake (ADFI), and the feed conversion ratio (FCR) was calculated as the ADFI divided by the ADG.

Sample collection

At the end of the experiment, two broilers per replicate were randomly selected and weighed individually. Blood was drawn from the wing vein, and serum was separated by centrifugation at 4000 × g for 10 min and then stored at −80°C for subsequent analysis. After blood collection, the broilers were killed by cervical dislocation. The immune organs, including the thymus and spleen, were isolated and weighed to calculate the organ index, which is expressed as the ratio of the organ weight (g) to live body weight (kg). The cecal contents were collected and immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80°C for further gut microbiological analysis.

Analysis of serum biochemical indices

Serum levels of total protein, albumin, triglyceride (TG), total cholesterol (TC), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), creatinine, superoxide dismutase (SOD), glutathione peroxidase (GSH-Px), total antioxidant capacity (T-AOC), and malondialdehyde (MDA) were determined using commercial kits (Nanjing Jiancheng Institute of Biological Engineering, Nanjing, China). Serum levels of immunoglobulin G (IgG), immunoglobulin A (IgA) and immunoglobulin M (IgM) were measured using enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay kits (Beijing Solarbio Science & Technology Co., Ltd., Beijing, China). The experimental procedures were strictly conducted following the manufacturer’s instructions.

Metabolomic analysis

Broiler serum samples (100 μL) were mixed with 400 μL of precooled methanol/acetonitrile (1:1, v/v) by vortexing for 60 s and then subjected to low-temperature placing at −20 °C for 1 h to induce precipitation. The protein precipitate was centrifuged at 14,000 × g for 20 min at 4°C, and the supernatant was collected for lyophilization. Afterward, the lyophilized sample was dissolved in acetonitrile/water (1:1, v/v) and centrifuged at 14,000 × g for 15 min at 4°C to obtain the supernatant for use. UHPLC/QTOF-MS analysis of serum samples was conducted using an AB Triple TOF 6600 mass spectrometer coupled with an Agilent 1290 Infinity LC system. Electrospray ionization (ESI) in both positive and negative ion modes was used to examine each sample. Chromatographic and mass spectrometric conditions were as described by Wang et al. (2020). Samples were analyzed in random order, with QC samples inserted into the run order, which were prepared by pooling an equal volume of each sample. The raw data were converted into .mzXML format by ProteoWizard, after which peak alignment, retention time correction, and peak area extraction were performed using the XCMS software. After metabolite structure identification, missing values were imputed using the k-nearest neighbours (KNN) algorithm, and data preprocessing was conducted using a Pareto scaling algorithm. Then, multivariate statistical analysis, such as orthogonal partial least squares discriminant analysis (OPLS–DA), was conducted using SIMCA 14.1 software. On the basis of the variable importance in the projection (VIP) obtained by the OPLS–DA model and the P value adjusted by the Benjamini and Hochberg (BH) method, metabolites with a VIP > 1 and BH-adjusted P < 0.05 were determined to be significantly differential. These differential metabolites were subsequently clustered and visualized using a heatmap generated by an online data analysis platform (https://cloud.metware.cn). Metabolic pathway enrichment analysis of the differential metabolites was performed using the KEGG database (http://www.genome.jp/kegg/).

Analysis of the gut microbiota by 16S rRNA sequencing

Total genomic DNA was extracted from cecal contents using the CTAB/SDS method. The V3–V4 region of the 16S rRNA gene was amplified using the forward primer 338F (5′–ACTCCTACGGGAGGCAGCA–3′) and the reverse primer 806R (5′–GGACTACHVGGGTWTCTAAT–3′). Sequencing libraries were generated using the NEB Next® Ultra™ DNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina (NEB, USA) following the manufacturer’s recommendations, and index codes were added. Library quality was assessed on a Qubit@ 2.0 fluorometer (Thermo Scientific) and an Agilent Bioanalyzer 2100 system. Finally, the library was sequenced on an Illumina MiSeq platform. Sequence analysis was performed by the UPARSE software package using the UPARSE-OTU and UPARSE-OTUref algorithms, and sequences with a similarity of ≥97% were assigned to the same operational taxonomic units (OTUs). Taxonomic annotation of OTUs was performed using the Greengenes 13_8 database. On the basis of the OTU information, the alpha diversity was calculated by the Vegan package of the R software (V4.4.2) and visualized in OriginPro 2019b (Northampton, MA, USA). For beta diversity analysis, principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) and nonmetric multidimensional scaling (NMDS) were conducted based on the Bray–Curtis distance, and analysis of similarity (Anosim) was conducted to evaluate differences in microbial structure and composition across groups using the R package. Linear discriminant analysis effect size (LEfSe) analysis (https://www.omicstudio.cn) with a minimum LDA threshold of 4.0 was used to identify the significantly abundant bacterial taxa among the sample groups. The obtained P values were further subjected to a false discovery rate (FDR) analysis using the BH method.

Data analysis

The experimental unit was the cage for performance data (n = 5 per group), whereas individual birds (two broilers per cage) were considered as experimental units for organ indices, serum biochemical parameters, gut microbiota and serum metabolites (n = 10 per group). Data were assessed for normality using the Shapiro-Wilk test using SPSS 20.0 software (IBM Corp., NY, USA). Since the serum TG concentration, GSH-Px activity, and T-AOC level data were not normally distributed, differences in these parameters among groups were analysed by nonparametric statistical analysis (Kruskal-Wallis test). If the Kruskal-Wallis test was significant, post-hoc pairwise comparisons were conducted using Mann-Whitney U test with Bonferroni correction. For data comforming to normal distribution, one-way ANOVA with Duncan’s post hoc test was performed for comparisons between multiple groups. The data are expressed as the means ± standard errors of the means (SEMs) for descriptive consistency, and P < 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance. The Spearman correlation between differential metabolites and the representative genus was plotted as a heatmap using the Pheatmap package of R software.

Results

Effects of AMSL on broiler growth performance

As shown in Table 3, compared with those of the CG, the ADFI, ADG, and FCR were not significantly affected by dietary supplementation with AMSL from 8 to 21 days of age (P > 0.05). During the later stage of broiler feeding (22–49 days of age), the ADG in the EG3 group was significantly lower than that in the CG (P < 0.05); thus the FCR in this group was higher than that in the CG group (P < 0.05). During the entire experimental period (8–49 days of age), supplementation with AMSL had no effect on the ADFI (P > 0.05); however, the inclusion of AMSL at a dose of 5% in the EG3 group resulted in decreased ADG and increased FCR (P < 0.05). In contrast, the inclusion level of AMSL at 1–3% had no significant effect on the broiler growth performance (P > 0.05). In general, dietary inclusion of 1% to 3% AMSL did not negatively affect the growth performance of broiler chickens, whereas the addition of 5% AMSL impaired the growth performance.

Effects of AMSL on organ indices and serum biochemistry of broilers

As shown in Table 4, compared with the CG, supplementation with AMSL in the feed led to an increase in the spleen index (P < 0.05), with increases of 17.5% (EG1), 21.7% (EG2), and 20.0% (EG3). No significant differences in the thymus index were observed among the groups (P > 0.05). In addition, compared with control chickens, chickens fed AMSL had significantly increased total serum protein level (P < 0.05) and significantly decreased serum AST activity (P < 0.05). There were no significant differences in the levels of albumin, triglyceride, total cholesterol, ALT or creatinine among the different groups (P > 0.05).

Effects of AMSL on the antioxidant and immune status of broilers

As shown in Table 5, compared with the CG, the groups supplemented with 3% (EG2) and 5% (EG3) AMSL presented significantly higher serum activity of SOD (P < 0.05), and the GSH-Px activity and T-AOC level were also enhanced by addition of 5% AMSL (P < 0.05). However, compared with the CG, supplementation with AMSL did not significantly alter serum MDA levels (P > 0.05), although the experimental groups presented numerically lower values. With respect to serum immune indices, significant increases in serum IgG and IgM levels were observed in the EG2 and EG3 groups compared with those in the control group (P < 0.05). Enhanced IgA secretion was observed in broilers treated with AMSL at all levels compared with that in the control group (P < 0.05).

Effects of AMSL on serum metabolites

Considering the results of growth performance, serum antioxidant and immune indices collectively, it can be concluded that the 3% AMSL dosage had better effects. Therefore, subsequent studies were primarily conducted between the CG and the EG2. We identified 391 metabolites for further statistical analysis, with 208 metabolites in positive ion mode and 183 metabolites in negative ion mode. OPLS–DA revealed a significant separation of serum metabolites between the CG and EG2 group in both positive (Fig. 1A) and negative ion modes (Fig. 1B). The R2Y(cum) and Q2(cum) values in positive ion mode were 0.990 and 0.592, respectively, and those in negative ion mode were 0.997 and 0.650, respectively, all exceeding 0.5, indicating that this OPLS–DA model fit the data very well and had good predictive ability. Validation of the OPLS–DA model was performed through 200 permutation tests; as shown in Fig. 1C (positive-ion mode) and Fig. 1D (negative-ion mode), the permuted R2 and Q2 values on the left were lower than the corresponding original points on the right, and the Q2 regression lines had negative intercepts on the y-axis, suggesting that the OPLS–DA model was not random and did not overfit.

Ten differential metabolites in positive ion mode (Table S1) and 6 differential metabolites in negative ion mode (Table S2) were subsequently identified on the basis of a VIP > 1 and BH-adjusted P < 0.05 for OPLS-DA. Hierarchical clustering analyses were performed on these significantly differential metabolites, and heatmaps were generated, as shown in Fig. 1E (positive-ion mode) and Fig. 1F (negative-ion mode). Compared with those in the CG, 5 metabolites were upregulated, and 5 were downregulated in the EG2 group in positive ion mode, whereas 3 metabolites were upregulated, and 3 were downregulated in negative ion mode. KEGG pathway enrichment analysis was then performed on these combined 16 differential metabolites, and potential enrichment in pathways such as purine metabolism, glycerophospholipid metabolism, and nitrogen metabolism were observed (P < 0.05) (Fig. 2). However, none of the pathways achieved statistical significance with BH-adjusted P value below 0.05 (Table S3). This may be attributed to the low number of differential metabolites in this study and the limited annotations available for them.

Effects of AMSL on the composition of the gut microbiota

Broilers fed 3% AMSL showed better growth performance, antioxidant capacity and immune function. Therefore, the effect of the EG2 group on the intestinal flora was further investigated in this study. A total of 2,191,260 reads were obtained from all cecal content samples. After quality filtering, 2,177,200 reads remained, with an average of 108,860 reads per sample. A total of 1575 bacterial OTUs were identified in the cecal samples; the number of shared OTUs between the CG and EG2 group was 867, and the numbers of OTUs specific to the CG and EG2 were 479 and 229, respectively. To determine the richness and diversity of the gut microbiota, we assessed alpha diversity using the Chao1 and Shannon indices. As shown in Fig. 3A, compared with the CG, 3% AMSL intervention had no significant effect on the total number of species (Chao1 index, P > 0.05) but significantly decreased the species diversity (Shannon index, P < 0.05) of the cecal microbiota (Fig. 3B), suggesting that the addition of 3% AMSL to the diet may increase the proportion of dominant species and decrease the distribution of other species. In addition, PCoA and NMDS analyses were performed to assess beta diversity. PCoA revealed that the CG and EG2 group formed separate clusters with some overlap (Fig. 3C), and there were significant differences in microbial community structure between the two groups, as supported by Anosim analysis (R = 0.309, P = 0.001). The results of the NMDS analysis verified these results, with a stress value of 0.168 (less than 0.20, Fig. 3D).

To further understand the influence of 3% AMSL on the cecal microbial composition, the relative abundance of the gut microbiota at the phylum and genus levels was characterized. The 10 most abundant phyla and genera are shown in Fig. 3E and Fig. 3F, respectively, with the remaining bacteria grouped as “Others”. At the phylum level, Firmicutes, Proteobacteria and Bacteroidetes were the predominant phyla, collectively constituting more than 90% of the entire phylum level. At the genus level, there were many unclassified taxa in the CG, and the remaining gut microorganisms in this group were mainly Lactobacillus, Pseudomonas, Bacteroides, SMB53, and Escherichia. However, Lactobacillus was the most dominant genus in the EG2 group, followed by Candidatus Arthromitus, Escherichia, and Bacteroides. Biomarkers that were significantly different between the CG and EG2 group were identified by LEfSe (LDA > 4, P < 0.05). Cladograms of LEfSe revealed that Firmicutes, Bacilli, Lactobacillales, Lactobacillaceae and Lactobacillus were significantly enriched in the EG2 group, while Bacteroidetes, Bacteroidia, Bacteroidales, Bacteroidaceae, Bacteroides, Proteobacteria, Gammaproteobacteria, Pseudomonadales, Pseudomonadaceae, Pseudomonas, Clostridia, Clostridiales, Ruminococcaceae, Lachnospiraceae and Ruminococcus were significantly enriched in the CG (Fig. 4A). The bacterial characteristics of each group at all levels are shown in Fig. 4B. The differentially abundant taxa identifed by LEfse analysis were also statistically significant at BH-adjusted P < 0.05 (Table S4). These results indicate that the use of 3% AMSL may positively affect the microbial communities in broilers.

Correlation analysis for the microbiota and metabolites

To determine the interdependent relationship between the gut microbiota composition and serum endogenous metabolites, Spearman’s correlation analysis was conducted based on the intensity of differential metabolites and the microbial composition at the genus level (top 10). As shown in Fig. 5, the enrichment of Lactobacillus in broilers after AMSL intervention was positively associated with upregulated DL-lactate, Gly-Arg, Arg-Glu, uric acid, Phe-Tyr, adenine, and l-Glutamate, but negatively related to downregulated glycerophosphocholine, sphingosine-1-phosphate, 1-oleoyl-l-α-lysophosphatidic acid, 1-palmitoylglycerol, uracil, 1-myristoyl-sn‑glycero-3-phosphocholine, 1-palmitoyllysophosphatidylcholine, and 1-stearoyl-2‑hydroxy-sn‑glycero-3-phosphocholine.

Spearman correlation analysis of serum differential metabolites and the top 10 cecal microbiota at the genus level. The “*” means P < 0.05, “**” means P < 0.01, and “***” means P < 0.001.

However, the marked microorganisms in the CG, including the genera Bacteroides, Ruminococcus, and Pseudomonas, exhibited completely different metabolite correlations with the Lactobacillus genus. Specifically, the genera Bacteroides and Ruminococcus were positively associated with sphingosine-1-phosphate but negatively associated with DL-lactate, Gly-Arg, and Arg-Glu. There were positive correlations between the Pseudomonas genus and 1-oleoyl-l-α-lysophosphatidic acid, glycerophosphocholine, and 1-stearoyl-2‑hydroxy-sn‑glycero-3-phosphocholine, whereas the increased abundance of Pseudomonas in the CG caused decreases in levels of DL-lactate, uric acid, Phe-Tyr, hypoxanthine, adenine, and l-Glutamate. With respect to the other genera, SM53, Turicibacter, Escherichia, and Candidatus Arthromitus showed no correlations with the differential metabolites in serum, and the correlations between Megamonas and the differential metabolites showed a partial overlap with those of the Ruminococcus genus. Interestingly, the genera not assigned to specific taxa (unclassified) appeared to present a composite profile of correlations from other bacteria, excluding Lactobacillus. In general, the association pattern between Lactobacillus and differential metabolites was entirely distinct from that of other microbial genera and even exhibited opposing correlation trends.

Discussion

The plant Astragalus membranaceus is a highly valuable medicinal herb resource in China, with its root ranking among the top ten most frequently used traditional Chinese medicinal materials annually (Zhang et al., 2024). Consequently, substantial amounts of AMSL are generated as byproducts, making its utilization a pressing issue for advancing green agriculture and sustainable industrial development. Although preliminary studies have demonstrated the beneficial effects of AMSL in broiler chicken farming (Xi et al., 2014; Xue and Bai, 2014), it remains unclear whether AMSL exert influences on host metabolism and gut microflora. To address this gap, our study comprehensively evaluated the regulatory effects of AMSL on immunity, metabolism, and intestinal microbiota in broilers, aiming to provide a scientific basis for the development of AMSL as a functional feed or feed additive in poultry production.

Our results revealed that, compared with the CG, dietary supplementation with 5% AMSL markedly decreased the ADG and increased the FCR from 22 to 49 days of age and throughout the entire experimental period. In contrast, lower inclusion levels of AMSL (1% and 3%) had no significant negative effects on broiler growth performance. Our results are consistent with those of the study of Che et al. (2019), who reported that the addition of 5% and 7.5% AMSL to pig feed significantly increased the ADFI and FCR compared with those of the 2.5% AMSL group and the control group; moreover, the ADG tended to decrease with increasing dietary AMSL levels from 2.5% to 7.5%, although the difference was not statistically significant. These findings indicate that moderate AMSL supplementation does not affect the growth performance of livestock or poultry, whereas excessive amounts might reduce farming efficiency. It is important to note that the experimental diets in the present study were not isocaloric or isonitrogenous. Because AMSL contain less crude protein and provide lower metabolizable energy than the basal diet, increasing inclusion of AMSL progressively diluted dietary energy and protein levels (Table S5). Consequently, the reduced growth performance observed in the 5% AMSL group may be mainly attributed to nutritional dilution. The high crude fiber content of AMSL may have further contributed to reduced nutrient digestibility (Ding et al., 2021). Future studies using isonitrogenous and isocaloric diets are required to clarify the specific effects of AMSL on animal growth independent of nutritional factors. Moreover, a study from Han et al. (2024) indicates that the addition of 2% unfermented Astragalus Radix powder to the diet of broilers had no effect on ADG, ADFI, or FCR, whereas the addition of the same amount of fermented Astragalus Radix powder increased ADG and reduced FCR. This effect may be associated with the decomposition of crude fiber and the transformation of bioactive substances in Astragalus Radix induced by fermentation (Li et al., 2025). These findings suggest that fermenting AMSL seems to be a promising approach to utilize AMSL, since in this way, the optimal balance between enhancing production efficiency and delivering functional benefits could be achieved.

Serum biochemical parameters are useful for assessing the functional status of an organism. Among them, the total serum protein concentration is indicative of nutritional status and protein metabolism (Tothova et al., 2019), and elevated serum protein levels indicate better liver function and nutrient utilization in poultry (Mahfuz et al., 2019). This study revealed that diluting broiler diets with 1–5% AMSL significantly increased total serum protein levels compared with those in the control group, demonstrating that appropriate AMSL supplementation improves protein utilization in broilers. These findings are consistent with the study by John (2024), who reported that Dracaena mannii leaf meal at an inclusion concentration of 400 g/kg in broiler diets can increase the total serum protein concentration. In this study, dietary supplementation with AMSL did not significantly affect the blood levels of albumin, total cholesterol, triglycerides, ALT or creatinine in broilers. Creatinine is a waste product of protein metabolism excreted by the kidneys, and its level can serve as an indicator of renal function (Wasung et al., 2015). Our findings indicate that AMSL at inclusion levels up to 5% in the diet did not induce significant nephrotoxicity in broilers. Furthermore, serum AST levels were significantly lower in broilers fed 1%, 3%, and 5% AMSL than in those in the CG, suggesting that dietary AMSL at levels less than 5% does not cause hepatocellular damage. In fact, the protective effects of Astragalus saponins and polysaccharides on alcohol- and chemical-induced liver injury have been revealed (Zhou et al., 2021; Xiao et al., 2025), and we can speculate that AMSL may have such effects because the active components in AMSL are similar to those in its roots, including polysaccharides and saponins.

Intensive feeding can easily induce oxidative stress in broilers, which in severe cases may disrupt normal metabolism and growth and even lead to disease (Peng et al., 2023). Endogenous enzymes such as SOD and GSH-Px play crucial roles in maintaining the body’s redox balance and protecting cells from oxidative damage. This study revealed that supplementation with 3% and 5% AMSL increased serum SOD activity, while addition of 5% AMSL enhanced GSH-Px activity and T-AOC level as well, suggesting the potential antioxidant effects of AMSL in broilers. This is consistent with the findings reported by Zhang et al. (2024), who demonstrated that the inclusion of ginseng stems and leaves at doses of up to 5% in broiler diets could significantly increase serum SOD activity and T-AOC levels, whereas the addition of ginseng stems and leaves at doses of 1.25% and 2.5% did not have such effects. In recent years, the antioxidant effects of Astragalus membranaceus have been increasingly elucidated through both in vitro and in vivo studies (Yao et al., 2024), and flavonoids and phenolic acids may be the primary contributors to this function (Dai et al., 2024). The observed increase in antioxidant capacity in broilers following AMSL supplementation is likely associated with its high flavonoid content, which is 14.77 mg/g (Yang et al., 2025). Flavonoids are recognized natural antioxidants that function through dual mechanisms, including direct scavenging of reactive oxygen species and serving as enzymatic modulators to activate antioxidant defenses, thus enhancing the overall antioxidant function of the animal (Dias et al., 2021). Furthermore, MDA serves as an indicator of lipid peroxidation and a crucial biomarker of cellular oxidative stress damage. In this study, despite numerically lower serum MDA concentrations in the AMSL treatment group than in the control group, the difference was not significant. Collectively, these results indicate that AMSL can increase the antioxidant defense capacity in broilers, with an appropriate dosage being 3% or higher.

The thymus and spleen are important immune organs in birds with inherent connections. The thymus is responsible for the proliferation of T lymphocytes, and once mature, these lymphocytes are transported to the spleen, whereas the latter generates mature B lymphocytes with a principal function in antibody production (Song et al., 2021). The development of immune organs and the levels of immunoglobulins can reflect the immune status of an animal. Typically, an elevated immune organ index and increased secretion of immunoglobulins indicate improved immunity (Li et al., 2022). In this study, compared with the control group, dietary supplementation with AMSL promoted the development of the spleen and increased the secretion of serum IgG, IgA and IgM in broilers. However, serum IgG and IgM levels exhibited significant increases only when the AMSL supplementation dose was no less than 3%, indicating that an appropriate amount of AMSL in the diet can enhance the immune function of broilers. This finding is consistent with the study of Alagawany et al. (2023), who reported that dietary supplementation with Astragalus membranaceus powder at concentrations of 1–5 g/kg linearly affected immunological indices (IgG and IgM) in quail breeders. Another study revealed that after treatment with Astragalus polysaccharides, the thymus and spleen of mice clearly improved compared with those in the model group (Zhao et al., 2019). However, in this study, only a nonsignificant increase in the thymus index was observed, suggesting that the components contained in AMSL may have a more pronounced effect on humoral immunity than on cellular immunity.

In this study, 8 upregulated and 8 downregulated differential metabolites were identified after the AMSL intervention at the dose of 3%. The top 5 upregulated differential metabolites according to VIP values were DL-lactate, hypoxanthine, Arg-Glu, Phe-Tyr, and uric acid, which are related to purine metabolism, glycolysis and amino acid metabolism; whereas the top 5 downregulated differential metabolites were 1-stearoyl-2‑hydroxy-sn‑glycero-3-phosphocholine, glycerophosphocholine, 1-Oleoyl-l-α-lysophosphatidic, uracil, and 1-Myristoyl-sn‑glycero-3-phosphocholine, which are involved mainly in lipid metabolism and pyrimidine metabolism. The KEGG pathway analysis of differential metabolites showed that no significant enrichment was obtained at FDR < 0.05; however, three pathways including purine metabolism, glycerophospholipid metabolism, and nitrogen metabolism were significant at P < 0.05. Our results are consistent with those of Gui et al. (2024), who demonstrated that Astragali radix could modulate the human immunity by modifying the metabolism of amino acids, lipids, and purines. A study of the effects of dietary Astragalus polysaccharide supplementation on broiler chickens challenged with necrotic enteritis also supports our results (Song et al., 2022).

Our study revealed that cecal microbiota composition significantly changed in broilers fed diets containing 3% AMSL compared with that in those fed basal diets, and the EG2 group markedly enriched the genus Lactobacillus, whereas the CG enriched the genera Bacteorides, Ruminococcus, and Pseudomonas. It seems that supplementation with 3% AMSL in broiler diets promotes the growth of Lactobacillus, and these dominant bacteria may subsequently inhibit the growth of undesirable microorganisms in the gastrointestinal tract of chickens. This may be the reason why the Shannon index in the EG2 group decreased compared with that in the CG in our study, suggesting that the low microbiota diversity was induced by the high abundance of Lactobacillus species in the EG2 group. Lactobacillus species are widely believed to be beneficial for both humans and animals, and increased abundance of these bacteria is associated with the health of broilers (Dempsey and Corr, 2022). For example, the Lactobacillus salivarius and Lactobacillus reuteri were reported to increase the body weight gain of chickens and reduce the number of E. coli and total aerobic bacteria in the cecum of chickens (Tsega et al., 2024). Another study revealed that Lactobacillus was negatively associated with Bacteroides in the cecal microbial community (Fan et al., 2023). Our study revealed similar results, with an increase in Lactobacillus abundance in the EG2 group and a reduction in Bacteroides abundance. Bacteroides is commonly considered beneficial for the host since it can utilize complex carbohydrates to generate different types of short-chain fatty acids; however, some species of the Bacteroides genus have been reported to have pathogenic potential (Wexler and Goodman, 2017; Chung et al., 2018). Moreover, Bacteroides has been reported to be negatively correlated with immunoglobulins and positively correlated with inflammatory cytokines in broilers (Wu et al., 2021). The beneficial effects of AMSL on the gut microbiota may occur at the expense of some intestinal bacteria, including Bacteroides. A study by Liu et al. (2021) also supports our results, which revealed that broilers consuming γ-irradiated Astragalus polysaccharides had a lower abundance of Bacteroides. Ruminococcus is important for degrading cellulose; however, the role of several Ruminococcus species in health and disease is still unclear, and even some Ruminococcus species are clearly linked to specific diseases (Valentino et al., 2024). Pseudomonas is ubiquitous bacteria that might be related to a wide range of infections and affect intestinal health (Zhang et al., 2024), while the addition of 3% AMSL significantly decreased the relative abundance of Pseudomonas, regulating the structure of the intestinal flora. These results suggest that 3% AMSL supplementation may be beneficial for modulating the intestinal microbiota of broilers.

Correlation analysis between differential metabolites and the top 10 cecal microbiota at the genus level revealed that the correlation profile of Lactobacillus was entirely distinct from that of other bacteria. Specifically, the enriched Lactobacillus genus in EG2 was positively related to metabolites associated with amino acid, sugar, and purine metabolism but negatively related to certain metabolites involved in lipid metabolism, indicating that 3% AMSL may promote the absorption and utilization of proteins and affect the metabolism of purine and lipids. This may explain the increase in total serum protein levels and improvement in immune status observed in the broilers after the AMSL intervention in this study. In contrast, Bacteroides and Ruminococcus, which were significantly enriched in the CG, downregulated those metabolites associated with amino acid and sugar metabolism. Pseudomonas predominantly upregulated lipid metabolism-related metabolites and downregulated amino acid, purine, and sugar metabolisms-related metabolites. These findings indicate that Lactobacillus plays a unique endogenous metabolic regulatory role in the guts of broilers, whereas other bacterial genera demonstrate a certain degree of synergistic metabolic regulation. This distinction may further elucidate the action of AMSL on gut microflora of broilers. Notably, the correlation patterns of Lactobacillus with four metabolites (DL-lactate, Arg-Glu, Gly-Arg, and sphingosine-1-phosphate) were opposite to those of Bacteroides and Ruminococcus. Specifically, Lactobacillus abundance was positively correlated with DL-lactate, Arg-Glu, and Gly-Arg, while negatively correlated with sphingosine-1-phosphate, whereas Bacteroides and Ruminococcus showed opposite correlations with these metabolites.

Increased serum concentrations of dipeptides such as Arg-Glu and Gly-Arg may indicate increased intake, breakdown, and absorption of proteins, suggesting positive effects of 3% AMSL supplementation in broilers. Lactate is the fermentation product of lactate bacteria in the intestinal tract, and l- and d- are two configurations of lactate. l-lactate has been reported to suppress colitis in mice by promoting epithelial cell migration (Yu et al., 2021), and d-lactate has been shown to enhance mouse immunity in a dose-dependent manner (Yan et al., 2022). Thus, we speculate that the positive correlation between DL-lactate levels and Lactobacillus abundance may be helpful for maintaining gut health and enhancing broiler immunity, whereas the negative correlations between lactate levels and Bacteroides and Ruminococcus abundance may affect the stability of the intestinal tract. Sphingosine-1-phosphate is a signaling lipid involved in numerous cellular events, including proliferation, differentiation, apoptosis and inflammation (Mihanfar et al., 2019). Serum sphingosine-1-phosphate has been reported to be positively correlated with the severity of coronary artery stenosis (Deutschman et al., 2003). In brief, we demonstrated that 3% AMSL intervention modulated metabolism in broilers, and there were associations between serum differential metabolites and the gut microbiota. However, the gut microbiota and metabolomic analyses were conducted only between the control group and the 3% AMSL group, which constitutes a limitation of the present study. Future work incorporating more dose groups will be essential to confirm the biological effects of AMSL.

Conclusion

The supplementation of moderate amounts of AMSL in the diet promoted spleen development and enhanced the serum antioxidant and immune status but had no negative effect on the growth performance of the broilers. Taken together, 3% provided the best overall balance between health related indices and growth performance under the conditions of this study. Subsequent 16S rRNA sequencing analysis revealed that 3% AMSL could modulate the cecal microbiota composition in broilers, significantly promoting the growth and enrichment of the Lactobacillus genus while inhibiting the growth of Pseudomonas, Ruminococcus, and Bacteroides. Furthermore, 3% AMSL significantly regulated the serum levels of endogenous metabolites involved in purine, amino acid and lipid metabolism. This study evaluates the application potential of AMSL in broiler farming, providing a scientific basis for its use as a functional feed or feed additive in poultry farming and thus promoting the in-depth development and high-value utilization of AMSL.
