Comparison of antigen-specific B cell responses reveals disparity in immunogenicity and memory B cell formation across COVID-19 vaccine platforms
Authors: Chang Guo, Xin Chai, Maidaiti Baerlike, Yingping Liu, Yao Wang, Fei Shao, Qingrui Huang, Weiguo Zhang, Shan Cen, Yijie Dong, Yunlong Cao, Jinghua Yan, Xuyu Zhou, Zhaolin Hua, Baidong Hou
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Various vaccine technologies have been employed in the coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) vaccines, including whole inactivated virus (WIV), recombinant protein, mRNA, and nanoparticle vaccines. To elucidate the cellular mechanisms underlying the immune responses elicited by different vaccines, we examined and compared antigen-specific B cell responses targeting the receptor-binding domain (RBD) of the viral spike protein. We found that the nanoparticle vaccine pathogen-like antigens-RBD (PLA-RBD) and the mRNA vaccine demonstrated superior immunogenicity compared with the WIV vaccine and the RBD-dimer, a recombinant protein vaccine. Interestingly, the WIV vaccine contains toll-like receptor ligands that enhance IgG2a/c class-switching. For the mRNA vaccine, although it induces robust germinal center responses and T follicular helper (Tfh) cells, it has limited ability to induce memory B cells and long-lived plasma cells. These results indicate that vaccine formats significantly influence both the quantity and quality of immune responses, providing valuable insights for the future development of vaccines.

INTRODUCTION

The unprecedentedly rapid deployment of the coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) vaccines, targeting the severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2) viruses that emerged in 2019, represents a remarkable accomplishment in human history. Various technology platforms, including inactivated virus, recombinant protein, mRNA, and viral vector, were employed in the vaccine development [[1], [2], [3], [4]]. The most advanced candidates underwent phase III clinical trials for each category, demonstrating vaccine efficacy ranging from 80% to over 90% [[5], [6], [7]]. Notably, this is the first time in vaccine history that such diverse formats have been utilized to combat a single disease.

Although ultimately evaluated by their efficacy of protection, different vaccine formats elicit immune responses presumably through different pathways and engage various cellular components to establish immune protection [8,9]. This could potentially lead to significant variations in the levels of antibodies induced, the duration of immunity, as well as the accompanying side effects. Therefore, a comprehensive understanding of the immune responses induced by different types of vaccines is necessary when considering the choice of vaccine formats.

For COVID-19, it has been shown that neutralizing antibody levels correlate well with immune protection, suggesting that humoral immunity plays a major role in defense against the SARS-CoV-2 viruses [[10], [11], [12]]. Since germinal center (GC) responses induced upon viral infections or vaccinations are essential for the generation of high-affinity anti-viral antibodies, as well as long-lived plasma cells and memory B cells, the key components of humoral immune memory, a direct examination of antigen-specific GC responses will provide additional valuable information beyond antibodies alone for assessing vaccine-induced humoral responses [[13], [14], [15], [16]]. Given the common antigenic target among most COVID-19 vaccines, namely the spike protein or its receptor-binding domain (RBD) [1,2], assessing RBD-specific GC responses elicited by different vaccination strategies will allow for a direct comparison of the immunogenicity disparities related to the vaccine formats.

In this study, we evaluated the humoral responses elicited by four vaccine types: whole inactivated virus vaccine (WIV), recombinant protein vaccine, mRNA vaccine, and nanoparticle vaccine. While the first three vaccine types have been widely utilized during the COVID-19 pandemic [17,18], the nanoparticle vaccine showed promising preclinical results but lacked extensive clinical evaluation [[19], [20], [21], [22], [23], [24], [25]]. Due to the distinct immunization protocols associated with different vaccine formats, a simultaneous comparison of all vaccines proved impractical. Thus, we conducted pairwise comparisons by testing each of the three established vaccines individually, using the nanoparticle vaccine as a concurrent comparator. The nanoparticle vaccine used in this study, pathogen-like antigens-RBD (PLA-RBD), was constructed by covalently linking recombinant RBD proteins to a bacterial phage backbone [21,22]. Each nanoparticle carried 20 to 25 copies of RBD. Since the phage capsid can encapsulate the host single-stranded RNA (ssRNA) during assembly, and these ssRNAs can serve as natural toll-like receptor 7 (TLR7) stimulants [[26], [27], [28], [29], [30], [31], [32]], no additional adjuvants are required when administering PLA-RBD.

Distinctive characteristics were observed for each vaccine type. As expected, the recombinant protein vaccine exhibited the lowest immunogenicity. In contrast, the PLA-RBD nanoparticle and mRNA vaccines showed strong and equivalent immunogenicity, as demonstrated by the robust GC responses they invoked. However, the mRNA vaccine induced significantly fewer memory B cells compared with the PLA-RBD vaccine, suggesting an imbalanced output of memory B cells from the mRNA vaccine-induced GC response. The WIV vaccine mimicked the PLA-RBD vaccine in retaining some TLR stimulant activities, which contributed to the IgG2a/c class switch in mice. Despite this, the WIV vaccine lacked the full immunogenicity exhibited by the PLA-RBD nanoparticle vaccine, indicating fundamental differences between the two vaccine types. In summary, vaccine formats significantly influence both the quantity and the quality of immune responses. Our results will be of interest to the future development of other vaccines.

RESULTS

The WIV-based COVID-19 Vaccine Induces Short-lived GC Responses and Generates Limited Memory B Cells

The long-standing history of WIV as a safe and effective vaccine format contributed to its selection as a primary candidate for COVID-19 vaccine development, leading to the creation of vaccines such as CoronaVac and BBIBP-CorV in China, and BBV152 in India [17,[33], [34], [35]]. Clinical trials of WIV vaccines demonstrated high seroconversion rates of neutralizing antibodies following a two-dose immunization regimen. Additionally, these trials showed around 80% efficacy against symptomatic infection within 2–3 months post-vaccination [[36], [37], [38], [39], [40], [41]]. However, the antibody titers induced by WIV vaccines were relatively moderate compared with other vaccine formats, such as mRNA-based vaccines [[42], [43], [44], [45], [46], [47], [48], [49], [50]]. More importantly, the induced antibody levels decayed rapidly, declining to near-threshold levels within 6 months post-vaccination [51,52]. Antibody decline seems to be a common issue with most COVID-19 vaccines, including mRNA-based vaccines [[53], [54], [55]], necessitating booster immunizations to maintain effective herd immunity.

To explore the cellular basis underlying the immunogenicity and memory duration of WIV vaccine-induced immunity, mice were immunized intraperitoneally (i.p.) with CoronaVac and then examined for both serum antibody levels and antigen-specific B cell responses, with PLA-RBD used for parallel comparison (Figure 1A). The WIV vaccine induced lower levels of anti-RBD antibodies after the first immunization, but elicited similar levels of PLA-RBD after the second immunization (Figure 1B). Furthermore, the anti-RBD levels remained stable for at least 8 weeks after the second immunization for both WIV and PLA-RBD vaccines (Figure 1B). Regarding the RBD-specific B cell response, the WIV vaccine induced a moderate RBD-specific GC response at 2 weeks post-first immunization compared with PLA-RBD, but this response decayed rapidly (Figure 1C-1D). After a second injection, WIV boosted RBD-specific GC B cell numbers to levels comparable with those induced by PLA-RBD; however, these levels again decreased by more than 90% within 2 weeks (Figure 1D). In contrast, the GC reaction induced by PLA-RBD was much more prolonged, and a second injection did not further increase the GC B cell numbers (Figure 1D). Reflecting the GC response, the WIV vaccine also induced substantially fewer RBD-specific Ig-switched (swIg) memory B cells than PLA-RBD (Figure 1E). The WIV-based COVID-19 Vaccine Depends on B Cell MyD88 for Class-switching to IgG2a/c

Inactivated vaccines generally exhibit lower immunogenicity compared with live virus-based vaccines, necessitating the use of adjuvants [56]. Both CoronaVac and BBIBP-CorV WIV vaccines contain aluminum hydroxide (alum) [34,57], while BBV152 includes an additional TLR7/8 agonist to enhance the T helper cell (Th)1-skewed response [33]. Since alum alone often induces a Th2-oriented response in mice [58], we investigated whether the WIV vaccine formulated with alum alone in this study would induce higher levels of IgG1 compared with IgG2a/c antibodies (Figure S1A). Surprisingly, the WIV vaccine induced robust levels of anti-RBD IgG2a/c as PLA-RBD with no significant differences in the IgG isotypes induced by WIV and PLA-RBD vaccines (Figure S1B). Further examination of the Ig isotypes in RBD-specific GC B cells induced by the WIV vaccine revealed a higher proportion of IgM+ GC B cells compared with PLA-RBD immunization, but a significantly higher proportion of IgG2a/c+ than IgG1+ GC B cells (Figure S1C–S1D). Interestingly, similar to mice, CoronaVac predominantly induces IgG1, which is the equivalent isoform of mouse IgG2a/c, in humans [59]. Thus, despite being adjuvanted with alum alone, the WIV vaccine used in this study primarily induces a Th1-skewed antibody response. It is noteworthy that although specific Ig isotypes often correlate with specialized Th-cell subsets, the induction of particular Th-cell subsets needs to be assessed independently.

Previous studies have demonstrated that virus-like particles containing nucleic acids can engage B cell TLR signaling pathways to promote antibody responses, including GC responses and class-switching to IgG2a/c [[28], [29], [30],60,61]. While PLA-RBD is designed to mimic such virus-like particles, WIV vaccines are derived from natural viral particles and retain particle features post–beta-propiolactone (BPL) inactivation. To investigate whether B cell TLR signaling is involved in the WIV and PLA-RBD-induced immune responses, we immunized mice deficient in MyD88 in B cells (Myd88fl/fl Cd79a-Cre, BMKO) and their littermates with WIV and PLA-RBD (Figures 2A and S2A).

For PLA-RBD immunization, BMKO mice exhibited significant decreases in anti-RBD antibodies (Figure S2B), as well as RBD-specific GC B cells and swIg memory B cells (Figure S2C–S2D). Additionally, class-switching to IgG2a/c was significantly impaired in the BMKO mice (Figure S2E–S2F). Together, these results confirmed that PLA-RBD relies on B cell TLR signaling to elicit the immune response.

In contrast, WIV-induced total anti-RBD antibodies, RBD-specific GC B cells, and swIg memory B cells (Figure 2B-2D) were not affected in the BMKO mice. However, class-switching to IgG2a/c was significantly reduced, as indicated by both decreased RBD-specific IgG2a/c antibodies (Figure 2B) and IgG2a/c+ GC B cells (Figure 2E-2F). Moreover, it has been shown that IgG2b and IgG2a/c induced by inactivated SARS-CoV-2 virus are significantly reduced in TLR7-deficient mice [62]. All clinically approved COVID-19 WIV vaccines are produced by BPL inactivation [[33], [34], [35],41]. BPL reacts with nucleophilic reagents, including nucleic acids and proteins, and has long been used to inactivate various viruses [[63], [64], [65]]. However, it is typically undetermined whether the process inactivates viral proteins, viral genomes, or both, as inactivation efficacy is evaluated by the cytopathic effect. Thus, nucleic acids could potentially escape inactivation even if the virus particles lose infectivity. The dependence of the WIV vaccine on B cell MyD88 for class-switching suggests that at least some nucleic acids were not completely inactivated.

Overall, despite the WIV vaccine resembles PLA-RBD in terms of particle form, viral antigen display, and encapsulated nucleic acids, it induces significantly lower levels of antibodies and GC responses compared with PLA-RBD and largely does not rely on B cell TLR signaling. Various factors, including antigen denaturation due to BPL inactivation, might contribute to the reduced immunogenicity of WIV vaccines [66,67].

The Recombinant RBD-dimer Vaccine Requires Repeated Immunizations to Achieve an Adequate Response

In contrast to conventional vaccine technologies reliant on viral cultures, the utilization of heterologously expressed recombinant viral proteins is increasingly prevalent in vaccine development. These vaccines typically target specific viral coat proteins, aiming to elicit neutralizing antibodies [1,68]. To enhance the immunogenicity of recombinant proteins, dimerization or trimerization of target subunits is often employed [[69], [70], [71]]. For COVID-19, several trimer-stabilized S-protein-based vaccines, formulated with various adjuvants, have undergone clinical trials, demonstrating efficacy ranging from 70% to 90% [72,73]. Additionally, a dimeric form of RBD, consisting of a tandem repeat of two identical RBD subunits within a single chain, has shown significantly enhanced immunogenicity compared with monomeric RBD [70]. The commercialized version of this vaccine, ZF2001, formulated with alum, has demonstrated approximately 80% protective efficacy in clinical trials [74,75].

To assess the immunogenicity of protein subunit-based vaccines, mice were i.p. immunized with the RBD-dimer [70], and examined for both serum antibody levels and antigen-specific B cell responses in spleen and mesenteric lymph nodes (mLNs), with PLA-RBD used for parallel comparison (Figures 3A and S3A). Following the first immunization, the RBD-dimer induced minimal levels of anti-RBD IgG compared with PLA-RBD. However, there was a substantial increase approaching PLA-RBD levels after the second immunization, which remained stable for two months thereafter (Figure 3B). In line with the antibody response, a single dose of RBD-dimer immunization induced almost undetectable levels of RBD-specific GC B cells in both spleen and mLNs, but secondary immunization markedly boosted the GC responses in both organs (Figures 3C and S3B). In contrast, a single immunization with PLA-RBD was sufficient to induce a robust GC response in both spleen and mLNs (Figures 3C and S3B). The peak GC response induced by the RBD-dimer occurred one week after the second immunization and declined to baseline level eight weeks thereafter (Figures 3E–3F and S3C–S3D). Local Immunization of the PLA-RBD Vaccine Induces GC Responses in Both Draining LNs and Spleen

Although i.p. immunization is commonly used in mice, intramuscular (i.m.) injection is the most common vaccination route in humans. To investigate whether i.m. immunization generates a similar level of immune response as i.p., RBD-specific antibodies were examined in mice i.m. injected with RBD-dimer or PLA-RBD (Figure 4A). A single dose of i.m RBD-dimer elicited very low levels of anti-RBD antibodies. The antibody levels significantly increased after the second i.m. immunization but remained much lower than those induced by PLA-RBD (Figure 4B). Furthermore, RBD-dimer via i.m. injection induced lower levels of anti-RBD antibodies compared with i.p. injection (105 for i.m. vs 106 for i.p.) (Figures 3B and 4B), suggesting that i.m. immunization of RBD-dimer is less efficient than i.p. In contrast, i.m PLA-RBD elicited similar levels of anti-RBD antibodies after both the first and second immunizations compared with i.p. immunization (Figures 3B and 4B). To further investigate the mechanisms behind these impacts, RBD-specific B cells were examined in both the draining LNs (inguinal) and the spleen of the RBD-dimer-immunized mice. Unlike i.p. immunization, where both the draining LNs (mesenteric) and spleen were engaged, i.m. RBD-dimer only elicited GC responses in the draining LNs (Figures 4C–4D and S4A-S4D). In contrast, i.m. PLA-RBD induced robust GC responses in both the draining LNs and the spleen (Figures 4C–4D and S4A-S4D), with the splenic GC response nearly equivalent to i.p. immunization. Consistent with the RBD-specific GC responses, i.m. RBD-dimer only induced RBD-specific swIg memory B cells in the draining LNs, whereas i.m. PLA-RBD induced memory B cells in both the draining LNs and the spleen (Figure 4E-4F).

Previous studies using other model antigens have demonstrated that local immunizations, such as subcutaneous (s.c.) injections, primarily induce responses in the draining LNs with minimal responses in remote LNs or the spleen [76]. Our results with RBD-dimer are consistent with these observations. However, PLA-RBD induced robust splenic responses in both i.m. and i.p. immunization conditions. Together, the difference in the ability to induce splenic responses between the RBD-dimer and PLA-RBD vaccines might explain the varying effects of the immunization routes on the strength of immune responses.

The mRNA Vaccine Induces a Limited Number of Memory B Cells and Long-lived Plasma Cells Despite Robust GC Responses

In addition to WIV and recombinant protein-based vaccines, mRNA-based COVID-19 vaccines were rapidly developed and have been widely used during the pandemic [18]. BNT162b2 and mRNA-1273, the two approved mRNA COVID-19 vaccines, confer over 90% efficacy up to six months post-immunization [[77], [78], [79], [80]]. The higher efficacy of mRNA vaccines compared with WIV vaccines correlates with the higher antibody titers they induce.

To evaluate the immune responses induced by mRNA vaccines, a preclinical prototype of an mRNA COVID-19 vaccine, produced similarly to BNT162b2 and mRNA-1273, was injected i.m. into mice (Figure 5A). A single dose of the mRNA vaccine elicited a strong antibody response within two weeks, comparable with that of PLA-RBD immunization (Figure 5B). Concurrently, the mRNA vaccine induced a strong RBD-specific GC response in the draining LNs (Figure 5C). Further analysis of the splenic responses revealed that i.m. mRNA vaccine elicited only limited and transient RBD-specific GC responses (Figure S6A–S6C), resulting in very few swIg memory B cells in the spleen (Figure S6D). To evaluate whether the mRNA vaccine could induce long-lived plasma cells, RBD-specific antibody-secreting cells (ASCs) in the bone marrow (BM) and spleen were examined at eight weeks post-immunization. The results showed significantly fewer ASCs in both BM and spleen induced by the mRNA vaccine compared with PLA-RBD (Figure 5G–5H). Consistently, the serum anti-RBD antibody levels were also significantly lower in the mRNA-immunized mice than in the PLA-RBD-immunized mice (Figure 5B).

Taken together, although the mRNA vaccine is potent in eliciting strong GC responses, it has limited ability to induce memory B cells and long-lived PCs, the two main components of humoral memory.

Early post-immunization, the majority of CD4+CXCR5+PD1+ cells were Tfh cells (Figure S7C), confirming that the mRNA vaccine induced a large number of Tfh cells during the initial immune response. To evaluate Tfr cell induction, we observed that although the ratio of Tfr cells increased from seven days to eight weeks post-immunization, their absolute number did not significantly change (Figure S7B). In PLA-RBD immunized mice, both the number and the ratio of Tfr cells in the CXCR5+PD1+ population remained constant throughout the observation period (Figure S7B–S7C). Thus, the mRNA vaccine induced a robust Tfh cell response without a corresponding increase in Tfr cells. Whether an overwhelming level of Tfh cells influences the generation of memory B cells is unclear and requires further investigation.

DISCUSSION

During the COVID-19 pandemic, various mechanistically distinct vaccine formats have been developed and applied clinically worldwide [2]. While different types of vaccines exhibit varying levels of protective efficacy and safety profiles, the precise mechanisms underlying these differences are not fully understood. By focusing on the immune responses targeting the same antigen, we aim to investigate how different vaccine formats affect the strength of immunogenicity as well as other characteristics of the immune response, such as memory B cell formation.

Our findings reveal significant disparities in the induction of antigen-specific GC B cells and memory B cells among the tested vaccines, despite comparable levels of antigen-specific antibodies in mice. This suggests that antibody titers alone may not fully depict the breadth of immune responses. In humans, assessing antigen-specific GC responses is challenging because these responses are confined to draining lymph nodes or the spleen, making direct comparisons difficult. Therefore, our mouse model study is crucial as it allows for a direct comparison of antigen-specific B cell responses among different vaccine formats.

Among the four vaccine formats evaluated, the nanoparticle vaccine PLA-RBD and the mRNA vaccine demonstrated superior immunogenicity. A single dose of either PLA-RBD or the mRNA vaccine was sufficient to elicit substantial antibody titers and robust GC responses in mice. In contrast, the WIV and RBD-dimer vaccines required two doses to achieve optimal GC responses. While the low immunogenicity of the RBD-dimer was expected, the reasons for the WIV vaccine’s low immunogenicity remain unclear.

Electron microscopy of WIV vaccines has confirmed their intact viral particle structures [[33], [34], [35]]. Our findings indicate that the IgG2a/c class-switching induced by the WIV vaccine is dependent on B cell MyD88, suggesting the presence of native nucleic acids in the WIV vaccine [29,62,81]. This aligns with a previous study showing that viral nucleic acids in an influenza WIV vaccine act as TLR ligands, enhancing immunogenicity [82]. Hence, contrary to the commonly held belief that all viral nucleic acids are inactivated during WIV production, our results indicate otherwise. However, this raises the question of why the WIV vaccine exhibited significantly lower immunogenicity compared with the PLA-RBD vaccine. Both WIV and PLA-RBD are particle-based multivalent antigens, with only limited differences in size (WIV: ∼100 nm, PLA-RBD: ∼30 nm) and epitope density (WIV: ∼50, PLA-RBD: ∼20 per particle) [21,[83], [84], [85]], and both contain TLR stimulants. Yet they showed marked differences in GC response induction. One possible explanation is that the inactivation process of WIV may partially denature antigens, reducing immunogenicity. Further studies are needed to understand how to retain the immunogenicity of WIV vaccines.

Among WHO-approved COVID-19 vaccines, mRNA vaccines consistently demonstrate stronger immunogenicity, as evidenced by higher induced neutralizing antibody titers, which correlate with protection efficacy [77,78]. Our mouse study supports this, showing the superior immunogenicity of mRNA vaccines over WIV and RBD-dimer vaccines. However, despite inducing a robust GC response, the mRNA vaccine did not generate as many memory B cells and long-lived plasma cells as the PLA-RBD vaccine. Interestingly, the mRNA vaccine induced significantly higher numbers of Tfh cells compared with PLA-RBD. Although Tfh cell numbers are generally positively correlated with GC B cell numbers, the memory B cells that emerge from GC B cells often exhibit lower affinity for antigen binding, likely associated with reduced signals from Tfh cells [86]. This raises the question of whether the excessive Tfh cells or other inflammatory factors induced by the mRNA vaccine might limit the generation of memory B cells. Further investigation is needed to understand the underlying mechanism and its implication for long-term immunity.

Whether our observations in mice can be directly applied to humans remains uncertain. However, there has been controversy about the longevity of mRNA-induced antibodies [53], highlighting the need for direct evidence of mRNA vaccine-induced memory B cells and long-lived plasma cells. Fine-needle aspiration of axillary LNs in humans vaccinated with the mRNA vaccine revealed robust antigen-specific GC responses [87,88], similar to what we observed in mice. Memory B cells and BM plasma cells were also identified in humans. However, evidence suggests that the quality of memory B cells induced by mRNA vaccines is inferior to those induced by natural infection [89]. It is noteworthy that a single dose of the mRNA vaccine elicits a relatively short-lived GC response in mice, as shown in this and the previous studies [90]. In humans, two doses of mRNA vaccination ensured a prolonged GC response [87,88]. Interestingly, while the antibody levels induced by the 2-dose regimen of mRNA vaccination declined rapidly, a third dose produced a relatively stable antibody response [91]. This suggests that although the mRNA vaccines may not efficiently induce memory B cells, repeated immunizations might compensate for or overcome this limitation.

In summary, our study highlights the distinct immunogenic profiles of various COVID-19 vaccines, revealing unexpected features of the WIV and mRNA vaccines. Further research is needed to elucidate how to optimize these different vaccine types.

Limitation

Clinical observations indicate that antibody decline is common among COVID-19 vaccines [51,52], irrespective of their initial immunogenicity. In our study, we found that both WIV and mRNA vaccines, the two most widely used types, generated a limited number of memory B cells in mice. Whether this observation relates to the relatively rapid decline of vaccine-induced antibodies in humans needs further investigation.

In this study, we primarily focused on comparing the immune responses of B cells. We anticipate that different vaccine formats will also significantly influence the generation of antigen-specific T-cell subsets. Theoretically, both CD4+ and CD8+ T-cell responses may contribute to vaccine efficacy, although most evidence of COVID-19 suggests that humoral immunity correlates more strongly with immune protection [[10], [11], [12]].

We measured anti-RBD antibodies rather than neutralizing antibodies. Although anti-RBD antibody levels have been shown to correlate well with neutralizing antibody levels [21,[92], [93], [94]], it remains possible that the quality of these antibodies, in terms of their ability to neutralize SARS-CoV-2 variants, may differ.

We did not include vector-based vaccines in our comparison as they are expected to be more potent in inducing CD8+ T cell responses. Several studies have suggested that vector-based vaccine-induced antibody levels are not superior to those induced by other formats [95,96].

The WIV and recombinant protein vaccines in our study used alum as an adjuvant. Other WIV and recombinant protein vaccines have included TLR stimulants as additional adjuvants [33], which might enhance their immunogenicity compared with the vaccines used in this study.

This study used PLA-RBD as a representative nanoparticle vaccine, although ferritin-based vaccines are also widely reported and used [25,[97], [98], [99], [100], [101], [102], [103], [104]]. The two differ in how adjuvants are added, and further testing is needed to determine whether this difference affects memory B cell formation or other aspects of the immune response.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Animals

All mice used in this study were housed under specific pathogen-free conditions, and their use was approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee of the Institute of Biophysics, Chinese Academy of Sciences. C57BL/6 mice were bred in-house or purchased from GemPharmatech Co., Ltd (Jiangsu, China). MyD88fl/fl Cd79a-Cre mice were previously described by Hou et al. [29]. An approximately equal number of male and female mice, aged 8–16 weeks, were used for immunization.

Vaccines and Immunization

The PLA-RBD was constructed as previously described [21]. A freeze-dried form manufactured under good manufacturing practices (GMP) conditions by Rongsen Biotechnology Ltd. (Beijing, China) was used in this study. The WIV vaccine (CoronaVac) was provided by Sinovac Biotech Ltd. (Beijing, China) and has been previously described [34]. The recombinant RBD-dimer was generated as previously described [70]. The mRNA vaccine, encoding a trimer-stabilized form of the spike protein of SARS-CoV-2 and formulated with lipid nanoparticles, was manufactured under GMP conditions and provided by RinuaGene Biotechnology Ltd. (Jiangsu, China).

For immunization, 10 μg of PLA-RBD, 1.5 μg of CoronaVac, 10 μg of RBD-dimer, or 5 μg of LNP-mRNA vaccine was used for each injection. Alum adjuvant (Cat#77161, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Massachusetts, USA) was added for RBD-dimer immunization. The immunization routes and schedules are indicated in the main text.

Enzyme-linked Immunosorbent Assay

RBD-specific antibodies were determined as previously described [21]. Serially diluted sera were incubated on plates coated with purified RBD, and horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated antibodies were used for detection. These included anti-mouse IgG (H+L) (Bethyl Laboratories, Texas, USA), anti-mouse IgA (Bethyl Laboratories, Texas, USA), anti-mouse IgG1, anti-mouse IgG2b, anti-mouse IgG2a/c, and anti-mouse IgG3 (Southern Biotech, Alabama, USA). The HRP substrate 3,3′,5,5′-tetramethylbenzidine (Sigma–Aldrich, Missouri, USA) was used, and the optical density at 450 nm was measured using a microplate reader (Multiskan FC, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Massachusetts, USA). Data were analyzed with GraphPad Prism. Antibody titers were determined as the reciprocal of the highest dilution that gave an optical density value above ten times the standard deviation value measured from the serum-free wells.

Fluorescent Labeling of RBD and Enrichment of RBD-specific B Cells

Fluorescently labeled RBD tetramer was prepared as described previously [21]. Briefly, biotinylated RBD was mixed with fluorochrome-conjugated streptavidin, either BV650 streptavidin (BioLegend, California, USA) or PE streptavidin (BioLegend, California, USA), at room temperature for 60 min to generate fluorescently labeled RBD tetramer.

Antigen-specific B cells were enriched as previously described [105,106]. Lymphocytes from mouse spleens or lymph nodes were incubated with RBD-PE and RBD-BV650, then incubated with anti-PE microbeads (Miltenyi Biotec, North Rhine-Westphalia, Germany) and isolated using a magnetic column (Miltenyi Biotec, North Rhine-Westphalia, Germany).

Flow Cytometry

Fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS) buffer was used for cell staining, and Cytofix/Cytoperm solution (BD Biosciences, New Jersey, USA) was used before intracellular staining. For B cell responses, antibodies and reagents used for flow cytometry included PE-CF594 anti-B220 (RA3-6B2), allophycocyanin-Cy7 anti-CD19 (1D3), PE-Cy7 anti-IgM (II/41), FITC anti-IgM (Jackson ImmunoResearch, Pennsylvania, USA), BV711 anti-IgD (11-26c.2a), FITC anti-GL-7 (GL7), eF450-anti-GL-7 (GL7), AF700 anti-CD38 (90), PerCP-Cy5.5 anti-CD8 (53-6.7), BV650 streptavidin (BioLegend, California, USA), PE streptavidin, Pacific Orange streptavidin (Invitrogen, Massachusetts, USA), biotin anti-IgG1 (A85-1), and biotin anti-IgG2a(b) (IgG2c) (5.7).

For T cell responses, antibodies and reagents used for flow cytometry included anti-CXCR5 Biotin (2G8), anti-PD1 FITC (RMP1-30), anti-CD44 PE (IM7), Fixable Viability Dye eFluor™ 506 (Invitrogen, Massachusetts, USA), anti-CD4 APC-Cy7 (GK1.5), and anti-Foxp3 e450 (FJK-16s).

All data were collected on an LSR II cytometer (Becton Dickinson, USA) and analyzed using FlowJo software (Tree Star, California, USA).

ELISpot Assay for Antibody-secreting Cells

To detect anti-RBD antibody-secreting cells (ASCs), splenocytes and bone marrow cells from mice immunized with mRNA or PLA-RBD were incubated in RBD protein pre-coated plates at 37 °C for 5 h. The plates were then washed and incubated with HRP-conjugated anti-mouse IgG. 3-Amino-9-ethylcarbazole (AEC) (Sigma–Aldrich, Missouri, USA) was used as the HRP substrate. Spots were scanned and counted using an ImmunoSpot analyzer (Cellular Technology Limited, Ohio, USA).

Statistical Analysis

GraphPad Prism was used to generate the data graphs and perform the statistical tests indicated in the manuscript. The Mann–Whitney test was used for statistical analysis. Results were considered statistically significant with P < 0.05 (ns: no significant difference; ∗P < 0.05; ∗∗P < 0.01; ∗∗∗P < 0.001).
