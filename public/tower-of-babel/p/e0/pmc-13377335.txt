Timing of conception after methotrexate and subsequent pregnancy outcomes: A retrospective cohort study
Authors: Bar Rosh, Joul Hadad, Naomi Gronich
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveMethotrexate is widely used to treat ectopic pregnancy (EP). Concerns about its teratogenicity have led to uncertainty regarding the optimal interval before attempting conception. Guidelines recommend a 3‐month washout period, while the manufacturer advises 6 months. Evidence on the safety of early conception is limited. We aimed to evaluate whether conception within 1–3 months after methotrexate treatment for EP is as safe as conception after longer intervals.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective cohort study of women treated with 30–150 mg methotrexate for ectopic pregnancy in Clalit Health Services hospitals (2010–2021). Subsequent pregnancies were categorized by time from treatment to conception. Outcomes included major congenital malformations, spontaneous abortion, preterm birth, and low birth weight. Adjusted odds ratios (aORs) were estimated using generalized estimating equations.ResultsThe study included 711 cases: 38 conceived within 1–3 months, 111 within 3–6 months, and 562 after >6 months. Major congenital malformations were diagnosed in 70 infants, with no significant difference between groups. Although numerically higher rates of congenital malformations associated with methotrexate embryopathy, preterm birth, and low birth weight were observed in the 1–3 months group, these differences were not statistically significant. Adjusted ORs for congenital malformations were 0.87 (95% confidence interval [CI]: 0.245–3.14) for 1–3 months and 1.01 (95% CI: 0.48–2.13) for 3–6 months, compared to >6 months.ConclusionConception 1–3 and 3–6 months after methotrexate treatment for ectopic pregnancy does not appear to increase the risk of major fetal malformations, or other unfavorable obstetric outcomes. Prospective studies in larger cohorts are warranted.Keywords: congenital malformations, ectopic pregnancy, methotrexate, pregnancy

INTRODUCTION

Ectopic pregnancy (EP) is a significant cause of morbidity and mortality. 1 The management of EP has changed over the years presenting several possible interventions depending on different factors including maternal comorbidities, serum beta‐human chorionic gonadotropin (beta‐hCG), hemodynamic stability, and signs for impending or ongoing rupture of the EP. While surgical approach is considered the gold standard treatment, methotrexate (MTX) has similar efficacy and fertility outcomes when indicated, 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 , 5 and has gained popularity in recent years.

MTX inhibits dihydrofolate reductase, thus affecting deoxynucleic acid (DNA). It is used in high dose as chemotherapy for malignant diseases, and when given in low doses, it is used for the treatment of autoimmune diseases. MTX teratogenicity is related to its folic acid antagonism which may expose the embryo to significant malformations, such as neural tube defects. 6 , 7 The theoretical rationale for a preconception washout period after MTX treatment exists despite the drug's rapid systemic clearance. While MTX is primarily cleared by the kidneys with a relatively short half‐life of 3–15 h, 8 , 9 , 10 its potential teratogenic impact in the period following treatment is theoretically linked to the long‐term sequestration of its metabolites. Laboratory‐based studies suggest that active MTX metabolites can persist in certain tissues, such as the liver, for several months. 11 , 12 It is important to note, however, that while this persistence has been observed in laboratory settings, evidence of long‐term metabolite retention in human hepatic tissue remains limited and has not been definitively established in clinical studies. Experimental studies have associated MTX exposure to embryo death in mice, rats, rabbits, and monkeys. 13 , 14 Moreover, case reports have described MTX embryopathy that included growth deficiency, and head, face, limbs, and cardiac malformations, associated with even low dose of MTX (10 mg/week). 13 , 14 , 15 , 16 There is only scarce evidence regarding the optimal safe interval between MTX treatment for EP and a following conception. The uncertainty around the recommended safe interval time imposes great challenge to clinicians in the management of women, especially in cases of unplanned pregnancies shortly after MTX treatment, as to whether a termination of pregnancy should be recommended. Moreover, this uncertainty might expose women, who seek to conceive soon after treatment, to a significant emotional difficulty and distress. In clinical practice, the recommended washout period is a minimum of 3 months, 2 , 4 , 17 , 18 while the manufacturer recommends a washout period of 6 months, 10 mainly based on the experimental animal studies and a few case reports.

In this study we explored, in a population‐based clinical database, if conception shortly after 30–150 mg MTX treatment (1–3 months and 3–6 months) is as safe as later conception (>6 months following MTX).

MATERIALS AND METHODS

This study was conducted using the Clalit Health Services (CHS) database, which covers over half of the Israeli population (~4.9 million). CHS integrates data from primary care, specialty clinics, hospitalizations, laboratories, and pharmacies, and maintains a validated chronic disease registry based on ICD‐9 codes, laboratory results, and medication use. A detailed description of the Israeli health system and the CHS database is provided in Appendix S1. The study was approved by the institutional review board of Lady Davis Medical Center and the Data Utilization Committee of CHS. Owing to the retrospective nature of the study, a waiver of informed consent was granted. The study followed the Strengthening Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) reporting guideline.

The cohort

This retrospective cohort study consisted of all CHS female members that were hospitalized in a total of 14 (nationwide) CHS hospitals' obstetrics and gynecology departments and treated with MTX (ATC code L01BA01) 30–150 mg dose, between the years 2010–2021. This dose range was chosen since for EP an intermediate dose (1 mg/kg or 50 mg/m2 body surface area) is typically used. 19 Within this group, we identified all live births and spontaneous abortions (including complete, incomplete, and missed abortion) that occurred at least 30 days after the last MTX administration. Conception date was defined as the index date, calculated by adding 14 days to the last menstrual period (LMP) date recorded in the physician encounters. In clinical practice, this date is verified by early ultrasound examination; if a discrepancy is found, the physician manually corrects the LMP field in the electronic health record to align with the biometric findings (>4 days between 7 and 10 weeks of gestation, >6 days between 11 and 13 weeks of gestation and >9 days between 14 and 20 weeks of gestation). Consequently, the index date reflects the most accurate clinical estimate of the pregnancy start date. Live births resulting from assisted reproductive therapy were included. We identified the pregnancies' newborns from hospitalization records at birth. The newborns were considered eligible for the study only if live birth was no later than October 1, 2022, to have sufficient follow up period to identify the study's outcomes; spontaneous abortions were included until December 31, 2023, representing the most recent available data at the time of extraction, since no additional follow up was needed for abortions.

Within this group we excluded all women with a membership of less than 6 months in CHS prior to index date, malignancy prior to index date, multiple gestations at the index pregnancy, termination of the index pregnancy (TOP), and prescription for other teratogenic drugs including higher MTX dose, from 90 days before conception date, and during pregnancy (Table S1), until live birth or abortion (Figure 1).

Flow chart outlining the derivation of the study population.

Since this was a retrospective population‐based study using comprehensive healthcare data, we included all eligible individuals during the study period; therefore, no prior sample size calculation was required.

Exposure

MTX time‐interval was defined as the interval between the last MTX administration and the consecutive conception date of pregnancy that resulted in live birth or abortion. In cases of multiple pregnancies (resulting in live birth or abortion) following MTX administration, creating different time intervals from the last MTX administration, the exposure was measured for each event separately.

The comparative groups in the study were pregnancies and abortions, any time after MTX treatment, categorized into 1–3, 3–6 or >6 months from the last MTX treatment for EP.

MTX dose was captured as the total cumulative dose (in mg) administered during the treatment course for the index EP. This included single or multiple administrations if required. The exposure interval was measured from the date of the last MTX administration to the subsequent conception date. To account for the possible effect of cumulative MTX doses, we stratified the cohort by the median accumulated MTX dose within a month.

Outcomes

All outcome variables were captured from the CHS database by ICD9 codes from the patients and the infants' records during the study period. The primary outcome was any documented major congenital malformation, as defined by the European Platform on Rare Disease Registration 20 (Table S2). The presence of malformations was assessed at 1 year of age in the infants' records of the index pregnancy. In case of more than one major congenital malformation in the same infant, we counted it as one for the analysis. In addition, we assessed any documentation of preterm delivery, low birth weight (<2500 g), congenital malformations associated with MTX embryopathy (Table S3), and spontaneous abortion. Due to the lack of fetal diagnostic data we included spontaneous abortions in the evaluation of secondary and composite outcomes. The composite outcome consisted of any type of documented major congenital malformation, spontaneous abortion, preterm delivery, and low birth weight. This composite was used as a global measure of adverse pregnancy outcomes, reflecting the multifaceted nature of potential MTX‐induced embryotoxicity, which may affect both structural development and pregnancy maintenance. Covariates

Multiple covariates were assessed at the index date, including sociodemographic data: population sector: Arab/Jews, maternal age, and socioeconomic status (SES): low, middle, high, based on the SES scores of the clinic neighborhood as defined by the Israeli Central Bureau of Statistics; medical services use before index date including number of primary physician office visits and obstetrics physician visits in the 6 months‐period prior to the index date; comorbidities including diabetes mellitus, cardiovascular disease, smoking, obesity, hypertension, chronic kidney disease, liver disease; and prescriptions of high dose folic acid supplementation≥5 mg (measured as dispensing of at least two prescriptions, any time between 100 days before conception date and 100 days after conception date).

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics of the baseline characteristics of women included in the cohort are presented using counts (%) for categorical variables and mean (SD) or median (interquartile range) for continuous/ordinal variables.

The distribution of MTX time‐interval (exposure variable) is presented and summarized as continuous as well as by defined cutoffs (1–3 months, 3–6 months, >6 months from last MTX). Comparisons of baseline demographic and clinical characteristics between groups for discrete and continuous covariates were performed using generalized estimating equations (GEE) to account for the covariance in multiple pregnancies in the same woman, and the order of the exposure group. For analyses involving comparisons of a relatively rare binary baseline variables, an Fisher exact test was conducted. P value for trend was calculated across the exposure categories (1–3, 3–6, and >6 months) to evaluate temporal relationship. GEE was also utilized to assess the association between time from MTX treatment to conception and the study outcomes, treating the interval as a continuous measure.

Association between exposure categories and the composite outcome as well as with each of the composite outcome components are described using counts (%) and summarized using odds ratio (OR) compared to the >6 months interval reference. GEE was used to estimate OR, adjusted for possible confounders, considering the order of the exposure group and multiple pregnancies in the same woman. To ensure the stability of our multivariable models given the number of events in the early exposure groups, we utilized a streamlined set of prespecified confounders. The adjusted models included maternal age, pre‐gestational diabetes, prescription of 5 mg folic acid supplementation, number of primary and obstetric physician visits, and the cumulative MTX dose within a month. This targeted selection of variables was chosen to balance the need for confounding control with the statistical requirement for an adequate ratio of events to independent variables. A P value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Data analysis was performed using IBM SPSS statistics version 24 (IBM. Ney York), and SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute).

RESULTS

A total of 852 patients were hospitalized in obstetrics and gynecology departments of CHS hospitals during 2010–2021 and treated with 30–150 mg MTX. Among them, we identified 1032 cases (903 live births and 131 abortions) any time following MTX administration. We excluded 321 cases (71 abortions and 250 live births) according to the exclusion criteria. Of the excluded abortions, 58 were TOPs. A subanalysis by exposure interval showed that five occurred in the 1–3‐month group, four in the 3–6 month group, and 42 in the >6‐month group (with 7 cases lacking a recorded LMP to determine timing). These cases were excluded from analysis, as medical indications for the procedures could not be determined. The remaining 711 cases constituted the final cohort. Among this cohort we identified 38, 111, and 562 live births/spontaneous abortions that occurred 1–3 months, 3–6 months, and >6 months after MTX treatment, respectively. A subset of 193 women contributed multiple pregnancies to the study, some of which fell into different exposure categories. A flow chart outlining the derivation of the study population is presented in Figure 1.

The mean maternal age at index date was 31.64 ± 4.81 years. Patients that conceived 1–3 months following MTX administration were younger than those with an index pregnancy of >6 months following MTX (27.92 ± 5.31 years and 32.25 ± 4.63, respectively, P < 0.0001) (Table 1). In addition, patients that conceived 1–3 months following MTX administration had higher number of primary physician office visits in the 6 months prior to index date, while those who conceived 3–6 months following MTX, were more likely to have higher number of obstetrics physician visits prior to the index date (Table 1). There was no difference between the groups in baseline comorbidities and in the percentage of patients receiving 5‐mg folic acid supplementation prescriptions. The mean cumulated MTX dose was 93.37 ± 61.34 mg, and 88.99 ± 30.03 mg, for the 1–3 months and the 3–6‐months interval groups, respectively. Overall, 70 infants were identified with major congenital malformations during the study period. The crude ORs were 0.73 (95% confidence interval [CI]: 0.23–2.6) and 0.82 (95% CI: 0.43–1.78), for the 1–3 months and 3–6 months following the last MTX exposure, respectively. In a multivariable analysis, conception 1–3 months and 3–6 months after the last exposure to MTX was not associated with an increased risk of major congenital malformations with ORs of 0.877 (95% CI: 0.245–3.14) and 1.01 (95% CI: 0.48–2.13), respectively (Table 2). The most prevalent major congenital malformations (in total and across all exposure groups) were ventricular septal defects and congenital anomalies of skull and bones (15, 22 cases, respectively) (Table S4).

A total of 25 preterm births, 54 low birth weight infants (<2500 g), 60 spontaneous abortions and 20 infants with congenital malformations associated with MTX embryopathy were documented during the study period. Although we identified a numerically higher rate of congenital malformations associated with MTX embryopathy in the 1–3 months interval than in the 3–6 months interval, and compared to the reference group, this trend was not statistically significant. Higher rates of preterm births and low birth weights were also found in the 1–3 months group, compared to the reference group, but these were also not statistically significant. There was no association between conception 1–3 and 3–6 months after the last MTX treatment and increased risk of the composite outcome (adjusted ORs 0.78, 95% CI: 0.34–1.78; 0.97, 95% CI: 0.59–1.62, respectively) (Table S5, Figure 3).

Association between conception 1–3 months (red) and 3–6 months (blue), compared to conception >6 months following methotrexate for ectopic pregnancy, and secondary and composite outcomes, multivariable analysis, CHS 2010–2023.

To study the possible effect of cumulated MTX dose, we assessed trends across exposure groups for the study primary and secondary outcomes, stratified by the median cumulative doses (≤75 mg and >75 mg). There was no significant trend for association across exposure groups with major congenital malformation or any of the secondary outcomes, when accounting for cumulated MTX doses (Table S6).

Finally, to assess for a possible temporal relationship between the time of MTX administration and pregnancy outcomes, we treated the interval as a continuous measure in months. This analysis confirmed the lack of a significant trend for the primary outcome of major congenital malformations (0.999; 95% CI: 0.89–1.15). Similarly, no significant continuous association was found for the composite outcome (1.02; 95% CI: 0.93–1.12) or for individual secondary outcomes, including preterm delivery and spontaneous abortion. DISCUSSION

This study evaluated, in population‐based clinical data, if conception 1–3 and 3–6 months after MTX treatment for EP is as safe as a later conception. We have not identified a significant association between conception at these intervals after MTX treatment and increased risk of major congenital malformations. There were numerically higher rates of congenital malformations associated with MTX embryopathy specifically, preterm birth, and low birth weights in the 1–3 months interval group, which was not statistically significant in this cohort. The results were consistent in a subanalysis designed to minimize the potential impact of cumulated MTX dose from multiple administrations of MTX. The lack of association was further supported by an analysis of association with time to conception analyzed as a continuous variable. By modeling the interval in months across the entire cohort, we found that the risk of adverse outcomes remained stable over time, with narrow CIs around the null effect. This suggests that the study did not overlook a gradual increase in risk that might have been obscured by categorical grouping. However, while the continuous model provides additional statistical reassurance, we remain cautious in our interpretation of the 1–3‐month interval specifically. Due to the limited number of cases in that early window, we cannot entirely exclude the possibility of a modest increase in risk that might only be detectable in a larger subpopulation.

There is a paucity of data on which to rely on when recommending a safe time‐interval between MTX treatment for EP and the following conception. The current approach is based, mainly, on animal studies and case reports. 13 , 14 , 15 , 16 Most guidelines recommend an interval of at least 3 months after the last MTX treatment, 2 , 4 , 17 , 18 whereas the manufacturer advises a stricter 6‐month washout period. 10

Our findings are in alignment with a retrospective cohort study conducted in 2009 21 aimed to determine whether MTX treatment for EP resulted in teratogenicity or other bad obstetric outcomes. Similar rates of congenital malformation, preterm birth, low birth weight, fetus small for gestational age, and abortions were reported when conception <6 months and >6 months following MTX treatment, were compared. However, it should be noted that this was a single‐center study with a relatively small sample size (45 pregnancies with conception date less than 6 months after MTX treatment, and 125 pregnancies in the study as a whole). Additionally, the comparison groups were more broadly categorized, and the mean exposure interval in the group that conceived <6 months after MTX treatment was 3.6 ± 1.7 months, making it difficult to draw conclusions about an interval of only 1–3 months. Contrary to our findings, Lagarce et al. 22 reported a higher rate of major congenital malformations compared to the general population, among women who conceived within 3 months after the last MTX treatment. Yet, this was also a single‐center study, and the rate was compared to the rate in the general population. In addition, none of the documented malformations was consistent with MTX embryopathy.

The observed malformation rate of 9.8% is higher than general population 23 but was consistent across all groups, including the reference group (10.1%). While the lower rate in the 1–3 months group (7.9%) is encouraging, the small number of events in this interval limits our ability to draw definitive conclusions. This might be attributed to extensive pregnancy follow‐ups performed in Israel as part of the subsidized health services system, and higher rates of consanguineous marriages, especially in the Arab communities in Israel with lower rates of TOPs when a congenital malformation is diagnosed. 24 , 25 It is also possible that women treated with MTX were monitored more closely during and after pregnancy, thus increasing the detection rate of malformations overall. It should be noted that the reference group in this study comprised only of women who received MTX, thus reducing detection bias between groups.

These findings suggest that a 1–3‐month washout period may be considered, but clinicians should remain cautious and discuss the limitations of current evidence with patients. The potential teratogenic effects of MTX and its complex pharmacokinetics highlight the risk of fetal exposure for women treated with MTX shortly before conception. The uncertainty regarding the recommended safe conception interval poses significant challenge for clinicians. Our findings provide further confirmation for the current practical guidelines, which are less strict than MTX label, regarding the safe conception interval of 3–6 months.

Given the relative rarity of major congenital malformations and intrinsic differences in populations, we encourage further investigation with larger sample sizes of different populations to determine the optimal safe interval between MTX treatment for EP and a subsequent conception to confirm or refute our findings.

Strength and limitations

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first population‐based study that explored the safe conception interval following 30–150 mg MTX therapy for EP. A major methodological strength of this study is the use of an internal reference group consisting solely of women who also received MTX for an EP, but at a later interval (>6 months). By comparing women with the same underlying clinical indication and history of drug exposure, we inherently controlled for the baseline risk and shared characteristics of this specific patient population. This ensures that the observed malformation rate in the reference group represents a cohort‐specific baseline, reflecting, probably, the risk profile of women with prior EP and higher maternal age under rigorous 1‐year pediatric surveillance. Furthermore, our ability to include an analysis of the cumulative MTX dose serves as an additional strength of this study, allowing for a more comprehensive assessment of safety beyond the time interval alone. Baseline demographic and clinical variables enabled further minimization of potential biases.

The present study had several limitations; as with any retrospective study that is based on routinely collected clinical and administrative data, a possible limitation may be related to the completeness and the accuracy of data. Yet, there is no reason to expect that these errors would occur more frequently in any of the compared groups. In addition, possible cognitive or relatively minor malformations might be diagnosed in the infant later than 1 year of age, hence missed while reviewing the infants' records at year one, but these missing diagnoses are expected to be non‐differential. Therefore, any bias resulting from misclassification or missing diagnosis is expected to bias the results toward the null. A significant limitation of this study is the exclusion of TOPs. As the CHS database does not consistently record the specific medical indications or fetal findings for TOPs, these could not be included in the malformation analysis. This exclusion may lead to an underestimation of the true incidence of major congenital malformations, particularly those that are severe or lethal in utero. This bias would likely be non‐differential across groups; supporting this, the distribution of TOPs across the exposure categories followed a pattern similar to that of live births, suggesting that women in the early conception group were not disproportionately undergoing TOPs. However, if women conceiving within 1–3 months were more likely to undergo TOP due to concerns about MTX exposure or detected anomalies, our results might underestimate the risk in this specific interval. Nonetheless, results should be interpreted as primarily reflecting outcomes in pregnancies that proceeded to late second trimester or live birth. An additional limitation is the fact that no standardized protocol for EP management existed across all CHS hospitals. Nevertheless, as the focus in the current study was on MTX exposure, specifically, variation in ectopic pregnancy management is unlikely to have had substantial influence on findings. Finally, the study groups were relatively small, specifically the group consisting of pregnancy that followed MTX by 1–3 months thus the relatively wide CIs presented.

CONCLUSIONS

Conception within 3–6 months following 30–150 mg MTX treatment does not seem to increase the risk of major congenital malformations, low birth weight, preterm delivery, or spontaneous abortions. These findings are consistent with current clinical practices suggesting a 3‐month washout period. While conception within 1–3 months showed no statistically significant increase in risk, these early interval results should be considered preliminary and require confirmation in larger, multicenter cohorts.
