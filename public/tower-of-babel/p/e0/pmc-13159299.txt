Effects of transcranial direct current stimulation and neural mobilization on pain and disability in people with chronic radicular pain: a protocol for a sham-controlled randomized trial
Authors: Aryostennes Miquéias da Silva Ferreira, César Augusto Medeiros Silva, Rodrigo Pegado de Abreu Freitas, Liane de Brito Macedo, Clécio Gabriel de Souza
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundRadicular pain is a very common condition that occurs when spine discopathy leads to nerve root damage; it radiates to the leg with somatotopic distribution. It could cause high levels of pain and disability. When it becomes chronic, central sensitization may be present, which can lead to neuropathic pain. Neural mobilization is a manual approach recommended for the treatment of this condition. Transcranial direct current stimulation (tDCS) has emerged as an alternative treatment for neuropathic chronic pain in an attempt to modulate brain function. The aim of this study is to verify if the effects of tDCS add benefits in decreasing pain intensity, neuropathic symptoms, and disability in individuals with chronic radicular pain when associated with neural mobilization.MethodsIt is a protocol for a randomized controlled trial to be conducted with individuals with chronic radicular pain divided in two groups: Experimental group (active tDCS + neural mobilization) and control group (sham tDCS + neural mobilization). The primary outcome will be pain intensity, measured through the numerical pain scale; secondary outcomes are disability, assessed by Roland Morris, and neuropathic symptoms, assessed by Douleur Neuropathique Questionnaire and Pain Detect Questionnaire, range of motion on the Single Leg Raise Test, pressure pain threshold, and two-point discrimination. Perceived Global Effect Scale will also be used. The assessment will be performed before and after the five consecutive days’ intervention protocol and at 7 and 14 days of follow-up for pain intensity. Data will be analyzed by SPSS software; Kolmogorov-Smirnov and Levene tests will be applied to verify the normality and homogeneity of the sample. Then, a repeated measure ANOVA will be performed for comparison over time. A statistical significance level of 5% and a 95% confidence interval will be assumed.DiscussionThis study would elucidate the possible tDCS effects on radicular pain, which could be pointed out as a new therapeutic tool for this difficult management condition.Trial registration numberRBR-2d3jj9t/U1111-1289-8248 REBEC. Background

Radicular pain is a clinical condition of pain related to conditions of the lumbar spine, with a poor prognosis, intense perceived pain, impairment of functional capacity, limitation of activities, and restriction of participation; for these reasons, it can be considered a difficult condition to manage [1]. Although “sciatica” has been a widely used term, it does not represent all situations that have pain radiating down the leg but describes the painful symptomatology descending through the ischial tuberosity, with dermatotopic distribution, in the course of a lumbosacral nerve root [2] Currently, radicular pain, with or without radiculopathy, is a more appropriate term [3].

It occurs when there is nerve root involvement, commonly related to discopathy and vertebral stenosis. Diagnosis is based on clinical findings; leg pain is considered worse than lower back pain and increases on exertion and the Single Leg Raise Test (SLRT) [4, 5]. The pain may indicate that different structures of the lumbar spine are involved, but clinical tests are not reliable in identifying any specific structure. The clinical symptoms can include muscle weakness, sensitivity, and reflex deficits, and people with this condition tend to be more severely affected and have a worse prognosis [5].

Pain perception is predominantly due to a neuropathic mechanism [6], and its most common cause is the radiculopathy [7], which leads to the somatosensory nervous system [8]. Symptoms include a burning sensation, electric shock, and tingling and can be present even after the anatomopathological changes have resolved [9]. Usually, it is considered the 3-month timepoint to be classified as chronic pain with peristent symptoms [6, 9].

Conservative approaches should be the initial treatment options and should include interventions on nonneural tissues and neurodynamic mobilization techniques [10]. Neural mobilization is able to reduce the intensity of pain radiating to the leg and improve physical function [11] and is superior to other modalities. The slump and single leg raise have been identified with a good level of evidence for the treatment of radicular pain to reduce pain and functional disability [11].

Adaptations have been identified in different regions of the brain related to chronic pain. Homeostatic control of neuronal plasticity is impaired in the primary motor cortex, probably due to a failure to reverse excitation after an excitatory input and to combat excessive increases in corticomotor excitability [12].

Noninvasive neuromodulation techniques have emerged with the aim of modulating pain based on brain function, including transcranial direct current stimulation (tDCS) [13]. Many studies have shown its effects on conditions involving chronic pain syndromes, such as fibromyalgia and migraine [14, 15]. The primary motor cortex is the most effective target area to be stimulated for pain treatment. It is assumed that neuromodulation induces immediate and long-lasting analgesic effects on cortical excitability in the stimulated area and the adjacent neuronal network [16].

There are few studies that have investigated the effects of tDCS in the radicular pain population, and none associated with neural mobilization was found. It is believed that its neuropathic characteristics involve the phenomenon of central sensitization and maladaptive neuroplastic changes that could be sensitive to the effect of tDCS. For this reason, an intervention model was proposed with the association of the two techniques, since the approach to chronic musculoskeletal conditions has limited effect when based on an isolated therapy [5, 12, 17].

Thus, this study describes a double-blind randomized controlled trial, which aims to verify whether the effects of tDCS add benefits on pain intensity, neuropathic symptoms, and functional disability in individuals with chronic lumbosciatalgia when associated with neural mobilization techniques.

Materials and methods

Study design

This describes a double-blinded sham-controlled randomized clinical trial protocol, registered in the clinical trials platform and approved by the research ethics committee (protocol number 3.737.749) of the Faculty of Health Sciences of Trairi—Federal University of Rio Grande do Norte (FACISA/UFRN). Participants will be randomized into 2 groups, each containing 25 individuals: 1 group will receive active tDCS and the other sham tDCS; both associated with neural mobilization in the course of five consecutive days intervention. The research will be conducted according to the requirements of the Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials guidelines (CONSORT) [18]. The protocol was designed according to the Recommendations for Interventional Trials (SPIRIT) [19] and Template for Intervention Description and Replication (TIDieR) [20] (Supplementary File 1).

Eligibility criteria

This will include men and women aged between 18 and 64 years with persistent radicular pain for at least 3 months [21, 22], average pain intensity greater than 2 (NPS > 2) in the latest 2 weeks [22, 23], neuropathic pain classified by the DN4 [24], and the presence of at least two of these clinical criteria: positive nerve tension test (SLRT), neurological deficit, pain below the knee, pain more present in the leg than in the lower back, and subjective sensory changes [25].

This will exclude volunteers with any tDCS contraindication (epilepsy, devices implanted in the skull, major psychiatric or neurological diseases, severe systemic diseases, and pregnancy) that have undergone physical therapy treatment in the latest 6 months before, have used corticosteroid spine injection in the latest 6 months, have present known signs of severe spinal pathology (cauda equina syndrome, trauma, fractures, or cancer), and have history of abdominal, lumbar, or lower limb surgery in the last year.

Participants

The sample will include 50 volunteers with chronic radicular pain, recruited by publicizing on a local radio station and from the patients’ waiting list of the Physiotherapy School Clinic of the University and contacted by telephone to clarify doubts and perform the screening for inclusion. After being informed about the aims and procedures of the study, eligible individuals will provide a written informed consent form to the researcher who will be conducting the assessment. The participants’ autonomy and anonymity guarantee were respected, ensuring their privacy confidential data, as ruled by Resolution 466/2012 of the Brazilian National Health Council. An independent researcher was responsible for randomization and to numerically code the participants’ data.

Procedures and interventions

This study will proceed at the Laboratory of Human Motricity and Physiology of the University. Interventions will be carried out during five consecutive days by two experienced physiotherapists qualified to apply techniques. Before intervention, all the participants will be informed about the possible sensations that could occur and the adverse effects described in the literature. Then, the adverse effects questionnaire for tDCS will be applied [26].

In the first assessment, a blinded researcher will collect information through an assessment form developed by the researchers, where participants’ personal and clinical information is listed. Scales and questionnaires about pain intensity, neuropathic symptoms, and disability will also be applied. This will be performed before (T0) and after treatment (T1) and at 7- (T2) and 14-day (T3) follow-up for pain intensity. Previously, the research team will be trained to reduce the risk of bias and error rates by performing pilot tests. The assessment spends 30 min, and the intervention lasts 45 min, delivered by one-to-one service.

Participants will be instructed not to change their current routine in terms of physical activity and medications; implementing intervention will not require alteration to usual care pathways. The only condition is not to be undergone other physical therapist treatments during the trial. Intervention could be discontinued if any adverse event happens or if a participant quits. Sample size calculation

The sample calculation was performed in G*Power program [34], based on a repeated measures ANOVA with time and group interaction between subjects, with data from the pilot study. For primary outcome, a partial eta squared of 0.031, the effect size (f) was 0.1788. Considering α = 0.05, power (1-β) = 0.80, four measurements and two groups. The assumed correlation between baseline and follow-up assessments and among them was (ρ) = 0.5. The nonsphericity correction value applied was (ε) = 1. Then, a total sample size of 44 participants was obtained. Considering follow-up losses, a value of 15% was added, reaching a final sample of 50 participants, 25 in each arm.

Randomization, blinding, and concealment

Randomization process will be conducted by an independent investigator who will not be involved in other study procedures, using the website www.sealedenvelope.com. After the initial assessment, volunteers will be randomly assigned; the 1:1 allocation of subjects will be confidential, done through opaque envelopes, to which only the researcher that applies tDCS intervention will have access. The random allocation of each participant will be revealed immediately before the first day intervention. The participants selected at the screening will be randomly assigned to two groups: one will undergo active tDCS and the other sham tDCS, both associated with neural mobilization.

Several measures will be taken to ensure that blinding is not compromised. All participants will be informed of the aim of the study, which is to test two different techniques. All assessments will be carried out by an investigator who will not be involved in the interventions and who will remain blinded to the treatment groups. The results of assessments and functional tests will not be disclosed to the therapists. At the end of the protocol, each participant will be asked which intervention they believe they received, in order to determine the efficacy of blinding. Finally, the researcher in charge of statistical analysis will not take part in the assessments or interventions and will receive the database without the subjects or groups being identified. The unblinding during the trial will be allowed only in cases of severe adverse events investigations.

Blinding test

Blinding will be assessed by asking patients which treatment they believe they underwent, that is, whether they feel they were allocated to real or sham tDCS intervention.

Research team

Four independent physiotherapists’ researchers will collaborate with the study at different times. The first blinded researcher will be responsible for the assessments and reassessments; the second will apply the intervention protocol with tDCS (not blinded); the third, also blinded, will apply the intervention with neural mobilization, and the fourth will perform the blinded statistical analysis.

Two trained therapists will be delivering the intervention every day: the one who will make the assessments will be present on the day previously scheduled with the participant to perform the evaluations and on the last day of the intervention, when he will do the reassessments. One more researcher will be in daily contact with the participant, confirming their attendance via messaging app, and will also conduct the follow-up evaluation of the primary outcome after the intervention; this also is a retention strategy. Also, the researcher responsible for the integrity of the work as a whole will be daily available for solving unexpected events and for auditing trial conduct and group management weekly. A Data Monitoring Committee was not considered as this was a low-risk and closed protocol intervention.

Researcher training

In order to standardize the procedures and promote data quality, a series of training stages will be implemented before the study to conduct assessments and interventions and reach a consensus among the researchers involved.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses will be performed by a blinded statistician using commercial software. For statistical analysis, the independent variables of group (active tDCS, sham tDCS) and time (T0, T1, T2, and T3) will be considered for each dependent variable: pain intensity, neuropathic symptoms, and functional disability. The Kolmogorov–Smirnov test will be applied to verify the distribution of the data, and the Levene test will be used to analyze the homogeneity of variance.

A repeated measures analysis of variance will be conducted for the primary and secondary outcomes, with time as the within-subject factor and group as the between-subjects factor. The interaction of time and group and inter-group and intragroup differences will be analyzed for all variables. Finally, it will be analyzed whether there are differences between the groups at the different intervention times.

Treatment of missing data will be done through missing data imputation, with the last data collected being imputed. An intention-to-treat analysis will be performed to ensure randomization effects so that prognostic factors are evenly distributed in both groups. The effect size can be calculated between groups for variables that show intergroup differences at some point, with the respective 95% confidence interval. The level of statistical significance adopted will be 5%.

Communication plans

If changes are needed in the study method or intervention, it will be notified to the Research Ethics Committee for review. At the end of the study, the results will be reported to the participants in the form of lectures, showing the effects found in the evaluated variables. If one technique is found to be superior, the most effective intervention will be guaranteed to the subjects.

Patient public involvement

No patients were involved in this study as it is a protocol design. However, in the development of the clinical trial, patients could be consulted.

Discussion

There is a lack of studies in the literature about the use of NIBS for neuropathic pain in individuals with chronic radicular pain. This is the first study to use a tDCS sham-controlled group in individuals with leg radicular pain associated with manual therapy techniques, with concealed allocation and blinding of the outcome assessor and biostatistician. This study will demonstrate the effect of the short-term application (2-week follow-up after interventions) of tDCS in individuals with chronic radicular pain. The researcher who will deliver the noninvasive brain stimulation will not be blinded. The study results will be disseminated to the population through an interview on a radio station show and by phone contacting participants after submission to a peer-reviewed journal and scientific meetings.

Trial status

Research is in progress and in recruitment phase.
