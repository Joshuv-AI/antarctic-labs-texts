Robotic retroperitoneal partial nephrectomy: rediscovering a proven approach in the era of minimally invasive surgery
Authors: Michele Slawitz, Francesco Dinale, Annalisa Patera, Giulia Di Marco, Giulio Gaetano Guarino, Tommaso Bocchialini, Francesco Ziglioli, Davide Campobasso, Salvatore Micali, Umberto Vittorio Maestroni
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractRenal cell carcinoma (RCC) accounts for approximately 3% of all malignancies, with its incidence steadily increasing due to the widespread use of abdominal imaging techniques and the improved detection of asymptomatic renal masses. Traditionally, radical nephrectomy (RN) was considered the gold standard for the surgical treatment of renal tumors. However, over the past decades, advancements in minimally invasive surgical techniques have led to a paradigm shift in favor of nephron-sparing approaches, particularly partial nephrectomy (PN). The introduction of robot-assisted partial nephrectomy (RAPN) has further refined this approach by enhancing surgical precision, reducing invasiveness, and improving outcomes in terms of renal function preservation. This manuscript describes a standardized and reproducible technique for retroperitoneal robot-assisted partial nephrectomy (rRAPN) using the da Vinci Xi system, illustrated through two representative cases involving right- and left-sided posterior renal tumors. Key steps in the procedure included the creation of a retroperitoneal space, identification of crucial anatomical structures, excision of the tumor using a clamp-less technique, the suture of the resection bed and the application of hemostatic agents to control bleeding. Postoperative care focused on monitoring renal function, detecting complications, and ensuring optimal recovery. Both patients had successful outcomes, with minimal blood loss, stable renal function, and negative surgical margins. Furthermore, there were no postoperative complications, and both patients were discharged within 4 or 5 days of the procedure. Retroperitoneal access offers distinct advantages, particularly for patients with previous abdominal surgeries or for those with tumors located in the posterior renal region. This approach provides oncological and functional outcomes that are comparable to transperitoneal techniques while reducing perioperative morbidity. The detailed steps outlined in this study aim to facilitate the broader adoption of rRAPN, emphasizing its potential to improve the management of select renal neoplasms. By standardizing this technique, we aim to further refine the role of retroperitoneal robot-assisted surgery in treating RCC and promote its integration into routine clinical practice.Keywords: Robotic, renal cancer, partial nephrectomy (PN), retroperitoneal

• The retroperitoneal robot-assisted approach for partial nephrectomy is an effective minimally invasive option, especially for posterior located tumors, offering good perioperative, functional, and oncological outcomes. Introduction

Background

Renal cancer ranks as the 14th most common neoplasm, accounting for approximately 3% of all cancers, with an increasing incidence in recent decades due to the rise in incidental diagnoses. In 2020, there were about 400,000 new cases of renal cell carcinoma (RCC) worldwide. The widespread use of abdominal imaging has contributed to the detection of a greater number of early-stage, asymptomatic renal masses. The incidence of RCC varies geographically, being higher in Europe and North America compared to other regions. Despite the increasing incidence, overall mortality rates for RCC have been declining (1,2).

Recognized risk factors for RCC include smoking, obesity, insulin resistance, hypertension, and chronic kidney disease (2,3). Approximately 6–9% of kidney cancers involve germline mutations in genes associated with cancer predisposition (3), although the actual incidence of genetic variants predisposing to RCC may be higher than current estimates.

Newly diagnosed tumors that are T1a or T1b are candidates for nephron-sparing surgery, which is considered the gold standard for treating T1a tumors, most T1b tumors, and some highly selected T2 neoplasms, such as in patients with a solitary kidney or chronic kidney disease, when technically feasible (4).

Rationale

Historically, the gold standard treatment for solid renal lesions was considered radical nephrectomy (RN), which negatively impacts renal function and increases the incidence of cardiovascular diseases and associated health complications. With advancements in science, technology, and knowledge in tumor biology, the trend in urology has shifted towards surgical techniques that prioritize the preservation of renal function and minimize surgical invasiveness. Over the past two decades, the preferred approach, particularly for partial nephrectomy (PN), has shifted from open surgery to minimally invasive techniques (5,6). In the era of laparoscopic surgery, PN was considered a complex and strenuous procedure, primarily due to the challenges of suturing the resection bed, which also prolonged the warm time during clamp-tumorectomy (5). However, with the advent of robot-assisted surgery, characterized by the miniaturization of movements and enhanced needle rotation, surgical comfort has improved and the complexity has become more dependent on the lesion’s characteristics and surgeon’s expertise (6).

To date, only one prospective randomized controlled trial involving patients with organ-confined RCCs smaller than 5 cm has been published (7), though it was prematurely closed. The study demonstrated comparable cancer-specific survival between PN and RN, with a hazard ratio of 2.06 [95% confidence interval (CI): 0.62–6.84].

A prospective study comparing robot-assisted and open partial nephrectomy (OPN) by the same surgeon found robot-assisted partial nephrectomy (RAPN) had lower blood loss and shorter hospital stays, while ischemia time, operative time, complications, creatinine changes, and pathological margins were comparable (8).

Comparing open, laparoscopic, and robot-assisted PN, a retrospective propensity-score-matched study reported similar rates of local recurrence, distant metastasis, and cancer-related death after a median follow-up of 5 years with lower estimated blood loss and length of hospital stay in the robotic group (9).

The transperitoneal robot-assisted partial nephrectomy (tRAPN) approach is the preferred method for laparoscopic kidney surgery, owing to surgeons’ anatomical familiarity, simpler trocar placement, and the availability of a larger working space. It has a less steep learning curve compared to the retroperitoneal robot-assisted partial nephrectomy (rRAPN) approach, where creating the retroperitoneal space and operating within it can be difficult, potentially increasing the risk of renal vascular injuries (10).

Previous abdominal surgery can lead to adhesions and alterations in the normal anatomy, creating a hostile peritoneal cavity that increases the risk of surgical complications for patients undergoing transperitoneal renal surgery.

A recent systematic review and meta-analysis, which included 22 studies and 5,675 patients, aimed to compare perioperative outcomes between the two surgical approaches, revealing that rRAPN has a lower rate of complications, shorter operative time, reduced estimated blood loss, less need for blood transfusions, and a shorter length of stay. However, the rates of positive surgical margins, major complications, and conversion to radical or open surgery were similar for both approaches (11).

Therefore, retroperitoneal surgery can be considered a valid alternative to the transperitoneal approach, particularly in patients with a history of previous abdominal surgery or in case of simultaneous bilateral RAPN (12). Considering tumor location, a large retrospective study hypothesized better outcomes with tRAPN for anterior and medial tumors and with rRAPN for posterior tumors. The study included 1,169 patients and concluded that both approaches offer equivalent perioperative morbidity as well as comparable functional and pathological outcomes, regardless of tumor location (13).

Despite the growing interest in rRAPN, particularly for patients with a hostile abdomen and/or posteriorly located tumors, there is limited literature detailing these techniques.

Objectives

In this context, we present a reproducible approach to rRAPN using the da Vinci Xi surgical system, illustrated with two examples involving right and left-sided procedures for posteriorly located renal neoplasms. We detail the surgical technique of rRAPN, including preoperative preparations and requirements, patient characteristics, step-by-step procedural descriptions, and postoperative considerations. We present this article in accordance with the SUPER reporting checklist (available at https://jovs.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/jovs-25-14/rc).

Preoperative preparations and requirements

All procedures performed in this study were in accordance with the ethical standards of the institutional and/or national research committee(s) and with the Helsinki Declaration and its subsequent amendments. Written informed consent was obtained from the patients for the publication of this study, accompanying images and video. A copy of the written consent is available for review by the editorial office of this journal.

The procedures were performed in 2024 at the Department of Urology of the University Hospital of Parma by two surgeons, one at the console and the assistant surgeon at the patient’s bedside. The primary surgeon has over fifteen years of experience in laparoscopic surgery and has been performing robotic partial nephrectomies for the past six years, having completed more than 150 cases.

Under general anesthesia, the patient is intubated and positioned in the lateral decubitus position. The surgical bed is flexed to maximize the space between the iliac crest and the 12th rib.

The da Vinci Xi Surgical System® (Intuitive Inc., Sunnyvale, CA, USA) is positioned on the anterior side of the patient. A 30-degree robotic camera is utilized, providing a downward-looking view for the procedure. Instrument selection depends on the surgeon’s preference but typically includes monopolar curved scissors in the first arm and Maryland bipolar forceps or Cadiere forceps, with ProGrasp in the third and fourth arms.

A small incision (~1 cm) is made at the top of the 12th rib to access the retroperitoneal space. This is achieved by passing through Scarpa’s fascia and sequentially dissecting the external oblique, internal oblique, and transversus abdominis muscles using an “open” technique. The retroperitoneal space is created digitally by finger dissection, reflecting the peritoneum medially and downward. Balloon dissectors may also be used as an alternative, but in our experience, finger dissection is effective.

Under digital guidance, two additional 8-mm robotic trocars are placed about 6 cm medial to the initial port along the same oblique line. A third 8-mm robotic trocar is inserted in the posterior axillary line, approximately 6 cm lateral to the camera port. An additional 12-mm assistant trocar is positioned caudal to the first and second trocars in a triangulation manner.

The 12-mm trocar is inserted at an oblique angle to accommodate the AirSeal® System (CONMED, Utica, NY, USA), which provides CO2 insufflation and smoke evacuation, ensuring controlled pressure and optimal visibility during surgery. The surgical specimen is eventually extracted through this incision (Figure 1). The assistant operates from a position posterior to the patient, utilizing the AirSeal trocar as the sole access port. This port plays a crucial role in enabling the introduction of the suction device, which is not only used for draining fluids and smoke but also proves valuable in tissue dissection and manipulation. Additionally, this access port allows the assistant surgeon to place clips, insert gauze, needles, and hemostatic agents as needed.

Patient positioning and port placement for right rRAPN. (A) The patient was positioned in the right lateral decubitus position, and the surgical bed was flexed between the iliac crest and the 12th rib to optimize exposure of the operative field. (B) For right-sided retroperitoneal kidney surgery, the port configuration consisted of the ProGrasp forceps, monopolar curved scissors, and Maryland bipolar forceps introduced from medial to lateral, with the camera inserted through the trocar positioned at the top of the 12th rib. A 12-mm assistant AirSeal trocar is placed caudally to posterior port, arranged in a triangular configuration. rRAPN, retroperitoneal robot-assisted partial nephrectomy.

Pneumoretroperitoneum was established at a pressure of 10 to 15 mmHg. After optical inspection of the newly created retroperitoneal cavity and the laparoscopic takedown of any adhesions or fibrous bands, the robotic system was docked. Under direct vision, the instruments were inserted through the ports as described. From medial to lateral, the ProGrasp or Cadiere forceps, monopolar curved scissors, and Maryland bipolar forceps were introduced.

Patient selection criteria

Careful patient selection is essential for optimizing outcomes in rRAPN. Ideal candidates generally present with posteriorly located, small to moderate renal masses (typically ≤4 cm) and low to intermediate anatomical complexity (14), as indicated by nephrometry scores [e.g., RENAL score ≤9 (15), PADUA score ≤9 (16), or C-index >2 (17)]. Tumors with exophytic growth patterns and polar, posterior or lateral location are particularly favorable (14,18).

Patient-related factors must also be considered. While obesity is not an absolute contraindication, a high body mass index (BMI) may reduce working space in the retroperitoneum and increase technical complexity (19).

Similarly, comorbidities that affect lateral decubitus positioning (e.g., musculoskeletal disorders or severe cardiopulmonary disease) or tolerance to pneumoretroperitoneum [e.g., chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD)] warrant careful preoperative assessment (20).

Rather than absolute contraindications, clinical decision-making often involves weighing relative factors such as prior retroperitoneal surgery, anterior or hilar tumor location, or elevated nephrometry scores requiring complex vascular control or reconstruction (18,21). In such cases, a transperitoneal approach may offer better exposure and ergonomics. Ultimately, the choice between retroperitoneal and transperitoneal access should be tailored to the individual patient, considering tumor characteristics, patient anatomy, and surgeon experience, rather than relying on rigid contraindications.

Step-by-step description (Video 1)

Right and left retroperitoneal robot-assisted partial nephrectomy (rRAPN) performed using the da Vinci Xi system in two cases.

Case 1: left rRAPN

A 71-year-old female with a history of hypercholesterolemia, colonic diverticulosis, appendicectomy, and mastectomy with axillary lymphadenectomy followed by hormone therapy was incidentally found to have a 19 mm × 18 mm posterior-superior renal mass in the left kidney (Figure 2). The patient was asymptomatic, and the lesion was assigned a Nephrometry score of 4P. The positioning of the patient and the robotic system, trocar placement, and robotic docking are performed as previously described and are mirrored depending on whether the procedure involves the right or left side.

Preoperative CT of left renal lesion (as shown by the arrow). (A) Transverse and (B) coronal plane of CT scan that shows a 19 mm × 18 mm posterior-superior renal lesion in the left kidney with inhomogeneous contrast-enhancement in arterial phase. CT, computed tomography.

The procedure starts with retroperitoneal access, identifying the psoas muscle and aorta as key landmarks. The retroperitoneal space is carefully accessed, and the psoas muscle and aorta are visualized to orient the surgeon during the procedure. Cadiere was used to maintain the peritoneum and bowel loops medially. Next, the ureter is identified and visualized to avoid any damage during dissection. The perinephric fat is then carefully dissected to expose the vascular pedicle, which is essential for further steps. The left renal artery is isolated and placed on a tape to be suspended, ensuring it remains accessible for later steps of the procedure if necessary.

Once the vascular structures are appropriately managed, the surgical enucleation begins with full exposure of the posterosuperior renal lesion to clearly delineate its boundaries and the transition zone between the tumor and healthy renal parenchyma. A thin rim of fibrous peritumoral capsular tissue is intentionally preserved and later used for gentle traction of the mass. Prior to resection, meticulous mobilization of the kidney is performed to ensure optimal handling and visualization throughout the excision.

In this case, the tumor enucleation was initiated by incising the lateral (superior, as seen in the surgical video) margin of the mass with robotic scissors. Careful identification and tracking of the tumor pseudocapsule allowed for a bloodless dissection plane. Following the pseudocapsular plane is particularly advantageous in off-clamp procedures, as it enhances hemostasis and reduces unnecessary parenchymal loss.

Traction was applied using the Cadiere forceps to elevate the lesion and improve exposure of the dissection plane, while the Maryland forceps were employed during dissection for gentle retraction and precise bipolar coagulation of any encountered vessels. The tumor is then excised using a careful clamp-less technique, minimizing the risk of ischemia and preserving renal function. After excision, the tumor is placed into an endo-bag for safe removal from the body.

Selective bipolar coagulation is again applied to any visible vessels using Maryland forceps to achieve hemostasis. During this step, the robotic needle large driver is used to replace the scissors, enabling precise suturing.

A continuous renal medullary suture with PDS® (New Jersey, USA) 3/0 needle 22 mm is then applied, and Hem-o-lok (Wayne, USA) clips are placed at the ends of the suture to generate hemostatic traction. This ensures that any remaining bleeding points are effectively controlled.

Following this, a hemostatic agent composed of an equine collagen matrix coated with fibrinogen and thrombin is applied to the resection bed to promote clot formation and improve hemostasis. To ensure the maximum adhesion of the hemostatic agent to the tissue, a wet gauze is placed on the agent for at least 2 minutes. This step helps ensure that the hemostatic material adheres well to the tissue and provides optimal effectiveness (Figure 3). Once hemostasis is confirmed, Gerota’s fascia is closed with Hem-o-lok and a tubular surgical 21 Fr drain is placed in the renal fossa to prevent fluid accumulation and promote proper healing. Finally, the endo-bag containing the excised tumor is extracted through the incision for trocar AirSeal, ensuring a minimally invasive removal. The procedure concludes with the closure of the surgical incisions, ensuring proper wound healing and a successful outcome.

Intraoperative steps of left rRAPN. (A) Identification of psoas muscle and abdominal aorta. (B) Exposure of renal lesion. (C) Enucleation of tumour using monopolar scissor and Maryland for dissection. (D) Application of TachoSil. (E) Final effect of hemostatic agent application. rRAPN, retroperitoneal robot-assisted partial nephrectomy.

Case 2: right rRAPN

A 65-year-old male with a significant medical history, including anxiety, hypertensive heart disease, chronic renal disease (baseline creatinine: 1.37 mg/dL), bilateral simple renal cysts, carotid atheromatosis, and prior surgical interventions [open cholecystectomy for necrotizing hemorrhagic pancreatitis, right shoulder arthroscopy, and transurethral resection of the prostate (TURP)], was found to have an incidental 27 mm × 23 mm right posterior-inferior exophytic renal mass during routine evaluation (Figure 4). The patient was asymptomatic, and the lesion was assigned a Nephrometry score of 4P. The procedure begins with access to the retroperitoneum, where the psoas muscle, inferior vena cava, and gonadal vein are identified, ensuring proper orientation for the dissection. As in the previous case, the ureter is visualized to avoid injury during the surgical steps.

Preoperative CT of right renal lesion (as shown by the arrow). (A) Transverse and (B) coronal plane of CT scan that shows a 27 mm × 23 mm posterior-inferior renal lesion in the right kidney. CT, computed tomography.

After accessing the retroperitoneal space, the Gerota’s fascia is incised, and the perinephric fat is carefully dissected, allowing access to the vascular pedicle. The right renal artery is isolated and placed on a tape, similar to the previous procedure, to maintain control during the following steps. Once the vascular structures are managed, the renal lesion, located in the posterior-inferior part of the kidney, is exposed. The tumor is excised using the clamp-less technique, as previously described, and placed into an endo-bag for safe removal. A crucial aspect during enucleation is the use of the ProGrasp forceps to manipulate the lesion by grasping a portion of fibroadipose tissue preserved during the isolation of the lesion. This technique ensures optimal control and visibility of the resection plane, facilitating precise surgical execution. Bipolar coagulation is selectively applied to visible vessels using Maryland forceps, ensuring hemostasis, and the robotic large needle driver replaces the scissors for more precise suturing, as mentioned in the earlier procedure. A continuous suture is applied to the renal medulla, with Hem-o-lok clips used at the suture ends to maintain hemostatic traction.

Following the same approach as before, a hemostatic agent (TachoSil®, Osaka, Japan) is applied to the resection bed, and a wet gauze is placed over it for 2 minutes to ensure maximum adhesion to the tissue. Subsequently, TABOTAMP® (New Jersey, USA) was applied as an additional hemostatic agent to the resection margin. This product is not routinely used by our surgical team as a hemostatic agent, nor is it typically applied to the resection bed. In the present case, it was employed to complete the coverage of the resection area that was not entirely covered by TachoSil®. Once hemostasis is confirmed, a surgical drain is placed, the Gerota’s fascia is reconstructed and the following steps are similar to those previously described (Figure 5).

(D) Exposition of right renal lesion. (E) Continuous renal medullary suture with MonoPlus® 3/0 needle 22 mm. rRAPN, retroperitoneal robot-assisted partial nephrectomy.

Postoperative considerations and tasks

Case 1: left rRAPN

The surgery lasted a total of 115 minutes, with the docking of the robotic system taking 25 minutes. The time spent on robotic operations was 80 minutes, during which an estimated blood loss of 100 cc was recorded. The patient experienced a slight drop in hemoglobin, from 12.8 g/dL preoperatively to 11.5 g/dL postoperatively, with a decrease of 1.3 g/dL.

Postoperatively, the patient’s kidney function remained stable, with no significant variations in creatinine levels. Preoperative creatinine was 0.4 mg/dL, and it remained at 0.4 mg/dL after the surgery. There were no early or late postoperative complications, and the patient was discharged on the 5th postoperative day. The surgical drain was removed on the 2nd day after the operation.

Histological examination of the excised tissue revealed a solid neoplasm measuring 1.6 cm × 1.6 cm, diagnosed as renal clear-cell carcinoma, grade 2 according to the World Health Organization-International Society of Urological Pathology (WHO-ISUP) classification, staged as pT1a based on the tumor-node-metastasis (TNM) system and with negative surgical margins.

Case 2: right rRAPN

The procedure was completed in 130 minutes, with the robotic docking taking 25 minutes. The console time itself lasted for 90 minutes. Estimated blood loss was slightly higher than in the first case, at 150 cc, and there was a hemoglobin decrease of 1.8 g/dL, from 16.3 g/dL preoperatively to 14.5 g/dL postoperatively. In terms of postoperative recovery, the patient’s creatinine levels remained stable, with a slight decrease from 1.5 mg/dL preoperatively to 1.4 mg/dL postoperatively. There were no complications observed in the immediate postoperative period, and the patient was discharged on the 4th day after surgery.

Histological examination revealed a 2.5 cm × 2.5 cm × 2.3 cm solid mass, identified as a renal clear-cell carcinoma, grade 1 according to the WHO-ISUP classification and negative surgical margins. The tumor was staged as pT1a, consistent with the TNM system.

Both patients were seen for follow-up 6 months after the surgery, with a thoraco-abdominal computed tomography (CT) scan showing no signs of metastasis or local recurrence, confirming the success of the tumor resection and the absence of disease progression. On physical examination, no laparoceles or medium-term complications were observed, indicating a smooth recovery without issues related to the surgery. Both patients have fully recovered and returned to their preoperative health status. They continue with clinical and radiological follow-up in accordance with European guidelines for the long-term monitoring of patients with RCC (4). This follow-up approach is essential to ensure the early detection of potential recurrences or complications, providing adequate risk monitoring and effective management of their long-term health.

Tips and pearls

The rRAPN technique can be successfully performed by following proper preoperative preparations and requirements. Robotic docking requires slightly more time compared to the transperitoneal approach; however, it provides direct access to the kidney and vascular pedicle, reducing bowel manipulation and peritoneal trauma. To facilitate the learning curve, it is advisable to begin with small, posterior, and highly exophytic lesions, as they are easier to approach in the retroperitoneal space. In the initial cases, clamping the arterial supply may improve visibility and reduce blood loss, allowing for a more controlled tumor resection. Various techniques are available for closing the resection bed, but selective coagulation during tumor excision is essential to minimize bleeding and maintain a clear surgical field. Additionally, before starting the resection phase, it is crucial to preplace sutures for medullary hemostasis and, if necessary, for cortical closure. Having these sutures ready within the surgical field significantly enhances the efficiency and effectiveness of the closure process.

We routinely perform medullary sutures only, followed by the application of a hemostatic agent to the resection bed, as in the cases described. In instances of particularly deep resection beds or markedly endophytic lesions (endophytic component >1/3 of the lesion diameter), we also place one or more cortical sutures using 3/0 Vicryl (New Jersey, USA), depending on the extent of the resection surface.

Discussion

The trend toward earlier diagnosis, largely driven by the increased use of imaging, has led to earlier detection of small, localized tumors, which are more amenable to nephron-sparing treatment like PN (22,23). This paradigm shift from RN to nephron-sparing approaches has improved survival rates while minimizing long-term complications such as chronic kidney disease and cardiovascular risks (24).

While OPN was once the norm, minimally invasive techniques, including laparoscopic and robot-assisted surgery, have gained prominence in recent years. These approaches offer the advantage of reduced blood loss, shorter recovery times, and smaller incisions, all while achieving oncological outcomes comparable to open surgery (7,8).

The robot-assisted approach enhances precision through improved visualization, instrument dexterity, and reduced tremor. This has been particularly beneficial in retroperitoneal surgery, where space limitations and the proximity of critical structures pose challenges. Retroperitoneoscopy provides direct access to the kidney, minimizes bowel manipulation, and offers a clear anatomical view that facilitates enucleation, particularly in posteriorly located renal tumors (10). Complications of the retroperitoneal technique are similar to those of the more common transperitoneal approach and include vascular injuries, visceral injuries, and late complications as incisional hernia (25). Among these, peritoneal tears are the most common during access and dissection, occurring in 5.3% of patients (25,26). They can happen during port insertion or dissection and are preventable by inserting ports under direct vision and tilting the patient laterally. If a tear occurs, it can usually be managed without converting to open surgery by using techniques such as inserting an intravenous cannula to vent CO2, retracting the peritoneum, or intentionally widening the tear to equalize pressure. As an alternative, conversion to a transperitoneal laparoscopic approach may be considered.

The cases described in this article underscore the efficacy and safety of rRAPN.

In Case 1, a 71-year-old female with a small renal mass in the left kidney underwent a successful rRAPN and tumor was removed using a clamp-less technique, minimizing ischemia and preserving renal function. The procedure was completed with minimal blood loss (100 cc) and no significant changes in renal function, highlighting the safety and efficacy of this minimally invasive approach. Similarly, the Case 2, involving a 65-year-old man with a posteriorly located renal mass, demonstrated successful outcomes despite slightly longer operative time and higher blood loss. Both cases highlight the benefits of this minimally invasive technique in terms of preserving renal function and achieving negative surgical margins with favorable oncological results. These cases underscore the potential of robotic-assisted surgery in managing localized RCC, particularly in patients with posterior renal tumors where the retroperitoneal approach is beneficial (27).

An important aspect of our experience was the adoption of a clamp-less technique in rRAPN. In both cases, the renal hilum was dissected preventively for safety, but tumor excision proceeded without hilar clamping. This approach avoids warm ischemia and preserves renal perfusion, though it may slightly increase intraoperative blood loss.

A recent meta-analysis of over 2,000 patients (702 off-clamp vs. 1,373 on-clamp) confirmed this balance: while blood loss was higher in the off-clamp group, major complications were less frequent (1.9% vs. 5.6%), and no differences in renal function or oncologic outcomes were observed (28). Similarly, studies like the CLOCK trial and Anderson et al. showed that off-clamp PN is non-inferior in selected patients, with the added flexibility of intraoperative conversion to clamping when necessary (29-32).

Overall, the choice between on- and off-clamp should be tailored to tumor complexity, surgeon expertise, and individual patient factors.

Comparative studies between tRAPN and rRAPN approaches further support the efficacy of rRAPN.

A large multi-institutional study by Porpiglia et al. revealed that the retroperitoneal approach was associated with shorter operative times, reduced intraoperative complications, faster recovery, and earlier postoperative recovery of bowel function (14). These advantages stem from reduced bowel handling and early vascular pedicle identification. Importantly, postoperative complication rates and positive surgical margin rates were comparable between the two approaches, demonstrating that rRAPN does not compromise oncological safety. Although the tumors in our report were small, available evidence, such as the RECORD 2 study (14), shows that retroperitoneoscopic access is feasible even for larger renal masses, with perioperative outcomes comparable to the transperitoneal approach. Thus, the choice of access largely depends on surgeon experience rather than tumor size.

Despite these compelling benefits, the adoption of rRAPN remains limited in clinical practice (33). Over two decades, only 10% of patients undergoing RAPN were treated using the retroperitoneal approach (27). This underutilization reflects several potential barriers, including limited familiarity with the technique among surgeons, perceived technical challenges in retroperitoneoscopy, and possibly a lack of widespread dissemination of its benefits through training programs and professional guidelines.

Conclusions

The retroperitoneal robot-assisted approach for PN represents a viable and effective surgical option for select cases of RCC, particularly for posterior tumors and in patients with complex abdominal anatomy or a history of prior abdominal surgery. The technique combines the benefits of a minimally invasive approach with the precision of robotic assistance, offering favorable perioperative, functional, and oncological outcomes. However, the broader adoption and dissemination of the retroperitoneal robot-assisted approach require dedicated training and the consolidation of technical expertise, given the steeper learning curve compared to the transperitoneal approach. As the field of urologic oncology continues to evolve, the retroperitoneal robotic approach may play an increasingly pivotal role in managing localized renal tumors, emphasizing the need for ongoing research, education, and refinement of surgical techniques.
