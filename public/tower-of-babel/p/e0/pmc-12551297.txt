Long non-coding RNAs: key orchestrators of macrophage polarization in breast cancer
Authors: Farid Ghorbaninezhad, Ahmad Ghorbani Vanan, Samaneh Rostami, Fatemeh Vaezi, Reyhaneh Doachi, Zahra Aalipour Hafshejani, Yahya Ehteshaminia, Mahsa Nouri, Niki Ghambari Mohammadi, Maryam Talebileili, Safa Tahmasebi, Elham Safarzadeh
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBreast cancer (BC) development is significantly affected by the tumor microenvironment (TME), in which macrophage polarization plays a central role. Macrophages can assume pro-inflammatory M1 or anti-inflammatory M2 phenotypes, with M1 macrophages suppressing and M2 macrophages facilitating tumor growth. Accumulating evidence indicates long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs) as significant regulators of macrophage polarization in BC. LncRNAs can promote either M1 or M2 phenotypes, hence modulating tumor growth and metastasis. Accordingly, this review aims to shed light on the mechanisms by which lncRNAs regulate macrophage polarization and to explore their impact on BC development, offering insights into potential therapeutic strategies targeting the tumor immune microenvironment.Keywords: Macrophage polarization, Breast cancer, M1 macrophage, M2 macrophage, Long non-coding RNAs, LncRNAs

Introduction

Breast cancer (BC) is a heterogeneous malignancy and remains the most prevalent cancer among women worldwide [1]. In 2023, more than 2.3 million new cases were reported, marking a slight increase compared with previous years [2–4]. While lung cancer continues to rank highest in overall incidence and mortality across both sexes, BC has emerged as the leading cause of cancer-related mortality among women [3]. BC incidence varies internationally, with the highest rates observed in high-income and industrialized countries, while mortality remains disproportionately higher in low-income settings [5, 6]. This trend is primarily driven by the western lifestyle, which is linked to poor dietary choices, smoking, high stress, and low levels of physical activity [6]. Although the precise etiology of BC remains unclear, numerous risk factors have been identified. These include biological and demographic variables (such as sex and age), socioeconomic status, hormonal and reproductive influences, genetic predisposition, and lifestyle-related factors. Specifically, hormone replacement therapy, dietary habits, obesity, use of hormonal contraceptives, alcohol consumption, and exposure to ionizing radiation have all been implicated in BC development [5, 7, 8].

Long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs) have a central role in BC development and progression by regulating gene expression, modulating immune cell behavior, and mediating key molecular pathways involved in tumor progression [9]. LncRNAs are comprised of a minimum of 200 nucleotides but are not able to encode proteins. Abnormal expression levels of lncRNAs are linked to several malignant biological reactions, including tumor development, cell expansion, angiogenesis, epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT), and metastasis (Fig. 1) [10, 11]. Growing evidence indicates that the development and progression of cancer are regulated by complex and specialized roles played by lncRNAs, often serving as oncogenes or growth stimulants [12]. Various lncRNAs that promote apoptosis and have oncogenic functions are overexpressed during the advancement of BC [13]. BC overexpresses LncRNA-HOXA11-AS, which contributes to the metastasis and spreading of cancer cells [14]. Research has demonstrated that increased amounts of LncROPM are present in malignant breast tissues and other solid tumors, showing a positive association with the degree of malignancy and poor prognosis in patients with breast tumors. This lncRNA can be utilized as a novel diagnostic and prognostic indicator, in addition to serving as a treatment option for individuals with BC [15].

Involvement of lncRNAs in cancer. The abnormal expression of various lncRNAs in tumor cells can drive tumor progression through several mechanisms. These include destabilizing the genome and elevating mutation rates, inhibiting apoptosis, promoting cell proliferation, upregulating oncogene expression, enhancing cellular metabolism, conferring resistance to anticancer drugs, altering the microenvironment, polarizing macrophages from M1 to the M2 phenotype, suppressing immune cell activity, and facilitating angiogenesis and migration

Macrophages are immunological constituents that play a crucial role in promoting the progression of various types of cancer. In BC, they represent up to 50% of tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs), significantly influencing tumor development. The diverse biological roles of macrophages, including their pro-inflammatory and anti-inflammatory activities, are reflected in TAMs, which display remarkable heterogeneity and functional plasticity. When activated within the tumor microenvironment (TME), macrophages can differentiate into either the classically activated M1 phenotype, which exhibits anti-tumor activity, or the alternatively activated M2 phenotype, which promotes tumor growth [16, 17]. The macrophage polarization phenomenon is a highly regulated process, influenced by numerous internal and external factors that can modify their phenotype [4].

In light of this, a growing body of evidence suggests that lncRNAs are key factors in regulating macrophage polarization within the context of BC [18–20]. In this review, we explore the dynamic roles of lncRNAs in regulating macrophage polarization and their consequential impact on BC progression and metastasis. By dissecting both promotive and inhibitory regulatory pathways, we aim to illuminate the underlying mechanisms governing macrophage behavior within the TME. Our investigation underscores the urgent need to identify more precise biomarkers and effective therapeutic strategies, paving the way for innovative approaches in BC management.

LncRNAs: biogenesis, structure, and function

LncRNAs are RNA transcripts exceeding 200 nucleotides in length and are believed to encompass over 19,000 genes within the human genome [21].

Similar to the synthesis of transcripts that code for proteins, lncRNAs are formed in the cell’s nucleus. In this regard, lncRNAs can be transcribed from almost any region of the human genome, often in orientations distinct from protein-coding genes, depending on their proximity to coding sequences. Certain lncRNAs, known as anti-sense lncRNAs, overlap coding genes on the opposite strand, whereas sense lncRNAs are transcribed from regions that overlap one or more exons of coding transcripts. Additional lncRNAs originate from non-coding DNA sequences, such as enhancers or introns. Some possess their own promoters and regulatory elements and are transcribed from intergenic regions that do not intersect with any previously annotated coding genes [22–24]. These molecules are produced via various mechanisms, including RNase P cleavage, which generates mature 3′ ends that are subsequently capped by snoRNA-protein (snoRNP) complexes to create circular structures [25]. RNA transcription necessitates the involvement of RNA polymerase (Pol), specifically Pol II and Pol III. Nonetheless, it is Pol II that is primarily responsible for executing the transcription of lncRNAs [25].

LncRNAs exert their functions through a variety of molecular mechanisms (Fig. 2) [26, 27]. At the chromatin level, they regulate key processes such as dosage compensation, genomic imprinting, chromatin modification, and remodeling. Beyond chromatin regulation, lncRNAs play crucial roles in metabolic and developmental pathways and modulate gene expression via transcriptional, post-transcriptional, translational, and epigenetic mechanisms through interactions with DNA, RNA, and proteins. They also regulate alternative splicing, cell differentiation, and the cell cycle [26, 27]. LncRNAs also modulate key cellular signaling pathways, including TP53, nuclear factor kappa B (NF-κB), PI3K/AKT, and Notch, through interactions with intracellular signaling molecules [26, 27].

The capacity of lncRNAs to engage with a variety of molecular entities highlights the importance of understanding their localization, local interactions, and structure to predict their functions [11, 26–34]. Accordingly, lncRNAs can localize to various cellular compartments. Following their synthesis and processing, they enter the cytoplasm, where they are believed to undergo distinct sorting processes that either target them to specific organelles or allow them to diffuse throughout the cytoplasm and interact with various RNA-binding proteins (RBPs). Several lncRNAs also remain in the nucleus, where they associate with chromatin. The cellular localization of lncRNAs influences their function: nuclear lncRNAs are essential for transcriptional regulation, chromatin modification, and RNA processing, whereas cytoplasmic lncRNAs contribute to mRNA stability, translation, and modulation of cell signaling pathways [33, 35, 36].

The function of lncRNAs is also predominantly determined by their secondary and tertiary structures, rather than primary sequence alone [28, 37]. The functionality of lncRNAs is closely linked to their structural conformations: Watson-Crick base pairing allows them to interact directly with various nucleic acids, including mRNAs, microRNAs, and DNA [28, 37]. These interactions, together with the ribose backbone, facilitate the formation of secondary structures such as helices, hairpin loops, bulges, and pseudoknots, which in turn support more complex three-dimensional configurations. The formation of thermodynamically stable structures represents a critical stage of lncRNA biogenesis, enabling precise regulation of their activity in response to temporal and spatial cues. Such structural versatility allows lncRNAs to mediate interactions and functions analogous to both RNA and protein molecules [28, 37].

LncRNAs and BC

Given their diverse regulatory functions, lncRNAs have emerged as critical regulators in the pathogenesis of various cancers, including BC, where they influence both primary tumor development and metastatic progression. In this regard, numerous studies have extensively implicated lncRNAs in the context of BC, classifying them into oncogenic and tumor-suppressive types [38–41].

Oncogenic lncRNAs can activate oncogenic pathways or inhibit mechanisms that suppress tumors by causing dysregulation in several signaling pathways. These lncRNAs have become essential regulators in the etiology and course of BC, influencing the illness at different phases from onset to metastasis. Accordingly, these lncRNAs affect resistance to drugs, apoptosis, and the growth of BC cells. Moreover, the oncogenic lncRNAs are pivotal in the metastatic processes of BC, influencing EMT, invasion, and the dynamics of malignant cell movement. They may support the adaptation of these cells to new microenvironments, thereby promoting their survival and proliferation in remote locations [25, 42, 43]. Several lncRNAs, including Metastasis-associated lung adenocarcinoma transcript 1 (MALAT1) [44], HOX transcript antisense intergenic RNA (HOTAIR) [45], and nuclear-enriched abundant transcript 1 (NEAT1) [46], have been well characterized for their functions in BC metastasis and poor outcomes in BC patients. Some of these lncRNAs affect the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway, which is frequently dysregulated in BC and contributes to cell proliferation and survival. Others may influence the Wnt/β-catenin pathway implicated in cell differentiation and metastasis [47, 48]. The role of oncogenic lncRNAs in activating oncogenic pathways or inhibiting tumor-suppressing mechanisms through the dysregulation of various signaling pathways has led to increased interest in their potential as therapeutic targets in the context of BC.

Tumor-suppressive lncRNAs, in contrast, attenuate cancer progression by downregulating oncogenes, suppressing EMT, and promoting apoptotic signaling [49, 50]. Several lncRNAs, including SEAS1 [51] and TRG-AS1 [52], have been identified as negative regulators of BC cell growth and metastatic potential. Given their roles in restraining cancer progression, further research aimed at restoring or enhancing the expression of these inhibitory lncRNAs could provide promising therapeutic avenues for BC treatment.

Owing to their dysregulated expression and pivotal roles in BC, lncRNAs hold promise as biomarkers for early diagnosis and prognosis assessment. Furthermore, therapeutic strategies that target oncogenic lncRNAs or restore the function of tumor-suppressor lncRNAs offer potential avenues for novel BC treatments (Table 1) [35, 53].

Oncogenic LncRNAs

Within the spectrum of lncRNAs, oncogenic lncRNAs constitute a subclass that exhibits enhancer-like properties, thereby contributing to BC initiation and progression (Fig. 3) [54–56]. Among them, NEAT1 is widely expressed in mammalian cells and serves as an essential structural scaffold for the formation of paraspeckles [57, 58]. Upregulation of NEAT1, mediated through modulation of miR-211, promotes epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT) and induces resistance to 5-fluorouracil (5-FU) chemotherapy via the miR-211/HMGA2 axis [59].

Another oncogenic lncRNA, plasmacytoma variant translocation 1 (PVT1), is significantly elevated in both BC cell lines and patient plasma. PVT1 enhances FOXQ1 expression by sponging miR-128-3p, thereby promoting EMT, and also interacts with the UPF1 protein to facilitate tumor progression [60]. Similarly, long intergenic non-protein coding RNA 02273 (LINC02273) drives BC metastasis through direct interaction with heterogeneous nuclear ribonucleoprotein L (hnRNPL). This interaction regulates the expression of anterior gradient protein 2 (AGR2), thereby accelerating metastatic dissemination [61].

Moreover, lncRNA-CDC6 functions as a competitive endogenous RNA (ceRNA) by sponging microRNA-215 (miR-215), thereby relieving miR-215–mediated repression of CDC6 mRNA and ultimately enhancing CDC6 expression. In turn, CDC6 promotes BC cell proliferation and metastasis, highlighting its potential as a prognostic biomarker in clinical practice [62]. In addition, SPINT1 antisense RNA 1 (SPINT1-AS1) is significantly upregulated in the serum of BC patients and is associated with poor prognosis. Mechanistically, SPINT1-AS1 may promote tumor progression and metastasis by sponging let-7a/b/i-5p [63]. The small nucleolar RNA host gene 6 (SNHG6) is also implicated in BC, where its overexpression correlates with enhanced proliferation, migration, and EMT [64]. One of its downstream effectors is laminin subunit gamma 1 (LAMC1), a key extracellular matrix component that is markedly upregulated in BC. LAMC1 facilitates tumor progression by promoting proliferation, migration, invasion, and EMT through activation of the PI3K/AKT pathway [65]. MiR-543, in contrast, acts as a tumor suppressor by inhibiting proliferation and inducing apoptosis. SNHG6 acts as a sponge for miR-543, thereby relieving its repression of LAMC1 and establishing the SNHG6/miR-543/LAMC1 regulatory axis. Consistent with this mechanism, SNHG6 and LAMC1 are upregulated, whereas miR-543 is downregulated, in BC tissues and cell lines. Silencing SNHG6 suppresses proliferation, migration, invasion, and EMT while enhancing apoptosis via the miR-543/LAMC1/PI3K/AKT pathway [65].

Another study has also highlighted the role of LINC00536 in BC. LINC00536 binds to miR-4282, leading to increased expression of centromere protein F (CENPF) [66]. LINC00536 is markedly overexpressed in BC tissues, and higher expression levels are associated with poorer survival outcomes [67–69]. Conversely, miR-4282 is downregulated in several human malignancies, including BC [70]. In BC tissues, especially invasive and metastatic cases, miR-4282 expression is substantially reduced. Restoration of miR-4282 suppresses BC cell proliferation, induces cell-cycle arrest at the S phase, promotes apoptosis, and reduces migratory and invasive capacity [71]. Elevated CENPF expression correlates with poor prognosis and bone metastases in BC, driven by activation of the PI3K/AKT/mTORC1 signaling pathway [72].

Tumor-suppressive LncRNAs

Alongside their supportive functions in expansion and dissemination, lncRNAs also make a suppressive contribution to BC progression and metastasis (Fig. 3).

Patients with elevated levels of lncRNA LINC00460 tend to have a longer overall survival rate [73]. It has been established that LINC00982 functions as a tumor suppressor in several human malignancies. It has been reported that LINC00982 is significantly reduced in BC tumor cells, and low levels are linked to an unfavorable prognosis for patients with BC [74]. Research has shown that LINC00982 hinders cell progression, movement, and penetration by inhibiting the signal transducer and activator of transcription 3 (STAT3)/ NF-κB signaling pathway [74].

Moreover, LINC00982 sponges miR-765 and subsequently increases DPF3 expression, inhibiting tumor growth and aggressive characteristics [74]. Hu et al. have discovered a novel lncRNA SEMA3B-AS1, also known as SEAS1, that plays a role in triple-negative BC (TNBC) tissue progression. SEAS1 downregulation is related to a poor prognosis for TNBC individuals. According to their results, reducing SEAS1 increased TNBC cell expansion, spreading, and invasion, whereas overexpressing it had the reverse impact [51]. TRG-AS1 acts as a ceRNA that suppresses bone metastasis in BC by competitively interacting with miR-877-5p, thereby enhancing the expression of WISP2 [52]. Feng et al. discovered that the lncRNA PCAT19 overexpression in human BC tissues negatively impacts the expression of miR-210, miR-331, miR-769, miR-33a, and miR-301a, along with six tumor-suppressing genes: ADAMTS18, CDH5, EDNRB, PROX1, RASL10A, and SYNM. These factors were identified to develop a ceRNA network, which subsequently inhibited the proliferation of BC cells [75].

Macrophage polarization and BC

Macrophages play critical roles in both the TME and the immune system. Owing to their heterogeneity and plasticity, these long-lived myeloid cells can undergo phenotypic polarization into either the M1 or M2 state in response to specific stimuli within the TME [16, 20].

M1 macrophages, typically associated with anti-microbial and anti-tumor activity, are induced by lipopolysaccharide (LPS), interferon-γ (IFN-γ), and tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α). They produce pro-inflammatory cytokines and chemokines, such as inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS), interleukin-1β (IL-1β), TNF-α, and IL-6, thereby contributing to immune surveillance and tumoricidal activity [76–78]. Notably, BC patients exhibit reduced numbers of M1 macrophages, suggesting impaired anti-tumor immunity [79, 80]. Certain therapeutic agents can restore this phenotype. For example, docetaxel (DTX) promotes M1 polarization by inducing Ccl3 expression through ROS-mediated modulation of the cAMP-response element binding protein (CREB) pathway. This induction enhances macrophage phagocytosis of malignant and cancer stem cells and is essential for the therapeutic efficacy of DTXs [81].

In contrast, M2 macrophages are enriched in BC tissues and are associated with immune suppression and tumor progression [79, 80]. TAMs, which predominantly exhibit the M2 phenotype, are driven by Th2-type cytokines such as IL-4 and IL-13. M2 macrophages are characterized by high expression of arginase-1 and IL-10 and contribute to angiogenesis, invasion, and metastasis through the secretion of various chemokines and growth factors [82–84]. Clinically, advanced BC (stages II–III) is associated with a significantly higher proportion of the M2c subset, which secretes IL-10 and fosters disease progression, underscoring the tumor-promoting nature of anti-inflammatory macrophage phenotypes [85, 86].

Recent evidence has further elucidated molecular drivers of M2 polarization in BC. Zeb1-expressing tumor cells promote M2 polarization via lactate production and activation of the PKA/CREB signaling pathway, suggesting that disrupting this metabolic–immunological interaction may represent a therapeutic strategy [87]. Similarly, ANO6 expression in BC is linked to stromal enrichment, enhanced M2 polarization, and immune evasion. High ANO6 levels correlate with increased immune checkpoint expression, reduced tumor mutational burden, diminished immune infiltration, and poor responses to immunotherapy [88]. In TNBC, YTHDF2 promotes pro-tumoral macrophage polarization by upregulating M2-associated markers while repressing M1-associated markers, as demonstrated by single-cell sequencing [89]. Another regulator, Sohlh2, modulates M2 polarization in TNBC through transcriptional control of liver X receptor alpha (LXRα), a key mediator of lipid efflux in macrophages. TRIM21-mediated degradation of Sohlh2 alters this axis, and targeting the TRIM21/Sohlh2/LXRα pathway has been proposed as a therapeutic strategy to inhibit M2 polarization and TNBC progression [90].

Collectively, these findings underscore the dual roles of macrophages in BC, where M1 macrophages exert anti-tumor effects, while M2 macrophages foster tumor growth and immune evasion. Importantly, lncRNAs have emerged as key regulators of macrophage polarization in BC, a topic discussed in detail in the following section.

LncRNA roles in macrophage polarization in BC

According to recent research, lncRNAs play a crucial role in regulating the expression of specific genes across various diseases, particularly in malignant tumors. These characteristics position lncRNAs as promising indicators for the rapid detection of cancer and the development of effective treatment strategies [91, 92]. Additionally, significant research has focused on how these molecules influence the polarization of macrophages into M1 or M2 phenotypes in different types of cancer, especially BC.

For example, it has been noted that lncRNA PCAT6 induces M2 macrophage polarization, reducing the ratio of M1 macrophages. Its significant increase in cholangiocarcinoma (CCA) patients and TAMs underscores its role in modulating immune responses, involving the generation of ROS, mitochondrial stress, and M2 polarization via RhoA and miR-326 signaling. This lncRNA was already known to be oncogenic, promoting tumor advancement in different types of human cancers [93].

Moreover, PCAT6 is implicated in the advancement of gastric cancer (GC) by competing with miR-30 and also in ovarian cancer by suppressing PTEN [94, 95]. The lncRNA ANCR is notably overexpressed in GC, facilitating its advancement. It achieves this by inhibiting the polarization of macrophages toward the M1 phenotype through the downregulation of FOXO1, which in turn boosts GC cell infiltration and migratory potential [96]. Conversely, lncRNA LOC339059 is significantly decreased in GC tissues and acts as a tumor suppressor by modulating the tumor’s immune microenvironment. It exhibits a negative correlation with immune checkpoint molecules, such as programmed death-ligand 1 (PDL-1) and cytotoxic T-lymphocyte associated protein 4 (CTLA-4), as well as M2 macrophage markers (CD204, CD206, CD163), by downregulating the IL-6/STAT3 signaling pathways, indicating its role in immune regulation within GC tissue [97].

In colorectal cancer (CRC), PCAT6 represses apoptosis via NOL3 regulation through EZH2 [98]. Additionally, Lin et al. found that the lncRNA MIR155HG promotes the progression of CRC and enhances resistance to oxaliplatin by modulating the miR-650/ANXA2 pathway, leading to M2 phenotype polarization. Their research also indicated that silencing either MIR155HG or ANXA2 resulted in decreased M2 phenotype polarization and diminished CRC cell proliferation, movement, infiltration, and resistance to oxaliplatin [99]. However, lncRNA NBR2 is downregulated in CRC, as noted in a study by Xiaoting et al. This study mentions that the overexpression of NBR2 promotes M1 polarization by targeting miR-19a, thereby enhancing the anti-tumor properties of macrophages and inhibiting CRC cell proliferation and migration. Additionally, NBR2 activates the AMPK signaling pathway in TAMs, thereby suppressing pathways such as HIF-1α and AKT/mTOR that promote M2 polarization [100].

Consequently, the role of lncRNAs in macrophage polarization has been firmly demonstrated across multiple malignancies, and BC is no exception. Notably, lncRNAs can influence the polarization process toward either M1 or M2 phenotypes, highlighting their potential significance in BC immunology and therapeutic interventions.

M2 macrophage

Investigations have shown that lncRNAs bind to the genome to modulate the expression of coding proteins, influence the polarization of macrophages towards the M2 phenotype, and impact cell differentiation and proliferation [18, 102, 103].

In this regard, a novel lncRNA, NR_109861 (NR-109), induces macrophages to resemble M2 cells rather than M0 or M1 cells. This lncRNA alters the M2 phenotype via a positive feedback mechanism involving NR_109, FUBP1, and MYC. Researchers knocked down NR-109 in M0 macrophages, resulting in hindered M2-like polarization. Conversely, when they induced NR-109 expression in both M0 and M1 macrophages, there was an increase in polarization toward the M2 phenotype. NR-109 also binds to the C-terminal region of FUBP1 and competes with JVT-1 to prevent the ubiquitin-mediated proteasomal degradation of FUBP1. FUBP1 regulates the MYC factor, leading to increased expression; conversely, MYC promotes NR-109 expression [104].

LncRNA-00657 is another lncRNA implicated in BC progression. Depletion of lncRNA-00657 results in reduced migration, invasion, and cell proliferation in BC cells. It has been determined that this lncRNA plays a crucial part in the polarization of macrophages towards the M2 phenotype, which in turn contributes to cancer invasion [105].

Additionally, Zong and colleagues demonstrated that SNHG1 is another lncRNA that promotes the M2 polarization of macrophages. The researchers discovered that silencing SNHG1 inhibits tumorigenesis and angiogenesis. Furthermore, silencing SNHG1 results in a decrease in the phosphorylation of STAT6, a crucial factor in M2-like polarization [106].

LncRNA-00514 is another important lncRNA that promotes BC malignancy by triggering the M2 polarization according to Tao et al. According to their results, overexpressing pcDNA-mediated lncRNA-00514 and knocking down siRNA-mediated lncRNA-00514, respectively, increases and decreases M2 macrophage polarization and cancer cell invasion. They have also suggested that lncRNA-00514 increases the expression of JAG1 by phosphorylating STAT3. This is because STAT3 levels have a direct effect on JAG1 expression. On the other hand, JAG1 regulates Notch signaling, which produces the cytokines IL-4 and IL-6, converting macrophages into the M2 phenotype [107].

LncRNA-GNAS-AS1 facilitates the increased growth and spreading of ER+ BC cells. This lncRNA interacts with miR-433-3p in such a way that downregulation of GNAS-AS1 leads to overexpression of miR-433-3p, whereas overexpression of GNAS-AS1 inhibits the expression of miR-433-3p. The overexpression of GNAS-AS1 and the subsequent suppression of miR-433-3p lead to the polarization of macrophages into the M2 phenotype, which, in turn, enhances the proliferation and invasion of BC. It is important to note that miR-433-3p functions to inhibit GATA3, a transcription factor essential for M2 polarization. Conversely, overexpressing GNAS-AS1 markedly elevates the GATA3 level, resulting in increased M2 polarization [108].

Zhou et al. reported that lncRNA-p21 has a crucial part in the M2 polarization of macrophages. Their study demonstrated that knocking down this lncRNA increased the percentage of M1 macrophages. Furthermore, the research illustrated that knocking down lncRNA-p21 induced functional reversion in TAMs by promoting MDM2 to play an antagonizing role for TP53. This mechanism ultimately reduced BC invasion and inhibited migration (Fig. 4) (Table 2) [109].

The pros and cons of lncRNA’s clinical application in BC

As highlighted, the impact of lncRNAs on BC pathogenesis is evident, particularly through their ability to interfere with signaling pathways vital for cellular metabolism, proliferation, invasion, metastasis, angiogenesis, apoptosis, and drug resistance [110]. Evidence indicates that lncRNAs demonstrate pronounced abnormal expression in the lesions and precancerous tissues associated with BC patients [111].

As previously highlighted, lncRNAs are significant biomarkers in BC diagnosis and prognosis, as they exhibit enhanced reliability over alternative molecular entities [112, 113]. This phenomenon is especially critical for lncRNAs encapsulated within exosomes, as the lipid bilayer provides a protective environment, leading to increased stability compared to lncRNAs that exist in a free form [110]. Besides, tissue-specific expression profiles of lncRNAs can improve the specificity and sensitivity associated with BC diagnosis.

Evidence indicates that H19 has a sensitivity of 56% for identifying metastatic BC [114], in contrast to CA-15-3, which shows a sensitivity of 42% in the same diagnostic framework [115]. This suggests that including lncRNAs can significantly improve gene signatures for clinical trials. Moreover, it has been demonstrated that the determination of treatment efficiency and BC survival in patients relies on the presence of lncRNAs in body fluids [116, 117].

The ability to block specific oncogenic lncRNAs or reactivate the action of tumor suppressor lncRNAs can open avenues for novel therapeutic approaches in managing BC [35, 53]. Additionally, lncRNAs can be exploited in BC treatment because they influence macrophage polarization. Given that lncRNA expression patterns vary uniquely among different subtypes of BC, there exists an opportunity to leverage their potential in medical applications, thereby enabling customized treatment approaches that reflect the individual lncRNA profiles of patients [118].

Nevertheless, evaluating their limitations is essential when considering the translation and clinical application of lncRNAs in BC. A significant factor is the considerable variability in lncRNA expression patterns observed among diverse patients and even within different tumor regions. This variability is likely to lead to diagnostic errors, thus posing challenges to the standardization of lncRNA-based diagnostic tests [119, 120]. Applying lncRNA-based diagnostic techniques and treatment modalities in clinical settings entails various potential risks. The sensitivity and specificity of lncRNAs as biomarkers may not be sufficient when used alone [110]. Additionally, detecting lncRNAs in the circulatory system is a suitable approach for diagnostic markers; however, there is a lack of adequate and convenient detection methods [118]. It is imperative to meticulously validate the specificity of lncRNA-targeted strategies to avert adverse effects that might lead to unforeseen cellular responses [121].

A notable challenge remains in formulating reliable delivery approaches for lncRNA-targeted therapeutic interventions. Ensuring the safety and effectiveness of these strategies involves the precise delivery of therapeutic agents to the intended tissues, thereby avoiding adverse effects [122]. The limited evidence regarding the biological roles of lncRNAs, beyond their clinical applications, impedes their recognition as standard molecular biomarkers in clinical practice [123].

Conclusions and future prospects

LncRNAs are increasingly recognized as a distinct class of RNA molecules that regulate gene expression as well as a wide range of physiological and pathological processes. Emerging evidence indicates that lncRNAs play essential roles in governing BC invasion, proliferation, metastasis, and progression. The high tissue- and cell subtype-specific expression patterns of lncRNAs render them promising candidates for use as diagnostic, prognostic, predictive, and therapeutic biomarkers in BC.

Macrophages, a key component of the TME, exhibit two major polarization states, M1 and M2, which play opposing roles in tumor biology. M1 macrophages function as potent anti-tumor effector cells, whereas M2 macrophages promote tumor growth and progression. Given the pivotal role of macrophage polarization in cancer, lncRNA-mediated regulation of M1/M2 switching represents a promising therapeutic strategy for BC. Recent studies suggest that several lncRNAs are critically involved in modulating macrophage polarization, thereby enhancing their therapeutic potential. LncRNAs that promote M1 polarization may improve anti-cancer efficacy and serve as favorable prognostic markers, whereas those that induce M2 polarization are associated with poor clinical outcomes. Inhibition of M2-promoting lncRNAs could suppress tumor progression and enhance treatment responses.

Consequently, therapeutic strategies targeting TAMs aim to reprogram them from an M2 to an M1 phenotype, suppress lncRNAs that drive M2 polarization, and augment lncRNAs that promote M1 polarization. These interventions hold considerable potential to redefine future therapeutic strategies for BC. Nonetheless, significant challenges remain regarding the clinical translation of lncRNA-based therapeutics. Addressing issues such as off-target effects and unintended consequences of lncRNA modulation is critical prior to clinical application. Accordingly, future research should focus on rigorous preclinical evaluation and risk assessment to establish both the safety and therapeutic efficacy of these interventions.
