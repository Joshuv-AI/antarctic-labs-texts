Construct validity and reliability of the Abridged Zarit Burden Interview Arabic version in a sample of parents of children with chronic conditions
Authors: Hebah Almulla
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDespite the widespread use of the Zarit Burden Interview (ZBI) to measure caregiver burden, confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) specific to its abridged Arabic version is lacking in the literature. Therefore, this study aimed to examine the construct validity and reliability of the abridged Arabic version of the ZBI among a sample of parents who are caregivers of children with chronic conditions in Saudi Arabia.MethodsThis psychometric study recruited 206 parents who were primary caregivers of at least one child diagnosed with a chronic condition. The participants completed the Arabic version of the 12-item ZBI. Construct validity was assessed via CFA, and internal consistency reliability was computed using Cronbach’s alphas.ResultsThe12-item model demonstrated suboptimal fit (χ²(53) = 143.13, CFI = 0.886, TLI = 0.858, RMSEA = 0.091, SRMR = 0.077). After modifying the model based on factor loadings and modification indices, a 10-item model had good fit (χ²(33) = 58.23, CFI = 0.963, TLI = 0.950, RMSEA = 0.061, SRMR = 0.050). Internal consistency reliability showed acceptable Cronbach’s alphas for the total scale and the two subscales (α = 0.84, 0.85, and 0.74).ConclusionsThe 10-item Arabic ZBI demonstrated acceptable construct validity and reliability among parents of children with chronic conditions. This brief, culturally appropriate tool may support routine assessment of caregiver burden among parents of pediatric patients.Trail registrationNot applicable.

Background

Parents play a key role as primary caregivers when their children are diagnosed with chronic health conditions. The ongoing multidimensional strain associated with caring for an ill child who cannot care for themselves beyond typical parenting responsibilities is known as caregiver burden [1]. Additional physical and psychological illness demands when a child is ill disrupt family routines and finances, causing emotional distress, which jointly negatively impacts parents’ well-being and quality of life [2, 3]. A systematic review and meta-analysis have highlighted the need for early screening of at-risk parents and focused interventions to address caregiver burden [4], underscoring the need for valid and reliable tools to facilitate early identification.

Given the important implications of caregiver burden, the development of valid and reliable measurement instruments is of critical importance to support research and clinical practice. Despite an increased focus on caregiver burden in parents of children with chronic conditions, a number of gaps in the psychometric evaluation of existing instruments remain.

The ZBI is one of the most widely used instruments for assessing caregiver burden across diverse clinical populations [1, 5]. The ZBI was originally developed by Zarit et al. in 1980 to assess caregivers of individuals with cognitive impairment, particularly those with dementia. The original 29-item ZBI was later shortened to a 22-item version [6, 7]. Shorter version of the ZBI with 12 items were subsequently developed by Bédard et al. [8] to reduce respondent burden while maintaining psychometric strength. This abridged version is one of the most frequently used forms of the ZBI and preserves the underlying dimensions of the original scale. The abridged 12-item ZBI has been widely translated and psychometrically evaluated across various cultural contexts and caregiving populations. Previous studies confirmed its reliability among caregivers of individuals with dementia in Cantonese [9], Thai [10], and Spanish [11] versions. In addition, the Thai version has been validated among caregivers in palliative care settings [12]. Likewise, the English version was validated among Canadian caregivers of individuals with dementia [13] and among caregivers of terminally ill cancer patients [14]. However, previous validation studies have been conducted almost exclusively among caregivers of adult patients, particularly in dementia, palliative, and oncology settings. Previous validation studies across different caregiving populations and cultural contexts have consistently supported a two-factor structure of the abridged ZBI consisting of personal strain and role strain [6, 7, 12, 13, 15].

Although several studies have examined caregiver burden in parents of children with chronic conditions in the Arabic language context, there is a lack of psychometrically validated instruments specifically developed or validated for this population. Numerous studies in the Middle East context have reported high levels of caregiver burden among parents of children with chronic conditions [16–18]. However, these studies used different measurement tools, and in many cases these tools were not formally validated, which is an important measurement gap. Based on the previous cross-cultural adaptation efforts, an Arabic version of the short form of abridged ZBI has been developed using standard cross-cultural translation processes to facilitate its use with the Arabic-speaking population [15, 19]. Initial psychometric assessment among caregivers of cancer patients demonstrated acceptable reliability, concurrent validity, and a two-factor structure consistent with prior literature [15]. Nonetheless, this validation effort was limited to a specific caregiving population and was based on an exploratory m factor analysis methodology.

To date, the construct validity of the Arabic version has not been examined using CFA, nor has it been explored in a population of parents of children with chronic conditions. Given that prior studies across diverse caregiving populations have consistently supported the two-factor structure of the abridged ZBI, CFA is an appropriate approach to examine whether this established structure can be replicated in this new population. Therefore, further validation using CFA in a different caregiving context is warranted to confirm the applicability of the instrument.

Aims

This study aimed to examine psychometric properties of the abridged Arabic version of the ZBI in a sample of parents caring for children with chronic conditions in Saudi Arabia. Specifically, the study had three aims: (1) to assess construct validity using CFA based on prior evidence [15]; (2) to evaluate internal consistency reliability using coefficient alphas; and (3) to provide preliminary evidence of known-group validity.

Methods

Design, setting, and sample

Data for the cross-sectional psychometric analysis were obtained from a larger completed study that, in addition to assessing caregiver burden, included a broader pool of variables [20]. Convenience sampling was used to collect data from tertiary hospitals in Saudi Arabia with pediatric inpatient and outpatient units.

Eligible participants were parents of at least one child with a chronic condition who had been the child’s main caregiver for at least a year and could read and understand Arabic to complete the scale items. Parents who did not have main responsibility of taking care of their child and those who could not read and understand the Arabic scale items were excluded.

Data collection procedure

Data were collected over a four-month period via a data collection tool designed in QuestionPro.

Measures

The Abridged Zarit Burden Interview – Arabic version (ZBI-A)

The Abridged Zarit Burden Interview (ZBI-12) is a 12-item self-report instrument used to assess caregivers’ perceived burden of caring for individuals unable to care for themselves [8]. Parents responded to each of the 12 items of the scale, which were rated on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 0 (never), 1 (rarely), 2 (sometimes), 3 (quite frequently), to 4 (almost always), yielding a total score of 0 to 48. Higher total scores indicated higher levels of caregiver burden. Total scores are typically categorized into three suggested scoring levels: if the score is (0–10), it indicates no burden to mild burden, if the score is (11–20), it indicates moderate burden, and if the score is above 20, it indicates high burden [5, 8]. The scale consists of two correlated factors: personal strain (nine items) and role strain (three items) [8]. All scale items’ wording were consistently modified using “your child” instead of “your relative” to reflect a child-focused caregiving context. The original English version of the ZBI-12 has excellent reliability, with Cronbach’s alpha coefficients of 0.88 for the total scale, 0.89 for the personal strain subscale, and 0.77 for the role strain subscale [8]. The Arabic translation used in the present study after obtaining permission, also had adequate reliability, with Cronbach’s alphas of 0.77 (total scale), 0.81 (personal strain), and 0.74 (role strain) [15].

Ethical consideration and data collection

All procedures involving human participants were performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Ethics approval was obtained from the selected hospitals. Research assistants (RAs) within the study sites were trained to screen for, invite and enroll eligible caregiver parents. During recruitment, parents were asked to formally confirm their ability to understand Arabic.

Once a parent agreed to participate, the RA explained the aim of the study in-person and provided a QR code generated via QuestionPro to electronically access the study data collection tool using their personal devices. For those who did not have a suitable device or whose devices have a limited screen size, a tablet (iPad) was offered to complete the study tool. In addition, invitation flyers containing the QR code were posted in the in-hospital pediatric units and clinics’ waiting areas to emphasize parents’ self-enrollment. A cover letter outlining the key elements of the study, eligibility, and informed consent associated with checking agreement boxes was provided before rating the ZBI scale items. Parents were assured that their participation would be anonymous and that identification codes rather than personal information would be used. Moreover, using QuestionPro features to design the data collection tool helped ensure complete responses and minimized missing data.

Statistical analysis

IBM SPSS (version 28) and Mplus (version 8.0) were used for data analysis. Descriptive statistics, including means, standard deviations, frequencies, percentages, skewness, and kurtosis, were computed to obtain sample characteristics and distribution of the ZBI-12 scale items. To examine known-group validity, differences in ZBI scores across parent gender, caregiving duration, and educational level were assessed using independent-samples t-tests. CFA was performed without a priori EFA, as the primary objective was to confirm the well-established two-factor theoretical structure within the current study population [20, 21].

The sample size was determined based on recommended guidelines of five to ten participants per estimated parameter CFA [22]. Because the most complex model assessed in this study involved estimating 28 parameters (18 path coefficients and 10 covariances), approximately 104–280 participants were required for robust model estimation; our sample of 206 parents the minimum recommended threshold. The suitability of the data for factor analysis was confirmed by a Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin (KMO) value of 0.861 and a significant Bartlett’s Test of Sphericity (χ² = 830.288, df = 66, p < 0.001), after which CFA was conducted.

All CFA assumptions were verified. Items had linear relationships with their respective factors, and error terms (uniqueness) were uncorrelated with factors [21]. The expected values of uniqueness equaled zero, and the data were adequately represented by a single covariance matrix and mean vector [21]. Additionally, participants’ responses were independent [21].

We specified CFA models and evaluated their fit. In Model 1, we tested the original two-factor structure [8], with all 12 items loading on the two correlated factors [8, 15]. In Model 2, we refined Model 1 by removing two items with weak factor loadings. Then, we assessed factor covariances, standardized path coefficients, and modification indices in each model [21]. In Model 3, which was the final modified model, we adjusted the modification indices and correlated residuals based on the results of Model 2. Although the ZBI items are ordinal, maximum likelihood estimation was employed, and indicators were treated as continuous data. Methodological evidence suggests that Likert-scale items with five or more response categories can be treated as approximately continuous, with comparable performance to ordinal estimators under many conditions [23]. Latent factors were standardized by setting the mean and variance to 0 and 1, respectively.

Model fit was evaluated using established indices: Chi-square (χ²) test (p > 0.05), Root Mean Square Error of Approximation (RMSEA) ≤ 0.06, Standardized Root Mean Square Residual (SRMR) ≤ 0.08, Tucker-Lewis Index (TLI) ≥ 0.95, and Comparative Fit Index (CFI) ≥ 0.95 [20].

Internal consistency reliability for the two subscales (personal strain and role strain) and the total scale was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha calculated using IBM SPSS (version 28). An alpha coefficient of 0.70 or greater indicated acceptable reliability [24]. The reliability outcomes of the original 12-item model (Model 1) were compared with those of the final modified 10-item model (Model 3).

To examine known-group validity, ZBI total scores were compared across selected demographic and caregiving-related groups previously associated with caregiver burden in prior studies including parent gender, caregiving duration, and education level. No significant differences were found between mothers and fathers (p = 0.373), between parents with shorter and longer caregiving duration (p = 0.310), or between educational levels (p = 0.261). These results suggest that caregiver burden was not significantly different between the assessed groups in the present sample.

Construct validity

Table 3 summarizes the common fit indices of the three CFA models. The original 12-item two-factor model (Model 1) demonstrated suboptimal fit to the data (χ² = 143.13, df = 53, CFI = 0.886, TLI = 0.858, RMSEA = 0.091, SRMR = 0.077). Inspection of standardized factor loadings showed that items 3 and 10 had comparatively low factor loadings (0.40 and 0.42, respectively). Therefore, these items were removed, resulting in a 10-item model (Model 2) that showed improved fit (χ² = 81.00, df = 34, CFI = 0.932, TLI = 0.910, RMSEA = 0.082, SRMR = 0.054).

Internal consistency reliability

Table 4 compares the reliability of the total ZBI scale and its two factors in the original 12-item model (Model 1) and the final 10-item model (Model 3). The total ZBI scale in both the 12-item and 10-item models had good reliability (α = 0.841). Moreover, the personal strain factor showed high reliability in both models (α = 0.854). Discussion

This is the first study to systematically examine the construct validity and reliability of the abridged Arabic version of the ZBI using CFA in a sample of Arabic-speaking parents caring for children with chronic conditions. The results of this study build on Bachner’s [15] initial psychometric work, including the translation process and a preliminary examination of the factor structure via EFA [15]. In contrast, we evaluated the scale in a different population to address an important measurement gap in the pediatric caregiving context.

The CFA findings in this study are consistent with the ZBI theoretical framework, which supports the two-factor structure of personal strain and role strain [6]. This is consistent with the findings of a recent systematic review, which emphasized that caregiver burden is multidimensional rather than a single factor [25]. Furthermore, these findings agree with the two-factor solution reported in a CFA using the abridged ZBI version among Thai informal caregivers of palliative care patients [12]. However, variation in the dimensionality has been reported across psychometric studies using the ZBI. For instance, a unidimensional structure was identified among Peruvian caregivers of persons with intellectual disabilities [26], a three- to four-factor structure among Thai caregivers of Alzheimer’s patients [10], and a five-factor structure among Chinese caregivers of schizophrenia patients [9]. These conclusions jointly suggest that caregiver burden is not unidimensional, although the number of factors varies across contexts.

In contrast, the two-factor structure identified in the current study offers a parsimonious yet theoretically relevant representation of caregiver burden. This may reflect the caregiving context for parents of children with chronic conditions, in which burden is primarily experienced through emotional strain and role-related needs, corresponding to the core domains of the ZBI. The different factor structures across studies are probably influenced by the ZBI version, the caregiving context, and cultural background. This variation is further supported by substantial heterogeneity across populations and context in a recent meta-analysis using the ZBI short version [27].

Regarding scale indicators, the 12-item ZBI model with its underlying two factors demonstrated a suboptimal fit, whereas the refined 10-item model showed an acceptable fit. Continuous psychometric testing and scale refinement align with the historical development of shorter ZBI versions, in which items were either removed or retained based on their conceptual clarity and psychometric analyses [7, 8].

The removal of two items (3 and 10) suggests that some ZBI items function differently in various caregiving contexts. Similar to previous psychometric studies that used CFA and found variability in construct validity across caregiving roles, clinical populations, and cultural backgrounds [9, 10]. These findings, along with our results, demonstrate that caregiver burden depends on the context and that one cannot assume structural invariance across populations.

The removal of item 3 (“Do you feel angry when you are around your child?”) may be explained by its floor effect and weak loading, reflecting limited variability. In the Saudi Islamic context, expressing anger toward a sick child may be constrained by social desirability and cultural norms, where family caregiving is ingrained as a religious responsibility and a lifelong commitment associated with patience and spiritual reward [28]. This is consistent with evidence revealing that caregivers in Saudi Arabia may be reluctant to endorse burden and negative emotions toward their family care recipient due to cultural and religious expectations [29]. Consequently, negative affect may be underreported.

Similar culturally based reasoning may account for the removal of item 10 (“Do you feel uncertain about what to do about your child?”) and the elevated mean of item 11 (“Do you feel you should be doing more for your child?”). Because caregiving is strongly shaped by familial, cultural, and religious norms, caregivers may be less likely to acknowledge uncertainty in their roles, thereby increasing their sense of responsibility [28, 29]. This may explain why they feel that they are not doing enough, while also being less likely to endorse uncertainty. This divergence may contribute to the weak performance of some items and the inflation of others, introducing construct-irrelevant variance and influencing the interpretation of ZBI scores in this population.

Allowing correlation between the residuals of ZBI items (1 and 2) further enhanced model fit. Both items reflect overlapping content related to time demands and competing responsibilities, which are particularly salient for parents who, in addition to their work and family roles, must manage the care of their ill child. Previous conceptual work indicated that role conflict and sustained caregiving pressure are core elements of caregiver burden [1], thereby theoretically supporting the plausibility of this correlation.

The satisfactory internal consistency reliability is consistent with prior studies on ZBI validation [8, 12, 13]. Improvement in the reliability of the role strain factor (alpha coefficient from 0.651 to 0.736) after removing items with weak factor loadings suggests a more coherent measurement of this construct among parent caregivers. The removed item 10 conceptually shows weak alignment with the role strain factor, as it measures perceived uncertainty about how to manage the child’s condition rather than the responsibilities and demands that define caregiving role strain. Because of this poor conceptual fit, removing such items is known to increase item homogeneity and thereby enhance internal consistency reliability [30].

Importantly, this study expands the validation of the abridged ZBI to parents of children with chronic conditions in and Arabic-speaking population. In Saudi Arabia and other Arabic-speaking countries, recent studies have reported elevated burden among parents of children with chronic conditions [16, 18] and paid attention to the importance of burden assessment in this population.

The findings of this study support the modified Arabic version of the ZBI-10 as a brief and psychometrically valid tool for assessing caregiver burden among parents.

This study has several limitations. First, while CFA was used here based on previous evidence of a two-factor structure of the ZBI in different caregiving populations, this factor structure has not been tested in a sample of parents of children with chronic conditions. Thus, the use of CFA here assumes a degree of structural generalizability but does not allow for population-specific variation in how parents experience caregiver burden. Exploratory or mixed analytic approaches may be a fruitful area for future research.

Second, the data used for psychometric testing were collected at a single time point and were limited to Saudi parents which may affect the generalizability of the findings. Third, convergent and discriminant validity were not assessed. Finally, although the correlated residuals within the modified model are theoretically justified, they should be cross-validated in independent samples. Future studies should use longitudinal data to assess test-retest reliability and examine diverse contexts among Arabic-speaking populations to enhance generalizability of the findings.

Conclusions

The results of the present study provide empirical evidence supporting the modified Arabic version of the ZBI-10 among parents of children with chronic conditions. The proposed revised two-factor model demonstrated adequate construct validity and reliability. The Arabic ZBI-10 is a brief, psychometrically robust, and culturally appropriate tool for identifying caregiver burden in parents of children with chronic conditions. Its use may facilitate early identification of at-risk caregivers and inform supportive interventions. Future studies should replicate these findings in a broader Arabic population and examine additional psychometric properties. Furthermore, researchers should consider evaluating the psychometric performance of the Arabic ZBI within specific diagnostic subgroups (e.g., cancer, cerebral palsy, or other chronic conditions), as caregiving experiences and demands may differ across illness types.
