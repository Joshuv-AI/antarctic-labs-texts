Comparative efficacy and safety of immune checkpoint inhibitors combined with antiangiogenic agents for unresectable hepatocellular carcinoma in patients with Child-Pugh A versus B cirrhosis: a single-center, retrospective study
Authors: Dan Liu, Zirong Yang, Lilin Wang, Lina Ma, Wei Hou, Shan Ren, Sujun Zheng, Junfeng Lu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundImmune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) combined with antiangiogenic agents (AAs) have been recommended as the first-line systemic treatment for unresectable hepatocellular carcinoma (uHCC). However, most Phase III clinical trials have excluded patients with Child-Pugh B (CP B) cirrhosis. This study aimed to evaluate the efficacy and safety of ICIs combined with AAs in uHCC patients with CP B cirrhosis and compare the outcomes with those in patients with Child-Pugh A (CP A) cirrhosis.MethodsThe uHCC patients who received ICIs plus AAs between September 2020 and November 2024 at Beijing You ‘an Hospital were retrospectively analyzed. Tumor response and treatment related adverse events (TRAEs) were compared between the patients with CP A and CP B cirrhosis to assess the efficacy and safety of the treatment.ResultsA total of 94 patients were included in the study, including 63 patients with CP A cirrhosis and 31 with CP B cirrhosis. For the entire cohort, the objective response rate (ORR), disease control rate (DCR), median progression-free survival (mPFS) and median overall survival (mOS) were 44.7%, 72.3%, 6.3 months, and 28.3 months. The four indicators mentioned above were 50.8% vs. 32.3% (P = 0.089), 76.2% vs. 64.5% (P = 0.234), 6.6 months vs. 5.8 months (P = 0.524) and 39.2 months vs. 15.9 months (P = 0.035) in the CP A group and CP B group, respectively, with only mOS showing a statistically significant difference between the two groups. The Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group performance status score (ECOG PS) 2, prior treatment and non-simultaneous locoregional therapies (LRTs) were identified as independent predictors of worse ORR. The CP B and Barcelona Clinic Liver Cancer (BCLC) stage C/D were identified as independent predictors of poorer OS. A total of 93.6% of patients experienced at least one TRAE of any grade, with 27.7% experiencing grade ≥ 3 TRAEs. The incidence and severity of TRAEs were similar between the patients with CP A and CP B cirrhosis. Additionally, Child-Pugh score improved in 32.3% of patients with CP B cirrhosis after systemic treatment.ConclusionThe combination of ICIs and AAs showed favorable clinical benefits, safety and tolerability in uHCC patients with CP B cirrhosis, with some patients experiencing improvements in Child-Pugh score. More well-designed studies with larger sample sizes are warranted to further determine the efficacy and safety of systemic therapy in this population.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12885-025-15126-4.

Introduction

Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) is one of the most common malignant tumors worldwide [1]. It is the fifth most common cancer and the third leading cause of cancer-related mortality globally [2, 3]. The treatment of HCC involves a multidisciplinary therapy with various therapeutic options. Common treatment methods include hepatectomy, liver transplantation, ablation therapy, endovascular interventional therapy, radiation therapy, and systemic therapy. Due to the insidious onset of HCC, most patients are diagnosed at advanced stages, losing the opportunity for curative treatment.

Systemic therapy is increasingly playing an important role in the treatment of unresectable hepatocellular carcinoma (uHCC) in recent years. Immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs), such as programmed death-1/programmed death ligand-1 (PD-1/PD-L1) inhibitors or cytotoxic T lymphocyte-associated protein-4 (CTLA-4) inhibitors, in combination with antiangiogenic agents (AAs), have been widely used in uHCC patients, which can control disease progression, extend the median overall survival (OS) [4–6]. However, most previous phase III clinical trials primarily included uHCC patients with Child-Pugh A (CP A) cirrhosis, excluding the majority of uHCC patients with Child-Pugh B (CP B) cirrhosis [6–9]. In real-world clinical practice, there is limited research on the efficacy and safety of ICIs combined with AAs in uHCC patients with CP B cirrhosis, and the needs of systemic therapy for uHCC patients with CP B cirrhosis remain inadequately addressed.

In the retrospective study, we investigated the efficacy and safety of ICIs combined with AAs in uHCC patients with CP B cirrhosis, analyzed the risk factors for tumor response and survival and compared the results with those of uHCC patients with CP A cirrhosis.

Materials and methods

Study subjects

This retrospective study enrolled patients with uHCC who received ICIs in combination with AAs at First Department of the Hepatology Center, Beijing You ‘an Hospital, between September 2020 and June 2024. The observation deadline was November 31, 2024. Inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) age ≥ 18 years; (2) diagnosis of uHCC confirmed by imaging or histopathology; (3) received ICIs combined with AAs at least three months; (4) presence of at least one measurable tumor lesion based on modified response evaluation criteria in solid tumors (mRECIST) [10, 11]. These patients were followed up for at least 12 weeks until death, or the observation endpoint, or loss to follow-up. Patients were excluded if they had severe comorbidities (such as severe cardiac and renal insufficiency) or malignancies in other organ systems or history of organ transplantation, or if they had cytomegalovirus, epstein-barr virus or human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) co-infection. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Beijing You ‘an Hospital, Capital Medical University (Approval No. [2022]112), with informed consent waived due to its retrospective study.

Therapeutic drugs

The ICIs in this study were administered intravenously every 3 weeks at the following doses: sintilimab (Innovent Biologics, China) 200 mg, camrelizumab (Hengrui Pharma, China) 200 mg, atezolizumab (Roche, Switzerland) 1200 mg. Bevacizumab (Innovent Biologics, China) was initially administered intravenously at a dose of 7.5 mg/kg, followed by 15 mg/kg for subsequent doses every 3 weeks. Lenvatinib (Eisai, Japan) was administered at a dose of 8 mg/day (body weight < 60 kg) or 12 mg/day (body weight ≥ 60 kg). Sorafenib (Bayer Schering Pharma, Germany) was administered orally at a dose of 400 mg, twice daily. The choice of systemic therapy was based on the following criteria: (1) Chinese clinical practice guidelines for hepatocellular carcinoma diagnosis and treatment [12, 13]; (2) endoscopic assessment of gastroesophageal varices (GOV), wherein patients with absent or mild GOV received ICIs combined with bevacizumab, while those with moderate-to-severe GOV received ICIs plus oral antiangiogenic agents; and (3) patient’s medical insurance coverage. Systemic therapy continued until disease progression, intolerable toxicity, or death occurred. During treatment, dose adjustment was made based on the patient’s tolerance and the toxicity of the drugs. For patients eligible for transcatheter arterial chemoembolization (TACE), ablation, or radiotherapy, the decision was made after thorough multidisciplinary evaluation. Patients with chronic hepatitis B (CHB) were treated with nucleos(t)ide analogues (NAs) for antiviral therapy.

Tumor response and assessments

Dynamic computed tomography or magnetic resonance imaging was performed every 12 weeks from baseline. Tumor responses were assessed by mRECIST. Complete response (CR) was defined as the complete disappearance of enhancement in all target lesions during the arterial phase, with the short axis of all target lymph nodes reduced to less than 10 mm. Partial response (PR) was defined as a ≥ 30% reduction in the sum of the maximum diameters of target lesions (enhanced during the arterial phase) compared to the baseline sum of maximum diameters, while progressive disease (PD) was defined as a ≥ 20% increase in the sum of the maximum diameters of target lesions (enhanced during the arterial phase) compared to the smallest sum of maximum diameters observed during the treatment, with an absolute increase of at least 5 mm, or the appearance of new lesions. Stable disease (SD) was defined as no significant change in the sum of diameters between PR and PD. The objective response rate (ORR) was the sum of CR and PR, and the disease control rate (DCR) was defined as the sum of CR, PR, and SD. Progression free survival (PFS) and OS were defined as the time from the initiation of treatment to disease progression or death from any cause. The treatment related adverse events (TRAEs) during the treatment were recorded and graded according to the National Cancer Institute (NCI) Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events V 5.0 (CTCAE 5.0).

Statistical analyses

Baseline characteristics and TRAEs were expressed using descriptive statistics. Categorical data were analyzed using the Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test. Continuous variables were presented as the mean ± standard deviation or median (interquartile range). The Kaplan-Meier method was used to estimate OS and PFS, with comparisons made using the log-rank test. The Cox proportional hazards regression model and Logistic regression model were employed to identify factors associated with survival and tumor response, with all covariates showing a P value < 0.05 in univariate analysis subsequently included in multivariate analysis. A two-tailed P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS version 25.0 and GraphPad Prism version 10.1.2.

Results

Baseline characteristics

A total of 143 patients received ICIs combined with AAs, among whom 94 patients (63 with CP A cirrhosis and 31 with CP B cirrhosis, 46, 17, 14, 8 and 9 patients with CP scores of 5, 6, 7, 8 and 9, respectively) met the inclusion criteria for this study. The mean follow-up duration was 22.6 ± 11.2 months. Most patients (78.7%) had an Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group performance status (ECOG PS) score of 0 or 1. Eighty patients (85.1%) had a history of CHB. Sixty patients (63.8%) were classified as Barcelona Clinic Liver Cancer (BCLC) stage C or D. At the end of follow-up, 52 patients (55.3%) had died, including 32 (50.8%) in the CP A group and 20 (64.5%) in the CP B group (P = 0.208). Sintilimab was the most frequently administered (n = 64, 68.1%), followed by camrelizumab (n = 25, 26.6%) and atezolizumab (n = 5, 5.3%). Among the AAs, lenvatinib was the most commonly prescribed (n = 71, 75.5%), followed by sorafenib (n = 16, 17.0%) and bevacizumab (n = 7, 7.5%). Additionally, appropriate locoregional therapies (LRTs) were administered to eligible patients, with 60.6% receiving TACE, 26.6% undergoing ablation, and 1.1% receiving radiotherapy. All the 80 patients with CHB received NAs antiviral therapies throughout the treatment period, with the following regimen distribution: entecavir (n = 38), tenofovir alafenamide (n = 13), tenofovir disoproxil fumarate (n = 7), ETV + TAF combination (n = 15), and ETV + TDF combination (n = 7). Tumor response and survival

Based on the mRECIST criteria for assessing tumor response, 7 patients (7.4%) achieved CR, 35 patients (37.2%) achieved PR, 26 patients (27.7%) had SD, and 26 patients (27.7%) had PD. The ORR and DCR were 44.7% and 72.3%, respectively, both of which were higher in the CP A group than those in the CP B group (50.8% vs. 32.3%, P = 0.089; 76.2% vs. 64.5%, P = 0.234), but no statistically significant difference was observed (Table 2). However, the univariate and multivariate regression analyses identified the ECOG PS 2, prior treatment and non-simultaneous LRTs as independent predictors of worse ORR (see Additional file 1). The mOS in the CP A group was significantly higher than that in the CP B group (39.2 months [95% CI 23.2–55.1] vs. 15.9 months [95% CI 10.6–21.1], P = 0.035) (Fig. 2), while the mPFS was slightly higher in the CP A group compared to the CP B group (6.6 months [95% CI 4.8–8.5] vs. 5.8 months [95% CI 3.8–7.8], P = 0.524) (Fig. 2). To avoid collinearity and overadjustment, the factor of macrovascular invasion, a component of the BCLC staging system, was excluded from the multivariate OS model.

Safety

In the whole cohort, 88 patients (93.6%) experienced at least one TRAE of any grade, with liver injury being the most common TRAE (69.1%). Twenty-six patients (27.7%) experienced grade ≥ 3 TRAEs, with upper gastrointestinal bleeding (6.4%) being the most frequent (see Additional file 3). There was no significant difference in the overall TRAE incidence and the grade ≥ 3 TRAE incidence between the CP A and CP B groups. Among the 6 patients with ALBI grade 3 liver function, only 1 patient experienced one grade 3 TRAE, with platelet count dropping to 14 × 109/L and no serious consequence. Sixteen patients (17.0%) discontinued or interrupted treatment due to TRAEs, including 12 patients (19.0%) in the CP A group and 4 patients (12.9%) in the CP B group (P = 0.456). The treatment discontinuation rates in the CP A and CP B groups were 9.5% and 3.2% (P = 0.419), respectively. Overall, both groups of patients exhibited good tolerability. And none of the patients with CHB experienced hepatitis B virus (HBV) reactivation.

Child-Pugh score improvement

In this cohort of 31 uHCC patients with CP B cirrhosis, effective virological control was maintained (undetectable or low-level HBV DNA through preemptive antiviral therapy) in all the patients with HBV-related HCC. Ten patients (32.3%) demonstrated CP score improvement (B→A), distributed as: CP-7 (n = 4), CP-8 (n = 4), CP-9 (n = 2). This subgroup exhibited significantly higher ORR versus non-improvers (60.0% vs. 19.0%, P = 0.040), with tumor responses comprising 6 partial responses, 3 stable disease, and 1 progressive disease (see Additional file 4). Notably, hepatic function improved prior to the tumor response. Among the 6 patients with ALBI grade 3, 2 patients achieved CP scores improvement (see case 5 and case 8 in Additional file 4).

Discussion

The synergistic anti-tumor effects of combining ICIs with AAs are well-established and widely acknowledged in the literature [14]. These effects have been substantiated in numerous clinical trials [6, 9, 15–18]. This study further supports the efficacy of this combination therapy in patients with uHCC in real-world settings. The mPFS of 6.3 months observed in this study is consistent with the results of prior clinical trials (4.6–14.3 months). Additionally, the mOS appeared to be longer (28.3 months vs. 18.2–21.2 months). This discrepancy may be attributed to the lower proportion of patients with BCLC stage C/D HCC (63.8% vs. 74.2–82.0%) and distant metastasis (43.6% vs. 61.3–63.0%), and more patients receiving LRTs in this study. BCLC stage C/D was independently associated with worse OS. Moreover, patients with distant metastasis exhibit a trend towards shorter mOS in this study. Other real-world studies have similarly demonstrated that BCLC stage C and the presence of distant metastasis are correlated with poorer OS [19, 20]. Furthermore, the ORR in this study was also promising, showing a significant improvement compared to previous clinical trials (44.7% vs. 21.0–36.1%) [6, 9, 15–18]. Patients with ECOG PS 2 were usually excluded in clinical trials, while this study included these patients, and ECOG PS 2 was identified as an independent predictor of lower tumor response. Previous real-world data also indicated that impaired ECOG PS was associated with a poor treatment response rate in patients with HCC or lung cancer [20, 21]. Another independent predictor of worse ORR is prior treatment (including surgical resection, TACE, ablation and radio therapy in this study), which has been rarely reported in previous studies. The observed manifestation potentially stems from the intrinsically aggressive recurrence behavior of HCC, which necessitates iterative locoregional interventions, further studies are warranted to elucidate the underlying pathophysiological drivers. The subgroup analyses were conducted because of the heterogeneous mix of ICI and AA combinations included in this study, which indicated no statistically significant associations with ORR or OS. However, this finding may not accurately reflect the true clinical scenario. Due to the limited sample sizes in these subgroups, further studies with expanded cohorts are necessary to determine whether significant differences in efficacy and survival outcomes exist between them.

To minimize the confounding effects of cirrhosis-related mortality, most previous trials excluded the majority of patients with CP B cirrhosis [6, 9, 15–18]. However, managing uHCC patients with CP B cirrhosis remains a significant challenge for clinicians in real-world settings. Our study demonstrated that patients with CP B cirrhosis achieved statistically comparable efficacy in terms of mPFS, ORR and DCR when compared to those with CP A cirrhosis, although the outcomes were slightly inferior. This may be attributed to the more severe immunosuppression within the liver microenvironment associated with advanced hepatic dysfunction [22], as well as the more extensive LRTs received by patients with CP A cirrhosis [23]. The comparability of these results between CP A group and CP B group is consistent with that reported in a systematic review about atezolizumab-bevacizumab in patients with uHCC, except mPFS, significantly shorter in CP B group (P < 0.001) [24]. Notably, the mOS for the CP B group was significantly shorter than that of the CP A group, also the CP B status was independently associated with poorer OS, consistent with the two systematic reviews about patients with advanced HCC receiving systemic therapies [23, 24]. This difference is likely closely associated with the poor survival outcomes of patients with decompensated cirrhosis. Interestingly, a more favorable mOS was observed in patients with CP B cirrhosis in this study compared to other cohorts reported [23, 24]. This may be attributed to the moderately impaired liver function (with a median bilirubin level of 32.5 µmol/l and a mean albumin level of 31.5 g/l), a high percentage of patients with ALBI grade 2 (80.6%) and a favorable tumor response (32.3%) in this CP B cohort. ALBI grade can more effectively predict the survival of patients with HCC, used widely in routine clinical practice, because it eliminates the need for subjective variables of ascites and encephalopathy [25, 26]. Kulkarni AV, et al. reported that in terms of both OS and PFS of patients with HCC who received systemic therapy were significantly longer in ALBI grade 1 group than that in ALBI grade 2 and 3 groups [24]. In this study, a consistent pattern was observed across patients with varying ALBI grades, though without achieving statistical significance. This may be related to that the majority of patients had an ALBI grade 2 and the limited sample sizes of patients with ALBI grade 1 and 3. These findings suggest that the combination of ICIs and AAs may have significant therapeutic potential for uHCC patients with CP B cirrhosis.

LRTs have been shown to exhibit synergistic anti-tumor effects when combined with ICIs in HCC, primarily by exposing a variety of tumor antigens and modulating the immune response in a favorable manner [27]. However, the hypoxic environment created following TACE may promote angiogenesis and vascular remodeling [28]. Interestingly, antiangiogenic therapy has the potential to normalize tumor vasculature, reduce hypoxia, and alleviate the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment [29]. Therefore, a triple therapy regimen consisting of ICIs, AAs and LRTs could be expected to offer more effective treatment for uHCC patients. Indeed, our study demonstrated a higher ORR and a longer OS in the group receiving simultaneous LRTs, which also independently predicted better tumor response. Several real-world studies have reported that triple therapy independently predicted longer PFS [30–33].

This study observed controllable toxicity and good tolerance among patients. The TRAE profile was generally consistent with the known adverse events (AEs), and the incidence of grade ≥ 3 TRAEs (27.7%) was also similar with that reported in previous clinical trials (19.4%−77.4%) [6, 9, 15–18]. These findings suggest that the combination of ICIs and AAs can be considered both safe and tolerable for uHCC patients with CP A as well as CP B cirrhosis. Notably, 32.3% of CP B patients with sustained undetectable HBV DNA levels showed an improvement in their CP scores from class B to A after systemic therapy, who had higher ORR than that in patients with no CP score improvement in CP B group. We considered that was primarily attributable to anti-tumor response. The CheckMate 040 trial also reported that five out of six responders to nivolumab improved their liver function from CP B to CP A [34]. This suggests that response to treatment and reduction of tumor burden may improve liver function [23], for instance, by reducing biliary obstruction or paraneoplastic effects, thereby stabilizing synthetic function and portal hemodynamics and reducing ascites. The observed improvement in CP score is likely to hold significant clinical implications. First, liver function has been shown to be one of the independent predictors of prognosis in patients with HCC [23, 24]. Its improvement directly indicates a longer expected OS and significantly reduces the risk of hepatic decompensation (such as ascites, bleeding, or hepatic encephalopathy) or even liver failure, which can lead to death in these patients [35]. Second, restoration of CP class A status may expand subsequent therapeutic options, enabling patients to access later-line systemic regimens, participate in new clinical trials, or become eligible for more aggressive local interventions such as TACE or ablation—approaches often avoided in CP B patients due to higher risks of liver decompensation [36]. Finally, early CP improvement may serve as an indicator of treatment efficacy, potentially guiding decisions regarding therapy escalation or maintenance. Further studies of large sample size involving uHCC patients with CP B cirrhosis under systemic treatment are needed to confirm.

There are several limitations to our study. First, it was a retrospective analysis with a relatively small sample size. Second, the baseline differences between the two groups may introduce potential biases. Third, the varying regimens of ICIs combined with AAs, as well as the inclusion of local treatments in the study may influence the efficacy and survival outcomes in the study. Finally, in patients with CHB, some did not have regular quantification of hepatitis B surface antigen and HBV DNA levels, which consequently limited our ability to assess the relationship between changes in these markers and tumor response.

Conclusion

In brief, we demonstrated that the combination of ICIs and AAs showed promising efficacy and good tolerance in uHCC patients with CP B cirrhosis. Additionally, liver function was improved in some patients with CP B cirrhosis during treatment. Early initiation active systemic therapy may optimize benefits of uHCC patients with impaired liver function. Despite these initial outcomes demonstrating clinical potential, therapeutic strategies for uHCC patients with CP B cirrhosis still lack consensus in clinical practice. In the future, further exploration of early predictors of treatment efficacy will provide valuable insights to better guide personalized treatment.
