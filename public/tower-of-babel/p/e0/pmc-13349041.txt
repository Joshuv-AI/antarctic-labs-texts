Statistical Reporting Quality in Dental Research: A Comparative Analysis by Journal Impact and Study Design
Authors: Manuel Antonio Mattos-Vela, Martha Cecilia Rodríguez-Vargas, Teresa Angélica Evaristo-Chiyong, Dora Noelia Gómez-Meza, Víctor Fernando Lamas-Lara
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Determine the statistical report quality of articles published in dental journals according to the level of impact of the journal and the study design.Methods:A random sample of original articles published in four journals of General Dentistry indexed in Scopus was evaluated, two high-impact (quartile 1) and two medium-impact (quartile 3). The study design was recorded as observational, experimental, or other. The quality of each report was assessed through seven statistical error indicators. Two statisticians independently evaluated the articles and then contrasted their results; in the case of discrepancies in the ratings, they reached an agreement to establish a consensual criterion. Chi-square, Fisher’s exact, Mann-Whitney U, and Kruskal-Wallis tests were applied with post hoc Dunn-Bonferroni correction.Results:The presence of at least one statistical error was high (85.6%), with 123 (83.7%) and 44 (91.7%) articles in high- and medium-impact journals, respectively. Two statistical errors were more common (32.3%). We found a relationship between the presence of some errors (P = 0.009) and the number of statistical errors (P = 0.011) with the study design.Conclusions:There is a low-quality statistical report of the evaluated dental articles, as errors are very common; among these errors, inaccurate descriptions of the P values and a lack of justification for the sample size were the most common. The observational studies were also of higher quality than the experimental studies.

Introduction

Medical research has contributed to the development of science and solutions to health problems. However, for these reports to be useful, they must be communicated in a complete, clear, and transparent manner. For this reason, reporting guidelines were introduced to improve the quality of research communication, enabling better evaluation of methodological quality, clearer understanding of results, and usefulness in systematic reviews.[1234]

There is evidence that the use of these guidelines improves research reports.[5] On the other hand, articles published in medical and dental areas present reports with some deficiencies, especially from a statistical perspective.[67891011]

Inadequate reporting of statistical procedures can lead to misinterpretation of the methods and findings of a study.[12] Various investigations have evaluated statistical errors and statistical reports. Kim et al.[9] and Ambrosano et al.[13] analyzed statistical errors in dentistry articles and reported that, in the first study, 51.1% of the articles contained at least one statistical error, whereas in the second study, these errors decreased over the years. With respect to the quality of the statistical reports, 66.3% of the articles in the physiotherapy area did not indicate how the sample size was determined.[5] In addition, 42.1% of the articles in the area of nutrition described inadequate statistical techniques used.[7]

In a study that evaluated the quality of the reports of dental studies through surveys, more than 90% of the articles did not mention the methods for calculating the response rate, the analysis of the error of nonresponse, and the handling of missing item data.[11] Only two studies in the dental area that specifically evaluated the quality of the statistical report were found. One focused on survival analysis reports and found quality to be poor, with incomplete and unclear descriptions.[14] The second study[10] evaluated the quality of the statistical reports of articles that applied different statistical methods. However, these two studies were conducted when the guidelines for good reporting first appeared. It is not known whether, after 10 years of validity of these guidelines, the statistical report on dental research has changed.

On the other hand, high-impact journals are expected to publish articles of higher methodological quality and good reporting. However, a study that compared the frequency of statistical errors in radiology articles of journals of major and minor impact reported no difference between them.[8] There is no information regarding the statistical reporting quality in dental journals with different impact ratings or whether high-impact journals are more reliable in terms of the accuracy and transparency of their statistical reports. It is also not clear whether the statistical report varies according to the study design.[15] Therefore, the objective of this study was to determine the statistical report quality of articles published in dental journals according to the level of impact of the journal and the study design.

Methods

Type of study

Descriptive cross-sectional research was performed.

Population and sample

The population consisted of articles published in 2022 in four journals of General Dentistry indexed in Scopus. Two of these journals were considered high impact (Journal of Dental Research and Journal of Dentistry), and the other two medium impact (General Dentistry and Indian Journal of Dental Research). The first two were selected because they were in quartile 1 (Scimago Journal and Country Rank: SJR) and were evaluated in a previous study,[10] which allows comparison of the present results. The last two journals were included because they are in quartile 3 (SJR), with the expectation of containing lower-quality articles. Quartile 2 journals were not chosen because we consider the quality gap was not as obvious.

The inclusion criterion was original research articles, and the exclusion criterion was review articles, clinical cases, letters to the editor, editorials, and short reports, as well as research using only descriptive statistics or a qualitative or mixed approach.

For the sample calculation, the formula was applied to estimate a proportion in a finite population (n = 465), using a p (proportion) of 0.68 on the basis of a previous study,[10] a confidence level of 95% and an accuracy error of 0.05. Stratified random sampling was used according to the impact level of the journal (high: n = 350, proportion 0.753; medium: n = 115, proportion 0.247). The sample size obtained was 195 articles (147 and 48 for each type of journal).

Data collection procedure

The evaluation of the articles included their appendices or annexes. The type of journal (high impact, medium impact) and the study design (observational, experimental, other) were identified in each selected article. The statistical report quality was also assessed by identifying statistical errors or deficient reports, according to the seven criteria established by Vähänikkilä et al.[10] incomplete description of statistical procedures, unreported statistical software, unreported sample size, lack of justification for the reported sample size, reported inexact P values, problems in tables or figures, and dredging of data with multiple comparisons. An article was considered to have problems with the statistical report when it presented at least one poor reporting criterion, whereas it was considered acceptable when none of the criteria were found.

Two expert evaluators in statistics conducted an independent evaluation of the articles and then contrasted their results. In the case of discrepancies in ratings, they agreed to establish a consensual approach. Before the study, a pilot test was carried out with a sample of fifteen articles for the training and calibration of the evaluators.

Data analysis

Data processing and analysis were performed through the statistical program SPSS v 25 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Descriptive statistics were applied to the study variables through frequency distribution tables. To compare the presence and number of errors in the statistical report according to the study design and impact level of the journal, the Chi-square, Fisher’s exact, Mann-Whitney U, and Kruskal-Wallis tests were applied with post hoc Dunn-Bonferroni correction, all at a level of significance of 5%.

Characteristics of the evaluated articles

Among the seven errors in the statistical report, those most frequently presented in the evaluated articles were descriptions of inexact P values (n = 119, 61%) and a lack of justification for the sample size (n = 98, 50.3%). The least common error was the lack of a sample size report (n = 1, 0.5%). When each statistical error was compared with the type of journal using the Chi-square and Fisher’s exact tests, only the incomplete description of the statistical procedures and the lack of reporting of the software used were associated with the type of journal (P = 0.02 in both cases). These two errors were found more frequently in articles published in medium-impact journals (39.6% and 29.2%, respectively) than in those published in high-impact journals (22.5% and 14.3%, respectively) [Table 2].

In general, the presence of some errors in the statistical report was high (85.6%), with 123 (83.7%) articles in high-impact journals and 44 (91.7%) articles in medium-impact journals. There was no statistically significant difference between them (P = 0.17) [Table 2].

When the errors were compared with the design type using the Fisher’s exact test, incomplete descriptions of the statistical procedures (P = 0.003), a lack of justification of the sample size (P < 0.001), inexact P values (P < 0.001) and the presentation of at least one statistical error (P = 0.009) were significantly related to the study design. The nonjustification of the sample size (60.9%) and the inexact reporting of the P = 71.9% had higher frequencies in the experimental design than in the observational design. The presence of at least one statistical error was greater in the other designs (100%) and experimental designs (90.6%) [Table 3].

When evaluating the number of errors according to the design, it was found that an error was presented more frequently in the observational studies (29.5%), whereas in the experimental studies and other designs, two errors predominated, with 36.7% and 66.7%, respectively. Using the Kruskal–Wallis test, the mean number of statistical errors was found to vary according to the type of research design (P = 0.011), being greater in the experimental designs (2.09 ± 1.15) than in the observational designs (1.57 ± 1.4) [Table 4].

Discussion

The articles evaluated in this research were in charge of two statistical reviewers, which allowed a better review of the errors of the report, since, before any discrepancy could discuss and agree, what did not happen in a previous study,[10] which also considered the same error indicators, in which there was only one evaluator. Peer review, to which every manuscript is submitted to a biomedical journal, also considers that there will be a better evaluation of the report when it is exposed to the scrutiny of several judges.[16]

The present study revealed a high percentage of problems (85.6%) in the statistical report of the articles, which coincides (91.0%) with what was reported by Vähänikkilä et al.[10] in five representative dental journals of various dental specialties and with Günel Karadeniz et al.[8] in 20 radiology journals (93.6%). However, it differs from what was reported by Schaafsma et al.[7] in dietary research articles (42.1%), although this difference could be attributed to the fact that they used other criteria to evaluate statistical quality and that only one journal was evaluated. In addition, Layton and Clarke[14] reported poor quality in survival analysis reports in 50 dental journals. This indicates that there is a lack of knowledge or little consideration of the guidelines for good reports that dental journals require in their guidelines for authors.[1718] It also underlines the need for journal reviewers and editors to strengthen compliance with these guidelines to ensure clear, complete, transparent, and useful reports that help reduce research waste.[161819]

Editors of dental scientific journals should be aware of the low quality of statistical reports of their articles and the need to improve this aspect to ensure useful reports for the scientific community. In this sense, any research work should support a statistical advisor or a member of the research team with solid knowledge of statistics, both for the performance of the statistical analysis and for the correct reporting of the results.[20] It is also crucial to train reviewers and editors in statistical aspects to ensure that published research reports are clear and accurate.

Among the two variables related to the presence and number of statistical errors, only the design type showed an association; the experimental studies presented the highest proportion in the presence of some error and the highest mean in the number of errors compared with the observational studies. This result highlights the need to strengthen the knowledge of researchers regarding the management of this design, specifically the aspects that should be reported.[21] Experimental designs are more complex and generally require more knowledge, experience and resources for their implementation.[52223]

In the present study, the type of journal was not related to the statistical report quality in the evaluated articles, which is consistent with the findings of Günel Karadeniz et al.,[8] who reported no differences in the number of statistical errors between major and minor impact journals, considering the value two of the impact factor index as a cutoff point for classifying journals. However, more research is needed to confirm this initial observation, considering that high-impact journals are expected to maintain more rigorous standards in the selection of articles they publish, both in terms of methodological quality and in the report, attracting more experienced researchers, although a study in 24 dental journals found the highest annual mean number of articles published in the journal and the percentage of systematic reviews as factors associated with the quartile position of the impact factor of a journal.[24]

Although our study was based on a representative sample of articles from four journals published in a single year, these results cannot be extrapolated to all dental journals in quartiles 1 and 3. Additional studies, including multiple years of publications and a larger number of journals, are needed to validate these findings.

Conclusions

We conclude that the quality of the statistical reports of the evaluated dental articles is low, as errors are very common; among these errors, an inexact description of the P values and a lack of justification for the sample size were the most common. In addition, the observational studies presented a higher quality than the experimental studies did, whereas there was no difference in quality according to the level of impact of the journal.
