Retinal Vascular Features and Risk of Colorectal Cancer Incidence and Mortality: A Population-Based Cohort Study
Authors: Wenli Zhang, Lei Liu, Min-Er Zhong, Cong Li, Yun Ren, Zhenchao Du, Yan Wang, Jingyan Peng, Ying Yao, Xue He, Heng Li, Zhishen Peng, Tengda Huang, Yong Li, Honghua Yu, Danli Shi, Xiaohong Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionColorectal cancer (CRC) screening remains limited by invasiveness and suboptimal sensitivity of current methods. Retinal microvasculature, a noninvasive window into systemic vascular health, may reflect CRC-associated angiogenesis and inflammation. We investigated whether artificial intelligence (AI)-derived retinal vascular features (RVFs) are associated with CRC risk and prognosis.MethodsThis analysis included UK Biobank participants with baseline fundus imaging. Multidimensional RVFs, including tortuosity, branching angle, caliber, density, and complexity, were quantified using the Retina-based Microvascular Health Assessment System (RMHAS). Associations of RVFs with incident CRC and mortality among patients with CRC were evaluated using univariable and multivariable Cox regression models.ResultsAmong 55,798 participants (mean age 55.47 ± 8.09 years; 55.07% female), over a median follow-up of 11.32 years, 652 incident CRC cases (including 488 colon cancers and 260 rectal cancers) and 171 post-CRC deaths were documented. After full adjustment, four RVFs showed significant associations with CRC risk: increased arterial tortuosity (hazard ratio [HR] 1.03, 95% confidence interval [CI] 1.01–1.06), wider venular minimum branching angle (HR 1.09, 95% CI 1.02–1.17), smaller arterial maximum branching coefficient (HR 0.90, 95% CI 0.82–0.99), and reduced venular angular asymmetry (HR 0.89, 95% CI 0.82–0.96). For mortality, higher venular tortuosity (HR 1.14, 95% CI 1.00–1.31) and increased arterial maximum length diameter ratio (HR 1.16, 95% CI 1.01–1.32) were associated with elevated risk, whereas venular caliber (HR 0.81, 95% CI 0.67–0.98) and venular length diameter ratio (HR 0.82–0.84) were inversely associated with mortality. Distinct RVF patterns were observed between colon and rectal cancers.ConclusionSpecific AI-derived RVFs were independently associated with CRC risk, its subtypes, and mortality. These findings suggest that retinal microvascular changes may reflect systemic processes related to carcinogenesis and point to the potential of retinal imaging as a noninvasive tool for CRC risk stratification.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s40123-026-01418-w.

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC), the third most prevalent malignancy worldwide, accounts for over 1.9 million new cases and approximately 900,000 deaths annually [1]. The striking disparity in 5-year survival rates, exceeding 90% with early detection but plummeting below 15% for advanced-stage disease [2], underscores the critical need for early detection through screening. However, current screening modalities, including colonoscopy, fecal occult blood test (FOBT), and carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA) test, face limitations regarding invasiveness, suboptimal sensitivity, and cost barriers to population-wide implementation [3–5]. These challenges necessitate the development of novel noninvasive biomarkers for population-scale risk stratification.

The vascular paradigm of CRC pathogenesis provides a mechanistic foundation for biomarker development [6, 7]. As an angiogenesis-dependent malignancy, CRC exhibits characteristic upregulation of vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) secreted by tumor cells [8, 9]. Circulating VEGF can be detected in peripheral blood and influences microcirculatory function systemically [10–12]. This pathophysiological cascade suggests that tumor-derived angiogenic factors may induce quantifiable remodeling in peripheral vascular beds.

The retinal microvasculature, uniquely observable through noninvasive imaging, has emerged as a promising window into systemic pathophysiological processes. Accumulating evidence demonstrates that retinal vascular alterations reflect both traditional CRC risk factors, such as diabetes, smoking, and hypertension [13–15], and cancer-specific processes including inflammation and pathological angiogenesis [16], hallmarks of CRC development [7, 17]. Although preliminary studies have linked retinal vascular changes to gastrointestinal malignancies [18], existing research relies solely on crude vascular density metrics, overlooking the rich architectural signatures captured by modern imaging. Given the established sex differences in CRC incidence and the potential hormonal influences on angiogenesis, sex-specific associations between retinal microvasculature and CRC warrant investigation.

Recent advances in artificial intelligence (AI) have transformed retinal analytics. The Retina-based Microvascular Health Assessment System (RMHAS) quantifies hundreds of retinal vascular features (RVFs) within 2 s [19], enabling multidimensional assessment of vascular architecture including density, complexity, tortuosity, caliber, and branching angle. The retinal vascular omics approach may capture subtle microcirculatory disturbances preceding clinical CRC manifestations, potentially reflecting early tumor-associated angiogenesis or paraneoplastic vascular effects.

We therefore hypothesize that deep learning-derived retinal vascular phenotyping can reveal CRC-associated microvascular alterations independent of conventional risk factors. Through establishing a robust association between retinal vascular profiles and CRC risk, this study aims to lays the groundwork for an innovative cancer screening paradigm, potentially enabling risk-stratified colonoscopic surveillance and personalized preventive interventions in the future.

Methods

Study Population

This is a retrospective analysis of prospectively collected data from the UK Biobank, a population-based longitudinal study with over 500,000 individuals aged 40–69 years across 22 assessment centers from 2006 through 2010. Participants were free of CRC at baseline and were followed over time to ascertain incident outcomes. Baseline assessments collected participant data on demographics, socioeconomic status, medications, lifestyle factors, systemic and ophthalmic disease histories. Health-related outcomes were obtained via linkage to Hospital Episode Statistics (HES) and death registers. Ocular assessments including retinal imaging were performed at six assessment centers between 2009 and 2010. The overall study protocol and examination procedures have been previously published in detail elsewhere [20].

Ethical Approval

The UK Biobank received ethical approval from the North West Multi-centre Research Ethics Committee (11/NW/0382). The current study complied with all ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. This work was performed under UK Biobank Application 86091. All participants provided written informed consent at the time of enrollment in the UK Biobank; no additional consent was required for this sub-analysis.

RVFs Quantification

Retinal imaging was performed using the Topcon 3D OCT-1000 Mk2 spectral-domain optical coherence tomography device (Topcon, Tokyo, Japan) to capture 45° non-dilated fundus photographs for each eye. Our analytical protocol prioritized right eye images for retinal RVFs extraction, with left eye images serving as alternatives when right eye data were unavailable.

For automated RVF quantification, we employed the RMHAS, which demonstrates high precision and computational efficiency in vascular segmentation [19]. RMHAS has been previously validated across multiple fundus camera models and demonstrated good to excellent reproducibility (intraclass correlation coefficients ≥ 0.75 for most parameters). The technical specifications of this algorithm are accessible online (https://www.retinavessel.com/). The RMHAS processing pipeline, including representative fundus images from a participant without CRC and a participant with CRC, is shown in Supplementary Material Fig. 1. Briefly, the pipeline initially evaluated image quality through an automated grading module, followed by segmentation of the eligible images into arteries, veins, and optic disc. From these segmentations, we derived five major vascular parameters: (1) tortuosity, (2) complexity, (3) density, (4) caliber, and (5) branching angle (Supplementary Material Table 1). The extensive microvascular network, comprising hundreds of individual vessel segments per image, was comprehensively characterized by computing central tendency, variability, and distribution shape for each vascular parameter, as previously reported [21] (Supplementary Material Table 2).

Outcome Ascertainment

CRC cases, including subtypes, and death events were identified using International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision (ICD-10) codes from medical records and death registry records (Supplementary Material Table 3). Mortality was defined as all deaths occurring after CRC diagnosis, as cancer-specific death certification is subject to well-documented inaccuracies [22–24]. The follow-up period was calculated from enrollment until the earliest of (1) CRC diagnosis, (2) death, (3) loss to follow-up, or (4) the study cutoff date.

Covariates Assessment

The selection of potential confounding variables was guided by existing literature [25–29], with these factors subsequently incorporated as adjustment variables in our analysis. Our models accounted for demographic characteristics (age, sex, ethnicity, and educational qualification); socioeconomic status (Townsend deprivation index [TDI]); lifestyle factors (smoking status, alcohol consumption, red meat and processed meat consumption, physical activity); anthropometric measures (body mass index [BMI]); family medical history (specifically CRC); medication use (nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs [NSAIDs]); and personal medical history (prior non-CRC malignancies, inflammatory bowel disease [IBD], and non-communicable chronic diseases [NCDs] including hypertension, diabetes mellitus, and cardiovascular disease). Complete definitions for all variables are provided in Supplementary Material Table 4.

Statistical Analyses

Baseline characteristics were summarized as mean ± standard deviation (SD) for continuous variables and numbers (percentages) for categorical variables. Group comparisons used unpaired t tests for continuous variables and chi-square tests for categorical variables. Physical activity data, with 17.42% missingness, were assigned to an “unknown” category. Remaining covariates with minimal missing data (< 2%) were addressed via mean imputation for continuous variables or mode imputation for categorical variables. Detailed missing data patterns are available in Supplementary Material Table 5.

We standardized all RVFs to enable interpretation in SD units. Cox proportional hazards regression models were performed to quantify associations between RVFs and clinical outcomes, and the results were expressed as hazard ratios (HRs) with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs). First, we conducted initial screening of RVFs potentially associated with incident CRC and post-CRC mortality using unadjusted models and further applied false discovery rate (FDR) correction to P values of CRC. RVFs demonstrating significant associations were selected for subsequent multivariable analyses. We constructed three progressively adjusted Cox regression models: model 1—unadjusted analysis; model 2—adjusted for baseline age and sex; model 3—additionally adjusted for ethnicity, educational qualification, TDI, smoking status, alcohol consumption, red meat and processed meat consumption, physical activity, BMI, NSAIDs use, history of NCDs, IBD, non-CRC malignancies, and family history of CRC.

We categorized RVFs into tertiles to evaluate potential dose–response relationships with clinical outcomes. Subsequent subgroup analyses examined whether these associations varied across predefined strata: age (< 60 years vs ≥ 60 years), sex (female vs male), smoking status (never vs previous/current), and history of NCDs (no vs yes). To assess result robustness, we conducted four sensitivity analyses. First, we excluded CRC cases occurring within the first 2 years of follow-up to minimize reverse causation. Second, we repeated analyses after excluding participants with baseline non-CRC malignancies. Third, we performed complete-case analyses by excluding participants with missing data. Finally, we applied propensity score matching (PSM) using nearest-neighbor methodology to balance baseline characteristics.

All statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.4.2 (R Core Team, Vienna) and Stata/MP 18.0 (StataCorp LLC, College Station). Statistical significance was defined as a two-sided P value < 0.05.

Results

Among these 652 incident CRC cases, the median time from baseline to CRC diagnosis was 7.16 years (IQR 4.14–9.65 years), with more than 75% of cases diagnosed more than 4 years after baseline assessment. RVFs and Incident CRC

Our analysis identified 59 RVFs across five major categories that showed significant associations with incident CRC (Fig. 2). After adjustment for age and sex in model 2, four RVFs maintained statistically significant associations. In the fully adjusted model 3, these four RVFs showed persistent significant associations. Specifically, the variability of arterial tortuosity demonstrated a positive association with CRC risk (std, HR 1.03, 95% CI 1.01–1.06, P = 0.004). For arterial and venular branching angle variability, a smaller arterial maximum branching coefficient (HR 0.90, 95% CI 0.82–0.99, P = 0.034), a lower median absolute deviation (MAD) of venular angular asymmetry (HR 0.89, 95% CI 0.82–0.96, P = 0.003), and a higher venular minimum branching angle (HR 1.09, 95% CI 1.02–1.17, P = 0.011) were associated with increased CRC risk (Table 2). When stratified by tertiles, participants in the highest tertile of venular angular asymmetry MAD had significantly lower CRC risk compared to the lowest tertile (P for trend = 0.013), while those in the highest tertile of venular minimum branching angle showed significantly elevated risk in fully adjusted models (P for trend = 0.022) (Supplementary Material Table 6).Fig. 2Retinal vascular features associated with colorectal cancer incidence and mortality in univariable Cox model. RVFs and Mortality in Patients with CRC

In the analysis of mortality among patients with CRC, 43 RVFs from five major categories initially demonstrated significant associations (Fig. 2). Following adjustment for age and sex in model 2, seven RVFs remained statistical significance. The fully adjusted model 3 revealed six RVFs with persistent significant associations (Table 2). Venular distribution shape tortuosity (skewness of curve angle) was positively associated with mortality (HR 1.14, 95% CI 1.00–1.31, P = 0.045), while venular distribution shape caliber (kurtosis of maximum caliber) showed a negative association (HR 0.81, 95% CI 0.67–0.98, P = 0.035). The length diameter ratio of venular variability and distribution sharp demonstrated consistent inverse relationships with mortality across multiple parameters (max, HR 0.82, 95% CI 0.69–0.96, P = 0.014; std, HR 0.84, 95% CI 0.72–0.99, P = 0.033; kurt, HR 0.82, 95% CI 0.68–0.99, P = 0.036). While the arterial maximum length diameter ratio was associated with increased mortality risk (HR 1.16, 95% CI 1.01–1.32, P = 0.030). However, categorization of RVFs into tertiles did not reveal significant dose–response relationships (all P for trend > 0.05; Supplementary Material Table 7).

RVFs and CRC Subtypes

In our subtype analysis, we identified 142 RVFs associated with colon cancer and 7 associated with rectal cancer (Fig. 3). In model 2, 11 RVFs for colon cancer and 1 for rectal cancer remained significant. In model 3, eight RVFs for colon cancer and one RVF for rectal cancer retained significant associations. For colon cancer, several arterial features showed positive associations with risk: variability of arterial tortuosity (std, HR 1.04, 95% CI 1.01–1.06, P = 0.003), arterial caliber variability (maximum length diameter ratio, HR 1.09, 95% CI 1.00–1.18, P = 0.044; std of length diameter ratio, HR 1.09, 95% CI 1.00–1.18, P = 0.045; minimum of mean caliber, HR 1.08, 95% CI 1.00–1.16, P = 0.040). Venular branching characteristics also showed positive associations: central tendency (median branching coefficient, HR 1.12, 95% CI 1.03–1.21, P = 0.008) and variability (minimum branching angle, HR 1.12, 95% CI 1.04–1.21, P = 0.002). Inverse associations were observed for arterial and venular angular asymmetry MAD (artery, HR 0.90, 95% CI 0.83–0.98, P = 0.018; vein, HR 0.89, 95% CI 0.82–0.97, P = 0.011) (Table 3). For rectal cancer, only the arteriole to venule ratio (AVR) showed a significant inverse association (HR 0.88, 95% CI 0.78–1.00, P = 0.044) (Table 3). When participants were stratified by RVF tertiles, those in the highest tertile for venular median branching coefficient and minimum branching angle showed significantly increased colon cancer risk (both P for trend < 0.05), while those in the highest tertile for arterial angular asymmetry MAD had significantly reduced risk (P for trend < 0.05; Supplementary Material Table 8). Subgroup Analyses

In addition, we performed subgroup analyses stratified by age, sex, smoking status, and baseline NCDs to evaluate the associations between RVFs and clinical outcomes using multivariable Cox models (Supplementary Material Tables 10–13). Following full adjustment, the branching angle category of RVFs exhibited significant associations with incident CRC across all examined subgroups, while retinal tortuosity feature showed statistically significant association in subgroups of age ≥ 60 years, male patients, previous/current smokers, and individuals with NCDs. For mortality, retinal caliber features demonstrated significant associations in subgroups of age < 60 years, age ≥ 60 years, male patients, previous/current smokers, never smokers, and those with NCDs. Retinal tortuosity features primarily significant in subgroups of age ≥ 60 years and previous/current smokers. When we examined colon cancer specifically, branching angle features maintained significant associations in all subgroups except never smokers. Retinal caliber features were significantly associated in subgroups of age < 60 years, age ≥ 60 years, female patients, previous/current smokers, and NCDs. Additional RVFs showed predominant significance in male patients, aged 60 years or older, individuals with smoking history, and patients with NCDs. For rectal cancer outcomes, significant associations with RVF were uniquely observed in the subgroup of age < 60 years.

Sensitivity Analyses

In sensitivity analyses (Supplementary Material Tables 14–16), we consistently observed associations between RVFs and clinical outcomes across four robustness checks: (1) exclusion of cases occurring within the first two follow-up years, (2) exclusion of participants with baseline non-CRC malignancies, (3) exclusion of individuals with missing baseline data. Through PSM, we successfully matched 651 incident CRC cases with 3229 controls. Post-matching evaluation revealed standardized mean differences (SMDs) below 0.1 for all included variables, indicating excellent intergroup balance (Supplementary Material Table 17). The multivariate Cox regression results after PSM remained largely consistent with our primary analysis (Supplementary Material Table 18).

Discussion

In this large population-based study, we investigated the associations between quantified RVFs and incident CRC, its subtypes, and mortality among patients with CRC. Our findings demonstrate that specific alterations in retinal vascular architecture, including tortuosity and branching angle, are independently associated with CRC development, while tortuosity and caliber are linked to subsequent mortality. Moreover, distinct RVF patterns emerged between CRC subtypes: tortuosity, branching angle, and caliber were associated with colon cancer, whereas only caliber showed a significant association with rectal cancer. To our best knowledge, this is the first study to evaluate the relationship of AI-quantified RVFs and CRC. These results suggest that noninvasive retinal imaging may provide a novel avenue for CRC risk stratification and prognostication. The observed hazard ratios, while modest, were highly significant and consistent across sensitivity analyses. This is not unexpected given that each retinal microvascular feature likely captures only one facet of a complex systemic process. The true clinical potential may therefore lie in combining these features into a composite score.

The long interval between baseline and CRC diagnosis (median 7.16 years) supports the hypothesis that these systemic changes precede overt malignancy. Consistent with the prospective analytical framework of our study, this long latency suggests that baseline RVFs should be interpreted as systemic microvascular indicators assessed years before CRC onset, rather than markers of concurrent malignancy.

Our study identifies several key RVFs associated with incident CRC. Increased variability in arterial tortuosity, wider venular minimum branching angle, smaller arterial maximum branching coefficient, and lower MAD of venular angular asymmetry were associated with elevated CRC risk. These findings align with the understanding that systemic pathological processes, including those driven by malignancy, can manifest in the microvasculature [16–18]. Increased tortuosity may reflect vascular remodeling in response to chronic inflammation or altered hemodynamics [30, 31], both implicated in CRC pathogenesis [7, 17]. Altered branching patterns may indicate suboptimal vascular network geometry, which may impair tissue perfusion and oxygenation [32, 33] or reflect systemic endothelial dysfunction mediated by early tumor-derived angiogenic factors [8, 10]. This extends the established paradigm of retinal vasculature as a systemic disease biomarker, as previously demonstrated for cardiovascular [34] and neurological conditions [35]. Unlike previous optical coherence tomography angiography (OCTA)-based studies limited to unidimensional vascular density parameters [18], our AI-powered approach captures a comprehensive spectrum of retinal microvasculature alterations, thereby generating multiparametric biomarkers that more fully characterize CRC-associated microvascular remodeling.

For mortality among patients with CRC, distinct RVFs emerged as prognostic indicators. Increased skewness of venular curve angle (tortuosity) and a larger arterial maximum length diameter ratio were associated with higher mortality, whereas greater kurtosis of venular maximum caliber and increased venular length diameter ratio variability (max, std, kurt) correlated with lower mortality. These associations suggest that retinal microvasculature may reflect not only CRC risk but also disease aggressiveness. Systemic inflammation, a known driver of CRC progression, may lead to increased tortuosity [30], and elevated inflammatory markers are linked to worse survival in patients with CRC [36]. The divergent prognostic implications of arterial versus venous caliber likely reflect distinct mechanisms. Increased arterial length diameter ratios may indicate elevated vascular resistance and impaired perfusion, potentially exacerbating tumor hypoxia and treatment resistance [37]. Conversely, increased venular caliber and higher variability in length diameter ratios may reflect adaptive vascular remodeling, such as improved venous drainage capacity or reduced flow resistance [38], which could mitigate tissue congestion and hypoxia, thereby attenuating systemic inflammation and metastatic dissemination [37], which are factors known to worsen CRC prognosis [36, 39].

Sub-analyses revealed partially distinct RVF signatures for colon and rectal cancer. Colon cancer was associated with a broader array of features, including arterial tortuosity, arterial caliber, and vascular branching characteristics. For rectal cancer, only the AVR showed a significant inverse association. These differences may reflect distinct etiological and pathological characteristics of colon versus rectal cancers, including variations in anatomy, histology, molecular signatures, and metastatic patterns [40, 41].

The potential pathophysiological mechanisms linking RVFs to CRC are multifaceted. CRC development is intrinsically linked to angiogenesis driven by VEGF [6, 8], which can induce systemic endothelial changes measurable in distant microvascular beds like the retina [10, 11]. High VEGF levels are associated with poor prognosis [42], further supporting the association between RVFs and CRC outcomes. Chronic systemic inflammation, a hallmark of CRC [17], can impair vascular health, leading to endothelial dysfunction, altered vessel tone, and structural remodeling [43], which could be captured by RVF analysis. Moreover, shared risk factors such as smoking [13, 44], may contribute to the observed associations. RVFs may therefore represent an integrated measure of these complex interacting pathways. The observed associations may reflect two non-mutually exclusive pathways. First, RVFs may capture CRC-associated systemic processes, such as subclinical tumor-derived VEGF release and paraneoplastic vascular remodelling, which can precede clinical diagnosis by years. Second, RVFs may also represent the cumulative end-organ burden of traditional CRC risk factors, which are known to induce measurable retinal microvascular changes. Even if RVFs primarily reflect the latter, they could still serve as an integrated, noninvasive, and scalable indicator for population-based CRC risk stratification. However, we emphasize that the current study design does not allow us to distinguish whether the observed RVF alterations are specific to CRC or represent a general marker of malignancy-associated vascular remodelling that may be shared across multiple cancer types.

Our findings suggest potential clinical value. RVFs analysis could provide a noninvasive, cost-effective, and scalable method for population-level CRC risk stratification. As an adjunct rather than a replacement for established methods, retinal imaging warrants further investigation to determine whether integrating RVFs with conventional risk factors improves predictive accuracy. However, sensitivity and specificity must be rigorously validated in external cohorts before clinical implementation. Furthermore, future studies across multiple cancer types are needed to determine whether the observed RVF alterations are specific to CRC or represent a shared oncologic vascular response.

Our study has several strengths, including the large UK Biobank sample, automated RVF quantification [19], extensive confounder adjustment, and consistent sensitivity analyses. Nevertheless, certain limitations warrant consideration. First, despite extensive adjustments, residual confounding cannot be fully excluded; therefore, our findings demonstrate statistical associations rather than a CRC-specific causal pathway. Second, the UK Biobank cohort is predominantly of white European ancestry, which may limit generalizability to other ethnic groups. Future studies in more diverse populations are needed to validate our findings across different ethnic groups. Third, the limited number of rectal cancer cases and deaths may have reduced statistical power. Fourth, retinal imaging was performed at a single time point; longitudinal data on RVF changes could better reveal dynamic relationships with CRC progression. Future studies incorporating serial retinal imaging and longitudinal assessment of circulating angiogenic factors are needed to establish temporal relationships and potential causality. Fifth, analysis of all deaths after CRC diagnosis (due to death certificate misclassification [22–24]) may dilute CRC-specific associations. Sixth, we cannot determine whether the observed RVF alterations are specific to CRC or represent a broader indicator of systemic cancer-related angiogenic activity, as other cancer types were not assessed in this cohort. Future multicancer studies are warranted. Seventh, although we have discussed the potential clinical utility of RVFs, their sensitivity, specificity, and incremental value over existing prediction models must be rigorously evaluated in external cohorts before clinical implementation. Eighth, we did not use dimensionality reduction methods such as partial least squares (PLS) to explore latent structures within the high-dimensional RVFs. Future studies may employ such approaches to identify composite vascular signatures. Finally, further research is needed to elucidate how CRC processes affect the retinal microvasculature.

Conclusion

AI-derived retinal vascular features are significantly associated with CRC incidence, its subtypes, and mortality, suggesting retinal imaging as a noninvasive approach to CRC risk assessment. We emphasize that these retinal imaging findings should currently be interpreted as a nonspecific indicator of cancer-associated systemic vascular remodelling, rather than a CRC-specific diagnostic signal. Future work should integrate RVFs with conventional risk factors into multimodal systems, and multicancer studies are warranted to assess specificity.
