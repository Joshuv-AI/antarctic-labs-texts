Distinguishing acute and chronic TMD in adolescent patients
Authors: Yeon-Hee Lee, Q-Schick Auh, Seonggwang Jeon, Seong-Woo Jang, Tae-Seok Kim
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This retrospective cross-sectional study aimed to elucidate the clinical and imaging characteristics of chronic temporomandibular disorder (TMD) compared to acute TMD in adolescents, and to identify factors associated with symptom chronicity. The study included 158 adolescent patients with TMD (103 females and 55 males; mean age, 15.08 ± 2.23 years), classified into acute TMD (symptom duration < 6 months) and chronic TMD (symptom duration ≥ 6 months) groups. We identified the adolescent patients with TMD and retrospectively reviewed all clinical reports, panoramic radiography (PR) and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) images of their temporomandibular joints. The overall female-to-male ratio among adolescent TMD patients was 1.87:1, with no significant difference between groups. Treatment duration was significantly longer in the chronic TMD group (13.95 ± 6.79 months) than in the acute group (8.02 ± 6.22 months, p < 0.001). On PR, both anterior (2.41 ± 0.67 vs. 1.83 ± 0.64 mm, p < 0.001) and posterior joint spaces (2.48 ± 0.73 vs. 2.19 ± 0.69 mm, p = 0.012) were significantly smaller in chronic TMD patients. The nasomaxillary (Na-Mx) midline discrepancy was significantly greater in chronic TMD patients (1.41 ± 1.06 vs. 1.06 ± 1.11 mm, p = 0.045), and anterior disc displacement (ADD) on MRI was significantly more prevalent in chronic TMD patients (86.7% vs. 68.0%, p = 0.007) than in acute TMD patients. Chronic TMD patients also showed higher rates of bruxism (22.7% vs. 39.8%, p = 0.026), bad posture (54.2% vs. 34.7%, p = 0.016), sleep problems (26.5% vs. 10.7%, p = 0.014), headache (25.3% vs. 12.0%, p = 0.042), and irregular diet (30.1% vs. 12.0%, p = 0.007). In logistic regression analysis, treatment duration ≥ 1 year showed the strongest association with chronic TMD (OR = 8.643, 95% CI 3.285–22.738, p < 0.001). The next strongest predictor was anterior joint space narrowing (OR = 7.225, 95% CI 2.550–20.470, p < 0.001), followed by ADD on MRI (OR = 5.448, 95% CI 1.681–17.651, p = 0.005), Na-Mx discrepancy (OR = 5.119, 95% CI 1.720–15.233, p = 0.003), and bruxism (OR = 4.702, 95% CI 1.595–13.865, p = 0.005). In adolescent TMD patients, symptom chronicity was associated with longer treatment duration and structural changes, such as facial midline deviation and ADD. These associations highlight the importance of early diagnosis and timely management to prevent progression and minimize long-term impact.

Introduction

Temporomandibular disorder (TMD), a representative musculoskeletal disease of the orofacial area, is an umbrella term for the pain and dysfunction of the temporomandibular joint (TMJ) complex and masticatory muscles1. TMD has multifactorial etiology, overload to TMJ, microtrauma, microtrauma due to various parafunctional oral habits and bruxism, deleterious body posture, malocclusion, growth abnormalities, and psychological stress2,3. The typical symptoms of TMD are TMJ noise such as clicking and crepitus, TMD pain in the TMJ and masticatory muscles and can be accompanied by restricted mandibular movement, ear pain, tinnitus, and headaches3. According to the Diagnostic Criteria for Temporomandibular Disorders (DC/TMD), diagnosis is established through a combination of patient-reported symptoms, clinical examination findings, and, when indicated, radiographic or MRI evaluation4. The term TMD encompasses various subcategories, including joint disorders (e.g., disc displacement, degenerative joint disease), muscle disorders (e.g., myalgia, myofascial pain), and headache attributed to TMD. While pain is a predominant feature, TMD may also present solely with functional limitation or joint sounds without pain, highlighting the heterogeneity of the condition5. Conversely, isolated signs or symptoms, such as joint noise without pain or functional impairment, do not necessarily fulfill the diagnostic criteria for TMD6. However, these aspects remain underexplored in adolescent patients with TMD.

TMD is more prevalent in women than in men, with a female-to-male ratio of approximately 1.5–2.24:17,8. TMD is common, affecting about one-third of young adults9. While the peak prevalence is typically seen in individuals in their 20–40 s, in South Korea, it is most prevalent in those in their 20 s, followed by mid-to-late teenagers10. A meta-analysis by Minervini et al. reported a considerably higher prevalence of TMD in children and adolescents aged 8–19 years, ranging from 20 to 60%11. When TMD persists, especially when symptoms begin and continue during adolescence, it can lead to permanent physical or skeletal damage, such as malocclusion or facial asymmetry, which may be accompanied by a discrepancy in the midline alignment of the nasal bone, maxilla, and mandible12–14. Despite the notable prevalence of TMD in adolescents and the critical importance of addressing the permanent physical damage associated with persistent symptoms, research on TMD in this age group remains significantly underdeveloped compared to that on adult patients.

Adolescent TMD and adult TMD need to be examined in a connected manner while also being investigated separately. Experiencing chronic pain during adolescence may be associated with decreased academic performance, reduced concentration, psychological distress, and subsequent social outcomes15. In addition, chronic pain originating in adolescence warrants attention, as it can lead to physical, psychological, and social impacts that persist into adulthood16. According to Lee et al., TMJ noise and TMD pain were observed in over 80% of the adolescent TMD patients, and both pain intensity and the degree of mandibular dysfunction increased with age within the adolescent period17. In children and adolescent with juvenile TMJ osteoarthritis (TMJ-OA) may lead to pain, restricted jaw function, facial growth disturbances, and/or decreased quality of life. With advances in imaging technologies, TMJ-OA is no longer obscured, and much has become clearer through the use of panoramic radiography (PR) or magnetic resonance imaging (MRI)18,19. In TMD patients, radiographic joint space narrowing is more frequently observed in those with chronic TMD compared to those with acute TMD20. There has been speculation that joint space narrowing is associated with the progression of OA; on MRI, joint space narrowing has been related to disc tearing, anatomical joint structure damage, or degeneration21. However, this parameter has not been investigated in adolescent TMD patients with respect to prolonged symptom duration.

Prevention of musculoskeletal disease is fundamentally more effective than treatment. However, if treatment is necessary, early intervention with an accurate diagnosis is preferable to managing the condition at a chronic stage22. TMD is not a singular disorder but rather an umbrella term encompassing a wide range of joint and muscular pathologies affecting the TMJ and its surrounding structures. The manifestation of TMD symptoms is highly variable among individuals, influenced by the complex interplay of biopsychosocial, environmental, and genetic factors, and can also exhibit temporal complexity within the same individual23,24. This variability presents significant challenges in the precise diagnosis and effective management of TMD. Similar to other musculoskeletal disorders, chronic TMD pain may involve neuropathic components and peripheral and central sensitization, resulting in more complex conditions, posing challenges for both patients and clinicians25. Consequently, acute TMD has a high propensity to progress to a chronic state. However, in many cases of musculoskeletal disorders, adjusting daily parafunctional habits or body posture and appropriately managing affected areas at an early stage can prevent symptoms from becoming chronic26. Clinical and radiographic characteristics distinguishing acute from chronic TMD in adolescents have yet to be clearly identified. Currently, clinical and radiographic characteristics distinguishing acute from chronic TMD in adolescents have yet to be clearly identified. If this study can clarify these aspects in adolescents, it may facilitate the early control of these key factors or help prevent the chronicity of TMD symptoms.

Given these gaps in knowledge, the present study aimed to investigate the clinical and radiographic features of adolescent TMD patients aged 12–18 years, categorizing them into acute and chronic TMD groups. Radiographic assessments on panoramic radiographs (PR) included joint space narrowing, the presence of TMJ-OA, and nasal–maxilla–mandible axis discrepancies indicating facial midline deviation. On MRI, both TMJ-OA and disc displacement were evaluated. With a focus on chronic TMD, factors most strongly associated with symptom chronicity were identified through various statistical analyses, together with other clinical and radiographic parameters of significance. The analysis targeted TMD pain and related findings, excluding cases presenting solely with asymptomatic joint sounds or other non–pain-related TMD diagnoses, to enable a more precise evaluation of factors linked to pain chronicity. Prognostic factors, including treatment duration until completion and pain intensity at treatment conclusion, were also assessed, and their relationship to the interval from symptom onset to the initial hospital visit was examined. It was hypothesized that repetitive microtraumas, such as bruxism, or persistent poor posture would contribute to symptom chronicity, and that longer symptom duration would result in extended treatment periods. This investigation represents the first comprehensive approach to elucidating factors contributing to TMD symptom chronicity in adolescents, underscoring its relevance for future research. In addition, a machine learning approach was applied to balance the distribution of acute and chronic cases and augment the sample size, enabling a direct comparison between the SMOTE-adjusted results and those derived from the raw dataset.

Methods

The research protocol was reviewed in compliance with the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the Institutional Review Board of Kyung Hee University Dental Hospital in Seoul, South Korea (IRB No-KH-DT23016). For participants under the age of 18, informed consent for study participation was obtained from their parents or legal guardians. All variables were assessed and collected by two orofacial pain and TMD specialists (Y.H.L. and T.S.K.) using internationally recognized, standardized DC/TMD Axis I criteria. Prior to the study, both examiners underwent calibration training to ensure diagnostic consistency, and periodic calibration exercises were conducted during the study period to maintain reliability. Inter-rater reliability for categorical variables, assessed using kappa statistics, ranged from 0.85 to 0.89. For continuous measurements, intra-class correlation coefficients ranged from 0.78 to 0.84. In all cases of disagreement, consensus was reached after multiple rounds of discussion.

Study population

The study population comprised 158 consecutive adolescent patients with TMD (103 females and 55 males, mean age = 15.08 ± 2.23 years), who visited Kyung Hee University Dental Hospital between January 2018 and August 2024. A specialist with > 10 years of experience diagnosed TMD based on the DC/TMD Axis I4. We identified the adolescent patients with TMD and retrospectively reviewed all clinical reports, panoramic radiography (PR) and MRI images of their TMJs. The TMD symptom duration reported by patients was recorded in months, with 6 months used as the threshold to classify the patients into two groups: acute TMD (symptom duration < 6 months) and chronic TMD (symptom duration ≥ 6 months)27. In this study, we investigated the clinical characteristics associated with chronic TMD in comparison to acute TMD, as well as factors related to the increase in symptom duration.

The exclusion criteria were serious previous injuries, such as unstable multiple traumas to the orofacial area and maxillary and mandibular fractures. Those with systemic diseases potentially affecting the TMJ, such as rheumatic diseases, systemic osteoarthritis, pregnancy, psychological problems, and psychiatric or neurological disorders, were also excluded. Cases in which the structure of the TMJ complex was not clearly distinguished on PR and/or MRI were excluded28.

Sample size

For sample size calculation, the G*Power software (latest version 3.1.9.7; Heinrich-Heine-Universität Düsseldorf, Düsseldorf, Germany) was used. A total of 134 participants (alpha error = 0.05, actual power = 0.95) was determined as the target sample size, and 158 adolescent TMD patients were actually recruited.

Pain intensity and treatment duration

Pain intensity was assessed using the visual analog scale (VAS)29. Specifically, the VAS score recorded at the initial visit (VAS-I) and the VAS score recorded at the final visit when the treatment was completed (VAS-F) were evaluated. The VAS ranges from 0 to 10, with 0 indicating no pain and 10 representing the worst imaginable pain. Treatment duration was calculated as the number of months between the first visit and the last visit. The last visit was defined as the final scheduled follow-up at which the patient’s symptoms had improved sufficiently that no further hospital visits were considered necessary by both the patient and the clinician30. The proportion of adolescent TMD patients with a treatment duration ≥ 1 year was compared between groups.

Chief complaints and contributing factors for TMD

In adolescent TMD patients, six chief complaints were investigated2,31: TMJ noise, TMD pain, muscle stiffness, locking, bruxism, and uncomfortable occlusion. The presence of each parameter was recorded based on patient reports and assessed dichotomously as either “yes” or "no." The criteria for each chief complaint are as follows: (1) TMJ noise: Refers to sounds such as clicking and crepitus originating from the TMJ during both functional and non-functional movement of the mandible. (2) TMD pain: Pain associated with TMD, involving the TMJ structures. (3) Muscle stiffness: Stiffness, heaviness, or discomfort in the muscles during function or at rest. (4) Bruxism: Clenching or grinding of the teeth during waking or sleeping. (5) Locking: Refers to jaw locking with a mouth opening of less than 35 mm, indicating limited mouth opening as reported by the patient. (6) Uncomfortable occlusion: Refers to an abnormal or awkward sensation related to one’s occlusion, regardless of whether an actual malocclusion is present. Contributing factors included the presence of tinnitus, psychological stress, sleep problems, and headache within the last month, a history of orthodontic treatment, habitual bad posture, and an irregular diet. Each of these seven factors was also assessed dichotomously. According to the Standardized Tool for the Assessment of Bruxism criteria, the diagnosis of bruxism in this study corresponds to “possible bruxism”, as it was based on self-report and clinical signs without polysomnographic confirmation32.

Panoramic radiographic parameters

PRs were taken for all subjects using the same panoramic dental imaging unit (Planmeca Promax; Planmeca OY, Helsinki, Finland) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The head was positioned using a chin rest and bite guide. Optimal image density and contrast were achieved with the following exposure settings: 84 kVp, 16 mA, and 16 s. The magnification factor was 1.20. PR data were saved as Digital Imaging and Communications in Medicine (DICOM) files, and a Picture Archiving Communication System (PACS; Infinitt Healthcare, Seoul, Korea) was used to establish reference lines and generate quantitative measurements. PR images were independently and subjectively evaluated by two experts who were blinded to patient clinical information that could bias interpretation of TMJ alterations.

TMJ space was measured at the anterior and posterior regions. The anterior joint space was assessed by drawing a perpendicular line from the center of the condyle to the Frankfort horizontal line, followed by measuring the anterior and posterior joint spaces at 60° from the horizontal axis in both directions. The distance between the outermost points of the cortical lines of the condyle and temporal bone was measured in millimeters. Joint space narrowing was defined as an anterior joint space of less than 2 mm or a posterior joint space of less than 3 mm. In this study, the terms “anterior” and “posterior” are used to describe the front and back portions of the condyle as visualized on the PR relative to the facial midline, and do not correspond to the true anatomical anterior and posterior condylar spaces.

To assess midline deviation, a midline was drawn from the center of the nasal bone and maxillary bone to the ground on panoramic radiographs. The presence of nasomaxillary (Na-Mx) discrepancy was determined based on the alignment of these midlines, and if they did not align, the degree of nasomaxillary midline discrepancy was measured. Similarly, a midline was drawn from the center of the maxilla and mandible to the ground, and the presence of maxillomandibular (Mx-Mn) discrepancy was determined based on alignment. If misalignment was present, the degree of maxillomandibular midline discrepancy was also quantified. All measurements were conducted using the PACS system on panoramic radiography and recorded in millimeters. All measurements were performed in millimeters using the PACS system on the panoramic radiography, and the values from both the right and left sides were averaged to obtain a single parameter value per individual, which was then used for statistical analysis. PR images were analyzed dichotomously by the experts and defined as either “no evidence of OA” (non-OA, normal) or “TMJ–OA”. A normal condyle was defined as round or oval in shape in the axial plane and convex, round, or flat in the coronal plane.

Midline discrepancy

Quantitative measurements of the anterior and posterior joint spaces were conducted on panoramic radiography, and the presence of joint space narrowing was assessed. To evaluate midline deviation, it was determined whether the midlines of the nasal bone and maxillary bone aligned on the panoramic radiograph (i.e., the presence of a nasomaxillary (Na-Mx) discrepancy). If they did not align, the degree of nasomaxillary midline discrepancy was also measured. Additionally, it was assessed whether the midlines of the maxilla and mandible aligned (i.e., the presence of a maxillomandibular (Mx-Mn) discrepancy), and if not, the extent of the discrepancy was quantified. All measurements were performed in millimeters using the PACS system on the panoramic radiography.

MRI image acquisition

High-resolution MR images were obtained using a 3 T MRI system (Signa™ Genesis, GE Healthcare, Chicago, IL, USA) with a 6 cm × 8 cm diameter surface coil. MRI examination was dependent on the MR sequences and protocol of the Kyung Hee University Medical Center. All scans involved sagittal oblique sections (section thickness, 3 mm or less; field of view, 15 cm; matrix dimensions, 256 × 224 matrix), and spin-echo sagittal MR images were obtained on the axial localizer images. T2-weighted images (T2WIs) were obtained using a 650/14 repetition time (TR)/echo time (TE) and 2650/82 TR/TE sequences. The PD images were obtained using a 2650/82 TR/TE sequence. MRI protocols for TMJ evaluation are identical to our previous studies3,29.

ADD and TMJ-OA on MRI

The left and right sides of a single patient with bilateral temporomandibular joints (TMJs) and anterior disc displacement (ADD) were evaluated separately on T2WIs and PD images. The MRI imaging indicators for assessing ADD in patients with TMD were as follows28: ADD of the TMJ was defined as the posterior band of the articular disc being displaced anteriorly beyond the normal range in the closed-mouth position. The non-ADD was defined as the posterior band of the articular disc being located at the 12 o’clock position relative to the condylar apex in the closed-mouth position. TMJ–OA on MRI was diagnosed based on the presence of one or more of the following33: condylar flattening, subchondral sclerosis, surface irregularities, erosion, or condylar deformities associated with these features, including the presence of osteophytes. Two experts with more than 10 years of experience analyzed the MRI findings with the naked eye, while the patients’ clinical information was blinded. A patient was considered to have a diagnosis of TMJ-OA or ADD if either side (right or left) or both sides of the TMJ exhibited these conditions.

Logistic regression analysis with machine learning

A logistic regression classifier was developed to identify chronic TMD. The dataset comprised 75 acute and 83 chronic cases, and two models were trained: a baseline model with the original data and a second model using the Synthetic Minority Oversampling Technique (SMOTE) to balance classes (1:1) in the training set. Data were split into training (80%) and testing (20%) sets with stratification, and continuous features were normalized using training-set statistics. Oversampling was applied only to the scaled training data. Both models used a sigmoid output layer, binary cross-entropy loss, the Adam optimizer (learning rate = 0.01, batch size = 32), and were trained for 100 epochs. Model performance was evaluated on the held-out test set using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves with 95% confidence intervals derived from 1000 bootstrap resamples, with the area under the ROC curve (AUROC) as the primary evaluation metric.

Statistical methods

Data were analyzed using the Statistical Package for Social Sciences (SPSS) for Windows (version 26.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Descriptive statistics were presented as mean ± standard deviation or as frequencies with percentages, as appropriate. The normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Normally distributed variables were analyzed with parametric tests (e.g., independent t-test), while non-normally distributed variables were analyzed using non-parametric methods. For correlation analyses, Spearman’s rank correlation was applied, as the data did not meet the assumption of normality. Categorical data were analyzed using the χ2 test with Bonferroni correction for equality of proportions. The strength of associations between categorical variables was further evaluated using Cramer’s V, which ranges from 0 to 1, with higher values indicating stronger associations34. Multivariate stepwise logistic regression analysis was performed to identify risk factors for chronic TMD compared to acute TMD. All parameters showing significant associations with chronic TMD were simultaneously considered to calculate odds ratios (ORs). To move beyond isolated relationships, we also applied 2D and 3D visualizations to provide an intuitive understanding of the associations between chronic TMD and related variables. The visualization was conducted using Python (version 3.9.7; Python Software Foundation, DE, USA) and R (version 4.0.2; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). A two-tailed p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Demographics and clinical characteristics

A total of 158 adolescent TMD patients (103 females and 55 males, mean age: 15.08 ± 2.23 years) were included in this study. The overall female-to-male ratio among the adolescent TMD patients was 1.87:1. Symptom duration of TMD was categorized as either acute TMD (symptom duration < 6 months) or chronic TMD (symptom duration ≥ 6 months). In the acute TMD group (47 females, mean age: 14.92 ± 2.27 years) and the chronic TMD group (56 females, mean age: 15.23 ± 2.19 years), there was no statistically significant difference in female-to-male ratio or mean age (p > 0.05). Interestingly, the mean treatment duration was significantly different between the acute TMD group (8.02 ± 6.22 months) and the chronic TMD group (13.95 ± 6.79 months) (p < 0.001). Furthermore, the proportion of patients with a treatment duration exceeding one year was significantly higher in the chronic TMD group compared to the acute TMD group (68.7% vs. 22.7%, p < 0.001). This indicates that the chronic TMD group had a significantly longer treatment duration and a higher likelihood of receiving treatment for more than one year, with 68.9% of chronic TMD patients undergoing treatment for over a year. There was no significant difference in the VAS at the initial visit (VAS-I) between the acute and chronic TMD groups (3.35 ± 2.12 vs. 2.89 ± 2.19, p = 0.188). Similarly, there was no significant difference in the final VAS score (VAS-F) between the two groups at the conclusion of treatment (0.43 ± 0.70 vs. 0.41 ± 0.83, p = 0.189) (Table 1).

Chief complaints and contributing factors for TMD

In this study, 158 adolescent TMD patients were classified into acute TMD and chronic TMD groups based on the duration of TMD symptoms, and their chief complaints and contributing factors were investigated. Chief complaints included TMJ noise, TMD pain, muscle stiffness, locking, bruxism, and uncomfortable occlusion. TMJ noise was the most common chief complaint in the chronic TMD group and was significantly more prevalent compared to the acute TMD group (61.3% vs. 79.5%, p = 0.014). TMD pain was the most common chief complaint in the acute TMD group; however, there was no significant difference between the acute and chronic TMD groups in terms of prevalence (80.0% vs. 73.5%, p = 0.354). Bruxism was observed in 20% of acute TMD patients and 39.8% of chronic TMD patients, with a significant difference between the groups (p = 0.026). Uncomfortable occlusion was also significantly more common in the chronic TMD group compared to the acute TMD group (20.0% vs. 38.6%, p = 0.014). Muscle stiffness (49.3% vs. 42.2%) and locking (60.0% vs. 62.7%) showed no significant difference between the acute and chronic TMD groups (p > 0.05).

The contributing factors for TMD included tinnitus, psychological stress, sleep problems, headache, orthodontic treatment, bad posture, and irregular diet. Among these factors, bad posture was the most commonly observed in both groups, with a significantly higher prevalence in the chronic TMD group compared to the acute TMD group (34.7% vs. 54.2%, p = 0.016). Notably, more than half of the chronic TMD patients reported having bad posture. Sleep problems (10.7% vs. 26.5%, p = 0.014), headache (12.0% vs. 25.3%, p = 0.042), and irregular diet (12.0% vs. 30.1%, p = 0.007) were also significantly more prevalent in chronic TMD patients compared to acute TMD patients (all p < 0.05). The prevalence of tinnitus (17.3% vs. 22.9%) and psychological stress (30.7% vs. 33.7%) was higher in the chronic TMD group than in the acute TMD group, while orthodontic treatment (32.0% vs. 21.7%) was less prevalent in the chronic TMD group, but none of these differences were statistically significant (all p > 0.05) (Table 2).

Panoramic radiographic findings

On panoramic radiographs, the anterior joint space was significantly smaller in chronic TMD adolescent patients compared to acute TMD patients (2.41 ± 0.67 vs. 1.83 ± 0.64 mm, p < 0.001). Similarly, the posterior joint space was also significantly smaller in chronic TMD patients compared to acute TMD patients (2.48 ± 0.73 vs. 2.19 ± 0.69 mm, p = 0.012). Thus, both the anterior and posterior joint spaces were significantly reduced when TMD symptoms persisted for more than 6 months compared to less than 6 months. Anterior joint space narrowing was observed significantly more frequently in chronic TMD patients than in acute TMD patients (30.7% vs. 58.6%, p = 0.001). Posterior joint space narrowing was observed in over 70% of both acute (77.3%) and chronic (72.3%) TMD groups, with no significant difference between the groups (p = 0.583).

The amount of nasomaxillary (Na-Mx) discrepancy was significantly greater in chronic TMD adolescent patients compared to acute TMD patients (1.06 ± 1.11 vs. 1.41 ± 1.06 mm, p = 0.045). Similarly, the amount of maxillomandibular (Mx-Mn) discrepancy was significantly greater in chronic TMD patients than in acute TMD patients (0.63 ± 1.04 vs. 0.98 ± 1.10 mm, p = 0.042). Na-Mx discrepancy was more frequently observed in chronic TMD patients than in acute TMD patients (53.3% vs. 71.1%, p = 0.032). Likewise, Mx-Mn discrepancy was also more commonly observed in chronic TMD compared to acute TMD (26.7% vs. 50.6%, p = 0.003). Therefore, when TMD symptom duration exceeds 6 months, the likelihood of facial asymmetry, specifically midline discrepancies involving the nasal bone, maxilla, and mandible, increases compared to a symptom duration of less than 6 months. However, the prevalence of TMJ osteoarthritis (TMJ-OA) on panoramic radiographs showed no significant difference between the acute and chronic TMD groups (29.3% vs. 24.1%, p = 0.476) (Table 3).

MRI findings of acute and chronic TMD

An interesting finding emerged when evaluating the most common factors observed on MRI, TMJ-OA, and ADD. TMJ-OA was observed much more frequently on MRI than on panoramic radiographs, with over 50% of patients in both groups having this condition. The prevalence of TMJ-OA on MRI showed no significant difference between acute and chronic TMD patients (53.3% vs. 56.6%, p = 0.749). In contrast, ADD was significantly more prevalent in chronic TMD patients compared to acute TMD patients on MRI (68.0% vs. 86.7%, p = 0.007). Notably, Cramer’s V analysis showed that the presence of TMJ-OA on MRI had a significant relationship with the presence of ADD on MRI (r = 0.233, p = 0.005), as well as a significant association with decreased anterior joint space narrowing on PR (r = 0.117, p = 0.026). In contrast, TMJ-OA on PR showed no significant association with anterior joint space narrowing on PR (Table 3).

Correlations with symptom duration

According to the results of Spearman’s correlation analysis, symptom duration was strongly positively correlated with treatment duration (r = 0.42, p < 0.001), indicating that an increase in symptom duration was significantly associated with an increase in treatment duration. Symptom duration was also negatively correlated with anterior joint space (r = − 0.30, p < 0.001), suggesting that longer symptom duration was associated with decreased anterior joint space. Additionally, symptom duration showed a positive correlation with the amount of Na-Mx discrepancy (r = 0.23, p = 0.003) (Fig. 1). There was a positive correlation between VAS-I and VAS-F, indicating that a higher initial VAS score was associated with a higher final VAS score (r = 0.31, p < 0.001). However, VAS-I and VAS-F did not show a significant correlation with either Na-Mx discrepancy or Mx-Mn discrepancy amount.

Logistic regression analysis for chronic TMD

To identify predictors of chronic TMD compared to acute TMD, logistic regression analysis was performed using both a crude model and an adjusted model controlling for age and sex. In the logistic regression analysis, factors associated with chronic TMD included treatment duration ≥ 1 year, anterior joint space narrowing, ADD on MRI, Na-Mx discrepancy, and bruxism. In the crude model, treatment duration over 1 year showed the strongest association with chronic TMD (OR = 8.145, 95% CI 3.201–20.727, p < 0.001). The next strongest predictor was anterior joint space narrowing (OR = 6.060, 95% CI 2.223–16.524, p < 0.001), followed by ADD on MRI (OR = 5.115, 95% CI 1.632–16.037, p = 0.005), Na-Mx discrepancy (OR = 4.708, 95% CI 1.630–13.595, p = 0.004), and bruxism (OR = 4.625, 95% CI 1.579–13.548, p = 0.005). In the adjusted model, treatment duration over 1 year also showed the strongest association with chronic TMD (OR = 8.643, 95% CI 3.285–22.738, p < 0.001). The next strongest predictor was anterior joint space narrowing (OR = 7.225, 95% CI 2.550–20.470, p < 0.001), followed by ADD on MRI (OR = 5.448, 95% CI 1.681–17.651, p = 0.005), Na-Mx discrepancy (OR = 5.119, 95% CI 1.720–15.233, p = 0.003), and bruxism (OR = 4.702, 95% CI 1.595–13.865, p = 0.005) (Table 4).

Discussion

In this study, factors influencing TMD symptom chronicity in adolescents were investigated, primarily by elucidating the clinical and radiographic characteristics of chronic TMD patients compared to those with acute TMD. In logistic regression analysis, treatment duration ≥ 1 year showed the strongest association with chronic TMD (OR = 8.643). The next strongest predictor was anterior joint space narrowing (OR = 7.225), followed by ADD on MRI (OR = 5.448, Na-Mx discrepancy (OR = 5.119), and bruxism (OR = 4.702). Due to the potential risk of TMD in adolescents having a negative long-term impact on their physical, psychological, and social well-being, researchers and clinicians should focus more on adolescent TMD and actively work to prevent its progression to a chronic state. Chronic TMD, particularly when associated with significant macrotrauma or unresolved, repetitive microtrauma, may be influenced by central sensitization and psychosocial factors, which can amplify pain perception and contribute to symptom persistence9,31,39. Although these aspects were beyond the scope of the present analysis, their potential impact on adolescent TMD warrants consideration in future research.

Typically, musculoskeletal pain can be treated effectively during the acute phase through localized inflammation and regional pain control40. However, once symptoms persist for over six months and enter the chronic phase, pain management becomes more complex, and treatment becomes more challenging and difficult22. One key finding in the chronic TMD group was that the treatment duration was significantly longer (13.95 ± 6.79 months) compared to the acute TMD group (8.02 ± 6.22 months). Moreover, the proportion of patients with a treatment duration of ≥ 1 year was significantly higher in the chronic TMD group than in the acute TMD group, and having a treatment duration of ≥ 1 year was found to be a strong factor, increasing the likelihood of chronic TMD by 8.643 times. In other words, in adolescent TMD patients, as symptom duration increases, particularly beyond six months into a chronic phase, the treatment duration is likely to extend beyond one year. This supports our initial hypothesis that a longer symptom duration is associated with a prolonged time to achieve full recovery.

Another important finding was that in chronic TMD patients, the anterior joint space on PR was significantly smaller compared to acute TMD patients, and anterior joint space narrowing was also a key predictor of the progression to chronic TMD. Joint space narrowing generally occurs as the articular cartilage undergoes degeneration, leading to a reduction in the space between adjacent bones. This reduction can impede normal joint movement, resulting in pain, stiffness, and restricted range of motion41. In chronic mechanical low back pain, lumbar space narrowing has been associated with the presence of anterior osteophytes and increased disability42. In the present study, anterior joint space narrowing of the TMJ observed on PR was associated with disc displacement on MRI, suggesting that such anatomical changes may contribute to prolonged and recurrent TMD. While PR offers a convenient and widely available imaging modality, its diagnostic accuracy for TMJ-OA is generally lower than that of cone-beam computed tomography, which is considered the gold standard43,44. Moreover, PR is inherently subject to image distortion and magnification45, which can limit the precision of joint space measurements and potentially affect quantitative interpretation. Therefore, these results should be interpreted with caution. Nevertheless, PR remains the most widely used imaging technique worldwide, and its inclusion in this study enhances the generalizability and accessibility of the findings for both clinicians and researchers.

Whether TMJ joint space narrowing correlates positively with age, as in other joints, remains unclear. In the knee or hip joint, joint space narrowing typically increases with age in the context of degenerative joint disease, reflecting age-related degenerative changes in the articular cartilage46,47. This pattern is generally observed across most weight-bearing and non–weight-bearing synovial joints. However, the prevalence of TMD does not follow the same age-related trend observed in other joints9. TMD prevalence peaks in young adults aged 20–40 years48, and its characteristics may differ across age groups10,49. In this study, an increase in age among adolescents did not correspond to a further reduction in TMJ joint space. Unlike weight-bearing joints, the TMJ is subject to various unique psychosocial influences and oral parafunctional habits, which may affect its structural changes over time50. The anterior joint space was significantly smaller in chronic TMD patients compared to acute TMD patients, even among adults20. Although age may contribute to joint space narrowing and anatomical joint structure deterioration, it is likely more closely related to symptom chronicity in TMD patients.

A deviation in the central axis of the facial anterior region was observed in chronic TMD. Clinically, facial asymmetry is commonly observed in TMD patients. The progression of unilateral or bilateral ADD and/or TMJ-OA, along with changes in occlusion, can accelerate facial asymmetry51,52. The mandibular condyle growth center is located in the condylar surface layer53, and alterations in joint biomechanics associated with ADD and/or TMJ-OA may contribute to abnormal loading of the condylar and temporal bone surfaces. Such abnormal loading has been linked to restricted condylar growth, condylar cartilage degeneration, and imbalances in bilateral muscle activity, which in turn may result in differences in the size and shape of the right and left condyles, discrepancies in the lengths of the mandibular ramus and mandibular body, and the occurrence of malocclusion19,33,54. The results of this study showed that the chronicity of TMD symptoms was associated with a midline discrepancy between the nasal, maxillary, and mandibular bones. The amount of Na-Mx discrepancy was significantly greater in chronic TMD adolescent patients compared to those with acute TMD. Growth and associated hormonal changes in adolescence can further complicate and serially influence the development of midline discrepancies in the facial bones55. In adolescent TMD patients, these anatomical changes can persist into adulthood, having permanent or semi-permanent effects on the facial region, which requires careful attention.

Among the clinical characteristics, bruxism was a significant predictor of chronic TMD, observed in approximately 40% of chronic TMD patients compared to 22.7% in the acute group. Bruxism has been considered a major contributing factor to both the persistence and exacerbation of TMD56. Bruxism is a condition characterized by repetitive clenching or grinding of teeth and/or by bracing or thrusting of the mandible during wakefulness and/or sleep57. It is sometimes considered a form of rhythmic masticatory muscle activity that may help reduce psychological stress, alleviate emotional tension, and does not necessarily cause pain in the TMJ or adjacent structures58. Furthermore, bruxism has been associated with the occurrence of headaches, but it has been suggested that there is insufficient scientific evidence to support a clear association with orofacial pain or TMD pain59. However, in sleep studies accompanied by polysomnography, bruxism was identified as a significant risk factor for TMD pain60,61. In this study, bruxism was investigated as a general term, without distinguishing between sleep bruxism and awake bruxism. From a pathophysiological perspective, during the process of bruxism, it is not only the maxillary and mandibular teeth that come into contact, but also the entire mandible moves, resulting in mechanical friction between the condylar head and the temporal bone62. The prevalence of sleep bruxism in children and adolescents has been reported to be as high as 49%, and sleep bruxism has been identified as a risk factor for TMD in this age group63. Therefore, it is highly likely that a specific relationship exists between persistent and repetitive bruxism and the chronicity of TMD. In addition, bad posture, sleep problems, headache, and irregular diet were also significantly more prevalent in chronic TMD patients compared to acute TMD patients. In the Orofacial Pain: Prospective Evaluation and Risk Assessment project for adult TMD, greater parafunction, restricted jaw function, more frequent headaches, and a higher prevalence of neural or sensory medical conditions were identified as risk factors for chronic TMD23. This study, which aims to predict chronic TMD in adolescents, found both similarities and differences compared to previous studies.

This study has several limitations. First, as it was conducted at a single institution, there may be a selection bias. Therefore, to validate the results and findings of this study, a multicenter study involving more participants is needed. Additionally, this study was retrospectively designed with a cross-sectional approach. Although we identified the clinical and radiographic characteristics that distinguish chronic TMD from acute TMD, as well as significant predictors of chronic TMD using various statistical analyses, a further study with a prospective design is necessary to explore causal relationships among these parameters. Moreover, while addressing chronic TMD, due to practical limitations of clinical trials, we could not address peripheral sensitization, central sensitization, or neuropathic or neuronal symptoms, nor did we cover psychosocial factors of patients. Of course, it is true that integrating all these aspects in clinical trials, animal studies, or cellular experiments comprehensively is challenging. Although adolescent TMD patients were diagnosed according to DC/TMD criteria by TMD specialists, we did not perform an analysis based on specific TMD subgroups. Our findings may potentially vary depending on the classification of TMD into arthrogenous, myogenous, and mixed types. It should be noted that the DC/TMD is validated for individuals ≥ 18 years; thus, future studies using criteria specific to adolescents or children are warranted. Finally, while a larger sample size would be ideal, the rarity of adolescent TMD cases with MRI makes this difficult in practice; as a supplementary approach, we applied the SMOTE, which yielded results consistent with the original analysis, suggesting that similar conclusions may be obtained even with an increased sample size. However, we aim to continue our research efforts to focus on the prevention of TMD and to prevent the chronicity of TMD symptoms, thereby helping to ensure that acute TMD does not progress to chronic TMD in adolescent patients and ultimately improving their pain management and quality of life.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study identified key clinical and radiographic predictors of TMD symptom chronicity in adolescents—prolonged treatment duration, anterior joint space narrowing, ADD, Na–Mx discrepancy, and bruxism—underscoring the importance of early intervention to prevent progression from acute to chronic TMD. Despite limitations, the findings offer valuable insights into the distinct characteristics of chronic TMD in adolescents and support continued research to improve management and quality of life in this population.
