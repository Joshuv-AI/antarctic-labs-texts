The degradation of maternal RNA-binding protein 4E-T regulates translational activation to ensure maternal-to-zygotic transition in mouse embryos
Authors: Pinhua Wang, Siqi Wang, Haojie Yin, Maosheng Ye, Mingrui Li, Shuai Zhou, Yufeng Wang, Yueshuai Guo, Longfei Jia, Xuejiang Guo, Qiang Wang, Yue Hu, Ran Huo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The maternal-to-zygotic transition (MZT) is essential for early embryonic development, comprising zygotic genome activation (ZGA) as well as the degradation of maternal RNAs and proteins, whereas our understanding of repressors’ role in this process remains limited in mammals. In our study, we inhibited protein degradation at the 1-cell stage using the proteasome inhibitor MG132 and observed impaired pre-implantation development and defective ZGA. Proteomic analysis of MG132-treated embryos showed that significantly up-regulated proteins in abundance were enriched for RNA-binding proteins (RBPs). Further functional screening highlighted one critical factor, 4E-T (EIF4ENIF1), whose overexpression caused 2-cell stage arrest and failure of ZGA initiation. Moreover, the abnormal accumulation of 4E-T translationally repressed key factors of the 1-cell embryo, including ZBED3 and KDM4A. Mechanistically, the reprsssion is linked to direct interactions of 4E-T with eIF4E and eIF4E1B. These findings suggest that specific maternal RBPs may act as repressors that require precise degradation post-fertilization to relieve translational repression and ensure successful MZT.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s00018-026-06328-5.

Introduction

Embryonic development is the beginning of life, and the regulatory mechanisms of this process have always been a central problem in developmental biology. After fertilization, the highly differentiated oocyte undergoes a remarkable transformation to the totipotent embryo through the maternal-to-zygotic transition (MZT), involving two signature and interconnected events: degradation of maternal factors and zygotic genome activation (ZGA) [1]. ZGA is the gradually transcriptional process with two successive waves, including minor ZGA and major ZGA [2]. In mice, minor ZGA occurs during the S phase of the 1-cell stage, activating a small number of transcripts, followed by major ZGA that occurs from the mid-to-late of 2-cell stage, driving the expression of abundant genes [3]. The accumulation of transcriptional activators, such as transcription factors: OBOXs [4], KLF17 [5], TPRXs [6], YY1 [7], and OTX2 [8, 9] which help launch ZGA in mammals. However, recent evidence underscores that the timely removal or degradation of maternal repressors is equally critical as the proper initiation of ZGA during MZT [10]. Thus, the successful MZT depends not only on the setup of ZGA but decisively on the clearance of repressive influences.

Maternal repressors are subject to active degradation mechanisms besides passive dilution with cell division. For instance, maternal mRNAs are degraded in two waves: maternal decay (M-decay), which is initiated during meiotic maturation and driven by maternal factors, and zygotic decay (Z-decay), which is mediated by zygotic factors and depends on ZGA [10]. In mouse embryos, these processes lead to the elimination of approximately 90% of maternally inherited transcripts by the end of the 2-cell stage [11]. Concurrently, the degradation of maternal proteins is primarily facilitated by the autophagy and ubiquitin-proteasome system (UPS) pathways. Maternal deletion of Atg5 (autophagy-related 5) results in developmental arrest at 4- to 8-cell stage [12], and inhibition of autophagy by 3-methyladenine (3-MA) significantly reduces the proportion of embryos that develop to the blastocyst stage [13], underscoring the essential role of autophagy in pre-implantation development. In addition, proteomic analyses reveal that ubiquitination-related components are highly expressed in zygotes [14]. The ubiquitin-proteasome pathway has been demonstrated to be critical for the initiation of ZGA, the proteasome inhibitor MG132 treatment to inhibit UPS at 1-cell or 2-cell stage embryos impairs some ZGA genes transcription [15, 16]. Furthermore, MG132 treatment at 1-cell stage causes developmental arrest at the 1- or 2-cell stage in a dose-dependent manner [15]. In Drosophila embryos, 2% of maternal proteins are rapidly degraded during MZT after fertilization, and these include post-transcription repressors: Cup, TRAL, ME31B, and SMG [10, 17]. The ubiquitin E3 ligase complex CTLH targets Cup, TRAL, and ME31B, while the ubiquitin E3 ligase complex SCF targets SMG, mediating the degradation of these four factors to relieve translational repression and ensure the successful MZT [18, 19]. However, in mammals, the specific maternal proteins whose degradation is essential for MZT and early embryogenesis, as well as the specific regulation pathways involved, remain to be clearly defined.

In this study, we inhibited the protein degradation in the late 1-cell stage mouse embryos by the proteasome inhibitor MG132, leading to developmental arrest, defective ZGA, and translational repression. We found excessive accumulation of RNA-binding proteins (RBPs) in the proteomic analysis of MG132-treated embryos. Further screening out 4E-T (Eukaryotic translation initiation factor 4E transporter, gene name: Eif4enif1) as a key factor: its abnormal accumulation caused developmental arrest and defective ZGA. Detailed analyses showed that abnormal accumulation of 4E-T repressed zygotic translation activities by interacting with eIF4E and eIF4E1B. Additionally, ribosome profiling with sequencing (Ribo-seq) analysis indicated that abnormal accumulation of 4E-T impairs translational activation of several mRNAs. Some of them are translationally up-regulated in zygotes and essential for normal pre-implantation development and MZT.

Materials and methods

Mice and cell

ICR mice were purchased from Nanjing Medical University and housed in the animal facility at Nanjing Medical University. All animal maintenance and experimental procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Nanjing Medical University (IACUC-2006046). 293 T (ATAC, CRL-3216) cells were cultured in DMEM (Gibco, 11995073) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% penicillin streptomycin solution at 37 °C under 5% CO2.

Collection and culture of oocyte and embryo

5 IU of pregnant mare serum gonadotropin (PMSG) (Ningbo Sansheng Pharmaceutical Corporation) and 5 IU of human chorionic gonadotropin (hCG) were intraperitoneally injected into ICR female mice aged 8–10 weeks for the superovulation experiment. 48 h after PMSG, hCG was administered. 14–16 h after hCG injection, MII oocytes were extracted from the oviduct ampullae, and the cumulus mass was eliminated using 0.5 mg/ml hyaluronidase (Sigma).

In vitro fertilization (IVF) procedures were conducted to collect embryos. Sperm were extracted from ICR male mice aged 10–18 weeks and allowed to capacitate for 1 h in HTF fertilization media (Easycheck, M1135). Subsequently, HTF drops containing COCs were added with scattered spermatozoa. Cumulus cells and extra sperm were eliminated by washing after co-incubated for 4 h at 37 °C. For the culture of pre-implantation embryos, zygotes were placed into KSOM medium (Nanjing Luanchuang Corporation) and cultured at 37 °C under 5% CO2 until the appropriate experimental stage was reached.

MG132 treatment

Following IVF, zygotes were cultured in KSOM supplemented with MG132 (Sigma, M7449) from 12 to 18 h post-insemination. After being washed different, the treated embryos were subsequently transferred to KSOM for culture. Control embryos were cultured in KSOM supplemented with 0.1% dimethylsulfoxide (DMSO).

EU and HPG incorporation assay

Embryos were cultured in KSOM with 1 µM 5-ethynyl uridine (EU) for 2 h to assess transcription activity. Embryos were cultured in M2 medium (Nanjing Luanchuang Corporation) with 50 µM L-homopropargylglycine (HPG) to assess protein synthesis. The EU RNA Synthesis Kit with Alexa Fluor 488 (Beyotime, R0301) and HPG Protein Synthesis Kit with Alexa Fluor 488 (Beyotime, R1202) were used to perform fixation, permeabilization, and staining, as per the manufacturer’s instructions. The embryos were then scanned and imaged with a confocal laser scanning microscope (LSM700, Zeiss). The signal was assessed and quantified with ImageJ software.

Western blotting

Oocytes or embryos were collected in RlPA lysis buffer (Beyotime, P0013B) with protease inhibitor cocktail (CWBIO, CW2200) and phosphatase inhibitor cocktail (CWBIO, CW2383). Protein loading buffer (FDBIO, FD002) was added to the samples, and were boiled for 10 min. Following SDS-PAGE separation, the samples were transferred to PVDF membranes (Millipore, IPVH00010). The membranes were blocked with 5% low-fat dry milk (TBST) for 2 h at room temperature and then incubated overnight in primary antibody at 4 °C. Membranes were washed three times with TBST, incubated with the corresponding secondary antibody for 2 h at room temperature. Protein bands were detected using an enhanced chemiluminescence kit (FDBIO, FD8020). The quantitative analyses were measured by ImageJ software to calculate the ratio of objective protein/loading control. The antibodies used are listed in Supplementary Table S1.

Real-time qPCR and analysis

Total RNA was extracted from oocytes or embryos using TRIzol (lnvitrogen, 15596018). According to the manufacturer’s protocol, cDNA synthesis and RT-qPCR were conducted using HiScript RT SuperMix (Vazyme, R323-01) and SYBR Green PCR Master Mix (Vazyme, Q712) with an ABI Q5 Real-time PCR System (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The 2− ΔΔCT method was used to normalize the internal control levels to calculate the relative mRNA levels. The primers used are listed in Supplementary Table S1.

Immunofluorescence staining

The oocytes or embryos were fixed for 30 min at room temperature using 4% paraformaldehyde (Sigma, P6148) in PBS, permeabilized for 30 min using 0.5% Triton X-100 in PBS, blocked for 1 h at room temperature using 3% bovine serum albumin (BSA), and then incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C. Following PBS wash, oocytes or embryos were incubated for 1 h at room temperature with Alexa Fluor-conjugated secondary antibodies (1:500 dilution). Subsequently, stained with Hoechst 33342 (KeyGen BioTECH, KGA212-10) for 10 min. The pictures were acquired with a confocal laser scanning microscope (LSM 700, Zeiss). ImageJ software was used to measure and quantify the signal. The antibodies used are listed in Supplementary Table S1.

In vitro transcription and microinjection

The mouse full-length Atxn2, Rbm38, Secisbp2l, Lsm14a, Lsm14b and Eif4enif1 were cloned into T7-driven vectors with mScarlet-tag and mRNAs were synthetized in vitro by using HiScribe T7 ARCA mRNA Kit (New England Biolabs, E2060S) following the manufacturer’s instructions. The final concentration of mRNA was diluted to 500 ng/µl before injection. Zygotes were microinjected with approximately 10 pl of mRNAs. Then, the microinjected zygotes were cultured in KSOM at 37 °C with 5% CO2.

Co-immunoprecipitation assays

All expression constructs were generated by cloning into the pcDNA3.1 expression vector. 293 T cells were co-transfected with FLAG-4E-T/FLAG-4E-T(Δ4E-BM) and MYC-eIF4E/MYC-eIF4E1B using ExFect (Vazyme, R323-01). After 48 h, collected cells were washed once with cold PBS, and lysed in cold RlPA lysis buffer (Beyotime, P0013C) with protease inhibitor cocktail and phosphatase inhibitor cocktail for 10 min on ice. Lysates were cleared by centrifugation, and protein concentration was measured using BCA kit (Beyotime, P0010). Supernatant was then incubated with FLAG beads (Smart-Lifesciences, SA042005) for ~ 4 h at 4℃. Finally, beads were washed with lysis buffer three times and resuspended in 2× protein loading buffer. Samples were heated for 10 min at 95℃ following western blotting as previous description.

Proteomics and data analysis

For one replicate of each condition, 200 oocytes or embryos were lysed in urea lysis buffer [20]. The proteins were quantified, reduced and digested. Subsequent to digestion, the isolated peptides were subjected to the tandem mass tag (TMT) labeling in accordance with established protocols [21]. The TMT-labelled peptides were further separated using high-pH reversed-phase (HP-RP) fractionation technology based on the ACQUITY UPLC M-class system (Waters) equipped with a BEH C18 column (300 μm×150 mm, 1.7 μm; Waters), resulting in the generation of 30 fractions, which were then dried using a SpeedVac concentrator. All sample fractions were successively resuspended in 0.1% formic acid and analyzed using the Orbitrap Fusion Lumos mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) in conjunction with an Easy-nLC 1200 (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The mass spectrometry acquisition was conducted in a data-dependent manner.

The raw files were searched against the Universal Protein Resource (UniProt) mouse proteome database using the MaxQuant software (version 1.6.5.0). Employing Trypsin/P enzyme specificity for searches, permitting a maximum of two missed cleavages. Carbamidomethyl (C) as well as TMT-tagged lysine and peptide N-termini were set as fixed modifications. Oxidation(M) and acetyl (protein N-term) were searched as variable modifications. The FDR for both peptides and proteins was ≤ 0.01. The MaxQuant identification results combined with a local modified Libra algorithm were used to determine the relative expression values of each protein [22]. Significant different proteins were calculated using the unpaired two tailed Student’s t-test (fold change > 1.2, P value < 0.05). Enrichment analysis of Gene Ontology (GO), including biological process and molecular function terms, was carried out using the clusterProfiler R package (version 1.6.5.0) [23].

RNA-seq and data analysis

RNA-seq library construction and sequencing were carried out using 15 oocytes or embryos of each group in three separate experiments. After washed with PBS, the collected oocytes or embryos were immediately transferred in lysis buffer (0.2% Triton X-100 and 2 U/ul RNase inhibitor) and then frozen at −80℃. Upon collection, samples were thawed on ice, 1ul of 10 μm oligo-dT primer and 1ul of 10 mM dNTP mix (Thermo Fisher Scientific, R0192) were added to the lysis buffer. The synthesis and amplification of cDNA were conducted according to the Smart-seq2 methodology [24]. In accordance with the manufacturer’s guidance, sequencing libraries were generated by fragmenting full-length cDNA libraries with the TruePrep DNA Library Prep Kit (Vazyme, TD502). All libraries were sequenced using Illumina NovaSeq 6000 sequencers with paired-end 150-bp sequencing.

The quality of RNA-seq data was evaluated using FastQC (version 0.12.1). Then the reads were mapped to the mm10 genome using STAR (version 2.7.11b) [25]. The gene expression level was determined using RSeQc (version 5.0.3) according to the mm10 refFlat database annotation from the UCSC genome browser [26]. From three replicates, we determined the average fragments per kilobase of transcript per million mapped reads (FPKM). The gene count matrix was generated using featureCounts (version V4.0.0) [27]. The differentially expressed genes were tested using the R package DESeq2 (version 1.38.3) with log2 fold change > 1 and adjusted P value < 0.05.

To identify stage-specific gene clusters, a Shannon-entropy-based method was employed, genes exhibiting entropy scores below 2 were identified as candidates for stage-specific genes [28, 29]. The FPKM value for 1-cell is the average of the FPKM value of 13 and 17 hpi control embryos. The FPKM value for early 2-cell is the FPKM value of 25 hpi control embryos. The FPKM value for late 2-cell is the average of the FPKM value of 31,33 and 39 hpi control embryos. Genes with higher FPKM (FPKM > 5) in MⅡ oocytes, but with lower FPKM (FPKM < 5) in late 2-cell embryos were defined as maternal genes. Minor ZGA genes were identified as not expressed in MⅡ oocytes (FPKM < 0.5) and lowly expressed in late 2-cell embryos (FPKM ≤ 5), but up-regulated in 1-cell or early 2-cell embryos (FPKM > 2). Major ZGA genes were identified as not expressed in MⅡ oocytes (FPKM < 0.5) and lowly expressed in 1-cell or early 2-cell embryos (FPKM < 5), but highly up-regulated in late 2-cell embryos (FPKM > 5).

Ribo-seq and data analysis

Control and 4E-T-overexpressing embryos were collected at 1-cell stage (17 hpi). 50 embryos were lysed in 300 ul iced lysis buffer (20 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.4, 150 mM NaCl, 5 mM MgCl2, 1 mM DTT, 100 µg/ml CHX, 1% Triton X-100; 25 U/ml Turbo DNase (Ambion, AM2239)) for 10 min on ice. The lysate was then treated with 1 ul RNaseI 100 U/µl (Ambion, AM2295) and incubated at room temperature for 45 min with gentle mixing. The nuclease digestion was stopped by adding 10 µl SUPERase·In (Ambion, AM2696). Subsequently, ribosome-protected fragments (RPFs) were separated by the conventional Ribo-seq [30]. RPFs library construction was conducted using Ezra-seq approach as previously described [31]. All libraries were sequenced on Illumina NovaSeq 6000 sequencers.

The Ribo-seq raw reads were trimmed by fastp (version 0.23.4). The quality of sequencing data, both before and after trimming, was assessed using FastQC (version 0.12.1). Trimmed reads were filtered by SortMeRNA (version 4.3.6) [32]. The clean reads were mapped to mm10 genome by STAR (version 2.7.11b) [25], then quantified by Salmon (version 1.10.3) [33]. Gene-level abundance counts were derived from transcript-level estimates using the tximport R package (version 1.20.1). FPKM values were computed for each gene from their read counts and effective lengths. Pearson correlation coefficients between replicates were calculated using log2 (FPKM + 1) values for all genes. Read distribution for UTR and CDS were characterized by RseQC (version 5.0.3) [26]. The distribution of RPF 5’ ends across the three translation frames was assessed using the quality module of Ribo-TISH (version 0.2.7) [34]. The read length distribution for uniquely mapped reads was obtained from ribotricer (version 1.3.3) [35]. For visualization, a smoothing parameter (span of 0.2) was applied. For integrated analyses, we used FPKM + 10 (RPF) and FPKM + 1 (RNA) to calculate fold changes for differential translation and gene expression analyses between the two comparison groups [36]. Genes were defined as a gene detected in both RNA-seq and Ribo-seq (FPKM > 0.1) and showing at least 1.5 absolute log₂ fold change. Constants 1 and 10 were added to avoid dividing small numbers.

Statistical analyses and graphs

Statistical analyses were implemented with R (RNA-seq, Proteomics and Ribo-seq) and GraphPad Prism 9.0 (Quantification). Quantitative analyses were presented as the mean ± SEM. P values < 0.05 were regarded as significant (*p < 0.05; *p < 0.01; ***p < 0.001). Schematics were downloaded from www.biorender.com.

Results

The inhibition of UPS-mediated protein degradation by MG132 impairs embryonic development and ZGA

To investigate the role of UPS in pre-implantation development, we first detected the dynamics of proteasomal activity in early mouse embryos by monitoring poly-ubiquitinated protein levels. Western blot analysis showed an accumulation of poly-ubiquitinated proteins at 12 h post-insemination (hpi) (Fig. S1a, S1b), reflecting active UPS-mediated degradation during this period. Subsequently, we treated the 1-cell embryos (12–18 hpi) with the 1µM proteasome inhibitor MG132 (Fig. 1a) under previously optimized conditions that ensure effective yet transient suppression of proteasomal activity with the least non-specific developmental arrest. Specifically, 1µM MG132 treatment would not lead to the immediately embryonic arrest at 1-cell stage, however it significantly impacted subsequent embryonic development (Fig. S1c). Embryos exposed to MG132 exhibited a pronounced delay and arrest at the 2-cell stage, with the majority failing to progress to the 4-cell stage on schedule, in contrast to control embryos, which developed normally (Fig. 1b and c). Given the established correlation between 2-cell arrest and ZGA [37], a crucially cellular event in pre-implantation development. We next assessed nascent transcriptional activity using 5-ethynyl-uridine (EU) incorporation. To comprehensively characterize the transcriptional alterations induced by proteasome inhibition, we performed RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) on control and MG132-treated embryos between 13 and 39 hpi (Fig. 1a). And the transcripts of control and MG132-treated embryos were compared at the same time points and developmental stages. Transcriptional dynamics were largely unaffected during the late 1-cell stage (13 and 17 hpi) in MG132-treated embryos. However, profound disruptions emerged at the 2-cell stage, coinciding with the major ZGA (Fig. 1d; Supplementary Table S2). By categorizing differentially expressed genes into pre-defined three clusters (maternal, minor ZGA and major ZGA) (Fig. S1f; Supplementary Table S3), we found that MG132 treatment significantly suppressed the expression of major ZGA genes while upregulating transcripts associated with maternal RNAs (Fig. 1e). These findings were validated by quantitative PCR (qPCR) analysis of selected genes from the maternal and major ZGA clusters (Fig. S1g). Together, these results suggest that the inhibition of UPS-mediated protein degradation with MG132 disrupts the timely clearance of maternal transcripts and the proper ZGA, ultimately impairing pre-implantation development.

Identification of proteins accumulated in MG132-treated embryos

To investigate the functional impact of proteasome inhibition, we first confirmed the accumulation of poly-ubiquitinated proteins in MG132-treated embryos by immunoblot analysis. The significant effect of MG132 (markedly increased poly-ubiquitinated proteins) was observed at 17 hpi, 5 h after MG132 treatment (Fig. S2a, S2b). To systematically identify proteins whose expression is regulated by the UPS during this critical stage, we performed tandem mass tag (TMT)-based quantitative proteomic analysis. Protein expression profiles were performed across MII oocytes, late 1-cell embryos (12 and 17 hpi), and early 2-cell embryos (25 hpi) (Fig. 2a). And the protein expression of control and MG132-treated embryos were compared at the same time points and developmental stages. Principal component analysis (PCA) indicated good intra-group repeatability and clear separation between control and MG132-treated embryos (Fig. 2b). Since MG132 was applied from 12 to 18 hpi, we focused on the differentially expressed proteins at 17 hpi to capture early effects of UPS inhibition. As a result, a total of 5,643 proteins were identified. Consistent with the role of MG132 in blocking protein degradation, the majority of differentially expressed proteins (95 out of 146, 65.07%) were up-regulated in abundance following MG132 treatment (Fig. 2c). GO (Gene Ontology) enrichment analysis indicated that the up-regulated proteins were significantly associated with RNA binding and regulation of translation in both MF (molecular function) and BP (biological process) categories (Fig. 2d, S2c). Notably, 13.68% (13 out of 95) of the up-regulated proteins were RNA-binding proteins (RBPs) (Fig. S2d), which was significantly more than identified RBPs of the total proteome (7.96%, 449 out of 5,643; Fisher’s exact test: P value = 0.037) (Fig. S2e). Further proteomic inspection of expression dynamics revealed that most of these up-regulated RBPs were rapidly degraded after fertilization under physiological conditions (Fig. 2e). These results suggest that the timely degradation of specific RBPs mediated by the UPS is critical for normal embryonic development and the proper initiation of ZGA.

Abnormal accumulation of 4E-T in zygotes causes developmental arrest and defective ZGA

To identify RBPs whose degradation after fertilization is crucial for embryogenesis, we selected six candidate RBPs (ATXN2, RBM38, SECISBP2L, LSM14A, LSM14B, 4E-T) that are normally degraded after fertilization, referencing the mouse oocyte RBPome database [39]. These six RBPs exhibited a fold change greater than 1.45 at 17 hpi, and have documented roles in translational regulation [40–44] (Fig. S2d). We then microinjected in vitro-transcribed mRNA encoding these RBPs into WT zygotes to mimic their abnormal accumulation (Fig. 3a). While overexpression of all six RBPs impaired development to varying degrees, embryos overexpressing 4E-T exhibited the most severe defects, with the majority arresting at the 2-cell stage and failing to progress to the blastocyst stage (Fig. 3b and c). Furthermore, a previous study has reported that 4E-T undergoes ubiquitination in HEK293 cells, indicating that 4E-T may serve as a substrate for UPS-mediated degradation [45]. We next asked whether 4E-T accumulation affects transcriptional activation during the maternal-to-zygotic transition. Using 5-ethynyl uridine (EU) incorporation assays, we observed differential EU signals in 4E-T-overexpressing 2-cell embryos, but not in zygotes, indicating delay and dysregulation in the timing of transcriptional activation, a phenotype reminiscent of that caused by MG132 treatment (Fig. S3c, S3d). To comprehensively define the transcriptomic changes, we performed RNA-seq on control and 4E-T-overexpressing embryos collected at 1-cell (13 and 17 hpi) and 2-cell (25 and 33 hpi) stages. This analysis revealed minimal differential gene expression at the 1-cell stage but significant transcriptomic alterations at the 2-cell stage (Fig. 3d; Supplementary Table S5). Comparison with pre-defined gene clusters (maternal, minor ZGA and major ZGA) showed that 4E-T overexpression led to a pronounced suppression of major ZGA transcripts and a concomitant enrichment of maternal transcripts (Fig. 3e), a finding validated by qPCR (Fig. S3e). These findings indicate that the accumulation of 4E-T severely compromises developmental potential by specifically disrupting the major ZGA.

As an RNA-binding protein, 4E-T (EIF4ENIF1) is highly expressed in mouse oocytes [46]. To define its physiological dynamics, we assessed 4E-T protein levels by western blot and immunofluorescence during early development. Both methods showed a sharp decline in 4E-T abundance after fertilization, with minimal levels maintained through the 2-cell stage (Fig. 3f and g). In summary, our data demonstrate that the timely degradation of 4E-T following fertilization is a critical prerequisite for successful pre-implantation development and the proper initiation of the ZGA program.

To check translational activity in zygotes, we performed an L-homopropargylglycine (HPG) incorporation assay to detect nascent protein synthesis. Interestingly, MG132-treated zygotes exhibited a pronounced reduction in HPG signal compared with controls (Fig. S4c, S4d). We then overexpressed either wild-type 4E-T or the binding-deficient mutant (4E-T-Δ4E-BM) in zygotes (Fig. S4e). While overexpression of wild-type 4E-T significantly suppressed global translation, zygotes overexpressing the 4E-T-Δ4E-BM mutant showed no such repression (Fig. 5e and f). Consistent with these findings, embryonic development was severely compromised in 4E-T-overexpressing zygotes, which largely arrested at the 2-cell stage; in contrast, expression of the binding-deficient mutant did not cause significant developmental arrest (Fig. 5g and h). To further confirmed the accumulated 4E-T through binding to eIF4E/eIF4E1B to exert effect, we tested whether overexpression of eIF4E and/or eIF4E1B could counteract 4E‑T‑mediated repression (Fig. S4f). Indeed, we found that co‑expression of eIF4E or/and eIF4E1B partially rescues the developmental arrest caused by 4E‑T overexpression, more embryos which overexpressing eIF4E and/or eIF4E1B developed to the 4-cell and blastocyst stage (Fig. S4g). Given that both eIF4E and eIF4E1B are essential for early embryogenesis [53, 54], we propose that aberrantly accumulated 4E-T sequesters these critical initiation factors, thereby obstructing their function and preventing the translational activation required for ZGA and subsequent development. Together, these results demonstrate that 4E-T functions as a translation repressor in zygotes, and that its deleterious effects depend on physical interaction with eIF4E and eIF4E1B.

Discussion

The maternal-to-zygotic transition (MZT), also referred to as the oocyte-to-embryo transition, marks the shift from maternal control to zygotic autonomy [10]. This process is driven by two key events: the degradation of maternal factors and zygotic genome activation (ZGA), with maternal degradation initiating prior to ZGA. Notably, maternal proteins are quickly degraded once they are no longer needed. A recent proteomic study revealed that 554 proteins are down-regulated in abundance from human MI oocyte to 1-cell embryo (log2 fold change > 0.5), and 308 proteins are down-regulated from mice MII oocyte to late 1-cell embryo (log2 fold change > 0.5) [57]. In eukaryotes, the ubiquitin-proteasome system (UPS) serves as a major pathway for intracellular protein degradation in eukaryotes. It involves a cascade of enzymes: E1 (ubiquitin-activating enzyme), E2 (ubiquitin-activating enzyme) and E3 (ubiquitin ligase), that mediate ubiquitin attachment to the substrate proteins, targeting them for degradation by the 26 S proteasome. The role of UPS-mediated maternal protein degradation has been continuously investigated. During Drosophila MZT, the E3 ubiquitin ligase C-terminal to Lis1 Homology (CTLH) complex degrades Cup, TRAL, ME31B and the E3 ubiquitin ligase Skp/Cullin/F-box-containing (SCF) complex degrades SMG to eliminate translational repression [19]. In vertebrates, after fertilization, the SCF complex recognizes phosphorylated EMI2/XERP1, promoting its ubiquitination and degradation. This in turn relieves inhibition on the anaphase-promoting complex/cyclosome (APC/C) [58, 59]. Reactivated APC/C, together with its coactivator CDC20, targets cyclin B for degradation, enabling exit from MII [60]. In mice, the RING-type E3 ligase RFPl4 directs the proteasomal degradation of Cyclin B1 to regulate oocyte meiosis to mitosis progression [61]. And maternal protein, PIASy (protein inhibitor of activated STATy) is degraded via the UPS, its overexpression results in 2-cell stage arrest and compromised zygotic transcription [62]. Furthermore, our previous studies suggested that deletion of the E3 ligase RNF114 leads to 2-cell arrest and defective major ZGA, and that the timely degradation of its substrates TAB1 and CBX5 is essential for embryogenesis [63, 64]. In addition, proteasomal inhibition by MG132 during the G1 phase of the 1-cell embryo causes developmental arrest and delays minor ZGA [15]. MG132 treatment also delays RNA polymerase II recruitment to pronuclei and impairs transcription of key major ZGA genes at the 2-cell stage [16]. These results indicate that proteasome inhibition exerts stage-dependent effects on embryonic development and ZGA progression, underscoring the crucial role of maternal protein degradation during the 1-cell stage in facilitating ZGA.

Following the UPS, autophagy is the second most recognized degradation pathway for proteins. A recent study found that autophagy inhibition (autophagy inhibitor chloroquine treatment) gave rise to an obvious delay in ZGA-dependent maternal mRNA clearance (Z-decay), leading to defective ZGA and embryonic arrest at various stages. Maternal Suv39h2 mRNAs are degraded through autography, which reduced H3K9me3 modification on MERVL retrotransposons [65]. Unlike UPS, autophagy is largely associated with the degradation of non-protein targets during early embryogenesis. It mediates the turnover of cytoplasmic organelles, and is implicated in the clearance of maternal mRNA [65–67]. Taken together, intersection between UPS and autophagy are indispensable for the proper degradation and storage of maternal material to sustain normal pre-implantation development.

During Drosophila MZT, RNA-binding proteins, including Cup, TRAL, ME31B, and SMG, have been well characterized as translational repressors, clearance of whose guarantee an orderly MZT [19]. While the significance of degrading specific maternal proteins as repressors for MZT remains poorly understood in mammals, just as the role of the eIF4E-binding protein 4E-T during mammalian maternal-to-zygotic transition has not been previously reported. In this study, we applied the proteasome inhibitor MG132 during the G2 phase of the 1-cell (12–18 hpi), a stage characterized by abundant poly-ubiquitinated proteins. MG132 treatment resulted in developmental delay and arrest, along with disruption of the ZGA transcriptional program, in line with previous studies [15, 16]. Transcriptome analysis further confirmed a failure in major ZGA and, unexpectedly, revealed dysregulated maternal RNA metabolism at the 2-cell stage, marked by persistent accumulation of maternal transcripts. A plausible explanation is that the stabilization of maternal proteins interferes with ZGA-dependent maternal mRNA clearance (Z-decay), which is mediated by zygotic factors. The comparable numbers of higher- and lower-level genes in MG132-treated versus control 2-cell embryos may further reflect defective Z-decay. To explore how maternal protein accumulation impedes development and ZGA, we conducted proteomic profiling of MG132-treated embryos. We found that 65.07% of differentially expressed proteins were up-regulated in the 1-cell embryo after proteasomal inhibition. GO analysis indicated strong enrichment of these accumulated proteins in RNA-binding and translational regulation functions. Notably, these RNA-binding proteins (RBPs) normally decline in abundance from MII oocytes to the late 2-cell stage. By cross-referencing with a published oocyte RBPome [39], we identified six highly up-regulated RBPs (ATXN2, RBM38, SECISBP2L, LSM14A, LSM14B, and 4E-T) that are substantially degraded after fertilization in WT embryos. Overexpression of each RBP in zygotes, mimicking their abnormal accumulation, resulted in developmental defects to varying degrees. And all the six RBPs have been reported to be involved in translation regulation. The absence of ATXN2 resulted in decreased protein synthesis and increased phosphorylation of ribosomal protein S6 [40]. RBM38 inhibited the translation of p53 mRNA by interacting with eIF4E [44]. SECISBP2L could promote the translation of selenoprotein DIO2 and participate in oligodendrocyte differentiation [43]. LSM14A as a translation repressor in Xenopus oocytes [42]. LSM14B and 4E-T as components of the mitochondria-associated ribonucleoprotein domain (MARDO) in oocytes, and participated in maternal mRNA translation repression [41]. Given that the overexpression of these six RBPs in zygotes could impair embryonic development to varying degrees, it is reasonable to speculate that abnormal accumulation of RBPs may affect embryonic development by affecting translation. Notably, excess 4E-T produced the most severe phenotype, causing 2-cell arrest and significant down-regulation of major ZGA genes. In addition, unexpectedly, 4E-T-overexpressing and MG132-treated embryos showed abnormal transcriptional activation at the late 2-cell stage (32–34 hpi) (Fig. S1e, S3d). We compared expressed major-ZGA genes at 25 hpi control with 25, 31, 33 hpi upon MG132 treatment or 4E-T overexpression. The results showed that the overlap of major ZGA genes were progressively increased from 25 to 33 hpi (Fig. S3f, S3g), indicating that these major ZGA genes, which normally be activated early, were not completely expressed upon ZGA initiation in MG132-treated or 4E-T-overexpressing embryos; instead, they were progressively expressed in increasing number as development proceeded. And the expression level (FPKM value) of these delay-activated ZGA genes also were significantly increased from 25 to 33 hpi, reflecting a combination of delayed and mistimed activation of major ZGA genes.

4E-T has been identified as a component of MARDO in full-grown mammalian oocytes, where it contributes to maternal mRNA storage, degradation and translational repression to support meiotic maturation [41]. In vertebrate oocytes, 4E-T is essential for maintaining prophase-I arrest via translational repression, a role demonstrated by the fact that its acute loss via the fast-acting TRIM-Away technique triggers spontaneous meiotic resumption in mouse and frog oocytes [68]. Our findings further indicate that abnormal accumulation of 4E-T in zygote enhances its translational repression activity, likely through interactions with eIF4E and germ cell-specific eIF4E1B. However, the precise mechanisms by which 4E-T represses translation remain incompletely resolved. One proposed model is that 4E-T, as an eIF4E-binding protein (4E-BP), competes with eIF4G for binding to eIF4E, thereby disrupting the assembly of the eIF4F initiation complex [50]. Previous studies have established that translational activation prior to ZGA is essential for ZGA and embryonic development [5, 36, 48], and the germ cell-specific eIF4E1B has been defined as a key regulator of maternal mRNA translation necessary for ZGA [54]. A novel insight from our study is that accumulated 4E-T represses the translation of several key genes, such as Zbed3 and Kdm4a, which are normally translationally up-regulated after fertilization. ZBED3 deficiency has been shown to cause developmental delay beyond the 2-cell stage [55], while KDM4A ensures proper ZGA by preventing aberrant H3K4me3–H3K9me3 bivalency, thereby supporting normal pre-implantation development [56].

Double-stranded DNA together with histone constitute chromatin, in which histone modifications can regulate chromatin accessibility according to specific modifications, histone types, and targeted amino acid residues, thereby activating or inhibiting gene expression [69, 70]. Generally, H3K9me3 is thought to be associated with transcriptional repression [71]. The repressive H3K9me3 is deposited in female pronuclei in fertilized eggs. The asymmetric distribution, mediated by EHMT2 and SETDB1, protects DNA in female pronuclei from TET3-mediated demethylation, maintaining the silencing of specific genes [72]. Excessive H3K9me3 in somatic cell nuclear transfer (SCNT) embryos prevents the removal of topologically associating domain (TAD), results in developmental disorders and ZGA failure, overexpression of demethylase KDM4D or knockdown of methyltransferases Suv39h1/2 can be rescued [73, 74]. A recent study found that overexpression of Suv39h2 in zygotes caused ZGA-associated genes down-regulate and developmental arrest of embryo [65]. Loss of the H3K9me3 demethylase KDM4A results in the spread of aberrant H3K9me3 over the broad H3K4me3 region (bdH3K4me3) and leads to insufficient activation of ZGA-deficient transcription [56]. We verified the aberrant elevation of H3K9me3 in female pronuclei in abnormally accumulated 4E-T zygotes. Thus, impaired ZGA due to abnormal accumulation of 4E-T is closely related to the elevation of the repressive histone modification H3K9me3. Such an excessive heterochromatic barrier may restrict timely promoter access by transcription factors and RNA polymerase II, resulting in impaired transcriptional activation of ZGA genes. While we cannot completely rule out direct transcriptional interference, this chromatin‑mediated mechanism provides a compelling framework for understanding how translational repressor can ultimately impact on the transcriptional program.

Furthermore, our proteomic data revealed that other MARDO components, ZAR1 and LSM14B, which also known as translational repressors in oocytes [41, 75], were sharply degraded after fertilization and accumulated abnormally upon MG132 treatment. Although we did not observe MARDO-like granules in 4E-T-overexpressing zygotes, the co-accumulation of ZAR1 and LSM14B alongside 4E-T prompts us to hypothesize that these proteins may cooperate to repress translation in the zygote, rather than 4E-T acting as a primary nucleator of RNP granules. This repressive function might operate through a more diffuse mechanism or require specific conditions. Elucidating the nature of this potential cooperation, particularly whether it involves direct protein interactions, constitutes an important avenue for future investigation.

In summary, our findings support this model in which the rapid degradation of specific maternal RNA-binding proteins after fertilization is essential to alleviate translational repression, thereby permitting the subsequent translational activation required for ZGA. Inhibition of UPS-mediated protein degradation, leading to developmental arrest and a failure to initiate major ZGA, causes the abnormal persistence of maternal RNA-binding protein: 4E-T. The accumulated 4E-T exerts translational repression, which in turn depletes the synthesis of key developmental proteins like ZBED3 and KDM4A. Moreover, we uncover accumulated 4E-T induces abnormal elevation of the repressive histone modification H3K9me3 in the female pronucleus, representing a chromatin-based mechanism linking to translational repressor dysfunction during MZT.
