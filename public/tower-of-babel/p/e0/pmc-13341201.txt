A Case Report of Deep-Seated Satellite Lesions in DNET : Is It a Tumor?
Authors: Sung Hyun Kang, Sokhoeun Heng, Se Hoon Kim, Seung-Ki Kim, Ji Hoon Phi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Dysembryoplastic neuroepithelial tumor (DNET) is a benign, pediatric brain tumor characterized by focal seizures, often progressing to drug-resistant epilepsy. Satellite lesions (SLs), small lesions adjacent to the main tumor mass typically embedded within normal parenchyma, are identified in approximately 50% of DNET cases and may contribute to recurrent seizures if not totally removed. However, detailed histopathological descriptions of SL remain limited to date. We report on an 8-year-old boy with medically intractable seizures and a T2 hyperintense mass in the left frontal lobe with smaller deep-seated SLs. The lesions were near totally removed, with deep-seated SLs successfully removed by performing a wider resection including the adjacent normal white matter with the aid of intraoperative ultrasonography, rather than through direct visual inspection. Histological analysis of deep-seated SLs showed homogeneous, typical DNET features identical to the main mass, including glioneuronal columns with oligodendrocyte-like cells and floating neurons within a mucoid matrix, confirming that deep-seated SLs are true tumors. In order to reduce the risk of recurrence, maximal safe resection of deep-seated SLs with the aid of ultrasonography might be helpful.

INTRODUCTION

Dysembryoplastic neuroepithelial tumor (DNET), one of glioneuronal and neuronal tumors according to the 2021 World Health Organization (WHO) classification of tumors of the central nervous system (CNS), is a benign tumor occurring mostly in children and adolescents [4]. Focal seizures with or without impaired awareness are the most common symptoms of DNET, which gradually progress to chronic drug-resistant epilepsy [5]. Satellite lesions (SLs), small dots or crescent-shaped lesions adjacent to the main tumor mass but surrounded by normal parenchyma, are found in up to 50% of DNETs. Complete resection including both the main mass and SLs is known to be most crucial for tumor control, and presence of SLs is known to be related with the risk of seizure recurrence [6,8]. Although DNET is benign tumor with fairly good surgical outcome with a median gross total removal rate of 79% and median seizure freedom rate of 86% [1], SLs often hamper complete resection. SLs located near the cortex are relatively easy to remove, but deep-seated SLs are hard to remove because removal of even small areas can damage important tracts and can cause massive neurological deficits. Therefore, neurosurgeons might face difficulty in planning operations with DNETs accompanying deep-seated SLs since maximal resection may do more harm than good.

The three characteristic pathological features of DNET are multinodular intracortical pattern, glioneuronal columns with oligodendrocyte-like cells (OLCs) and floating neurons in the mucoid matrix [5]. We have discussed about the pathological feature of SL in a previous study [3], but the SLs were not removed en bloc but rather in small, piecemeal specimens. Some SLs were shown as true tumors, while other SLs were revealed to be reactive gliosis and foamy macrophage infiltration. Thus, it remained unclear whether SL was a homogeneous lesion or a heterogeneous lesion composed of true tumor and inflammatory change such as gliosis.

In this case report, we showed the pathological feature of SL distinctive from adjacent normal tissues by sampling a wider region. Also, we suggest the use of ultrasonography to identify and remove deep-seated SLs accurately and minimize postoperative neurological sequelae.

CASE REPORT

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Seoul National University Hospital (approval No. H-2502-051-1612).

An 8-year-old boy visited the hospital due to recurrent seizures that started a month ago. He had no other neurological abnormalities. Brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) showed a T2 hyper-intense mass with multicystic structures in the left frontal cortex and adjacent white matter without enhancement (Fig. 1A-C). Two deep-seated T2 hyperintense lesions medial to the main mass were identified at the periventricular white matter. Patient’s age, clinical presentation and imaging features suggested DNET as the most likely diagnosis. Despite the combination therapy of oxcarbazepine, topiramate, clobazam, the frequency of seizure increased from once a month to twice a week. Follow-up MRI after 6 months and 1 year showed no evident change of tumor size. Craniotomy and tumor removal was performed due to medically intractable seizures. The main mass was clearly delineated with surrounding normal tissues and was delicately dissected. After removal of the main mass, a normal-looking layer of white matter was identified (Fig. 1D). With the help of intraoperative ultrasonography, hyperechoic foci were identified just beneath the white matter (Fig. 1E), matching with the SLs identified in preoperative MRI. SLs were removed en bloc with surrounding white matter, revealing part of ependymal lining of the lateral ventricle (Fig. 1F). There was no change of motor evoked potential and somatosensory evoked potential during the surgery. The postoperative MR image showed the disappearance of the SLs, replaced by a dark hemorrhagic signal (Fig. 1G). Near total removal was achieved with possible lesions of the main mass remaining near the superior frontal gyrus. The patient recovered without neurological deficits, and has been seizure-free for 6 months. Follow-up MRI after 6 months showed no size change of the possible remaining lesions of the main mass (Fig. 1H). Tapering of anti-seizure medication is in progress.

The main mass showed typical features of DNET, a multinodular pattern with glioneuronal columns, OLCs, and floating neurons in the mucoid matrix (Fig. 2A-C). Oligodendrocyte transcription factor 2 (OLIG-2) was expressed in the OLCs and dense staining of the Alcian blue indicated mucoid matrix (Fig. 2D and E).

On the specimen including the SL, SL exhibited homogeneous glioneuronal component, pathognomonic element of DNET with the presence of mucoid matrix (Fig. 3). SL was distinctive from the adjacent normal tissue in Hematoxylin & Eosin stain (Fig. 3A). In contrast to the densely bluish stained area of the normal white matter, SL was faintly stained by Luxol fast blue (Fig. 3C). SL showed pure features of DNET, identically with those of the main mass.

DISCUSSION

In brain tumor classification, the role of molecular diagnostics is increasing rapidly. Yet, according to the most recent version of WHO classification of tumors of the CNS, the most important diagnostic criterion for DNET remains the histopathological component, specifically, the presence of the specific glioneuronal element. Fibroblast growth factor receptor 1 gene alteration and methylation profiles are used as supplementary tools in unresolved cases [7]. Although SLs were mentioned as nodular foci beneath the main mass at the original description of DNET by Daumas-Duport et al. [2], previous literature lacks a detailed histopathological description of SL. In this study, we examined the histology of SL in depth by sampling a wider region including adjacent normal tissues.

SL demonstrated typical DNET characteristics, identical with the main mass, as observed in our previous study [3]. We enhanced our previous work in two ways. First, we performed more staining methods such as Luxol fast blue, Alcian blue and OLIG-2, which revealed that SL had a homogeneous histological feature distinct from adjacent normal tissues, suggesting SL can be described as a true tumor. Second, we successfully identified and excised most of the SLs by performing a wider resection including the adjacent normal white matter with the aid of intraoperative ultrasonography, rather than through direct visual inspection. To the best of our knowledge, no previous literature has described the sonographic characteristics of DNET. In this case report, we demonstrated that SLs of DNET showed nodular contour with hyperechoic signals. Since SLs typically present as multiple small nodules, complete visual identification and removal can be challenging, often resulting in suboptimal clinical outcomes [8]. Using intraoperative ultrasonography may partially address this challenge. However, this method is more suitable for tumors located in non-eloquent areas, as in this case, because it results in greater removal of normal white matter. Surgery in our previous study [3] could not employ this method due to the tumor’s location in an eloquent area, near the primary motor cortex.

Limitations

There are a few limitations in this case report. First, since some SLs in our previous study have been identified as gliosis [3], some deep-seated SLs might not be true tumors. Further studies with more number of patients might help identify the proportion of true tumors in SLs. Second, there have been some doubts that although the main mass and SL appear to be separated in MRI, it may have been microscopically connected. We tried to sample the intervening white matter between the main mass and SL to analyze this. However, since the distance between the main mass and SL was very close (approximately 1 mm) and we attempted to sample SL only after completing resection of the main mass, we failed to sample the intervening white matter. Thus, the possibility of microscopic infiltration or extension between the main mass and SL could not be completely disproved. In future studies, sampling the SL together with a portion of the main mass, with guidance of intraoperative ultrasonography, can be a feasible alternative for successfully obtaining the intervening white matter layer.

CONCLUSION

With the assistance of intraoperative ultrasonography, we successfully removed deep-seated SLs of DNET without direct visual inspection and confirmed that deep-seated SLs exhibit identical histological characteristics to the main mass, supporting their identity as true tumors. Therefore in case of deep-seated SLs, use of ultrasonography might be helpful in reducing the risk of recurrence.
