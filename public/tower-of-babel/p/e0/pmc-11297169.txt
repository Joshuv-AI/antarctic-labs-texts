Association of plant-based diet indexes with the metabolomic profile
Authors: Elaine Hillesheim, Wenxuan Liu, Xiaofei Yin, Thomas Smith, Lorraine Brennan
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Plant-based diets have gained attention for their potential benefits on both human health and environmental sustainability. The objective of this study was to investigate the association of plant-based dietary patterns with the endogenous metabolites of healthy individuals and identify metabolites that may act as mediators of the associations between dietary intake and modifiable disease risk factors. Adherence to plant-based dietary patterns was assessed for 170 healthy adults using plant-based diet indexes (PDI). Individuals with higher healthful PDI had lower BMI and fasting glucose and higher HDL-C, while those with higher unhealthful PDI had higher BMI, triacylglycerol and fasting glucose and lower HDL-C. Unhealthful PDI was associated with higher levels of several amino acids and biogenic amines previously associated with cardiometabolic diseases and an opposite pattern was observed for healthful PDI. Furthermore, healthful PDI was associated with higher levels of glycerophosphocholines containing very long-chain fatty acids. Glutamate, isoleucine, proline, tyrosine, α-aminoadipate and kynurenine had a statistically significant mediation effect on the associations between PDI scores and LDL-C, HDL-C and fasting glucose. These findings contribute to the growing evidence supporting the role of plant-based diets in promoting metabolic health and shed light on the potential mechanisms explaining their beneficial health effects.

Introduction

Research interest grows around plant-based diets as a means to optimise both human health and environmental sustainability1. While sustainable food systems are closely intertwined with all United Nations Sustainable Development Goals2, a low intake of plant food groups is among the top dietary risk factors contributing to mortality according to the Global Burden of Diseases report3. Plant-based diets are characterised by an elevated consumption of plant foods, such as fruits, vegetables, grains, legumes, nuts etc. and their derivates, and may involve a restriction or exclusion of animal products in comparison to conventional diets4,5. Over the past two decades, various diet quality indexes were developed to assess the quality of plant-based diets6. A recent systematic review identified a total of 35 unique plant-based diet indexes published between 2007 and 20226. Notably, the plant-based diet index (PDI) proposed by Satija et al.7 emerged as the most frequently employed. This index generates three dietary scores that not only evaluate the overall consumption of plant-based foods (overall PDI) but crucially distinguish between their qualities (healthful PDI and unhealthful PDI).

Epidemiological studies have extensively explored the associations between PDI scores and the incidence of non-communicable diseases and their risk factors. For example, higher adherence to healthful PDI has been consistently associated with a lower incidence of type 2 diabetes, while associations with overall PDI and unhealthful PDI are less clear7–11. Similar findings were reported for the incidence of cardiovascular diseases (CVD)12–14, CVD and all-cause mortality14–16 and their risk factors, including associations with higher BMI and blood pressure and pro-atherogenic lipid profile17,18. In summary, these results indicate that not only the amount of plant foods consumed is important, but the quality of such foods is key when studying impacts on health.

Understanding the physiological impacts and molecular mechanisms of plant-based diets is crucial for continuing to support their recommendation. In recent years, targeted metabolomic platforms have emerged as highly sensitive and reproducible tools, making them valuable for quantitative metabolomics analysis in nutrition research19. Their outputs can enable the identification of metabolite signatures associated with plant-based diets and aid the investigation of mechanisms linking diet to health20. Nevertheless, a limited number of studies have used metabolites to explain the associations between plant-based diets and disease risk factors or disease incidence. A recent study demonstrated that, in Danish overweight and obese individuals, plasma metabolites related to food and food-related microbes accounted for 12% and 14% of the association between the metabolic syndrome and healthful PDI and unhealthful PDI, respectively21. Similarly, a plasma metabolite score associated with overall PDI explained 37% of the risk of type 2 diabetes in three large American cohorts20.

In the present study, the association of plant-based dietary patterns with endogenous metabolites of healthy individuals was investigated. Additionally, we identified metabolites that may act as mediators for the associations between dietary intake and modifiable disease risk factors.

Methods

Study design

The A-DIET Confirm (ADC) is an observational study designed to examine the habitual dietary intake of free-living individuals during a period of four months. The detailed study information was previously described22. Briefly, healthy men and women, aged 18–60 years old, with a body mass index (BMI) between 18.5 and 30 kg/m2 and not taking any prescribed medication or supplements were included. Individuals diagnosed with any health condition, pregnant or lactating were excluded. Study visits occurred once a month for four consecutive months, preferably in the same period of each month. In total, 191 individuals were recruited, but 5 did not have blood available on the first visit and 16 dropped out after the first visit. One hundred seventy individuals who had blood available on the first visit and completed two or more visits were included in the analyses. Data was collected following a 12-h overnight fast and included anthropometry (weight, height, waist circumference and hip circumference), dietary intake and blood sample. The study protocol was approved by the ethics review board of the University College Dublin (LS-16-91-Gibbons-Brennan), conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and all individuals enrolled provided written informed consent.

Dietary intake and plant-based diet indexes

Habitual dietary intake was assessed by a 24-h dietary recall administered at each visit. The protocol was based on the U.S. Department of Agriculture Automated Multiple-Pass Method23 and the portion sizes and conversion to grams were determined using a photographic food atlas when the accurate amount of consumed food was not known24. Energy and nutrients were estimated independently by two investigators using Nutritics software (Dublin, Ireland) and cross-checked for any discrepancies. For the calculation of the plant-based diet indexes, mixed dishes and beverages were disaggregated into ingredients using Irish25 and British26 food composition databases. Overall PDI, healthful PDI and unhealthful PDI scores were then calculated as described by Satija et al.7. Foods were classified into 18 food groups, which included seven healthy plant foods groups (whole grains, fruits, vegetables, nuts, legumes, vegetable oils, tea & coffee), five less healthy plants food groups (fruit juices, refined grains, potatoes, sugar-sweetened beverages, sweets & desserts) and six animal foods groups (animal fat, dairy, eggs, fish & seafood, meats, miscellaneous animal-based foods). The daily individual intake (grams/day) in each food group was determined and the mean was used to classify the individuals into quintiles of the food groups. In case of a high prevalence of null consumption in a food group, all individuals with null consumption were assigned to the lowest quintile and the remaining individuals were classified into quartiles. Quintiles received consecutive scores from 1 to 5 with the direction of scoring varying according to the dietary index. For overall PDI, all plant foods groups were scored positively (quintile 1 = 1, quintile 5 = 5), while for healthful PDI the healthy plant food groups were scored positively and the less healthy food groups were scored reversely (quintile 1 = 5, quintile 5 = 1) and for unhealthful PDI the healthy plant food groups were scored reversely and the less healthy food groups were scored positively. Animal food groups were scored reversely in all indexes. The 18 food group scores for an individual were summed to obtain their indexes, with a possible total score range from 18 to 90 points. Sensitivity analyses were performed by excluding the individuals classified as energy misreporters using the average energy intake. The Goldberg cut-off method27, with the basal metabolic rate estimated using Henry’s equations and a PAL of 1.5528, was applied. The within-subject variation in energy intake was 21.0% and 28 (16.5%) individuals were identified as energy misreporters (under-reporters = 27, over-reporters = 1). A total of 142 individuals were included in the sensitivity analyses.

Clinical biochemistry

Blood was collected by a trained phlebotomist using a lithium heparin tube and processed following a standardised procedure (centrifuged at 1800g for 10 min at 4 °C and stored in 500 µL aliquotes at − 80 °C). Plasma samples from visit 1 were used for clinical biochemistry and metabolomic analyses. Blood biochemistry markers included plasma glucose, total cholesterol, high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) and triacylglycerol (all in mmol/L) with typical coefficients of variation of < 2% with the exception of HDL-C which was < 4%. Clinical chemistry markers were measured on a Roche 702 clinical chemistry analyser using Roche reagents and methodology.

Metabolomic analyses

Plasma samples underwent targeted metabolomic analysis using the AbsoluteIDQ p180 kit (Biocrates, Austria). Samples from an individual were prepared in the same batch with positions randomised in the assay plate. Ten μL of samples (phosphate-buffered saline, calibration standard solutions, quality controls, plasma or pooled plasma) were added to the capture assay plate and left to dry at room temperature for 30 min. Subsequently, samples were derivatised with 50 μL of a solution containing 5% phenyl isothiocyanate in ethanol:water:pyridine (1:1:1 volume ratio), followed by a 25-min incubation period at room temperature and a 60-min drying period under a nitrogen stream. Metabolites were extracted with 300 μL of 5 mM ammonium acetate in methanol by shaking the plate for 30 min, followed by centrifugation at 500 g for 2 min. For LC–MS/MS analysis, 150 μL of eluate was diluted with equal volume of HPLC water, while for FIA-MS/MS analysis, 50 μL of eluate was diluted with 450 μL of methanol.

The analysis was carried out on a Sciex QTRAP 6500+ mass spectrometer coupled to a Sciex ExionLC series UHPLC system following previously published methods29. Amino acids and biogenic amines were identified using LC–MS/MS in positive mode, and lipids and the sum of hexoses were identified using FIA-MS/MS in positive mode. A total of 137 metabolites were identified encompassing 20 amino acids, 10 biogenic amines, free carnitine, 9 fatty acylcarnitines (C), 14 sphingomyelins (SM), 10 monoacylglycerophosphocholines (LPC), 35 diacylglycerophosphocholines (PC), 37 1-alkyl,2-acylglycerophosphocholines (PC-O) and the sum of hexoses.

Quantification of amino acids and biogenic amines was achieved using isotopically labelled internal standards and seven-point calibration curves in the AB Sciex Analyst software v 1.7.2. Lipids and the sum of hexoses were semi-quantitatively measured using 14 internal standards in MetIDQ software. MetIDQ software was also used to assess data quality through the accuracy and reproducibility of quality controls. Metabolites concentrations are reported in micromoles, with only those with levels above the limit of detection in more than 75% of the samples being included in statistical analyses.

Statistical analyses

Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation. Normality of data was determined through visual inspection of histograms and Q–Q plots. Demographic, metabolic and dietary variables not normally distributed were transformed prior analyses using log10. Differences in mean PDI scores between sexes were analysed using independent t-test. Associations between PDI scores and continuous variables were analysed using multivariate linear regression adjusted for sex, age and energy intake. A p value ≤ 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Metabolite levels were scaled prior analyses using z-score. Associations between PDI scores and metabolite levels were analysed using multivariate linear regression adjusted for sex, age and energy intake. Sensitivity analyses were performed by additionally adjusting the multivariate analyses for BMI and by excluding energy misreporters. Benjamini–Hochberg procedure was used to control multiple testing within PDI scores and a false discovery rate (FDR) ≤ 0.10 was considered statistically significant. Partial R2 for PDI scores were calculated using the R package “car” version 3.1-2.

We performed causal mediation analysis to investigate the role of metabolites as mediators in the association between PDI scores and clinical biochemistry markers. Only the non-lipid metabolites significantly associated with healthful PDI or unhealthful PDI scores in the multivariate analyses were investigated as mediators. The causal mediation analysis was performed as described in Tingley et al.30 using the R package “mediation” version 4.5.0 with 1000 nonparametric bootstrap simulations. The models included PDI scores as predictors, metabolite levels as mediators, clinical biochemistry markers as outcomes and sex, age and energy intake as covariates. For mediation analysis, statistical significance was defined as p value ≤ 0.05. All analyses were performed using R version 4.3.2 (www.r-project.org).

While associations with SM 33:1;O2 were negative, tyrosine and PC 32:1 were negatively associated with healthful PDI and overall PDI but positively associated with unhealthful PDI. PDI scores-metabolites associations in relation to type 2 diabetes risk

PDI scores have been repeatedly associated with type 2 diabetes (T2D) risk8–10,31. Similarly, a systematic review of prospective observational studies identified several plasma and serum metabolites significantly associated with T2D risk32. We plotted the associations between PDI scores and metabolites observed in A-DIET Confirm against the summary relative risks of metabolites associated with T2D risk32 to explore the direction of these associations (Fig. 4). Individuals with a higher intake of healthy plant foods (higher healthful PDI) had higher levels of metabolites associated with lower T2D risk (PC 42:0, PC 42:1, PC 42:2, PC 42:4, PC O-42:1, PC O-42:3 and PC O-42:4) and lower levels of metabolites associated with higher T2D risk (isoleucine, leucine, valine, tyrosine, α-aminoadipate, PC O-30:0 and PC O-38:3) (Fig. 4A). An opposite pattern was observed for unhealthful PDI, where individuals with a higher intake of less healthy plant foods had lower levels of metabolites associated with lower T2D risk (PC 38:0, PC 42:0, PC 42:1, PC 42:2, PC 42:4, PC O-38:0, PC O-40:1, PC O-42:2 and PC O-42:3) and higher levels of metabolites associated with higher T2D risk (alanine, glutamate, methionine, tyrosine and PC 38:3) (Fig. 4B). Although several associations between metabolite levels and healthful PDI and unhealthful PDI did not reach significance, the direction of most associations agreed with the literature for the risk of T2D. The mediation effects of metabolites

Mediation analysis revealed that six metabolites mediated the association between PDI scores and blood biochemistry markers (Fig. 5; Supplementary Table S4). Kynurenine mediated the associations of unhealthful PDI with both HDL-C and triacylglycerol, but in opposite directions; this means, unhealthful PDI was associated with a higher kynurenine which was associated with a lower HDL-C and higher triacylglycerol. Tyrosine mediated the positive association of healthful PDI and the inverse association of unhealthful PDI with HDL-C, while isoleucine mediated the inverse association of healthful PDI and the positive association of unhealthful PDI with LDL-C. The inverse association between healthful PDI and LDL-C was also mediated by α-aminoadipate. Discussion

Adherence to plant-based dietary patterns, determined by plant-based dietary indexes, was associated with metabolic parameters and the endogenous metabolomic profile of healthy individuals. The quality of the plant-based diet was a determinant factor of these associations, with healthful PDI being associated with a favourable metabolic profile and unhealthful PDI being associated with a less favourable metabolic profile. The associations between these scores and levels of metabolites were in directions consistent with previous associations between the metabolites and risk of T2D. In addition, we identified non-lipid metabolites that mediated the association of plant-based diets with clinical biochemistry markers considered modifiable disease risk factors for cardiometabolic diseases.

Our results align with previous research indicating that the quality of plant-based diets is a crucial determinant of their health outcomes8,13,17. Specifically, we observed that individuals adhering to a diet rich in healthful plant foods demonstrated a more favourable metabolic profile characterised by lower BMI, lower fasting glucose and higher HDL-C. Conversely, individuals with higher consumption of unhealthful plant foods presented adverse metabolic outcomes, including higher BMI, fasting glucose and triacylglycerol, and lower HDL-C. Diets based on whole and minimally processed plant foods are believed to exert beneficial effects on glycaemic control and insulin resistance through a variety of mechanisms which include mainly a higher intake of fibre and antioxidants that can modulate postprandial glucose response, satiety and gut microbial profile33. Concomitantly, healthful plant-based diets tend to be low in saturated fat and high in unsaturated fats which provenly modulate the lipid profile33,34. This modulation was demonstrated to go beyond the overall lipid composition to affect the size of lipid particles, with individuals who adhere to unhealthful plant-based diets presenting a pro-atherogenic lipoprotein profile with lower concentrations of HDL-C and higher concentrations of LDL-C, small LDL-C, non-HDL particles and medium and large VLDL17. Surprisingly, although overall PDI was associated with a lower BMI, we did not observe associations with clinical biochemistry markers. Overall PDI was associated with a higher intake of fibre and a lower intake of saturated fats; however, there were also less beneficial associations such as with a higher intake of energy and carbohydrates while there was no association with the intake of unsaturated fats, which may help explain the absence of more positive outcomes.

We identified several endogenous metabolites associated with the quality of plant-based diets, shedding light on potential mechanisms linking dietary intake to cardiometabolic risk factors. Individuals adhering to healthful plant-based diets exhibited, amongst others, lower levels of branched-chain amino acids, tyrosine, proline and α-aminoadipate, which have been linked to cardiometabolic risk factors and outcomes32,35,36. Serotonin was the only amino acid associated with higher healthful PDI and has been previously associated with higher intake of whole grains37. Furthermore, healthful PDI was associated with higher levels of C14:1 and C18:1. In the EPIC cohort, levels of fatty acylcarnitines were influenced by intrinsic and lifestyle factors in different directions highlighting the complexity of the topic38. However, in a cohort of 1936 Italian adults, the levels of C14:1 and C18:1 were associated with lower risk of hypertension and T2D, respectively39. On the contrary, individuals with higher adherence to unhealthful plant-based diets displayed more numerous and stronger associations, with higher levels of amino acids and biogenic amines being associated with cardiometabolic risk factors and outcomes. Only a few studies have associated directly PDI scores with levels of metabolites20,40. Yet, unhealthful PDI was similarly associated with higher levels of proline and glutamate20. Glycerophosphocholines emerged as major contributors to the associations between plant-based diet indexes and metabolites. Notably, a pattern of association considering their carbon chain length was observed where glycerophosphocholines containing 40 to 44 carbons were associated with higher healthful PDI. Analysis of the composition of these lipids revealed that they are composed mostly of species containing ≥ 22 carbons, i.e., very long-chain fatty acids (VLCFAs)41. Plant foods such as vegetable oils, green leafy vegetables and nuts contain marked amounts of α-linolenic acid42, considered a precursor of VLCFAs such as EPA and DPA, which are essential for growth, development and maintenance of health43. Associations of glycerophosphocholines containing VLCFAs were negative with unhealthful PDI and overall PDI indicating that individuals with these dietary patterns might not get the health benefits of the VLCFAs without the intake of healthy plant foods. In addition, unhealthful PDI was associated with higher levels of shorter chain diacylglycerophosphocholines. Individually, the significantly increased lipids were previously associated with higher levels and changes in triacylglycerol and glucose44,45 and with increased risk of T2D and total mortality32,34,46. Sphingomyelin levels were low across all PDI scores, which reflects the characteristic low intake of animal-derived foods of these dietary patterns47.

The associations between PDI scores and metabolite levels were further explored in the context of T2D risk. The distinctive patterns of metabolites associated with both PDI scores (present data) and T2D risk32 support the quality of plant-based diets in disease prevention. Several of these metabolites such as glutamine, isoleucine, methionine, proline, tyrosine and PC 38:3 have been used to compose metabolite profiles related to plant-based diets which were able to predict incident T2D20. Isoleucine, for example, explained 10.6% of the association between a healthful PDI metabolite profile and T2D incidence20. Further work is now needed to demonstrate in intervention studies that increased consumption of healthful plant-based diets modulates these metabolites that are associated with disease risk.

Importantly, our study extends beyond associations by exploring the potential mediation effects of non-lipid metabolites on the relationship between plant-based diets and clinical biochemistry markers. For example, tyrosine and isoleucine mediated the associations of healthful PDI and unhealhtful PDI with HDL-C and LDL-C levels, respectively. Isoleucine, classified as a branched-chain amino acid, has been linked with adverse metabolic outcomes in observational studies32. Additionally, kynurenine emerged as a mediator linking unhealthful plant-based diets with HDL-C and triacylglycerol, suggesting a potential role of tryptophan metabolism in mediating the cardiometabolic effects of dietary patterns. Indeed, a kynurenine risk score, composed of 5 metabolites in the tryptophan-kynurenine pathway, and a 1-year increase in tryptophan levels were both associated with an elevated incidence of the composite CVD48. Remarkably, the harmful effect of an unfavourable kynurenine risk score was partially mitigated by consuming a Mediterranean diet high in plant foods such as olive oil, nuts, fruits, vegetables and cereals. Finally, glutamate and proline mediated the associations between PDI scores and glucose concentrations. Proline can be produced from excess glutamate and these two amino acids are implicated in glucose homeostasis through regulation of insulin secretion49,50. However, it should be noted that associations observed between PDI scores and metabolites and their effects as mediators generally presented small magnitudes. Clinical biochemistry markers are influenced by numerous factors such as genetic predisposition and lifestyle choices, where dietary patterns constitute only one facet. In addition, diet patterns may affect these markers through diverse mechanisms and pathways. Therefore, the mediation effect of individual metabolites may be naturally smaller in magnitude compared to the direct effect of a complete dietary pattern. Taken together, these findings shed light on different mechanisms through which plant-based diets may impact metabolic health.

The scope of this study is limited by the metabolomic data available as a wider selection of metabolites could provide further insights into the associations between plant-based dietary patterns and metabolic health. Furthermore, analyses were carried out in one population group and more diverse populations should be analysed in the future to test the robustness of the results. Strengths of the study include detailed dietary data available from a study specifically designed to assess dietary intake. In addition, the study includes only healthy individuals not taking any medication or supplementation, which enables a focused examination of the associations of plant-based dietary patterns with the metabolic profile and reduces the influence of other confounding factors.

In conclusion, our study highlights the interplay between plant-based dietary patterns, metabolites and metabolic health parameters. The findings underscore the importance of considering the quality of plant-based diets in assessing their health effects and promoting metabolic health. By identifying endogenous metabolites associated with plant-based diets and their roles as potential mediators in the association with clinical biochemistry markers, we provide insights into some mechanisms through which plant-based diets may act to prevent cardiometabolic diseases. These results contribute to the growing body of evidence supporting the role of plant-based diets as a means of improving overall health and reducing the burden of chronic diseases.
