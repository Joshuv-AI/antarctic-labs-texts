Epitope-spanning antigenic variation reprograms immunodominance and broadens immunity in sequential influenza vaccination
Authors: Xiu-Feng Wan, Minhui Guan, Pradeep Balamalaliyage, Hanqiao Chen, Kritika Prasai, Ana Alcala, John Driver, Alicia K. Olivier, Weihong Gu, Christina Frymire, De Darling Melany Carvalho Madrid, Cheng Gao, Chengcheng Wang, Tao Li, Wikanda Tunterak, Qiongying Yang, Ashwin Ramesh, Muzaffar Ali, David Smith, Lei Li, Andrea J. Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Immune imprinting, in which prior antigenic exposures biases recall toward dominant epitopes, constrains the breadth and durability of influenza vaccine protection. Here we show that targeted variation across multiple hemagglutinin (HA) head sites (A, B, and D) between sequential A(H3N2) vaccines reprograms epitope hierarchy—redirecting recall toward conserved, subdominant head and stem epitopes. In a controlled ferret model mimicking imprinting-like recall in humans, antigenically distant priming accelerates neutralizing antibody induction, broadens reactivity, enhances cross-protection, and reduces viral shedding after drifted virus challenge. Epitope mapping and structural analysis confirms redirection toward conserved epitopes; single-cell transcriptomics and ELISpot assays reveal amplified germinal center B cell and Th1 responses. This “epitope hierarchy reshaping” links targeted antigenic variation to enhanced B cell competition, amplified T cell help, and improved viral control. This principle is likely applicable to vaccines against other rapidly evolving viruses where strong imprinting effects similarly limit immune breadth.

Introduction

Protective immunity to influenza is strongly influenced by immune imprinting—the tendency of the adaptive immune system to recall responses against antigens encountered early in life, even when they no longer match circulating viruses1,2. This phenomenon, often termed original antigenic sin3, arises because the immune system preferentially recalls memory B cells targeting epitopes that were most accessible and immunodominant during the first exposure, skewing antibody production toward highly variable epitopes on the hemagglutinin (HA) head, limiting the breadth, potency, and durability of vaccine-induced protection. Human studies reinforce this imprinting effect: preexisting immunity shaped cross-reactive responses to the 2009 pandemic H1N1 virus4 and to H5N1 viruses5, underscoring that early-life exposures durably constrain antibody specificity and breadth. As a consequence, seasonal vaccines frequently underperform when drifted strains emerge6, and repeated vaccination with antigenically similar formulations can further entrench these narrow specificities7–9.

The standard practice of updating vaccines with strains only modestly drifted from their predecessors often reinforces existing immunodominance hierarchies—amplifying strain-specific antibodies while failing to expose conserved targets. To overcome this imprinting trap, multiple strategies have been explored in influenza, including a range of “universal” vaccine concept10. One major approach focuses on eliciting antibodies to the conserved HA stem11–13, which is structurally recessed and immunologically subdominant, making them difficult to engage at protective titers14. Other strategies exploit antigenic diversity to broaden reactivity, including sequential heterologous immunizations that expand cross-reactive antibody pools and recruit antibodies targeting conserved regions within both HA head and stalk15, chimeric and mosaic HA constructs that redirect responses toward the conserved HA stalk16,17, computationally optimized broadly reactive antigens18, and multivalent mRNA vaccine cocktails encoding diverse HA groups19. Although these approaches demonstrate that antigenic diversity can enhance breadth, the mechanistic basis by which defined patterns of antigenic variation reprogram immunodominance within the native HA trimer remains incompletely understood. This gap motivates a central question: How can vaccine antigens be configured so that the immune system re-prioritizes conserved epitopes without sacrificing potency against contemporary viruses?

We reasoned that the answer might lie not in removing variable head epitopes (e.g., headless HA) but in strategically varying them. Building on theoretical work suggesting that pre-existing antibodies can modulate epitope accessibility20, we hypothesized that introducing epitope-spanning antigenic differences across the major head sites (A, B and D) between prime and boost could disrupt memory-biased recall, allowing naïve or low-frequency memory B-cells to engage conserved subdominant regions within the HA head. We term this approach “epitope hierarchy reshaping,” reflecting the intentional re-ordering of immunodominance patterns to broaden coverage while preserving potency.

Here, we test whether epitope-spanning antigenic variation across dominant hemagglutinin head sites between sequential influenza vaccines can reshape immunodominance rather than reinforce imprinting-biased recall. Using a controlled ferret model, we show that antigenically distant priming followed by boosting with an intermediate strain redirects antibody responses toward conserved subdominant epitopes, enhances cross-protective immunity, and limits viral replication upon heterologous challenge. These findings establish epitope hierarchy reshaping as a mechanism by which targeted antigenic variation reprograms immune recall without sacrificing potency. This principle provides a general framework for improving vaccines against antigenically evolving viruses where immune imprinting constrains protective breadth.

Results

Epitope-spanning prime–boost broadens antibody specificity and improves protection

To test whether coordinated antigenic variation across multiple HA head sites (which we term “epitope-spanning variation”) broadens humoral immunity, we used a controlled ferret model, a well-validated model that mirrors key clinical and immunological features of human influenza infection21. We primed ferrets with either A/Panama/2007/1999 (H3N2) (abbreviated as PAN/99) or A/Perth/16/2009 (H3N2) (PER/09), boosted with A/Texas/50/2012 (H3N2) (TX/12), and challenged with A/Switzerland/9715293/2013 (H3N2) (SWZ/13) (Fig. 1a). These four strains represent progressive antigenic drift across the five major HA antibody binding sites22, with a 14.55-unit antigenic distance between PAN/99 and TX/12 and 2.86 units between PER/09 and TX/12 (each unit = a 2 log₂ HI difference; Supplementary Information Supplementary Fig. S1 and Supplementary Data S1). Following challenge, hemagglutination inhibition (HAI) and microneutralization (MN) titers against TX/12 and SWZ/13 rose more rapidly and reached higher levels in PAN/99-primed (gPAN/99) compared to PER/09-primed (gPER/09) animals (Fig. 1c and Supplementary Data S2 and S3). gPAN/99 serum bound SWZ/13 HA with higher avidity (Fig. 1d and Supplementary Fig. S2). Neither priming alone nor a single vaccination with TX/12 elicited high titers, indicating that both sequence divergence and sequential exposure were required.

The gPAN/99 animals shed virus for only one day, compared to two days in gPER/09 and single-dose, and four days in unvaccinated mock controls (Fig. 1e). Viral loads at early timepoints were similar in gPER/09 and single-dose groups. No infectious virus was detected in respiratory tissues by 3 dpi. Nasal turbinate immunohistochemistry confirmed the lowest viral antigen concentrations in gPAN/99 and the highest in unvaccinated mock controls (Fig. 1f and Supplementary Fig. S4). Notably, all virus-challenged ferrets shed substantial viral RNA in nasal swabs even at 5 dpi, despite the absence of detectable infectious virus at this point (Supplementary Fig. S3).

To define epitope features linked to enhanced protection, we tested our system using two additional priming strains—A/Wisconsin/67/2005 (H3N2) (WIS/05) and A/Brisbane/10/2007 (H3N2) (BNE/07)—that predate PER/09. WIS/05 resembled PAN/99 in spanning multiple head sites, whereas BNE/07 resembled PER/09 with more restricted divergence (Fig. 1b and Supplementary Data S1). Antigenic distances from TX/12 were 7.19 units (WIS/05) and 5.56 units (BNE/07) (Supplementary Fig. S1). WIS/05 substitutions occurred mainly in sites A and B, whereas BNE/07 changes were concentrated in site B.

WIS/05 priming recapitulated the broader antibody response and reduced viral replication seen with PAN/99, whereas BNE/07 resembled PER/09 (Supplementary Figs. S2–S3 and Supplementary Data S2–S4). Despite similar two-day shedding, viral titers at 2 dpi were significantly lower in WIS/05 than in BNE/07 or single-dose groups; no differences were observed between BNE/07 and single-dose animals.

Together, these results show that priming with strains differing from the boost at multiple major head sites—particularly A, B, and D—expands antibody breadth and enhances protection, establishing that variation across multiple head sites is a key driver and measurable correlate of reprogrammed antibody specificity.

Epitope-spanning variation re-orders immunodominance toward conserved HA head and stem epitopes

We asked whether widening the antigenic variation between prime and boost shifted antibody focus away from variable immunodominant sites toward conserved, subdominant epitopes in both the HA head and stem. To track epitope dynamics, we used a 2491-peptide tiling microarray with a one-amino-acid offset that spans the HA sequences of the prime (PAN/99 or PER/09), boost (TX/12) and challenge (SWZ/13) strains (Fig. 2a). A single vaccination with TX/12 induced antibodies targeting a broad range of epitopes across both the HA head and stem (Fig. 2b and Supplementary Data S5). The HA head responses were primarily at the predominant epitopes across sites A-E23. These included conserved epitopes shared across H3N2 strains, such as residues 85–100 (H3 numbering) (Site E), 245–255 (Site D), and 280–300 (Site C), as well as variable, strain-specific regions like residues 130–140 and 145–150 (Site A). Antibodies targeting conserved stem epitopes at residues 385–400, 435–450, and 475–490, which are typically subdominant despite being broadly conserved across H3N2 viruses, were also observed. Priming with PAN/99 or PER/09 alone, without boosting, elicited similar epitope profiles, with broad antibody targeting focused on immunodominant regions in the HA head and three conserved epitopes in the HA stem (Fig. 2b and Supplementary Data S5).

After TX/12 boosting, the divergence between gPAN/99 and gPER/09 became clear. In gPAN/99, enhanced responses were observed primarily at conserved regions in both the HA head (residues 25–40 and 280–295 [Site C]) and stem (residues 385–400 and 435–450), with the most pronounced signal at residues 280–295 (Fig. 2c and Supplementary Fig. S5). These conserved-site responses were broad within each region and showed higher overall signal intensity compared to sera after priming but before boosting. In contrast, gPER/09 animals exhibited amplified responses predominantly targeting epitopes initially stimulated during priming (Fig. 2c and Supplementary Fig. S5). These epitopes were broadly distributed across the HA, including major antibody binding sites within the HA head that display sequence variations in the heterologous challenge strain. This “reinforcement” pattern reflects memory-biased recall when inter-vaccine divergence is limited. Although gPER/09 exhibited a greater number of discrete peaks, these were typically narrow, indicating antibodies that target only a few or even single residues within each region (Fig. 2c and Supplementary Fig. S5).

Thus, introducing multi-head-site antigenic divergence between prime and boost reconfigures immunodominance, suppresses recall to variable sites, promotes conserved head and stem epitopes, and broadens functional protection.

Structural mapping confirms refocus toward conserved HA head and stem epitopes

To determine the molecular basis by which epitope-spanning antigenic variation reconfigures antibody targeting, we performed electron microscopy-based polyclonal epitope mapping (EMPEM) to identify epitopes after challenge. IgG was purified from ferret sera in the gPAN/99 and gPER/09 groups, digested to Fab, and incubated with recombinant SWZ/13 HA (residues 1–469) to form HA–Fab complexes, which were then imaged by negative-staining EM combined with 2D classification and 3D reconstruction (Supplementary Figs. S6, S7). We selected ferret T1218 from gPAN/99 (Fig. 2c, left) and ferrets T1220 (Fig. 2c, right) from gPER/09 for EMPEM because their serum have the highest serological titers against the challenge strain SWZ/13.

In the gPAN/99 group (ferret T1218: prime PAN/99, boost TX/12, challenge SWZ/13, sera collected at 3 dpi), 2D class averages revealed three major structural classes of SWZ/13 HA–Fab complexes (Fig. 3a). All three classes of Fab complexes displayed a trimeric HA molecule bound to a single Fab (Fig. 3b), with binding sites closely matching to residues 20–35 (Cluster I), 280–295 (Cluster II, antibody-binding site C), and 385–395 (Cluster III, HA stem) (Fig. 3c–e and Supplementary Fig. S8), all of which are conserved among the priming, booster, and challenge strains. Importantly, the 3D reconstruction maps of the three HA-Fab complexes match well with their respective molecular models, with a correlation coefficient of at least 0.87 (Supplementary Data S6). Among the 26,630 particles analyzed, Cluster II was predominant (71.37%), followed by Cluster III (22.22%) and Cluster I (6.40%). To further assess potential intra-group variability, we analyzed HA–Fab complexes from an additional gPER/09 ferret (T1082, prime PER/09, boost TX/12, challenge SWZ/13), whose serum collected at 3 dpi exhibited lower HAI but comparable MN titers against the challenge strain. EM analysis of 3734 HA–Fab particles revealed only a single major class, with Fab binding exclusively to the HA stem (residues 385–400) (Supplementary Fig. S9), underscoring heterogeneity in epitope dominance across individuals. Observation of only a single HA-Fab class may also be partially due to the relative low antibody titer of this ferret serum sample (Supplementary Data S2, 3).

Together, these structural data show that antigenically distant priming reduces recall against variable immunodominant sites and redirects immunity toward conserved HA head and stem regions. Conversely, antigenically similar priming reinforces strain-matched epitopes, sustaining recall bias. These results indicate that leveraging epitope-spanning antigenic variation can be used as a strategy to mitigate recall biases shaped by prior exposures and expanding access to conserved, protective epitopes beyond the HA stem.

Antigenic variation reprograms B- and T-cell coordination to enhance cross-reactive immunity

Since the magnitude and quality of Ab responses are shaped by complex B/T-cell interactions, we profiled the transcriptional landscape and antigen-specific responses across key immune compartments. Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) of spleen cells revealed that ferrets primed with the antigenically distant PAN/99 strain (gPAN/99) had increased frequencies of germinal center B-cells, plasma cells, and multiple T-cell subsets, including Th1, Th17, Tregs, and CD8+ effector T-cells, compared to those primed with the closely related PER/09 (gPER/09). In contrast, gPER/09 animals had higher concentrations of naïve and follicular B-cells, suggesting less activation and differentiation (Fig. 5a, b). Differential gene expression (DEG) analysis revealed elevated expression of cytokines (e.g., IL6, IL4, CCL5, IL1B, TNFSF11) in B- and T-cells from gPAN/99, but not from gPER/09, relative to Mock (Fig. 5c, d), suggesting a more robust proinflammatory and B-cell-supportive environment. An Ingenuity Pathway Analysis of these DEG revealed that immune subsets in gPAN/99 animals exhibited stronger activation of adaptive immune programs compared to gPER/09 (Supplementary Fig. S10; Supplementary Information Results). CD4⁺ Th1 cells showed enrichment of IL-17 signaling and PI3K-related pathways, supporting enhanced effector differentiation and B/T-cell coordination (Supplementary Fig. S10b). In the humoral compartment, follicular, germinal center, and marginal zone B-cells in gPAN/99 upregulated PI3K, IL-17, and IL-8 signaling—key pathways for B–T-cell crosstalk, germinal center formation, and antibody maturation (Supplementary Fig. S10a). After challenge, mock animals without vaccination and those given a single vaccination exhibited distinct diversification at these sites: HA:219S emerged as the dominant variant with low entropy by 4–5 dpi, while HA:246 diversified with high entropy to include D, T, and H substitutions (Fig. 7 and Supplementary Fig. S13).

Notably, ferrets primed with the antigenically distant PAN/99 (gPAN/99) displayed patterns similar to unvaccinated and single-vaccination animals, whereas those primed with the more closely related PER/09 (gPER/09) exhibited distinct site-specific outcomes (Fig. 7 and Supplementary Fig. S13). At HA:219, gPAN/99 viruses converged on HA:219S by 4–5 dpi, while gPER/09 viruses retained a heterogeneous mixture of S, F, and Y. In contrast, at HA:246, gPAN/99 viruses diversified to N, T, and H, whereas gPER/09 viruses preserved the baseline N.

Comparable trends were observed in additional groups primed with either antigenically distant (WIS/05) or closely related (BNE/07) strains. The WIS/05 group resembled gPAN/99 in evolutionary patterns, while BNE/07 mirrored gPER/09 (Supplementary Fig. S13).

Collectively, priming with antigenically distant strains reshapes the immune landscape, imposing distinct within-host selective pressures and generating diversification trajectories that resemble those seen after single or no vaccination. Notably, this diversification was largely restricted to the later stages of infection (4–5 dpi), consistent with limited immune-driven variation during acute replication.

Discussion

Our study demonstrates that strategically spacing epitope-spanning antigenic variation between sequential influenza vaccines can reconfigure immunodominance by redirecting antibodies toward conserved HA head and stem epitopes, thereby providing a mechanistic framework for mitigating recall biases from prior antigenic exposure. Repeated seasonal influenza vaccination remains essential for protecting high-risk populations, and an optimal influenza vaccination strategy should elicit broad protection against emerging variants24. However, vaccine effectiveness can decline when vaccine antigens are closely matched to those from prior years25–27. In such contexts, immune recall often reinforces narrowly focused, strain-specific antibody responses1–3, limiting breadth and durability against drifted viruses. Pre-existing immunity can bias CD4⁺ T-cell help9,28, suppress recall germinal-center responses29, and limit the maturation of broadly reactive antibodies. Although epidemiological and serological studies have linked exposure history to reduced antibody breadth7–9, the mechanisms by which successive antigenic encounters reshape epitope targeting and modulate B- and T-cell responses have remained unclear. Here, in a controlled priming–boosting model that recapitulates key features of human recall21, we show that well-spaced antigenic divergence across major HA head sites can disrupt memory dominance, expand germinal-center output, and broaden functional protection.

Previous strategies to mitigate recall biases have largely targeted single epitopes. In influenza, these include stem-only immunogens30,31, hyperglycosylated HA head variants32, and heterosubtypic prime–boost regimens33–35, which can bias responses toward conserved regions but often compromise neutralization of circulating strains and do not address coordinated multi-site reprogramming within the native HA trimer. Comparable single-epitope designs have been explored extensively for HIV-1, where sequentially engineered Env trimers are used to recruit and mature a single broadly neutralizing antibody lineage by masking or altering competing sites36–40. These HIV studies showed that sequential antigenic variation can redirect antibody maturation, but under fundamentally different constraints: HIV vaccine design typically shepherd one or a few lineages toward a fixed epitope, whereas influenza requires simultaneous reordering of multiple lineages across a drifting, polyclonal landscape.

By embedding targeted multi-site variation within the native HA trimer and testing it through whole-virus immunization and challenge, we directly link multi-epitope hierarchy reshaping to coordinated B- and T-cell responses and viral control. Ferrets primed with antigenically distant strains (e.g., PAN/99) and boosted with TX/12 mounted robust responses to conserved HA head epitopes, particularly site C (residue 280–295), resulting in higher-avidity antibodies, broader protection, and reduced viral shedding (Fig. 1). These results highlight conserved head epitopes as accessible, underutilized targets for cross-protective immunity —complementing, but not replacing, stem-focused strategies11–13.

Mechanistically, substitutions across the major immunodominant head sites (A, B, and D) between prime and boost strains disrupt recall of dominant memory B-cell clones, thereby enabling the expansion of naïve or subdominant clones. In contrast, when these head sites remain relatively conserved, memory dominance persists and narrows antibody specificity. Our data define a broader framework—epitope hierarchy reshaping—in which antigenic variation actively reorders B-cell competition, rather than relying solely on passive steric masking, to redirect the immune response. This model reconciles prior concepts such as epitope masking41–43 and antigenic seniority44, emphasizing that sufficient divergence—not merely the presence of immune memory—is both necessary and sufficient to override original immunodominance hierarchies.

Interestingly, animals with prior H3 exposure exhibited higher early viral titers after challenge than naïve controls, likely due to a transient imprinting-biased recall in which pre-existing, largely non-neutralizing antibodies may initially limit effective clearance of antigenically drifted variants before broader recall responses are established. Similar early kinetic patterns have been observed in heterologous influenza challenge models, where mismatched prime–boost vaccination exacerbated clinical disease and delayed viral control45, as well as in modeling studies showing that prior exposure history modulates recall kinetics and antibody boosting46.

At the cellular level, distantly primed animals showed enhanced germinal center output and greater representation of Th1, Th17, and effector CD8 + T-cell subsets (Fig. 5). These changes were accompanied by upregulation of cytokines and signaling pathways (e.g., IL6, PI3K47, BCR signaling48) that support high-affinity antibody production (Supplementary Fig. S10). In contrast, closely matched vaccination yielded higher proportions of naïve and follicular B-cells and fewer effector T-cells, consistent with limited germinal center activity and reduced antibody maturation49.

Spatially, recall responses were strongest in mediastinal lymph nodes (MedLNs), which drain the respiratory tract and act as the frontline for antigen encounter. These responses emerged earlier and reached higher magnitudes than those in the spleen or PBMCs, indicating rapid mobilization of local memory upon heterologous challenge (Fig. 6). In contrast, responses in popliteal lymph nodes—draining the vaccine site50—remained low, highlighting that anatomical proximity to infection, rather than immunization, determines the magnitude of the recall response. These local responses underscore how tissue-proximal recall orchestrates systemic protection.

Despite consistent group-level trends, we observed notable inter-individual variation in epitope targeting (Figs. S5–S7). For instance, one gPER/09 animal mounted a stem-biased response, deviating from its cohort (Supplementary Fig. S9). Such heterogeneity may reflect stochastic factors in B-cell repertoire formation or selection, as previously observed in both animal and human studies51,52. Ferrets, like humans, are genetically outbred, and this diversity likely contributes to the range of immune outcomes observed in our study. Nevertheless, directional shifts in antibody specificity and transcriptional programs were reproducible across animals, supporting the conclusion that antigenic variation reconfigures recall at the population level.

Reshaping antibody responses had downstream effects on viral evolution. While population immunity drives antigenic drift43,53–55, the extent to which vaccines shape within-host diversity remains debated55–57. We found that priming with antigenically similar strains focused immune pressure on shared HA head sites, selecting polymorphisms at positions HA:219 and HA:246. In contrast, distant priming yielded more diverse viral populations resembling those in unvaccinated or single vaccination animals. Egg-adaptive mutations have been reported at multiple HA positions; to ensure that observed immune effects reflected true antigenic selection rather than culture artifacts, all challenge viruses were propagated in MDCK-SIAT1 cells58. Overall, these findings suggest that recall biases from prior exposures not only shape immune memory but also modulate the intrahost selective landscape—highlighting how vaccine history may influence early steps in antigenic drift.

In addition to reduced infectious viral shedding, we detected persistent viral RNA in nasal swabs in the absence of recoverable infectious virus (Supplementary Fig. S3). This pattern is consistent with rapid control of productive replication by the reshaped immune response, while residual RNA or non-infectious particles persisted. Such a dissociation between RNA detection and infectious virus may have implications for interpreting PCR-based surveillance and for understanding how vaccine-driven immunity influences transmission potential.

While our ferret model recapitulates key aspects of influenza infection and immunity21, several limitations merit consideration. As is common for controlled ferret influenza studies, all experimental groups were conducted with matched cohort sizes (n = 8); however, the absolute number of animals constrained by practical and ethical considerations may limit the ability to detect the full extent of inter-individual variability that could emerge in larger populations. Unlike humans, ferrets are immunologically naïve and lack layered exposure histories; thus, our model represents a controlled imprinting scenario rather than the complexity of lifelong human exposures. We used whole-virus inactivated vaccines without adjuvants, which differ from the split, egg-based formulations typically used in humans and may elicit distinct innate and adaptive responses. Adjuvants, though not tested, could further modulate germinal center activity and broaden epitope targeting. In addition, our mapping methods prioritized linear epitopes; although EM confirmed major footprints, conformational sites may have been overlooked due to limited resolution or titer constraints. Finally, this study focused solely on HA; future work should examine the contribution of neuraminidase and other viral proteins to recall biases and cross-protection.

Although our model provides mechanistic insight under controlled priming conditions, implementation in humans will require navigating complex, layered exposure histories. Our data do not imply exclusive recall of conserved epitopes; rather, they show that sufficient multi–head-site divergence can shift the balance of germinal-center competition toward conserved head and stem clones, even as hypervariable-site memory is also recalled. This represents a conceptual design principle rather than a prescriptive regimen: sequential antigens engineered to vary across major HA head sites may rebalance recall and expand breadth. Such strategies could be explored in next-generation platforms such as recombinant or mRNA-based HA immunogens, where head-site configurations can be rationally designed. Future work will be needed to determine how effectively multi-site variation modulates recall in humans with diverse exposure histories.

In summary, this study establishes a mechanistic framework for modulating immunodominance through multi-epitope antigenic variation in sequential vaccination. By introducing targeted variation across multiple HA head sites between sequential vaccines, we reconfigured epitope hierarchies, broadened protective coverage, and linked these changes to coordinated B- and T-cell responses and control of viral replication. These findings suggest a design principle in which controlled, epitope-spanning antigenic divergence can be used to steer immune recall toward underutilized but protective epitopes without compromising potency against circulating strains. Such an approach could be integrated into seasonal influenza vaccine updates to preempt drift and extend breadth, and may inform next-generation immunogens for other antigenically dynamic pathogens, including SARS-CoV-2, dengue virus, and RSV.

Vaccines preparation

The vaccine seeds were propagated in 9 to 11-day-old specific pathogen-free embryonated chicken eggs (VALO BioMedia North America LLC, Adel, Iowa, USA) following the WHO manual with minor modifications59. Allantoic fluid was centrifuged at 3700 x g for 30 min, and the supernatants were combined and inactivated with 0.04% Formaldehyde solution (Sigma-Aldrich, Cat. 252549, St. Louis, MO, USA) under constant stirring (250 rpm) at 4 °C for 72 h. Inactivated viral suspension was passaged three times in MDCK-Siat1 cells, and cell culture supernatants were collected for HA assay to confirm the completion of viral inactivation. The inactivated viral suspension was purified through sucrose density gradient (30, 40, 50, and 60%) ultracentrifugation. The purified virus was stored at − 80 °C until use. Purified virus proteins were separated by SDS-PAGE, and the concentrations of HA proteins corresponding to the band size of 55 KD were estimated based on the densities of Coomassie Brilliant Blue staining using Bovine Serum Albumin (BSA) as the standard.

Animal experiments

A total of 56 ferrets (Mustela putorius furo; 4 to 6-month old; Triple F Farm, Gillett, PA) were screened for antibodies against the vaccine strains (PAN/99, WIS/05, BNE/07, PER/09 and TX/12), the challenge strain (SWZ/13), and three contemporary seasonal influenza viruses A/Indiana/02/2020 (H3N2), A/Maryland/02/2021 (H3N2), and B/Michigan/01/2021, and were confirmed to be seronegative. These ferrets were randomly divided into 7 groups with 8 animals (4 males and 4 females) per group. Ferrets were primed (referred to as VAX1) with one of four vaccine strains (PAN/99, WIS/05, BNE/07, or PER/09) or sterile PBS as a negative control. Twenty-eight days post-VAX1, they were boosted (referred to as VAX2) with either TX/12 or sterile PBS. All immunizations were administered intramuscularly in the rear legs (close to popliteal lymph nodes) with 15 μg of HA diluted in 500 μL of PBS (250 μL each leg). Twenty-eight days post-VAX2, all ferrets were challenged intranasally with SWZ/13 (referred to as Challenge) at a dose of 106 TCID50/mL/ferret (500 μL per nostril). The immunization groups were assigned as follows: (A) Group PAN/99 (gPAN/99): PAN/99 as VAX1, TX/12 as VAX2, challenged with SWZ/13; (B) Group WIS/05 (gWIS/05): WIS/05 as VAX1, TX/12 as VAX2, challenged with SWZ/13; (C) Group BNE/07 (gBNE/07): BNE/07 as VAX1, TX/12 as VAX2, challenged with SWZ/13; (D) Group PER/09 (gPER/09): PER/09 as VAX1, TX/12 as VAX2, challenged with SWZ/13; (E) Single VAX2 (gSingle): PBS as VAX1, TX/12 as VAX2, challenged with SWZ/13; (F) No vaccination (Mock): PBS as VAX1, PBS as VAX2, challenged with SWZ/13; (G) Negative control (Negative): PBS as VAX1, PBS as VAX2, not challenged. Blood was collected at the following time points: before VAX1 (pre-VAX1), 7 days post-VAX1, 28 days post-VAX1 (before VAX2), 7 days post-VAX2, 28 days post-VAX2 (before challenge) from the jugular vein, at 3 and 5 dpi via jugular vein or heart. Nasal washes were collected daily after the challenge for five days. All procedures, including immunization, bleeding, nasal washes and challenge were performed when ferrets were lightly anesthetized with isoflurane. On 3 and 5 dpi, four animals (two males and two females) from each group were euthanized. Nasal turbinate, soft palate, trachea (upper and lower), five lung sections (cranial right, middle right, caudal right, cranial left, and caudal left), lymph nodes (mediastinal and popliteal) and spleen were collected for various analyses.

Quantitative RT-PCR

Viral RNA was isolated from nasal washes or homogenized tissues using the MagMAX™ Pathogen RNA/DNA Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat. 4462359, Waltham, MA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Viral RNA copy numbers were quantified using the TaqMan Fast Virus 1-Step Master Mix (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat. 4444434, Waltham, MA, USA) and the primers targeting IAV matrix gene, along with the FAM-labeled probes and BHQ1 quencher. The sequences of IAV matrix gene-based primers were as follows: forward primer (5’-GACCRATCCTGTCACCTCTGAC-3’), reverse primer (5’-AGGGCATTYTGGACAAAKCGTCTA-3’), and TaqMan® Probe (5’-TGCAGTCCTCGCTCACTGGGCACG-3’) (Eurofins Genomics, Louisville, KY, USA). The cycling program was set as follows: one cycle at 50 °C for 5 min, one cycle at 95 °C for 20 s, followed by 40 cycles at 95 °C for 3 s and 60 °C for 30 s. The reactions were performed in a QuantStudio 6 Flex Real-time PCR System (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). A standard curve was established using the obtained average threshold cycle (Ct) values plotted against the concentrations of serially diluted pHW2000 plasmid containing the target IAV M segment. Viral RNA copies were determined via linear regression using Microsoft Excel. The detection limit was 3.65 log10 copies/g for tissues and 3.65 log10 copies/mL for swabs, respectively.

Next generation sequencing and analyses

Amplicon libraries were prepared using the Illumina DNA Prep kit, then sequenced with MiSeq Reagent Kit v3 (600-cycle) and MiSeq sequencing system (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA). Total reads ranged from 767 to 2280 for each sequence, with an average of 1641 reads (standard deviation (SD) = 527). Obtained paired-end reads were checked for quality, then mapped to a reference genome A/Switzerland/9715293/2013 (H3N2) (GenBank Accession No: OQ350035, OQ350030, OQ350036, OQ350034, OQ350033, OQ350031, OQ350029, OQ350032), and lastly assembled using the Iterative Refinement Meta-Assembler (IRMA) flu pipeline, with a quality score threshold of 20 ref. 60. The consensus sequence and sequence quality report were obtained, and BAM files were sorted for each gene segment. A Python script was developed to extract the combined consensus sequence for each gene segment, which was later aligned using the BioEdit v7.2.6.1 tool utilizing the ClustalW multi sequence alignment.

Genetic diversity

For the polymorphism analysis, the BAM-formatted sequence data were sorted using samtools and re-aligned to the reference sequence with bwa, based on a consensus sequence. The re-aligned BAM files were processed through the DiversiTools pipeline (http://josephhughes.github.io/DiversiTools/) to obtain detailed amino acid polymorphism data at each position. The top three most frequent amino acids (Top, Secondary, Tertiary) were identified for each position, along with their respective frequencies (TopAA%, SndAA%, TrdAA%).

The genomic diversity between the challenge virus and viruses isolated from nasal washes was accessed based on consensus variations and intra-host single-nucleotide variants (iSNVs). Consensus variation calling was based on nucleotide mutations in the consensus genomic sequences, while iSNV calling was conducted as described previously61,62. Sequencing reads were paired-end mapped to the reference genomes of SWZ/13 (GenBank accession numbers: OQ350035, OQ350030, OQ350036, OQ350034, OQ350033, OQ350031, OQ350029, OQ350032) using DiversiTools.

To minimize the potential false-positive results, those sites with a sequence read depth < 100 × were excluded from iSNV analyses. In addition, the following criteria were used to ensure high-quality iSNVs: (1) minor allele frequency > 5%; (2) depth of the minor allele > 5; and (3) strand bias ratio of the minor allele to major allele < 10-fold. To avoid errors from read mapping, serial adjacent iSNVs (distance < 50 bp) containing > 5 iSNVs were filtered out to avoid mapping errors.

Hemagglutination inhibition (HAI) assay

HAI assays were performed using 0.5% turkey erythrocytes (LAMPIRE Biological Laboratories, Cat. 7209403, Pipersville, PA, USA) as described in the Manual for the Laboratory Diagnosis and Virological Surveillance of Influenza by the World Health Organization59. HAI titers were calculated as the reciprocal of the last HAI-positive serum dilution. Samples with no HAI titer at 1:10 were considered negative.

Recombinant HA expression

The HA of PAN/99, WIS/05, BNE/07, PER/09, TX/12, and SWZ/13 with residues 1–469 (without removing the signal peptide sequence), were cloned into the pCAGGS plasmid (NR-52309). A “foldon” sequence64 was added at the C-terminus, followed by an additional His6 tag. Thus, the cloning sequence included HA (residues 1–469)-GS-foldon-GG-His6. The plasmids were codon-optimized and synthesized by Gene Universal (Newark, DE, USA). A total of 100 µg of plasmids were transfected into 100 mL of suspension ExpiCHO-S cells (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat. A29127). The transfection was performed using the ExpiFectamine CHO Transfection Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat. A29129) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The transfected cells were cultured in ExpiCHO Expression Medium (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat. A29100) at 37 °C with 8% CO2 in a shaking incubator at 125 rpm. The expressed proteins were purified using HisTrap HP columns (Cytiva, Cat. 17-5247-01, Marlborough, MA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The purified proteins were quantified by SDS-PAGE using Bovine Serum Albumin (BSA) as the standard. The identity and purity of expressed proteins were further confirmed using mass spectrometry at the MU Proteomics Center.

Anti-HA ELISA and avidity assays

Nunc MaxiSorp microtiter plates (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat. 439454, Waltham, MA, USA) were coated with 2 µg/mL of recombinant hemagglutinin (HA) in pH 7.4 phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) at 4 °C overnight. Plates were then blocked at room temperature for 1 h with blocking buffer (PBS containing 0.1% Tween 20 and 5% Blotting-Grade Blocker, Bio-Rad, Cat. 1706404, Hercules, California, USA). Ferret antisera were serially diluted in blocking buffer and incubated on the plates at room temperature for 1 h. For avidity assays, the plates were washed and overlaid with 100 µL/well of 4 M urea for 15 min, followed by thorough washing. Bound antibodies were detected using peroxidase-conjugated goat anti-ferret IgG (Novus Biologicals, Cat. NB7224, Centennial, CO, USA). Color development was achieved by adding Pierce™ 1-Step™ Ultra TMB-ELISA Substrate Solution (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat. 34029, Waltham, MA, USA) for 10 min, and the reaction was stopped by adding 50 µL of 1 N H2SO4 (Fisher Chemical, Cat. SA8184, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) diluted in Milli-Q water. Optical density at 450 nm (OD 450) was measured using a BioTek Cytation 5 Cell Imaging Multimode Reader (Agilent, Santa Clara, CA, USA). The avidity index was calculated based on the area under the entire antibody titration curve.

Immunohistochemistry staining and pathology evaluation

The respiratory tissues collected at 3 and 5 dpi were fixed in 10% neutral buffered formalin solution. The nasal turbinates were decalcified for 2-3 days with Kristenson’s decalcification solution followed by tap water rinse. All tissues were paraffined-embedded, sectioned at 5 µm and stained with hematoxylin and eosin. For immunohistochemistry, unstained nasal sections or lung sections were deparaffinized and then were retrieved with DIVA Decloaker (Biocare Medical, Pacheco, CA) in for 5 min at 110 oC in a Decloaking Chamber (Biocare Medical, Pacheco, CA). Slides were blocked with Background Punisher (Biocare Medical, Pacheco, CA) for 10 min and then stained with anti-NP monoclonal antibody at 1:500 (Cat. # NR-43899, BEI Resources, NIAID, NIH) followed by anti-mouse HRP polymer (Cat. # MC541H, Biocare Medical, Pacheco, CA). Slides were treated with the chromogen (Cat. # DB801L, DAB Chromogen, Biocare, Pacheco, CA) and then were counterstained with hematoxylin.

Immunostained nasal turbinate slides were scored for NP positivity in five low magnification fields65 (100 × magnification, FN = 22, field of view area = 3.8 mm2) as follows: 0 – no immunoreactive cells, 1 – less than 20 immunoreactive cells, 2 – between 20 – 50 immunoreactive cells, 3 – greater than 50 immunoreactive cells. Scores were averaged for each section. All slides were evaluated blindly by a boarded veterinary pathologist.

High-density tiling peptide microarray design, hybridization, and data analyses

A high-density PEPperCHIP® peptide microarray (PEPperPRINT GmbH, Heidelberg, Germany) was designed to identify residue-specific antibody binding to hemagglutinin (HA) and neuraminidase (NA)-derived peptides. HA sequences were fragmented into 15-mer peptides with a 14-amino acid overlap, ensuring comprehensive epitope coverage. Each peptide was flanked by GSGSGSG linkers at the N- and C- termini to minimize truncation artifacts. The microarray contained 2491 unique peptides, printed as single spots in duplicate subarrays (4982 total spots), framed by 18 hemagglutinin (HA, YPYDVPDYAG) control peptides for internal assay validation. The peptide library represented unique sequences across HA and NA proteins from A/Panama/2007/1999 (PAN/99), A/Wisconsin/67/2005 (WIS/05), A/Brisbane/59/2007 (BNE/07), A/Perth/16/2009 (PER/09), A/Texas/50/2012 (TX/12), and A/Switzerland/9715293/2013 (SWZ/13), as well as an H1N1 subtype virus.

Prior to the main assay, microarrays were pre-stained with goat anti-ferret IgG (H + L) DyLight680 (Cat#: ab112768; Abcam) to assess potential non-specific interactions with peptide sequences. For immune assays, microarrays were blocked with Rockland MB-070 blocking buffer for 30 min at room temperature before incubation with serum samples. Ferret sera were diluted 1:1000 in incubation buffer (PBS, 0.05% Tween 20, 10% blocking buffer) and incubated for 16 h at 4 °C under orbital shaking at 140 rpm. Following serum incubation, arrays were washed three times (10 sec each) with PBS-Tween 20 (0.05%). Secondary antibody staining was performed using goat anti-ferret IgG (H + L) DyLight680 (Cat#: ab112768; Abcam) (1:5000) for 45 min at room temperature, followed by additional washes. Arrays were subsequently stained with mouse monoclonal anti-HA (12CA5) DyLight800 (PEPperPRINT GmbH, Heidelberg, Germany) (0.2 µg/mL) for 45 min at room temperature, followed by a final washing step.

Microarrays were scanned using an Innopsys InnoScan 710-IR Microarray Scanner at a 20 µm resolution. Scanning was conducted at low laser power, with a gain of 50 for 680 nm (red) and 10 for 800 nm (green). Fluorescence intensities were quantified using PepSlide® Analyzer (PEPperPRINT GmbH, Heidelberg, Germany), and data were processed in Excel-based mapping and intensity analysis files. Spot intensities were extracted from 16-bit grayscale TIFF images, ensuring a high dynamic range. The software algorithm identified raw, foreground, and background signals and flagged artifacts based on predefined thresholds. A maximum spot-to-spot deviation of 40% was tolerated, beyond which the signal intensity was set to zero.

Epitope mapping was performed by correlating intensity maps with visual microarray scans. Interactions were color-coded, with red indicating high signal intensity and white indicating low intensity. Averaged spot intensities were plotted across the array from top-left to bottom-right to assess signal-to-noise ratios. When amino acid contributions to antibody binding were unclear, residues were labeled in gray for contextual interpretation.

Background interactions of the secondary antibody (DyLight680) with basic and aromatic peptide motifs were quantified and integrated into data corrections. Assay integrity was confirmed by robust HA control peptide staining (YPYDVPDYAG) and HA content peptides containing the DVPDYA epitope (DyLight800, green).

A total of 41 ferret sera were analyzed, categorized into four groups, including (1) the gPAN/99 group consisted of 8 post-VAX1 and 8 post-VAX2 sera, (2) the gPER/09 group included 8 post-VAX1 and 8 post-VAX2 sera, (3) the gSingle group contained 8 post-VAX sera, and (4) the Negative control group consisted of one pooled serum sample from 8 ferrets. These samples were included to characterize antibody responses to each epitope across HA and NA proteins and to compare their dynamics after the first and second vaccinations in gPAN/99 and gPER/09, as well as in those who received a single vaccination.

HA–Fab complex generation and purification

2 – 4 mg total IgG was purified from 0.5 ml ferret sera by using Protein A/G resin (Cat# PI20421, Thermo Scientific). Fab and Fc fragments were generated by papain digestion using immobilized papain (Cat# 44985, Thermo Scientific). Fc fragments were removed by Protein A resin (Cat# 44985, Thermo Scientific), producing ~ 600 μg purified Fab. Purified Fab was incubated with 10 μg recombinant HA (SWZ/13, residues 1–469) at 4 °C for 5 h to form HA–Fab complexes. Complexes were purified by size-exclusion chromatography (Superdex 200 Increase 10/30 GL column on an FPLC system), and elution profiles were monitored by absorbance at 280 nm. HA–Fab complexes eluted earlier than free Fab, corresponding to higher molecular weight species. Purified HA–Fab fractions were concentrated and subjected to transmission electron microscopy (TEM) for structural characterization.

Electron microscopy, data collection and image processing

To prepare negative stain grids, the HA–Fab complex was diluted to a final concentration of 30 μg/mL, and the sample was then applied to a Formvar/Carbon 400 mesh copper grids (Cat# 01754-F, Ted Pella) that had been glow-discharged with a PELCO easiGlow Glow Discharge Cleaning system (Ted Pella) for 30 s at 15 mA. After sample binding and washing, the grids were stained with 7.5% uranyl formate for 30 s, followed by blotting to remove the excess stain. Grids were imaged on a JEOL JEM-1400 Flash Transmission Electron Microscope equipped with a 15 megapixel AMT NanoSprint15 sCMOS camera. Over 1000 images were collected at 200 kV, 30,000x magnification, and 2.18 Å/pixel. Micrographs were processed by CryoSPARC (v4.3.0)66, and a final set of 500,000 to 1,500,000 particles was picked and subjected to several rounds of reference-free 2D classification. Particles corresponding to distinct classes of the HA-Fab complex were selected and used to generate a final 3D reconstruction in CryoSPARC.

Model building

The HA model (PDB: 6PDX, residues 1–469) and the Fab fragment structure (PDB: 8IB1) were used for docking. Reconstructions of each HA–Fab complex were analyzed using UCSF ChimeraX (v1.90). Fab fragments from each reconstruction were separated and superimposed to generate the final model.

Structural visualization

The HA protein was visualized in PyMOL using the template HA protein structure of A/Hong Kong/1/1968 (H3N2) (accession number: 4we4)67 from the Protein Data Bank (PDB, https://www.rcsb.org/).

Antigenic cartography

AntigenMap (https://sysbio.missouri.edu/software/AntigenMap/)68 was created by using HAI data published previously22. A titer of < 1:10 was used as the low reactor threshold, and a low rank (rank = 2) matrix completion was used to minimize noise from the HAI data. A single unit on the antigenic map represents a log2 unit in HAI titers.

Cell isolation

Six mL of peripheral blood samples were collected from the ferret’s cava vein in EDTA preloaded tubes (367899, BD) and were immediately inverted 8–10 times to avoid coagulation. The PBMCs separations were done within 3-4 h after the sample collection using Fycoll-Hypaque (Cat # 17144002, Cytvia) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Purified PBMCs were resuspended in 1 mL of freezing media (FBS, 10% DMSO) and were aliquoted for storage in liquid Nitrogen until use (Cat # 229925, Celltreat). Spleen and lymph nodes (mediastinal and popliteal) were collected at 3 and 5 dpi and were immediately preserved on ice in DMEM with 10% FBS (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat. A5256801, Waltham, MA, USA). The lymph nodes were trimmed of fatty tissue, minced into small pieces, and homogenized in chilled media using a glass homogenizer. Homogenates were passed through a 100 µm filter (Fisherbrand, 22-363-549) to remove clumped cells and flowthrough was centrifuged at 50 x g for 3 min. The cell pellets were resuspended in 2 mL of freezing media (FBS with 10% DMSO) and aliquoted for storage in liquid nitrogen for later use.

Flow cytometry

Ferret splenocytes were screened for immune cell profiles at 3 and 5 dpi using flow cytometry. Five million live cells were aliquoted into FACS tubes. The cells were washed with PBS, vortexed, and centrifuged at 400 x g for 3 min at 4 °C. The LIVE/DEAD™ Fixable Near-IR stain (Thermo Fisher, Cat. L34975) was used as a viability dye according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Afterwards, the cells were washed with PBS and centrifuged. The supernatant was discarded, and the cells were resuspended. Next, 10 μl of an extracellular antibody cocktail was added to the cells, which were incubated at 4 °C for 20 min. Immune cell populations were identified by staining with antibodies against the surface molecules CD4 (PE; Sino Biological, Cat. 60003-MM02-P), CD8α (Alexa Fluor 700; Thermo Fisher, Cat. 56-0086-42), and IgG/IgA/IgM (FITC; Sigma, Cat. SAB3700800-1MG). Afterwards, the cells were fixed and permeabilized with the BD Cytofix/Cytoperm kit from BD Biosciences according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Cells were then restained with antibodies against CD3 (Alexa Fluor Cat. 647; Santa Cruz, Cat. SC-20047 Cat. AF647) and CD79α (PerCP-Cy5.5; Thermo Fisher, Cat. 45-0792-42). After incubation, washing and resuspension, cells were acquired on a Cytek Aurora flow cytometer. Data were analyzed using FlowJo software, version 10.10.0.

Single-cell RNA sequencing

Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) was performed on spleen cells harvested from ferrets infected with gPAN/99 (n = 3), gPER/09 (n = 3), or a negative control (n = 3). Single-cell cDNA libraries were prepared using the 10\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\times$$\end{document}× Chromium Next GEM Single Cell 3′ reagent kit (v2) (10 x Genomics, Pleasanton, CA), following the manufacturer’s instructions. Sequencing was carried out on an S4 flow cell of the NovaSeq 6000 sequencer (Illumina, San Diego, CA) to generate paired-end reads. Sequence reads were aligned to the ferret reference dataset (MusPutFur1.0) and ferret mitochondrial genome (accession NC_020638.1), and barcode gene matrices were generated using Cell Ranger v8.0.0 (10 x Genomics). Clustering analyses were performed using the Seurat package (v4.4.0) in R69 Pre-analysis quality control was performed by removing genes expressed in < 3 cells and excluding cells with aberrantly high or low gene counts and high mitochondrial gene expression. Highly variable genes were used for downstream analysis. Batch correction of samples was performed using Seurat (v4.4.0). Afterward, a standard clustering workflow was carried out. Briefly, the linear dimension reduction of principal component analysis (PCA) analysis was performed in the integrated datasets, and then the most variable principal components were selected to construct a shared nearest neighbor graph, next, the Louvain algorithm was implemented to cluster cells, that were visualized using non-linear dimensional reduction Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP). Cell lineages were manually annotated based on known marker genes found in the literature, in combination with algorithmically defined differentially expressed genes (DEG) in each cluster. Because ferret immune subsets are not fully defined and validated, these annotations represent putative transcriptional states rather than functionally confirmed lineages.

Ingenuity pathway analysis (IPA)

DEG were identified based on an adjusted p-value < 0.05 and a log2 fold change > 1. The list of DEG, including Ensembl gene IDs, avg_log2FC, fold changes, p-values, and adjusted p-values, was uploaded to the Ingenuity Pathway Analysis (IPA) platform (QIAGEN Inc., https://digitalinsights.qiagen.com/products-overview/discovery-insights-portfolio/analysis-and-visualization/qiagen-ipa/). The Core Analysis function was used to identify canonical pathways significantly associated with the DEG. The analysis was configured to focus on mammalian systems (i.e., human, mouse, and rat), using the Ingenuity Knowledge Base as the reference set. Confidence levels were set to “experimentally observed,” and all available data sources were included.

Following core expression analyses, upstream regulator analysis was performed to predict transcription factors and signaling molecules driving the observed gene expression changes, with z-scores > 2 or < − 2 indicating activation or inhibition. Pathways and genes were considered significant if they had a -log(p-value) greater than 1.3 (p-value < 0.05) and a z-score greater than 2 (indicating pathway activation) or less than − 2 (indicating pathway inhibition). Significantly enriched canonical pathways, particularly those related to the immune system, were identified and visualized through IPA to assess their biological relevance to the study.

Statistical analyses

One-way ANOVA was performed using GraphPad software to compare the mean TCID50 values among groups at 2 dpi, applying the Geisser-Greenhouse correction for sphericity and Tukey’s correction for multiple comparisons. For avidity data, a two-tailed Student’s t test was performed to test the hypothesis that, at each time point (Post-VAX1, Post-VAX2, 3 dpi, 5 dpi), the mean avidity index [area under the curve (AUC)] against the HA proteins of the booster vaccine strain or the challenge strain was equal between the gPAN/99 and gPER/09 groups. For pathogenesis and IHC, ordinal data were analyzed using Mann–Whitney U tests for two-group comparisons or Kruskal–Wallis tests for the comparison of three groups, followed by Dunn’s post-hoc-tests for subsequent pairwise comparisons. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA), and statistical significance was accepted as p ≤ 0.05 for all analyses.
