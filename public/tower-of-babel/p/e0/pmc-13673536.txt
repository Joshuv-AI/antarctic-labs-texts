Reactive hydrogen in KH or KOH for catalytic NH3 synthesis
Authors: Peikun Wang, Wenbo Gao, Yanbo Deng, Fei Chang, Qianru Wang, Sheng Feng, Jianping Guo, Ping Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Potassium (K) has long been employed as a key catalyst promoter for ammonia synthesis. Its chemical state and promoting mechanism, however, remain elusive. Here, we study the influence of different anionic counterparts of K (i.e., H− and OH−) on its promoting capability and function in ammonia synthesis catalysis. When potassium is in the form of hydride (KH), it can enhance the ammonia formation rate of the Fe/carbon nanotube (CNT) catalyst by two orders of magnitude; while it is in the form of hydroxide (KOH), the rate increment is only ∼5 times. We show that both KH and KOH can promote N removal from Fe nitride under a hydrogen atmosphere, where an intermediate KNH2 species may form and further be hydrogenated to NH3. Isotopic measurements show that H atoms in KH and KOH are involved in the formation of NH3. For the KH-Fe catalyst, the hydridic H of KH is reductive and may undergo a redox reaction with adsorbed N (Nad) on Fe to form KNHx species and further be hydrogenated to NH3 and create a clean Fe surface for further nitrogen activation. For the KOH-Fe catalyst, the protonic H of KOH may protonate Nad on Fe to form NH3 through the intermediate species of K–Fe–O and KNH2. This work reveals the crucial role of hydrogen in ammonia synthesis catalysis and, moreover, could inspire revisiting of the role of alkali promoters in industrial ammonia synthesis catalysts.

Introduction

Alkalis are commonly indispensable components in heterogeneous catalysts.1,2,3,4 One typical example is the K-promoted iron catalyst that has been applied in the Haber-Bosch (H-B) ammonia synthesis process for more than a century. The strong promoting effect of K on Fe-based catalyst was first discovered by Mittasch and Frankenburg at the beginning of the 20th century.5 Three orders of magnitude of increase in specific activity were obtained upon introducing 1%–2% of K to Fe.6 Such a prominent effect of K as well as other alkali and alkaline earth metals stimulated considerable research interests.7,8,9,10,11,12 It has been suggested that the alkalis could donate electrons to Fe, and thus Fe has more electrons to feed back the antibonding orbitals of adsorbed dinitrogen species to facilitate the dissociation of N≡N bonds, which has been considered to be the rate-determining step (RDS) for ammonia synthesis.7,8,10 Other proposals, on the other hand, discuss that the alkalis may destabilize the surface NHx (x = 0, 1, 2, and 3) species in favor of NH3 desorption to free active sites for the catalysis.9,10 Recent theoretical calculations correlated the activity enhancement to promoter-induced reduction in spin moment of Co metals.13 Such controversial interpretations reflect the complexity of the interaction among the transition metal, alkali promoter, and reacting species. Although alkali promoters can significantly enhance the ammonia synthesis rate, the H-B process is still operated under elevated temperature (400°C–550°C) and pressure (10–30 MPa). Ammonia synthesis under mild conditions is a long-sought-after goal. Furthermore, there is increasing interest in employing NH3 as a carbon-free energy or hydrogen carrier and fuel to harvest and store renewable energies. This approach not only enables more sustainable fertilizer production but also opens new opportunities for decentralized energy storage and transport.14,15,16,17 Given this demand, a series of active catalysts or functional materials containing alkali or alkaline earth metals has been developed.4 These include alkali or alkaline earth metal hydrides,18,19,20,21,22 complex transition metal hydrides,23 oxyhydrides,24,25 and nitridehydrides.26,27 An in-depth understanding of alkali promotion may provide insight and opportunity to tackle such a practical and fundamental challenge.

Due to the fact that the precursors of alkalis are usually their hydroxides, nitrates, or carbonates, alkalis are believed to be bound with oxygen under ammonia synthesis reaction conditions.28 As examples, van Ommen et al. suggested that the chemical form of K is KOH, which is thermodynamically stable under ammonia synthesis conditions,29 while Paál et al. proposed that a K–O–Fe layer is the actual surface phase.30 Recently, we reported that, when Li or Ba is present in its hydride form (i.e., LiH or BaH2), it can enhance the ammonia synthesis activities of 3d transition metals (e.g., V, Cr, Mn, Fe, Co, and Ni) more efficiently than its oxide counterpart.18,20,21 Moreover, we also found that, when alkali or alkaline earth metals combine with transition metals to form complex transition metal hydrides, which possess a electron- and hydrogen-rich group, such as Li4FeH6, Li4RuH6, and Ba2RuH6, they can lead to ammonia production with superior kinetics under mild conditions via an associative mechanism.23,31 Furthermore, a set of transition-metal-free hydrides (e.g., potassium hydride-intercalated graphite [KH0.19C24] and barium hydride) could afford ammonia synthesis via a catalytic or chemical looping route.22,32 As K is an indispensable component in industrial iron-based catalysts, it is interesting to figure out whether K in hydride form (KH) would play a similar role as LiH on Fe. KH is an ionic hydride and has applications in hydrogen storage,33 organic synthesis as a strong base,34 and catalysis.35 H in KH is hydridic and can easily undergo a redox reaction with protic H of NH336 or H2O, forming H2, KNH2, or KOH, respectively. KNH2, on the other hand, can be hydrogenated to KH and NH3 via heterolytic H2 splitting at ∼50°C.36 The self-decomposition of KNH2 gives off K, N2, H2, and NH3.37 It can also react easily with H2O, forming NH3 and KOH. Such chemistry among K, H, N, and O (Figure 1), especially the variations of the chemical state of H from −1 (KH), 0 (H2), and +1 (KNH2 and KOH), would have a profound impact on ammonia synthesis. In this paper, we endeavor to gain insight into the role of H in KH (hydridic) and KOH (protonic) in ammonia formation. For this purpose, carbon nanotubes (CNT)-supported Fe catalysts promoted without KH or KOH were prepared, tested, and characterized. Our experimental results reveal that the KH-promoted Fe catalyst outperforms the KOH-promoted one; both hydridic H (KH) and protic H (KOH) are involved in NH3 formation.Figure 1Representative chemical reactions of the K/H/N/O systemHydridic H (H¯) and protic H (H+) are highlighted in blue and red, respectively.

Materials and methods

Preparation of Fe/CNTs

The CNT-supported (supplied by Prof. H.B. Zhang, Chemistry Department, Xiamen University, China) Fe catalyst was prepared by wetness impregnation of CNTs with a tetrahydrofuran (THF) solution of Fe2(CO)9. After removing THF, the powder was heated to 400°C under an N2-3H2 gas flow at a ramping rate of 2°C min−1 and held at 400°C for 1 h. The nominal weight ratio of Fe and CNTs is ∼0.1.

Preparation of KH-Fe/CNTs and KH-Fe

The xKH-Fe/CNT catalysts were prepared by incipient impregnation of as-prepared Fe/CNTs in a K-ammonia solution (with a K:Fe molar ratio of x:1), where K converted to KNH2. The excess NH3 was evacuated, and then the obtained samples were treated with a N2-3H2 flow at 300°C to convert KNH2 to KH (KNH2+H2 → KH+NH3; Figure S9).

Preparation of KOH-Fe/CNTs

The xKOH-Fe/CNT catalysts were prepared by incipient impregnation of as-prepared Fe/CNTs in a KOH-ethanol solution (with a K:Fe molar ratio of x:1) after ultrasonic treatment for 1 h. The ethanol was carefully evacuated at 60°C, and then the mixture was heated to 120°C under vacuum conditions for 1 h.

Preparation of Fe/MgO and KH-Fe/MgO

MgO was obtained according to a literature report.38 The preparation methods for Fe/MgO and KH-Fe/MgO were identical to those used for Fe/CNTs and KH-Fe/CNTs, respectively, except that the CNT support was replaced with an MgO support.

Preparation of Fe2N

Fe2N was prepared from Fe2O3 by the temperature-programmed reaction method. 5 g Fe2O3 was heated in a NH3 flow from room temperature to 500°C and held at that temperature for 80 h. Fe2N was confirmed by XRD characterization.

Preparation of Fe2N-4KH/4KOH

Fe2N-4KH/4KOH was prepared by ball-milling the mixture of Fe2N and KH/KOH with a Fe2N to KH:KOH molar ratio of 1:4 on a Retsch planetary ball mill (PM400) at 150 rpm for 5 h.

Preparation of KFeO2

KFeO2 was prepared in the following way. A mixture of Fe2O3 and KOH (with a small excess of KOH) was pretreated by ball-milling. Then, the mixture was heated to 500°C for 5 h under an Ar flow, and pure KFeO2 was confirmed by XRD characterization.

NH3 synthesis measurement

NH3 synthesis activity was tested on a quartz-lined stainless steel fixed-bed reactor under a continuous flow of syngas (N2:H2 = 1:3), as shown in Figure S1. Typically, 30 mg of catalyst was loaded to test the reproducibility of the catalytic activities and to ensure the absence of heat and mass transfer limitations. The temperature was raised at a ramping rate of 5 K min−1 under a given pressure and flow rate. The NH3 production rate was measured with a conductivity meter (Mettler Toledo SevenMulti) by conducting the exhaust gas to a diluted sulfuric acid solution and calculating the change in proton conductivity with time. The activity at each temperature was recorded after an on-stream time of at least 1 h. This setup was also used for temperature-programmed reaction (TPR) and isotope tracing experiments, where the effluent gas was analyzed using a mass spectrometer (Hiden HPR20).

Tests of reaction orders

NH3 reaction order was determined using the method reported by Hagen et al.39 N2 reaction order was determined by Equation 1 by changing the partial pressure of N2 while keeping a constant partial pressure of H2. This method can eliminate the effect of NH3 concentration change under different N2 partial pressure. The H2 order was obtained in a similar way:(Equation 1)lnr−αlnPNH3=lnk+γlnPH2+βlnPN2=lnK’+γlnPN2,where r is the rate of ammonia formation, k is the rate constant, and α, β, and γ represent the reaction order of NH3, N2, and H2, respectively.

Isotope tracing experiments

The 3KH-Fe/CNT sample (30 mg) was first heated to 300°C under a flow of 1N2-3H2 until a steady state was achieved. Then, it was substituted by H2 and He flow at 300°C for 2 h, respectively. Then, a mixture of N2 and D2 gases (N2:D2 = 1:3) at a total flow rate of 30 mL min−1 was introduced into the reactor. The flow rates and pressures of all gases were 30 mL min−1 and 1 MPa. The mass spectrum was used to monitor the gas phase products. m/z = 20, 19, 18, and 17 were detected, and all species’ relative sensitivities were set to 1. Unlike m/z = 20 and 19, the signals at m/z = 18 and 17 could arise from multiple species. To resolve the contributions of the individual ammonia isotopologues (ND3, ND2H, NDH2, NH3) signals change during the experiment, a blank experiment was also conducted without loading the catalyst. The original data were treated using the following method:IND3=I20−I20,blankIND2H=I19−I19,blankINDH2=I18−I18,blank−IND3×0.8−IND2H×0.8×1/3INH3=I17−I17,blank−IND2H×0.8×2/3−INDH2×0.8×2/3.

Ix stands for the mass spectrum signal intensity of species x, and according to the properties of NH3 and the mass spectrometer, the intensity of NH2 (a fragment of NH3) is about 80% that of NH3.

The KOH-Fe/CNT sample (35 mg) was treated using similar procedures, and the operating temperature was 325°C, with the flow rate of different gases at 15 mL min−1. As described above, part of the exhaust gas was analyzed by mass spectrometer. To determine the amounts of the different ammonia species, the remaining exhaust was conducted to the diluted sulfuric acid solution to calculate the ammonia formation rate under the N2-3D2 flow.

XRD measurements

X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns were recorded on a PANalytical X’pert diffractometer using a homemade sample cell covered with Kapton film to avoid air contamination.

TEM measurements

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) observation was conducted on a JEM-2100 at 200 kV.

FT-IR measurements

Fourier-transform infrared (FT-IR) spectroscopy were conducted on a Tensor II FT-IR spectrometer in diffuse reflection mode.

AC-HAADF-STEM measurements

Aberration-corrected high-angle annular dark-field scanning TEM (AC-HAADF-STEM) was conducted on a Titan Themis ETEM G3 microscope with image corrector at 300 kV and Hitachi HF5000 with aberration corrector at 200 kV. Electron energy-loss spectroscopy (EELS) analyses were performed on the STEM model on a Hitachi HF5000 microscope with a probe aberration corrector and a cold-field emission gun operated at 200 kV.

XPS measurements

X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) characterization was conducted on an ESCALAB XI+ spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) equipped with an ultrahigh vacuum (UHV) transfer module that maintains sample integrity by preventing air exposure during transfer from the glovebox to the analysis chamber. The spectra were recorded using mono-chromated X-ray irradiation with Al Kα (hν = 1,486.7 eV, where h is Planck's constant and ν is photon frequency) as the X-ray source. All spectra were acquired under UHV conditions (pressure ∼5 × 10 mbar) at room temperature. We calibrated for the charging effect by considering the C 1s peak at 284.6 eV.

Results and discussion

The 10 wt % Fe/CNT catalyst was prepared and tested as a reference. It has an ammonia synthesis rate of ∼120 μmolNH3 gcat−1 h−1 at 300°C and 1 MPa (Figure 2A), which is comparable to that of the reported 10 wt % Fe/C catalyst.39 The addition of KOH or KH leads to significantly enhanced activity. As shown in Figure S2, when the molar ratio of KOH:Fe is optimized to 7:1, the ammonia synthesis rate at 300°C increases to 1,030 μmolNH3 gcat−1 h−1. The optimized molar ratio of KH to Fe in the KH-Fe/CNT catalyst is ∼3:1. This catalyst shows superior activity especially at lower temperatures; i.e., the ammonia synthesis rate reaches 1,480 and 5,930 μmolNH3 gcat−1 h−1 at 250°C and 300°C, respectively. The activity of the KH-Fe/CNT catalyst is among the highest levels of Fe-based ammonia synthesis catalysts (Table S1) and is nearly an order of magnitude higher than that of Cs-Ru/MgO (600 μmolNH3 gcat−1 h−1) under the same reaction conditions. The TEM observation of those Fe-based catalysts collected after catalytic test shows that the average diameter of iron particles of KOH- and KH-Fe/CNT is around 5.5 nm (Figure S2). The weak diffraction peaks of Fe further indicate that it exists as small nanoparticles (Figure S3). Based on the mean particle size of Fe, the dispersion of Fe and corresponding turnover frequency (TOF) values were calculated and are summarized in Table 1. The TOF of the KH–Fe/CNT catalyst is ∼5.09 × 10−3 s−1, about three times higher than that of KOH–Fe/CNTs (∼1.28 × 10−3 s−1) at comparable Fe dispersion and particle size. K (KH or KOH)-promoted Fe/CNTs catalysts exhibit TOFs that are two orders of magnitude higher than that of Fe/CNTs (∼1 × 10−5 s−1). This clearly demonstrates that the chemical state of potassium (KH vs. KOH) strongly influences its promotional effect on Fe-based catalysts for ammonia synthesis. Stability tests show that KH-Fe/CNTs and KOH-Fe/CNTs can basically stably operate for 100 h at 300°C and 350°C, respectively, producing about 300 and 120 mg of ammonia, respectively, which is far greater than the catalyst amount of 30 mg (Figures S4 and S5).Figure 2Catalytic performance and apparent activation energies of several Fe-based catalysts for ammonia synthesis(A) Ammonia synthesis rate as a function of temperature.(B) Arrhenius’ plots of different catalysts.Reaction conditions: N2:H2 = 1:3 with a weight hourly space velocity (WHSV) of 60,000 mL gcat−1 h−1 and pressure of 1 MPa.Table 1Properties of a series of Fe-based catalystsCatalystFe loading (wt %)Mean particle size of Fe (nm)aFe dispersion (%)bNH3 synthesis rate (μmolNH3 gcat−1 h−1)cTOF (10−3 s−1)cFe/CNTs102.541.3310.013KH-Fe/CNTs8.25.422.159285.097KOH-Fe/CNTs5.95.721.110261.28aThe mean particle size of Fe was determined by TEM measurements.bThe dispersion of Fe was based on Borodziński and Bonarowska.40cConditions: pressure, 10 bar; temperature, 300°C; flow rate, 30 mL min−1.

The apparent activation energies (Ea) of those Fe-based catalysts were derived from the Arrhenius plots shown in Figure 2B. The addition of KOH and KH reduces the Ea values from 90.6 ± 6.3 kJ mol−1 for the Fe/CNTs to 69.0 ± 2.0 and 62.9 ± 2.5 kJ mol−1, respectively. The smaller Ea of KH-Fe/CNTs correlates with their faster ammonia production rates at lower temperatures. It should be noted that the Ea of KOH-Fe/CNTs is almost identical to that of a multi-promoted industrial Fe catalyst (KM1, 70 kJ mol−1)39 at a similar pressure and temperature range, implying that the KOH-Fe/CNTs catalyst may have some features in common with the industrial catalyst.

The reaction orders with respect to N2, H2, and NH3 were also determined, as shown in Figures S7–S9 and summarized in Table S2. The reaction orders of N2 for KOH-, KH-, and unpromoted Fe/CNTs are all close to unity, suggesting that the RDS is the dissociation of N2, which is in agreement with that observed in conventional Fe- or Ru-based catalysts.39,41 In terms of H2 and NH3 reaction orders, they are more positive for KH- and KOH-promoted catalysts than unpromoted Fe/CNTs. Comparatively, the KH-Fe/CNTs and KOH-Fe/CNTs have a more positive H2 order and less negative NH3 order than Fe/CNTs, which likely originates from modifications to the hydrogenation of surface Nad species and the NH3 desorption step. The difference in reaction orders between KH–Fe/CNTs and KOH–Fe/CNTs may be associated with the distinct chemical states of potassium (i.e., KH vs. KOH), which induce different surface reaction pathways.

Our previous work showed that LiH, as a strong reducing agent, can remove nitrogen atoms from transition metal nitrides to form thermodynamically more stable LiNH2/Li2NH species and a nitrogen-deficient metal nitride (or metal).18 One may consider that KH would play a similar role as LiH. However, potassium imide (K2NH) is unknown42 and perhaps thermodynamically unstable. To test whether KH is able to abstract N from Fe nitride, we chose an N-rich iron nitride, Fe2N, as a model compound and conducted Ar temperature-programmed decomposition (Ar-TPD) and H2 temperature-programmed reaction (H2-TPR) experiments on a ball-milled KH-Fe2N sample. As shown in Figure 3A, a significant amount of H2 release can be observed above 240°C, which may come from the self-decomposition of KH.43 The XRD pattern of the sample collected after Ar-TPD to 400°C shows the presence of K, Fe metal, and Fe4N (Figure S10). Such experimental observations suggest that KH undergoes self-decomposition, and the overall process does not resemble the interaction of LiH and Fe2N; i.e., the reaction between LiH and Fe2N takes place at a temperature as low as 150°C and the solid products are Fe and Li2NH (shown in Figure S11). In a flow of H2, on the other hand, the KH-Fe2N sample releases NH3 at relatively lower temperatures (starting from ∼170°C), showing a main peak and a shoulder at 250°C and 325°C, respectively (Figure 3A). Metallic Fe and KH are the solid products (Figure S10). The H2-TPR profile of neat Fe2N also contains an NH3 signal but featuring a much broader temperature range from 300°C to 580°C (Figure S12A). The solid product still contains an Fe4N phase (Figure S12B), which is similar to a previous report (i.e., the presence of Fe4N after hydrogenation of ε-Fe3N1+x at 400°C44) and signifies the slow kinetics in the hydrogenation of neat Fe2N to NH3. The comparison of Ar-TPD and H2-TPR results of these two samples clearly evidence that the nitrogen of Fe2N can be facilely removed and hydrogenated to NH3 in the co-presence of KH and H2. It is interesting to compare the roles of LiH and KH in nitrogen transfer and hydrogenation of Fe nitride. The direct N transfer from FeNx to LiH is thermodynamically favorable and has been verified experimentally (Figure S11). KH, on the other hand, only promotes N conversion to NH3 in the presence of H2. We propose that the reactions R1 and R2 of mild exo- and endothermicity could occur, which are thermodynamically feasible under low temperatures (Table S3). R1 can be interpreted as a redox between H2, hydridic H (KH), and Fe2N to give rise to protic H (KNH2) and metallic Fe. Experimentally, we conducted H2-TPR of KNH2 (R2), which showed that NH3 formation can take place at a temperature as low as 40°C (Figure S13), in good agreement with a literature report.36 Under such a proposal, the R1 would be kinetically slow; Figure 3A shows that the formation of NH3 occurs at 170°C, which is ∼130°C higher than that of neat KNH2 (Figure S13).

The chemical form and promoting mechanism of K on the iron-based catalyst have been debated for decades. In the preparation of conventional Fe catalysts, the precursors of K are usually KNO3, K2CO3, or KOH.28 Based on chemical analysis and thermodynamic considerations, van Ommen et al. concluded that part of K reacted with Al2O3 and that the other part was in the form of KOH rather than metallic K, K2O, KH, or KNH2.29 The TPD and H2-TPR results shown above, however, stimulated us to re-investigate the role of KOH in the ammonia synthesis reaction. The interaction between Fe2N and KOH may provide useful information,45 so Ar-TPD and H2-TPR measurements were performed. A significant amount of H2 and trace amounts of NH3, H2O, and N2 can be detected during the Ar-TPD treatment (Figure 3B). Fe metal and a K–O–Fe ternary oxide (KFeO2) are the solid products (Figure S14). To balance the reaction, the rest of the elements may compound into KNH2, which is known to decompose to K metal and NH3, H2, and N2 at elevated temperature.37 Because the Ar-TPD stopped at 500°C, metallic K would melt and flow out of the sample bed. We thus tentatively propose the reactions R3 and R4 for the interaction of KOH and Fe2N in an Ar atmosphere. It should be noted that, under realistic reaction conditions, the surface species may involve K–O–Fe and K–N–H intermediate species rather than well-defined KFeO2 and KNH2, enabling the catalytic cycle.

The H2-TPR profile of KOH-Fe2N (Figure 3B) shows that a significant amount of NH3 can be released at temperatures above 225°C, which is ∼50°C higher than that of the KH-Fe2N sample but ∼100°C lower than the neat Fe2N. Moreover, the XRD pattern shows that only Fe can be seen in the post H2-TPR sample (heated to 500°C) (Figure S14). These results show that lattice nitrogen in Fe2N can also be facilely hydrogenated to NH3 in the co-presence of KOH and H2. When quickly cooling down the sample from 340°C to room temperature and collecting a sample for XRD characterization, we observed weak KFeO2 diffractions (Figure S15). It is interesting to check the behavior of KFeO2 under a hydrogen atmosphere. We thus synthesized KFeO2 (XRD pattern shown in Figure S16) and conducted an H2-TPR experiment. As shown in Figure S16A, this ternary oxide can be hydrogenated to release water at temperatures above 250°C (we suspect that the H2O signal observed in the Ar-TPD at high temperatures, shown in Figure 3B, is related to the hydrogenation of KFeO2), and XRD characterization verifies the formation of Fe and KOH (Figure S16B). The hydrogenation of KFeO2 can thus be described as R5. Based on the Ar-TPD, H2-TPR, and XRD results, we suppose that KFeO2 may be involved in the formation of NH3 from Fe2N+KOH+H2 via R3→R2→R5→R6. It is worth mentioning that potassium iron oxide has been considered a key catalytically active phase in ethylbenzene dehydrogenation46,47,48,49 and was also found to be uniformly distributed at the centers of magnetite dendrites of a fused iron Fischer-Tropsch catalyst.50 Paál et al. proposed that a surface Fe–O–K layer is the active phase under working conditions of ammonia synthesis30:(Equation 3)Fe2N+2KOH→Fe+KFeO2+KNH2ΔH298Ko=77.8kJmol−1ΔG298Ko=77.8kJmol−1(Equation 4)KNH2→K+H2+12N2+NH3ΔH298Ko=119kJmol−1ΔG298Ko=51.5kJmol−1(Equation 5)KFeO2+32H2→Fe+KOH+H2OΔH298Ko=20.1kJmol−1ΔG298Ko=22.2kJmol−1(Equation 6)KNH2+H2O→KOH+NH3ΔH298Ko=−109.8kJmol−1ΔG298Ko=−111.0kJmol−1(Equation 7)KH+H2O→KOH+H2ΔH298Ko=−125.1kJmol−1ΔG298Ko=−107.8kJmol−1.

Comparing the experimental data and proposals for the H2 interactions with Fe2N-KH and Fe2N-KOH, one may find that KNH2 is responsible for the NH3 formation in both systems, while R3 is kinetically and thermodynamically difficult, rendering NH3 formation from Fe2N+KOH+H2 at relatively higher temperatures compared with Fe2N+KH+H2.

Another derivation is that H in KH and KOH may have chance to be bound with N, forming NH3 (see R1 and R3). To verify this, transient isotopic exchange experiments were conducted on the KH- and KOH-Fe/CNTs catalysts, respectively (see the supplemental information for experimental details). A N2-3D2 mixture gas was introduced to the 3KH-Fe/CNT and 7KOH-Fe/CNT catalysts pretreated in an He flow at 300°C and 325°C, respectively, and the signals of ammonia isotopomers (ND3, ND2H, NDH2, and NH3) were monitored simultaneously by using an online mass spectrometer. The results are presented in Figure 4, where it can be clearly seen that, for both catalysts, the signal of ammonia appears right upon introducing the N2-3D2 mixture. H-containing ammonia gases (i.e., NH3, NDH2, and ND2H) form and remain in the outlet gas stream during a testing period of 20 min. More interestingly, for both systems, the signal of NH3 appears first, followed by NDH2, ND2H, and ND3. This sequence suggests that the activated N atoms are hydrogenated to ammonia by hydrogen from the KH or KOH rather than directly by the hydrogen from the gaseous phase (D2) in the initial stage. Semi-quantitative analysis shows that the amount of H atoms for forming ammonia is greater than what can be accommodated by surface Fe atoms (Table S4) and substantial participation of lattice H of KH or KOH in the catalysis. The catalysis occurring on the catalyst surface contains a set of elementary reactions that would have different chemical potentials from the reactions we proposed for the bulk-phase KH-Fe2N and KOH-Fe2N; i.e., the electronic and structural properties of the surface and bulk phase are different. However, as indicated in the reactions R1 and R3, the removal of N from Fe2N to Fe may pass through Fe4N or another N-poor phase that resembles the surface Fe4N phase identified in the conventional Fe catalyst under working conditions.51 We therefore would like to correlate the reaction scheme derived from the Ar-TPD, H2-TPR, and isotopic characterization results with the catalytic mechanism of KH-Fe and KOH-Fe to discuss a different viewpoint of the role of alkalis in ammonia catalysis. As shown in the proposal (Scheme 1), K is actively involved in NH3 synthesis by exchanging partners with O/H/N. Both KH and KOH help with N removal from Fe, creating free sites for N2 activation. The site where NH3 is formed has a K component rather than neat Fe, as interpreted previously. H2 activation, on the other hand, may also involve the contribution of K; i.e., direct hydrogenation of KNH2 to KH and NH3 is kinetically facile, where H2 does not undergoes homolytic splitting but, rather, disproportionation to protic H (NH3) and hydridic H (KH). Such viewpoint should be further evaluated by advanced experimental and theoretical investigations and differs from the previous interpretation, where K works as an electronic promoter that provides electrons to Fe.

The above section discussed the role of KOH and KH in Fe-based catalysts for ammonia synthesis, focusing on the different mechanistic pathways from the perspective of phase transitions. In fact, establishing the correlation between the structure of catalysts, including both macroscopic architecture and electronic structure, and its catalytic activity is critical. AC-HAADF-STEM characterization was employed to study the morphological structure of KH-Fe/CNTs (Figures 5A–5C). It can be observed that Fe nanoparticles are uniformly dispersed on the surface of CNTs. EELS mapping and high-resolution EELS line scans both show that K is distributed on the surface of Fe, suggesting that Fe and KH jointly form active sites for catalyzing ammonia synthesis. XPS characterization was used to investigate the chemical states of K and Fe in the KH-Fe/CNT and KOH-Fe/CNT catalysts. As shown in Figure 5D, the K 2p XPS spectrum of KH-Fe/CNTs exhibits a 0.6 eV positive shift compared to KOH-Fe/CNTs. According to the literature, the binding energy of the K 2p electron decreases when metallic K is exposed to oxygen, which is contrary to the typical relationship between metal oxide states and binding energy.52 This behavior may be attributed to the influence of the Madelung potential.53 Here, we propose that, in KH, the highly localized negative charge of H− combined with the compact ionic lattice generates a significantly larger (more positive) Madelung potential at the K site, shifting the K 2p core level to a higher binding energy. In contrast, in KOH, the charge in OH− is more delocalized, and the hydrogen-bonded framework produces a weaker electrostatic field. Therefore, these results indicate that the K species in KH-Fe/CNTs exist in a more electron-rich environment compared to those in KOH-Fe/CNTs. The Fe 2p XPS spectra of both KH-Fe/CNTs and KOH-Fe/CNTs display four distinct peaks: at 724.0 and 711.0 eV, corresponding to the 2p1/2 and 2p3/2 binding energies of Fe(II), and at 720.5 eV and 707.2 eV, corresponding to those of Fe(0), respectively (Figure 5E). Surface science and theoretical studies suggest that Fe in Fe-based catalysts predominantly exists in the metallic state.54,55,56 However, numerous studies indicate the coexistence of both metallic and oxidized Fe species in Fe-based catalysts through ex situ characterization, which is consistent with our observations.57,58 Here, for both KH- and KOH-promoted Fe/CNTs, the chemical state of Fe remains nearly identical, suggesting that the electronic promotion effect is not significant in this system even though the electronic states of K in KH and KOH differ. Additionally, a higher proportion of metallic Fe is observed in the KH-Fe/CNTs compared to the KOH-Fe/CNTs, which may be attributed to the stronger reducibility of KH over KOH, facilitating the transfer of Fe surface species. In addition, the IR spectrum of the spent KH–Fe/CNTs catalyst shows a distinct K–H stretching vibration at ∼940 cm−1, consistent with the literature value, suggesting that KH is retained in the catalyst during the reaction (Figure S17).59 Recent studies have shown that a conductive carbon support can facilitate the migration of H+ and e−, which helps establish a connection between the promoter and the active metal, thereby enhancing the activity of Ru-BaO catalysts.60 Furthermore, a carbon support with a low work function exerts a stronger promoting effect. To investigate whether such an effect exists in our system, we also used MgO as a support for the Fe-KH catalyst in ammonia synthesis (Figure S18). The similar activity observed for MgO and CNTs suggests that the support primarily influences the dispersion of the active components, Fe and KH. Conclusion

In summary, we have developed a highly active KH-Fe/CNT catalyst for ammonia synthesis. The TOF for the KH-Fe/CNT sample is ∼3 times and 2 orders of magnitude higher than that of KOH-Fe/CNTs and Fe/CNTs, respectively. The presence of KH and KOH can both promote hydrogenation of Fe nitride to NH3. More importantly, the present results reveal the important role of potassium and reactive hydrogen species in ammonia synthesis over the Fe catalyst. K keeps changing partners during the catalysis. H species in KH and KOH are likely involved in NH3 formation via different reaction paths. The hydridic H of KH is reductive and undergoes a redox reaction with FeNx to form metallic Fe and NH3 under a hydrogen atmosphere. The protic H in KOH, on the other hand, protonates activated N, forming NHx species. We proposed that the possible formation of KH and KNH2 transient species may play a role in this process. This work provides new insights into the chemical state and functional role of K as well as other alkali and alkaline earth metals in ammonia synthesis catalysis. For example, BaO is an effective promoter for Ru and Co metals in ammonia synthesis. It is interesting to figure out whether BaH2 or BaNH-like species could be similarly involved in the nitrogen activation and hydrogenation to ammonia. Advanced characterization techniques as well as theoretical calculations could be very powerful to get more detailed information regarding this aspect.
