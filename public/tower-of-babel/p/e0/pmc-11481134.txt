Point prevalence, characteristics and treatment variations for preterm infants with bronchopulmonary dysplasia in China: a ‘snapshot’ study
Authors: Jianhua Wang, Ge Yang, Zhiqiang Cai, Huayan Zhang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThe incidence of bronchopulmonary dysplasia (BPD) is increasing, but data on its prevalence and management variations remain insufficient in China. The study aims to investigate its point prevalence and variations in BPD care.Setting, patients and interventionsA multicentre cross-sectional study was conducted in 37 NICUs. ‘Snapshot’ clinical data on 18 June 2021 for individual patients born at <32 weeks gestation age (GA) were collected. BPD was defined based on the National Institute of Child Health and Human Development (NICHD) 2001 criteria and two newer criteria (NICHD 2018 and Jensen 2019).ResultsA total of 1044 infants born at <32 weeks GA were included, of which 72% were born at ≥28 weeks GA and 95.8% were born at ≥750 g. At the time of ‘snapshot’, 563 were ≥28 days old and 281 were ≥36 weeks postmenstrual age (PMA). The prevalence of BPD was 78.2% per NICHD 2001 definition. Infants with BPD were more likely to be born earlier with a lower birth weight and to have complications. Among infants who reached 36 weeks PMA, the point prevalence and severity of BPD differed across BPD definitions. Respiratory support and treatments for BPD also significantly varied.ConclusionPoint prevalence of BPD is remarkably high in China. The prevalence of BPD was lowest according to the NICHD 2018 definition, whereas the NICHD 2001 definition classified most patients with severe BPD. We found infants with BPD have more complications and significant differences in BPD treatments between centres. Efforts to prevent BPD and standardise care are warranted in China.

Introduction

Bronchopulmonary dysplasia (BPD), first described in 1967 by Northway et al,1 is a prevalent complication of premature infants causing significant burden.2 BPD remains a leading morbidity in premature infants <32 weeks gestational age (GA) with varied prevalence. When compared with other common complications of prematurity including necrotising enterocolitis, severe brain injury, late-onset sepsis (LOS) and severe retinopathy of prematurity (ROP), BPD was the only neonatal morbidity that had shown an increased trend in the US National Institute of Child Health and Human Development (NICHD) Neonatal Research Network centres between 1993 and 2012.3 BPD prevalence was 12%–15% reported by the neonatal networks in Canada, Japan and Switzerland, while 25%–30% were reported by the Korea Neonatal Network and Vermont Oxford Network.48 A study of 11 high-income countries reported that 10.8%–37.1% of preterm infants were affected by BPD between 2007 and 2015,9 indicating significant variations in prevalence by centres and regions. One of the underlying causes of these variations might be our evolving understanding of BPD and the coexistence of multiple BPD diagnostic criteria.

In China, there has been a scarcity of multicentre data investigating BPD prevalence using standard criteria until the newly established Chinese Neonatal Network reported an incidence of 29.2% in 2019 in its member hospitals.10 However, the diagnostic criteria for BPD vary across nations, and there have been no published data on the variations of BPD management in China. A baseline overview of prevalence and treatments for BPD in China is of pivotal significance to the identification of potential areas of improvement and promoting evidence-based best practices for BPD management. We, therefore, conducted a multicentre study including 37 major delivery centres and children’s hospitals in China to obtain the baseline prevalence data and an overview of BPD management. In this study, we aim to investigate the ‘snapshot’ point prevalence of BPD and compare the clinical characteristics and treatment variations of BPD in premature infants born at <32 weeks GA in China.

Methods

Study population

A multicentre cross-sectional study was conducted in 37 delivery centres and children’s hospitals from 6 geographical regions in China. At least one large delivery hospital and one children’s hospital from each region participated in the study. In addition to 30 tertiary delivery hospitals or children’s hospitals from 21 provinces or municipalities in China, we also included 7 secondary centres in Guangdong province. All preterm infants born at <32 weeks GA and were inpatient at the participating centres on the day of ‘snapshot’ (18 June 2021) were included.

Study procedures

The investigation instrument was a predetermined standardised form and it was distributed online to investigators in participating centres. The online forms and data were collated by a designated person of the Guangzhou Women and Children’s Medical Center.

The clinical form was designed to collect data on the characteristics of participating centres and clinical data of preterm infants admitted. The form consisted of two main parts: (1) centre characteristics including Neonatal Intensive Care Unit(NICU) size, the number of deliveries in 2020, the number and percentage of preterm infants with GA <32 weeks and transferred patients, yearly NICU admission rate and (2) clinical data of preterm infants including demographical characteristics (eg, GA and birth weight), respiratory support treatments for BPD at four time point (ie, within 24 hours after birth, 28 days after birth and 36 weeks postmenstrual age (PMA), the day of ‘snapshot’), data of respiratory support, surgical procedures, management and follow-up programme for infants with BPD.

BPD diagnostic criteria

BPD was diagnosed by each of the three criteria, that is, Eunice Kennedy Shriver NICHD 2001 criteria,11 NICHD 2018 criteria2 and Jensen 2019 criteria.12 Preterm infants who were born <32 weeks GA with postnatal age ≥28 days and continuous oxygen supplement were eligible for BPD diagnosis by NICHD 2001. BPD severity was identified based on respiratory support and oxygen supplement at 36 weeks PMA with no respiratory support or oxygen supplement as mild BPD. Those who had a fraction of inspired oxygen (FiO2)<0.3 were diagnosed as moderate BPD, and those having FiO2≥ 0.3 or the need for positive ventilation or high flow nasal cannula (HFNC)≥3 L/min were classified as BPD.

Infants were eligible for NICHD 2018 criteria assessment if (a) born at <32 weeks with PMA ≥36 weeks, (b) non-missing data of FiO2, chest X-ray (CXR), respiratory support and oxygen supplement at 36 weeks PMA, (c) ventilation support can maintain arterial oxygen saturation between 90% and 95% for more than consecutive 3 days at 36 weeks PMA. Infants with no oxygen supplement/respiratory support or HFNC <3L/min with FiO2<0.21 or CXR showed no signs of BPD characteristics and were classified as no BPD. The grading of BPD severity was identified as per the respiratory support and oxygen supplement at 36 weeks PMA.2 The BPD diagnosis by Jensen 2019 criteria12 was made based on respiratory support, regardless of oxygen supplement. The criterion of non-BPD is no respiratory or oxygen support at 36 weeks PMA, whereas O2 supplement in the hood/isolette or HFNC ≤2 L/min is classified as BPD grade 1, HFNC >2 L/min or requiring nasal continuous positive airway pressure (nCPAP)/nasal intermittent positive pressure ventilation (NIPPV)/bilevel positive airway pressure (BiPAP) support is classified as BPD grade 2, and those who need invasive mechanical ventilation (MV) are classified as BPD grade 3.

The primary outcome is to assess the point prevalence of BPD in infants who were at risk of developing BPD, that is, born §amp;lt;32 weeks GA and at a postnatal age ≥28 days/PMA ≥36 weeks on the day of ‘snapshot’.

Patient and public involvement

Given that the study participants were neonates, direct involvement of patients or the public in the study’s design, conduct, reporting or dissemination was not appropriate or feasible. This limitation arises because neonates cannot provide informed input into these processes. Consequently, the study was developed without the inclusion of patient or public feedback.

Statistical analysis

The data with normal distribution are shown as mean±SD. χ2 test or Fisher’s exact test was used to compare the enumeration data between groups. The difference was statistically significant if p<0.05. SPSS software (V.25.0, SPSS) was used for statistical analysis.

Results

Patient and centre characteristics

Of the 37 centres, 27 provide delivery service with annual delivery rates ranging from 196 to 29 399 in the year 2020. Of these, 13 hospitals had an annual delivery rate of over 10 000 and delivered on average 247 infants (range 138–366) at <32 weeks GA. Inborn infants accounted for more than 50% of patients in this study.

A total of 1044 preterm infants born at <32 weeks GA were included, with 446 female infants (table 1). The infants were born at an average gestational age (GA) of 28.6±1.8 weeks with 71.6% of infants born at ≥28 weeks and 95.8% born at ≥750 g. At the time of ‘snapshot’, the average PMA was 33.8±4.3 weeks, and the mean of postnatal age was 36.7±31.4 days. Around three-fourths of the patients received antenatal dexamethasone (74.6%) and surfactant after birth (72.9%). 563 infants were at a postnatal age of ≥28 days and 281 were at or over 36 weeks PMA.

As GA increased from 25 to 31 weeks, point prevalence shows a decreasing trend (online supplemental material e-figure 2). The NICHD 2001 and Jensen 2019 criteria presented similar point prevalence and a consistent trend of decline while the NICHD 2018 criteria show a drastic decrease of BPD point prevalence at 27 weeks GA and further decline afterwards.

Clinical characteristics of infants with versus without BPD

Table 3 shows the variations of clinical characteristics, complications and treatments in infants with versus without BPD by NICHD 2001 criteria. Preterm infants diagnosed with BPD had a significantly higher proportion of GA less than 28 weeks and birth weight less than 750 grams, though no difference in sex was found. Significantly higher rates of complications were found in infants with BPD: 95% of the preterm infants had respiratory distress syndrome (RDS), 28% had ROP and 27% had intraventricular haemorrhage (IVH). Infants with BPD were also more likely to have patent ductus arteriosus (PDA), LOS and ventilator-associated pneumonia (VAP) than their counterparts. Significant treatment variations were observed because Infant with BPD were more likely to use diuretics, bronchodilators, corticosteroid and caffeine and receive treatments for PDA.

Treatment variations of infants with BPD across centres

Among infants with BPD, 14.6% of infants received invasive MV whereas 33.3% of infants had respiratory support with a nasal cannula and 21.2% of infants received no respiratory support (table 4). As for the variations between centres, 0%–28.6% of infants received invasive MV, ranging significantly across centres (p<0.001). The use of non-invasive respiratory support (eg, N-CPAP or nasal cannula) and oxygen supplement significantly varied between centres with ≥20 inpatient infants with BPD on the day of ‘snapshot’. However, no significant variations in respiratory support were observed either by three BPD criteria (online supplemental material e-table 1) or by centre types (ie, delivery centres vs children’s hospitals) (online supplemental material e-table 2).

In terms of the medications, 38.1% of infants with BPD received caffeine and 7.1% received bronchodilators. There were also significant variations between centres in the administration of diuretics (0%–40%, p=0.009), bronchodilators (0%–26.9%, p=0.018), inhaled (5–68.2%, p<0.001) and intravenous corticosteroids (0%–29.2%, p=0.013) and caffeine (5%–66.7%, p<0.001), whereas no significant difference between centres was found in the use of antibiotics in infants with BPD (5%–24%, p=0.69).

Discussion

The ‘snapshot’ of BPD point prevalence ranged from 42.6% to 78.2%, with severe/grade 3 BPD from 26.6% to 58.3%, suggesting a significantly high prevalence of BPD (particularly severe disease) in the NICUs in China. We found remarkable variations in clinical characteristics, complications and treatments for BPD. There remain substantial gaps in the best practices and optimal management of BPD in China.

The understanding of BPD is an evolving process, as evidenced by the changes in BPD definitions1 13 and diagnostic criteria.2 11 12 The current three BPD criteria rely on clinical characteristics, particularly respiratory modalities and oxygen supplements.2 11 12 The use of various BPD definitions results in a difference in BPD prevalence. Compared with NICHD 2018, NICHD 2001 and Jensen 2019 criteria diagnosed more than 50% of infants with BPD, consistent with prior studies.14 15 Over 60% of infants had moderate to severe BPD, indicating the substantial disease burden of BPD in China. The point prevalence of BPD by NICHD 2001 criteria is higher but no significant difference in the point prevalence was found using the NICHD 2001 and Jensen 2019 criteria. We speculate extremely preterm infants, particularly those born at <28 weeks, are more likely to require a longer duration of oxygen therapy after birth. Thus, 36 weeks PMA can be a more appropriate time point to assess chronic lung disease in extremely preterm infants. The BPD prevalence was inversely proportional to the increase of GA and birth weight.8 16 It is evident that the BPD prevalence in our cohort was 2–3 times higher than previous reports from the USA,17 Japan18 and middle-east region,19 especially among extremely and very preterm infants. There remain substantial gaps for improvement in the early postnatal management of preterm infants to prevent BPD.

Our data revealed a difference in BPD prevalence between delivery hospitals and children’s hospitals, likely attributed to the transfer of established patients with BPD to children’s hospitals and representing the regionalisation of BPD care in China. Previous data support centralising neonatal care for high-risk pregnant women and newborns as a strategy to mitigate BPD and benefit its prevention.18 However, the regionalisation or deregionalisation of BPD care may vary for established patients with BPD and preterm infants at risk of BPD. A US study showed the increased risk-adjusted BPD prevalence in level II NICUs than in level IV NICUs in California, indicating the need for regionalisation of NICU care early after birth to reduce BPD prevalence.20 The hospital variations of BPD prevalence also suggest how hospital environments and diverse interventions influence the development of BPD.

Current data suggest that the use of antenatal steroids and early postnatal surfactant therapy contributes to reducing the mortality rate of premature infants, its impact on mitigating BPD appears to be neutral.21 22 We found that 74.6% and 72.9% of infants received antenatal steroids and surfactant therapy early after birth in our data. This aligns with the 72% of surfactant therapy reported in the USA, but there is a notable disparity compared with the rate of antenatal steroid use (88.8%).17 The proportion of systemic or inhaled corticosteroids in infants with BPD was higher and variations were also found across centres. The controversy of postnatal steroids to prevent BPD may result in these variations. Studies on systemic steroids suggest that early dexamethasone treatment can reduce BPD risk, but it is related to both short-term and long-term adverse effects,23 whereas late steroid use also increases the risk of long-term adverse outcomes.24 Furthermore, the evidence does not support the early inhaled corticosteroids to prevent BPD and otherwise suggests potential adverse effects on mortality.2527 Although there is an ongoing controversy in the use of steroids, our study found notable discrepancies in its utilisation: certain centres reported nearly one-third of preterm infants received systemic steroids, and almost 70% were prescribed inhaled steroids. This highlights the need to improve our understanding of evidence-based practice on steroids and balance its treatment efficacy with side effects.

In our study, complications are more common in preterm infants with BPD. Preterm infants diagnosed with BPD using the NICHD 2001 criteria11 had the highest proportion of complications (ie, RDS, ROP, IVH, PDA, LOS and VAP). However, our study cannot compare the predictive ability of different diagnostic criteria for long-term prognosis due to the lack of data on outcomes and follow-ups. In addition, we found significant variabilities in the use of respiratory support and oxygen supplement between centres. Evidence suggests MV-induced lung injury increases the risk of BPD,28 and thus, there has been an increasing trend of using non-invasive ventilation modalities (eg, CPAP) than endotracheal ventilation during the past two decades.29 Nevertheless, previous data show that despite the use of less invasive ventilation after birth, the respiratory outcomes of preterm infants did not significantly improve29 and the prevalence of BPD has been increasing.18 This suggests that the benefits of solely decreasing invasive ventilation support and transitioning to non-invasive modalities may be less significant than controlling other BPD risk factors. Titrating respiratory and oxygen support for preterm infants with evolving or established BPD can be sophisticated. Significant variations in respiratory support modes were found in the proportion of using invasive MV ranged from 0% to 28.6% across these centres; some centres predominantly used high-level non-invasive ventilation modalities such as CPAP/BiPAP/NIPPV, while others preferred using a nasal cannula. These differences may be related to the severity of the disease but are also influenced by physicians’ preferences. Our study also found some centres were still using oxygen hood or isolette with no flow or pressure support. Since these supports pose risks of hyperoxia and cannot reduce airway dead space and maintain airway open to recruit lung volume, we do not recommend their use in patients with BPD.

Recommendations suggest that patients with moderate to severe BPD should have multidisciplinary management.30 Surgical procedures, for example, tracheostomy or gastrostomy, were rarely performed in the cohort of this study (data are not shown), significantly contrasting to the large multicentre BPD point prevalence study in the USA.31 The limited application of these treatments in our patients may be related to insufficient awareness among doctors, limited interdisciplinary collaboration and inadequate postdischarge management capabilities.

This study has several strengths. First, this study is the first multicentre study including major delivery centres and children’s hospitals from representative geographical regions in China to investigate BPD point prevalence and variations in patient characteristics and treatments. It is the pivotal ‘first step’ up to the best practice of BPD and serves as the basis for designing further clinical trials and quality improvement initiatives for BPD prevention in China. Second, the use of a standardised instrument further strengthens the data collection, ensuring data quality to objectively reflect the current situation of Chinese population. While this study is limited by its cross-sectional ‘snapshot’ design, and it was not intended to identify the incidence of BPD in China. Second, we did not collect follow-up or prognostic data on the development of infants. And lastly, we were unable to identify if the variations in treatments for BPD infants resulted from institutional or provider differences in BPD management.

To conclude, our study reported a significantly high BPD point prevalence, and variations in clinical characteristics and treatments between infants with BPD and without BPD in China. Medication and respiratory support for infants with BPD were also drastically varied across institutions. Further studies should focus on quality improvement initiatives to investigate and standardise the optimal management of patients within China.
