A Generative AI Framework for Pharmacokinetic Clinical Study Report Authoring
Authors: John Samuelsson, Samuel Blakeman, Ezra Alexander, Srividya Neelakantan, Ravi Shankar Prasad Singh, May Garrett, Allison Murphy, Jing Zhu, Subha Madhavan, Sheraz Khan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTClinical Study Reports (CSRs) constitute the final consolidation of findings from clinical studies and routinely include Pharmacokinetic (PK) results. To assist with PK results authoring, we developed a generative Artificial Intelligence (AI) based method that employs a hierarchical, chained large language model (LLM) framework with in‐context learning to draft PK results directly from study Tables, Listings, and Figures (TLFs), with optional human input. Unlike traditional fine‐tuning approaches, our method does not require large datasets or extensive compute, while producing outputs closely aligned with established CSR structure, tone, and analytical conventions using fewer than a dozen example reports. To assess performance, AI‐generated reports and manually expert‐written CSRs were evaluated in two blinded review sessions by clinical pharmacologists and pharmacometricians, focusing on relative bioavailability (rBA) and drug–drug interaction (DDI) studies. The AI‐generated reports achieved an average reporting quality score of ~90% relative to the manually written CSRs. Together, these results demonstrate a practical, scalable solution for assisting PK report authoring in clinical studies, potentially reducing authoring time while maintaining high‐quality standards.

Introduction

Clinical Study Reports (CSRs) serve as the definitive documentation of clinical trial outcomes for regulatory submission and scientific communication [1]. Among their core components, the pharmacokinetic (PK) section plays a critical role in characterizing drug disposition and informing dose selection, safety, and efficacy assessments [2]. The PK section is particularly prominent in Phase 1 studies that focus on aspects relating to dosage, formulation, and drug safety [3]. However, analyzing Tables, Listings, and Figures (TLFs) outputs and authoring PK narratives in a manner consistent with organizational style guides and regulatory expectations can be time consuming. This effort is compounded by tight timelines, standardized narrative structures, and the need for precision in reporting across diverse study designs.

Recent advances in generative artificial intelligence (AI), particularly large language models (LLMs), is showing promise to transform clinical documentation and PK reporting in biomedical research [4, 5, 6]. Some studies have evaluated the use of LLMs in specific upstream steps of the PK reporting process, for example, generating Nonlinear Mixed Effects Modeling (NONMEM) code, and explaining and interpreting their outputs [7, 8]. While several reviews have discussed the prospect of utilizing generative AI in the downstream reporting workflow where generated reporting outputs such as TLFs are used to author the CSR, there is a striking paucity of studies to date who have presented, implemented and evaluated real technical solutions for this purpose [9, 10, 11, 12, 13].

Landman et al. [14] reported a challenge where six teams were tasked with developing a generative‐AI system for automating the authoring of the safety sections of CSRs, which were then evaluated by a team of medical writers, statisticians, and data scientists. The challenge was limited to the safety section; however, the study highlighted shortcomings in reporting quality, underscoring the need for human intervention. Bracken et al. [15] performed a systematic review of AI driven documentation systems in healthcare settings and noted variations in reporting quality across studies and reported concerns among health care professionals regarding the accuracy and consistency of AI‐generated reports. Meanwhile, work in the medical AI literature demonstrates that few‐shot approaches can produce domain‐appropriate clinical rationales with limited task‐specific data, an appealing property for Phase 1 PK studies where corpora can be scarce [16, 17, 18].

Collectively, the current literature supports an optimistic outlook that generative AI can accelerate PK reporting and harmonize narrative quality, but no study to date, to the authors' knowledge, has presented a validated technical solution for generating holistic PK CSRs for clinical studies. The present study aimed to address this gap by developing and validating a generative AI tool that can assist in the authoring of full PK CSRs directly from TLFs in clinical studies.

The PK sections of CSRs, however, present some unique challenges. They typically adhere to strict styling and analytical conventions that are not captured well by simple zero‐shot LLM‐prompting. While fine‐tuning LLMs could be an option to address this, it generally requires large amounts of training data and compute [6, 19]. Furthermore, the fast pace of progress in LLM development means that new LLMs are often published that outperform older versions, leading to a situation where fine‐tuned LLMs are quickly made redundant by new LLMs that outperform earlier versions without fine‐tuning.

With these considerations in mind, we developed PK‐CSR.Gen, a generative AI methodology that relies on in‐context learning with dynamically mapped few‐shot examples orchestrated in a hierarchical chained LLM architecture to ensure adherence to established CSR structure, tone, and analytical conventions, while minimizing computational overhead and eliminating the need for fine‐tuning [6]. The proposed solution produces PK sections of CSRs directly from the study's protocol and TLFs saved as PDF documents or HTML tables. We also built a graphical user interface and added an optional mode that we call “Copilot” (not to be confused with the Microsoft software) that stops at pre‐defined checkpoints and awaits human input. The main objective was developing a solution that scales efficiently across different study types, is easy to use with minimal human effort, and has the potential to reduce manual authoring time while maintaining high reporting quality with only a handful of training examples.

Methods

Overall System Architecture

PK‐CSR.Gen is designed as a two‐stage pipeline: (i) Parsing and Data Preparation and (ii) Draft Generation. A flowchart overview of the algorithmic workflow is shown in Figure 1. The first stage transforms archived, final approved CSRs into a set of structured paragraph objects with mappings to the specific TLF reports they are referencing or describing. This serves as a reusable knowledge base for few‐shot in‐context learning used when generating new reports. The second stage generates new PK report drafts from a study's protocol and TLFs by dynamically retrieving matched examples. This architecture deliberately avoids model fine‐tuning, favoring in‐context learning to ensure scalability, reduce computational overhead, and simplify maintenance as models evolve.

Pipeline overview. (a) Parsing of past CSRs. This parsing workflow segments the report into paragraph objects which are classified into categories, for example, main text or figure title, and mapped to the figures or tables in the report they are describing. The figures and tables in the report are in turn mapped to their respective TLF file. (b) Generation of a new PK‐CSR report. The study protocol is loaded, and a report outline is generated. The proposed figures and tables in the report outline are mapped to TLF files of the study, and these TLF files are mapped to similar TLFs in the parsed example studies. Paragraphs from past studies describing these similar TLFs are retrieved. The report is then generated using these mapped examples from past parsed studies. The program stops and await user input at steps marked with [checkpoint] in the “Copilot” mode. The program runs end‐to‐end without stopping in “Auto” mode.

Parsing and Data Preparation

Figure 1a shows an overview of the parsing workflow. Final approved CSRs, study protocols, and TLFs were sourced from internal repositories and standardized to address formatting inconsistencies, for example, heading normalization, Optical Character Recognition (OCR) conversion of TLF PDFs for machine readability, styling harmonization of objects like figures titles, captions, and table labels. The CSRs used were relative bioavailability (rBA) or drug–drug interaction (DDI) studies [20, 21]; the same workflow was used for both, but the matched examples were limited to the same study types. The example CSRs were sourced opportunistically from colleagues within the organization by requesting recently approved PK reports for rBA and DDI studies. No formal selection criteria beyond study type and availability of the complete report package (final approved CSR, study protocol, and source TLFs) were applied. This pragmatic approach reflects real‐world conditions where the pool of accessible reports may be limited.

The report documents (in docx format) were loaded into a parsing script where the tables were replaced with delimited text and figure images with descriptions. We used OpenAI's GPT‐4o model for this image‐to‐text description task [22]. These conversions turned the report into one long text. The text was then segmented into paragraph objects. Each paragraph object was then categorized into one of six categories: heading (with heading level), main‐text paragraph, figure title, TLF, figure caption, and table title. This segmentation and subsequent categorization were rule‐based to ensure deterministic classification.

Next, the paragraphs that were categorized as main‐text or figure captions were processed to find which TLFs they were referencing or describing. For figure captions, this is done heuristically based on their placement: a pointer walks backwards from the figure caption and adds all TLF paragraph objects until it reaches a paragraph object that is not a TLF object. This is done because some figure captions describe multiple figures. For main text paragraphs, the paragraph‐to‐TLF mapping was done using LLM calls in a self‐consistency arbitration approach: two passes with a lightweight reasoning model (o1‐mini), followed by escalation to a higher‐capacity model (o1) in cases of disagreement [23, 24]. This mapping distinguishes between referenced and described TLFs, reflecting CSR conventions where figures are often referenced without detailed description, while tables are typically both referenced and described, albeit not necessarily in the same paragraph.

Figures and tables in the report were linked to their respective source documents through filename matching and standardized titles using few‐shot prompting with the o1 model [22]. Full TLF titles were then extracted from the mapped source documents to enable cross‐study equivalence mapping. The output of this stage, a structured set of paragraph objects and TLF associations, forms the foundation for dynamic example retrieval used for in‐context learning during generation.

Draft Generation Pipeline

Figure 1b shows an overview of the generation workflow. For a new study, the generation pipeline begins by summarizing the study protocol and generating a report outline. This outline specifies section headings, figure/table placement, and TLF references, and is constructed by matching the study to prior examples of similar type (e.g., rBA or DDI) using protocol‐outline pairs. The TLF references are then matched to the available TLF source documents for the study. The TLF source documents are in turn matched to similar‐type TLFs in other studies in the parsed training data bank, for example, PK parameter description tables or concentration‐time plot figures. Because the TLF documents from other studies were mapped to paragraph objects in the CSRs of their respective studies in the parsing workflow, we can dynamically retrieve relevant examples of main text paragraphs and figure captions for use in few‐shot prompting in the generation workflow. Workflow Modes

A graphical user interface accessible in a web application was developed. The workflow supported two operational modes: Copilot mode: Interactive generation with predefined checkpoints (see Figure 1) for SME review and iterative refinement. There were two checkpoints: the first after report outline generation, where the report author can give feedback after which the report outline will be regenerated based on the user input. The second checkpoint was after the report had been generated, after which the report author can give feedback and regenerate specific paragraphs. Auto mode: End‐to‐end automated draft generation. Users upload the protocol and TLFs, select study type (rBA or DDI), and the report will be generated without awaiting author feedback at checkpoints.

Models and Inference Parameters

LLM processing was done using the OpenAI models GPT‐4o, o1‐mini, o1, and o3 accessible through the OpenAI API (Application Programming Interface) [6, 22, 24, 25]. All LLM calls were made at zero temperature. For o3, medium reasoning effort was used. Figure 1 outlines which models were used in which steps in the workflow process.

Prompt Engineering

The prompt engineering strategy employed a modular design with 21 task‐specific prompt templates organized into five functional categories: (1) document structure prompts for report outlining and protocol summarization, (2) TLF identification and cross‐study matching prompts, (3) paragraph generation prompts for main text descriptions, references, figure captions, and conclusions, (4) TLF‐to‐paragraph mapping prompts with arbitration for disagreements, and (5) table processing and standardization prompts. Each prompt was designed as a Jinja2 template with defined input variables enabling dynamic content injection. Few‐shot examples were dynamically populated based on TLF similarity mappings rather than being hard‐coded. All prompt templates are provided in Table S1. Context window limits were managed implicitly through the hierarchical architecture, which segments the overall task into focused subtasks, each with a targeted prompt and only the most relevant context. For large documents, text was preprocessed and summarized to fit within model context limits.

Evaluation Design and Outcomes

Evaluation of the generated reports happened in two batches: one focused on rBA studies and the other on DDI studies. Evaluation was done by clinical pharmacologists and pharmacometricians, the SMEs who led the authoring of PK reports. Reviewers were given both AI‐generated reports and the final approved CSRs without being told which report was which; the evaluation was therefore blinded. Raters were instructed to grade reports in two Likert scale assessments (see Table 1): Accuracy (1–5) and Required Content Change (1–5) [26]. They were also free to give any free text feedback. rBA evaluation: Three reports were reviewed by six raters; 10% trimmed mean scores computed across sections (Results, Discussion, Conclusion) and raters. DDI evaluation: Four reports reviewed by 15 raters; 10% trimmed means were computed across rates (reviewers were only asked to grade the report in its entirety, not by section in this iteration).

Results

Figure 3 presents pooled grading results across studies. For DDI reports, the 10% trimmed mean accuracy scores were 4.36 (SE = 0.145) for AI‐written and 4.73 (SE = 0.134) for SME‐written reports (ratio = 92%). Content change scores were 4.00 (SE = 0.129) for AI and 4.82 (SE = 0.116) for SME (ratio = 83%). In rBA studies, accuracy scores were 4.50 (SE = 0.118) for AI and 4.86 (SE = 0.094) for SME (ratio = 93%), while content change scores were 4.07 (SE = 0.151) for AI and 4.71 (SE = 0.121) for SME (ratio = 86%). Issues raised in free text feedback by reviewers can largely be organized into four main categories: (i) Formatting errors, which accounted for the majority of reported issues; (ii) terminology inconsistencies; (iii) semantic miscalibrations, where word choices, while not factually incorrect, did not align with domain conventions; and (iv) contextual inaccuracies, where the model inferred context not explicitly stated in the source data. Formatting issues were mostly noted for PK parameters that use subscript, for example, Cmax and Tmax. While the system correctly formatted these parameters in most cases by character matching, LLM output would eventually produce new variants that were not caught in the character matching step, for example, producing “C_(max)” instead of “Cmax” or “C_max”. Some inconsistences across the paragraphs in the reports were also highlighted by the reviewers, for example, analytes being referred to by their full names in some paragraphs and acronyms in others. Semantic miscalibrations mainly centered on suboptimal word choices like using the word “moderate” instead of “small” for a 9% decrease in Cmax. The contextual inaccuracies were generally minor, for example, mentioning “pre‐specified” bioequivalence criteria when this criterion was based on common practice found in earlier reports rather than being specified in the study protocol or statistical analysis plan, and hence not “pre‐specified”.

Regarding the self‐consistency arbitration strategy used for paragraph‐to‐TLF mapping, approximately 25% of mapping tasks resulted in disagreement between the two o1‐mini passes, triggering escalation to the o1 model for final arbitration. This escalation rate reflects the inherent ambiguity in mapping text paragraphs to tables and figures presenting overlapping data and determining whether a paragraph describes versus merely references a given TLF—a distinction that is nuanced even for human readers.

Discussion

PK reporting demands strict adherence to stylistic conventions, analytic rigor, and precise linkage to TLFs. Conventional zero‐shot prompting rarely meets these requirements, while fine‐tuning approaches generally require large, continuously updated corpora. Our method offers a pragmatic alternative: dynamic few‐shot in‐context learning anchored by TLF‐aware mappings and structured paragraph objects, preserving institutional tone and analytical structure without retraining. The arbitration strategy (dual o1‐mini passes with escalation to o1) minimized mapping errors at low computational cost, and the deliberate division of labor across models—o3 for structured referencing and conclusions, o1 for instruction‐sensitive narrations, and GPT‐4o for multimodal captioning—enhanced robustness and style fidelity.

Reviewer feedback confirmed that most edits were stylistic rather than substantive. The feedback could largely be divided into four categories: formatting errors, terminology inconsistencies, semantic miscalibrations, and contextual inaccuracies. While formatting errors can be amended by improved deterministic character filtering or LLM standardization, terminology inconsistencies, semantic miscalibrations, and contextual inaccuracies could likely all be addressed by a post‐processing agent that reads the study protocol and statistical analysis plan along with the report draft in their entirety—this is made possible by the greatly expanded context windows of newer models like Claude Opus 4.6 with a context window of up to 1 M tokens. Adaptive correction agents that learn from SME edits are also a feasible approach. Implementing automated numeric concordance checks and terminology glossaries could further reduce manual intervention.

Despite promising results, limitations include the modest sample size, single‐organization style guide, and evaluation restricted to rBA and DDI studies. Study types with unique TLF structures or less standardized reporting patterns may require specialized narrative modules. Broader validation across diverse Phase 1 designs, for example, Single Ascending Dose (SAD) and First‐In‐Human (FIH) studies is also warranted. While the output is only meant as a first draft for SMEs to use as a starting point, governance considerations—traceability of generated text to source data, immutable logs, and documented human review—remain critical for adoption. Specifically, the validation relied on seven reports across two study types (rBA and DDI) from a single organization, which limits generalizability. While rBA and DDI studies have relatively standardized TLF structures and reporting conventions—which likely contributed to the high quality scores—more complex and variable study designs, such as First‐in‐Human oncology trials or multiple ascending dose studies, may present greater challenges due to less standardized TLF formats, more complex PK analyses, and more heterogeneous reporting patterns. The few‐shot approach may need to be augmented with study‐type‐specific narrative modules and larger example pools for such designs, and multi‐organization validation remains a critical next step.

Based on author experience, manual PK CSR authoring typically requires several hours to several days depending on report complexity, study design, and the number of TLFs involved. The average AI generation time of 18.2 min therefore represents a substantial reduction in initial drafting time. However, the AI‐generated output is intended as a first draft requiring SME review and refinement, and the total end‐to‐end time saving depends on the extent of revisions needed. A formal time‐motion study comparing AI‐assisted versus fully manual authoring workflows would be valuable for quantifying the precise efficiency gains. A formal usability study on the benefits of using Auto vs. Copilot mode would also be useful to evaluate the comparative gains and practical utility of these two workflows.

A key design principle of PK‐CSR.Gen is that all LLM interactions occur through API calls without model fine‐tuning, making model substitution straightforward. This was validated during development as the system transitioned between multiple OpenAI model versions (e.g., from gpt‐4o to o1‐mini to o1 to o3) as newer, more capable models became available. The same API‐based architecture could accommodate open‐source alternatives (e.g., Llama, Mistral) served through compatible inference endpoints, provided they offer sufficient reasoning and instruction‐following capability, thereby mitigating risks associated with dependence on any single model provider.

The current system does not include explicit mechanisms for detecting corrupted or missing TLF files, nor does it employ confidence scoring for individual outputs. However, the hierarchical system design combined with the few‐shot in‐context learning approach constrains the output space by anchoring generation to concrete examples from approved CSRs; the self‐consistency arbitration flags disagreements in TLF‐mapping tasks, providing a form of uncertainty detection; and the Copilot mode enables SME review at predefined checkpoints. Explicit error detection for input data quality and confidence scoring represent important areas for future development. Similarly, for dense or poorly formatted PDFs where OCR confidence is inherently low, the multimodal image interpretation capability (GPT‐4o) provides an alternative pathway by interpreting TLF images directly rather than relying on text extraction; however, integrating OCR confidence thresholds and automated quality checks on extracted data would improve robustness for such documents.

PK‐CSR.Gen is designed as an authoring aid that produces first drafts for SME review; the generated content is not intended for direct regulatory submission without expert review and approval. The system maintains traceability by operating from source TLFs and protocols, and the Copilot mode provides predefined checkpoints for human oversight. Institutional governance frameworks—including documented human review, audit trails, and immutable generation logs—are critical for responsible adoption. The ultimate responsibility for the accuracy and completeness of CSR content rests with the authoring scientists and the sponsoring organization, consistent with current regulatory expectations for AI‐assisted documentation.

Conclusions

This study demonstrated that a generative‐AI system leveraging a hierarchical, dynamic few‐shot in‐context learning workflow can produce PK CSR drafts that closely mirror SME‐authored reports using only a small set of examples and without fine‐tuning. In blinded evaluations across rBA and DDI studies, the approach achieved approximately 90% quality scores relative to the final SME‐written CSRs, demonstrating the potential of meaningful efficiency gains without compromising accuracy or adherence to established reporting conventions.
