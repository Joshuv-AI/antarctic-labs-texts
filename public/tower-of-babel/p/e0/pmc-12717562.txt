Effects of short-term sleep reduction and aerobic exercise on metabolism and inflammation in healthy adults
Authors: Gracie L. Dupuis, Jocelyn Kaveney, Eric Lessard, Braden Wilson, Graham R. McGinnis, Ryan K. Perkins
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The purpose of this study was to explore the effects of short-term sleep reduction on metabolic variables at rest and during acute aerobic (AE) exercise in healthy adults. This study followed a within subjects, repeated measures design. Following resting measures, recreationally active adults (n = 11; 5 female, 6 male; 26.4 ± 5.4 yrs) completed a 45 min AE challenge on a cycle ergometer at 70 % of their VO2max after 7 nights of habitual sleep (CON) and then 7 nights of sleep reduction (SR) (i.e., sleeping ∼3 h less per night). Carbohydrate and fat oxidation rate, and energy expenditure were assessed via indirect calorimetry. Soluble TLR4 (sTLR4) and IL6 in the blood was assessed via ELISA. Results show sleep was reduced 31 % (p ≤ 0.05) during the SR period (CON: 8.1 ± 0.3 h/night; SR: 5.4 ± 0.2 h/night). Sleep efficiency increased (+5 ± 2 %), while average number of awakenings (−42 ± 4 %) and sleep fragmentation index (−24 ± 8 %) decreased in SR (all p ≤ 0.05). SR increased resting carbohydrate oxidation (+45 ± 1 %), kcals from carbohydrates (+45 ± 1 %), and total kcal utilization (+23 ± 0 %) compared to CON (all p ≤ 0.05). Seated resting heart rate increased 5 ± 0 % (p ≤ 0.05), while mean arterial pressure decreased 3 ± 0 % (p ≤ 0.05) following SR. Resting circulating sTLR4 and IL6 remained unchanged following SR (p > 0.05). IL6 increased significantly after AE in both CON (+161 ± 68 %) and SR (+133 ± 37 %), with no difference between groups, while sTLR4 remained unchanged (p > 0.05). Metabolic and cardiovascular responses during exercise were not different between CON and SR (p > 0.05). Average RPE during AE was higher in SR than CON (+3 ± 0 %, p ≤ 0.05). These findings show that short-term sleep reduction influences resting metabolic and cardiovascular function.

Introduction

Quality and quantity of sleep (i.e. proper sleep hygiene) is crucial for the function of metabolic pathways, hormonal regulation, inflammation, and the cardiovascular system (Medic et al., 2017; Kim et al., 2015; Brandenberger and Weibel, 2004; Koop and Oster, 2021; Lamon et al., 2021). Due to the importance of these processes, the American Academy of Sleep Medicine and Sleep Research Society recommend adults sleep between 7 and 9 h per night to achieve optimal health (Watson et al., 2015). Despite these recommendations, 32 % of U.S adults do not achieve 7–9 h of sleep per night (C. f. D. C. a. Prevention). While 68 % of adults do meet nightly sleep recommendations, 25 % report experiencing poor sleep (i.e., sleep quality) (N. S. Foundation).

Toll-like receptors are pattern recognition receptors that regulate the inflammatory environment in many tissues via NFκB (Moresco et al., 2011; Kawai and Akira, 2011). One night of sleep reduction (i.e. 4 h of sleep) in healthy adults significantly increased mononuclear cell NFκB activation (Irwin et al., 2009). A study on self-reported sleep parameters (n = 2221) found that individuals who chronically receive “insufficient” had significantly higher PBMC TLR4 gene expression than individuals that reported sleep habits as “sufficient” (Aho et al., 2016a). Although few interventions have investigated the effects of sleep reduction on the TLR4 system, select evidence indicates that five nights of reduced sleep upregulate leukocyte TLR4 gene expression in healthy men (Aho et al., 2016b).

TLR4 is a known regulator of substrate metabolism in muscle (Pillon and Krook, 2017; Frisard et al., 2010). Increased activation of the TLR4 pathway favors carbohydrate oxidation at rest rather than fat oxidation, and contributes to insulin resistance in skeletal muscle (Frisard et al., 2010; Kim and Sears, 2010). Sleep loss increases the binding of transcription factors located within the TLR4 pathway, indicating sleep reduction may lead to increased TLR4 expression and subsequent skeletal muscle metabolic dysregulation (Irwin et al., 2006, 2009; Carroll et al., 2015). Individuals that sleep 5–6 h per night exhibit higher HOMA-IR than individuals who meet the 7–8 h nightly sleep recommendations. Short-term sleep reduction (i.e., 2 weeks) resulted in a significant increase of carbohydrate oxidation, which may increase the risk of insulin resistance (Nedeltcheva et al., 2011). Similarly, sleep reduction in healthy individuals resulted in a 40 % reduction in glucose clearance, and a 30 % decrease in acute insulin response to glucose during an intravenous glucose tolerance test (Spiegel et al., 1999). However, it is commonly accepted that regular exercise improves insulin sensitivity and glucose tolerance (Smutok et al., 1993; Perseghin et al., 1996; Shojaee-Moradie et al., 2007; Moghetti et al., 2016; Röhling et al., 2016). 5 nights of sleep reduction (i.e., 4 h per night) significantly increased the plasma glucose response during an OGTT. However, 5 nights of sleep reduction combined with 3 high-intensity interval training sessions attenuated the impairment in glucose response caused by sleep loss. (Saner et al., 2021). Similarly, individuals who report insufficient sleep and a sedentary lifestyle have a 1.41 greater risk of developing metabolic syndrome than those who report chronic insufficient sleep but an active lifestyle (Chou et al., 2023).

Preliminary studies show that exercise may decrease TLR4 activity through its proteolytic cleavage by A disintegrin and metalloprotease 10 (ADAM10) (Iwami et al., 2000; Langjahr et al., 2014; Koo et al., 2017). While reduced sleep shifts substrate metabolism, a reduction in TLR4 on the membrane might mitigate this effect and therefore regulate metabolism function (Frisard et al., 2010; Perkins et al., 2023). However, sTLR4 has not yet been examined in the context of reduced sleep or acute exercise. Therefore, the purpose of this study was to explore the effects of short-term reduction in sleep on metabolic and cardiovascular variables at rest and during aerobic exercise in healthy adults. We hypothesized that sleep reduction would result in an increased reliance on carbohydrate metabolism at rest and during aerobic exercise. Secondarily, we explored the effects of sleep reduction and aerobic exercise on circulating sTLR4. We hypothesized sleep reduction would impair an exercise-induced increased in circulating sTLR4 levels.

Materials and methods

Subjects

Healthy participants (n = 11; 5 female and 6 male), between the ages of 18 and 40 (26.4 ± 5.4 yrs) were recruited and completed this research study (Fig. 1). To determine eligibility, potential subjects completed a series of questionnaires aiming to assess overall health and current sleep hygiene. Eligibility to participate included: achieving recommended hours of sleep per night (i.e. 7–9 h) (Watson et al., 2015), lack of current sleep disorders (e.g. insomnia, sleep apnea), being free from acute or chronic illness (e.g. CVD, cancer, diabetes) and orthopedic limitations, nonsmokers/no use of other forms of tobacco, and recreationally active as stated by the ACSM guidelines (i.e. moderate intensity for 30 min 5 times per week or vigorous intensity for 20 min 3 times a week) (ACSM). All subjects provided written and verbal consent to participate and privacy of all participants has been observed. Study overview and testing procedures

This study followed a within subjects, repeated measures design, with an initial control period followed by an experimental period (Fig. 2). This approach has been used in previous sleep studies and served as a general template for this study design (Irwin et al., 2009; Carroll et al., 2015; Cedernaes et al., 2016). In total, participants reported to the laboratory on three occasions, each of which was in the fasted state (i.e., no food intake within the past 10 h), having not exercised for 48 h, and having not consumed alcohol or caffeine for 12 h. The first laboratory visit consisted of a screening process. After screening was complete, participants began the control period (CON) in which they were asked to resume their regular daily life including following normal sleep (i.e., 7–9 h per night) and activity habits for the next 7 days. In addition, though not directly controlled or monitored, participants were also instructed to continue their routine dietary habits over the course of the study. After the control period, participants reported to the laboratory for visit 1, which served as post-testing for the control period. Following the completion of the control period, participants began the sleep reduction intervention portion (SR). During SR, participants were instructed to reduce their sleep by staying awake 3 h later per night while waking up at the same time for 7 days (i.e., targeting ∼5 h of sleep). After the 7-day SR period, participants reported to the laboratory for follow up testing in visit 2.Fig. 2Overall timeline and measurements taken during each study visit. Screening visit measures included completion of five questionnaires assessing health history, exercise and sleep habits. Three questionnaires were used to assess different aspects of sleep history and habits: Munich Chronotype Questionnaire (MCTQ), Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI), and Morning-Eveningness Questionnaire (MEQ). The MCTQ allowed participants to self-report their preferred timing of sleep and wakefulness on workdays and free days (Santisteban et al., 2018). The PSQI assessed overall sleep quality by providing questions regarding sleep duration, sleep latency, sleep disturbances, any tiredness or lack of motivation throughout the day and use of medication to aid sleep (Divani et al., 2022). The MEQ assessed where participants fell on a scale ranging from definite morning type to definite evening type by asking a range of hypothetical questions about sleep and performance of specific activities (Burgess et al., 2018). Questionnaires were used to ensure participants achieved greater than 7 h of sleep per night, exhibited normal sleep behaviors, had minimal disruptions that could impact sleep quality according to the PSQI score (i.e. a score of ≤5), and fell within moderate morning to moderate evening on the MEQ scale.

Questionnaires were followed by height, weight, a body composition assessment via dual X-ray absorptiometry (Horizon DXA System, Hologic, Marlborough, MA) and a maximal cardiovascular fitness assessment (VO2max). VO2max was assessed using a stationary cycle ergometer (Monark, LC 7TT). Participants began cycling at 50 W for the initial 2 min of testing, then workload increased by 20 W each minute until volitional fatigue was reached. Expired air was collected and measured via indirect calorimetry (PARVO Medicis metabolic cart, Sandy, UT, USA). Rating of perceived exertion (RPE) (Williams, 2017) and heart rate (Polar H10 Heart Rate Sensor and Kubios HRV Scientific Software ver. 4.1.2.) (Tarvainen et al., 2014) were assessed in the second minute of each 2 min stage. VO2max was determined in accordance with ACSM criteria (Beltz et al., 2016).

Visits 1 and 2 followed the same structure with measures of seated resting heart rate and blood pressure, and fingerstick assessments. Prior to exercise, heart rate and blood pressure were assessed during the final minute of a 5 min seated rest period. Fingerstick blood assessments were performed to obtain circulating glucose (Contour next ONE), cholesterol (cobas, Accutrend Plus), and hemoglobin and hematocrit (EKF Diagnostics STANBIO, HemoPoint H2).

Prior to exercise testing, a basal blood draw was performed via venipuncture in the median cubital vein into serum separator tubes (SST) and ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA)-coated vacutainers. During visits 1 and 2, participants completed a 45 min acute submaximal aerobic exercise challenge on a cycle ergometer at a workload corresponding to 70 % of their VO2max. This exercise stimulus was selected because of its regular demonstration in eliciting a robust inflammatory response, and when performed regularly leads to improvements in metabolic and cardiovascular system function (Kanaley et al., 2022; Piercy et al., 2018; Moritz et al., 2021) Participants rested on the cycle ergometer for 3 min prior to exercise. Resting measures including heart rate, RPE, lactate (nova biomedical, lactate plus) and indirect calorimetry were obtained during the final minute of rest. Heart rate and RPE were recorded during the last minute of every 5 min period, while lactate was recorded every 15 min. Indirect calorimetry was monitored for 5 min every 15 min of the exercise test. The last minute of the 5 min period was averaged to ensure participants reached their target steady state VO2. The VO2 and VCO2 readings were used to calculate carbohydrate and fat oxidation rates along with energy expenditure derived from carbohydrates and fats (Cheneviere et al., 2010; Frayn, 1983). Indirect calorimetry was also assessed to ensure participants were maintaining 70 % of their VO2max. If the participant's measured VO2 was not within ±5 % of their target VO2, then workload was subsequently adjusted. This workload was replicated for each participant following the SR period during visit 2 (10 of 11 participants). One participant was unable to maintain the assigned workload following the SR period due to fatigue and workload was reduced to match the workload in visit 1 (i.e., 12.2 min earlier). The AE challenge was performed in a thermoneutral environment with convection from airflow. Water was provided ad libitum. A second blood draw was performed 30 min post exercise in the seated position to allow for plasma volume shifts to occur (Dill and Costill, 1974). EDTA tubes were immediately placed on ice and centrifuged at 4 °C and 3000 rpm for 10 min. SST tubes sat at room temperature for 30 min to allow for blood to clot, then were centrifuged at 20 °C, and 3500 rpm for 10 min. After each tube was centrifuged, the plasma/serum was transferred via pipet into cryogenic tubes and stored at −80 °C. Total plasma sTLR4 (MBS167606, MyBioSource) and serum IL6 (D6005B, R&D Systems) concentrations were measured according to manufacturer guidelines via enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA).

Sleep monitoring

Sleep habits were assessed during the CON and SR periods using daily sleep diary entry (Consensus Sleep Diary) to characterize when participants got into bed, attempted to fall asleep, woke up, etc. Secondly, sleep was indirectly assessed using wrist actigraphy (wGT3X-BT) in a subset of participants (6 of 11) due to technical limitations, enabling verification of total sleep duration. Total sleep time (TST), sleep latency (i.e. time to sleep), sleep efficiency (i.e. percentage of time spent asleep while in bed), number of awakenings during sleep, average length of awakenings during sleep, and sleep fragmentation index (i.e. sleep disruption) were recorded.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed via GraphPad Prism version 10.4.2 (GraphPad Software, Boston, Massachusetts USA). Pre vs post exercise responses in sTLR4 and IL6 were compared using a repeated measures analysis of variance (2x2 ANOVA). Differences in resting metabolic and cardiovascular function between CON and SR were compared using a one-tailed paired t-test. Physiological responses (e.g., heart rate, RPE) were averaged during the acute exercise session and compared between CON and SR using one-tailed paired t-tests. Pearson's correlations were used for correlative analysis. Significance was set at p ≤ 0.05. Values are expressed as mean ± SE.

Sleep data

Mean total sleep time (TST), measured via Consensus Sleep Diary (CSD), was reduced by 31 % (p ≤ 0.05) from CON (8.1 ± 0.3 h/night) to SR (5.5 ± 0.0 h/night) and confirmed via actigraph (CON: 7.7 ± 0.3 h/night; SR: 5.4 ± 0.2 h/night). Based on actigraph-derived sleep measures, sleep latency remained unchanged from CON to SR (CON: 9.4 ± 3.6 min; SR: 4.8 ± 1.1 min; p > 0.05). Sleep efficiency increased by 5 % (CON: 87.2 ± 2.6 %; SR: 90.9 ± 1.5 %; p ≤ 0.05), while the average number of awakenings was reduced by 42 % (CON: 18.2 ± 2.6; SR: 10.4 ± 1.2; p ≤ 0.05). However, the average duration of awakenings during the night did not differ between conditions (CON: 2.7 ± 0.4 min; SR: 2.6 ± 0.5 min; p > 0.05). The actigraph-derived sleep fragmentation index also decreased by 24 % from CON to SR (CON: 26.4 ± 2.4 %; SR: 19.4 ± 1.3 %; p ≤ 0.05).

Resting cardiovascular measures

Resting seated heart rate increased 5 % in SR (CON: 72.5 ± 4.2 bpm; SR: 76.0 ± 4.5 bpm; p ≤ 0.05). Resting systolic blood pressure (CON: 115.3 ± 0.6 mmHg; SR: 113.7 ± 0.8 mmHg), diastolic blood pressure (CON: 69.5 ± 0.7 mmHg; SR: 67.5 ± 0.6 mmHg), pulse pressure (CON: 45.8 ± 0.6 mmHg; SR: 46.3 ± 0.8 mmHg), and O2 pulse (CON: 4.6 ± 0.4 mmHg; SR: 4.9 ± 0.5 mmHg) remained unchanged between CON and SR (all p > 0.05). However, mean arterial pulse pressure (MAP) was significantly lower after SR (CON: 84.7 ± 0.6 mmHg; SR: 82.9 ± 0.8 mmHg; p ≤ 0.05).

Discussion

The present study investigated the effect of short-term sleep reduction on metabolic variables at rest in healthy adults. The secondary aim of this study was to examine the effect of sleep reduction on metabolic responses during an acute aerobic exercise trial. We hypothesized that 7 nights of reduced sleep would increase reliance on carbohydrate metabolism at rest and during acute aerobic exercise. Our results indicated that sleep reduction increased resting carbohydrate oxidation rate, utilization of Kcals derived from carbohydrates, and total utilization of Kcals. Though metabolic variables remained unchanged during aerobic exercise, participants’ overall average rating of perceived exertion during the trial was significantly higher following sleep reduction. Additionally, sTLR4, a novel inflammatory marker included in this study, did not change following sleep reduction or in response to acute exercise.

To confirm participants adhered to the sleep protocol (i.e., resuming normal sleep of ∼8 h per night during CON; and staying up ∼3 h later per night during SR), participants were monitored in two ways (consensus sleep diary and actigraph monitoring). During the SR period total sleep time (TST) was reduced by 31 % as reported via consensus sleep diary (CSD) and confirmed via actigraph monitoring. Along with the reduction in sleep quantity, actigraph data provided information to support an increase in sleep quality during SR. In the current study, sleep reduction resulted in a 5 % increase in sleep efficiency, a 42 % reduction in average number of awakenings, and a 24 % decrease in sleep fragmentation index. This trend in improved sleep quality following SR is consistent with other similarly conducted sleep studies (Singh et al., 2023) (Wilckens et al., 2024).

Our findings indicated a short-term reduction in sleep is accompanied by a 45 % increase in resting carbohydrate oxidation, partially confirming our hypothesis. Resting energy expenditure from carbohydrates (+45 %), and total energy expenditure (+23 %) significantly increased following the sleep reduction period. However, resting fat oxidation rate and energy expenditure from fats remained unchanged in response to sleep reduction. The limited number of studies pertaining to sleep reduction and change in metabolic variables proves difficult to interpret due to the amount of discrepancy between them. More specifically, short term (i.e., 3–7 nights) sleep reduction (i.e., ∼3 h reduction per night) has been shown to increase resting carbohydrate oxidation rate (Nedeltcheva et al., 2011; Grant et al., 2022), have no effect of carbohydrate or fat oxidation rate (Singh et al., 2023), and increase resting fat oxidation rate (Rao et al., 2015). Three nights of fragmented sleep (i.e., being awoken during the night for ∼1 h) in healthy premenopausal women resulted in an increase in resting carbohydrate oxidation rate, increased RER, and a reduction in fat oxidation rate. (Grant et al., 2022). Two weeks of sleep reduction (i.e., sleeping 5.5 h/night; staying up ∼2 h later in the evening, and waking up ∼1 h earlier in the morning) in overweight/obese adults resulted in increased resting carbohydrate oxidation rate (Nedeltcheva et al., 2011). However, a 38 % reduction in sleep (i.e. staying awake 20 % later in the evening and waking up 20 % earlier in the morning) for 4 nights in postmenopausal women had no impact on resting carbohydrate and fat oxidation (Singh et al., 2023). Five nights of sleep reduction (i.e., 4 h in bed per night; staying awake 20 % later in the evening and waking up 20 % earlier in the morning) in healthy adults resulted in a significant increase in fat oxidation rate (Rao et al., 2015). Though it is clear sleep reduction influences metabolic factors at rest, differences in population and study protocols may lead to different outcomes. Normal nocturnal sleep (i.e., 7–9 h) is typically characterized by early-night sleep which is predominantly non rapid eye movement (NREM) sleep, and late night sleep which is predominantly rapid eye movement (REM) sleep (Wu et al., 2010; Markov and Goldman, 2006). NREM sleep (i.e. deep sleep) is characterized by a reduction in heart rate and blood pressure, regular breathing patterns, along with a peak in growth hormone secretion. While REM sleep is characterized by variable heart rate and breathing, along with decreased peripheral blood flow to tissues such as skeletal muscle (Feriante and Araujo, 2018). Due to these notable differences, variation in methodologies across sleep reduction studies may serve as an explanation for discrepancy among results. Given our cohort is a young active healthy population, it is noteworthy that reducing sleep for one-week resulted in a significant shift in substrate utilization.

A modest but significant increase in resting seated heart rate was found after sleep reduction. While systolic blood pressure, diastolic blood pressure and pulse pressure remained unchanged following sleep reduction, mean arterial pressure decreased. An increase in resting heart rate as a result of sleep reduction has commonly been reported across various other studies, though there is some disagreement with changes in blood pressure and mean arterial pressure (Sauvet et al., 2010; Grimaldi et al., 2016). One night of total sleep deprivation in healthy adults resulted in a significant increase in resting heart rate and systolic blood pressure (Sauvet et al., 2010). Eight nights of reduced sleep (i.e., staying up ∼3 h later than normal) resulted in an increased resting heart rate. In this same study, systolic blood pressure, diastolic blood pressure, and mean arterial pressure remained unchanged (Grimaldi et al., 2016). Though not measured in the current study, elevated cortisol is suggested to play a role in increased resting heart rate, potentially explaining the elevated heart rate accompanied by sleep reduction (De Vente et al., 2003; Floyd et al., 2007).

Few studies have examined the effect of sTLR4 following acute exercise, and no studies to date have explored the effect of sTLR4 in response to short-term sleep reduction. Multiple mechanisms are suspected to play a role in the proteolytic cleavage of TLR4 from the cell membrane, including ADAM10, which has been shown to increase in response to an acute bout of aerobic exercise (Iwami et al., 2000; Langjahr et al., 2014; Braley et al., 2016). TLR4 is a known regulator of substrate metabolism, and when expression of the TLR4 pathway is increased, resting carbohydrate oxidation is favored over fat oxidation (Pillon and Krook, 2017; Frisard et al., 2010; Kim and Sears, 2010). Despite this metabolic role, sTLR4 remained unchanged before and after the aerobic exercise challenge during the control and sleep reduction periods. Similarly, a study examining circulating sTLR4 in normal and abnormal glucose tolerance individuals found no significant differences following acute aerobic exercise in either group (Perkins et al., 2023). However, basal sTLR4 was 36 % lower in glucose intolerant individuals compared to their healthy counterparts, suggesting a potential link between chronic metabolic dysfunction and reduced sTLR4 (Perkins et al., 2023). In the present study, a significant correlation was observed between resting carbohydrate oxidation and the change in IL-6 following the aerobic exercise test under normal sleep conditions. This relationship was absent following sleep restriction, suggesting that short-term sleep loss may disrupt the normal coupling between metabolic state and inflammatory responsiveness. While sleep reduction had no measurable effect on circulating sTLR4 levels, the altered IL-6 response implies that metabolic-immune interactions may still be impaired, potentially through mechanisms independent of sTLR4. Given that chronic sleep loss is associated with impaired glucose tolerance and increased inflammation, it is possible that more prolonged sleep disruption could influence sTLR4 levels over time (Medic et al., 2017).

Following resting measures, participants completed a 45 min acute aerobic cycle ergometer test at 70 % of their VO2max. While metabolic responses during this aerobic test were similar during the control and sleep reduction periods, a modest but significant increase in overall average rating of perceived exertion (RPE) was found. This increase in RPE appears to be driven by the second half of the aerobic exercise challenge. Studies examining the effects of sleep reduction on exercise performance and rating of perceived exertion have yielded differing results. One night of partial sleep deprivation in aerobically trained adults resulted in increased rating of perceive exertion and reduced performance during a 12 min self-paced aerobic exercise challenge (Souissi et al., 2020). However, one night of partial sleep reduction (i.e., 3 h of sleep) in aerobically trained men resulted in no change in rating of perceived exertion during a 20 min cycling time trial (Dean et al., 2023). While participants completed the same exercise trial during the CON and SR periods, the perceived exertion of that effort was significantly higher following sleep reduction. This indicates sleep reduction increases feelings of fatigue and difficulty during exercise.

While many interesting findings were noted, this study is not without limitations. While sleep was not directly supervised, sleep was tracked via two commonly utilized methods (i.e., CSD and actigraph monitoring). Though we have high confidence in the TST reported by participants and confirmed via actigraph monitoring we cannot conclusively determine when participants were sleeping. Due to technological limitations, actigraph data was only acquired from 6 out of 11 participants. However, the actigraph data and CSD entries from those 6 participants were similar, indicating the CSD entries accurately reported TST. Though sTLR4 appears to be influenced by exercise (Ducharme et al., 2023), it is still somewhat unknown the influence of mode, intensity, and duration on the effect of these responses. In the present study, sleep was reduced by ∼3 h in a relatively small, young, healthy population. Participants stayed up later in the evening but not altering their final wakeup time. Due to the discrepancy among common sleep reduction methodologies and in this type of population, our results may lack generalizability. Similarly, this study only investigated healthy young adults and therefore the result may not apply to certain clinical populations or age groups. Lastly, dietary intake was not assessed or monitored throughout the study. It is possible that eating habits changed during the SR period, which may affect metabolic function (Schmid et al., 2008; Broussard et al., 2016; Klok et al., 2007; Horton et al., 1995).

In summary, we aimed to investigate the impact of short-term sleep reduction on metabolic variables at rest and during aerobic exercise in young, healthy individuals. Partially supporting our hypothesis, we show that reducing sleep for only 7 days resulted in an increase carbohydrate oxidation rate, Kcals derived from carbohydrates, and total Kcal utilization at rest. These variables remained unchanged between the control and sleep reduction period in response to an acute bout of aerobic exercise. These findings suggest that even short-term sleep reduction may alter resting metabolic processes, which could have implications for athletes managing training and recovery, shift workers with irregular sleep schedules, and the general population in terms of energy balance and dietary planning. Future research should examine these effects in larger and more diverse populations, including older adults and clinical groups, while incorporating objective sleep measures such as polysomnography and stricter dietary control to better understand the metabolic consequences of sleep reduction.
