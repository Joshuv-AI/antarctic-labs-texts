Right Ventricular Reconstruction After Complete Excision of a Huge Cardiac Fibroma
Authors: Sheng Yang, Lanlin Zhang, Songbo Dong, Shangdong Xu, Jun Zheng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCardiac fibromas are rare, benign tumors predominantly seen in children, with few cases reported in young adults. Their management is extremely challenging and remains controversial, especially regarding giant cardiac fibroma.Case SummaryA 26-year-old asymptomatic woman was incidentally diagnosed with a large (114 × 67 mm) right ventricular fibroma via comprehensive imaging evaluation. She underwent successful complete surgical excision and right ventriculoplasty. Histopathology confirmed fibroma. Her postoperative recovery was uneventful, and over a 3-year follow-up period, she remained asymptomatic with no tumor recurrence and preserved right ventricular function.ConclusionsComplete resection of large cardiac fibromas is feasible with meticulous reconstruction, preserving function.

History of Presentation

A 26-year-old asymptomatic Asian female with no prior episodes of chest discomfort, syncope, or cyanosis was incidentally found to have a right ventricular (RV) mass during routine transthoracic echocardiography. The patient was physically active and reported no cardiorespiratory symptoms. On admission, vital signs were within normal limits. Cardiac auscultation revealed no audible murmurs or abnormal heart sounds.Take-Home Messages•Complete surgical resection of large right ventricular fibromas can be safely achieved with meticulous planning and layered myocardial reconstruction, preserving cardiac function and preventing complications such as intramyocardial hematoma.•Multimodal imaging guides surgical strategy for optimal outcomes in complex cardiac tumors.

Past Medical History

The patient had no significant past medical or chronic disease history and no family history of cardiac disease or other genetic diseases.

Differential Diagnosis

The differential diagnoses considered rhabdomyoma and malignant cardiac tumors such as sarcoma. Rhabdomyoma was deemed less likely given the patient's age, the solitary lesion, and the lack of association with tuberous sclerosis. Sarcoma was considered unlikely, as the lesion lacked heterogeneous enhancement, myocardial infiltration, or extracardiac extension, and its metabolic activity was more consistent with benign cardiac tumors and lower than the typical threshold reported for malignant lesions (>2.5). A preoperative biopsy or intraoperative frozen section was not performed given the intramyocardial location, the large tumor size causing RV inflow tract compression, and the associated risks of bleeding, ventricular rupture, and arrhythmia.

Investigations

Initial electrocardiogram demonstrated sinus arrhythmia with complete right bundle branch block. Chest radiography revealed mild cardiomegaly without other structural abnormalities. Transthoracic echocardiography identified a large (114 × 67 mm), well-circumscribed, homogeneous mass arising from the RV free wall, causing moderate impairment of ventricular wall motion (Figure 1A). Subsequent cardiac computed tomography angiography (CTA) and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) confirmed an intramyocardial tumor with well-defined margins, surrounded by preserved normal myocardium. The mass demonstrated tissue density comparable to adjacent myocardium on CTA. Cardiac MRI demonstrated preserved biventricular systolic function despite significant extrinsic compression of the right ventricular inflow tract (Figures 1B to 1D). Fluorine-18 fluorodeoxyglucose positron emission tomography–computed tomography (18F-FDG PET-CT) revealed mildly increased metabolic activity within the lesion (SUVmax: 2.2) (Figures 1E and 1F). Comprehensive imaging evaluation showed no evidence of invasion into surrounding cardiac structures or extracardiac extension. Management

The patient underwent open heart surgery via standard median sternotomy. Upon entry, approximately 300 mL of yellow-tinged pericardial effusion was noted. Cardiopulmonary bypass was initiated by cannulating the ascending aorta and superior and inferior vena cava. A firm, grayish-white tumor extensively occupied the anterior and diaphragmatic walls of the RV, with only minimal residual contractility observed in the overlying myocardium. Transtricuspid inspection revealed near-total obliteration of the RV cavity and significant inflow tract compression, though the main pulmonary artery and right atrium remained uninvolved. The mass was intramyocardial in location, encased between attenuated layers of ventricular myocardium. The epicardium was incised at its thinnest portion overlying the tumor (Figure 2A). The tumor was completely excised, leaving a crateriform defect in the anteroinferior RV wall while maintaining ventricular cavity integrity. Meticulous hemostasis was performed, with careful attention to ligating severed coronary artery branches to prevent intramyocardial hematoma formation. Intermittent antegrade blood cardioplegia perfusion precisely identified untied vessels through leakage during perfusion.

Next, we restored RV integrity by carefully suturing the space between the 2 myocardial layers. The first continuous suture was placed 5 to 10 mm from the cavity margin, passing through both layers in one direction, with a second suture applied in the opposite direction. After knotting, the layers were approximated, and a second suture line was added 5 to 10 mm beyond the first to reinforce stability. Except for a small area of outer myocardial loss (Figure 2C), the ventricle wall was reconstructed. This defect was repaired with a trimmed autologous pericardial patch. Three interrupted mattress sutures further secured the pericardium to the myocardium. Each stitch passed from inside the ventricle through the inner myocardial layer and the pericardial patch, resulting in a stable and continuous ventricular wall (Figure 2D).

A thorough inspection confirmed the adjacent coronary artery, tricuspid valve apparatus, and papillary muscles remained uninvolved, with only tricuspid annular dilatation observed. After successful De Vega annuloplasty, cardiopulmonary bypass was discontinued, with spontaneous return of cardiac rhythm; no hematoma was observed at the repair site. Postoperative echocardiography demonstrated preserved cardiac function, with only expected regional hypokinesis at the tumor resection site.

Outcome and Follow-Up

The patient recovered well after surgery and was discharged 1 week postoperatively. The resected specimen appeared as a well-circumscribed, grayish-white elliptical mass weighing 390 g and measuring 15 × 10 × 7.5 cm (Figure 3A). Histology revealed dense collagen bundles with scattered fibroblasts, lacking atypia or mitoses. Immunohistochemistry showed vimentin positivity and negativity for desmin, SMA, S-100, and CD34, with a Ki-67 index <1%. This profile is typical of cardiac fibroma and distinguishes it from rhabdomyoma (vacuolated, glycogen-rich cells with muscle marker positivity) and sarcoma (higher cellularity, atypia, mitoses, and elevated Ki-67) (Figures 3B to 3D).Figure 3Analysis of the Resected Specimen(A) Macroscopic view of the excised mass, weighing 390 g and approximately 17 × 10 × 7.5 cm in size. At the 3-year postoperative follow-up, the patient had recovered well, fully returning to normal work and daily life, with no reports of palpitations, chest tightness, or shortness of breath. Contrast-enhanced chest CT and cardiac MRI indicated a well-defined cardiac contour, a well-shaped reconstructed RV, no contrast agent filling defects or abnormal soft tissue shadows on the enhanced scan, and no signs of tumor residue or recurrence on the myocardial delayed enhancement sequence (Figures 4A and 4B). The RV volume and systolic function were both normal (Figures 4C and 4D), and echocardiography showed a tricuspid annular plane systolic excursion (TAPSE) of 20 mm. Discussion

Primary cardiac tumors are exceptionally rare, with an estimated incidence of 0.0017% to 0.019%. Among these, benign tumors account for approximately 75% of cases.1 Cardiac fibromas represent the second most frequent benign cardiac tumor, predominantly affecting pediatric populations, with only about 15% of cases occurring in adolescents and adults.2 Although these tumors are more frequently associated with conduction abnormalities and cardiac dysfunction, their propensity for embolic events is relatively low.3 The optimal management of asymptomatic patients remains controversial.4 However, many clinicians advocate for elective surgical intervention, given that the potential benefits—particularly in mitigating the risks of ventricular arrhythmias and sudden cardiac death—outweigh the procedural risks.5,6 Complete surgical resection has been associated with favorable short- and long-term outcomes.7 In this report, we describe the case of a young asymptomatic woman who underwent successful complete excision of a large RV fibroma.

This case highlights not only the rarity of such tumors in adults but also the technical challenges associated with resecting a massive intramyocardial lesion while preserving ventricular function. Complete tumor resection was essential while minimizing damage to cardiac function. The outer myocardial layer overlying the mass was thin, with no observable contraction or diastolic movement during the cardiac cycle. To reduce myocardial injury, we carefully selected the incision site at the area where the tumor coverage was the thinnest, ensuring a balance between effective tumor removal and preservation of cardiac function.

Ventriculoplasty was required because of the resultant wall defect. The inner myocardial layer remained intact and was deemed sufficiently robust to support RV function. A critical challenge was preventing intramyocardial hematoma. In contrast to a case reported by Stamp and Larbalestier,8 where a Blake's drain was used to prevent hematoma formation and was removed after protamine reversal, our approach relied on secure layered reconstruction without the need for drainage.

We were initially concerned that excessive RV resection might result in postoperative right heart failure. However, prior studies have shown that even large RV fibromas (up to 114 × 67 mm) can be completely excised with meticulous technique, without impairing cardiac function.9 In our case, thorough hemostasis was first achieved at the dissection surface. We then used multiple concentric continuous sutures to integrate the myocardial layers securely. The remaining epicardial defect was repaired with a pericardial patch, followed by reinforcement using interrupted mattress sutures and a polyester patch. This method effectively prevented intramyocardial hematoma, as confirmed intraoperatively and on postoperative contrast-enhanced CT. At the 3-year follow-up, CT and MRI confirmed a well-shaped RV with preserved structure and function, and no evidence of tumor recurrence. Long-term follow-up is recommended for these patients to monitor ventricular function and detect recurrence. Transthoracic echocardiography is performed at 3, 6, and 12 months postoperatively, then annually. Cardiac MRI is obtained at 1 year and every 2 to 3 years thereafter, or earlier if clinically indicated.

In this case, the strategy of complete tumor resection combined with anatomical reconstruction of the RV was successfully implemented to treat a large cardiac fibroma. We hope this report contributes to a better understanding of the available treatment options for cardiac tumors, particularly RV fibromas, and provides valuable insights for future clinical management.
