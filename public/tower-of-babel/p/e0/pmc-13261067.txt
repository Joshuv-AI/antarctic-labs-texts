Structural insights into HSV-1 origin unwinding by the viral proteins UL9 and ICP8
Authors: Andrey G Baranovskiy, Lucia M Morstadt, Eduardo E Romero, Nigar D Babayeva, Tahir H Tahirov
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractHerpes simplex virus type 1 (HSV-1) causes lifelong infections in human cells and is associated with a range of diseases. HSV-1 DNA replication requires seven viral proteins, including the major DNA-binding protein ICP8, the origin-binding protein UL9, and proteins that comprise the helicase–primase and DNA polymerase complexes. UL9 functions as a DNA helicase that specifically recognizes and binds to the viral origins of replication, OriS and OriL. Here we report the cryo-EM structure of the UL9/ICP8/DNA/ATPγS complex at an overall resolution of 3.18 Å. This structure revealed that UL9 employs an α-helix to separate the DNA strands and captures the initial step of OriS unwinding, in which the C-terminal domain of UL9 specifically binds to the major groove of a DNA double helix, while the N-terminal helicase domain engages the unwound leading and lagging strands. ICP8 interacts with the extreme C-terminal region of UL9, preventing UL9 dimerization. Simultaneously, it binds and stabilizes the leading-strand DNA adjacent to UL9. Together, these findings provide mechanistic insight into UL9-driven DNA unwinding and the cooperative action of UL9 and ICP8 at HSV-1 replication origins, establishing a structural framework for the rational interpretation of prior biochemical data and for the design of new antiviral drugs.

Introduction

The herpes simplex virus 1 (HSV-1) is one of eight known human herpesviruses and is associated with a variety of diseases, including encephalitis and keratitis. It establishes lifelong infections in human cells with alternating cycles of latency and reactivation [1]. The HSV-1 genome is a linear 152-kbp double-stranded DNA (dsDNA) molecule that, after infection, circularizes within the nucleus, enabling rolling-circle replication [2, 3]. Seven essential replication factors have been identified, including the helicase–primase complex, the DNA polymerase complex, the single-stranded DNA (ssDNA) binding protein ICP8, and the origin-binding protein UL9 [4–6]. Viral DNA synthesis begins at three redundant origins of replication: OriL and two copies of OriS [7]. OriS contains a complex recognition system comprising three binding sites for UL9 (Box 1, Box 2, and Box 3), arranged in order of decreasing binding affinity and importance for viral replication [8, 9]. All three sites exhibit a specific spatial configuration, and two of them (Box 1 and Box 2) are required for initiation of DNA replication, which is preceded by ATP-dependent unwinding of OriS [10, 11].

UL9 is a 94 kDa protein with a modular architecture and belongs to superfamily 2 (SF2) of DNA/RNA helicases [12–14]. UL9 consists of two main domains: an N-terminal helicase domain (NTD, residues 1–532) and a C-terminal domain (CTD, residues 540–851), connected by a flexible linker (Fig. 1A). The helicase domain contains two conserved subdomains, 1A and 2A (residues 1–241 and 242–532, respectively) and exhibits ATPase and helicase activities [3, 15–17]. The CTD includes a region (residues 540–740) that interacts with the helicase domain, as well as two functional subdomains (Fig. 1A): the dsDNA-binding domain (dsDBD, residues 743–810) [18–20] and the ICP8-binding domain (IBD, residues 818–851) [21, 22]. Under physiological conditions, UL9 forms a head-to-tail dimer that binds double-stranded OriS [10, 11, 16, 23].

ICP8 is the major ssDNA-binding protein of HSV-1 and has a molecular mass of 128 kDa (Fig. 1A). X-ray crystallographic analysis revealed that ICP8 has a large NTD and a small CTD connected by a flexible linker [24]. The NTD of ICP8 comprises two subdomains: a head subdomain formed by residues 547–650 and 776–912 and a body subdomain formed by the remaining NTD residues. The CTD plays a role in ICP8 oligomerization and filament formation and contains a helical bundle followed by a disordered tail of approximately 60 amino acids [25, 26]. ICP8 exhibits an approximately fivefold higher affinity for ssDNA than for dsDNA and binds ssDNA cooperatively, with maximal activity observed at 150 mM salt [27].

It has been known for decades that ICP8 is required for ATP-dependent unwinding of the origin of DNA replication by UL9 [16, 28–30]. However, the mechanism underlying the cooperative action of these proteins has remained unclear. Here we present the cryogenic electron microscopy (cryo-EM) structure of the UL9/ICP8/OriS complex at the initial stage of OriS unwinding, which reveals cooperative binding of the leading strand by ICP8 and UL9.

Materials and methods

DNA and ATPγS substrates

Oligonucleotides representing the HSV-1 leading strand (40-mer 5′-CGAAGCGTTCGCACTTCGTCCCAATCTAACAATATCAATA; complementary part is underlined; the OriS Box 1 is in italic) and the lagging strand (31-mer 5′-ATACAATCAAGGACGAAGTGCGAACGCTTCG) were obtained from IDT Inc. The DNA fork structure, containing a 21-bp duplex and 19/10-nt 3′/5′-flaps, was prepared at 150 μM concentration in the buffer containing 20 mM HEPES, pH 7.5, 100 mM NaCl, 1 mM ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid, by heating the oligonucleotides for 1 min at 90°C and gradual cooling to room temperature for 1 h. ATPγS was obtained from Jena Bioscience (Germany).

Protein expression and purification

The genes encoding full-length HSV-1 ICP8 (UniProt: P04296) and UL9 (UniProt: P10193) were codon-optimized for expression in bacterial cells, synthesized by GenScript, and cloned into pASHSUL-1 plasmid [31] to have a cleavable N-terminal His-Sumo tag. ICP8 and UL9 were expressed in Escherichia coli strain BL21 (DE3) at 16°C for 15 h following induction with 0.3 µg/ml anhydrotetracyclin. Afterward, cells were harvested by centrifugation at 4000 × g for 15 min, aliquoted, and kept at −80°C. ICP8 purification scheme includes chromatography on a Ni-IDA column (Bio-Rad), His-Sumo tag digestion during overnight incubation on ice, and two additional chromatographic steps using SP HiTrap and then Heparin HP HiTrap columns (Cytiva). UL9 was purified in a similar way, except the order of Heparin and SP HiTrap columns was reversed. The purity of samples was analyzed by sodium dodecyl sulphate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (Supplemental Fig. S1). Protein concentrations were estimated by measuring the absorbance at 280 nm and using the extinction coefficients calculated with ProtParam [32]. Protein purification details are provided in the Supplementary Methods.

Cryo-EM sample preparation, data collection, and processing

The UL9/ICP8/DNA complex was prepared by mixing stoichiometric amounts of pure proteins and a forked DNA in a buffer containing 20 mM HEPES–Na, pH 7.5, 150 mM NaCl, 1.5% glycerol, and 1 mM DL-Dithiothreitol (DTT), and sample concentrating to ∼7.5 mg/ml. The holey carbon copper support electron microscopy grids (C-Flat 1.2/1.3, 300 mesh) were glow-discharged for 15 s at 15 mA negative current (PELCO easiGlow) before use. CHAPSO, MgCl2, and ATPγS were added to the complex at the final concentration of 3, 2, and 2 mM, respectively, before depositing a 2 µl sample at the protein concentration of 5 mg/ml onto the glow-discharged grid. The grids were blotted for 3 s at 5°C with a blot force of 3 and at 100% relative humidity and plunged frozen into liquid ethane using a Vitrobot Mark IV (Thermo Fisher Scientific). A total of 25 508 EER raw movies were acquired, using a 200 keV Glacios fringe-free and energy-filtered Cryo-transmission electron microscope equipped with a Falcon 4i direct electron detector using the EPU automated acquisition software (Thermo Fisher Scientific) with ‘‘Faster Acquisition’’ mode (AFIS) enabled. A slit width of 10 eV was used with the Selectris energy filter, along with a 20 µm condenser aperture allowing the collection of three shots per hole. Data were collected at an effective pixel size of 0.72 Å/pixel, a defocus range from −0.8 to −2.6 µm, and an exposure time of 3.14 s with a total dose of 60.0 e /Å2.

The data processing pipeline outlined here is schematized in Supplemental Fig. S2. Data were processed using CryoSPARC (ver. 4.7) [33]. The movies were aligned via patch motion correction, and their contrast transfer function (CTF) parameters were estimated using the patch CTF estimation. A total of 3332 341 particles were extracted from 22 312 curated micrographs using the blob- and template-based particle-picking approaches. One round of 2D classification and several rounds of ab initio 3D reconstruction and heterogeneous refinement were conducted to clean the dataset from the broken and low-quality particles. The obtained particles were re-extracted with a Box size of 322 Å and 1.6× binning and underwent additional cleaning, yielding 411 805 particles. 3D variability analysis revealed notable relative movement between UL9 and ICP8 (Supplemental Movies 1–3).

Soft masks for UL9/DNA and ICP8/DNA were created in UCSF ChimeraX (ver. 1.8.1) [34] and used for particle subtraction and local refinement. Using the density-subtracted particles for UL9/DNA, local refinement resulted in the reconstruction at 3.0 Å for ICP8/DNA. Using the density-subtracted particles for ICP8/DNA, the local refinement generated a reconstruction for UL9/DNA at 3.17 Å. Local maps were combined in Phenix (ver. 1.21.2) [35] using the consensus map as a guide.

Model building and refinement

The initial model was built using AlphaFold 3-generated structures of UL9 and ICP8 [36] as starting models, which were fitted into the final cryo-EM map using ChimeraX. Individual UL9 and ICP8 domains were placed independently into the density, and substantial rebuilding of the UL9 structure was required. Therefore, model rebuilding and DNA placement were performed using Coot [37]. Poor quality of EM density for UL9 regions 1–19 and 266–282 and ICP8 regions 1–7, 293–305, and 1040–1196 precluded unambiguous placement of the corresponding residues. After several rounds of manual model building, the structure was refined using the Phenix “real-space refinement” module [35]. Refinement and validation statistics are provided in Supplemental Table S1. ChimeraX (ver. 1.8.1) and PyMol Molecular Graphics System (version 2.6, Schrödinger, LLC) were used for figure preparation. Except for the experimental cryo-EM maps shown in Fig. 1A and Supplemental Fig. S2, all other surface representations were generated from atomic coordinates and rendered using PyMOL.

Results

Overall architecture of the HSV-1 UL9/ICP8 complex bound to a forked DNA

Previous studies have highlighted the important role of ICP8 and the 3′-flap of forked DNA in stimulating UL9 helicase activity [16, 21, 28, 30, 38, 39]. To gain structural insights into the cooperative action of UL9 and ICP8 during OriS unwinding, we used cryo-EM single-particle analysis of a complex assembled with stoichiometric amounts of UL9, ICP8, and forked DNA (Fig. 1A). Both proteins were overexpressed in bacterial cells and purified to near homogeneity (Supplemental Fig. S1). The assembled complex represents a defined step of DNA unwinding at the HSV-1 replication origin, corresponding to an established replication fork with UL9 bound to Box 1, activated by ICP8, and poised for processive duplex unwinding. The UL9/ICP8/DNA complex measures 129 Å in length, 139 Å in height, and 76 Å in width. Its structure revealed a bilobed architecture with a 1:1:1 stoichiometry (Fig. 1B and C) and a significant degree of motion between the two lobes (Supplemental Movies 1–3). The DNA duplex, with dsDBD of UL9 bound, protrudes from the complex and exhibits some flexibility as well (Supplemental Movie 3).

For the ICP8/DNA lobe, local refinement gave a reconstruction at 3.0 Å resolution, in which ICP8 adopts a conformation similar to that observed in the X-ray structure of its apo-form (root mean square deviation (RMSD) of 1.3 Å over 928 Cα atoms) [24]. Low-sigma-level maps (Supplemental Fig. S3) revealed additional density attributed to the CTD of ICP8. Although the CTD is highly flexible due to the lack of stable interaction with the rest of the complex, the presence of the density indicates preferential occupancy at this location. Interestingly, this position nearly overlaps with the locations of the CTDs of two DNA-free ICP8 molecules in the asymmetric unit of the crystal structure (Supplemental Fig. S3). Local refinement of the UL9/DNA lobe improved map quality, resulting in a reconstruction at 3.17 Å resolution. Notably, the conformation of UL9 in the complex with ICP8 and forked DNA differs significantly from that observed in the UL9 homodimer bound to dsDNA, which were discussed below.

We observed EM density for all 21 base-pairs of the DNA duplex bound to the dsDBD, for two nucleotides of the 5′-flap interacting with the helicase 2A subdomain, and for 18 nucleotides of the 3′-flap, of which seven and eleven nucleotides interact with the helicase domain of UL9 and with the cleft at the body-head junction of ICP8, respectively. Clear density was also observed for ATPγS located between the 1A and 2A subdomains of UL9 and bound to a Mg2+ ion. ICP8 also coordinates a Zn2+ ion using the residues C499, C502, C510, and H512. The zinc-binding motif is positioned distantly from the DNA and ATP-binding sites, suggesting primarily a structural role.

UL9–ICP8 Interface

ICP8 is expected to interact with UL9 to facilitate assembly of the UL9/ICP8/DNA ternary complex at the origin of replication and to enhance the dsDNA-unwinding activity of UL9 [21, 28, 30, 39, 40]. Consistent with this, the cryo-EM structure reveals physical contacts between UL9 and ICP8 within the UL9/ICP8/DNA complex at three distinct areas: a major interaction area (I), additional interaction area (II), and an area where contacts are mediated by ssDNA (III) (Fig. 2A). In the major interaction area, most contacts are formed by UL9 C-terminal residues E828–L851 engaging residues K806–K869 located on the upper surface of the ICP8 head domain (Fig. 2B). This interface buries 1807 Å² of protein surface and includes 13 intersubunit hydrogen bonds: two main-chain–main-chain, seven side-chain–main-chain, and four side-chain–side-chain interactions (Fig. 2B). In addition, UL9 residues V846, F848, and L851 insert into a hydrophobic pocket on the ICP8 surface, and the UL9 W839 side chain packs parallel to that of ICP8 R849, further stabilizing the hydrophobic interface (Fig. 2D). In the area II, two potential hydrogen bonds are observed: between the main-chain oxygen of UL9 A545 and the side chain of ICP8 R480, and between the side chains of UL9 E549 and ICP8 K1010 (Fig. 2C). Long-range ionic interactions are possible between the side-chains of UL9 E546 and ICP8 R519. Analysis of protein dynamics suggests that oscillatory movements within the complex may promote the formation of additional transient intersubunit contacts in area II (Supplemental Movies 1–3). Together, these interactions, along with the ssDNA-mediated contacts, substantially restrict the movement of ICP8 relative to UL9, which is anchored at the major interaction site.

UL9–ICP8 Interactions with DNA

The cryo-EM maps revealed a well-defined DNA structure, except for the disordered nucleotides: one at the 3′-flap and eight at the 5′-flap termini (Supplemental Fig. S4A). The duplex exhibits increased mobility toward its distal end, particularly across the five terminal base pairs that do not engage in protein contacts (Supplemental Fig. S5). Aside from these regions, the maps allowed accurate modeling of DNA, enabling unambiguous placement of every resolved nucleotide. Protein–DNA interactions within the UL9/ICP8/DNA complex are extensive and can be categorized into four regions: (I) UL9 interactions with the DNA duplex, (II) UL9 interactions with the lagging strand 5′-flap, (III) UL9 interactions with the leading strand 3′-flap, and (IV) ICP8 interactions with the leading strand 3′-flap (Fig. 3).

For amino acids, participating in hydrophobic or stacking interactions with DNA, their labels are shown in orange boxes. Duplex interactions are primarily mediated by dsDBD of UL9 (Fig. 1). Nonspecific contacts with the DNA phosphate backbone involve four H-bonds with UL9 backbone NH groups and H-bonds with nine side chains (Fig. 3). Specific interactions include hydrophobic stacking of H702 with the methyl groups of dT8D and dT9D bases (D indicates chain D, the leading strand), and hydrogen bonds between the K758 backbone oxygen and the dC10D base, between the K578 side chain and the bases of dG11D and dC12D, and between the side chain of R783 and the dG7D base, as observed in the complex of UL9 homodimer bound to a Box 1-containing duplex (UL92/DNA) [41]. The dsDBD makes only nonspecific contacts with the lagging strand (chain E), utilizing the side chains of amino acids T750, Y786, K793, R810, as well as the main-chain NH group of T750 (Fig. 3). Moreover, R526 and R529 from the helicase domain engage the phosphate of dG11E and the bases of dC20D and dC21D, respectively. 3D variability analysis revealed that the dsDBD–duplex contacts are dynamic (Supplemental Movie 1), consistent with a more relaxed network of hydrogen bonds compared to the UL92/DNA structure. This flexibility likely relaxes the specific protein-dsDNA contacts and facilitates a transition to a processive unwinding state.

The mechanism of DNA strand separation in UL9 differs from that of SF2 helicases containing a canonical β-hairpin pin [42–44]. In UL9, the “pin” is formed by residues L522 and Q525 within the α-helix spanning residues 506–531. During fork progression, their side chains are positioned to push apart the bases of the lagging and leading strands, respectively (Fig. 3 and 4A). In addition to the steric barrier created by these pin residues, DNA strand separation is further facilitated by a network of protein–DNA interactions at the fork. First, the phosphate group of dC22D is rotated by 180° relative to its position in duplex DNA and is stabilized by three hydrogen bonds. This rearrangement enables flipping of the leading-strand base dC22D (Fig. 4A). Second, the space vacated by the flipped dC22D is occupied by the side chain of Q525, which forms a hydrogen bond with the base of dA10E, thereby mimicking continuation of the duplex through a pseudo base-pair. The position of the dA10E base is further stabilized by a hydrogen bond with R526 and by stacking interactions with L522 (Fig. 4A). Moreover, the dA9E base stacks against F519 and forms a hydrogen bond with R523. Unlike canonical SF2 helicases, in which one of the displaced DNA strands becomes disordered [42–44], UL9 maintains defined positions for the first two nucleotides (dA10E and dA9E) of the displaced lagging strand (5′-flap). Interactions of UL9 with both strands at the fork enable not only complete base-pair separation but also significant destabilization of the adjacent base pair, as evidenced by pronounced elongation of the hydrogen bonds between dC21D and dG11E (Fig. 4A).

The seven-nucleotide segment of the leading-strand 3′-flap, immediately adjacent to the duplex region, engages extensively with the UL9 helicase domain (Fig. 3). Nucleotides dC22D–dA24D contact the helicase subdomain 1A, whereas nucleotides dC26D–dA28D interact with helicase subdomain 2A (Fig. 3 and 4B). Only the backbone phosphate of dT25D, positioned between the two helicase subdomains, fails to make protein contacts. The helicase domain forms 13 hydrogen bonds with the ssDNA phosphate backbone, 3 of which are mediated by backbone amide groups and 10 by side chains. In addition, the hydrogen bonds are formed between the dC22D, dT25D, and dT27D bases and the backbone amide of Y374, the Q182 side chain and the R113 side chain, respectively. Base positioning is further stabilized by stacking interactions contributed by Y140, T187, Y374, P376, and M578 (Fig. 4B).

dA29D is positioned between UL9 and ICP8 without forming stable stacking interactions of its base with either subunit, aside from a potential hydrogen bond between its base and the backbone oxygen of UL9 T138. This loosely constrained placement provides room and flexibility for relative movements of UL9 and ICP8 (Supplemental Movies 1–3).

The next ten-nucleotide segment of the leading-strand 3′-flap, nucleotides dC30D–dT39D, interacts with ICP8 by resting in an extended surface channel located between the head and body domains (Fig. 4C). No density was observed for the terminal nucleotide, dA40D. Despite the longer stretch, DNA contacts with ICP8 are less extensive than those observed for the 3′-flap bound to UL9. ICP8 forms only six hydrogen bonds with the ssDNA phosphate backbone and five with the bases (Fig. 3). In contrast to UL9-ssDNA interactions, the hydrogen bonds in the ICP8/ssDNA complex are mediated mainly by side chains, except dA31D base interaction with the backbone oxygen of L778. Furthermore, ssDNA is additionally stabilized by an interaction between the dA38D base and the phosphate oxygen of dA37D. Base-stabilizing hydrophobic interactions involving Y543, K559, F779, L797, R793, Y988, M991, and F998 further contribute to ssDNA positioning (Fig. 4C).

Rearrangement of UL9 structure upon formation of the UL9/ICP8/DNA complex

Comparison of the UL9/ICP8/DNA and UL9₂/DNA [41] structures revealed that ICP8 binding promotes dissociation of the DNA-free UL9 subunit from the UL9₂/DNA complex and facilitates the remaining duplex-bound UL9 to transition into the UL9/ICP8/DNA complex (Fig. 5). This process is accompanied by extensive structural rearrangements resulting from loss of the UL9–UL9 interface and the establishment of new intramolecular UL9 contacts as well as intermolecular interactions among UL9, ICP8, and DNA. First, the helicase domain undergoes a dramatic relocation: it rotates 140° and translocates, fully extending the linker (residues 532–540) between the helicase domain and the CTD (Fig. 5A and Supplemental Fig. S6). This movement results in a linear shift of the α-carbons of the active site residues D174 and E175 by an average of 78 Å. Second, the C-terminal residues beginning at S818 are rearranged due to the loss of interactions with the symmetry mate in the UL9 homodimer and the formation of new interactions with ICP8 (Fig. 5B). Third, the DNA duplex, together with dsDBD, tilts by 19°, positioning the replication fork junction at helicase pin domain residues L522 and Q525 and enabling interactions of the DNA 3′ and 5′-flaps with the helicase domain (Fig. 5C). Fourth, extensive restructuring occurs in both the helicase domain and the CTD to create complementary, intertwined surfaces for tight interaction (Fig. 5D). CTD residues 690–723 refold, causing a substantial reorientation of the α-helix and shortening it by two residues, from 702–713 to 704–713. Residues 630–636 also underwent a positional shift without a change in their overall fold. The refolding of the helicase domain is even more pronounced, involving the N-terminal residues up to G39, residues 403–458, and 502–513. The refolded segment 403–458 is well stabilized in the UL9/ICP8/DNA complex, whereas residues 434–458 are disordered in the UL9 homodimer.

Finally, additional conformational changes affect the ATP-binding pocket, primarily as a result of ssDNA binding (Fig. 6A). While the rearrangements described above do not directly involve the conserved helicase motifs, these latter changes do, including refolding of conserved Motif V (residues 351–355) and shifts and refolding within a larger segment (residues 111–168) encompassing conserved Motifs Ia and II. These changes influence ATP docking and conformation. Indeed, although ATPγS density is clearly observed in both the UL9/ICP8/DNA (Fig. 6B and Supplemental Fig. S4B) and UL9₂/DNA complexes [41], the nucleotide occupies markedly different positions in the two structures (Fig. 6C and Supplemental Fig. S7). In the UL9/ICP8/DNA structure, the γS-phosphate of ATPγS forms hydrogen bonds with the P-loop and the N-cap of the α-helix in conserved motif I and additionally interacts with R389 (motif VI) and T88 (motif I) (Supplemental Fig. S7A and B). The β-phosphate also contributes to N-capping of the motif I α-helix and interacts with R122 (motif Ia). The α-phosphate forms a single hydrogen bond with T89 (motif I), while the adenine base of ATPγS interacts with E125, located immediately C-terminal to motif Ia. A different hydrogen-bonding network is observed in the UL9₂/DNA complex [41]. In this structure, R122 interacts with the ATPγS sugar moiety, and no interactions are observed with T88, T89, or E125. Instead, K87 (motif I) interacts with the α- and β-phosphates, while R112 (motif Ia) contacts the γ-phosphate (Supplemental Fig. S7C). Notably, although P-loop engagement and N-capping of the motif I α-helix are preserved, they are mediated by the α-phosphate in UL9₂/DNA rather than the γS-phosphate, as observed in the UL9/ICP8/DNA complex. One of the reasons for the difference in ATPγS binding is the loss of hydrogen bonds between the γS-phosphate and the R112 side chain in the UL9₂/DNA complex; in the UL9/ICP8/DNA complex, R112 is repositioned to interact with DNA. An additional contributing factor is the refolding of a portion of Motif V, which in the UL9/ICP8/DNA complex partially occupies space closer to the γS-phosphate position observed in UL9₂/DNA.

Structural changes in ICP8 induced by UL9 and DNA binding

The crystal structure of DNA-free ICP8 revealed two independent ICP8 molecules in the asymmetric unit [24]. Molecule B was better ordered and was therefore used for subsequent comparative analyses. Superposition of the DNA-free ICP8 structure with that in the UL9/ICP8/DNA complex revealed several notable differences. When only the body domains are superimposed (RMSD of 1.1 Å over 728 Cα atoms), the head domain is displaced relative to the body domain by an average of 2.5 Å, most likely due to insertion of ssDNA between these domains (Fig. 7). Binding of ssDNA stabilizes and orders several loops directly involved in ssDNA interactions (Fig. 7). Specifically, residues 780–799 adopt a coil followed by an α-helix; the loop encompassing residues 989–996 becomes stabilized as a random coil; and residues 554–573 form a loop containing two short α-helices. Stabilization of the latter loop additionally constrains residues 637–638 from a neighboring loop. Moreover, three loops distant from the ssDNA, residues 75–80, 394–398, and 479–483, also become ordered, indicating an overall increase in structural stability of ICP8 within the UL9/ICP8/DNA complex compared with the DNA-free form.

Discussion

Recently, the cryo-EM structures of an inactive dimeric form of UL9 in complex with OriS were reported [41]. These structures revealed a head-to-tail dimerization mode that prevents the helicase domain from interacting with ssDNA and from hydrolyzing ATP. Gustavsson et al. also showed that the presence of a 3′-flap facilitates the conversion of UL9 from a dimer to a monomer [41]. However, severe preferred orientation bias and the limited resolution of the DNA-binding domain precluded model building for this complex. Of note, UL9 in complex with ICP8 and forked DNA fits well into the cryo-EM map of UL9 bound to mini-OriS*, which contains both a hairpin and a 3′-flap (Supplemental Fig. S8). Thus, in both cases, the presence of a 3′-flap promotes UL9 conversion into its active form, while ICP8 stabilizes it. Together, these studies indicate that, in the presence of dsDNA alone, the equilibrium between monomeric and dimeric UL9 is shifted toward the dimer, and that a DNA 3′-flap alone is insufficient to stabilize the monomeric UL9 and its complex with ssDNA. Accordingly, low-resolution electron microscopy experiments have shown that UL9 and ICP8 form a stable pre-unwinding complex on oriS-containing super-twisted DNA, which was designed to facilitate duplex melting in AT-rich regions [29].

Our study provides structural insight into how ICP8 and the 3′-flap activate UL9 by converting it from a dimer to a monomer. The head domain of ICP8 binds to the extreme C-terminal region of UL9, which plays a key role in UL9 dimerization [41]. This physical interaction provides a molecular basis for their cooperative action and robust unwinding of replication origins [29]. Moreover, the interaction ensures precise alignment of the DNA-binding sites of ICP8 and UL9, facilitating efficient handover of the leading DNA strand between the two proteins. As a result, UL9 and ICP8 form a continuous DNA-binding interface accommodating a 15-bp DNA duplex and 18 nucleotides of the leading strand. In addition, this mode of interaction allows a degree of flexibility, whereby coordinated movement of both proteins may facilitate DNA translocation (Supplemental Movies 1–3).

The structure obtained in this study provides a mechanistic explanation for the vast body of biochemical data on the cooperative action of ICP8 and UL9 to date [16–18, 28, 30, 40, 45–47]. Early studies showed that UL9 specifically recognizes origins of replication, while ICP8 strongly stimulates its helicase activity [9, 16]. The structure of the UL9/ICP8/DNA complex poised for OriS unwinding reveals the basis for UL9 activation by ICP8 and for the sequence specificity of OriS recognition by UL9. Deletion of 27 residues at the UL9 C-terminus increased helicase activity eightfold but abolished the stimulatory effect of ICP8 [40]. Consistently, UL9 residues 828–851 form the primary UL9–ICP8 interaction interface, comprising both hydrophilic and hydrophobic contacts (Fig. 2). Using negative-stain electron microscopy, highly ordered DNA unwinding by the UL9/ICP8 complex was observed in the presence of ATP and forked DNA, whereas no unwound structures were observed with UL9 alone [45]. Subsequently, Lehman’s group demonstrated that a 3′ tail positioned downstream of Box I, where the AT-rich linker between Boxes I and II is located, must be at least 18 nucleotides in length (exactly as revealed by the structure; Fig. 3) to efficiently stimulate the Box 1 unwinding by the UL9/ICP8 complex [46, 47]. Consistent with structural data presented here, ICP8 provides maximal stimulation of UL9 helicase activity at equimolar protein concentrations by increasing UL9 affinity for DNA and its processivity [30].

The structure demonstrates that UL9 activation results from a dramatic structural reorganization that unlocks the ssDNA-binding cleft of the helicase domain. Moreover, in the monomeric ssDNA-bound form of UL9, the ATP-binding site undergoes reorganization, leading to a distinct positioning of ATP. Together with previous studies, this work unveils an intriguing regulatory mechanism employed by HSV-1 to control its propagation within human cells. Notably, such radical conformational changes observed in UL9 have not been reported for other SF2 helicases.

The stimulatory effect of ICP8 on OriS unwinding by UL9 is mediated both by its interaction with the CTD of UL9 and by stabilization of the DNA 3′-flap in the unwound state. The 18-bp AT-rich region between Boxes 1 and 2 has a probability of melting at 37°C [47], transiently exposing the leading strand for interaction with ICP8 and the helicase domain of UL9. Notably, 18 nucleotides represent the minimum length of ssDNA required for loading of the UL9/ICP8 complex, according to the structure (Fig. 3). In addition, the AT-rich spacer between Boxes 1 and 2, which contains six (AT) repeats, is likely to contribute to local duplex destabilization, as short AT-rich tandem repeats are known to facilitate strand mis-annealing, slippage, and reduced thermodynamic stability of the DNA duplex [48, 49]. It has been proposed that two UL9 homodimers bound to Boxes 1 and 2 of OriS induce deformation and melting of the DNA duplex between them [41, 50]. It remains unclear whether UL9 or ICP8 binds first to the temporarily exposed 3′-flap, or they initially form a complex bound to the Box 1, followed by capture of the melted leading strand. Consistently, together, UL9 and ICP8 can unwind the AT-rich spacer without ATP hydrolysis [47].

SF2 helicases typically translocate along nucleic acids via an ATP-dependent inchworm mechanism, in which the two RecA-like domains undergo coordinated cycles of closure and opening coupled to ATP binding and hydrolysis [51, 52]. In this model, alternating interactions of the domains with the nucleic acid drive directional movement. Indeed, comparison of UL9 helicase domain structures in the UL92/DNA complex with and without ATPγS revealed that ATPγS binding induces subtle conformational changes that may couple ATP hydrolysis to DNA unwinding [41]. Similar differences in the positioning of subdomains 1A and 2A are observed when comparing ATPγS-bound and nucleotide-free UL9 helicase domains from the UL9/ICP8/DNA and UL92/DNA complexes, respectively (Supplemental Fig. S9). These observations suggest that an inchworm mechanism of translocation is, at least in part, applicable to UL9. However, UL9 also appears to deviate from the canonical inchworm model. In addition to the conserved helicase core, UL9 contains a distinct pin domain that is structurally and functionally divergent from those found in other SF2 helicases. Rather than acting solely as a passive strand-separating wedge, the UL9 pin domain, together with its specific DNA contacts, suggests a more complex mechanism in which DNA distortion and base flipping contribute to strand separation. These features indicate that UL9 may couple ATP-driven conformational changes to DNA unwinding in a manner that differs from classical inchworm translocation.

In summary, cryo-EM structural studies provide key insights into the herpesvirus origin firing process, enabling speculation about its potential mechanism. The unique UL9–ICP8 interaction interface, which plays a critical role in initiating HSV-1 DNA replication, represents a promising target for antiviral drug development. Additional potential targets include the DNA-interacting interfaces of UL9 and ICP8, as well as the ATP-binding pocket of UL9.
