Topical Lipolytic Enzymes Combined With a Multichannel Energy‑Based Device for Cellulite Treatment: A Randomized Exploratory Pilot Study
Authors: Jesús Rodríguez Lastra, Sidra Kouser
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimsCellulite is a highly prevalent condition characterized by dimpling and surface irregularities of the skin. Energy‐based devices (EBDs), including radiofrequency (RF), are widely used for non‐invasive body contouring, although evidence remains heterogeneous. Topical lipolytic enzymes have been explored in dermatologic applications, but data for cellulite remain limited. This exploratory randomized pilot study evaluated the preliminary efficacy and safety of adding topical lipolytic enzymes to a standardized high‐power multichannel RF‐based EBD protocol for thigh cellulite.MethodsTwenty women were randomized (1:1) to EBD alone or EBD plus topical enzymes (n = 10 per group). Mean age was 51.5 ± 12.1 years in the EBD group and 44.3 ± 13.4 years in the EBD+enzymes group; baseline BMI did not differ significantly between groups. Baseline cellulite grade distribution was imbalanced (Grade IV present only in the EBD+enzymes arm), and analyses were not adjusted for BMI or baseline grade. Ten sessions were administered over 5 weeks. Thigh circumference and ultrasound‐measured subcutaneous tissue thickness (5–18 MHz linear probe) were assessed at baseline and post‐treatment; standardized photographs were obtained for qualitative comparison. Within‐group changes were analyzed using repeated‐measures ANOVA and between‐group differences using one‐way ANOVA (two‐sided α = 0.05).ResultsMean thigh circumference decreased by 6.1 cm in the EBD group and 14.8 cm in the EBD+enzymes group (between‐group p = 0.020). Ultrasound‐measured subcutaneous tissue thickness decreased by 8.77 mm and 14.64 mm, respectively (between‐group p = 0.019). No serious adverse events occurred.ConclusionGreater reductions in thigh circumference and subcutaneous tissue thickness were observed with EBD plus topical enzymes versus EBD alone. This pilot study was not powered to control for potential confounding factors. Differences in BMI or baseline cellulite grade between groups may have influenced the observed effects. Therefore, the findings should be interpreted with caution and confirmed in larger, adequately powered randomized studies.

Introduction

Cellulite is a highly prevalent condition characterized by dimpling and surface irregularities of the skin, commonly described as an “orange peel” appearance. Although primarily considered an esthetic concern, advanced stages may be associated with discomfort or pain [1]. Cellulite occurs predominantly in women, particularly in the thighs, buttocks, and hips [2]. Prevalence estimates range from 85% to 98% among adult females [3], and the condition is also referred to as nodular liposclerosis or edemato‐fibrosclerotic panniculopathy [1]. Histologically, cellulite is marked by an irregular dermo–hypodermal interface with enlarged adipose lobules, known as adipose papillae, separated by thin fibrous septa arranged in a radial pattern [4].

Several hypotheses have been proposed to explain its pathophysiology. Sexual dimorphism plays a central role, as hormonal influences contribute to dermal thinning, alterations in the fibroseptal network, vascular changes, and inflammation [4]. Female sex, aging, and elevated body mass index (BMI) have been identified as risk factors for the development or worsening of cellulite [5]. Differential diagnosis is also important, as cellulite may be confused with lipedema, a condition with a distinct etiology and clinical behavior.

A wide range of treatments has been proposed, including weight reduction, massage, topical agents, fillers, herbal preparations, and various energy‐based modalities such as laser, thermal devices, and acoustic wave therapy. However, despite their popularity, many of these interventions lack robust evidence of efficacy [6]. Radiofrequency (RF) and other energy‐based devices (EBDs) have gained attention due to their favorable safety profile, minimal downtime, and potential effectiveness, although evidence remains limited [7].

Lipolytic enzymes such as hyaluronidase and collagenase have been used topically in dermatology with limited supporting evidence [8], and injectable formulations have shown efficacy for submental fat reduction [9]. However, no published studies have evaluated their use in cellulite. Regulatory restrictions in the United States and Europe currently limit these enzymes to topical application, making topical delivery a practical constraint [10].

Although electroporation was not used in the present study, experimental work has suggested that RF at approximately 1 MHz may influence membrane mechanical properties, providing a theoretical rationale to explore combined topical and RF‐based treatment without assuming enhanced cutaneous delivery [11]. To our knowledge, no studies have evaluated topical enzymes combined with a standardized RF‐based EBD protocol for cellulite. Therefore, this exploratory randomized pilot study aimed to assess the preliminary efficacy and safety of adding topical enzymes to a standardized high‐power multichannel RF‐based EBD protocol for cellulite‐related outcomes.

Materials and Methods

Study Design and Ethics

The cohort consisted of 20 participants assigned to two parallel groups: 10 subjects received EBD plus topical enzyme treatment, and 10 subjects received the enzyme‐free EBD‐only protocol. In accordance with the exploratory pilot design, this sample size was considered sufficient to assess the feasibility of the procedure and estimate the variability of the measurements for future confirmatory studies.

This randomized, parallel‐group clinical study was conducted at INNEO Clinic (Sant Joan Despí, Barcelona, Spain). The protocol was reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee of the Autonomous University of Barcelona, Spain (CEEAH CA31/2021), and adhered to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. All participants provided written informed consent prior to inclusion in the study, including consent for the publication of clinical images.

Randomization (1:1) was based on a computer‐generated sequence created by an independent researcher not involved in recruitment, treatment, or assessment. Group assignments were placed in sequentially numbered, opaque, sealed envelopes that were stored securely and opened in order only after eligibility confirmation, informed consent, and baseline assessments. Allocation concealment was maintained until the envelope was opened at the time of assignment. Participant flow through the trial is shown in Figure 1.

Participants

Twenty women aged 18–65 years with cellulite grades I–IV on the buttocks and posterior thighs (Nürnberger and Müller classification) were enrolled through online advertisements. Participants agreed to maintain stable dietary habits and physical activity throughout the study.

Treatment Protocol

Both groups received treatment with the Capenergy C.4.0 device following a standardized protocol using its four‐channel configuration (each channel comprising one active and one passive plate). During the first phase, two 200 cm2 active plates were applied to the area of greatest cellulite involvement for 20 min at high power, with two channels used on each side simultaneously. In the second phase, cross‐fire treatment with capacitive and resistive electrodes was delivered for 10 min in total across both sides, while the remaining channels were used concurrently to perform lymphatic drainage with two transmitting plates placed on the soles of the feet and two receiving plates on the lower dorsal region. Total session duration was approximately 30 min. Both groups completed 10 sessions, administered twice weekly over 5 weeks. The 10‐session course with 30 min duration (twice weekly for 5 weeks) was selected to standardize dosing and ensure feasibility within the exploratory pilot design.

EBD‑Only Group

Participants received the standardized RF (EBD) protocol. A neutral hydrating serum was applied solely to facilitate electrode movement.

EBD + Enzymes Group

Lyophilized lipase and collagenase vials were reconstituted with 10 mL of physiological saline. A total of 2.5 mL was applied to each trochanteric area during the active plate phase, and an additional 2.5 mL during the cross‑fire phase.

Enzymes

The enzymes used are 10 mL vials containing collagenase of bacterial origin (Clostridium histolyticum) and lipase of animal origin (porcine pancreas). The concentration is equivalent to 850 U/vial for collagenase activity and 3700 U/vial for lipase. All batches have the same enzymatic activity. Once opened, the solution remained stable for 2–8 h and was used within this time window.

Outcome Measures

Outcome evaluators were blinded to group allocation throughout the study. Participants were not blinded due to the nature of the intervention; however, all assessments were performed by an independent evaluator to minimize detection bias.

Anthropometry

Thigh circumference was measured at predefined anatomical points using a standard measuring tape. Measurements were obtained at baseline and after the final session.

Ultrasound Assessment

Subcutaneous fat thickness was evaluated using an Alpinion E‑CUBE i7 ultrasound system with a 5–18 MHz linear probe. The most affected area, identified by pronounced orange‑peel appearance, was marked with a dermatological pencil to ensure reproducibility. Three measurements were taken at each site and averaged. Assessments were performed at baseline and post‑treatment by the same trained operator.

Photographic Evaluation

Clinical photographs were obtained under standardized conditions, including fixed camera‐to‐subject distance, consistent room lighting, and identical patient positioning. Images were captured at the same angle (posterior and lateral views) using a neutral standing posture, with anatomical reference points (greater trochanter, gluteal fold) used to ensure reproducibility. All photographs were taken before and after treatment using the same device and settings. Cellulite severity was graded using the photonumerical scale described by Cohen et al. [12].

Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), and categorical variables as counts and percentages. Where applicable, 95% confidence intervals (CI) are reported. Normality of distributions was assessed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov (K–S) test. Baseline differences between groups were evaluated using Student's t‑test for continuous variables and the χ 2 test for categorical variables. Within‑group pre‑ to post‑treatment changes were analyzed using repeated‑measures analysis of variance (ANOVA), and between‑group differences were assessed using one‑way ANOVA with post hoc tests. All statistical tests were two‑sided, with a pre‑specified significance level of α = 0.05. In addition to p values, effect sizes were computed to assess the magnitude of the observed differences. Cohen's d was calculated for within‑group pre–post changes and for between‑group comparisons. For ANOVA models, partial eta squared (η 2p) was extracted as a measure of effect size. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software, version 25.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). No participants were lost to follow‐up, and no missing data occurred; all analyses were conducted on complete cases (n = 20).

All primary analyses were pre‑specified a priori in the study protocol. Between‑group comparisons were considered exploratory and intended to generate hypotheses rather than confirm treatment effects, consistent with the pilot nature of the study. Statistical reporting adhered to the SAMPL (Statistical Analyses and Methods in the Published Literature) guidelines [13].

Side Effects

None of the patients experienced pain during treatment. A few participants reported a mild sensation of heat, which was considered tolerable. Transient erythema at the treatment site, attributable to subcutaneous heating, was occasionally observed and resolved spontaneously within 20 min. No additional adverse effects were recorded.

Discussion

The growing demand for non‑invasive treatments for cellulite has led to increased use of energy‑based devices (EBDs), which have demonstrated favorable safety profiles and modest clinical benefits in previous studies [14]. RF‑induced heating can remodel collagen septa and improve skin surface irregularities [15, 16]. However, the magnitude of improvement reported in the literature varies considerably, with reductions in thigh or gluteal circumference typically ranging from 0.5 to 5 cm depending on device type, treatment parameters, and patient characteristics [17, 18, 19, 20].

In the present study, both groups demonstrated reductions in circumference and subcutaneous tissue thickness, with greater improvements observed in the group receiving EBD combined with topical enzymes. While these findings suggest a potential synergistic effect, they must be interpreted cautiously due to the small sample size and baseline imbalances between groups, particularly in cellulite severity, which may have influenced the magnitude of change.

Several mechanisms may contribute to the observed improvements. Controlled hyperthermia can induce fibroblast activation and extracellular matrix remodeling [21], while RF‑induced heating may promote adipocyte apoptosis through calcium‑dependent pathways [22]. Enzymatic agents such as collagenase and lipase have been explored for their lipolytic and fibrinolytic properties, although evidence for their topical use remains limited and largely preclinical [8, 23]. Although their thermostability has been described [24], transdermal delivery and local bioactivity were not directly evaluated in the present study, and mechanistic conclusions cannot be drawn.

Although electroporation was not used, previous work suggests that RF frequencies around 1 MHz may increase membrane deformability [11], potentially facilitating the diffusion of topically applied compounds. Nevertheless, this remains speculative and requires dedicated mechanistic studies.

Photographic assessments were consistent with quantitative findings; however, participants were not blinded due to the nature of the intervention, which may introduce performance bias. Overall, the results support the feasibility and short‑term safety of combining high‑power multichannel EBD with topical enzymes, but the methodological limitations prevent definitive conclusions regarding efficacy.

The main limitations of this study include the small sample size, lack of participant blinding, absence of long‐term follow‐up, and baseline imbalances between groups. These factors limit the internal validity and generalizability of the findings. Future randomized controlled trials with larger cohorts, standardized baseline matching, blinded assessments, and mechanistic evaluations are needed to confirm the potential benefit of this combined approach.

Conclusion

High‐power multichannel EBD treatment was associated with reductions in thigh circumference, subcutaneous tissue thickness, and visible cellulite irregularities. Greater improvements were observed when topical collagenase and lipase were added to EBD; however, these findings should be interpreted with caution due to the small sample size, baseline imbalances, and lack of long‐term follow‐up. Larger, methodologically robust studies are required to confirm efficacy, safety, and durability.
