Waist circumference is an important determinant of relative muscle strength in patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus
Authors: Yi-Lun Chiang, Guan-Yu Su, Shiow-Chwen Tsai, Chiao-Nan Chen, Chii-Min Hwu, Tsung-Hui Wu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground:Muscle damage has recently been recognized as a complication of type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM). Impaired muscle function adversely affects physical activity, quality of life, and mortality risk. Moreover, waist circumference (WC) is associated with visceral fat and insulin resistance. This study investigated the correlation between relative muscle strength and WC in patients with T2DM.Methods:Patients aged 50 years or older with T2DM were recruited from Taipei Veterans General Hospital. WC was measured at the umbilicus level. Total muscle mass was assessed using bioelectrical impedance analysis. The muscle strength of handgrip, knee extension, ankle dorsiflexion, and ankle plantar flexion were evaluated using a hand-held dynamometer.Results:A total of 252 patients (mean age 69.4 years; 122 men and 130 women) were enrolled. They were stratified into three groups according to WC (<85, 85-94, >94 cm). Patients with a larger WC had a higher body mass index and greater muscle mass but had lower relative muscle strength of handgrip, knee extension, ankle dorsiflexion, and ankle plantar flexion. Of note, patients with abdominal obesity (WC ≥90 cm in men and WC ≥80 cm in women) had significantly lower relative muscle strength of handgrip and lower limb compared to those without in both sexes. Multivariable analyses revealed that WC was significantly and negatively correlated with relative muscle strength after adjusting for other clinical variables.Conclusion:WC, independent of muscle mass, is a good determinant of relative muscle strength in patients with T2DM. Measuring WC can help identify patients with T2DM at a high risk of low relative muscle strength, thereby broadening the scope of health screening in this population.Keywords: Relative muscle strength, Type 2 diabetes mellitus, Waist circumference

1. INTRODUCTION

Muscle strength declines with age.1 However, the rate of muscle strength decline is relatively high in patients with diabetes mellitus.1 Consequently, these patients may experience a reduction in or the loss of functional independence,2 which can increase the likelihood of hospitalization and place a substantial burden on health-care systems.3 Evidence indicates that decreased muscle strength is associated with increased morbidity and mortality risks.4–7

Several neurological and skeletal muscle related factors contribute to the development of dynapenia.8 Skeletal muscle–related factors include muscle size, muscle fat content, and intrinsic force-generating properties. Although individuals with greater muscle mass are theoretically expected to have higher muscle strength, a longitudinal study revealed no association between muscle strength and muscle mass. Hence, a panel of experts recommended relative muscle strength (strength per unit of muscle mass) instead of muscle strength as a more appropriate indicator of muscle function.9 Normalization of muscle strength is crucial in patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM). Evidence suggests that muscle strength is lower in patients with T2DM than in healthy individuals with comparable body composition.10 A population-based study revealed age-related reductions in the relative muscle strength of upper and lower limb muscles in both men and women.11 Furthermore, limited mobility due to T2DM is associated with reduced relative muscle strength.12

Waist circumference (WC) can predict the strength of lower limb muscles in women with obesity.13 In patients with T2DM, visceral fat accumulation is associated with reduced muscle strength normalized to muscle mass.14 WC is an indirect measure of visceral fat content. However, few studies have explored the association between WC and relative muscle strength in patients with T2DM. Accordingly, the present study investigated the association between WC and relative muscle strength in patients with T2DM.

2. METHODS

2.1. Patients

This cross-sectional study included 252 ambulatory patients aged ≥50 years with a known history of T2DM for more than 12 months. These patients were recruited from the diabetes clinics at Taipei Veterans General Hospital. During routine follow-ups, physicians invited consecutive eligible patients to participate in this study. Patients were informed about the study protocol, and those who agreed to participate were scheduled for baseline assessments. We excluded patients with severe heart failure (New York Heart Association functional class III or IV), severe muscular disorders, acute infection, or peripheral or central neuropathy unrelated to diabetes; pregnant women; and bedridden individuals. Written informed consent was obtained from all patients. Baseline assessments comprised a questionnaire survey, clinical examinations, and blood and urine sample collection.

2.2. Measurements

Body mass index was calculated as body weight (kg) divided by body height squared (m2). WC was measured to the nearest millimeter at the level of the umbilicus. The patients were stratified into three groups on the basis of their WC: <85, 85 to 94, and >94 cm. Abdominal obesity was defined as a WC of ≥90 cm in men and ≥80 cm in women.

Seated blood pressure was measured using an automated blood pressure recorder (HEM-7310; Omron Healthcare Inc., Kyoto, Japan). Body composition was assessed through bioelectrical impedance analysis (InBody 3.0; Biospace Co. Ltd., Tokyo, Japan). Moreover, grip strength was measured using a Jamar® hand dynamometer (Model 5030J1; Patterson Medical, Warrenville, IL). Using a hand-held dynamometer (microFET2™; Hoggan Scientific, LLC, Salt Lake City, UT), we measured the strength of the dominant leg’s knee extensor in the sitting position and that of the ankle dorsiflexor and plantar flexor in the supine position. Each muscle group was measured three times, and the maximum value for each muscle was recorded.15 Relative muscle strength was calculated as the ratio of muscle strength to total muscle mass.16 Fasting plasma glucose, glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), and creatinine levels were measured using overnight fasting blood samples.

We collected data on each patient’s demographic characteristics and physical activity levels. Physical activity levels were measured using a validated Chinese questionnaire, in which the patients self-reported the duration of time spent engaging in activities at five intensity levels during a typical day over the preceding 2 weeks.17 These five activity levels were heavy (eg, running), moderate (eg, aerobic dancing), slight (eg, casual walking), sedentary (eg, sitting and standing), and basal (eg, sleeping and lying down). A physical inactivity score was calculated as the ratio of sedentary hours to waking hours.17

2.3. Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are presented as means ± SDs, and categorical variables are presented as numbers and percentages. Data on ALT levels exhibited a substantially skewed distribution; therefore, logarithmic transformation was applied to reduce the skewness before analysis. The significance of between-group differences in categorical variables was analyzed using a χ2 test. By contrast, the significance of between-group differences in continuous variables was determined using Student t test and one-way analysis of variance with post-hoc Duncan tests. Univariate linear regression was performed to identify demographic and biochemical factors potentially associated with relative muscle strength. Multivariate models were constructed using factors that reached significance in the univariate regression. All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS Statistics (version 18.0; SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL). A p value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

3. RESULTS

Table 1 presents the baseline characteristics of the patients. Their mean age was 69.4 (range: 51-94) years, and their mean duration of diabetes was 15.8 years. The patients were divided into three groups on the basis of WC tertiles: <85, 85 to 94, and >94 cm. The mean ages of three groups were 69.4, 70.2, and 68.7 years, respectively. Body mass index and muscle mass were lower in patients with WC <85 cm than in patients with WC >94 cm and patients with WC 85 to 94 cm. Physical inactivity score, fasting plasma glucose, and HbA1c were similar in the three groups. No significant between-group differences were observed in muscle strength (Table 2). However, relative muscle strength decreased progressively with increasing WC (Table 3).

The patients were also divided into subgroups on the basis of WC cutoffs of 90 cm in men and 80 cm in women. Those with abdominal obesity (WC ≥90 cm in men and WC ≥80 cm in women) had less relative muscle strength of handgrip and lower limb muscle than those without abdominal obesity (Supplementary Table S1, https://links.lww.com/JCMA/A347). The reduction in relative muscle strength among patients with abdominal obesity was observed in both men and women (Supplementary Tables S2 and S3, https://links.lww.com/JCMA/A347).

The relative strength of right handgrip was negatively correlated with age (β = −0.036, p = 0.0010), sex (β = −0.96, p < 0.001), and WC (β = −0.040, p < 0.001) but positively correlated with estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR; β = 0.0090, p = 0.017) and logarithmic transformation of ALT (β = 1.024, p = 0.028). Multivariate analysis revealed factors significantly associated with relative muscle strength (Tables 4 and 5). Relative handgrip strength was significantly associated with age, sex, and WC (right hand: R2 = 0.25, p < 0.001, left hand: R2 = 0.31, p < 0.001). Furthermore, relative knee extension and ankle plantar flexion strength were significantly associated with age, sex, WC, and HbA1c (knee: R2 =0.14, p < 0.001; ankle plantar flexion: R2 =0.20, p < 0.001). Relative ankle dorsiflexion strength was significantly associated with WC and eGFR (R2 = 0.10, p < 0.001).

4. DISCUSSION

Our findings reveal that patients with T2DM and a larger WC had lower relative muscle strength of handgrip, knee extension, ankle dorsiflexion, and ankle plantar flexion. A multivariate model adjusted for clinical covariates demonstrated a significant and negative correlation between relative muscle strength and WC. These findings suggest that WC is a suitable predictor of relative muscle strength in patients with T2DM.

Visceral fat accumulation is negatively associated with the ratio of handgrip strength to arm muscle mass in patients with T2DM.14 In these patients, decreased muscle strength is associated with a risk of unfavorable outcomes, including hospitalization, cardiac events, and mortality.18 In line with these findings, our study demonstrated that the negative association between WC and relative muscle strength remained significant after adjustment for covariates. These results suggest that WC measurement can help identify patients with T2DM who have an increased risk of low relative muscle strength.

The association between high WC and low relative muscle strength in patients with T2DM is multifactorial. Fat tissue may increase the production of proinflammatory cytokines (eg, interleukin-6), which are associated with decreased muscle mass and muscle strength.19 In the InCHIANTI study that examined older patients with obesity, patients with lower muscle strength had higher levels of interleukin-6 and C-reactive protein.20 Fat accumulation occurs not only in the abdominal region but also in muscle tissue. Visceral fat content is significantly correlated with intermuscular fat tissue and intramyocellular lipid content.21,22 A previous study reported that the content of intramyocellular lipid droplets was two-fold higher in muscle fibers sampled from individuals with obesity than in those sampled from individuals without obesity; additionally, intramyocellular lipid content was inversely correlated with myofiber contraction.23 Lipid accumulation in muscle is associated with reduced muscle strength.24,25 Furthermore, adults with obesity have a higher proportion of type I muscle fibers, which generate less power than do muscle fibers in adults without obesity.23

In the present study, the relative strength of upper limb muscle was significantly correlated with age and sex. Yokoyama et al26 also reported a correlation between handgrip strength and age (men: β = −0.40, p < 0.001; women: β = −0.27, p < 0.001). Evidence indicates that relative muscle strength decreases with age in both men and women.11,27 Age-related muscle atrophy is characterized by the loss of type II muscle fibers and decreased contractility.28 In a related study, relative handgrip strength was associated with sex, with an odds ratio of 18.09 (p < 0.001).29 Another study demonstrated sex-based differences in muscle fiber composition, with type I fibers accounting for 36% and 44% of the total biopsy area in men and women, respectively.30 These differences are attributable to the higher proportion of type I fibers in women and sex-specific hormonal effects.

We observed significant associations of relative knee extension strength and relative ankle plantar flexion strength with age. These findings are consistent with those of Lynch et al,11 who reported a significant quadratic negative correlation between age and relative leg strength, and those of Lindle et al,31 who reported a significant correlation between age and relative knee muscle strength. However, our results differ from those of Mori et al,32 who observed no difference in knee extension strength between older (≥65 years) and younger (<65 years) patients with T2DM. Metter et al33 evaluated muscle mass by using three approaches: by measuring creatinine levels in 24-hour urine samples, by determining cross-sectional areas from the circumferences of extremities, and by evaluating fat-free mass through dual energy X-ray absorptiometry. The second and third approaches, but not the first approach, revealed age-related reductions in relative strength of upper and lower limb muscles.33 The discrepancies among these related studies could be attributed to variations in the definitions of relative muscle strength and in research designs.

The present study observed that eGFR was significantly associated with relative ankle dorsiflexion strength. Loss of lean body mass, particularly skeletal muscle in the extremities, is significantly associated with a reduction in eGFR.34 Mori et al32 demonstrated a significant association between dynapenia and eGFR in patients with T2DM; however, they observed no association between eGFR and knee extension strength. By contrast, our univariate analysis revealed a significant correlation between eGFR and relative ankle dorsiflexion strength, and this association remained significant after adjustment for covariates. Further studies are needed to examine the association between renal function and the relative strength of lower limb muscle.

Our multivariate analysis indicated that relative knee extension strength and relative ankle plantar flexion strength were correlated with HbA1c. Previous studies have reported associations between poor glycemic control and relative handgrip strength and knee extension strength.35,36 Hyperglycemia may cause a decline in relative muscle strength through the accumulation of advanced glycation end products.16 Additional studies are required to elucidate the mechanisms underlying the association between hyperglycemia and decreased relative muscle strength.

The present study has some limitations that should be considered. First, relative muscle strength was evaluated as the ratio of muscle strength to total muscle mass instead of regional muscle mass. Evidence suggests that the loss of muscle mass is greater in the lower body than in the upper body.37 Therefore, calculating relative muscle strength on the basis of total muscle mass may have underestimated the muscle quality of the lower extremities. Second, we did not collect data on duration of diabetes, and this clinical factor was not adjusted for in our analysis. Third, we included only patients with T2DM aged 50 years or above because older individuals have a higher risk of muscle strength decline. Future studies should determine the association between WC and relative muscle strength in younger patients with T2DM. Finally, the cross-sectional design and small sample size are additional limitations of this study.

In conclusion, our findings suggest that WC is a good determinant of relative muscle strength in patients with T2DM. Measuring WC may enhance the identification of patients with T2DM who have a high risk of low relative muscle strength.
