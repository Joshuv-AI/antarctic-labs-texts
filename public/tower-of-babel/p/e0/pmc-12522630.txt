“Voices through masks: a stylistic analysis of selected Covid-19 pandemic poems”
Authors: Neveen Galal-Eldin, Amal Zaki Eldin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractTimes of pandemics usually witness a remarkable surge of interest among various poets who, in response to the impending threats and anxiety brought about by the uninvited guest, provide multiple ways of portraying the crisis and its inevitable impact. Pandemic poetry provides a fertile source of data for relating language to its poetic artistic function in general and for studying some linguistic and stylistic features that characterize language in pandemic poems in particular. This study seeks to depict and analyze the stylistic features employed in four Covid-19 pandemic poems with the objective of linking poetic style to the specific representations of experience associated with the pandemic. The samples of the study are four English-language pandemic poems selected from online sources on Covid-19. The qualitative approach is employed to collect, classify and analyze the selected samples of poems inductively in order to find out the distinctive stylistic features of the selected Covid-19 pandemic poems. The framework of analysis employs Leech and Short's (2007) method of stylistic analysis in which four categories are tackled: lexical categories, grammatical categories, figures of speech and context and cohesion. The foregrounding theory also serves as a strategy employed for analyzing the selected poems in order to highlight the prominent features in the text, thereby directing focus towards the thematic concerns linked to the pandemic. The stylistic analysis serves as a tool for readers to comprehend the themes of the selected poems in relation to linguistic choices while revealing deeper layers of interpretation and appreciation of language use in pandemic poems. The selected Covid-19 pandemic poems masterfully communicate medical themes through various stylistic elements, bringing together the fields of medicine, poetry and stylistics. The scientific examination of the virus and its physical and psychological consequences together with the representation of the healthcare system during the pandemic formed the subject matter of the four selected poems. Repetition, figures of speech, paradox, hyperbole and parallelism, are among the most frequently used stylistic devices that managed to underscore the ideas of uncertainty, confusion, anxiety, and pain associated with the Covid-19 pandemic.Keywords: Covid-19 pandemic poetry, Medical themes, Leech & Short’s Model (2007), Stylistic analysis, Foregrounding strategy

Introduction

Language and literature are deeply interconnected and mutually enriching. Literature, thus, cannot be investigated in depth apart from language (Leech, A Linguistic Guide 1). Therefore, in examining any literary text, a linguistic analysis cannot be neglected. Literary writing reflects each writer’s individual style and technique, shaping how they depict ideas and convey emotions. The writers’ stylistic techniques are related to their perspectives and sentiments reflected in the literary texts.

Style and stylistics

The notion of style is referred to as the way language is used in a given context, by a certain person, for a certain purpose (Leech and Short 9). Carter and Stockwell [3] define the notion of style as:

A choice of certain structures over others available in the language. To claim that style is a choice is not, of course to claim that it is always a conscious choice. Indeed if one had to make all phonological, syntactic, semantic and pragmatic choices consciously, it would take a very long time to say anything at all. In literature, as in all discourse, a sense of the best way of putting something can be intuitive or conscious; the result as far as the reader is concerned will be much the same. (44)

In general terms, stylistics is the study of style in texts and how language use varies according to varying circumstances in terms of time, discourse and authorship (Leech, Language in Literature 54) [11]. Earlier, Leech simply defined stylistics as “the study of the use of language in literature” (1) and accordingly, it aims at “explaining the relation between language and artistic function” (Leech and Short 11). Running on the same lines, Widdowson views stylistics as “the study of literary discourse from a linguistic orientation” (4) [21]. In modern approaches to language, stylistics is seen as “a method of textual interpretation in which primacy of place is assigned to language (Simpson 2) [18]. Specifically, Wales argues that the goal of stylistics is “not simply to describe the formal features of texts for their own sake, but in order to show their functional significance for the interpretation of the text; or in order to relate literary effects to linguistic' causes' where these are felt to be relevant.” (340) [20].

Stylistics serves as a link between both linguistics and literary criticism as it provides a way of mediating the two disciplines (Widdowson 4) [21]. Wales attributes the variety of stylistic approaches into “the main influences of different branches of linguistics and literary criticism” (399). Leech and short provide a distinction between two viewpoints. The linguist seeks to answer the question “why does the author choose this form of expression” while the literary critic attempts to answer “how is such-and such an aesthetic effect achieved through language”. (11, emphasis in original) [12].

Covid-19 in poetry

The Covid-19 global pandemic has brought wide-ranging changes in every aspect of our lives. This global crisis has spread across the entire planet of earth in a very short time, dramatically affecting individuals and communities in unanticipated ways. It has changed human interaction, social relations, healthcare systems and economic conditions. The virus has literally disrupted and destroyed the lives of many people all over the world who have been obliged to be confined at home for infection or fear of it. Lockdown, isolation, social distancing and restrictive movements due to this pandemic have stimulated poets during the outbreak of Covid-19 (2020–2021) to respond with a surge of poetic thoughts and ideas reflecting on the particular challenges faced during difficult time and creatively put into words. As evidenced by the sheer number of the globally published volumes, anthologies and individual poems of the period, poets of different cultures and backgrounds responded prolifically to this global health emergency and its massive effects. They felt compelled to express their emotions, transfer and share their experience through the medium of poetry, which usually steps in as a source of healing and solace for poets and readers alike at the time of sorrow and suffering.

Statement of objectives

This study aims to identify the different types of stylistic devices employed in some selected poems written during the outbreak of Covid-19 with the purpose of connecting poetic style to Covid-specific representations of experience in a way that resonates with readers. It also seeks to point out artistic or aesthetic value of each stylistic device. Moreover, it is intended to find out, through stylistic analysis of the four selected Covid-19 poems, how stylistic devices serve to communicate medical themes, thereby highlighting the intersection of poetry, stylistics and medicine.

Significance of the study

The current study deals with the efficiency of the stylistic tools used in pandemic poems in communicating medical themes and engaging the reader to understand and process the pandemic experience. The stylistic analysis can provide a deeper comprehension and appreciation of language use in pandemic poems. It equally allows for a profounder understanding of how language operates not only to convey meaning but also to shape the perception of medical concepts and health-related topics.

Data collection

For the purpose of collecting data for the study, samples of pandemic poems were downloaded from websites and e-books on the internet. The data of this study includes four English pandemic poems selected from four websites. The four poems were chosen from amongst more than 350 poems included in 6 volumes (Covid-19 Pandemic Poems, Volumes I, II, III, IV, V, VI), 3 online anthologies (Poetry and Covid, Lockdown Poems, Behind the Mask: 40 Quarantine Poems from Humboldt County) and a number of individual poems, all published online.

The poems constituting the sample of analysis were selected according to predesigned criteria. They were written during the years 2020–2021 following the onset of the Covid19 pandemic. They have relatively different authorial backgrounds and cultures – British-Australian, Indian, American based in France and another in USA. This variety serves to demonstrate the global impact of the pandemic experience. On the thematic level, the four poems feature conspicuous medical themes, and each poem shifts the focus towards a specific aspect of the Covid experience. Moreover, the selected poems represent different forms, stylistic devices and poetic techniques which collectively and effectively contribute to the overall communication of the themes and draw a comprehensive profile of the experience.

The selected poems are “Under the Microscope” [14] by Jacqueline Pemberton, “A Recompense” [5] by Ancy Eapen, “Personal neuroses and pandemic” [2] by Wendy Butler, and “Pas De Pandemic” [9] by Molly Headley.

Literature review

Research on pandemic literature has grown rapidly into a fairly attractive field of study after the Covid-19 pandemic has raised its head at the beginning of 2020 in different disciplines including psychology, literature, linguistics and social sciences. Various researchers have tackled specific categories of pandemic literature developing new theories or adapting theories put forth by former researchers. Over the few years following the start of the pandemic there was a sustained and progressively heightened interest among researchers in exploring the language of pandemic literature. Among recent material that deals with Pandemic poetry in particular are papers published after the outbreak of the Covid pandemic.

Based on pandemic poetry in the anthology of Covid-19 pandemic poems, vol. I, II, III, Haydar's paper [8] examines the themes and tools employed in covid-19 pandemic poems. The mask and social distancing are the main key terms depicted in the selected poems. They are tackled in two perspectives; firstly they are portrayed as tools of evil power to control people, then they are positively described as proper tools to save people's lives from the pandemic. The study concludes that the themes of covid-19 poems basically focus on the climate change and the conspiracy theory.

Rezeki's research [16] has been conducted in order to illustrate the types of messages conveyed in five poems on Covid-19 as well as to identify the different types of figurative language employed in the selected poems. The study makes use of Leech's [10] theory to identify types of figurative language in combination with Nurgiyantoro's theory (1995) to explain their meanings. The study concludes that the selected poems employ eight types of figurative language including metonymy (28.9%), simile (16.9%), metaphor (14.5%), personification (13.3%), hyperbole 13.3%, irony 8.5%, oxymoron 3.7%. and litotes 1.2%. Therefore, the most dominant type employed in the selected covid-19 poems is metonymy. Moreover, there are two types of messages conveyed, namely social and moral messages.

Relying on pandemic digital poetry, Abu Zaid [1] provides a thematic analysis of two online social media poems. The researcher's two selected poems tackle the social, psychological, economic and political consequences of covid-19 pandemic from different perspectives. One poem tackle some of the devastating aftermaths of Covid crisis and some of the shared topics among people while the second deals with the positive sides of the crisis and give some optimistic suggestions to overcome its terrible effects. The study also shows the important role played by the social media digital poetry in the pandemic global quarantine and the aftermaths of the virus.

Dera's article [4] reveals how poetry readers respond to the phenomenon of “corona poetry” by evaluating their attitudes towards poems written on Covid-19. It focuses on the opposition between autonomous and heteronomous poetry, hence revealing whether readers of poetry primarily assert the argument that a poem is a linguistic work of art, or rather assess the genre in view of its ideological, social or therapeutic function. By analyzing the results of a questionnaire that has been distributed among readers of poetry in the Netherlands and Belgium, the study demonstrates that their attitude towards corona poems is primarily negative. In view of readers’ reflections on corona poetry, heteronomous arguments seem to prove more dominant than autonomous arguments.

Responding to Covid poetry, Giovanelli [7] seeks to integrate text and reader response data analysis in order to examine the pandemic experience in Michelle Witthaus'poem, “the new shape of fear”. The data analysis reveals that participants interpret and discuss the poem by drawing on foregrounded language features in the text, reflecting on their own pandemic experiences and demonstrating empathy with the experiences of others. These findings demonstrate that Covid poetry may have particular interpretive effects that are geared towards self-and other understanding. Overall, the study sets out some first steps towards examining the stylistic features and interpretive effects of this emerging sub-genre of covid poetry.

In her article, Pokhrel [15] explores the use of pathos in four pandemic poems.it also discusses vivid pictures of the pathetic state of the characters in the poems. Her study concludes that the analyzed pandemic poems share some common themes: those which evoke strong positive emotion of joy, excitement and hope as well as themes of negative emotions of fear, sadness, agony and sympathy. Moreover, the poems explore pathos with regard of their composition, thematic presentation, and stylistic and characteristic features such as anecdote, imageries, metaphors and emotionally charged words.

Gambari-Olufadi and Saihu- Abdul Akeem's [6] study examines selected poems in Tanure Ojaide's Narrow Escapes on Covid-19 pandemic. The study adopts a stylistic perspective to investigate how language is manipulated to convey the poetic contribution to the trendy subject. Three poems were purposely selected and analyzed with the tooklits of critical stylistics. The data analysis has revealed that the poet's intent on the pandemic in combination with his linguistic choices such as pun, personification, allusion and analogy that run through the lines of the selected data. The study concludes that the deployment of the critical stylistic tools for the analysis of the selected poems has unraveled the underlying sense which is conveyed by the poet in a melodious manner.

To date, recent studies on Covid-19 have shown a noticeable surge of interest in the study of pandemic poetry as an interesting topic of research. There have been limited publications in which researchers have paid attention to studying themes and ideas of pandemic poems. However, only few studies have undertaken a stylistic analysis research on Covid-19 pandemic poetry. Therefore, the current review shows that there is a gap in literature concerning linguistic and stylistic aspects of pandemic poetry. In order to fill the gap, the current study sheds the light on the language use of pandemic poetry and presents a stylistic analysis of the language used in covid-19 pandemic poetry, employing a set of isolated linguistic devices and stylistic features used for artistic purposes. It is intended to provide a better comprehension of the use of style in different types of covi-19 pandemic poems. It also seeks to realize how stylistic analysis relates medical themes to linguistic choices.

Theoretical framework

This study draws on Leech and Short’s stylistic model [12] in combination with the theory of foregrounding as its theoretical framework. It employs key aspects of their approach, including lexical and grammatical categories, figures of speech, and the concept of foregrounding, which emphasizes deviations and repetitions that enhance literary expression. These frameworks provide tools for analyzing how language is used creatively and meaningfully in the selected poems.

Leech & Short [12] model

Leech and Short present “an overall'theory'or'model'of prose style” which provides “a general informal classification of features of style as a tool of analysis which can be applied to any text” (3). They provide a heuristic checklist of linguistic and stylistic categories. The checklist works on'a systematic basis'in collecting applicable linguistic data from the text with the focus on stylistic value. It is based on four levels of language analysis. These levels include lexical categories, grammatical categories, figurative language and context and cohesion without neglecting semantic categories.

In this study, the selected poems will be analyzed in terms of lexical categories, grammatical categories and figures of speech. Lexical categories generally refer to vocabulary choices selected for certain purposes. Grammatical categories focus on the sentence structure and types of sentences. As for figures of speech, the researchers look for forgrounded features that depart from the regular rules. Along with traditional poetic figures of speech such as metaphor, simile, metonymy, synecdoche, paradox and irony, schemes and tropes are significant categories in this study. In his earlier A Linguistic Guide to English poetry [10], Leech defines schemes as “foregrounded repetitions of expression” and tropes as “foregrounded irregularities of content” (74). He further states that schemes include figures such as “alliteration, anaphora and chiasmus” while he refers to tropes as devices that involve alteration of the normal meaning of an expression such as “metaphor, irony and synecdoche” (74).

Foregrounding theory

Among the different stylistic theories, the foregrounding theory is considered the most familiar and influential in literature. The concept of'foregrounding'covers several meanings in stylistics. It is formally regarded as “deviation, or departure, from what is expected in the linguistic code or the social code expressed through language; functionally, it is a special effect or significance conveyed by that departure” (Leech, Language in Literature 3). Therefore, it can be used as an analytic tool employed to evaluate literary texts or to describe their literary and cultural significance (Van Peer & Hakemulder 547) [19].

Van Peer & Hakemulder state that “foregrounding” refers to “specific linguistic devices, i.e., deviation and parallelism, that are used in literary texts in a functional and condensed way” (546) [19]. According to this theory, “literature- by employing unusual forms of language-breaks up the reader's routine behavior: common place views and perspectives are replaced by new and surprising insights and sensations” (Van Peer & Hakemulder 546). In this way, such foregrounded devices can serve to heighten and deepen the meaning of the text and enable the reader to gain an aesthetic experience. The writers also associate foregrounding with new information inferred by the reader against old information that constitutes the background of the text (Van Peer & Hakemulder 547) [19]. Accordingly, in the side of analysis, the normal use of language is regarded as'background'in opposition to the foregrounded features which appear as prominent features because of their abnormality. Therefore,'foregrounding'works as a technique for attracting the reader's attention to particular prominent features in order to draw attention to what is said through how it is said.

Method of analysis

The current study describes and analyzes the stylistic devices employed in the use of the language of pandemic poems. The analysis is conducted adhering to the heuristic checklist of stylistic categories provided by Leech and Short [12] with emphasis on the foregrounding theory. It is intended to reveal the artistic quality of language use in pandemic poems with emphasis on language function of isolated stylistic features. These features include lexical choices (e.g. repetition, collocation, and rhyme), syntactic patterns (e.g. parallelisms), figures of speech (e.g. metaphor, simile, irony, overstatement and pun), and cohesive ties (e.g. substitution, ellipsis…etc.). These stylistic devices can provide significant implications for the study of pandemic poetry.

Selected poems for analysis

The Covid-19 pandemic crisis and its massive global impact managed to bring together people of different nationalities and affiliations and represented fertile ground for interdisciplinary research topics. Poetry in particular has been one of the sites of human expression of the innermost thoughts and emotions. The overwhelming effect of the Covid pandemic did find its way in the poetry of the period that was at once an outlet for the feelings of pain, uncertainty, suffering and loss and a comforting force that calms down the trauma while offering hope and solace. Covid poetry helped readers align their own memories and experiences with those expressed in the poems and thus come to terms with the challenges of the new, undesired experience while fostering empathy and compassion.

In addition to the emotional content, these poems incorporate elements that shed light on the scientific and medical aspects of the virus. From references to microscopic inspection, viral transmission to descriptions of the physical symptoms and psychological consequences, the poetry explores the intersection of medicine and human experience. Through employing several stylistic devices, poets bridge the gap between scientific, medical data and personal narrative, creating a nuanced reflection on the impact of Covid-19.

This study attempts, through stylistic analysis of Covid-19 poems, to uncover how stylistic devices serve to communicate the representation of the virus, the healthcare system during the pandemic, the physical impact of the coronavirus and its psychological ramifications as represented in the selected poems.

“Under the microscope”: representation of the virus

The science lab microscopic inspection of the Covid-19 virus found its way to the imagination of poets, who, amidst the states of fear and uncertainty, managed to visualize the unknown and unwelcome intruder. Jacqueline Pemberton, a British-Australian poet, offers in her poem “under the microscope” an imaginative representation of the virus, employing several stylistic devices, especially figures of speech, modality and parallelism. According to Leech and Short, the analogizing sense in figures of speech can be revealed through metaphor (71). The poem begins with three successive metaphors where the unknown virus is analogized to “an undiscovered planet,” “an exotic flower”, “a sea anemone” (a jellyfish-, coral-like aquatic stinging animal), all of which are syntactically parallel, implying a semantic relation.

On first sight it could have been

an undiscovered planet,

an exotic flower or a sea anemone,

We might have puzzled at its origin,

Been bewildered by its purpose. (Pemberton)

The modifiers in the syntactically parallel phrases, i.e. premodifiers (1 past participle, 1 adjective, 1 noun) + nouns, all refer to the anonymous, mysterious and uncontrollable qualities of the coronavirus.

This uncertainty is multiplied with the aspects of modality as can be seen in the first line of stanzas 1, 2: “On first sight it could have been,” “We might have puzzled,” “what might have been soft roses” (emphasis added), implying an escalation in the degree of uncertainty about what this virus really is. They are of “epistemic modality” as the speaker is signaling doubt, uncertainty and little degree of knowledge (Saeed 135). The epistemic modals, John Saeed [17] contends, also signal the speaker's judgment about the way the world is (135). The feelings of uncertainty and confusion while forming a judgement of the complex situation are even more foregrounded through the use of the evaluative adjectives “puzzled” and “bewildered.”

However, this uncertainty about what the virus really is turns into certainty about its fatal effects. The initial modals give way to “are” and “is” used as main verbs with a more detailed metaphor embodying the fierce attack of the virus:

are lethal blooms; stamens spiked

with lipid poison to pierce our defences

when we are at our weakest, (Pemberton)

This builds up and foregrounds the sense of tension and conflict between a powerful, multiplying, exotic organism, and weakened, bewildered, defenseless human bodies. The virus is depicted as a being with full agency as evident in the parallel sentence structure of the following lines:

Embeds itself into the woodwork,

Tucks into every frightened corner

and steals the cushions of our dreams. (Pemberton)

The virus constitutes the subject of transitive verbs “tucks, “embeds”, “steals”, all of which refer to forceful, and even violent action. This representation of the virus culminates int the final stanza and takes the shape of violent “hijacking” of the human body:

Latching onto the lining of our lungs

It tears straight to the heart of us,

Hijacking cells in a forced and fatal coupling,

A brutal consummation: a pitiless gestation. (Pemberton)

The lexical items pertaining to violence abound in the last two lines “Forced, fatal, brutal, pitiless” with alliteration and semi-alliteration enhancing the phonological effect.

The poem as a whole offer a representation of the virus that stresses the notion of uncertainty about its nature but ends on a note that describes the fatal outcome of its existence. This effect is stylistically achieved through the use of modality, lexical choices, parallelism, and metaphors. These foregrounded stylistic elements force the reader into a shocking awareness of the “monstrous” nature of the coronavirus, releasing the reader’s both empathy and fear.

“A recompense”: the physical impact of the virus

The actual experience of being infected by the coronavirus and having to go through all the painful symptoms constituted the subject matter for many Covid-19 poems. The Indian poet Ancy Eapen’s poem “Recompense” introduces a real-life Covid infection experience of a hospitalized man who had to endure the suffering, pain, and even the expectation of death. The poem is distinguished by its narrative element while presenting a more realistic content than the previous poem.

The poem opens with a foregrounded phonological feature, the alliterative “white walls.” The repetition of the semi vowel/w/, enacts the actual “wailing” of the patient’s physical pain.

White walls stare

at me,

As minutes go by.

Doctor ’n nurse

talking in hushed whispers, (Eapen)

The personification of the hospital white walls, the agent or the subject of the sentence who “stare at” the speaker, reduced to the object pronoun “me,” communicates the dire feeling of helplessness, physical confinement and isolation in quarantine. The onomatopoeic “hushed whispers” of the “doctor ‘n nurse” enhances the overall atmosphere of silence and isolation. The deliberately ambiguous “why” which ends the second sentence foregrounds the sense of loss and uncertainty. The overall visual and auditory images drawn in the first few lines reflect the confusion and the disturbed perception of an isolated patient, overwhelmed with pain.

The speaker moves on to hyperbolically account for the physical symptoms he is experiencing in two syntactically parallel sentences, each of which constitutes a simile:

My chest pains.

as if being pricked.

by a thousand needles.

My mind seems.

to be falling off.

A steep precipice..

His “chest pain” is compared to the painful “prick[ing] by a thousand needles” while his mind is deteriorating in a way analogous to falling off a steep cliff. The syntactic parallelism, the two similes and the hyperboles underscore the severity of the speaker’s health condition and the physical impact of the virus.

The patient is held captive by the coronavirus. Away from his “wife” and “two children,” the quarantined speaker “lie[s] … isolated” and helpless in the face of his overwhelming pain:

My wife, my two children.

all left behind—

I lie here, isolated.

in antiseptic confinement.

The oxygen mask.

Clamped to my nostrils.

seem more suffocating..

I cry out..

And no sound is heard. (Eapen).

The polysyllabic “antiseptic” and “confinement” are lexically foregrounded, linking the supposedly life-guarding medical sterilization to the feeling of imprisonment. On the lexico-semantic level, paradox is used twice to account for the speaker’s inability to comprehend or cope with his deteriorating health. The “oxygen mask” paradoxically becomes “more suffocating”; his loud cries are without “sound”. The use of sensory images in the above lines, especially the visual, olfactory and auditory, serve to contribute to the creation of a vivid physical reality of the Covid experience, which many readers can relate to.

Towards the end of the poem, the speaker finally comes to the realization that his “soul is getting ready/to leave the diseased body” and his heart is “awaiting for the final signal/from the brain/to stop the beat.” The lexical repetition of the possessive pronoun “my” preceding “heart”, “chest”, family, “two children”, etc., is juxtaposed with the use of the definite article “the” in “the diseased body”, “the brain”, as if they no longer belong to the speaker, implying a total loss of control and submission to the life-annihilating virus.

Just like in Pemberton’s poem, Eapen’s “Recompense” highlights the agency of the virus in the face of the defenseless human victim. However, in Eapen’s poem, attention is shifted from the attempt to discover and understand the epidemic, to a more detailed depiction of its physical results. Alliteration, paradox, personification, imagery in addition to specific phonological and syntactic features, all contribute to the creation of the poem’s meaning and effect. The stylistically foregrounded features of the poem collectively force the readers into recalling and reliving the traumatic Covid experience. The strong sensory images in particular engage the reader's senses, making the poem more influential and memorable.

“Personal neuroses and pandemic”: Covid-triggered psychological disorders

In her poem “Personal neuroses and pandemic,” the American poet Wendy Butler sheds light on the psychological impact of the global pandemic and the traumatic experience that people had to go through. According to Pierre Janet, one cause of neurosis is when the mental force of a traumatic event is stronger than what someone can counter using their normal coping mechanisms (Nickerson) [13]. The overwhelmingly stressful situations imposed by the pandemic were definitely beyond most people’s ability to cope, causing psychological problems and traumas of varying degrees. The persona’s neurotic symptoms are stylistically presented through elements like fragmented and inconsistent speech patterns, excessive lexical repetition, ellipsis, paradox, ambiguity, absence of punctuation marks (except for 3 question marks).

Starting with two declarative sentences, semantically connected with a causal relation, the initial sentences sound super-logical, making the point that this is a time for practical, rational, and realistic action because “People are dying.”

There is no time to obsessively self-reflect.

People are dying.

There is only time (Butler).

These are followed by the elliptical statement “There is only time” which sounds like a sentence fragment as the readers would expect a completion responding to the question ‘time for what?’.

What follows instead is a pause followed by a series of rhetorical – and ironically self-reflective—questions, signaling an abrupt and unexpected shift in tone, diametrically opposing the initial logical and practical propositions. The contradictory ideas and attitudes expressed by the speaker throw doubt on his/her mental and psychological stability as shown in the rest of the poem:

Why didn’t I say that?

Have you got it did you recover?

Why did I do that and what can it mean? (Butler)

This neurotic tendency of the persona is particularly evident in lines 4 and 6 in the lexical repetition of “why” (lines 4, 6), the ambiguity of the antecedent(s) of the repeated anaphoric references “that” and “it”, and the fact that speaker is actually asking himself/herself. In line 5, however, the pronoun “it” in “have you got it” can be understood as reference to the coronavirus as it is promptly followed by asking about recovery:

The absence of a question mark between the two successive questions (line 5), together with the lack of periods at the end of all sentences, underscore the speaker’s anxiety and lack of control over speech, which can be read as a compulsive talking disorder, most probably caused by the traumatic pandemic experience.

A further change in tone accompanies the shift from the interrogative to the imperative sentence type in lines 7:

Don’t get too close.

I wish I could perfect connect (Butler).

Although it is not clear whether the addressee is the persona or another character, the hyperbolic “too close” preceded by the negation “don’t” highlights the speaker’s obsession with social distancing as a preventive measure against Covid19 infection. A contradictory statement immediately follows, expressing an apparently impossible “wish” for “connect[ing].” The lexical choice of the word “perfect” accentuates the speaker’s compulsory behavior and obsessive thoughts of “perfection.”

In an attempt to regain control over the conflicting desires, the persona resorts to the imperative mood again in lines 9 and 10. He/she changes his/her mind again and starts with opposing and contradicting what has just been said indicated by the use of “No”, followed by the imperative “listen” and 3 lexically parallel imperative verbs all denoting a repetition of an action of self-control and equanimity.

No listen

Re-emerge, replenish, reconstruct (Butler)

The concluding lines epitomize the poem’s ongoing conflict and contradiction through a final paradox.

Time will tell

Time won’t tell anything (Butler)

Leech and Short in their book A Linguistic Guide to English Poetry [10] describe paradox as “a statement which is absurd because self-evidently false” (132). The absurdity of the underlying paradox, the lexical repetition of “time” at the beginning and end of the poem (4 times), and the hyperbole of utter hopelessness at the end “anything” all reflect the persona’s obsessive fear of “time”. Time, during Covid19 pandemic, had contradictory meanings: time carried more deaths, more infection cases, and undiscovered and continuous mutations. Simultaneously, time carried the possibility of discovering a cure, or a vaccine or ending the lockdown.

Unlike the previous poems, Butler’s poem stylistically enacts the psychological trauma of the pandemics. Fragmentation and repetition, ellipsis, paradox, ambiguity, together with graphological features are some of the stylistic tools through which the poem managed to communicate the perplexity and the neurotic tendencies caused by the Covid pandemic. The poem depicts the psychological turmoil that many readers who went through the Covid experience can understand and reflect on.

“Pas de pandemic”: Covid and the healthcare system

The Covid-19 pandemic dramatically influenced healthcare systems worldwide. This new pandemic placed unprecedented strain on hospitals and medical staff. Molly Headley, an American based in Paris, offers in her poem “Pas De Pandemic” an ingenious representation of the healthcare service during the pandemic. She argues that “from a great distance, with no context or emotion, the rush and routines of a hospital staff treating patients could look like a choreographed dance” (Headley). The poem offers a variety of conceptual and linguistic peculiarities and hence is selected for stylistic analysis.

Initially, what strikes the reader from the very beginning is the use of two different languages, namely French and English. The poem develops through the tension between the implications of the expressions of both. The title “Pas De Pandemic” foreshadows this tension through mixing both languages in one phrase that negates the existence of the pandemic. It is particularly telling that the negation “Pas de” is in French while the English word “pandemic” is in English. This duality and the implied tension are continued through the 4 stanzas of the poem. The title prefigures the upcoming tension between the abstract, dream world, the one of beauty and enjoyment as we are going to see later, and the cruelty and harshness of the Covid-19 reality. The author herself argues that she was inspired by “the human need to make the actualities of dying from this disease more abstract.”

The poem consists of 21 lines and is divided into 4 stanzas of unequal lengths: 2 lines, 6 lines, 7 lines, 6 lines respectively. This division of the poem is a clear deviation from the traditional stanzaic norms of English poetry. Furthermore, the poem does not follow a specific rhyming pattern nor a particular metrical form. The most foregrounded linguistic feature lies in the fact that the first line of each stanza refers to some of the basic steps of ballet dance in French and this is followed by what would be read as a tentative English (literal) translation that refers to the “the rush and routines of a hospital staff treating patients”, which, according to Headley, “could look like a choreographed dance.”

The poem reads as follows:

1.

tomber/pas de bourrée/glissade/jeté

fall down/stumble like a drunk/glide/throw it away

2.

tomber/pas de bourrée/brisé volé

this is about falling

it’s about the bones of the feet fighting the floor

it’s about being broken mid-flight

it’s about movement or maybe it’s not

translations twist the tongue.

3.

porte des bras/échappé/échappé/échappé/contretemps

In the end, the arms flail out as if carried off

by the bloom of a hot air balloon

try to grab it, circle it round

escape, escape, escape, they say

they reach out

beating against time.

4.

encore/tomber/…

like a nightingale, a voice wakes the dark

tilling the rows of bleached beds

ventilating butterflies

falling once more

silent

Graphologically, the poem has some peculiar features in terms of capitalization and punctuation. In the first stanza no punctuation marks are used as it consists mainly of verbs in the imperative form separated by slashes. The second stanza consists of 5 lines, 1 sentence each, that equally deviate from the graphological rules of capitalization and punctuation except for the period at the end of the last line. The third stanza, which is the longest, is graphologically foregrounded. It includes 1 instance of capitalization at the beginning of the second line, 1 comma in the same line, 1 more comma in the fourth, 3 commas in the fifth and a period at the end of the stanza. The fourth and last stanza display no use of capitalization and only 1 comma. The ellipsis indicated by the three periods “…” at the end of the first line draws attention to what is unsaid or intentionally omitted. Although no periods are used, the use of a single word at the end of the poem is a distinct graphological feature. “[S]ilent,”a single word line, acts like a period that signals the end of the poem, denoting the stop of all movements and sounds which can be interpreted as death.

Structurally, the second stanza in particular is foregrounded through parallelism. The sentences in this stanza are structurally parallel and follow a predominantly simple subject and predicate structure:

this is about falling

it’s about the bones of the feet fighting the floor

it’s about being broken mid-flight

it’s about movement or maybe it’s not

translations twist the tongue. (Headley)

What is special about the subject in the above lines is that the pronouns are used as “dummy subjects” that have no specific referent. Both lexical repetition and structural parallelism draw further attention to this undefined subject. The use of the coordination conjunction “or” together with the modal “maybe” implies uncertainty and indefiniteness. A possible implication is that there is an omnipresent though unidentified entity that resists definition which is a vibrant reference to the Covid-19 situation. This implication is enhanced in the third stanza which depicts the efforts of the medical staff to control the situation quickly in the same way a ballet dancer seems to grab and circle the air in the first position.

Many phonological features are employed for specific effects throughout the poem. in stanza 2, the alliteration of the fricative/f/in falling, feet, fighting, floor, flight might imply suffering during movement, aided by the alliteration of the/b/sound in Bones, being, broken. The repetition of the nasal/m/sound and the alteration with the nasal/n/creates an effect of moaning and pain. In stanza 3, another phonological feature is employed through the use of long vowels and diphthongs in “flail, bloom, balloon, reach, beating, against, time” which gives the effect of a prolonged or a slow process that is done with difficulty in the medical staff struggle against time, trying to save patients’ lives.

Apart from the lexically foregrounded technical jargon of ballet, the poem is written mostly in simple, mono- or di-syllabic words (fall down, drunk, bones, feet) except for a few complex words in the last stanza: nightingale, ventilating, butterflies. The lexical choices underscore both the similarities and the conflict between the realm of dance and crude actualities of hospitals and health workers during the pandemic. The verb “Stumble”, for example, which means “to trip or momentarily lose one's balance” or “to walk unsteadily or clumsily” is juxtaposed with the smooth and structured ballet movements, juxtaposing the accidental, awkward and clumsy stumbling with the carefully organized movements of the ballet dance that follow specific rules regarding the positions of the arms, feet, and body. The lexical repetition of “escape” in stanza 3 after the verbs “grab” and “circle” preceded by “try”, implies a failure to control the pandemic or to escape or evade infection.

As for figurative language, the poem as a whole can be seen as an extended metaphor where the movements of hospital staff are compared to ballet steps and movements. Another predominant figure of speech is simile, which is used in stanzas 1, 3 and 4. Using both figures of speech encapsulates the desire to make this unknown, abstract entity of the pandemic more fathomable.

Headley’s poem stands out among the selected poems as more abstract. What is depicted here is not the virus itself, nor its physical or psychological outcomes. The actual medical staff efforts and the realities of death are represented in an abstract manner, through analogizing them to the choreographed ballet dance. Several lexical, graphological, structural and phonological features contribute to the meaning and effect of the poem. These specific features immerse the reader in the world of the poem, making them think deeply about their own experiences with hospitals and healthcare during Covid, while connecting them to the struggles and emotions captured in the verses.

Conclusion

The selected poems that focus on the Covid pandemic effectively convey medical themes through a diverse range of stylistic devices. These poems intricately weave together the realms of medicine, poetry, and stylistics, creating a rich interdisciplinary ground of understanding and reflection. The scientific exploration of the virus, along with its physical and psychological effects, and the complexities of the healthcare system during the pandemic, serve as central themes in the selected poems. The selected poems highlight the challenges and experiences faced by medical professionals and patients alike.

Among the stylistic devices employed throughout these works, repetition, figurative language, paradoxes, hyperbole, and parallel structures are frequently utilized. These techniques effectively intensify the predominant sentiments of uncertainty, confusion, anxiety, and suffering that have become synonymous with the Covid-19 pandemic. By employing these stylistic tools, the poets not only articulate their ideas but also evoke profound emotional responses from readers, inviting them to deeply engage with the themes presented.
