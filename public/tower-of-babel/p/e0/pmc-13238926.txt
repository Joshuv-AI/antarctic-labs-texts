Expanding the scope of pediatric epilepsy surgery: Access, indications, and outcomes in a modern cohort
Authors: John R McLaren, William Muñoz, Hunki Kwon, Alexandra L Geffrey, Pranav Nanda, Ronald Thibert, Lily Grossmann, Steven Stufflebeam, Camilo Jaimes, Britt Carlson Emerton, Kristopher T Kahle, R Mark Richardson, Catherine J Chu

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveExpanded indications, diagnostic tools, and treatment options have transformed the landscape of modern pediatric epilepsy surgery. INTRODUCTION

Epilepsy is a common neurological disorder impacting 1%–1.5% of children. 1 Approximately one‐third of children with epilepsy continue to have uncontrolled seizures despite maximal medical therapy. 2 , 3 For these patients, an overwhelming body of evidence supports the use of neurosurgical intervention to improve outcomes. 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 Historically, epilepsy surgery has been an underutilized option, attributed to knowledge gaps, complex evaluations, and socioeconomic bias impacting access. 9 We consider all children with drug‐refractory epilepsy (DRE) to be candidates for some form of surgical therapy.

In the pediatric population, the landscape of epilepsy surgery has evolved over the last decade. Advanced diagnostic imaging—including higher‐resolution MRI and advanced functional techniques—has improved detection of subtle pathologies, accurate localization of functional brain tissue, and improved surgical targeting. 6 , 10 Utilization of stereotactic EEG has enabled evaluation of deeper or non‐lesional foci with better safety than subdural grids. 11 , 12 In parallel, treatment options have also expanded, including the introduction of surgical techniques such as minimally invasive laser interstitial thermal therapy (LITT), 13 deep brain stimulation (DBS), 14 and responsive neuromodulation (RNS). 14 , 15 DBS and RNS are only approved by the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) for adults (>18 years old), but off‐label use in children is becoming a standard of care in many centers. Despite this increase, data on patient selection criteria, optimal neuromodulation targets, and safety data in the pediatric population are somewhat limited.

In parallel with advances in treatment options, the indications for epilepsy surgery have expanded. In a randomized controlled trial, children with DRE who received epilepsy surgery not only had a greater likelihood of seizure‐freedom, but also had improved IQ, social and developmental outcomes, and quality of life compared to those who received continued treatment with antiseizure medications (ASMs) alone. 5 Reducing ASMs after epilepsy surgery results in further improvement in cognitive function. 16 Thus, expert consensus now recommends epilepsy surgery be offered to pediatric patients with lesional epilepsy, even in the setting of seizure control with ASM. 17 , 18 In addition, with expanded treatment options and reduced risks, neurosurgical options can be considered for children with generalized or multifocal seizures 11 or focal epileptic encephalopathy even in the absence of epileptic seizures. 8

Given these evolving paradigms and relative paucity of published data in this new environment, our study aimed to characterize a modern pediatric epilepsy surgery cohort. We examined clinical and demographic characteristics, temporal course, diagnostic evaluation, treatment choice, and 12‐month clinical outcomes. By analyzing current practice patterns, we hope to clarify how advances in surgical indications and treatments are translating into real‐world care and provide further evidence for the utility of these contemporary diagnostic and therapeutic approaches.

METHODS

Patients and characteristics

We performed retrospective analysis of 100 consecutive epilepsy surgery procedures performed within our pediatric epilepsy surgery program between February 2020 and April 2024. No patients were excluded from the analysis. Each case was discussed in the pediatric multidisciplinary epilepsy surgery conference, which included clinicians in pediatric epilepsy, functional neurosurgery, neuroradiology, and neuropsychology. Electronic medical records were retrospectively reviewed for age (months), sex, race, ethnicity, primary language, insurance, age of seizure onset, age of epilepsy diagnosis, age of first visit with an epileptologist, epilepsy type (focal, multifocal, generalized or combined), epilepsy etiology, the presence of an epileptic encephalopathy, presurgical diagnostic studies, age of presentation at surgical conference, neurosurgical procedure, pre‐ and postoperative ASMs, baseline seizure frequency, 12‐month postoperative seizure‐outcome, and any adverse events related to surgery. This project was approved by our hospital's Institutional Review Board.

Surgical procedures

Epilepsy surgical procedures included any surgical diagnostic procedures (SEEG, subdural intraoperative or extraoperative electrocorticography) and treatment procedures. Treatment procedures were further categorized as those with the goal of cure of the target seizure type (resection, ablation, and hemispherotomy) and those with the goal of seizure reduction but not cure, including corpus callosotomy, neuromodulation alone, or neuromodulation coordinated with another treatment procedure.

Outcome measures

We report on the temporal course of clinical care, what procedures occurred, and safety outcomes across 100 consecutive procedures (including each diagnostic and therapeutic procedure separately). In addition, we report clinical characteristics and outcomes for the resulting 62 therapeutic procedures in 58 patients, separating therapeutic procedures into palliative versus curative procedures. We note that while treatment procedures were recommended in the four remaining patients (one hemispherotomy and three RNS implantations in four separate subjects) following diagnostic SEEG, these were not yet completed at the time of submission, and therefore not included in the outcomes analysis. Baseline seizure frequency was defined as the mean individual seizure rate over the 12‐month period immediately preceding the treatment surgery, except when seizures occurred in clusters and caregivers provided values for clusters only. Primary outcomes for patients who underwent treatment with the goal of cure were 12‐month postoperative Engel and ILAE scores. 19 , 20 Patients with non‐disabling focal seizures only since surgery or generalized seizures only during ASM withdrawal and subsequently >1 year seizure‐freedom were considered Engel IB and ID, respectively, both of which were also categorized as ILAE 3 outcomes. If patients underwent a second procedure due to surgical failure within less than 12 months, seizure‐outcome for the first procedure was categorized as Engel IVB and ILAE 5. One patient with a developmental and/or epileptic encephalopathy (D/EE) without seizures in the 12 months prior to surgery was excluded from seizure‐outcome analysis. Primary outcome for patients who underwent RNS implantation alone or in combination with palliative resection or callosotomy, where the goal of seizure reduction, was the proportion of seizure reduction for the seizure type targeted by the RNS in the prior 28‐days at 12 months postoperatively (e.g., although one patient experienced cure from drop seizures after corpus callosotomy, seizure‐outcome analysis focused on response of focal seizures to RNS). For one patient that underwent palliative resection, Engel/ILAE outcome is reported separately. Secondary outcomes included complication rate and antiseizure medication reduction at 12 months postoperatively. Complications were identified as any adverse events that required neurosurgical intervention and/or caused clinical symptoms.

Statistical analysis

Demographic data were characterized using descriptive statistics. Proportional differences between groups were compared using χ2 tests. Differences in the means between groups were compared using one‐way ANOVA and by post hoc two‐sample t‐tests. Changes in the temporal course of surgical treatment of DRE were tested using two‐way ANOVA and post hoc paired t‐tests. Uncorrected p‐values are provided and reported as significant if they survived false discovery rate (FDR) correction with significance set at p < 0.05.

RESULTS

Surgical cohort versus local demographics

A total of 100 consecutive diagnostic and therapeutic surgical procedures for epilepsy performed on 62 unique patients were included (31 females). The patient cohort reflected the Massachusetts and New England populations 19 in terms of sex and race (p ≥ 0.7, all tests). The patient cohort included a larger proportion of Hispanic patients than the Massachusetts (p = 8.3 × 10−4) and the New England population (p < 1.0 × 10−5), and trended to include fewer patients with private insurance compared to the New England population (p = 0.06). A larger proportion of patients identified English as their primary language compared to the Massachusetts population (p = 2.8 × 10−2) but did not differ from the New England region (p = 0.7). Temporal course to surgical treatment of DRE

The temporal delay from epilepsy diagnosis to first surgical treatment procedure was a median of 6.28 years (IQR 4.22–12.06, Figure 1). The age at epilepsy diagnosis was a median of 3.10 years (IQR 1.50–7.84). Patients had their first evaluation by an epileptologist at a median age of 9.14 years (IQR 4.81–14.80). After the first evaluation by an epileptologist, patients completed a presurgical evaluation and were first presented at the multidisciplinary epilepsy surgery conference at a median age of 13.19 years (IQR 8.33–17.47). The median age at the time of the first surgical treatment procedure was 13.60 years (IQR 8.81–18.09).

The largest contribution to treatment delay for patients was the time from epilepsy diagnosis to epileptologist evaluation (median 3.04 years, IQR 1.43–5.37, range 0.00–18.37 years), which was significantly longer than the time from first conference presentation to first surgical treatment procedure (median 0.09 years, IQR 0.02–0.28, range 0.00–1.26 years; p = 1.2 × 10−9). Presurgical diagnostic workup

All 62 patients underwent a standard workup including video‐EEG and 3 T MRI. Most patients underwent neuropsychological testing (80.7%, n = 50). Across all patients, 79.0% (n = 49) underwent interictal PET, 59.7% (n = 37) underwent MEG, 24.2% (n = 15) underwent functional MRI, 16.1% (n = 10) underwent 7 T MRI, 14.5% (n = 9) underwent diffusion tensor imaging (DTI) for white matter tractography, and 8.1% (n = 5) underwent ictal and interictal SPECT as part of their presurgical evaluation.

A larger proportion of patients with focal or multifocal epilepsy underwent interictal PET (focal: 84.8%, p = 1.3 × 10−4; multifocal: 100%, p = 4.0 × 10−5) and MEG (focal: 66.7%, p = 0.003; multifocal: 73.3%, p = 0.003; combined: 100%, p = 0.02) than those with generalized‐onset only seizures (PET: 25.0%; MEG: 16.7%). More patients with any focal or multifocal epilepsy (lesional or non‐lesional) underwent PET (focal lesional: 82.8%, p = 3.9 × 10−4; focal non‐lesional: 100%, p = 0.009; multifocal lesional: 100%, p = 2.3 × 10−4; multifocal non‐lesional: 100%, p = 0.009) than those with generalized non‐lesional epilepsy (25.0%). Similarly, patients with any focal (lesional or non‐lesional) or multifocal lesional epilepsy were more likely to undergo MEG (focal lesional: 62.1%, p = 0.008; focal non‐lesional: 100%, p = 0.003; multifocal lesional: 72.7%, p = 0.007) than those with generalized non‐lesional epilepsy (16.7%). There were no detectable differences between the percentage of patients who received any neurodiagnostic test by race, ethnicity, sex, native language, insurance, epilepsy type, or lesional versus non‐lesional status. Surgical treatments and strategy

Sixty‐four surgical treatment procedures were performed in 58 patients (Figure 3; we note that treatment procedures were recommended in the remaining 4 patients but not yet performed). Thirty‐seven of the treatment procedures (57.8%, n = 34 patients) were pursued with the goal of epilepsy cure. We note that curative procedures were only pursued in patients with focal (n = 31) or multifocal epilepsy (n = 3). Three patients who did not obtain seizure‐freedom each underwent an additional curative procedure, one of whom subsequently underwent a palliative procedure. Curative procedures performed included 27 (73.0%) resections, 6 (16.2%) ablations with LITT, and 4 (10.8%) functional hemispherotomies.

Twenty‐seven of the treatment procedures (42.2%, n = 25 patients) were performed with the goal of seizure reduction without expectation of cure. Staged palliative operations performed on separate days were counted as separate procedures, and combined procedures performed on the same day were counted as one. These included 18 patients (72.0%) who underwent RNS system implantations alone without additional treatment procedure, 3 patients (12.0%) with dual craniotomy for combined resection and RNS implantation, 1 patient (4.0%) with staged resection and RNS implantation, 1 patient (4.0%) with staged RNS implantation and corpus callosotomy, and 1 patient (4.0%) with RNS implantation after two prior failed attempts at curative treatment procedures (1 LITT followed by 1 resection). There was also one patient (4.0%) with multifocal epilepsy who underwent palliative resection. RNS targets included bilateral centromedian nucleus of the thalamus in 66.6% (n = 16), bilateral centromedian and pulvinar nucleus of the thalamus in 8.3% (n = 2), left frontal cortical seizure focus in 8.3% (n = 2), bilateral pulvinar nucleus of the thalamus in 4.2% (n = 1), right centromedian nucleus of the thalamus with right frontal cortical seizure focus in 4.2% (n = 1), left pulvinar nucleus of the thalamus with left temporal cortical seizure focus in 4.2% (n = 1), and bilateral hippocampi in 4.2% (n = 1). There were no VNS or DBS procedures performed in this cohort.

Surgical efficacy across seizure types and treatments

Of the 34 patients who underwent 37 treatment procedures with the goal of cure, one patient with D/EE 8 without seizures in the 12 months prior to surgery who subsequently remained seizure‐free was excluded from the seizure‐outcome analysis. Thus, seizure outcomes are reported for only 33 patients who underwent 35 treatment procedures with the goal of cure. We note that the excluded patient remained seizure‐free after resection and that inclusion/exclusion of this subject did not impact the percent cure in this cohort. In the 33 remaining patients who underwent 35 treatment procedures, 77.1% of procedures (n = 27) and 81.8% of patients (n = 27) achieved Engel I outcomes and ILAE 1 or 3 outcomes at 12 months. 22.9% of procedures (n = 8) and 18.2% of patients (n = 6) resulted in Engel class III or IV and ILAE class 4 or 5 outcomes. Two of the eight patients with surgical failure underwent at least one repeat procedure (mean 0.03 years between procedures, range 0.01–0.05). Of these, 50.0% (n = 1) achieved a 12‐month Engel 1 and ILAE 1 outcome after the final treatment procedure (repeat resection) and 50.0% (n = 1) had an Engel IIIA and ILAE 4 outcome after the second attempted curative procedure (initial LITT followed by resection) and subsequently RNS placement and were responders. We also note that the patient who underwent staged corpus callosotomy and RNS was cured of the atonic seizures after corpus callosotomy, but underwent RNS implantation to address their focal seizures and was included in the palliative outcomes analysis. In the one patient with multifocal epilepsy who underwent resection with the goal of palliation/seizure reduction, the outcome at 12 months was Engel IIIA and ILAE 5. DISCUSSION

We report detailed characterization of 100 consecutive surgical procedures in a modern pediatric epilepsy surgery program. In our cohort, surgical treatments were offered across all epilepsy types and a broad range of epilepsy etiologies. Notably, over half of patients underwent SEEG, which accounted for over one‐third of surgical procedures. In addition, nearly half of the treatment procedures included RNS implantation. Across the expanded etiologies and treatments, 80% of patients achieved surgical goals.

This study demonstrated encouraging progress toward timely and equitable access to pediatric epilepsy surgery. Where prior studies have highlighted inequities in access, 21 , 22 , 23 , 24 , 25 our surgical cohort mirrored state and regional demographic distributions in terms of race and sex. Importantly, the proportion of Hispanic children and children with public insurance who received surgery was higher than expected based on regional demographics, indicating successful inclusion of this historically underserved population. Furthermore, no disparities were observed in access to advanced diagnostic tools or surgical timelines. In addition, time from diagnosis to surgery was just over 6 years in our cohort, much shorter than the average of two decades in one historical cohort that included children and adults. 26 Notably, several recent pediatric epilepsy studies report similar periods of 5.1–6.3 years, suggesting a consistent improvement in treatment delays in the modern era of pediatric epilepsy surgery across sites. 27 , 28 , 29 , 30 As earlier treatment optimizes developmental and seizure outcomes, more work is still required to reduce the multi‐year wait for appropriate surgical treatment. Because epilepsy surgery requires multidisciplinary collaboration, solutions also require institutional investment across disciplines. For example, at our institution, decreasing the often‐prolonged wait time for sedated imaging would require more pediatric anesthesiologists. Further, institutional resources must scale with program growth. For example, as our surgical volume has increased, more access to operating rooms and inpatient beds are required to prevent the wait time to surgical interventions from extending.

Modern pediatric epilepsy intracranial surgical practices supported broader eligibility criteria beyond traditional focal, lesional epilepsy. In our cohort, patients with generalized, multifocal, and non‐lesional epilepsy were routinely considered for surgery, and one patient underwent surgery despite seizure control on medication. 8 Thus, this cohort reflects evolving practices that emphasize cognitive outcomes in addition to seizure control. In our cohort, outcomes did not differ between lesional and non‐lesional patients, suggesting that early intervention should be considered for surgical candidates independent of MRI findings. 17 , 18 Notably, during the presurgical workup, a high proportion of patients underwent advanced diagnostic imaging including 79.0% who underwent PET and 59.7% who underwent MEG. These non‐invasive tests facilitate hypothesis building for SEEG planning and localization of the epileptogenic zone, which is often smaller than an anatomical lesion 31 , 32 and has become a routine part of presurgical planning at our institution. We also note that thalamic SEEG has become routinely used in many centers, including our own, to identify and guide placement of thalamic RNS based on patient‐specific seizure dynamics. 33 , 34 , 35 The use of advanced diagnostic testing may have contributed to expanded eligibility and excellent outcomes in this cohort. For example, it was only after advanced neurodiagnostic imaging that a previously unrecognized focal cortical dysplasia was discovered in our oldest patient (26 years), prompting SEEG implantation followed by resection with an Engel ID (ILAE 3) outcome.

Surgical seizure outcomes in this study were excellent across both curative and palliative interventions. Among patients undergoing curative surgery, 81.8% achieved Engel I outcomes at 12 months, a rate consistent with or exceeding prior multicenter reports. 27 , 36 , 37 , 38 Additionally, antiseizure medications were reduced in 69.4% of these patients, further supporting cognitive recovery and improved quality of life. Among patients treated with RNS, 79.2% were responders and 37.5% were super‐responders at 12 months, aligning with the growing evidence that RNS is an effective treatment for both focal and generalized pediatric DRE. 39 , 40 , 41 , 42 , 43 Importantly, outcomes did not differ significantly across epilepsy type, lesion status, or etiology, suggesting that modern tools enable precise targeting and effective intervention in diverse clinical contexts.

Although Engel and ILAE scores remain the gold standard, they do not adequately capture functional, cognitive, or quality‐of‐life improvements. Further, they do not capture seizure outcomes adequately for disease‐modifying interventions like RNS, where patients are expected to have increased seizure control with time. Patients with significant seizure reduction but without complete seizure‐freedom may experience meaningful life changes not reflected in these scales. This limitation supports prior calls for more holistic, pediatric‐specific outcomes assessments that include neurodevelopmental, psychosocial, and family‐centered metrics. 44 , 45 , 46

Several limitations merit discussion. First, no patients underwent diagnostic monitoring with subdural electrodes, or received VNS or DBS. As each of these surgical options is available at our institution, this cohort reflects our program's evolution in treatment strategy away from these modalities but may not reflect others' practices. In the analysis of treatment delay, while we were able to conclude that the largest contribution to delay was time from epilepsy diagnosis to epileptologist evaluation, we were not able to resolve time from epilepsy presentation to diagnosis of drug refractory epilepsy in our dataset; this would have helped clarify the most actionable timepoints to target for improvements, as has been demonstrated in prior reports. 26 Follow‐up duration was limited to 12 months; longer‐term outcomes, particularly for RNS and cognitive development, remain to be evaluated. Although we note that no family suffered undue financial burdens due to RNS surgery, insurance denials were common and required appeals to be overturned. Additionally, as this was a retrospective study, estimates of seizure frequency and antiseizure medication reduction relied on chart‐review and caregiver reporting. Meanwhile, despite data supporting viable and safe ASM withdrawal initiation in children who are seizure‐free postoperatively, 47 the practice of post‐surgical ASM withdrawal in children after epilepsy surgery remains controversial and lacks recognized standards 48 ; children who underwent curative treatment surgeries had a mean reduction of 2 ASMs by 1 year postoperatively. This reflects our practice to aggressively reduce ASMs in order to further optimize the cognitive benefits of surgery by minimizing exposure to the negative impact of ASMs on cognitive function in the developing brain. 8 , 49 , 50 We generally initiate the ASM taper after twice the duration of the seizure‐free interval in the year prior to surgery has elapsed. Thus, medication taper started within the first month postoperatively in some children and in the first 3 months in most; therefore, our center's practice may not be reflective of others. Furthermore, given our cohort size, we had insufficient power to detect differences in outcomes across treatment modalities. Future work including multi‐institutional collaborations is required to evaluate these effects and fully describe the entire epilepsy surgical panorama currently available in pediatric epilepsy.

Significance

Overall, this study provides compelling evidence that modern pediatric epilepsy surgery is safe and effective across a range of epilepsy types and procedures, including RNS use that is currently off‐label. Further, in our cohort, the expanded indications and treatment options coincide with improved access and decreased treatment delay without compromising outcome. Thus, these data indicate encouraging progress in pediatric epilepsy care through modern surgical approaches, which provide more options for a larger group of children who have not historically been considered good surgical candidates.
