A novel broad-spectrum phage lysin CP02 for biocontrol of Clostridium perfringens in poultry meat
Authors: Yuqing Zhou, Hanli Kang, Li Qin, Yifeng Ding, Yalu Ji, Wenyu Han, Jingmin Gu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Clostridium perfringens (C. perfringens) is a major foodborne pathogen, and the increasing prevalence of antimicrobial-resistant strains necessitates novel control strategies to prevent foodborne illnesses. In this study, ten novel phage lysins were identified in the whole-genome sequences of C. perfringens and C. perfringens phages. The homology of these lysins with other reported C. perfringens phage lysins was no more than 27%. Among these, CP02 exhibited the broadest lytic range, lysing 100% (93/93) of tested C. perfringens strains representing toxin types A, B, C, D, and G in spot assays, while showing no activity against non-target bacteria. Meanwhile, CP02 demonstrated rapid bactericidal activity, achieving a 3.17–5.04 Lg CFU/mL reduction in brain heart infusion (BHI) liquid medium within 15 min at 200 μg/mL. CP02 retained over 80% activity at 4–55 °C and 35.6% activity at 75 °C, maintained over 70% activity across pH 3–10 and NaCl concentrations up to 1000 mM, showing robust environmental tolerance. Furthermore, the activity of CP02 was independent of the presence of metal ions, and D8, Y60, E96 and N135 were identified as essential catalytic sites. CP02 at 50 μg/mL inhibited biofilm formation by 55.88–71.97% and removed 67.28-78.33% of pre-formed mature biofilms. In food matrices, CP02 (200 μg/mL) reduced C. perfringens counts by 2.05–2.59 Lg CFU/mL in chicken breast and duck meat at 25 °C, and by 1.46–2.34 Lg CFU/mL at 4 °C. These results demonstrated that CP02 is a promising biocontrol agent to control C. perfringens contamination in poultry products.

Introduction

C. perfringens is a major foodborne pathogen capable of producing more than 17 toxins and is classified into seven types (A–G) based on its toxin profile (Kiu and Hall, 2018). Among these, type A is primarily responsible for food poisoning (Feng et al., 2010; Stanley et al., 2012; Van Immerseel, De Buck, Pasmans, Huyghebaert, Haesebrouck and Ducatelle, 2004). Pathogenesis occurs following the ingestion of heavily contaminated food (>106 CFU/g), which leads to sporulation in the intestine and the release of C. perfringens enterotoxin (CPE) (Li & Mcclane). This enterotoxin induces acute gastroenteritis characterized by diarrhea and abdominal distension, and severe cases may progress to intestinal hemorrhage, necrosis, and death (H. Liu et al., 2016).

In recent years, foodborne outbreaks caused by C. perfringens have been increasingly reported worldwide. The China National Center for Food Safety Risk Assessment (CFSA) documented multiple outbreaks in 2025 linked to school canteens and banquets, primarily involving chicken, duck, and other meat products. Surveillance data indicate that C. perfringens accounts for more than one million foodborne outbreaks annually in the United States and more than four million cases globally (Freedman et al., 2016; Scallan Walter, Cui, Tierney, Griffin, Hoekstra and Payne, 2025). A large-scale outbreak in Norway involving ready-to-eat meat products affected more than 100 individuals (Bhattacharya et al., 2020). Additionally, C. perfringens exhibits multidrug resistance, with significantly elevated resistance rates to tetracycline (45.2%), clindamycin (38.7%), and chloramphenicol (22.6%) among food and animal isolates, further intensifying its public health threat (Fang et al., 2025).

Traditional control strategies for C. perfringens contamination include chemical preservatives (e.g., nitrate, nitrite, and organic acid salts) and physical methods (e.g., heat treatment, and refrigeration) (Ji et al., 2025; Zhou et al., 2022). However, these approaches have notable drawbacks. Chemical agents may alter food flavor and texture, pose potential health risks, and promote bacterial tolerance (Bajpai et al., 2012; Mokgatla et al., 2002; Pitino et al., 2023; Żbikowska et al., 2020). Physical methods such as heating can degrade heat-sensitive nutrients and impair sensory quality (Nam et al., 2002). Moreover, many conventional antimicrobial agents exhibit broad-spectrum activity that may indiscriminately affect beneficial microbiota in food matrices (Nowak et al., 2016). These limitations underscore the urgent need for safe, efficient, and environmentally friendly alternatives.

Phage lysins are peptidoglycan hydrolases encoded by bacteriophages that specifically cleave bacterial cell walls (Ha et al., 2018; Schmelcher and Loessner, 2016). In the context of increasing antimicrobial resistance, lysins have emerged as promising substitutes due to their target specificity, high efficacy, and rapid bactericidal action (Fathima and Archer, 2021; Lee et al., 2023). Compared with whole phages, lysins generally exhibit a broader lytic spectrum—spanning strains, species, and even related genera—thereby overcoming the inherent lytic-range limitation of phages (Kim et al., 2022; Yang et al., 2021). Recent studies have demonstrated the potential of lysins in food safety applications. For instance, ClyH, a lysin that targets Staphylococcus aureus (S. aureus), effectively eliminated bacteria in milk and cheese while maintaining stability under various temperature and pH conditions (Yang et al., 2014). LysSTG2 showed efficacy against Pseudomonas aeruginosa and Pseudomonas putida in contaminated chicken breast and water (AuthorAnonymous et al., 2021). Our group previously identified Lys19, a phage lysin effective against Streptococcus suis (S. suis) and C. perfringens in pork and lettuce, with good pH and thermal stability (Ji et al., 2025). However, Lys19 possesses a relatively narrow lytic range, and its antibacterial activity against C. perfringens in poultry meat—particularly chicken and duck—remains unverified. As proteinaceous agents, lysins do not compromise food sensory attributes and are digestible in the human gastrointestinal tract, resulting in no residual risks. Therefore, the discovery and characterization of novel broad-spectrum lysins are critical for controlling foodborne pathogen contamination.

In this study, we identified and characterized a novel broad-spectrum lysin, CP02, that targets C. perfringens. Its biological properties and lytic activity were evaluated, and the three-dimensional (3D) structures of its functional domains were predicted to identify key amino acid residues critical for catalytic activity. Furthermore, the antibiofilm capacity of CP02 and its ability to inhibit bacterial growth in food matrices (chicken breast and duck meat) were assessed. These findings provide a theoretical basis for the application of lysins as biocontrol agents to mitigate bacterial contamination in food products.

Materials and methods

Bacterial strains and materials

A total of sixty C. perfringens isolates were obtained from fecal and tissue samples collected from pig, cattle, and poultry farms in Jilin Province. The standard strain ATCC13124 was obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC), and strains CVCC54, CVCC56, CVCC64, CVCC1168, and CVCC2030 were obtained from the China Veterinary Culture Collection Center (CVCC). The other 31 strains of C. perfringens were acquired from Liaocheng University (Shandong, China). Sixteen strains of Escherichia coli (E. coli), 20 strains of Salmonella, 10 strains of Staphylococcus aureus, and 10 strains of Listeria monocytogenes (L. monocytogenes) were acquired from laboratory collections. Comprehensive details of the bacterial strains used in this research, which were preserved at −80 °C in 20% glycerol, are provided in Supplementary Table S1.

Yeast extract, tryptone and BCA protein assay kits were obtained from Thermo Scientific (Waltham, USA). Sodium chloride (NaCl) was obtained from Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Hi-Tech Park Haibo Biotechnology Co., Ltd. provided BHI broth, Tryptose Sulfite Cycloserine (TSC) agar base, Columbia blood agar plates, round bottom vertical anaerobic culture bags, and anaerobic gas-producing bags. A HisPur Ni-NTA column was obtained from GE HealthCare Technologies, Inc. (Chicago, USA), and DH5α- and BL21 (DE3)-competent cells were obtained from Tiangen Biotech Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China). Isopropyl β-D-1-thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG) was procured from Sigma‒Aldrich Corporation (St. Louis, Missouri, USA). The vectors pET-15b and pDBHisMBP-3C(B11) were purchased from Sangon Biotech Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China), and ampicillin sodium and kanamycin were acquired from Solarbio Science & Technology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China).

Mining, expression, and purification of C. perfringens phage lysins

A total of 10 sequences predicted to encode phage lysins were identified from the complete genomes of C. perfringens and C. perfringens phages, which were retrieved from the database of the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI, USA). The amino acid sequences of these proteins were uploaded to NCBI for BLASTP analysis and compared with the amino acid sequences of 30 previously reported C. perfringens lysins using DNAMAN software. Proteins with low amino acid sequence homology were used for construction, expression and purification. The sequence information is listed in Supplementary Table S2.

The genes encoding lysin CP01, lysin CP02, lysin CP03, lysin CP04, lysin CP0530, lysin CP0730, lysin CP0830, lysin CP0930, lysin CP1030 and lysin CP1830 were synthesized and cloned, and inserted into the pET-15b vector or pDBHisMBP-3C (B11) vector. The resulting recombinant plasmids were transformed into E. coli BL21 (DE3) for cultivation in Luria–Bertani (LB) medium supplemented with 1 mg/mL ampicillin or kanamycin at 37 °C. Protein production was induced with 1 mM IPTG at 16 °C for 17 h. The cells were then harvested by centrifugation (8000×g, 4 °C), resuspended in PBS (pH 7.5), and lysed by sonication. The lysate was purified by HisPur Ni-NTA chromatography, and the eluted protein was concentrated via 0.22 μm ultrafiltration (3500×g, 4 °C). Imidazole was removed by a phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) wash step during centrifugation (3500×g, 40 min, 4 °C). Finally, protein purity and concentration were assessed by 12% sodium dodecyl sulfate‒polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS‒PAGE) and a BCA assay, respectively, before the purified proteins were flash-frozen and stored at −80 °C.

Antibacterial range of C. perfringens phage lysins

The antibacterial spectrum of the purified lysins was evaluated in accordance with previously described methods (Ma et al., 2022; R. Zhao et al., 2024). Briefly, a 100 μL aliquot of each tested bacterial suspension was combined with 10 mL of LB semisolid medium (0.6% agar), and then the mixture was evenly poured onto tryptone yeast (TY) or LB solid medium. Ten microliters of each purified lysin were spotted onto an agar plate after drying, and the plate was incubated anaerobically at 37 °C for 18–24 h. A strain was considered susceptible to CP02 when a transparent or semi-transparent lysis zone was observed, scoring as 0 to 4 according to the criteria described as follows. "+4″ indicates a completely transparent patch; "+3″ indicates that the patch is mostly transparent but with a slight hazy background; "+2″ means there is obvious turbidity in the clear dissolution area; "+1″ indicates a single opaque patch; "0″ means there is no dissolution area. The lysis rate was calculated as the percentage of the strains that produced a clear zone (score ≥ "+1″) in the spot assay relative to the total number of strains tested.

Lytic activity of lysin CP02

To assess the immediate lytic effect of CP02, bacterial suspensions of CBCP-N04, CBCP-N06, LHCP-N01, SLCP-N04, cp05, cp11, cp12, cp15, cp24, CVCC54, CVCC56, CVCC64, CVCC1168, CVCC2030, and ATCC13124 were incubated with 200 μg/mL of CP02 under anaerobic conditions at 37 °C for 15 min, respectively. The resulting decrease in bacterial viability relative to that in the PBS control group was used as the evaluation metric.

To monitor the kinetic effects, bacterial suspensions (OD600nm ≈ 0.6∼0.8) of C. perfringens cp12, cp15, ATCC13124 and CVCC2030 were cocultivated with 50, 100, and 200 μg/mL CP02 anaerobically at 37 °C. Aliquots were collected every 15 min for 1 h, serially diluted in sterile PBS, and plated for viable count determination.

Stability of lysin CP02

CP02 stability was evaluated under a range of environmental and chemical conditions, including temperature (4–75 °C), pH (3–11), NaCl concentration (0–1000 mM), and the presence of ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA). In the pH and NaCl stability assays, CP02 (100 μg/mL) was resuspended in PBS adjusted to the corresponding pH or NaCl level and incubated with a C. perfringens cp12 bacterial suspension (OD600nm ≈ 0.6∼0.8) anaerobically at 37 °C for 1 h. Temperature stability was assessed by preincubating CP02 at different temperatures (4–75 °C) for 1 h before anaerobic coculture with the bacteria. To examine metal ion dependence, CP02 was pretreated with 100 mM EDTA and then desalted using a 10 kDa molecular weight cutoff (MWCO) ultrafiltration device (3500×g, 15 min) to remove EDTA. Following each treatment, viable colonies were enumerated on BHI agar plates. PBS buffer served as the blank control in all the experiments. Relative activity was expressed as the percentage of CP02 activity under each test condition relative to its maximum observed activity.

Biofilm experiments

Biofilm formation capacity

The biofilm formation capacities of 51 selected C. perfringens strains were assessed using a microtiter plate assay (Ming et al., 2025). Briefly, 100 μL of each bacterial suspension (1 × 106 CFU/mL) was mixed with an equal volume of BHI and transferred to individual wells of a 96-well plate. In addition, 200 μL of BHI was used as a blank control. Next, the samples were washed three times with PBS after they were incubated anaerobically at 37 °C for 48 h, fixed with methanol, stained with crystal violet (CV) and dried. Afterward, glacial acetic acid was added, and the samples were incubated at 37 °C. Biofilm formation was assessed by measuring the OD590nm value and classified as strong, moderate, weak, or absent according to previously established criteria (Xi et al., 2024).

In order to ensure sufficient stability of the biofilm, we extended the biofilm formation time was extended to 72 h for six strong biofilm-forming strains (cp29, HNCP-Z01, HNCP-Z04, HNCP-Z05, HNCP-Z06, and HNCP-Z10). Biofilms were cultured anaerobically at 37 °C for 72 h, with culture medium refreshed every 24 h to maintain nutrient availability and remove planktonic cells. After 72 h, biofilm biomass was quantified by CV staining.

Preventive ability against biofilms

To evaluate biofilm inhibition, six strongly biofilm-forming strains (1 × 106 CFU/mL) were dispensed into 96-well plates (100 μL/well). The prevention groups were supplemented with 100 μL of 20 or 50 μg/mL CP02. Controls included a positive control (100 μL bacterial suspension mixed with 100 μL BHI) and a blank control (200 μL BHI). Following 48 h, 72 h of incubation at 37 °C, respectively, the residual biofilms were quantified by CV staining and OD590nm measurement. The biofilm inhibition rate (BIR) was proposed to describe the preventive effect of CP02 on the formation of biofilms by C. perfringens. BIR was calculated as follows:(1)BIR = [(OD590nm control - OD590nmtreatment)/OD590nm control] × 100%.Where OD590nm control and OD590nm treatment represent the OD590nm of the blank control group and the CP02 treatment group, respectively.

Clearance capability against biofilms

Following formation, the biofilms were gently rinsed three times with PBS. CP02 was then applied at 20 and 50 μg/mL to the clearance groups, and an equal volume of PBS was added to the positive control groups. After a 1 h anaerobic incubation at 37 °C, the remaining biofilm was stained with crystal violet and quantified by measuring the OD590nm. The biofilm clearance rate (CIR) was used to describe the elimination effect of CP02 on the formation of biofilms by C. perfringens. CIR was calculated as follows:(2)CIR = [(OD590nm control - OD590nmtreatment)/OD590nm control] × 100%.Where OD590nm control and OD590nm treatment represent the OD590nm of the blank control group and the CP02 treatment group, respectively.

Prediction of 3D structure models and analysis of essential amino acids

The amino acid sequence of CP02 was analyzed for conserved domains using multiple bioinformatics tools. Briefly, HMMER v3.3.2 was used to search against the Pfam-A database (v38.1). InterProScan v 5.77-108.0 was employed to query against integrated databases including Pfam, SMART, and CDD for comprehensive domain annotation. The three-dimensional structure of each domain was predicted using AlphaFold2 and visualized using PyMOL v2.5.4.

Putative key residues for enzymatic activity were inferred from SYBYL software analysis and the literature (Tamai et al., 2014). Next, the predicted sites were mutated to alanine and these mutant plasmids were transformed into E. coli BL21 (DE3)-receptive cells. The successfully constructed mutant plasmids were subsequently expressed and purified as previously detailed. The primers used in this study are listed in Supplementary Table S3. The reaction system and operation procedures for constructing the CP02 mutants are presented in Supplementary Tables S4 and S5.

To identify the key amino acid residues in CP02, the bactericidal activity of its mutant variants was assayed by the colony counting method. Briefly, approximately 40 min of anaerobic cultivation at 37 °C was performed on mutant protein (100 μg/mL) mixed with C. perfringens cp12. The mixture was subjected to gradient dilution, and the bactericidal activity was determined by counting the viable bacteria. PBS was added as a negative control, and native CP02 was added as a positive control.

Application of lysin CP02 on chicken breast and duck meat

The raw chicken breast and duck meat used in the experiments was purchased from a supermarket at Jilin University in Changchun, China. The sample processing steps were adjusted appropriately according to previous methods (Rafiq et al., 2025; X. Yao et al., 2025). Prior to use, the chicken breast and duck meat were sterilized by spraying with 70% ethanol and then rinsed with sterile PBS to remove impurities. Next, the meat was cut into small pieces (1 × 1 cm) and placed in sterile culture dishes. Afterward, each side of the meat surface was exposed to ultraviolet light for 30 min to achieve sterilization. Prior to inoculation, the sterility of all the meat samples was verified by homogenization with PBS, plating on BHI agar, and confirming that no endogenous C. perfringens or other contaminating bacteria were present. To assess the bactericidal activity of CP02 in food models, sterile chicken and duck meat samples were inoculated with C. perfringens cp12 (1 × 105 CFU/mL, 100 μL), dried for 30 min in a biosafety cabinet, and topically treated with 100 μL of CP02 (200 μg/mL). The control groups were administered only PBS. All the samples were incubated anaerobically at 25 °C (3–24 h) or 4 °C (12–72 h). Bacterial survival was determined at specified intervals using PBS-based serial dilution and plate counting.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics. For experiments independently repeated at least three times, the data are expressed as the mean ± standard error of the mean. Intergroup differences were assessed by one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey's test. Significance was defined as P < 0.05.

Lytic ranges of C. perfringens phage lysins

Lytic range profiling was conducted on 3 g-positive bacteria and 2 g-negative bacteria, namely, C. perfringens, S. aureus, L. monocytogenes, E. coli and Salmonella, as listed in Supplementary Table S1. The purified proteins formed bright and clear inhibition zones on most TY agar plates containing C. perfringens bacterial suspensions, indicating that lysins had bactericidal effects on these strains (Fig. S2).

Among the 10 purified lysins, CP02 exhibited the broadest lytic spectrum, producing clear lysis zones against all 93 tested C. perfringens strains, corresponding to a strain coverage rate of 100% (93/93) in the spot assay (Fig. 2). This activity encompassed five major toxin types (A, B, C, D, and G). However, notable differences were observed in the isolation numbers and distribution among different toxin types. Among the strains, type A constituted the predominant susceptible group, comprising 69 strains (74.2% of the total) and harboring the cpa gene, with a broad lytic range including humans, poultry, swine, bovine, and ovine, which was associated with gas gangrene and necrotic enteritis. Type C accounted for 22.58% (21/93) of the cases and carried the cpa and cpb genes; this type mainly infected piglets, calves, and lambs and caused necrotizing enteritis and hemorrhagic enteritis. Types B, D, and G each accounted for only 1.08% (1/93). Type B (cpa, cpb, etx) infected neonatal lambs and foals (lamb dysentery), type D (cpa, etx) targeted sheep and goats (enterotoxemia), and type G (cpa, netB) primarily infected broilers (necrotic enteritis) (Table 2). Lytic activity of lysin CP02

Fifteen strains of C. perfringens were selected and incubated anaerobically with 200 μg/mL of CP02 for 15 min for the preliminary determination of their lysis capacity. When 200 μg/mL CP02 was added to C. perfringens, the counts of viable bacteria in the BHI liquid medium significantly decreased, killing approximately 3.17∼5.04 Lg CFU/mL of viable bacteria (Fig. 3, P < 0.001).Fig. 3Preliminary determination of the antibacterial activity determination of CP02 (200 μg/mL) on C. perfringens strains in BHI broth within 15 min.Fig. 3

Preliminary determination of the antibacterial activity determination of CP02 (200 μg/mL) on C. perfringens strains in BHI broth within 15 min.

Additionally, CP02 was capable of continuously controlling the growth of C. perfringens in all the test samples (Fig. 4). With respect to C. perfringens cp12, the higher the concentration of CP02 added, the greater the lytic efficiency. The lytic effect of CP02 on C. perfringens cp12 was dose dependent. CP02 at a concentration of 200 μg/mL reduced the number of viable bacteria by approximately 4.34 Lg (CFU/mL) (P < 0.0001) (Fig. 4A). Discussion

C. perfringens is a major foodborne pathogen responsible for food poisoning and gastrointestinal infections in humans (Stanley et al., 2012). The widespread use of antibiotics has led to the frequent detection of resistant strains in food, animal, and clinical samples, posing increasing threats to public health (Brinck et al., 2023; Hamed et al., 2021; L. Liu et al., 2022). Traditional control methods that rely on thermal processing or chemical preservatives often compromise food quality or raise concerns about chemical residues (Mokgatla et al., 2002). Therefore, there is an urgent need for safe, efficient, and environmentally friendly alternatives to control C. perfringens contamination. Bacteriophage lysins have emerged as promising antibacterial agents due to their safety, specificity, and efficacy. However, current research has focused predominantly on lysins targeting a single toxin type, with few studies addressing the activity of lysins against multiple toxin types (Ji et al., 2025; Wu et al., 2025; X. Zhao et al., 2023). Furthermore, investigations into the application of lysins as food preservatives are rare, and their antimicrobial mechanisms are still poorly understood (J. Liu et al., 2024; Yao et al., 2025, Yao et al., 2025).

Broad-spectrum phage lysins hold significant promise for food biopreservation, particularly in the context of increasing antimicrobial resistance (Garvey, 2022). Unlike chemical preservatives, lysins exhibit “scalpel-like” precision, selectively eliminating target pathogens such as C. perfringens without disrupting beneficial microbiota or food quality—an essential feature for decontaminating meat, eggshells, and ready-to-eat products (Han et al., 2022). CP02, a novel phage-derived lysin characterized in this study, has an excellent intraspecies lytic spectrum that distinguishes it from all previously reported C. perfringens-targeting phages and endolysins. CP02 achieved 100% lysis against 93 tested C. perfringens strains spanning five toxin types (A, B, C, D, and G), including the epidemiologically dominant types A (74.19%) and C (22.58%), precisely the types responsible for most foodborne outbreaks and enteric diseases in humans and livestock. This comprehensive coverage substantially exceeds that of the well-characterized endolysin cpp-lys, which is active against seven toxin types but lacks quantitative strain coverage data (X. Zhao et al., 2023). Although PlyDolk21 shows broad activity against the tested C. perfringens strains, its evaluation was limited to only 10 strains (Seo et al., 2025). The cross-species lysin Lys19 demonstrates activity against S. suis, but its lytic rate against C. perfringens is only 57%, revealing a trade-off between intergeneric breadth and intraspecies completeness that CP02 avoids (Ji et al., 2025). Phages evaluated in this comparison exhibit severely restricted lytic ranges, with vB_CpeP_15N3 specific to type C, CPD2 active against only a single isolate, and vB_CpS_CP5S restricted to ATCC 13124 (Shin et al., 2023; Wu et al., 2025). The strict specificity of CP02 for C. perfringens—with no detectable activity against other gram-positive or gram-negative bacteria—preserves its utility for targeted applications without off-target effects on beneficial microbiota. Mechanistically, this unique profile of CP02 likely stems from its modular architecture. The N-terminal GH25 domain cleaves the conserved β-1,4-glycosidic bond in the peptidoglycan backbone, whereas the tandem C-terminal SH3b domains confer strain-specific binding by recognizing species-specific epitopes (Binte Muhammad Jai et al., 2020; Pérez-Dorado et al., 2007). This dual-domain arrangement enhances avidity and broadens strain recognition (Huang et al., 2015). Functionally, the SH3b domains anchor the enzyme to the cell wall, concentrating the GH25 catalytic domain at its site of action for efficient cleavage (Huang et al., 2015). Thus, CP02 acts as a molecular “scalpel,” integrating conserved catalytic activity with specific targeting to achieve potent, species-restricted lysis—an advantageous balance for both therapeutic and food preservation applications.

For phage lysins to be effective at controlling food contamination, their robustness under variable processing and storage conditions is critical (Kocot et al., 2023). As shown in Table 3, CP02 demonstrates robust thermal stability (retaining 35.6% activity at 75 °C, surpassing cpp-lys and Lys19) and broad pH tolerance (pH 3–10, comparable to the most robust endolysins) (Ji et al., 2025; Shin et al., 2023; X. Zhao et al., 2023; Zuikarnain et al., 2026). CP02 demonstrated potent and rapid lytic activity against C. perfringens in vitro, achieving a 3.17–5.04 log CFU/mL reduction within 15 min—a bactericidal efficacy surpassing those of several previously reported C. perfringens lysins (Ji et al., 2025; Tian et al., 2022). To evaluate its practical applicability as a food preservative, chicken breast and duck meat were selected as representative matrices due to their high susceptibility to C. perfringens contamination and widespread consumption (Barnas et al., 2024; Bhattacharya et al., 2020). The tested temperatures (25 °C for ambient storage and 4 °C for refrigeration) were chosen to reflect real-world food preservation scenarios (Rafiq et al., 2025; X. Yao et al., 2025). CP02 retained significant antibacterial activity under both conditions, demonstrating a temperature adaptability that outperforms most C. perfringens phages and lysins. Despite these promising results, further evaluation in more complex food matrices and optimization of formulation stability for long-term storage are warranted. CP02 exhibits a modular architecture characteristic of gram-positive phage lysins, comprising an N-terminal GH25 family catalytic domain and two tandem C-terminal SH3b cell wall-binding domains—a structural organization that underpins its excellent intraspecies lytic spectrum (100% across five toxin types) while maintaining strict specificity for C. perfringens (Loessner, 2005; Schmelcher et al., 2016). Through sequence alignment with the homologous lysin Psm and alanine-scanning mutagenesis, four residues—Asp8 (D8), Tyr60 (Y60), Glu96 (E96), and Asn135 (N135)—were identified as indispensable for catalytic activity, with mutation to alanine resulting in complete loss of bactericidal function (Tamai et al., 2014). Based on the canonical catalytic mechanism of GH25, Glu96 and Asp8 likely function as the acid/base pair facilitating glycosidic bond cleavage, whereas Tyr60 contributes to substrate binding and transition state stabilization, and Asn135 may participate in coordinating the catalytic water molecule (Davies et al., 2003; Vocadlo et al., 2001). Three-dimensional modeling revealed that these essential residues cluster within the conserved active site cleft of the GH25 domain, explaining how CP02 achieves potent catalytic activity despite low overall sequence homology (26.07%) with previously reported C. perfringens lysins (Ji et al., 2025; Seo et al., 2025; Shin et al., 2023). The presence of two tandem SH3b domains likely enhances binding avidity through multivalent interactions, contributing to the broad strain recognition observed in this study. These findings provide a molecular foundation for understanding CP02's superior lytic activity and establish a framework for rational engineering—enabling targeted mutagenesis to modulate its stability, alter its substrate specificity, or generate chimeric lysins with enhanced properties for food safety applications. Compared with other C. perfringens-targeting lysins, such as Lys19 (requiring five essential residues) and LysCPD2 (single-domain amidase), the unique GH25 catalytic mechanism and dual-domain architecture of CP02 offer distinct advantages in terms of its lytic spectrum, stability, and engineering potential (Ji et al., 2025; Shin et al., 2023).

Biofilms pose a significant food safety risk due to their inherent resistance to cleaning, leading to persistent contamination in processing environments (Zhang et al., 2025). Our study demonstrated that CP02 exhibits potent dual antibiofilm activity against mature biofilm-forming C. perfringens strains. At a low concentration (20 μg/mL), CP02 significantly prevented biofilm formation and effectively disrupted mature biofilms. This superior eradication suggests that CP02 efficiently penetrates the biofilm matrix to lyse its constituent cells (Gungor et al., 2024). Despite these moderate baseline values, CP02 treatment achieved statistically significant and biologically meaningful reductions, with up to 76.9% removal of pre-formed biofilms at 50 μg/mL (P < 0.0001). These results underscore the potential of CP02 as an effective antibiofilm agent against C. perfringens in food processing environments.

Conclusion

In summary, we identified and characterized CP02 as a novel phage lysin against C. perfringens. Its key strengths lie in its unique combination of properties: broad intraspecies activity (lysing all five toxin types), potent biofilm disruption, and sustained efficacy in food matrices (chicken and duck meat). These distinctive features, along with its stability under various conditions, highlight the potential of CP02 as a promising biocontrol agent for the food industry.
