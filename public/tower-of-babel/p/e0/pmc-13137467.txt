Left atrial reservoir strain and the left atrioventricular coupling index in association with left ventricular diastolic dysfunction in non-dialysis chronic kidney disease with preserved ejection fraction: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Baoling Zhang, Yang Dong, Yuemei Li, Panpan Shao, Xuemeng Chen, Runyu Zhang, Zhining Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLeft ventricular diastolic dysfunction (LVDD) is common in chronic kidney disease (CKD) despite preserved left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF), whereas conventional echocardiographic grading may be challenging in this setting. We examined the patterns of left atrial reservoir strain (LASr) and the left atrioventricular coupling index (LACI) across CKD stages and their associations with echocardiography-defined LVDD in non-dialysis CKD patients with preserved LVEF.MethodsIn this single-center cross-sectional study, non-dialysis CKD patients with LVEF ≥ 50% and sinus rhythm were consecutively enrolled between November 2024 and November 2025, together with healthy controls with a comparable age and sex distribution. CKD was classified by estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) as G1–2 (≥ 60), G3 (30–59), and G4–5 (< 30 mL/min/1.73 m²). Phasic left atrial strain was measured by two-dimensional speckle-tracking echocardiography. LACI was calculated as minimal left atrial volume divided by left ventricular end-diastolic volume × 100%. LVDD was classified using a guideline-based approach, with a prespecified alternative strategy when tricuspid regurgitation velocity was not measurable. Multivariable logistic regression, receiver operating characteristic analysis, reclassification analysis, and decision-curve analysis were used to assess independent associations and incremental value.ResultsA total of 177 CKD patients and 50 controls were included. LASr progressively declined with worsening CKD (controls: 42.93 ± 5.62% vs. G4–5: 29.28 ± 6.10%; P for trend < 0.001), whereas LACI increased across CKD stages (P for trend = 0.003). LVDD was present in 39.0% of the CKD cohort and was more frequent in G4–5 disease (60.9%). After adjustment for age, systolic blood pressure, hemoglobin, eGFR, and left ventricular mass index, LASr was independently associated with lower odds of LVDD (per 1 SD increase: OR 0.455, 95% CI 0.290–0.714), whereas LACI was independently associated with higher odds of LVDD (per 1 SD increase: OR 3.156, 95% CI 1.914–5.204). Adding LASr and then LACI to the clinical base model improved discrimination for LVDD (AUC: 0.763, 0.821, and 0.872, respectively; both DeLong P < 0.05 vs. the preceding model). Reclassification analyses and decision-curve analysis also supported incremental value. The main findings remained directionally consistent after additional adjustment for diuretic use and after LVDD was reclassified according to the 2025 ASE update.ConclusionsIn non-dialysis CKD patients with preserved LVEF, lower LASr and higher LACI were associated with echocardiography-defined LVDD. LASr and LACI may provide complementary information on left atrial dysfunction and atrioventricular volumetric alteration and may improve identification of LVDD when added to a clinical model.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12872-026-05755-3.Keywords: Chronic kidney disease, Left atrial strain, Left atrioventricular coupling index, Diastolic dysfunction, Speckle-tracking echocardiography, Preserved ejection fraction

Introduction

Chronic kidney disease (CKD) is increasingly recognized as an expanding global public-health burden. According to GBD 2023, the age-standardized prevalence in adults is about 14.2%, and CKD-attributable mortality continues to rise [1]. Cardiovascular disease (CVD) remains the dominant cause of mortality among people with CKD [1, 2]. Declining renal function and cardiovascular injury can reinforce each other through multiple pathways, including volume overload, neurohormonal activation, and inflammation-driven fibrosis, thereby creating a vicious cycle and accelerating disease progression [2, 3]. CKD-related cardiomyopathy is commonly characterized by concentric left ventricular (LV) remodeling, myocardial fibrosis, and reduced ventricular compliance. These changes restrict LV filling and increase diastolic pressures, such that subclinical impairment of diastolic function may be present even when left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) is preserved [4]. Because left ventricular diastolic dysfunction (LVDD) is common in CKD and is associated with adverse outcomes [5], identifying echocardiographic markers that better reflect diastolic abnormalities in non-dialysis CKD remains clinically relevant.

Echocardiography is the first-line tool for assessing left ventricular diastolic function. Current guidelines recommend a multiparametric approach incorporating mitral inflow and tissue Doppler indices (including E/e′ and e′), left atrial volume index (LAVI), and tricuspid regurgitation velocity (TRV) for grading diastolic dysfunction [6, 7]. However, in routine echocardiography, a measurable TRV signal is not always obtainable, particularly when image quality is suboptimal [8]. The absence of a measurable TRV does not reliably exclude elevated pulmonary pressures [9], which may complicate guideline-based diastolic assessment when TRV-dependent algorithms are applied. In addition, filling-pressure surrogates such as E/e′ may be influenced by volume status, which is often unstable in CKD [4, 10]. LAVI primarily reflects structural remodeling related to chronic pressure or volume overload and may be less sensitive to earlier functional abnormalities [7].

Quantified by two-dimensional speckle-tracking echocardiography, left atrial reservoir strain (LASr) typically declines before overt left atrial (LA) enlargement becomes apparent. LASr is closely associated with filling pressures, exercise capacity, and clinical outcomes, suggesting its role as a complementary marker in the assessment of LVDD [11–13]. In CKD populations, lower LASr has also been linked to a higher risk of cardiovascular events, suggesting potential value for risk stratification [12]. Nevertheless, LASr mainly reflects intrinsic LA mechanics and does not directly incorporate the atrioventricular volumetric relation at end-diastole.

The left atrioventricular coupling index (LACI), unlike single-chamber metrics, is intended to characterize atrioventricular coupling and is typically defined as the ratio of minimal LA volume to LV end-diastolic volume; in community-based studies, higher LACI has been associated with cardiovascular events [14]. More recently, LACI has been explored in specific CKD-related settings, including CKD with diabetes and CKD with heart failure with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF), suggesting potential clinical utility [15, 16]. Despite these advances, evidence remains limited regarding how LACI changes with worsening renal function in non-dialysis CKD with preserved LVEF and how it relates to LVDD in this setting. Therefore, the present study investigated the patterns of LASr and LACI across CKD stages in non-dialysis patients with preserved LVEF and examined their associations with echocardiography-defined LVDD.

Methods

Study population

Between November 2024 and November 2025, non-dialysis patients with CKD were consecutively recruited in a single-center, cross-sectional observational study from the Department of Nephrology at the First Affiliated Hospital of Jinzhou Medical University. CKD was defined according to KDIGO criteria as structural and/or functional kidney abnormalities persisting for more than three months [3]. Eligible participants were required to have preserved left ventricular systolic function (LVEF ≥ 50%) and sinus rhythm. Individuals were excluded if they had: (1) documented coronary artery disease, congenital heart disease, cardiomyopathy, or more than mild valvular heart disease; (2) persistent atrial fibrillation or other clinically significant arrhythmias; (3) receipt of renal replacement therapy (maintenance hemodialysis or peritoneal dialysis); or (4) inadequate acoustic windows resulting in insufficient image quality for reliable speckle-tracking–based strain analysis.

A total of 285 candidates were initially screened. After excluding participants with poor echocardiographic image quality (n = 26), incomplete baseline data (n = 21), definite cardiac disease (n = 24), or initiation of dialysis (n = 37), 177 non-dialysis CKD patients constituted the final cohort. Renal function was assessed by estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR), estimated with the Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology Collaboration (CKD-EPI) equation [17]. Based on baseline eGFR, participants were categorized into G1–2 (≥ 60 mL/min/1.73 m²), G3 (30–59 mL/min/1.73 m²), or G4–5 (< 30 mL/min/1.73 m²). A control group of 50 healthy subjects with a comparable age and sex distribution was recruited and screened to exclude cardiac and renal disease by clinical evaluation and echocardiography. Ethical approval (KYLL2024505) was obtained from the Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Jinzhou Medical University. All procedures conformed to the Declaration of Helsinki. Prior to participation, each subject received a detailed explanation of the study and provided written informed consent.

Clinical data and laboratory testing

Within 24 h of enrollment, participants underwent standardized clinical evaluation. We collected demographic and anthropometric data, calculated body mass index (BMI), and recorded smoking and alcohol use. Seated blood pressure was measured twice after adequate rest using a cuff sphygmomanometer, and the mean systolic blood pressure (SBP) and diastolic blood pressure (DBP) were used for subsequent analyses. Hypertension was defined as SBP ≥ 140 mmHg and/or DBP ≥ 90 mmHg, or current antihypertensive treatment [18]. Diabetes mellitus was defined as fasting plasma glucose ≥ 7.0 mmol/L and/or glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) ≥ 6.5%, or current glucose-lowering therapy [19]. Morning fasting venous samples were analyzed in the central laboratory for serum creatinine (SCr), blood urea nitrogen (BUN), cystatin C, β2-microglobulin, serum calcium, hemoglobin, and a full lipid profile (total cholesterol [TC], high-density lipoprotein cholesterol [HDL-C], and low-density lipoprotein cholesterol [LDL-C]).

Conventional echocardiography

A single sonographer with > 7 years of experience acquired all transthoracic echocardiograms using a Philips EPIQ 7 C platform (X5-1 phased-array probe, 1–5 MHz). Examinations were performed in the left lateral decubitus position with ECG monitoring, and the operator remained blinded to clinical information. Structural indices were obtained following American Society of Echocardiography (ASE) recommendations [20], including left atrial anteroposterior diameter (LAD), left ventricular end-diastolic internal diameter (LVIDd), interventricular septal thickness at end-diastole (IVSd), and left ventricular posterior wall thickness at end-diastole (LVPWd). Left ventricular mass (LVM) was calculated using the ASE-recommended formula and indexed to body surface area to derive the left ventricular mass index (LVMI); relative wall thickness (RWT) was also calculated. Diastolic Doppler assessment included transmitral E and A velocities (E/A) and tissue Doppler–derived e′ velocities at the septal and lateral annulus, from which the mean E/e′ ratio was derived. LA and LV volumes, LVEF, and LAVI were quantified by the biplane Simpson method.

LA strain and LACI

LA strain acquisition and analysis followed the EACVI/ASE/Industry Task Force consensus recommendations on atrial deformation imaging [21]. Apical four-chamber cine loops (five consecutive cycles; 60–80 frames/s) were exported in digital imaging and communications in medicine (DICOM) format and analyzed offline in QLab 13.0. With the “Auto Strain LA” module, the LA endocardial contour was traced while excluding the pulmonary vein ostia and LA appendage; manual refinement was applied when required. Strain curves were zeroed at QRS onset, and the following parameters were obtained: LASr, LA conduit strain (LAScd), and LA contraction strain (LASct). LACI was calculated from same-cycle biplane Simpson volumes using minimal LA volume at end-diastole (LAVmin) and LV end-diastolic volume (LVEDV): LACI = (LAVmin / LVEDV) × 100%.

Assessment of diastolic function

Diastolic function was classified according to the 2016 American Society of Echocardiography/European Association of Cardiovascular Imaging (ASE/EACVI) recommendations [6], which served as the prespecified primary framework for LVDD classification in this study. TRV feasibility was recorded during image analysis. When TRV could not be reliably measured, a prespecified three-parameter approach was applied using LAVI > 34 mL/m², average E/e′ > 14, and reduced e′ (septal e′ < 7 cm/s or lateral e′ < 10 cm/s). LVDD was assigned when at least two of these criteria were present; otherwise, diastolic function was considered normal. Participants were then categorized as LVDD or non-LVDD. Sensitivity analyses were performed to assess the robustness of this strategy, including comparison with the standard four-parameter algorithm in the subgroup with measurable TRV and a post hoc analysis based on the 2025 ASE update [7] (see Additional file 1: Tables S6 and S9–S10).

Reproducibility

To evaluate measurement reproducibility, 30 participants were randomly selected for repeated analyses. Intra-observer agreement was assessed by reanalyzing the same images after an interval of at least two weeks by the original observer. Inter-observer agreement was assessed by an independent observer, also blinded to clinical information. Intraclass correlation coefficients (ICC) with 95% confidence intervals were calculated for LASr and LACI to assess intra- and inter-observer reproducibility.

Statistical analysis

Analyses were performed in SPSS 25.0, GraphPad Prism 10.0, and R version 4.4.0. Distributional assumptions were checked using the Shapiro–Wilk and Levene tests. Continuous variables are summarized as mean ± SD or median (IQR), and categorical variables as n (%). Group comparisons used parametric or nonparametric methods as appropriate (t test/Mann–Whitney for two groups; ANOVA/Kruskal–Wallis for ≥ 3 groups with least significant difference (LSD) or Tamhane’s T2 post hoc testing). P for trend was assessed across CKD stages within the CKD cohort (G1–2 = 1, G3 = 2, G4–5 = 3) using Spearman’s rank correlation. χ²/Fisher’s exact tests and Spearman’s ρ were used for categorical comparisons and correlations, respectively. LVDD was the dependent variable in hierarchical logistic regression: Model 1 adjusted for age, SBP, hemoglobin, eGFR, and LVMI; Model 2 added standardized LASr; Model 3 further added standardized LACI. Results are reported as OR with 95% CI. Discrimination was assessed using ROC curves and the area under the curve (AUC), and AUCs were compared by DeLong’s method. Incremental performance was further assessed using continuous net reclassification improvement (NRI) and integrated discrimination improvement (IDI), with 95% confidence intervals estimated by bootstrap resampling. Decision-curve analysis was also performed to evaluate the potential clinical net benefit of the hierarchical models across a range of threshold probabilities. Two-sided P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline clinical characteristics

A total of 177 non-dialysis CKD patients and 50 healthy controls were included in the final analysis. When stratified by CKD stage (as defined in Methods), 62, 51, and 64 patients were assigned to G1–2, G3, and G4–5, respectively. Age, sex distribution, and LVEF did not differ significantly across CKD stages (all P > 0.05). With worsening CKD stage, the prevalence of hypertension and diabetes increased, accompanied by higher SBP and DBP, whereas hemoglobin declined in a stepwise pattern (all P for trend < 0.05) (Table 1).

Cardiac structural and functional parameters

Echocardiographic assessment demonstrated progressive increases in LVMI, RWT, and E/e′ with worsening CKD (all P for trend < 0.001). LAVI also exhibited an upward trend and was higher in G4–5 than in controls and the G1–2 group (P for trend = 0.001) (Table 1).

Regarding LA function, LASr was highest in healthy controls and declined steadily as renal function deteriorated (42.93 ± 5.62% in controls vs. 29.28 ± 6.10% in G4–5; P for trend < 0.001). In contrast, LACI increased across CKD stages (P for trend = 0.003) (Table 1; Fig. 2). LAScd showed an early reduction in CKD and continued to decrease with disease progression, falling from 25.45 (19.37–32.30)% in controls to 13.40 (10.80–15.85)% in G4–5 (P for trend = 0.041). LASct did not differ significantly among stages (P = 0.188).

Prevalence of LVDD and between-group comparisons

Based on the 2016 ASE/EACVI criteria, LVDD was identified in 69 CKD patients (39.0%). TRV was measurable in 53 of 177 CKD participants (29.9%). The prevalence of LVDD increased across eGFR categories: 25.8% (16/62) in G1–2, 27.5% (14/51) in G3, and 60.9% (39/64) in G4–5 (P for trend < 0.001) (Table 1).

Compared with patients without LVDD, those with LVDD had higher SBP, LVMI, LAVI, and E/e′, together with lower eGFR and hemoglobin (all P < 0.001). LA deformation analysis showed that LASr, LAScd, and LASct were all reduced in the LVDD group (all P < 0.05), whereas LACI was markedly higher (P < 0.001). Although LVEF differed statistically between the two groups, values in both remained within the preserved range (Table 2).

Correlation analyses

In the CKD cohort, LASr correlated with higher eGFR (ρ = 0.260, P < 0.001) and lower E/e′ (ρ = −0.258, P = 0.001). LACI showed the opposite pattern, correlating inversely with eGFR (ρ = −0.245, P = 0.001) and positively with E/e′ (ρ = 0.252, P = 0.001) (Fig. 3).

Independent associations and incremental value of LASr and LACI for LVDD

In multivariable logistic regression adjusted for age, SBP, hemoglobin, eGFR, and LVMI, each 1-SD increase in LASr was associated with lower odds of LVDD (OR = 0.455, 95% CI 0.290–0.714; P = 0.001), whereas each 1-SD increase in LACI was associated with higher odds of LVDD (OR = 3.156, 95% CI 1.914–5.204; P < 0.001) (Table 3). The estimates were consistent after additional adjustment for diuretic use (Additional file 1: Table S7). The clinical base model yielded an AUC of 0.763 (95% CI 0.692–0.835). Adding LASr improved discrimination (AUC = 0.821; DeLong P = 0.026), and further adding LACI increased the AUC to 0.872 (DeLong P = 0.013) (Table 4; Fig. 4). Decision-curve analysis showed greater net benefit for the model incorporating both LASr and LACI than for the clinical model and the model including LASr alone (Fig. 5). Continuous NRI and IDI also improved further after LACI was added to the model already containing clinical variables and LASr (Additional file 1: Table S11).

Sensitivity analyses

Sensitivity analyses supported the robustness of the main findings. In the subgroup with measurable TRV, classification based on the prespecified three-parameter strategy showed high agreement with the standard four-parameter algorithm (Additional file 1: Table S6). In a post hoc sensitivity analysis aligned with the 2025 ASE framework, the prevalence of LVDD increased from 39.0% under the prespecified 2016-based definition to 47.5% using the 2025-aligned definition. Lower LASr and higher LACI remained independently associated with LVDD, and the AUC increased from 0.761 for the clinical base model to 0.782 after adding LASr and to 0.817 after further adding LACI (Additional file 1: Tables S9–S10).

Reproducibility

Intra-observer reproducibility was excellent for both indices, with ICC of 0.972 (95% CI 0.943–0.987) for LASr and 0.937 (95% CI 0.872–0.969) for LACI (all P < 0.001). Inter-observer reproducibility was also high, with ICC of 0.924 (95% CI 0.847–0.963) for LASr and 0.900 (95% CI 0.800–0.951) for LACI (all P < 0.001) (see Additional file 1: Table S8).

Discussion

In this study of non-dialysis patients with CKD and preserved LVEF, LASr and LAScd decreased progressively as renal function declined, whereas LACI increased. Multivariable analyses further showed that lower LASr and higher LACI were independently associated with LVDD after adjustment for age, SBP, hemoglobin, eGFR, and LVMI. In addition, adding these two markers to the clinical base model improved discrimination for LVDD. These findings suggest that, in this population, LVDD is not characterized solely by left atrial enlargement, but also by impairment of left atrial function and alteration in atrioventricular volumetric relations.

The findings for LASr are broadly consistent with previous reports in CKD populations. Gan et al. showed that abnormal left atrial strain may precede overt left atrial enlargement in non-dialysis CKD and was associated with adverse cardiovascular outcomes [12]. Nguyen et al. likewise reported progressive impairment of left atrial reservoir and conduit function across CKD stages, whereas changes in contractile function were less pronounced [22]. In the present study, LASr and LAScd changed in the same direction. Left atrial reservoir function is influenced by left atrial compliance, left ventricular longitudinal shortening, mitral annular motion, and diastolic loading conditions, and in populations with preserved LVEF it has also been linked to elevated filling pressures and identification of HFpEF [11, 23, 24]. In this context, the decline in LASr observed in CKD is more likely to reflect impairment of left atrial function than structural enlargement alone.

LASct did not differ significantly across CKD stages, although it was lower in patients with LVDD than in those without LVDD. This pattern suggests that phasic left atrial dysfunction may not evolve uniformly. Reservoir and conduit function are more readily affected by reduced atrial compliance and impaired ventricular relaxation, whereas active atrial contraction may remain compensatory for some time [11, 25]. This may explain why LASct did not parallel the changes seen in LASr and LAScd.

Compared with LASr, LACI appears to reflect a different aspect of diastolic remodeling, namely the volumetric relation between the left atrium and left ventricle at end-diastole. In the present study, LACI increased across CKD stages, was higher in patients with LVDD, and remained independently associated with LVDD even after LASr was included in the model. An increase in LACI is unlikely to represent left atrial enlargement alone. Rather, it may result from the combination of a larger LAVmin and a relatively constrained LVEDV. LAVmin more closely reflects residual atrial volume after emptying, whereas LVEDV reflects ventricular chamber capacity after diastolic filling. Their ratio therefore provides a volumetric expression of the relation between residual atrial volume and ventricular accommodation at end-diastole. Prior studies have also suggested that LAVmin may correlate more closely than LAVmax with left ventricular filling pressure, NT-proBNP, and prognosis [26–28].

Most direct evidence on LACI has come from community-based cohorts and heart failure populations. In the Multi-Ethnic Study of Atherosclerosis (MESA), LACI was independently associated with cardiovascular events, incident atrial fibrillation, and heart failure [14, 29]. In patients with heart failure, higher LACI was also associated with more advanced diastolic dysfunction and worse outcome [30]. At the same time, results have not been entirely uniform across populations. Fortuni et al. found that, in chronic heart failure overall, LAVmin reflected elevated LVEDP better than LAVmax, whereas in the subgroup with preserved LVEF the reverse was observed [28]. This suggests that the performance of volumetric markers may depend on ejection fraction, remodeling pattern, and loading conditions.

Direct studies using atrioventricular volumetric LACI in CKD remain limited. It is also important to distinguish the present index from that reported by Ji et al., who studied a left atrial volumetric/mechanical coupling index defined as LAVI divided by a′, which reflects the relation between atrial volume and active mechanical function rather than atrioventricular volumetric coupling [31]. The two measures should therefore not be considered interchangeable. In the present study, non-dialysis patients with CKD and preserved LVEF were specifically examined, and atrioventricular volumetric LACI was analyzed in relation to echocardiography-defined LVDD together with LASr. The persistence of the association between LACI and LVDD after inclusion of LASr suggests that these two indices do not capture identical information. LASr appears to reflect left atrial mechanical function more directly, whereas LACI more closely reflects end-diastolic atrioventricular volumetric relations.

This combination of findings is compatible with the pattern of myocardial remodeling described in CKD. Patients with CKD are exposed to abnormal pressure and volume loading, often in the presence of anemia, inflammation, neurohormonal activation, and accumulation of uremic toxins, all of which may contribute to left ventricular hypertrophy, interstitial fibrosis, and impaired compliance [32]. Experimental studies have shown that indoxyl sulfate can promote cardiac fibroblast collagen synthesis and cardiomyocyte hypertrophy, and may aggravate inflammation and fibrosis through the AhR, NF-kB, and NLRP3 pathways [33–35]. Clinical data have also linked reduced LASr with upregulation of fibrosis-related gene expression in atrial tissue [36]. Taken together, lower LASr and higher LACI may reflect different aspects of the same remodeling process, with the former more closely related to impaired atrial mechanics and the latter to altered atrioventricular volumetric relations at end-diastole.

The present findings also suggest that, in CKD patients with preserved LVEF, conventional structural indices such as LAVI alone may not fully represent the functional interaction between the left atrium and left ventricle. LASr and LACI provide information on atrial mechanics and end-diastolic atrioventricular volumetric relations, respectively. When added to the clinical base model, they improved model performance, and both reclassification analyses and decision-curve analysis supported their incremental value. The 2025 ASE update places greater emphasis on left atrial functional information in the assessment of diastolic function, which is in line with the direction of the present findings [7].

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, this was a cross-sectional study, and the temporal sequence between abnormalities in LASr or LACI and the development of LVDD cannot be established. Second, LVDD was defined by echocardiographic criteria rather than invasive hemodynamic measurements; accordingly, the findings should be interpreted in relation to echocardiography-defined LVDD. Third, volume status may fluctuate in CKD, and both atrial strain and volumetric indices are load dependent. Although adjustment for diuretic use, reclassification according to the 2025 ASE criteria, and the agreement analysis in the subgroup with measurable TRV did not materially alter the overall direction of the findings, confirmation in prospective studies will still be needed. Finally, as LACI is a relatively new index, its reference range, inter-platform consistency, and stability across different remodeling patterns remain to be clarified.

Conclusions

Among non-dialysis CKD patients with preserved LVEF, lower LASr/LAScd and higher LACI were observed with worsening CKD stage. Lower LASr and higher LACI were independently associated with echocardiography-defined LVDD, and adding these indices to a clinical base model improved model discrimination for LVDD. These findings suggest that left atrial functional indices and atrioventricular volumetric relations may provide complementary information in the evaluation of LVDD in this population.
