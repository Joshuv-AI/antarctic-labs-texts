Statistical optimization for removal of basic dyes from aqueous solutions using chitosan-assembled zinc oxide nanocomposite
Authors: Xue Li, Yanfeng Fang, Xiaoping Tang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn this work, the removal of basic blue 41 (BB41) and basic red 46 (BR46) dyes from aqueous solutions was systematically studied using chitosan-assembled zinc oxide nanocomposite (CTS/ZnO) as an efficient adsorbent. The synthesized CTS/ZnO nanocomposite was characterized by X-Ray diffraction (XRD), point of zero charge (pHpzc), Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR), and scanning electron microscopy (SEM) analyses, confirming its crystalline porous-like surface structure with a rough and heterogeneous surface morphology and a pHpzc of 5.6. The effects of four critical parameters— CTS/ZnO dosage, solution pH, and initial concentration under ultrasonic irradiation —were evaluated using response surface methodology (RSM) within the framework of central composite design (CCD). Second-order polynomial models were established to predict the adsorption performance, and analysis of variance (ANOVA) confirmed the high adequacy of the models (R² > 0.99). The optimum conditions were found at pH of 7, CTS/ZnO dosage of 0.18 g, initial concentration of 16 mg L⁻¹, and sonication time of 36 min. Under optimum conditions, the removal efficiencies reached 96.19% for BB41 and 91.87% for BR46. Regeneration studies revealed that the adsorbent maintained more than 60% of its original capacity after four adsorption–desorption cycles, highlighting its reusability and stability. Furthermore, real water tests demonstrated removal efficiencies between 88.01% and 82.38% for BB41 and BR46, confirming the practical applicability of CTS/ZnO nanocomposite for environmental remediation. Overall, the findings suggest that CTS/ZnO nanocomposite is a promising, low-cost, and recyclable adsorbent for the simultaneous removal of organic dyes from contaminated waters.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-43229-w.Keywords: Central composite design, Chitosan-assembled zinc oxide nanocomposite, Wastewater treatment, Response surface methodology, RemovalSubject terms: Chemistry, Environmental sciences, Materials science, Nanoscience and technology

Introduction

The contamination of aqueous solutions with synthetic dyes has emerged as one of the most pressing global environmental challenges1,2. These pollutants are commonly discharged from industries such as electroplating, mining, textiles, and pharmaceuticals, and they persist in water bodies due to their chemical stability and resistance to natural degradation3. The presence of such substances not only deteriorates water quality and disrupts aquatic ecosystems but also poses severe risks to human health, including carcinogenicity, mutagenicity, and organ toxicity4,5. Therefore, developing effective and sustainable approaches for the removal of dyes has become a crucial priority in environmental remediation research.

BB41 is a synthetic cationic azo dye widely used in textile dyeing, paper printing, and acrylic fiber processing. Its molecular structure consists of aromatic rings and azo linkages, which impart high chemical stability and strong chromophoric properties, enabling intense coloration even at low concentrations6. Owing to its structural stability, BB41 exhibits significant resistance to photodegradation and biodegradation, making it poorly removable by conventional wastewater treatment methods and frequently detected in industrial effluents. In aquatic environments, the presence of BB41 can reduce light penetration and hinder photosynthetic activity in primary producers, thereby disrupting aquatic ecosystems7. Furthermore, BB41 has been reported to exert toxic effects on marine organisms and may cause mutagenic and cytotoxic impacts, raising concerns regarding its persistence and potential ecological and human health risks8.

BR46 is a synthetic azo dye commonly employed in textile, leather dyeing, and printing applications. Its complex aromatic structure and positively charged nitrogen-containing groups contribute to its high solubility in water, strong color intensity, and remarkable resistance to chemical and photolytic degradation9. As a result, BR46 is often inadequately removed during conventional wastewater treatment processes and can persist in industrial effluents. In aquatic systems, BR46 can significantly impair light transmission, leading to the inhibition of photosynthesis and reduced oxygen production in aquatic flora10,11. Additionally, exposure to BR46 has been associated with toxic, genotoxic, and potentially bioaccumulative effects in aquatic organisms, which may propagate through the food chain and pose long-term risks to environmental and human health12.

Several treatment technologies have been proposed for removing dyes from aqueous solutions, including chemical precipitation, membrane separation, ion exchange, electrocoagulation, and advanced oxidation processes13–17. While these methods can achieve considerable pollutant reduction, they are often associated with limitations such as high operational costs, complex handling, production of secondary sludge, and insufficient efficiency at low concentrations18. Adsorption, on the other hand, has been recognized as a highly effective and economical technique, offering advantages such as operational simplicity, reusability of adsorbents, and suitability for treating pollutants at trace levels19,20. The key factor determining the success of adsorption lies in the development of high-performance and low-cost adsorbent materials.

Among the emerging strategies for the removal of dyes from aqueous solutions, the use of chitosan (CTS)–based nanocomposites have attracted considerable attention. Chitosan, a biopolymer rich in amino (–NH₂) and hydroxyl (–OH) functional groups, exhibits excellent ion-exchange and chelation abilities21. When combined with zinc oxide (ZnO), which provides high surface activity and photocatalytic properties, the resulting CTS/ZnO nanocomposite demonstrates enhanced adsorption capacity, improved thermal and chemical stability, and remarkable regeneration efficiency compared to pristine chitosan22. The synergistic interaction between CTS and ZnO not only increases the number of active adsorption sites but also strengthens the structural integrity of the composite, allowing it to perform efficiently over a broad pH range and multiple adsorption–desorption cycles23. Moreover, owing to its biocompatibility, low cost, and environmentally benign nature, the CTS/ZnO nanocomposite represents a highly promising adsorbent for the treatment of dye-contaminated wastewater.

For efficient adsorption performance, parameters such as adsorbent dosage, time, pH, and pollutant concentration must be systematically optimized. RSM offers a powerful statistical approach for modeling and optimizing such processes by evaluating both individual and interactive effects of variables24. CCD, a widely used RSM framework, enables accurate prediction of adsorption performance while minimizing the number of required experiments25. Moreover, ultrasound-assisted adsorption has recently gained attention as an innovative strategy that enhances mass transfer, prevents particle agglomeration, and exposes additional adsorption sites through cavitation effects26. Integrating ultrasound treatment with statistical optimization is expected to yield superior adsorption efficiency.

The main advantages of the present study include the application of a CTS/ZnO nanocomposite combined with ultrasonic irradiation for the efficient removal of cationic dyes, as well as the use of RSM to optimize the operational parameters with a reduced number of experiments. The ultrasonic-assisted process enhanced mass transfer and shortened the contact time, leading to high dye removal efficiencies under mild experimental conditions. In addition, the CCD-based statistical analysis provided reliable predictive models with high coefficients of determination, allowing accurate evaluation of the individual and interactive effects of the operating variables.

Despite these advantages, some limitations should be acknowledged. The adsorption experiments were conducted in batch mode using synthetic dye solutions, which may not fully represent the complexity of real industrial wastewater. Moreover, the reusability and long-term stability of the CTS/ZnO nanocomposite were not investigated in this study, and further research is required to evaluate its performance in continuous systems and real effluents.

Materials and methods

Materials and equipment

All reagents were of analytical grade and purchased from Merck (Germany). Zinc oxide (ZnO), acetone (C3H6O), nitric acid (HNO3), sodium hydroxide (NaOH), acetic acid (CH3COOH), ethanol (C2H6O), hydrochloric acid (HCl), and toluene (C6H5CH3) were used as starting materials without further purification. The pH of solutions was adjusted using 0.1 M NaOH or 0.1 M HCl solutions. Aqueous solutions of BB41 and BR46 were prepared in double-distilled water and diluted to the desired concentrations for experimental runs. The characteristics of BB41 and BR46 are presented in Table 1. The concentration of dye was determined spectrophotometrically (UV-Vis, UV-2550) at its maximum absorption wavelength. Ultrasonic-assisted adsorption experiments were performed in a SONICA 3200 ultrasonic bath (50 kHz, 100 W). The crystalline structure of the CTS/ZnO was analyzed using XRD (Stoe Stadi-P) in the range 2θ = 10°–80°. Functional groups were examined using FTIR (Nicolet Nexus 870) within 4000–400 cm⁻¹. The particle size distribution was studied by SEM (KYKY EM-3200).

Synthesis of CTS/ZnO nanocomposite

To synthesize the CTS/ZnO nanocomposite, 0.75 g of ZnO was dispersed in 100 mL of 1% (v/v) CH3COOH solution under continuous magnetic stirring. A few drops of HNO3 were then added to facilitate dissolution. Subsequently, 1 g of CTS was slowly introduced into the mixture and sonicated for 1 h to ensure uniform dispersion. While stirring, 2 M NaOH solution was added dropwise until the pH of the suspension reached 11. The resulting mixture was then heated in a water bath at 60 °C for 3 h. After cooling, the formed precipitate was filtered and dried in an oven at 70 °C for 1 h. The physicochemical characteristics of the CTS/ZnO nanocomposite were examined using pHpzc, FTIR, XRD, and SEM analyses.

Adsorption experiments

Batch experiments were conducted according to the CCD matrix to evaluate the removal performance of CTS/ZnO nanocomposite for BB41 and BR46. In each run, a known mass of CTS/ZnO was added to 50 mL of aqueous solution containing predetermined concentrations of BB41 and BR46. The pH of the solution was adjusted to the desired value using 0.1 M HCl or 0.1 M NaOH before adsorption. The suspensions were then placed in an ultrasonic bath and exposed to sonication for the contact time specified in the design. Determination of pHpzc of CTS/ZnO

The pHpzc of CTS/ZnO was determined using the solid addition method. A series of 0.01 M NaCl solutions (100 mL) was prepared, and their initial pH values were adjusted over a range of 2–9 using either 0.1 M HCl or 0.1 M NaOH. A fixed amount of CTS/ZnO (0.05 g) was added to each solution, and the suspensions were equilibrated under continuous shaking for 24 h at 25 °C. After equilibrium, the pHfinal values were measured. The difference between the pHinitial and pHfinal values (ΔpH) was plotted against the initial pH. The point at which the curve crossed the line ΔpH = 0 was taken as the pHpzc of CTS/ZnO. This value indicates the pH at which the surface of the adsorbent carries no net charge. At solution pH values below the pHpzc, the CTS/ZnO surface is positively charged, while at values above the pHpzc, the surface charge becomes negative, strongly influencing the electrostatic interactions between dyes and the adsorbent surface.

Results and discussion

Characterization studies

Figure S1a shows the FTIR spectrum of the CTS/ZnO. The broad absorption band observed at 3366 cm⁻¹ corresponds to the stretching vibrations of –OH and –NH groups. The peaks at 2917 cm− 1 and 2873 cm− 1 are related to C–H stretching vibrations. The band at 1651 cm− 1 is assigned to the amide I group, which arises from C = O stretching coupled with N–H bending. The peak at 1586 cm− 1 is attributed to N–H bending of the amino group. The absorption band at 1417 cm− 1 corresponds to C–N deformation of amine groups, while the one at 1381 cm− 1 is related to the –COO⁻ group of carboxylate salts. A strong and broad peak at 1156 cm− 1 is characteristic of the glycosidic linkage in polysaccharide units. The bands at 1076 cm− 1 and 1027 cm− 1 are assigned to O–H stretching and C–O–C stretching vibrations within the glucose ring, respectively. Finally, the absorption band at 540 cm⁻¹ corresponds to the O–Zn–O stretching vibration, confirming the formation of ZnO within the CTS matrix30,31.

SEM was employed to investigate the surface morphology of the CTS/ZnO (Fig. S1b). The micrograph clearly demonstrates that the CTS/ZnO surface exhibits a porous and nearly spherical morphology. In addition, slight particle agglomeration can be observed, which is likely caused by the presence of capping or stabilizing agents, resulting in a somewhat rough surface texture. It is also evident that the ZnO nanoparticles are irregularly dispersed within the CTS matrix. The particle size of the CTS/ZnO was found to be approximately 70 nm, confirming the nanoscale nature of the synthesized material.

The result determining the pHpzc of CTS/ZnO is depicted in Fig. S1c. The pHpzc of CTS/ZnO was determined to be 5.6, indicating that the surface of the adsorbent is positively charged at pH values below 5.6 and negatively charged above this value. This property plays a key role in adsorption since BB41 and BR46 are cationic dyes, both of which exhibit pH-dependent interactions with the CTS/ZnO surface.

Figure S1d presents the XRD pattern of the CTS/ZnO. The diffraction peaks observed at 2θ = 31.61°, 34.52°, 36.30°, 47.53°, 57.34°, 62.81°, 65.96°, 67.83°, and 69.13° correspond to the (100), (002), (101), (102), (110), (103), (200), (112), and (201) crystal planes of ZnO, respectively, which are in good agreement with the standard JCPDS card No. 36-145132. According to the Debye–Scherrer equation, the average crystallite size of CTS/ZnO was estimated to be approximately 50 nm, which is consistent with the SEM observations. These results confirm that the CTS/ZnO was successfully synthesized without any damage to the crystalline structure of ZnO, indicating the effective incorporation of ZnO nanoparticles within the chitosan matrix.

Furthermore, the textural properties of the synthesized CTS/ZnO were evaluated by N2 adsorption–desorption analysis at 77 K (Fig. S1e). The obtained isotherm exhibited a typical type IV behavior according to the IUPAC classification, accompanied by a noticeable hysteresis loop at relative pressures (P/P0) above 0.7, indicating the mesoporous nature of the composite structure. The formation of mesopores can be attributed to the porous chitosan matrix and the distribution of ZnO nanoparticles, which generate interparticle voids and enhance the accessibility of adsorption sites. The specific surface area and total pore volume of the CTS/ZnO nanocomposite were determined to be 39.85 m2 g− 1 and 0.253 cm3 g− 1, respectively. The presence of a mesoporous structure is advantageous for the adsorption of relatively large dye molecules, as it facilitates mass transfer and improves adsorption performance.

Response surface plots

The three-dimensional response surface plots were generated to examine the interactive effects of operating parameters on the removal of BB41 and BR46. These plots enabled a clear visualization of the system behavior, where red regions indicated conditions of high adsorption efficiency and blue regions denoted poor performance.

The effect of CTS/ZnO dosage is presented in Fig. 1a. As the amount of CTS/ZnO increased from 0.05 g to 0.18 g, the removal efficiencies of both BB41 and BR46 improved markedly due to the larger number of available active sites. Beyond 0.18 g, however, the increase was negligible, suggesting that adsorption sites had become saturated and excess particles did not contribute to further removal. A similar trend was observed by Hasan et al. (2023) in their study on Pb (II) ion adsorption using conjugate adsorbent33.

The three-dimensional plots of removal of (a) BB41 and (b) BR46.

The effect of initial pollutant concentration is illustrated in Fig. 1a. At lower concentrations, a high percentage of BB41 and BR46 was removed due to the abundance of vacant sites on the CTS/ZnO surface. As the concentrations increased, however, the removal efficiency decreased, reflecting the limited availability of adsorption sites relative to the number of pollutant molecules. This behavior is consistent with findings reported by Yang et al. (2022) for the adsorption of dye and drug using graphene oxide-based materials34.

The role of solution pH is shown in Fig. 1b. Maximum removal occurred at neutral conditions (pH of 7). At acidic pH values, the abundance of protons competed with cationic species for active sites, leading to reduced uptake. At alkaline pH, partial aggregation of BB41 molecules decreased their availability for adsorption. Considering the pHpzc of CTS/ZnO (5.6), the CTS/ZnO surface is positively charged at lower pH and negatively charged at higher pH, which strongly influences electrostatic interactions with BB41 and BR46. Comparable results were reported by Yazid et al. (2024) for the adsorption of cationic pollutants on activated carbon35.

The influence of ultrasound contact time is presented in Fig. 1b. Removal efficiencies of both BB41 and BR46 increased progressively with the extension of ultrasound contact time from 10 to 36 min, which can be attributed to enhanced dispersion of particles and improved mass transfer induced by acoustic cavitation. Prolonging the time beyond 36 min produced only marginal changes, indicating that equilibrium had already been attained. Similar improvements in adsorption under ultrasonic conditions were previously observed by Sadaiyan et al. (2024) in their work on dye removal using ultrasound-activated processes36.

Together, the response surface plots demonstrate that optimal removal was achieved at moderate pollutant concentrations, sufficient adsorbent dosage, neutral pH, and an irradiation time of around 30 min, conditions that are well aligned with practical water treatment scenarios.

Numerical optimization

Numerical optimization based on the desirability function approach was carried out to determine the operational conditions that maximize the removal of BB41 and BR46. The models developed from CCD were used to simultaneously evaluate the effects of initial concentration, CTS/ZnO dosage, pH, and ultrasound contact time, and the optimization criteria were set to achieve maximum removal efficiency within the studied ranges. The results of the optimization are summarized in Table S2. The optimal conditions were obtained at a CTS/ZnO dosage of 0.18 g, an initial concentration of 16 mg L⁻¹, a solution pH of 7, and an ultrasound contact time of 36 min. Under these conditions, the models predicted a removal efficiency of 94.75% for BB41 and 91.01% for BR46, with a desirability value of 0.998, indicating that the suggested point satisfies the optimization criteria with high confidence. To verify the validity of the optimization, three replicate experiments were conducted under the predicted conditions. The observed removal efficiencies were 96.19 ± 2.4% for BB41 and 91.87 ± 2.3% for BR46, which were in excellent agreement with the predicted values. This close correlation between experimental and modeled results confirms the reliability of the developed regression models and demonstrates the effectiveness of CCD–RSM for optimizing adsorption processes. Under the optimum conditions, qe values were 4.28 mg g− 1 for BB41 and 4.08 mg g− 1 for BR46.

Selection of desorption solvent

In order to evaluate the reusability of CTS/ZnO, different eluents were tested for their efficiency in desorbing the adsorbed BB41 and BR46 from the spent adsorbent. Ethanol, acetone, hydrochloric acid, and sodium hydroxide solutions were selected based on their common use in regeneration studies (Fig. 2). After each adsorption cycle, the loaded CTS/ZnO was separated, washed with distilled water to remove unbound residues, and then treated with 5 mL of the selected eluent under sonication for 10 min. The concentration of BB41 and BR46 released into the solution was determined by UV-Vis spectrophotometry to assess the desorption efficiency. Among the tested solvents, hydrochloric acid exhibited the highest regeneration performance, achieving desorption efficiencies of 92.4% for BB41 and 85.7% for BR46. The strong protonation of the CTS/ZnO surface under acidic conditions likely weakened the electrostatic interactions between the CTS/ZnO and the cationic pollutants, facilitating their release. Ethanol and acetone resulted in moderate desorption (75–50%), whereas sodium hydroxide solution was less effective, possibly due to the competitive adsorption of OH⁻ ions. These results demonstrate that acidic treatment is the most suitable strategy for restoring the adsorption capacity of CTS/ZnO.

Regeneration and reusability

The reusability of CTS/ZnO was assessed through four adsorption–desorption cycles using HCl as the eluent, which had previously shown the highest desorption efficiency. After each adsorption step under optimal conditions, the spent adsorbent was separated, washed with distilled water, and subjected to desorption treatment. The regenerated adsorbent was then reused in the subsequent adsorption cycle under the same experimental conditions. As shown in Fig. 3, the adsorption efficiency of CTS/ZnO gradually decreased with increasing cycle number. The removal efficiency for BB41 declined from 94.2% in the first cycle to 69.5% in the fourth cycle, while BR46 removal decreased from 91.1 to 61.1% over the same period. The reduction in performance can be attributed to incomplete desorption of pollutants, partial loss of active sites, and possible structural changes in the adsorbent during repeated acid washing. Nevertheless, even after four consecutive cycles, CTS/ZnO retained more than 60% of its initial adsorption capacity, indicating a satisfactory level of stability and reusability.

Real water sample analysis

To evaluate the practical applicability of CTS/ZnO, adsorption experiments were conducted using real water samples. The samples were first filtered to remove suspended solids and then spiked with known concentrations of dye (21 mg L⁻¹) to simulate contaminated water conditions. The adsorption tests were carried out under the optimized operational parameters established in Sect. 3.4. The results, presented in Table 4, revealed that the removal efficiencies in real water matrices were slightly lower than those obtained in distilled water, which can be attributed to the presence of competing ions and natural organic matter. Specifically, the removal efficiency for BB41 was 88.01%, while that for BR46 reached 82.38%. Despite the modest decrease compared to synthetic systems, the overall performance of CTS/ZnO remained high, confirming its robustness and suitability for real-world applications. These results highlight that CTS/ZnO can effectively operate under complex water matrices, demonstrating its potential for practical environmental remediation.

Comparison with other adsorbents

A comparative evaluation of CTS/ZnO with other adsorbents reported in the literature provides useful insights into its relative performance. As summarized in Table 5, various materials such as activated carbons derived from agricultural byproducts, natural clays, and modified zeolites have been employed for the removal of dyes. Although many of these materials demonstrated acceptable efficiencies, they often required higher dosages or longer equilibrium times. For instance, Cocos Nucifera shell achieved over 80% dye removal, but demanded contact times exceeding two hours and relatively large amounts of adsorbent37. Similarly, some hen feather-based adsorbents were effective for BR46 uptake, yet their optimal performance was obtained only under strongly acidic conditions, which may limit practical applications38. In contrast, the present study demonstrated that CTS/ZnO could achieve more than 91% removal of BB41 and BR46 using only 0.18 g of adsorbent and an ultrasound contact time of 36 min under neutral pH conditions. This superior performance is primarily attributed to the high surface area and abundant ion-exchange sites of CTS/ZnO, which facilitate rapid mass transfer and strong electrostatic interactions with cationic pollutants. Comparable but less efficient outcomes have been reported for other adsorbents; for example, the pistachio shell achieved good dye adsorption but required longer times than CTS/ZnO39. Overall, the results clearly establish that CTS/ZnO offers a distinct advantage over many conventional adsorbents by combining high removal efficiency, rapid adsorption, and favorable working conditions. Moreover, the use of CCD–RSM optimization in this study reduced experimental effort and reagent consumption, further supporting the practical and sustainable application of CTS/ZnO in wastewater treatment.

Conclusion

This study demonstrated the effective removal of cationic dyes using a CTS/ZnO nanocomposite under ultrasonic-assisted conditions, highlighting the importance of combining eco-friendly adsorbents with process intensification techniques. Rather than focusing solely on individual parameters, the application of RSM enabled a systematic evaluation of the interactive effects of operational variables, leading to optimized dye removal performance with reduced experimental effort. The developed quadratic models exhibited excellent predictive ability with R² values above 0.99 and a non-significant lack of fit. The optimum operating conditions were identified as a CTS/ZnO dosage of 0.18 g, initial concentration of 16 mg L⁻¹, solution pH of 7, and an ultrasound contact time of 36 min. Under these conditions, removal efficiencies reached 96.19% for BB41 and 91.87% for BR46, which were in close agreement with the predicted values. The calculated qe of CTS/ZnO under the optimized conditions was 4.28 mg g− 1 for BB41 and 4.08 mg g− 1 for BR46, confirming the efficient dye uptake performance of the synthesized nanocomposite. Regeneration studies demonstrated that CTS/ZnO maintained more than 60% of its initial capacity after four consecutive adsorption–desorption cycles, highlighting its stability and reusability. Furthermore, experiments with real water samples confirmed the practical applicability of the CTS/ZnO, yielding removal efficiencies of 88.01% for BB41 and 82.38% for BR46. Comparison with other adsorbents reported in the literature indicated that CTS/ZnO provides distinct advantages, including higher efficiency at lower dosage, shorter contact times, and effective performance under neutral pH conditions. Based on the current results, several future aspects of this study can be identified. The optimized ultrasonic-assisted CTS/ZnO adsorption system demonstrated high removal efficiency under mild operating conditions, indicating its potential applicability in real wastewater treatment. Future studies should focus on evaluating the performance of the CTS/ZnO nanocomposite using real industrial effluents containing multiple contaminants to assess its practical feasibility. In addition, regeneration and reusability tests are recommended to determine the long-term stability and economic viability of the adsorbent. Further investigations involving kinetic, isotherm, and thermodynamic analyses under optimized conditions would provide deeper insight into the adsorption mechanism. Moreover, scaling up the process and integrating the CTS/ZnO system into continuous treatment units could support its application in sustainable wastewater management.
