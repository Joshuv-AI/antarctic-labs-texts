Distal Biceps Tendon Anatomical All‐Suture Anchor Repair
Authors: Afsana Hasan, Angela Alder‐Price, Jarret Woodmass, Rodrigo Brandariz, Keigo Honoki, Jeff Leiter, Darren Hart
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis technical note describes a single‐incision, onlay, double all‐suture anchor technique for distal biceps tendon repair. The method enables anatomic reattachment of the tendon onto the ulnar aspect of the radial tuberosity, optimizing footprint restoration and tendon‐bone contact area compared with conventional single‐incision techniques. This configuration provides secure fixation that permits immediate postoperative motion and offers a secondary anchor for added stability should one anchor fail.

This video shows the anatomy and step‐by‐step surgical technique for the distal biceps anatomical all‐suture anchor repair on a left cadaver arm. The patient is placed supine with the arm on a hand table. The radial tuberosity footprint is prepared and the distal biceps tendon is whipstitched with suture tape. Two 2.9 mm JuggerLoc unicortical anchors are placed into the ulnar aspect of the radial tuberosity, 20 mm apart. The tendon is passed through the proximal ZipLoop and the whipstitch limbs are tied to the distal ZipLoop. The distal ZipLoop is then tensioned to reduce the tendon‐to‐bone, followed by tightening of the proximal ZipLoop to provide an additional point of fixation. Optional reinforcement is shown by passing 1 limb of each ZipLoop suture through the tendon with a Mayo needle. Video content can be viewed at https://doi.org/10.1002/atn2.70204.

Distal biceps tendon ruptures occur at an incidence of approximately 2.5 per 100,000 patient‐years and typically occur in physically active middle‐aged men (mean 46 years). 1 Operative repair is associated with superior outcomes, including greater flexion and supination strength, endurance, and patient‐reported function, compared with nonoperative management. 2 , 3 Surgical repair can be performed via a single‐ or double‐incision approach. 4 Although the single‐incision method carries a higher risk of lateral antebrachial cutaneous nerve irritation, the double‐incision approach is associated with increased rates of heterotopic ossification and posterior interosseous nerve (PIN) palsy. 5 , 6 , 7 With no significantly different rate of rerupture or function between the 2 approaches, 8 , 9 , 10 there has been a trend in the literature toward refining the single‐incision technique to improve anatomical restoration.

Multiple fixation options have been described, including bone tunnels, cortical buttons, suture anchors, and interference screws, without a clear consensus regarding superiority. 11 , 12 Although bicortical metal buttons provide excellent pull‐out strength, 13 , 14 newer all‐suture anchors offer comparable biomechanical and clinical outcomes 15 , 16 , 17 while minimizing the risk of fracture. 7 , 15 , 16 , 17 , 18 They can be easily inserted unicortically, avoiding risk to the PIN. However, the optimal number, size, and configuration of all‐suture anchors for distal biceps repair remain undefined. The purpose of this technical note is to describe a reproducible single‐incision technique using two 2.9 mm JuggerLoc all‐suture anchors (Zimmer Biomet, Warsaw, IN) to achieve secure, anatomic reattachment of the distal biceps tendon onto its native footprint.

Patient Positioning and Exposure

The patient is positioned supine with the arm abducted on an arm table. A standard single anterior incision is made over the antecubital fossa. The lateral antebrachial cutaneous nerve is identified and protected throughout. The distal biceps tendon is located, and the degenerative stump is debrided.

Tendon Preparation

The distal 2 to 3 cm of the tendon is whipstitched using a #2 high‐strength suture tape with approximately 5 passes. The suture is then back‐passed to lock the configuration and brought out through the deep surface of the tendon. The needle is removed.

Preparation of the Insertion Site

With the forearm in full supination to expose the ulnar aspect of the radial tuberosity, the bony surface is cleared of soft tissue and lightly decorticated with a curette or rasp to enhance healing.

Anchor Placement

Two 2.9 mm all‐suture anchors (JuggerLoc with ZipLoop, Zimmer Biomet, Warsaw, IN) are inserted unicortically into the anatomic footprint (Figure 1).

Tendon Fixation

The tendon is first passed through the proximal loop of the anchor construct. The whipstitch suture limbs are tied to the distal ZipLoop suture using alternating half‐hitches and cut. The distal ZipLoop is then gradually tensioned to draw the tendon down onto the prepared footprint, an especially useful maneuver in retracted cases (Figure 2). The proximal ZipLoop is subsequently tightened for additional compression and stability.

Left Arm: Cadaveric arm showing the tendon passed beneath the proximal ZipLoop. The distal ZipLoop is being tensioned to reduce the tendon‐to‐bone.

For added security, one or both limbs of each ZipLoop suture may be passed through the tendon with a Mayo needle and tied over the tendon surface. The final construct shows firm tendon apposition to bone with dual‐anchor fixation (Figure 3).

Left Arm: Cadaveric arm showing the final repair construct with the tendon secured flush to the bone using dual all‐suture anchors.

Postoperative Rehabilitation

The limb is placed in a sling for comfort and protection for 4 to 6 weeks. Immediate, unrestricted active and passive elbow range‐of‐motion exercises are commenced. Progressive strengthening begins at 12 weeks postoperatively.

DISCUSSION

The described technique provides a safe, reproducible, and anatomic method of distal biceps tendon repair using 2 unicortical all‐suture anchors with self‐tensioning ZipLoop fixation (Table 2).

The native distal biceps footprint measures approximately 21 × 7 mm on the ulnar aspect of the radial tuberosity, with the long head inserting proximally and the short head distally. 19 , 20 , 21 Nonanatomic reattachment onto the central or radial surface of the tuberosity can significantly reduce supination strength, with cadaveric studies showing up to a 40% reduction in torque when repaired nonanatomically. 22 , 23

Neither bone tunnel nor surface tendon‐bone fixation has been proven superior in promoting tendon‐to‐bone healing in animal, biomechanical, or clinical studies. 24 , 25 Traditional bicortical fixation, first described by Bain et al., provides excellent strength but carries a small risk of PIN injury. 26 More recent studies have shown no significant biomechanical difference between intramedullary and extramedullary button fixation. 27 As a result, unicortical fixation techniques have been developed to allow drill holes to be directed as radially as possible, maintaining anatomical placement while minimizing risk to the PIN.

Although the present method restores the footprint more anatomically than anterior fixation techniques, it does not separately reproduce the distinct long‐ and short head insertions. This represents a potential limitation, but the design prioritizes greater tendon‐to‐bone contact and improved construct stability within the anatomical footprint.

The mean force required to actively flex the elbow to 90° against gravity is 50 N, 28 whereas the rupture threshold of the intact distal biceps tendon is approximately 204 N. 29 Modern fixation constructs—whether buttons, anchors, or screws—typically exceed this requirement in biomechanical testing. 13 , 14 , 18 , 30 , 31 , 32 Although bicortical buttons have shown the highest pull‐out strength (approximately 342 N), studies have shown that 2 all‐suture anchors provide comparable maximum strength. 13 , 30

The 2.9 mm anchors used in this technique provide greater pull‐out resistance than smaller anchors. 33 They require smaller drill holes than metallic anchors or cortical buttons, potentially reducing the risk of iatrogenic fracture and heterotopic ossification. 5 , 16 The dual‐anchor construct, spaced approximately 10 mm apart, increases the tendon‐bone contact and provides redundancy—ensuring that even if one anchor fails, tendon apposition is maintained. 34 This configuration also distributes load across the tendon and minimizes gap formation during cyclic loading. 18 , 34 The self‐tensioning ZipLoop mechanism draws the tendon securely to bone and is particularly useful for retracted tendons. Early recovery and minimal time off work are highly valued by this patient group; therefore, a fixation method strong enough to enable early mobilization without compromising integrity is desirable. The described construct provides a strong initial fixation that permits immediate range‐of‐motion exercises without splinting.

Previously described all‐suture anchor techniques have used smaller (1.35‐2.6 mm) anchors, end‐on reattachment configurations, and more complex suture‐passing steps. 15 , 16 , 35 , 36 , 37 The present technique employs a side‐by‐side onlay configuration that increases tendon‐bone contact, and the second anchor functions as a safeguard. The combination of larger anchors and the self‐tensioning ZipLoop system enables secure compression of the tendon onto its anatomic footprint using a straightforward, reproducible sequence of steps. The procedure is clearly displayed in the accompanying surgical video, making the technique accessible even to surgeons less familiar with all‐suture anchor constructs.
