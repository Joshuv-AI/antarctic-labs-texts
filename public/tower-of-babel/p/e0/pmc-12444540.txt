Cu Substitution on LaCu x Ni1–x O3 Perovskite Structure and Catalytic Activity for Glycerol Conversion Using Various Glycerol Feeds under Base- and H2‑Free Conditions
Authors: Darine Denala, Wutthikrai Busayaporn, Wanwisa Limphirat, Sompin Mahakot, Sirirat Jitkarnka
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Cu was foreseen by our group as a high potential promoter in the LaNiO3 perovskite catalyst for producing biobased chemicals from glycerol transformation, especially when inserted into the structure. Thus, LaCu x Ni1–x O3 perovskites were specifically synthesized and characterized to acquire geared structures and compositions for such a purpose. Subsequently, the materials were tested as catalysts under an alkaline-free and external H2-free environment, known to be a sustainably friendly condition that usually gives a low yield of desired products. Moreover, not only was pure glycerol employed as the raw material, the activity was also tested using a real glycerol crude from a biodiesel-producing company in Thailand. As a result, the newly synthesized perovskite catalysts can incredibly perform well even when the real crude glycerol was employed as the feed without alkaline or H2 addition. Such successes would make the direct utilization of industrial glycerol crudes, without prior purification, possible.

Introduction

Greenhouse gas (GHG) concentrations in the earth's atmosphere have been reported to rise over time, causing climate change. To limit the impacts of climate change, the “Net Zero” policy has been launched and applied, and as a result, fossil fuels were replaced by bioenergy as the primary energy source. The use of bioenergy has increased on average by 7% per year between 2010 and 2021, with its demand increasing throughout the year, including biofuel. Biodiesel is a biofuel that has been widely used, and its production involves a transesterification reaction that produces glycerol as a byproduct. However, compared to those of other biofuels, the growth of biodiesel demand was at a slower pace due to its high price. For instance, in 2022, global biodiesel prices rose by 20–30%. Furthermore, biodiesel production usually has a high manufacturing cost, which may jeopardize its economic feasibility. One of the ways to overcome this problem is by utilizing the use of its byproduct, that is, glycerol. Glycerol is a triol compound that is considered as one of the top 12 building block chemicals due to its ability to be transformed into high value-added chemicals, such as organic acids (lactic acid, oxalic acid, citric acid, and glyceric acid) and diols (1,2-propanediol and 1,3-propanediol). In general, industrial glycerol is usually manufactured with two specifications, that is, pure glycerol (99.5% purity) and crude glycerol (60–80% purity). Without purification, the crude glycerol obtained from biodiesel production usually contains some impurities such as water, ash, methanol, and matter organic non-glycerol (MONG). Such impurities causes the purification of crude glycerol expensive in order to achieve a high purity of glycerol. Therefore, any direct utilization of crude glycerol without purification could be beneficial in reducing the investment of the entire process.

Glycerol transformation to biobased chemicals has been extensively studied throughout the years, with the general catalytic pathways driven through acid and basic sites, as shown in Scheme . In the acid-catalyzed pathways, acetol serves as an intermediate for both 1,2-propanediol and lactic acid formation, as it can be converted to 1,2-propanediol via hydrogenation reaction or undergo the oxidation to pyruvaldehyde, which leads to lactic acid formation. , Moreover, in the oxidizing and basic environments, 1,2-propanediol can also be further converted into lactic acid. For the base-driven routes, pyruvaldehyde is a key intermediate for the formation of both lactic acid and 1,2-propanediol. Depending on the reaction conditions, if sufficient hydrogen is available for hydrogenation reaction, then 1,2-propanediol can be formed, whereas lactic acid is produced through the Cannizzaro reaction over Lewis acid sites in an alkaline environment. , Additionally, if water is present in the feedstock, then aqueous phase reforming (APR) and water–gas shift (WGS) reactions can simultaneously occur, generating in situ H2.

General Pathways of Glycerol Transformation to Biobased Chemicals

Several researchers have attempted to produce 1,2-propanediol and lactic acid from glycerol using various catalysts comprising noble and non-noble metals, including Pt, Au, and Co; however, a dilute aqueous glycerol solution was mostly used as a feed. For instances, the catalytic activity of Pt/Sn-MFI catalysts was investigated on the glycerol conversion to lactic acid using an aqueous glycerol solution (0.2 M) and O2 as the oxidant under a mild condition. As a result, the catalyst was able to achieve 89.9% glycerol conversion and 80.5% lactic acid selectivity. Such a high activity reportedly resulted from the hydrophilicity of the Sn-MFI surface, which enhanced the adsorption of glycerol, resulting in a higher glycerol conversion. Other studies have also reported the high activity of their catalysts on transforming glycerol to lactic acid using CuO/ZrO2 (95% yield), Cu/γAl2O3 (70.8% selectivity), or CuO/AC (92.6% selectivity). However, such a high activity was attributed to the presence of an O2 gas or an alkali. Similarly, many studies reported the high catalyst activity on 1,2-propanediol formation, but yet external H2 gas was supplied to the reactor. , Nevertheless, the addition of an external alkali or a gas would not be favored due to the additional costs of treatment and gas handling; thus, alkali-, O2-, and H2- free conditions are much preferred.

Furthermore, only a few studies have utilized highly concentrated glycerol feeds in the experiments due to several challenges, such as the formation of undesired side products and mass transfer limitation caused by the high viscosity of glycerol, leading to low conversion and yields. Even if pure glycerol was utilized, it was usually diluted only around 2–3 M with the addition of an alkali. Previously, our group has investigated the conversion of pure glycerol into biobased chemicals using LDH-derived catalysts that had been modified by non-noble metals, including Cu, Zr, and Ni under base- and H2-free conditions. As expected, the conversion remains challenging with the formation of unwanted byproducts. Recently, some appropriate catalysts were strategically designed, based on the advances of reaction through the pathways and the selection of catalyst compounds, aiming to convert pure glycerol into lactic acid under base-, H2-, and O2-free conditions. The 25.8% selectivity of lactic acid with 53.8% glycerol conversion was achieved by using embedded composited catalysts. Such good activity was attributed to the catalyst design.

Formerly, numerous studies regarding the utilization of crude glycerol as a feed have been conducted, aiming to produce valuable chemicals; however, they were mostly accomplished via a fermentation process. Some attempts have been done on the catalytic conversion of crude glycerol to various chemicals, such as aromatics, carboxylic acids, hydrogen, and glycerol carbonate. For instances, the formation of lactic acid and 1,2-propanediol from crude glycerol was investigated over Pt/ZrO2, CaO, and Cu-B/Al2O3. As usual, most studies relied on either an external alkali or H2 gas to drive the formation of the desired products. Hence, the conversion of crude glycerol to 1,2-propanediol or lactic acid under base- and H2-free conditions remains challenging. Additionally, since crude glycerol consists of various impurities, a sufficiently high conversion and yield of the desired products becomes even more difficult to achieve as the impurities can interfere with the adsorption of glycerol during the reaction. For instances, the effects of several impurities, such as CH3COOH, CH3OH, and KOH, were studied for their influences toward aqueous phase reforming of glycerol. As a result, the presence of CH3OH and CH3COOH as impurities suppressed the conversion. Additionally, some other impurities such as fatty acid salts, stearic acid, and long-chain alkanes also reportedly inhibited the reaction by competitively adsorbed on the catalyst surface. Thus, an appropriate catalyst is required to achieve high catalytic activity in glycerol transformation to biobased chemicals no matter what pure, diluted, or crude glycerol is utilized as a feedstock. Each feed presents its own challenges, for example, unwanted side products in the case of using pure glycerol or interference from water and/or the other impurities in diluted and crude glycerol.

Perovskite oxides, one of the perovskite-class materials that have the general formula of ABO3, are promising materials for catalysis applications due to their versatility. The acid–base and redox properties of a perovskite can be tuned by changing the A and B sites of its structure. Hence, perovskites are suitable materials for reactions requiring both oxidation and acid–base sites. It was reported that LaBO3 supported AuPt catalysts for glycerol oxidation to valuable chemicals using aqueous glycerol (0.3 M) in the presence of NaOH as a base. It was found that the B sites of the perovskites controlled the overall reaction pathways, whereas LaCrO3, LaFeO3, and LaNiO3 perovskites were found to have a relatively high selectivity toward lactic acid. The best catalytic activity was achieved on LaCrO3- and LaNiO3-supported perovskites with ∼95 and ∼80% glycerol conversion, respectively. The presence of Cr and Fe in the B site of perovskites was found to govern only the dehydration pathways, leading to the lactic acid formation, while that of Ni was reported to govern both oxidation and dehydration pathways. Moreover, the activity of AuPt/LaBO3 was compared with that of its single oxides (AuPt/MO), and it was found that the single oxide was less selective than the perovskite-supported catalyst. Although perovskites are considered as a group of promising materials, not so many studies have been done on the uses of perovskite materials as the catalysts for crude glycerol valorization.

Apparently, perovskite oxides exhibit promising activity for the oxidation reaction due to their high redox properties. Moreover, according to the literature review, the structure and properties of perovskite oxides can be adjusted by modifying the B site in order to achieve specific activity that can selectively drive the elementary reactions in the pathways of glycerol oxidation. Furthermore, not a number of extensive studies nor the conversion of crude glycerol toward chemicals has been accomplished, especially using perovskite oxide catalysts under the environments of different glycerol feedstock. LaNiO3 was chosen as the base perovskite because it can govern both oxidation and dehydration reactions. , Thus, in this work, the activity of LaCu x Ni1–x O3 perovskite catalysts was therefore studied on glycerol transformation to products using various types of feeds without the addition of an external base or any gas. Cu was chosen to partially replace some of the Ni sites to promote the Cannizzaro reaction, expectedly leading to high lactic acid formation. The Cu insertion in the perovskite oxide was first investigated on the change of its structural environment and the catalytic activity using pure glycerol as a feedstock. Then, the best catalyst was chosen to be further investigated for its catalytic activity using a freshly prepared simulated crude glycerol and a real crude glycerol taken from a biodiesel production plant as the feeds.

Results and Discussion

Physical Properties of LaCu x Ni1–x O3

Figure illustrates the XRD patterns of the synthesized LaCu x Ni1–x O3 samples and reference materials. To understand the general properties and the effect of Cu substitution in the perovskite structure, the parent LaNiO3 was synthesized and employed as the reference sample. The diffraction peaks of LaNiO3 (PDF 01-070-5757) were observed with a small peak formed at 44°, which correspond to NiO (PDF 03-065-2901), indicating that the LaNiO3 sample also contained some traces of impurities, as also reported in ref . With the substitution of 2.6% Cu (x = 0.1), a decrease in the intensity of the characteristic LaNiO3 peak centered at 32.75° was observed (Figure a), along with the evolution of the LaCu x Ni1–x O3 peaks. The increasing amount of Cu (x = 0.3) in the composition seems to alter the overall perovskite structure. Namely, the crystallinity of LaNiO3 peaks was further decreased, and new peaks that correspond to CuO (35.5°) and La2CuO4 (31.1°) phases start to be detected in the XRD profiles. At the highest Cu substitution (x = 0.5), both CuO and La2CuO4 peaks become more defined, showing that the excess Cu (x ≥ 0.3) triggers the formation of those phases as similarly reported in ref . Since Cu insertion was conducted in an oxygen-lean environment, some Cu3+ ions were thus reduced to Cu2+, causing a decrease in lattice volume (Figure S1 in the supplementary document) and tensile strain due to the ion mismatch in the perovskite system. Therefore, in order to reduce the elastic energy in the structure caused by the presence of Cu2+, some of Cu2+ species were segregated toward the surface of the perovskites. Likewise, the broadening of the characteristic NiO peak is observed in Figure b after Cu species were inserted into the perovskite structure, suggesting that the presence of oxygen vacancies induced the exclusion of not only CuO but also NiO from the structure.

The physical properties and elemental composition of LaCu x Ni1–x O3 samples are also summarized in Table . The surface area of the perovskite samples is consistent with that of the samples in the previous report. Generally, the samples have a low specific surface area (<20 m2/g) due to the high calcination temperature applied to obtain the perovskite-type oxides. There is no significant change in the surface area when Cu starts to insert into the perovskite structure. At Cu ratios of 0.1 and 0.3, a slight increase in the surface area is observed. However, when the ratio of Cu is increased to 0.5, the surface area also decreases to 12.1 m2/g. This similar fashion is also found in the total pore volume of the perovskite, where significant decreases are observed when the Cu ratio is increased to 0.5, probably caused by the CuO phase that segregates outside the perovskite structure and fills the perovskite pores.

Investigation on the Structure of LaCu x Ni1–x O3

The changes in the local environment of LaCu x Ni1–x O3 perovskite samples after Cu substitution were further investigated using XPS, XANES, and EXAFS. The XPS spectra of LaCu x Ni1–x O3 samples were examined and are presented in Figure . The La 3d core level of the lanthanum perovskites exhibits a typical La 3d spectrum consisting of the multiplet splitting of 3d5/2 and 3d3/2. The deconvolution of La peaks for all samples reveals only one peak at around 833.5–834.6 eV, which corresponds to La3+, obviously contributed from La2O3 as the parent. Moreover, the observed binding energy of La 3d for all LaCu x Ni1–x O3 samples is slightly higher than that of La2O3 (833.2 eV), showing that La3+ is located inside the perovskite structure. Furthermore, no change in the La 3d profiles is observed when Cu (x = 0.1) starts to substitute into the Ni sites. However, when the amount of Cu further increases from 0.3 to 0.5, a slight shift of La 3d peaks toward a higher binding energy is observed, indicating a change in the local environment of La due to the appearance of Cu2+ ions. It can generally be explained that the charge imbalance in the perovskite was compensated by the formation of oxygen vacancies on the surface; therefore, the shift toward a higher binding energy was possibly caused by the electronic surface modification due to the Cu2+ insertion in the lattice.

XPS spectra of (a) La 3d, (b) Ni 2p, and (c) Cu 2p of the LaCu x Ni1–x O3 perovskite catalysts.

The analysis of Ni 2p spectra of LaCu x Ni1–x O3 samples is complex because the Ni 2p3/2 peak was overlapped with that of the La 3d3/2 peak. The deconvolution of the Ni 2p3/2 region exhibits two peaks at ∼853.5 and ∼854.7 eV that correspond to Ni2+and Ni3+, respectively. Moreover, the broad peak at 863.7 eV is a satellite peak of Ni3+, indicating the formation of LaNiO3 and the transformation of Ni3+ ↔ Ni2+, whereas the Ni2+ peak at 853.5 eV is mostly contributed from the NiO phases formed outside the perovskite. The effect of Cu substitution in the structure can also be monitored on the Ni 2p peaks. It can be observed that the intensity of the Ni3+ peak is decreasing along with the increase in the Cu ratio (x = 0.1 to 0.5), supporting that Cu is gradually incorporated into the structure, possibly replacing Ni3+ and forming Cu3+ ions, as also supported by the change on the elemental composition in Table . Moreover, the formation of a shoulder at 853.5 eV on the Ni 2p spectra becomes more well-defined at a higher Cu ratio, which represents the evolution of Ni2+ species (see also Table ). Previously, the XRD patterns have shown a significant increase in the NiO crystallite size at a high Cu ratio (x ≥ 0.3), and the XPS results also confirmed the existence of Ni2+, which are likely to be contributed from the exclusion of Ni from the perovskite structure and then the formation of NiO clusters. The findings are also consistent with the information observed from the Cu 2p profiles in Figure c. Namely, at the beginning of Cu insertion (x = 0.1), the deconvolution reveals two peaks at ∼932 and ∼935 eV, which correspond to the Cu+ and Cu3+ states, respectively. At a higher Cu ratio (x ≥ 0.3), changes occur in which both Cu2+ (933.3 eV) and Cu3+ states (935 eV) are observed with no detection of the Cu+ peak. Moreover, the intensity of the satellite peak at a binding energy of 940–945 eV decreases along with the increasing Cu:Ni ratio, which is correlated with oxygen deficiencies in the perovskite structure. Since the Cu precursor was not introduced under high-pressure oxygen conditions, the absence of oxygen made some of the Cu ions preferably reside in the stable form (Cu2+), which consequently leads to an exclusion of Cu from the perovskite structure at a higher Cu ratio.

In addition, the oxidation states of the elements and local environment in the perovskites can be obtained using XANES and EXAFS. The La L3-edge, Ni K-edge, and Cu K-edge X-ray absorption near edge structure (XANES) spectra for LaCu x Ni1–x O3 perovskites are presented in Figure a–c. The La L3-Edge spectrum shows a white line at around ∼5490 eV, attributed to the electronic transition of 2p3/2 to 5d transition. The measured XANES spectra after substitution of Cu are located at an energy higher than that of the La2O3 reference, demonstrating that the La3+ ions are located in the perovskite structure. Moreover, a change in the width of the white line can be observed with the increasing Cu:Ni ratio. Namely, the narrow white lines, observed on the LaNiO3 and LaCu0.1Ni0.9O3 spectra, indicate that a La3+ cation is surrounded with nine adjacent oxygen atoms. With the increasing amount of Cu:Ni ratio to 0.3, the white line is getting wider, indicating the decrease in the amount of oxygen surrounding the La3+ cation.

XANES spectra at (a) La L3-Edge, (b) Ni K-Edge, and (c) Cu K-Edge of the LaCu x Ni1–x O3 perovskite catalysts.

The Ni K-edge spectra of LaCu x Ni1–x O3 samples and their references are presented in Figure b. The XANES spectra of all samples reveal a weak pre-edge peak at around ∼8334 eV, which represents the quadrupole-allowed electronic transition of 1s to 3d that adopts octahedral geometries. Moreover, the main edge peak of LaCu x Ni1–x O3 samples lies in between NiO (+2) and NiO2 (+4) standard references, indicating the oxidation states of Ni inside the perovskite structure ranges between +2 and +4. To measure the exact oxidation state of the Ni inside LaCu x Ni1–x O3 perovskites, the edge peak energy (E0) of the Ni K-edge of the references was plotted against its oxidation state, and the polynomial equation obtained from the data fitting was used to calculate the oxidation states of the elements. The results are listed in Table . It was found that the oxidation states of Ni in the LaCu x Ni1–x O3 perovskite samples are in between 2 ≤ x ≤ 3, evidently confirming that both Ni2+ and Ni3+ are present in the samples simultaneously.

The Cu K-edge XANES spectra of LaCu x Ni1–x O3 perovskites and their standard references are illustrated in Figure c. In comparison with the references, the LaCu x Ni1–x O3 samples show a significantly lower intensity of the pre-edge peak at a higher energy position, which indicates an octahedral geometry. A strong white line at ∼8995 eV appears in all LaCu x Ni1–x O3 samples, representing the dipole-allowed electronic transition from 1s to 4p. Likewise, to confirm that Cu ions had occupied the Ni sites, the oxidation state of Cu was also determined by using the same method as employed in the Ni case. Apparently, the oxidation states of Cu in the perovskite are in the range of 2.6–2.8, which is close to 3, indicating that Cu had partially replaced the Ni sites of the perovskite structure. Furthermore, in order to elucidate the environment of Cu in the perovskites, the EXAFS analysis is next discussed.

The EXAFS analysis was conducted to obtain details regarding the change in the local environment surrounding the central atoms (Ni and Cu) after the substitution of Cu in the LaNiO3 perovskite structure. From the Ni K-edge EXAFS spectrum of LaNiO3 in Figure a, the first shell formed at ∼1.5 Å is contributed from the Ni–O scattering path of the perovskite structure. The existence of NiO results in the formation of a peak at ∼2.5 Å, which is assigned to Ni–Ni scattering. The third peak with a shoulder at ∼3.1 and ∼3.5 Å is ascribed to the Ni–La and Ni–O–Ni scattering paths from the perovskite, respectively. The bond distances of all scattering paths were then calculated from EXAFS and are shown in Table , which are consistent with the previously reported values.

The Cu substitution into the LaNiO3 structure was also investigated using the Cu K-edge EXAFS spectra (Figure b). At x = 0.1, the displayed spectrum is similar to that of LaNiO3 with the first shell, centered at 1.5 Å, which refers to the Cu–O scattering path of the perovskite. The second peak at 2.5 Å, contributed from the NiO impurity phase, can still be observed in the spectrum; however, the intensity of the peak is lower than that of the reference sample. The third shell with a shoulder at 3.1 and 3.5 Å is contributed from Cu–La and Cu–Ni scattering paths of the perovskites. It is evident that Cu added in a low amount did not alter the bond distances of all scattering paths, as Cu3+ and Ni3+ ions possess a similar ionic radius (Cu3+ = 0.54 Å and Ni3+ = 0.56 Å), which further confirms that Cu3+ had replaced some of the Ni sites. Overall, the EXAFS spectrum at x = 0.1 is significantly different from that of LaNiO3, suggesting that all Cu ions were successfully inserted into some of the Ni sites, forming a LaCu0.1Ni0.9O3 perovskite. With the increasing Cu:Ni ratio to 0.3, the EXAFS spectrum is still quite similar to that of LaNiO3; however, the intensity of the perovskite peaks decreases. Furthermore, the bond length of the Cu–La scatter group changes, that is, the addition of Cu leads to the elongation in the Cu–La bond length of the first scatter group to 3.31 Å. This is possibly due to the evolution of Cu2+ ions since Cu is not stable in Cu3+ state. On the other hand, the second scatter group that consists of six La atoms has a shorter radial distance (3.22 Å) than that found in the reference, which could be an indication that Cu ions do not only partially replace the Ni sites but also the excess is excluded from the perovskite structure, forming CuO clusters on the surface. Additionally, the EXAFS fitting results also confirm a good fit with the CuO structure (space group: Fm3̅m). Moreover, the Cu 2p XPS spectra (Table ) have also revealed the increase in the CuO phase with the increasing Cu:Ni ratio. Furthermore, at the Cu:Ni ratio of 0.5, it appears that the overall EXAFS spectrum further changes. It is found that the coordination number of Cu–O significantly decreases to 5.8, and the alteration in bond distance of Cu–La is also observed along with a decrease in the coordination number of the second scatter group of Cu–La, indicating that more Cu ions were excluded from the perovskite structure. Moreover, the bond distance of Cu–Ni also significantly decreases at this ratio, implying that more CuO clusters are present outside the perovskite structure.

In summary, Cu ions have successfully substituted for the Ni sites of the perovskite structure. The oxidation state of Ni and Cu lies within the range 2 ≤ x ≤ 3, as proven by the XPS and XANES analyses. Additionally, the exclusion of Cu from the perovskite structure and the subsequent formation of CuO on the surface, proven by XRD and EXAFS results, were found to impact the overall oxidation state of Cu in the material. Morphology

The FE-SEM images of the LaCu x Ni1–x O3 samples are depicted in Figure a–d. The LaNiO3 reference has a rhombohedral structure, with an average particle size of 185.2 nm. When Cu ions start to be inserted into the structure, agglomeration starts to occur, resulting in a larger particle size with the average of 200–300 nm. The increasing amount of Cu results in the alteration of the rhombohedral structure, and at the highest Cu ratio, x = 0.5, the surface of the sample appears to be deposited by the excluded CuO clusters. Figure a–d­(3–7) reveals the EDX analysis of LaCu x Ni1–x O3 samples, and the elemental mapping shows that La (white), Ni (green), and Cu (blue) elements are present in the catalysts. Moreover, the denser distribution of Cu in the EDX mapping and the evolution of some agglomerates prove that more Cu atoms exist in the perovskite at x = 0.5, which is consistent with the results obtained from all characterization techniques.

Acid–Base Properties of LaCu x Ni1–x O3

The acidity and basicity of the perovskite catalysts are summarized in Table . Normally, the NH3-TPD profile of a perovskite oxide is composed of two peaks, that is, a peak located at <300 °C, corresponding to the weak acid sites, and the second peak located at 300–400 °C, attributed to the medium to strong acid sites. Figure shows that the parent LaNiO3 only possesses weak and medium to strong acid sites with the total acid density of 0.490 mmol/g. When Cu starts to substitute at the Ni sites (x = 0.1) inside the perovskite structure, the acid strength is enhanced, evidenced by an increase in the amount of medium acid sites to 0.326 mmol/g. The total acid density remains the same after such a low substitution by Cu. At a higher amount of Cu (x = 0.3), a new peak at >400 °C evolves possibly due to the presence of CuO that has been proven to be excluded from the structure (see Figure ). Moreover, the formation of oxygen vacancy after Cu replacement might also contribute to the increase in acidity. Since Cu3+ is unstable, it is partially reduced to Cu2+ (Table ), resulting in the formation of oxygen vacancies and an increase in the total acidity to 1.127 mmol/g. At the highest Cu ratio (x = 0.5), the desorption peaks shift toward a lower temperature, indicating a decrease in acid strength, possibly caused by a higher amount of CuO aggregates excluded from the structure and partially covering the acid sites of the perovskite catalyst.

In case of basicity, the CO2-TPD profile generally reveals three desorption regions: the first peak that appears at <300 °C is attributed to the weak basic sites, the second peak formed at 300–400 °C corresponds to the medium basic sites, and the last peak at >400 °C refers to strong basic sites. For LaNiO3, the CO2 profile shows the main peak located at around ∼390 °C with a short shoulder peak at <300 °C, showing that the catalyst is mainly composed of weak and medium basic sites. At a low Cu substitution (x = 0.1), an increase in the medium basic density is observed without the peak of weak basic sites, indicating that the presence of Cu in the perovskite structure improves the basic strength. A further increase in the amount of the Cu ratio to 0.3 and 0.5 results in a new peak formation at above >400 °C, possibly contributed from the presence of CuO clusters outside the perovskite structure, since the intensity of this peak increases with the amount of the CuO clusters.

Hence, the presence of Cu (both Cu3+ and Cu2+) in the Ni sites of the perovskite increases the medium acid and basic densities of the catalysts, while the existence of CuO outside the structure increases the overall basic strength of the perovskite.

Influence of Cu Insertion on the Catalytic Activity of LaCu x Ni1–x O3

The catalytic activity of LaCu x Ni1–x O3 on glycerol conversion was investigated and then compared to those of LaNiO3- and La2O3-based catalysts (NiO/La2O3, CuO/La2O3, and La2O3), as shown in Figure a. As a result, La2O3-based catalysts are found to relatively have a low conversion (39.6, 21.5%, and 15.5% for NiO/La2O3, CuO/La2O3, and La2O3, respectively). In contrast, all perovskite-based catalysts give a significantly higher conversion, and the parent LaNiO3 exhibits the highest catalytic activity (91.6%), indicating that the perovskite structure is required to achieve high performance. However, the substitution of Cu into LaNiO3, forming LaCu x Ni1–x O3, gradually decreases the glycerol conversion to 87.6, 80.7, and 78.9% for x = 0.1, 0.3, and 0.5, respectively, which appear to be related to the increase in Cu sites, particularly Cu3+ sites in relation to the decrease in Ni3+ sites, as confirmed by the gradual decrease in the frequency (TOF) for glycerol in Figure b upon % Cu substitution. Therefore, the Ni3+ sites are responsible for converting glycerol to products.

The distribution of products is depicted in Figure c. It can be seen that the parent LaNiO3 perovskite produces 23.9% lactic acid, 20.7% 1,2-propanediol, 19.2% acetone, 2.6% trioses (glyceraldehyde and dihydroxyacetone), and some other byproducts. The traces of trioses found in the product distribution suggest that both lactic acid and 1,2-propanediol are formed via dehydrogenation pathways. The insertion of Cu at x = 0.1 into the LaNiO3 structure results in the increases in both lactic acid and 1,2-propanediol selectivity to 28.3 and 35.3%, respectively. Since the B site of a perovskite sample is generally the Lewis acid center of the structure, an improvement in lactic acid formation is possibly attributed to the new Lewis acid sites created by the Cu3+–O pair inside the perovskite in this particular case. Additionally, besides creating new Lewis acid sites, the presence of Cu3+ ions is also believed to promote the dehydration of glyceraldehyde to pyruvaldehyde, as indicated by the decrease in glyceraldehyde selectivity from 1.9 to 0.1% after the addition of 0.1 Cu to LaNiO3 (Table S2 in the Supplementary Document). Moreover, the formation of lactic acid usually requires a homogeneous basic condition to achieve a high yield, and the impact of heterogeneous basic sites is also hereby examined and found to promote lactic acid formation. Namely, the highest lactic acid yield is achieved on the catalyst that possesses the highest basicity (Figure d). A similar trend has also been reported in another study, in which the combination of Lewis acid and basic sites enhanced lactic acid production. Additionally, despite the absence of an external H2 gas, an increase in 1,2-propanediol formation is also observed, indicating that simultaneous in situ hydrogen formation also occurs, which is probably driven by glycerol reforming. ,

However, at x = 0.3, the selectivity of both lactic acid and 1,2-propanediol is enhanced to 21.5 and 28.5%, respectively, while acetone formation increases to 22.9%. Furthermore, acetone selectivity is elevated from 16.1% (x = 0.1) to 26.6% (x = 0.5), accompanied by a decline in lactic acid selectivity from 28.3 to 13.2%, evidently related to CuO formation that appears at x ≥ 0.3 and coexists with the LaCu x Ni1–x O3 perovskites (Figure ). It can be inferred that CuO (Cu2+) promotes acetone production while suppressing lactic acid formation, likely by enhancing the acidity of the catalysts and facilitating the dehydration of 1,2-propanediol to acetone. This is supported by the product distribution observed by using CuO/La2O3 and CuO+LaNiO3 catalysts (Figure c andTable ). Namely, La2O3, solely employed as a catalyst mostly gives 6.6% acetone selectivity. Then, the selectivity is significantly enhanced to 16.2% using CuO/La2O3. A similar finding is also found in the case of a physical mixture (CuO + LaNiO3). Hence, it can be concluded that the extra-framework CuO (Cu2+) drives acetone formation, whereas Cu incorporated in the LaCu x Ni1–x O3 perovskite structure favors the lactic acid formation.

Performance of LaCu0.1Ni0.9O3 Catalysts Using Industrial Crude Glycerol as a Feed

The LaCu0.1Ni0.9O3 catalyst was consequently chosen to be further investigated for its activity in the situation when crude glycerol feed is supposed to be utilized. As a base case, the simulated crude glycerol (70% pure glycerol diluted with 30% water) was first employed in order to understand the effect of water on the activity. Table reveals that under the simulated crude environment, the activity was slightly dropped to 84.2%, as compared to the pure glycerol environment, indicating that the presence of water molecules only slightly interferes with the adsorption of glycerol molecules on the LaCu0.1Ni0.9O3 surface. However, no production of lactic acid is observed in conjunction with the formation of 1,2-propanediol as the major product, with 63.5% selectivity (Table ). The presence of water likely shifts the product distribution by facilitating in situ hydrogen formation via aqueous phase reforming (APR) of glycerol, as water molecules provide the hydroxyl groups required to drive the reaction. Additionally, Ni species (Ni3+ and Ni2+) in the perovskite can also promote the APR, as these sites are known to effectively break the C–C bonds, further enhancing the formation of in situ H2 that is supplied to the acetol hydrogenation to 1,2-propanediol. Furthermore, since the water gas shift (WGS) reaction can also occur concurrently with APR, , it can generate additional in situ H2, further promoting the hydrogenation steps and increasing 1,2-propanediol selectivity.

Conclusions

In this work, the LaCu x Ni1–x O3 perovskite oxides were successfully synthesized and Cu was found to partially substitute the Ni sites of the perovskite samples. The incorporation of Cu at x = 0.1 was found to enhance both lactic acid and 1,2-propanediol selectivity, owing to the new Lewis acid sites created by the Cu3+–O pairs and the basicity of the catalysts. Moreover, an increase in 1,2-propanediol selectivity indicated that Cu3+ promoted in situ H2 production that was supplied to the hydrogenation to 1,2-propanediol. At x ≥ 0.3, the exclusion of Cu from the perovskite structure took place, forming CuO outside the structure, which affected the overall activity. It was found that the presence of CuO did not only lower the activity of the catalyst but also altered the reaction pathways, favoring acetone production rather than lactic acid and 1,2-propanediol. As a result, Cu located inside the perovskite structure and the catalyst basicity were the ones playing important roles on the activity and selectivity. Furthermore, the performance of the selected LaCu0.1Ni0.9O3 catalyst was tested under the environment of three feeds, that is, pure glycerol, simulated crude glycerol, and real crude glycerol. It was found that the presence of water and other impurities in both simulated and crude glycerol feeds interfered with the adsorption of glycerol molecules on the catalyst surface, resulting in the lower conversion of glycerol. However, the presence of water and methanol helped to generate the in situ hydrogen and then suppressed the other byproduct formation, leading to the higher selectivity of 1,2-propanediol. Evidently, 64.7% 1,2-propanediol selectivity (using the simulated crude glycerol) and 87.5% (using the real crude glycerol) were achieved over LaCu0.1Ni0.9O3 perovskite oxide as the catalyst. In addition, the real crude glycerol can be potentially utilized directly without purification as a raw material for the production of some biobased chemicals.

Methodology

Catalyst Preparation

The LaNiO3 parent perovskite was synthesized according to the previous reported method. 6.5 g of La­(NO3)3·6H2O and 4.35 g of Ni­(NO3)2·6H2O were dissolved in 30 mL of deionized water. The pH of the solution was adjusted to 13 by dropping 6 M KOH little by little into the mixture under continuous stirring. Then, the mixture was transferred into an autoclave and hydrothermally treated in an oven at 220 °C for 48 h. Later, the obtained catalyst was washed with deionized water and ethanol until its pH reached 7. Finally, the catalyst was dried in the oven at 65 °C overnight, followed by calcination at 800 °C in air with ramp rate 3 °C/min for 3 h.

The LaCu x Ni1–x O3 catalysts were synthesized by using the same procedure as above with some modification. The amount of La­(NO3)3·6H2O was fixed at 6.5 g, and then the amounts of Ni­(NO3)2·6H2O and Cu­(NO3)2·3H2O were adjusted according to the required ratio. For LaCu0.1Ni0.9O3, 3.92 g of Ni­(NO3)2·6H2O and 0.3625 g of Cu­(NO3)2·3H2O were used. For LaCu0.3Ni0.7O3, 3.045 g of Ni­(NO3)2·6H2O and 1.0875 g of Cu­(NO3)2·3H2O were introduced, whereas 2.175 g of Ni­(NO3)2·6H2O and 1.8125 g of Cu­(NO3)2·3H2O were employed to synthesize LaCu0.5Ni0.5O3.

The CuO/La2O3 and NiO/La2O3 samples were synthesized using an impregnation method. For CuO/La2O3, 0.2 g of Cu­(NO3)2·3H2O was dissolved in a minimum volume of deionized water, and the Cu precursor was then dropped slowly onto 2 g of La2O3, followed by evaporation on a hot plate for 80 °C under stirring and calcination in air at 300 °C with a 3 °C/min ramp rate. In the case of NiO/La2O3, 9.6 g of Ni­(NO3)2·6H2O was dissolved in a minimum volume of deionized water. Next, the Ni precursor was dropped slowly onto 6 g of La2O3, followed by evaporation on a hot plate for 80 °C under stirring and then calcination in air at 500 °C with a 3 °C/min ramp rate.

The physically mixed sample of CuO and LaNiO3 was prepared by grinding 0.9 g of LaNiO3 catalyst with 0.1 g of CuO using a mortar and pestle for uniform distribution.

Catalyst Characterization

The crystallinity and composition of the catalyst samples were determined by powder X-ray diffraction (Rigaku, SmartLab) in the range of 5–80° with a scan of 0.02° (2θ) steps/2.0 s. Before analysis, the sample was first hand-ground using a mortar and pestle into a fine powder. An X-ray fluorescence (XRF) spectrometer was employed to determine the elemental concentration of the samples. A surface area analyzer (Quantachrome, QS-evo MP) was employed to determine the specific surface areas, pore sizes, and total pore volumes of the catalysts. The morphology of the catalysts was observed by using a Hitachi S-4800 field emission scanning electron microscope (FE-SEM), and elemental mapping was conducted using an EDX detector. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) was carried using the Axis UltraDLD to determine the oxidation state of elements in the samples.

The acidity of the catalysts was determined using NH3-temperature-programmed desorption (NH3-TPD, BELCAT II). Prior to the experiment, a catalyst sample was first pretreated at 200 °C under He gas (flow rate: 30 mL/min) for 60 min. Then, the sample was cooled down to 60 °C and 5%NH3/He was then preadsorbed on the catalyst at 60 °C for 30 min (flow rate: 10 mL/min). After that, the physisorbed NH3 was flushed with He for 30 min at 100 °C (flow rate: 30 mL/min). Then, the sample was heated from 100 to 700 °C with ramp rate 10 °C/min. Likewise, the catalyst basicity was determined using the CO2-temperature-programmed desorption (CO2-TPD, BELCAT II). A catalyst sample was first pretreated at 200 °C under He gas (flow rate: 30 mL/min) for 60 min. Then, the sample was cooled down to 60 °C and 10%CO2/He was then preadsorbed on the catalyst at 50 °C for 30 min (flow rate: 10 mL/min). After that, the sample was flushed with He for 30 min at 100 °C (flow rate: 30 mL/min). Then, the sample was heated from 100 to 750 °C with a ramp rate of 10 °C/min.

X-ray absorption near edge structure (XANES) and extended X-ray absorption fine structure (EXAFS) were performed in the transmission mode for the La and Ni K-edges and fluorescence mode for the Cu K-edge at room temperature in order to investigate the change in the perovskite environment after the substitution of Cu into the Ni sites at Beamline 8 of the Synchrotron Light Research Institute (SLRI), Thailand. The XANES and EXAFS data were then processed using ATHENA and ARTEMIS software. The catalyst composition was determined using linear combination fitting (LCF), while the coordination number and type of neighboring atoms were identified using the ARTEMIS program.

Catalytic Activity Testing

The catalytic activity was carried out in a PARR reactor (model: 4576) equipped with a stirrer and automatic temperature controller. A 40 mL portion of glycerol feeds, that is, pure glycerol (99.5% glycerol, Sigma-Aldrich), simulated crude glycerol (70 wt % glycerol diluted with 30 wt % water), and an industrial crude glycerol composed of 62.3 wt % glycerol, 28.81% water, 6.43 wt % MONG, 2.421 wt % ash, and 1.24% methanol received from Global Green Chemicals Company Limited (Thailand) and 1 g of a catalyst were loaded into the reactor, and the air inside was removed by purging with N2 gas for 10 min. The experiments were conducted at 180 °C at the stirring rate of 300 rpm, under autopressure for 4 h without external addition of an alkaline or any gas. After stirring for 4 h, the reactor was then cooled down to room temperature. The catalyst was separated from the liquid product using a centrifuge, and later, the liquid product was diluted and analyzed using a 1D-Mode Pegasus LECO GC-TOF/MS.
