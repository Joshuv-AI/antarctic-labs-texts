Bacillus mycoides bacteremia after dog bite trauma in a patient on chronic suppressive antibiotics
Authors: Caroline Diggins, Carissa Tedeschi, Julia Jandro, Kristen Hysell
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBacillus mycoides (B. mycoides) is a Gram-positive bacillus belonging to the Bacillaceae family, found in soil and plant rhizospheres. Infection of humans by B. mycoides is exceedingly rare, previously reported only twice in the literature. Here, we present a third case of B. mycoides infection, specifically a bloodstream infection in a patient following a dog bite to the hand. The patient, who had a chronic Left Ventricular Assist Device (LVAD) in place and was on a chronic suppressive regimen of doxycycline and cefadroxil for a preexisting driveline infection, developed B. mycoides bacteremia despite ongoing antibiotic therapy. She was successfully treated with intravenous ampicillin-sulbactam plus vancomycin, followed by oral ciprofloxacin upon discharge. The patient’s recovery was uncomplicated and the final susceptibility testing confirmed pan-susceptibility to all tested agents. This case highlights the potential pathogenicity of B. mycoides in humans and raises questions on the risk factors for infection and optimal management of B. mycoides infections in the setting of relatively limited clinical data.Keywords: Infection, Bacteria, Bacteremia, Bacillus mycoides, Susceptibility

Introduction

Bacillus mycoides (B. mycoides) is a Gram-positive bacillus in the Bacillus cereus sensu lato species group [1], [2], [3]. B. mycoides is found commonly in soil and plant rhizospheres [1], [2], [3], [4]. Reports of B. mycoides as a human pathogen are uncommon. To date, only one case of B. mycoides bacteremia has been reported in the literature, to our knowledge [4]. Similarly, we found only one report of non-bacteremia B. mycoides infection in the literature, specifically causing endogenous endophthalmitis [5]. This stands in contrast to other members of the Bacillus cereus sensu lato species group such as human pathogens Bacillus anthracis (B. anthracis) and Bacillus cereus sensu stricto (B. cereus) [2], [4]. B. anthracis is known to cause skin, gastrointestinal, pulmonary, neurologic, and systemic infections that can manifest with a wide range of symptoms such as necrotic lesions, gastrointestinal bleeding, respiratory distress, meningitis, and sepsis, depending on the route of infection [6]. B. cereus is associated with foodborne illnesses (vomiting and diarrhea), as well as invasive infections including bacteremia [7].

Here, we describe a second case of B. mycoides bacteremia presumed to be secondary to trauma following a dog bite to the patient’s hand.

Case information

A 31-year-old patient with history of non-ischemic cardiomyopathy supported by a Heartmate III left ventricular assist device (LVAD) as a bridge to potential cardiac transplantation (not currently listed) presented to the Emergency Department (ED) one day after sustaining a dog bite to the dorsum of her left hand. Her past medical history was notable for chronic methicillin-sensitive Staphylococcus aureus LVAD driveline infection for which she was on suppressive doxycycline (100 mg twice daily) and cefadroxil (500 mg twice daily).

On initial presentation, the patient had a superficial wound over the dorsum of the left hand without erythema, drainage, or systemic symptoms. Two sets of peripheral blood cultures were obtained, but the patient left the ED prior to receiving antibiotics. Over the next few days, she developed worsening fatigue, palpitations, and subjective fevers. Her blood cultures returned positive with B. mycoides in three out of four bottles across two sets (Table 1), and she was notified to return to the hospital for further treatment. She returned to an affiliated hospital where two more sets of blood cultures were drawn (Table 1) and was subsequently transferred to tertiary care center for continued management given her LVAD.

On admission to tertiary care center the patient was tachycardic but afebrile. She reported persistent swelling and tenderness of the left hand, but denied fever. She was admitted to the hospital for further management. Labs at admission were notable for white blood cell count of 8000/uL (71 % neutrophils) and elevated liver enzymes: ALT 86 U/L, AST 111 U/L, and alkaline phosphatase 108 U/L (previously 61, 55, and 67, respectively, 11 days prior). Albumin was 4.9 g/dL (reference range 3.3–5.0 g/dL) and total protein was 8.2 g/dL (reference range 6.0–8.3 g/dL). Her basic metabolic panel was normal with a creatinine at baseline (0.78 mg/dL). A final two new sets of blood cultures were drawn (Table 1), and she was subsequently started on empiric intravenous vancomycin and ampicillin-sulbactam. Her suppressive doxycycline and cefadroxil were temporarily held. Other home medications she was taking at time of presentation included naltrexone, sacubitril-valsartan, spironolactone, and warfarin.

On Infectious Diseases consultation, the LVAD driveline site appeared clean with scant serous drainage, but no skin erythema, tenderness, or swelling. A wound culture was collected on day of admission from the LVAD driveline site, prior to antibiotic initiation, that ultimately grew methicillin-sensitive Staphylococcus aureus and Corynebacterium species (striatum amycolatum group), but not B. mycoides. The left hand exhibited a necrotic laceration with mild edema and faint erythema (Fig. 1). Radiograph of the hand showed soft tissue swelling with laceration, but no evidence of fracture. The patient was seen by the Plastics & Reconstructive Surgery team who debrided eschar associated with the wound and found no evidence of deeper tissue involvement or abscess. No tissue pathology or cultures were sent. A transthoracic echocardiogram showed no evidence of valvular vegetations.

Regarding potential exposures, the patient described breaking up a fight between her dog and another dog, during which time she sustained the bite. She reported that both dogs were up to date with vaccinations. She had no recent travel history, no water exposures, no recent insect bites, and no history of intravenous drug use. She did endorse prior gardening and working with horses, but not around the time of trauma or symptom onset. She denied any changes at the driveline site prior to admission and reported full adherence to suppressive antibiotics up through day of admission.

During her hospitalization, the patient experienced clinical improvement with resolution of hand swelling, erythema, and pain. She remained afebrile and normotensive to borderline hypertensive, and her white blood cell count remained within normal limits throughout her admission. Her repeat blood cultures were no growth (Table 1). She was discharged on hospital day four. A time of discharge, susceptibility testing for B. mycoides was still pending, and she was ultimately narrowed to oral ciprofloxacin (500 mg by mouth twice daily) to complete a 14-day course. Suppressive cefadroxil and doxycycline were resumed.

At one-month outpatient follow up, the patient had completed the full course of ciprofloxacin and symptoms had fully resolved. Final susceptibility testing from her initial set of blood cultures confirmed appropriate coverage (Table 2).

Discussion

We report what is, to our knowledge, the second documented case of B. mycoides bacteremia. The previously reported case involved a 65-year-old patient with cutaneous lupus erythematosus and Waldenström macroglobulinemia (not receiving systemic immunosuppression) who was hospitalized following cardiac arrest. Blood cultures obtained at presentation grew B. mycoides, and the infection was presumed to have originated from cutaneous environmental exposure and subsequent invasive infection when the patient had intra-osseous, arterial, and venous access placed. Notably the patient was found down on a barge that had carried agricultural products [4]. The patient was treated with vancomycin. In a PubMed search of key terms “infection” and “Bacillus mycoides” we found only one other instance of B. mycoides infection, specifically endogenous endophthalmitis in a patient who had recently experienced a spontaneous abortion and blood transfusion. Culture of the vitreous showed B. mycoides sensitive to amikacin, cefazolin, ofloxacin, gentamicin, vancomycin, gatifloxacin, moxifloxacin, ciprofloxacin, and chloramphenicol, with resistance only to cefuroxime. The patient received vancomycin and ceftazidime via intravitreal injections and ciprofloxacin by mouth [5].

Species within the Bacillus genus are recognized as potential pathogens, including in vulnerable or device-dependent populations. They have been found to cause bacteremia [8], [9], with a 1987 analysis of Cleveland hospitals finding 38 instances of serious, non-gastrointestinal Bacillus infections, nearly 80 % of which involved bloodstream infection [8]. Speciation was assessed for 15 of the 38 Bacillus isolates, the majority of which were identified as B. cereus. Fourteen patients with Bacillus species bloodstream infection had an intravascular device, and 4 of these patients were treated by having their intravascular device removed without supplemental antibiotics [8]. The patient in the present report had an LVAD in place, but this was not thought to be the source of her infection given the temporal proximity of the dog bite to her positive blood cultures and LVAD exit site wound culture with no B. mycoides growth.

Taxonomically, Bacillus weihenstephanensis, proposed as a distinct species by Lechner et al. in 1998 [10], has since been reclassified by some authors as part of B. mycoides [1]. Given this, we performed a literature review for B. weihenstephanensis infections in humans, using terms “infection” and “Bacillus weihenstephanensis” in PubMed, that yielded no results [1]. This further underscores the rarity of clinical disease attributable to this lineage.

While precise environmental source of B. mycoides in this case is unclear, its presence in soil and plant rhizospheres [1], [3], [4] makes contamination of the dog bite wound highly plausible. However, because no cultures, pathology, or microbiological samples were collected from the hand wound, it is difficult to definitively implicate the wound as the cause of bacteremia. Fortunately, B. mycoides did not cause severe systemic manifestations in this case, as the patient remained hemodynamically stable and afebrile. This observation is supported by the findings of Celandroni et al. that none of the virulence genes tested in their analysis, such as cytotoxin K, hemolysins, and proteases, were present in B. mycoides, and B. mycoides was not able to swim or swarm, properties associated with bacterial virulence, in contrast to the majority of B. cereus strains [11]. B. mycoides strains have, however, been found to have plasmids that could be implicated in pathogenicity and antibiotic resistance, and to have a relatively high level of within-species genetic variation more broadly, which has been suggested to allow for environmental adaptability [1].

Regarding treatment, the patient’s initial therapy included vancomycin and ampicillin-sulbactam, followed by outpatient ciprofloxacin based on its high oral bioavailability [12], [13]. Published data have shown B. mycoides to be susceptible to a broad range of antibiotics including ciprofloxacin, levofloxacin, moxifloxacin, tetracycline, tigecycline, vancomycin, chloramphenicol, erythromycin, gatifloxacin, gentamicin, and rifampin [11], [14]. The final susceptibility testing in our case confirmed B. mycoides to be pan-susceptible to all agents tested.

A notable aspect of this case was the development of B. mycoides bacteremia despite the patient’s ongoing suppressive antibiotic regimen of cefadroxil and doxycycline. Beta-lactam resistance in Bacillus species is variable. A 2007 analysis found that four of the five B. mycoides isolates tested were resistant to ampicillin, penicillin, and oxacillin [14]. Although beta-lactam susceptibility was not tested in this case, the isolate was found to be sensitive to tetracycline, suggesting that factors other than outright resistance may have contributed to breakthrough infection.

One possible explanation for treatment failure is the known effect of food or supplements on bioavailability of tetracycline, which can decrease circulating antibiotic concentrations [15], [16], [17]. While the patient did not explicitly report taking supplements that could interfere with antibiotic absorption, such interactions remain an important consideration in the context of chronic suppressive therapy.

We also note that there have been reports of heterogeneity in resistance within the tetracycline class of antibiotics [18], including against Bacillus species [19]. It is possible that B. mycoides in this case was less susceptible to doxycycline despite tested tetracycline susceptibility, though this pattern would be counter to that observed in other Bacillus species [19].

Of note, although the first blood cultures drawn were positive for B. mycoides, the following two sets, drawn upon re-presentation, but before initiating antibiotics, yielded no growth. This raises the possibility of transient bacteremia or the failure of the second and third sets of cultures to detect low bacterial loads. This could suggest the patient’s suppressive antibiotics may have had some therapeutic benefit, and also highlights the complexities of diagnosing Bacillus infections, particularly in patients on suppressive antibiotics.

Conclusion

B. mycoides is a rarely reported human pathogen, and this case represents, to our knowledge, only the second known instance of bacteremia due to this organism. This infection likely resulted from environmental contamination of a traumatic dog bite wound, rather than from the patient’s indwelling LVAD. The clinical course was mild and responsive to treatment, and the isolate was pan-susceptible to antibiotics, including ciprofloxacin. Given the organism’s environmental presence, low virulence profile, and recognition as a species closely related to pathogens in device-dependent hosts, clinicians should maintain a high index of suspicion for true infection when Bacillus species are isolated from blood cultures in appropriate clinical contexts.
