Lower estimated glomerular filtration rate relates to cognitive impairment and brain alterations
Authors: Shady Rahayel, Rémi Goupil, Dominique Suzanne Genest, Florence Lamarche, Mohsen Agharazii, Violette Ayral, Christina Tremblay, François Madore
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractINTRODUCTIONChronic kidney disease (CKD) is associated with cognitive decline and changes in brain structure. However, their associations remain unclear, particularly the selective vulnerability characteristics that make some brain regions more vulnerable.METHODSWe investigated the baseline association between estimated glomerular filtration rates (eGFR) and cognitive function in 15,897 individuals from the CARTaGENE cohort. We performed vertex‐based magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) analyses between eGFR and longitudinal cortical thickness in the 1397 participants who underwent brain MRI after 6 years. Imaging transcriptomics was used to characterize the gene expression and neurodegenerative features associated with this association.RESULTSLower eGFR correlated with reduced cognitive performance and brain structure. Brain regions associated with eGFR were enriched for mitochondrial and inflammatory‐related genes. These associations occurred independently from age, sex, education, and body mass index (BMI), Framingham risk score, and white matter lesion volume.DISCUSSIONThis study highlights the link between reduced eGFR, cognitive impairment, and brain structure, revealing some of the kidney–brain axis mechanisms.Highlights Lower eGFR is associated with reduced cognitive abilities.Structural brain changes are mediated by eGFR levels.Specific gene expression patterns correlate with lower eGFR and brain changes.Mitochondrial and inflammation‐related genes were enriched in these patterns. Keywords: atrophy, brain, chronic kidney disease, cognition, glomerular filtration rate, MRI

INTRODUCTION

Chronic kidney disease (CKD) is characterized by the gradual loss of kidney function, as indicated by lower estimated glomerular filtration rates (eGFR) and/or albuminuria. 1 It affects 10% of the global population 2 and is increasingly recognized as a major risk factor for cognitive decline and neurodegenerative conditions such as Alzheimer's and Parkinson's diseases. 3 , 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 This suggests that CKD has systemic effects that may affect the brain, referred to as the kidney–brain axis. However, the mechanisms associating CKD with cognition and neurodegeneration remain unclear.

Previous studies demonstrated that lower eGFR is associated with changes in brain structure, 9 manifesting as alterations in white matter tissue, connectivity, and brain atrophy. While the relationship between lower eGFR and decreased white matter integrity has been well‐documented, 10 , 11 the relationship with brain atrophy is less clear, 12 , 13 , 14 , 15 especially regarding why certain brain regions are more susceptible to the effects of lower eGFR. Understanding the local features of brain regions more susceptible to develop atrophy is important, as brain atrophy is a hallmark of neurodegenerative diseases affecting cognition. 16 In degenerative neurocognitive disorders, brain atrophy is not random and has been shown to follow specific constraints dictated by the brain's regional cellular characteristics, notably through gene expression, and connectivity architecture. 17 , 18 Understanding the features that render some brain regions more vulnerable to atrophy in association with lower eGFR may identify pathways to target for preventing CKD‐related neurodegeneration and imaging markers to identify patients at risk of cognitive decline. In particular, understanding how various levels of eGFR relates to brain neurodegeneration in still healthy individuals may provide key knowledge in individual risk prediction.

In this study, we investigated the relationship between lower eGFR and cognition in a population‐based cohort of 15,897 individuals with still relatively healthy levels of eGFR as well as the longitudinal association between eGFR and structural brain changes in a subset of 1397 individuals who underwent brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) after 6 years. We examined the gene expression features of the brain regions associated with lower eGFR levels and assessed the spatial alignment between the eGFR‐atrophy brain pattern and the atrophy patterns associated with other neurodegenerative conditions. We hypothesized that reduced eGFR would be associated with lower cognitive performance and brain atrophy and that the spatial pattern of atrophy would be associated with distinct gene expression and neurodegenerative features.

METHODS

CARTaGENE cohort

The CARTaGENE cohort is a population‐based study of men and women aged 40–69 years, selected from Quebec's urban population between 2009 and 2010 to investigate chronic disease determinants. 19 To recruit a representative population sample, participants were selected from the four main metropolitan areas, randomly chosen based on provincial health insurance registries, and contacted through a call center to get enrolled in the study and schedule an interview date at one of the clinical assessment sites. Of the 146,407 participants randomly picked, 65,595 (44.8%) were unreachable and 2776 (1.9%) were considered ineligible. The ineligible participants included 2372 individuals who were considered physically or mentally inapt to participate (1.6% of the initial study population, 85.4% of the ineligible participants), therefore excluding participants with cognitive impairment sufficient to interfere with the study protocol. Of the 20,007 participants who signed consent form, a total of 19,996 participants underwent at least one physical measure (see below) and were considered as the initial cohort for the present study (Figure 1 for a flowchart). At baseline, participants underwent sociodemographic and lifestyle questionnaires, anthropometric measurements, and blood sample collection. After 6 years, some participants were invited to undergo imaging studies. The current study focused on participants who had complete clinical data from the baseline and underwent a brain MRI scan at follow‐up.

Clinical variables

At baseline, standardized questionnaires collected demographic and medical information across eight domains: socio‐demographic factors, lifestyle, mental state, psychosocial environment, personal and family history of disease, healthcare use, medication use, and reproductive health. It was based on 14 questionnaires, including the Patient Health Questionnaire, the General Anxiety Scale, the Job Content Questionnaire, and the International Physical Activity Questionnaire. Declared health conditions required physician diagnosis. Details regarding the standardized source questionnaires used have been described in detail elsewhere. 19

Physical measurements were used to calculate the body mass index (BMI), and blood samples were collected for biochemical analysis, including glucose, hemoglobin A1c, lipid panel, and eGFR, the latter computed using the CKD Epidemiology Collaboration formula. 20 The diagnosis of diabetes was based on a glycosylated hemoglobin A1c level of ≥ 6.5%, a fasting glucose level of ≥ 7.0 mmol/L, or the use of hypoglycemic medications. The cardiovascular disease risk was assessed using the composite Framingham risk score, which estimates the 10‐year risk of cardiovascular disease outcomes by incorporating age, sex, diabetes status, total cholesterol, high‐density lipoprotein cholesterol, smoking status, systolic blood pressure, and BMI. 21

Cognitive assessment

At baseline, participants underwent a cognitive assessment composed of three distinct tasks administered through a computerized touch‐screen interface, namely a two‐choice reaction time task, paired associates learning task, and a verbal and numeric reasoning task. In the reaction time task, participants were required to quickly press a button with their dominant hand when a symbol on the screen matched a previously displayed symbol. This task consisted of 60 presentations, divided into four response‐stimulus‐interval groups. Performance was measured by calculating the average time in milliseconds needed to accurately identify matching symbols. Reaction times below 50 ms were excluded as anticipatory responses, and times exceeding 2000 ms were excluded since stimuli disappeared after this interval. In the paired associates learning task, designed for assessing working memory, participants had to remember and recall the positions of seven targets sequentially presented on the screen. Upon their disappearance, participants indicated their positions. The performance metric was the number of guesses required to correctly identify all seven locations, with scores ranging from 7 (best) to 30 (worst). To control for outliers, performances requiring more than 30 guesses were assigned a score of 30. In the reasoning task, designed to assess executive functioning and fluid intelligence, participants attempted to solve a series of 12 verbal and numeric reasoning problems within a 2‐min time frame. The performance here was quantified by the number of problems correctly solved, with scores ranging from 0 (worst) to 12 (best).

MRI acquisition and processing

After 6 years, a subset of 1397 participants underwent brain MRI. FreeSurfer 7.2.0 was used to process the T1‐weighted brain MRI scans to generate whole‐brain vertex‐based maps of cortical thickness. 22 , 23 The processing is briefly described in Supplementary Material 1. Cortical thickness was measured at each vertex to investigate the associations between eGFR and brain morphology across the cortical sheet. The processing also quantified areas of white matter hypointensities using spatial intensity gradients across tissue classes, 24 , 25 an approach that correlates with volumes derived from fluid‐attenuated inversion recovery images 26 and used to account for white matter lesion volume.

Imaging transcriptomics

To investigate the genetic features of the regions where lower eGFR related to cortical thickness, the cortical maps were parcellated using the Desikan‐Killiany atlas to quantify the average thickness across brain regions. 24 The abagen 27 tool was next used to extract the brain maps of gene expression of over 20,000 genes, which were quantified across 3702 tissue samples obtained from six healthy post‐mortem brains. 28 The processing steps of gene extraction are described in Supplementary Material 2. Partial least squares regression was used to identify the gene expression components associated with the regional brain patterns representing the association between eGFR and cortical thickness. 29 This method decomposes two matrices, X and Y, to derive components from X (i.e., the 15,633 genes across the 68 brain regions) that explain the maximal covariance with Y (i.e., the vector of 68 regional t‐values of the association between eGFR and morphology). 29 These t‐values were derived from linear regression models that quantified, in every brain region, the influence of eGFR (regressor of interest) on cortical thickness (outcome) while accounting for age, sex, BMI, Framingham risk score, and white matter lesion volume (regressors of no interest). This resulted in a vector where each brain region represented the association between eGFR and cortical thickness after removal of unwanted variance. The components’ significance was tested against the variance explained by 10,000 null models that preserved spatial autocorrelation between brain regions. 30 A bootstrapping resampling technique with 5000 repetitions was next applied to identify the genes contributing most to each component.

Functional enrichment analyses were done on the ranked gene lists to reveal the biological processes and cellular components enriched in the eGFR‐thickness gene expression components using WebGestalt and the Gene Ontology knowledge base. 31 , 32 The analysis included 1000 permutations, and from this, we interpreted the top 10 most significant biological processes and cellular components. Only terms that survived the false discovery rate‐corrected statistical threshold of p < 0.05 were considered significant. Analyses were repeated using BMI, Framingham risk score, or white matter lesion volume instead of eGFR to ascertain that results were specific to eGFR.

Spatial mapping analyses

We performed spatial mapping analyses to investigate whether the pattern of association between eGFR and cortical thickness aligned with neurodegeneration. 33 Using previously published FreeSurfer‐processed maps of cortical thickness changes from Alzheimer's disease and isolated rapid eye movement (REM) sleep behavior disorder, 34 a sleep disorder where over 90% of patients develop a synucleinopathy such as Parkinson's disease, 35 , 36 we assessed this alignment. The Alzheimer's disease map was derived from 78 patients from the Alzheimer's Disease Neuroimaging Initiative, 37 , 38 while the REM sleep behavior disorder map was derived from 138 patients diagnosed using polysomnography. 39 Spearman's correlations were calculated between the neurodegenerative brain maps and the t‐value map of the eGFR‐cortical thickness association, controlling for age, sex, BMI, Framingham risk score, and white matter lesion volume.

Statistical analysis

Demographic and clinical variables

Statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics (version 29.0). Baseline characteristics of the MRI cohort and eGFR groups are presented as means and standard deviations for continuous variables and as proportions and percentages for categorical variables. Between‐group t‐tests and chi‐square tests were used to assess differences for continuous and categorical variables.

Association between eGFR and cognitive performance

Using baseline data from the CARTaGENE cohort, we conducted multivariate linear regressions to examine the associations between eGFR and cognitive performance. Reaction time, working memory, and reasoning task performances were divided into quartiles, and regression models were used to identify significant differences in eGFR across these quartiles. Participants were also stratified by baseline eGFR levels, and multivariate regression models assessed whether cognitive performance on the three tasks differed significantly across eGFR levels. All models were adjusted for age, sex, income, education, smoking, alcohol intake, hypertension, dyslipidemia, BMI, diabetes, cardiovascular disease history (including stroke), and psychoactive medication use.

Cortical thickness analyses

Regression analyses of eGFR on cortical thickness at each vertex of the cortical surface were performed using general linear models, while controlling for age, sex, BMI, Framingham risk score, and white matter lesion volume. To examine sex effects, we conducted vertex‐based analyses to compare slopes between men and women, controlling for the same covariates except sex.

To assess the mediating effect of eGFR on the relationship between cognitive performance and cortical morphology, we analyzed vertex‐wise patterns linking cortical morphology with reaction time, working memory, and reasoning tasks, adjusting for the same covariates. We then examined whether regression slopes between cognitive performance and brain structure varied across eGFR categories: normal (≥ 90 mL/min/1.73 m2), mildly decreased (60–89 mL/min/1.73 m2), and moderately decreased (30–59 mL/min/1.73 m2). All vertex‐wise analyses were performed separately for each hemisphere, with significant clusters determined at p < 0.05 using a Monte Carlo simulation approach.

Consent statement

All subjects provided informed consent. Procedures were conducted in accordance with the ethical guidelines set forth in the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by institutional ethics committees of the Sainte‐Justine University Health Centre and Hôpital du Sacré‐Cœur de Montréal.

RESULTS

Demographics

A total of 20,007 participants were recruited for the CARTaGENE baseline cohort, with 19,996 undergoing at least one physical measure (Figure 1). After excluding 4099 participants (20.5%) due to missing eGFR values, cognitive testing data, or use of cholinesterase inhibitors, 15,897 participants remained for analysis of the eGFR‐cognitive performance relationship. A subset of 1397 participants underwent T1‐weighted brain MRI 6 years after baseline. Of these, 61 (4.4%) were excluded due to missing clinical information or quality control, resulting in a sample of 1336 participants.

Participants who underwent imaging were 53.9 ± 7.5 years, with 49% being women (Table 1). The time interval between baseline and MRI was 6.2 ± 0.9 years. Of the imaging participants, 634 (47.5%) had normal eGFR (G1), 659 (49.3%) had mildly decreased eGFR (G2), and 43 (3.2%) had moderately decreased eGFR (G3). The G3 group was older and had higher BMI, Framingham risk scores, and white matter lesion volumes compared to the G2 group, which also differed from the G1 group on these variables.

Baseline eGFR is associated with lower cognitive performance

Among the 15,897 participants who underwent cognitive testing, a gradual decline in reaction time, working memory, and reasoning was observed with decreasing eGFR levels (p < 0.001 for each task), after adjusting for age, sex, income, education, smoking, alcohol use, hypertension, dyslipidemia, BMI, diabetes, cardiovascular history, and psychoactive medication usage (Table 2). Within each eGFR level, lower cognitive performance was consistently associated with a decline in eGFR (reaction time: β eGFR = –6.6 (95% CI: –10.4 to –2.8), p = 0.001; working memory: β eGFR = –0.22 (95% CI: –0.4 to –0.027), p = 0.026; reasoning: β eGFR = –0.086 (95% CI: –0.035 to ‐0.014), p = 0.001).

Baseline eGFR is associated with changes in brain morphology

In the 1397 participants who underwent brain MRI, we found that lower eGFR was associated with cortical thinning in frontal and posterior brain regions and thickening in temporal and cingulate areas (Figure 2A). The relationship between lower eGFR and cortical thinning was significant in the left frontoparietal cortex, precuneus and cuneus, and in the right dorsolateral prefrontal cortex, precuneus, and inferior parietal cortex (Table S1 and Figure 2A). There was no significant sex interaction. Secondary vertex‐wise analyses performed to assess the relationship between eGFR and cortical thickness inside each eGFR level separately also revealed the presence of significant patterns in each category (Table S2 and Figure S1).

The eGFR mediates the relationship between cognition and morphology

We investigated the neural underpinnings of cognitive performance and whether eGFR levels mediated the associations between brain morphology and cognition. Lower reasoning performance was associated with cortical thinning in the sensorimotor, parietal, and posterior temporal cortices (Table S1 and Figure 2B). Longer reaction times were associated with reduced thickness in the bilateral prefrontal, anterior temporal, parietal, and occipital cortices (Table S1 and Figure 2B). No associations were found between working memory and cortical thickness.

We found that eGFR levels significantly mediated the relationship between reasoning performance and cortical thickness in the posterior superior temporal cortex (Table S1 and Figure 2C). This effect was observed when comparing participants with normal eGFR to those with moderately decreased eGFR (Table S1 and Figure 2C). While no association existed between reasoning performance and thickness for individuals with normal eGFR (r = –0.01, p = 0.41), a significant association emerged in those with mildly decreased eGFR (r = 0.11, p = 0.006), and it was stronger in those with moderately decreased eGFR (r = 0.46, p = 0.008) (Figure 2C). No mediating effects of eGFR were found for reaction time, suggesting that eGFR specifically mediates the relationship between reasoning performance and brain morphology.

Regional gene expression underlies eGFR‐thickness associations

Lower eGFR correlated with thinning in the left medial orbitofrontal cortex (r = 0.08, p FDR = 0.0015) and thickening in the right rostral anterior cingulate cortex (r = –0.11, p FDR = 0.0007) (Table S3 and Figure 3A). Of the five gene expression components tested in our imaging transcriptomics pipeline, two components were significant, explaining respectively 36.8% (p = 0.006) and 23.2% (p = 0.048) of the variance between eGFR and thickness (Figure 3B).

In the first component, lower eGFR correlated with thinning in dorsolateral prefrontal and parietal cortices, which were positively weighted, and with thickening in temporal and anterior cingulate cortices, which were negatively weighted (r = 0.61, p < 0.001, Figure 3C, Figure 3D). Thinning regions showed higher mitochondrial gene expression, overexpressing genes involved in mitochondrial RNA processes and potassium ion transport (Table S4 and Figure 4A). Thickening regions were enriched for genes related to protein complex remodeling (Table S4 and Figure 4A), including apolipoprotein E and angiotensinogen. Mitochondrial and membrane‐related genes were enriched in thinning regions, while glial cell projections and major histocompatibility complex (MHC) protein‐related genes were enriched in thickening regions (Table S5). The second component showed a similar pattern (r = 0.48, p < 0.001, Figure 4B and 4C) but without enriched biological processes (Table S6). The associations map over regions associated with neurodegeneration

Using atrophy patterns of Alzheimer's disease (66.0 ± 4.9 years, 51% women) and isolated REM sleep behavior disorder (67.0 ± 6.3, 19% women), 34 an early pathognomonic feature of dementia with Lewy bodies and Parkinson's disease, 40 there was no spatial correlation between the brain map of regions where lower eGFR related to increased thickness and the atrophic regions in Alzheimer's disease, although a trend could be seen (r = 0.23, p = 0.056). In contrast, there was a significant correlation between the brain map of regions where lower eGFR related to decreased thickness and atrophic regions in isolated REM sleep behavior disorder (r = ‐0.48, p < 0.001).

DISCUSSION

In this study, lower eGFR was associated with poorer cognitive performance in reasoning, working memory, and reaction time, which correlated with structural brain changes. Notably, eGFR mediated the relationship between reasoning and brain morphology. Imaging transcriptomic analysis revealed that lower eGFR was associated with transcriptomic patterns resembling those seen in tauopathies and synucleinopathies.

CKD has been associated with cognitive decline, neurodegenerative risk, and brain structure changes. 3 , 4 , 9 In this large population‐based cohort, lower eGFR was specifically associated with reduced reasoning and memory performance and slower reaction times, reflecting the impact of CKD on attention and executive functions. 4 Importantly, the mediation effect of eGFR on brain structure was observed only in individuals with reduced eGFR levels, suggesting early associations between kidney function, cognition, and brain structure.

Regions where lower eGFR correlated with increased cortical thickness were enriched with genes involved in protein remodeling, particularly the apolipoprotein E (APOE) gene, a key player in Alzheimer's disease. 41 Importantly, APOE status was not measured in participants but was derived from transcriptomic expression inside each brain region. The AGT gene, linked to angiotensin regulation, 42 and MHC genes 43 were also enriched in these areas, suggesting that increased cortical thickness may indicate inflammation in regions affected by tauopathies. Conversely, regions where lower eGFR correlated with cortical thinning showed an enrichment of mitochondrial gene expression, overlapping with atrophy patterns seen in prodromal synucleinopathies, such as isolated REM sleep behavior disorder, a precursor to Parkinson's disease and dementia with Lewy bodies. 40 , 44 Atrophy in this disorder is increased in regions overexpressing genes involved in oxidative phosphorylation, 34 suggesting that mitochondrial involvement is a shared factor in both synucleinopathies and the kidney–brain axis. Lower eGFR has been related to a higher risk of prodromal Parkinson's disease and parkinsonism, 5 , 7 and this may be driven by increased inflammation and oxidative stress. 8 , 45

This study has some limitations. First, brain atrophy was assessed using T1‐weighted imaging, which limits the understanding of white matter tracts. Future research should include diffusion MRI and advanced tractography to explore the relationships between eGFR, cognitive function, and white matter changes. Second, eGFR was calculated based solely on creatinine levels, but previous studies indicate that using other measures like cystatin C or combined markers does not significantly change the results. 12 Third, we adjusted for cardiovascular risk factors using the composite Framingham risk score to avoid losing statistical power by introducing categorical variables for individual risk factors. Fourth, most participants had eGFR levels within a narrow range and very mild CKD, which may limit the generalizability of the results to those with more severe kidney disease. However, even within this narrow range, we found significant association between eGFR, cognitive performance, and brain imaging. Finally, cognitive testing was also done at the baseline evaluation, limiting our understanding of how the decline in cognition over time relates to the baseline eGFR levels. Future studies with longitudinal cognitive assessments should assess the relationship between decline on specific cognitive domains and eGFR.

In conclusion, this study demonstrates the relationship between eGFR, cognitive performance, and brain structure in healthy individuals from a large population‐based cohort. It supports the idea that the effect of eGFR on brain structure is not random and targets regions with distinct gene expression patterns.
