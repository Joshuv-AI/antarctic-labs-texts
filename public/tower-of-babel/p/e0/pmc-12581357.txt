DNMT3B blocks TAL1-mediated PKM2 transcriptional repression to promote non-small cell lung cancer progression through inducing glycolysis
Authors: Yucheng Guo, Bin Xu, Weimin Mao, Zhao Zhao, Zhao Li, Zhengzhou Qiu, Changying Guo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundNon-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) is a major subtype of lung cancer, with high mortality and limited treatment approaches. This paper explores the function of TAL1 in NSCLC progression and glycolysis and its mechanism.MethodsBioinformatics analysis screened out TAL1 and the upstream and downstream molecules. MTT, EdU, wound healing assay, Transwell assay, and TUNEL were utilized to detect the malignant phenotype of A549 and H460 cells. Western blot analysis was conducted to detect the expression of the proliferation-associated protein (Ki67), EMT-associated proteins (E-cadherin, N-cadherin), and glycolysis-associated proteins (GLUT1, LDHA, and PDK1). Cellular metabolism assays detected changes in glucose metabolites. A xenograft model was constructed, and the mouse tumor weight and volumes were measured periodically. Dual-luciferase assays and ChIP assays were performed to authenticate the transcriptional regulation of TAL1 on PKM2 and the relationship between DNMT3B and TAL1.ResultsTAL1 was lowly expressed in NSCLC, and TAL1 overexpression prevented the proliferation, migration, and invasion and elevated apoptosis. TAL1 inhibited PKM2 transcription, and overexpression of PKM2 reversed the trend of overexpression of TAL1 and promoted glycolysis. DNMT3B inhibited TAL1 expression through methylation modification. DNMT3B overexpression facilitated NSCLC cell growth and promoted glycolysis, and further overexpression of TAL1 reversed this trend. In vivo experiments showed that overexpression of TAL1 inhibited NSCLC progression, while combined overexpression of PKM2 promoted NSCLC progression. Overexpression of DNMT3B promoted NSCLC progression, and combined knockdown of PKM2 inhibited NSCLC progression.ConclusionDNMT3B activates glycolysis and promotes NSCLC progression by mediating methylation modification of TAL1 and inducing PKM2 transcription.Keywords: Non-small cell lung cancer, TAL1, PKM2, DNMT3B, Glycolytic pathway

Background

Lung cancer is among the most frequent cancers and the predominant cause of cancer-related deaths globally, with a projected 2.2 million new diagnoses and 1.79 million deceases annually [1]. Among non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) (85% of total diagnoses of lung cancer), adenocarcinomas (LUAD) are the most common subtype, with a low 5-year survival rate [2]. Surgical resection is the standard option for early-stage NSCLC, while chemotherapy is extensively applied for advanced NSCLC patients; despite the success of targeted therapies, resistance unavoidably occurs [3]. Therefore, identifying appropriate targets is promising for improving NSCLC treatment and patient outcomes.

T-cell acute lymphocytic leukemia 1 (TAL1, also known as SCL) is a pivotal transcription factor in hematopoiesis [4]. TAL1 is involved in a vascular anti-oncogenic axis and positively correlates with LUAD patient survival [5]. However, its specific role and downstream targets in NSCLC progression remain to be further explored. Glucose metabolism is a known hallmark of NSCLC, and cancer cells reprogram glucose metabolism reactions into glycolysis [6]. Pyruvate kinase M2 (PKM2), a major enzyme of glycolysis, is critical in tumor metabolism and is overexpressed in various cancers, with significant associations with tumor cell proliferation and metastasis and poor survival [7]. Xenograft tumors with Shikonin (a PKM2-specific inhibitor) treatment exhibited markedly diminished Ki-67, N-cadherin, and p-STAT3 levels, and increased E-cadherin levels, indicating inhibited epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) in hepatocellular carcinoma [8]. High PKM2 expression in tumor tissues is associated with poor survival in lung squamous cell carcinoma (LUSC) patients and positively correlated with immune cell markers and tumor purity [9]. Mechanistically, overexpression of c-Myc enhanced alternative splicing of PKM to PKM2, contributing to the metabolic reprogramming of NSCLC cells [10]. Our preliminary bioinformatics prediction identified PKM2 as a target of TAL1 in NSCLC cells, which piqued our attention that TAL1 might regulate glycolysis in NSCLC by manipulating the transcription of PKM2.

TAL1 has over 90% hypermethylation frequencies in LUAD samples and is downregulated in LUAD tissues [11], indicating its dysregulation in NSCLC might be related to DNA methylation. DNA methyltransferases (DNMT1, DNMT3A, and DNMT3B) catalyze DNA methylation to cause alterations in gene expression and regulate biological functions and pathological characteristics during lung cancer [12]. DNMT3B is elevated in nasopharyngeal carcinoma tissues and predicts poor outcomes, while DNMT3B knockdown curbs migration and invasion by preventing EMT [13]. More relevantly, lung cancer patients with high DNMT3B expression have poor prognoses [14]. DNMT3B is positively correlated with glucose uptake in papillary thyroid cancer [15]. Hence, we speculated that DNMT3B also regulated glycolysis in NSCLC through the TAL1/PKM2 axis. Therefore, this paper aims to illustrate the function of TAL1 in NSCLC progression and glycolytic metabolism and its potential mechanism.

Results

TAL1 is lowly expressed in NSCLC

NSCLC samples and control samples (Fig. 1A) from the GEO database were selected from the GSE18842, GSE19188, and GSE159857 datasets, and differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were screened with adjusted p < 0.05 (by Benjamini & Hochberg) and |Log2 FoldChange| ≥ 1 as the thresholds. The human transcriptional factor (TF) list was downloaded from HumanTFDB (http://bioinfo.life.hust.edu.cn/HumanTFDB). We identified 14 upregulated TFs and 37 downregulated TFs by integrating the three datasets using Jvenn (https://jvenn.toulouse.inrae.fr/app/example.html) (Fig. 1B). The interactions among the 51 TFs were explored through the String database (https://cn.string-db.org/) and imported into Cytoscape software to select the top three scoring genes as hub genes through the BottleNeck algorithm, which were FOS [16], TAL1, and FOXM1 [17] (Fig. 1C). The specific effect of TAL1 on NSCLC has not been studied. Meanwhile, TAL1 was under-expressed in the NSCLC datasets of GSE18842 (Log2 FoldChange = -1.981), GSE19188 (Log2 FoldChange = -1.941), and GSE159857 (Log2 FoldChange = -1.361). TAL1 was lowly expressed in both LUAD and LUSC in the UALCAN database (https://ualcan.path.uab.edu/index.html) (Fig. 1D).

To validate TAL1 expression in NSCLC, we selected various NSCLC cell lines (H1299, HCC827, A549, H460, and PC-9), and BEAS-2B cells were used as a control. RT-qPCR (Fig. 1E) and WB (Fig. 1F) found that TAL1 was lowly expressed in NSCLC cells compared to BEAS-2B cells.

Upregulation of TAL1 inhibits NSCLC progression

A549 and H460 cells were selected for further analysis. RT-qPCR (Fig. 2A) and WB (Fig. 2B) found that OE-TAL1 infection resulted in an increase in TAL1 expression in A549 and H460 cells. MTT assay (Fig. 2C) showed that TAL1 overexpression decreased the proliferative capacity. EdU assay (Fig. 2D) further showed that A549 and H460 cells in the DNA replication phase were greatly reduced by overexpression of TAL1. The Ki-67 index has been identified as an effective marker of proliferation in malignant tumors [18]. E-cadherin, an epithelial cell-cell adhesion molecule, is a hallmark of EMT, while N-cadherin, a mesenchymal adhesion molecule, is associated with enhanced motility and invasion during EMT [19]. WB (Fig. 2E) revealed that overexpression of TAL1 reduced the protein expression of Ki67 and N-cadherin, and restored E-cadherin. In the wound healing assay (Fig. 2F) and Transwell assay (Fig. 2G), overexpression of TAL1 substantially decreased the wound healing rate and invasive ability. TUNEL assay (Fig. 2H) unveiled that overexpression of TAL1 enhanced the apoptosis rate.

TAL1 inhibits PKM2 transcription

We took the intersections of DEGs obtained from the GSE18842, GSE19188, and GSE159857 datasets, and 769 intersecting genes were obtained (Fig. 3A). KEGG enrichment analysis was done on the KOBAS website (https://cn.string-db.org/) and ranked according to the p-value, and the pathway with the smallest p-value was KEGG PATHWAY hsa01100: Metabolic pathways (Fig. 3B). Hence, we selected 81 genes on this pathway for the subsequent analysis. To more accurately get the central genes in the pathway, the interactions of these targets were analyzed through the STRING database (Fig. 3C). PKM2 was found to be the only intersecting gene among the top three clusters through the BottleNeck algorithm, the EcCentricity algorithm, and the Betweenness algorithm in Cytoscape (Fig. 3D). The correlation between PKM2 and TAL1 was explored in the LUAD and LUSC datasets of the GEPIA database (http://gepia.cancer-pku.cn/), and TAL1 was negatively correlated with PKM2 by Spearman analysis (Fig. 3E). PKM2 was highly expressed in the GSE18842 (Log2 FoldChange = 1.284), GSE19188 (Log2 FoldChange = 1.011), and GSE84776 (Log2 FoldChange = 2.297) datasets. PKM2 was similarly highly expressed in LUAD and LUSC in the UALCAN database (Fig. 3F), which was also verified by RT-qPCR (Fig. 3G) of PKM2 expression in NSCLC cell lines and BEAS-2B cells.

TAL1 had a binding site (Fig. 3H) on the PKM2 promoter (chr15: 72229519–72229784) in JASPAR (https://jaspar.elixir.no/). Meanwhile, TAL1 acts as a transcriptional repressor in cervical cancer [20]. Therefore, we speculated that TAL1 also plays a role in transcriptional repression in NSCLC. RT-qPCR (Fig. 3I) revealed that PKM2 expression was reduced in A549 and H460 cells after OE-TAL1 infection. Dual-luciferase assay (Fig. 3J) showed that overexpression of TAL1 decreased PKM2 luciferase activity. ChIP assay (Fig. 3K) uncovered that anti-TAL1 was significantly enriched at the PKM2 promoter, and the enrichment was further elevated after TAL1 overexpression.

TAL1 inhibits NSCLC progression by inhibiting PKM2 and blocking glycolysis

Colony formation assay (Fig. 4A) showed that overexpression of PKM2 increased colony formation after 14 days of culture. MTT (Fig. 4B) results found that overexpression of PKM2 increased the proliferative capacity. EdU assay (Fig. 4C) results further showed that overexpression of PKM2 significantly enhanced A549 and H460 cells at the stage of DNA replication. The wound healing assay (Fig. 4D) and Transwell assay (Fig. 4E) manifested that overexpression of PKM2 increased the migration and invasion capacities of NSCLC cells. TUNEL (Fig. 4F) noted that overexpression of PKM2 decreased the apoptosis rate. In addition to the direction upregulation of lactate levels in A549 and H460 cells (Fig. 4G), overexpression of PKM2 was found to upregulate protein expression of glycolysis-related markers, GLUT1, LDHA, and PDK1 levels in the presence of TAL1 overexpression (Fig. 4H).

TAL1 inhibits glycolysis and NSCLC progression in vivo by targeting PKM2

The A549 cell line was selected for xenograft models as it showed the lowest TAL1 expression in Fig. 1F. Mouse tumors were observed and weighed (Fig. 5A), and tumor volume was measured periodically (Fig. 5B). Tumor size and weight in the OE-TAL1 group were markedly smaller than those in the OE-NC group; the combined overexpression of PKM2 led to an increase in tumor size and weight. WB (Fig. 5C) showed that TAL1 was elevated and PKM2 was reduced in tumor tissues of mice in the OE-TAL1 group; whereas the combined overexpression of PKM2 resulted in the unchanged expression of TAL1 and the elevated expression of PKM2. IHC (Fig. 5D) detected the levels of glycolysis-related proteins GLUT1, LDHA and PDK1, proliferation-related protein (Ki67), and apoptosis-related protein (Cleaved-Caspase3) in mice, and it was found that overexpression of TAL1 downregulated GLUT1, LDHA, PDK1, and Ki67 and upregulated Cleaved-Caspase3; combined overexpression of PKM2 upregulated GLUT1, LDHA, PDK1, and Ki67 and downregulated Cleaved-Caspase3. TUNEL (Fig. 5E) detection showed that overexpression of TAL1 significantly increased apoptosis, while combined overexpression of PKM2 repressed the apoptotic cells in the tumor tissues.

TAL1 is inhibited by DNMT3B-mediated methylation modification

We investigated the methylation level of TAL1 in the LUAD and LUSC datasets in the UALCAN database and found that TAL1 was hypermethylated in both LUAD and LUSC (Fig. 6A). MethPrimer database (http://www.urogene.org/cgi-bin/methprimer/methprimer.cgi) revealed the presence of CpG islands in the promoter region of TAL1 (chr1: 47234335 − 47232335) (Fig. 6B). Moreover, correlation analysis using SMART (http://www.bioinfo-zs.com/smartapp/) revealed a significant negative correlation between methylation levels at the TAL1 promoter and TAL1 expression levels in LUAD/LUSC (Fig. 6C). Similar promoter-expression relationships have been reported for other cancer/testis antigen genes, where promoter hypomethylation reactivates expression in tumors [21, 22]. We then explored the relation between the expression of DNMTs and TAL1 methylation in the LUAD and LUSC datasets in the LinkOmics database (https://www.linkedomics.org/) by selecting the Meth450 platform and the Hiseq platform. Pearson’s analysis uncovered that DNMT3B expression was positively correlated with the methylation of TAL1 in LUAD and LUSC (Fig. 6D). Meanwhile, the UALCAN database showed that DNMT3B was highly expressed in both LUAD and LUSC (Fig. 6E). As revealed by RT-qPCR (Fig. 6F), DNMT3B was highly expressed in NSCLC cell lines relative to BEAS-2B cells.

Three shRNAs (KD-DNMT3B#1, KD-DNMT3B#2, KD-DNMT3B#3) were designed to knock down DNMT3B expression in A549 and H460 cells. RT-qPCR (Fig. 6G) found that DNMT3B expression was reduced by all of them, and KD-DNMT3B#1 with the best efficiency in the knockdown was selected for subsequent experiments. RT-qPCR (Fig. 6H) revealed that DNMT3B knockdown upregulated TAL1 expression. Dual-luciferase assay (Fig. 6I) demonstrated that DNMT3B knockdown upregulated TAL1 luciferase activity. ChIP assay (Fig. 6J) noted that knockdown of DNMT3B significantly reduced the enrichment of anti-DNMT3B at the promoter of TAL1. qMSP assay (Fig. 6K) showed that DNMT3B knockdown notably lowered TAL1 DNA methylation level.

DNMT3B inhibits TAL1 expression to activate glycolysis in NSCLC cells

Colony formation assay (Fig. 7A) showed that overexpression of DNMT3B increased colony formation, and combined overexpression of TAL1 decreased colony formation. EdU assay (Fig. 7B) further showed that overexpression of DNMT3B significantly increased NSCLC cells at the DNA replication stage, and overexpression of TAL1 decreased A549 and H460 cells in the DNA replication stage. Transwell assay manifested that overexpression of DNMT3B elevated the migratory (Fig. 7C) and invasive (Fig. 7D) abilities of cells, and overexpression of TAL1 decreased the migration and invasion of NSCLC cells. TUNEL (Fig. 7E) analysis exhibited that overexpression of DNMT3B reduced the apoptosis rate, while combined overexpression of TAL1 elevated the apoptosis rate. Overexpression of DNMT3B upregulated the lactate level in A549 and H460 cells, which was reduced by overexpression of TAL1 (Fig. 7F). WB (Fig. 7G) found that overexpression of DNMT3B upregulated GLUT1, LDHA, and PDK1 levels, and simultaneously overexpression of TAL1 downregulated these levels.

DNMT3B affects NSCLC progression in vivo by regulating the TAL1/PKM2 axis

Tumor volume and weight in the OE-DNMT3B group were significantly larger than those in the OE-NC group, while combined knockdown of PKM2 resulted in decreased tumor weight (Fig. 8A) and volume (Fig. 8B). WB (Fig. 8C) assay revealed elevated expression of DNMT3B and PKM2, and decreased expression of TAL1 in the OE-DNMT3B group. By contrast, the combined knockdown of PKM2 resulted in unchanged expression of DNMT3B and TAL1 and decreased PKM2 expression. IHC (Fig. 8D) revealed that overexpression of DNMT3B upregulated GLUT1, LDHA, PDK1, and Ki67 levels, and downregulated Cleaved-Caspase3, and combined knockdown of PKM2 repressed proliferation- and glycolysis-related markers. TUNEL (Fig. 8E) results indicated that overexpression of DNMT3B reduced the apoptosis, while combined knockdown of PKM2 restored the apoptotic cells upon overexpression of DNMT3B.

Discussion

Aerobic glycolysis is the fundamental feature of tumor metabolism disturbance that facilitates tumor survival and acidification of the tumor microenvironment, leading to drug resistance [6]. Therefore, targeting glycolytic metabolism could be a potential therapeutic strategy for NSCLC. This paper elucidated that DNMT3B activated glycolysis and stimulated NSCLC progression by reducing methylation modification of TAL1 and ensuing PKM2 transcription induction.

Bioinformatics analysis unveiled that the transcription factor TAL1 was reduced in LUAD and LUSC. TAL1 is recognized as a master oncogenic transcription factor in T-cell acute lymphoblastic leukemia [23]. Our experimental data showed that TAL1 upregulation prevented NSCLC progression, reduced GLUT1, LDHA, PDK1, and Ki67 levels, and elevated Cleaved-Caspase3. Even though the downregulation of TAL1 has been correlated with worse overall survival for lung cancer patients [24], its functional role in NSCLC has not been revealed. Our investigation first underscores its anti-tumor effects in NSCLC. As a member of the class II bHLH family of transcription factors that form heterodimers with the class I bHLH proteins, TAL1 has been reported to regulate other transcription factors, including GATA3, RUNX1, and MYB [25]. Here, we dissected the downstream target of TAL1 by focusing on metabolic pathways. Further algorithmic analysis filters out PKM2. Further investigations found that TAL1 was negatively correlated with PKM2 transcription in NSCLC cells by binding to its promoter.

Metabolic reprogramming in cancer results in cancer cells with different phenotypic characteristics, including cell proliferation, migration, and angiogenesis, and metabolic disorders of cancer cells create a microenvironment that is conducive to cancer growth [26]. PKM2 was highly expressed in LUAD and LUSC, and overexpression of PKM2 upregulated lactate levels in A549 and H460 cells and promoted NSCLC progression. NSCLC cell lines showed upregulated PKM2 levels, and targeting PKM2 by shRNA in NSCLC cells significantly reduced cell viability and colony formation, while PKM2 targeting by small molecule inhibitor slowed tumor growth of human NSCLC xenografts [27]. More relevantly, PKM2 overexpression increased glucose uptake and lactate production in A549 cells, while PKM2 suppression hindered proliferation and migration in xenografts [28]. PKM2 inhibitor compound 3 K induces apoptosis, autophagy, and glycolytic dysfunction, thus preventing ovarian cancer cell growth [29]. As for the possible translational application, the PKM2 inhibitor PKM2-IN-1 induced cell apoptosis and inhibited proliferation and GLUT1 levels in A549 cells [30]. Nevertheless, due to its suboptimal solubility and relatively intricate pharmacological activity, there remain certain safety concerns when employing PKM2 inhibitors directly in cancer therapy [31]. Consequently, the restoration of TAL1 expression holds considerable application potential in the future treatment of NSCLC.

TAL1 was downregulated in 89% of lung cancer samples and might be frequently and strongly silenced by hypermethylated CpG sites, supporting TAL1 as a potential tumor suppressor and diagnostic biomarker of LUAD [11]. For the upstream mechanism of TAL1, database analysis revealed that DNMT3B expression was positively correlated with TAL1 methylation in LUAD and LUSC. High DNMT3B expression was considerably related to poor prognosis only in young NSCLC patients [32]. ΔDNMT3B is the predominant form of DNMT3B transcripts in NSCLC cell lines and primary tissues, and ∆DNMT3B4-del is a critical factor for aberrant DNA methylation during lung tumorigenesis [33]. Our findings uncovered that DNMT3B was highly expressed in both LUAD and LUSC, and overexpression of DNMT3B promoted upregulated lactate levels, GLUT1, LDHA, and PDK1 levels in NSCLC cells. Additionally, in vivo experiments demonstrated that overexpression of DNMT3B increased the weight and volume of tumors. Restoration of DNMT3B increased the proliferation, colony-forming, and invasive ability, while repressing apoptosis of A549 and NCI-H520 cells in the presence of microRNA‑203a‑3p [34]. DNMT3B-mediated FAM111B methylation accelerated invasion and glycolysis of papillary thyroid cancer cells, and papillary thyroid cancer patients with upregulated glucose uptake exhibited higher DNMT3B levels [15]. Silencing of DNMT3B inhibited EMT in nasopharyngeal carcinoma cells, induced cell cycle arrest and apoptosis, and increased radiosensitivity [13]. Overall, we may provide the first evidence that DNMT3B promotes glycolysis in NSCLC.

In summary, TAL1 prevented glycolysis and NSCLC progression by transcriptional repression of PKM2, while TAL1 was inhibited by DNMT3B-mediated methylation modification. Inhibition of PKM2 and DNMT3B may hinder glycolysis and thus prevent NSCLC cell proliferation, representing valuable therapeutic approaches for NSCLC. Nevertheless, clinical trials are warranted before these novel therapy strategies truly come to the forefront of clinical practice.

Materials and methods

Cell culture and treatment

NSCLC cell lines H1299 (CL-0165), HCC827 (CL-0094), A549 (CL-0016), and H460 (CL-0299) were purchased from Procell (Wuhan, Hubei, China). PC-9 cells (SNL-152) were procured from Sunncell (Wuhan, Hubei, China), and human normal lung epithelial cells BEAS-2B (CRL-3588) were procured from ATCC (Manassas, VA, USA). All human lung cancer cell lines were STR-identified before purchase. A549 cells were cultured in Ham’s F-12 K medium (PM150910, Procell) supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% P/S, and H1299, HCC827, H460, PC-9, and BEAS-2B cells were cultured in RPMI-1640 medium (R8758, Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) and placed in a humidified environment containing 5% CO2 at 37 °C.

Upon reaching 75% confluence, A549 and H460 cells at 1 × 105 cells/well were transferred to 24-well plates and cultured overnight at 37 °C. The medium and lentivirus (1 mL) were added to a 1.5 mL centrifuge tube, and the cells were collected 72 h after infection and screened using puromycin. All lentiviruses (OE-TAL1, OE-PKM2, KD-DNMT3B, KD-PKM2, and OE-DNMT3B, titer: >109 TU/mL) used in this experiment were constructed by VectorBuilder (Guangzhou, Guangdong, China).

RT-qPCR

Total RNA was extracted using the TRIzol kit (R0016, Beyotime, Shanghai, China) and reverse transcribed into cDNA using the PrimeScript RT kit (RR037Q, Takara, Dalian, Liaoning, China). cDNA was amplified using the Real-Time Fluorescent qPCR kit (FP209, Tiangen, Beijing, China), and qPCR was performed using the 7500 Real-Time PCR System (4351104, Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc., Waltham, MA, USA) for qPCR. β-actin served as an internal reference. Relative changes in gene expression were analyzed using the 2−∆∆CT method. The primer sequences are listed in Table 1.

Western blot (WB) analysis

A549 and H460 cells and mouse tumor tissues were lysed on ice for 30 min using radioimmunoprecipitation assay buffer (89901, Thermo Fisher) containing protease and phosphatase inhibitors. Total protein density extracted from the supernatant of cell lysates was assayed using a BCA kit (PK10026, Proteintech, Wuhan, Hubei, China). Next, proteins were separated using a 10% SDS-polyacrylamide gel and transferred to PVDF membranes. The membrane was sealed with 5% skim milk powder for 1 h at 37 °C and incubated with the corresponding primary antibodies overnight. The membrane was probed with the horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-coupled secondary antibody (1:2000, ab6721, Abcam, Cambridge, MA, USA) for 45 min at room temperature (RT). β-actin (1:1000, ab8227, Abcam) was used as an internal reference. Finally, the intensity of protein expression was measured using an ECL reagent (PK10003, Proteintech).

The used primary antibodies encompassed TAL1 (1:1000, A12927, ABclonal, Wuhan, Hubei, China), PKM2 (1:1000, 4053, CellSignaling, Beverly, MA, USA), DNMT3B (1:1000, 67259, CellSignaling), Ki67 (1:1000, ab92742, Abcam), E-cadherin (1:1000, A24874, ABclonal), N-cadherin (1:5000, ab76011, Abcam), glucose transporter type 1 (GLUT1, 1:1000, A6982, ABclonal), lactate dehydrogenase A (LDHA, 1:1000, 3582, CellSignaling), and pyruvate dehydrogenase kinase 1 (PDK1, 1:1000, NBP2-44132, Novus Biologicals, Littleton, CO, USA).

Colony formation experiment

After successful lentiviral infection, A549 and H460 cells were seeded in 6-well plates at 500 cells/well at 37 °C in 5% CO2. After 14 days, the cells were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) and stained with crystal violet staining solution (C0121-100 ml, Beyotime). The colonies were observed under a microscope, and the number of colonies was counted using ImageJ software.

MTT assay

Lentivirus-infected A549 and H460 cells were seeded in 96-well plates for 24 h and incubated with 20 µL of MTT reagent (40201ES72, Yeasen, Shanghai, China) for 0, 24, 48, 72, and 96 h. The supernatant was discarded after centrifugation, and 200 µL of dimethyl sulfoxide (D8418, Sigma-Aldrich) was added to the precipitated cells. Absorbance was measured at 570 nm.

EdU staining

EdU assays were performed on A549 and H460 cells using the E-Click EdU Cell Proliferation Imaging Assay Kit (E-CK-A377, Elabscience, Houston, TX, USA). Lentivirus-infected A549 and H460 cells were seeded in 6-well plates and pre-incubated with 10 µM EdU solution for 24 h, fixed with 1 mL of PBS containing 4% PFA for 15 min, washed with 1 mL of PBS containing 3% BSA, and incubated with 1 mL of PBS containing 0.3% Triton X-100 for 20 min (all at RT). Next, 500 µL of Click reaction solution was added to each well for 30-min incubation in the dark. After nuclear staining with 500 µL of DAPI working solution for 10 min in the dark, the sample was observed under a fluorescence microscope.

Wound healing assay

Lentivirus-infected A549 and H460 cells were seeded in 6-well plates. When the cell confluence reached 100%, the cells were washed with PBS and cultured in a serum-free medium for 24 h. A p200 pipette tip was then used to create a scratch in the cell monolayer, after which the medium was replaced with a fresh serum-free medium. Wound healing was observed by microscopy at 0 and 24 h. Wound healing areas were counted.

Transwell assay

For migration assays, lentivirus-treated A549 and H460 cells were seeded at 1 × 104 cells in Transwell chambers (3472, Corning Inc., Corning, NY, USA). For the invasion assay, lentivirus-treated A549 and H460 cells were seeded at 1 × 104 cells in Transwell chambers coated with 50 µL of 250 µg/mL of Matrigel (M8370, Solarbio, Beijing, China), and the chambers were added with 200 µL of FBS-free medium. Then, 800 µL of medium supplemented with 20% FBS was loaded into the lower chamber. After 24 h, any residual cells from the upper chamber membrane were removed using a cotton swab, and the cells that migrated or invaded the lower chamber were fixed with 4% PFA for 30 min and stained with 0.1% crystal violet staining solution for 15 min. Images were captured using an optical microscope, and migrated and invaded cells were counted using ImageJ software.

Cellular metabolism experiments

Lactate concentration was measured using a lactate assay kit (A019-2-1, Jiancheng, Nanjing, Jiangsu, China). A549 and H460 cells successfully infected with lentivirus were cultured in 6-well plates. After 48 h, the cells were centrifuged and lysed, and the supernatant was taken. The supernatant, enzyme working solution, and color developer were added together into a test tube, mixed well, and bathed in water at 37 °C for 10 min. Next, the termination solution was added and mixed well, and the absorbance was measured at 530 nm using a microplate reader. Lactate concentration = (Aassay-Ablank/Astandard-Ablank) × Cstandard/Cpr, where Cstandard refers to standard concentration, 3 mmol/L, and Cpr refers to cell homogenate protein concentration, gprot/L.

Xenograft tumor model

Four-week-old BALB/c nude mice (211) procured from Vital River (Beijing, China) were grown under pathogen-free conditions. The mice were randomized into the following groups: overexpression (OE)-negative control (NC), OE-TAL1, OE-TAL1 + OE-NC, OE-TAL1 + OE-PKM2, OE-DNMT3B, OE-DNMT3B + KD-NC, and OE-DNMT3B + KD-PKM2. A549 cells (5 × 106) that had undergone successful lentiviral infection were administered subcutaneously into the flanks of the mice. Tumor volumes were measured and recorded every three days. Tumor size was determined as 1/2 (length × width²). After 15 days, the mice were executed by 100 mg/kg of sodium pentobarbital (i.p.). Mouse tumor tissues were collected and weighed. A portion of the transplanted tumor tissue was cryopreserved. Another portion was fixed in 4% PFA at 25 °C for 24 h, dehydrated using a gradient ethanol solution, cleared with xylene, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned into 5-µm-thick slices. All animal experiments were approved by the Ethics Committee of Jiangxi Cancer Hospital.

TUNEL assay

Apoptosis rates in A549 and H460 cells and mouse tumor tissues were detected using the One-Step TUNEL Apoptosis Detection Kit (HY-K1078, MedChemExpress, Monmouth Junction, NJ, USA). For NSCLC cells, after discarding the culture medium, the cells were fixed with 4% PFA for 30 min at 4 °C and incubated with permeabilization solution for 5 min at RT. For mouse tumor tissues, paraffin-embedded sections were deparaffinized with xylene for 5 min at RT, soaked in anhydrous ethanol for 5 min, followed by gradient ethanol for 2 min sequentially. Proteinase K working solution was added and incubated for 15 min at RT. For processed cell and tissue sections, 50 µL of TUNEL working solution was added at 37 °C for 60 min of incubation away from light. An antifade mounting medium (HY-K1042, MedChemExpress) was added for observation under a fluorescence microscope.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC)

The 5-µm-thick sections were immersed in citrate buffer solution and heated in a microwave oven at 850 W for 10 min for antigen retrieval. Tissue sections were incubated in 3% H2O2 for 15 min at RT and sealed with 10% goat serum (C0265, Beyotime) for 30 min. The tissues were incubated with primary antibodies against GLUT1 (1:50, A6982, ABclonal), LDHA (1:200, 3582, CellSignaling), PDK1 (1:500, NBP2-44132, Novus Biologicals), Ki67 (1:1000, ab92742, Abcam), and Cleaved-Caspase3 (1:400, MAB835, Novus Biologicals) at 4 °C overnight and with goat anti-rabbit secondary antibody coupled to HRP (1:1000, ab6721, Abcam) for 30 min. Finally, the sections were stained at room temperature using the DAB HRP color development kit (P0203, Beyotime), counterstained with hematoxylin for 30 s, dried in an oven at 65 °C, treated with ethanol and xylene, and dried naturally. Finally, the sections were observed under a microscope, and the staining results were expressed as a positive cell rate.

Dual-luciferase assay

TAL1 or PKM2 promoter sequences were inserted into the pGL3-basic reporter vector. A549 and H460 cells were transiently transfected with the prepared vectors. After 48 h, cell lysates were further examined for luciferase activity using a luciferase reporter gene assay kit (RG088S, Beyotime). Renilla luciferase activity was used as a control.

Chromatin Immunoprecipitation (ChIP)

A549 and H460 cells were cross-linked with 1% PFA, and the formaldehyde was quenched with glycine, followed by lysis with ChIP lysis buffer for 10 min at 37 °C. The chromatin was sonicated into 200 bp fragments. A proportion of the DNA sample (2%) was collected as Input, and the lysates were incubated with the corresponding antibodies anti-DNMT3B (1:100, NBP2-59291, Novus Biologicals), anti-TAL1 (1:1000, GTX116020, GeneTex, Irvine, CA, USA), control antibody IgG (1:500, NB810-56910-10 mg, Novus Biologicals) and precipitated, and the DNA-protein immunocomplexes were collected using Pierce protein A/G-agarose beads and treated with RNase A and proteinase K. qPCR was performed using promoter-specific primers for TAL1 and PKM2.

qMSP

Genomic DNA was isolated from A549 and H460 cells using the Monarch Spin gDNA Extraction Kit (T3010S, New England Biolabs, Ipswich, MA, USA). A total of 2 µg of genomic DNA from each sample was processed using the EZ DNA Methylation-GOLD Kit (D5007, Zymo Research, Irvine, CA, USA), in which sodium bisulfite converted unmethylated cytosine to uracil. Modified DNA was analyzed for qMSP using TaqMan Universal Premix II Non-UNG Kit (4440038, Thermo Fisher).

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using Prism 10.4.2 (GraphPad, San Diego, CA, USA). Data are presented as mean ± standard error of the mean. Comparisons were made using unpaired t-tests, one-way or two-way ANOVA, and the Tukey test was used for post hoc tests. The cellular experiments were repeated three times, and the animal experiments had five mice in each group, which was significant at p < 0.05.
