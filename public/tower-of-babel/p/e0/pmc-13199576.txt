Age-grading and species identification of male mosquito Anopheles gambiae s.l. using mid-infrared spectroscopy and machine learning
Authors: Roger Sanou, Emmanuel P. Mwanga, Bazoumana B. D. Sow, Hamidou Maïga, François Wiminga, Doreen Josen Siria, Adrien Marie Gaston Belem, Roch K. Dabiré, Klaas Wynne, Heather M. Ferguson, Mario González-Jiménez, Simon A. Babayan, Fredros O. Okumu, Francesco Baldini, Abdoulaye Diabaté
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Most malaria vector control strategies target female Anopheles mosquitoes that transmit parasites, but emerging approaches such as gene drive, sterile insect technique, or Wolbachia require the release of males. Successful deployment depends on reliable knowledge of the species composition and age structure of male populations to overcome potential barriers to mating. Although approaches including near-infrared spectroscopy have been explored for male species identification and age grading, these methods remain constrained by limited validation, scalability, and accuracy under operational field conditions. Here we tested mid-infrared spectroscopy (MIRS) coupled with machine learning (ML) as a rapid and cost-effective approach for classifying species and age of male mosquitoes within the Anopheles gambiae complex. We used male mosquitoes from two laboratory-reared colonies in Burkina Faso (Anopheles coluzzii and An. gambiae) to develop a MIRS-ML model for species and age group (1–4, 5–10, 11–17 days) prediction under controlled conditions. This model was tested against a genetic and environmentally variable dataset consisting of male offspring obtained from gravid or blood fed Anopheles coluzzii and An. gambiae females collected from houses from two villages, Vallée du Kou and Soumousso and reared to adulthood in a semi-field system. The MIRS spectra from 2,120 males, representing both species, all age groups and both laboratory and semi-field backgrounds, were analysed using an extreme gradient boosting (XGBoost) algorithm to assess the ability to correctly predict age group and species. The XGBoost model classified mosquito species (86%) and age groups (85%) accurately in laboratory data, but performance declined under semi-field conditions (64% for species, 50% for age), reflecting environmental variability. Incorporating semi-field samples through transfer learning improved accuracy to 73% for species and 70% for age, underscoring both the limits of laboratory-only models and the value of transfer learning for enhancing generalisability in field settings. Our results demonstrate that mid-infrared spectroscopy with supervised machine learning (MIRS-ML) holds potential as a rapid tool for identifying the species and age group of cryptic male malaria vectors and represent one of the first applications of this approach to male Anopheles gambiae s.l. evaluated under semi-field conditions. However, before the approach can be used, larger datasets are needed to improve the classification algorithms and validate them for prediction in field populations.

Introduction

Historically, malaria vector research has focussed on female mosquitoes as they transmit parasites, with the ecology of male mosquitoes being understudied1. However, through their role in mating, male mosquitoes play an important role in determining the viability of offspring (fertility of eggs) and the stability of vector populations2,3. Thus, knowledge of their ecology, fitness and mating behaviour can be instrumental for vector control4,5. Vector control strategies depend on a deep understanding of the demographic characteristics of wild mosquito populations. Critical data on abundance, species distribution, and age structure are essential for selecting the most effective interventions6. As resistance to nearly all classes of insecticides used for malaria vector control is now widely established7,8, alternative approaches are urgently needed.

One promising strategy focuses on the use of genetically modified (GM) males to either suppress or replace wild populations with parasite resistant ones, thereby reducing malaria transmission9,10. Releasing sterile GM males that mate with wild females can be used in gene drive for mosquito suppression11. Historically, suppression methods, such as the Sterile Insect Technique (SIT), highlight the effectiveness of insect-based interventions12. This technique, which involves the mass release of sterilized insects to control pest populations, has demonstrated remarkable success; for instance, it led to the complete eradication of the screwworm in North America13, underscoring the potential of male-targeted strategies in vector control12. A similar sterilising outcome could be achieved using innovative biocontrol tools like Wolbachia14–16. Indeed Wolbachia-induced cytoplasmic incompatibility can be exploited using male releases to target female fertility. This method holds great promise in controlling diseases such as dengue fever, Zika virus, and malaria17,18.

To implement such approaches successfully – whether based on replacement or suppression of mosquito populations - it is crucial to thoroughly characterise the male mosquitoes that will be released in terms of quality and fitness. Similarly, it will be necessary to characterise the wild male population at release sites - including understanding the species composition and age structure, as required to assess whether released males can compete and mate with wild males under natural conditions. Successful implementation will require careful screening and selection of mass reared male mosquitoes with optimal mating capabilities, including the ability to survive well in the wild.

In addition to gene drive and Wolbachia approaches, other novel vector control approaches that involve targeting male mosquitoes are under development. This includes targeting males during swarming with space spraying5,19. In Burkina Faso, for example, targeting swarms has led to a dramatic reduction in mosquito population densities in some field trials, though challenges related to sustainability, safety, technical feasibility, and regulation remain20. With ongoing understanding of male mosquito biology, we can look forward to a future where vector control becomes even more efficient and sustainable through innovative methods like gene drive and male mosquito swarm targeting.

Overall, these male mosquito-focused approaches underscore the crucial role that male mosquitoes will play in next-generation vector control strategies. Assessment of the viability and impact of these approaches will require measurement of the demography and species composition/structure/survival of wild male mosquitoes in field populations targeted for male-based control measures to assess the efficacy and impact of these interventions. However, at present there is no validated method for assessing the age of wild males. Female mosquitoes can be age-graded on the basis of morphological changes in their ovaries21–23. Similarly, there is some evidence that morphological changes in male reproductive organs can also be used to estimate their age24–27, but these approaches have not been widely used and remain to be thoroughly validated. For species identification, morphological differences cannot distinguish male mosquitoes from cryptic species groups (e.g. An. gambiae s.l.), thus, more expensive and time-consuming molecular approaches required for identification, such as PCR28–30. Recent studies have demonstrated the ability of near-infrared spectroscopy (NIRS, 12,000–4,000 cm-1) to predict and determine with high accuracy the age and species of female mosquitoes up to 90% and 95% respectively and for male age group up to 90%31,32, whereas mid-infrared spectroscopy (MIRS, 4,000–400 cm− 1) has achieved up to 95% accuracy for mosquito age and species in the laboratory and 98% in semi-field conditions33. While near-infrared spectroscopy (NIRS) has been explored for both male and female mosquito age and species classification due to its speed and portability, mid-infrared spectroscopy (MIRS) offers greater molecular specificity and generalisability to more variable datasets, making it particularly valuable for semi-field and field evaluations where environmental and genetic variability is greater33. As infrared light interacts with the different vibrational modes of molecules in the mosquito cuticle providing information on its composition – which changes between species and during ageing34,35- the analysis of spectral data with machine learning (ML) algorithms allow to predict the age and discriminate the species of virtually any mosquito33,36.

Although so far infrared spectroscopy approaches have been tested for prediction of age and species in female mosquitoes, the underpinning principles by which they work (e.g. change in mosquito cuticle composition with age and species) should also apply to males, as recently shown in Anopheles gambiae31 and Aedes albopictus37. Thus, infrared spectroscopy could provide an efficient and accurate approach for tracking the demography and species composition of male malaria vectors in the field. To test it, here we investigated the use of MIRS-ML to accurately classify the species and age of male An. gambiae s.l. (An. coluzzii and An. gambiae) from laboratory colonies and wild F1s, reared under both controlled and natural semi-field conditions in Burkina Faso.

Materials and methods

Rearing males from laboratory colonies for age and species prediction experiments

Anopheles gambiae and Anopheles coluzzii mosquitoes from laboratory colonies at Institut de Recherche en Sciences de la Santé (IRSS) and from field were used. Both species were reared in the lab and semi-field system conditions to create laboratory and genetic/environmental variation, respectively. These mosquitoes were reared until adulthood and then collected at time points for both datasets as described in the analysis section.

These Anopheles gambiae and Anopheles coluzzii laboratory colonies are maintained in insectaries at the IRSS under controlled temperature (29 ± 2 o C) and relative humidity conditions (80% ± 10% RH), and under a 12 h:12 h (light: dark) photoperiod. These laboratory colonies were established in 2012 from wild populations in the Vallée du Kou and Soumousso, through collection of resting gravid and blood fed female mosquitoes inside houses38. Adult mosquitoes were fed with 10% sugar solution ab libitum via wet cotton, and females provided with blood meals from direct feeding on rabbits. For blood feeding purposes, a rabbit was kept in a hutch and placed on top of mosquito cage over 30 min to allow females feed directly. Oviposition bowls were set up 48 h days after blood feeding to allow for oviposition; with subsequently collected eggs moved into standard circular plastic rearing trays (28 cm diameters and 11 cm of deep filled with tap water). Groups of 300 first instar larvae were put in each bowl (filled with 1 L of tap water) and fed on TetraMin ® fish food daily. Pupae were collected and put into mosquito cages for emergence. To produce age-matched mosquitoes, pupae were put in adult cages (30 × 30 × 30 cm, metal frame covered with netting) on the same day. The day on which adults emerged was considered day 0. Each day, from 1–17-day post emergence, adult male mosquitoes were collected from each cohort to generate samples of different ages for scanning. Collections of ~ 25 male mosquitoes continued with different cohorts until target sample sizes for each age group were obtained for scanning. These male mosquitoes were killed using chloroform and put into tubes with silica for at least three days in fridge at + 2 to +8o C, to allow mosquitoes to desiccate prior to scanning following established procedure36.

Collecting males from field populations for age and species composition prediction under semi-field conditions

Mosquitoes collected from two field sites in Burkina Faso, Soumousso and Vallée du Kou 5 (VK5), then reared in the semi-field facility at Vallée du Kou 7 (VK7) until adulthood (Fig. 1).

Soumousso (11°04′ N, 4°03′ W) (Fig. 1) is located in the south of Bobo-Dioulasso. In this relatively dry setting, the dominant species are Anopheles gambiae, followed by Anopheles coluzzii, Anopheles funestus, and Anopheles arabiensis. In this area, mosquito density is lower compared to Vallée du Kou, and the dynamics of the mosquito population follows the two main seasons, with fewer mosquitoes in dry season compared to rainy season.

Vallée du Kou (VK) (11˚23’ N, 4˚24’ W) (Fig. 1) is an irrigated rice field area since 1970. The site is characterized by wooded savannah and over 1,200 ha and contains seven discrete villages (VK1-7). Few insecticides are used to protect the rice from pests, but they are extensively used in the surrounding villages in cotton fields. High mosquito density is observed throughout the year but accentuated in August-September corresponding to the peak of the rainy season.

Female mosquitoes, resting on walls, blood fed or gravid, were haphazardly collected by trained technicians using mouth aspirators from inside houses on walls from July to November 2020. These mosquitoes were transferred immediately into standard cages (30 × 30 × 30 cm) with net for transport to IRSS, during which cages were covered with a wet cloth to avoid desiccation. Mosquitoes were provided with access to a 5% glucose solution during transport. These wild-caught mosquitoes were then transferred to a semi-field facility system at IRSS for rearing, where conditions as sun, light, humidity, and temperature were similar to the field39. These mosquitoes were provided with 5% glucose for 48 h after collection and then moved into individual oviposition cups (with 10 ml of water) at 72 h post collection. After two days in oviposition cups, females that laid eggs were removed using forceps or a mouth aspirator, killed, and transferred into labelled tubes (same labelling as the cup) in ethanol 80% for molecular species identification analyses following existing protocols40. After identifying the species of these F0 females by PCR (as either An. gambiae and An. coluzzii) their offsprings were pooled by species and reared to adulthood in cages within the semi-field facility. Similar to the protocol for mosquitoes in laboratory colonies, the day of adult emergence was defined as day 0, with F1 male mosquitoes collected on each day from 1 to 17 post emergence to generate cohorts of different age for scanning. Approximately 25 males per day and per species were collected from rearing cages and killed using chloroform, then, immediately transferred into 1.5 ml Eppendorf tubes with silica and put into fridge at + 2 - +8o C for storage before processing.

Mid-infrared spectrometer

The Bruker ALPHA Fourier-transform infrared (FTIR) spectrometer was used for this study. This spectrometer includes a Platinum attenuated total reflectance (ATR), and a platinum diamond sampling module with a spectral range of 375–7500 cm − 1 and with a spectral resolution at 5 cm − 141. The infrared optical window was equipped with zinc selenide (ZnSe) to accommodate high humidity conditions41. The spectrometer unit and the Bruker Opus software version 7.5 were provided and installed at IRSS in 2018 for research purposes.

MIRS scanning method

Cohorts of male mosquitoes of different age and species reared from laboratory and Semi field Facility System (SFS) cohorts were used for scanning. Samples were removed from the fridge and left at laboratory ambient temperature for ~ 15 min on bench prior to scanning.

When the sample were sufficiently dry, they were scanned individually on the ATR module of the FTIR spectrometer. Mosquitoes were placed individually on the scanner, with their head/thorax positioned in the centre of diamond platinum and pushed down by the anvil to hold them in a stable position. Then scanning was initiated, with the light spread along the sample in contact with diamond to collect an individual spectrum from each mosquito between 4,000 and 400 cm-1, with a resolution of 5 cm-1. Each mosquito sample was scanned 32 times in 30s to generate a spectrum36 that was recorded in absorbance units using Bruker OPUS software ( Bruker Optics. OPUS spectroscopy software. 2019).

The current mid-infrared spectroscopy (MIRS) workflow enables high-throughput scanning of mosquitoes, with processing rates between 60 and 100 mosquitoes per hour depending on sample handling and operator experience42. Although near-infrared spectroscopy (NIRS) generally offers faster acquisition32 and greater portability, MIRS provides increased biochemical specificity that can provide increased generalisability and accuracy to classify age and species of variable datasets. This trade-off suggests that while NIRS may be advantageous for rapid low-resolution field screening, MIRS could represent a robust and complementary approach when higher analytical precision and generalisability are required for operational decision-making.

MIRS data analysis and processing

Male mosquito spectra collected from Bruker Opus software were firstly curated for analysis using Loco Mosquito 5.3, a custom program written in Python 3.6. Mosquito spectra with low intensity or a significant atmospheric intrusion were discarded automatically using this program36. These MIR spectra were then analysed using the Python programming language with scikit-learn library [scikitlibrary.com]. The Extreme gradient boosting models (XGBoost) were trained and used to predict the age class and species of each male mosquito. Male mosquito age was defined into three categorical classes: 1–4 days representing young males with no mating experience, 5–10 days (intermediate age), and 11–17 days for (old). These categorical age classes and species names (i.e., An. coluzzii and An. gambiae) were used as labels for supervised training, with the intensities of male spectra used as features. Two datasets were used for this analysis: a laboratory dataset containing 1058 spectra used for model training and testing, and a semi-field dataset consisting of 1365 spectra to test the transferability of laboratory-based models to semi-field predictions. The laboratory spectra dataset was partitioned into stratified training (80%, n = 846) and test (20%, 212) sets, accounting for both age categories and species during stratification. The training set was comprised of 500 An. coluzzii and 346 An. gambiae, with 348, 220, and 278 mosquitoes in the 1–4, 5–10, 11-17-day age categories, respectively (Table 1). The test set consisted of 126 An. coluzzii and 86 An. gambiae, with 87, 55, and 70 mosquitoes in the 1–4, 5–10, 11–17-day age categories, respectively (Table 1). To ensure the model did not rely predominantly on the majority class, the number of samples in each class was balanced. Random under-sampling was performed on the training set, resulting in 346 mosquitoes for both An. coluzzii and An. gambiae classes, and 220 mosquitoes for each of the 1–4, 5–10, 11–17 days age categories (Table 1). The test dataset was maintained with the original class imbalance, as imbalance primarily affects model training by introducing bias.

To ensure data uniformity, the spectra data were standardised to a mean of 0 and a standard deviation of 1. The extreme gradient boosting (XGBoost) model was used for training due to its ability to account for non-linear patterns in the data, and it has previously been demonstrated effective in predicting mosquito age classes of female An. arabiensis and An. funestus43. The model was trained and evaluated using K-fold cross-validation (k = 5). Classification accuracy calculated as the percentage of correct classifications divided by the total predictions. The XGBoost models trained with laboratory data were then tested for prediction on the semi-field dataset. The semi-field dataset contained a total of 695 An. coluzzii and 670 An. gambiae mosquitoes. Additionally, the dataset consisted of 277, 551, and 537 mosquitoes in the 1–4, 5–10, 11–17 days age categories, respectively.

Transfer learning

To reduce the effort required for field data collection, we employed a transfer-learning approach. This method leverages the knowledge gained from a lab-trained model, developed using a dataset of laboratory-reared mosquitoes, to identify a minimal amount of semi-field data needed33. We aimed to evaluate whether the existing lab-trained model can effectively predict the age and species of male mosquitoes in a semi-field setting, based on prior training with laboratory data. In this study, “transfer learning” involved adding a subset of semi-field samples to the calibration set, which reduced test set independence; this was applied as a research strategy to explore model adaptation rather than as evidence of a universally generalised calibration.

Results

The XGBoost model demonstrated high classification accuracy on the laboratory test set, achieving 86% accuracy in predicting male mosquito species (Fig. 2a). Additionally, the model efficiently predicted the 1–4, 5–10, 11–17 days age categories regardless of their species identity with a classification accuracy of 85% (Fig. 2b).

We then tested the accuracy of the MIRS laboratory models on age and species prediction on the F1 field collected male mosquitoes reared under semi-field conditions. The XGBoost model had poorer performance on this data set, as it classified species with 64% accuracy (Fig. 3a) and 50% accuracy overall for age group classification (Fig. 3b).

Prediction accuracy was highest for younger and older age groups (71% and 60% for 1-4- and 11-17-days age classes, respectively), but accuracy was very low for the middle age class (5–10 days; 21% accuracy) (Fig. 3b).

Although these predictions were above random classification (50% for species, 33% for age), suggesting that some signals from the MIRS can still be detected by the model, their overall accuracy was low. We thus attempted to further improve the generalisability of the lab-trained XGBoost models using a transfer learning approach. Here the training set of the XGBoost laboratory-based model was augmented with small subsets of data from the semi-field data set, comprising 104 An. coluzzii and 100 An. gambiae samples, with 83, 41, and 80 mosquitoes in the 1–4, 5–10, 11–17-day age categories, respectively. With the addition of this additional data, the classification accuracy for species in the semi-field data set rose from 64% to 73%, and the age group classification accuracy from 50% to 70% (Fig. 4).

To understand the spectral features utilised by the XGBoost model for both age and species classification we analysed the mean feature importance of the most relevant wavenumbers contributing to the predictions (Fig. 5). In both models, the spectral region between 1770 and 1700 cm⁻¹ stood out, corresponding to the amide vibrational modes of proteins36. This observation is consistent with previous MIRS-ML studies, underscoring the relevance of these bands as they contain information on the quantity, environment, and conformation of proteins within the insect cuticle33,36,37,41. Both models also relied on the vibrational modes associated with C–H stretching, particularly linked to cuticular waxes. This is biologically meaningful since the wax layer composition varies with age and between species, as previously demonstrated33. Notably, the age classification model showed a strong dependence on wavenumbers associated with C–O vibrational modes, indicating that chitin content is an important contributor to the model’s predictions, with the band at 689 cm⁻¹ emerging as particularly relevant. In contrast, this chitin-associated signal appeared less important for species classification. Moreover, the species model shows a concerning reliance on the CO₂ absorption band at 2315 cm⁻¹. Since spectra contaminated with CO₂ are systematically excluded during pre-processing, this band likely reflects baseline fluctuations related to light scattering effects on the mosquito cuticle rather than genuine chemical features.

Discussion

This study was carried out to investigate whether the MIRS-ML method can classify the age or species of male mosquitoes from the An. gambiae s.l. group, from both laboratory colonies and the offspring of wild females reared in semi-field conditions. Much progress has been made in recent years towards in using Infrared spectroscopy to identify mosquito traits from cuticle analysis. Recent studies using MIRS and NIRS, revealed that infrared spectroscopy is reliable in predicting the age and species of malaria vectors in many studies33,36,37,41.

In addition to previous NIRS applications to male mosquitoes, our study assessed male age and species classification, thereby extending the evaluation of MIRS to both sexes under laboratory and semi-field conditions.

While previous work on infrared (NIRS and MIRS) focussed primarily on female mosquitoes, here we showed that MIRS coupled with machine learning could also be applied to accurately predict the mosquito male age and species, under both laboratory and semi-field conditions.

This work demonstrates that infrared spectroscopy could provide a useful and much needed tool for rapid and low-cost surveillance of male mosquitoes, as required to guide the deployment of novel interventions. These results are in line with previous findings where models built solely on laboratory data often fail to generalise to field conditions without incorporating field-derived data33,44.

Specifically, laboratory-trained models often fail to generalise under field conditions because calibration captures homogeneous, laboratory-specific signatures rather than universally transferable features. In contrast, semi-field and natural environments introduce variability in temperature, humidity, mosquito behavior, and physiological or genetic differences between lab-reared and wild populations, all of which challenge model performance. Transfer learning provides a practical solution by fine-tuning models with small, representative subsets of field data, enabling robust features learned under controlled settings to be recalibrated to accommodate natural variability. Although this improves accuracy, it reduces test set independence and does not constitute a fully generalised calibration. Evidence from previous studies32,43–45 shows that incorporating field-derived data through transfer learning significantly enhances predictions of mosquito age and species, supporting an adaptive strategy in which laboratory models are incrementally refined with sentinel site or early deployment samples to sustain performance across diverse environments and strengthen vector control surveillance applications46.

In addition to the approaches tested here, based on cuticular analysis, assessment of male mosquito demographics could be enhanced by further integration of morphological assessment of the testes into the predictive framework. Integrating age-related changes in these other morphological features could further improve classification accuracy. Evaluating testicular morphology—such as size, structure, and developmental stage—provides valuable insights into the reproductive status and overall fitness of male mosquitoes45. Testicular morphology in male mosquitoes can be a useful tool for species identification, reproductive and mating status assessment, and understanding evolutionary adaptations. Studies have shown that genital structures, including testes, vary across species and can inform vector control strategies including surveillance protocols for species resolution, reproductive fitness monitoring, and the strategic deployment of male-based vector control interventions27,45,46. Previous studies indicate that testicular features correlate strongly with mating success and male fitness26,45. Integrating testis morphology assessment data into machine learning models can enhance their predictive accuracy, ensuring that they more accurately predict the biological and reproductive attributes of field populations. Testis morphology is not captured in infrared spectra, and its inclusion would require a multimodal approach in which independent morphological measurements are combined with spectral features within a machine learning framework.

Effective deployment of vector control strategies, such as gene drive, Wolbachia and sterile insect technique depends on reliable knowledge of the species composition and age structure of male populations, yet existing approaches—including NIRS—remain constrained by limitations in accuracy, validation, and scalability when applied to wild males under operational conditions.

NIRS and MIRS each offer distinct advantages for mosquito surveillance. NIRS is faster, portable, processing hundreds of specimens per hour with minimal preparation, though its accuracy can vary under field conditions. MIRS provides higher biochemical specificity and accuracy for age and species classification, but at the expense of throughput, portability, making the two approaches complementary rather than mutually exclusive.

One of the main limitations of this study lies in the restricted sample size and population diversity used for model training. The reduced accuracy observed in semi-field conditions suggests that the current model may not generalize well across varied ecological contexts or genetic backgrounds. Future work should incorporate larger sample sizes, including more diverse mosquito populations from different geographic settings and environmental conditions, to improve model robustness. Additionally, refining spectral resolution and exploring complementary physiological markers could help address uncertainties in age classification and species differentiation, particularly for mid-aged groups and cryptic species.

Conclusion

These findings offer valuable insights for optimizing the design and implementation of innovative malaria vector control strategies, particularly those involving the release of laboratory-reared male mosquitoes or the targeted suppression of wild male populations. They enable rapid monitoring of shifts in male mosquito species composition in settings undergoing releases of genetically modified strains. Additionally, they provide a means to assess the age structure—and by extension, the survival dynamics—of wild males before and during interventions, helping to evaluate whether male survival is being impacted as expected.
