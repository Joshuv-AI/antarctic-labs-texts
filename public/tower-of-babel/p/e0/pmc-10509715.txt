Introduction

Tessier 7 craniofacial congenital cleft is an infrequent abnormality occurring in approximately 1 out of 80,000 to 1 out of 300,000 live births [[1], [2], [3]]. It constitutes around 0.3 to 1.0 % of the complete range of cleft anomalies [[1], [2], [3]]. Since the year 2000, only a total of 24 cases of this condition have been recorded in the available literature [4]. This current case represents the 25th instance and is likely the inaugural case reported from Nepal. It is also referred to as a lateral facial cleft, transverse facial cleft, or congenital macrostomia [[5], [6], [7]]. It can manifest as an isolated occurrence or be concomitant with other craniofacial anomalies such as Treacher Collins syndrome, Oculo-auriculo-vertebral spectrum, Hemifacial microsomia or Goldenhaar syndrome [[1], [2], [3]].

The classification of craniofacial clefts was initially established by Paul Tessier in 1976, encompassing 15 distinct types designated as Tessier numbers 0 through 14 [6,7]. The categorization is based on the specific pattern of the cleft in relation to the orbital region [6,7]. This classification has gained widespread recognition and adoption [1]. In accordance with this classification system, Tessier 7 cleft involves both soft tissue and skeletal abnormalities [8]. The soft tissue cleft originates from the oral commissure and extends laterally, reaching varying distances towards the tragus of the ear [8]. The skeletal anomalies influence the zygomatic, maxillary, and mandibular structures on the same side [8].

The precise etiology of Tessier 7 cleft remains uncertain [4]. It arises from anomalous fetal development, stemming from the non-fusion of the embryonic maxillary and mandibular processes of the first branchial arch [9]. Usually, the cleft is unilateral and its extent is limited to the anterior border of the masseter muscles [1] This condition impacts all layers of the cheek, encompassing the buccal mucosa, muscles, subcutaneous tissue, and skin [1]. Muscle diastasis is evident around the commissures, involving the disorganization of muscle fibers on the affected side, specifically the risorius, Zygomaticus major, depressor anguli oris, and orbicularis oris muscles [7,10]. Clinically, these clefts are marked by macrostomia, leading to challenges in functions such as chewing, swallowing, speech and causing issues like excessive drooling of saliva, along with aesthetic concerns [2].

The management approach for Tessier 7 cleft entails a series of surgical interventions, including commissuroplasty and sequential layer-wise closure encompassing mucosa, muscles, and skin [1,2]. During the procedure, a novel commissure of the mouth is constructed, utilizing the unaffected side of the commissure as a reference [1,2]. Mucosal closure is executed through linear suturing [1,2]. The muscles mentioned earlier are repositioned to establish a new modiolus at the corner of the mouth [1,2]. Regarding the skin, treatment modalities encompass linear closure or z-plasty techniques [1,3].

The primary objective of Tessier 7 cleft surgery is to attain a normal and symmetrical commissural opening, thereby restoring functional aspects such as chewing, swallowing, and speech, while also addressing aesthetic concerns [2].

This case was surgically managed by Consultant Oral and Maxillofacial Surgeon, Dr. Manoj Adhikari, at Sri Birendra Hospital, a tertiary-level goverment facility located in Chhauni, Kathmandu, Nepal. The work has been reported in line with the SCARE criteria [11].

Presentation of case

A 3-year-old male child, accompanied by his parents, was seen at the Oral and Maxillofacial Surgery Department. They reported that the child was experiencing difficulties related to chewing, swallowing, excessive saliva drooling, speech, and aesthetic concerns. The patient had no noteworthy medical, dental, familial, or medication-related history.

Upon examination, a complete cleft, measuring 25 mm, was observed on the right corner of the mouth, extending from the skin to the inner mucosal lining. Additionally, there were skin tags present in front of the ear (preauricular region) and on the right cheek (Fig. 1). A non-contrast Computed Tomography (NCCT) scan of the facial region revealed the presence of a hypoplastic coronoid process and distorted condylar process of the mandible on the corresponding side (Fig. 2). However, it is noteworthy that the patient remains asymptomatic with no discernible complications attributable to this osseous anomaly. Routine baseline blood tests, electrocardiogram (ECG), chest x-ray, and viral marker tests all yielded results within the normal range.

The preoperative extraoral view illustrates a Tessier 7 cleft on the right side of the face, accompanied by the presence of cutaneous tags.

A volumetric Rendering Technique (VRT) image of a non-contrast computed tomography (NCCT) scan of the face reveals a hypoplastic coronoid process and a distorted condylar process on the right side of the mandible.

A comprehensive surgical plan was formulated to address the case, necessitating general anesthesia for the procedure. The patient was positioned supine, and orotracheal intubation was carried out. Preparatory steps were taken, including marking and measurement. Specifically, the distance from the center of the cupid's bow to the commissure on the unaffected side of the upper lip was measured and replicated on the cleft side. This procedure was similarly conducted for the lower lip.

An incision was made over the cleft area, exposing deeper anatomical structures (Fig. 3). The mucosal layer was meticulously sutured in a linear manner. Notably, the orbicularis oris, zygomaticus major, risorius, and depressor anguli oris muscles were meticulously isolated. The upper group of muscle fibers was layered over the lower group and subsequently sutured together. This technique resulted in the creation of a novel modiolus and commissure of the mouth on the cleft side (Fig. 4). Closure of the skin was performed in a straight-line manner.

An intraoperative view illustrating the underlying anatomical structures subsequent to the excision of surplus tissue from the cleft area.

An intraoperative view illustrating the newly established commissure resulting from the suturing of muscles at the modiolus.

Following the completion of the surgery, the patient was extubated and exhibited satisfactory recovery. Postoperative care entailed the prescription of antibiotics and analgesics. The surgical outcomes, both aesthetically and functionally, were deemed satisfactory. Specifically, the patient displayed symmetrical oral commissure, absence of saliva drooling, enhanced clinical appearance, and improved speech and feeding abilities.

A follow-up evaluation, conducted six months post-surgery revealed the absence of scar hypertrophy, contracture, or lateral displacement of the commissure (Fig. 5). The child remained devoid of complications and exhibited excellent health and well-being.

A postoperative extraoral view, taken six months after the procedure, illustrating the surgically reconstructed cleft site.

Discussion

Tessier 7 craniofacial congenital cleft is an infrequent anomaly, occurring at incidence rates ranging from 1 in 80,000 to 1 in 300,000 live births [[1], [2], [3]]. This particular cleft anomaly constitutes approximately 0.3 % to 1.0 % of the entire spectrum of cleft conditions [[1], [2], [3]]. The condition exhibits a higher prevalence among males than females [4]. Unilateral manifestation is observed in most of the cases [7,10,12] The documented instances of this anomaly in the medical literature have been limited, with only 24 cases reported since 2020 [4]. This current case marks the 25th instance and is potentially the inaugural report from Nepal. This case report outlines the clinical presentation of a 3-year-old male child presenting with a right-sided Tessier 7 congenital facial cleft.

Regarding the modiolus reconstruction, a technique involving the suturing of the orbicularis oris, zygomaticus major, risorius, and depressor anguli oris muscles has been recommended in the literature [7], which we employed in this particular case. However, it's worth noting that certain surgeons have reported achieving successful outcomes without strictly adhering to these specified techniques [2].

Regarding the closure of the skin, two primary techniques can be employed: straight-line closure or the utilization of Z-plasty [2]. It has been theorized that Z-plasty yields superior outcomes compared to straight-line closure, as the latter method can lead to commissure contracture and lateral displacement. However, no definitive studies have substantiated this claim [2]. Some research posits that any lateral displacement stemming from straight-line closure is counterbalanced by the rehabilitated sphincter function of the orbicularis oris muscle [12].

Notably, there are studies suggesting that straight-line skin closure produces more favorable results in cases of transverse facial clefts compared to Z-plasty technique [10]. There's also a recommendation for employing a combination of straight-line skin closure followed by a small Z-plasty at the end, intended to prevent the formation of dog ears [7].

In this specific case, we opted for the straight-line skin closure method and achieved a high-quality outcome.

Patients exhibiting congenital macrostomia deformities should undergo surgical intervention at the earliest opportunity to optimize speech, feeding, and aesthetic outcomes [6].

These congenital anomalies often exhibit potential associations with diverse syndromes [7]. Consequently, their prenatal identification is imperative. Utilizing two-dimensional, three-dimensional, or four-dimensional ultrasound techniques proves to be an accurate, dependable, and non-invasive approach for detecting craniofacial anomalies prior to birth [13].

The notable strength of this rare case report lies in the successful surgical intervention, effectively addressing all presenting issues without encountering any complications. Importantly, no limitations were identified within the scope of this case study.

Conclusion

Given the rarity of Tessier 7 craniofacial cleft as a congenital anomaly, a comprehensive understanding of the surgical intricacies becomes imperative to achieve favorable cosmetic and functional outcomes. Within the context of surgical repair for such uncommon cleft conditions, the pivotal steps encompass the reconstruction of a novel commissure, establishment of a new modiolus, and meticulous skin closure.
