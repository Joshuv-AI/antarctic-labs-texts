The immune protection of OMP16-specific IgM against Brucella infection
Authors: Yunyi Zhai, Kaihui Sun, Ye Yuan, Gawa Wudong, Shuan Qu, Yaxin Li, Yuanhao Yang, XiaoFang Liu, Dong Zhou, Wei Liu, Yaping Jin, Aihua Wang
Published: 2026

Background

Brucellosis, a globally distributed zoonosis, poses a significant threat to public health and livestock economies, with an estimated annual incidence of 2.1 million human cases [1]. Brucella evades host immunity through its intracellular survival strategy, often leading to chronic infection [2, 3]. Current antibiotic therapies are hampered by prolonged treatment duration, high relapse rates, and emerging drug resistance, complicating pathogen eradication [4]. Moreover, no effective human vaccine exists, and available animal vaccines remain inadequate [5]. These challenges underscore the urgent need for novel immunotherapeutic approaches, particularly antibody-based treatments and multi-epitope vaccines.

Antibody-based therapeutics show considerable promise for anti-infective applications due to their high specificity and versatile effector functions [6]. IgG antibodies, with their diverse mechanisms such as antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity (ADCC) and opsonophagocytosis, along with an extended half-life, are typically the preferred choice for therapeutic development [7, 8]. In contrast, IgM—the first antibody isotype produced in an immune response—is often regarded primarily for its role in complement activation. Although the high-affinity Fc receptor for IgM (FcμR) is predominantly expressed on B cells (and T/NK cells in humans) and is not associated with opsonic function, the unique pentameric structure of IgM confers a powerful capacity to activate the classical complement cascade [9–11]. This enables IgM to both directly lyse pathogens via the membrane attack complex and indirectly enhance phagocytic clearance through complement receptor-mediated mechanisms, offering a dual effector strategy that may be advantageous against intracellular pathogens like Brucella [12].

Concurrently, multi-epitope vaccine design has emerged as a promising strategy in vaccinology, centering on the identification of highly conserved and immunogenic epitopes [13, 14]. As a key component of the Brucella outer membrane protein family, outer membrane protein 16 (OMP16) plays an essential role in maintaining bacterial structural integrity and mediating immune recognition. Studies have shown that OMP16 is highly conserved across major Brucella species and can elicit protective immune responses, underscoring its potential as a vaccine antigen [15, 16]. However, the systematic characterization of OMP16’s antigenic epitopes and their immunological properties remains incomplete, limiting its application in next-generation vaccine design. Furthermore, the protective mechanisms of OMP16-specific IgM antibodies are not fully elucidated—particularly the molecular basis of epitope specificity, binding behavior, and immunoprotective effects—warranting further investigation.

In this study, we successfully generated two OMP16-specific IgM monoclonal antibodies (D3 and F5) using hybridoma technology. Fine epitope mapping identified two linear epitopes of OMP16, which displayed ideal immunogenic features and high conservation across Brucella species—key attributes for broad-spectrum multi-epitope vaccine development. Analysis of critical binding residues elucidated precise molecular interactions governing antibody-antigen binding, supporting the rational design of epitope-based vaccines with enhanced immunogenicity. Functionally, the two IgM antibodies exhibited distinct effector profiles. Although both mediated macrophage killing via complement activation, F5 uniquely demonstrated direct bacteriolysis, while D3 specifically enhanced phagocytosis. This functional divergence between antibodies targeting the same antigen reveals a complex layer of immune regulation. These findings provide novel strategies for antibody-based therapy against brucellosis and valuable insights for multi-epitope vaccine design, demonstrating broad applicability across both immunotherapy and vaccine development.

To elucidate the molecular basis of antigen recognition, total RNA was extracted from the hybridoma cells for variable region sequencing, yielding complete amino acid sequences of the heavy and light chain variable regions for both D3 and F5 (Fig. 1D and E). Characterization of the CDR landscapes provides a foundation for subsequent fine epitope mapping and mechanistic studies of antibody-antigen interactions.

Epitope identification and conservation analysis of OMP16 monoclonal antibodies

To precisely map the epitopes targeted by the D3 and F5 monoclonal antibodies, we designed a series of truncated OMP16 fragments (Fig. 2A). The protein sequence, excluding its signal peptide and transmembrane regions, was divided into three overlapping segments (P1–P3). Initial Western blot screening showed that the D3 antibody bound to both P1 and P2, while F5 recognized P2 and P3 (Fig. 2B), indicating that their epitopes reside within the P1–P2 and P2–P3 overlapping regions, respectively. These regions were then finely truncated to generate shorter peptides: P4–P6 (covering the D3 epitope region) and P7–P9 (covering the F5 epitope region). Notably, both epitopes are located in spatially exposed regions of OMP16, conferring high accessibility for antibody interaction. To assess their broad-spectrum potential, multi-species sequence alignment was performed. The epitopes recognized by D3 and F5 were found to be highly conserved across major Brucella species, including B. abortus, B. melitensis, B. suis, and B. canis (Fig. 2C). This high conservation suggests that both antibodies may offer cross-species protective activity. These findings not only precisely identify the linear epitopes of D3 and F5 on OMP16 but also confirm their structural accessibility and conservation, providing a molecular basis for understanding antibody function and a foundation for developing broad-spectrum, epitope-based immunotherapies.

Analysis of key amino acid residues in OMP16 monoclonal antibody-antigen binding

To elucidate the binding specificity between the IgM monoclonal antibodies (D3 and F5) and the OMP16 antigen, we employed an integrated strategy of deep learning-based structural modeling, molecular docking, and alanine-scanning mutagenesis to identify key interacting residues (Fig. 3A, G). First, high-precision three-dimensional models of OMP16 (without its signal peptide and transmembrane domains) and the antibody variable regions were generated using deep learning algorithms. Rigid molecular docking indicated that D3 binds the OMP16 77–84 region (77TLSKQAQW84) primarily through its heavy-chain CDR. Antigen residues Ser79 (A79), Ala82 (A82), and Gln83 (A83) showed close spatial proximity (van der Waals distance < 4 Å) to heavy-chain residue H113, with A83 also forming a hydrogen-bond network with light-chain residue L38 (Fig. 3B). Alanine-scanning simulations predicted a substantial increase in binding free energy change (ΔΔG) upon mutation of A83, underscoring the importance of its polar side chain for interface stability (Fig. 3C). Given that A82 is an alanine in the wild-type sequence, we performed in silico saturation mutagenesis to assess the impact of other substitutions at this position. This analysis predicted that substitution of A82 with glutamic acid would be highly deleterious to binding (Supplementary Data). For the F5 antibody, molecular docking localized its binding site to the OMP16 120–127 region (120RDFLASRG127). Docking profiles revealed that antigen residues A120 (Arg), A121 (Asp), and A122 (Phe) engage in multivalent interactions with heavy-chain CDR residues (e.g., H109, H58, H110), while A120 also contacts light-chain residue L38 (Fig. 3E). Alanine-scanning energy calculations showed significant increases in binding energy upon mutation of A120, A121, or A122 (Fig. 3F). Consistent with this, dot-blot assays verified that mutations at these sites severely impaired or abolished F5 binding (Fig. 3H), confirming their role as functional hot spots.

Using this multi-scale approach—computational prediction, energy evaluation, and experimental validation—we precisely identified key OMP16 residues for D3 and F5 binding and revealed the molecular basis for high-affinity interactions mediated primarily by heavy-chain CDR regions. Notably, both epitopes contain charged (e.g., A83-Gln, A121-Asp) and aromatic (A122-Phe) residues, features that may enhance specificity through hydrogen bonding and hydrophobic effects. These findings provide key targets for the rational design of OMP16-directed antibody therapeutics.

OMP16-specific IgM monoclonal antibodies mediate bacteriolysis via complement activation

To evaluate the direct bacteriolytic effect of OMP16-specific IgM monoclonal antibodies (D3 and F5) on Brucella abortus A19, an in vitro assay was established using fresh guinea pig serum as the complement source. The results showed no significant difference (p > 0.05) in viable Brucella counts between the D3-treated group and the PBS or IgM isotype control groups. In contrast, the F5-treated group exhibited a significantly reduced bacterial count (p < 0.001), indicating that F5 effectively activates complement and lyses Brucella (Fig. 4A and B). This functional disparity may arise from differences in the spatial conformation of the epitopes recognized by D3 and F5. Other factors, such as epitope accessibility, antigen-binding valency, and antibody affinity, may also influence the complement activation threshold. OMP16 monoclonal antibodies mediate opsonophagocytosis via the complement system

To investigate whether the OMP16-specific IgM monoclonal antibodies D3 and F5 could enhance macrophage-mediated clearance of Brucella abortus A19 via complement-dependent mechanisms, an in vitro cell infection model was established. The results revealed a clear functional divergence. Phagocytosis was significantly enhanced only when macrophages were treated with both the D3 antibody and active complement serum (p < 0.001). In contrast, no significant phagocytic increase occurred with D3 in heat-inactivated serum, or with F5 under either active or inactivated complement conditions (p > 0.05), compared to PBS or isotype controls (Fig. 5A, B). This indicates that D3 enhances phagocytosis in a strictly complement-dependent manner, likely through classical pathway activation and C3b opsonization. However, in intracellular killing assays, both D3 and F5 significantly improved the macrophage capacity to kill internalized Brucella in the presence of active complement (p < 0.001) (Fig. 5C). The distinct roles of D3 (phagocytosis and killing) and F5 (killing only) underscore the functional complexity of IgM-mediated immunity against intracellular bacteria, offering new insights into the role of IgM in anti-infective responses.

Protective efficacy of OMP16 monoclonal antibodies in mice

Although in vitro assays confirmed that the D3 and F5 monoclonal antibodies mediate bacteriolysis and macrophage opsonization via complement-dependent pathways, their protective efficacy required evaluation within a complete immune system. We therefore established a Brucella abortus A19 mouse infection model to simulate passive immunization and assess the in vivo protective capacity of both IgM antibodies. Compared to the PBS and IgM isotype control groups, administration of either D3 or F5 significantly alleviated systemic symptoms of infection. Discussion

Brucellosis poses significant therapeutic challenges due to the intracellular nature of the pathogen, which limits antibiotic efficacy and increases relapse risk. These limitations underscore the urgent need for novel immunotherapeutic strategies [17, 18]. While IgG antibodies are often favored for therapeutic development owing to their diverse effector functions—such as antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity and opsonophagocytosis—and extended half-life, IgM has been traditionally viewed as an early-response antibody [19, 20]. However, the pentameric structure of IgM confers superior complement activation capacity. This potent activation directly mediates bacterial lysis via membrane attack complex formation and enhances opsonophagocytosis and intracellular killing by macrophages through complement receptors, collectively constituting a dual mechanism highly effective against intracellular pathogens such as Brucella. This study evaluates the immunoprotective potential of IgM by generating monoclonal antibodies targeting the key outer membrane protein OMP16 of Brucella and systematically characterizing their epitopes and functions in vitro and in vivo, offering new perspectives for brucellosis control.

To generate Brucella OMP16-specific monoclonal antibodies, we produced two IgM-κ antibodies (D3 and F5) using hybridoma technology. Fine peptide truncation scanning mapped their linear epitopes to 77TLSKQAQW84 (D3) and 120RDFLASRG127 (F5). Both epitopes reside in accessible extracellular regions of OMP16 and are highly conserved across major Brucella species, supporting broad-spectrum protective potential. Integrated computational simulations (e.g., molecular docking, alanine scanning) and experimental validation (dot blot) identified key binding residues (A79, A82, A83 for D3; A120, A121, A122 for F5) that critically govern antigen–antibody specificity, affinity, and downstream immune effector functions [21, 22].

Based on the characterization of the two monoclonal antibodies’ sequence structures and epitope profiles, we further evaluated their immunotherapeutic potential. IgM is recognized as the most efficient complement activator among antibody isotypes due to its multivalent structure. Complement-dependent bacteriolysis assays confirmed this but revealed a key divergence: the F5 antibody mediated strong complement-dependent bacterial lysis, whereas D3 showed minimal activity. This functional difference may arise from distinct spatial conformations of their respective epitopes [23, 24]. The F5 epitope (120RDFLASRG127) may facilitate the formation of an antigen–antibody complex that efficiently recruits and activates C1q, thereby triggering the classical complement pathway and direct bacterial lysis [25]. These findings highlight that epitope selection critically influences effector function potency in therapeutic antibody design.

Unlike IgG, IgM does not engage Fcγ receptors on macrophages. Its bona fide high-affinity Fc receptor, FcμR, is expressed mainly on murine B cells and has minimal expression on murine macrophages [9, 26]. Instead, IgM exerts effector functions largely through potent complement activation. In this study, the D3 antibody significantly enhanced macrophage phagocytosis of Brucella abortus A19 in the presence of active complement. This enhancement is likely mediated by deposition of complement opsonins such as C3b on antibody-coated bacteria, which facilitates recognition and uptake via macrophage complement receptors [25, 27]. Although the F5 antibody also potently activated complement, it did not significantly increase initial bacterial uptake. Nevertheless, both D3 and F5 markedly enhanced intracellular killing of Brucella in a strictly complement-dependent manner, as heat inactivation of complement abolished the effect.

The distinct phenotype of the F5—enhanced killing without increased phagocytosis—points to a potential mechanism. We propose that robust complement activation by F5, demonstrated via direct bacteriolysis, likely produces anaphylatoxins C3a and C5a [28]. C5a signaling through macrophage C5aR1 can induce intracellular antimicrobial programs such as ROS production and phagolysosomal maturation, potentially enhancing killing independently of phagocytic uptake [29, 30]. Additionally, sublytic deposition of the membrane attack complex (MAC) on bacteria may alter membrane permeability, making internalized bacteria more susceptible to intracellular killing [31, 32]. This hypothesis aligns with our data and published work but requires direct validation through further analysis of complement activation products and macrophage activation states.

Based on the in vitro immunoprotective profiles of the two monoclonal antibodies, we established a mouse infection model to evaluate the protective efficacy of D3 and F5 in passive immunization. Intraperitoneal administration of either antibody significantly alleviated infection-induced weight loss and splenomegaly, while persistently reducing bacterial load in the spleen during both early and late stages of infection. These results align with in vitro evidence of complement-mediated bacteriolysis and enhanced macrophage killing. As a macrophage-rich immune organ, the spleen represents a key site where the complement-macrophage pathway clears pathogens [33, 34]. The successful in vivo protection demonstrates the physiological relevance and therapeutic potential of this IgM-driven mechanism, which relies on complement activation rather than Fcγ receptors, even against intracellular pathogens such as Brucella.

Conclusions

This study systematically mapped the linear epitopes and key binding residues of OMP16-specific IgM monoclonal antibodies D3 and F5, which are highly conserved across major Brucella species. Functionally, both antibodies activated the complement system, directly lysing bacteria and enhancing macrophage phagocytosis and intracellular killing—a dual mechanism particularly advantageous against intracellular pathogens. Unlike reported OMP16-specific IgG antibodies that rely on FcγR-mediated phagocytosis and NK cell-dependent ADCC [35], IgM leverages its pentameric structure to potently activate complement. This complement-driven strategy overcomes the cell-mediated limitations of IgG. In summary, this work provides promising epitope candidates and antibody molecules for brucellosis vaccine and therapeutic development, while offering a conceptual framework for targeting other intracellular pathogens.

Limitations and future perspectives

This study has several limitations. First, although our in vitro data demonstrate a compelling complement-dependent mechanism, we did not assess direct in vivo correlates—such as levels of complement activation fragments or the specific recruitment and activation state of splenic phagocytes. Thus, the mechanistic link between the observed systemic protection and the proposed complement-mediated opsonophagocytosis and killing requires validation in an infected host. Second, while the targeted OMP16 epitopes are highly conserved, the protective efficacy of the D3 and F5 antibodies was evaluated only against Brucella abortus A19. Their breadth of protection across other clinically relevant Brucella species and strains remains to be tested. Future work should prioritize establishing the in vivo mechanism. Subsequently, evaluating these antibodies against a panel of diverse Brucella isolates will be essential to fully assess their translational potential.

Methods

Bacterial strains, cells, and experimental animals

The Brucella abortus A19 strain was provided by the Shaanxi Veterinary Drug Inspection Institute and was cultured in Tryptic Soy Broth (TSB) with shaking or on Tryptic Soy Agar (TSA) plates statically at 37 °C under 5% CO2. Escherichia coli BL21(DE3) and DH5α competent cells (TIANGEN Biotech, China) were used for recombinant protein expression and were grown in LB broth or on LA plates at 37 °C. The murine monocyte-macrophage cell line RAW264.7, maintained in our laboratory, was cultured in high-glucose DMEM (L110KJ, BasalMedia Technologies, China) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (ZETA LIFE, USA) at 37 °C with 5% CO2. Female BALB/c mice (6–8 weeks old, specific pathogen-free) were purchased from Chengdu DOSSY Experimental Animal Co., Ltd. and acclimatized for at least 1 week in an SPF facility with ad libitum access to food and water under a 12-h light/dark cycle. All experiments involving live Brucella were conducted in compliant Biological Safety Laboratories. All animal studies were reviewed and approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Northwest A&F University (Approval No. IACUC-2025–1230), and all procedures were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations.

Preparation and analysis of mouse-derived OMP16 monoclonal antibodies

To generate specific monoclonal antibodies targeting the Brucella OMP16 protein, the classical hybridoma technique was employed. High-purity recombinant His/GST-OMP16 protein for monoclonal antibody production was generated using a prokaryotic expression system [36]. Six-to-eight-week-old female BALB/c mice were immunized with recombinant His-OMP16 protein. Splenocytes were aseptically isolated and fused with mouse SP2/0 myeloma cells using 50% PEG-4000 (Solarbio, China). The fused cells were then cultured in HAT (S912JV, BasalMedia Technologies, China) selection medium at 37 °C under 5% CO2. After 10–14 days, culture supernatants were screened by indirect ELISA with GST-OMP16 as the coating antigen. Positive clones were subcloned at least three times by limiting dilution, yielding two stable monoclonal hybridoma lines, D3 and F5, which consistently secreted OMP16-specific antibodies. Antibody isotyping was performed using a commercial mouse monoclonal antibody isotyping kit (Proteintech, China). To confirm specificity, indirect ELISA was conducted against a panel of antigens—His-tag, GST-tag, His-OMP16, GST-OMP16, and whole Brucella abortus A19 lysate—using D3 or F5 as the primary antibody.

Protein expression and peptide synthesis

For precise epitope characterization, a series of overlapping truncated OMP16 peptides were designed and expressed. The extracellular region of OMP16 was first divided into three overlapping fragments (P1–P3), which were cloned into a pGEX-4T-1 vector and expressed as GST fusion proteins. Based on initial screening, the reactive region was further subdivided into shorter overlapping peptides (P4–P9). All peptides were purified by GSH-affinity chromatography (GenScript Biotech, China), and their reactivity with the D3 and F5 antibodies was confirmed by Western blot.

To identify key residues for antigen–antibody interaction, peptides corresponding to the wild-type D3 (77TLSKQAQW84) and F5 (120RDFLASRG127) epitopes, as well as alanine-substitution mutants, were synthesized by GenScript Biotech (Nanjing, China) (purity > 95%). Mutant peptides were designed based on integrated results from molecular docking, alanine-scanning, and saturation mutagenesis analyses. Specifically, residues A79 and A83 within the D3 epitope were each substituted with alanine. In light of structural modeling and saturation mutagenesis data predicting a critical role for the A82 (Alanine) side chain, it was substituted with glutamic acid. Within the F5 epitope, residues A120, A121, and A122 were each replaced with alanine. These peptides were subsequently used in dot-blot assays to validate the functional importance of the identified residues for antibody binding.

Antibody variable region sequencing and protein three-dimensional structure modeling

To elucidate the molecular recognition characteristics of the D3 and F5 monoclonal antibodies, their variable region sequences were first determined. Total RNA was extracted from hybridoma cell lines cultured to the logarithmic growth phase. cDNA was synthesized using a RACE amplification kit (Takara Bio, Japan), and the variable region gene fragments of the heavy and light chains were amplified by nested PCR. The purified PCR products were ligated into the pMD19-T vector and transformed into E. coli DH5α competent cells. Positive clones were selected for Sanger bidirectional sequencing, and the resulting sequences were aligned and analyzed using the online tool AbSeek to accurately identify the complementarity-determining regions (CDRs) and framework regions.

Based on these sequences, three-dimensional structural models of the antigen–antibody complexes were constructed using deep learning models. The structure of the OMP16 protein (lacking the signal peptide and transmembrane regions) was predicted with AlphaFold 2.0, a deep learning system that integrates co-evolutionary analysis and physical constraints; the resulting model showed high reliability (pLDDT > 90). In parallel, the amino acid sequences of the D3 and F5 variable regions were input into the antibody-specific modeling platform ABodyBuilder2 to generate accurate three-dimensional structures of the Fv fragments.

Molecular docking and alanine-scanning mutagenesis analysis

To elucidate antigen–antibody interactions, molecular docking and energy analyses were performed. Using the pre-constructed three-dimensional structures of the OMP16 protein and the fragment variable regions (Fv) of the D3 and F5 antibodies, rigid molecular docking was conducted with ZDOCK 3.0.2. Approximately 104 binding poses were systematically sampled and evaluated, from which the top 10 highest-scoring complexes were selected. These complexes were then refined using the RosettaDock suite through side-chain conformational sampling and rigid-body fine-tuning to optimize interface complementarity. The model with the lowest calculated binding free energy was selected as the final representative structure for detailed interface analysis.

To quantify the contribution of individual epitope residues to antibody binding, alanine-scanning mutagenesis was employed. The change in binding free energy (ΔΔG) upon mutation of each residue to alanine was calculated using the Molecular Mechanics-Poisson Boltzmann Surface Area (MM-PBSA) method. Residues with a ΔΔG > 1.0 kcal/mol were defined as binding hot spots, indicating their significant role in stabilizing the interaction.

Western blot and dot blot analysis

To precisely characterize the antigen-recognition properties and perform fine epitope mapping of the D3 and F5 monoclonal antibodies, we used Western blot and dot blot analyses. For Western blot, GST-tagged truncated OMP16 peptides (P1–P3) were separated by electrophoresis on 12.5% SDS-PAGE gels and transferred onto PVDF membranes. The membranes were then incubated with either D3 or F5 monoclonal antibody, followed by an HRP-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgM secondary antibody (ImmunoReagents, USA). Signals were detected using ECL chemiluminescence (Cwbiotech, China). Based on initial screening results, positive regions were further mapped using shorter overlapping peptides (P4–P9) via the same Western blot protocol to identify minimal linear epitopes.

To validate key binding residues predicted by molecular docking, dot-blot assays were performed. Synthetic wild-type and mutant epitope peptides (5 μg per spot) were spotted onto nitrocellulose membranes. Subsequent antibody incubation and detection steps matched those of the Western blot. The contribution of each residue to antibody-binding affinity was evaluated by comparing signal intensity changes between wild-type and mutant peptides.

Complement-dependent bacteriolysis assay

To evaluate the ability of OMP16-specific IgM monoclonal antibodies to activate the classical complement pathway and mediate bacterial lysis, an in vitro complement-dependent bacteriolysis assay was established [37, 38]. Briefly, Brucella abortus A19 in the exponential growth phase was resuspended to 103 CFU/mL in PBS. A 100 µL aliquot was incubated with 40 µg of the respective monoclonal antibody (D3 or F5) at 37 °C for 30 min; PBS and a non-targeting mouse IgM isotype control antibody (clone MM-30, Abcam, UK) served as negative controls. Then, 10% fresh guinea pig serum (as a complement source) was added, and incubation continued at 37 °C for 60 min. The reaction mixture was serially diluted tenfold, and 100 µL of each dilution was spread on Tryptic Soy Agar (TSA) plates. Plates were incubated inverted at 37 °C for 72 h before colony-forming units (CFUs) were counted. For direct visualization of bacteriolysis, a spot assay was also performed on a subset of the bacterial suspension.

Complement-dependent opsonophagocytosis and intracellular killing assay

To evaluate the ability of IgM monoclonal antibodies to enhance macrophage-mediated immunity via the complement system, a macrophage infection model was established to assess opsonophagocytosis and intracellular killing [39]. Briefly, the murine macrophage cell line RAW264.7 was seeded in 24-well plates at 2 × 105 cells per well and cultured overnight at 37 °C with 5% CO2 to allow adhesion. Brucella abortus A19 (log-phase) was pre-incubated for 30 min at 37 °C with 40 μg/mL of D3 or F5 monoclonal antibody, a non-targeting mouse IgM isotype control antibody, or PBS to form immune complexes. These complexes were added to macrophages at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 100, together with 10% fresh guinea pig serum (active complement) or heat-inactivated serum (complement-negative control). After 2 h of infection, the supernatant was removed, and extracellular bacteria were eliminated by incubating cells with 50 μg/mL gentamicin for 1 h, followed by three gentle washes with cold PBS. To evaluate phagocytosis, a subset of cells was lysed with 0.1% Triton X-100, and lysates were serially diluted, plated on TSA, and incubated at 37 °C for 72 h before colony-forming units (CFUs) were counted to determine initial bacterial uptake. To assess intracellular killing, another subset of cells was maintained for 48 h in medium containing 25 μg/mL gentamicin before lysis and CFU enumeration. The macrophage killing index was calculated as: [(initial bacterial count − 48-h bacterial count) / initial bacterial count] × 100%.

Mouse infection model and in vivo protective efficacy evaluation

To evaluate the immunoprotective effects of D3 and F5 monoclonal antibodies against Brucella infection in vivo, a passive immunization mouse model was established [38]. Six-week-old specific pathogen-free (SPF) female BALB/c mice (n = 6 per group) received an intraperitoneal injection of 80 μg D3 or F5 monoclonal antibody 24 h before infection, while control groups were administered PBS or an IgM isotype antibody. On the day of infection, all mice were challenged intraperitoneally with 5 × 107 CFU of Brucella abortus A19 to induce acute infection. The mice were monitored daily for 21 days, and body weight changes were recorded to evaluate systemic physiological status. On days 3, 7, 14, and 21 post-infection, mice were euthanized, and spleens were aseptically collected and weighed to calculate the spleen index (spleen weight/body weight ratio × 100). Splenic tissues were homogenized, serially diluted, and plated on TSA plates. After 72 h of incubation at 37 °C, colony-forming units (CFUs) were counted to quantify the bacterial load. For visual assessment of bacterial burden, part of the spleen was fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned. The sections were subjected to immunofluorescence staining using a polyclonal antibody against Brucella as the primary antibody and an Alexa Fluor 555-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG (Beyotime, China) as the secondary antibody, with DAPI for nuclear counterstaining. Bacterial distribution and load were qualitatively analyzed by confocal microscopy.

Statistical analysis of data

All quantitative experiments were independently repeated at least three times, and data are presented as mean ± standard deviation. Statistical analyses were performed with SPSS 25.0 software. Multiple-group comparisons were conducted using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA); where significant differences were detected, pairwise comparisons were made with Tukey’s post hoc test. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05, with asterisks in figures denoting the following levels: *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, and ***p < 0.001. All graphs were created using GraphPad Prism 9.0 software for clear visualization of the statistical results.
