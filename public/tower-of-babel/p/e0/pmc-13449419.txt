Early outcomes of four-layer compression therapy in chronic venous ulcers: A 2-year prospective study in resource-limited setting
Authors: Kajan Raj Shrestha, Ujma Shrestha
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundVenous leg ulcers represent the most advanced stage of chronic venous insufficiency and are associated with significant morbidity and reduced quality of life. Compression therapy is the cornerstone of management, with four-layer compression dressing widely regarded as the standard of care. However, evidence regarding its effectiveness in resource-limited settings remains limited. This study aimed to evaluate the early clinical outcomes of four-layer compression therapy in patients with venous leg ulcers.MethodsThis prospective observational study included 39 patients treated with four-layer compression therapy over a 2-year period. Baseline demographics, ulcer characteristics, and comorbidities were recorded. Ulcer size, exudate, pain score, and Venous Clinical Severity Score were assessed at baseline and follow-up. The primary outcome was reduction in ulcer area at 4 weeks, whereas secondary outcomes included time to healing, symptom improvement, and complications.ResultsThe mean age was 44 ± 10.55 years, with a male predominance (1.29:1). A previous history of deep vein thrombosis was documented in 92.3% of patients, which was consistent with ultrasound finding of chronic deep vein thrombosis. The mean baseline ulcer area was 16.88 ± 14.65 cm2, with a duration of 5.6 ± 2.6 months. At 4 weeks, ulcer area reduced by 91 ± 5.66%, leaving a residual area of approximately 1.5 cm2. Mean healing time was 4 ± 2 weeks. Venous Clinical Severity Score improved significantly from 25 ± 4.55 to 16 ± 3.88 (P < .001). Exudate and pain scores improved by 90% and 95%, respectively. Minor complications included maceration, dermatitis, and infection.ConclusionsFour-layer compression therapy demonstrated excellent early healing and clinical improvement, supporting its effectiveness in resource-limited settings.

Venous leg ulcers represent the most advanced manifestation of chronic venous insufficiency and constitute a significant health care burden worldwide.1,2 They account for approximately 70% to 80% of chronic lower limb ulcers and are associated with prolonged morbidity, reduced quality of life, and substantial health care costs.2,3 The pathophysiology involves sustained venous hypertension resulting from venous reflux, obstruction, or a combination of both, which ultimately leads to microcirculatory changes, tissue inflammation, and ulcer formation.3

One of the major etiological factors contributing to venous ulceration is deep vein thrombosis (DVT), which may lead to post-thrombotic syndrome and chronic venous hypertension.4,5 Patients with venous ulcers often present with pain, exudation, and recurrent infections, making effective and timely treatment essential. The management of venous ulcers requires a comprehensive multidisciplinary approach including wound care, infection control, compression therapy, limb elevation, offloading, smoking cessation, nutritional optimization, weight control, and correction of underlying venous pathology.6,7

Compression therapy is widely recognized as the cornerstone of venous ulcer management because it reduces venous hypertension, improves venous return, and enhances microcirculatory function. Among the various compression modalities available, four-layer compression bandaging has been shown to provide sustained graduated compression and is considered one of the most effective systems for promoting ulcer healing. Several clinical trials and systematic reviews have demonstrated improved healing rates with multilayer compression compared with single-layer or noncompression dressings.7, 8, 9

The severity and progression of venous disease are often assessed using the Venous Clinical Severity Score (VCSS), which evaluates clinical features such as pain, edema, ulcer characteristics, and need for compression therapy. Improvement in this score has been used as an important indicator of clinical response to treatment.9,10

Despite the proven effectiveness of compression therapy, outcomes may vary depending on patient compliance, ulcer characteristics, and the presence of underlying venous pathology. In many developing countries, including Nepal, limited access to specialized wound care services and delayed presentation often result in prolonged ulcer duration and increased complications. Furthermore, there is a paucity of local data evaluating the clinical outcomes of multilayer compression therapy in venous ulcers.

Therefore, the present study aimed to evaluate the early clinical outcomes of four-layer compression dressing in patients with venous ulcers, focusing on ulcer size reduction, improvement in clinical severity, and treatment-related complications.

Methods

This was a prospective observational study conducted over a 2-year period (January 2024 to December 2025) at a tertiary care vascular surgery center in Kathmandu, Nepal. Written informed consent was taken from all the participants at the time of application of four-layer bandage, including permission for the use of anonymized clinical data for research and publication. This study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki and its later amendments, and written approval was obtained from the Institutional Review Committee of Everest Hospital. The study aimed to evaluate the early outcomes of four-layer compression dressing in patients presenting with venous leg ulcers. Patients presenting with clinically diagnosed venous leg ulcers were assessed for eligibility. A total of 39 patients who met the inclusion criteria were enrolled in the study.

Inclusion criteria

Patients were included if they met the following criteria:•Age ≥ 18 years•Presence of active venous leg ulcer•Ulcer consistent with chronic venous disease based on clinical assessment•Patients willing to undergo four-layer compression therapy and follow-up

At enrollment, demographic characteristics, occupational history, smoking status, and comorbidities were recorded. Particular attention was given to previous episodes of DVT, which may contribute to post-thrombotic venous hypertension.

Ulcer characteristics documented included ulcer duration, location, ulcer area (cm2), ulcer depth, presence of infection, exudate level, and pain score using a visual analog scale. Disease severity was assessed using the VCSS at baseline and during follow-up.

All patients received four-layer compression dressing, applied according to the standard multilayer compression technique. The four-layer compression system consisted of:(1)padding layer,(2)support bandage,(3)elastic compression layer, and(4)cohesive outer bandage, applied to achieve graduated compression of approximately 28 to 35 mmHg at the ankle.

Dressings were changed periodically based on wound condition and exudate level. Patients with mixed arteriovenous ulcers were included only if ankle-brachial index was ≥0.8, and compression was modified accordingly.

All patients received local wound care, antibiotics for 2 weeks in cases with suspected infection and debridement when required. Selected patients underwent adjunct venous procedures, including perforator ligation, venoplasty, or venous stenting based on clinical and imaging findings. Compliance was assessed at each follow-up visit based on the patient-reported daily use, dressing integrity, missed appointments, and clinician evaluation. After ulcer healing, patients were advised to continue maintenance compression therapy using class II compression stockings along with limb elevation and lifestyle modification. The primary outcome was percentage reduction in ulcer area at 4 weeks.

Secondary outcomes included time-to-wound healing, reduction in exudate, reduction in pain score, improvement in VCSS and treatment-related complications. Patients were followed for 3 to 6 months after healing to assess recurrence.

Data were analyzed using descriptive statistics. Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation, whereas categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages. Changes in clinical parameters such as ulcer size and VCSS before and after treatment were compared using the paired t-test. A P value < .001 was considered statistically significant.

Results

A total of 39 patients with venous ulcers were included in the study over a 2-year period (January 2024 to December 2025). The age ranged from 20 to 65 years, with a mean age of 44 ± 10.55 years. There were 22 males and 17 females, giving a male-to-female ratio of 1.29:1. Regarding occupation, foreign employment (n = 15) and housewives (n = 13) constituted the majority of patients, followed by farmers (n = 6) and students (n = 4). Most patients were overweight, with 28 patients (71.8%) having a body mass index between 25 and 30 kg/m2, and most of them have never smoked till date (n = 20; 51.28%).

Discussion

Venous leg ulcers represent the most advanced clinical manifestation of chronic venous insufficiency and arise primarily due to sustained venous hypertension, valvular incompetence, and microcirculatory dysfunction.2, 3, 4 These pathological changes lead to chronic inflammation, tissue hypoxia, and progressive skin breakdown in the lower extremities. Despite advances in venous disease management, venous ulcers remain a major cause of morbidity and health care burden worldwide. The present study evaluated the clinical characteristics and treatment outcomes of venous leg ulcers managed with multilayer compression therapy, and the findings are largely consistent with previously reported epidemiological and therapeutic data.5, 6, 7, 8

The demographic profile of patients in this study revealed a mean age of 44 ± 10.55 years, with a slight male predominance (male-to-female ratio 1.29:1). Previous epidemiological studies have generally reported that venous leg ulcers are more prevalent among elderly populations. For instance, Nelzen et al11,12 demonstrated that chronic leg ulcers occur predominantly in older individuals receiving primary health care services. Similarly, Baker et al4,5 reported that venous ulceration represents a significant health problem among older adults due to progressive deterioration of venous valve function and reduced mobility. In contrast, the relatively younger age distribution observed in our cohort may reflect regional socioeconomic and occupational factors in Nepal, where a large proportion of the working population is engaged in physically demanding activities that involve prolonged standing or heavy labor.

Occupational distribution in the present study showed that foreign workers and housewives constituted the majority of affected individuals. These findings highlight the role of occupational and lifestyle factors in the development of chronic venous disease. Prolonged standing, reduced opportunities for limb elevation, and increased hydrostatic venous pressure contribute to venous hypertension and eventual ulcer formation.13, 14, 15 Similar associations between occupational strain and venous ulceration have been described by Abbade and Lastoria,1 who emphasized that occupational activities involving prolonged standing significantly increase the risk of chronic venous insufficiency.

Another important risk factor identified in the present study was overweight status, with 71.8% of patients having a body mass index between 25 and 30 kg/m2. Obesity has been widely recognized as an important contributor to chronic venous disease. Increased body weight leads to elevated intra-abdominal pressure and impaired calf muscle pump function, which subsequently increases venous pressure in the lower limbs. These mechanisms contribute to venous reflux and the development of venous ulceration.16,17 Similar findings have been reported by Baker et al4,5 in epidemiological investigations of chronic venous ulcers.

A particularly notable observation in this study was the high prevalence of previous DVT (92.31%) among patients. Post-thrombotic syndrome remains one of the most important causes of chronic venous insufficiency. Damage to venous valves and persistent venous obstruction following thrombosis led to chronic venous hypertension and impaired venous return.14,15 The pathophysiological importance of venous reflux and obstruction in venous ulcer development has been extensively described in consensus guidelines by Nicolaides et al,16,17 who highlighted that chronic venous hypertension ultimately results in microcirculatory dysfunction and tissue injury.

The mean duration of ulcer before presentation was 5.6 ± 2.6 months, indicating delayed presentation to specialized care. Such delays are common in venous ulcer management and contribute to increased morbidity and health care costs.14,16 Studies by London and Donnelly18 have similarly reported that venous ulcers frequently persist for extended periods before effective treatment is initiated.

Consistent with classical descriptions of venous ulceration, the gaiter region was the most common site of ulceration, accounting for 61.54% of cases in the present study. The gaiter area around the medial malleolus is particularly susceptible to ulcer formation due to increased hydrostatic pressure and microvascular changes associated with chronic venous insufficiency. These findings correspond with previously reported epidemiological patterns of venous ulcer distribution.12,14,16

The clinical characteristics of ulcers in this cohort also reflected typical features of venous ulceration. The mean ulcer size was 16.88 ± 14.65 cm2, and infection was present in 84.62% of patients. Large ulcer size and prolonged ulcer duration are recognized predictors of delayed healing. Prognostic studies conducted by Phillips et al19,20 have demonstrated that ulcer size and duration are among the most important determinants of healing outcomes in venous ulcer disease.

Compression therapy remains the cornerstone of venous ulcer management because it directly addresses the underlying pathophysiological mechanism of venous hypertension. By improving venous return, reducing edema, and enhancing microcirculatory perfusion, compression therapy significantly accelerates ulcer healing.21,22 Evidence supporting the effectiveness of compression therapy is well established. Systematic reviews by Cullum et al23 have shown that compression therapy significantly increases healing rates compared with noncompression treatment. Furthermore, meta-analysis conducted by O’Meara et al24 demonstrated that multilayer compression systems, particularly four-layer bandaging, are more effective than single-layer compression systems in promoting ulcer healing.

In the present study, patients received multilayer compression therapy with an average of 5 ± 2 dressing applications, and high compliance was observed in 94.8% of patients. Patient adherence is a critical determinant of treatment success. Studies by Van Hecke et al15 have emphasized that poor adherence to compression therapy is one of the most significant barriers to ulcer healing and contributes substantially to recurrence. Similarly, Annells et al9 highlighted the importance of patient willingness and cooperation in achieving optimal outcomes with compression therapy.

Adjunctive procedures were required in a small number of patients in this study, including debridement, varicose vein surgery, perforator ligation, venoplasty, and venous stenting. These interventions address underlying venous reflux or obstruction that contributes to persistent venous hypertension. Current guidelines for the management of chronic venous disease, as described by Nicolaides et al,16,17 recommend correction of venous pathology in selected patients to improve ulcer healing and reduce recurrence.

The clinical outcomes observed in this study were encouraging. Ulcer area decreased by 91 ± 5.66% within 4 weeks, and the mean time to complete healing was 4 ± 2 weeks. In addition, significant improvements were observed in clinical symptoms. Exudative fluid production decreased by approximately 90%, and pain reduction was reported in 95% of patients. These improvements reflect the beneficial physiological effects of compression therapy in reducing edema and improving venous hemodynamics.7 Studies by Veraart and Neumann8 have demonstrated similar benefits of compression therapy in reducing lower limb edema and improving interface pressure.

Disease severity also improved significantly in this study as measured by the VCSS. The mean VCSS decreased from 25 ± 4.55 at baseline to 16 ± 3.88 after treatment, representing a statistically significant improvement (P < .001). Improvement in VCSS reflects not only ulcer healing, but also reduction in associated symptoms such as edema, pain, and inflammation.

The results of this study are consistent with findings from several landmark trials in venous ulcer management. The Effect of Surgery and Compression on Healing And Recurrence (ESCHAR) trial demonstrated that compression therapy is highly effective in promoting ulcer healing, whereas the addition of venous surgery significantly reduces recurrence rates.25 Similarly, a randomized controlled trial conducted by Moffatt et al26 established the four-layer compression bandage system as the gold standard treatment for venous ulcers, demonstrating healing rates of approximately 70% to 80% at 12 weeks.

In addition, the Cochrane systematic review conducted by Nelson et al23,24,27 concluded that multilayer compression systems are significantly more effective than single-component compression therapy in promoting ulcer healing. The favorable outcomes observed in the present study, including rapid ulcer size reduction and significant clinical improvement, support these conclusions.

Complications related to treatment were minimal in this study. Minor complications occurred in 20.5% of patients, with maceration being the most common. These complications were mild and easily managed with appropriate wound care adjustments. Importantly, no ulcer recurrence was observed during the 3 to 6 month follow-up period. Recurrence remains a major challenge in venous ulcer management, as emphasized by Vowden and Vowden,3 who reported high recurrence rates in long-term follow-up studies.

Overall, the findings of the present study align with existing evidence from randomized trials and systematic reviews. The rapid healing outcomes observed in this cohort may be attributed to high patient compliance, early wound care, appropriate antibiotic therapy, and the use of effective multilayer compression therapy.7,16,24,28

Conclusions

The present study demonstrates that four-layer compression therapy is an effective, practical, and affordable treatment modality for venous leg ulcers, resulting in rapid ulcer healing, symptomatic improvement, and reduction in disease severity scores. The favorable outcomes observed were likely influenced by the high level of patient compliance.

Our findings are consistent with previous major clinical trials and systematic reviews supporting multilayer compression therapy as the cornerstone of venous ulcer management. Importantly, this study highlights the feasibility of implementing standardized four-layer compression therapy in a resource-limited health care setting such as Nepal. Patients in low- and middle-income countries often face delayed presentation, financial constraints, geographic barriers, occupational demands, and limited access to specialized wound care services. Despite these challenges, satisfactory healing outcomes were achieved. Four-layer compression therapy therefore represents a simple, cost-effective, and clinically effective strategy for venous ulcer management in low-resource settings. Further large-scale studies with longer follow-up are recommended.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the sample size was relatively small, which may limit the generalizability of the findings. Second, the study was conducted at a single tertiary care center, which may introduce institutional bias and may not reflect outcomes in other health care settings.

Third, the follow-up duration was limited to 3 to 6 months, which may not adequately capture long-term recurrence rates of venous ulcers. Long-term follow-up studies are required to assess durability of healing after compression therapy.

In addition, although most patients had a history of DVT, the study did not stratify outcomes based on the severity of post-thrombotic disease or duplex ultrasound findings, which may influence healing rates.

Finally, the absence of a control group treated with alternative compression modalities limits the ability to directly compare the effectiveness of four-layer compression with other treatment strategies.

Despite these limitations, the study provides valuable clinical data regarding the early effectiveness of four-layer compression therapy for venous ulcers in a resource-limited setting.
