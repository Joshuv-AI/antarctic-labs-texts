Association between medical school expectations, professional fulfillment, and mental health among medical students: a mixed-methods study
Authors: Cristiana NP Araujo, Adia Gomes, Lisa J Merlo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis mixed-method study aimed: (1) to determine associations between medical student training expectations, satisfaction with school-based wellness resources, self-reported wellbeing, professional fulfillment, and commitment to their career choice, and (2) to explore medical students’ perspectives regarding the ways medical schools could better support student wellbeing.MethodsMedical students (N = 715; 60.1% female) from seven Florida medical schools completed an anonymous online survey. Responses were analyzed descriptively and qualitatively.ResultsMost students reported a decline in mental health since beginning medical school and indicated experiencing at least one burnout symptom via the Single-Item Burnout Measure. Having unmet training expectations was associated with worsened mental health (ρ = 0.465; p < .001) and increased burnout (ρ = − 0.434; p < .001). Students reporting unmet training expectations (ρ = 0.482; p < .001), lower professional fulfillment (ρ = 0.505; p < .001), worsening mental health (ρ = 0.450; p < .001), and more burnout (ρ = − 0.428; p < .001) also reported lower likelihood of re-choosing a career in medicine. Qualitative analysis demonstrated five key targets to enhance student wellbeing: (1) Social, emotional, and physical support; (2) Scheduling; (3) Academic support; (4) Finances; and (5) Organizational culture.ConclusionsMedical schools should prepare incoming students for the realities of medical training and promote dialogue between different stakeholders when developing or reevaluating wellbeing initiatives to enhance the culture of wellness within the learning environment.Clinical trial registrationNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-026-08824-5.Keywords: Wellness curriculum, Health promotion, Medical education, Professional fulfillment, Student wellbeing

Background

Medical school is a challenging endeavor with significant cognitive and emotional stressors [1]. Many students report physical and mental health decline during their medical education [2–4], with worse physical, emotional, and overall health at the end of their first year of school compared to the time of matriculation [2]. In addition, medical students report higher levels of perceived stress, anxiety, and depression than age-matched peers [3], with burnout, depression, and fatigue increasing during medical school [5–7]. Poor mental health, including depression, burnout, comorbid mental illness, and stress, may put medical students at risk for suicidal ideation and suicide attempts [8].

Importantly, the challenges of medical education often become apparent early in training, with burnout reported among undergraduate premedical students [9, 10] in addition to first year medical students [11]. Burnout, which is characterized by emotional exhaustion, depersonalization (or cynicism), and a sense of low personal accomplishment [12], can lead to reduced empathy [13, 14], compromised quality of patient care, and increased risk of medical errors [15, 16]. Research has demonstrated that levels of burnout and distress among medical students exceed those found in the general population or among peers in other educational settings [11, 17–23]. Studies have identified various factors contributing to these issues, including the intense academic workload, lack of work-life balance, the emotional toll of patient care experiences, challenges in studying and time management, relationship conflicts, medical school peer relations, health concerns, and financial stressors [1, 24]. Of concern, recent research has suggested that experiencing depression during medical training is associated with additional depressive episodes later in life [25]. This, coupled with concerns about the impact of poor mental health on patient care, demonstrates the critically important ramifications of adequately supporting psychological wellbeing among physicians-in-training.

Indeed, promoting medical students’ wellbeing is essential for fostering professional fulfillment and career satisfaction [26], and for preventing medical school dropout [27, 28] and early attrition from the workforce, which contribute to the growing shortage in the healthcare workforce [29, 30]. Several strategies have been proposed, including curriculum changes to incorporate wellness programming, stress management workshops, and mentorship programs, among others [1, 31]. Despite these efforts, gaps remain in understanding how the alignment (or misalignment) of students’ expectations versus the realities of medical education contribute to their overall satisfaction, wellbeing, and professional fulfillment [32, 33]. Similarly, there is a scarcity of data documenting how student satisfaction with training and sense of professional fulfillment are associated with their psychological health and wellbeing [24]. To address these gaps, the present study used a mixed-methods approach assessing two primary aims: (1) to examine the associations between medical student training expectations, satisfaction with their school’s wellbeing initiatives, commitment to their career choice, professional fulfillment, and self-reported wellbeing; and (2) to explore medical students’ perspectives regarding the ways medical schools could better support student wellbeing.

Methods

Procedures

All procedures were reviewed and approved as Exempt by the University of Florida Institutional Review Board (#IRB202300151) in accordance with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. The Council of Florida Medical School Deans also approved the study. Administrators (i.e., the Deans or their appointees) from each Florida medical school (N = 10) were asked to share the study invitation email with their students in March 2023, with the specific date of dissemination chosen by each school in accordance with their academic calendar. The anonymous online survey was administered through Qualtrics. Technical difficulties resulted in the survey being closed prematurely, which prevented participation from three of the medical schools and limited the number of students from participating schools who completed the survey, but did not affect already-collected data.

Under the exemption approved by the IRB, the requirement for written consent was waived to ensure participant anonymity. Participants reviewed a Research Information sheet containing all the necessary elements of informed consent, then indicated their agreement to participate before accessing the survey. The survey was primarily comprised of brief validated assessment tools, screeners, and items from commonly-used measures. Additional items were developed by the study team to assess relevant variables of interest. The full survey took approximately 15 min to complete. At the end of the survey, participants followed a link to a different form, hosted on the Research Electronic Data Capture (REDCap) platform [34, 35], to provide an email address at which they could receive a $10 electronic gift card as compensation for their time. REDCap is a secure, web-based software platform designed to support data capture for research studies, providing (1) an intuitive interface for validated data capture; (2) audit trails for tracking data manipulation and export procedures; (3) automated export procedures for seamless data downloads to common statistical packages; and (4) procedures for data integration and interoperability with external sources.

Participants

Eligible participants were current Florida medical students. Inclusion criteria were: current enrollment in one of the 7 medical schools that distributed the survey invitation, and accessing the survey before it closed. Given the large expected N, the sample size requirement was not calculated in advance. A convenience sample of 786 medical students was obtained. Data were excluded from analyses if the participant failed to complete the survey (n = 57) or reported giving ≥ 3 false answers on the survey or did not respond to the item assessing false answers (n = 14). Thus, the final study sample consisted of 715 medical students (n = 541 allopathic students, 75.7%; n = 174 osteopathic students, 24.3%), though response rates varied slightly across survey items due to limited missing data. Although it was not possible to determine precisely how many students received the study invitation email, publicly-available school enrollment data suggest that approximately 20% of the total population of eligible medical students was included in the study. Measures

For the current study, the subset of survey items relating to the specific study aims were utilized. These are detailed below.

Training-related expectations and fulfillment

Three items developed for this study assessed student expectations of and experiences with training using a 5-point Likert-type scale. Items included: “How fulfilling is your professional training?”; “So far, how well has your training experience matched your expectations?”; and “If you knew before applying to professional school what you now know about the training process/experience, how might that have impacted your decision to pursue this profession?”

Satisfaction with wellbeing supports

Two items developed for this study assessed student satisfaction with school-sponsored initiatives to support student wellbeing. The items were rated on a 5-point Likert-type scale, and included: “How satisfied are you with the wellbeing content within your required curriculum?” and “How satisfied are you with the wellbeing programming offered by your school outside the required curriculum?”

Changes in mental health during medical school

The single item drawn from previous research [4], “Since beginning professional school, how has your mental health changed?” was rated on a 7-point Likert-type scale.

Burnout

The Single-Item Burnout Measure [12] was adapted slightly to assess burnout related to medical school.

Open-ended question

At the end of the survey, students provided free responses to the prompt, “If your school could do one thing in the next 6 months to improve student wellbeing, what should it be? Please be as specific as possible.”

Data analysis

All data were analyzed using IBM SPSS version 29.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, Illinois, USA). Descriptive statistics (frequencies) were calculated for all variables. Spearman’s rank correlation coefficients (ρ) were used to examine the associations between variables. A p-value of 0.05 was used to determine statistical significance. Following convention [36], the strength of the relation between variables was interpreted as perfect (ρ = 1.0), strong (ρ = 0.7–0.9), moderate (ρ = 0.4–0.6), weak (ρ = 0.1–0.3), or none (ρ = 0.0). Kruskal-Wallis H tests were conducted to examine differences across medical school years, with Bonferroni correction for pairwise comparisons. Qualitative data were examined using thematic analysis [37]. The students’ responses were coded by the first author (CNPA) using NVivo. The codes were organized into initial themes by CNPA. The first and final authors (CNPA and LJM) refined the themes through discussion. Students’ quotes were selected to illustrate each theme. Some quotes were edited slightly (e.g., typos and grammar mistakes were corrected) to improve readability.

Results

Quantitative results

As seen in Table 2, the overwhelming majority of students (81.4%) reported feeling at least somewhat fulfilled by their medical school training, and most (72.3%) also reported that their training experience met or exceeded their expectations. Almost three-quarters of students reported they probably (34.7%) or definitely (38.4%) would choose the medical profession again if given the option. Despite this, more than half of students (51.6%) reported at least one burnout symptom, and 72.6% indicated that their mental health had worsened since beginning medical school. Of note, almost half of the students (42.3%) reported dissatisfaction with the wellbeing content of their required curriculum, and less than a third (29.9%) were satisfied. In addition, 30.5% reported being dissatisfied with extracurricular programming to support wellbeing, and only 35.4% were satisfied.

Descriptive statistics

As seen in Table 3, reporting that training experiences matched (or exceeded) expectations was associated with higher levels of fulfillment (ρ = 0.460; p <.001) and greater likelihood of choosing a career in medicine if they had the opportunity to make the decision again (ρ = 0.482; p <.001). Feeling fulfilled in medical school was also moderately correlated with the likelihood of choosing a career in medicine again (ρ = 0.505; p <.001). By contrast, reporting that training experiences did not meet expectations was associated with worse mental health since beginning medical school (ρ = 0.465; p <.001) and more symptoms of burnout (ρ = − 0.434; p <.001). Worsened mental health and higher burnout were moderately correlated with a decreased likelihood of choosing a career in medicine again (ρ = 0.450; p <.001, and ρ = − 0.428; p <.001, respectively).

Additional analyses showed statistically significant differences across medical school training years. Training fulfillment varied significantly (H(3) = 15.097, p =.002), with first-year students reporting lower levels than second- and third-year students. Training expectations also differed significantly by year (H(3) = 19.683, p <.001), with first-year students reporting greater unmet training expectations than all other years. Significant differences across training years also emerged related to the likelihood of choosing the same career again (H(3) = 28.949, p <.001), with both first and fourth-year students reporting lower likelihood compared to second- and third-year students. In addition, changes in mental health since beginning medical school were significantly different across year of training (H(3) = 12.283, p <.0060, with third-year students reporting significantly greater decreases in mental health relative to first-year students. By contrast, the difference in burnout levels across years (H(3) = 37.480, p <.001) reflected less burnout among first-year students compared to all other years.

Social, emotional, and physical wellness dimension

Increasing school-sponsored wellness activities

Many student responses reflected a desire for medical schools to enhance programming and resources dedicated to student wellness. For example, several students suggested increasing the number of wellness activities [e.g., “Having wellness events more than one week a year” (P534)] and scheduling wellness-promoting activities rather than didactic wellness sessions [e.g., “Actually have wellbeing events into the curriculum. We only really had zoom lectures for wellbeing, which is a one size fits all generic presentation” (P30)]. Some suggested specific preferred wellness activities, requesting “more group wellness sessions (ex. yoga, meditation, cooking classes, group therapy)” (P34).

Implementing mandatory wellness check-ins

Students also proposed implementing periodic mandatory wellness check-ins to reach struggling students who might not seek help independently. For example, “mandate counseling once a semester to ensure students are happy and feel well, not just that they are academically on track. Or it could be once a year, but most times people need help they are not in a place to reach out” (P149). The mandatory wellness check-ins were also highlighted as a way to facilitate access to care [e.g., “I feel that having mandatory check-ins can be helpful to make sure we are able to speak with someone and allow people to feel more comfortable asking for help” (P359) and “I think that every single medical student would benefit from at least one session with a therapist/counselor, but they may feel embarrassed or guilty if they were to ask for help” (P551)].

Increasing and facilitating access to subsidized mental health

Students highlighted the need to increase access to mental healthcare provided by schools [e.g., “Make more availability for therapy through the school. I was told I could only have therapy once every 8 weeks through school” (P58), and “Offer more regularly-available appointments with counseling or increase the number of counselors available. I often have to wait 3 weeks or more for an appointment” (P251)]. Some students highlighted the need for services with flexible hours [e.g., “more availability especially for the clinical year students who have a less flexible schedule and might need counseling at odd times of day.” (P623)], and free [e.g., “offer free therapy sessions to students” (P202)] or low-cost mental healthcare [e.g., “offer low cost access to mental health counselling.” (P45)].

Reducing barriers to mental healthcare

Students suggested reducing barriers to mental healthcare, such as confidentiality concerns, stigma, perceived punishment, and lack of access. Specifically, they expressed concerns about dual role conflicts [e.g., “I would have to let a faculty member assess me mentally. Although I understand it is in the mindset to help, it’s daunting knowing your professional school’s faculty will know of any mental health issues” (P178)] and potential privacy violations [e.g., “I would like for mental health help to be completely confidential. One of the major reasons I avoided seeking psychological help from my school’s program was because it came with a disclaimer that all information may be shared with the school” (P244)]. Students described how stigma discouraged them from seeking care [e.g., “I have always felt that I would be seen as weak for not dealing with my problems on my own” (P226)], and contributed to fears that seeking help would negatively impact career prospects [e.g., “Due to mental health stigma in professional environments, I could not possibly justify seeking help from a program that may or may not lower my chances of a better residency match” (P244)]. Some students even reported fears of being punished because of mental health issues [e.g., “My school needs to break down barriers between administration and students. In the past, students have been punished (suspended, required medical leave of absence, etc.) for bringing up mental health concerns” (P209)] or engaging in self-care [e.g., “[The school should offer] excused absence because even though we are ‘excused,’ it is 100% held against us, so we all tend not to go to the doctor or dentists for an entire year. I think about getting sick every day and how terrified I am that I would not get any support from my school” (P50)].

Occupational/scheduling dimension

Increasing time-off

Students generally perceived the time commitment for medical school to be excessive [e.g., “We are all here because we are familiar with sacrificing time with loved ones, disciplined schedules, and the obligatory all-nighters or sleepless weeks. When these occasional sacrifices become the daily norm is when discipline begins to feel futile and life a Sisyphean exercise” (P.54)]. Many students emphasized a desire for more time-off [e.g., “Not over packed schedule so students can’t enjoy a holiday. Not busy work. Let students have a moment to breathe. Let them have a weekend. Let students be people” (P439)]. Specifically, they indicated it would help to have more scheduled breaks during clerkships and clinical rotations, allowing them time to meet personal and school obligations. They suggested schools, “provide more breaks during and between clerkships. Particularly, full days off for didactics, at least one day off prior to shelf exams, a few days off between each 12-week block. We have so little time to schedule appointments, see family/friends, or even take care of ourselves. Having those few extra days would make a world of difference” (P124). One student requested that the schools, “allow for time off throughout core clinical rotations. We are expected to work for 9 months straight with no vacation time. Nobody does this and it should be illegal. We are also expected to work at the level of the residents with no compensation, no free lunch, etc. Yes, we are here to learn, but we are not here to be abused” (P132). Others suggested that schools provide “mental health days, no questions asked” (P406) and “emphasize the need for mental wellness days/breaks and integrate it into the curriculum” (P638). Finally, students indicated that they would prefer if schools “increase protected time for studying” (P472) and “provide free time instead of giving lectures on “wellness” and having us complete assignments regarding this” (P6).

Optimizing the school schedule

Several requested schedule adjustments, noting that the volume of activities slated within a given time period is frequently overwhelming:

This institution has no regard for how long it takes to study for exams and how much time each student puts into their material every day. It is impossible to have a 12-hour study day to fit in board material, class material, osteopathic lab material, and attending lecture itself and still maintain a modicum of mental health and self-care. It is physically impossible to fit everything into one day (P521).

Some suggested improving the distribution of activities throughout the academic year [e.g., “Better scheduling to space out exams so students are not unnecessarily bombarded by them all at once” (P33)], explaining, “Often as a student, it feels that there is no communication between the different module directors as several exams/quizzes/mandatories are scheduled in the same week. This slight change would help students’ mental and physical wellbeing significantly” (P394). Specifically, the students felt they would benefit from a less intense schedule before exams [e.g., “Every Tuesday we have mandatory events from 9am (sometimes 8am) to 4pm, and it’s exhausting. A lot of times we have a full day like these a couple days before an exam which is really stressful” (P612)] and after exams [e.g., “Allocate 1–2 days post exams with no necessary study requirements” (P615)].

Sharing curricular schedule as early as possible

Students also recommended that the medical schools provide timely access to the curricular schedule [e.g., “releasing the class/rotation schedule far in advance would help a lot with anxiety” (P58)] to allow students to plan accordingly [e.g., “provide students with their schedules months in advance so we can plan trips to see our loved ones” (P115)]. Finally, some recommended more flexible scheduling, suggesting schools “be open to rescheduling mandatory events” (P96), including allowing accommodation for health problems [e.g., “Increase flexibility related to health issues/timing of courses” (P142)].

Academic support dimension

Providing more mentorship

Students requested more mentorship, explaining, “They could have better guidance. It feels like students are on their own the whole time and it’s a stressful enough time. It would help to have someone check in on how we are doing and not just bombard us with tasks and never truly listen to us” (P330). They also recommended more formal career advising [e.g., “One of my biggest anxieties was related to career planning, so ensuring that career advising for students is structured, formalized, and consistent throughout the 4 years would be important” (P80)]. Specifically, they suggested assigning both faculty mentors [e.g., “establish 1:1 faculty-student mentorship program” (P416)] and peer mentors [e.g., “Matched mentor for underclassmen to spend days with upperclassmen in upper phases to see what the daily flow and responsibilities are like” (P229)] would be helpful.

Enhancing student-centric approach

Students noted that it would benefit them if the medical schools considered their needs more [e.g., “My school could take a more student-centric approach to improve student wellbeing” (P228)]. In general, they requested that schooling be more tailored to their needs and that schools prioritize their learning process [e.g., “Students, students’ needs, and helping us succeed need to be the priority– not making more money and expanding to fill the pockets of the higher ups” (P269)]. Specifically, students felt medical schools should be more responsive to student concerns/requests [e.g., “listen to student feedback and make appropriate changes.” (P649)], with some suggesting that schools are not sufficiently receptive to their input [e.g., “ask for students’ opinions when making changes to our schedule/curriculum; listen to students on areas where there is room to grow and make change; feels that school does not care about our opinions or what can make the school better” (P421)]. Finally, some students suggested that medical schools could specifically improve accommodations for students who are struggling, saying the schools should, “Be more considerate toward personal/familial struggles of students and how to adjust their personal curriculum accordingly if needed” (P93).

Financial wellness dimension

Alleviating financial distress

Student responses suggested that medical schools could do more to alleviate students’ financial distress. Specifically, they requested that schools “increase availability of financial resources” (P84), provide meal vouchers or subsidized healthy food [e.g., “Better access to healthy lunches at a reduced cost to the students while we are required to be on campus” (P705)], increase access to affordable housing [e.g., “student housing–affordable” (P335)], and lower tuition [e.g., “reduce tuition costs” (P99)].

Organizational culture dimension

Promoting psychological safety

Students noted the importance of promoting psychological safety for all members of the medical school community through efforts to combat discrimination [e.g., “Condemn signs of ignorance, racism, and discrimination among faculty and staff publicly during town halls as a way to signal support of minority students” (P412)]. Others lamented what they perceive as a non-supportive environment [e.g., “My school’s administration has established a fear-based, big-brother culture based on aggression towards students. I don’t know how this culture could or will change” (P230)], and expressed a desire to be treated with more respect [e.g., “Tell the residents/attendings/whatever clinical staff to at least acknowledge students and treat them like people and not ghosts. Very terrible for the wellbeing when we are forced to be in a certain place with certain people and then they don’t even acknowledge us. They don’t have to be our best friend, but at least integrate students into the team” (P72)]. They recommended that medical schools reduce the pressure for high achievement [e.g., “Not put such unnecessary pressure on us to succeed or meet metrics. They focus too much on their success, not the student’s” (P553)] and decrease competitiveness among students [e.g., “Eliminate class rank, which creates an overly competitive environment” (P10). They requested more empathy regarding their struggles [e.g., “More empathy from faculty and staff when it comes to voicing issues regarding mental health, scheduling, and academic burden.” (P494)], and more realistic expectations for students compared to other members of the clinical care team [e.g., “I think they are not empathetic to the new medical school student and have unrealistic expectations for ‘professionalism’ in 2023” (P655)].

Integrating the wellness concept into the culture

Although students acknowledged that their schools offered wellness promotion sessions, they reported a desire to better integrate the wellness concept into the culture of the organization. Specifically, students requested changes in organizational policies to reduce student stressors [e.g., “It would be nice to know that administration genuinely cares about student wellbeing, and that policies for classes, absences, and exams reflect that care. It seems like while on the surface student wellbeing is acknowledged, it is constantly ignored or dismissed in practice” (P79)]. They indicated limited interest in learning personal resilience strategies to manage stress, in favor of decreasing the workload and expectations coming from the school [e.g., “Our school has hosted sessions to speak about student wellness, but administration seems to stick to the sentiment that stress is normal. However, seeing the amount of stress that medical students at my institution endure every week, I know that’s not normal” (P. 44)].

Improving communication

Finally, students noted that insufficient communication from the school negatively impacts student wellbeing, and requested improved communication [e.g., “Be clear with communication. Often times, there are difficulties in understanding what is expected of students and this causes us distress when we don’t know what we need to do to meet expectations within courses” (P388)]. This related to both clarifying what is expected of students [e.g., “My school could work to provide more transparency around course outlines, expectations, and requirements” (P228)] and providing information with sufficient notice [e.g., “More timely communication with students about important things. Better coordination of communication between students and preceptors and faculty” (P47)].

Discussion

This study examined how medical students’ training expectations, commitment to their career choice, and satisfaction with their school’s wellbeing resources are associated with mental health changes, professional fulfillment, and symptoms of burnout during medical school. It also explored medical students’ perspectives on ways their medical school could better support student wellbeing. The majority of students reported feeling at least somewhat fulfilled by their medical school training, and most indicated their training experiences met or exceeded their expectations. However, as seen in previous research with Swedish medical students [38], training expectations varied, with first year students in this cohort most likely to report that their experiences did not meet their expectations. In addition, over half of the students reported at least one burnout symptom, and a significant portion indicated that their mental health had worsened since beginning medical school, echoing findings of previous research showing that burnout among medical students exceeds levels found in the general population and among peers in other educational settings [17–19, 21, 22].

The research on undergraduate pre-medical student wellbeing is limited, but anecdotal evidence suggests that many students arrive to medical school with a history of burnout that could make them more vulnerable to future episodes. In addition, exposure to highly-politicized policy discussions regarding the future role of physicians in the healthcare workforce may increase student distress [39], and increased pressure on applicants to complete “gap year” experiences may also negatively impact their wellbeing as medical students [40]. In this sample, rates of burnout and mental health decline increased after the first year of school, but first-year students reported less fulfillment from their training than students in the second year and beyond. The extant literature emphasizes the importance of aligning student expectations with the realities of the medical environment to effectively mitigate burnout and promote mental health [1, 41]. Despite this, our results demonstrated that many students hold inaccurate expectations related to the medical school workload, schedules, grading procedures, and associated stress, potentially contributing to increased distress and career choice regret.

Importantly, reporting that medical school did not meet the students’ expectations was associated with greater burnout and worsening of mental health, lower levels of fulfillment, and a decreased likelihood of re-choosing a career in medicine. These results were consistent with research involving Saudi Arabian medical students [42], which found greater burnout among students who were dissatisfied with their studies and among those who intended to change colleges (i.e., drop out of medical school). Reporting career choice regret (i.e., indicating they probably or definitely would not choose a career in medicine again) was more common in this sample than previous research [43], but showed a similar pattern, with graduating students reporting more regret than second year students. Thus, in order to demonstrate their commitment to student wellbeing, medical schools should work to ensure that incoming students are prepared for the realities of medical training/careers prior to matriculation. Specifically, medical schools could consider changes to the pre-medical curriculum and/or application requirements, improved communication to their admitted applicants, and/or enhanced orientation experiences for incoming students. They should also clearly communicate policies related to potential challenges that may arise when pursuing medical education while raising a family [44] or managing personal health concerns [45]. In addition, medical schools may wish to consider whether recent changes to their programs/curricula (e.g., in response to changes in the USMLE Step exam or the COVID-19 pandemic) may have negatively impacted the learning experience and may need further adjustments.

Indeed, the results suggested a disconnect between the schools’ efforts to promote student wellbeing and students’ perceptions of those efforts. Despite significant investment in developing/expanding wellbeing curricula and wellness programs in medical schools over the past decade [46] and limited research showing effectiveness of some wellness initiatives (e.g., [47, 48]), more comprehensive assessments are needed to assess the impact of these efforts across the field of medical education (such as those conducted to evaluate curricular changes to address other systemic issues; e.g., [49]). Some students reported a perceived lack of empathy from faculty and staff related to students’ wellbeing, which may reflect insufficient efforts to meaningfully incorporate the wellbeing supports now required by accrediting bodies, limited faculty/staff training to recognize students’ psychological concerns and provide support [50], changes in societal norms related to training and working environments [51], and/or misunderstanding/mistrust between medical students and faculty regarding student needs [52]. For example, Khatskevich and colleagues [32] showed that faculty at one institution overestimated the importance of certain stressors for medical students (e.g., in-house and medical licensing examinations and patient interactions), while students identified finding research opportunities as a more significant source of stress, likely due to increasing pressure among residency applicants to produce scientific publications [53, 54]. Importantly, student concerns may vary depending on the reputation/resources of their medical school and their desired specialty. Conversely, students often underestimate the importance of required educational experiences, particularly when schools do not explicitly communicate the rationale for them. As seen in prior research [31, 55, 56], students suggested that transparency, clear communication, and responsiveness to feedback are essential to promote student wellbeing. Encouraging more open communication may assist all stakeholders in meeting their mutual goals of student success and wellbeing. Initiatives such as town hall meetings and ongoing dialogue in response to student feedback would likely enhance the efficacy and impact of student wellness resources and programming, while simultaneously enhancing psychological safety within the institutions.

Of course, medical education entails necessary discomfort to attain the required knowledge and professional experience, including heavy workload and some unmotivating tasks [57]; therefore, significantly reducing the requirements or completely eliminating stress is not feasible. Though it may be helpful to make incremental changes to the curriculum and learning environment to reduce stress when possible, helping students to reframe reasonable challenges/discomfort as necessary and integral to the learning process may be more valuable. Given the high-stress nature of the medical environment, it is crucial for medical students to build the resilience needed throughout different stages of their medical career [58]. However, our findings suggested that many students do not recognize the value of educational experiences promoting resiliency skills and strategies. A significant portion of students expressed dissatisfaction with the wellness/wellbeing content of both their required curriculum and extracurricular programming, suggesting that current efforts may fall short of addressing the unique needs of medical students [24, 31]. Schools may need to enhance communication regarding the rationale for lessons on time- and stress-management, cognitive restructuring, growth mindset, mindfulness, etc., and implement practical learning activities to maximize application of the material being presented. Quality mentoring can also help students strengthen resilience, navigate challenges, and achieve work-life balance. Similar to a previous study [31], students requested more mentorship from faculty and senior students. Both group-based faculty mentoring and peer-to-peer mentoring have been associated with increased social support, student satisfaction, professional growth [59], improved academic performance, and reduced dropout [60] and stress rates [58, 61].

Finally, students suggested increasing the number of wellness activities in place of presentations/lectures, implementing mandatory wellness check-ins, and reducing barriers to mental healthcare. Research supports the effectiveness of many wellness initiatives (e.g., mental and physical health services, meditation, therapy dog visits, aromatherapy, and art therapy) in reducing burnout levels [62]. Additionally, mental health interventions offered by medical schools reduce distress, depression, anxiety, and suicide risk [63]. However, as seen in previous research [31], the results indicated that stigma toward mental healthcare remains a major barrier to help-seeking among medical students.

Limitations and future research

The results should be interpreted within the context of some limitations. First, although the sample was relatively large, it was drawn from a single state, and technical difficulties prevented participation from some students who may have opted to participate. Thus, the data may not be representative of the full population of medical students. Second, the reliance on self-report measures introduces the potential for response bias; however, this limitation is somewhat mitigated by the topic of interest (namely, student perceptions and perspectives), which cannot reliably be measured through more objective means. Third, the cross-sectional design limits the ability to establish causal relationships between the variables of interest or to observe changes across student’s medical school tenure. Fourth, due to the length of the overall survey, brief instruments were used to reduce survey burden. Although the Single-Item Burnout Measure [12] is useful for screening, its lack of total score and sub-scores limits the detail that can be obtained from the data. Future research should aim to overcome these limitations by employing more robust measures and longitudinal study design to better ascertain causality, and expanding the sample to include medical students from diverse geographic and educational backgrounds. Studies are needed to explore the specific mechanisms through which satisfaction and fulfillment are associated with mental health outcomes, as well as the role of interventions designed to align students’ expectations with the realities of medical education. Finally, research exploring the perspectives of other stakeholders (e.g., medical school faculty, administration, residency program directors) would provide additional insight regarding how to feasibly support student wellbeing throughout medical education.

Conclusion

Despite the efforts of medical schools to enhance wellness programming and resources in recent years, medical students report burnout, worsening mental health, and dissatisfaction with the wellbeing content of their required curriculum and extracurricular programming. This study suggests that inaccurate training expectations are associated with worse mental health, greater burnout, less fulfillment, and lower satisfaction with career choice. By highlighting valuable suggestions for medical schools to enhance their wellbeing curricula and programming, this research contributes to the ongoing dialogue on how to best support the wellbeing of medical students. Medical schools can use student recommendations to adapt existing wellness programs/resources to better meet their students’ needs. In addition, the feedback can be used to highlight areas where schools may need to increase communication and transparency to realign student expectations with the realities of medical education and practice.
