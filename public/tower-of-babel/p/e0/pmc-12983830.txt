Pretransplant mortality in children awaiting living donor liver transplantation: a prospective center-based study
Authors: Tri Hening Rahayatri, Ni Made Sarastri Widyani, Hanifah Oswari
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHigh mortality in children awaiting liver transplantation is a significant concern. At Cipto Mangunkusumo Hospital, the only active and established pediatric living-donor liver transplantation (LDLT) center in Indonesia, similar challenges persist. This study aims to determine the mortality rate, time to death or transplantation and causes of death in children listed for LDLT at our center.MethodsThis prospective single-center cohort study was conducted from January 2023 to December 2024. Consecutive sampling included all eligible children age 0–18 listed for LDLT. Participants were monitored from listing until pretransplant death, transplantation, loss to follow-up or study completion. The primary outcome was all-cause mortality on the LDLT waiting list.ResultsSixty-two children were enrolled; 64.51% (n=40) were female with biliary atresia accounting for 72.58% of diagnoses. Malnutrition was identified in 70.97% (n=44) and 35.48% (n=22) had a history of cytomegalovirus (CMV) infection. Median duration from listing to death was 87.5 days. Median time to transplantation was 263.5 days. During follow-up, 22 children died with sepsis as the leading cause, 22 underwent LDLT, 13 were lost to follow-up and 5 remained on the waiting list. Donor scarcity contributed substantially to prolonged listing and loss to follow-up.ConclusionsChildren listed for liver transplantation experience a high waiting list mortality at our center. This is primarily due to infection, malnutrition and socioeconomic factors. Sepsis persists as the principal cause of death. LDLT remains the only feasible option for children requiring liver transplantation in Indonesia.Keywords: Mortality, Pediatrics

Introduction

Liver transplantation (LT) is the gold-standard treatment for end-stage liver disease. However, the number of children requiring transplantation continues to exceed the availability of suitable liver grafts, resulting in significant delays to timely transplantation. This disparity is further confounded by challenges in graft size matching.1 More than 1 in 10 children under 2 years of age die while awaiting LT and more than 40% remain on the waiting list for over a year. During this time, they may experience progressive deterioration in organ function, growth and neurodevelopmental outcomes.2,4 While data from Asia are scarce, one single-center study from Japan reported a 3% overall waiting list mortality in pediatric LT.5

In countries where deceased donor LT (DDLT) is available, organ allocation systems prioritize children under 12 years of age based on the Pediatric End-Stage Liver Disease (PELD) score; this reflects the estimated risk of 3-month waiting list mortality. Adolescents aged 13–18 years are prioritized using the Model for End-Stage Liver Disease (MELD) score. However, PELD score often underestimates the actual mortality risk, particularly in children under 2 years of age. Consequently, letters of exception by the United Network of Organ Sharing (UNOS) and Regional Review Board (RRB) are often used to secure additional points in patients whose clinical severity is not adequately reflected by PELD score to increase listing priority.6 7 In centers offering both DDLT and living donor LT (LDLT), the latter serves as a valuable alternative, resulting in shorter wait times. LDLT, in which a portion of the liver from a healthy living adult is transplanted into the recipient, represents an important strategy to expand the donor pool.8 9

LDLT is more prevalent in countries with lower gross domestic product per capita, particularly across Asia, the Middle East and Africa.10 Since the first pediatric LT in Indonesia in 2010, Cipto Mangunkusumo Hospital (CMH) has performed LDLT for all children requiring transplantation. Despite being the sole pediatric LT center in Indonesia with an established and active program, children listed for LDLT in our center continue to face significant risks of deterioration and poorer prognosis. This is often due to delays in referral, advanced disease status at the time of listing and severe malnutrition with pre-existing infections.11 12

This study aims to determine (1) the mortality rate, (2) time to death or transplantation and (3) causes of death in children listed for LDLT in our center. We will investigate the pretransplant course and identify clinical and non-clinical factors associated with prolonged listing and mortality.

Methods

Study design and setting

This prospective single-center cohort study investigated children listed for LDLT between January 2023 and December 2024, with a follow-up period of 9 months. The study was conducted at CMH, a national referral hospital in Indonesia with an established pediatric liver transplant program.11 12

Participants

Children aged 0–18 years with no previous history of liver transplant who were listed for LDLT during the study period were eligible for inclusion. Children with incomplete clinical data at listing or those who declined to participate were excluded. Participants were enrolled using consecutive sampling.

Data collection

Data were collected prospectively from the time of listing until one of the following conditions occurred: pretransplant death, transplantation, lost to follow-up or study completion (September 2025). Two primary sources were used for data collection: (1) review of medical records throughout the follow-up period and (2) interviews with caregivers. Interviews gathered sociodemographic data and information on disease history along with any sociocultural issues that arise during listing. Clinical data including diagnosis, laboratory values and complications were extracted from medical records.

Follow-up and outcome measures

Participants were followed until the primary end point of either pretransplant death, loss to follow-up or transplantation. Follow-up was conducted periodically every 90 days and concluded on either outcome occurrence or end of study period, whichever comes first. The primary outcome was all-cause mortality while on the waiting list. Other outcome measures were waiting list time, PELD score, nutritional status, cytomegalovirus (CMV) infection status and socioeconomic factors affecting wait list time.

PELD score was divided into five groups, (−10 to −1; 0 to 10; 11 to 20; 21 to 30; ≥31).13 CMV infection status was determined using PCR. Nutritional status was measured using middle upper arm circumference (MUAC) and divided into three groups based on MUAC Z-score: severely malnourished (Z≤−3), malnourished (−3<Z≤−1) and normal (Z> −1).14

Statistical analysis

This study was descriptive in nature. Continuous variables were summarized using medians, while categorical variables were expressed as frequencies and percentages. Kaplan-Meier analysis was applied to compare overall survival between groups. All analyses were conducted using SPSS V.27 and a two-sided p value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

We recruited 62 children awaiting LDLT with a median age of 13 months (range 2–197 months). Forty were female and 22 were male. Biliary atresia accounted for 72.58% of underlying diagnoses. One child was over 12 years old (16.4 years old and female); she was lost to follow-up and MELD scores were unavailable. Only PELD scores were analyzed for the remaining 61 children. Among our study participants, 70.97% were malnourished and 35.48% had the history of CMV infection. Additional characteristics are presented in table 1.

Secondary outcomes

The most common cause of delay in our center was difficulty in identifying a suitable donor (n=5) followed by family reluctance (n=4). Three children were lost to follow-up and declined to provide any reason (n=3), while financial constraints were reported in one case (n=1). Among the five children who remained on the waiting list at the end of the study period, the primary reasons for continued listing were unavailability of donor (n=2), family-related delays (n=2) and hospital admission (n=1).

Discussion

Pretransplant mortality remains a critical concern in pediatric LT, with contributing factors including disease severity at listing, delays in donor evaluation and system inefficiencies.2 15 16 While several studies have examined outcomes following pediatric LDLT, with intention-to-treat analyses showing a 72% reduction in mortality risk for children with a potential living donor, few have focused on children who die before receiving a transplant, particularly within the context of LDLT programs.9 Understanding the characteristics and clinical trajectories of this population is crucial for identifying modifiable delays, optimizing the timing of listing and donor preparation and ultimately reducing waiting list mortality.

This is a prospective center-based study involving 62 children, predominantly female (64.5%) with the predominant underlying cause being biliary atresia (72.58%); among those with biliary atresia, 34 of 45 had no history of Kasai portoenterostomy. In LDLT, ideally, the procedure should be performed without delay. Shorter waiting times are associated with better functional outcomes. Moreover, reducing prolonged hospitalization and progressive malnutrition may prevent further developmental impairment, particularly in younger children.16 17 However, this study demonstrated that candidates waited approximately 263.5 days from listing to transplantation. The median time from listing to death was 87.5 days.

Globally, LT wait-list mortality in children younger than 2 years varied between 0% and 20%.18 In the USA, the overall pretransplant mortality rate for pediatric liver transplant candidates in 2023 was approximately 6.7 deaths per 100 patient-years, or about 8%–12%, with a median waiting time of 100 days.18 19 In Japan, a single-center study reported 3% pretransplant mortality rate for pediatric DDLT in a LDLT program using data from July 2010 to February 2019.5 In our cohort, there were 22 mortality events (35.48%). The leading cause of mortality was sepsis (31.82%), predominantly caused by hospital-acquired pneumonia. Two deaths occurred outside of our center; the exact causes could not be confirmed (9.09%). We observed a higher waiting list mortality rate when compared with other centers.5 18 19 This may be partially explained by the younger median age of our cohort (13 months), as children under 2 years of age are known to have higher waiting list mortality than older pediatric candidates.7

The PELD score has been used for over two decades to prioritize LT in children younger than 12 years based on 90-day waiting list mortality risks (i.e., death without receiving a transplantation).7 In contrast, no statistically significant difference in survival was observed across PELD groups in this study. However, 26.1% of those with scores 0–10, 50.0% in those with scores 11–20, 60.0% in those with scores 21–30 and 50.0% in those with scores >31 died. Of note, one child with a PELD score of −1 died within 128 days of listing due to hemorrhagic shock caused by rupture of esophageal varices. One study from Vietnam similarly demonstrated that higher PELD scores in children with biliary atresia and awaiting transplantation were associated with an increased risk of death.20

In our study, the indication for transplantation for all children was cholestatic liver diseases, mainly biliary atresia. Clinical complications of malnutrition in children with chronic cholestasis include growth failure, rickets, bleeding and serious infections. Pawłowska21 reported that approximately 60% of children undergoing assessment for LT are malnourished.21 This is consistent with our findings that 41.94% and 29.03% children were malnourished and severely malnourished, respectively. In chronic liver disease, malnutrition results from reduced nutrient intake, increased energy expenditure, decreased fat-soluble vitamin absorption and impaired protein and carbohydrate metabolism.22 23 Declining nutritional status and malnutrition are major factors that adversely affect survival, both on the waiting list and following surgery.21 Optimizing nutrition prior to transplantation is therefore critical. Children with cholestatic liver disease require up to 80% more calories than healthy children. Aggressive nutritional support prior to LT improves patient and graft survival. Nutritional management strategies include the use of thick enteral formulas, increased provision of medium-chain triglycerides and placement of nasogastric feeding tubes.24 Our center requires at least a malnourished nutritional status before undergoing transplantation and time needed for nutritional management contributes to LT delays in our cohort.

A previous study conducted in our center showed that nutritional status influenced the incidence of infection in pediatric LDLT and outcome.25 In addition, increased post-transplant mortality in biliary atresia has been linked to severe nutritional deficiency.26 There was no statistically significant difference in survival based on nutritional status in this study. However, mortality events were more frequent among malnourished (n=8) and severely malnourished (n=6) children compared with those with normal nutritional status (n=1). This may support the established association between malnutrition and adverse outcomes.

Another potential comorbidity evaluated in this study was prior CMV infection. We found that 35.48% of patients had a history of CMV infection at the time of listing. CMV infection is one of the risk factors for early hepatic artery thrombosis following LT27 where it influences long-term survival and graft viability.28 CMV infection is associated with increased risk of bacterial, other viral and invasive fungal infections.29 Moreover, CMV infection is an independent risk factor for graft rejection in organ transplant recipients.30 With this in mind, international guidelines recommend pre-emptive therapy for CMV.31 In our center, CMV infection confirmed by PCR is treated with 6-week pre-emptive valganciclovir therapy until PCR negative. Although a prior study identified CMV as a critical determinant of patient survival in LT,31 our Kaplan-Meier survival analysis stratified by CMV infection status showed no statistically significant difference in waiting list survival.

One of the leading reasons for lost to follow-up in our cohort was potential donor unavailability and ineligibility. LDLT, which mostly uses living-related donors, such as parents is now the only feasible method of LT in Indonesia. This restriction is primarily caused by low rates of dead organ donation, the lack of a nationally recognized registration system for deceased donor organs and low public awareness of the LT program.12 A number of other variables such as persistent legal, cultural and religious issues in determining brain death along with postmortem organ donation, donor ineligibility following the pretransplant screening process and donor safety concerns continue to exacerbate donor scarcity. This is despite strong parental willingness to act as donors. Moreover, other family members might influence parental decision-making, and some parents decline participation in donor prehabilitation programs or the procedure itself.

Family blood group incompatibility negatively impacts LT. Our center only performs transplantation with ABO-compatible grafts, due to significant mortality associated with ABO incompatible (ABOi) transplantation. While this approach prioritizes safety, we acknowledge that it further restricts the donor pool for our patients. Early experience with ABOi grafts in pediatric liver transplant reported incidences of antibody-mediated rejection, vascular thrombosis and biliary complication.32 Recipient age is also a predictive factor of outcome, with age greater than 1 year at the time of ABOi LT being associated with higher morbidity and mortality.33 34 However, recent data indicate that short-term and long-term outcomes of ABOi improve with immunosuppression. This includes desensitization, post-LT immunosuppression and antibody-mediated rejection management.35 Moreover, outcomes improve as institutional experience improves.36

Beyond clinical factors, there are socioeconomic issues that might affect listed children. Our center is located in Jakarta, the capital city of the country, where we receive nationwide referrals. Once listed for transplantation, families are often required to remain in Jakarta throughout the pretransplant preparation period, incurring higher living costs. Parents may have to leave their job, temporarily or permanently, to support treatment of their child. Even though national insurance has greatly helped potential recipients, it lacks coverage in certain domains, such as donor screening costs and the specialized nutrition supplementation agents needed to improve nutritional status of the child. This may prolong time on the waiting list or contribute to loss to follow-up. In addition, as the sole active pediatric LDLT center and national referral hospital, transplant scheduling may be hampered by facility constraints combined with a high volume of referrals for both LT and other complicated pediatric surgical conditions. As clinical prioritization is often based on patient condition and severity at admission, this might contribute to the waiting time at our center.

LDLT is intended to reduce waiting time, with the theoretical expectation of minimal or no waiting list due to wider donor availability. In pediatric patients, suitable donors could be sourced from relatives, particularly parents. However, in practice and especially in developing countries such as Indonesia, significant waiting times persist despite our ongoing efforts to provide established pediatric LT care. Key contributors to delayed transplantation include donor availability and ineligibility, malnutrition and socioeconomic challenges that affect pretransplant readiness and continuity of care.

In conclusion, high mortality among pediatric LDLT candidates persists in our center, predominantly due to sepsis, malnutrition and socioeconomic challenges. Sepsis remains the leading cause of mortality, underscoring the vulnerability of this population and the need for early medical optimization and timely transplantation. Malnutrition and socioeconomic challenges delay donor-recipient evaluation and prolong waiting times, thereby increasing the risk of mortality among these vulnerable children.
