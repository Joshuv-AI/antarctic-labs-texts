Associations of Neprilysin and Its Coding Gene Variation With Hypertension in Chinese Adults
Authors: Junting Hu, Jing Duan, Jiexiang Jing, Qiu Zhang, Xianfeng Yu, Rongyan Zhang, Yuting Jia, Mingzhe Du, Penghao Wang, Xueyang Zhang, Yibing Jin, Xiangdong Yang, Mingzhi Zhang, Hao Peng, Yun Shen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTNeprilysin (NEP) degrades several vasoactive peptides and may contribute to blood pressure maintenance. This study aims to examine the associations between serum NEP levels and variants at its coding gene and hypertension in a general Chinese population. Leveraging the baseline blood specimens of the Gusu cohort, serum NEP was assayed, and 14 single‐nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) in the MME gene were genotyped (10 passed the Hardy‐Weinberg equilibrium test) by MassArray for 2286 participants (mean age 52 years, 61.4% females). The associations of serum NEP and SNPs with hypertension were examined by logistic regression. Major conventional risk factors, including lifestyle and metabolic factors, were adjusted for. We found that participants with a higher level of serum NEP were more likely to have prevalent hypertension (OR = 1.07, 95%CI: 1.02–1.13, p = 0.006 for log2‐transformed NEP). The SNP rs9864287 was associated with hypertension susceptibility (P < 0.05), but this association was not significant after correction for multiple comparisons. These results suggested that there was a weak association between serum NEP and hypertension in Chinese adults, independent of conventional risk factors. The role of NEP in hypertension remains to be elucidated.

Introduction

Neprilysin (NEP), a membrane‐bound zinc‐dependent endopeptidase widely expressed in the kidney and heart, degrades several vasoactive peptides, including natriuretic peptides (NPs), bradykinin, endothelin I, and substance P, and participates in the renin‐angiotensin system (RAS) [1, 2]. Multidisciplinary studies have suggested its potential role in the development of hypertension. For example, cardiac overexpression of NEP has been shown to promote the degradation of NPs, leading to exaggerated hypertrophic and fibrotic responses to pressure overload in mice [3]. Genetic variation at the Membrane Metalloendopeptidase (MME), the coding gene of NEP, has been associated with the susceptibility to hypertension in European populations [4]. Currently, a novel angiotensin receptor NEP inhibitor (ARNI) has been approved for the treatment of hypertension and prevention of heart failure in elderly hypertensive patient [5]. Although clinical trials have confirmed remarkable blood pressure‐lowering effects of ARNI [6, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11], a considerable part of patients exhibited inadequate responses to this drug [8, 9]. We hypothesized that circulating NEP levels may affect blood pressure reduction in response to ARNI. A better understanding of the association between circulating NEP and hypertension is urgent. To date, circulating NEP has been associated with heart failure [12] and diabetes [13] in clinical studies, but its distribution and association with hypertension in the general population have not been well defined. Therefore, we aimed to examine the associations of serum NEP and its coding gene variation with hypertension in a general population of Chinese adults participating in the Gusu cohort.

Methods

Study Participants

The Gusu cohort is a community‐based prospective cohort study of cardiovascular disease and its risk factors in Chinese adults. The study design, survey methods, and laboratory techniques have been described previously [14]. Briefly, 2498 unrelated individuals aged over 30 years were recruited from eight communities randomly selected from Gusu district, Suzhou city in 2010. The protocols of the Gusu cohort have been approved by the Soochow University Ethics Committee. Written informed consent was obtained from all study participants. Leveraging the baseline data and blood samples, we examined the levels of serum NEP and genotypes of the MME gene. After excluding 213 participants with missing data on serum NEP, a total of 2286 participants were included in the final analysis.

Measurement of Blood Pressure and Definition of Hypertension

Three blood pressure measurements were performed after the participants had been resting for at least 5 min in a relaxed, sitting position by trained staff using a standard mercury sphygmomanometer and a cuff of appropriate size, according to a common protocol [15]. The first and fifth Korotkoff sounds were recorded as the systolic blood pressure (SBP) and diastolic blood pressure (DBP), respectively. The mean of the three measurements was used in the analyses. According to the Chinese guidelines for the management of hypertension, hypertension was defined as SBP≥140 mmHg and/or DBP≥90 mmHg or use of antihypertensive medications in the last two weeks [16].

Measurement of Serum NEP

Blood samples were drawn at baseline examination after an overnight fast. Serum samples were isolated and frozen at ‐80°C until laboratory testing. Quantitative sandwich enzyme immunoassay (RayBiotech) was used to quantify NEP levels in the serum samples. This method has been demonstrated to be reliable for serum or plasma with prolonged storage [17]. The measurements were performed by skilled staff in the Jiangsu Provincial Key Laboratory of Geriatric Disease Prevention and Translational Medicine at Soochow University, with the blinding of participants’ information. A standard curve was constructed and from which NEP concentrations of unknown samples were determined. Intra‐ and inter‐assay coefficients of variation were less than 10% and 12%, respectively.

Selection and Genotyping of Single‐Nucleotide Polymorphism (SNP)

Genomic DNA was extracted from venous blood using a QIAamp DNA Blood kit (Qiagen GmbH, Hilden, Germany) and was quantified using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Inc., Waltham, MA, USA). For the candidate gene MME, a set of tagSNPs was initially selected using the Chinese (CHB and CHS) population from 1000 Genomes Project Phase 3 with Tagger using a minimum minor allele frequency (MAF) of 0.05 and an r2 threshold of 0.8. SNPs that had been reported in association with cardiovascular phenotypes in previous studies were preferred, and SNPs that failed in initial assay design were replaced by equivalent tagSNPs. Finally, a total of 14 SNPs in the MME were genotyped using the Agena MassArray Analyzer 4 system (Agena Bioscience, USA).

Collection of Risk Factors for Hypertension

Information on demographic characteristics (age, sex, and education level), lifestyle risk factors (cigarette smoking and alcohol drinking), and medical history was obtained by trained staff using standard questionnaires in Chinese. Current smoking was defined as having smoked at least 100 cigarettes in one's life, having smoked cigarettes regularly, and smoking currently. Drinking was classified as current drinkers or not. Current drinkers were those who had consumed any alcoholic beverage ≥ 12 times during the past year. Body weight (kg) and height (cm) were measured when participants wore light clothes and no shoes by trained staff. Body mass index (BMI) was calculated by dividing weight in kilograms by the square of height in meters (kg/m2). Blood samples were collected by venipuncture after a requested overnight fast (at least 8 h). Glucose and blood lipids, including total cholesterol, triglycerides, high‐density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL‐C), and low‐density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL‐C), were measured for all participants by standard laboratory methods. The detailed methods of data collection were presented elsewhere [14].

Statistical Analysis

To carefully examine the role of NEP in hypertension, we examined the associations of serum NEP levels and related SNPs with hypertension individually. Characteristics of study participants were presented according to quartiles of serum NEP. Log2‐transformation was applied to maximize the distribution normality of serum NEP, and the generated values (Log2‐NEP) were used in downstream analysis. To examine the observational association between serum NEP and hypertension, we constructed a logistic regression model in which hypertension (y/n) was the dependent variable and serum NEP (Log2‐NEP or categorical NEP in quartiles) was the independent variable, adjusting for age, sex, education level, smoking, drinking, BMI, HDL‐C, LDL‐C, and fasting glucose. This association was additionally examined in pre‐specified subgroups by conventional risk factors to test the robustness. To examine whether the MME gene variation was associated with the susceptibility to hypertension, a logistic regression model was similarly constructed with each SNP as the independent variable in additive, dominant, and recessive genetic models. The false discovery rate (FDR) approach was applied to control multiple testing by adjusting for the total number of SNPs tested. All statistical analyses were conducted using R version 4.2.1. A two‐tailed p value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Sensitivity Analysis

Antihypertensive medications, for example, ARNIs, ACEIs, and ARBs, are known modulators of NEP activity. To examine whether and to what extent antihypertensive medications influenced our results, the association between serum NEP and hypertension was repeatedly examined after excluding participants under antihypertensive treatment. To evaluate whether the missingness of SNPs raised selection bias, the prevalence of hypertension and serum NEP levels were compared between participants with missing data on SNPs and those without.

The Genetic Association Between the MME Gene Variation and Hypertension

According to the allele frequencies in the participants free of hypertension, ten of the 14 SNPs genotyped at the MME gene (Table S1) passed the Hardy‐Weinberg equilibrium test and were included in subsequent analyses. Linear regression failed to find a statistically significant association of these SNPs with serum NEP (Table S2). We further examined whether these SNPs were associated with hypertension. The SNP rs9864287 (OR = 1.16, 95%CI: 1.01–1.34, p = 0.045 for the additional allele of A) showed a significant association with the susceptibility to hypertension (Table 3). Participants with the TA or AA genotypes at rs9864287 had a 47% higher risk of hypertension than those carrying the TT genotype (OR = 1.47, 95%CI: 1.09–2.01, p = 0.013). Participants carrying the TT genotype at rs1126662 had a 23% increased risk of developing hypertension compared to those carrying CC and TC genotypes (OR = 1.23, 95%CI: 1.02–1.50, p = 0.034). However, these associations no longer survived after correction for multiple testing (all FDR‐P > 0.05).

Results of Sensitivity Analysis

After excluding participants with antihypertensive medications, the association between serum NEP and hypertension did not change a lot (Table S3). Although many participants received antihypertensive medications in our study, the association between serum ENP and hypertension may not be driven by antihypertensive medications. The prevalence of hypertension and serum NEP levels were comparably distributed between participants with missing data on genotype and those without (Table S4), indicating that missingness of SNPs may not raise concerns about selection bias.

Discussion

In a large general population of Chinese adults, we examined the associations of NEP and its coding gene variations with hypertension. Participants with a higher level of serum NEP were more likely to have a higher risk of prevalent hypertension. The SNP rs9864287 showed an association with hypertension susceptibility (P < 0.05), but this association was lost after correction for multiple testing, probably due to a limited sample size or weak‐effect SNPs. In consideration of the robust association between serum NEP and hypertension independently of metabolic factors, our results suggested that NEP may participate in the development of hypertension through mechanisms beyond metabolic factors, but this role remains to be elucidated.

In line with our study, the potential role of NEP in hypertension has also been suggested by previous studies. For example, overexpression of NEP enhanced the degradation of ANP and attenuated the biological effects of NPs [18]. Except for the modulation in NPs, recent studies confirmed the participation of NEP in the RAAS and SNS regulation [9, 19]. In the spontaneously hypertensive rats, removal of the renal nerves attenuated renal NEP activity, augmented circulating NPs levels, and improved cardiac parameters in the setting of heart failure [9]. In a two‐kidney one clip rat model of renovascular hypertension, the renal expression of NEP is significantly decreased in the clipped kidney compared with sham controls [10]. In humans, a small study including 534 family members in the UK reported a positive correlation between circulating NEP and blood pressure [20]. While another cross‐sectional study reported an opposite result, that lower NEP was paradoxically associated with a higher prevalence of hypertension in a general population, including 1536 participants in the USA [21]. The heterogeneity of ethnic populations probably contributes to the inconsistent findings via the diversity of NEP expression and function [22]. We are the first to investigate the association between serum NEP and hypertension in the Chinese Han population. These findings, including ours, suggest that NEP may play a role in hypertension, but this role remains to be elucidated.

To date, the underlying mechanisms remain unclear and are critical for clinical translation. Blood pressure maintenance is a complex and dynamic process involving multiple crucial systems, including RAAS [23], SNS (Sympathetic Nervous System) [24], bradykinin‐prostaglandin system [25], vascular endothelial relaxation system [26], and water‐sodium homeostasis. Angiotensin II, bradykinin, NPs, and endothelin all take a pivotal part in the modulation of arterial tension and volume equilibrium. Furthermore, the metabolism of these peptides is basically maintained by 3 kinds of enzymes, including ACE, ECE (Endothelin converting enzyme), and NEP. Among them, NPs are the fundamental substrates of NEP, which is proven to be important in the maintenance of cardiovascular positive benefits through vasodilation, natriuresis, and anti‐fibrotic effects [27, 28]. In light of our findings, the association between serum NEP and hypertension supports the mechanistic basis of ARNI.

In addition to NEP, the genetic variations at its coding gene MME were also studied in relation to hypertension. After multivariate adjustment and correction for multiple testing, none of the ten SNPs genotyped was significantly associated with hypertension in our study. In contrast, rs10513475 at the MME gene was significantly associated with the susceptibility to hypertension in European populations [4]. Another study, including 95 patients with heart failure treated with nesiritide (a B‐type NP synthesized using recombinant DNA technology), found that rs16824656 in the MME gene was significantly associated with changes in blood pressure after infusion [29]. Although not yet functionally validated, genetic variation in the MME gene may be a core determinant of NPs' function and response to targeted pharmacological therapy. However, the genetic association did not survive multiple‐testing correction in our study sample, likely due to limited sample size or the presence of week‐effect SNPs.

This study has several strengths. First, we investigated the association between serum NEP and hypertension in a general population in a Chinese community and emphasized that the higher serum NEP, the higher the prevalence of hypertension. Second, we obtained major conventional risk factors to mitigate their confounding effects. Our first observation of the relationship between serum NEP and hypertension may shed light on future studies of hypertension mechanisms and the development of drug individualization strategies related to NEP assessment. Nevertheless, the study also has several limitations. First, given the predominantly Asian composition of our study sample, these results must be cautiously interpreted and may lack generalizability to other populations. Second, the cross‐sectional design prevents causal inference. The underlying causality still needs more evidence. Third, the levels of NEP were measured in serum samples stored at −80°C for at least 10 years in our study. The stability of serum NEP under prolonged storage remains unaddressed, although recombinant NEP is reported to be stable for 6–12 months at −80°C in the lyophilized form. However, we believed that the values of serum NEP obtained could reflect the relative differences among our participants because all serum samples were stored under identical conditions. Fourth, as a transmembrane protein, the serum concentrations of NEP may not be in accordance with its activity, which is a potent limitation of serological biomarker evaluation and further integration in clinical practice of NEP. We only examine the serum concentration of NEP, but whether the activity measurements in vitro can reflect those in vivo is controversial. Further studies are warranted to explore the correlation between concentration and activity of NEP in different methods and confirm the standard methodology and further application in detection and optimal individualized therapy for hypertension. Fifth, although many confounding factors were obtained and controlled, data on sodium intake, which is a known modulator of NEP activity, were not available in our study. Whether and to what extent the association between serum NEP and hypertension was affected by sodium intake was unclear. Sixth, the association between serum NEP and hypertension observed in our study is not strong, suggesting a limited clinical significance. Although ARNI, a combination of NEP inhibitor with valsartan, has been approved for patients with hypertension, this significant association indicates that serum NEP may be used in precision medicine to identify individuals who may benefit most from ARNI. However, the clinical application of serum NEP still needs more evidence.

Conclusion

Our study demonstrated that serum NEP was significantly associated with hypertension in Chinese adults, independent of conventional risk factors. Further studies on the causality and application of NEP in the prevention and management of hypertension are warranted.
