Influence of sequential ultrasonication and heat-moisture treatment on the physicochemical properties and structural profile of corn starch
Authors: Kai-min Yang, Chien-Shan Chiu, Yung-Jia Chan, Wen-Chien Lu, Ching-Jung Lin, Zeng-Chin Liang, Po-Hsien Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Graphical abstract

Introduction

Starch is a ubiquitous carbohydrate polymer that underpins the texture, stability, and processability of many staple foods and starch-based materials. However, native starches often exhibit narrow processing tolerance, typically characterized by rapid swelling, high peak viscosity, pronounced shear and thermal breakdown, and strong setback during cooling, which limit their use in modern formulations and motivate modification strategies. In response to increasing demand for clean-label and low-additive structuring approaches, physical modification methods have gained renewed attention because they can reshape starch functionality without introducing chemical substituents or reaction residues [1].

Heat-moisture treatment (HMT) is a hydrothermal modification performed at a restricted moisture content (commonly 10-30% w/w, typically <35%) and at an elevated temperature above the glass transition but below full gelatinization, for durations ranging from minutes to hours [2]. HMT is widely recognized as an environmentally friendly route to reprogram starch by inducing rearrangement within amorphous domains and at crystalline-amorphous interfaces, often increasing gelatinization while reducing swelling and viscosity development, and altering retrogradation behavior [3]. Recent studies have further shown that HMT can modulate gel stability and processing performance through time-temperature-moisture-dependent changes in chain association and ordered structure, with outcomes that can vary by botanical origin and HMT severity [2], [4]. Current evidence suggests that HMT serves as an effective step for conditioning structures, enhancing thermal robustness, and adjusting digestibility-related characteristics [2]. However, its effects are highly dependent on the accessibility of water into the granules and the level of reorganization achieved within a specific moisture content and time frame.

Ultrasonic treatment (UST) modifies starch primarily through acoustic cavitation, generating microstreaming, localized shear, and transient high-energy events that can disrupt granule surfaces, alter amorphous packing, reduce short-range order, and change particle and aggregate structure. Modern syntheses and reviews emphasize that UST can be a rapid, green, and controllable method to tailor starch solubility, hydration, rheology, and digestibility, with effects governed by ultrasonic frequency, power (energy) input, medium viscosity, temperature, and solids concentration [5]. Recent work on flour/starch systems further supports that delivered ultrasonic energy can systematically reshape pasting and viscoelastic responses through structural perturbation and altered water-polymer interactions [6]. These findings highlight UST as an accessibility modulator that can accelerate structural change pathways; however, the direction of change, such as increased and decreased order indices or viscosity, can be non-monotonic across energy windows [6].

Despite their promise, single-step HMT or UST has practical drawbacks. HMT frequently increases thermal resistance but may also overly suppress swelling and viscosity, limiting texture development in applications requiring controlled thickening; additionally, the reorganization can be heterogeneous due to moisture gradients and diffusion-limited transport in granules, leading to variable functional outcomes [1]. UST, while fast, can produce competing effects, which is simultaneous disruption and re-association, yielding condition-sensitive, sometimes inconsistent property shifts, and may introduce aggregation, dispersion artefacts depending on solids content and energy density [5]. In short, HMT tends to be strong on thermodynamic reorganization but limited by transport and access, whereas UST is strong on transport and access, but can be limited by state dependence and nonlinearity of cavitation effects.

A logical strategy is therefore to combine HMT and UST so that the advantages of one treatment compensate for the limitations of the other. In dual-physical modification systems, hydrothermal conditioning can establish a reorganized granular architecture, whereas subsequent ultrasonic treatment can selectively disrupt weak amorphous domains, alter molecular packing, and modulate hydration behavior. Previous studies on HMT-ultrasound-modified starches have primarily focused on changes in digestibility, crystallinity, or thermal properties and generally demonstrated that the combined treatment can produce synergistic modifications beyond those achieved by either technique alone [7], [8]. Furthermore, few studies have integrated low-field nuclear magnetic resonance (LF-NMR), magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), rheological analysis, and multivariate statistical approaches to establish the relationships between structure, water mobility, and functionality in HMT-UST-modified starch systems [9]; Lu, Chan, Liang, et al., 2025). However, a systematic understanding of how different HMT durations influence the subsequent ultrasonic response of starch, particularly in relation to water-state distribution, molecular mobility, pasting characteristics, and viscoelastic behavior, remains limited.

Therefore, the present study investigated the effects of sequential HMT (0, 3, 6, and 9 h at 95 °C) and UST (0, 40, 80, and 120 kJ) treatments on corn starch. Structural, thermal, hydration, pasting, rheological, and water-state properties were comprehensively characterized using X-ray diffraction, Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy, differential scanning calorimetry, rapid visco analysis, dynamic rheology, LF-NMR, MRI, principal component analysis, and Pearson correlation analysis. It was hypothesized that HMT duration would determine the susceptibility of starch granules to ultrasonic disruption and reorganization, thereby governing water mobility and ultimately controlling the functional properties of the modified starch.

Materials and Methods

Materials

Commercial native corn starch (maize; Zea mays L. endosperm) was obtained from Tairoun Products Co., Ltd. (6F, No. 206, Nanking E. Rd., Sec. 2, Taipei City, Taiwan; catalogue no. 350028). The starch was used as received (not laboratory-extracted or further purified). According to the manufacturer’s certificate of analysis and nutrition label, the product contained 85.0 g total carbohydrate per 100 g, with 0 g protein, 0 g total fat, and 0 g sugars; sodium 10 mg per 100 g; and a declared energy value of 340 kcal per 100 g. The starch used in this study contained 27.38 wt% amylose and 72.62 wt% amylopectin. The estimated weight-average molecular weights (MW) were 8.0 × 105 g·mol−1 for amylose and 8.8 × 107 g·mol−1 for amylopectin, giving an estimated overall starch MW of 6.43 × 107 g·mol−1. Unless otherwise specified, reverse osmosis (RO) water was used throughout.

Sample preparation and processing design

Corn starch was processed using a two-factor experimental matrix combining heat-moisture treatment (HMT; 95 °C for 0, 3, 6, or 9 h) and probe ultrasonication (UST; 0, 40, 80, or 120 kJ delivered energy over 10 min). For thermal conditioning, starch was packaged in sealed pouches to minimize moisture loss and limit exposure to oxygen. After each processing step, samples were dried, milled, and sieved prior to subsequent treatments and analyses.

Heat-moisture treatment (HMT)

For each batch, 200 g of corn starch was adjusted to 30% (w/w) moisture by adding an appropriate amount of distilled water, and then thoroughly mixed. The moistened starch was sealed in vacuum retort pouches and equilibrated at 4 °C for 24 h to promote uniform water distribution. HMT was conducted in a temperature-controlled water bath at 95 °C for 0, 3, 6, or 9 h. Immediately after heating, pouches were rapidly cooled in an ice-water bath and then held at 4 °C for 12 h. The starch treated was dried in a convection oven at 40 °C to constant mass, ground, and retained for subsequent ultrasonication.

Ultrasonic treatment (UST)

HMT-pretreated starch was further processed using a probe-type ultrasonic liquid processor (VCX750, Sonics & Materials, Inc., Newtown, CT, USA) at 20 kHz. Sonication was performed for a fixed duration of 10 min, targeting delivered energies of 40, 80, or 120 kJ, as recorded by the instrument. After ultrasonication, samples were dried, milled, and passed through a 0.7 mm sieve. The resulting starch powders were stored in airtight containers at ambient temperature until structural and physicochemical analyses.

Morphological characterization of starch granules

Granule morphology and birefringence were assessed by polarized light microscopy using an optical microscope (BX53, Olympus, Tokyo, Japan) equipped with a CCD camera. Starch suspensions (1% w/v) were prepared in reverse osmosis (RO) water and gently mixed to ensure uniform dispersion. A drop of suspension was placed on a clean glass slide, a coverslip was applied, and excess liquid was removed to minimize granule movement. Images were acquired at 200× magnification under bright-field and cross-polarized illumination. Granule integrity and internal molecular orientation were qualitatively evaluated by the presence and definition of the Maltese cross pattern.

Surface ultrastructure was examined by scanning electron microscopy (SEM) using a tabletop instrument (TM4000Plus, Hitachi, Tokyo, Japan) operated at 10 kV. Starch powders were mounted on aluminum stubs using double-sided conductive carbon tape and sputter-coated with a thin platinum layer under vacuum to improve conductivity. Representative micrographs were collected (typically at 800× magnification) to visualize treatment-induced changes in granule surface topography, including fissures, pitting, and fragmentation associated with the HMT-UST processing history.

Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy

Short-range molecular order was assessed using a Fourier transform infrared spectrometer (Cary 630 FT-IR, Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA). Spectra were acquired at room temperature over 650-4000 cm−1 with a resolution of 4 cm−1 and 32 scans per sample. Transmittance spectra were converted to absorbance using formulation (1):(1)A=-log0.01×Twhere T is transmittance (%). Absorbance values at 1047, 1022, and 995 cm−1 were extracted to compute the band ratios R1047/1022 and R995/1022, which are commonly used to indicate short-range ordered structure and double-helical order within starch, respectively:(2)R995/1022=A995A1022(3)R1047/1022=A1047A1022

X-ray diffraction (XRD) and relative crystallinity (RC)

The crystalline structure of starch samples was determined using an X-ray diffractometer (LabX XRD-6000, Shimadzu, Kyoto, Japan) operated at 40 kV and 30 mA with Cu Kα radiation (λ = 1.5406 Å). Starch powder (0.3 g) was gently packed into a sample holder and levelled to obtain a flat, uniform surface. Diffractograms were collected over a 2θ range of 10-40° using a step size of 0.02° and a scanning rate of 2° min−1. Relative crystallinity (RC) was quantified in OriginPro 2024b (OriginLab Corp., Northampton, MA, USA) by deconvoluting the crystalline peak area from the amorphous background. RC was calculated as formula (4):(4)Relativecrystallinity(%)=AcAc+Aa×100%where Ac and Aa represent the integrated areas of the crystalline and amorphous regions, respectively.

Differential scanning calorimetry (DSC)

Thermal transitions and gelatinization properties were evaluated using a differential scanning calorimeter (DSC 4000, PerkinElmer, Waltham, MA, USA). Starch (5 mg, dry basis) was weighed into a medium-pressure aluminum pan and mixed with 10 μL RO water. Pans were hermetically sealed and equilibrated at room temperature for ≥24 h to ensure homogeneous hydration. An empty sealed pan served as the reference. Samples were held at 25 °C for 3 min and then heated from 25 to 155 °C at 10 °C min−1. Onset (T0), peak (Tp), and conclusion (Tc) temperatures, as well as gelatinization enthalpy (ΔH, J g−1 dry starch), were obtained from thermograms using Pyris software (PerkinElmer). Gelatinization enthalpy (ΔH, J/g) was calculated by integrating the area of the gelatinization endothermic peak between To and Tc after baseline correction and normalizing the value to the dry starch mass present in each sample. Measurements were performed in triplicate.

Hydration properties (swelling power and water solubility)

Water solubility (WS) and swelling power (SP) were determined following a modified AACC Method 56-20. Starch (0.5 g, dry basis; W1) was dispersed in 30 mL RO water in a pre-weighed centrifuge tube and heated at 70, 80, or 90 °C for 30 min with intermittent agitation. After cooling to room temperature, samples were centrifuged at 3500 × g for 30 min. The supernatant was decanted into a pre-weighed crucible and dried at 120 °C to constant mass to obtain the mass of soluble (W3). The wet sediment mass was recorded as W2. WS and SP were calculated as:(5)WS(%)=W3W1×100%(6)SP%=W3W1×1-WSwhere W1 is the initial dry starch mass (g), W2 is the mass of wet sediment (g), and W3 is the mass of dried soluble recovered from the supernatant (g).

Pasting properties (RVA)

Pasting behavior was characterized using a Rapid Visco Analyzer (RVA TECMASTER 3D+, Newport Scientific, Inc., Jessup, MD, USA). Starch suspensions (10.7% w/v) were prepared by dispersing 3.0 g starch in 25.0 mL RO water to a total mass of 28.0 g. The test profile comprised: holding at 35 °C for 1 min, heating to 95 °C at 6 °C min−1, holding at 95 °C for 4 min, cooling to 35 °C at 6 °C min−1, and holding at 35 °C for 5 min. The paddle speed was 960 rpm for 10 s and then 160 rpm for the remainder. Pasting temperature (PT), peak viscosity (PV), trough viscosity (TV), and final viscosity (FV) were recorded. Breakdown (BV) and setback (SV) were calculated as:(7)BV=PV-TV(8)SV=FV-TV

Dynamic rheology

Starch gels for rheological testing were prepared using an RVA to standardize thermal history. A 5% (w/w) suspension was prepared by dispersing 1.5 g starch in 26.5 mL RO water (total mass 28 g). The RVA program was: 50 °C for 1 min, heating to 95 °C at 11 °C min−1, holding at 95 °C for 2 min, cooling to 50 °C at 11 °C min−1, and holding at 50 °C for 3 min. Mixing was 960 rpm for 10 s followed by 160 rpm thereafter. Dynamic measurements were performed on the resulting gels using a rheometer (MCR 92, Anton Paar, Graz, Austria) equipped with a 50 mm parallel-plate geometry. The plate gap was set to 0.1 mm. Frequency sweeps were conducted from 1 to 10 rad s−1 at 1% strain, previously confirmed to be within the linear viscoelastic region. Storage modulus (G′), loss modulus (G′′), and loss tangent (tan δ = G′′/G′) were recorded.

Low-field nuclear magnetic resonance (LF-NMR)

Water mobility in starch gels was evaluated by LF-NMR following standardized gel preparation using an RVA (RVA 4500, Perten Instruments, Sydney, Australia). Corn starch (1.5 g) was dispersed in 26.5 mL RO water (total mass 28.0 g) and processed using the following profile: 50 °C for 2 min, heating to 95 °C at 8.5 °C min−1, holding at 95 °C for 2.5 min, cooling to 50 °C at 11.5 °C min−1, and holding at 50 °C for 5 min. Stirring was 960 rpm for 10 s followed by 160 rpm for the remainder. Immediately after preparation, gels were transferred into glass vials and measured using an LF-NMR spectrometer (NMI20-015V-I, NIUMAG, Suzhou, China) equipped with a 25 mm probe at 25 °C. Transverse relaxation time (T2) was obtained using the Carr-Purcell-Meiboom-Gill (CPMG) sequence with the following parameters: frequency offset 250 kHz, repetition time 3000 ms, number of scans 6, echo time 0.20 ms, and 18,000 echoes. The T2 relaxation distributions were derived by multi-exponential inversion using MultiExp Inv Analysis software (NIUMAG).

Statistical analysis

All measurements were conducted in triplicate and reported as mean ± standard deviation. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS (v20.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Differences among treatment groups were evaluated by one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by Duncan’s multiple-range test for post hoc comparisons when significance was detected. A probability level of p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results and Discussion

Polarized light microscopy (PLM) showed that the control starch retained pronounced birefringence with a clear Maltese cross, indicating preserved radial molecular orientation within granules (Fig. 1c). Across the HMT-UST matrix, birefringence generally remained observable, but the Maltese cross became progressively less distinct in a treatment-dependent manner. This attenuation was consistent with partial relaxation and disruption of internal order and increased scattering from altered granule surfaces and clusters, rather than with a complete loss of granule identity, which is consistent with the known effect of HMT in promoting molecular rearrangement without full gelatinization [10]. Hydrothermal conditioning has been widely reported to alter the crystalline-amorphous organization and reduce optical anisotropy in starches, reflecting reorganization of double helices and lamellae under restricted-moisture conditions [3].

SEM further supported a granule/cluster-level restructuring (Fig. 1d). To quantitatively verify the aggregation observed in the representative micrographs, particle size distribution analysis was performed (Fig. 1e). The resulting D10, D50, and D90 values provided population-scale evidence that HMT-induced granule clustering was the dominant morphological response, thereby complementing the qualitative SEM observations. Untreated starch displayed comparatively smoother granules, whereas HMT-UST treatments produced rougher topography and frequent multi-granule agglomerates (highlighted in Fig. 1d). These morphological patterns were quantitatively reflected by particle size distribution analysis (Fig. 1e), and the corresponding D10, D50, and D90 values are summarized in Table S1. The control exhibited a narrow distribution (D10 = 7.23 ± 0.08 µm; D50 = 15.79 ± 0.09 µm; D90 = 27.27 ± 0.65 µm). When ultrasound was applied without prior HMT (0 h), only minor increases were observed in sample W0U120 (D10 = 7.53 ± 0.09 µm; D50 = 16.50 ± 0.20 µm; D90 = 29.76 ± 0.64 µm), indicating that, under the present conditions, UST alone did not generate sustained aggregation or major granule fragmentation at the population scale. In contrast, introducing HMT (≥3 h) led to a step change toward much larger apparent particle sizes, strongly indicating agglomeration rather than enlargement of individual granules. For example, W3U0 increased to D50 = 30.51 ± 0.36 µm and D90 = 139.35 ± 3.17 µm, and the upper tail remained high across 6-9 h treatments (Fig. 1e). This behavior was consistent with HMT enhancing interparticle adhesion via increased chain mobility in amorphous regions, strengthened associative interactions upon cooling or drying, and the potential formation of intergranular bridges, all of which favor clustering [11]. Similar HMT-induced changes in granule integrity and aggregation propensity have been described for cereal starches subjected to hydrothermal treatment [3].

Notably, the influence of UST on particle size was non-monotonic once granules were HMT-conditioned, implying competing cavitation mechanisms. At 3 h HMT, intermediate ultrasound energy amplified aggregation to a maximum at W3U80 (D10 = 12.12 ± 0.13 µm; D50 = 43.05 ± 1.12 µm; D90 = 188.17 ± 5.81 µm), whereas the highest energy partially reduced the median and upper-tail diameters (W3U120: D50 = 36.30 ± 0.22 µm; D90 = 167.80 ± 3.59 µm). A comparable but less pronounced trend was observed at 6 h HMT, where the highest ultrasonic energy (120 kJ) yielded lower D50 (33.42 ± 2.05 µm) and D90 (165.52 ± 9.03 µm) values than the U40 and U80 treatments, suggesting partial disruption of previously formed aggregates under stronger cavitation conditions. For 9 h HMT, the largest clusters occurred without ultrasound (W9U0: D50 = 41.46 ± 1.67 µm; D90 = 197.06 ± 6.02 µm), while applying UST reduced the extreme upper tail, such as in sample W9U80 (D90 = 165.58 ± 2.04 µm; W9U120: D90 = 166.26 ± 3.83 µm). This pattern suggested that, in HMT-conditioned systems, moderate cavitation may increase surface roughness and promote intergranular association (thereby increasing D50/D90), whereas higher delivered energy can disrupt weakly bound clusters and limit the growth of large agglomerates through enhanced microjetting and shear [12]. Such dual roles of ultrasound promoting aggregation via surface activation yet dispersing clusters at higher intensities have been reported for ultrasound-modified starches and are strongly dependent on the initial granular state and processing severity [13].

Therefore, Fig. 1 established that HMT was the dominant driver of granule aggregation (with major increases in D50 and especially D90), while UST provided an additional tuning dimension that could either amplify or partially reverse clustering depending on energy input and HMT duration. The persistence of birefringence in PLM, alongside SEM-visible agglomerates and the pronounced expansion of the particle size upper tail, indicated that the coupled process primarily restructured granule assemblies rather than fully destroying granule identity at this stage.

Short- and long-range structural ordering and thermal transitions under the HMT-UST map

Fig. 2 integrated FTIR, XRD, and DSC to resolve how sequential HMT and UST reprogrammed starch order across length scales. In the FTIR spectra (Fig. 2a), all samples exhibited the typical starch fingerprint region (1200-900 cm−1) without the appearance of new functional-group bands, indicating that HMT-UST primarily altered molecular packing or organization rather than introducing new chemical moieties. To facilitate visualization of treatment-induced spectral variations, enlarged views of the 970–1060 cm−1 and 3300–3500 cm−1 regions are provided in Supplementary Fig. S3, highlighting changes in short-range molecular order and hydrogen-bond-related O–H stretching vibrations, respectively. Quantification by absorbance ratios (Fig. 2b) showed that the short-range order index R1047/1022 decreased from 0.71112 in the control to a narrower, lower range across treated samples (0.68-0.70). For instance, R1047/1022 declined to 0.69 for W0U80-W0U120 and to 0.68-0.69 for W6U80-W6U0. This systematic suppression suggested partial disruption and loosening of ordered packing in the near-surface and amorphous-crystalline interfacial regions, consistent with ultrasound- and hydrothermal-induced perturbation of helical arrangements [14]. In contrast, the double-helical or order-related ratio R995/1022 exhibited a broad, non-monotonic response spanning 0.83-1.83, indicating that HMT-UST produced multiple local conformational states rather than a single more or less ordered trajectory. HMT alone lowered R995/1022 markedly (W3U0 = 0.83; W6U0 = 0.91) relative to the control (1.24), consistent with partial helix disintegration and increased amorphous contribution under restricted moisture. When ultrasound was introduced, several conditions showed large increases in R995/1022, most prominently at 40 kJ (W0U40 = 1.83; W3U40 = 1.73; W6U40 = 1.62). This pattern indicated that moderate UST enhanced structural contrast in the 995-1022 cm−1 region, likely by preferentially disrupting amylose-rich amorphous domains and exposing or repacking residual ordered motifs, and allowing limited chain rearrangement into helical conformations during post-treatment drying and cooling. Prior sonication studies have reported that ultrasound-induced cavitation can exert dual and sometimes opposing effects on starch molecular organization [15], [16]. The localized shear forces, microjets, and transient high-pressure environments generated during cavitation may disrupt hydrogen bonds and ordered regions within starch granules, resulting in decreased short-range molecular order. On the contrary, partial fragmentation of starch chains may increase molecular mobility and facilitate reassociation or reorganization of glucan segments during subsequent stabilization, thereby promoting partial recovery of ordered structures. Therefore, FTIR order indices such as R1047/1022 and R995/1022 may either increase or decrease depending on ultrasound intensity, treatment duration, and the initial structural state of the starch granules. Similar ultrasound-dependent restructuring behavior has been reported for tapioca starch and arrowhead starch, where cavitation simultaneously induced molecular disruption and reorganization within amorphous and crystalline domains [15], [16].

XRD diffractograms (Fig. 2c) indicated that all samples retained the typical A-type crystalline pattern of corn starch, with principal reflections around 15°, 17-18°, and 23° (2θ), demonstrating that the long-range crystalline type was preserved across the processing map [17]. The corresponding relative crystallinity values are summarized in Table S2. Relative crystallinity (RC) varied modestly from 18.53% to 21.90% (Fig. 2c). The control exhibited an RC of 19.37%. HMT for 3 h reduced RC in the absence of UST (W3U0 = 18.96%) and reached the minimum at W3U40 (18.53%), implying that early-stage HMT combined with moderate UST most strongly disrupted long-range order. With longer HMT, RC tended to recover or increase under selected UST energies, reaching 21.90% for both W9U40 and W9U120 (and 21.90% for W9U40), while W9U80 dropped to 18.99%. Fig. 2d reinforced that ultrasound mainly modulated peak intensities or baseline (order fraction) rather than shifting crystalline polymorph, consistent with the view that cavitation preferentially perturbs amorphous regions and crystalline-amorphous interfaces while leaving the underlying A-type lattice largely intact. Comparable findings, A-type retention with treatment-dependent intensity changes, have been reported for ultrasound-treated corn starch and related systems [17], [18].

DSC provided a sharper readout of how processing altered the energetic stability of ordered domains (Fig. 2e). Detailed DSC parameters (To, Tp, Tc, and ΔH) for all treatments are presented in Table S3. Compared with the control (To = 67.65 °C; Tp = 70.87 °C; Tc = 76.08 °C; ΔH = 3.88 J/g, obtained from endotherm peak integration), HMT induced substantial upward shifts in gelatinization temperatures even when ΔH decreased. For example, W3U0 increased To to 73.85 °C and Tp to 77.40 °C (Tc = 85.10 °C) while ΔH decreased to 2.47 J/g; W6U0 further increased To to 75.30 °C and Tp to 78.93 °C with ΔH = 2.55 J/g. The highest peak temperatures occurred at prolonged HMT with ultrasound, for example, W9U120 (Tp = 80.20 °C; To = 75.54 °C), whereas the lowest enthalpy was observed at an intermediate HMT-UST condition (W6U80: ΔH = 1.57 J/g). These trends implied that HMT-UST increased the thermal resistance to gelatinization initiation (higher To/Tp) by restricting water penetration and strengthening (redistributing) chain associations, yet simultaneously reduced the net meltable double-helical perfection under certain coupled conditions (lower ΔH). A common result of HMT is the shift of gelatinization endotherms to higher temperatures and a reduction in enthalpy as treatment severity increases, as reported for corn starch and other starch types [19].

The joint interpretation of FTIR-XRD-DSC shows a distinction between the amount of long-range crystallinity present (RC changed only moderately) and the stability or accessibility of the ordered domains to water and heating (To/Tp increased significantly). In terms of mechanical insights (Fig. 2f), the data supported a two-stage pathway: at intermediate HMT durations (3-6 h), partial disintegration and defect formation in double helices/lamellae reduced ΔH (W3U80: 2.12 J/g; W6U80: 1.57 J/g), despite elevated To/Tp, consistent with more constrained but less energetically coherent crystallites. With prolonged HMT (9 h), the system appeared to transition toward reorganization/annealing into more stable packing, reflected by the highest Tp values (79.36-80.20 °C) and a rebound of ΔH in some conditions (W9U0: 3.30 J/g, approaching the control), even though RC did not increase uniformly. Such DSC-based mechanistic interpretations are consistent with multiscale studies showing that starch gelatinization involves progressive disruption from weaker to stronger ordered structures and that processing history can shift the balance between crystallite perfection, defect density, and water accessibility [20].

Thus, Fig. 2 demonstrated that HMT established a high-temperature, water-restricted architecture (higher To/Tp/Tc), while UST provided an additional tuning lever that could either accentuate disordering (lower R1047/1022, reduced ΔH at W6U80) or promote partial re-ordering of local motifs (elevated R995/1022 at 40 kJ) without changing the A-type crystalline polymorph. This multi-scale order–stability decoupling is central to the concept of programmable starch states and helps explain why subsequent functional properties (hydration, pasting stability, and water mobility) can vary markedly even when XRD patterns appear broadly similar.

Hydration behavior of HMT-UST starches

Swelling power (SP) and water solubility (WS) were determined at 70, 80, and 90 °C to evaluate how HMT-UST reprogrammed hydration and leaching (Fig. 3a-b). For all samples, SP increased with temperature, consistent with thermally enhanced hydration. The control showed SP values of 7.52 ± 0.43, 7.97 ± 0.65, and 9.24 ± 0.70 g/g at 70, 80, and 90 °C, respectively. Ultrasound without HMT increased WS most clearly at 80 °C (W0U80: 5.07 ± 0.84%; W0U120: 6.60 ± 2.28%), consistent with cavitation-enhanced permeability and leaching reported for ultrasound-modified corn starch [21]. HMT lowered WS at 70 °C during prolonged treatments (W9U0: 1.40 ± 0.52%), whereas WS at 80 °C could increase (W9U0: 5.47 ± 0.93%), indicating that swelling restriction and solubilization were partly decoupled.

As a result, Fig. 3 indicates that HMT was the primary factor in reducing swelling, particularly at 70 °C. This finding aligns with the higher gelatinization transition temperatures noted by DSC in Section 3.2, as well as with the enhanced intra-granular associations characteristic of HMT starches. UST provided a secondary tuning lever that increased solubilization and, at moderate HMT severity (3 h), partially restored swelling by enhancing water access to the granule structure [22].

Introducing HMT produced a much larger shift in RVA behavior. PT increased sharply to 87.93-88.86 °C at 3 h HMT (W3U0-W3U120) and further to 89.96-90.83 °C at 6-9 h HMT (W6-W9 series). Meanwhile, PV decreased markedly from 3559.67 cP (control) to 1970.50-2020.00 cP in the W3 series and further to 1354.00-1626.00 cP in the W6-W9 series. Most notably, BV decreased dramatically from 1403.33 cP in the control to 222.25-282.25 cP in the W3 series and further to 126.33-199.00 cP in the W6-W9 series, indicating substantially improved resistance to shear-induced granule disintegration. Likewise, SV declined from 4136.67 cP (control) to 2141.67-2395.50 cP after 3 h HMT and further to 1175.33-1472.67 cP after 6-9 h HMT, reflecting a pronounced reduction in short-term retrogradation during cooling. In contrast to ultrasound alone, the sequential HMT-UST treatments produced starches with significantly higher PT and markedly lower PV, BV, and SV (p < 0.05). These coordinated changes indicate that the sequential HMT-UST treatment generated a synergistic functional response by combining HMT-induced molecular reorganization with ultrasound-mediated structural tuning, resulting in delayed gelatinization, restricted granule swelling, enhanced paste stability during heating, and reduced retrogradation tendency.

The mechanistic schematic (Fig. 4b) was consistent with these trends: HMT strengthened intragranular associations and reduced swelling/disintegration, thereby elevating PT and suppressing PV, while the coupled process reduced granule fragility (lower BV) and limited reassociation during cooling (lower SV). These RVA outcomes aligned with the hydration results (Section 3.3), where HMT strongly suppressed swelling at 70-80 °C, indicating reduced water accessibility and delayed paste development [23].

The RVA pattern observed here was higher PT with lower PV/BV/SV after HMT, which is consistent with prior reports for corn starch subjected to HMT, where rearrangement and densification of amorphous regions and strengthened chain interactions require higher thermal input for paste formation and reduce granule collapse under shear [24]. Ultrasound has also been reported to alter pasting by cavitation-induced damage to amorphous domains and changes in water permeability, often reducing paste viscosity and/or modifying stability depending on treatment intensity [25]. In the present matrix, ultrasound primarily acted as a secondary tuning factor within each HMT level, for example, the modest PV differences within W3 or W6-W9, whereas HMT duration dominated the global shift toward high-PT, low-breakdown pasting states.

Dynamic rheology (Fig. 4c-e) further demonstrated that HMT-UST reduced gel strength and increased viscous character after RVA-standardized gel preparation. Across all samples, G′ exceeded G″, confirming weak-gel behavior; however, the control showed the highest average G′ (197.65 kPa) and G″ (19.26 kPa) with the lowest loss factor (tan δ = 0.097). All treated samples exhibited markedly lower moduli. Ultrasound alone reduced average G′ to 82.53-88.68 kPa (W0U40-W0U120), and tan δ increased to 0.120-0.122, indicating a less elastic network. HMT caused a stronger drop in gel strength: average G′ decreased to 26.42-58.33 kPa in the W3 series and to 8.83-47.66 kPa in the W6-W9 series. The most compliant gel occurred at W9U80 (average G′ = 8.83 kPa; G″ = 2.14 kPa), and it also showed the highest tan δ (0.238), indicating the greatest shift toward viscous dissipation. These rheological changes are consistent with the RVA findings (lower FV and SV) and suggest that the coupled processing reduced the extent and/or effectiveness of network formation during cooling, likely by limiting granule swelling and altering the availability/organization of leached polymers that participate in gelation [26]. The interpretation that higher tan δ corresponds to a less structured, softer gel and lower elasticity is consistent with rheological analyses of cereal starch gels in which decreased G′ and increased tan δ reflect weakened gel networks [27].

Hence, Fig. 4 demonstrated that HMT-UST programmed starch leads to delayed paste formation (indicated by a higher pasting temperature), reduced viscosity during buildup (reflected in lower peak and final viscosities), significantly improved stability against thermal-shear stress (evidenced by a marked reduction in breakdown viscosity), and the formation of weaker, more dissipative gels upon cooling (characterized by a lower G′ and a higher tan δ). These functional shifts are consistent with HMT-driven reinforcement of granule architecture and reduced swelling propensity, while ultrasound provided an additional, condition-dependent perturbation of amorphous regions that subtly adjusted viscosity and viscoelastic balance within each HMT regime.

Introducing HMT further restricted water mobility. At 3 h HMT, T2,3 decreased from 541.587 ms in the control to 219.639 ms in W3U0. Following UST, T2,3 remained substantially lower than the control, ranging from 204.907 to 235.429 ms across W3U40-W3U120. The semi-bound water fraction exhibited T2,2 values of 8.407-15.703 ms and P2,2 values of 1.612-1.913%. The largest semi-bound fraction occurred at W3U80 (P2,2 = 1.913%), together with an elevated bound-water fraction (P2,1 = 1.384%), consistent with increased water interaction within a more developed polymer-water interfacial environment. At 6 h HMT, the strongest restriction of free-water mobility appeared at W6U80, where T2,3 reached 144.812 ms (P2,3 = 98.304%), compared with 191.164 ms in W6U0. At 9 h HMT, T2,3 remained shortened relative to the control, and the lowest value among all treatments occurred at W9U120 (T2,3 = 135.099 ms; P2,3 = 98.674%), indicating the most constrained free-water pool in the present matrix. Collectively, the relaxation results demonstrated that HMT-UST substantially reduced the mobility of free water (T2,3) and subtly redistributed minor water populations (P2,1 and P2,2), thereby linking processing history to water management at the gel scale.

MRI proton-density maps (Fig. 5c) provided spatial corroboration of the LF-NMR findings. Across the processing matrix, the images showed treatment-dependent differences in signal intensity distribution, with several HMT-UST gels exhibiting more homogeneous water distribution and reduced evidence of localized high-mobility water regions compared with the control. The qualitative shifts in MRI patterns were consistent with the shortened T2,3 values: as water mobility became increasingly restricted, the gels tended to display less spatial heterogeneity, supporting the interpretation that coupled processing promoted a tighter, more uniform water-confining microstructure.

In terms of mechanistic insights, the HMT-driven and UST-driven diagrams (Fig. 5d-e) were consistent with a two-part control knob over water states. HMT (3-6 h) likely increased chain association and reorganized amorphous (crystalline) interfaces, generating more confinement sites that converted part of the mobile water into less mobile pools (shorter T2,3; modest increases in P2,2 and/or P2,1 under some conditions). With prolonged HMT (9 h), reorganization of ordered domains and matrix densification reasonably further restricted water motion, explaining the very low T2,3 values observed for W9U120. At the same time, UST introduced cavitation-driven disruption and restructuring that could enhance confinement; for example, the W6U80 and W9U120 samples show a notable reduction in T2 and T3, or an increase in matrix heterogeneity, depending on the preconditioned state. This behavior is consistent with the non-monotonic structure-function relationships observed previously in FTIR, XRD, DSC, RVA, and rheology analyses.

These interpretations align with LF-NMR principles for starch gels: shorter T2 components indicate stronger water confinement and enhanced polymer-water interactions, while changes in T2 distributions reflect network development and retrogradation in starch systems [9]. In addition, recent LF-NMR work on corn starch gelatinization explicitly links T2 components to water redistribution between intra- and extra-granular regions, supporting the use of T2 shifts as a sensitive readout of processing-induced changes in water accessibility and confinement [30].

In short, Fig. 5 demonstrated that HMT-UST acted as a programmable platform not only for starch structure but also for water-state engineering, with the dominant signature being a strong reduction in free-water mobility (T2,3 shortened from ∼542 ms to ∼135-270 ms), accompanied by treatment-dependent modulation of minor bound/semi-bound fractions. This water-state control provides a mechanistic bridge from the processing map to the observed improvements in pasting stability and altered viscoelastic behavior in prior sections.

Multivariate integration of structure-function-water relationships across the HMT-UST map

Principal component analysis (PCA) summarized the coupled effects of HMT duration and UST energy on starch structure, hydration, pasting, rheology, and water states (Fig. 6a). The first two components accounted for 71.13% of the total variance (F1 = 56.69%, F2 = 14.45%). This indicates that most variability driven by treatment could be captured by a low-dimensional structure related to water and functional space. Along F1, samples formed three interpretable clusters. The control and ultrasound-only (W0U40-W0U120) groups were grouped on the positive F1 side and aligned with high swelling and pasting variables (SP-70/80/90; PV, BV, SV; and ΔH), indicating a native-like and high-viscosity state. In contrast, the longer HMT regimes (W6 and W9 series) clustered toward negative F1 scores and aligned with higher thermal stability indices (PT and DSC transition temperatures T0, Tp, Tc) and crystallinity-related directionality, consistent with a restricted hydration, high-PT, low-breakdown state. The Pearson correlation matrix (Fig. 6b) provided mechanistic support for these PCA linkages by quantifying the dominant trade-offs. Pasting temperature (PT) correlated strongly and positively with DSC transition temperatures, while it correlated strongly and negatively with viscosity build-up and paste fragility. Consistent with RVA physics, viscosity parameters were tightly coupled: PV correlated positively with BV (r = 0.95) and SV (r = 0.98), indicating that treatments suppressing peak swelling also suppressed shear-thinning breakdown and cooling setback. Hydration indices tracked these functional outputs: SP-70 correlated positively with PV (r = 0.89) and negatively with PT (r = −0.83), demonstrating that early-stage swelling capacity largely governed peak viscosity and the onset of pasting.

Water-state variables (LF-NMR-derived fractions) showed a complementary, internally consistent structure. The free-water fraction P2,3 (%) was strongly anticorrelated with the less mobile pools (P2,3-P2,1, r = −0.95; P2,3-P2,2, r = −0.87), indicating a conserved redistribution between mobile and bound water populations as processing history altered the gel microenvironment. In the PCA biplot, the water-state vectors were positioned away from the high-viscosity/hydration vectors, consistent with the observation that HMT-UST could reduce water mobility (shorter T2) without necessarily increasing swelling; water became more confined rather than more absorbed in the sense of granule expansion.

The overall mechanistic synthesis (Fig. 6c) integrated the multivariate analyses with the structural evidence obtained throughout the study. HMT appeared to proceed through a progressive two-stage process. During the early-to-intermediate treatment period (3-6 h), partial relaxation of amorphous domains and disruption of imperfect double helices increased molecular mobility and facilitated structural rearrangement. Upon prolonged treatment (9 h), starch chains underwent further reassociation and reorganization, resulting in a more thermally resistant and hydration-restricted architecture characterized by higher To, Tp, and Tc values, increased pasting temperature, and reduced swelling power and peak viscosity. Subsequent UST served as a secondary structural modulator, preferentially disrupting amorphous regions and reducing helical integrity via cavitation-induced mechanical effects. Consequently, HMT established the primary reorganized framework, whereas UST provided an additional level of control over short-range order, water mobility, and functional performance. This multiscale coupling between structural organization, thermal stability, hydration behavior, and water confinement is consistent with recent structure-function studies of physically modified starch systems [9], [31].

In practical terms, Fig. 6 established that the HMT-UST map programmed corn starch along a dominant axis from a high-swelling and high-viscosity regime (control and W0Ux) to a high-PT and low-breakdown regime (W6-W9), with W3 acting as an intermediate, tunable zone. Quantitative correlations confirmed that this shift was governed primarily by a strong inverse relationship between swelling-driven viscosity development and thermal resistance to gelatinization, and by a coupled redistribution of water from mobile to more confined states as the matrix became less permissive to swelling and granule collapse. This integrated interpretation is consistent with recent mechanistic work linking physical processing to coordinated changes in pasting, rheology, and water mobility in starch-based matrices [32], [33].

Conclusion

This study established a sequential HMT-UST strategy as a controllable physical approach for programming the structural and functional properties of corn starch. HMT acted as the primary driver of starch transformation, increasing gelatinization and pasting temperatures while reducing swelling, viscosity development, and short-term retrogradation. UST served as a secondary tuning factor that modulated granule accessibility and short-range molecular order without altering the native A-type crystalline polymorph. Dynamic rheology and LF-NMR/MRI further demonstrated that the coupled treatment systematically regulated gel viscoelasticity, water mobility, and water distribution, confirming that water-state engineering is a key mechanism governing the programmable starch states. These findings demonstrate that coupling HMT with UST provides a scalable physical strategy for producing starches with improved thermal stability, paste stability, and process tolerance. This study was limited to corn starch and a specific HMT-UST processing matrix. Therefore, the applicability of the proposed strategy to other botanical starches and processing conditions remains to be established. Future studies should investigate nutritional functionality, such as starch digestibility and resistant starch formation, employ advanced molecular characterization techniques to elucidate structural evolution, and evaluate the performance of HMT-UST-modified starches in practical food systems. These efforts will further facilitate the industrial application of this programmable physical modification strategy.
