Selective Segregation of Thermo‐Responsive Microgels via Microfluidic Technology
Authors: Anjali Sharma, Fabian Rohne, Daniela Vasquez‐Muñoz, Se‐Hyeong Jung, Nino Lomadze, Andrij Pich, Svetlana Santer, Marek Bekir
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractSeparation of equally sized particles distinguished solely by material properties remains still a very challenging task. Here a simple separation of differently charged, thermo‐responsive polymeric particles (for example microgels) but equal in size, via the combination of pressure‐driven microfluidic flow and precise temperature control is proposed. The separation principle relies on forcing thermo‐responsive microgels to undergo the volume phase transition during heating and therefore changing its size and correspondingly the change in drift along a pressure driven shear flow. Different thermo‐responsive particle types such as different grades of ionizable groups inside the polymer matrix have different temperature regions of volume phase transition temperature (VPTT). This enables selective control of collapsed versus swollen microgels, and accordingly, this physical principle provides a simple method for fractioning a binary mixture with at least one thermo‐responsive particle, which is achieved by elution times in the sense of particle chromatography. The concepts are visualized in experimental studies, with an intend to improve the purification strategy of the broad distribution of charged microgels into fractioning to more narrow distribution microgels distinguished solely by slight differences in net charge.Keywords: charge separation, charged and thermo‐responsive microgels, microfluidics, volume phase transition temperature

Introduction

Microgels are currently key materials for a huge variety of applications as part of modern high‐end technology, due to the ability to undergo material property chromism and are used for the design of biomaterials,[ 1 , 2 ] 3D bioprinting,[ 3 ] development of sensors,[ 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 ] drug delivery,[ 8 , 9 , 10 ] composition manipulation,[ 11 , 12 , 13 ] viscosity modifiers and lubricants,[ 14 ] stimuli‐responsive color‐changing systems,[ 15 , 16 ] and many others.[ 17 , 18 , 19 , 20 ] The reason for these diverse applications is the unique ability of the polymer network to respond to alteration of environmental conditions in terms of conformational changes over multiple possible stimuli such as solvent composition, light[ 21 , 22 , 23 ] and with greatest value for biomedical applications, pH and temperature.[ 24 , 25 , 26 ]

Microgels can be tuned synthetically in such way, that multiple stimuli behaviors are included into one material. The combination yields a double stimuli‐responsive behavior of such microgels, which are particularly interesting for biomedical applications as the combination of pH and temperature response allows for precise control of APIs (active pharmaceutical ingredients)[ 27 ] at targeted body location.[ 28 , 29 , 30 , 31 ]

One of the most known examples of thermo‐ and pH‐responsive microgels is poly(N‐isopropylacrylamide‐co‐acrylic acid, pNIPAM‐PAA, where the pNIPAM network to the presence of carboxylic groups from dissociation within the microgel, charges effectively the microgel. The PAA affects the volume phase transition temperature (VPTT) significantly such that the VPTT point of pNIPAM shifts from 32 to 72 °C for (pNIPAM‐PAA‐20 mol%).[ 32 ]

Currently, there are many protocols for the synthesis of microgels.[ 33 ] But for the microgels exceeding the size of 1 µm, most of them are synthesized over the microfluidic technology to template their size and shape.[ 34 , 35 , 36 , 37 , 38 ]

For instance, preparation of such charged microgels as reported in the literature[ 32 ] using the microfluidic technology will yield a natural distribution of crosslinked PAA inside the pNIPAM, where individual microgels will have varying quantities of crosslinked PAA but the total sample including all microgels will have the average anticipated crosslinking ratio of PAA, respectively. The consequence is an average VPTT point resulting from a broad distribution of individual VPTT points for each microgel. For example, such a tendency is crucial, if someone requires the microgels with precise variation of the VPTT point shift as an internal temperature calibration but the real calibration samples vary from an average value including a standard deviation. Here the optimum calibration sample would be such one, where the charge distribution with respect to the microgels ensemble is minimized as much as possible because then an almost “perfect linear relation” between the VPTT point and adjusted temperature control can be set. In practice, the use of microgels for heating sensors is actually used in laboratory as well, due to the read (∼size change) is a parameter with small amount of degrees of misinterpretation. This is already demonstrated in practice to double‐check and control the applied temperature of the heating stage for optical microscopy.[ 32 , 39 ] Thus, microgels with very accurate control of charge distribution are useful at least in imaging methods and presumably many more.

Although microgel synthesis protocols have been optimized over time, there is still lack of a method to purify such microgels with respect to minimizing the charge distribution of microgels. Normally such distribution contains microgels of almost equal size, so a separation over simple size exclusive separation techniques likewise the Dead‐End filtration,[ 40 ] centrifugation,[ 41 ] cross‐flow‐filtration[ 42 ] and Flow‐Field Flow Fractionation[ 43 ] is not working, and an adequate separation technique is up to now and to our knowledge still yet missing.

Accordingly, mixtures of microgels of either same material, similar in size but different in charge, as they typically leave the reaction batch, one can effectively separate such microgels by making use of a suitable temperature control in combination with the microfluidic technology to achieve significant different elution times, i.e., like typical chromatographic devices are operating but only suitable for microgels in size scale of tens of microns. This potential separation method is illustrated by several proof‐of‐concept experimental realizations and can be expanded to other microparticles as well.

Results and Discussion

The principle of fractioning equally sized microgels differing in charge density relies on the ability to undergo the volume phase transition at different temperatures. The volume phase transition temperature (VPTT) is shifted to higher values with an increasing amount of ionizable monomers inside the polymer matrix.[ 32 ] Thus with temperature increase consecutive collapse of the microgels starting from neutral and proceeding to microgels of larger charged density occurs. This is used here to selectively collapse different microgels over a precise control of the temperature in the appropriate range as illustrated in Figure 1d (red rectangle). Such generated size difference is utilized to fractionate microgels by microfluidics since microgels are propelled with different averaged velocities in external flow (Figure 2 ).

This principle is illustrated in detail with the following microgels: uncharged pNIPAM (poly‐N‐isopropylacrylamide) and charged pNIPAM‐PAA poly(N‐isopropylacrylamide‐co‐acrylic acid) immersed in an aqueous solution with dissolved cationic surfactant. The PAA ratio is consecutively set to a value of 5 mol %. The binary mixture of pNIPAM and pNIPAM‐PAA is heated up from room temperature (T = 25 °C) to 45 °C displayed in Video S1 (Supporting Information) and causes the pNIPAM to collapse at 32 °C, while the pNIPAM‐PAA charged microgels are still in a swollen state (Figure 1b). Heating further to a temperature of 45 °C leads also to the collapse of pNIPAM‐PAA (VPTT ≈42 °C) (Figure 1c). Figure 1d depicts the change of the normalized diameter (D/D 0) of differently charged microgels as a function of temperature. In the temperature range between 35 and 41 °C (ΔT = 6 °C) such a binary mixture of microgels can be selectively collapsed. Here we choose on purpose differently sized microgels for a better visualization of pNIPAM and pNIPAM‐PAA.

The equally sized microgels (≈34 µm) differing in the charged density can be easily identified by heating over VPTT of neutral microgel (pNIPAM), since charged pNIPAM‐PAA microgel remains swollen (Figures 1e,f; Video S2, Supporting Information). The change in diameter on shrinkage is from 34 to 14 µm (Figure 1g), resulting in a reduction of the size more than two times.

Important to mention is, that dispersion contains a cationic surfactant (c = 1 mm), which reduces the attractive surface forces between the glass substrates and dispersed micro‐sized objects. This renders the microgels to be overcharged by the surfactant[ 44 ] which than move freely along the bottom glass interface and do not to stick partially on the bottom interface as we observed while operating in pure Millipore water. For example, cationic surfactants are known to complex with the microgels[ 45 ] or to adsorb at glass–water interfaces, thereby changing the effective surface charge of the microgel and of glass[ 46 ] to high positive potentials (see Figure S6, Supporting Information), where to equally charged glass–microgel interface do prefer to repel from each other and then the DLVO forces[ 47 ] provides a lubrication regime. This kind of system manipulation can easily be removed afterward since surfactants usually have dynamic interaction potential with interfaces,[ 46 ] and a simple removal of the surfactant after successful fractioning can be done over dialysis against Millipore water. Adhesion avoidance is not only limited to cationic surfactants and can be extended with anionic surfactants as well. However, if the use of surfactants must be absolutely avoided a carboxylization of the channel surface via plasma treatment would be another attractive strategy.

When the same binary mixture of equally sized, charged, and non‐charged microgels are suspended under a pressure‐driven flow in a rectangular microfluidic channel (height = 0.54 mm, width = 3.8 mm), the sedimented microgels experience a shear force illustrated in Figure 2c and follow with a drift velocity proportional to the microgels size, i.e., collapsed microgels are slow, swollen microgels are fast. Here the motion of the binary mixture of pNIPAM and pNIPAM‐PAA at a constant flow rate of 70 µL min−1 is measured, where the temperature gradually increases from 25 to 36 °C with a heating rate of 0.13 °C s−1. During the entire Video S3 (Supporting Information) the motion of the particles is tracked, while the track of single particles below and above a critical size of D = 31 µm is recorded at the critical temperature of pNIPAM (Figure 2b).

At T = 25 °C the average velocity of the microgels binary mixture (Figure 2d, black line) cannot be distinguished as being 35 ± 8 µm s−1 for both microgels. However, on approaching a critical temperature of 32 °C (t = 50 s), the averaged velocity of tracked pNIPAM (red data) drops to 18 ± 6 µm s−1 during collapse to the compact state (diameter is ≈14 µm). In comparison, the charged microgel keeps its size, while the average velocity (green data) is similar to the black data displayed in Figure 2d.

The separation mechanism is based on the different shear stresses experienced by particles of different sizes driven by unperturbed fluid flow inside a microfluidic channel. For instance, when having a rectangular channel geometry with a height of 0.54 mm as illustrated in Figure 3a, a fluid flow profile in a laminar regime (typical Re numbers < 0.01) is parabolic. However, relative to the size scale of the microgel (several µm) the curvature of the parabolic flow near the bottom wall is quasi‐linear (Figure 3b). While keeping other parameters fixed but only changing the flow rate, experimental data in Figure 4 exhibit a linear dependency of the particle velocity on the flow rate, which is in reasonable agreement with theoretical[ 48 ] and experimental expectations.[ 49 ] When both microgel types have similar sizes, either fully swollen (Figure 4a) or fully collapsed (Figure 4c), their velocity difference is rather neglectable, and then effective fractioning becomes impossible. But when the sizes are noticeably different the velocity is consequently for all flow rates deviating between the swollen (pNIPAM‐PAA) in comparison to the collapsed (pNIPAM) microgels (Figure 4b). The ratio of the velocities (pNIPAM‐PAA/pNIPAM) exhibits a linear trend as a function of the flow rate for equally sized microgels (Figure S5a, Supporting Information), for two temperatures, i.e., at T = 25 °C and T = 45 °C, however, this does not apply for T = 35 °C, where two different states of sizes are present. Interpreting the data from Figure S5a (Supporting Information) we think that this increasing relative velocity results from stronger dispersion of the microgels during the flow into the bulk solution during flow. This is because an increase in the fluid flow induces an increasing rotation of the microgels and thus into an increasing angular velocity of the particle.[ 50 ] This increases the transverse pressure difference around the particle and then the particle experiences a bigger impact of the lift force from the bottom wall, the so called Magnus lift force.[ 51 , 52 ] Thus more and more microgels do not remain sedimented anymore and this may explain the strong fluctuations at later times (Figure S5, Supporting Information) obviously this is more probable at higher than lower flow rates and for bigger‐sized microgels, too. Thus, we suggest the potential users always to perform such fractioning at lower flow rate due to it gives a better control about state of sedimentation and then Equation (2) predict a linear dependency of the microgel size versus the velocity, key for this separation technique.

To demonstrate the simplicity of fractioning inside an elongated rectangular channel we use thermo‐ versus non‐thermo‐responsive micro‐sized particles. Motivated by the given approach in the literature[ 53 ] we apply a periodic cycle of (I) heating the system to fractionally collapse the MGs, (II) then switching on the flow in the direction toward the channel outlet for a certain time while MG are collapsed, (III) cooling the system to swell the MGs back to initial size and (IV) reverse the flow direction while MGs are swollen. Steps (I)–(IV) represent one separation cycle (see Figure 7a) and yield a local fractioning over the use of multiple separation cycles n. This is possible due to the velocity difference in step (II) being ∆U > 0 according to size differences between collapsed and swollen particles in the binary mixture while in step (IV) ∆U ≈ 0. This principle of fractioning is demonstrated for an area 9.9 mm2 = 3.3 × 3.3 mm2, where a binary mixture of equally sized (D ≈ 34 µm) pNIPAM‐MGs and polystyrene particles (PS) in ratio ≈1/4 is adjusted. We performed data recording near the microfluidic channel outlet position (see details in Section S4, Supporting Information). Due to the different refracting index of PS versus the MG´s the particles can be visually distinguished from optical microscopy, PS as black dotted rings and MGs as white spheres (see Figure 7d,e). In the experimental setup, we perform Steps (I)–(IV) (Figure 7a) and count the MGs and PS particles after each separation cycle displayed in Videos [Link], [Link], [Link] (one video corresponds to one separation cycle, Supporting Information). We heated the system up to 45 °C to be absolutely sure, that pNIPAM‐MGs remained during the forward flow collapse (step (I), (II)) while PS has no size change, and thus provides a strong difference in translational velocity. Heating was on purpose set to 45 °C to maintain a sufficient strong temperature gradient during fluid flow operation although data exhibited lately entered MGs to be swollen and are in the transfer to collapse. In the reverse flow the temperature is set to 25 °C and the MGs are swollen (step (III), (IV)) and have a similar velocity as PS particles. We set the flow rate at 150 µL min−1, which yields to fast separation and achieved spatial fractioning for the investigated area and the entire microfluidic channel in a short time frame of ≈360 s (3 ∙ 120 s ≈ 3 cycles). With each cycle, the number of PS decreases and vice versa for the MGs (Figure 7b), where PS leaves the entire channel after the 3 cycles. This is experimentally confirmed by scanning at room temperature with optical video microscopy the entire microfluidic channel (moving sample position stage relative to the objective), where Video S8 (Supporting Information) reveals the completely purified microgel fraction. The demonstrated principle confirms the potential of a fast and convenient tool to separate such particle mixtures, which yield up to 100% separation efficiency at the last separation cycle (Figure 7c; details in Section S4, Supporting Information). Besides, the principle of oscillating flow directions (Figure 7) not only allows a spatial fractioning at the desired area, but also minimizes the needed channel length as described in Figure 6.[ 53 ] Note, that the optimal number of separation cycles depends on relative velocity differences of dispersed particles and relative particle position inside the channel.[ 53 ]

Finally, what is important is, is to provide a sufficiently strong temperature gradient to maintain one microgel fraction collapsed versus the other. Further, this principle of fractioning has its limits in the relative VPTT changes of the particle mixtures. If too narrowed no selective size changes can be achieved and thus no differences in motion. Then other approaches must be taken into account, for instance over the use of light‐induced chemical activity.[ 49 ] Besides showed example is suitable for low to moderate particle concentration and leads to smaller quantities of separated particles per unit time and unit volume. Thus, it remains a very simple solution at the lab scale to post‐purify MG fractions after a synthesis.

The proposed strategy of purification uses elongated channels with sedimented microgels and conventional microfluidics, where flow remains almost within the Stokes flow region with very low Reynolds number (Re ≪ 1). However, this strategy of microgel separation is not limited to conventional microfluidics and can be extended to inertial microfluidics (≈1 < Re < ≈100), too, thus between Stokes and turbulent regimes. In inertial microfluidics two counteracting inertial effects are important, the shear gradient lift force and the wall lift force, which laterally migrate different‐sized particles into different positions of the channel when moving along the flow streamline.[ 50 , 54 ] Above the VPTT of pNIPAM one can consider the binary mixture of microgels in first approximation to be a binary mixture of different‐sized particles and there are other promising separation possibilities via the microfluidic technology.[ 50 , 55 , 56 , 57 ] All those techniques use the inertial momentum to passively guide particles into superposition with respect to the channel position and then differently sized particles can be separated over different outlet positions.[ 54 , 58 ] The most common examples are the Pinched Flow technique,[ 59 , 60 , 61 ] Zigzag elongated microfluidic channel,[ 62 , 63 , 64 ] contraction–expansion array[ 65 , 66 , 67 ] or spiral microfluidic channels.[ 68 , 69 , 70 , 71 ] These methods can be applied for thermo‐responsive MG separation, too. However, it is important to precisely control the fluid and microfluidic channel temperature to avoid temporal size changes of the MG or internal temperature gradients, which then may influence the lateral microgel migration across the channel cross‐section. The advantages of the afore mentioned techniques is that the separation is not based on microgel sedimentation and can be performed continuously usually also under higher flowrates (≈1 < Re < ≈100). Thus, such methods provide a greater throughput in comparison to the simple rectangular elongated channel but also requires a much more complex system (channel geometry, multiple flows, etc.).

With this proposed strategy after a synthesis, a naturally widened charge distribution around an average charge distribution of the microgels can be narrowed by a cumulative fractioning at two or more temperatures: below and above the VPTT of the desired charge distribution. An example is shown in Figure 8 . Assuming after a synthesis to target a PAA content of 5 mol% with yielding a real distribution range of 4–7 mol%. The purification toward 5 mol% can be achieved over a two‐step fractioning. The first one heats up the MG dispersion to 40 °C and extracts the swollen (PAA 5–7 mol%) against the collapsed (4 mol%) MG. Second, the principle is repeated at 45 °C, and the collapsed 5 mol% against the remaining unwanted swollen impurity (6–7 mol%) are now extracted again.

Principal guideline for thermo‐responsive microgel (µ‐range, 1–100 µm) purification with respect to embedded PAA content. Microgel synthesis is performed via oil‐droplet formation and after the synthesis microgel are purified from unreacted and unwanted molecular impurities. Purified microgels dispersed in a water‐surfactant solution with wide PAA distribution are purified using strategies of inertial microfluidics. Purification is achieved in other double iterations with different solvent temperatures: below and above VPTT of unwanted PAA distribution.

Conclusion

Here we introduce a simple method for the separation of differently charged and thermo‐responsive microgels using microfluidic technology. The principle of the separation relies on the linear drift velocity relation of sedimented particles as a function of the particle size along a pressure‐driven laminar fluid flow. Microgel separation can be achieved when a selective volume phase transition of one microgel type of a binary mixture is induced under precise control of applied heat while the second microgel type remains swollen. The relative motion difference between swollen and collapsed microgels consecutively remains for a broad range of applied fluid flow rates, however, the better precise control is toward slower flow rates. This is not only limited to microgels but can also be used for micro‐sized objects, where one part is thermo‐responsive, like pNIPAM microgels, while the counterpart, for instance polystyrene beats, is not.

This method illustrates first time the potential of a new purifying strategy with respect to charged microgels, where a broad distribution of charged microgels of equal size can be fractioned into several fractions with a smaller charge distribution of microgel ensembles, yet with current methods rather challenging. Such a new purification strategy might be a key requirement for the improvement of designing microgels in the micrometer range, where a precise material response is key for the targeted application, yet in many biomedical applications essential.

Experimental Section

Materials

NIPAM (N‐Isopropylacrylamide, 97%, recrystallized), AAc (Acrylic acid, 99%, anhydrous, contains 200 ppm MEHQ as inhibitor, filtered in column with silica filled), BIS (N,N′‐Methylenbis(acrylamide), 99%), APS (Ammonium persulfate, ≥98%), span 80, 1.4‐dioxane (≥99%), TEMED (N,N, N′,N′‐tetramethyl ethylenediamine, 99%) were obtained from Sigma–Aldrich. isopropanol (≥99.6%) and hexane (≥98.5%) was obtained from VWR. PDMS kit (Sylgard 184) was purchased from Downsil. HFE (Novec 7500) was provided by 3 m. Krytox FSH was obtained from Chemours. Polystyrene particles (D = 34 μm) were obtained from micromod GmbH.

Microgel Synthesis

The synthesis is performed according to work from Lorenzo et al.[ 34 ] NIPAM (93.5 mg, 95%, 0.83 mol L−1), Acrylic acid (2.98 µL, 5%, 0.04 mol L−1) BIS (3.4 mg, 0.02 mmol, 2.5 mol % based on monomer concentrations), APS (10 mg) were dissolved in water (1 mL). The mixture was degassed under Argon for 5 min and it was transferred into glass syringes. At the cross‐junction of the PDMS device, water‐in‐oil droplets were formed by flow‐focusing of the water phase exerted by the oil phase. (Figure S1, Supporting Information) For the continuous phase, fluorocarbon oil (HFE‐7500) containing 1.8 vol% of Krytox FSH was used. The following volume flow rates were used to generate emulsions w/o: water phase: 50 µL h−1, oil phase: 60 µL h−1. Droplets were collected in 2 mL of vials for 4 h and were allowed to react overnight at room temperature. On the next day, the fluorocarbon oil was removed, and the obtained microgel particles were washed with different solvents. Between washing steps, the microgels were allowed to sediment or float on the solvent, and the solvent was then removed and replaced by fresh solvents. The procedure was carried out with plain HFE‐7500 three times, with hexane containing 1 wt.% span 80 one time, with hexane three times, with isopropanol three times (with centrifuge 5000 rpm for 10 min), with 1, 4 dioxane three times (with centrifuge 5000 rpm for 10 min) and with water (MiliQ) three times (with centrifuge 8000 rpm for 10 min). Finally, microgels were redispersed in fresh water.

Device Fabrication

Microfluidic devices are fabricated by soft lithography.[ 32 ] AutoCAD (Autodesk, USA) software was used to design the microfluidic channel in a 2D drawing, which was printed on a high‐resolution (25.000 dpi) dark‐field photomask. Photolithography was applied to pattern an epoxy‐based photoresist (SU‐8) on a silicon wafer using the printed photomask as a template. Soft lithography on the patterned silicon wafer was used to produce PDMS‐based microfluidic devices. Briefly, Silgard 184 (Dow Corning, USA) kit was used to prepare a mixture containing one part curing agent and ten parts PDMS base. The mixture was poured on the previously prepared silicon wafer and excess air was removed under high vacuum for 30 min. The mixture was heated at 65 °C overnight (12 h). The cured PDMS mold was peeled off from the silicon wafer and washed with isopropanol and water three times. The PDMS mold and a glass slide were treated with oxygen plasma (100 W, 40 s) and covalently bound together via a condensation reaction. The device was heated at 120 °C, 5 min for total completion of the bonding and evaporating water generated from the condensation reaction.

Synthesis of Microgels Over for Microfluidic Devices

Microfluidic experiments were conducted on a microfluidic system consisting of two syringe pumps (PHD Ultra, Harvard Apparatus, Holiston, U.S.A.), to control the flow rates of the two different phases. An inverted microscope (Motic AE2000, TED PELLA, INC., Redding, CA) equipped with a camera (Flea3, Point Grey, Richmond, CA) was applied to observe flow and droplet formation. The PDMS microfluidic device was connected to the system using fine‐bore polyethylene tubing (0.86 mm inner diameter, 1.52 mm outer diameter). The microchannels had a rectangular cross‐section with a uniform height of 20 µm.

ζ‐Potential measurements were done with a Zetasizer Nano ZS (Malvern Analytical GmbH). For measuring the zetapotential of the microgels the electrophoretic mobility was measured at room temperature in Millipore water and aqueous solution with dissolved cationic photosensitive surfactant.[ 72 ] ζ‐potentials were calculated from the electrophoretic mobility using the Smoluchowski equation.[ 73 ] For measuring the surface ζ‐potential (SZP) of the glass interface a surface ζ‐potential cell (ZEN1020, NanoZS, Malvern Panalytical GmbH) for flat surfaces was used. The SZP cell with inserted planar glass interface was placed in a cuvette filled with an aqueous surfactant solution of desired concentration and dispersed commercial tracer polystyrene particles. The ζ‐potential of a the glass surface was calculated from the measured electrophoretic mobility of the tracer particles as a function of axial displacement from the interface (125, 250, 375, and 500 µm). This is automatically calculated by Zetasizer Nano ZS software.

Heating of Microscope Samples

The temperature was controlled using a homemade heating stage.[ 32 ] The heating stage contained an indium tin oxide (ITO) coated glass slide connected to two silver electrodes. The ITO layer was facing the bottom glass of the sample (microfluidic channel). After an internal calibration from the previous protocol[ 32 ] a potential was applied to the electrodes in a voltage range from (0–30 V) to heat precisely glass slide up to max. 60 °C. The temperature was calibrated by measuring the resistance from a PT100 resistance thermometer attached to the glass side. The rate of temperature change was 1 °C per 10 s.

Measurements of Particle Drift Motion Along a Pressure‐Driven Flow Using Video Microscopy

An inverted microscope Olympus IX73 equipped with a red LED (λ = 625 nm, M625L1‐C1 (Thorlabs GmbH) light source was used for the measurements. Data were recorded with video microscopy using a CCD camera (Hamamatsu ORCA‐Flash4.0 LT (C11440)) at 30 frames per second. Commercial microfluidic chamber μ‐slideIV with a glass bottom cover slip (Ibidi GmbH) was used.

Data Analysis

First, the raw video data from microfluidic device experiments was processed using the software package (Fiji). The entire process included a conversion into single‐frame images (2048 × 2048 pixel), with subsequent 8‐bit grayscale processing to manipulate the local contrast between the microgels and background using the normalized local contrast plugin. For the latter following parameters were set: Block radius x and y were set to 400 with a center and stretch algorithm and a standard deviation of 2.50. After contrast enhancement all single images were converted into binary pixel information using the so‐called default threshold algorithm from the software package. Then background noise reduction and black‐and‐white image quality enhancement with respect to microgels were performed using the “Fillholes,” “Erode,” and “Dilate” algorithm from the Fiji software.

In the second step, the tracking is done with self‐made python script, where object detection is done from as‐identified contours and the center of mass is calculated for each object, which further on served as location coordinate. Object tracking is calculated over minimum distance object within the next frame and yield into individual particle trajectory as well as its momentum velocity (frame‐to‐frame) by multiplying the traveled distance with the framerate, and the particle radius. Statistical analysis of individual velocities is performed over the calculation of mean, median velocities observed frame to frame including the standard deviation (see Statistical Analysis Section). The difference in behavior of median and mean thereby allows for detection of potential biases affected the mean due to extreme outliers. Second, the mean velocity of each particle over its complete trajectory is then correlated to its size (diameter). Analysis is done via python software using several software packages: Bokeh, Numpy, OpenCV‐python, Matplotlib, Openpyxl, Pandas, SciPy.
