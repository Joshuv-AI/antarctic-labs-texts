Bridging Genotypic and Phenotypic Drug Susceptibility Testing for Mycobacterium tuberculosis: A Baseline Prior to the Widespread Use of New and Repurposed Anti-Tuberculosis Drugs
Authors: Kuenyoul Park, Heungsup Sung
Published: 2026

Drug-resistant tuberculosis (DR-TB) represents a major global public health threat. In 2023, 551 individuals in Korea were notified as having multidrug-resistant or rifampicin-resistant TB (MDR/RR-TB), accounting for 2.8% of all TB cases [1]. All-oral regimens based on bedaquiline (BDQ), delamanid (DLM), pretomanid (PMD), and linezolid (LZD) have become preferred treatment options for MDR-TB [2]. Consequently, the clinical importance of accurate and rapid drug susceptibility testing (DST) has increased.

Whole-genome sequencing (WGS) can be used to characterize bacterial strains with drug resistance [3], and WGS-based genotypic DST (gDST) has been integrated into routine diagnostic workflows for TB in several countries [4, 5]. In 2024, the WHO included targeted next-generation sequencing (tNGS) as a follow-on diagnostic test for the detection of additional drug resistance after TB confirmation in its updated Module 3 guidelines [6]. However, the accuracy of catalog-based gDST is constrained by the regional and lineage composition of the evidence base. In this context, systematic gDST data on Korean M. tuberculosis isolates are relatively scarce.

In this issue of Annals of Laboratory Medicine, Kang et al. [7] report a comparative study from Korea addressing this gap. The authors compared phenotypic DST (pDST) with gDST based on WGS and Sanger-based targeted sequencing in 75 drug-resistant M. tuberculosis clinical isolates collected in Korea between 2005 and 2009. The resistance burden was substantial: 97.3% of isolates were resistant to isoniazid (INH) and rifampicin (RIF), 74.7% to moxifloxacin, and 82.7% to pyrazinamide (PZA), with 78.6% classified as pre-extensively drug-resistant. Resistance-associated mutations were detected in 98.7% of INH- and RIF-resistant isolates, confirming a high concordance between gDST and pDST for key first-line drugs other than PZA. None of the 27 isolates tested against BDQ, DLM, PMD, and LZD exhibited phenotypic resistance. The authors attributed this finding to the fact that the specimens were collected before these new and repurposed agents were widely used for MDR-TB treatment. This finding is in line with recently reported elevated minimum inhibitory concentrations (MICs) for BDQ, DLM, PMD, and LZD in 24.5%, 18.4%, 4.1%, and 4.1%, respectively, of 49 Korean isolates collected between 2017 and 2022 [8]. Hence, the 2005–2009 data from Kang et al. [7] provide a baseline that predates the selective pressure imposed by the widespread clinical adoption of these agents, and the observation that BDQ MICs were elevated in 24.5% of isolates collected in 2017–2022 highlights the need for contemporary surveillance.

A noteworthy finding was the discordance between WGS and targeted sequencing results for RIF and PZA resistance-associated variants. Two RIF resistance-associated variants (rpoB c.513_516del and rpoB c.531C>T) were detected only by Sanger sequencing and were not reported in the final WGS results. This discrepancy may partly reflect the stringent allele frequency (AF) threshold (>95% for the final report, with initial calling at ≥10%) used in WGS analysis [7]. This threshold merits contextual consideration: the first edition of the WHO M. tuberculosis mutation catalog and the CRyPTIC Consortium both used a fraction of read support (FRS) of 0.90 as the standard variant-calling threshold [9], meaning that the 95% criterion was more stringent than the already-conservative international conventions. Brunner and Fowler [9] demonstrated that lowering the FRS threshold from 0.90 to 0.05 in a dataset of 35,538 clinical isolates significantly increased the sensitivity of RIF gDST from 94.3% to 96.4% without a significant change in specificity. Furthermore, the authors noted that the 0.90 threshold, while effective at suppressing false positives from sequencing errors, may mask heteroresistance and minor resistant subpopulations [9]. For PZA, the discordance was even more pronounced: pncA mutations were detected only by targeted sequencing in 12 isolates and only by WGS in four isolates [7]. Unlike rpoB mutations clustered in a short resistance-determining region, pncA resistance variants are dispersed throughout the coding region and promoter, including insertions/deletions (indels) and large deletions [10, 11]. Targeted sequencing captures minor variants and indels that stringent AF thresholds may filter in WGS, whereas WGS detects variants outside primer-defined regions. Combined with PZA pDST limitations, this dispersed distribution explains the bidirectional discordance observed.

Dynamic changes in the classification of resistance-associated mutations over time were also observed. Kang et al. [7] compared the variants identified in their isolates against successive editions of the WHO mutation catalog and found that rpoB p.Ser450Gln had been downgraded to “variant associated with resistance—interim,” whereas gyrB p.Ser447Phe had been upgraded from “variant of uncertain significance.” Additionally, four novel katG variants and 10 pncA variants not listed in the catalog were identified [7]. Regional specificity is further illustrated by a study reporting that the patterns of gyrA/gyrB mutations mediating fluoroquinolone resistance in isolates from Korea differ from those in other regions [12]. These findings highlight the continued need for mutation databases with adequate regional and lineage representation.

Although the study by Kang et al. [7] is limited by the pre-new-drug collection period (2005–2009) and modest sample size, it has made meaningful contributions. First, it provides genotypic resistance data on first- and second-line drug-resistance mutation profiles that have been lacking for Korean M. tuberculosis isolates. Second, it reports no phenotypic resistance to BDQ, DLM, PMD, or LZD among the 27 isolates tested from that period. Third, it shows that WGS and targeted sequencing should be regarded as complementary rather than competing approaches. The high concordance between gDST and pDST for key first-line drugs other than PZA observed in this study and in other catalog-based analyses [13] has important clinical implications because culture and pDST for M. tuberculosis require several weeks. Therefore, gDST may help shorten the time to treatment initiation by supporting earlier treatment decisions. For this approach to be safely adopted, however, several elements require standardization: (1) AF thresholds for variant calling and heteroresistance detection policies, (2) interoperability of variant detection ranges between WGS and tNGS platforms, (3) consistency of reporting across WHO catalog edition transitions, and (4) external quality assessment programs based on reference materials. In particular, the AF threshold for gDST varies across studies and, given that this threshold directly affects the balance between sensitivity and specificity as well as heteroresistance detection, a consensus or guideline on AF thresholds for TB gDST is needed. The observations from this study contribute regional evidence to these globally relevant standardization discussions. Continued surveillance of M. tuberculosis isolates from diverse geographic and lineage backgrounds using WGS will be essential for guiding treatment decisions and strengthening TB control strategies worldwide.
