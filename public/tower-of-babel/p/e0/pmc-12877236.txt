Aging-related changes in protein and fat intake and their related meal types factors by longitudinal birth cohort analysis using the National Health and Nutrition Survey in Japan from 2001 to 2019
Authors: Midori Ishikawa, Tetsuji Yokoyama, Osamu Hemmi
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Aging-related changes in protein/fat intake and meal types were analyzed using a birth cohort analysis of National Health and Nutrition Survey in Japan.•Protein/fat intake initially decreased or remained unchanged with aging but increased 8–15 years later.•This finding was significantly observed in the intakes from noodle- type meals than in rice- and bread- type meals intakes.•Thus, protein/fat intake increase may be associated with noodle-type meal intake.Keywords: Ageing, Protein and fat intake, Meal type, Birth cohort analysis, National Health and Nutrition Survey, Japan

Introduction

Understanding the long-term changes in nutrient and food intake from young adulthood to older age, as well as clarifying their related dietary factors, have become increasingly important in preventing frailty, undernutrition [[1], [2], [3]], and obesity [4,5]. Various studies have examined the usual nutrient intake of individuals and the appropriateness of dietary evaluation from adulthood to older age, as well as dietary factors influencing obesity and undernutrition [3,[6], [7], [8]] and aging-related changes in nutrient intake [2,6,9]. In older age, the intake of some macronutrients, such as protein, decreases with aging [2], and high or low body mass index might be associated with the type of food groups consumed [[6], [7], [8], [9]]. Further, the excessive or insufficient intake of macronutrients such as protein and fat may be related to meal type including the combination of dishes or food groups identified by diet assessment methods [7,[10], [11], [12]].

A longitudinal research methodology assessing the change in meal type, quality, and food combination over time is needed [3,[13], [14], [15]]. Currently, related research reports remain limited. Further, the intake situation may be hypothesized to differ depending on the generation. Therefore, long-term trends in macronutrient intake with aging and aging-related changes in macronutrient intake from meal types and food groups by generation should be clarified.

Birth cohort analysis is one of the research methods used to examine this hypothesis [15,16]. The effects of aging, generation, and era on nutrient intake can be examined by tracking the same age group in a certain year (same year of birth) and analyzing intake changes by generation. In other words, the group-level variations in cross-sectional data are assessed using the birth year of each respondent, and the sociopolitical moments (e.g., changes in policy or public awareness) that can mitigate or enhance nutritional status risks are determined [17]. Identifying dietary factors associated with nutritional issues may highlight novel ways to decrease the risk factors in future prevention efforts, and may identify age effects that could exist [18].

The Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare Japan reports on the National Health and Nutrition Survey in Japan (NHNS-J), a cross-sectional survey conducted annually. In addition, the nutrient and food group intakes were evaluated by age group annually and the trends were reported over time [19,20]. However, differences in intake were assessed based on differences in the survey year, not necessarily on aging-related changes. It was hypothesized that aging-related changes in nutrient intake and their related “meal type and food group” (hereinafter called “meal type”) could be identified by birth cohort analysis using NHNS-J.

In previous studies using birth cohort analysis, several reports focused on health behavior, disease onset and mortality rates [15,16,21], but few focused on macronutrient intake and meal type. Therefore, using NHNS-J data for birth cohort analysis, this study aimed to clarify aging-related changes in the intakes of energy and macronutrients and those in the intake of macronutrients from meal type by generation.

Methods

Database used

National Health and Nutrition Survey in Japan (NHNS_J)

Details of the NHNS_J have been published elsewhere [[22], [23], [24]]. In summary, the NHNS_J began as the National Nutrition Survey after World War II, when the General Headquarters of the Allied Forces investigated the nutritional status of the civilians undergoing food shortages. Next, the survey was conducted yearly under the Nutrition Improvement Act. In 2003, under the Health Promotion Act, the survey was renamed as the NHNS_J—including the Nutritional Intake Status Survey, Physical Status Questionnaire, and Lifestyle Habits Questionnaire—to monitor various aspects of health promotion policies and assess the risk for noncommunicable diseases. NHNS_J is conducted yearly using a stratified cluster sample design to obtain a representative sample of the Japanese population. Notably, the surveys in 2012 and 2016 were conducted with an expanded sample to compare the data across 47 prefectures.

Food and macronutrient Intake (Nutritional Intake Status Survey) [19,[22], [23], [24]]

Nutritional Intake Status Survey is generally conducted on a designated day in November, excluding Sundays and public holidays. The survey uses semiweighing 1-day dietary records of households with a proportional distribution within the house [22,23]. First, trained investigators visit each household to distribute a self-administered questionnaire on food intake and to instruct the household members who are usually responsible for food preparation on how to measure food and beverage quantities and how to complete the questionnaire. Furthermore, the household representatives are instructed on how to use a scale to weigh each food and beverage item consumed in the household; they are also asked to record this information and the allocation of shared dishes across individual household members in the questionnaire. When investigators visit households to collect the survey questionnaires, they identify and fill in any missing information and correct any errors. Investigators input the dietary intake data using a software specifically developed for this survey. To calculate nutrient intake from the dietary records, the surveys use the Standard Tables of Food Composition in Japan. This table revises the number of foods and the nutrient values per 100 g for each food every 5 years [25]. Subsequently, the intake of each food item, as well as the 18, 37, and 99 foods groups, are calculated in the National Institute of Health and Nutrition (NIHN)—a research institute under the Ministry of Health, Labor, and Welfare in Japan responsible for compilation. Macronutrients (e.g., energy, protein, fat, and carbohydrates) and micronutrients (e.g., minerals and vitamins) are measured for the nutrient intake.

Database creation

We submitted an application for the provision of information on the Nutritional Intake Status Survey and Physical Status Questionnaire (2001–2019) of official statistics (National Nutrition Survey and NHNS-J) to the Ministry of Health, Labor and Welfare, based on the provisions of Article 32 of the Statistics Act. The reason why we obtained the data from 2001 to 2019 is that the NHNS_J was suspended from 2020 to 2021 because of the COVID-19 pandemic, and resumed in 2022; however, the 2022 results have not yet been published. Therefore, we utilized the data that were available up to the most recent year. After approval, we collected the necessary data for the analysis.

Using the data recorded between 2001 and 2019, we created a standard cohort table to identify variables (energy, macronutrients, food groups, age, etc.) that can track changes. Thereafter, we created a database. Pregnant and lactating women were excluded because their nutrient intake differed from those in the same age group. Regarding the NHNS_J database, the NIHN considers how to handle outliers and decides on the database annually when compiling the data [19]. Therefore, no extreme outliers were included. We used the entire database for the analysis, with no data being excluded.

Ethical considerations

Regarding the ethics approval and consent to participate for this study, the anonymity was maintained for all study participants selected from the acquired database, with no physical or mental intrusion. The obtained data were encrypted and stored on a specific computer in accordance with the information security policy of our institution. The Ethics Committee of the National Institute of Public Health (Wako City, Saitama Prefecture) waived the requirement of ethical approval (NIPH-IBRA#12378) on July 14, 2022.

Changes in energy, protein, fat, and carbohydrate intake with aging by age group from 2001 to 2019 (birth cohort analysis)

All analyses were conducted separately for men and women because the macronutrient intake varies significantly between sexes and between different age groups according to the previous NHNS_J results. We first checked the possibility of analyzing the changes over time in the distribution of energy, protein, fat, and carbohydrate intake for each year from 2001 to 2019 and then identified any discrepancies in the means and standard deviations. The sampling method for target households and the number of respondents differ between the regular survey years [19] and the expanded survey years (2012 and 2016 with a larger sample size) [19,22]. Nonetheless, energy, protein, fat, and carbohydrate intake were normally distributed in all years, with no difference in the distribution between the regular and expanded survey years. Therefore, aging-related changes were analyzed using data from all years between 2001 and 2019. The mean of energy, protein, fat, and carbohydrate intake was calculated by age group, particularly in their 20s (20–29 years), 30s (30–39 years), 40s (40–49 years), 50s (50–59 years), and 60s (60–69 years), in 2001.

Next, by the age group in 2001, the mean intake of energy, protein, fat, and carbohydrate for each year from 1 year later (2002) to 18 years later (2019) (18 points) was calculated. For example, in the age group of 20–29 years, the age group will be 21–30 years after 1 year, 22–31 years after 2 years, 23–32 years after 3 years, and 38–47 years after 18 years.

For aging-related changes in intake over time, significant differences in such changes were assessed using the analysis of variance (with multiple comparisons by the Tukey–Kramer method). The results were shown in a line graph (3-year moving average). Our target age group was up to their 60s in 2001. We excluded those in their 70s (70–79 years) in 2001, considering that they would turn 88–97 years old after 18 years (2019), and approximately 1–5 respondents were included in their corresponding age group (90s), making the analysis impossible. Supplementary Material 1 shows the demographic data of the study participants (n = 139,876); men and women, respectively, comprised 47.0% and 53% of total participants, with a mean ± SD age of 54.9 ± 14.5 and 55.4 ± 14.4 years) (Supplementary Material 1: details of the participants from 2001 to 2019 by age group).

Extraction of meal types related to changes in protein and fat intake

For men and women, the change in energy and macronutrients, especially in protein and fat intakes, was confirmed to initially decrease or remain the same with aging in almost all age groups but increased in the middle of the period. Therefore, the analysis proceeded, focusing on protein and fat intake.

First, factor analysis was performed to extract meal types and classify food groups focused on staple foods [13], using all data from 2001 to 2019. We focused on staple foods because the traditional Japanese meal pattern consists of a staple food and the main and side dishes. In the daily meals of Japanese people, the foods chosen as main and side dishes to eat with the staple food vary depending on the food type chosen as the staple food (rice, noodles, or bread). Additionally, many foods are used for preparing main and side dishes [26]. Therefore, this study conducted a factor analysis to identify how the foods that are used for the main and side dishes differ depending on the staple food type (rice, noodle, or bread meal type). For the analysis, a larger number of foods groups would allow for more appropriate classification. Thus, of the 18, 36, and 99 food groups available, the 99 food groups were used.

The Varimax method was used to rotate the identified factors by orthogonal transformation. Determining whether a factor should be retained was based on the natural interpretation of rotated factors with eigenvalues of >1 and a scree plot for the corresponding factors (i.e., a steep decline in the eigenvalue for the next factor; satisfied for three factors). After extracting three factors, 99 food groups were classified into each factor, and each factor was given a meal type name. Then, we discussed whether the food groups classified into each factor would not feel strange in daily food life. Each food group was classified into one meal type (factor), and the same food group was never included in two or more factors.

For both sexes, “rice type” comprised rice, sugars and sweeteners, soybeans, tofu, spinach, radish, Chinese cabbage, pickled vegetables, seaweeds, mushrooms, horse mackerel/sardines, tuna/billfish, squid/octopus, sake (alcohol), tea, soy sauce, and miso. “Noodle type” included wheat flour, Japanese udon/Chinese noodles, pasta, carrot, green pepper, cabbage, onion, shrimp/crab, beef, pork, chicken, eggs, vegetable oil, animal fats and oils, beer, western liquor/others, salt, and mayonnaise. In “bread type,” bread, instant Chinese noodles, tomato, vegetable juices, strawberry, banana, jam, milk, cheese, fermented milk/lactic acid beverages, butter, margarine, cakes and pastries, biscuits, candy, coffee, and cocoa drinks were included.

Changes in protein and fat intake from each meal type with aging by age group from 2001 to 2019 (birth cohort analysis)

The mean intake of energy, protein, and fat from each meal type was calculated yearly from 2002 to 2019 (1–18 years later) in the study population in 2001. First, to estimate protein and fat intake from meal type in participants aged 20s to 60s in 2001, the value of weight of each food group multiplied by the weighted average component value per 1 g of each food group was totaled. Then, the protein and fat intake amount from 2001 to 18 years later (2019) was calculated. In addition, the protein and fat intake were estimated from the sum of the estimated intakes for each of the three meal types. Subsequently, the estimated total intakes confirmed that the values were very similar to the total intakes of the original database.

Aging-related changes in protein intake, protein-to-energy ratio, fat intake, and fat-to-energy ratio from each meal type by the generational age group were shown in the graphs. Regarding these changes, Joinpoint regression analysis was conducted to observe for any slope change and determine the year when the slope changed before and after (joinpoint) by age group [15,27,28].

For statistical analysis, analysis of variance (with multiple comparisons by the Tukey–Kramer method) was conducted for aging-related changes in protein intake, protein-to-energy ratio, fat intake, and fat-to-energy ratio by meal type. Joinpoint regression analysis was also conducted by meal type.

All statistical data were analyzed using SAS software version 9.4 (SAS Institute, Inc.) and Joinpoint software version 5.0.2 (National Institute of Health, USA, Joinpoint statistical software for the analysis of trends) [27]. The significance level (P) was set at <0.05.

Results

Aging-related changes in energy, protein, fat, and carbohydrate intake

Fig. 1 shows the changes with aging in the mean intake of energy, protein, fat and carbohydrate and mean protein-to-energy ratio and fat-to-energy ratio by age group (20s to 60s) from 2001 to 18 years later in men and women. In men, energy and protein intake initially decreased or remained the same with aging in all age groups but increased in the middle of the period. Fat intake also initially remained the same with aging in all age groups but increased in the middle of the period. However, carbohydrate intake continuously decreased with aging in all age groups. All of these results were also observed in women. In addition, the changes in protein-to-energy ratio and fat-to-energy ratio alongside aging were similar to those in protein and fat intakes.

Fig. 2(2–1) shows aging-related changes in the mean intake of protein from meal type by age group (20–60s) from 2001 to 18 years later in men and women. In men, protein intake from the rice type continuously decreased with aging in all age groups, but that from noodle and bread types remained unchanged with aging for the first few years and then increased with aging. In women, similarly, protein intake from the rice type decreased with aging in all age groups, but that from noodle and bread types initially remained unchanged with aging and then increased with aging. Fig. 2(2-2) shows the age-related changes in the mean protein-to-energy ratio based on meal type by age group (20s–60s). The changes in protein intake and protein-to-energy ratio by meal type demonstrated a similar trend.

Discussion

Relationship between changes in protein and fat intake and their intake from meal types

In the NHNS-J results reported by the Ministry of Health, Labor, and Welfare, changes in nutrient intake by age group differed because of the variations in the survey year and not necessarily in aging-related change [29,30]. The present study confirmed aging-related changes in nutritional intake by generational age group using NHNS-J data. In addition, this study confirmed aging-related changes in nutrient intakes from meal types and related food group combinations [31,32]. Many studies on changes in nutrient and dietary intakes with aging focused on nutrient or food groups for disease onset and mortality rates [14,33]. This study included the importance of monitoring aging-related changes in meal types, which could be significant. Furthermore, the new findings of this study showed that the total and noodle type’s intakes of protein and protein-to-energy ratio and intake of fat and fat-to-energy ratio initially showed a decreasing trend for a while from 2001 but increased with aging significantly 8–15 years later in almost all generational groups. In other words, the increase in protein intake and protein-to-energy ratio and fat intake and fat-to-energy ratios in recent years was mainly due to those from the noodle type.

Based on Japan’s nutrition policy, many studies concentrated on the nutritional balance of meals centered on rice (the traditional Japanese staple food group) dishes for “rice type meals [34].” In addition, a good-quality meal might improve subjective well-being and health, even in older people with illness, which was also reported [35]. Based on such reports, nutrition education, such as “Eat Meal with Staple, Main, and Side Dishes,” has been promoted [34]. However, this study showed that in all generations, protein and fat intake from rice type meals continued to decline with aging, while the intake of noodle type meals increased significantly, and bread type meals tended to increase. Several studies involving Japanese people showed that eating noodles and bread positively affects fat intake. However, research on this topic, such as nutritional balance and the quality of meal type included portion sizes, is still limited; thus, further research is required [[36], [37], [38]]. Considering the type of staple food (i.e., rice, noodles, or bread) and its combination with other foods when adhering to a balanced diet is necessary.

Extraction of the meal types focusing on staple foods and the classification of food groups in Japanese people

In this study, we extracted three meal types (rice, noodle, and bread) by factor analysis focusing on staple food groups [13], which food groups were listed in each meal type. Several longitudinal studies have examined the relationship between disease or frailty prevention, meal type, dietary pattern, and nutrient intake [21,39].

Factor analysis revealed the types of staple food (i.e., rice, noodles, or bread) and combinations of food groups frequently included in these meal types [40]. The food groups classified as “rice,” “noodle,” and “bread” were reminiscent of the Japanese cuisine “Washoku,” [41,42] “pasta or ramen,” and “breakfast,” respectively. In particular, several legumes (5 food groups for men and women) and fish and shellfish (11 food groups for men; 10 food groups for women) were included under the rice type; meats (4 food groups for men; 6 food groups for women) in noodle type; and dairy products (4 food groups for men; 3 food groups for women) in bread type. For vegetables, for men and women, spinach, radish, Chinese cabbage, and pickled vegetable were classified under the rice type; carrots, green peppers, cabbage, and onions under the noodle type; and tomatoes and vegetable juices under the bread type.

The U.S. study reported that trends in high- and low-quality carbohydrates primarily reflected higher estimated energy from whole grains and reduced estimated energy from added sugars, respectively [43]. Tracking the combination of food groups classified by staple-focused meal type may be useful for identifying the changes in meals and nutrient intake commonly consumed by Japanese people.

Limitations and strengths

This study has several limitations. First, this study was not able to verify the extent to which aging-related changes in macronutrient intake were influenced by changes in macronutrient intake from meal type. In addition, although the effects of aging and generation were analyzed by tracking the same age group, the effect of the era was not investigated because the intake from the noodle type increased at the same period in all generations. Hence, further research is needed.

The second limitation pertains to the macronutrient intake. Nutrient intake was calculated using the Standard Tables of Food Composition in Japan. It revised the number of foods and the nutrient values per 100 g for each food every 5 years [25]. During the study period (2001–2019), 1,878 foods were listed in 2005 and 2010, but this number increased to 2,191 in 2015. In other words, the diversity of Japanese diets is expanding, and the variety of foods consumed (including processed foods) is increasing. Further, this study used the 99 food groups classified by the Ministry of Health, Labor and Welfare, and the food groups were classified into 1,878 or 2,191 foods. Nutrient intake may slightly differ from 2001 until 2019 because of revisions to food composition tables. However, given that the Ministry of Health, Labor, and Welfare’s report uses the same database to analyze changes in nutrient intake over time and that our study also used the same database, no major problems would be raised for nutrient intake. In addition, further analysis is needed to explore the protein and fat intake trajectories of the entire population, with calendar age as the time variable to ascertain whether the changes result from advancing age, rather than time alone.

Despite the abovementioned limitations, by conducting birth cohort analysis using the NHNS-J database, this study was able to clarify aging-related changes in protein and fat intake, protein and fat-to-energy ratios, and those from three meal types by generation. Further, this finding showed that the increase in protein and fat intake and protein and fat-to-energy ratios may be associated with the intake of noodle type meals. This study may be useful for future nutrition policy.

Conclusions

This study assessed aging-related changes in protein and fat intakes and protein and fat-to-energy ratios from meal type in Japanese through birth cohort analysis, and found that the increase of the intakes and the ratios over time observed among Japanese adults may be related to the intake of food groups from noodle type meals. Considering the type of staple food (i.e., rice, noodles, or bread) and its combination with other foods when adhering to a balanced diet is necessary.
