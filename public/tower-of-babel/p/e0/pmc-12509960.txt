Serum Neurofilament Light Chain Correlates With Clinical Severity and Predicts Mortality in Anti-IgLON5 Disease
Authors: Antonio Farina, Macarena Villagrán-García, Amna Abichou-Klich, Marie Benaiteau, Emilien Bernard, Françoise Bouhour, Virginie Desestret, Bastien Joubert, Geraldine Picard, Anne-Laurie Pinto, Lea Pons, Krzysztof Smolik, Stephane Thobois, Sophie Trouillet-Assant, Jerome Honnorat
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background and ObjectivesAnti-IgLON5 disease manifests by various neurologic symptoms, the severity of which can be evaluated using the anti-IgLON5 composite score (ICS). This study assessed the correlation of neurofilament light chain (NfL) and glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP) with the ICS and investigated these biomarkers as predictors of long-term clinical severity and mortality in anti-IgLON5 disease.MethodsPatients diagnosed with anti-IgLON5 disease at a national reference center (2016–2024) with available serum and CSF samples were included. NfL and GFAP concentrations were measured in these samples using Simoa assay Neurology 2-Plex B Kit. The severity of symptoms was classified according to the ICS, which was retrospectively evaluated at diagnosis, at last clinical evaluation, and at any other timepoint when samples were collected.ResultsThirty patients (60% male, median age 72 years) were included. Serum NfL concentration was significantly correlated with the total ICS (rho = 0.38, p = 0.025) and its partial bulbar score (rho = 0.39, p = 0.020); serum GFAP concentration was significantly correlated only with the bulbar ICS (rho = 0.34, p = 0.044). CSF NfL and GFAP concentrations were not significantly correlated with the total ICS nor any of its partial scores. Anti-IgLON5 antibody CSF titers, but not serum titers, showed a significant inverse correlation with the total ICS (rho = −0.44, p = 0.04). In 26 patients sampled <4 months after diagnosis, neither NfL nor GFAP predicted the total or partial ICS at last clinical evaluation (median 18 months after diagnosis), but serum NfL increased the risk of 1-year mortality independently of age (hazard ratio for each 10 pg/mL increase: 2.05, 95% CI [1.21–3.45], p = 0.007). In patients with bulbar involvement (n = 22), serum NfL concentration was lower than in 10 controls with bulbar amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (median interquartile range [IQR] 26 pg/mL [21–51] vs 158 pg/mL [100–340], p < 0.001) and higher than in 10 controls with bulbar myasthenia gravis (median [IQR] 15 pg/mL [8–26], p = 0.040).DiscussionIn anti-IgLON5 disease, serum NfL and GFAP are elevated and correlate with the clinical severity, especially of bulbar symptoms. In clinical practice, serum NfL could be useful for disease monitoring and to predict the risk of death.

Introduction

Anti-IgLON5 disease manifests by various neurologic symptoms, including sleep disturbances, bulbar dysfunction, gait instability, movement disorders, cognitive impairment, and neuromuscular manifestations, the severity of which can be evaluated using the anti-IgLON5 composite score (ICS).1,2 The disease hallmark is the presence of autoantibodies targeting the IgLON5 protein, with autopsy findings suggesting a transition during the disease course from autoimmune mechanisms to neurodegeneration, characterized by tau protein deposition and neuronal loss.3 This process may increase the concentration of biomarkers such as neurofilament light chain (NfL) and glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP), holding potential for disease monitoring and outcome prediction.4 One study found that in anti-IgLON5 disease, elevated serum NfL and GFAP concentrations were associated with poor outcomes.5 However, the use of the modified Rankin Scale (mRS) in this study limited the ability to precisely evaluate a possible correlation of these biomarkers with the severity of specific clinical manifestations. In this article, we assessed the correlation between NfL and GFAP concentrations and the ICS, and we investigated these biomarkers as predictors of long-term clinical severity and mortality in patients with anti-IgLON5 disease.

Methods

Patients and Biomarkers

Of all patients with anti-IgLON5 disease diagnosed at the French Reference Centre on Autoimmune Encephalitis (February 2016–March 2024),2 those with available serum and/or CSF samples were included (eFigure 1, eTable 1). Clinical and paraclinical data were retrospectively collected. The ICS was used to retrospectively identify the symptoms and classify their severity,1 which was evaluated at diagnosis, at last clinical evaluation, and at any other timepoint when samples were collected. Since death is not graded in the ICS, for patients who had died by the end of the study period, the ICS was rescored at last alive visit before death. The mRS was also retrospectively assessed at the same timepoints as the ICS. Titration of anti-IgLON5 antibodies (IgLON5-abs) in serum and CSF was performed by end point dilution using cell-based assays on HEK293 cells transfected with IgLON5-coding plasmids; titers were determined as the lowest dilution with positive signal. NfL and GFAP concentrations were measured using a digital ELISA Simoa with a commercially available 2-plex kit (Neurology 2-plex kit, Quanterix Corp, Boston, MA) on a HD-X platform. All available samples were tested, and sera and CSF taken from the same patient and at the same time were defined as paired. Sera from disease controls with bulbar dysfunction were also tested: amyotrophic lateral sclerosis ([ALS], n = 10) or myasthenia gravis ([MG], n = 10), diagnosed at the Hospices Civils de Lyon (2001–2024). These samples were collected within 4 months of diagnosis and were negative for anti-IgLON5 antibodies. The Serum NfL Reference App for the Simoa NF-light assay (HD-X) was used to calculate the age-adjusted Z-score for serum NfL.6

Statistical Analysis

Categorical variables were described by the frequency and percentage for each category; continuous variables were described by median (interquartile range [IQR]). Comparisons of the variables were performed using the Fisher exact test for categorical variables and the nonparametric Wilcoxon-Mann-Whitney test for continuous variables. A Spearman rank correlation coefficient was calculated to assess the correlations between continuous variables. To account for the influence of age, a semipartial correlation was used to examine the relationship between NfL or GFAP and ICS while controlling NfL or GFAP for the age at the time of sampling.7 In addition, to mitigate the influence of extreme outliers, we conducted a sensitivity analysis by winsorizing NfL and GFAP concentrations at the 5% level (i.e., values below the 5th percentile were set to the 5th percentile, and values above the 95th percentile were set to the 95th percentile). Age-adjusted linear and Cox regression analyses were performed to assess NfL/GFAP effects on ICS at last visit and 1-year mortality, respectively. Univariable linear and Cox regression analyses were also performed to assess the effects of IgLON5-abs titers on ICS at last visit and mortality, respectively. The performance of serum NfL and GFAP to discriminate between IgLON5 disease and ALS/MG was assessed using Receiver Operator Characteristic curves, with area under the curve (AUC) estimated using DeLong method.8 Optimal biomarker cutoffs were determined by the Youden index. Statistical analyses were performed using RStudio, version 3.4.0 (R foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). All p-values were two-tailed, and p-values < 0.050 were considered statistically significant.

Standard Protocol Approvals, Registrations, and Patient Consents

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the Hospices Civils of Lyon (N 23-5083). Informed consent was obtained from all patients.

Data Availability

Any additional data not provided in the manuscript or in the supplementary material will be provided by the corresponding author on reasonable request.

Results

Patients and Biomarkers

Thirty patients (60% male, median [IQR] age at diagnosis 72 [65–78] years), all with ≥1 serum sample and 22/30 with 1 CSF sample, were included. At diagnosis, 29 patients (97%) had involvement of ≥2 ICS domains, including sleep disorders (28/30, 93%), bulbar symptoms (26/30, 87%), movement disorders (26/30, 87%), cognition problems (21/30, 70%), and/or others (24/30, 80%; Table 1).

In patient sera (n = 35, collected a median [IQR] 2 [1–5] months after diagnosis, in 22/34 cases before any immune active treatment, and in no case after plasma exchange), the median ([IQR] NfL concentration was 25 [18–38] pg/mL, the median [IQR] serum NfL Z score was 1.64 [0.81–2.14]), the median (IQR) GFAP concentration was 141 (90–212) pg/mL, and the median (IQR) IgLON5-abs titers were 1:40,960 (1:20,480-1:81,920). In patient CSF (n = 22, collected a median [IQR] 1 [0–2] month after diagnosis, in 17/21 cases before any immune active treatment, and in no case after plasma exchange), the median (IQR) NfL concentration was 1,409 (1,128–1,872) pg/mL, the median (IQR) GFAP concentration was 7,904 (3,534–10,259) pg/mL, and the median (IQR) IgLON5-abs titers were 1/640 (1/320–1/2,560). In paired serum and CSF samples (n = 20), NfL concentrations were correlated (rho = 0.51, p = 0.018), unlike GFAP concentrations (rho = 0.37, p = 0.095; eFigure 2) or IgLON5-abs titers (rho = 0.094, p = 0.69), that were not significantly correlated.

Both serum and CSF NfL concentration were significantly correlated with the age at sampling (rho = 0.56, p < 0.001 and rho = 0.43, p = 0.037, respectively); the serum GFAP concentration, but not the CSF concentration, was also significantly correlated with the age at sampling (rho = 0.35, p = 0.037; eFigure 3). Neither NfL nor GFAP concentrations were significantly correlated with the disease duration from clinical onset at the time of sampling (eFigure 4). IgLON5-abs titers in serum and CSF were not significantly correlated with the age at sampling (rho = 0.034, p = 0.85) nor the disease duration from clinical onset (rho = −0.15, p = 0.54).

Correlation Between Biomarkers and the ICS at the Time of Sample Collection

Serum NfL concentration was significantly correlated with the total ICS (rho = 0.38, p = 0.025; Figure 1A) and its bulbar score (rho = 0.39, p = 0.020; Figure 2A); serum GFAP concentration was significantly correlated only with the bulbar score (rho = 0.34, p = 0.044; Figures 1C and 2C). Similar correlations were observed after winsorizing serum biomarkers values at the 5% level (eFigure 5 and 6). A semipartial correlation adjusting the concentration of serum biomarkers by the age at sampling led to similar results (eTable 2). In addition, serum NfL Z-score was also significantly correlated with the total ICS (rho = 0.43, p = 0.011, Figure 1B) and with the bulbar score (rho = 0.41, p = 0.015), while it was not significantly correlated with any other partial score Figure 2B. CSF NfL and GFAP concentrations were not significantly correlated with the total ICS nor any of its partial scores (eFigure 7 and 8). Serum NfL concentration was also significantly correlated with the mRS (rho = 0.34, p = 0.046; eFigure 9). Neither NfL nor GFAP were significantly correlated with the IgLON5-abs titers in serum or CSF (eFigure 10). IgLON5-abs CSF titers, but not serum titers, showed a significant inverse correlation with the total ICS (rho = −0.44, p = 0.04), while neither the CSF nor the serum titers were significantly correlated with any partial score (eFigure 11).

In 4 patients, serum and CSF samples were collected <30 days of intensive care unit (ICU) admission and/or critical illness; in these patients, serum NfL (median [IQR] 55 pg/mL [46–78]) and serum GFAP (median [IQR] 238 pg/mL [223–365]) were significantly higher compared with the rest of the cohort (median [IQR] 23 pg/mL [17–36] and 116 pg/mL [84–187], respectively), while CSF biomarkers were not significantly different between these groups (eTable 3). NfL and GFAP concentrations were not significantly different in samples taken from immune active treatments-naïve patients compared with samples taken from patients on immunotherapy (eTable 4).

Biomarker Analyses in Patients Sampled at Diagnosis

In the 26 patients sampled <4 months after diagnosis (20 with serum and CSF, 6 with only serum; 22/26 before any immune active treatment), the mortality was 35%, and the last clinical evaluation was in median (IQR) 18 (8–27) months after diagnosis. Serum NfL concentration at diagnosis was associated with an increased risk of 1-year mortality, independently of age (hazard ratio, HR for each 10 pg/mL increase: 2.05, 95% CI [1.21–3.45], p = 0.007; Figure 4A). None of the other biomarkers, including the IgLON5-abs titers in serum and CSF, significantly affected the 1-year mortality risk (eTable 5). Neither NfL nor GFAP concentration at diagnosis predicted the total or partial ICS at last clinical evaluation, (eTable 6) nor was significantly associated with sex, pattern of clinical onset, CSF pleocytosis, or brain MRI atrophy or inflammatory findings (eTables 7 and 8). IgLON5-abs titers in serum and CSF at diagnosis did not predict the total or partial ICS at last clinical evaluation (eTable 6).

Patients with bulbar involvement (n = 22) had lower median (IQR) serum NfL concentration than ALS controls (26 [21–51] pg/mL vs 158 pg/mL [100–340], p < 0.001) but a higher median (IQR) concentration than MG controls (15 [8–26] pg/mL, p = 0.040). Serum NfL Z-score was also significantly lower in anti-IgLON5 disease compared with ALS (1.70 [0.92–2.51] vs 3.45 [3.29–3.78], p < 0.001), while it was not significantly different between anti-IgLON5 disease and MG (1.28 [0.47, 1.83], p = 0.3], Figure 4B). Serum NfL concentration discriminated anti-IgLON5 disease from ALS (AUC 0.986, 95% CI [0.959–1]; optimal cutoff 58 pg/mL; sensitivity 91%, 95% CI [77–100]; specificity 100%, 95% CI [60–100]) and from MG (AUC 0.732, 95% CI [0.523–0.940]; optimal cutoff 17.5 pg/mL; sensitivity 60%, 95% CI [20–80]; specificity 82%, 95% CI [36–82]). Serum NfL Z-score discriminated anti-IgLON5 disease from ALS (AUC 1.000, 95% CI [1–1]; optimal cutoff 3.1; sensitivity 100%, 95% CI [100–100]; specificity 100%, 95% CI [100–100]), while it did not discriminate anti-IgLON5 disease from MG (AUC 0.630, 95% CI [0.419–0.840]). Serum GFAP concentration was not significantly different between patients with anti-IgLON5 disease and ALS or MG controls (Figure 4B). Of note, patients with anti-IgLON5 disease were significantly older and had a significantly longer interval from clinical onset to diagnosis compared with both bulbar ALS and MG patients (eTable 9).

Discussion

The findings of this study underscore the potential utility of serum biomarkers of neurodegeneration, particularly NfL, for disease monitoring and predicting mortality in anti-IgLON5 disease. A previous study reported elevated serum NfL and GFAP concentration in anti-IgLON5 patients compared with healthy controls and that GFAP correlated with the extent of neurologic involvement.5 In this study, serum NfL and GFAP levels were similarly elevated and correlated with clinical severity, particularly bulbar symptoms. This is clinically meaningful, as bulbar dysfunction affects ∼75%–90% of patients at diagnosis and increases the risk of mortality due to central hypoventilation, airway obstruction, and aspiration pneumonia.1,2,9 Remarkably, anti-IgLON5 patients with bulbar dysfunction had lower serum NfL concentration than ALS controls, despite being older and having a longer interval to diagnosis. This suggests that, even when accounting for these confounding factors, elevated serum NfL reliably distinguishes ALS from anti-IgLON5 disease, as previously demonstrated for other ALS mimics.10 By contrast, although serum NfL concentration was higher in anti-IgLON5 disease than in bulbar MG, the longer interval to diagnosis and, especially, the older age in the former could account for this difference. Accordingly, age-adjusted serum NfL Z-score was significantly lower in anti-IgLON5 disease than in ALS, but not significantly higher in anti-IgLON5 disease compared with MG.

Unlike serum biomarkers, neither CSF NfL nor GFAP significantly correlated with total and partial ICS scores, potentially due to the small sample size and greater interindividual variability in CSF measurements. Alternatively, this may reflect the contribution of peripheral nerve damage to clinical severity, leading to more pronounced serum NfL elevation compared with CSF.11 Furthermore, the observation that serum, but not CSF, NfL levels were higher in patients in critical condition or recently admitted to the ICU might suggest that compromised blood brain barrier integrity facilitates increased NfL leakage into the serum.

Disappointingly, serum NfL concentration at diagnosis did not predict ICS at the last clinical evaluation, challenging its role as a long-term predictor of severity as suggested by a previous study that used a nonspecific severity scale (mRS).5 However, in patients with longitudinal sera, the dynamics of serum NfL mirrored the evolution of the ICS. This highlights that the need for future studies to evaluate whether monitoring serum NfL level kinetics could provide more valuable prognostic information than a single measurement, as shown in multiple sclerosis.12-14

Importantly, high serum NfL concentration at diagnosis was associated herein with increased risk of death, consistent with prior findings.5 This suggests its potential utility for identifying patients who require interventions to prevent anti-IgLON5 disease-related mortality, which is mostly due to obstructive and/or central apnea (e.g., mechanical ventilation and/or tracheostomy), and who might benefit from aggressive immunotherapies, which are effective especially if administered within the first year after clinical onset.2,5

As observed in a previously published cohort,5 higher IgLON5-abs titers were detected in the serum compared with the CSF. However, notable differences in the present cohort included remarkably higher IgLON5-abs titers in both fluids, the absence of a correlation between antibody titers in paired serum and CSF samples, and no positive correlation between serum titers and the interval from clinical onset to diagnosis. Based on recent neuropathologic findings, IgLON5-abs deposition may precede tau accumulation and neuronal loss in brain regions affected by the disease.15 Consequently, high CSF IgLON5 antibody titers may reflect an early stage of the disease. Although no correlation was found between CSF titers and time from clinical onset, CSF titers were inversely correlated with disease severity, further supporting the association between elevated CSF titers and an initial inflammatory phase of the disease, during which substantial disability has not yet accrued.

Although this is the first study correlating serum biomarkers of neurodegeneration with a disease-specific scale (ICS) in anti-IgLON5 disease, important limitations include its retrospective design, which may have introduced inaccuracies in ICS scoring, and the small sample size of both the anti-IgLON5 disease cohort and the ALS and MG controls. Thus, the findings of the study need to be interpreted with caution and to be confirmed in larger and, ideally, prospective cohort.

In conclusion, the findings of this study support the clinical utility of measuring serum NfL in anti-IgLON5 disease for disease monitoring and to predict the risk of death.
