Ultrafiltration rate and mortality in patients undergoing extended-hours haemodialysis
Authors: Takahiro Imaizumi, Masaki Okazaki, Manabu Hishida, Nobuhiro Nishibori, Shimon Kurasawa, Toru Kondo, Fumika Kaneda, Hiroshi Kaneda, Shoichi Maruyama
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundClinical guidelines recommend maintaining ultrafiltration rate (UFR) below 10–13 ml/h/kg for patients undergoing conventional haemodialysis to reduce adverse outcomes. However, achieving this target may compromise nutritional status, particularly in underweight patients. We aimed to explore the association of the interaction between UFR and body weight with mortality.MethodsWe analysed the LIBERTY cohort data to assess the association between UFR (net ultrafiltration volume divided by session length) and mortality. We employed three multivariable Cox regression models: baseline (long-term effects), time-dependent (short-term effects), and time-averaged (cumulative effects). Contour plots examined the UFR and post-dialysis weight interactions. Dialysis and laboratory parameters were electronically collected and averaged quarterly.ResultsAmong 614 patients (mean age 62 years; 65% male) undergoing extended-hours haemodialysis, the median net ultrafiltration volume was 3.0 (IQR, 2.4–3.7) kg/session, with dialysis session length of 21 (18–24) hours/week. The median scaled and unscaled UFR were 7.4 (6.0–9.1) mL/h/kg and 434 (334–531) ml/h, respectively. Over a median follow-up of 6.2 (3.5–10.1) years, 225 patients died. The association between UFR and mortality was significantly modified by post-dialysis weight. Higher-weight patients showed increased mortality with higher UFR in baseline and time-averaged models, while lower-weight patients showed poorer outcomes with lower UFR in time-dependent models. These findings were particularly pronounced in older patients.ConclusionUFR-mortality association is significantly modified by body weight in extended-hours haemodialysis patients. These findings highlight individualized UFR management needs and potential nutritional intervention benefits for underweight patients.

INTRODUCTION

Greater interdialytic weight gain (IDWG) in patients undergoing haemodialysis is associated with poorer survival outcomes [1–4]. Rapid ultrafiltration rate (UFR) increases can exceed plasma refilling, causing intravascular volume depletion. This leads to haemodynamic instability and intra-dialytic hypotension [5–7]. High UFR may also cause myocardial stunning [8] and endotoxemia via bacterial translocation, highlighting the circulatory stress [9].

The UFR is typically calculated by dividing the volume of fluid removed during a dialysis session by either the dialysis session length alone [10] or by the product of session length and post-dialysis body weight [11]. Both shorter dialysis session length and greater IDWG independently correlate with adverse survival [12]. In conventional dialysis practice, limiting IDWG has been the primary strategy for maintaining UFR within a safe range, with dialysis session lengths typically fixed between 3 and 5 hours [13]. For underweight patients, this approach creates particularly challenging situations, as the safety margin of UFR becomes extremely narrow, often leading to worsening dialysis-related symptoms and overall health deterioration.

Extended-hours haemodialysis with a liberalized diet maintained nutritional status and skeletal muscle mass as demonstrated in our prior study [14]. This improvement may be attributed to reduced ultrafiltration volume per unit time during extended dialysis sessions, which creates room for increased dietary intake [15]. Particularly in patients >70 years old, extended-hours haemodialysis showed improved survival compared to conventional haemodialysis [16]. We demonstrated that plasma concentrations of essential amino acids were higher in patients undergoing extended-hours haemodialysis than in those undergoing conventional dialysis [17]. Thus, increased IDWG resulting from dietary intake is not necessarily associated with poor prognosis and may even lead to improved prognosis in certain populations.

Older Japanese patients on dialysis often have smaller body sizes, and applying conventional UFR indicators can severely restrict dietary intake. Existing research suggests that the relationship between IDWG and survival is potentially modified by nutritional status, with high IDWG not necessarily indicating poor outcomes, especially in patients with low serum albumin levels [18]. A previous study from the Dialysis Outcomes and Practice Patterns Study found that patients in Japan had the lowest mortality rate despite having the largest IDWG [19, 20]. Based on these findings, we hypothesized that the relationship between UFR and survival outcomes in patients undergoing extended-hours haemodialysis with a liberalized diet might be modified by body weight. This study aimed to examine the prognostic relevance of UFR across different levels of post-dialysis body weight.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Patients and data source

We used data from the LIBeralized diet Extended-houRs haemodialysis TherapY (LIBERTY) cohort. The study was conducted in the Medical Corporation Kamome Clinic, which provides daytime and nocturnal extended-hours haemodialysis, defined as a weekly dialysis session length ≥18 hours. Details have been described elsewhere [14]. Liberalized diet, as defined here, is the consumption of a diet similar to that of individuals without chronic kidney disease. Patients were offered a single regular intra-dialytic meal during each haemodialysis session with average 700–800 kcal energy, 30 g protein, 2.5–3 g salt, and 400 mg phosphate.

We included outpatients who underwent daytime in-centre haemodialysis between January 2008 and December 2017. In addition to incident patients examined in our previous study [14], this analysis included prevalent patients already receiving extended-hours haemodialysis, enabling broader assessment of UFR-mortality relationships. We excluded patients whose data were not available electronically, those on nocturnal haemodialysis, and those with follow-up periods <6 months. The study was conducted following the Declaration of Helsinki, and the protocol was approved by the institutional review board in Nagoya University Graduate School of Medicine. Written informed consent was waived as participants did not undergo invasive procedures, and the analysis was performed using anonymized existing clinical data.

Variables

We extracted demographic, laboratory, and prescription data, and dialysis conditions from electronic health records. Dialysis parameters included dialysis times per week, dialysis hours per session, pre- and post-dialysis body weight, blood flow rate, and UFR. All parameters were routinely recorded and extracted for analysis.

We summarized data on weekly dialysis hours, weekly dialysis frequency, blood flow rate, and body mass index (BMI) based on the post-dialysis body weight as quarter means. While most patients underwent dialysis three times a week, some patients underwent a fourth session every one to four weeks. Patients who regularly underwent dialysis more than three times a week were included in the ‘>3 times’ category. Time since starting dialysis is the time from the start of any type of dialysis to the start of extended-hours haemodialysis.

For prescription medicines, we summarized the number of antihypertensive agent classes, defined as the number of uses among the following six categories: α-blockers, β-blockers, calcium-channel blockers, renin-angiotensin system inhibitors (either angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors or angiotensin receptor blockers), diuretics, and mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists.

Regarding laboratory data, we calculated quarterly mean pre-dialysis values for haemoglobin (g/dl), serum creatinine (mg/dl), albumin (g/dl), potassium (mEq/l), phosphorus (mg/dl), and corrected calcium (mg/dl), which were measured monthly. Blood tests were taken at the beginning of the week before the dialysis session, as is customary in Japan. Serum albumin levels were measured using the modified bromocresol purple method. Corrected calcium was calculated using the following equation: serum calcium + (4 − serum albumin) if the albumin level was <4.0 g/dl. Single-pool Kt/V urea and normalized protein catabolism rate (nPCR) data, calculated using the formulae reported by Shinzato et al. [21], were available every 6 months.

Endpoint

All-cause mortality as the primary outcome was assessed from the electronic health record. Censoring events included discontinuation of extended-hours haemodialysis and transfer to other facilities, and administrative censoring on 31 December 2021. Survival information was also collected for patients who died within 3 months of transfer.

Ultrafiltration rate measurement

UFR has been defined as the ultrafiltration volume during haemodialysis divided by the length of the treatment session. UFR can be defined in two different ways: prescribed UFR and delivered UFR [10, 11]. Prescribed UFR is defined as IDWG (ml) divided by prescribed dialysis session length (h), which represents actual ultrafiltration volume without considering fluid addition during a haemodialysis session. IDWG was calculated as the mean of each pre-dialysis weight (kg) minus the preceding post-dialysis weight (kg) from the previous session. Relative IDWG (%) was defined by dividing IDWG by post-dialysis weight.

On the other hand, delivered UFR is defined as the net ultrafiltration volume during dialysis treatment (ml) divided by the length of the treatment session. Net ultrafiltration volume was calculated as IDWG plus total fluid intake during a dialysis session (including intra-dialytic meal consumption). Since extended-hours haemodialysis with a liberalized diet allows patients to take meals and drinks during a dialysis session [14], we therefore used the quarter mean of delivered UFR as the main exposure (Fig. 1a). UFR measures can be scaled by body weight, obtained by dividing by post-dialysis weight (kg). All parameters were routinely recorded in the electronic health records and extracted for analysis.

Statistical analysis

To summarize the baseline characteristics of study participants, continuous variables were expressed as mean (standard deviation) or median [interquartile range (IQR)] as appropriate. Categorical variables were expressed as numbers and their proportions (%).

Multivariable Cox proportional hazards models were used to examine the association between UFR and all-cause death. For survival analysis, UFR and other dialysis-related measures were updated every 3 months (Fig. 1b). Multivariable adjustment was performed using baseline variables including age, sex, location of dialysis facilities (urban or rural centres), duration of dialysis, cause of dialysis, and history of cardiovascular disease (CVD), and time-updated variables including dialysis treatment time per week, treatment-resistant hypertension (requiring three or more classes of antihypertensive agents), albumin, nPCR, corrected calcium, phosphorus, haemoglobin, and potassium levels (Fig. 1c).

We used an interaction model with continuous post-dialysis weight and unscaled UFR; the results of this analysis are presented visually in contour plots as in a previous study [22]. In this continuous model, the reference values of 60 kg post-dialysis weight and 400 ml/h unscaled UFR were chosen to correspond approximately to the median values. In the analysis, we set three different time frames for the UFR: baseline, time-dependent, and time-averaged models for the long-term, short-term, and cumulative effects on survival, respectively, to help better understand different temporal perspectives of UFR’s impact on survival. In the time-dependent models, we separated the observation periods by three months (i.e. quarters) and examined the association of the mean UFR in the previous quarter with the outcome within each quarter and aggregated the results [23]. In the time-averaged models, the mean UFR from the baseline to the previous quarter was calculated, and then the association with the outcome was examined in a time-updated manner. We performed stratified analyses by age (65 years or older) and BMI tertile (<21, 21–<24, and ≥24 kg/m2). We also performed the analyses in patients whose time since starting dialysis was <6 months to investigate whether similar associations were observed in incident dialysis patients.

In addition, we demonstrated marginal plots of predicted hazard ratios (HRs) across levels of unscaled UFR levels for three different body size scenarios: 40, 60, and 80 kg. Multivariable adjustment was performed using the same covariates in the contour plots. As a sensitivity analysis, we examined the baseline model after excluding patients who underwent more than three sessions per week and time-dependent and time-averaged models after excluding quarterly records with an average of more than three sessions per week. We also examined effect modification between IDWG and outcome by bodyweight.

We also examined the association of scaled UFR with the outcome. Assuming a non-linear relationship between scaled UFR and the outcome, we also performed restricted cubic spline (RCS) analysis with three knots at the 10th, 50th, and 90th percentiles of UFR. P values <.05 were considered to be statistically significant. Stata MP v.18.0 was used for statistical analysis. (StataCorp. TX, USA).

RESULTS

Baseline characteristics

Of the 763 outpatients who underwent in-centre haemodialysis at Kamome Clinic between January 2008 and December 2017, we excluded patients whose data were not available electronically (N = 58), those who underwent nocturnal haemodialysis (N = 56), and those whose follow-up period was <6 months (N = 35) (Supplementary Fig. S1).

The final study population comprised 614 patients with a mean age of 62 ± 14 years; 65% were male, 45% had diabetes mellitus, and 29% had a history of CVD (Table 1). The median weekly dialysis hours were 21 (IQR, 18–24) hours. The median scaled and unscaled delivered UFR were 7.4 (IQR, 6.0–9.1) ml/h/kg and 434 (IQR, 334–531) ml/h, respectively. The median IDWG was 2.5 (IQR, 1.9–3.2) kg, which was moderately correlated with delivered unscaled UFR (r = 0.48) (Supplementary Fig. S2). The median net ultrafiltration volume was 3.0 (IQR, 2.4–3.7) kg.

Follow-up and outcomes

During a median of 6.2 (IQR, 3.5–10.1) years, 225 patients died, and 156 patients discontinued extended-hours haemodialysis and were transferred to other facilities. The distributions of unscaled UFR over time remained largely unchanged overall and in all patient subsets (Supplementary Fig. S3).

Association between UFR and overall survival

The contour plots revealed distinct patterns of association between UFR, post-dialysis weight, and mortality across different time frames (Fig. 2). In both baseline and time-averaged models, higher post-dialysis weight combined with higher UFR was associated with increased mortality risk. Conversely, the combination of lower post-dialysis weight and lower UFR was associated with higher mortality risk, particularly in the time-dependent model. These associations were more pronounced in older individuals compared to younger individuals (Supplementary Fig. S4) and in patients in the lowest BMI tertile compared to higher BMI tertiles (Supplementary Fig. S5).

In patients newly started on extended-hours haemodialysis, higher UFR was associated with mortality as the baseline body weight was higher; however, the strong association of lower UFR with mortality in those with lower baseline weight was not as pronounced as in the overall results (Supplementary Fig. S6).

The marginal plots demonstrated differential impacts of UFR across body weight categories (Fig. 3). For patients weighing 40 kg, HRs increased as UFR decreased, with this effect being particularly pronounced in the time-dependent model (Fig. 3b). For patients weighing 80 kg, HRs increased as UFR increased. However, for patients with a body weight of 60 kg, the risk of death remained constant regardless of UFR level. Similar results were obtained in the sensitivity analysis (Supplementary Fig. S7) and in the association between IDWG and outcome (Supplementary Fig. S8).

Categorical and spline analyses

Using categorical analysis of scaled UFR, we found that in the baseline model, UFR <5 ml/h/kg was significantly associated with all-cause mortality {HR 1.93 [95% confidence interval (CI), 1.15–3.24]}, while other categories showed no significant association (Supplementary Table S1). In the time-dependent model, both the highest and lowest categories of UFR were associated with mortality [HR 1.86 (95% CI, 1.11–3.12)] for the highest category and HR 1.55 (95% CI, 1.07–2.26) for the lowest category). In the time-averaged model, UFR ≥9 ml/h/kg was associated with increased mortality [HR 1.60 (95% CI, 1.07–2.38)].

RCS analysis confirmed these findings (Supplementary Fig. S7). Baseline and time-dependent models demonstrated that lower UFR was associated with higher HR for death, while the risk of death increased with higher UFR regardless of the model used.

DISCUSSION

Our analysis revealed contrasting associations between UFR and mortality depending on body weight in patients undergoing extended-hours haemodialysis. For individuals with higher post-dialysis weight, increased UFR was associated with poorer survival, whereas those with lower body weight demonstrated worse outcomes with decreased UFR. Notably, this was particularly pronounced in older individuals with lower body weight. Our analytical approach used contour plots to map the interaction between post-dialysis weight and unscaled UFR, uncovering survival associations that traditional analyses could not capture. This visualization revealed two distinct patterns: increased mortality in patients with higher post-dialysis weight with increased UFR, and increased mortality in patients with lower post-dialysis weight with decreased UFR in short-term analyses. These findings suggest individualized UFR management strategies based on patient body weight. For lower-weight individuals, increasing food intake may be beneficial, whereas for higher-weight individuals, minimizing IDWG could be advisable. The combined approach of extended-hours haemodialysis and liberalized diet may be a promising option for individuals susceptible to protein-energy wasting [20, 24].

The contrasting effects of UFR across different body weights probably reflect distinct underlying mechanisms. Individuals with high post-dialysis weight may be more susceptible to congestion, possibly due to cardiac abnormalities such as LVH [25]. For these patients, higher UFR may exacerbate cardiovascular stress and compromise outcomes. Conversely, low UFR in patients with lower post-dialysis weight may serve as a surrogate for short-term health deterioration or inadequate nutritional intake. This approach could be particularly beneficial for individuals with lower post-dialysis weight who are at risk of undernutrition.

Patients on haemodialysis face nutritional challenges including protein-energy wasting, enhanced catabolism, and substantial amino acid losses during dialysis sessions (4–13 grams per session) [26]. Intra-dialytic oral nutrition including full meals or oral nutritional supplements improves protein homeostasis and nutritional indicators, particularly in patients with lower weight [27, 28, 29]. However, concerns about intra-dialytic hypotension and reduced dialysis adequacy have limited its implementation in conventional haemodialysis [30]. Extended treatment duration fundamentally addresses these concerns. The lower UFRs achievable with extended-hours haemodialysis (median 7.4 ml/h/kg in our study) minimize hemodynamic instability, while prolonged session duration ensures adequate solute clearance. These findings suggest that intra-dialytic oral nutrition should be implemented in conjunction with adequate treatment time to safely realize its nutritional benefits while avoiding traditional complications. This may explain the superior nutritional outcomes observed in extended-hours haemodialysis.

Supporting this concept, previous metabolomic studies found that extended-hours haemodialysis was associated with higher levels of essential plasma amino acids than those on conventional haemodialysis [17, 31]. Our previous study showed the 4-year BMI trajectory was significantly better maintained with extended-hours haemodialysis [17]. When interpreting these findings, the influence of residual kidney function is critical [32]. Even with minimal IDWG, it can create scenarios where low UFR does not necessarily indicate inadequate food intake.

Despite liberalized salt intake and standard dialysate sodium at 140 mEq/l, our findings showed that the median relative IDWG of 4.3 (3.4–5.3)% was within the range recommended by clinical guidelines [33, 34]. This somewhat unexpected finding suggests physiological changes unexplainable by a traditional two-compartment model for sodium equilibrium between intravascular and interstitial spaces. A third-compartment model involving sodium-sequestered storage in muscle and skin tissues may explain these observations [35]. This phenomenon requires further investigation, particularly regarding how extended-hours haemodialysis affects tissue sodium dynamics and thirst regulation.

This study has several limitations. First, our dataset lacked information on residual kidney function and urine volume. Although most incident haemodialysis patients lose residual kidney function within a year, a change associated with higher mortality [36], the rate of decline varies significantly. Patients with comorbid conditions typically experience a more rapid decline in residual kidney function [37]. Conversely, patients maintaining stable conditions within safe UFR ranges are more likely to preserve residual kidney function [32]. While some patients may have retained residual kidney function, the overall 5-year survival rate exceeding 80% suggests that even our time-dependent model likely captures outcomes predominantly after residual kidney function deterioration. Second, we were unable to compare our findings with conventional dialysis data. The Medical Corporation Kamome Clinic exclusively provides extended-hours haemodialysis, potentially introducing selection bias through patient preferences and treatment indications [38]. However, this limitation is partially mitigated in the northern facilities, which are located in rural areas where conventional haemodialysis options are limited. Finally, our study did not collect patient-reported outcomes [39, 40]. Further research is needed to assess quality of life and post-dialysis fatigue to provide a more comprehensive understanding of treatment benefits [41, 42].

In conclusion, our study demonstrated a significant interaction between unscaled UFR and post-dialysis weight associated with all-cause mortality in patients undergoing extended-hours haemodialysis with a liberalized diet. While patients with higher post-dialysis weight showed better outcomes with lower UFR values, nutritional interventions may be particularly important for this vulnerable subgroup. These findings provide valuable insight into the practical application of extended-hours haemodialysis as an alternative regimen for populations vulnerable to frailty. Future research should focus on developing individualized UFR management protocols and investigating the mechanisms underlying tissue sodium dynamics in extended-hours haemodialysis.
