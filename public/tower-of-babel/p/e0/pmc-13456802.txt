Atypical glomus tumors of the lower extremity: a case report of two patients

Introduction

Glomus tumors are rare mesenchymal neoplasms originating from a specialized neuromyoarterial glomus body that regulates cutaneous blood flow and thermoregulation[1]. They account for 1–5% of hand tumors and 1–2% of all soft tissue tumors[2,3]. Although classically located in the subungual region of digits, these tumors may rarely arise in other locations of the upper extremity, including the wrist and forearm, and have also been reported in extradigital and deep locations, including the lower extremity and deep neurovascular structures[4,5].

The etiology of glomus tumors remains uncertain. Most cases are sporadic, although familial glomangiomas are linked to genetic mutations in the glomulin gene[6,7]. Several mechanisms have been proposed to explain the characteristic pain, including cold-induced vasodilation, mast cell degranulation, and small unmyelinated nerve fibers within the tumor[8]. The classic triad of paroxysmal pain, pinpoint tenderness, and cold hypersensitivity is typical of subungual tumors but may be incomplete or absent in extradigital lesions, contributing to diagnostic delays[1,4].

The current classification distinguishes benign glomus tumors from glomus tumors of uncertain malignant potential (GT-UMP) and malignant glomus tumors, the latter being associated with atypical features such as large size, deep location, or increased mitotic activity[2,5]. Given the severe pain these tumors produce despite their small size and the possibility of malignant transformation, prompt recognition and definitive treatment remain essential.

The study at hand reports two cases of missed glomus tumors in atypical locations of the lower extremity. To our knowledge, there is a scarcity of literature reporting these findings, hence the theme of this case report and the importance of being vigilant in diagnosis and treatment. Both oral and written informed consent were obtained regarding the case study and future publications, and the work has been reported in line with the SCARE criteria[9].

Case presentation

Patient A is a 51-year-old hypertensive male complaining of anterior right hip pain of 12 years' duration. The thigh pain was nonspecific and became refractory to pain killers. Over the years, it was not just related to activities but became more severe during the 3 years prior to presentation, interfering with ambulation. X-ray was negative, and MRI of the spine did not show a disc or nerve compression as the cause of right thigh weakness. Nerve conduction studies and electromyography did not show evidence of neuropathy. On presentation to our clinic, he had right thigh tenderness with painful hip range of motion. Hip MRI showed a 0.8 × 1.3 cm intermediate signal intensity lesion in the anterolateral aspect of the right hip, anterior to the intertrochanteric area, medial to the gluteus medius muscle, and lateral to the iliacus muscle, suspicious for a nerve sheath tumor or vascular malformation (Fig. 1). The decision to proceed with surgical excision was discussed with the patient and their family after explaining the benefits and risks of the procedure. Based on MRI localization of the lesion in the anterolateral aspect of the hip, a limited anterior approach to the proximal femur/hip was used. A longitudinal skin incision was made, centered over the palpable area of maximal tenderness and projected over the interval between the tensor fascia lata/gluteus medius laterally and the sartorius/rectus femoris-iliopsoas medially. The deep fascia was incised in line with the skin incision, and the intermuscular plane was developed carefully by blunt dissection. The lesion was approached by gentle separation of the soft tissues medial to the gluteus medius and lateral to the iliacus, corresponding to its location on the preoperative MRI. Once the mass was identified in the soft tissue plane anterior to the intertrochanteric region, it was circumferentially dissected free from surrounding tissues and excised en bloc. The lesion was macroscopically dark blue in color, smooth, firm but compressible (Fig. 2). Histopathology revealed a tumor consisting of uniform, small, round to oval cells with a cookie-cutter appearance and pale eosinophilic cytoplasm. It was highly vascular and stained positive for smooth muscle actin but negative for chromogranin, synaptophysin, or S-100, consistent with a benign glomus tumor. Following excision, the patient's symptoms improved until the last follow-up at 1 year, during which they had regained full hip range of motion and strength with physical therapy assistance; no recurrence was recorded. Patient B is a 48-year-old healthy male presenting to the orthopedics clinic with 5 years of medial left knee pain. He was ambulatory and performing all daily activities but experienced persistent pain despite the use of nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs and physical therapy. Initial knee X-ray and MRI performed 5 years prior were normal, and the patient continued to require pain killers. At our clinic, the physical exam was unremarkable for instability, joint line tenderness, crepitation, or range of motion limitation, and the patient denied any previous trauma; nonetheless, there was noticeable quadriceps weakness and atrophy compared to the right leg, and there was focal deep pain overlying the adductor canal medially with localized hyperesthesia. Repeat MRI revealed a 1 × 1 cm intermediate signal intensity lesion in the subcutaneous fat over the adductor canal on axial T1-weighted imaging that showed enhancement with slightly irregular contours on fat-saturated T1-weighted images after gadolinium contrast. A coronal T2 gradient echo sequence demonstrated a hyperintense lesion with a chemical shift artifact appearing as a low signal band at the inferior edge (Fig. 3). Given the imaging findings and persistent symptoms, the decision to proceed with surgical excision was agreed upon with the patient and family. The mass was marginally excised and sent for histologic examination. The lesion, macroscopically, was red in color, smooth, sharply circumscribed, and compressible. On histopathology, it consisted of a mass of polygonal cells that were well encapsulated with areas of cystic changes (stain: hematoxylin and eosin; original magnification, 10×). The cells were monotonous and cuboidal; they had rounded nuclei and eosinophilic cytoplasm (stain: hematoxylin and eosin; original magnification, 40×) (Fig. 2). After excision, the pain tremendously improved, and the patient regained quadriceps strength by 18 months with physical therapy. He was followed for 15 years without signs of recurrence.

Discussion

Glomus tumors typically arise in regions rich in glomus bodies, especially the subungual areas of the digits[1]. However, extradigital tumors comprise a large portion of cases, with some series reporting that over 60% occur outside the digits[8,10,11]. Atypical locations include the forearm, wrist, volar pulp, thigh, pretibial region, lateral leg, and medial knee, as well as deeply seated or neurovascular sites such as the brachial plexus[4,5,12,13]. Visceral locations have also been documented[6]. Superficial tumors are generally sub-centimetric, whereas deep lesions may reach several centimeters and may exhibit atypical features[6].

Clinically, patients often describe severe focal pain, paroxysmal in nature, and disproportionate to tumor size[1]. Pinpoint tenderness is highly characteristic, though cold sensitivity may be absent in extradigital tumors. Diagnostic delays often extend over several years, with average wait times reported to be between 5 and 7 years[4]. Missed glomus tumors are frequent, particularly when symptoms present in locations attributed to radiculopathy, tendinopathy, neuromas, or psychiatric etiologies[4,12]. Physical examination cues, including Love’s pin test, Hildreth’s test, and the cold sensitivity test, assist in subungual glomus tumor diagnosis; nonetheless, they are often incomplete in extradigital tumors, where cold hypersensitivity is frequently absent, and the triad may be reduced to isolated point tenderness[1,4,8].

Ultrasound, as an initial modality, can reveal a well-demarcated, hypervascular nodule and is both rapid and cost-effective[3]. MRI is traditionally the most sensitive modality for detecting glomus tumors and demonstrates a well-circumscribed, T1-hypointense, T2-hyperintense lesion with intense gadolinium enhancement[1]. False-negative MRI studies are not infrequent, particularly in extradigital or very small lesions, and a negative MRI does not exclude the diagnosis when clinical suspicion remains high[1,12]. Plain radiographs may demonstrate characteristic scalloping or erosion of adjacent bone in subungual or intraosseous presentations[6]. Despite advances in imaging, clinical suspicion remains the cornerstone of diagnosis, particularly for extradigital tumors where classic features may be absent.

The differential diagnosis is broad and includes schwannoma, traumatic neuroma, hemangioma, leiomyoma cutis, melanocytic lesions, and venous malformations[8,12]. Intraosseous lesions may mimic osteoid osteoma, enchondroma, epidermal inclusion cysts, or metastasis[14]. Histologically, glomus tumors consist of uniform, round, or polygonal glomus cells arranged around vascular structures in a characteristic perivascular pattern[6]. Atypical and malignant variants are rare. Criteria associated with malignancy include size greater than 2 cm, deep location, atypical mitotic figures, high nuclear grade, or increased mitotic rate, as described by Folpe and colleagues[15].

Surgical excision is the definitive treatment for all glomus tumors, providing reliable and often immediate pain relief[1,3]. Various approaches are used depending on the location, especially for extradigital lesions. Large or infiltrative tumors may require extended resection or reconstruction, and adjuvant radiation has been used in GT-UMP involving deep neurovascular structures, such as the brachial plexus[5]. Recurrence rates range from 12% to 33% due to incomplete excision[3,7].

Conclusion

Extradigital glomus tumors, particularly in the lower extremity, remain a diagnostic challenge due to their atypical locations, refractory nature, and frequently incomplete clinical triad. These two cases demonstrate that prolonged, unexplained focal pain – especially when disproportionate to examination and imaging findings – should prompt consideration of a glomus tumor even in anatomically uncommon sites of the lower extremity. MRI may aid in diagnosis, but clinical suspicion is paramount. Complete marginal excision remains curative and provides dramatic symptom relief. Increased awareness of these rare presentations can reduce diagnostic delays, prevent unnecessary investigations, and improve patient outcomes.
