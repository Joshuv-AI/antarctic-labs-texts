A new hybrid echocardiography and arterial pressure waveform approach for non-invasive reconstruction of the entire left ventricular pressure waveform
Authors: Coskun Bilgi, Rashid Alavi, Jiajun Li, Wangde Dai, Abdallah Elkhal, Ray V Matthews, Robert A Kloner, Niema M Pahlevan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimsNon-invasive estimation of left ventricular pressure (LVP) is crucial for managing cardiovascular diseases such as heart failure and myocardial infarction (MI). Current clinical practices rely on invasive catheterization, limiting its feasibility for routine or longitudinal monitoring. This study evaluates the accuracy of a novel LVP reconstruction algorithm in preclinical (rat) experiments.Methods and resultsUsing a standard coronary occlusion/reperfusion model (n = 39 rats), we validated our algorithm across three physiological states: baseline, myocardial ischaemia, and MI. LVP waveforms were reconstructed using only carotid pressure waveforms and echocardiographic measurements. Algorithm performance was assessed by comparing reconstructed LVP waveforms to invasively measured LVP, using key haemodynamic metrics such as left ventricular end-diastolic pressure (LVEDP) and the subendocardial viability ratio (SEVR). Agreements between waveforms were assessed using intraclass correlation coefficients (ICC), normalized Euclidean distance (NED), and differences in harmonic modulus. The algorithm accurately estimated LVEDP across all physiological states (mean absolute error: 1.5 mmHg), with strong correlation to invasively measured LVEDPs (r = 0.91). Predicted SEVR also showed strong agreement with measured values (r = 0.96). The algorithm captured the expected LVEDP elevation and SEVR reduction during myocardial ischaemia, and the metric's partial recovery after reperfusion. Waveform-level agreement demonstrated near-perfect alignment, with high ICC (98.5%), low NED (0.062), and minimal harmonic modulus differences (0.043) for all tested cases.ConclusionThis study demonstrates that LVP can be accurately reconstructed using the proposed algorithm in rats. Our algorithm reliably captured key LVP metrics and waveform features across varying physiological states, supporting its potential for cardiac monitoring.

Introduction

The left ventricular pressure (LVP) waveform provides critical insights into ventricular function and disease states such as heart failure (HF), making it an essential tool for both clinicians and cardiovascular researchers.1–3 The gold-standard method for measuring LVP is invasive catheterization; however, the procedural complexity and inherent risks limit its use in routine clinical and experimental settings.4 As a result, considerable efforts have been directed towards developing mathematical models to reconstruct LVP non-invasively using accessible physiological inputs.5–7

A widely adopted mathematical approach for reconstructing LVP involves the use of the LV elastance model, which imposes a predefined pressure waveform shape and estimates its parameters based on measurable haemodynamic quantities such as brachial or aortic pressures, aortic flow, or valvular events.5,7,8 However, these models face key limitations, particularly during the diastolic phase when the LV becomes decoupled from the arterial system. Elastance-based models often rely on empirical assumptions that result in fixed LV end-diastolic pressure (LVEDP) values or significantly overestimated systolic pressures. Such oversimplifications diminish the model’s sensitivity to pathophysiological changes and restrict their clinical relevance, especially in the assessment of HF.6,9,10

To overcome these limitations, we previously developed a five-step LVP reconstruction algorithm that does not rely on the elastance model, thereby avoiding fixed assumptions about LVEDP and eliminating dependence on predefined waveform shapes.11 Our algorithm integrates non-invasive carotid pressure waveforms and basic cardiac imaging inputs with physiological boundary conditions and a physics-inspired ventricular filling model to reconstruct the LV pressure waveform. In a recent study, we applied this method to a human cohort comprising both healthy individuals and HF patients, demonstrated the model’s sensitivity by generating LV pressure–volume (PV) loops, and derived key metrics to evaluate ventricular function.11

While this analytical method has shown promise in non-invasively capturing LV dynamics in humans, this study provides the first direct invasive validation of the algorithm in an acute preclinical model. We tested the algorithm using a standard coronary occlusion/reperfusion protocol in 39 rats, comparing model-predicted LVP waveforms against high-fidelity invasive measurements. Simultaneous carotid artery and LV pressure recordings were obtained using high-fidelity catheters, while cardiac imaging was conducted via echocardiography. The experimental protocol includes three distinct physiological states: baseline healthy conditions, 15 min after coronary artery occlusion for myocardial ischaemia, and 3 h following reperfusion of the coronary artery for the myocardial infarction (MI) case. LVP was reconstructed at each state using carotid pressure waveforms and echocardiographic measurements. To evaluate the algorithm’s performance, we compared key haemodynamic metrics obtained from the reconstructed and invasively measured LVP waveforms, including left ventricular end-diastolic pressure (LVEDP), peak systolic pressure, end-systolic pressure, and the subendocardial viability ratio, known as the Buckberg index.12 In addition to the pressure-based comparisons, we assessed the overall waveform agreement using intraclass correlation coefficients (ICC), normalized Euclidean distance (NED), and differences in harmonic modulus. These metrics quantified how accurately the reconstructed waveforms reflected the original waveform’s shape, amplitude, timing, and frequency content. All comparisons were performed across baseline, ischaemia, and infarction states to assess the algorithm’s accuracy and robustness under varying physiological conditions.

Methods

Preclinical data

In this study, we used preclinical data from a subset of animals enrolled in our retrospective MI studies, all of which followed the same myocardial ischaemia/infarction procedure. These studies consist of acute MI diagnostics via intrinsic frequencies,13 and acute MI-NAD study. Specifically, we included rats with successful, simultaneous recordings of LV pressure, carotid artery pressure, and transthoracic echocardiography. This subgroup comprised 39 Sprague–Dawley rats (11 females; age: 7 ± 1 weeks) undergoing standard coronary occlusion/reperfusion model surgeries.13

All procedures in this study were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committees at Huntington Medical Research Institutes and were performed in accordance with the NIH guidelines for the care and use of laboratory animals (NIH publication No. 85-23, National Academy Press, Washington, DC, revised 2011).

Standard coronary occlusion/reperfusion rat model

We used the standard coronary occlusion/reperfusion model on fully anaesthetized rats to induce myocardial ischaemia and infarction. Full anaesthesia was achieved using intra-peritoneal ketamine and xylazine based on the animal’s weight (intra-peritoneal ketamine 75 mg/kg body weight and xylazine 5 mg/kg). After a neck incision, we isolated the carotid artery and jugular vein at each side for catheter placements. Left ventricle catheterization was conducted through the right carotid artery, while another catheter was placed to the left carotid artery for simultaneous pressure measurements. Then, left thoracotomy was performed to expose the heart where the chest cavity was opened through an incision in the fourth left intercostal. The pericardium was gently removed to expose the anterior surface of the LV. The proximal left coronary artery was subsequently isolated and mechanically occluded for 30 min to ensure infarction.14–16 Then, coronary artery reperfusion was performed for 3 h. Along the surgical procedures, Echocardiography was conducted at baseline (prior to occlusion), 15 min after occlusion (when most cells in the risk region are ischaemic but infarction is not yet well developed), and 3 h after reperfusion following a 30 min coronary artery occlusion (here the MI is well delineated using standard triphenyl tetrazolium chloride staining) to collect the necessary parameters of the algorithm at each physiological state. More details regarding the surgical procedure and experimental analysis are provided in Supplementary data online, Supplement A.

Successful simultaneous pressure (both LV and carotid) and echocardiography measurements were available for 26 baseline cases, 25 occlusion cases, and 20 reperfusion cases, totalling 71 cases for the reconstruction algorithm to be tested.

Invasive haemodynamic measurements

Continuous invasive haemodynamic measurements and general tracings were obtained throughout the procedure, from the induction of anaesthesia to the end of the reperfusion period. Left ventricular pressure, carotid pressure, volume, ECG signals (recorded using 29-gauge needle electrodes), and body temperature were continuously monitored and recorded. Pressure waveforms were measured using 2F high-fidelity piezo-tipped micro-manometer catheters (Millar Mikro-Tip® rat PV catheter), which were calibrated prior to each experiment. The body temperature was maintained and monitored using a rectal thermocouple probe. Data acquisition and analysis were conducted using LabChart-Pro software (ADInstruments, Ltd) via a PowerLab 4/35 system (ADInstruments, Ltd).

Echocardiography

Cardiac imaging was performed using a Philips iE33 E cart echocardiography system with a high-frequency probe suitable for small animal imaging. For each imaging session, three types of echocardiographic data were collected: (i) B-mode for visualizing overall cardiac structure at parasternal long- and short-axis planes, (ii) M-mode at the parasternal long-axis plane, and (iii) pulse-wave Doppler waveforms.

The echocardiography images were manually digitized using the open-source browser-based tool, WebPlotDigitizer (v5.2, https://automeris.io/). The upper and lower echogenic bands in the M-mode images were manually traced, corresponding to the anterior and posterior left ventricular walls. Points representing the endocardial (inner) and epicardial (outer) boundaries of each wall were extracted to generate time-resolved wall motion profiles. In addition, to quantify longitudinal motion of the LV base, the long-axis B-mode echocardiographic videos were digitized frame by frame. The distance between the apex and the mitral annular plane was tracked over time for the LV base displacement profile. Isovolumetric relaxation time (IVRT) was quantified from Doppler echocardiography by identifying the time interval between aortic valve closure and mitral valve opening on the transmitral Doppler flow waveform. These temporal landmarks were manually annotated on each Doppler image to derive IVRT for each case.

Left ventricular pressure reconstruction algorithm

We applied our previously established five-step left ventricular pressure reconstruction algorithm, which utilizes the carotid pressure waveform (Pcarotid) along with left ventricular imaging obtained via echocardiography.11 Figure 1 summarizes the methodology and framework of the reconstruction algorithm and illustrates preclinical experiments. The first step of the algorithm models the pressure decay at end-systole by extending the arterial pressure waveform from the end-systolic inflection point to the dicrotic notch. The second step in the left ventricular pressure reconstruction process models the pressure decay over time using an exponential decay function, commonly referred to as the relaxation time (τ) method.17 The third step is the ventricular filling algorithm, which models the ventricle as a thick-walled viscohyperelastic cylinder that inflates during diastole by simultaneously elongating and expanding radially. This approach associates the geometric changes (e.g. wall thickness, radial displacement) of the myocardial wall and its material properties (e.g. stiffness) to the ventricular pressure change during the filling period, similar to Laplace’s law used to estimate pressure in the lungs.18 The material properties are estimated individually for each animal and physiological state using an optimization scheme that enforces smooth continuity of the pressure waveform. In Step 4, the systolic upstroke during pre-ejection period is reconstructed by smoothly connecting the pressure waveform from end-diastole to the opening of the aortic valve. The final step is the equality of the arterial pressure waveform to the LV when the aortic valve is fully open. The summary of the pressure reconstruction algorithm is provided in Algorithm 1. Comprehensive mathematical details and governing equations corresponding to each step are provided in Supplementary data online, Supplement B. Waveform-to-waveform comparison analysis

The waveform-to-waveform quantitative agreement between the predicted and measured waveforms were evaluated using ICC, NED, and difference of harmonics modulus.19–21 ICC was computed using a two-way random effects model with absolute agreement. ICC values closer to 1 indicate excellent waveform agreement. NED was used to measure overall waveform dissimilarity by normalizing waveform amplitudes. A NED value of 0 represents a perfect match between waves, and a value lower than 0.1 indicates very similar morphology. Difference of modulus of harmonics analysis evaluates the agreement of the waves in the frequency domain. A higher overlap of the modulus spectrum between measured and reconstructed waveforms represents better alignment of the harmonics, reflecting preserved dynamics. Further details on waveform-to-waveform comparison analysis and methods are provided in Supplementary data online, Supplement C.

Haemodynamic analysis

The accuracy of the reconstructed pressure waveforms was also evaluated by comparing common haemodynamic metrics derived from the predictions to those obtained from invasive catheter-based measurements across the three physiological states: baseline, myocardial ischaemia, and infarction. Various haemodynamic parameters and indices, such as LV end-diastolic pressure, LV systolic pressure, systolic pressure-time index (SPTI), diastolic pressure-time index (DPTI), and subendocardial viability ratio (SEVR) were used to analyse and compare the LVP waveforms (see Table 2). SPTI, DPTI, and SEVR were calculated from both measured and reconstructed pressure waveforms using the method described by Salvi et al.22 SEVR, computed as the ratio of DPTI to SPTI, represents the balance between oxygen supply and demand and serves as an index of subendocardial perfusion. These comparisons assess the accuracy of the model in estimating clinically important metrics without direct invasive pressure recordings.

Statistical analysis

The strength of association between haemodynamic metrics of the reconstructed and measured waveforms was assessed by Pearson’s correlation coefficient (r) and its corresponding p-value (calculated for each parameter and condition). We also used the limits of agreement (LoA) analysis23 where LoA is defined as the mean difference between the measured and predicted values ± 1.96 times the SD of the differences.

The clinical utility of the proposed reconstruction algorithm was evaluated by comparing key haemodynamic metrics obtained from the reconstructed and invasively measured left ventricular pressure waveforms. Table 2 presents the mean ± SD and range for the groups of baseline, ischaemia, and infarction states for each metric: LVEDP, systolic pressure, end-systolic pressure, systolic pressure-time integral, diastolic pressure-time integral, and the subendocardial viability ratio. The agreements are assessed by Pearson’s correlation coefficient (r-value) and the limits of agreement with mean difference. P-values associated with the correlation coefficient are included to indicate the significance of the correlations.

LVEDP derived from reconstructed waveforms closely matched catheter-based measurements across all physiological conditions. Mean LVEDP values from measured waveforms were 7.8 ± 3.1 mmHg (baseline), 11.3 ± 3.6 mmHg (ischaemia), and 5.0 ± 2.7 mmHg (infarction), compared to 7.1 ± 3.2 mmHg, 11.1 ± 4.9 mmHg, and 4.8 ± 2.7 mmHg from reconstructed waveforms. The limits of agreement were observed to be narrow (<4.35 mmHg) across all physiological states, with minimal bias (maximum: −0.71 mmHg), indicating strong agreement without systematic deviation. The algorithm reliably reproduced the expected elevation in LVEDP during coronary occlusion and its reduction following reperfusion. Similar agreement was observed for left ventricular systolic pressure, with closely aligned mean values across all states. Pressure–time indices, SPTI and DPTI, calculated from the reconstructed waveforms were strongly correlated with those derived from catheter-based measurements (r > 0.91 in all physiological conditions). Consequently, the subendocardial viability ratio (SEVR, also known as the Buckberg index12) was consistently estimated across physiological states. Notably, the algorithm captured the reduction in SEVR during ischaemia and its partial recovery following reperfusion, mirroring trends observed in the reference data.

The accuracy of the algorithm in estimating LVEDP was assessed using scatter and Bland-Altman plots (Figure 3). In the scatter plot (Figure 3A) with a total of 71 cases, predicted LVEDP values showed strong overall agreement with invasive measurements (r = 0.91). Blue, red, and black markers denote baseline, ischaemia, and infarction conditions, respectively. The results show that data points are clustered closely around the ideal line (perfect accuracy), with minor underestimation observed at higher LVEDP values during the occlusion phase.

Discussion

This study demonstrates the utility of our novel non-invasive LVP reconstruction algorithm by directly validating its accuracy against invasive catheter-based pressure measurements in a controlled preclinical rat model. While the same algorithm has previously been applied in humans using non-invasive inputs in chronic heart-failure patients,11 that study lacked invasive validation to confirm accuracy. In contrast, the current study employs a controlled preclinical model to directly assess the algorithm’s performance in acute physiological states, including myocardial ischaemia and infarction. The use of such a rat model allows for controlled experiments to assess the algorithm's performance across distinct physiological states, including baseline, acute myocardial ischaemia following coronary occlusion, and established MI during the post-reperfusion recovery.13,24

To streamline clinical translation, the reconstruction algorithm was designed to use only non-invasive inputs: a calibrated carotid pressure waveform and routine cardiac imaging data to capture key LV geometric and timing parameters. While this study used invasive carotid pressure measurements, these waveforms can also be obtained non-invasively (e.g. tonometry or smartphone-based methods25) supporting future outpatient or home-based implementation. The accurate reconstruction of LV pressure and key metrics such as LVEDP and SEVR across baseline, ischaemia, and infarction states in a controlled preclinical model (invasive validation) strengthens confidence in its clinical potential, while the non-invasive nature enables accelerated implementation in outpatient workflows or even as an at-home cardiovascular monitoring tool.

LVEDP is a key cardiovascular marker of diastolic function, critical for guiding HF management and risk stratification.26 The accurate estimation of LVEDP across baseline, ischaemic, and infarcted states highlights the clinical promise of our proposed algorithm for non-invasive pressure assessment. Unlike prior models that rely on fixed assumptions or generalized parameter values,6,27,28 our approach dynamically adapts to physiological changes by optimizing material properties during ventricular filling. This adaptability, supported by tracking LVEDP elevation during acute coronary occlusion,24 is particularly important for longitudinal monitoring in dynamic haemodynamic states. Considering the invasive nature of direct LV pressure measurement, a non-invasive method for robust estimation of LVEDP offers significant advantages for both acute and chronic disease management. However, we acknowledge that in acutely decompensated or haemodynamically unstable patients, invasive monitoring remains the clinical standard and would not be replaced by this approach. The principal utility of the proposed method lies in sub-acute, chronic, or outpatient settings, where patients may go long intervals without imaging or haemodynamic assessment, resulting in limited insight into evolving haemodynamic changes. In these contexts, on-demand estimation of LV pressure (particularly LVEDP) could complement routine clinical and echocardiographic evaluation, enabling earlier therapeutic adjustments prior to overt clinical deterioration. These findings support the potential integration of the algorithm into future clinical applications, where it may serve as an adjunct to echocardiography or cardiac MRI, enabling evaluation of filling pressures without additional burden to the patient.

In addition to LVEDP, the reconstruction model’s ability to accurately estimate indices such as SEVR, SPTI, and DPTI reinforces its clinical utility by capturing the dynamics of myocardial oxygen supply and demand. These metrics, particularly SEVR, are clinically relevant in ischaemia, where impaired subendocardial perfusion often precedes ventricular dysfunction. The algorithm’s capacity to replicate the characteristic drop in SEVR during coronary occlusion and its partial recovery following reperfusion highlights the potential in longitudinal haemodynamic monitoring.

The algorithm’s consistent performance across physiological states was further supported by detailed assessments of waveform morphology. ICCs, NED, and harmonic modulus analyses demonstrated strong agreement between reconstructed and catheter-derived LV pressure waveforms, indicating alignment in overall shape, timing, amplitude, and frequency content. This fidelity suggests that the model not only provides accurate pressure estimates but also preserves the morphological features of the waveform using only carotid pressure and basic cardiac imaging.

Overall, the proposed algorithm offers a robust method for estimating the full LV pressure waveform, excelling in predicting pressure-based metrics across varying physiological states. Its ability to track LVEDP and SEVR with high accuracy is invaluable in clinical care of HF and MI,29–31 serving as a low-cost, scalable tool for longitudinal assessment as an alternative to the traditional invasive measurement techniques. When combined with emerging artificial intelligence (AI)-based tools for automated image segmentation and quantification,32–34 the proposed method could enable instantaneous estimation of left ventricular pressure directly from routinely acquired cardiac imaging, together with a brachial pressure measurement.35 The algorithm, being fully analytical and physiologically grounded, may also serve as a valuable reference model to improve the accuracy and generalizability of physics-based machine-learning approaches aimed at enhancing non-invasive haemodynamic assessment.36–38 Furthermore, it could substantially enhance the calculation of novel echocardiographic indices such as myocardial work (MW),39,40 which estimate LV pressure throughout the cardiac cycle using empiric, population-based reference curves.28 Incorporating individualized pressure waveforms reconstructed by this method could refine MW metrics and other pressure-dependent clinical indices to improve their diagnostic precision.

Limitations and future work

The reconstruction algorithm relies on assumptions that there are no significant valvular diseases and, as such, cannot be applied in cases where left ventricular and carotid pressures differ substantially, such as in aortic stenosis. Baseline testing was performed on young, healthy rats, and the algorithm was evaluated only under acute ischaemia and MI conditions. Future studies are needed to validate our algorithm in older rats and as well as other cardiovascular pathologies (e.g. using spontaneously hypertensive rats) or chronic conditions involving LV remodelling, to assess its generalizability more comprehensively. Moreover, since the experiments did not include rats with substantially elevated LVEDP values (>20 mmHg), the algorithm should also be tested under high-LVEDP conditions to evaluate its robustness. Notably, in our previous study, the algorithm was able to generate elevated LVEDP values in a clinical sample with chronic cardiovascular conditions,11 although invasive validation was not available for those patients. As a future direction, we aim to adapt the study protocol to a clinical setting, where the algorithm can be validated using invasive LV measurements in humans. While this study was conducted in a controlled preclinical model, future work should assess the diagnostic utility of reconstructed LV pressure waveforms in clinical scenarios where LVEDP estimation is critical (e.g. HFpEF, unexplained dyspnoea) or where echocardiographic evaluation is technically challenging (e.g. chronic ventricular remodelling, atrial fibrillation, severe mitral regurgitation). In addition, the algorithm has not yet been tested in patients with significant valvular disease, another setting where it could be particularly valuable. Further studies should evaluate the method’s longitudinal responsiveness and its ability to track dynamic changes in LV pressure, particularly during chronic remodelling or in response to therapeutic interventions. Although the current study utilized invasively measured carotid pressure waveforms, such waveforms can be obtained non-invasively e.g. via tonometry, optical tonometer, or a smartphone camera,21,25,32 facilitating translation to clinical and outpatient settings.

Conclusions

This study introduces a novel analytical algorithm for reconstructing the entire left ventricular pressure waveform using only carotid pressure and echocardiographic imaging data. Through invasive validation in a controlled preclinical model, the algorithm demonstrated high accuracy in reproducing the full waveform, with excellent agreement for clinically relevant metrics such as LVEDP and the subendocardial viability ratio across baseline, myocardial ischaemia, and infarction states. These results confirm the physiological validity of the method and its robustness across diverse haemodynamic conditions. Given that all required inputs can be obtained non-invasively, the approach offers a scalable solution for integrating LV pressure waveform analysis into routine cardiac evaluation. This technique holds strong potential to enhance non-invasive assessment of cardiac function and enable more accessible, longitudinal monitoring in both clinical and outpatient settings. Furthermore, incorporation of physiologically accurate reconstructed pressure waveforms may improve the accuracy and generalizability of emerging AI-based models for cardiac function assessment.
