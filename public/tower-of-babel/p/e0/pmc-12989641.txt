Physiological Processing of Everyday Aversive Mental Imagery
Authors: Xuan Yang, Sewon Oh, Jacob Stanley, Sarah Hammons, Ashley Anderson, Esha Gandhi, Douglas H. Wedell, Svetlana V. Shinkareva
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTEveryday aversive experiences, such as a crying infant or the sound of construction, are not life‐threatening, yet they can strongly shape affective experience and physiological state. While most affective imagery research has focused on fear and anxiety, the mechanisms underlying imagery of mild, everyday aversive events remain underexplored. Across five experiments, we systematically investigated behavioral and physiological responses to aversive and appetitive mental imagery. Experiment 1 revealed robust corrugator EMG potentiation but comparatively weaker autonomic responses during aversive imagery compared with audiovisual and auditory stimulation. Experiments 2 and 3 identified key methodological factors that determine imagery potency, including imagery duration, instructional focus, and prompt design. Using an optimized paradigm, Experiment 4 demonstrated coherent changes across subjective valence and arousal ratings, EMG corrugator potentiation, heart rate deceleration, and skin temperature decreases for aversive compared with appetitive imagery, with effects modulated by individual differences in imagery vividness. Experiment 5 confirmed that these effects reflected genuine affective experience rather than semantic knowledge of the prompts. Together, these findings establish baseline physiological and subjective markers of everyday aversive imagery in typical young adults and emphasize the critical role of paradigm optimization in eliciting reliable affective imagery responses. By shifting focus from extreme to everyday aversive experiences, this work provides a framework for studying heightened sensitivity to ordinary stimuli in both basic and clinical contexts.

Introduction

Recognizing and responding to aversive stimuli is essential for human survival. Aversiveness can be conceptualized as a graded stimulus property that varies in intensity and survival relevance, with highly aversive stimuli such as threat or attack carrying imminent danger, whereas mildly aversive stimuli such as infant crying or environmental noise elicit negative affect without posing a threat. Research has extensively examined the physiological and neural mechanisms underlying aversive processing in fearful or life‐threatening contexts, establishing a well‐documented understanding of how the body reacts under extreme conditions. These findings have been critical for explaining psychopathological symptoms such as phobia, depression, and anxiety (Holmes and Mathews 2010; Lang and Cuthbert 1984; Lang and McTeague 2009; Rottenberg et al. 2005). In contrast, the aversive stimuli people typically encounter in daily life are annoying without being dangerous, but can still elicit marked emotional shifts and bodily responses. According to the Defense Cascade Model (Bradley et al. 2001; Bradley and Lang 2007), the human body is a defensive motivational system that is dynamically organized to detect, evaluate, and respond to threat through a sequence of qualitatively distinct stages, rather than a single uniform response. Consequently, the level of activation in this defensive system and the pattern of measurable physiological responses vary qualitatively as a function of threat intensity and imminence. Many everyday aversive experiences are mild in nature and therefore may engage defensive processing states that differ fundamentally from those elicited under high‐threat conditions. Emotional responses such as annoyance to environmental noise are widely acknowledged as risk factors for both mental and physical health (Gong et al. 2022). However, how the body processes these everyday, low‐intensity aversive events remains poorly understood. Clarifying these mechanisms would not only address a critical gap in the field but also offer insights into conditions such as misophonia, a condition characterized by strong aversive reactions to everyday sounds (Kumar et al. 2017), obsessive‐compulsive disorder (OCD), post‐traumatic stress disorder (PTSD), and other disorders characterized by heightened reactivity to otherwise tolerable stimuli.

Previous studies have shown that aversive and appetitive states can be reliably differentiated through electromyographic (EMG), electrodermal, and cardiovascular measures. Electromyogram activity over corrugator supercilii (EMGc), a facial muscle related to brow‐lowering, has been considered a gold standard measure for aversiveness. EMGc potentiates more for aversive stimuli compared with non‐aversive stimuli (Bradley et al. 2001; Bradley and Lang 2000; Brown and Schwartz 1980; Codispoti et al. 2001, 2008; Lang et al. 1993; Sato and Kochiyama 2022; Schwartz et al. 1980). EMG activity over zygomaticus major (EMGz), a facial muscle related to lip‐pulling, has a closer correspondence to pleasure (Bernat et al. 2006; Codispoti et al. 2008; Dellacherie et al. 2011; Grewe et al. 2007). However, evidence has revealed a quadratic relationship between EMGz and valence, that is, this muscle potentiates for extreme valenced events without differentiating between positive and negative valence (Bradley and Lang 2000; Lang et al. 1993; Larsen et al. 2003). Startle blink reflex, as measured by EMG over the orbicularis oculi muscle beneath the eye, is another indicator of aversiveness across sensory modalities, which is potentiated for highly arousing aversive stimuli and inhibited for appetitive stimuli and low to moderately arousing aversive stimuli (Bradley et al. 1990, 2001; Bradley and Lang 2007; Cuthbert et al. 1996). Electrodermal activity (EDA), which reflects sweat glands activity under the control of sympathetic nervous system, is more closely linked to arousal than to valence. The accentuated skin conductance in response to both pleasant and unpleasant arousing stimuli has been reported from various stimulus types and body sites (Bradley et al. 2001; Bradley and Lang 2000, 2007; Codispoti et al. 2001; Itkes et al. 2017; Palomba et al. 2000; Sato et al. 2020; Vaccaro et al. 2024; van Dooren et al. 2012). Heart rate (HR) is innervated by both sympathetic (HR acceleration) and parasympathetic (HR deceleration) nervous systems. An initial heart rate deceleration following aversive stimulus presentation has been consistently observed under varying aversive contexts, including viewing pictures, hearing sounds, and watching films (Baldaro et al. 2001; Bradley et al. 2001; Bradley and Lang 2000; Lang et al. 1993; Palomba et al. 2000). Skin temperature (SKT) is another marker for arousal, indexing the sympathetic‐induced changes in microcirculation (Kistler et al. 1998). Decreases in skin temperature due to reduced skin blood flow from cutaneous vasoconstriction have been associated with both arousing aversive and appetitive states (Ioannou et al. 2014; Levenson et al. 1991; Sato et al. 2020; Sato and Kochiyama 2022; Sinha 1996) and are similar to EDA in differentiating arousal levels (Sato and Kochiyama 2022). Enhanced skin conductance responses, HR deceleration, and inhibited startle reflexes during the processing of aversive stimuli may indicate heightened sensory intake and orienting when stimuli are moderately aversive and arousing, whereas sustained increases in skin conductance, HR acceleration, and startle potentiation may reflect full activation of the defensive motivational system in response to highly aversive and arousing stimuli, indexing an action‐oriented disposition toward behavioral responding (Bradley et al. 2001; Bradley and Lang 2007).

Over the past five decades, evidence has demonstrated that somatic and autonomic responses can be elicited not only by externally induced aversive stimuli but also by internally generated aversiveness during affective imagery. According to Lang's Bio‐Informational Theory (1979), mental imagery of affective events activates an associative network in the brain that integrates stimulus information (sensory and contextual features of the imagined event), semantic information (idiosyncratic memories derived from prior experience or knowledge), and response information (somatic and autonomic responses corresponding to real‐world coping actions). Consistent with this framework, physiological responses during endogenously induced affective imagery closely resemble those elicited by external stimuli, including potentiated facial muscle activity (Brown and Schwartz 1980; Schwartz et al. 1976, 1980), increased skin conductance (Acosta and Vila 1990; Carroll et al. 1982), decreased finger skin temperature (Levenson et al. 1991; Sinha 1996), potentiated startle reflex (Bradley and Lang 2007), and enlarged pupil diameter (Henderson et al. 2018). Heart rate is the only marker that shows different change patterns between external affective stimuli perception and internal affective mental imagery. As opposed to the sustained HR deceleration typically observed during the perception of emotional pictures, sounds, and films, affective imagery has often been associated with sustained heart rate acceleration (Acosta and Vila 1990; Carroll et al. 1982; Lang et al. 1980; Levine et al. 2016; Miller et al. 1987, 2002; Vrana et al. 1989), particularly for self‐relevant scenes or following imagery training (Acosta and Vila 1990; Miller et al. 2002), which may suggest a different underlying cognitive process (Bradley and Lang 2007). This acceleration has been interpreted as reflecting action engagement prompted by the imagined scene rather than enhanced sensory intake (Bradley and Lang 2007). However, inconsistent results have also been found showing sustained HR deceleration during fearful imagery, which is similar to the exogenously induced fear bradycardia (Mueller et al. 2019).

Individual differences in the vividness of visual mental imagery have been shown to modulate physiological responses to aversive stimuli. Miller et al. (1987) observed significantly greater HR acceleration in good versus poor imagers when imagining fear and anger events. Wicken et al. (2021) found that aphantasia participants who report being unable to generate visual imagery did not show the increase in skin conductance that the general population exhibits when imagining frightening stories. These results support Lang's (1979) Bio‐Information Theory that vivid mental imagery consisting of rich stimulus‐information would correspond to heightened physiological responses (Lang and Cuthbert 1984). However, much of the prior research on affective imagery has concentrated on fear, anger, and anxiety, often with the goal of developing imagery‐based training programs for phobias, anxiety disorders, and related conditions (Ji et al. 2016). It remains unclear how imagery ability influences physiological responses when the aversive content is mild (e.g., the constant buzzing of a fly trapped inside a room) rather than extreme or life‐threatening (e.g., being alone and surrounded by a gang in a dark alley late at night). A deeper investigation into the physiological distinctions between aversive and non‐aversive imagery could extend our understanding of affective imagery and establish a comparative baseline for studying physiological responses to aversive sounds, providing valuable insights into conditions such as misophonia.

The present study aimed to examine the underlying physiological mechanisms during perception and imagery of mildly aversive stimuli across five experiments. Experiment 1 compared behavioral and physiological responses to aversive versus non‐aversive states across three modalities: audiovisual, auditory, and mental imagery. Experiments 2 and 3 evaluated the effectiveness of the experimental paradigm in eliciting affective mental imagery, focusing on the sensory modality during imagery (attending to sounds or scenes) and validating the effectiveness of imagery prompts in inducing aversiveness. Experiment 4 employed a modified paradigm to further examine subjective and physiological responses between aversive and appetitive imagery, while also assessing the influence of mental imagery vividness on physiological reactivity. Finally, Experiment 5 tested whether affective changes induced by imagery were driven by semantic knowledge of the prompts rather than genuine affective experience during imagery.

Experiment 1

Experiment 1 aimed to compare behavioral and physiological responses to aversive and non‐aversive states across three modalities: audiovisual, auditory, and mental imagery. The following set of hypotheses was examined: Facial, electrodermal, cardiac, and skin temperature changes will be elicited when processing aversive audiovisual (H1a) and auditory (H1b) stimuli. Specifically, we predict that aversive stimuli would elicit heightened EMGc potentiation, lower EMGz potentiation, HR deceleration, increases in EDA, and decreases in finger SKT compared to non‐aversive stimuli. We also predict that aversive mental imagery would elicit heightened EMGc potentiation, lower EMGz potentiation, increases in EDA, and decreases in finger SKT, compared to non‐aversive mental imagery. However, rather than HR deceleration, we predict HR acceleration during aversive imagery compared to non‐aversive imagery. Participants with higher vividness of mental imagery will show more negative valence ratings, higher arousal ratings, and heightened physiological responses during aversive compared to non‐aversive imagery.

Methods

Participants and Experimental Procedure

Sixty‐nine undergraduate students (M age = 20.41, SDage = 2.46, 55 females and 14 males) were recruited through the participant pool of the psychology department at the University of South Carolina. After signing the informed consent in accordance with the Institutional Review Board at the University of South Carolina, participants completed two questionnaires assessing the vividness of mental imagery. They were then equipped with devices for physiological monitoring (see physiological recording section for details). This was followed by three practice trials, corresponding to the three presentation modalities, to familiarize participants with the experimental procedure before proceeding to experimental trials (Figure 1A).

Experiment procedure and design for Experiment 1. (A) Example of an experimental sequence. The experiment consisted of 12 blocks, four blocks per modality: Audiovisual, auditory, and imagery. Twelve‐block sequences were randomized with one constraint: Blocks from the same modality were not presented back‐to‐back. (B) Each block consisted of 10 trials of the same modality, five aversive and five non‐aversive. Trials were presented in random order.

Stimuli

The stimuli consisted of 5‐s audiovisual clips that varied in aversiveness. A larger number of audiovisual clips were recorded in‐house using hand‐held devices, depicting everyday situations with both auditory and visual components capable of eliciting broad affective responses. All clips were trimmed to a 4:3 aspect ratio (640 × 480 pixels) and a fixed duration of 5 s. The audiovisual clips were then separated into audio‐only and silent video tracks. The audio tracks were converted to monophonic format and normalized to a uniform loudness level of −16 loudness units relative to full scale (LUFS) using Audacity 3.6.3. The normalized audio tracks were then reintegrated with the silent videos to create the audiovisual stimuli. To select audiovisual stimuli whose unisensory components alone can evoke affective responses, instead of collecting ratings from the audiovisual clips, we collected aversiveness ratings from auditory and visual components separately, using a −9 (very aversive) to 9 (very pleasant) Likert scales, as well as valence and arousal ratings using a valence‐by‐arousal grid across two online experiments (N = 49 in total). Based on these ratings, we selected 20 aversive and 20 non‐aversive audiovisual clips such that the difference between aversive categories was maximized for both auditory and visual components. Compared with non‐aversive sounds, aversive sounds were rated as more aversive, t(38) = 25.4, p < 0.001, more negatively valenced, t(38) = 27.4, p < 0.001, and more arousing, t(38) = 7.70, p < 0.001. Compared with non‐aversive silent videos, aversive silent videos were rated as more aversive, t(38) = 29.5, p < 0.001, more negatively valenced, t(38) = 28.6, p < 0.001, and more arousing, t(38) = 4.61, p < 0.001. For the mental imagery condition, brief descriptive phrases prompting participants to imagine the corresponding sounds (e.g., “Rhythmic piano playing”) were used as imagery cues. In total, the stimulus set comprised 120 unique stimuli (20 exemplars × 2 aversiveness levels × 3 modalities).

Experimental Design

To examine the effect of aversiveness on physiological measures across modalities, we employed a 2 (Aversiveness: aversive, non‐aversive) × 3 (Modality: audiovisual, auditory, and imagery) within‐subject factorial design. For each of the three modalities, the 40 stimuli (20 aversive and 20 non‐aversive) were randomly assigned to four blocks. Each block consisted of five aversive and five non‐aversive stimuli, and stimuli were presented in a random order. Each trial was designed to last 20 s to ensure enough time for physiological responses' activation and recovery. Each trial began with a 1–2 s fixation cross, followed by a 5 s stimulus presentation, another fixation cross presented for 10 s, and a rating screen for 3–4 s (Figure 1B). All texts were displayed in white color against a black background. Participants rated each stimulus on valence and arousal using a valence‐by‐arousal grid, with valence ranging from −4.5 (negative) to 4.5 (positive) and arousal from 0 (low) to 9 (high).

Physiological Data Acquisition

Facial muscle and autonomic responses during the experiment were recorded using a BIOPAC MP150 recorder (Biopac Inc., Goleta, CA, USA) with a sampling rate at 1000 Hz. The acquisition procedure of physiological signals was identical to that reported in our previous study (Oh et al. 2025). Specifically, EMG was recorded over corrugator supercilii and zygomaticus major on the left side of the participants' face using a BioNomadix wireless amplifier. EMGc was captured with two Ag/AgCl electrodes placed above the eyebrow (one electrode directly above the left eyebrow on the imaginary vertical line crossing the inner corner of the eye, and the other approximately 1 cm lateral to the first). EMGz was captured with one electrode placed at the center of the imaginary line joining the left cheilion and the preauricular depression and the other placed 1 cm inferior and medial to the first electrode along that imaginary line. A ground electrode for EMG was placed in the middle of the inner eyebrows. EDA was recorded with two Ag/AgCl electrodes placed to the left hand's medial phalanges of the index and middle fingers using a GSR100C amplifier. ECG was recorded in a lead II configuration with one electrode placed below the right collar bone (cathode) and the second electrode placed below the lowest left rib (anode) using an ECG100C amplifier. No additional ground cable was attached considering we have concurrent EDA data collection. SKT was recorded with a sensor placed at the palm side of the proximal phalanx of the left thumb using a SKT100C amplifier.

Preprocessing and Data Reduction

Raw EMG signals were preprocessed in AcqKnowledge 5.0 (Biopac Inc., Goleta, CA, USA) using a band‐stop filter at 60 Hz and a band‐pass filter (20–500 Hz), followed by rectification and computation of a windowed mean with a 250‐ms time interval. Data reduction and feature extraction were then performed in Python using two complementary approaches: an epoch‐wise (time‐resolved) analysis and a trial‐wise (time‐averaged) analysis. For the epoch‐wise analysis, the area under the curve (AUC) was computed for each consecutive 1‐s interval within the 15‐s analysis window (5 s of stimulus presentation followed by 10 s of post‐stimulus fixation), yielding 15 epochs per trial. A log10 transformation was applied to the AUC values to reduce non‐normality. To facilitate between‐subject comparisons, baseline AUC values were computed using the same procedure for each 1‐s interval immediately preceding trial onset, and these baseline values were subtracted from the corresponding post‐onset epochs. The resulting baseline‐corrected values were then z‐scored across trials and epochs within each participant, separately for EMGc and EMGz. This procedure yielded a 15‐point z‐score time series for each trial and each participant. For the trial‐wise analysis, the AUC was computed over the entire 15‐s analysis window for each trial. The same log‐transformation and baseline correction steps as the epoch‐wise processing were applied. The resulting values were then z‐scored across trials within each participant, resulting in a single z‐score per trial and participant, separately for EMGc and EMGz.

Phasic EDA signals were calculated online in AcqKnowledge by applying a 0.05‐Hz high‐pass filter to the tonic EDA signals. The resulting phasic signals were then smoothed offline by averaging across every 50 samples and transformed to absolute values. The same AUC computation, log10 transformation, baseline correction, and within‐participant z‐scoring procedures used for the EMG analyses were applied to the EDA data, yielding fifteen 1‐s epochs per trial and participant for the epoch‐wise analysis and a single value per trial and participant for the trial‐wise analysis.

R‐peaks were automatically detected from the raw ECG waveform using Acqknowledge. QRS complexes that were not detected or were affected by motion artifacts were manually inspected and corrected. For the epoch‐wise analysis, mean HR was computed for each 1‐s interval within the 15‐s analysis window based on inter‐beat intervals. The mean HR during the 1‐s pre‐stimulus window was subtracted from each 1‐s interval as a baseline correction. The resulting values were then z‐scored across trials and epochs within each participant, yielding a 15‐point z‐score time series for each trial and each participant. For the trial‐wise analysis, the same data reduction method was applied to the entire 15‐s analysis window, resulting in a single z‐score per trial and participant.

For the epoch‐wise analysis, raw SKT signals were averaged for every 1 s interval within the 15‐s analysis window following stimulus presentation, baseline‐corrected for the mean of the 1 s pre‐stimulus window and standardized across trials within participants, resulting in a 15‐point z‐score time series per trial and participant. For the trial‐wise analysis, the same data reduction method was applied to the entire 15‐s time window following stimulus presentation, resulting in a single z‐score per trial and participant.

Behavioral Measures

Vividness of Auditory Imagery: The Vividness subscale of the Bucknell Imagery Scale (Halpern 2015; BAIS‐V) was used to measure the vividness of auditory imagery. The BAIS‐V questionnaire shows high internal consistency in the original study (Cronbach's alpha = 0.83). Each of its 14 items instructs participants to construct an auditory image specific to a real‐life scenario. Participants were asked to rate the vividness of those auditory images using a 7‐point Likert scale, with 1 representing “No image is present at all” and 7 representing “The image is as vivid as the actual sound”. Higher scores on the BAIS‐V reflect greater vividness of auditory imagery.

Vividness of Visual Imagery: The Vividness of Visual Imagery Questionnaire (Marks 1973; VVIQ) was used to measure the vividness of visual imagery. This measure shows high internal reliability with a split‐half reliability of 0.85 in the original paper. This questionnaire consists of 16 items. Participants were asked to construct a visual image specific to a scenario and rate the vividness on a 5‐point Likert scale, with 1 representing “Perfectly clear and as vivid as normal vision” and 5 representing “No image at all, you only ‘know’ that you are thinking of the object.” Lower scores on the VVIQ reflect greater vividness of visual imagery.

Analysis

Measure‐specific data from a few participants were excluded due to technical difficulty during data collection. EMG data were unavailable for five participants due to equipment limitations during data collection. Six participants were excluded for BAIS‐V and five participants were removed for VVIQ due to missing data. The final sample sizes were as follows: n = 64 for EMGc, EMGz and HR; n = 69 for EDA and SKT; n = 63 for BAIS‐V; and n = 65 for VVIQ. For each physiological measure, outlier detection was performed within each participant using the trial‐wise data aggregated across the 15‐s time window. Any trial with a value exceeding three standard deviations from that participant's mean was considered an outlier and removed from subsequent analyses. Missing responses and responses under 200 ms for valence and arousal ratings were excluded. The percentages of the trials that were removed were 2.01% for EMGc; 2.16% for EMGz; 0.42% for EDA; 1.00% for HR; 0.92% for SKT; and 4.02% for valence and arousal ratings. For the epoch analysis, the trials identified as outliers from the trial‐wise data were also excluded.

To validate our stimuli manipulation, we used a 2 (aversiveness: aversive, non‐aversive) × 3 (modality: audiovisual, auditory, imagery) repeated‐measures ANOVA to analyze the data for subjective valence and arousal ratings, separately. Planned t‐tests were applied to examine if there was a significant difference in valence ratings between aversive and non‐aversive conditions, separately for each modality. Given our theory‐driven a priori hypotheses that stimulus aversiveness would influence subjective ratings and physiological responses across channels and modalities, no corrections for multiple comparisons were applied unless otherwise stated. Results are reported using uncorrected two‐tailed tests at α = 0.05. Post hoc t‐tests were applied to examine if there was a significant difference in arousal ratings between aversive and non‐aversive conditions, separately for each modality. Bonferroni correction was applied to account for the three comparisons across three modalities.

We conducted an epoch‐based analysis to examine the temporal dynamics of aversiveness‐related differences across physiological channels using cluster‐based permutation testing implemented in MNE‐Python. For each participant, modality, and physiological channel, continuous signals were segmented into 1‐s epochs and averaged across trials within each stimulus condition (aversive vs. non‐aversive), yielding time‐series data with 15 epochs per condition. Group‐level statistical comparisons between aversive and non‐aversive conditions were performed separately for each modality and physiological channel using a non‐parametric cluster‐level permutation test. For each epoch, condition differences were quantified using an F‐statistic, and clusters were formed by applying an automatically determined cluster‐forming threshold corresponding to an uncorrected p value of 0.05 for the F‐distribution. Temporal clusters were defined as contiguous 1‐s epochs exceeding this threshold. Statistical significance was assessed via permutation testing with 5000 iterations. In each permutation, condition labels were randomly reassigned across whole epochs while preserving the temporal structure within epochs. For each permuted dataset, the maximum cluster‐level F‐statistic across time was retained to construct a null distribution. Observed cluster statistics were then compared against this null distribution to obtain family‐wise error–corrected p values. This approach controls the family‐wise error rate across time while accounting for temporal dependencies in the physiological signals and does not assume independence between adjacent time points (Maris and Oostenveld 2007; Sassenhagen and Draschkow 2019).

We fit linear regression models to examine the unique effect of aversiveness while accounting for the potential effect of arousal and other confounding variables. The trial‐wise data aggregated across the 15‐s time window was used. For audiovisual and auditory modality, the physiological responses of each channel were modeled as a function of stimuli condition (aversive vs. non‐aversive), with the subjective arousal rating for each trial, age, and sex included as covariates. For the imagery condition, BAIS‐V and VVIQ were additionally included as covariates.

To test the effect of imagery ability on behavioral ratings and physiological responses (H3), we conducted multiple linear regression analyses. For both subjective ratings and physiological responses, we first calculated a difference score by subtracting the average response to non‐aversive trials from that of aversive trials within the imagery modality. Because auditory and visual imagery abilities were moderately correlated (r = −0.40, individuals with higher auditory imagery ability also tend to exhibit higher visual imagery ability), modality‐specific imagery abilities were modeled separately to avoid multicollinearity. To examine the influence of imagery ability on subjective valence, we used BAIS‐V or VVIQ to predict valence difference scores, while controlling for arousal difference scores, age, and sex. To examine the influence of imagery ability on subjective arousal, we used BAIS‐V or VVIQ to predict arousal difference scores, while controlling for valence difference scores, age, and sex. To assess the impact of imagery ability on physiological responses, we conducted separate regression models for each physiological channel. In each model, BAIS‐V or VVIQ was used to predict the corresponding physiological difference score, with valence difference scores, arousal difference scores, age, and sex included as covariates.

Results

Manipulation of Aversiveness

We presented participants with 20 aversive and 20 non‐aversive stimuli across three different modalities to elicit behavioral and physiological responses. As expected, stimuli were clearly differentiated by perceived aversiveness, based on average valence ratings (Figure 2A). All aversive stimuli had comparable valence ratings (≤ −1.5 on a −4.5 to 4.5 scale, where −4.5 indicates very negative and 4.5 indicates very positive), with one exception: the audiovisual and imagery versions of “Someone squishing wet clothes” (Figure 2A). Non‐aversive stimuli had valence ≥ −1, and arousal ratings were significantly higher for aversive than non‐aversive stimuli (M aversive = 5.90, M non‐aversive = 5.31), t(78) = 4.57, p < 0.001, Cohen's d = 1.02.

After Bonferroni correction, the post hoc comparisons showed that the valence ratings were significantly lower for auditory (M auditory = −0.84, SDauditory = 1.90) compared with audiovisual (M audiovisual = −0.37, SDaudiovisual = 2.21), t(68) = −8.92, p < 0.001, Cohen's d = 0.63, and compared with imagery (M imagery = −0.27, SDimagery = 2.18), t(68) = −10.16, p < 0.001, Cohen's d = 0.71. Valence ratings were not significantly different between audiovisual and imagery.

For arousal ratings, we found significant main effects of modality, F(2, 136) = 10.90, MSE = 1.00, p < 0.001, η2 = 0.03, and aversiveness, F(1, 68) = 12.92, MSE = 2.67, p < 0.001, η2 = 0.05 (Figure 2). The interaction effect was not significant. After Bonferroni correction, the post hoc t‐tests showed significant differences between the arousal ratings for aversive and non‐aversive stimuli for audiovisual (M aversive = 5.99, SDaversive = 0.48, M non‐aversive = 5.39, SDnon‐aversive = 0.62), t(68) = 3.13, p < 0.01, Cohen's d = 0.38, auditory (M aversive = 6.08, SDaversive = 0.51, M non‐aversive = 5.59, SDnon‐aversive = 0.45), t(68) = 3.42, p < 0.01, Cohen's d = 0.41, and imagery (M aversive = 5.63, SDaversive = 0.55, M non‐aversive = 4.96, SDnon‐aversive = 0.67), t(68) = 3.56, p < 0.01, Cohen's d = 0.43. After Bonferroni correction, the post hoc comparisons showed that the arousal ratings were significantly higher for audiovisual (M audiovisual = 5.69, SDaudiovisual = 0.63), t(68) = 2.98, p = 0.012, Cohen's d = 0.34, and auditory (M auditory = 5.83, SDauditory = 0.53) compared with imagery (M imagery = 5.30, SDimagery = 0.69), t(68) = 3.77, p < 0.01, Cohen's d = 0.41. There was no significant difference between auditory and audiovisual.

Physiological Responses

For EMGc, aversive audiovisual stimuli elicited significantly greater potentiation than non‐aversive stimuli across the entire 15‐s time window (Figure 3). For auditory stimuli, aversive sounds produced significantly greater potentiation than non‐aversive sounds during 4–15 s following stimulus onset (Figure 3). Consistent with this temporal pattern, linear regression for the aggregated signal over the 15‐s window showed significantly stronger potentiation for aversive auditory stimuli, β = 0.13, SE = 0.04, t(123) = 3.48, p < 0.001, controlling for arousal, age, and sex (Figure 4A). For mental imagery, aversive imagery produced greater potentiation than non‐aversive imagery during 2–15 s after onset (Figure 3). The linear regression for the signal aggregated over the 15‐s window likewise indicated significantly stronger potentiation for aversive compared with non‐aversive imagery, β = 0.12, SE = 0.04, t(123) = 3.18, p < 0.005, after controlling for arousal, age, and sex. This effect remained significant when additionally controlling for VVIQ and BAIS‐V scores, β = 0.12, SE = 0.04, t(105) = 3.01, p < 0.005 (Figure 4A).

For EMGz, aversive audiovisual stimuli elicited significantly greater potentiation than non‐aversive stimuli during 3–15 s following stimulus onset (Figure 3). For mental imagery, no significant differences were observed between aversive and non‐aversive conditions in either the epoch‐based or regression analyses.

Linear regression on the averaged 15‐s signal showed no significant aversiveness effect after controlling for arousal, age, and sex. For auditory stimuli, aversive sounds produced significantly greater deceleration during 1–7 s (Figure 3), and regression analysis indicated that mean heart rate over the 15‐s window was significantly lower for aversive compared with non‐aversive stimuli, β = −0.10, SE = 0.04, t(123) = −2.48, p = 0.014, after controlling for arousal, age, and sex (Figure 4D). The Effect of Imagery Vividness

We next examined whether individual differences in imagery vividness predicted behavioral and physiological responses. For behavioral ratings, after controlling for age and sex, neither VVIQ nor BAIS‐V scores significantly predicted the difference in valence or arousal ratings between aversive and non‐aversive stimuli. For physiological responses, after controlling for age, sex, and the difference scores of valence and arousal ratings between aversive and non‐aversive conditions, VVIQ significantly predicted heart rate responses. Individuals with higher visual imagery ability (indexed by lower VVIQ scores) were associated with a larger difference in mean heart rate between aversive and non‐aversive imagery, β = −0.01, SE = 0.002, t(54) = −2.31, p = 0.025, after controlling for the effects of mean valence difference, arousal difference, age, and sex (Figure 4F), indicating that stronger visual imagery ability was linked to greater heart rate deceleration for aversive relative to non‐aversive imagery. BAIS‐V did not significantly predict heart rate deceleration. No significant effects of VVIQ or BAIS‐V were found for the other physiological channels.

Discussion

The primary goal of Experiment 1 was to examine somatovisceral responses when participants imagined aversive and non‐aversive naturalistic sounds. We compared the similarity of facial and autonomic responses under aversive and non‐aversive affective states across audiovisual, auditory, and imagery modalities. In addition, we also assessed the effect of individual differences in imagery vividness on the subjective ratings and physiological responses.

Our results replicated previous findings that exogenously induced aversive affective state correspond to coherent changes in subjective experience, facial expression, and electrodermal and cardiac activities for both audiovisual (H1a) and auditory modalities (H1b). Compared with non‐aversive stimuli, aversive audiovisual and auditory stimuli both induced EMG potentiation over the corrugator and sustained heart rate deceleration. There was a significant phasic EDA increase for aversive audiovisual stimuli, but not for aversive auditory stimuli. These findings were in line with a previous study in our lab using the same experimental protocol investigating the physiological responses to audiovisual, auditory, and visual stimuli (Oh et al. 2025), and also consistent with other studies using affective films (Golland et al. 2018; Sato et al. 2020; Sato and Kochiyama 2022) and affective sounds (Bradley and Lang 2000; Larsen et al. 2003). For EMG over zygomaticus major, we observed greater potentiation for non‐aversive compared with aversive audiovisual stimuli, but the opposite pattern for auditory stimuli. Such inconsistencies, also seen in our prior work (Oh et al. 2025) and other labs (Bradley and Lang 2000; Lang et al. 1993; Larsen et al. 2003), suggest that zygomaticus major potentiation can occur for both positive and negative stimuli, depending more on intensity of valence than on its polarity.

The central aim of the current study was to extend this investigation to imagery. Our results revealed significant differentiation in subjective hedonic valence ratings and EMG corrugator potentiation between aversive and non‐aversive states during imagery (H2). Visual imagery vividness predicted larger difference in HR when imagining aversive compared with non‐aversive stimuli, but not subjective ratings or other physiological responses (H3). Although previous evidence has reported coherent changes across facial and autonomic measures during aversive scenes imagery, including potentiated EMG corrugator, greater skin conductance activity, and accelerated heart rate (Bradley and Lang 2007). There are several potential explanations for the weak results in our experiment. First, early studies have shown that imagery scripts play a critical role in the success of the manipulation of physiological activation (Lang et al. 1980; Schwartz et al. 1976). Our imagery prompts (e.g., imagine the sound of “someone banging on piano keys”) were stimulus‐proposition‐laden and may have been too concise to induce mental images carrying strong affective content. Most of the studies investigating affective imagery use long detailed scripts containing rich information (Acosta and Vila 1990; Lang et al. 1980; Miller et al. 1987), including both the stimulus proposition and response proposition. Lang et al. (1980) found that a stimulus‐proposition‐laden script (e.g., “you are flying a kite on the beach on a bright summer day”) failed to elicit appreciable physiological responses during affective imagery, whereas a response‐proposition‐laden scripts (e.g., “you breathe deeply as you run along the beach flying a kite”) elicited significant responses in EMG, HR and skin conductance level.

Second, the mental imagery period in our study was relatively short (5 s) compared with previous research in which participants were instructed to imagine scenes for considerably longer durations, ranging from 30 s to several minutes (Acosta and Vila 1990; Lang et al. 1980; Schwartz et al. 1976, 1980). Five seconds may not have been long enough for participants to generate and engage in the imagery. Some of our imagery prompts require extra effort to retrieve the sounds from memory first (e.g., “Squeaky violin strings”). Thus, a shorter time window may not be sufficient to elicit a strong aversive affective state.

A third explanation is that the weak results across autonomic measures might reflect a modality effect of imagery. Our prompts emphasized auditory rather than visual content, whereas most affective imagery studies have used scene‐based (visual) scripts. Engen et al. (2018) found that auditory imagery showed poor efficacy in generating emotions as compared with visual imagery and bodily interoception.

A final explanation is that the mild, everyday aversive scenarios we used may not have been strong enough to elicit robust autonomic reactions. Although aversive imagery produced more negative subjective ratings and corrugator activity, it failed to significantly alter heart rate, skin conductance, or skin temperature.

Taken together, these findings suggest that the relatively weak autonomic responses observed during imagery may reflect limitations in script design and imagery duration, the effect of sensory modality, or the mild affective intensity of the stimuli. To address these possibilities, we conducted two online experiments to systematically evaluate the contribution of each factor to affective imagery.

Experiment 2

Experiment 2 aimed to examine (1) the time required to generate vivid aversive mental imagery and (2) whether the sensory modality of imagery could be dissociated through different prompt instructions. Moreover, in Experiment 1, aversive stimuli were compared with a broad non‐aversive category that combined both neutral and appetitive scenarios, potentially masking important distinctions along the valence dimension. To address this limitation, the design of subsequent experiments was refined by separating neutral and appetitive conditions and directly comparing them with aversive imagery. We adopted the term “appetitive” to distinguish this category from the broader “non‐aversive” classification used in Experiment 1 and to more precisely characterize positive, high‐arousal stimuli.

Methods

Stimuli

The prompts used in Experiment 1 were directly matched to the audiovisual stimuli, which constrained their wording and contextual richness. To address this limitation, a new set of prompts was developed for Experiment 2. Using OpenAI's ChatGPT‐4, we generated a series of short imagery prompts designed to vary in aversiveness and to include both visual and auditory components (e.g., “The deafening sound of a jackhammer at a construction site”). From this pool, a research assistant selected prompts that were likely to elicit vivid mental imagery and edited them for clarity and naturalness. This process resulted in a final set of 125 prompts.

Experimental Design

To examine if participants could differentiate between visual and auditory mental imagery, we used instructions that directed their attention to either imagined scenes or imagined sounds (Figure 5). In the auditory imagery condition, participants were instructed: “you will read prompts and imagine the described sounds as vividly as possible, as if hearing them in your mind's ear.” For the visual imagery condition, participants were instructed: “you will read prompts and imagine the described scenes as vividly as possible, as if seeing them in your mind's eye.” Trials were blocked by imagery modality, and the order of auditory and visual imagery was counterbalanced across participants. To accommodate the one‐hour session length, the 125 prompts were divided into two sets: experiment 2A consisting of 66 prompts, and experiment 2B consisting of 59 prompts. The same procedure was used for both experiments, with prompts presented in randomized order within each experiment to minimize order effect.

Illustration of trial sequence for Experiment 2. Trials were presented in blocks by imagery modality (visual vs. auditory), with block order counterbalanced across participants.

Each trial began with the prompt text displayed in black text against a white background for 7 s. After the text disappeared, participants were asked to continue imagining the described content and to press a button once their imagery reached peaked vividness. Participants then rated their emotional responses using a valence‐by‐arousal grid, with reaction time (RT) recorded for this rating. Finally, participants provided three additional ratings: imagery vividness (1= “no image at all, you only know that you are thinking of an object/sound” to 5 = “perfectly clear and as vivid as normal vision/hearing”), familiarity with the imagined scenes/sounds (1 = “not at all” to 5 = “extremely”), and difficulty of generating the mental imagery (1 = “not at all” to 5 = “extremely”).

Participants

Thirty‐four undergraduate students participated in experiment 2A (M age = 20.50, SDage = 3.19, 33 females and 1 male). A separate sample of thirty‐four undergraduate students participated in experiment 2B (M age = 20.71, SDage = 2.17, 27 females, 5 males, and 2 nonbinary participants). Across both experiments, the combined sample consisted of 68 participants (M age = 20.60, SDage = 2.71). All procedures were approved by and conducted in accordance with the Institutional Review Board at the University of South Carolina.

Data Cleaning and Analysis

Trials were excluded if the imagery generation time exceeded 20 s (5.96% of the total trials) or if the grid rating time exceeded 60 s (0.36% of the total trials). In addition, outlier detection was performed within each participant. Any trial with imagery generation times greater than three standard deviations above that participant's mean was considered an outlier and removed from subsequent analyses. For each prompt, imagery generation time and subjective ratings (valence, absolute valence, arousal, grid rating reaction times, vividness, familiarity, and difficulty) were averaged across participants, separately for auditory and visual imagery conditions. To test for differences between auditory and visual imagery, repeated‐measures t‐tests were conducted on these averaged measures.

Most prompts were rated as easy to imagine. Difficulty ratings (1 = not difficult at all, 5 = extremely difficult) were low for both auditory, M auditory = 1.83, SDauditory = 1.10, and visual imagery, M visual = 1.83, SDvisual = 1.09. The affective scenarios of the prompts were also rated as highly familiar for both auditory, M auditory = 3.72, SDauditory = 1.14, and visual imagery, M visual = 3.70, SDvisual = 1.17. There were no significant differences between visual and auditory mental imagery for difficulty, familiarity, vividness, reaction times of grid rating, valence rating, and arousal rating (ps > 0.192).

Discussion

Experiment 2 extended the findings of Experiment 1 by using an online behavioral rating paradigm to examine two possible explanations for the weak physiological responses to affective mental imagery observed earlier. The first question was whether the 5‐s imagery period used in Experiment 1 was insufficient to elicit vivid imagery. To address this, prompts in Experiment 2 were presented for 7 s, and participants were then given unlimited time to continue imagining until their imagery reached peak vividness. The second question concerned whether participants were able to selectively attend to a single sensory modality during imagery. To test this, instructions directed participants to focus either on auditory or visual aspects of the imagined scenes.

We found that the 5 s duration used in Experiment 1 was indeed too brief to elicit vivid mental imagery. On average, participants required approximately 2 additional seconds beyond the 7‐s reading period to generate vivid mental imagery. Behavioral ratings further demonstrated that participants were able to selectively attend to a single sensory modality: auditory imagery was associated with more extreme valence ratings and shorter generation times compared with visual imagery. Importantly, auditory and visual prompts did not differ in vividness, difficulty, familiarity, arousal, or rating latency, suggesting that the modality effect reflected a genuine difference in perceived aversiveness rather than methodological artifacts. These findings align with a growing view that mental imagery is inherently multisensory, rather than predominantly visual as traditionally assumed (Engen et al. 2018; Sulfaro et al. 2024), underscoring the importance of providing clear, modality‐specific instructions in future research. In contrast, the original instructions used in Experiment 1 (e.g., imagine the sound of “a baby crying”) may have been ambiguous for some participants and thus elicited heterogeneous imagery experiences, given the inherently multisensory nature of affective imagery. Taken together, the weak physiological effects observed in Experiment 1 may reflect both insufficient imagery duration and variability in participants' attentional focus across sensory modalities during imagery.

Interestingly, our findings suggest a modality advantage for auditory imagery, such that vivid auditory imagery was generated more rapidly and was associated with more extreme affective content compared with visual imagery. These results may be specific to our stimuli that were selected based on everyday aversive scenarios with clearly identifiable sounds. Alternatively, our results may reveal a real auditory advantage in mental imagery, aligning with a recent study that systematically examined multisensory experiences during mental imagery of the letter “O” and demonstrated a broad superiority of auditory imagery across multiple cognitive dimensions, including shorter onset latency, greater temporal consistency, and more automatic generation (Sulfaro et al. 2024). However, although it is out of the scope of the current study, future studies are needed to disentangle where this auditory advantage reflects a genuine property of mental imagery or arises from task‐ and stimulus‐related factors.

Experiment 3

Experiment 2 confirmed that both imagery generation time and instructions play an important role in shaping affective imagery and its associated emotional experience. However, because a different set of prompts was used in Experiment 1, the weak results observed there may also reflect characteristics of the prompts themselves. For instance, the prompts in Experiment 1 may have been more difficult to imagine vividly or may have described affective scenarios that were less familiar to participants, thereby limiting their emotional impact. To address these possibilities, we conducted an additional online behavioral rating experiment using a protocol modified based on the findings from Experiment 2. In this study, the prompts from Experiment 1 were re‐used alongside the prompts selected in Experiment 2, which had been shown to reliably elicit vivid imagery and strong emotional responses. Behavioral ratings were then compared across the two prompt sets to directly evaluate the effectiveness of the original Experiment 1 stimuli.

Methods

Stimuli

A total of 100 prompts were used. Sixty prompts were selected from Experiment 2A and 2B (20 appetitive, 20 aversive, and 20 neutral), and 40 prompts were re‐used from Experiment 1 (20 aversive and 20 non‐aversive).

Experimental Design

Considering that participants can naturally attend to different sensory components during mental imagery, we did not manipulate modality in this experiment. Instead, participants received the instruction: “You will read prompts and imagine the described scenes and sounds as vividly as possible, as if you are seeing and hearing them” (Figure 7). This was intended to induce consistent multimodal imagery across trials and participants. To facilitate comparison with Experiment 1, prompts were displayed for 5 s. Based on the findings in Experiment 2, which indicated that additional time was needed to generate vivid imagery, participants then imagined the scenes and sounds for a fixed 7 s period. Afterward, they completed the same ratings used in Experiment 2: grid‐based valence and arousal, difficulty, familiarity, and vividness. Prompts were randomly presented to participants to minimize order effect.

Illustration of trial sequence for Experiment 3. Participants were requested to focus on both auditory and visual aspects of mental imagery during each trial.

Participants

Twenty‐seven undergraduate students participated in Experiment 3 (M age = 19.67, SDage = 2.66, 23 females and 4 males). All procedures were approved by and conducted in accordance with the Institutional Review Board at the University of South Carolina.

Analysis

Trials with excessively long grid rating reaction times (> 10 s) were excluded. For each prompt, subjective ratings of valence, absolute valence, arousal, grid rating reaction times, vividness, familiarity, and difficulty were averaged across participants. Independent‐samples t‐tests were then conducted to compare the aversive prompts from Experiment 1 with those from Experiment 2. The same analyses were performed to compare the non‐aversive prompts from Experiment 1 with the appetitive prompts from Experiment 2.

Results

Valence and arousal ratings for the prompts used in Experiment 2 clustered into three distinct groups (Figure 8A), corresponding to aversive (negative valence, high arousal), neutral (neutral valence, low arousal), and appetitive (positive valence, high arousal) conditions. In contrast, several aversive and non‐aversive prompts from Experiment 1 overlapped with the neutral cluster from Experiment 2, suggesting weaker affective differentiation.

However, the appetitive prompts from Experiment 2 were rated as significantly more arousing (M = 5.66, SD = 0.73) than the non‐aversive prompts from Experiment 2 (M = 4.63, SD = 0.98), t(38) = 3.75, p < 0.001, Cohen's d = 1.19. Discussion

Experiment 3 used an online behavioral rating task to determine whether the weak physiological responses in Experiment 1 were due to difficulties in generating vivid affective mental imagery or to characteristics of the prompts themselves. To address these possibilities, the paradigm was modified based on the findings from Experiment 2 to allow sufficient time for imagery generation and to instruct participants to engage in both auditory and visual modalities, thereby reducing variability across individuals.

The results did not support the hypothesis that the prompts from Experiment 1 were inherently ineffective at eliciting vivid imagery. Sixty prompts from Experiment 2 that had previously been shown to evoke vivid imagery and robust emotional responses were compared with the original prompts from Experiment 1. Behavioral ratings revealed no significant differences between the two prompt sets in cognitive measures (difficulty, familiarity, vividness, or grid rating reaction times). Differences emerged only in affective experience: prompts from Experiment 2 elicited stronger aversive and appetitive responses than those from Experiment 1. These findings suggest that the weaker physiological effects observed in Experiment 1 may be attributable to the lower affective potency of its prompts rather than limitations in imagery vividness. Although the original prompts used in Experiment 1 (e.g., “A baby crying”) could elicit vivid mental imagery, the resulting affective states were less intense than those evoked by the modified prompts (e.g., “An inconsolable toddler's desperate, screeching cries of distress”) and therefore may have been insufficient to elicit robust emotion‐related physiological responses. Together, these results highlight the importance of using well‐validated, emotionally engaging stimuli in affective mental imagery research to ensure reliable engagement of emotional processes.

Experiment 4

Experiment 1 investigated behavioral and physiological responses to aversive imagery of everyday scenarios, comparing them with reactions to audiovisual and auditory‐only stimuli. Physiological responses were generally weaker than those reported in prior research, prompting further investigation into possible methodological causes. To address this, two online behavioral experiments (Experiments 2 and 3) were conducted. Subjective ratings of valence and arousal provided robust evidence that concise prompts depicting everyday aversive and appetitive scenarios can indeed evoke vivid and emotionally intense imagery. However, these studies also indicated that extended imagery duration, explicit and modality‐specific instructions, and more affectively potent prompts are critical for maximizing emotional engagement, potentially accounting for the weak physiological responses observed in Experiment 1. Building on these findings, Experiment 4 employed the validated paradigm from Experiment 3 and the most effective prompts identified therein to reexamine physiological responses to affective mental imagery under optimized conditions.

Methods

Stimuli

A separate online behavioral rating experiment was conducted to identify the most effective prompts for use in Experiment 4. Forty‐four undergraduate students (M age = 19.09, SDage = 1.16, 34 females and 10 males) rated 70 prompts selected from Experiment 3 (30 aversive, 30 appetitive, and 10 neutral) using the same paradigm employed in Experiment 3. Based on these ratings, 45 prompts were selected (20 aversive, 20 appetitive, and 5 neutral). The selected prompts differed only in valence and arousal ratings, while showing no significant differences in prompt length (average word count) or cognitive‐related measures, including difficulty, familiarity, vividness, and grid rating reaction times. Additionally, no significant differences were observed between appetitive and aversive prompts in the absolute value of valence and arousal (Figure 9).

Forty‐five prompts from Experiment 3 were selected based on the subjective ratings collected from a separate validation experiment. Experiment Design

We employed a one‐factor (Aversiveness: aversive, neutral, appetitive) within‐subject factorial design. Each of the 45 prompts (20 aversive, 20 appetitive, and 5 neutral) was presented twice. All prompts were randomly assigned to 10 blocks, each containing four aversive, four appetitive, and one neutral prompt. Within each block, prompts were presented in a random order. Each trial began with a 5 s fixation cross, followed by a 5 s prompt presentation. Participants then viewed a thinking bubble image for a 7 s imagery period, during which they were instructed to imagine the described scenes and sounds as vividly as possible. This was followed by a 5 s fixation cross and a self‐paced valence‐by‐arousal grid rating (Figure 10).

Trials were randomized within each of the 10 blocks.

Participants and Experimental Procedure

Fifty‐four undergraduate students participated in Experiment 4. One participant did not complete the experiment and was excluded, leaving a final sample of 53 participants (M age = 19.30, SDage = 1.25, 40 females and 13 males). After signing the informed consent, participants were familiarized with the imagery prompts used in the physiology experiment. They read each prompt, imagined the described scenes and sounds as vividly as possible, as if seeing and hearing them, and rated the vividness of their mental imagery for each prompt. Participants were then equipped with devices for physiological monitoring. Two practice trials were completed to ensure understanding of the experimental procedure before the experimental trials began (Figure 10A).

Physiological Recording, Preprocessing, and Data Reduction

The physiological recording and preprocessing procedures were identical to those used in Experiment 1. For the epoch‐wise analysis, time‐series data were extracted in 1 s intervals from the onset of prompt presentation to the end of the post‐stimulus fixation period (5 s prompt, 7 s imagery, and 5 s post‐stimulus fixation, 17‐s total). This yielded 17 z‐scored data points per trial and participant for each recording channel. For the trial‐wise analysis, data were similarly reduced across the same 17 s time window following stimulus onset, resulting in a single z‐score per trial and participant for each channel.

Analysis

Linear regression models identical to those used in Experiment 1 were conducted to examine the unique effect of aversiveness, controlling for arousal and demographic variables. Data were aggregated across the 17 s time window. For each physiological measure, outlier detection was performed within each participant. Any trial with a value exceeding three standard deviations from that participant's mean was considered an outlier and removed from subsequent analyses. Missing responses and responses under 200 ms for valence and arousal ratings were excluded. The percentages of excluded trials were 1.42% for EMGc, 1.57% for EMGz, 0.58% for EDA, 0.69% for HR, 0.66% for SKT, and 1.45% for valence and arousal ratings. The remaining trials were averaged within each stimulus condition and used as dependent variables. Independent variables included stimulus condition, subjective arousal ratings, age, sex, BAIS‐V, and VVIQ. We reported the uncorrected results of the planned contrast comparing Aversive vs. Appetitive conditions. For the post hoc contrasts comparing Aversive vs. Neutral and Appetitive vs. Neutral, we applied Bonferroni correction to account for the two comparisons conducted for each physiological channel.

Epoch analyses identical to those used in Experiment 1 were also conducted to examine the temporal dynamics of aversiveness differences. For each participant and physiological channel, the 1 s interval time‐series data were averaged across trials. Differences between aversive and appetitive, aversive and neutral, and appetitive and neutral conditions were tested for each 1 s interval using the MNE‐Python package. Statistical significance was assessed via permutation testing (5000 iterations) with shuffled physiological time series. Similarly to the GLM analyses, we reported the uncorrected results for the contrast Aversive vs. Appetitive, and Bonferroni‐corrected results for the contrasts Aversive vs. Neutral and Appetitive vs. Neutral.

The Effect of Imagery Vividness

Multiple linear regression analyses were conducted to examine the effect of imagery vividness on behavioral ratings and physiological responses during affective mental imagery. After controlling for age and sex, neither VVIQ nor BAIS‐V significantly predicted difference score for valence or arousal ratings between aversive and non‐aversive stimuli.

After controlling for age, sex, the difference score of valence and arousal ratings, BAIS‐V, but not VVIQ, significantly predicted the mean SKT difference between aversive and non‐aversive stimuli, β = 0.01, SE = 0.003, t(46) = 2.90, p < 0.01 (Figure 13F). Higher auditory imagery ability was associated with a larger difference in SKT when imagining aversive versus appetitive stimuli. No significant effects of VVIQ or BAIS‐V were found for any other physiological measures.

Discussion

Using a modified protocol and more affectively potent prompts based on the results from Experiments 2 and 3, Experiment 4 revealed coherent changes across subjective ratings of valence and arousal, EMG potentiation over the corrugator supercilii, heart rate, and finger skin temperature during affective mental imagery. These physiological responses were also modulated by individual differences in imagery ability, with skin temperature differences between aversive and appetitive imagery correlating with auditory imagery.

Prior research has emphasized that successful affective imagery critically depends on task instructions and prompt design (Bradley et al. 2023). For example, using instructions that encourage participants to “re‐experience” rather than simply “think about” emotional events, or prompts that include response propositions, elicit stronger physiological responses (Carroll et al. 1982; Lang et al. 1983; Schwartz et al. 1976). Our findings extend this work by demonstrating that longer imagery periods (5 s of reading followed by 7 s of imagery), clear multisensory instructions, and prompts enriched with descriptive language all enhance both subjective and physiological measures of affective engagement. These findings underscore the importance of carefully reporting and standardizing paradigms, instructions, and stimuli in mental imagery research to facilitate meaningful comparisons across studies.

Across both physiology experiments (Experiments 1 and 4), we found significantly greater EMG potentiation over the corrugator supercilii during affective imagery, consistent with previous research showing that corrugator activity is a robust marker of negative affect, regardless of whether stimuli are externally presented or internally generated (Oh et al. 2025). In contrast, EMG activity over the zygomaticus major was inconsistent across conditions and experiments, aligning with prior reports that EMGz is not a stable indicator of positive valence (Bradley and Lang 2000; Lang et al. 1993; Larsen et al. 2003).

Interestingly, we found sustained heart rate deceleration during imagery of both aversive and appetitive scenarios, rather than the acceleration we hypothesized (H2) and that has been reported in many previous studies (Bradley et al. 2023). According to the Defense Cascade Model, activation of the aversive motivational system is initially associated with heart rate deceleration, reflecting enhanced sensory intake, followed by acceleration when the organism transitions to action preparation. One plausible explanation for the sustained heart rate deceleration observed here is that the mild, everyday aversive scenarios used here were not strong enough to trigger action‐system responses (e.g., fight‐or‐flight) and thus did not elicit HR acceleration typically associated with action preparedness. A second explanation relates to prompt design: our prompts emphasized stimulus propositions rather than response propositions, likely leading participants to focus on reconstructing sensory details rather than bodily reactions, reflected in HR deceleration. Although there is no external sensory input during mental imagery, neuroimaging studies have shown that brain regions involved in sensory association and object representation are activated during mental imagery, suggesting that vivid mental imagery assembles to a “re‐experience” of actual perception (Bradley et al. 2023; Mechelli 2004; Zvyagintsev et al. 2013). Supporting our interpretation, we observed that individuals with better visual imagery ability, as indicated by lower VVIQ scores, exhibited larger heart rate differences between aversive and non‐aversive imagery. Moreover, Mueller et al. (2019) showed that detailed prompts containing both stimulus‐ and response‐propositions (e.g., stepping on a pin with a bare foot) can elicit strong fear imagery, yet still produce HR deceleration. Similarly, Lang et al. (1983) reported that good imagers initially showed HR deceleration during fearful imagery before response‐focused training, but shifted to HR acceleration after being trained to focus on actions and bodily responses. Thus, the HR deceleration observed here likely reflects an imagery strategy emphasizing perceptual reconstruction, paralleling sensory intake response to external stimuli. Future work should test this by manipulating response components in prompts or by comparing everyday versus life‐threatening aversive imagery.

We also observed a trend toward HR acceleration during neutral imagery and significant differences of the temporal dynamics between appetitive and neutral imagery, though this finding should be interpreted cautiously. To maximize statistical power for aversive vs. appetitive comparisons, we used fewer neutral trials (10) than aversive and appetitive conditions (40 each), limiting comparability. Future studies using balanced trial counts are needed to clarify the HR change during neutral imagery.

For EDA, differences between aversive and non‐aversive imagery were weak or nonsignificant, and effects disappeared after controlling for arousal, consistent with the view that skin conductance primarily indexes arousal during affective processing (Bradley et al. 2001; Bradley and Lang 2000, 2007; Codispoti et al. 2001; Itkes et al. 2017; Palomba et al. 2000; Sato et al. 2020; Vaccaro et al. 2024; van Dooren et al. 2012). Because our stimuli were designed to maximize valence separation while equating arousal across aversive and non‐aversive/appetitive conditions, any residual EDA differences (e.g., the significant EDA difference between aversive and appetitive imagery occurring at 6–7 s) likely reflect arousal rather than true aversiveness.

In contrast, SKT showed robust differentiation across affective conditions. Finger temperature decreased during aversive imagery relative to both appetitive and neutral imagery, while no difference was observed between neutral and appetitive imagery (which differed in arousal). Crucially, individual differences in auditory imagery ability predicted greater SKT differences between aversive and appetitive imagery even after controlling for arousal and other covariates, indicating that this effect cannot be attributed to arousal alone. Prior research suggests that skin temperature responses depend on measurement location (Ioannou et al. 2014). Fingertip temperature, in particular, has been shown to decrease in response to negative states such as anger, anxiety, fear, and sadness, and to increase in response to happiness (Kreibig 2010; Rimm‐Kaufman and Kagan 1996). Our findings in Experiment 4 replicate this pattern for aversive imagery, though not for the shorter audiovisual or auditory stimuli in Experiment 1. The timing of SKT responses may explain the discrepancy: fingertip temperature changes typically emerge around 15 s post‐stimulus (Kistler et al. 1998). Because Experiment 1 included only 5 s stimuli followed by a 10‐s recovery window, the 15‐s analysis period was likely too short to capture delayed SKT responses. In contrast, the significant effects observed between 12 and 17 s in Experiment 4 align with the expected lag reported in prior studies (Kistler et al. 1998). Thus, extended recording windows are essential for capturing the full thermoregulatory dynamics of affective imagery, and future research should examine whether SKT responses vary across sensory modalities.

Finally, an alternative explanation for the physiological responses observed here is that participants' valence ratings and physiological responses reflected semantic knowledge of the prompts (e.g., “I should feel sad when imagining a crying baby”) rather than genuine affective experience. Although we consider this unlikely, we conducted an additional online experiment to directly test and rule out this possibility.

Experiment 5

Experiment 5 was designed to rule out the possibility that changes in subjective ratings and physiological responses observed in earlier experiments were driven by participants' semantic knowledge of the prompts rather than genuine affective experiences during aversive or appetitive mental imagery. Prior studies have shown that semantic evaluation and affective experience can be dissociated by manipulating task instructions: one set directing participants to focus on their affective experience and the other on the semantic knowledge of the stimuli (Itkes et al. 2017, 2019; Itkes and Kron 2019). Based on this distinction, we hypothesized that valence and arousal ratings would differ significantly between the two instructional conditions, reflecting the presence of genuine affective engagement when participants focused on their emotional experience.

Methods

Stimuli

The same 45 prompts used in Experiment 4 (20 aversive, 20 appetitive, and 5 neutral) were included in this experiment, along with an additional five neutral prompts to increase statistical power for the neutral condition while keeping the length of the online experiment within one hour.

Participants

Thirty‐two undergraduate students (M age = 19.13, SDage = 1.31, 27 females and five males) participated in this experiment. All procedures were approved by and conducted in accordance with the Institutional Review Board at the University of South Carolina.

Experimental Design

The experimental paradigm was similar to that used in Experiment 3. Each imagery prompt was displayed on the screen for 5 s, followed by a 7 s imagery period during which participants were instructed to continue imagining the described content. The same instructions used in Experiments 3 and 4 were employed: “You will read prompts and imagine the described scenes and sounds as vividly as possible, as if you are seeing and hearing them.” After each imagery trial, participants rated their affective experience using a valence‐by‐arousal grid. The instruction “you will rate the prompts on the grid below in terms of valence (pleasantness or unpleasantness) and arousal (intensity) for how you feel about the prompt” was used to emphasize the affective experience. Participants then rated 12 discrete emotions on a 5‐point Likert scale. Finally, participants were asked to complete a second valence‐by‐arousal grid reflecting their semantic knowledge of the prompts, using the following instruction (Figure 14).You will be asked to rate the prompts again on the same valence‐arousal grid, but this time for the content of the prompt, which may be different from your feelings. To emphasize: … you must distinguish between your actual emotional response and the expected emotional content of the prompt. For example, if you are asked to imagine being “stuck in traffic,” you might recognize this as a negative situation where irritation or anger is typically expected. However, you may not personally feel that way while imagining it. When we ask you to rate “how you feel,” we want to know your genuine emotional response in the moment—how you actually feel during your imagery. When we ask you to rate “the content of the prompt,” we are asking how negative or positive you perceive the event to be, how you would expect to feel in that situation, and how a typical person might react—reflecting your knowledge and expectations about the event. “How you feel” and “the content of the prompt” may be the same or different, depending on the person and the situation. Analysis

For each prompt, subjective ratings of affective experience and semantic knowledge on valence and arousal were averaged across participants. Repeated measures t‐tests were conducted to compare ratings between affective experience and semantic knowledge.

Discussion

In this experiment, we employed the same paradigm and validated prompts from the previous experiments to elicit affective mental imagery but introduced two instructional conditions: one directing participants to rate their affective experience during imagery, and the other to rate their semantic knowledge of what the prompts described or how they should feel. When participants focused on their affective experience, they rated aversive imagery as more negative and more arousing than when focusing on semantic evaluation. Although these differences were numerically small, they were associated with large within‐condition effect sizes, with Cohen's d values of approximately 0.9 for both valence and arousal in the aversive condition (Cohen 2013; Lakens 2013). Together, these findings confirm that semantic knowledge and affective experience are dissociable processes and strengthen our interpretation across Experiments 1–4 that the observed behavioral and physiological responses were driven by genuine affective engagement during imagery, rather than by cognitive judgments based solely on the semantic content of the prompts.

General Discussion and Conclusions

The present study investigated behavioral and physiological responses to affective mental imagery of mild, everyday aversive and appetitive scenarios across five experiments. Collectively, the findings provide converging evidence that everyday aversive imagery can reliably elicit subjective affective experiences and selective somatovisceral responses. Across the two physiology experiments, our findings provide converging evidence that heightened EMGc potentiation is an indicator for aversive imagery compared with non‐aversive imagery, aligning with prior findings showing that EMGc is a gold‐standard indicator for hedonic valence. In contrast, EMGz is not a reliable indicator for appetitive processing, which also aligns with prior mixed findings in the literature (Bradley and Lang 2000; Lang et al. 1993; Larsen et al. 2003; Oh et al. 2025).

For other channels, the strength and consistency of these responses depend critically on task design and stimulus characteristics, including imagery duration, instructional focus, and prompt content. Notably, the absence of heightened EDA and HR during aversive imagery is not unexpected. Unlike prior studies that used imagery prompts designed to evoke intense fear or threat, our stimuli were intentionally constructed to reflect broad, everyday aversive and appetitive situations. Such scenarios are typically low in arousal and moderate in valence, and therefore may not fully engage aversive or appetitive motivational systems. Moreover, we selected aversive and non‐aversive stimuli in Experiment 1 as well as aversive and appetitive stimuli in Experiment 4 to be matched on arousal, despite statistical differences in subjective arousal ratings from physiology participants. According to the Defense Cascade Model (Bradley and Lang 2007), motivational system activation is closely indexed by arousal. Thus, comparable arousal levels across conditions may have resulted in similar activation of motivational systems, leading to nonsignificant contrasts between aversive and non‐aversive imagery. This interpretation is consistent with prior evidence that EDA primarily reflects subjective arousal rather than valence (Bradley et al. 2001; Bradley and Lang 2007; Lang et al. 1993; Sato et al. 2020; Sato and Kochiyama 2022; Ventura‐Bort et al. 2022). Moreover, for consistency, we applied a fixed analysis time window across physiological channels in both epoch‐wise and trial‐wise analyses. However, our epoch‐based results suggest distinct temporal dynamics across measures, with rapid facial muscle responses emerging shortly after stimulus onset and more delayed vascular responses reflected in SKT. These findings highlight the need for further methodological work to determine optimal aggregation strategies for different physiological signals.

Our findings partially supported the hypothesis that mental imagery ability influences physiological responses during affective mental imagery. Specifically, we found that individuals with higher visual imagery ability exhibited larger differences in HR change between aversive and non‐aversive imagery (Experiment 1), and that individuals with higher auditory imagery ability showed larger differences in SKT change between aversive and appetitive imagery (Experiment 5). However, neither auditory nor visual imagery ability significantly predicted subjective ratings or responses in the other physiological channels. At first glance, these results appear to contrast with prior findings demonstrating associations between physiological responses, neural activity, and individual differences in mental imagery ability (Acosta and Vila 1990; Fulford et al. 2018; G. A. Miller et al. 1987; Wicken et al. 2021). We interpret this inconsistency as likely arising from differences in stimulus characteristics. Our imagery prompts were designed to depict everyday scenarios, which may have reduced variability in imagery generation across participants. Consistent with this interpretation, we observed a ceiling effect in subjective vividness ratings and a floor effect in reported difficulty generating mental images (Experiments 2–4), suggesting that most participants were able to generate vivid mental images regardless of their scores on the VVIQ and BAIS‐V questionnaires. These findings indicate that the influence of imagery ability on affective and physiological responses may be most evident when stimuli place greater demands on imagery generation. Accordingly, future studies examining the effects of mental imagery ability should employ a broader range of stimuli, including scenarios that vary systematically in imagery complexity and difficulty.

Finally, our findings contribute to the ongoing debate regarding the reproducibility and specificity of physiological correlates of subjective emotional experience. Accumulating evidence suggests that associations between physiological responses and emotion are highly dependent on stimulus type, experimental paradigm, and sample characteristics (Koppold et al. 2026; Lang et al. 1993; Sato et al. 2021; Ventura‐Bort et al. 2022). Greater transparency in stimulus materials and analytic pipelines will be essential for facilitating cross‐study comparisons and cumulative progress.

The present findings meaningfully extend prior work that has largely focused on extreme or life‐threatening imagery. By examining responses to mild, everyday aversive scenarios, this study addresses an important gap in affective science and establishes baseline patterns of behavioral and physiological reactivity to common aversive experiences, providing a foundation for understanding emotional processing in individuals who exhibit heightened sensitivity to everyday stimuli, such as those with misophonia, OCD, or certain forms of PTSD. More broadly, these findings underscore the need for precise reporting of imagery prompt design, instructions, and experimental paradigm in mental imagery research, and they point toward promising new applications of affective imagery paradigms in both basic and clinical domains.
