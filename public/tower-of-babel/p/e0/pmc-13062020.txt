Ultrasound-assisted extraction, quantification and characterization of inulin from agave, chicory and Jerusalem artichoke
Authors: Sajjad Hussain, Muhammad Atif Randhawa, Allah Rakha, Muhammad Shahid
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study investigated the extraction, quantification and characterization of inulin obtained from agave (Agave americana) leaves, chicory (Cichorium intybus) roots and Jerusalem artichoke (Helianthus tuberosus) tubers. Ultrasound-assisted extraction (UAE) was employed under varying solid-to-liquid ratios (1:3–1:6) and ultrasonic frequencies (30–60 kHz), while maintaining a constant extraction time of 35 min and a temperature of 40 °C. Proximate and mineral profiling revealed agave to be richer in moisture and fat, chicory in protein and ash, and Jerusalem artichoke in fiber, with potassium and calcium were the dominant minerals across all sources. Inulin extraction efficiency increased significantly at a solid-to-liquid ratio (1:6) and an ultrasonic frequency (60 kHz). Among the tested samples, Jerusalem artichoke exhibited the highest inulin content (93.53 ± 3.81%), followed by chicory (90.39 ± 2.96%) and agave (78.99 ± 3.08%). Physicochemical and techno-functional evaluation showed that agave inulin contained high dry matter (92.86%), relatively high oil-holding capacity (3.27 g/g) and moderate solubility (74.32 g/L). Chicory inulin exhibited maximum solubility (114.66 g/L) with moderate dry matter (91.48%) and lower water-holding capacity (1.68 g/g). Jerusalem artichoke inulin presented the highest dry matter (94.89%) along with superior water-holding capacity (5.40 g/g) and comparartively lower solubility (4.76 g/L). Fourier Transform Infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy of inulin confirmed the presence of alcohol and glycosidic ether groups while X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns displayed broad amorphous halos with semi-crystalline features varying by source. In conclusion, Jerusalem artichoke proved to be the most promising source of inulin, demonstrating higher extraction efficiency and favorable techno-functional and structural properties for food and nutraceutical applications.

Introduction

Inulin is a water-soluble polysaccharide, a type of indigestible carbohydrate and classified as a fructan, composed of fructosyl units linked by β-(2→1) glycosidic bonds, with a terminal glucose moiety attached via an α-(1→2) linkage. It is widely distributed across more than 36,000 plant species. Inulin has been granted Generally Recognized as Safe (GRAS) status in the United States. Its discovery dates back to the early 19th century when Valentine Rose first identified and extracted inulin from the Elecampane root (Inula helenium), introducing it to the scientific community1,2. Inulin is predominantly found in the roots, tubers and bulbs of plant species belonging to the Poaceae, Liliaceae, Asteraceae and Amaryllidaceae families3. According to Mudannayake et al.4, inulin fructans naturally occur in various food plants, including chicory (Cichorium intybus), globe artichoke (Cynara scolymus), dahlia (Dahlia pinnata), leek (Allium ampeloprasum), asparagus (Asparagus falcatus), onion (Allium cepa), wheat (Triticum aestivum), banana (Musa sp.) and garlic (Allium sativum). It can be used as a bulking agent, sugar replacer and as a good source of fiber. It also works as prebiotic and enhances calcium absorption efficiency of the body5.

The increasing prevalence of non-communicable diseases such as diabetes, obesity, and gastrointestinal disorders is closely linked to modern lifestyle patterns characterized by high consumption of protein and fat-rich processed foods and insufficient dietary fiber intake2. Technological advancements have promoted sedentary lifestyles, contributing to increased metabolic health risks and persistently low dietary fiber intake, thereby increasing demand for functional ingredients such as inulin6. Although fiber enrichment of foods has been widely studied, industrial application remains constrained by the limited availability of economical, renewable inulin-rich plant sources and sustainable extraction methods with industrial relevance. Moreover, systematic investigations integrating extraction efficiency, scalability, and functional quality are scarce7. Importantly, studies on inulin extraction and comprehensive characterization from agave, chicory, and Jerusalem artichoke under Pakistani agro-climatic conditions are very limited, despite the strong influence of soil composition, climate and growing conditions on inulin yield, molecular structure, and functional properties7,8. This study addresses these gaps by generating an application-oriented dataset that integrates extraction performance with compositional and functional characteristics, providing practical insights for the utilization of locally available plant resources for functional food development and value-added industrial applications9.

The leaf powder of Agave americana contains higher amounts of dietary fiber, sugars and protein while exhibiting lower ash and lipid contents. Their leaves are good source of fructans as well. Owing to its good nutritional profile agave plant showed its potential role in food applications10. Jerusalem artichoke (Helianthus tuberosus) tuber is also efficient source of inulin as dietary fiber that has potential role in human health improvements. It originated in the north-central United States. It has a favorable chemical profile, containing approximately 80% water, 15% carbohydrate and 1–2% protein on a fresh weight basis. There are some trace amounts of mono and poly unsaturated fatty acids in the artichoke tubers as well11. Furthermore, it contains higher amounts of inulin (17–25%). From an ecological , it has great acceptability of cultivation in diverse climatic conditions. It is mostly suited to low acid soil with pH around 5.0–7.0 and shows sensitivity towards soil reactions12. Chicory (Cichorium intybus) is another herb rich in dietary fiber contents and cultivated worldwide. It has potential for the application in food industry such as salad dressings, tea blends and coffee supplements. Chemical composition of chicory root powder contained higher amount of polyphenols, inulin, oligofructose and sesquiterpene. Cichorium intybus L. is a perennial herb of the genus Cichorium (family Asteraceae). In the food industry, it is used in teas, tea blends, salads, and as a coffee additive, and serves as a valuable source of inulin1314. Different researchers reported that chicory root powder contains approximately 14% sucrose, 68% inulin, 5% cellulose, 4% ash, 6% protein, and 3% other components on a dry weight basis12.

The food industry is increasingly adopting sustainable and energy-efficient extraction technologies to reduce solvent consumption, energy demand and environmental impact. Conventional extraction processes are associated with high energy requirements, extensive solvent usage, and increased greenhouse gas emissions, with extraction steps accounting for a major portion of total processing energy15. Consequently, advanced non-thermal and low-solvent extraction techniques such as UAE, microwave extraction, supercritical and subcritical fluid extraction, ultrafiltration, flash distillation, and controlled pressure-drop processes have gained increasing attention. Among these technologies, UAE has emerged as a promising alternative due to its low energy consumption, reduced processing time, and enhanced extraction efficiency. Ultrasound promotes mass transfer through acoustic cavitation, where the collapse of microbubbles disrupts plant cell walls and facilitates solvent penetration and the release of intracellular compounds. This mechanism enhances delignification and reduces the structural recalcitrance of plant matrices such as agave by-products16.

Conventional thermal extraction methods often result in significant degradation of fructans due to prolonged exposure to elevated temperatures. Such degradation alters the degree of polymerization, which directly influences the functional properties of inulin, including solubility, viscosity, and prebiotic activity. In contrast, ultrasound treatment has been shown to minimize fructan degradation while significantly improving extraction efficiency17. Preservation of fructan integrity is therefore essential to maintain molecular structure and functional performance in food and nutraceutical applications. The degree of polymerization plays a critical role during UAE by affecting energy absorption and molecular stability. Controlled ultrasound conditions allow targeted modification of inulin structure, enabling the development of high-value products from artichoke and other fructan-rich plant sources18. Previous studies have demonstrated that optimized ultrasound parameters improve inulin recovery compared with conventional extraction. High-intensity ultrasound has been reported to enhance inulin extraction from burdock root through increased amplitude and appropriate sonication time, resulting in higher efficiency and improved performance19.

Recent comparative studies have further highlighted the advantages of ultrasound-based extraction methods. García-Villalba et al.20 reported that ultrasound–microwave-assisted extraction of fructans from Agave durangensis achieved higher yields with reduced extraction time, lower energy consumption, and the use of water as a solvent. Similarly, optimized UAE conditions at moderate temperatures and controlled ultrasonic power have been shown to improve fructan recovery while preserving molecular integrity21,22. These findings support the potential of UAE as an efficient and sustainable approach for inulin and fructan recovery.

The primary objective of this study was to investigate the UAE and quantification of inulin from agave, chicory, and Jerusalem artichoke, following proximate and mineral characterization of the raw materials. The secondary objective focused on a comprehensive evaluation of the physicochemical, techno-functional, and structural properties of the extracted inulin. This integrated dataset offers valuable insights for selecting optimal inulin sources to meet the increasing demand for functional and fiber-enriched foods.

Materials and methods

Materials

Fresh plant materials of agave (Agave americana) leaves and chicory (Cichorium intybus) roots were obtained from the experimental fields of the University of Agriculture, Faisalabad (UAF). Jerusalem artichoke (Helianthus tuberosus) tubers were collected from a privately cultivated agricultural farm in Raiwind, Lahore, Pakistan. All chemicals and reagents, including ethanol, acetone, acids, bases and mineral standard solutions were obtained from commercially available supplier i.e., Sigma-Aldrich. The K-FRUC enzymatic kit (Megazyme, Ireland) was used for inulin (fructan) quantification.

Sample preparation for proximate and mineral analysis

Agave leaves were washed, cut into uniform pieces, and briefly sun-dried under ambient conditions with adequate air circulation to remove surface moisture. Chicory roots and Jerusalem artichoke tubers were washed to remove adhering soil, longitudinally sliced into thin pieces, and sun-dried under the same conditions until surface moisture was eliminated. The prepared samples were stored in a desiccator until further analysis.

Proximate composition analysis

The proximate composition of the raw materials, including moisture, ash, crude protein, crude fat, crude fiber, and nitrogen-free extract (NFE), was determined in triplicate following the AOAC23 procedures. Moisture content was measured by hot air oven drying 5 g of the sample at 105 ± 5 °C until a constant weight was obtained (AOAC Method 926.12). Ash content was determined by incinerating the sample in a muffle furnace at 550 °C until a greyish-white residue was formed (AOAC Method 942.05). Crude protein was determined using the Kjeldahl method (AOAC Method 960.52) with nitrogen content multiplied by a factor of 6.25 to calculate crude protein. Crude fat was extracted from 5 g of the sample using a Soxhlet apparatus with n-hexane as the extraction solvent (AOAC Method 948.22). Crude fiber was determined by sequential digestion with 1.25% sulfuric acid followed by 1.25% sodium hydroxide, then dried and ashed in a muffle furnace (AOAC Method 978.10). Mineral analysis

Mineral composition was determined according to AOAC23 Method 3.014-016 using an Atomic Absorption Spectrophotometer (AAS) and a Flame Photometer. For sample preparation, 0.5 g of dried sample was digested with a 7:3 mixture of HClO₄ and HNO₃ until colorless fumes appeared, then diluted to 100 mL with deionized water and filtered. Potassium, sodium and calcium were quantified using a Flame Photometer (Sherwood Scientific Ltd., UK), while iron, zinc and magnesium were analyzed using an AAS (Varian AA240, Australia).

Sample preparation for inulin extraction

Agave leaves were washed, manually peeled to remove the outer epidermal layer, cut into uniform pieces, sun-dried under ambient conditions and ground into a slurry. The fresh material was immediately subjected to UAE, following the method of Sánchez-Madrigal et al.17 with slight modifications.

Chicory roots were washed, dried at 40 °C for 10 h, and ground to pass through a 100-mesh sieve (0.149 mm) using a laboratory grinder. The powdered sample was stored in a desiccator until extraction as per El-Kholy et al.24.

Jerusalem artichoke tubers were washed, sliced into 2 mm thick pieces, and dried at 60 °C for 10 h. The dried tubers were milled and sieved through a 0.85 mm mesh and stored in a desiccator until extraction, following Srinameb et al.25.

Inulin extraction

Prepared plant samples from Agave, Chicory and Jerusalem artichoke were subjected to UAE using distilled water as the extraction solvent. For each plant source, a fixed amount of prepared sample was dispersed in distilled water according to the predefined solid-to-liquid ratios (S:L). Ultrasonic treatment was applied for 35 min at 40 °C under four extraction treatments, combining two frequencies (30 and 60 kHz) with two S:L ratios (1:3 and 1:6), as detailed in Table 1. The extraction procedure was carried out with minor modifications based on the methods reported by Sánchez-Madrigal et al.17 and Xu et al.18.

After sonication, the extract was filtered to remove insoluble residues, and inulin was precipitated by the addition of ethanol and stored at 4 °C for 24 h. The precipitate was recovered by centrifugation, followed by solvent removal using rotary evaporation. Finally, the extract was lyophilized to obtain inulin powder, which was stored at 4 °C until further analysis following Bouaziz et al.10.

Quantification of inulin

Extracted inulin (fructans) was quantified using an enzymatic–spectrophotometric method employing the K-FRUC assay kit (Megazyme, Bray, Ireland), following the procedure (Figure 1) described by McCleary et al.26, with minor modifications. Physicochemical properties of inulin powder

The best inulin powder treatments obtained from UAE were selected and further refined by passing through a < 200 μm mesh, following Bouaziz et al.10. The fine fraction obtained was used for further analysis.

Dry matter (%)

Dry matter content of the inulin powders was determined by drying the sample in a hot-air oven at 105 ± 2 °C for 16 h, following the AOAC23 protocol. Results were expressed as percentage dry matter.

pH

The pH of inulin was measured at 25 °C using a calibrated digital pH meter, according to AOAC23.

Viscosity

Viscosity was determined on a 5% (w/v) inulin aqueous solution using a digital viscometer (Brookfield type), following AOAC23. Measurements were performed in triplicate at 25 °C.

Water solubility index (WSI)

The WSI was assessed according to the method of Ding et al.27 with minor modifications. Briefly, 0.5 g of inulin powder was dispersed in 10 mL of distilled water at 25 °C, shaken periodically for 30 min, and centrifuged at 3000 × g for 15 min. Techno-functional characteristics of inulin powder

Water holding capacity (WHC)

WHC was determined using the method of Bouaziz et al.28. 1.0 g of inulin was mixed with 15 mL of distilled water, soaked overnight, and centrifuged at 15,000 × g for 20 min. WHC was expressed as g of water retained per g of dry powder.

Oil holding capacity (OHC)

OHC was measured following Bouaziz et al.28. 1.0 g of inulin was mixed with 10 mL of corn oil, centrifuged at 7125 × g for 20 min, and the retained oil was recorded as g of oil per g of sample.

Swelling capacity

Swelling capacity was determined following AOAC23 with slight modifications. 1.0 g of inulin was mixed with 15 mL of distilled water and allowed to swell overnight. The retained water volume was measured and expressed as mL of water per g of dry inulin powder.

Structural analysis of inulin powder

Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy

FTIR spectroscopy was performed using a Cary 630 (Agilent Technologies, USA) FTIR spectrophotometer at the UAF, Pakistan, following the method described by Pasha et al.29. The spectrum was recorded over a range of 500–4000 cm⁻¹ using a standard KBr beam splitter, and results were interpreted using Sigma Aldrich reference charts. Each sample underwent 10 scans, with equipment optimization and ethanol cleaning between runs to ensure accuracy.

X-ray diffraction (XRD)

The crystallinity of inulin powder was determined using a D8 Advance X-ray diffractometer (Bruker AXS, Germany) located at Government College University Faisalabad (GCUF), employing Cu-Kα radiation (λ = 1.5406 Å), according to the method described by Lou et al.30. The diffraction patterns was recorded over a 2θ range of 10–60°.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using Statistix 8.1 software. Data obtained under a completely randomized design (CRD) were subjected to one-way and two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). When significant differences were observed, means were separated using Tukey’s honestly significant difference (HSD) test at a significance level of p < 0.05, following the principles described by Montgomery31.

Results and discussion

The proximate and mineral composition of the selected dietary fiber sources was determined to establish a comprehensive compositional basis for both the interpretation of inulin extraction behavior and the evaluation of nutritional and industrial relevance. Variations in moisture, total dietary fiber, protein, and ash contents influence the structural characteristics of plant matrices and may affect polysaccharide accessibility during UAE.

In addition, the mineral profile provides important information regarding the nutritional value of inulin-containing ingredients and supports the selection of suitable raw materials for food and nutraceutical applications, where mineral content is relevant for product formulation, labeling, and regulatory considerations. Collectively, this compositional characterization strengthens the study by enabling a more meaningful comparison of inulin yield, techno-functional properties and practical applicability of the extracted inulin from different plant sources.

Proximate composition of dietary fiber sources

The proximate composition of agave leaves, chicory roots and Jerusalem artichoke tubers demonstrated significant differences in most nutritional parameters evaluated (Table 2), with the exception of ash content. Agave leaves exhibited the highest moisture (85.23%) and crude fat (0.93%), while chicory roots contained the highest crude protein (2.39%) and ash (1.06%), as shown in Table 2. Jerusalem artichoke showed the greatest crude fiber content (5.55%). Moisture content was comparatively greater in agave leaves and Jerusalem artichoke tubers than in chicory roots. Moisture, crude protein, crude fiber, and NFE differed significantly among the samples. Ash and crude fat showed no significant differences.

These findings are consistent with previously published reports. For instance, Sánchez-Madrigal et al.17 and Romero-Lopez et al.32 reported similar moisture and fiber values for agave. Crude fiber values for chicory roots were consistent with Nwafor et al.33 and Mona et al.34, though some variation exists due to the basis of measurement (fresh vs. dry weight). Jerusalem artichoke’s crude fiber content was in agreement with findings by Rashid et al.35 and Semuli36, with variations attributed to cultivar differences, environmental conditions, and analytical methods. Overall, compositional differences highlight the diverse nutritional profiles of these fiber-rich sources.

Mineral contents of dietary fiber sources

Minerals are essential components of food that play vital roles in maintaining essential physiological functions including bone health, muscle activity, energy metabolism and immune system support. Consuming a balanced diet with adequate mineral intake is crucial for overall well-being and preventing mineral deficiencies37. In the current study, mineral contents varied notably among the dietary fiber sources (Table 3). Agave leaves were particularly rich in potassium (662.33 mg/100 g) and calcium (564 mg/100 g), while chicory roots showed higher sodium (55.44 mg/100 g) and zinc (3.06 mg/100 g) levels. Jerusalem artichoke tubers had comparatively lower overall mineral content but moderate potassium (436.67 mg/100 g) and iron (2.54 mg/100 g). Overall, mineral contents differed significantly among the three inulin sources. Bouaziz et al.10 conducted the study for mineral contents of agave plant (Agave americana L.) leaves as dry matter and found close values to our study for sodium (37 mg/100 g), calcium (571 mg/100 g), zinc (1.299 mg/100 g) and magnesium (86 mg/100 g). Nwafor et al.33 analyzed the mineral composition (mg/100 g) of chicory roots, reporting values close to those found in our study, with sodium at 67.42 ± 2.45, calcium at 181.26 ± 4.40, iron at 1.77 ± 0.21 and magnesium at 20.14 ± 1.69 mg/100 g.

Rashid et al.35 measured the mineral content of chicory roots and recorded sodium 48.93 ± 1.87 mg/100 g, potassium 287.66 ± 9.13 mg/100 g, calcium 138.91 ± 1.18 mg/100 g and magnesium at 21.77 ± 0.74 mg/100 g, close to our findings. Similarly, Park et al.38 reported values for sodium (57.4 mg/100 g), calcium (156.3 mg/100 g), zinc (2.58 mg/100 g) and iron (2.84 mg/100 g) which are in agreement with our results. Meanwhile, Jangra and Madan39 found zinc 2.43 ± 0.14 mg/100 g in chicory roots. Additionally, Mona et al.34 found 1.77 mg/100 g iron and 20.14 mg/100 g magnesium which support our results. The results of the current study align closely with those of Rashid et al.35, who also investigated the mineral composition of Jerusalem artichoke in mg/100 g and reported sodium at 3.74 ± 0.14, calcium 12.67 ± 0.40, magnesium 15.46 ± 0.49, iron 3.26 ± 0.10, potassium 393.61 ± 12.42 and zinc 0.11 ± 0.01. Munim et al.40 recorded sodium 4.00 mg/100 g, potassium 429 mg/100 g, calcium 14 mg/100 g and magnesium 17 mg/100 g in agreement with the current findings. The results were comparable with Kocsis et al.41 who found sodium ranging from 1.8 to 4.0 mg/100 g, potassium from 420 to 657 mg/100 g and calcium between 14 and 37 mg/100 g. Similarly, Long et al.42 reported potassium at 420–657 mg/100 g and calcium 14–37 mg/100 g matches the current study. Alongside Judprasong et al.43 also supports this study detecting calcium at 20 mg/100 g, zinc 0.27–0.28 mg/100 g, iron at 0.32 mg/100 g and magnesium at 16 mg/100 g. Additionally, Sawicka et al.44 documented calcium at 21.00 mg/100 g, zinc 0.18 mg/100 g and magnesium 26.00 mg/100 g, close to current findings.

Inulin extraction and quantification

Figure 2 illustrates the effect of UAE under varying S:L ratios and ultrasonic frequencies. The data show that inulin contents increased with the increasing of S:L ratio and frequency. For agave, the highest value observed was 78.99 ± 3.08 in treatment A4 at combination of a S:L (1:6) and frequency (60 kHz). The Lowest value observed was 48.82 ± 3.26 in treatment A1 due to S:L (1:3) and frequency (30 kHz). In chicory, maximum inulin obtained was 90.39 ± 2.96 in treatment C4 and minimum was 51.18 ± 1.39 in treatment C1. Similarly, in Jerusalem artichoke highest value of inulin observed was 93.53 ± 3.81 in treatment J4 and lowest value was 58.67 ± 1.85 in treatment J1.

In comparison maximum inulin content was obtained from Jerusalem artichoke tubers for all the treatments as compared to agave leaves and chicory roots. Whereas minimum inulin was attained from agave leaves. This can be concluded that Jerusalem artichoke tubers contain the highest amount of inulin as compared to agave leaves and chicory roots and highest rate of extraction of inulin could be obtained by increasing liquid ratio and frequency in UAE. Figure 2 demonstrates that inulin content differed significantly among plant sources and treatments. Within each source (agave, chicory, and Jerusalem artichoke), higher treatment levels resulted in a significant increase in inulin content.

The UAE process involves sound energy, which ruptures the cell wall of the plant material and facilitates the release of cell constituents through cavity formation. This process requires less time for extraction compared to conventional methods.

The present findings are consistent with earlier studies on inulin and carbohydrate extraction. Sánchez-Madrigal et al.17 demonstrated that higher ultrasound power density (120 mW/mL) and a S:L (1:6) yielded maximum carbohydrate recovery from older Agave tequilana plants. Similarly, Escobar-Ledesma et al.45 reported optimal inulin extraction from Agave americana at 80 °C, S:L ratio 1:5, and 100 min. García-Villalba et al.20 further highlighted that combined ultrasound–microwave extraction of fructans from Agave durangensis was more sustainable, requiring only 5 min, less energy, water as the solvent, and providing higher yields (72 g/100 g). Li et al.21 also confirmed that UAE performed best at 55 °C and 120 W ultrasonic power.

Comparable results were obtained by Abbasi and Farzanmehr46, who reported maximum inulin recovery with higher ultrasound power. Lingyun et al.47 achieved 83.6% recovery from Jerusalem artichoke tubers under neutral pH, 77 °C, 20 min, and solvent-to-solid 11:1 ratio. Likewise, Redondo-Cuenca et al.48 reported high inulin–fructooligosaccharide contents in chicory roots (70.5 g/100 g) and Jerusalem artichoke (81.1 g/100 g), compared to only 4.2 g/100 g in globe artichoke by-products. Noori49 also found 99.47% inulin recovery from Jerusalem artichoke at 70 °C, pH 7, 60 min, and a 10 g/100 mL ratio. Moreover, Esmaeili et al.50 confirmed that UAE is faster and more efficient than conventional techniques for isolating inulin and other bioactive compounds from burdock roots.

Physicochemical and techno-functional properties of inulin

The fine fraction of extracted inulin powder of particle size <200 μm was further used for measuring physicochemical and techno -functional properties.

The results for the physicochemical attributes (Table 4) of the extracted inulin i.e. dry matter, pH, viscosity and WSI indicate their mean values were 92.86 ± 2.94% (dry matter), 5.82 ± 0.03 (pH), 2.04 ± 0.08 cP (viscosity) and 7.12 ± 0.03% (WSI) for agave. Similarly, the average values of the physicochemical properties of inulin extracted from chicory were 91.48 ± 3.15% (dry matter), 5.54 ± 0.09 (pH), 1.65 ± 0.08 cP (viscosity) and 8.14 ± 0.18% (WSI). Physicochemical properties of the extracted inulin from Jerusalem artichoke were 94.89 ± 2.95% (dry matter), 6.22 ± 0.24 (pH), 1.56 ± 0.11cP (viscosity) and 7.25 ± 0.30% (WSI).

Most physicochemical properties of inulin differed significantly among sources (p < 0.05). pH, viscosity, and WSI showed significant differences, while dry matter content was not significantly different among agave, chicory, and Jerusalem artichoke inulin (means sharing the same letter) (Table 4).

The present findings are in close agreement with Bouaziz et al.28, who reported the physicochemical characteristics of inulin from Agave americana (dry matter 92.19%, pH 5.53, and inulin content 79.12%), values comparable to those obtained in this study. Similar pH ranges (5–7) were also noted by El-Kholy et al.51 for inulin extracted from chicory, artichoke, and commercial sources. Rashid et al.52 further described inulin from chicory roots and Jerusalem artichoke with dry matter of 91–94%, pH around 5.5, viscosity ranging from 1.6 to 2.2 cP, and water solubility indices between 6 and 8%, which are consistent with the present results. Likewise, Panchev et al.53 found Jerusalem artichoke inulin to have a pH of 6–6.4 and viscosity of 1.2–1.3 cP, while López-Molina et al.54 reported 95% dry matter and pH values of 5–7 in chicory and artichoke inulin.

Inulin is recognized as a valuable functional ingredient due to its nutritional and industrial roles. Shoaib et al.1 reported that chicory inulin appears as a fine, neutral-flavored powder, providing about 10% of the sweetness of sucrose, with long-chain forms losing sweetness entirely. It also functions as a bulking agent and improves mouthfeel when combined with high-intensity sweeteners. Its solubility is influenced by chain length and temperature, with short-chain inulin being more soluble22. While stable up to 100 °C, it undergoes hydrolysis at low pH and high temperatures6. Chicory inulin dissolves readily in water (~ 10% at 25 °C), forming low-viscosity solutions, and higher concentrations (> 25% for regular and > 15% for long-chain) exhibit gelling behavior55,56.

These findings also support the observations of Safaa et al.57, who highlighted inulin as the principal carbohydrate (≈ 94% fructose polymer) in Helianthus tuberosus tubers, commonly used for high-fructose syrup production through hydrolysis. Bouaziz et al.28 further demonstrated functional variations between agave inulin and commercial inulin in terms of solubility, water activity, protein, ash, and emulsifying properties. Additionally, water solubility index was shown to increase with higher processing temperatures58, consistent with the present study.

The present findings are in agreement with El-Kholy et al.51, who reported high OHC: 1.37–1.38 g/g and WHC: 0.68–0.70 g/g for chicory and artichoke inulin, alongside swelling capacity values of 4.0–4.5 mL/g, comparable to those observed in this study. El-Kholy et al.24 further confirmed a pH of 5.8, which is consistent with the present results.

Inulin’s high WHC is attributed to its hydrophilic regions, where hydroxyl groups form hydrogen bonds with water molecules. This property is influenced by particle size and porosity, as smaller particles improve hydration59. Commercial chicory inulin has shown solubility as high as 123 g/L, linked to its fine particle size and structural hydrophilicity60. Such solubility and hydration properties make inulin suitable for a wide range of food applications, including bakery, dairy, and beverages, without negatively affecting sensory or rheological quality.

X-Ray diffraction of inulin extracted from Agave, chicory and Jerusalem artichoke

XRD was used to analyze the structure of inulin extracted from different plant sources. Results of XRD of inulin in illustrated in Fig. 4. This figure shows broad, amorphous halos due to its low crystallinity. Broader peaks around 15–300 (2θ), which reflect short range molecular order. These characteristics indicate the predominently amorphous nature of semicrystalline inulin, unlike crystalline inulin, which has sharp, well-defined peaks due to its highly ordered state.

XRD pattern of agave inulin (Fig. 4a) exhibited broad bands with a broader peak at 17.070, 20.220 and 23.140 (2θ) indicating amorphous structure. This is because molecules in the amorphous state are disordered, resulting in scattered bands.

Results of XRD of inulin extracted from chicory are illustrated in Fig. 4b. This figure shows broad peaks which are indicator of amorphous structure of sample. XRD pattern showed broad bands at 17.330, 20.810, 24.340 and 27.020 (2θ) with small variation from agave XRD pattern. These bands reflect the amorphous structure of chicory inulin, where disordered molecules generate scattered bands.

Results of XRD of inulin extracted from Jerusalem artichoke are illustrated in Fig. 4c. XRD pattern showed a broad bands at 15.060, 19.850, 24.380 and 30.260 (2θ) with small variation from agave and chicory XRD pattern.

Sonication produced cavitation that develop new hydrogen bonds, that disturb the structural arrangement of long chain inulin molecules and formed irregular structure. This results in the formation of gel type network of long chain inulin which increased the crystal size and hardness.

The XRD results of the present study are consistent with Lou et al.30, who reported characteristic peaks at 2θ = 12.32°, 16.73°, 18.02°, and 21.68° in long-chain inulin, with ultrasound showing no significant effect on crystallinity. Similarly, Apolinário et al.61 described an amorphous pattern for Agave sisalana inulin, with variations attributed to plant part and age. Saavedra-Leos et al.64 further demonstrated that inulin exhibited amorphous bands at low water activity, which shifted toward crystalline arrangements with increasing hydration.

Comparable findings were noted by Lin et al.65, who observed broad amorphous peaks in inulin from chicory, Jerusalem artichoke, and Asparagi Radix, while highlighting that higher polymerization (DP > 25) favors crystallinity. de Marins et al.66 similarly reported major peaks at 2θ = 11.63°, 15.85°, 18.42°, and 21.35° for burdock root inulin. Terkmane et al.67 observed semi-crystalline patterns in globe artichoke inulin, with multiple diffraction peaks between 11.9° and 30.4°. Panchev et al.53 also confirmed semi-crystalline structures in Jerusalem artichoke inulin, with variations explained by plant variety, age, and environmental factors.

Conclusion

This study systematically demonstrated the effectiveness of ultrasound-assisted extraction (UAE) for inulin extraction from agave leaves, chicory roots and Jerusalem artichoke tubers under varying solid-to-liquid ratios and ultrasonic frequencies. Inulin extraction was markedly influenced by the extraction conditions, with a solid-to-liquid ratio (1:6) and a frequency of 60 kHz consistently increasing the amount of inulin extracted from all plant sources. Variations in raw material composition were reflected in the physicochemical and techno-functional properties of the extracted inulin. Agave-derived inulin exhibited superior oil-holding capacity, chicory inulin showed enhanced solubility, while Jerusalem artichoke inulin displayed the highest dry matter content along with better water-holding capacity. Structural characterization confirmed the presence of characteristic inulin functional groups and revealed predominantly amorphous structures with source-dependent semi-crystalline features. Overall, Jerusalem artichoke emerged as a particularly efficient and functionally promising source of inulin, highlighting its suitability for advanced food formulations and nutraceutical applications.

Despite these findings, the study has certain limitations. The degree of polymerization of inulin was not assessed, restricting a comprehensive understanding of its structural attributes. Furthermore, all experiments were conducted at the laboratory scale, which may limit the direct applicability of the results to pilot or industrial settings. Resource constraints also restricted the experimental scope, potentially affecting reproducibility and generalizability. Future research should focus on scaling up the extraction process, characterizing polymerization profiles and exploring the incorporation of inulin from economical sources such as agave, chicory and Jerusalem artichoke into a wider range of functional food products, including bakery items, beverages, dairy, confectionery, and plant-based alternatives.
