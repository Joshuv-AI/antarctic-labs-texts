The effect of aging and asymmetrical hearing on speech discrimination
Authors: Koichiro Wasano, Takashi Nakagawa, Kimitaka Kaga, Kaoru Ogawa
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSpeech discrimination (SpD) is exacerbated by hearing loss, but the relationship between pure-tone audiometry (PTA) and SpD is poorly described and understood in the aging process. It is also unclear whether severity of left-right (L-R) differences in PTA threshold disproportionately affects SpD.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective cross-sectional study of a broad age range (10–99 years) of patients in Japan. Demographic data, SpD, and PTA threshold data were collected for each patient and side (L-R). We evaluated the association between chronological age and overall SpD, and SpD according to severity of PTA-threshold difference for ear pairs. Asymmetries in L-R PTA thresholds were stratified into four equally spaced categories: <10dB L-R difference to ≥40 dB L-R difference.ResultsAmong the 2760 eligible participants, data from 5508 ears are analyzed. Overall SpD gradually decreases with age, with SpD scores in the oldest groups being significantly worse (Kruskal-Wallis followed by Dunn’s test; p < 0.0001) than those of the youngest group. Comparison of worse-hearing ears to better-hearing ears within the same severity of hearing impairment reveals significant differences on SpD in ears that have moderate or severe hearing impairment (p < 0.0001). Post-hoc analysis identifies significant differences in SpD between the worse-hearing ears and the better-hearing ears that have hearing differences of 20 dB or more.ConclusionsSpD is dramatically affected by age and asymmetrical hearing. Therefore, appropriate audiological interventions should be proactively considered according to an individual’s hearing level, age, and difference in L-R hearing levels.

Introduction

Hearing impairment contributes to social isolation, depression, cognitive decline, and poor verbal communication1–3, especially as one ages. Moreover, unilateral hearing loss impairs auditory discrimination, requiring increased listening effort to discriminate speakers4. This further degrades quality of life5. Also, in normative aging-hearing studies, it is clear now that hearing progressively declines across the adult life span4.

As good hearing and listening ability is necessary for effective oral communication6, it is important to also determine whether age-related hearing loss (AHL) contributes to poor speech discrimination (SpD) in older people. One widely cited review reported that 40% of people older than 65 years have hearing loss great enough to impair oral communication7. Similarly, early on, Jerger suggested that speech intelligibility declines disproportionately with auditory loss across the life span8. However, the study of Tereza de Matos et al. showed an unclear relationship between SpD and hearing loss9. Although they had a relatively small sample size, they reported that SpD could be normal in cases of moderate to severe hearing losses, but in other cases could also be abnormal. They had subjects up to ~73 years old, but it is difficult to discern from their results if an effect of aging was found. Another study concluded that AHL and pure-tone audiometry (PTA) results are moderately associated with SpD ability10. This conclusion, however, was based on a rather small sample size. Those authors also noted that SpD ability can show significant variability among patients with similar PTA results. On the other hand, Maeda reported that PTA is a good predictor of speech perception11. Clearly identifying and quantifying the factors underlying AHL and SpD problems is important if appropriate audiological interventions are to be recommended. Appropriate intervention might reduce social, psychological, and financial burdens on patients and society12.

The Global Burden of Disease Study 2019 estimated that the global economic cost of hearing loss is increasing rapidly, currently exceeding $981 billion (USD). Societal costs related to losses in quality of life consume 46.6% of that total, and 32.0% is consumed by additional costs related to poor health for those with hearing loss13. These trends prompted the World Health Organization (WHO) to highlight and raise awareness about hearing loss and what efforts are being made to prevent it; they also recommended that routine screening for hearing loss be considered14. This WHO statement also highlighted various attempts around the world to combat hearing loss14. Given the growing aging population and societal and personal costs, prioritizing research that identifies factors and interventions for hearing loss is paramount.

To develop a policy to address the increasing prevalence of hearing impairment, it is important to understand the actual situation of hearing loss and how it affects those who experience it, especially their ability to communicate and interact. Until now, PTA has been the gold standard for detecting and assessing AHL15–17, as age-related changes in hearing are readily seen in PTA measurements of various populations around the world, including in Japanese people18,19. However, the situation is more complicated. While PTA is most commonly used in cognitive healthcare settings to assess hearing20, some evidence suggests that effective oral communication is heavily dependent on SpD ability21, especially in noisy environments6. Although several studies to date have reported various audiological findings in older adults (e.g., PTA and SpD) and have analyzed how they are related9,11,22, these studies included only a limited number of participants, and the study populations had a narrow age range. It is possible, then, that detecting and effectively treating hearing loss early on might greatly delay the onset of dementia and increase quality of life.

This study aimed to clarify the relationships between AHL and SpD, especially in very old patients. Furthermore, to the best of our knowledge, no reports to date have demonstrated how the severity of left-right differences in pure-tone hearing affects SpD. The present study addresses these important issues by analyzing a relatively large dataset of audiometry and SpD data from individuals across age decades, from the second to tenth decades of life and shows how SpD is dramatically affected by age and asymmetrical hearing.

Methods

Subjects, study design, and data collection

We conducted a retrospective cross-sectional study of a broad age range of patients in Japan. The subjects were patients who underwent both SpD testing and PTA testing at the National Hospital Organization Tokyo Medical Center from April 2000 to March 2020. Cases with stable hearing who underwent both hearing and SpD tests within 3 months of each other were included. Some cases underwent both tests on the same day, but later showed changes in hearing; these cases were included, since their hearing was stable during both tests on the day of testing. In this study, sex was determined based on the national health insurance information of each participant. As SpD test is configured by Japanese language, the subjects were limited to the patients who could understand Japanese language.

A variety of etiologies can lead to HL. To reduce the possibility of confounding our age-related analyses by including all HL etiologies, we restricted for inclusion only cases having HL etiologies related to inner ear damage (i.e., AHL, noise-induced HL, HL by ototoxic drugs). Thus, we did not include data from patients diagnosed with retrolabyrinthine deafness (i.e., vestibular schwannoma, auditory agnosia, motor aphasia, and sensory aphasia); functional deafness (i.e., psychogenic deafness and feigning deafness); or auditory neuropathy spectrum disorder. We did not collect data about amplification achieved by hearing aids and cochlear implants.

This retrospective study follows the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) reporting guideline and was approved by the Tokyo Medical Center’s Institutional Review Board (IRB No. R20-087). Written informed consent was waived because of the study’s retrospective design. The personal details of the patients were removed and their data de-identified prior to analysis.

Hearing threshold tests: PTA

PTA was conducted according to the guidelines of the Japan Audiological Society23. Masking was used as necessary. Pure-tone air- and bone-conduction audiometric thresholds were obtained using regularly calibrated clinical audiometers in a sound-proof chamber dedicated to conducting hearing tests. According to the WHO classification for grades of hearing24, air- and bone-conduction hearing thresholds were calculated for each ear by averaging these thresholds separately for 500, 1000, 2000, and 4000 Hz.

The severity of hearing impairment in bone-conduction hearing thresholds was classified as mild (26–40 dB HL), moderate (41–60 dB HL), or severe (61–80 dB HL). This severity grading scheme is common in the field. For example, it was used in a study that analyzed the relationship between hearing severity and prevalence of dementia (25 20), and in another that examined the relationship between quality of life, anxiety, and depression and unilateral hearing loss25. The former study showed that the prevalence of dementia increased as hearing severity increased26, and the latter showed that unilateral hearing loss decreases quality of life and increases anxiety and depression levels26. Many other studies have also used this mild-moderate-severe grading scheme for HL severity. The wide adoption of this scheme means our results can be more easily compared to those in the literature.

Speech discrimination (SpD) test

Overview

SpD was assessed using the 67-S Japanese Nonsense Monosyllables test (67-S test), a test validated by the Japan Audiological Society27. In Japan, the 67-S test is the most commonly used test for assessing speech perception; it is widely used and approved for medical insurance evaluations. The 67-S consists of 20 Japanese monosyllables (a, o, u, ba, ga, ha, ta, wa, ne, te, ki, si, ni, ji, ri, mo, to, yo, ku, and su), which are individually presented to subjects; they are then required to orally repeat what they heard.

At our hospital, 67-S testing is done in a soundproof chamber dedicated to conducting hearing tests. Each subject is fitted with headphones, and the list of monosyllables from the 67-S test is presented through the headphones using calibrated clinical audiometers. The subjects are then asked to repeat the monosyllables they heard. The percentage of correctly identified monosyllables on the 67-S test represented the SpD score.

We conducted the SpD test separately for each ear. To avoid cross hearing during the test, speech noise that was 40 dB lower than the test sound was presented to the ear not undergoing the test.

Determination of starting volume and testing

Since HL differed across subjects and across ears, we needed to first determine the starting volume for the SpD test for each ear (i.e., speech recognition threshold). This meant the threshold volume level could be different for each ear, and thus the volume used to determine each ear’s highest SpD score could be different. For each ear being tested, the monosyllables list was initially presented at 15, 20, 25, or 30 dB louder than the subject’s hearing threshold, which had been determined beforehand by PTA. This was done to get a rough idea of which sound pressure (volume) to use at the start of the SpD test. The subject was asked to repeat the monosyllables heard, and the SpD score was calculated. The volume was then increased in 10 dB steps and the list was presented again. This procedure was repeated until the subject’s SpD score reached either a plateau or decreased as the volume of the presented monosyllables was increased. The maximum sound pressure delivered was 100 dB SPL. In broad outline, this procedure was similar to that reported in Kurioka et al. 28.

The SpD test with the highest percentage of correct answers was taken as the ear’s SpD score.

Analysis of different age groups

For analysis, the subjects were divided into three age groups: 10–39 year olds, 40–69 year olds, and 70–99 year olds. This was done to analyze aging trends. Since changes in hearing increases disproportionately in the oldest of old, we subdivided the group of 70–99 year olds further into three subgroups: 70–79 year olds, 80–89 year olds, and 90–99 year olds.

The SpD test and PTA test were administered by well-trained and experienced examiners. Audiometers that were used for the SpD and PTA were calibrated annually to ensure the accuracy and consistency of the data. Before determining the final hearing thresholds at each frequency, the examiner repeated the test twice to confirm that the thresholds were consistent. If the thresholds were inconsistent, the examiner repeated the tests until the thresholds were consistent. Therefore, the results could be considered to be reliable, even for younger participants in their early teens.

Statistics and reproducibility

All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 9.2.0 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). P < 0.05 was considered to be statistically significant. GraphPad was also used to construct all graphs and visual depictions of data. Our previous study revealed that hearing levels are not normally distributed19. Thus, we used non-parametric statistics in the present study.

In each severity level (mild, moderate, and severe), Kruskal-Wallis’s tests were performed on SpD scores to examine the difference between age groups; post-hoc pair-wise analyses were done using Dunn’s test to compare the youngest group (10–39 year olds) to the older age groups.

To examine the certainty of the relationship between PTA thresholds and SpD, we performed the same analysis above using single thresholds for 1000 Hz. This test frequency is demanded by law for hearing screening in Japan29,30.

To evaluate how left-right differences in HL affects SpD, we compared the SpD scores of worse-hearing ears to those of better-hearing ears. This comparison was done according to severity level (i.e., mild, moderate, severe). So, only within a severity level were SpD scores examined. The hearing level of the ears was determined according to their pure-tone bone conduction hearing levels, as described above. Worse-hearing ears were divided into four groups according to the air-conduction threshold differences between the left and right ears1: 10 dB HL or more and less than 20 dB HL2, 20 dB HL or more and less than 30 dB HL3, 30 dB HL or more and less than 40 dB HL, and4 40 dB HL or more. The better-hearing ears were considered to be ears in left-right ear pairs that either had no hearing difference from the opposite ear, or that had a left-right hearing difference of 10 dB HL or more (Supplementary Table 1).

A Kruskal-Wallis test was performed to compare differences in SpD between the worse ears and the better ears within the same severity of hearing impairment (mild, moderate, and severe). Post-hoc analysis was performed using the Dunn’s test to compare the better-hearing ears to worse-hearing ears. In the better-hearing ears, Man-Whitney test was performed to compare the ears of which hearing differences was less than 10 dB HL to the ears that had a left-right hearing difference of 10 dB HL or more for each hearing severity group.

Next, we focused specifically on ears that had hearing differences of 20 dB HL or more, as determined above using the Dunn’s test. We categorized these ears into two groups—“dominant ears” and “nondominant ears”—based on the results of Dunn’s test. “Nondominant ears” were defined as the worse-hearing ears having a significantly lower SpD score than the better ears. The remaining ears were designated as “dominant ears.”

Scatter plots of bone-conduction hearing thresholds versus SpD scores were constructed, and Boltzmann sigmoidal fitting was performed as described above. Then, dominant ears and nondominant ears were compared according to the severity of hearing impairment (mild, moderate, and severe) by using the Mann-Whitney test. Finally, we analyzed only the dominant ears and nondominant ears using Kruskal-Wallis’s tests on SpD scores to determine any differences between the age groups in each severity level (mild, moderate, and severe). We also did post-hoc pair-wise analyses using Dunn’s test on dominant ears and nondominant ears to compare the youngest group (10–39 year olds) to the older age groups.

Discussion

Hearing loss is a serious worldwide health problem, one associated with social and psychological dysfunction and declining quality of life. One prominent problem for older adults diagnosed with AHL is that their hearing loss is also associated with more difficulties understanding speech; in other words, their SpD is poor. The details about how aging and asymmetrical HL affect SpD are still being uncovered. Here, we analyzed SpD according to severity of PTA-threshold difference for ear pairs. We found that overall, SpD decreased significantly with age. SpD between the better-hearing ears and the worse-hearing ears also decreased when left-right hearing differences of ear pairs were 20 dB or more. The finding that SpD is dramatically affected by age and asymmetrical hearing loss has important implications for clinical practice.

Although a few studies have shown that aging and asymmetric hearing impairment decrease SpD31–35, they were unable to conclusively determine whether specific age-related trends exist. This is because their study samples were too small (i.e., they assessed only a few dozen subjects) and because of the aging process is heterogeneous (i.e., aging effects are much more variable in older age groups). In the present study, we addressed these questions by evaluating the association between age (10–99 years) and overall SpD, and, importantly, crosswise SpD according to severity of PTA-threshold difference for ear pairs. We also had data for many subjects and ages.

In PTA and SpD data collected from 5508 ears, we found that ears with mild or more severe hearing loss, as determined by PTA, showed a clear tendency of reduced SpD with increasing age. This result accords well with results in the literature4. In addition, when considering the standard deviation of data for the ears with mild and moderate hearing impairment, a clear tendency emerged for hearing impairment to be larger with increasing age (Supplementary Table 1). This means that hearing screening for elderly people with PTA only might be insufficient to accurately determine hearing deficits and implement appropriate interventions. Thus, it might be appropriate to also include a SpD test to the standard screening protocol in the clinic, especially for older patients.

When deciding whether to prescribe hearing aids for a hearing-impaired individual, it is imperative to understand the differences between younger and older people, even if their PTA hearing thresholds are similar. The effect of aging on SpD documented in this study should become part of the standardized information to help clinicians judge whether a patient’s SpD is consistent with his or her age and hearing thresholds.

Comparison of ears with a left-right hearing difference of 20 dB HL or more (i.e., non-dominant and dominant ears) revealed that SpD was significantly worse in the poorer ears with moderate or severe hearing impairment, regardless of age. These findings suggest that non-use of an ear (i.e., non-dominant ear) could lead to the decline of SpD. This might be avoidable by wearing a hearing aid in the worse ear. The prevalence of asymmetric HL, as based on the WHO classification for grades of hearing24, is estimated to be 2.77% when calculating the PTA at 0.5 to 4 kHz36. Analysis of hearing-aid use in U.S. veterans revealed that long-term use of hearing aids in worse ears was lower than use in better ears37. Because wearing a hearing aid in the worse ear might stave off a decline in SpD, an innovative method to fit hearing aids to worse ears should be developed so that wearing a hearing aid in the worse ear becomes standard and more popular.

Maintaining good SpD is key to engaging in effective communication, to avoiding isolation in society or at home, and to preventing severe illnesses such as depression or dementia. Moreover, falling is also known to be related to HL1–3,38. Although the progression of AHL is determined by genetic factors, it is moderated by various acquired factors such as loud noise exposure, ototoxic medicines, smoking, diabetes mellitus, otitis media, among others39. So, it is essential for individuals, especially those who are genetically predisposed to AHL, to avoid or reduce exposure to acquired factors. Doing so will slow down, and to some extent prevent, the progression of their HL.

Once HL is noticed by an individual, it should be diagnosed correctly, and discussion should begin with the patient about using hearing aids or having a cochlear implant appropriate to their hearing level40,41. In the present study, the ears with mild and severe hearing impairment showed a clear tendency of decreasing SpD with age. Considering that the SpD of worse ears was significantly worse than that of better ears in our study, in the future, we expect to examine whether intervention with hearing aids could preserve SpD, or mitigate it, and thereby avoid additional decline in the personal and social consequences of declining SpD.

This study had some limitations. We used a retrospective cross-sectional design, collecting data via chart review. This means a different pattern of results may emerge if the same subjects are repeatedly tested over their lifespans. We included patients who underwent both SpD testing and PTA. Therefore, this study had the possibility of selection bias. And there is a possibility that data that were not collected, such as use of hearing aids and presence of cochlear implants could be important in segregating SpD scores. While prospective random controlled trials are the gold standard to eliminate potential bias, it is hardly possible to carry out such trials. Further prospective studies that would minimize bias are expected to examine any possible changes in SpD by following many people from school-age to very old age. We believe the strengths of the present study, which includes a large sample size and a broad age span, are certainly valuable and the setting of this study could be acceptable, as it includes aspects of the real world and what occurs in a general population.

In conclusion, SpD is dramatically affected by age and asymmetrical hearing. For hearing screening of older people, speech perception should be properly evaluated. Appropriate audiological interventions should be proactively considered according to the hearing level, SpD, age, and left-right hearing differences. This means that clinical hearing assessments could become more costly and time-consuming. On the other hand, the present study suggests that if adjustments in testing are not made, it may be even more costly when social isolation, depression, cognitive decline, and poor verbal communication in older people continue at the present pace.
