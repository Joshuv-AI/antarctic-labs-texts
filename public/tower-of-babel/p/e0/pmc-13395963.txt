Association of Preoperative Prognostic Nutritional Index on Long‐Term Outcomes and Postoperative Complications of Stage I–III Colorectal Cancer
Authors: Eui Myung Kim, Jae Hyun Kang, Minsung Kim, Sang Nam Yoon, Min Jeong Kim, Jin Won Lee, Jong Wan Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundThe prognostic nutritional index (PNI), derived from serum albumin levels and lymphocyte counts, reflects both nutritional and immunologic status. This study aimed to evaluate the association between preoperative PNI and long‐term oncologic outcomes and postoperative complications in patients undergoing curative resection for colorectal cancer (CRC).MethodsA multicenter retrospective analysis was conducted on 3045 patients with stage I–III CRC who underwent curative surgery at five Hallym University‐affiliated hospitals between 2012 and 2023. Preoperative PNI, lymphocyte‐to‐monocyte ratio (LMR), neutrophil‐to‐lymphocyte ratio (NLR), and platelet‐to‐lymphocyte ratio (PLR) were calculated using laboratory values within 2 weeks prior to surgery. The optimal PNI cutoff for overall survival (OS) was determined by receiver operating characteristic analysis.ResultsThe optimal preoperative PNI cutoff was 43.4 (area under the curve = 0.643, sensitivity = 60.4%, specificity = 59.4%). Patients with low PNI (< 43.4) exhibited significantly poorer 5‐year OS (59.0% vs. 80.0%, p < 0.001) and recurrence‐free survival (RFS) (51.4% vs. 73.2%, p < 0.001) compared to the high PNI group. These associations remained consistent across all cancer stages. In multivariate analysis, low PNI was independently associated with OS (hazard ratio [HR] = 1.535, p < 0.001) and RFS (HR = 1.519, p < 0.001), as well as increased risk of severe complications (odds ratio = 2.503, p < 0.001). PNI showed better predictive performance than LMR, NLR, and PLR for these outcomes.ConclusionsPreoperative PNI is an independent predictor of oncologic outcomes and perioperative risk in CRC patients and may serve as a practical biomarker for preoperative risk stratification.

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) is the third most commonly diagnosed malignancy worldwide and is a leading cause of cancer‐related mortality. In terms of cancer incidence and mortality among both men and women in the United States, CRC ranks third, and in Korea, it is the fourth most common cancer in men, the third in women, and the third leading cause of cancer death in both sexes [1, 2].

Surgery and chemotherapy are the mainstay treatments for CRC, but surgery is associated with the risk of serious postoperative complications and recurrence, and some cases may be considered unresectable [3]. Postoperative (adjuvant) chemotherapy can cause side effects and medical costs that may lead to the discontinuation of treatment [4].

Although the tumor, node, metastasis (TNM) staging system remains the most widely used tool for predicting the prognosis of CRC, it does not fully account for individual variations in oncological outcomes [5], particularly among patients with stage II or III disease. Moreover, accurate TNM staging requires surgical resection and histopathological evaluation, limiting its utility for preoperative risk stratification.

Accordingly, there is increasing need for alternative biomarkers that are more economical, readily available, and less invasive to help predict the outcomes of CRC and to guide its treatment. Systemic inflammation and malnutrition are associated with immunosuppression [6], which may lead to tumor recurrence and negatively affect the long‐term prognosis of patients with malignancies [7, 8]. Several immunonutritional markers, including the lymphocyte‐to‐monocyte ratio (LMR), neutrophil‐to‐lymphocyte ratio (NLR), platelet‐to‐lymphocyte ratio (PLR), and nutritional risk index, are reportedly associated with cancer prognosis [9, 10, 11, 12]. More recently, several novel inflammation‐ and nutrition‐based biomarkers, including immune‐nutritional prognostic ratios and other inflammation‐based hematologic markers, have also demonstrated prognostic value in patients with CRC [13, 14].

The prognostic nutritional index (PNI), which is calculated using the serum albumin level and peripheral lymphocyte count, was first reported by Onodera in 1984 and reflects the patient's nutritional and immune status [15]. In previous studies, the PNI was identified as a prognostic indicator for survival in various malignancies, including gastrointestinal cancers, such as gastric and pancreatic carcinomas, as well as renal and lung cancers [16, 17, 18, 19].

In CRC, several studies have reported that a low preoperative PNI is associated with an increased risk of postoperative complications and poor prognosis after curative resection [20, 21, 22, 23, 24, 25]. Moreover, the PNI was also correlated with the outcomes of chemotherapy in patients with unresectable metastatic CRC [26, 27]. Alternatively, some studies have applied cutoff values for the PNI to classify patients into four groups of high and low PNI values before and after surgery and compared the prognosis of CRC among these groups [26, 28]. However, a notable limitation is that the cutoff values for low PNI varied across the earlier studies, hindering direct comparisons and generalization. To overcome this issue, a recent study evaluated the prognostic significance by assessing the perioperative changes in the PNI before and after surgery [29].

Therefore, this study evaluated the association between preoperative immunonutritional status, assessed using the PNI, LMR, NLR, and PLR, and the long‐term oncological outcomes and postoperative complications in patients who underwent curative resection for CRC. To our knowledge, this is one of the largest multicenter studies evaluating the prognostic value of PNI while simultaneously assessing oncologic outcomes, severe postoperative complications, and comparative performance against other inflammatory markers.

Patients and Methods

Study Population

We retrospectively reviewed the medical records of patients who were diagnosed with stage I–III CRC and underwent curative resection at five Hallym University‐affiliated hospitals (Kangnam, Kangdong, Chuncheon, Hallym, and Dongtan Sacred Heart Hospital; Republic of Korea) between January 2012 and December 2023. Patients were excluded for the following reasons: incomplete medical records, non‐adenocarcinoma histology (e.g., gastrointestinal stromal tumors, neuroendocrine tumors, or lymphomas), synchronous CRC, transanal excision or appendectomy alone, or treatment with neoadjuvant concurrent chemoradiotherapy.

The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Dongtan Sacred Heart Hospital (IRB: 2024‐12‐019). Because this was a retrospective cohort study, the requirement for informed consent was waived by the Institutional Review Board.

Data Collection

Preoperative inflammatory and nutritional markers were calculated using laboratory data obtained within 2 weeks prior to surgery. The PNI was calculated using the following formula: 10 × serum albumin concentration (g/dL) + 0.005 × total lymphocyte count (/mm3). Albumin and lymphocyte counts were measured according to the routine laboratory protocols of each participating institution. Because these parameters are routinely obtained as part of standard preoperative evaluation, missing data for PNI calculation were minimal, and only patients with complete data required for PNI calculation were included in the final analysis. Other systemic inflammation scores, including the LMR, NLR, and PLR, were also calculated. The LMR was defined as the absolute lymphocyte count divided by the absolute monocyte count. The NLR was calculated by dividing the absolute neutrophil count by the absolute lymphocyte count. The PLR was calculated by dividing the absolute platelet count by the absolute lymphocyte count. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was performed to determine the optimal PNI cutoff value for predicting overall survival (OS). The optimal cutoff was determined using the Youden index, which maximizes the sum of the sensitivity and specificity. The area under the curve (AUC) was also calculated to evaluate the discriminative ability of the PNI. Based on the determined cutoff value, patients were stratified into high and low PNI groups. Similarly, we determined the optimal cutoff values for the other systemic inflammatory markers (LMR, NLR, and PLR) using ROC curve analysis with OS as the endpoint. Differences in the areas under the ROC curves (AUCs) among PNI, LMR, NLR, and PLR were formally compared using the DeLong test.

Clinicopathological results were compared between the low and high preoperative PNI groups. The variables included patient characteristics (age, sex, body mass index, American Society of Anesthesiologists [ASA] score, tumor location, presence of obstruction, and postoperative complications) and pathological findings (presence of lymphovascular invasion [LVI] and perineural invasion, tumor size, number of harvested lymph nodes, histological grade, and tumor stage based on the 8th edition of the American Joint Committee on Cancer TNM classification) [30]. Postoperative complications were assessed according to the Clavien–Dindo classification system. Complications classified as grade ≥ III were considered severe [31]. Patients were followed up every 3 months for the first 2 years after surgery and every 6 months for the next 3 years. Each follow‐up included physical examinations and laboratory tests, including serum carcinoembryonic antigen and cancer antigen 19‐9. Chest and abdominopelvic computed tomography scans were performed every 6 months. Colonoscopy was conducted annually for the first 2 years and biennially thereafter.

OS was calculated from the date of surgery to the date of death from any cause or last follow‐up. Relapse‐free survival (RFS) was calculated from the date of surgery to the date of first recurrence, death from any cause, or last follow‐up.

The primary endpoint of this study was to evaluate the 5‐year OS according to the PNI. The secondary endpoints included the assessment of 5‐year RFS according to the PNI and to identify potential risk factors for OS, RFS, and severe complications. These analyses incorporated inflammatory markers, including the PNI, LMR, NLR, and PLR.

Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables are expressed as the mean and standard deviation, and were analyzed using Student's t‐test. Categorical variables are reported as the number and percent of patients, and were analyzed using the χ 2 test.

The OS and RFS rates were calculated using the Kaplan–Meier method, and survival curves were compared using the log‐rank test. The Cox proportional hazards regression model was used to assess potential risk factors for OS and RFS using the following factors: age (≥ 70 years), sex (male), ASA physical status score (≥ 3), obstruction, tumor location (rectum), T classification (T4), lymph node metastasis, LVI, perineural invasion, histological grade (poor/undifferentiated), severe complications, and inflammatory markers (preoperative PNI, LMR, NLR, and PLR). Collinearity was assessed by calculating the variance inflation factor (VIF) for each variable. Logistic regression was used to identify independent risk factors associated with postoperative severe complications. As a sensitivity analysis to account for potential inter‐center heterogeneity, cluster‐robust standard errors were calculated with hospital as the clustering variable for Cox and logistic regression models. The results were compared with those from the primary analyses. p values of < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS software version 28.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA).

Results

During a study period spanning more than 10 years, 3506 patients underwent curative resection for CRC. Of these, 38 patients were excluded from the study due to incomplete medical records, 35 patients due to non‐adenocarcinoma histology, 78 patients due to synchronous CRC, 39 patients due to transanal excision or appendectomy only, and 271 patients due to treatment with neoadjuvant concurrent chemoradiotherapy. After excluding these 461 patients, the final analysis comprised 3045 patients.

The Cutoff Value of Preoperative PNI

The optimal cutoff value for preoperative PNI was determined to be 43.4, yielding an AUC of 0.643 (95% CI = 0.621–0.665), with a sensitivity of 60.4% and a specificity of 59.4% (Figure 1). Based on this cutoff value, patients were divided into two groups: the low preoperative PNI group (PNI < 43.4; n = 863 [28.3%]) and the high preoperative PNI group (PNI ≥ 43.4; n = 2182 [71.7%]). The cutoff values for the LMR, NLR, and PLR were 3.7, 3.2, and 190, respectively. Among the evaluated inflammatory markers, PNI showed the highest AUC for predicting OS. DeLong test comparisons confirmed that the AUC of PNI was significantly higher than those of LMR, NLR, and PLR (Table 1).

Patient Characteristics

Table 2 summarizes the associations between the preoperative PNI status and clinicopathological characteristics. The low preoperative PNI group was older (71.9 vs. 65.7 years, p < 0.001) and had a higher proportion of patients with an ASA physical status of ≥ 3 (p < 0.001). The low preoperative PNI group also had higher rates of postoperative complications (p < 0.001) and severe complications (p < 0.001). Oncologic Outcomes

The median follow‐up period was 52.4 months (range: 2–112 months), and was 48.2 and 56.4 months in the low and high preoperative PNI groups, respectively. The 5‐year OS was significantly worse in the low preoperative PNI group than in the high preoperative PNI group (59.0% vs. 80.0%, p < 0.001) (Figure 2A). This difference remained consistent across tumor stages, with worse survival in the low preoperative PNI group for patients with stage I (69.1% vs. 89.1%, p < 0.001), stage II (59.2% vs. 77.5%, p < 0.001), and stage III (53.0% vs. 73.8%, p < 0.001) CRC (Figure 2B–D).

Patients with low PNI had significantly poorer 5‐year OS than those with high PNI (59.0% vs. 80.0%, p < 0.001) (A), consistently across stages: stage I (69.1% vs. 89.1%, p < 0.001), stage II (59.2% vs. 77.5%, p < 0.001), and stage III (53.0% vs. 73.8%, p < 0.001) (B–D).

Similarly, the 5‐year RFS was worse in the low preoperative PNI group than in the high preoperative PNI group (51.4% vs. 73.2%, p < 0.001) (Figure 3A). This pattern was evident in patients with stage I (64.9% vs. 84.3%, p < 0.001), stage II (50.2% vs. 68.4%, p < 0.001), and stage III (43% vs. 62.2%, p < 0.001) CRC (Figure 3B–D).

However, male sex was not significantly correlated with either outcome (Table 3).

Multivariate Analysis for OS and RFS

In the multivariable analyses, the following factors remained independently associated with poor OS: older age, higher ASA score, presence of obstruction, rectal tumor location, T4 stage, lymph node metastasis, elevated inflammatory markers, and low preoperative PNI (Table 4). For RFS, the independent prognostic factors were age, obstruction, rectal tumor location, T4 stage, lymph node metastasis, LVI, perineural invasion, and low preoperative PNI (Table 4). Notably, LMR, NLR, and PLR were not identified as independent prognostic factors for either OS or RFS in the multivariable analyses. Collinearity analysis yielded VIF values of 1.368 for LMR (cutoff: 3.7), 1.541 for NLR (cutoff: 3.2), and 1.393 for PLR (cutoff: 190), indicating no significant multicollinearity effect.

In the multivariable analysis, male sex, ASA score, obstruction, and rectal cancer were independent risk factors. Among the inflammatory markers, only low preoperative PNI remained as an independent predictor of severe complications (Table 5).

Discussion

In the present study of patients who underwent curative resection for CRC, those with a low preoperative PNI showed pathological features that were more aggressive, had worse OS and RFS, and had a higher incidence of severe complications, compared with patients with a high preoperative PNI. These differences persisted even when the analysis was stratified by cancer stage. Furthermore, low preoperative PNI was an independent prognostic factor for OS, RFS, and severe complications. Compared with the other systemic inflammatory markers (i.e., LMR, NLR, and PLR), preoperative PNI showed superior predictive value for postoperative outcomes.

Unlike the TNM staging system, which requires postoperative pathological evaluation, PNI can be assessed preoperatively using only the serum albumin level and the total lymphocyte count. Therefore, the PNI provides a relatively simple, cost‐effective, and clinically practical method to evaluate the patient's immunological and nutritional status before treatment. Serum albumin reflects the patient's nutritional condition and systemic inflammatory status [32], and lymphocytes play a central role in antitumor immunity by participating in cytokine production and exerting cytotoxic effects against tumor cells [33]. Hypoalbuminemia, which contributes to malnutrition and immunosuppression, was reported to be associated with poor prognosis, particularly in patients with advanced CRC [34]. Lymphopenia, a condition of abnormally low lymphocyte counts, weakens the host's immune surveillance capacity [35], increases susceptibility to tumor progression, and is associated with unfavorable long‐term outcomes [36]. Taken together, hypoalbuminemia and lymphopenia may reflect a state of systemic inflammation, malnutrition, and impaired antitumor immunity. These conditions can compromise tissue repair, wound healing, and host defense mechanisms, thereby increasing susceptibility to postoperative morbidity and adverse oncologic outcomes. Therefore, the PNI may serve as a comprehensive indicator of both nutritional and immunological vulnerability in patients with CRC.

The cutoff values for the preoperative PNI ranged from 40.0 to 49.1 in previous studies [12, 21, 23, 24, 25, 26, 27, 37, 38]. In the present study, the optimal cutoff value of the preoperative PNI was 43.4, which falls within this reported range. However, due to the lack of consensus on a standardized cutoff value, direct application of the PNI in clinical practice remains challenging. The varying cutoff values among studies may be attributed to differences in patient selection and study design. For instance, some studies exclusively included patients aged ≥ 80 years [37], and others focused solely on patients who underwent specific surgical techniques, such as laparoscopic surgery [20], or only included patients with colon cancer [20, 38]. Some studies included patients with unresectable stage IV disease [26, 27]. Although many studies determined the cutoff value based on OS, others used different endpoints, such as postoperative complications, cancer‐specific survival (CSS), or RFS [20, 23]. These discrepancies underscore the need for further large‐scale, standardized studies to establish clinically applicable cutoff values for the PNI.

We should also consider that several previous studies evaluated the PNI not only before treatments, such as surgery or chemotherapy, but also observed its change after treatment. Accordingly, some studies classified patients into four prognostic groups based on the pre‐ and postoperative PNI values using a predefined cutoff value [26, 28]. For example, Tamai et al. analyzed patients with stage II/III CRC who underwent curative resection and categorized the patients into the following four groups using a cutoff value of 45.6: high–high, high–low, low–high, and low–low. They reported that OS and RFS were significantly worse in the low–low group than in the other three groups, but the low–high group had comparable outcomes to the high–high group, suggesting that the recovery of nutritional status after surgery may lead to improved prognosis [28]. However, these analyses still rely on defining cutoff values. A more recent study compared the prognosis of patients divided into groups according to whether their PNI increased or decreased after treatment, without defining a cutoff value. In that study, the OS (p < 0.001) and RFS (p < 0.001) were significantly worse in patients whose PNI decreased compared with patients whose PNI increased. The perioperative change in PNI was therefore identified as a risk factor for OS (HR: 5.809, 95% CI = 3.451–9.779, p < 0.001) [29].

In the present study, the low preoperative PNI group had significantly higher rates of overall complications (p < 0.001) and severe complications (Clavien–Dindo grade ≥ 3; p < 0.001). The multivariable analysis revealed that preoperative PNI < 43.4 was an independent risk factor for severe complications (OR = 2.503, 95% CI = 1.734–3.612, p < 0.001). These results are consistent with earlier studies that highlighted the prognostic relevance of the PNI in patients who underwent surgery for CRC [21, 24, 29, 37]. Noh et al. [22] stratified preoperative PNI into deciles (< 40 to > 60) and reported a stepwise decrease in the complication rate with increasing PNI (PNI ≤ 40: 6.6%; 40–50: 5.3%; 50–60: 4.1%; > 60: 3.1%). This association is likely driven by the well‐documented links between systemic inflammation, malnutrition, and postoperative morbidity [21, 39].

In the present study, patients with low preoperative PNI had significantly worse OS (p < 0.001) and RFS (p < 0.001) than patients with high preoperative PNI. This result is consistent with previous studies showing that lower preoperative PNI is associated with worse survival outcomes, including OS [12, 20, 21, 23, 37, 38], RFS [12, 37], and CSS [37]. Although some studies reported no significant differences in survival when patients were stratified by pathological stage [21], our study showed that the differences in OS and RFS remained significant, even after stratifying patients by cancer stage. In the multivariable analyses, the inflammatory markers LMR, NLR, and PLR were not significantly associated with survival outcomes. By contrast, preoperative PNI < 43.4 was independently associated with worse OS and RFS (OS: HR = 1.535, 95% CI = 1.289–1.829, p < 0.001; RFS: HR = 1.519, 95% CI = 1.305–1.767, p < 0.001). In addition, patients with low PNI tend to be older and present with pathological features, both of which can contribute to the unfavorable clinical outcomes [21, 23, 24, 25]. In our study, the low preoperative PNI group was characterized by a higher frequency of advanced TNM stage, larger tumor size, and higher frequencies of LVI and perineural invasion, supporting the prognostic relevance of the PNI. Accordingly, the relationship between the PNI and the long‐term prognosis of patients with CRC appears to be reasonable.

Several limitations of this study should be noted. First, this study has the inherent limitations of a retrospective design, including the possibility of selection bias and unmeasured confounding factors. Because the ROC‐derived cutoff was generated and evaluated within the same cohort, optimism bias cannot be excluded. Formal internal validation was not performed; therefore, the proposed cutoff should be considered exploratory and warrants validation in independent cohorts. In addition, information regarding adjuvant chemotherapy was not consistently available across participating institutions and therefore was not included in the analysis. As a result, the potential influence of adjuvant treatment on long‐term oncologic outcomes cannot be excluded. Second, although this was a multicenter study, differences in laboratory measurements, perioperative management, and postoperative care among institutions may have influenced the results. In particular, minor inter‐center variability in albumin and lymphocyte measurements cannot be excluded because these laboratory tests were performed according to each institution's routine protocols. However, these tests are standardized components of routine clinical practice, and the associations remained consistent in cluster‐robust analyses accounting for hospital‐level clustering. The inclusion of multiple centers therefore reflects real‐world practice while supporting the robustness of our findings. Third, this study included only Asian patients, which may limit the generalizability of the results to other populations. However, given the known differences in body composition and nutritional status between Asian and Western patients, our findings provide meaningful evidence regarding the prognostic value of PNI in an Asian population. Nevertheless, the identified cutoff is within the range (40–49.1) of values reported in previous studies, suggesting that it is clinically reasonable. Finally, although PNI is not a novel biomarker, the large multicenter design of this study enabled simultaneous evaluation of long‐term oncologic outcomes and severe postoperative complications, as well as direct comparison with other commonly used inflammatory markers. These findings support the clinical utility of PNI as a simple and readily available tool for preoperative risk stratification in patients with stage I–III CRC.

Conclusion

The present study demonstrated that a low preoperative PNI is significantly associated with worse OS and RFS across all cancer stages, as well as an increased risk of severe postoperative complications in patients undergoing curative surgery for CRC. Notably, these associations remained consistent across all cancer stages. The preoperative PNI has emerged as an independent prognostic factor, demonstrating relatively better predictive performance compared with other systemic inflammatory markers, namely LMR, NLR, and PLR. These findings support the clinical utility of incorporating the PNI into routine preoperative assessment to guide individualized treatment strategies and optimize the surgical outcomes in patients with CRC.
