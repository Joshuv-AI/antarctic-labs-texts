Enhanced optical and electrical properties of polyvinyl alcohol polyethylene oxide nanocomposite films incorporating hybrid carbon nanofillers
Authors: H. M. Ragab, N. S. Diab, Rosilah Ab Aziz, Shimaa Mohammed Aboelnaga, Ammar Qasem, A. Al Ojeery, M. O. Farea
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This work reports the fabrication and characterization of PVA/PEO-based nanocomposite films reinforced with a synergistic combination of graphene nanoplates (GNPs) and multi-walled carbon nanotubes (MWCNTs) using a modified solution casting method. Structural analysis revealed that the incorporation of carbon nanofillers effectively reduced the crystallinity of the polymer blend, promoting an amorphous structure favorable for charge transport. FTIR results confirmed strong interactions between the polymer matrix and the nanofillers. Optical investigations showed a systematic narrowing of the optical band gap and an enhancement in the refractive index with increasing nanofiller content, indicating significant modification of the electronic structure. In addition, the nanocomposite films exhibited markedly improved AC electrical conductivity and dielectric performance due to the formation of an interconnected conductive network and enhanced interfacial polarization. The combined enhancement in optical and electrical properties highlights the effectiveness of the synergistic MWCNTs/GNPs reinforcement strategy. These results demonstrate that the developed PVA/PEO nanocomposite films are promising candidates for advanced optoelectronic devices, energy storage systems, and flexible electronic applications.

Introduction

Polymer blending combined with nanofiller incorporation is an effective strategy for tailoring the structural, optical, and electrical properties of polymeric materials for advanced technological applications. Compared with single polymers, polymer blends offer enhanced flexibility in property optimization, particularly for solid polymer electrolytes and optoelectronic materials, due to their tunable amorphous–crystalline balance and interfacial interactions1–4. The incorporation of conductive nanofillers further improves charge transport, dielectric response, and optical performance, enabling applications in energy storage, flexible electronics, and optoelectronic devices5–7.

Polyethylene oxide (PEO) is widely employed as a host polymer owing to its good film-forming ability, high segmental mobility, and favorable ion-transport characteristics8. However, its relatively low room-temperature conductivity and limited mechanical stability restrict its standalone use9,10. Suitable blending polymers include semicrystalline polymers like PVA and PVDF, as well as amorphous polymers like PVP and PMMA. Polyvinyl alcohol (PVA) is a widely used and essential conjugated polymer in blend technology due to its non-toxic, biodegradable, and environmentally friendly nature, along with its favorable optical properties, high dielectric strength, charge storage capabilities, and chemical stability11,12. Nevertheless, its broad direct band gap (ranging from 5.1 to 6.2 eV) can limit its utility in optical devices13. The conductivity of PVA can be improved by incorporating PEO, which facilitates the formation of three-dimensional network structures through hydrogen bonding between the hydroxyl side groups of PVA and the ether linkages of PEO14,15.

Several studies have reported enhanced optical and electrical properties of PVA/PEO-based nanocomposites through the incorporation of metal oxides, salts, rare-earth ions, and carbon-based fillers. Hadi et al.16 studied PVA/PEO/SnO₂ nanocomposites and found that higher SnO₂ concentrations enhance conductivity and UV absorbance while reducing the energy band gap. Krishnan and Ramesan17 studied eco-friendly polyethylene oxide/aluminum oxyhydroxide nanocomposite films prepared via a green aqueous route and reported enhanced thermal stability, mechanical strength, and dielectric performance particularly at 5–7 wt% AlOOH highlighting their potential for flexible energy storage devices. El- Saber et al.18 studied Eu³⁺-doped PEO–PVA solid-polymer electrolytes and found that increasing Eu³⁺ content reduces crystallinity, enhances optical conductivity, and introduces dual band gaps (2.0–2.8 eV and 4.0–4.38 eV). Meera and Ramesan19 investigated eco-friendly CMCS/PEO/γ-AlOOH nanocomposite films and reported significant improvements in thermal stability, tensile strength (39.6% increase at 5 wt%), and dielectric performance, with the 7 wt% γ-AlOOH sample exhibiting the highest conductivity (1.23 × 10⁻⁶ S/cm at 1 MHz) and dielectric constant (244 at 100 Hz), highlighting their suitability for flexible electronic applications. Aziz et al.20 studied the optical properties of polyvinyl alcohol/polyethylene oxide solid polymer blend electrolytes doped with aluminum salt and observed that increasing the salt concentration lowered the energy band gap, shifted the absorption edge, and increased the refractive index and extinction coefficient. Meera and Ramesan21 studied eco-friendly PVA/boehmite nanocomposites fabricated via a green water-based method and reported that boehmite incorporation significantly enhanced thermal stability, tensile strength (up to 39.98 MPa at 7 wt%), Young’s modulus (490 MPa), UV-blocking, and electrical performance—with 5 wt% boehmite showing the highest AC conductivity (3.56 × 10⁻⁷ S/cm)—highlighting their suitability for flexible energy storage and dielectric applications. However, most reported works have focused on single-type fillers, such as metal oxides, graphene derivatives, or carbon nanotubes individually. Despite the well-known complementary properties of graphene nanoplates (GNPs), high surface area and planar conductivity, and multi-walled carbon nanotubes (MWCNTs), one-dimensional conductive pathways and high aspect ratio, their synergistic hybrid incorporation into a PVA/PEO matrix has received limited attention, particularly with respect to the combined optical and electrical performance.

Although single nanofiller incorporation has been widely explored to enhance the thermal, mechanical, and electrical properties of PVA/PEO-based nanocomposites, the synergistic potential of combining multiple nanofillers remains largely unexplored. In particular, the hybrid incorporation of graphene nanoplatelets (GNPs) and multi-walled carbon nanotubes (MWCNTs) into PVA/PEO matrices offers the possibility of forming an interconnected conductive network, combining the high surface area and planar conductivity of GNPs with the one-dimensional conductive pathways and high aspect ratio of MWCNTs. Such a hybrid system is expected to enhance charge transport, interfacial polarization, and electronic structure more effectively than single fillers, while maintaining mechanical flexibility and processability. Therefore, the present work focuses on the fabrication of PVA/PEO nanocomposite films reinforced with hybrid GNPs/MWCNTs using a modified solution casting technique. The structural interactions, optical behavior, electrical conductivity, and dielectric properties of the films are systematically investigated to provide new insights into the role of hybrid carbon nanofillers in tuning multifunctional properties and advancing flexible optoelectronic and energy-related applications.

Experimental work

Chemicals

The materials used in this study included polyvinyl alcohol (PVA) with a molecular weight of 14,000 and polyethylene oxide (PEO) with a molecular weight of 4 × 104 g/mol, both sourced from Sigma-Aldrich, USA. Functionalized multiwalled carbon nanotubes (MWCNTs-COOH) with a length of 25 μm or greater, a diameter between 9 and 40 nm, and a purity of 95% were provided by Nanothinx, Greece. Graphene nanoplates (GNPs) with a particle size of 20 nm, a surface area ranging from 115 to 145 m2/g, and a high purity of 98.95% were also obtained from Sigma-Aldrich. Double distilled water (DDW) was employed as the solvent for the film preparation process.

Preparation of PVA/PEO-MWCNTs/GNPs nanocomposites

PEO and PVA were used as the polymeric host materials. Initially, PEO and PVA were dissolved separately in DDW under continuous magnetic stirring at 60 °C for 2 h to ensure complete dissolution. The two solutions were then mixed to obtain a homogeneous PEO/PVA blend with a weight ratio of 70/30 and further stirred for 1 h at room temperature. A hybrid carbon nanofiller composed of GNPs and MWCNTs was prepared in a fixed 50/50 wt% ratio. This ratio was selected to exploit the synergistic effect between the two fillers, where GNPs provide a large conductive surface area while MWCNTs act as one-dimensional conductive bridges, facilitating the formation of an interconnected conductive network within the polymer matrix. The required amounts of the hybrid GNPs/MWCNTs fillers (0.5, 1.5, 3.0, and 5.0 wt% relative to the total polymer weight) were dispersed in DDW and subjected to ultrasonication for 30 min using a probe sonicator operating at 40 kHz and 200 W to minimize agglomeration and ensure uniform dispersion. These filler concentrations were chosen to investigate the evolution of structural, optical, and electrical properties below and near the percolation threshold. The well-dispersed nanofiller suspensions were gradually added to the PEO/PVA solution under magnetic stirring and further stirred for 2 h to obtain a homogeneous nanocomposite solution. The resulting mixtures were cast into clean plastic Petri dishes and dried at ambient temperature for 48 h to allow slow solvent evaporation, followed by additional drying in a vacuum oven at 40 °C for 12 h to remove residual moisture. The free-standing films were carefully peeled from the Petri dishes, yielding uniform films with thicknesses ranging from 0.02 to 0.30 mm. Film thickness was measured using a digital micrometer with an accuracy of ± 0.01 mm, and the samples were subsequently stored in vacuum desiccators before characterization (Scheme 1).

Instruments

The structural characteristics of the samples were investigated using X-ray diffraction (XRD) performed on a Philips diffractometer with CuKα radiation (wavelength 1.541 Å). The X-ray tube operated at 30 kV, and scans were conducted at a rate of 3° per minute over a 2θ range from 5 to 70°. Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy, employing a JASCO single-beam spectrometer, was used to analyze the chemical composition within the range of 400 to 4000 cm−1. Optical properties were assessed by measuring UV–Visible absorption spectra (190–1000 nm) of the PVA/PEO blends with different MWCNTs/GNPs nanocomposite concentrations using a Perkin Elmer Lambda 650 spectrophotometer. The electrical properties, specifically conductivity and dielectric constants, were determined at room temperature across a frequency range of 0.1 Hz to 20 MHz using a Novocontrol turnkey 40 high-performance frequency analyzer.

Results and discussion

X-ray diffraction analysis

X-ray diffraction (XRD) analyses were conducted on MWCNTs/GNPs, pure PVA/PEO films and their corresponding PVA/PEO-MWCNTs/GNPs nanocomposite films with variable loadings across a 2θ range of 5 to 70°, as illustrated in Fig. 1a, b. Figure 1a shows the XRD pattern of the hybrid MWCNTs/GNPs nanofillers. A characteristic diffraction peak appears at 2θ = 11.29°, which is attributed to the (001) reflection plane of graphene-based structures22. In addition, two prominent diffraction peaks observed at 2θ = 32.25° and 42.33° are assigned to the (002) and (100) crystallographic planes of multi-walled carbon nanotubes (MWCNTs), respectively, corresponding to the hexagonal graphite structure (JCPDS No. 41-1487)23. The presence of these well-defined peaks indicates a good degree of crystallinity of the MWCNTs with a low content of amorphous carbon and impurities. The formation of our nanocomposite films is clearly demonstrated by the XRD diffraction patterns obtained during the various stages of fabrication. Figure 1b presents the characteristic XRD patterns of pure PVA/PEO and its corresponding nanocomposite films with MWCNTs/GNPs. These patterns are consistent with prior findings documented for pure PVA/PEO and MWCNTs/GNPs24,25. The semicrystalline nature of PVA/PEO is demonstrated by two intrinsic, sharp peaks observed at 2θ = 19 and 23° in its spectrum [PCPDF File Nos. 49–2200 and 49–2201]26. Furthermore, this spectrum is characterized by the presence of low-intensity peaks, which are associated with the PEO phases. The XRD pattern of the PVA/PEO-MWCNTs/GNPs nanocomposite shows a reduction in the intensity and an increase in the width of the two main diffraction peaks. This change indicates a decrease in the crystallinity of the polymer blend, possibly due to the coordination interactions between the functional groups present on the MWCNTs/GNPs and the PVA/PEO blend. Increasing the concentration of MWCNTs/GNPs leads to a notable decrease in the intensity and a significant broadening of the two dominant peaks in the XRD patterns. These observations indicate that the degree of crystallinity in the PVA/PEO-MWCNTs/GNPs nanocomposites is reduced, while the proportion of amorphous phases increases. This phenomenon is especially evident in the 5.0 wt% MWCNTs/GNPs sample, possibly because the interactions between the polymer blend and the MWCNTs/GNPs diminish the intermolecular interactions within the PVA/PEO matrix, thereby facilitating the movement of the polymeric chains within the amorphous domains27.

The formation of the nanocomposite is readily confirmed upon initial inspection, with the most prominent characteristic peaks of both pure PVA/PEO and MWCNTs/GNPs nanofillers easily detected in these patterns. Similar peaks, with a slight 2θ shift, are observed in a closer view of Fig. 1a. Furthermore, the intensity of the MWCNTs/GNPs nanofiller peaks shows a considerable increase with higher loading ratios in the nanocomposite films. The main characteristic peak of pure PVA/PEO remains present in the nanocomposite patterns, even with the addition of the nanofillers. FT-IR spectra

The FT-IR spectra of MWCNTs/GNPs, neat PVA/PEO polymer blend, and its corresponding PVA/PEO/MWCNTs/GNPs nanocomposite materials are presented in Fig. 2. Analysis of the spectra revealed distinct bands indicative of bending and stretching vibrations in the functional groups present in the fabricated films. In the MWCNTs/GNPs curve, the peaks at 2172 cm−1 and 1979 cm−1, are closely associated with the vibration of carbon atoms with dangling bonds in-plane termination of disordered graphite due to surface defects of carbon nanotubes and graphitic carbon23. For the neat PVA/PEO matrix, the spectrum shows distinct absorption bands for PEO at 1432 cm−1 (CH2 scissoring), 1369 cm−1 (CH2 asymmetric wagging), 1250 cm−1 (CH2 asymmetric twisting), and 1236 cm−1 (CH2 symmetric twisting). The characteristic peaks associated with PVA are observed at 1718 cm−1, corresponding to C═O stretching, and 1648 cm−1, corresponding to C═C stretching. Table 2 provides additional band assignments derived from the FT-IR spectra of the prepared nanocomposites. The FT-IR spectrum of the neat blend shows a prominent, broad band at 3303 cm−1, which is characteristic of OH stretching vibrations28,29. Additionally, an absorption peak at 2926 cm−1, assigned to CH2 asymmetric stretching, is present. The spectrum of the neat blend exhibits functional bands from both PVA and PEO, indicating their compatibility and complexation, likely facilitated by polymer-polymer interactions (Scheme 2). With increasing MWCNTs/GNPs concentration, a progressive reduction in the intensity of the majority of these absorption bands is noted30. Polymer-nanoparticle interactions are likely responsible for the interplay and complexation of the PVA/PEO blend with the MWCNTs/GNPs, as supported by the obtained data. Additionally, the double bond segments within the system are thought to provide favorable locations for the formation of polarons/bipolarons, which could act as hopping sites for charge carriers31,32.

SEM images

The surface morphology of the pure PVA/PEO polymer blend and its nanocomposite containing 5.0 wt% MWCNTs/GNPs were investigated using scanning electron microscopy (SEM), as shown in Fig. 3a and b. The SEM image of the pure polymer (Fig. 3a) shows a relatively smooth and homogeneous surface with minor roughness, characteristic of the PVA/PEO polymer blend. Upon addition of MWCNTs and graphene nanoplatelets (Fig. 3b), the SEM clearly reveals the presence of carbon nanotubes and graphene sheets distributed throughout the polymer, appearing as bright, elongated structures embedded in the polymeric matrix [40]. The uniform dispersion and visible interconnected network of these nanofillers confirm the formation of continuous conductive pathways, which are essential for enhancing the electrical conductivity of the composite [41].

Optical properties

Absorption and transmission spectra

Figure 4a shows the absorption spectrum obtained for the neat PVA/PEO matrix and the corresponding PVA/PEO-MWCNTs/GNPs nanocomposites with varying concentrations of the filler material. The neat PVA/PEO matrix exhibits transparency in the visible region of the electromagnetic spectrum, as evidenced by the lack of significant absorption. This transparency is consistent with the insulating nature of neat PVA/PEO. Photon absorption is dependent on the incident photon’s energy relative to the energy difference between electronic levels. When the photon energy is insufficient to bridge this gap, the material is transparent to the photon. However, if the photon possesses sufficient energy, absorption occurs, resulting in the excitation of valence electrons to a higher energy level. The shift and increased absorption in the filled films are thought to be caused by the significant absorption of the MWCNTs/GNPs complexes. An n–π* transition absorption band is observed at 212 nm [42]. The presence of this band is due to the semicrystalline structure of the PVA/PEO blend. Optical transmission spectra for the neat PVA/PEO and its nanocomposites with different MWCNTs/GNPs loadings are presented in Fig. 4b. The fabricated films are characterized by minimal absorption within the visible portion of the electromagnetic spectrum, specifically the 350 to 750 nm wavelength range. However, a reduction in transmission is evident inside the ultraviolet region (200–300 nm) owing to a sharp absorption feature, which may be attributable to the increase in MWCNTs/GNPs particle size in the host polymers [43]. A decrease in transmittance is observed with increasing MWCNTs/GNPs concentration in the polymer blend. The highest optical transmission, approximately 75%, is exhibited by the PVA/PEO polymer blend. However, a significant reduction to approximately 10% at approximately 375 nm is observed for the nanocomposite containing 5 wt% MWCNTs/GNPs.

An increase in nanoparticle concentration is correlated with a rise in the number of localized states. This, in turn, leads to greater disorder within the host polymer. Figure 7 presents the relationship between the extinction coefficient (k) and wavelength (λ). It is observed that k decreases as λ increases, suggesting a light scattering mechanism. Optical conductivity and optical dielectric properties

The balance between energy stored from incident photons and energy lost through free carrier absorption in an optical medium is captured by the complex dielectric permittivity, a key optical property. In this context, εr represents the optical dielectric constant, and εi denotes the optical dielectric loss. Figure 9a and b presents the calculated values of the real (εr) and imaginary (εi) components of the complex dielectric permittivity as a function of photon energy (hυ) for neat PVA/PEO and its corresponding nanocomposite materials. These calculations are based on the refractive index (n) and extinction coefficient (k) data obtained across the relevant wavelength range. The data indicate that εr is substantially greater than εi, with both exhibiting a gradual increase as hυ increases below the band gap. Moreover, an increase in both εr and εi is observed when the incident photon energy is equivalent to the band gap energy of the materials. At a wavelength of 500 nm, the real (εr) and imaginary (εi) parts of the complex permittivity for the neat PVA/PEO film were determined to be 5.127 × 10⁷ and 2.02 × 10⁵, respectively. In addition, the incorporation of MWCNTs/GNPs into the polymer blend results in an increase in both εr and εi values, which can be attributed to the defects generated by charge transfer interactions between the host polymers and the MWCNTs/GNPs dopant [51]. The relatively high εr values and low εi values exhibited by these nanocomposites suggest their potential application in optical energy storage devices.

Electrical conductivity

The AC conductivity as a function of frequency for neat PVA/PEO and its corresponding PVA/PEO-MWCNTs/GNPs nanocomposite is shown in Fig. 11. A material exhibiting high AC conductivity demonstrates a strong capacity for conducting alternating current. The AC conductivity of all samples, as presented in Fig. 11, is observed to increase as the frequency increases. The enhanced conductivity at lower frequencies suggests a contribution from interfacial polarization mechanisms. At higher frequencies, electronic polarization becomes the dominant factor. Moreover, the incorporation of MWCNTs/GNPs into the PVA/PEO matrix leads to a marked improvement in AC conductivity, with the magnitude of this improvement being dependent on the concentration of the nanofillers. At 1 kHz, the AC conductivity of the film with 0.5 wt% MWCNTs/GNPs were found to be 2.10 × 10⁻¹¹ S/cm. For films containing 1.5 wt% and 3.0 wt% MWCNTs/GNPs, the conductivities measured were 7.02 × 10⁻¹¹ S/cm and 2.75 × 10⁻¹⁰ S/cm, respectively. Adding MWCNTs/GNPs to the PVA/PEO matrix enhances the interactions between the hard and soft segments of the polymer, which results in a higher space charge density [55]. AC conductivity in semiconductors and composite materials is generally enhanced by ion mobility, driven by atoms with free electrons, leading to an increased frequency response [56]. Moreover, an increase in the conductivity of the nanocomposite was observed with the addition of MWCNTs/GNPs, possibly due to the bridging of gaps between localized states by the added molecules [57,58]. The number of charge carriers was significantly increased by the inclusion of MWCNTs/GNPs nanofillers, leading to the creation of a conductive network inside the PVA/PEO matrix. This network effectively promoted charge transport along the polymer backbone. The nanocomposite containing 5.0 wt% MWCNTs/GNPs exhibited the highest conductivity among the synthesized nanocomposites, reaching 5.77 × 10⁻⁹ S/cm at 1 kHz. This enhancement is likely due to the homogeneous dispersion of the MWCNTs/GNPs and the resulting improved intermolecular interaction. Optimized charge transfer and improved conductivity were observed due to the effective interaction of the well-dispersed MWCNTs/GNPs with the PVA/PEO composite [59].

Figures 12 and 13 show the frequency dependence of ε′ and tan δ at 30 °C for neat PVA/PEO and its corresponding PVA/PEO-MWCNTs/GNPs nanocomposite. A consistent trend is apparent in both figures: the dielectric permittivity is higher at lower frequencies and decreases significantly as the frequency increases [60]. The relatively high values of ε′ observed at low frequencies (Fig. 12) can be attributed to interfacial polarization effects occurring between the blended polymer and the electrodes. As the frequency increases, the dielectric constant (ε′) is observed to decrease due to the limited capacity of the dipoles inside the nanocomposite to align themselves with the rapidly oscillating electric field. The fast oscillation of the electric field at higher frequencies prevents dipoles from aligning effectively, leading to lower ε′ values. This results in reduced polarization and a lower dielectric constant, primarily due to the suppression of electrical polarization at these frequencies [62]. Incorporating MWCNTs/GNPs into the PVA/PEO matrix led to significant modifications in the dielectric constant (ε′). The ε′ value increased to 70 with the addition of 0.5% MWCNTs/GNPs and subsequently reached 3023 at 5.0% MWCNTs/GNPs. The observed increase in ε′ was dependent on the MWCNTs/GNPs content, suggesting that interactions between the nanoparticles and the functional groups inside the PVA/PEO blend significantly influence the dipolar alignment of the polymeric matrix [63]. The higher dielectric constant observed is likely a result of either the formation of a percolated network in the polymeric matrix or the increased localization of charge carriers in conjunction with mobile ions. Published studies indicate that the interfacial interaction between the polymeric materials and the NPs is a critical factor in determining the dielectric constant of polymeric nanocomposites [64]. As the concentration of metal oxide nanoparticles within the host polymer matrix increases, the dielectric constant is observed to decrease. This is likely due to the increasing population of immobile charge carriers within the matrix. A similar trend is observed for tan δ in Fig. 13, which demonstrates a clear reduction with increasing frequency. This frequency dependence is possibly caused by ionic migration within the nanocomposite materials, as these processes are generally more efficient at low frequency [65,66]. Charged species that are freely mobile, linked to functional groups with donor oxygen and nitrogen atoms, likely cause the increased tan δ values observed at low frequencies. Their movement leads to energy dissipation as heat, resulting in greater dielectric losses [56]. The decrease in the tan δ value at higher frequencies is likely due to the reduced time available for charge accumulation polarization to take place (Table 3). Potential phase transitions or structural modifications, effects related to particle size and dispersion, interfacial polarization at the boundaries of the nanocomposite components, and mechanisms involving electron and charge transfer facilitated by the presence of MWCNTs/GNPs are among the factors that may contribute to the observed decrease in dielectric losses of the nanocomposite at elevated frequencies [67].

Conclusion

This study demonstrates the successful development of PVA/PEO–MWCNTs/GNPs nanocomposite films with tailored optical, dielectric, and electrical properties through controlled incorporation of hybrid carbon nanofillers. Structural and spectroscopic analyses confirmed strong interactions between the polymer chains and nanofillers, leading to reduced crystallinity from 45.87% in neat PVA/PEO to 19.36% in the 5 wt% nanocomposite. Optical measurements showed a pronounced reduction in the direct and indirect band gaps, from 5.23 eV and 4.50 eV in the pristine blend to 3.52 eV and 1.77 eV at 5 wt% MWCNTs/GNPs, respectively, accompanied by a substantial increase in refractive index (from 1.932 to 6.554) and Urbach energy (from 0.23 to 0.52 eV), indicating enhanced charge carrier mobility and improved light–matter interaction. The formation of interconnected conductive networks resulted in a marked improvement in AC electrical conductivity and dielectric performance, particularly at higher nanofiller loadings. These results highlight that the simultaneous enhancement of dielectric constant, electrical conductivity, and optical response can be precisely tuned by optimizing MWCNTs/GNPs content. From an application perspective, the reduced band gap, enhanced refractive index, and improved charge transport make these nanocomposites promising candidates for solid polymer electrolytes, flexible energy storage devices, and flexible optoelectronic components such as sensors, transparent conductive films, and active layers. Future work will focus on optimizing filler alignment and dispersion to further enhance conductivity at lower filler concentrations, as well as evaluating long-term stability and device-level performance in practical applications.
