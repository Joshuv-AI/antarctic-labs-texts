Characteristics and implications of carbon and oxygen isotope of lacustrine carbonate rocks from Es4s in Dongying Sag
Authors: Jianqiang Gong, Zhimin Yang, Tao Chen, Lidong Wang, Zhiyong Liu, Qi Li, Xin Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study investigates the characteristics and paleoenvironmental implications of carbon and oxygen isotopes in lacustrine carbonate rocks from the Es4s of the Paleogene Shahejie Formation in the Dongying Sag, Bohai Bay Basin, China. Although lacustrine carbonate reservoirs account for a small proportion globally, they are an important and underexplored reservoir type in continental rift basins like the Dongying Sag. Meanwhile, a systematic study of carbon and oxygen isotope geochemistry and paleoenvironmental controls of lacustrine carbonate reservoirs are still required for better understand the reservoir potential of carbonate rocks. Thirty-five carbonate rock samples were collected from 14 wells, and their major and trace elements, as well as carbon and oxygen isotopes, were analyzed. The carbon and oxygen isotope compositions suggest a stable and shallow-lake depositional environment during the Es4s period, with a relatively closed lake system. The paleosalinity index (Z) ranges from 117 to 136, consistent with a closed, evaporative lacustrine setting. The paleotemperature ranges from 12 to 39 °C, reflecting a relatively warm paleoclimate environment. Carbon (δ13C: −2.281‰ to + 5.300‰) and oxygen (δ18O: −11.367‰ to − 5.018‰) isotope ranges align with typical lacustrine carbonates. The negative δ18O trend suggests episodes of freshwater influx, while the overall positive and clustered δ13C values predominantly reflect carbon cycling within an evaporative, closed lake basin. This research offers new perspectives on the paleoenvironmental conditions and depositional processes of lacustrine carbonate rocks in the Dongying Sag and has important implications for understanding the formation and distribution of carbonate reservoirs in this region.Keywords: Lacustrine carbonate rocks, Carbon and oxygen isotopes, Paleoenvironment, Dongying Sag, Es4s sub-memberSubject terms: Biogeochemistry, Climate sciences, Environmental sciences, Solid Earth sciences

Introduction

Carbonate reservoirs play a crucial role in the global inventory of oil and gas resources1–5. According to IHS (Information Handling Services, Inc., USA). As of April 2023, the global discovered reserves of carbonate reservoirs were about 385.2 × 109 t oil equivalent6. They account for about 70% of the world’s oil and gas resources7, of which about 50% are proved recoverable and about 60% are produced8–10. These reservoirs are widely distributed in regions such as North America, the Middle East, and Central Asia11. China also hosts substantial carbonate oil and gas resources, with large and medium-sized fields discovered in the Ordos, Tarim, Bohai Bay, and Sichuan Basins12. Consequently, carbonate reservoirs have become a key focus for oil and gas exploration and development in China13,14.

Compared to their marine counterparts, lacustrine carbonate rocks are far less abundant15, and exhibit greater heterogeneity due to complex controls from climate, tectonics, and provenance. Consequently, their formation mechanisms and paleoenvironmental contexts are less understood, posing challenges for reservoir prediction.

The Dongying Sag in the Bohai Bay Basin hosts significant lacustrine carbonate reservoirs within the upper sub-member of the fourth member of the Shahejie Formation (Es4s)16–18. Current reserve estimates (as of 2023) indicate proven reserves of 4.69 million tons with predicted reserves reaching 51.0 million tons. Previous investigations into the paleoenvironmental conditions of the Dongying Sag’s carbonate formations have employed various methodologies, including analyses of fossil assemblages and geochemical compositions, to reconstruct parameters such as paleo-water depth19,20, paleo-salinity21–23, paleo-climate24, paleo-morphology25, paleo-wind field19,26, paleo-source of materials27, etc. However, a comprehensive interpretation using carbonate carbon (δ13C) and oxygen (δ18O) isotopes—a powerful tool for delineating lake hydrology, salinity trends, and diagenetic environments—remains lacking for the Es4s interval.

In this study, stable carbon and oxygen isotopes were selected as the core geochemical indicators for reconstructing the paleoenvironment, primarily based on the following advantages: Firstly, δ13C and δ18O in lacustrine carbonate rocks are highly sensitive to lake hydrological conditions and basin characteristics, making them ideal proxies for reconstructing paleosalinity and paleohydrological states. Secondly, the co-variation pattern between these two isotopes provides a reliable and intuitive criterion for determining lake closure, which is one of the key objectives of this study. Furthermore, in Mesozoic–Cenozoic lacustrine carbonates with limited diagenetic overprint, δ13C and δ18O values can reliably retain primary environmental signals. When combined with empirical models, these isotopic proxies enable semi-quantitative reconstructions of paleoenvironmental parameters such as paleosalinity and paleotemperature. Therefore, carbon and oxygen isotope analysis has become an indispensable tool in paleolimnological environmental reconstruction. Compared to other geochemical proxies, these isotopic systems are relatively resistant to alteration during diagenesis, making them more reliable for reconstructing original depositional conditions. Accordingly, this study employs a systematic carbonate isotope approach to: (1) assess the degree of post-depositional alteration and constrain the diagenetic environment; (2) reconstruct key paleoenvironmental conditions, including paleosalinity and paleotemperature; and (3) evaluate the hydrological openness of the paleo-lake system during the Es4s deposition. The findings will provide new insights into the depositional and early diagenetic history of these economically important reservoirs.

Geological setting

The Dongying Sag located in the southeast of Bohai Bay Basin, the Qingtuozi and Guangrao Uplift to the east, the Luxi Uplift to the south, the Qingcheng and Binxian Uplift to the west, and the Chenjiazhuang Uplift to the north28. It consists of four major tectonic units: a northern steep-slope belt, a central uplift belt, a sag belt, and a southern gentle-slope belt29,30. It is characterized by the tectonic features of “north break and south super” and “lower break and upper twist”. The tectonic evolution of the Cenozoic includes three stages: a rifting, a fault-depression conversion, and a post-rifting thermal subsidence stage. The Es4s sub-member was deposited during the rifting stage24.

The study area is situated on the southern slope of the Dongying Sag. Although the region is widely recognized for its shale oil reservoirs, core observations and X-ray diffraction (XRD) analyses confirm the presence of carbonate-rich lithofacies within the Es4s (Table 1). These intervals exhibit high carbonate mineral content, with calcite and dolomite typically exceeding 50%, characterizing them as local lacustrine carbonate reservoirs and defining them as the target unit. Locally, thin beds of gray to dark gray limestone, muddy limestone, and gray to purple-red mudstone are developed.

Sampling and analytical methods

Sample description

Carbonate rock sections of the Es4s were sampled from 14 drilled wells in the study area (Fig. 2c). The selection of sampling principle and sampling method is crucial when taking the core sample for element testing, which directly affects the accuracy and reliability of the test results. Thus, the sample collection follows the principle of representativeness, meaning that the collected core samples should accurately reflect the lithological characteristics, element content, and distribution patterns of the formation. Therefore, factors such as stratigraphic structure and lithological variations should be fully considered to ensure the representative of samples when selecting sampling points. When sampling, we avoided selecting abnormal or contaminated rocks and took samples from fresh, non-altered rocks as far as possible, using a manual split coring method. Each sample was assigned a unique identifier, with its location, depth, and lithology documented for subsequent analysis. All collected samples were properly stored to prevent contamination, loss, or degradation. A total of 35 samples were collected for major and trace elements analysis and carbon and oxygen isotope analysis, with each sample weighing at least 100 g.

Analytical methods

The use of carbon and oxygen stable isotopes analysis to recover paleoenvironments is a common tool32–34. In terms of paleoenvironment stable isotopes can be used for stratigraphic division and correlation, and reconstructing paleotemperature of water35, paleosalinity of sedimentary environments36, paleo hydrologic condition37, paleo productivity38, paleoclimate39,40, and the process of sedimentary diagenesis41. At present, this method has been widely used in paleoceanography and Quaternary paleolake studies42–44.

The carbon and oxygen isotope samples collected during this study underwent the following processing and examination steps:

The samples were crushed using a JC6 jaw crusher and vibratory disk crusher from Beijing Gleiman Instrument and Equipment Co. Ltd. with a crushing mesh of 200 mesh (0.074 mm) prior to the commencement of the tests for subsequent geochemical testing.

Major elements were analyzed following GB/T 14,506. 28-2010 Methods of Chemical Analysis of Silicate Rocks Part 28: Determination of the amount of 16 major and minor components; GB/T 14,506. 14-2010 Methods of Chemical Analysis of Silicate Rocks Part 14: Determination of the amount of ferrous oxide; JC/T 1021. 2-2007 Methods of Chemical Analysis of Non-metallic Minerals and Rocks Part 2: Methods for chemical analysis of silicate rocks, minerals and siliceous raw materials. The instrument was an ARL AdvantXP+ / 2413 wavelength dispersive X-ray fluorescence spectrometer.

Carbon and oxygen isotope tests were performed using Somerset Fisher Delta V isotope mass spectrometer with a detection error of ± 0.02‰. The laser isotope analysis technique uses a high-energy focused laser beam to dissolve the sample and decompose the carbonate rock, thereby producing CO2 gas. It consists of three main steps: (1) Preparation of carbonate rock sample: the samples were cut into slices without polishing its surface. Before laser sampling of the samples, the samples were heated to approximately 100 °C using an electric heater and baked for a certain period to eliminate organic matter and excess water. (2) Sampling and purification: before laser sampling, the samples are observed with a microscope, the structure and composition of the samples are observed, so that we can selected the areas to be studied and analyze. (3) Isotope Mass Spectrometry Procedure: The gathered carbon dioxide gas is injected into the isotope mass spectrometer for analyzing the carbon and oxygen isotope composition of the carbonate rock samples, which is basically express in terms of δ13C(‰PDB) and δ18O(‰PDB). The mass spectrometer is capable of micro-area sampling of carbonate rock samples, which has the advantage of relatively high spatial resolution and accurate measurement.

Results

Data reliability

Carbonate rocks are prone to modification during late diagenesis, which can result in the loss of original depositional information. Therefore, carbon and oxygen isotopes need to be evaluated whether they can be used to represent the characterization of the original sediments45. We utilized Mn/Sr values and δ18O isotopic characteristics for assessment.

Mn/Sr. It has been shown that carbonate rocks experience a decrease in Sr content and an increase in Mn after deposition, especially under the influence of atmospheric water cycling46. Consequently, the Mn/Sr ratio serves as an indicator to ascertain the extent to which carbonate samples have been influenced by alteration during later stages. Kaufman et al. proposed that carbonate rocks with an Mn/Sr ratio below 10 can preserve their original carbon and oxygen isotopic composition. The data presented in Table 2 indicate that the Mn/Sr ratios of the collected samples are consistently below this threshold, implying that these samples are representative of the carbon and oxygen isotopic composition of the original carbonate rocks.

Oxygen isotope characterization. Oxygen isotopes are highly responsive to environmental alterations that occur after diagenesis. It is widely acknowledged that diagenesis significantly influences rocks, with substantial variations in the contents and compositions of carbon and oxygen isotope values occurring when δ18OPDB < − 10‰. When δ18OPDB < − 5‰, diagenesis still affects the rocks, but the changes in carbon and oxygen isotopic composition and content are relatively minor47. Based on the literature, Drummond et al. found that most of the oxygen isotope values are in the range of − 15‰~ 5‰48, while carbon isotope values exhibit both positive and negative trends22. In this paper, oxygen isotope values of the samples are all above − 10‰ (Table 2), indicating that the δ18O values are like those in other parts of the world, which illustrates the reliability of the sampling and test data.

In addition, δ13C and δ18O values are commonly used to analyze sedimentary environments of post-Mesozoic samples49. The negligible primary porosity of micrite-dominated rocks restricts fluid-rock interaction during diagenesis, thus suppressing diagenetic alteration50. Therefore, the isotope compositions are unlikely to have been substantially altered by late diagenesis and should largely reflect those of the original lake water. In summary, the samples used in this study are minimally influenced by diagenesis, largely preserve original sedimentary characteristics, and are suitable for paleoenvironmental analysis.

Characteristics of carbon and oxygen isotope compositions

The δ13C value of lacustrine carbonate rocks in China ranges from − 10.0‰ ~ +10.0‰, and most of them fall within − 5.0‰ ~ +5.0‰; the δ18O value ranges from − 20.0‰ ~ +2.0‰, and most of them are between − 15.0‰ ~ 0‰, with almost all negative values51. The range of carbon and oxygen isotope values in the study area covers over 75% of the full range (Table 2), with δ13C values ranging from − 2.281‰ to 5.601‰, with an average of 2.156‰, mainly concentrated in the range of 2‰ to 3‰. The δ18O values range from − 10.650‰ to − 5.018‰, with an average of − 7.985‰, basically concentrated in the range of − 8‰ to − 7‰. These test results are consistent with the characteristics of lacustrine carbonate rocks, and with the distribution pattern of isotopes in lacustrine carbonate rocks in China as mentioned previously.

Discussion

Diagenetic environment

Identification of carbonate rock formation environments is a crucial aspect in the study of carbonate rock diagenesis. Diagenetic environments can be determined by isotopes, trace elements, scanning electron microscopy, reflection of specular plutons, and clay mineralogy. The thin‑section photomicrographs, shown in Fig. 3, show well-preserved primary sedimentary structures in which pebbles and bioclasts retain intact morphology, grain boundaries are well defined, and point-like contacts predominate between grains, indicates limited compaction strength. For carbonate rocks formed in closed lakes, their genesis is intimately linked to lake water chemistry, and isotopic compositions reflect the conditions of the aqueous medium during formation52. Carbonate rock lithology and diagenetic environment collectively control both the values and the evolutionary trends of their carbon and oxygen isotopic compositions.

There are two main views on the classification of diagenetic environments: Chen53 classified diagenetic environments into atmospheric freshwater diagenetic environments, mixed-water diagenetic environments, seawater diagenetic environments, and shallow-deep buried diagenetic environments based on the role of different water media. Huang54 classified carbonate rock formation environments into early near-surface rock formation environments, epigenetic freshwater rock formation environments, late intermediate-deep burial rock formation environments, and hydrothermal rock formation environments based on the temporal and burial-depth evolution of diagenesis55. For this study, Chen’s framework was adopted because its focus on pore-fluid chemistry is more directly relevant to analyzing the diagenetic alterations observed in the core samples.

The carbonate rock isotope values obtained in this study align with the ranges of the shallow buried rock formation environment (δ13C ranges from − 0.2‰ to 3.2‰ and δ18O ranges from − 3.9‰ to − 11.3‰), and a few fall in the atmospheric freshwater rock formation environment (δ13C ranges from − 4.2‰ to 0.15‰, and δ18O ranges from − 5.9‰ to − 11.2‰)53. This suggests that the diagenesis occurred primarily in a shallow burial environment, with local overprinting by meteoric fluids (Fig. 4). The value of δ13C may change greatly in a burial environment. The positive δ13C shift observed in some samples may be related to two possible factors: (1) shallow burial diagenesis, which can drive carbon isotope enrichment56; and (2) the hydrologically closed nature of the paleo-lake. In such settings, prolonged water residence time would lead to the preferential evaporation of 12C-enriched water vapor, thereby increasing the δ13C value of the dissolved inorganic carbon pool.

Carbon and oxygen isotope distribution of Es4s in Dongying Sag.

In summary, the carbonate rocks within the study area originated in a shallow-lake depositional environment and have remained at a shallow burial depth within a near-surface environment. This interpretation is strongly supported by the absence of significant recrystallization, indicating they were never subjected to the elevated pressures and temperatures of deep burial. Crucially, the minimal diagenetic alteration in this shallow burial environment has preserved the primary geochemical signatures of the rocks. This ensures that the samples taken can be used to restore paleosalinity and paleotemperature57.

Paleosalinity (Z)

Variations in the evaporation-to-precipitation ratio directly affect lake water salinity, a relationship that is recorded by synchronous shifts in the oxygen isotopic composition of the carbonates. Therefore, an increase in the δ18O of lacustrine carbonate minerals is commonly interpreted as evidence for elevated paleosalinity. However, this relationship should be interpreted cautiously, as δ18O values can also be influenced by factors such as water temperature, the δ18O of inflow sources, and hydrothermal activity. Consequently, paleosalinity interpretations based on δ18O are most robust when integrated with other independent proxies, such as fluid inclusion salinity, strontium isotopes, or elemental ratios (e.g., Sr/Ca, Mg/Ca)22. The parameter Z, commonly cited as a paleosalinity index, requires careful interpretation in lacustrine settings. Lake salinity is governed by a complex hydrological balance, and the Z value integrates signals from both δ13C and δ18O isotopes. The δ18O component primarily reflects evaporation intensity and water source, which directly relate to salinity. In contrast, the δ13C component is strongly influenced by biological productivity and the dynamics of the dissolved inorganic carbon (DIC) reservoir within the lake, processes that are indirectly linked to salinity in a closed, evaporative setting but are not direct salinity proxies. Therefore, in lakes, the Z value is best interpreted not as a direct quantitative measure of absolute salinity, but as a composite geochemical indicator of hydrological closure and overall water-column stability, where high values signify intensified evaporative conditions and a restricted, productive lake environment. The Z value of carbonate rocks in this area is larger than 120, which is significantly higher than that of carbonate rocks formed under freshwater (< 120), consistent with a closed, evaporative, salinity-prone hydrological system during this period. The brackish-to-saline environment reconstructed in this study is consistent with multiple published paleosalinity indicators for the Shahejie Formation in the Dongying Sag. For example, Li et al. (1988) used the B/Ga ratio to indicate high salinity in the Shahejie Formation water body, suggesting a saline environment21; Liu et al. (1998), based on Sr/Ca and Mg/Ca ratios, concluded that the salinity during the Es4s period was relatively high22, Liu (2018), using the Sr/Ba ratio, inferred that the region was in a saline water environment19. Qian et al. (1982), employing improved boron calculation methods and paleontological assemblages to study paleosalinity variations in the Dongying Sag, suggested that the paleosalinity in Dongying Sag mainly ranged between 21‰ and 40‰59. The mutual confirmation from multiple indicators and methodologies significantly enhances the credibility of our paleosalinity reconstruction, indicating that the Es4s member developed in a saline, closed lake basin controlled by an arid climate and characterized by intense evaporation.

The narrow Z value range indicates a stable depositional environment for the Es4s Formation carbonates. While its use as a quantitative paleosalinity proxy needs further validation, the Z value shows a strong positive correlation with δ13C (R2 = 0.959, Fig. 5) but only a weak correlation with δ18O (R2 = 0.254, Fig. 6).

This result confirms that the calculated Z value is overwhelmingly a function of the carbon isotope signature. The strong coupling (R2 = 0.959, Fig. 5) indicates that the Z value primarily records processes within the lake’s carbon cycle, such as high photosynthetic productivity and consequent enrichment of the DIC pool in 13C and increased organic matter burial. In a hydrologically closed basin, these carbon cycle processes are intrinsically linked to a stable, evaporative setting that promotes salinity increase. Thus, the high Z values (> 120) observed here are interpreted as a robust qualitative indicator of a closed, evaporative lacustrine environment with elevated salinity, rather than a precise quantitative measure of salinity itself. This interpretation is strengthened by its consistency with independent elemental proxies that also suggest saline conditions. The weak correlation with δ18O further underscores that the Z value in this case does not function as a classical evaporation-driven salinity meter, but rather as a unique proxy for the coupled carbon-water dynamics in a terminal lake system.

Closed lakes and open lakes

Talbot (1990), through a global isotopic study of modern and ancient lacustrine carbonates, established that the correlation between δ13C and δ18O is a key indicator of hydrological openness in lake systems63. The isotopic composition of lacustrine carbonates in open lakes with short residence times is dominated by inflow signatures, and the two isotopes are decoupled. Conversely, in closed lakes with long residence times, evaporation controls water chemistry, preferentially enriching the lake in 18O through the loss of 16O64,65. In a productive closed lake, organic productivity acts as the primary driver of the carbon isotope system, enriching the DIC pool in 13C through the preferential biological removal of 12C. Furthermore, in a hydrologically closed basin, the DIC reservoir becomes increasingly enriched in 13C over time due to the cumulative effect of carbon removal processes, which favors the enrichment of 12C. Because both evaporation and productivity effects are maximized in closed lakes under arid conditions, the δ18O and δ13C values of primary carbonates often co-vary positively, creating a strong covariant trend22,66.

If δ18O is taken as the horizontal coordinate and δ13C is taken as the vertical coordinate, the δ18O and δ13C projection points of primary carbonate rocks in open lakes are scattered while the δ18O and δ13C projection points of primary carbonate rocks in closed lakes are linearly correlated. As the degree of closure increases, so does the correlation coefficient. In open freshwater lakes, δ18O and δ13C usually show negative values and the data points fall in the third quadrant, while in closed brackish and semi-saline lakes, δ18O has both positive and negative values and δ13C is basically positive, with the data points mostly falling in the first and second quadrants66. Consequently, through analysis of the carbon and oxygen isotopic variations in carbonate rocks, it is possible to infer whether paleolakes were closed or open during the corresponding period of carbonate deposition.

The carbon and oxygen isotope projection points in the carbonate rocks on Es4s in the study area basically fall in the closed lake interval, and the two show a clear trend of correlation. As noted above, linear isotopic trends in carbonates are characteristic of hydrologically closed basins and in this case show that Dongying Sag has been hydrologically closed recorded by the cores.

In this paper, the carbon and oxygen isotope characterization of carbon and oxygen isotope analysis of the depositional environment, paleosalinity, paleotemperature, and closure of the lake during the Es4s period suggests that the period was in a shallow lake environment, with small changes in the depositional environment, and belonged to a closed lake. The development of lacustrine carbonate rocks in the upper Es₄ was primarily controlled by paleosalinity and paleoclimate. A saline to brackish environment provided the chemical foundation for carbonate precipitation, while the intense evaporation-concentration process under an arid paleoclimate served as the core driver for lake water salinization and the eventual large-scale carbonate formation. Oxidizing conditions and a closed hydrological setting provided the long-term stable background necessary for this process.
