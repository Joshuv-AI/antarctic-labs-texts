Conservative versus surgical treatment in the management of cerebellar infarction: A meta-analysis
Authors: Yuqing Xiang, Jiahao Meng, Xuanyu Wang, Pan Liu, Yumei Wu, Miao He, Shuguang Gao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background:Recent guidelines do not clearly favor either conservative or surgical treatment for cerebellar infarction. We aim to compare the clinical outcomes of these 2 treatments in patients with cerebellar infarcts through meta-analysis.Methods:We systematically searched 4 databases – PubMed, Cochrane Library, Embase, and Web of Science – from inception to May 1, 2024 to identify eligible studies that compared surgical treatment and conservative treatment. Two authors independently extracted data on mortality, modified Rankin Scale, and Glasgow outcome scale for patients with cerebellar infarction.Results:We retrieved 12 eligible studies, including 1108 participants. No significant differences were observed in terms of mortality, modified Rankin Scale, acute cerebellar infarction between the 2 treatments. However, there was a statistically significant difference between surgery and no-surgery group regarding serious adverse outcomes with a Glasgow outcome scale score of 2 to 4, including moderate recovery, severe disability, persistent vegetative state (odds ratio: 2.14; 95% confidence interval: 1.05–4.36; P = .04; I2 = 0%), and in the consciousness dysfunction group (odds ratio: 0.32; 95% confidence interval: 0.12–0.88; P = .03; I2 = 64%).Conclusion:This meta-analysis of cohort studies indicated that surgery led to more serious adverse outcomes for patients with cerebellar infarction compared to conservative treatment, but showed more favorable outcomes for patients with consciousness dysfunction during follow-up. Further research is warranted to explore these aspects in depth.

1. Introduction

Stroke has already become the leading cause of death and disability worldwide, and the increasing global burden of stroke is attracting more and more people to this issue. Cerebellar infarction constitutes approximately 2% to 3% of all strokes.[1] Its nonspecific symptoms as dizziness, vertigo, and vomiting, often result in difficult diagnoses and longer onset-to-door time.[2] Due to the limited space within the posterior fossa, cerebellar infarction may lead to life-threatening complications, including transtentorial herniation, hydrocephalus, brainstem, and fourth ventricle compression, and ultimately death if left untreated.[3–7] These factors may contribute to the generally poor prognosis associated with posterior circulation strokes.[8]

The treatment of cerebellar infarction is mainly categorized into conservative and surgical treatment. Medical management involves osmotic therapy and other ancillary measures, such as elevating the head of the bed, hypothermia, barbiturates, and corticosteroids.[9] Neurosurgical options include extraventricular drainage (EVD), suboccipital decompressive craniectomy (SDC), SDC with necrosectomy and SDC with EVD.[10] Surgical indications have primarily been based on clinical deterioration status as measured by the Glasgow Coma Scale score.[1] Decompressive suboccipital craniectomy and EVD are currently recommended for malignant cerebellar infarcts (MPCI) in the guidelines of the American Stroke Association and the European Stroke Organization.[9] However, prolonged operating times, large skin incision, surgery-related tissue damage and complications make surgery particularly stressful and limited for elderly patients and those receiving anticoagulants.[11] In addition, there is limited data available on sociodemographic factors, treatment success rate, and long-term outcomes of patients with cerebellar infarction.[12,13] In 2023, a systematic review supported that there is no difference in survival or functional outcomes between surgery and conservative treatment for cerebellar infarction.[10] Consequently, the debate continues regarding which therapy is better for patients with cerebellar infarction, and there is no consensus on the optimal therapeutic option.[1,14,15]

There has been no meta-analysis comparing the 2 treatments in cerebellar infarcts in this field. The objective of this meta-analysis was to compare clinical outcomes of patients treated surgically or conservatively, and to provide a basis for medical decision-making.

2. Materials and methods

This meta-analysis was performed according to the preferred reporting items for systematic reviews and meta-analyses (PRISMA) guidelines.[16] The study protocol was registered on PROSPERO (CRD42024543487) on May 5, 2024. Data are available on request to the corresponding authors.

2.1. Searches

The following databases were searched from inception to May 1, 2024: PubMed, Embase, Cochrane Library, and Web of Science. A visual representation of our search strategy can be found in Figure 1. English terms include: cerebellar infarction, cerebellar infarcts, decompressive craniectomy, SDC, ventriculostomy, surgical therapy, and surgery. Taking PubMed as an example, the specific search strategy is detailed in eMethods 2, Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/Q580.

Search and selection of studies for inclusion.

2.2. Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The inclusion criteria for this meta-analysis of cohort studies were: studies focusing on patients diagnosed with cerebellar infarction; studies directly comparing the clinical outcomes of cerebellar infarction between surgery and conservative treatment and the study type is randomized controlled trials (RCTs) or cohort studies. The exclusion criteria were: studies where data cannot be extracted; and studies which full-text is not available. Any disagreements regarding the eligibility of full-text articles between the 2 groups were resolved by consulting with a senior author.

2.3. Data extraction

After eliminating duplicate articles, 2 reviewers were engaged to independently evaluate the titles and abstracts of initially retrieved studies. Data extraction was performed by the same 2 reviewers independently. The following characteristics of the enrolled studies were collected: first author, year of publication, country, study design, number of included patients, demographics statistics, and duration of follow-up. The following clinical outcomes were extracted: mortality at admission, 3 months, 6 months, 1 year and 2 years after surgery or conservative treatment; Glasgow outcome scale (GOS) at 3 months, 6 months and 12 months after surgery or conservative treatment; modified Rankin Scale (mRS) score at 3 months, 6 months and 12 months after surgery or conservative treatment; and adverse events. The primary outcome was mortality at follow-up. The secondary outcomes were mRS and final neurological outcome (GOS) at follow-up. In case of missing data, diligent attempts were made to contact the corresponding authors to obtain access to the original data. If unsuccessful, the plot digitizer tool would be used to extract data points from graphical representations. Any discrepancies or divergent interpretations that emerged during this data retrieval process were meticulously addressed through discussion and consensus-building.

2.4. Risk of bias

The same 2 reviewers conducted independent assessments on the included studies in terms of risk of bias using the Newcastle–Ottawa Scale (NOS). This tool comprises 3 specific domains: ascertainment of exposure, representativeness of the exposure, selection of no-exposure, no outcome indicators initially, comparability, assessment of outcome, adequate follow-up, integrity of follow-up. Each study acquires a NOS score eventually. Any discrepancies in assessments were resolved through discussion among the reviewers or by consulting with a senior author. We calculated the kappa statistic to assess the consistency of data extraction and quality evaluation.

Risk of publication bias was assessed using funnel plots, and we planned to test for funnel plot asymmetry (Egger linear regression test) for a given outcome if at least 10 studies were available for analysis. Additionally, to assess the quality of evidence, we will use the GRADE approach.

2.5. Data synthesis

Neurosurgical intervention was defined as any combination of ventriculostomy, cerebral shunting, ventricular drains, craniotomy or craniectomy, with or without necrosectomy. The mRS outcomes were extracted in the form of continuous variables as mean and standard deviation, and further presented as mean difference with 95% confidence interval (CI) using the inverse-variance method. The mortality and final neurological outcome were extracted in the form of dichotomous variables and then pooled by the Mantel–Haenszel method, also presented as odds ratio (OR) with 95% CI. The I2 statistic was utilized to assess the degree of statistical heterogeneity among the included studies. Random effects models were employed for analysis. All statistical analyses were conducted using Review Manager 5.4 software (Cochrane Collaboration).

3. Results

3.1. Identified studies

Figure 1 illustrates the literature search and selection process, which eventually yielded 12 articles, including 12 unique cohort studies. Among these, 2 studies (Wang et al,[7] Taneda et al[17]) compared the effects of surgical treatment or conservative treatment on acute cerebellar infarction. While 7 studies investigated the therapeutic effects of surgical or medical treatment on cerebellar infarction that demonstrate consciousness dysfunction. Twelve cohort studies were included. Studies were conducted across various countries, including 2 developing countries, and 9 developed countries. Regarding the outcomes, 8 studies examined only mortality, 3 studies examined mortality and mRS, 1 studies examined only mRS, with 7 studies specifically investigating severity. And to assess the inter-rater reliability of the study selection process, we calculated the kappa statistic, which resulted in a value of 0.82.

Among the 719 patients included, the combined mean age for those undergoing surgery was 59.11 years, whereas that for those undergoing conservative therapy was 69.78 years. Of the 12 studies included, there were 5 studies not providing information about the sex and age of the participants, and 7 studies did not provide specific follow-up time. 3.2. Mortality

Eleven studies reported the mortality of surgical treatment or conservative treatment during follow-up on cerebellar infarction, and the pooled results showed that there was no statistically difference between the surgery group and no-surgery group (OR: 0.51; 95% CI: 0.23–1.14; I2 = 61%; Fig. 2).

Findings of meta-analysis of primary outcomes between surgical treatment and conservative treatment for patients with cerebellar infarction. CI = confidence interval.

3.3. Modified Rankin scale and Glasgow Outcome Scale

Three studies reported the mRS of surgical treatment or conservative treatment during follow-up on cerebellar infarction: 0 = no symptoms, 1 = no significant disability despite symptoms, 2 = slight disability, impaired in previous activities but able to look after own affairs without assistance, 3 = moderate disability, needs help but able to walk without assistance, 4 = moderately severe disability, needs help in many activities of daily living, 5 = severe disability, bedridden, requiring constant nursing, 6 = death, and the pooled results showed that there was no statistically difference between the mRS score of surgery group and no-surgery group (Mean difference −0.33; 95% CI: −1.10 to 0.44; I2 = 0%; eFig. 1, Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/Q581). Two studies considered a mRS score of 2 or less a good outcome and a mRS score of 3 or more a poor outcome, and the results showed that there was no statistically difference between surgery group and no-surgery group (OR: 0.72; 95% CI: 0.26–2.01; I2 = 59%; eFig. 2, Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/Q581).

The final neurological outcome was evaluated during follow-up and expressed according to the GOS in 7 studies. Excluding death ending with a GOS of 1, the final outcome were divided into “good outcome” for those with a GOS of 5 (good recovery) and “severe adverse outcome” with a GOS of 2 to 4 (moderate recovery, severe disability, persistent vegetative state). Regarding severe adverse outcomes, the pooled results showed that there was statistically difference between the surgery group and no-surgery group (OR: 2.14; 95% CI: 1.05–4.36; I2 = 0%; Fig. 3), and the group with surgical treatment showed statistically higher risk than the group with conservative treatment (P = .04). While as for good outcome, the pooled results showed that there was no statistically difference between the surgery group and no-surgery group (OR: 1.55; 95% CI: 0.43–5.60; I2 = 66%; eFig. 3, Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/Q581).

Findings of meta-analysis of final neurological outcome (severe adverse outcome: GOS of 2–4) between surgical treatment and conservative treatment for patients with cerebellar infarction. CI = confidence interval, GOS = Glasgow outcome scale.

3.4. Consciousness dysfunction

Seven studies reported the clinical outcomes of surgical treatment or conservative treatment during follow-up on patients with cerebellar infarction that demonstrate varying degrees of disturbance of consciousness. As for the mortality, the pooled results showed that the surgery group has lower risk than no-surgery group statistically (OR: 0.32; 95% CI: 0.12–0.88, I2 = 64%; Fig. 4).

Findings of meta-analysis of primary outcome between surgical treatment and conservative treatment for patients with cerebellar infarction that demonstrate consciousness dysfunction. CI = confidence interval.

3.5. Subgroup analysis

There are six studies before 2000 and 6 studies after 2000 compared 2 treatments during follow-up. The pooled results before 2000 showed that surgery group has lower risk than no-surgery group statistically (OR: 0.25; 95% CI: 0.06–0.99), but the pooled results after 2000 showed that there was no statistically difference between the surgery group and no-surgery group (OR: 0.74; 95% CI: 0.40–1.36). In addition, 6 studies showed there was no statistically difference between the craniectomy group and no-surgery group (OR: 0.52; 95% CI: 0.14–1.99). Four studies showed that there was no statistically difference between the ventriculostomy group and no-surgery group (OR: 1.20; 95% CI: 0.47–3.06). And 2 studies showed that there was no statistically difference between the ventriculostomy group and no-surgery group (OR: 1.62; 95% CI: 0.11–23.42; eFig. 4, Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/Q581).

4. Discussion

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first meta-analysis of recent published studies to compare the clinical outcomes between surgical treatment and conservative treatment for patients with cerebellar infarction. The results of primary and noteworthy outcomes revealed no statistically significant differences between surgical treatment and conservative treatment in terms of mortality and mRS at follow-up. However, surgical treatment was associated with a statistically significant increase in severe adverse outcomes (moderate recovery, severe disability, persistent vegetative state). Cerebellar infarction typically results from ischemia in the posterior inferior cerebellar artery, anterior inferior cerebellar artery, superior cerebellar artery, or their combination.[16,18] Usual causes included embolism or large vessel atherosclerosis and vertebrobasilar dissection.[4,19,20] Due to nonspecific symptoms and the limitations of early computed tomography imaging, early diagnosis of cerebellar infarction can be challenging.[14] If treatment is not timely, patients with cerebellar infarction may develop very serious complications such as brain stem compression and/or obstructive hydrocephalus, resulting in high mortality rates, particularly in cases of comatose or brainstem response loss.[3,13] Decompressive suboccipital craniectomy is often necessary in the most severe cases,[13] although evidence supporting its efficacy in MPCI is limited.[21,22] Surgical intervention is typically urgent.[14] Although craniectomy for MPCI refractory to medical therapy is recommended by American Heart Association/American Stroke Association guidelines, the evidence for this remains inadequate (lack of relevant randomized controlled trials and recent meta-analysis).[23–26] Now the conservative therapy effectively and sustainingly has been widely used in MPCI or stable cerebellar infarction, while surgery has widely used in unstable cerebellar infarction.[10] The debate over optimal surgical methods remains ongoing and warrants further investigation.

Mortality is a primary outcome to assess the efficacy of surgical treatment or conservative treatment for cerebellar infarction. Our meta-analysis of 12 studies found similar mortality rates between the 2 groups over the follow-up period (3 months, 6months or 12 months, etc). There was no statistically significant difference between the 2 groups for the good recovery rates at follow-up. As for outcomes of patients with urgency of onset, there is no statistically significant difference between 2 treatments. However, our meta-analysis indicated that the surgical group showed superior outcome in mortality for patients with cerebellar infarction that demonstrated consciousness dysfunction. This phenomenon may be attributed to the rapid decompression of the posterior fossa such that any viable brain cells can be preserved timely after surgery. For individuals showing decreasing consciousness, surgery may represent a promising option. Overall, surgical therapy for cerebellar infarcts appears to have limited impact on functional outcomes and reducing mortality, except in cases of severe disease with decreasing consciousness and a high risk of raised intracranial pressure.

Adverse effects were also a critical parameter for evaluating the safety of surgery and conservative therapy. In our meta-analysis, 7 studies included were analyzed for GOS. All the patients except for death were divided into 2 groups (good outcome, GOS = 5; severe adverse outcome, GOS = 2–4) at follow-up, and the results suggested that as for good outcome there is no significant difference between surgical treatment and conservative treatment, and as for severe adverse outcome surgery is statistically higher risk than conservative group. Our meta-analysis indicated that the conservative group had superior outcomes on the GOS in cerebellar infarction at follow-up, while surgical group might result in a higher risk of severe outcome. However, more studies with large sample size were needed for further research.

Several limitations in our meta-analysis should be mentioned. First of all, the heterogeneity among included studies might have increased the bias, as factors such as the corticosteroids dosage, patient type, drug regimen, and administration methods varied across different studies. Nevertheless, we used a random effects model to estimate the true situation as accurately as possible. Secondly, patients undergoing surgery might also undergo conservative therapy at the same time, either during or after surgical intervention, which might influence the surgical outcomes, while the conservative therapy group did not undergo surgical intervention, which might not affect the comparison between 2 group. Thirdly, although we included as many recent comparative studies as possible, our dataset comprised only 12 cohort studies. We were unable to find any relevant randomized controlled trials, and consequently, the resulting data exhibited a wide CI. The quality of evidence would have been higher had subsequent randomized controlled trials been included. The quantity and quality of our research require further improvement. Fourthly, due to limited data, some of the subgroup analyses only included a small number of cohort studies for the outcomes of specific types of patients or different administration methods. Fifthly, also due to the limited data, analysis of outcomes for specific patients receiving specific methods of administration could not be performed. Lastly, due to unique data constraints and study design, we were unable to conduct interaction tests for subgroup analyses, Therefore, our interpretation of the subgroup analysis results provides more of a directional insight, and the interpretation of outcomes should be approached cautiously. Future research is needed to further explore these aspects.

5. Conclusion

This meta-analysis indicated that surgery did not exhibit statistically significant differences in mortality within general group in comparison with conservative therapy, but presented a statistically significant higher risk of severe adverse outcomes, including moderate recovery, severe disability, persistent vegetative state. However, in patients with cerebellar infarction accompanied by consciousness dysfunction, surgery yielded superior outcomes. We acknowledge the necessity for additional prospective controlled studies of high quality to substantiate our findings.
