Motor learning and dopamine-dependent striatal synaptic plasticity are controlled by astrocytic MEGF10
Authors: Young-Jin Choi, Youngeun Lina Lee, Yemin Kim, Jaeseon Jeon, Jae-Ick Kim, Won-Suk Chung
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Dopamine regulates motor learning by modulating striatal synaptic plasticity in medium spiny neurons (MSNs). Despite its well-established role in synaptic plasticity, dopamine’s involvement in glia-mediated synapse remodeling remains unclear. Here, we demonstrate that the astrocytic phagocytic receptor MEGF10 (Multiple Epidermal Growth Factor-like Domains Protein 10), but not MERTK (MER Proto-Oncogene, Tyrosine Kinase), is required for the elimination of corticostriatal excitatory synapses on MSNs during motor learning. Deletion of astrocytic Megf10 impaired long-term potentiation and depression (LTP and LTD), and reduced learning-induced increases in synaptic strength. Notably, chemogenetic activation of corticostriatal transmission or dopamine release from the substantia nigra pars compacta (SNc) selectively enhanced astrocytic synapse elimination. Furthermore, elevated dopamine and motor learning differentially regulated postsynaptic elimination in MSNs depending on dopamine receptor subtype, leading to MEGF10-dependent changes in synaptic remodeling and quantal properties. These findings identify astrocytic MEGF10 as a key mediator of dopamine- and activity-dependent synapse remodeling in the striatum.

Introduction

Motor learning is the capacity of animals to coordinate their movements in response to dynamic intrinsic and extrinsic cues to acquire new skills1,2. Previous studies on various hierarchies of motor learning tasks, including the rotarod task3–5 and the forelimb-reaching task6–8 have demonstrated that structural and functional synaptic plasticity in the corticostriatal excitatory circuit, especially from the primary motor cortex (M1) to the dorsolateral striatum (DLS), is significantly enhanced during motor learning. In vivo two-photon live imaging revealed that during motor learning, cortical pyramidal neurons undergo rapid formation and elimination of dendritic spines9–12. In addition, motor skill acquisition induces long-term potentiation (LTP) in the motor cortex by increasing the number of α-amino-3-hydroxy-5-methyl-4-isoxazolepropionic acid (AMPA) receptors, followed by enhanced glutamate release at the individual synapse level13. Importantly, recent studies have also shown that increased corticostriatal plasticity, particularly in cortical engram neurons, enhances synaptic connectivity, increases release probability, and promotes the formation of local clusters of dendritic spines in the DLS. These findings suggest that striatal neurons undergo structural and functional potentiation through corticostriatal connections during motor learning14,15.

The striatum in the basal ganglia is a core region for motor control and reward processing, where various inputs are integrated, including the largest connection from sensory and motor cortical subregions16. In addition, the substantia nigra pars compacta (SNc) sends its dopaminergic efferents to the striatum via the nigrostriatal pathway17. Dopamine is a well-characterized neuromodulator involved in voluntary movement, reward, and motivation18–20, and its disruption leads to diverse neurological disorders, such as Parkinson’s disease (PD) and substance use disorder21–25. It is important to note that dopamine regulates synaptic plasticity in medium spiny neurons (MSNs) in the striatum, influencing both synaptic potentiation and depression. For example, activation of dopamine receptor D1 (DRD1) promotes the rapid trafficking of N-methyl-D-aspartate (NMDA) receptors to dendritic spines, resulting in synaptic potentiation26. Conversely, activating dopamine receptor D2 (DRD2) in postsynaptic MSNs interacts functionally with presynaptic endocannabinoid receptors (CB1) to induce synaptic depression27. These DRD1 and DRD2-expressing MSNs (D1 and D2 MSNs) further project their inhibitory inputs to the internal and external segments of the globus pallidus (GPi and GPe), respectively, and the coordinated activities of D1 and D2 MSNs are critical for executing voluntary movements as well as reward-seeking behaviors28–30. Importantly, in PD, dopaminergic inputs from the SNc to the striatum are lost, leading to impaired coordination between D1 and D2 MSNs, eventually causing severe motor symptoms31,32. Despite these important roles, how dopamine precisely regulates structural synapse plasticity in the striatum during motor learning remains unclear, most likely due to technical constraints in investigating synapse remodeling processes in the striatum.

Previously, we established that astrocytes actively contribute to the activity-dependent synapse elimination that refines neural circuits during development by phagocytosing synapses via the MEGF10 and MERTK phagocytic pathways33. This phagocytic function of astrocytes also plays a critical role in the constant elimination of synapses in the adult hippocampus, regulating synaptic plasticity and recent memory formation34. Although these findings challenge the general consensus that microglia are the primary synapse phagocytes responsible for controlling synapse number in the brain35,36, much remains unknown about whether astrocytic phagocytosis serves as a general mechanism for synapse remodeling in adult brain circuits, and if so, how different brain circuits adapt to various intrinsic and extrinsic factors to facilitate this process.

In this study, we set out to investigate the roles of glial phagocytosis in mediating synapse elimination and plasticity of striatal D1 and D2 MSNs during motor learning. Through genetic, molecular, electrophysiological, and behavioral approaches, we revealed that astrocytic phagocytosis of corticostriatal synapses through MEGF10, but not MERTK phagocytic receptor, is essential not only for neuronal activity-dependent synapse elimination but also for maintenance and potentiation of synaptic transmission and plasticity in the striatum. Unlike astrocytes, other glial cells, such as microglia and oligodendrocyte precursor cells (OPCs), do not appear to be involved in striatal synapse remodeling. Intriguingly, we further found that the extent of synapse elimination by astrocytes is highly dependent on dopamine levels in the striatum. Deleting DRD1 and DRD2 in astrocytes did not affect dynamic changes in astrocytic elimination of D1 and D2 MSN postsynapses. This finding suggests that the coordinated activity of D1 and D2 MSNs directly dictates dopamine-dependent synapse elimination by astrocytes, ultimately maintaining corticostriatal transmission and driving striatal synaptic plasticity.

Results

MEGF10-dependent astrocytic engulfment of corticostriatal synapses

We used an adeno-associated virus (AAV)-based reporter system to investigate glial engulfment of corticostriatal synapses during repeated motor learning. In brief, presynaptic and postsynaptic markers (Synaptophysin1 and postsynaptic density protein 95 (PSD95).FingR, respectively) were labeled with mCherry-eGFP tandem dimer fluorescence proteins, enabling the monitoring of the acidic influx of synaptic markers into adjacent glial cells34,37–39 (Fig. 1a, f). Astrocytic engulfment of corticostriatal presynapses significantly increased in the dorsolateral striatum (DLS) after three days of repeated motor learning, correlating with improved motor skills following training (Fig. 1b–d). Similarly, astrocytic engulfment of striatal postsynapses also increased significantly during repeated motor learning in the DLS (Fig. 1g–i). Previous reports have shown that astrocytes utilize phagocytic receptors, MEGF10 and MERTK, to engulf synapses both during development and in adulthood. To assess the role of MEGF10 in synapse engulfment during motor learning, Megf10 was specifically deleted in astrocytes in a Cre-dependent manner34,40 (Aldh1l1-CreERT2; Megf10 fl/fl) to generate Megf10 conditional knockout (cKO) mice. Successful deletion of Megf10 in astrocytes was confirmed using RNA fluorescent in situ hybridization (Supplementary Fig. 2a, b). On the first day of training, Megf10 cKO mice showed no significant differences in the rotarod task compared to control mice, indicating similar baseline activity. Interestingly, from the second day onward, Megf10 cKO mice displayed impaired motor learning ability compared to control mice (Fig. 2a, b). Further examination of astrocyte-mediated synapse elimination revealed a significant reduction in both presynaptic and postsynaptic engulfment by striatal astrocytes in Megf10 cKO mice after motor learning (Fig. 2c, f). Interestingly, the basal levels of postsynapse elimination by astrocytes, but not presynaptic ones, were also reduced in Megf10 cKO mice (Fig. 2f), indicating the continuous postsynapse engulfment by astrocytic MEGF10 even before engaging in a specific motor learning paradigm. Additionally, we confirmed that control mice with or without AAV injection showed similar learning curves in the rotarod task, demonstrating that our AAV-based reporter expression itself did not affect motor learning (Fig. 1c, h and Supplementary Fig. 2c, d). To further explore the role of MEGF10 in the DLS, Cre-recombinase was expressed under the control of an astrocyte-specific GfaABC1D promoter41 and delivered via AAV to selectively delete Megf10 in DLS astrocytes (Fig. 2g). To assess the role of astrocytic MERTK during motor learning, Mertk was deleted in astrocytes (Aldh1l1-CreERT2; Mertk fl/fl). Successful deletion of astrocytic MERTK in the DLS was confirmed by immunohistochemistry (Supplementary Fig. 3a,b). Unlike Megf10 cKO, Mertk cKO mice exhibited normal motor learning comparable to control mice (Mertk fl/fl). Moreover, astrocytic engulfment of corticostriatal presynapses in the DLS normally increased in Mertk cKO mice after motor learning (Supplementary Fig. 3c–e). These findings suggest that DLS astrocytes primarily rely on MEGF10, rather than MERTK, for synapse engulfment during motor learning.

Moreover, to evaluate the role of astrocytic MEGF10-mediated synaptic phagocytosis in multiple forms of motor learning, we further tested the forelimb-reaching task. During seven days of training, the daily success rate of each animal and glial phagocytosis of corticostriatal synapses in the DLS were analyzed after learning (Supplementary Fig. 4a). Throughout the course of training, control mice (Megf10 fl/fl) successfully learned the task, showing a significant increase in success rate. In contrast, Megf10 cKO mice failed to learn the task (Supplementary Fig. 4b). Furthermore, astrocytic phagocytosis of corticostriatal presynapses and striatal postsynapses was significantly elevated in control mice after training, whereas astrocytes in Megf10 cKO mice exhibited markedly reduced phagocytic activity toward corticostriatal synapses (Supplementary Fig. 4c,e). Similar to the rotarod task, microglial phagocytosis remained unchanged during learning (Supplementary Fig. 4d,f). These results indicate that MEGF10-dependent phagocytic activity of DLS astrocytes is crucial for multiple forms of motor learning.

Astrocytic MEGF10 deletion disrupts corticostriatal synaptic transmission

To further understand the role of MEGF10-mediated astrocytic engulfment in synaptic function at corticostriatal synapses, we electrically stimulated the external capsule adjacent to the M1 motor cortex and the DLS, and performed whole-cell patch-clamp recording to measure excitatory postsynaptic currents (EPSCs) in striatal MSNs. We then compared the input-output (I/O) relationship of excitatory synaptic transmission between control and Megf10 cKO mice (Fig. 2i). Surprisingly, we observed a significant attenuation of corticostriatal excitatory transmission in Megf10 cKO mice compared to the control group, suggesting that synaptic strength is compromised following the impairment of MEGF10-mediated astrocytic engulfment (Fig. 2j). To investigate the underlying mechanism of weakened corticostriatal synaptic transmission, we examined the AMPA/NMDA ratio by pharmacologically isolating AMPA receptor-mediated currents42. Notably, the AMPA/NMDA ratio was markedly diminished in the MSNs of Megf10 cKO mice (Fig. 2k). This result indicates that the impairment of MEGF10-mediated astrocytic engulfment undermines excitatory synaptic transmission at corticostriatal synapses by reducing AMPA receptor-mediated postsynaptic efficacy.

Impaired long-term potentiation and depression by astrocytic MEGF10 deletion

Given the essential role of MEGF10-mediated astrocytic synapse engulfment in motor learning, we next investigated whether the conditional KO of astrocytic Megf10 also influences LTP at corticostriatal synapses, which is considered critical for motor learning and skill acquisition. To examine corticostriatal LTP, we evaluated two distinct forms of synaptic plasticity: the spike-timing-dependent plasticity (STDP) and high-frequency stimulation (HFS)-induced LTP43–45 (Fig. 3a, d). Using the STDP protocol, robust LTP at corticostriatal synapses was reliably induced in control mice and persisted for up to 60 minutes following induction. In contrast, Megf10 cKO mice exhibited significantly impaired corticostriatal LTP compared to control mice (Fig. 3b, c). This finding was further corroborated in the HFS-induced LTP experiment, where corticostriatal LTP was severely diminished in Megf10 cKO mice (Fig. 3e, f). Next, we further examined whether Megf10-dependent astrocytic phagocytosis contributes to long-term depression (LTD) in corticostriatal circuits, particularly in D2 MSNs, since these neurons have been reported to undergo synaptic depression during motor learning and dopaminergic modulation43,46,47. By crossing control and Megf10 cKO mice with Drd1a-tdTomato reporter mice and isolating D2 MSN responses, we found that LTD in D2 MSNs was robustly induced and persisted throughout the recording period in control mice. However, in Megf10 cKO mice, the magnitude of LTD was significantly smaller (Fig. 3g–i), indicating that astrocytic Megf10 is dispensable for LTD induction but required for its full expression.

MEGF10 deletion attenuates motor learning-induced enhancement of synaptic strength

To directly investigate how MEGF10-mediated astrocytic synapse engulfment contributes to synaptic adaptations during motor learning, we trained control and Megf10 cKO mice on the rotarod task. Following habituation to the rotarod apparatus or the completion of motor learning, we conducted ex vivo whole-cell patch-clamp recordings in striatal MSNs to compare I/O relationships of excitatory synaptic transmission at corticostriatal synapses between control and Megf10 cKO mice (Fig. 4a). Consistent with our findings (Fig. 2i, j), we observed that the deficits in MEGF10-mediated astrocytic synapse engulfment of excitatory synapses led to reduced synaptic strength at corticostriatal synapses in rotarod-habituated Megf10 cKO mice compared to rotarod-habituated control mice (Fig. 4b, e). After rotarod training, both control and Megf10 cKO mice exhibited steeper I/O curves compared to the habituation-only groups, indicating that motor learning enhances corticostriatal synaptic strength14,48,49 (Fig. 4c, d). However, when we analyzed EPSC amplitudes with fold-change normalization, we found that, unlike the robust main effect of learning and a highly significant stimulus × learning interaction observed in control mice, learning-related changes was not significant in Megf10 cKO mice (Supplementary Fig. 5). In addition, likely due to both compromised synaptic transmission and attenuated LTP, synaptic strength after motor learning in Megf10 cKO mice still remained comparable to that of habituation-only control mice (Fig. 4b, f). To further dissect the role of astrocytic MEGF10 specifically during the learning phase, we employed a secreted form of the MEGF10 receptor (secMEGF10) to acutely suppress MEGF10 activity by sequestering its potential ligand50. We also took advantage of the rtTA-doxycycline system51, which enabled spatiotemporal blockade of astrocytic MEGF10-mediated synaptic phagocytosis during motor learning sessions. Using this approach, we found that induction of secMEGF10 expression during motor learning significantly attenuated rotarod performance, as well as astrocytic phagocytosis of corticostriatal presynapses and postsynapses (Supplementary Fig. 6). These results suggest that, beyond its contribution to basal synaptic transmission, astrocytic MEGF10 also exerts a direct influence on synaptic plasticity during learning.

Cortical neuronal activity and dopamine-mediated synapse elimination in the striatum

It has been previously suggested that neural activity guides astrocytic and microglial engulfment of synapses during development and in the adult hippocampus33–36,52. Additionally, neural activity in the corticostriatal circuit has been shown to increase during motor learning. To investigate the role of neural activity as a regulator of astrocytic synapse elimination during motor learning, we monitored astrocytic engulfment of corticostriatal synapses following chemogenetic modulation of cortical neurons. In brief, an AAV expressing the excitatory DREADD (designer receptors exclusively activated by designer drugs)53 receptor hM3D(Gq) under the control of the neuron-specific human synapsin promoter (hSyn) was stereotaxically delivered to the primary motor cortex (M1). Chemogenetic activation of cortical neurons was confirmed by cFos expression in neurons transduced with the chemogenetic receptor (HA-tagged hM3Dq) (Supplementary Fig. 7a). When cortical neurons were activated through consecutive injection of clozapine N-oxide (CNO) for three days, we found that astrocytic engulfment of corticostriatal presynapses in the DLS was significantly increased (Fig. 5a, b and Supplementary Fig. 8a). Repeated activation of M1 neurons also enhanced astrocytic engulfment of striatal postsynapses (Fig. 5d, e and Supplementary Fig. 8c). To further dissect whether astrocytic engulfment differentially affects presynaptic versus postsynaptic compartments, we recorded corticostriatal EPSCs under conditions in which Ca²⁺ was replaced with Sr²⁺ to promote asynchronous neurotransmitter release and enable quantal analysis. Following repeated chemogenetic activation of M1 cortical neurons, control mice displayed a significant increase in quantal frequency together with a decrease in quantal amplitude, indicating enhanced presynaptic release probability accompanied by postsynaptic downscaling. In contrast, these activity-dependent alterations were absent in Megf10 cKO mice (Supplementary Fig. 9). These results demonstrate that MEGF10-dependent astrocytic phagocytosis is essential for both presynaptic and postsynaptic adjustment of corticostriatal transmission in response to elevated cortical activity.

Since neuronal and synaptic activity in the DLS is modulated by dopaminergic innervation from the SNc, we further assessed the effect of dopamine on astrocytic synapse engulfment in the striatum. To induce dopamine release in the striatum, an AAV expressing the hM3D(Gq) receptor under the control of a dopaminergic neuron-specific rhesus tyrosine hydroxylase promoter (rTH)54 was delivered to the SNc (Fig. 5g). When dopaminergic neurons in the SNc were activated through consecutive injections of CNO for three days (Supplementary Fig. 7b), we found that astrocytic engulfment of corticostriatal presynapses in the DLS significantly increased. However, this effect was substantially diminished in Megf10 cKO mice (Fig. 5h).

Dopaminergic neurons in the SNc have been shown to co-release glutamate55. Thus, we designed short hairpin RNAs (shRNAs) targeting Slc17a6 (VGLUT2), which mediates glutamate release from dopaminergic neurons56, to examine the potential contribution of glutamate co-release to striatal astrocytic phagocytosis. Slc17a6-targeting shRNAs were delivered to the SNc via AAV along with the chemogenetic receptor hM3D(Gq) (Supplementary Fig. 10a–d). After repeated activation of dopaminergic neurons, astrocytic phagocytosis of corticostriatal presynapses was still significantly increased in the Slc17a6 knockdown group, comparable to the control group (Supplementary Fig. 10e, f). These data suggest that glutamate co-release from dopaminergic neurons does not significantly contribute to astrocytic phagocytosis of corticostriatal synapses under our chemogenetic SNc manipulation.

Next, to determine whether striatal astrocytes directly sense dopamine in mediating DLS synapse elimination, we generated astrocyte-specific dopamine receptor-deleted mice (Aldh1l1-CreERT2; Drd1 fl/fl, Aldh1l1-CreERT2; Drd2 fl/fl) and measured astrocytic engulfment of corticostriatal presynapses following chemogenetic activation of dopamine release. Interestingly, after chemogenetic activation of SNc dopaminergic neurons, astrocytic engulfment of presynapses in the DLS increased normally in both Drd1 cKO and Drd2 cKO mice (Supplementary Fig. 7c–f). These results indicate that corticostriatal excitatory circuits and nigrostriatal dopaminergic circuits modulate striatal neuronal activity, subsequently influencing astrocytic engulfment of striatal synapses through MEGF10-dependent phagocytic machinery. Notably, we also found that chemogenetic activation of SNc dopaminergic neurons did not affect the total extent of astrocyte-mediated elimination of striatal postsynapses (Fig. 5i, j), which was unexpected given our previous results showing that both motor learning and chemogenetic activation of M1 neurons successfully increased astrocytic engulfment of striatal postsynapses (Fig. 1i).

Differential regulation of D1 and D2 MSN synapses by astrocytic phagocytosis

In the striatum, two distinct subpopulations of neurons, D1 and D2 MSNs, exist based on the types of dopamine receptors they express. Since these dopamine receptors are G-protein-coupled receptors (GPCRs) with distinct intracellular signaling pathways, increased dopamine levels activate D1 MSN activity while suppressing D2 MSN activity. Given their contrasting responses to dopamine, we hypothesized that glial engulfment of D1 and D2 MSN postsynapses might be differentially regulated when dopamine release is modulated.

To investigate potential differences between D1 and D2 MSN postsynapse elimination, we redesigned our synapse phagocytosis reporters to be expressed in a Cre-dependent manner and delivered them to Drd1a-Cre and Adora2a-Cre mice via AAV injection. Along with the phagocytosis reporter AAVs, DREADD AAVs were also injected into the SNc to either activate (hM3Dq) or silence (hM4Di) dopaminergic neurons (Fig. 6a). Surprisingly, we found that repeated activation of dopamine release into the DLS significantly reduced astrocytic engulfment of D1 MSN postsynapses (Fig. 6b and Supplementary Fig. 11a), whereas astrocytic phagocytosis of D2 MSN postsynapses was increased (Fig. 6c and Supplementary Fig. 11c). Consistent with our previous data, microglial engulfment of D1 and D2 MSN postsynapses remained unchanged after repeated dopamine release (Fig. 6b, c and Supplementary Fig. 11b, d). To further investigate the role of astrocytic MEGF10 in the elimination of striatal postsynapses, short hairpin RNA targeting Megf10 (shMegf10) was designed and delivered into the DLS (Fig. 6f). Astrocyte-specific expression of shMegf10 significantly reduced Megf10 transcripts and MEGF10 protein level in astrocytes, as confirmed by RNA in situ hybridization and immunohistochemistry (Supplementary Fig. 12). Importantly, we found that Megf10 knockdown strongly blocked the reduction of astrocytic engulfment of D1 MSN postsynapses following repeated dopamine release (Fig. 6g). In addition, Megf10 knockdown abolished the increase in astrocytic engulfment of D2 MSN postsynapses, returning it to basal levels (Fig. 6h).

Next, to determine whether motor learning also elicits differential regulation of astrocytic phagocytosis depending on MSN subtype, we directly measured glial phagocytosis of D1/D2 MSN postsynapses after the rotarod task. Interestingly, astrocytic phagocytosis of D1 MSN postsynapses was increased only during the early phase of learning, whereas that of D2 MSN postsynapses was elevated during the late phase (Supplementary Fig. 13). These findings suggest that the timing and pattern of striatal postsynaptic phagocytosis by astrocytes differ between D1- and D2 MSNs throughout motor learning.

We also performed quantal analyses of corticostriatal transmission in Drd1a-tdTomato reporter mice before and after rotarod training, substituting Ca²⁺ with Sr²⁺ to promote asynchronous release. In D1 MSNs, learning induced a robust increase in quantal frequency without a detectable change in quantal amplitude, indicating a selective presynaptic facilitation. In contrast, D2 MSNs exhibited increases in both quantal frequency and amplitude, reflecting coordinated presynaptic and postsynaptic adaptations (Supplementary Fig. 14). These results demonstrate that motor learning engages a common presynaptic strengthening at corticostriatal synapses across MSN subtypes, while driving additional postsynaptic potentiation selectively in D2 MSNs.

Taken together, these findings indicate an overall inverse correlation between MSN spine density and the extent of MSN postsynapse elimination by astrocytes following dopamine release. Based on these data, we propose the following model: Dopamine, in conjunction with the activation of corticostriatal circuits, induces distinct intracellular responses in D1 and D2 MSNs, leading to cell type-specific and temporally distinct potentiation or depression, as previously suggested. During this process, astrocytic MEGF10 facilitates the structural remodeling of MSN postsynapses by allowing the preservation of D1 MSN synapses in response to dopamine while actively eliminating weaker or thinner D2 MSN synapses that may arise from synaptic depression. This mechanism highlights the critical roles of astrocytic phagocytosis in dynamically shaping structural and functional plasticity in the striatum in response to dopamine signaling.

To investigate the functional effects of dopamine upregulation or downregulation on corticostriatal excitatory transmission in control and Megf10 cKO mice, we crossed Megf10 cKO mice with Drd1a-tdTomato mice, which selectively express a fluorescent protein, tdTomato, in D1 MSNs. Following the delivery of DREADDs via AAV injection, we activated excitatory and inhibitory DREADDs by injecting CNO for 3 consecutive days (Fig. 8a). Corticostriatal EPSCs were then recorded in striatal brain slices under conditions in which Ca2+ was replaced with Sr2+ in the bath solution to promote asynchronous neurotransmitter release, allowing for the analysis of quantal properties of these EPSCs. D1 MSNs and D2 MSNs were identified based on the presence or absence of tdTomato fluorescence, respectively. Their cell type was further validated through biocytin labeling followed by post hoc immunostaining (Fig. 8b). When dopamine release was upregulated by hM3Dq activation, quantal frequency at corticostriatal synapses of both D1 and D2 MSNs increased in the control group. Notably, quantal amplitude decreased exclusively in D1 MSNs, suggesting a dynamic postsynaptic adjustment to maintain synaptic homeostasis during dopamine-driven synaptic remodeling (Fig. 8c). Importantly, these MSN type-dependent alterations in quantal properties were completely abolished in Megf10 cKO mice (Fig. 8d).

Next, we suppressed dopamine release via hM4Di activation to examine the role of astrocytic synapse engulfment under dopamine-depleted conditions. In both control and Megf10 cKO mice, suppression of dopamine release via hM4Di did not alter quantal amplitudes as well as quantal frequencies at striatopallidal synapses of D1 MSNs. Interestingly, D2 MSNs in both control and Megf10 cKO mice exhibited a selective increase in quantal frequencies in response to reduced dopamine release (Fig. 8e, f). This increase in quantal frequency of D2 MSNs likely reflects abnormal strengthening of the indirect pathway, which does not involve astrocytic MEGF10 function. Furthermore, our findings align with previous reports demonstrating enhanced excitatory drive onto D2 MSNs under dopamine-depleted conditions60,61. Collectively, these results demonstrate that under conditions of increased dopamine release in the striatum, MEGF10-mediated astrocytic synapse engulfment plays a critical role in maintaining synaptic homeostasis at corticostriatal excitatory synapses. However, regardless of MSN subtype, MEGF10-dependent astrocytic phagocytosis appears to be dispensable for the functional adaptations of corticostriatal excitatory synapses when dopamine release is suppressed.

Discussion

In this study, we demonstrated that astrocytes play a key role in synapse engulfment in the DLS during motor learning. By taking advantage of the mCherry-eGFP reporter system, we were able to monitor active astrocytic phagocytosis of both corticostriatal presynapses and MSN postsynapses throughout motor skill acquisition. Our data show that this process is primarily governed by astrocytic MEGF10 rather than MERTK, and that it plays a critical role in maintaining synaptic transmission and plasticity through the selective elimination of DLS synapses. Throughout our research, microglia and OPCs did not appear to participate in synapse elimination during motor learning or chemogenetic modulation of striatal activity. We speculate that, in the adult brain under homeostatic conditions, where astrocytes form strong physical associations with synapses, astrocytes may have a strong advantage in engulfing synapses over other glial cells in response to changes in neuronal activity. A recent study also suggested that astrocytic processes in contact with synapses must first detach from them to allow microglial processes to access and eliminate those synapses62,63. Taken together, these findings suggest that learning-based synapse remodeling is preferentially controlled by astrocytic MEGF10, emphasizing the context-dependent roles of various glial cells and their phagocytic receptors in eliminating synapses.

Unexpectedly, our electrophysiological recordings demonstrated that loss of MEGF10 in astrocytes significantly reduced corticostriatal synaptic transmission, as evidenced by a decreased I/O relationship and a reduction in the AMPA/NMDA ratio. Given that astrocytes continuously eliminate MSN postsynaptic elements through MEGF10, this residual phagocytic function of MEGF10 appears to be essential for setting up basal synaptic strength in MSNs within the striatum. Importantly, Megf10 cKO mice further exhibited impaired LTP/LTD at corticostriatal synapses and attenuated enhancement of synaptic strength observed during motor training, indicating that astrocytic phagocytosis of corticostriatal synapses via MEGF10 is a crucial process for boosting functional synaptic plasticity during motor learning.

By modulating striatal activity through DREADD receptor expression in corticostriatal excitatory circuits, we found that activating cortical neurons in M1 increased astrocytic elimination of corticostriatal presynapses as well as MSN postsynapses. This finding is consistent with our prior research, demonstrating that an increase in neural activity generally enhances overall synapse remodeling processes, including synapse elimination, which is critical for subsequent synapse formation and plasticity processes.

Intriguingly, we found that chemogenetic activation of the dopaminergic neurons in the SNc significantly induced astrocytic engulfment of corticostriatal presynapses while differentially affecting the elimination of D1 and D2 MSN postsynapses. Consistent with the previous findings that dopamine dichotomously modulates MSN activity by either potentiating or depressing D1 versus D2 MSNs, our data show that the mechanisms and regulation of synapse elimination in D1 and D2 MSNs in response to dopamine release are fundamentally different. For D1 MSN postsynapses, elevated dopamine release significantly increased the population of stubby synapses, indicating that dopamine enhances the stability of synapses and synaptic transmission in D1 MSNs. As a potential mechanism, the overall activation of D1 MSN postsynapses by dopamine may reduce the number of synapses targeted for elimination by astrocytes, as evident by the decreased D1 MSN postsynapse elimination by astrocytes after boosting dopamine release. Interestingly, in Megf10 knockdown mice, the elimination of D1 MSN postsynapses paradoxically increased to WT levels. This discrepancy might also be explained by our data showing that both synaptic transmission and LTP at corticostriatal synapses are severely impaired in Megf10 cKO mice. In this case, D1 MSNs in Megf10 knockdown mice likely fail to achieve sufficient activation even in response to dopamine release, leaving more synapses vulnerable to elimination by remaining phagocytosis receptors in astrocytes. In contrast, for D2 MSNs, the enhancement of dopamine release significantly decreases the density of thin spines. Since astrocytic elimination of D2 MSN postsynapses was significantly increased, these two events may be directly related, such that dopamine-induced elimination of depressed D2 MSN postsynapses by astrocytes likely resulted in a reduced density of thin spines in D2 MSNs. In both cases, the extent of synapse elimination and changes in synaptic transmission were substantially affected by Megf10 knockdown, indicating the structural and functional plasticity of both D1 and D2 MSNs requires MEGF10-dependent astrocytic phagocytic function.

As mentioned earlier, our previous work has shown that synapse elimination can serve as a prerequisite for subsequent synapse formation and plasticity. In the hippocampal CA1 region, deletion of astrocytic phagocytic function leads to the accumulation of functionally weak synapses, accompanied by impaired synaptic plasticity34. Because both spatial and molecular resources for synapses are limited within a given circuit, the failure to eliminate weak synapses likely hinders new synapse formation and potentiation64,65. During motor learning, previous studies have reported that synapse formation and elimination occur simultaneously, collectively contributing to effective learning and memory formation14,66,67. Our finding that Megf10 cKO mice fail to elicit synaptic plasticity in response to motor learning, corticostriatal activation, or increased dopamine release together indicates that astrocytic phagocytosis of striatal synapses also serves as a critical prerequisite for normal synapse formation and plasticity.

Although our study has revealed roles for astrocytic MEGF10 in controlling synaptic transmission and plasticity in the striatum, many questions arise from our findings. First, how exactly do astrocytes determine which synapses to engulf? Our data indicate that neuronal activity is required to initiate synapse remodeling and elimination, potentially by segregating synaptic populations undergoing activity-dependent potentiation versus depression. Synapses that fail to be activated by either presynaptic or postsynaptic stimulation may progressively undergo LTD, leading to changes in synaptic membrane-bound signals. These changes may be recognized by astrocytic MEGF10, triggering subsequent MEGF10-dependent synapse engulfment. It is important to note that astrocytic MERTK does not appear to play a significant role in striatal synapse elimination during motor learning. Astrocytic MERTK receptor recognizes phosphatidylserine (PS) as an “eat-me” signal through bridging molecules, such as Gas6 and Pros1. Thus, it is plausible that MEGF10 may recognize different, yet unidentified, ligands that are preferentially exposed at depressed synapses during learning-based synapse remodeling. Identifying the ligand of MEGF10 will shed new light on the core mechanisms of astrocytic recognition and targeting of specific synapses for elimination. Second, as a way of modulating striatal activity, we separately controlled corticostriatal and nigrostriatal pathways using DREADD receptors. Although our approach allowed us to uncover the differential regulation of striatal synapse remodeling by these two pathways, it remains unclear how corticostriatal and nigrostriatal inputs are integrated with spatiotemporal regulation to modulate MEGF10-dependent synapse remodeling. Furthermore, previous studies have shown that striatal MSN activity can be influenced by multiple neuromodulators, such as adenosine and endocannabinoids68–70. Therefore, it would be interesting to determine how D1 and D2 MSNs differentially respond to various combinations of these neuromodulators during motor learning and how the resulting changes in MSN activity contribute to astrocyte-mediated synapse remodeling. Finally, our findings suggest that astrocytic phagocytosis may play a critical role in abnormal synaptic remodeling and pathology in PD. Although our experiments using hM4Di to suppress dopaminergic neuronal activity did not show preferential engulfment of D1 versus D2 MSN excitatory synapses by astrocytes, the duration of CNO treatment was limited to 3 days and resulted in only partial inhibition of dopamine release. Therefore, future studies should investigate whether astrocytic phagocytosis contributes to abnormal synapse remodeling and corresponding PD-like behavioral symptoms when dopamine release is chronically suppressed, mimicking the conditions observed in PD patients.

Methods

Animals

Aldh1l1-CreERT2 mice (FVB-Tg(Aldh1l1-cre/ERT2)1Khakh/J), Drd1 floxed mice (Drd1tm2.1Stl/J), and Drd2 floxed mice (B6.129S4(FVB)-Drd2tm1.1Mrub/J) were purchased from the Jackson Laboratory. Megf10 floxed (Megf10tm1(KOMP)Vlcg) mice and Mertk floxed (B6;129-Mertktm1Grl/J) mice were obtained from the Barres laboratory. Drd1a-Cre mice (Tg(Drd1a-cre)EY262Gsat/Mmcd) and Adora2a-Cre mice (B6.FVB(Cg)-Tg(Adora2a-cre)KG139Gsat/Mmcd) were gifted by Min Whan Jung’s lab (KAIST) and Jae-Ick Kim’s lab (UNIST), respectively. To generate astrocyte-specific Megf10, Mertk, Drd1, and Drd2 cKO mice, Aldh1l1-CreERT2 mice were crossed with Megf10 floxed, Mertk floxed, Drd1 floxed, and Drd2 floxed mice, respectively. Experiments were performed using male and female mice between 6 and 12 weeks of age. Detailed information on sex and age is provided in the corresponding Methods sections. All experiments in this study were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of Korea Advanced Institute of Science and Technology (KAIST) or Institute for Basic Science (IBS).

Viral vector generation

To generate pAAV-mDlx-PSD95.FingR-mCherry-eGFP-CCR5TC construct, PSD95.FingR and eGFP-CCR5TC construct from pCAG-PSD95.FingR-eGFP-CCR5TC (Addgene #46295) were amplified by PCR and subcloned into pAAV-mDlx-GFP-Fishell-1 (Addgene #83900). To generate pAAV-mDlx-DIO-PSD95.FingR-mCherry-eGFP-CCR5TC construct, PSD95.FingR-mCherry-eGFP construct from pAAV-mDlx-PSD95.FingR-mCherry-eGFP-CCR5TC and DIO construct from pAAV-EF1a-DIO-EYFP (Addgene #27056) were amplified by PCR, and both were subcloned into pAAV-mDlx-GFP-Fishell-1. To generate the pAAV-GfaABC1D-GFP construct, the eGFP construct from pAAV-CAG-GFP (Addgene #28014) were amplified by PCR and subcloned into pAAV-GfaABC1D-Cre (Addgene #1056063). To generate pAAV-rTH-HA-hM3Dq and pAAV-rTH-HA-hM4Di constructs, HA-hM3Dq and HA-hM4Di construct from pAAV-GFAP-HA-hM3D(Gq)-IRES-mCitrine (Addgene #50470) and pAAV-GFAP-HA-hM4D(Gi)-IRES-mCitrine (Addgene #50471), respectively, were amplified by PCR and subcloned into pAAV-rTH-PI-Cre-SV40 (Addgene #107788). To generate the pAAV-hSyn-HA-hM3Dq construct, HA-hM3Dq construct from pAAV-GFAP-HA-hM3D(Gq)-IRES-mCitrine were amplified by PCR and subcloned into pAAV-hSyn-hM3D(Gq)-mCherry (Addgene #50474). To generate pAAV-GfaABC1D-tagBFP-miR30a-shMegf10 and pAAV-GfaABC1D-tagBFP-miR30a-scramble construct, 22-mer for targeting shMegf10 (tgaatcttaaaaatgtgaatcc, gttattacagaacctacagtga) were designed as well as control sequence (cattgctggcacgaagattgac, gtagcagagcaccgtttacatg).

Adeno-associated virus (AAV) production

AAVs were produced as previously described71,72. First, the AAV2 ITR flaked target construct plasmid was cotransfected into cultured HEK293T cells along with capsid (pAAV9) and helper (pAdDeltaF6) plasmids through the PEI transfection method. Transfected cells were cultured (37 °C, 5% CO2) and collected 72 h after transfection. Harvested cells were lysed by a freeze-thaw cycle, and AAVs were collected by aqueous two-phase partitioning of polyethylene glycol and ammonium sulfate. Purified AAVs were concentrated after using an ultracentrifugal filter (50 kDa MWCO, Millipore). Finally, titers of concentrated AAVs were measured by qPCR with AAV2 ITR-targeting primers.

Virus injection

AAVs were delivered as previously described73. In brief, male and female mice aged 6–8 weeks were anesthetized with isoflurane and maintained during the stereotaxic surgery session. Concentration of isoflurane was controlled by a veterinary vaporizer (3% induction and 1% maintenance, SurgiVet), connected with a stereotaxic apparatus (KOPF Instruments). AAV9::hSyn-Synaptophysin1-mCherry-eGFP and AAV9::hSyn-HA-hM3Dq were injected into the primary motor cortex (AP: +0.9, ML: −1.5, DV: −0.8). AAV9::mDlx-PSD95.FingR-mCherry-eGFP, AAV9::mDlx-DIO-PSD95.FingR-mCherry-eGFP, AAV9::GfaABC1D-Cre/GFP, AAV9::GfaABC1D-tagBFP-miR30a-shMegf10/scramble, and AAV9::mDlx-DIO-eGFP were injected into the dorsolateral striatum (AP: 0.7, ML: −2.2, DV: −2.5). AAV9::rTH-HA-hM3Dq and AAV9::rTH-HA-hM4Di were injected into the substantia nigra pars compacta (AP: −3.1, ML: −1.3, DV: −3.9). 0.2 μL of AAVs were delivered to every target region at a speed of 0.05 μl/min controlled by a motorized syringe pump (Harvard Apparatus) connected with glass pipettes (WPI). After infusion, the surgical site was sealed with silk sutures, and the mice were fully recovered in a heated cage.

Clozapine N-oxide (CNO) injection

CNO (Sigma-Aldrich) was reconstituted in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO, LPS solution) and diluted in Dulbecco’s phosphate-buffered saline (DPBS, Welegene). The CNO solution was intraperitoneally delivered to the male and female mice aged 9–11 weeks at a dose of 0.5 mg/kg (hM3Dq) or 2 mg/kg (hM4Di) and injected three times at an interval of 24 h.

Accelerated rotarod task

Rota-rod (Med-associates) was used in this study. Male mice aged 9–11 weeks were placed on the rod (3 cm in diameter) at 30 cm high. Rod was motorized and linearly accelerated up to 80 rpm for 150 s, and the time latency for the mice to fall off was recorded for each trial. There was a 120-s break between each trial, and the motor performance of a session was measured by averaging the time latency of ten trials. Motor training sessions were done for 5 consecutive days. In the control session, mice were placed on the rod rotating in 4 for 10 min. Every mouse after the session were placed back to their home cage.

Forelimb-reaching task

Male mice aged 9–11 weeks were food-restricted to maintain their body weight to 90% of their natural weight for 48 h from the start of training. Traning chamber (85 mm × 150 mm × 200 mm) was made of transparent acryl at its front wall and other of non-transparent. One vertical slide (5 mm) was placed at the front wall so that mice can reach their forelimbs to the millet. Every single reach was recorded as a trial, and each trial was categorized into success, drop, or fail. Success trials were scored when mice successfully reached and grabbed the millet, followed by direct retrieval to their mouth. Drop trials were scored when mice successfully reached and grabbed the millet, but dropped during retrieval. Fail trials were scored when mice tried reaching, but failed to grab the millet. Each daily session was consisted of 20 trials within 15 min, and the session was closed when 20 trials or 15 min were over. Control mice were placed in the same test chamber with 20 millets for 15 min. Every mouse were placed back to their home cage after session.

Immunohistochemistry

Animals were anesthetized and perfused with DPBS followed by 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA, Bio-solution). After fixation, brains were obtained and postfixated overnight (4% PFA, 4 °C), followed by incubation in 30% sucrose (Sigma-Aldrich) in DPBS for at least 48 h. After, the brain was embedded in an OCT compound (Leica) and cryo-sectioned into 30 μm slices. Brain slices were incubated in blocking solution (4% bovine serum albumin, GoldBio; 0.3% Triton X-100, Sigma-Aldrich; in DPBS) for an hour at room temperature, followed by incubation in primary antibody solution (48 h, 4 °C). Primary antibodies were appropriately diluted in blocking solution (rabbit anti-S100β (1:500), Abcam #ab52642; guinea pig anti-S100β (1:500), Synaptic Systems #287004; rabbit anti-IBA1 (1:500), WAKO #019-19741; rabbit anti-tRFP (1:500), Evrogen #AB233; rat anti-mCherry (1:500), Invitrogen #M11217; chicken anti-GFP (1:2000), Aves Labs #GFP-1020; rabbit anti-c-Fos (1:500), Cell signaling technology #2250S; rabbit anti-HA (1:500), Cell signaling technology #3724; chicken anti-HA (1:500), Sigma-Aldrich #AB3254; sheep anti-TH (1:500), Sigma-Aldrich #AB1542; rabbit anti-TH (1:500), Sigma-Aldrich #AB152). Brain slices were washed with PBST (0.1% Tween-20, Sigma-Aldrich) and incubated in secondary antibody solution (3 h, RT). Secondary antibodies were appropriately diluted in PBST (Donkey anti-rabbit IgG (H + L) Alexa Fluor 405 (1:400), Abcam # Ab175649; Donkey anti-guinea pig IgG (H + L) DyLight 405 (1:500), Jackson Laboratory #706-475-148; Donkey anti-chicken IgY (IgG) (H + L) Alexa Fluor 488 (1:1000), Jackson Laboratory #703-545-155; Donkey anti-rabbit IgG (H + L) Alexa Fluor 488 (1:1000), Invitrogen #A-21206; Donkey anti-rat IgG (H + L) Alexa Fluor 594 (1:1000), Invitrogen #A-21209; Donkey anti-rabbit IgG (H + L) Alexa Fluor 594 (1:1000), Invitrogen #A-21207; Donkey anti-sheep IgG (H + L) Alexa Flour 594 (1:1000), abcam #ab150180). Brain slices were washed with PBST and mounted on a slide glass. Samples were covered with cover glass and mounting media (Vector Labs).

Fluorescence RNA in situ hybridization

Fluorescence RNA in situ hybridization was performed under the guide (RNAscope Multiplex Fluorescent Detection Kit v2, ACD bio #323110). Target mRNAs were detected by appropriate RNA probes (Slc1a3, ACD bio #430781-C1; Megf10, ACD bio #406641-C2; Tagbfp, ACD bio #539631-C3), and fluorescence dyes were conjugated (Opal reagent kit, Ayoka biosciences, #AKFP1487001KT, #AKFP1488001KT, #AKFP1496001KT). Samples were covered with cover glass and mounting media (Vector Labs). The expression level of Megf10 in astrocytes was measured by detecting Megf10 mRNA puncta in each Slc1a3-positive or Slc1a3 and Tagbfp-positive cell.

Confocal imaging

Images were obtained using an upright confocal microscope (LSM880, Zeiss). For imaging, appropriate lasers (405, 488, 594, and 633 nm) were selected, and the laser power and detector gain were adjusted to keep saturated signals below 1% of total signal intensity. A 40x oil immersion lens (NA = 1.4) was selected to obtain high image resolution for further analysis and quantification. To image dendritic spines in MSNs (Fig. 7), the Airyscan mode was used to increase image resolution. Every z-stack image was imaged as ten slices in 500 nm intervals for appropriate z-resolution. Acquired confocal z-stack images were further processed and analyzed using third-party software and associated plugins (Python, Fiji).

Glial engulfment analysis

Synapse engulfment by glial cells was analyzed as previously described34. In brief, to obtain mCherry-only signals from the confocal images, the signals in the eGFP channel were subtracted from the signals in the mCherry channel, to obtain mCherry-only signals. The colocalization of the mCherry-only image and the phagocytosing glia (S100B, IBA1) image channel were measured by using the distance analysis plugin (DiAna) in Fiji74.

Dendritic spine density analysis

Dendritic spine morphology and density were analyzed under the specific criteria previously described58. Each dendritic spine from the confocal images was classified by its head width and neck length. Dendritic spines with their head width over 0.6 microns were classified as mushroom spines. Among others, dendritic spines having the length-to-width ratio less than 1 were classified as stubby spines, and spines with their length less than 2 microns were classified as thin spines. Dendritic spine density was further measured as the number of dendritic spines per the length of dendrites.

Brain slice preparation for electrophysiology

Horizontal brain slices (300-μm thick) of both male and female mice, including the M1 cortex and dorsolateral striatum, were prepared for whole-cell patch-clamp recordings. Mice were anesthetized with isoflurane (Piramal Critical Care), decapitated, and their brains were rapidly removed and briefly immersed in cold ACSF containing 125 mM NaCl, 2.5 mM KCl, 1.25 mM NaH2PO4, 25 mM NaHCO3, 1 mM MgCl2, 2 mM CaCl2, and 15 mM glucose, oxygenated with 95% O2 and 5% CO2. Acute slices were prepared using a tissue vibratome (Leica, VT1200S) in ice-cold ACSF. Following sectioning, brain slices were initially incubated in ACSF for 20 min at 34 °C, then maintained at room temperature for an additional 30 min. After the recovery period, slices were transferred to a submerged recording chamber continuously perfused with ACSF at a flow rate of 2–3 ml/min, maintained at 30–31 °C. Electrophysiological recordings were conducted within 5 h after slice preparation.

Electrophysiology

Medium spiny neurons (MSNs) in the dorsolateral striatum were visually identified by using infrared differential interference contrast (IR-DIC) optics on an upright microscope (BX51WI, Olympus). Whole-cell voltage-clamp recordings were obtained with borosilicate glass pipettes (3.5–5.5 MΩ) filled with Cs+-based low Cl- internal solution containing 135 mM CsMeSO3, 10 mM HEPES, 1 mM EGTA, 3.3 mM QX-314, 0.1 mM CaCl2, 4 mM Mg-ATP, 0.3 mM Na3-GTP, 8 mM Na2-phosphocreatine (290–300 mOsm, pH 7.3 with CsOH). For LTP and LTD experiments, whole-cell recordings were performed with borosilicate glass pipettes (3.5–5.5 MΩ) filled with a K⁺-based internal solution containing 122 mM K-gluconate, 13 mM KCl, 10 mM HEPES, 0.3 mM EGTA, 10 mM Na₂-phosphocreatine, 4 mM Mg-ATP, and 0.3 mM Na₃-GTP (290–300 mOsm, pH adjusted to 7.35 with KOH). Excitatory synaptic currents evoked from the corticostriatal pathway were recorded under voltage-clamp conditions (liquid junction potential corrected), with the membrane potential held at −70 mV. Access resistance was maintained between 10 and 20 MΩ, and recordings were included in the analysis only if access resistance varied by less than 20% throughout the experiment. Whole-cell recordings were performed using a Multiclamp 700B amplifier (Molecular Devices). Signals were low-pass filtered at 2 kHz and digitized at 10 kHz using an NI PCIe-6259 data acquisition board (National Instruments). Data acquisition and online monitoring were conducted with WinWCP software (Strathclyde, http://spider.science.strath.ac.uk/sipbs/software_ses.htm), and offline analyses were performed using Clampfit 10.7 (Molecular Devices) and OriginPro 2017 (OriginLab). Quantal amplitude and frequency of EPSCs induced by asynchronous release were analyzed using Mini Analysis software (Minis). To ensure analytical accuracy, all detected events were manually inspected and validated. Corticostriatal axons were stimulated using a bipolar concentric electrode (CBAPB75, FHC) positioned on the external capsule at the interface between the M1 and the dorsolateral striatum. To measure AMPA/NMDA receptor ratios, MSNs were held at +40 mV to relieve the Mg²⁺ block of NMDA receptors, and evoked synaptic responses were recorded1. Dual-component EPSCs were recorded at +40 mV both in the absence and presence of D-AP5 (50 μM). The AMPA receptor-mediated component, measured in the presence of D-AP5, was subtracted from the composite EPSC recorded under control conditions to isolate the NMDA receptor-mediated current. Isolated NMDA receptor-mediated EPSCs were then averaged, and the AMPA/NMDA ratio was calculated as the ratio of the peak amplitude of the AMPAR-mediated EPSC to that of the NMDAR-mediated EPSC.

Spike-timing-dependent plasticity protocols

STDP experiments were conducted in the presence of 50 μM picrotoxin to block GABAA receptor-mediated inhibition. Electrical stimulation intensity was adjusted to evoke 100–300 pA EPSCs, with control stimuli delivered at 0.1 Hz. STDP protocols consisted of 100 pairings of pre- and postsynaptic stimulations at 1 Hz with a fixed Δt (Δt <10 ms), where presynaptic stimulation preceded postsynaptic activation45. Cortical inputs were stimulated presynaptically, while postsynaptic action potentials (three per 30 ms depolarizing step) were evoked in MSNs. Recordings included a 10-minute baseline period followed by at least 60 min of post-STDP monitoring. Long-term synaptic potentiation was assessed in the final 10 min of the recording. Data were excluded if access resistance varied by more than 25% during the experiment.

High-frequency stimulation-induced LTP protocols

High-frequency stimulation (HFS) consisted of four trains of cortical stimuli at 100 Hz for 1 s, with a 0.1 Hz interval between trains43,44. During each train, the postsynaptic MSN was depolarized from its holding membrane potential (−70 mV) to +10 mV, coinciding with presynaptic stimulation.

Low-frequency stimulation-induced LTD protocols

Low-frequency stimulation (LFS) consisted of 600 cortical stimuli delivered at 1 Hz43. During the stimulation, the postsynaptic MSN was depolarized from its holding membrane potential (–70 mV) to +10 mV, coinciding with presynaptic stimulation.

Biocytin labeling and immunostaining of biocytin-filled neurons

To identify the cell type of the recorded MSNs, a Cs-based internal solution supplemented with 0.2% biocytin (B4261, Sigma-Aldrich) was loaded into MSNs for 20 min. Following gentle withdrawal of the glass pipette, brain slices were fixed overnight at 4 °C in 4% PFA. On the following day, slices were washed with PBS and PBST for 1 h, and then incubated in a blocking solution containing 10% normal goat serum (NGS) and 2% bovine serum albumin (BSA) for 4 h at room temperature. Blocked sections were subsequently incubated with an anti-RFP antibody (guinea pig polyclonal, 1:1000, 390 005, Synaptic Systems) diluted in blocking solution at 4 °C for 3 days. After primary antibody incubation, slices were incubated with Alexa Fluor 594-conjugated secondary antibodies (goat anti-guinea pig, 1:1000, Invitrogen), together with Alexa Fluor 405-conjugated streptavidin (1:500, Invitrogen), both diluted in blocking solution at 4 °C for 2 days. Slices were then extensively washed with PBST and PBS for 12 h, and the slices were mounted onto slides using a mounting medium (P36934, Invitrogen). Fluorescent images were acquired using an LSM880 multiphoton confocal laser scanning microscope (Carl Zeiss), and cell type identification was performed using Zen imaging software (Carl Zeiss).

Fast-scan cyclic voltammetry (FSCV)

Extracellular dopamine release was measured using FSCV with carbon-fiber microelectrodes (7 μm diameter carbon fiber, with 50–100 μm of the tip exposed beyond the tapered glass seal). Recordings were conducted in the dorsolateral striatum within coronal brain slices. A triangular scanning waveform was applied to the carbon-fiber electrode, sweeping from −0.4 V to +1.3 V and back (relative to an AgCl reference electrode) over 8.5 msec at a scan rate of 400 V/sec, repeated at 10 Hz. Between scans, the electrode was held at −0.4 V. Background subtraction was performed by averaging ten baseline scans. Dopamine release was evoked by a bipolar concentric electrode (CBAPB75, FHC), positioned near the carbon-fiber electrode on the slice surface. Dopamine release was induced with a 2 msec electrical pulse (single stimulation) controlled by a DS-3 stimulus isolator (Digitimer, UK). Dopamine release was evoked by five consecutive stimuli as follows: the first stimulus was applied in the absence of CNO, the second stimulus was delivered 7 min after initiating CNO bath application, and the third, fourth, and fifth stimuli were administered at 3-min intervals during continued CNO application. The peak amplitude of dopamine release evoked by the first stimulus was normalized to 100%. Changes in dopamine release over time were calculated relative to this baseline to evaluate CNO-induced effects. The oxidation current generated by dopamine release was recorded and monitored using the TarHeel CV system. Dopamine concentrations were quantified by plotting the peak oxidation current of voltammograms over time. Calibration was performed at the end of each experiment by converting oxidation current to dopamine concentration using a standard dopamine solution (10 μM, Tocris, 3548) in ACSF. For FSCV experiments, CNO (Sigma, C0832) was prepared as a stock solution (20 mM) in DMSO and subsequently diluted into ACSF to a final concentration of 1 μM for application to striatal brain slices.

Statistics

All data were analyzed using the following softwares: Clampfit 10.7 (Molecular Devices), Mini Analysis (dervinism, Minis, GitHub75, available at https://github.com/dervinism/minis), OriginPro 2017 (OriginLab), Zen software (version 2.5, Carl Zeiss), and MATLAB (version R2024a, MathWorks). Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism (version 10, GraphPad software). Summary statistics were presented as mean ± SEM. Unpaired student t-tests, repeated measures two-way ANOVA with Holm–Sidak’s post hoc multiple comparison test were employed to assess statistical differences (two-sided) between the groups. A P value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001, ****p < 0.0001.
