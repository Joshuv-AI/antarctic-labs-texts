Radiation Therapy Without Anesthesia for a 7-Month-Old Child Using Audio-Visual Assisted Therapeutic Ambience in Radiation Therapy (AVATAR)
Authors: Andrada Turcas, Agnes Ewongwo, Lawrie Skinner, Oana Diaconu, Vlad Coltea, Andrada Marosan, Larisa Gheorghica, Anca Vancea, Crina Aczberger, Elena Savu, Emilia Mihut, Jonathan Muddle, Billy W. Loo, Larry Frye, Sarah S. Donaldson, Susan M. Hiniker
Published: 2026

Introduction

Radiation therapy (RT) plays a crucial role in the treatment of many childhood cancers.1,2 Precise delivery of RT is important for minimizing dose to critical structures and reducing late toxicity.3,4 However, young children often struggle with the need to remain still in the treatment room for sessions lasting up to 45 minutes, leading to frequent use of daily anesthesia over the treatment course.5,6 Though anesthesia utilization rates vary, up to 50% of children aged 7 to 8 are reported to require anesthesia during treatment, with rates potentially higher in limited settings.7

Daily anesthesia is resource-intensive, expensive, and confers an increased risk of potential short- and long-term toxicities, including infection, cognitive effects, and anxiety.8, 9, 10 Each treatment session demands considerable health care resources, including specialized personnel, fasting protocols, postprocedural recovery time, and infrastructure capacity. In resource-limited centers, challenges are further compounded by equipment shortages and limited availability of trained staff, all of which increase costs and lead to treatment delays.11, 12, 13 As such, there is an urgent need for accessible, sedation-free alternatives that enhance cooperation and reduce anxiety.

The Audio-Visual Therapeutic Ambience in RT (AVATAR) system, which streams video content during RT, offers a promising nonpharmacological alternative to daily anesthesia.9,14 We report the case of a 7-month-old who successfully completed RT without anesthesia using the AVATAR system. This case highlights the feasibility and effectiveness of nonpharmacological behavioral support techniques, even in very young children, and underscores the value of AVATAR as a potential standard adjunct for pediatric RT administration, particularly in low-resource settings.

Methods and Materials

A 7-month-old patient with stage III Wilms tumor of the right kidney was referred for RT at a comprehensive cancer center in Romania. Diagnosed at 4 months, he underwent emergency resection of a 15 cm intraperitoneal tumor with perioperative tumor rupture. Surgical pathology confirmed a mixed nephroblastoma with no anaplasia. He received postoperative chemotherapy per the International Society of Pediatric oncology-Renal Tumor Study Group (SIOP-Umbrella) protocol15 and was referred for whole abdominal RT due to tumor rupture.

Per institutional practice, all pediatric patients undergo a trial of sedation-free simulation. Due to the patient’s age, low weight, and limited resources, anesthesia was not feasible for this patient. Therefore, he would have been unable to receive RT if he had not been able to tolerate treatment awake. Computed tomography (CT) simulation was performed supine with pelvic-abdominal immobilization (Fig. 1). He received 11.25 Gy in 9 daily fractions (Equivalent dose in 2 Gray fractions 10.55 Gy, α/β =10) using a 2-arc 6 MV volumetric modulated arc therapy technique, with age-adjusted organ sparing (the remaining kidney, liver, and vertebral bodies).16,17 Treatment was delivered using a C-arm linear accelerator, with daily cone beam CT for setup verification. The treatment was well tolerated, with a mild increase in hepatic enzymes that normalized after administration of a hepatoprotective drug. The AVATAR design includes a pico projector mounted cephalad to the patient’s head using a custom table mount (Fig. 2). Video is projected forward or backward onto the custom, thin, low-atomic-number screen suspended above the patient, supported by a thermomolded plastic and carbon fiber structure. Anxiety was assessed at the simulation first, and at final fractions using the modified Yale Preoperative Anxiety Scale.18,19 Treatment duration (from vault entry to exit) was extracted from the planning system (Eclipse/Aria; Varian Medical Systems). For illustrative purposes, we evaluated a matched 4-year-old patient with Wilms tumor treated with whole abdominal RT under anesthesia using identical RT techniques and doses. Overall treatment times, including anesthesia induction, RT setup, and delivery time, were compared and analyzed descriptively. Written parental consent was obtained. Institutional board review was not required for this report.

Results

During the simulation, the infant was well-rested and fed, with adequate time since his last meal to minimize the risk of regurgitation. The child was calm and curious, allowing for easy immobilization. Mild discomfort was noted during placement of the warm thermoplastic mask over his chest and abdomen, but this was short-lived. He remained calm throughout the CT scan and marking process.

Before the first treatment, the infant appeared agitated and anxious, refusing to leave his mother’s arms to be positioned on the treatment table. He became visibly distressed during undressing, thereby signaling the need to use the AVATAR system. On hearing the audio and viewing the cartoon visuals, he calmed down, stopped crying, and directed his full attention to the audiovisual display. This allowed positioning and immobilization to continue without resistance. He remained still and quiet throughout the treatment, unaffected by staff activity or his mother leaving the treatment room.

Across subsequent fractions, the infant’s anxiety progressively decreased, often smiling and giggling while interacting with staff (Fig. 3). By the final fraction, he smiled and babbled throughout preparation and treatment, tolerating separation from his mother and mask placement without distress. The median treatment time per fraction was 21 minutes (IQR, 14). Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the youngest reported patient – 7 months old – to complete RT without anesthesia using AVATAR. Previously, Prasad et al20 documented AVATAR’s use in a 2-year-old, showing reduced anxiety and improvement in treatment efficiency. This case highlights the potential for sedation-free RT, even in infants, under appropriate conditions and with effective distraction tools. Avoiding daily anesthesia is especially meaningful in this age group, given the complex and high-risk nature of daily and extended anesthesia use in children under 3 years of age.21, 22, 23, 24 Anesthesia also dramatically increases resource demands – requiring fasting, specialized staff, extended recovery, and monitoring – placing added strain on health care systems, particularly in resource-limited settings.21,25,26

Romania, an Eastern European country with a population of 19 million, has approximately 709,000 children under the age of 15. Each year, around 400 pediatric cancer cases are diagnosed, with a 5-year survival rate of 69%.27, 28, 29 Of the country’s 39 RT departments—mostly private outpatient clinics—few treat children, and only 1 national center provides sedation for pediatric RT. Therefore, access to pediatric RT, particularly with sedation or anesthesia, is highly centralized and limited.30,31 As a result, our institution serves as the main referral center for patients requiring anesthesia and/or sedation for RT, potentially causing treatment delays and posing serious equity and accessibility challenges for families, especially those living far from the center.

Since launching anesthesia services for RT in March 2023, 96 children have received RT at our center: 31 with sedation, 43 awake, and 20 transitioned from sedation to awake using distraction techniques (eg, music, cartoons, and parental storytelling). The pediatric RT team follows a specific coaching workflow that begins with an introduction to the department, staff, and treatment machines. At the first visit, the feasibility of awake treatment is assessed, and patient preferences for audiovisual support are discussed with the parent and patient. Simulation is always attempted without sedation to evaluate tolerance. For patients aged >3 or 4 years, virtual reality glasses provide a tour of the department, guided by a friendly mascot, to ease anxiety before in-person visits. If the awake trial fails, anesthesia is used for simulation and treatment, except for children aged <1 year or weighing <10 kg, for whom anesthesia is typically not feasible due to infrastructure limitations. For these very young patients, the same structured workflow is applied, but it is tailored to their developmental level and tolerance. These patients are coached by the treating team to undergo awake treatment; if unsuccessful, RT is not used.

This case illustrates a low-risk, cost-effective alternative strategy that can improve patient experience and reduce burden on health care systems. By capturing the child’s attention with familiar and comforting stimuli, the AVATAR system fostered a calm, cooperative environment during RT. While not a substitute for anesthesia in all cases, particularly in children with severe anxiety or prolonged treatments, AVATAR offers a valuable alternative in selected cases. Its implementation is especially impactful in low- and middle-income countries, where anesthetic services are limited. Wider adoption of such nonpharmacological tools could expand access to timely, less invasive pediatric RT in resource-limited settings.32, 33, 34

Several reports have highlighted the benefits of the AVATAR system for pediatric RT, including improved cost-effectiveness, treatment efficiency, and resource utilization.14,20,35 Cost analyses further confirm its economic value, showing reductions in anesthesia use, payer charges, and treatment delays.34, 35, 36 In this case, treatment costs were 52% lower due to the avoidance of sedation, based on standard insurance billing rates. Furthermore, by minimizing the need for daily anesthesia, AVATAR reduces anesthesia-related risks while enhancing patient cooperation.20 While multicenter trials have validated AVATAR in well-resourced settings, this is the first study to demonstrate its feasibility and impact in a resource-limited setting, with comparable benefits.

Conclusions

This case provides proof of concept for the use of AVATAR in very young pediatric patients and highlights the value of integrating audiovisual distraction into pediatric RT protocols. Future studies are needed to better define clinical indications, assess the long-term impact, and determine health care costs. The international collaboration between the AVATAR-developing institution and the Romanian cancer center, which is now the first to treat an infant with AVATAR, has yielded valuable insights to expand its global applicability.
