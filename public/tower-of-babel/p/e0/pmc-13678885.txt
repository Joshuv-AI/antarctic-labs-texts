A randomized clinical trial comparing the effects of irrigation activation systems on postoperative pain in single-visit non-surgical endodontic retreatment
Authors: Begum Guler, Burcu Serefoglu Polat, Ilgın İlgenli, Cem Peskersoy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe aim of this study was to evaluate the effect of ultrasonic, sonic, and Er: YAG laser activations on postoperative pain during single-visit non-surgical endodontic retreatment of asymptomatic maxillary anterior teeth with periapical lesions following initial root canal treatment performed at least four years earlier.MethodsSeventy-five asymptomatic maxillary anterior teeth with periapical lesions following initial root canal treatment performed at least four years earlier were included. Root canal filling materials were removed using Remover rotary (Coltene MicroMega, Besançon, France) and H-type hand files and canals were reshaped with step-back technique. Final irrigation was performed with one of the activation systems: group 1: Ultra X (Eighteeth), group 2: EDDY (VDW), group 3: Er: YAG laser (LightWalker, Fotona). EDTA followed by NaOCl were activated in three cycles of 20 s in all groups. Canals were obturated with gutta-percha and BioRoot RCS and restored with composite. Pain levels were recorded using Visual analog scale (VAS) immediately after completion of treatment (0 h), and at 6, 12, 24, 48, 72 h, and on 7th day. Pearson Chi-square, Fisher’s Exact, and robust mixed repeated-measures analysis of variance tests were used for statistical analysis.ResultNo flare-ups occurred, and no analgesic medication was required. The activation systems had a comparable effect on postoperative pain at any time point (p > 0.05). Postoperative pain did not exhibit any statistically significant time-dependent differences with any of the activation devices within each group.ConclusionUltrasonic, sonic, and laser-assisted irrigation activation demonstrated comparable effects on postoperative pain in asymptomatic maxillary anterior teeth undergoing single-visit non-surgical endodontic retreatment.Trial registrationThis study was registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT07150585) on 29/08/2025. Retrospectively registered.Keywords: Er:YAG laser, Irrigation activation, Postoperative pain, Single-visit retreatment, Sonic irrigation, Ultrasonic irrigation

Background

Post-treatment apical periodontitis related to initial root canal treatment (IRCT) may be observed as periapical lesions, with or without clinical symptoms even in well-treated teeth. It has been reported that 89% of lesions begin to shrink or completely heal within the first year after IRCT [1]. According to the European Society of Endodontology (ESE), if a periapical lesion remains unchanged or has only diminished in size, it should be further assessed until healing occurs or for a minimum of four years. If the lesion persists after four years, the root canal treatment is usually considered to be associated with post-treatment disease [2].

In the presence of preoperative periapical lesions, whether treatment should be completed in one or multiple visits is debated, particularly for achieving microbial elimination [3]. But it has been shown that the number of visits in non-surgical endodontic retreatment (NSER) does not significantly affect treatment success [4] and postoperative pain (PP) levels [5]. Although multiple-visit NSER has traditionally been considered advantageous for teeth with persistent periapical lesions because intracanal medicaments may further enhance microbial control, current evidence does not support the superiority of this approach. In their comprehensive review of the factors influencing the outcome of IRCT and NSER, Gulabivala et al. [3] emphasized that the available evidence does not demonstrate a clear advantage of multiple-visit over single-visit NSER and highlighted the need for well-designed randomized clinical trials to strengthen the evidence base. Likewise, Karaoğlan et al. [4], Toia et al. [6], and Choudhari et al. [7] reported that the number of treatment visits does not significantly influence periapical healing or the overall outcome of NSER. Furthermore, Karaoğlan et al. [5] demonstrated that PP is comparable following single- and multiple-visit NSER. Since intracanal medicaments are omitted during single-visit NSER, maximizing the antimicrobial effectiveness of chemo mechanical preparation becomes particularly important. Recent experimental and clinical studies have suggested that contemporary irrigant activation techniques may enhance disinfection to a level comparable with that achieved by interappointment intracanal medication, thereby providing a biological rationale for the increasing use of single-visit NSER in appropriately selected cases [8].

Post-treatment apical periodontitis is primarily associated with microorganisms that survive the IRCT or recolonize the root canal system, particularly within anatomically inaccessible areas and beneath residual root filling materials. Their survival is largely attributed to their localization within these protected areas, where they remain inaccessible to both mechanical instrumentation and conventional irrigation. Therefore, successful NSER depends on effective removal of the previous root filling material, enhanced disinfection of the root canal system, and adequate elimination of microorganisms [3]. Since a considerable proportion of the root canal walls remains untouched after mechanical instrumentation, particularly in anatomically complex root canal systems, irrigation plays a critical role in eliminating residual biofilm, debris, and necrotic tissue [9]. Accordingly, greater emphasis has been placed on adjunctive procedures, such as irrigation activation, that maximize the antibacterial efficacy of irrigation solutions and improve irrigant penetration into anatomically complex areas of the root canal system that remain inaccessible to mechanical instrumentation. The effectiveness of irrigation depends not only on the chemical properties of the irrigants but also on their ability to reach and circulate throughout the complex root canal system. Therefore, various irrigation activation techniques have been developed to improve irrigant penetration, irrigant dynamics, and root canal disinfection, making them particularly relevant during root canal retreatment [10].

A meta-analysis demonstrated that irrigant activation, regardless of the method employed, is significantly more effective than conventional syringe irrigation (CSI) in removing debris and the smear layer [11]. Therefore, irrigant activation should be considered as an essential part of chemo-mechanical preparation, and ultrasonic, sonic systems and laser-assisted irrigation (LAI) are the most widely used methods for enhancing the disinfecting efficacy of irrigation solutions [12].

Ultrasonic devices operating at frequencies of 25–45 kHz generate rapid oscillatory motion, leading to the formation of air bubbles. The subsequent growth and collapse of these bubbles produce acoustic cavitation and acoustic microstreaming, thereby improving the cleaning efficiency of irrigation [13]. On the other hand, sonic devices operating at frequencies of 1,000–6,000 Hz produce oscillatory motion within the root canal but do not generate acoustic cavitation or acoustic microstreaming [14]. For optimal disinfection during LAI, the laser wavelength should be close to the absorption peak of water, as demonstrated by Erbium-doped Yttrium Aluminum Garnet (Er: YAG) (2940 nm) and Erbium, Chromium: Yttrium–Scandium–Gallium–Garnet (Er, Cr: YSGG) (2780 nm) lasers [15]. Energy absorbed by the irrigant rapidly increases its temperature, generating expanding vapor bubbles whose collapse produces cavitation and shock waves, enabling irrigant penetration into deeper and more complex root canal areas [16]. At present, Shock Wave-Enhanced Emission Photoacoustic Streaming (SWEEPS) represents the latest development in Er: YAG laser-activated irrigation. Unlike Photon-Induced Photoacoustic Streaming (PIPS), which generates photoacoustic streaming using a single 50 µs laser pulse, SWEEPS delivers two sequential ultrashort laser pulses with a pulse duration of 25 µs. The second pulse is emitted at an optimized time interval to accelerate the collapse of the primary cavitation bubble, thereby inducing secondary cavitation and generating stronger shock waves. This dual-pulse mechanism enhances irrigant streaming and energy transmission, particularly within the confined anatomy of the root canal system where complete cavitation bubble collapse is limited, thereby improving the disinfection efficiency of the generated shock waves [17]. Despite the widespread clinical use of irrigant activation techniques during NSER, evidence regarding their influence on PP remains limited [18, 19].

It is also known that the extrusion of irrigants into the periapical tissues during irrigation can cause chemical irritation, leading to PP [20]. Furthermore, studies have demonstrated that the fluid dynamics generated by irrigation activation techniques may increase irrigant extrusion into the periapical tissues, thereby contributing to PP [21, 22]. The incidence of PP following IRCT has been reported to range between 3% and 58%; however, evidence regarding PP following NSER remains limited. Previous studies have evaluated the effect of irrigation activation techniques on PP during IRCT [23, 24], but no study has directly compared ultrasonic, sonic, and laser-assisted irrigation techniques with respect to post PP during single-visit NSER of teeth with periapical lesions. Therefore, the aim of this randomized controlled trial was to compare PP among ultrasonic, sonic, and LAI techniques during single-visit NSER of asymptomatic maxillary anterior teeth with periapical lesions following IRCT performed at least four years earlier. The null hypothesis was that no significant differences would exist among the irrigation activation systems with respect to PP.

Materials and methods

Study design and setting

This randomized controlled trial was designed as a double-blind, single-centre, parallel-group superiority study, reported in accordance with the CONSORT 2025 Statement [25], and registered at clinicaltrials.gov (NCT07150585) on 29.08.2025. Ethical approval was obtained from the Local Clinical Research Ethics Committee (Approval No: 24 − 3/86), and the study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Participant recruitment took place between December 2024 and June 2025. All participants received verbal and written information about the study’s objectives, procedures, potential benefits, and risks, and written informed consent was obtained from each participant.

Sample size calculation

The sample size was calculated using G*Power version 3.1.9.6 (Heinrich-Heine University Düsseldorf, Germany). The primary endpoint used for the calculation was postoperative pain assessed using a visual analogue scale (VAS), based on the 6-hour postoperative VAS scores reported by Liapis et al. [26], where the greatest between-group difference was observed. The effect size (f = 0.358) was derived from these data. A priori power analysis was performed using F tests (ANOVA: repeated measures, between factors), assuming three study groups, seven postoperative measurements, a correlation among repeated measures of 0.5, a significance level (α) of 0.05, and a statistical power (1 − β) of 95%. Based on these assumptions, the minimum required sample size was calculated as 72 participants. To compensate for possible dropout, one additional participant was included in each group, resulting in a final target sample size of 75 participants (25 per group; allocation ratio 1:1:1).

Eligibility criteria

Medically healthy individuals aged 18–65 years who agreed to participate and attend follow-up visits were enrolled in this trial. Only asymptomatic maxillary anterior teeth, defined by the absence of spontaneous pain, tenderness to percussion or palpation, the absence of a sinus tract, and a baseline VAS score of 0, with completed root development, healthy periodontal status without mobility, a history of IRCT performed at least four years earlier, and periapical lesions measuring 2–5 mm with a Periapical Index (PAI) score of 4 [27], independently assessed by two blinded examiners at the time of recruitment, were included. Patients with systemic diseases, pregnancy, or known allergies to any of the materials used in this study, as well as teeth with open apices, periodontal pocket depth > 5 mm, attachment loss ≥ 3 mm and mobility, or non-restorable loss of tooth structure, were excluded.

All included teeth had previously been obturated with gutta-percha; however, the type of root canal sealer could not be determined. One author (B.S.P.) was responsible for participant recruitment and randomization. Allocation was concealed from the operator (B.G.) until the final irrigation protocol was assigned according to the automated randomization sequence.

Non-Surgical endodontic retreatment procedures

Preoperative periapical radiographs were obtained using the paralleling technique with a film holder (Endo-Bite; Kerr, Orange, CA, USA), exposure parameters of 70 kV, 8 mA, and 0.08 s, and photostimulable phosphor plates. All digital radiographs were transferred and stored using Digora™ software (Digora™ System, Soredex Orion, Helsinki, Finland). Periapical lesion detection and radiographic assessment were performed exclusively using these standardized digital periapical radiographs. Lesion size was determined by measuring the largest radiographic diameter using ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA). Before lesion measurements were performed, the images were calibrated using the known dimensions of the Digora™ phosphor plate. All lesion measurements were performed by an experienced endodontist following a calibration exercise on 10 representative periapical radiographs before the study, whereas PAI scoring was performed independently by two blinded examiners.

All NSER procedures were performed by the same operator (B.G.). Under rubber dam isolation, existing coronal restorations were removed using sterile diamond and carbide burs, and carious tissue was subsequently removed. In teeth restored with full-coverage crowns, the crowns were removed before NSER. The quality of the pre-existing coronal restorations was not assessed, as it was not a predefined study variable. Since all included teeth had already been diagnosed as requiring NSER, all existing restorations were removed before treatment. No teeth with intraradicular posts were included in the study. The access cavities were then refined to provide direct entry to the canal orifices and subsequently straight-line access to the canals. Previous root canal filling materials were removed using a combination of rotary Remover file (Coltene MicroMega, Besançon, France) (30.07) and Hedström files (Dentsply Maillefer, Ballaigues, Switzerland). Due to the passive tip design of the Remover file, a retention space of 1–2 mm was first created within coronal part of the gutta-percha using a #3 Gates-Glidden drill. The Remover file was used with an X-Smart Plus endodontic motor (Dentsply Maillefer, Ballaigues, Switzerland) (500 rpm, 2.5 Ncm) in 3 mm strokes to remove filling material from the coronal and middle thirds. The remaining 3 mm of filling material in the apical third was removed using Hedström hand files. After working length was determined with an apex locator (RayPex 6, VDW, Munich, Germany), the first Hedström file that reached the working length with slight binding was considered the initial apical file and used to determine the initial apical diameter. The canals were then reshaped using the step-back technique with Hedström files. Instrumentation was continued until no remnants of root filling material or decalcified dentin were visible on the instruments. The master apical file was enlarged to at least three ISO sizes larger than the initial apical file, with a minimum apical size of ISO 45, according to the original canal size and anatomy. Because the initial apical diameter varied among NSER cases, the final apical size ranged from ISO 45 to ISO 80. The final apical diameter was recorded for comparison among the study groups (Table 1).

Between each file change, the canals were irrigated using a 30-gauge irrigation needle (Kerr Hawe, Bioggio, Switzerland) with 2 mL of 2.5% sodium hypochlorite (NaOCl; Merck), followed by 2 mL of distilled water and subsequently 2 mL of 17% ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) (Sigma Chemical Co., St. Louis, MO, USA) to prevent direct interaction between the irrigants. In total, 20 mL of NaOCl and 8–10 mL of EDTA were used during removal of the existing root canal filling material and subsequent canal reshaping procedures for each tooth.

Apical patency was maintained throughout canal preparation. The adequacy of root canal filling removal was confirmed by the absence of filling material remnants on the instruments and by evaluation of the master cone radiograph for residual filling material along the canal walls. If residual filling material was detected, additional instrumentation was performed and the master cone radiograph was repeated until no visible remnants remained.

Final irrigation and activation protocol

All activation procedures were performed under rubber dam isolation after completion of canal preparation, and participants were not informed which irrigation activation system was used.

In the ultrasonic group, an Ultra X device (Eighteeth, Changzhou Sifary Medical Technology Co., Ltd.) equipped with silver 21 mm, 0.2-taper irrigation tip was used. The tip was positioned 2 mm short of the working length and moved in an up-and-down motion to activate 17% EDTA for three 20-second cycles (3 × 2 mL; total 6 mL), followed by irrigation with 2 mL of distilled water to prevent direct chemical interaction between the irrigants and subsequent activation of 2.5% NaOCl for three additional 20-second cycles (3 × 2 mL; total 6 mL).

In the sonic group, a polyamide EDDY tip (20 mm, 0.05 taper) was mounted on a sonic air scaler (TA 200, Micron, Japan). The tip was inserted 2 mm short of the working length and agitated in an up-and-down motion, activating 17% EDTA for three 20-second cycles (3 × 2 mL; total 6 mL). After irrigation with 2 mL of distilled water to prevent direct chemical interaction between the irrigants, 2.5% NaOCl was activated for three additional 20-second cycles (3 × 2 mL; total 6 mL).

In the laser group, an Er: YAG Lightwalker device in Shock Wave Enhanced Emission Photoacoustic Streaming (SWEEPS) mode (2940 nm, 20 mJ, 15 Hz, 0.3 W, 25 µs pulse duration) was used. A SWEEPS fiber tip mounted on the H14 handpiece was positioned stationary within the pulp chamber, with air and water pressures set to zero, and continuous irrigation was performed by a second operator. 17% EDTA was activated for three 20-second cycles (3 × 2 mL; total 6 mL), followed by irrigation with 2 mL of distilled water to prevent direct chemical interaction between the irrigants. Subsequently, 2.5% NaOCl was activated for three additional 20-second cycles (3 × 2 mL; total 6 mL). The activation protocol was performed without moving the fiber tip throughout the procedure.

Canals were finally rinsed with 5 mL of distilled water and dried with paper points. Root canal obturation was then performed using an ISO-standardized 0.02 taper master gutta-percha cone (DiaDent Group International Inc., South Korea) corresponding to the final master apical file, and BioRoot RCS sealer (Septodont, France) with the cold lateral compaction technique. Following placement of the master cone, a finger spreader was used to create space for accessory gutta-percha cones, which were inserted until complete obturation of the root canal was achieved. The canal orifices were sealed with a 3-mm layer of flowable composite (SDR; Dentsply, Konstanz, Germany) and the coronal restorations were completed with resin composite (Solare X, GC). Teeth requiring definitive prosthetic rehabilitation were referred to the Department of Prosthodontics following completion of the endodontic treatment. Final radiographs were taken using a parallel film holder to ensure the same positioning as the initial periapical radiographs.

Postoperative pain evaluation

Following single-visit NSER was completed, patients received standardized verbal and written postoperative instructions. The 100-mm VAS was explained to the patients in detail, and the VAS form was provided. Patients were instructed to record PP intensity immediately after completion of treatment (0 h), and at 6, 12, 24, 48, and 72 h, and on the 7th day after NSER. Patients were advised to take ibuprofen (400 mg) only if PP occurred and to record the number of analgesic tablets taken. They were also instructed to contact the treating endodontist if severe pain or swelling developed after treatment. At the end of the 1-week period, patients were asked to return the completed form in person to a researcher (I.I.) who was blinded to the group assignments. The PP marked by the patients was categorized for statistical comparison as follows: 0 indicated no pain; 1–3 represented mild pain; 4–6 moderate pain; and 7–10 severe pain.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics Version 23 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) and the R statistical computing environment through the Jamovi interface (Jamovi Project, version 2.6.26, Sydney, Australia). The distribution of continuous variables was evaluated using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Categorical variables were compared using Pearson’s chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test with Monte Carlo correction, as appropriate. Categorical postoperative pain intensity at each follow-up interval was compared among the three groups using Fisher’s exact test with Monte Carlo correction. Since continuous pain scores did not satisfy the assumption of normality, comparisons among groups at individual time points were performed using the Kruskal–Wallis test. Within-group changes over time were analyzed using the Friedman test, and Bonferroni correction was applied for multiple comparisons.

To evaluate longitudinal changes in postoperative pain while simultaneously accounting for the between-subject factor (group), the within-subject factor (time), and the group × time interaction, a robust mixed repeated-measures analysis of variance based on 5% trimmed means was performed using the “bwtrim” function from the WRS2 package in R. This method was preferred because postoperative pain scores exhibited non-normal and skewed distributions with extreme observations, conditions under which conventional repeated-measures ANOVA may produce unreliable statistical inference. The robust trimmed-mean approach provides valid statistical inference for repeated-measures data under violations of normality, heteroscedasticity, and the presence of outliers while maintaining appropriate Type I error control. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05.

Results

Initially, a total of 147 maxillary anterior teeth with periapical lesions requiring NSER were evaluated in the scope of the current study, 75 asymptomatic maxillary anterior teeth were included depending on inclusion criteria (Fig. 1). Participant recruitment took place between December 2024 and June 2025.

CONSORT flowchart of patients’ recruitment

The demographic data of the patients and the parameters related to preoperative IRCT, and postoperative NSER are summarized in Table 1. Following the NSER, no flare-ups were observed and no patient required analgesic medication, regardless of the activation system used. Although regression analysis models were attempted, the models could not be executed due to the very small number of patients reporting PP, and the effects of pre- and postoperative parameters on PP could not be statistically compared.

The distribution of categorical PP data for patients with no pain, as well as those with mild, moderate, or severe pain observed at each time interval in the experimental groups, is presented in Table 2 and Fig. 2. No statistically significant difference in PP intensity was observed among the experimental groups at any time point. When the patients’ mean pain levels were evaluated regardless of the different measurement time points, the activation systems were found to have similar effects on PP (p = 0.131). The highest mean pain value was observed in the EDDY sonic activation group (0.22), whereas the lowest mean value was recorded in the SWEEPS laser activation group (0.06). When irrigation activation methods were disregarded, PP was observed at similar levels across all measurement time points, indicating that time as an independent variable exerted no statistically significant influence on perceived pain (p = 0.348). The combined effect of measurement time points and activation systems on PP was also found to be statistically insignificant (p = 0.403). There were no statistically significant differences in mean PP levels between the irrigation activation systems at any of the evaluated time points (P > 0.05, Table 3). In addition, when each experimental group was evaluated individually, PP was found to show no statistically significant difference over time with any activation device (p > 0.05). Within-group changes over time were also evaluated using the Friedman test. No significant changes over time were observed in Group 1 (p = 0.179) or Group 2 (p = 0.139). Although a statistically significant overall change over time was detected in Group 3 by the Friedman test, Bonferroni-adjusted multiple comparisons revealed no significant differences between individual time points.

Discussion

Current evidence indicates that single-visit NSER is associated with healing outcomes comparable to those achieved with multiple-visit NSER in teeth with persistent apical periodontitis [4, 6]. Likewise, systematic reviews have shown no clinically meaningful differences in PP between the two treatment protocols, suggesting that the number of treatment visits should be determined according to the clinical characteristics of each case rather than following a universal treatment approach [7, 28]. Therefore, in carefully selected asymptomatic cases without clinical signs of acute infection, such as those included in the present study, a standardized single-visit NSER protocol represents a clinically acceptable treatment approach. In addition, the use of a standardized single-visit protocol minimized procedural variability, allowing a direct comparison of the PP associated with the different irrigation activation methods.

In NSER cases, to reduce the bacterial load and ensure the disinfection of the dentinal tubules, it is emphasized that the antibacterial efficacy during the irrigation stage should be maximized. The effect of ultrasonic, sonic, and Er: YAG laser irrigation activation on PP during single-visit NSER of asymptomatic maxillary anterior teeth with periapical lesions were evaluated in the current study. Based on the findings the present randomized clinical trial, the lowest pain level was observed in the SWEEPS laser group, followed by the ultrasonic and sonic groups, respectively. However, no statistically significant difference was found between the groups; thus, the null hypothesis was accepted.

Although long-term periapical healing is an important indicator of the biological success of NSER, PP represents an early patient-centered clinical outcome that reflects the patient’s immediate postoperative experience [29]. Because radiographic healing requires long-term follow-up and evaluates a different aspect of treatment success, the present report focused on the short-term PP associated with different irrigation activation methods, while long-term periapical healing was beyond the scope of this report [30].

In studies conducted during IRCT, evidence indicates that using irrigation activation system achieved higher antibacterial efficacy compared to CSI [12] and increases the success rate of the IRCT in long-term follow-up [31]. Despite all these known advantages of irrigation activation, over the past 25 years they were used only a limited number of prospective clinical studies during NSER and none of these studies directly evaluated their effect on NSER outcomes [32–34]. Only two prospective studies have compared irrigation activation methods in terms of PP during NSER [18, 19]. Among them, Ashraf et al. [18] evaluated XP-Endo Finisher, PUI, and CSI, while İnce Yusufoglu et al. [19] compared EDDY with manual dynamic activation. However, none have directly compared ultrasonic, sonic, and laser systems; therefore, the present study is the first to assess the effects of these three methods on PP during NSER.

It is well known that PP observed during endodontic treatments generally results from inflammation caused by chemical, mechanical, or microbial irritation in the periapical region during treatment. Although the incidence of PP following IRCT has been reported to range between 3% and 58% [35], and higher PP rates have been reported during NSER compared with IRCT, data specifically addressing the incidence of PP in NSER cases remain limited. Nosrat et al. [36] reported a flare-up incidence of 3.95% following NSER and the presence of systemic conditions such as diabetes, and preoperative pain, involvement of mandibular molars, presence of preoperative percussion pain and palpation tenderness, as well as the amount of debris extrusion caused by shaping systems, irrigation solutions, and activation devices, were found to significantly increase the likelihood of PP and flare-up occurrence. In the present study, all these factors that could potentially influence PP were meticulously standardized across all experimental groups to ensure methodological consistency.

Even though in vitro studies cannot fully replicate clinical conditions and provide only limited data, extrusion caused by irrigation activation devices has been extensively investigated, particularly regarding its association with PP [37, 38]; however, no consensus has yet been reached on which activation system causes the greatest extrusion. In most of these studies, PUI and SWEEPS laser activation were found to cause similar levels of extrusion [37, 39]. When sonic and ultrasonic activation devices were compared, comparable extrusion levels [40] were reported, whereas others indicated that EDDY resulted in greater extrusion [38]. Furthermore, in a study comparing all three activation methods (PUI, EDDY, Er: YAG Laser in SWEEPS mode) Karadağlı et al. [41] reported that the techniques produced equal levels of irrigant extrusion. The finding in the current study that all three irrigation activation methods resulted in similar levels of PP could be explained by the extrusion results reported by Karadağlı et al. [41]

Following removal of the coronal and middle thirds of the root canal filling material with the retreatment file system, apical preparation was completed using manual Hedström files in accordance with the manufacturer’s recommended NSER protocol. Previous laboratory studies and recent systematic reviews have reported that manual instrumentation may result in greater apical debris extrusion than engine-driven retreatment systems, particularly rotary NiTi instruments, mainly because of the push–pull filing motion. However, these findings are derived predominantly from in vitro studies that do not reproduce the resistance provided by the periapical tissues under clinical conditions [42, 43]. In the present study, the same NSER protocol, including manual Hedström file instrumentation, was applied to all experimental groups. Therefore, any potential influence of the instrumentation technique on apical debris extrusion was standardized across the study groups, making it unlikely that the instrumentation technique confounded the comparison of PP among the irrigation activation methods.

Since no previous study has evaluated the effects of these activation systems, the findings of the present study could only be compared with prospective clinical studies conducted on IRCT. Studies comparing sonic and passive ultrasonic activation systems [23, 44, 45] have reported similar levels of PP, and this finding is consistent with the findings of the present study.

On the other hand, Mittal et al. [46] reported that in single-visit IRCT of symptomatic mandibular and maxillary molars, the lowest pain levels were observed with SWEEPS, followed by PIPS, Ultra X, and CSI, with statistically significant differences among all systems. Similarly, Liapis et al. [26] observed that Er: YAG laser in PIPS mode caused significantly less pain at 6 h compared with PUI in both asymptomatic teeth with and without periapical lesions. They attributed this finding to the placement of the laser tip solely in the access cavity, thereby reducing fluid pressure and apical extrusion. In the present study, unlike previous reports, no significant difference in PP was observed between PUI and SWEEPS laser activation. This discrepancy is likely attributable to methodological heterogeneity across studies. Specifically, Mittal et al. [46] conducted their research exclusively on symptomatic molars, whereas Liapis et al. [26] did not standardize critical variables such as tooth type and location, or the presence/absence of periapical lesions, which may have influenced their results.

Erkan et al. [24] investigated the influence of the three activation systems utilized in the present study on PP in mandibular premolars diagnosed with irreversible pulpitis. Their findings indicated that PUI and sonic activation demonstrated similar effects, whereas laser activation with PIPS and SWEEPS resulted in significantly lower PP compared with both PUI and sonic activation. Although the findings of the present study regarding PUI and sonic activation align with those of Erkan et al. [24], the outcomes observed for laser activation differ from their results. The study by Erkan et al. [24] was conducted on symptomatic mandibular premolars, whereas the present investigation focused on asymptomatic maxillary central incisors. This discrepancy may be attributable to the influence of gravity in maxillary anterior teeth, potentially leading to reduced extrusion in the groups treated with PUI and sonic irrigation [47] and the absence of preoperative clinical symptoms.

These considerations emphasize that variations in study design and clinical characteristics may influence the observed outcomes of irrigation activation methods. Therefore, in the present study, a high level of standardization was ensured, allowing an isolated assessment of the effect of irrigation activation devices on PP. The findings of the present study should be interpreted in light of certain limitations. The study population was restricted to asymptomatic maxillary anterior teeth with periapical lesion; therefore, the findings cannot be directly generalized to symptomatic teeth, posterior teeth, or NSER cases with different clinical characteristics. In addition, radiographic assessment was performed using standardized digital periapical radiographs. Although periapical radiography is widely used for the clinical assessment of healing, it does not provide the three-dimensional information available with cone-beam computed tomography, which may allow a more sensitive assessment of periapical status. Furthermore, all NSER procedures were completed using a standardized single-visit protocol that included manual Hedström file instrumentation following rotary removal of the existing root canal filling material. Although this approach ensured methodological consistency among the study groups, the findings should be interpreted within the context of this specific NSER protocol. Finally, the present report evaluated only short-term PP, an early patient-centered outcome, whereas long-term periapical healing outcomes were not included in the present report. Further studies incorporating long-term radiographic follow-up are needed to determine whether different irrigation activation methods influence periapical healing.

Conclusion

Within the limitation of the present study, irrigation activation systems demonstrated equivalent influence on PP at each measurement interval during the single visit NSER. No flare-up occurred in any group following the NSER. This finding indicates that all three activation systems used in the present study can be safely employed during single-visit NSER of asymptomatic cases and do not significantly influence PP.
