Temperature-dependent thermoelectric behavior of metal-doped nanoporous carbon with tailored pore structures for multilevel temperature sensing
Authors: Minwook Park, Muhammad Toyabur Rahman, Seonghwan Kim, Hee Chang Jeon, Young-Seong Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMetal-doped nanoporous carbon (MDNC) derived from core–shell metal–organic frameworks (MOFs) exhibits temperature-dependent thermoelectric behavior that is highly sensitive to nanoporous structure and surface interactions. In this study, a pellet-based approach is employed to enable systematic investigation of the Seebeck coefficient and electrical conductivity of MDNC as a function of temperature. Differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) reveals a pronounced desorption process in the temperature range of 80–100 ℃, which is closely correlated with significant changes in electrical transport properties. To further elucidate this behavior, MDNC samples with different particle sizes (~ 200 nm and ~ 800 nm) are examined, demonstrating distinct temperature-dependent variations in both Seebeck coefficient and electrical conductivity. These results indicate that temperature-induced desorption plays a critical role in modulating carrier transport through changes in interfacial barriers, scattering mechanisms, and conductive pathway connectivity. The particle-size-dependent nanoporous structure further influences this behavior by altering adsorption/desorption characteristics. Overall, this study provides insight into the coupling between nanoporous structure and temperature-dependent transport behavior in MDNC, offering a framework for understanding and tuning thermoelectric responses in nanoporous carbon systems.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-60606-7.Keywords: Metal-doped nanoporous carbon, Metal–organic framework, Seebeck coefficient, Electrical conductivity, Water desorptionSubject terms: Energy science and technology, Materials science, Nanoscience and technology

Introduction

Nanoporous carbon has attracted significant attention as a thermoelectric material due to its unique structural and electronic properties1–4. In particular, its large specific surface area and well-developed porous structure enable strong interactions between charge carriers and surface states, leading to temperature-dependent variations in electrical conductivity and Seebeck coefficient5. Carbon-based materials, including carbon nanotubes (CNTs), graphene, and nanoporous carbon, have been widely explored for thermoelectric applications due to their tunable electrical and thermal transport properties6–8. Among these, nanoporous carbon is of particular interest because its porous structure introduces additional mechanisms, such as interfacial scattering and energy filtering, which can significantly influence carrier transport. Nanopores in carbon-based materials are key determinants of critical properties in applications such as nanoelectronics, energy storage, and membrane technologies, and similar nanopore-induced effects have also been reported in MOF-derived porous carbon materials9.

Thermoelectric behavior in nanoporous carbon materials is strongly governed by carrier transport mechanisms that are sensitive to surface states, interfacial barriers, and adsorption/desorption phenomena. In nanoporous carbon systems, adsorbed species can modify carrier concentration, scattering processes, and energy filtering effects, resulting in pronounced changes in thermoelectric properties. Previous studies have shown that porosity and interfacial effects play a critical role in determining thermoelectric performance through modulation of carrier transport and energy-dependent scattering processes10,11. However, the role of adsorption/desorption behavior, particularly in relation to nanoporous structure and particle size, remains insufficiently understood. In particular, the coupling between temperature-induced desorption processes and thermoelectric transport behavior has not been systematically investigated.

In this study, we investigate the temperature-dependent thermoelectric behavior of metal-doped nanoporous carbon (MDNC) derived from core–shell MOFs, with a particular focus on nanoporous structure and desorption-induced transport modulation. MDNC samples with different particle sizes (~ 200 nm and ~ 800 nm) were prepared, and their Seebeck coefficient and electrical conductivity were systematically analyzed as a function of temperature. Pelletization of carbon-based materials and thermoelectric measurements of porous carbons have been reported in previous studies; however, direct thermoelectric characterization of nanoporous carbon derived from core–shell MOFs, particularly in relation to desorption-induced transport behavior, remains limited. To address this, a pellet-based approach was employed to enable reliable thermoelectric measurements and to directly correlate nanoporous structure, particle size, and desorption behavior with transport properties. This approach overcomes the limitations of powder-based measurements, such as poor electrical connectivity.

and measurement instability. As a result, this work provides insight into structure–property relationships in nanoporous carbon systems and offers a practical route toward device-compatible thermoelectric applications.

The core part of the MOF was synthesized using Zn2+-based ZIF-8, while the shell part was prepared using Co2+-based ZIF-67 (Fig. 1a). When the material was synthesized by carbonization rather than being formed into pellets, the purple powder changed to black, confirming the formation of MDNC (Fig. 1b). The particle size of MDNC was controlled by adjusting the synthesis scale of the MOF precursor. Specifically, variations in the precursor volume during synthesis lead to differences in nucleation and growth conditions, resulting in distinct particle sizes while maintaining similar chemical composition and crystal structure1,12. Based on this, MDNC with a particle size of 800 nm (denoted as 800MDNC) and 200 nm (denoted as 200MDNC) were synthesized from 800 nm ZIF-8@ZIF-67 (denoted as 800ZIF-8@ZIF-67) and 200 nm ZIF-8@ZIF-67 (denoted as 200ZIF-8@ZIF-67), respectively, for comparison.

Before fabricating the pellet, TEM-EDS (Transmission electron microscopy-Energy dispersive spectroscopy) measurements of the hybrid nanoporous carbon with a core–shell structure clearly revealed the formation of a Zn–Co core–shell structure (Fig. 2). After pyrolysis, the structure was observed to shrink into a nanoporous carbon form. In addition, while the elemental compositions of Co and Zn were clearly distinguished in the ZIF-8@ZIF-67, in the nanoporous carbon form they were found to be arranged in an alloy-like configuration.

To characterize the surface morphology of the MDNC pellets after pyrolysis, SEM measurements were performed. The SEM images revealed that the surface of the 800MDNC pellet exhibited pronounced roughness originating from the presence of large particles, whereas the 200MDNC pellet, composed of relatively smaller particles, displayed a smoother and more compact surface (Fig. S1).

Temperature-dependent differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) measurements revealed a moisture desorption phenomenon in the temperature range of 80–100 ℃ (Fig. S2)14–17. DSC measurements were performed by temperature sweeping with an initial heating process followed by cooling at a constant heating/cooling rate of 10 ℃/min. To investigate the temperature-dependent moisture desorption behavior in more detail, DSC measurements were performed on two samples with different particle sizes. For the sample with a larger particle size of 800 nm, the DSC peak was asymmetric and distributed over a broad temperature range of approximately 80–100 ℃. In contrast, the sample with a smaller particle size of 200 nm exhibited a relatively symmetric DSC peak centered around 100 ℃. These results indicate that the moisture desorption behavior differs significantly depending on particle size. Furthermore, while a distinct desorption peak was observed during the first heating cycle, the peak disappeared during the second heating cycle, confirming that the adsorbed moisture was completely desorbed during the first heating process, resulting in the absence of a desorption peak upon reheating.

When nanoporous carbon samples are analyzed by DSC, the most common origin of a thermal peak observed in the temperature range of 80–100 ℃ is the evaporation of adsorbed moisture. Owing to their extremely high specific surface area and well-developed nanoporous structure, nanoporous carbons readily absorb moisture from the ambient environment. Accordingly, such thermal peaks are primarily attributed to the desorption of physically adsorbed water. In nanoporous carbon, water confined within nanopores can exhibit thermal behavior that differs from that of bulk water due to confinement effects such as capillary condensation. Although the boiling point of bulk water is 100 ℃, moisture adsorbed in porous materials typically evaporates over a broad temperature range, approximately between 50 ℃ and 120 ℃, forming a broad thermal peak during heating. In addition, in materials with flexible porous frameworks—such as metal–organic frameworks (MOFs) and porous carbons—the removal of confined moisture can induce contraction or rearrangement of the nanoporous structure. This process relieves internal structural stress, resulting in the release of heat that appears as an exothermic peak in DSC measurements.

Figure 4c presents the calculated power factor of the 800 nm MDNC sample. The temperature dependence of the power factor provides insight into the effective operating temperature range of the material. The 800 nm sample exhibits a pronounced increase in power factor at higher temperatures, indicating that the contribution of electrical conductivity becomes dominant in this region, leading to an enhanced thermoelectric response. Therefore, the 800 nm sample is more favorable for high-temperature sensing applications20.

For comparison, the 200 nm sample (Fig. 5c) shows a different behavior. The power factor decreases with increasing temperature, indicating that the thermoelectric response is more stable in the low-temperature region. This behavior suggests that the reduction in the Seebeck coefficient dominates over the increase in electrical conductivity at elevated temperatures, resulting in a decline in the overall power factor. Such characteristics imply that the 200 nm sample is more suitable for low-temperature sensing applications21,22. These results suggest that the effective sensing temperature range can be tuned by controlling the particle size of nanoporous carbon. By combining materials with different particle sizes, it may be possible to achieve multi-level temperature sensing across a wide temperature range.

The temperature-dependent thermoelectric behavior of nanoporous carbon can be understood as a result of the coupled interactions among interfacial energy barriers, adsorbed species on the porous surface, particle–particle interfaces, carrier transport, and electron energy filtering effects, all of which vary with temperature23,24.

The changes in the Seebeck coefficient and electrical conductivity around ~ 80 ℃ can be attributed to the desorption of adsorbed moisture. In nanoporous carbon, electrical transport is highly sensitive to surface conditions, and thus the mean free path of charge carriers is strongly influenced by temperature-dependent surface states, which in turn affects both the Seebeck coefficient and electrical conductivity25,26.

First, the temperature-dependent electrical conductivity can be explained based on the carrier transport pathways. The removal of adsorbed moisture improves the connectivity of electrical pathways and eliminates factors that hinder charge transport27.

Nanoporous carbon exhibits strong moisture adsorption due to its high surface area. Under ambient conditions, adsorbed moisture forms thin liquid films at the contact interfaces between carbon particles, which act as insulating layers. Since water has significantly lower electrical conductivity than carbon, these interparticle liquid layers function as resistive barriers that impede electron transport. As a result, electrical conductivity is suppressed at lower temperatures. When the temperature exceeds approximately 80 ℃, moisture desorption removes these insulating films, leading to improved connectivity of conductive pathways and a sharp increase in electrical conductivity.

In the case of larger particles, the electrical conductivity begins to increase rapidly near ~ 100 ℃, whereas for smaller particles, this transition occurs at lower temperatures, indicating particle-size-dependent desorption behavior28.

In addition, the abrupt and continuous increase in electrical conductivity suggests that the nanopore size distribution is relatively uniform. If a broad distribution of pore sizes were present, moisture would be desorbed sequentially from smaller nanopores, resulting in a stepwise change in conductivity. However, in this study, the uniform nanoporous structure leads to simultaneous desorption within a narrow temperature range. Second, the temperature-dependent Seebeck coefficient can be explained in terms of changes in carrier concentration and energy filtering effects27. The decrease in the Seebeck coefficient with increasing temperature, particularly after the plateau region, indicates a change in carrier concentration as well as a reduction in energy filtering effects. In general, the Seebeck coefficient is proportional to the average carrier energy and inversely proportional to carrier concentration24,25,27.

In nanoporous carbon, energy barriers can be formed due to interfacial effects, such as adsorbed moisture or solvent molecules on the pore surface. These adsorbed species increase electron scattering and induce potential barriers at the interfaces between carbon particles10,11. These barriers selectively block low-energy carriers while allowing high-energy carriers to pass, acting as an electron energy filtering mechanism. Therefore, when moisture is present, energy filtering is enhanced, leading to an increased Seebeck coefficient.

However, as the temperature increases and moisture desorption occurs, these interfacial barriers are reduced or eliminated. Consequently, low-energy carriers can move more freely, leading to an increase in electrical conductivity. At the same time, the average carrier energy decreases, resulting in a reduction of the Seebeck coefficient23,24,29.

This behavior is consistent with the relationship between carrier transport and Seebeck coefficient, where enhanced scattering increases the Seebeck coefficient, while the removal of scattering centers facilitates carrier transport but reduces the Seebeck coefficient25,29.

Therefore, the temperature-dependent behavior of electrical conductivity and Seebeck coefficient in nanoporous carbon can be explained by the combined effects of moisture desorption, interfacial barrier modulation, and changes in carrier transport mechanisms.

In this study, we present a pelletized synthesis of nanostructured metal-doped nanoporous carbon (MDNC) derived from core–shell MOFs and systematically investigate its temperature- dependent thermoelectric properties. Structural analyses confirmed that the phase transition behavior is preserved despite particle size reduction, while DSC measurements revealed moisture desorption in the 80–100 ℃ range. The electrical conductivity and Seebeck coefficient exhibited clear particle-size-dependent variations, attributed to changes in nanoporous structure, surface area, and effective metal doping. These results demonstrate that tailoring the nanoporous structure enables distinct thermoelectric responses across multiple temperature regimes, highlighting the potential of MDNC for high-sensitivity multilevel temperature sensing.
