Algorithm-driven HPV vaccine allocation paradigm for cervical cancer elimination targeting the Chinese population
Authors: Liangru Zhou, Weihua Luo, Ruixi Qin, Di Wang, Peipei Chai, Shuang Ma, Xin Zhang, Guoxiang Liu, Zhiwei Rong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study identifies optimal, fiscally sustainable HPV vaccination strategies for China using Bayesian optimization and transmission dynamics modeling. By integrating a demographic-based HPV transmission model with a decision-analysis framework, we simulated cervical cancer burden across varying time horizons (30–100 years). Our budget impact analysis, incorporating willingness-to-pay data from a multi-center contingent valuation survey of 787 parents, reveals critical funding dynamics. We found that achieving cervical cancer elimination within 40 years necessitates a 23.97% vaccination coverage among 15–17-year-old girls using the domestic nonavalent vaccine. Conversely, a rapid 30-year elimination target requires 76.66% coverage. At current market prices, autonomous consumer demand falls short, exposing a ¥35.93 billion funding gap for the 40-year target. To mitigate these financial barriers and ensure long-term fiscal resilience, we propose a tripartite financing mechanism—allocating costs among consumers (45.90%), the government (26.19%), and medical insurance (27.91%). These algorithm-driven findings provide an actionable, evidence-based framework for optimizing multi-party health financing and accelerating cervical cancer elimination in China.

Introduction

The World Health Organization (WHO) proposed a strategic goal to eliminate Cervical Cancer in 2020, with a standard of less than 4 new cases per 100,000 women per year1. To accelerate the elimination of cervical cancer, many countries have integrated human papillomavirus (HPV) vaccination into their immunization programs. Due to the differences in gender distribution of HPV infections, the disease burden of cervical cancer, and the affordability of HPV vaccines, vaccination strategies vary across countries. In the United States, the nonavalent HPV vaccine is administered to boys and girls aged 11–122. The United Kingdom3 and Australia4 target boys and girls aged 12–13 with the same nonavalent vaccine. Germany vaccinates girls and boys aged 9–14 with the nonavalent vaccine5. In Japan, girls from grade 6 of primary school to grade 1 of high school can receive the nonavalent vaccine for free6.

Although the first HPV vaccine was approved in China in 2016, the vaccination rate remains suboptimal. Data indicates that the cumulative coverage of the first dose among girls aged 9–14 is approximately 4% nationwide7. However, a significant policy shift recently occurred. Effective from November 10, 2025, China has integrated the HPV vaccine into the National Immunization Program, providing two doses of the bivalent vaccine free of charge to girls who have turned 13 (specifically those born on or after November 10, 2011), with a six-month interval between doses8. For females aged 9–45 outside this immunization program cohort, HPV vaccination follows a “voluntary, out-of-pocket, and age-appropriate selection” policy, allowing individuals to choose between bivalent, quadrivalent, or nonavalent vaccines based on their age, needs, and budget9.

Despite this progress, structural inequities in the financial burden of cervical cancer treatment remain a barrier. According to China’s 2024 medical insurance data, for Employee Basic Medical Insurance, the reimbursement rate within the policy scope is 84.8% (15.2% out-of-pocket), with an average out-of-pocket cost per instance of care of ~1779 yuan (≈USD 257.47). In contrast, for Urban and Rural Resident Basic Medical Insurance, the reimbursement rate drops to only 68.6% (31.4% out-of-pocket), with an average out-of-pocket cost of ~2326 yuan (≈USD 336.63). The out-of-pocket ratio for residents is 16.2 percentage points higher than for employees, and the actual out-of-pocket amount is 30% higher, highlighting a disproportionately heavy financial burden on rural and non-working populations10. Consequently, the elimination of cervical cancer in China is complicated by this intersection of a massive population base, the uneven distribution of medical resources, and substantial financial disparities.

Choosing the appropriate vaccination strategy is crucial for accelerating cervical cancer elimination. The main challenge in formulating a strategy is to optimize multi-dimensional, interdependent objectives. Limited health resources necessitate a balance between health outcomes and resource consumption, specifically, minimizing cervical cancer incidence (public health target) and maximizing health gains within budget constraints (health economics target). The strategy must also optimize the elimination timeline (timeliness target). Simultaneously reconciling these three objectives remains a formidable challenge in strategy formulation.

Research on HPV vaccination strategies can be primarily divided into two categories. One includes retrospective studies that summarize the HPV vaccination strategies in countries and regions, focusing on the content of these vaccination strategies. The other mainly focuses on evaluating HPV vaccination strategies, with an emphasis on assessing the effectiveness of the implementation strategies, as well as the models and methods used in the evaluation process.

The study evaluating an HPV vaccination strategy is usually an evaluation of a variety of pre-defined strategies, and the effectiveness/economy of each strategy are measured separately to select the optimal option. The models in these evaluations can be classified into dynamic and static models. Studies using static models primarily consider the role of HPV vaccination in preventing non-communicable diseases, such as cervical cancer, which include models such as the Markov cohort model11–13, PRIME model14, and CERVIVAC model15. Studies using dynamic models consider the impact of HPV infection and pathogens on disease and progression, such as the HPV-ADVISE model16, the mixed dynamic model17,18, and Harvard models19. Compared to static models, dynamic models consider the mutual influence between susceptible populations and estimate the herd immunity effects brought about by vaccination. However, many existing dynamic frameworks aggregate HPV subtypes to simplify computations. This structural aggregation can obscure the independent transmission dynamics of non-vaccine-targeted oncogenic strains, potentially leading to overly optimistic estimations of elimination timelines—especially when evaluating lower-valent vaccines. Furthermore, previous research has extensively explored the effectiveness and economy of pre-defined strategies, but has paid little attention to utilizing advanced optimization algorithms to actively design and develop optimal HPV vaccination pathways.

Using China as a representative case study, this research aims to address two critical challenges in cervical cancer elimination: identifying optimal vaccination strategies that balance public health targets with economic constraints and timelines, and establishing sustainable financing mechanisms based on real-world consumer demand. Specifically, we sought to determine the most cost-effective vaccination pathways under different elimination timelines and evaluate the affordability for various stakeholders, including consumers, medical insurance funds, and the government. Results

HPV vaccination strategy

The Bayesian optimization algorithm searched for the optimal vaccination strategy under six different scenarios (Supplementary Figs. S1 and S2, process of search strategy in the Supplementary Note S10). These six scenarios corresponded to the realization of the WHO goal of “elimination of cervical cancer” at different times, reducing the incidence of cervical cancer to 4 per 100,000 women within 30, 40, 50, 60, 80, and 100 years. The HPV vaccine types selected for the optimal vaccination strategy across all scenarios are domestic nonavalent vaccines. The target age for the optimal vaccination strategy is consistently 15–17 years old (named as [15, 18)). As the implementation time of the vaccination increases, the HPV vaccination coverage and ICUR of scenarios 2–6 decrease sequentially. This indicated that with sufficient strategy implementation time, lower coverage can achieve the established goals (Table 1). The deterministic sensitivity analysis indicated that the results were robust in scenarios 2–6, as all perturbed ICURs remained below three times the per capita GDP; in contrast, because the base-case ICUR in scenario 1 (30-year horizon) was already close to this threshold (approximately 210,000 yuan), some parameter perturbations resulted in loss of cost-effectiveness. Economic and clinical outcomes

Figure 2A shows the age-specific reduction in cervical cancer incidence relative to no vaccination across different time horizons. The reduction in incidence increased gradually over time and was most evident among women in the young and middle adult age groups, especially those aged approximately 18–40 years. By contrast, the reduction was relatively limited in the youngest and oldest age groups. Figure 2B shows the age-specific reduction in cervical cancer mortality relative to no vaccination. The mortality benefit appeared later than the incidence benefit and was concentrated mainly in older age groups. Figure 2C records the trend of the target number of vaccinations and the cost of each scenario after the implementation of the optimal vaccination strategy under each scenario. The optimal strategy in scenario 1 maintained the highest vaccination number, and even with only 30 years of implementation, its final cumulative vaccination number is still much higher than that of other scenarios. As the implementation time of the strategy prolongs, the vaccination number for the optimal strategy in scenarios 2–6 decreased sequentially. Although the implementation time of the strategy in scenario 6 is the longest, its cumulative vaccination population was the smallest. The cost of vaccination followed the same trend and pattern as the vaccination number.

Figure 2D shows the trend of health benefits and incremental costs over time when implementing the optimal vaccination strategy in different scenarios. The number of cervical cancers prevented, the number of cervical cancer deaths prevented, and the disability-adjusted life year (DALY) saved all increased monotonically, and the growth was slower in the early stage of strategy implementation (<20 years), followed by a faster growth rate, which generally conformed to the characteristics of an “S” function. In the early stages of strategy implementation, the values of various incremental results were very large (Fig. 2E). However, as the implementation timeline extends, the incremental cost per cervical cancer prevented, death averted, and DALY saved will exhibit a significant downward trend, eventually approaching zero or even falling below zero (becoming cost-saving) in the long term (Fig. 2E).

Beyond the visual trajectories described above, these quantitative results offer critical implementation insights for real-world vaccination programs. First, the divergence in decline trajectories across age groups in Fig. 2A, B reveals a distinct “demographic lag effect”, with younger age groups showing earlier and steeper declines in cervical cancer incidence and mortality, while older age groups exhibit more gradual declines over a longer time scale. This indicates that relying solely on vaccination cannot immediately alleviate the disease burden in the existing population, suggesting that screening interventions or catch-up campaigns must be maintained for older cohorts during the initial phase (e.g., the first 20–30 years) to fill the “protection gap.” Second, the net cost curve in Fig. 2C exhibits a significant “investment hump”: before achieving long-term savings for the medical insurance fund, the system must endure a prolonged net investment phase spanning several decades. This imposes clear requirements for the long-term planning of health fiscal budgets. Finally, Fig. 2E shows that the incremental cost per case prevented decreases exponentially over time, confirming significant increasing returns to scale as the intervention duration extends. This demonstrates that unwavering long-term adherence to high-coverage strategies is key to achieving optimal cost-effectiveness.

Financing strategy: individual payment

To obtain the necessary empirical parameters for modeling vaccine demand and assessing the financial feasibility of the proposed strategies, we conducted a multi-center field investigation to quantify consumer willingness-to-pay (WTP). The survey of WTP included 787 participants from three provinces in China: Zhejiang Province (33.04%), Hubei Province (34.18%), and Shaanxi Province (32.78%). There were 531 females (67.47%) and 256 males (32.53%). Other characteristics are shown in Supplementary Note S12 and Supplementary Table S4.

The demand curve for domestic nonavalent HPV vaccines among consumers at different income levels is shown in Fig. 3A. At any income level, as the price of HPV vaccines increases, consumer demand for nonavalent HPV vaccines decreases. Taking Scenario 2 as an example, to achieve the goal of eliminating cervical cancer within 40 years, it is necessary to ensure the coverage of the domestic nonavalent HPV vaccine for 15–17-year-old girls is 23.97% and above, and the total cost of HPV vaccination is ¥ 86,465.80 million. The price-demand function shows that under the market price of domestic nonavalent HPV vaccines (¥ 998), the coverage of consumer vaccination autonomy is only 14.01%, which is 9.96% away from achieving the target coverage of eliminating cervical cancer strategy, and there is a gap of ¥ 35,928.22 million in vaccination cost. When the vaccine price drops to ¥ 843.54, the target coverage for the optimal vaccination strategy can be achieved purely based on consumer demand.

Financing strategy: medical insurance payment

The changes in medical insurance fund expenditures under scenario 2 are shown in Fig. 3B. During the 40-year simulation period, when the medical insurance fund covered all vaccination expenses, its total expenditure showed a downward trend yearly. The medical insurance budget in 2019 would increase by ¥ 5273.25 million. Because HPV vaccination reduced the incidence of cervical cancer, the cost of cervical cancer treatment decreased correspondingly each year. Other scenarios show a similar trend. Detailed results are shown in Supplementary Fig. S4 and Tables S5–S10.

Financing strategy: individual-Medicare co-payment

Under scenario 2, based on consumers’ WTP, the actual demand for vaccines under current prices was 14.01%, which is 9.96% lower than the ideal vaccine coverage of 23.97% for cervical cancer elimination. To fill the gap in HPV vaccination costs, individual-Medicare co-payment financing strategy was introduced. When the individual burden was 58.45%, and the medical insurance burden was 41.55%, the medical insurance fund can be saved after 32 years of implementation (Fig. 3C). Adjusting the cost-sharing proportions based on the survey of consumer financing preferences (with an individual burden of 25.72% and a medical insurance burden of 74.28%), the medical insurance fund would remain in a net investment phase and fail to achieve cumulative net savings within the 40-year simulation period. Detailed results could be found in Supplementary Fig. S5 and Tables S11–S16.

Financing strategy: tripartite co-payment

The survey on consumer financing preferences for the HPV vaccine showed that consumers, government, and medical insurance should bear 45.90%, 26.19%, and 27.91% of the HPV vaccine vaccination costs. Under the principle of sharing vaccination costs based on consumer financing preference, the cumulative costs borne by medical insurance, the government, and individuals are shown in Fig. 3D, while the corresponding annual costs are presented in Supplementary Fig. S6. To achieve the goal of eliminating cervical cancer within 40 years, the government and medical insurance would need to expend a cumulative total of ¥ 22,845.00 million and ¥ 24,345.33 million. Detailed results could be found in Supplementary Tables S17–S22.

Discussion

This study used a Bayesian optimization algorithm to identify optimal HPV vaccination strategies in China across six time-horizon scenarios (30–100 years) and analyzed vaccine financing based on consumer demand and budget constraints. We found that the optimal, cost-effective (ICUR < 3 times GDP per capita) pathway consistently centers on vaccinating 15–17-year-old girls with the domestic nonavalent HPV vaccine. While some high-income countries implement gender-neutral vaccination, our algorithm-driven analysis indicates that targeting the 15–17 female cohort remains the most cost-effective entry point for China under current budget constraints. As the elimination timeline extends, the required vaccine coverage decreases; for instance, the 30-year rapid elimination (Scenario 1) necessitates significantly higher coverage and incurs a substantially larger financial burden compared to the 40-year goal (Scenario 2). While recent modeling studies, such as Shi et al.20, have emphasized the broad impact of universal catch-up vaccination across wider age groups in China, our algorithm-driven approach specifically identifies the 15–17 cohort as the optimal entry point for accelerated elimination under fiscal constraints. Ultimately, this provides a robust and feasible evidence-based framework for public health decision-making in China.

Our analysis of the consumer demand function highlights a significant price elasticity for the domestic nonavalent HPV vaccine. At the current market price, autonomous consumer demand falls short of the target coverage required for elimination (e.g., a 9.96% gap in Scenario 2), creating a substantial funding bottleneck. While centralized bulk-buying negotiations can leverage economies of scale to lower procurement costs for both purchasers and manufacturers21, price reductions alone remain insufficient to bridge this coverage gap. This price-demand mismatch exposes a critical trade-off in bipartite (individual-Medicare) financing models: aligning the cost-sharing structure with consumer willingness-to-pay (e.g., a 74.28% insurance burden) generates a substantial short-term fiscal ‘hump’ that the medical insurance fund cannot recover within a 40-year window. Consequently, relying solely on medical insurance as the primary payer is fiscally unsustainable for high-value vaccines. To ensure long-term resilience, the financing architecture necessitates a transition toward a tripartite mechanism, positioning medical insurance as a strategic co-financer augmented by government subsidies.

To bridge the funding gap and accelerate elimination, we propose a diversified policy framework. Given the fiscal constraints of single-payer systems, an architecture featuring government leadership and multi-party burden-sharing is necessitated. Strategically, government-manufacturer price negotiations can leverage economies of scale to reduce vaccine costs while maintaining corporate viability. Concurrently, incentivizing the inclusion of HPV vaccines in commercial health insurance establishes a multi-tiered payment system, thereby freeing public funds to provide targeted subsidies for low-income or underdeveloped regions. Furthermore, to mitigate the epidemiological lag associated with relying exclusively on the routine immunization of primary cohorts, we recommend a simultaneous one-time catch-up campaign for older age groups during the initial phase. This dual approach rapidly reduces population susceptibility and solidifies a herd immunity barrier.

Transmission dynamics models inevitably rely on complex parameters that are challenging to estimate accurately, potentially limiting the generalizability of the findings. The sample size of our WTP survey, while multi-centered, may not fully capture the vast socio-economic diversity across all Chinese provinces. Furthermore, given the computational time complexity constraints of our current Bayesian approach coupled with ordinary differential equations, future research should explore advanced network models—such as graph-based approaches or Laplace learning in Wasserstein space. These frameworks may offer significantly higher computational efficiency when handling unbalanced measure networks and complex population structures22,23. Additionally, our economic evaluation focused exclusively on cervical cancer incidence, omitting the vaccine’s broader protective effects against other HPV-related diseases. More comprehensive benefit analyses across diverse populations are warranted to formulate precise vaccination policies. Finally, future modeling must iteratively incorporate emerging interventions not captured in this analysis, such as single-dose regimens or novel vaccine options.

The application of optimization algorithms within the decision-making model for cervical cancer elimination ensured the methodological rigor, cost-effectiveness, and temporal precision of the proposed strategies. Concurrently, the empirical analysis of consumer demand and financing preferences provided an evidence-based framework for calibrating the distribution of multi-party financial contributions.

Methods

Study design

Based on the WHO position papers on HPV vaccines and established health economic evaluation frameworks24, we explicitly identified six core elements to construct the vaccination strategy: gender, vaccination age of student, vaccine type, vaccine dosage, vaccination site, and financing source. Specifically, the selection of vaccine types covers bivalent, quadrivalent, and nonavalent HPV vaccines. All three valencies were included as candidate options within the strategy screening scope to ensure the comprehensiveness of the analysis and the reliability of the conclusions. Six scenarios were set based on the time for achieving cervical cancer elimination, namely 30, 40, 50, 60, 80, and 100 years. The study took 2019 as the starting year for analysis, due to the availability of macroeconomic data, and to avoid the impact of the COVID-19 pandemic on the research outcomes.

To better reflect the differences in type-specific protection provided by different HPV vaccines, the model further incorporated HPV subtype groups into the age-structured propagation dynamics framework. Specifically, high-risk HPV types were classified into three subtype groups, including HPV16/18, HPV31/33/45/52/58, and other high-risk types, so that the vaccine-targeted and non-targeted oncogenic types could be represented separately in the model. For each subtype group, independent transmission and disease progression processes were described within the same population structure, while the overall cervical cancer burden was attributed across subtype groups using an approximate competing-risk approach. In this way, the model could distinguish the different long-term effects of bivalent and higher-valent vaccines on cervical cancer incidence, rather than representing vaccine efficacy as a single aggregated effect. At the same time, this grouped modeling strategy avoided the excessive state-space expansion and parameter identifiability problems that would arise if all HPV subtypes and their possible co-infection patterns were modeled explicitly. The detailed derivation of this subtype-group extension and its parameter calibration process are provided in Supplementary Notes S4 and S5.

Bayesian optimization algorithm

The vaccination strategy needs to achieve the target that the incidence of cervical cancer is less than 4 per 100,000 women, and to reduce the cost required to achieve this goal as far as possible. Here, we employed a cost-effectiveness analysis adopting a health system perspective to evaluate the economic efficiency of the vaccination strategies. The cost-effectiveness threshold was set at three times China’s 2019 per capita Gross Domestic Product (GDP), and relevant methodological details are provided in Supplementary Note S6. Furthermore, to improve the rigor of cost-effectiveness formulations in future stochastic-based infectious disease models, frameworks such as optimal transport should be considered for advanced uncertainty quantification, drawing upon successful modeling experiences in diseases like lymphatic filariasis and Lassa fever25,26. HPV vaccine demand and budget impact analysis

The study obtained the willingness to pay (WTP) of the girls’ parents for their children's HPV vaccination through a field survey. A cross-sectional survey was conducted from November 2022 to February 2023 in Zhejiang (Eastern China), Hubei (Central China), and Shaanxi (Western China). The survival function27,28 from survival analysis (Weibull regression model) was used to simulate the demand function (Supplementary Note S8). By analyzing the changes in consumers’ WTP at different vaccine prices, we aimed to clarify the consumer affordability within vaccine financing. To account for the well-documented hypothetical bias in contingent valuation, where stated intentions frequently exceed actual purchasing behavior, we applied a conservative calibration factor of 2.0 to translate stated willingness-to-pay into realistic autonomous market demand. This adjustment specifically addresses the well-documented ‘hypothetical bias’ in contingent valuation surveys—where respondents’ stated intentions often exceed their actual purchasing behavior—as recommended by the NOAA panel guidelines29. This adjustment translates the theoretical WTP into a realistic estimate of actual autonomous market demand. While the primary cost-effectiveness analysis adopts a macro-level health system perspective to inform government and medical insurance bodies, the WTP survey specifically targets the micro-level consumer perspective (parents of schoolgirls). Bridging these distinct stakeholders, adjusted for real-world behavioral conversion, allows us to construct a robust financing model. The study explored the potential impact of incorporating HPV vaccines in medical insurance on medical insurance funds through budget impact analysis, and combined consumer financing preference to analyze the multi-channel financing strategy involving consumers, government, and medical insurance. The model calculations mainly rely on epidemiological data, vaccine cost, vaccine efficacy, and national medical insurance data (Supplementary Note S9 and Supplementary Table S2). The data and sources are shown in Table 2. Based on the latest clinical guidelines for domestic nonavalent HPV vaccines, a two-dose schedule (total cost of ¥ 998) was applied for the primary target cohort (girls aged 15–17), while a three-dose schedule (total cost of ¥ 1497) was considered for older age groups in the analysis.
