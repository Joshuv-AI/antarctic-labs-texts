Real-Time IVUS–Guided Precision Stenting of the Ostial Left Anterior Descending Artery
Authors: Sri Ramaraju Namagiri, Hitesh Srinivasa Krishna Relangi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAccurate stent placement in ostial left anterior descending artery (LAD) lesions is challenging, as angiography alone often misidentifies the true ostial plane.Case SummaryA 63-year-old woman with unstable angina and significant ostial LAD stenosis underwent percutaneous coronary intervention after declining surgery. Real-time intravascular ultrasound (IVUS) imaging from the ramus ostium was used to visualize the LAD ostial plane continuously while withdrawing the stent. Precise alignment of the proximal stent edge with the true ostium was achieved and confirmed by final IVUS, demonstrating adequate expansion and absence of left main protrusion.DiscussionReal-time IVUS guidance enables direct visualization of the carina, plaque edge, and ostial plane, facilitating accurate positioning and minimizing geographic miss.Take-Home MessageContinuous IVUS visualization from a side branch provides a practical strategy for millimetric accuracy during ostial LAD stenting.Key words: intravascular ultrasound, myocardial revascularization, stents

History of Presentation and Past Medical History

A 63-year-old woman presented with a 1-week history of exertional angina that progressed to rest angina over the preceding day. She had a history of well-controlled hypertension, with no diabetes, dyslipidemia, or prior cardiovascular disease.

Differential Diagnosis

Considerations included unstable angina, non–ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction, and noncardiac chest pain.

Investigations

The patient's troponin I level was normal. Electrocardiography showed no acute ischemic changes. Echocardiography demonstrated preserved left ventricular systolic function without regional wall motion abnormalities.

Coronary angiography demonstrated ostioproximal left anterior descending artery (LAD) disease with a significant ramus lesion showing ostial sparing, preserved TIMI flow grade 3, and no significant left main coronary artery (LM) disease (Medina 0,1,0) (Figure 1, Video 1, Video 2, Video 3). The SYNTAX score was 23.

Coronary angiogram (A) RAO caudal view and (B) LAO caudal view demonstrating significant ostial LAD stenosis with preserved TIMI flow grade 3. The left main artery appears free of significant disease, and the ramus shows a discrete lesion with ostial sparing. LAO = left anterior oblique; RAO = right anterior oblique.

Management

The heart team recommended coronary artery bypass grafting, which the patient declined. A percutaneous revascularization strategy was therefore pursued, with percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) of the ostial LAD and ramus planned instead of dedicated LM bifurcation stenting because of the large ramus branch, acute angulation between the ramus and LAD, marked LM-LAD size mismatch, and the absence of significant LM disease (Videos 4 and 5).

PCI to the ramus

After adequate predilation, a 2.5 × 29 mm sirolimus-eluting stent was deployed in the ramus just distal to the ostium and was postdilated appropriately with a 2.75 × 8 mm NC (noncompliant) balloon, with good end result (Video 6).

Real-time intravascular ultrasound–guided PCI to the ostial LAD

The LAD was predilated using NC and cutting balloons. A sirolimus-eluting stent sized 3.0 × 32 mm was positioned beyond the ostium. An intravascular ultrasound (IVUS) catheter was advanced into the ramus and positioned at its ostium, allowing visualization of the LAD ostium.

With the IVUS catheter positioned at the ramus ostium, the true LAD ostial plane was defined as the cross-section corresponding to the origin of the LAD at the carinal flow divider between the LAD and ramus within the LM bifurcation, where the LAD lumen becomes continuous with the shared bifurcation lumen (Figure 2). Coaxial guide position, avoidance of deep engagement of the LM, along with IVUS catheter positioned within the ramus ostium were used to stabilize the guidewire-stent system and reduce catheter-induced motion.

Under continuous IVUS visualization, the stent was slowly withdrawn until the proximal stent edge aligned precisely with the ostial plane. During gradual withdrawal of the stent system under continuous imaging, the balloon shaft and wrapped balloon segment initially appear as low-profile structures with linear reverberation artifacts. As the proximal balloon marker enters the imaging plane, it produces a bright echogenic focus with a broad reverberation pattern but without a well-defined acoustic shadow. With further withdrawal, the proximal stent edge appears abruptly as a sharply defined circumferential echogenic ring accompanied by dense posterior acoustic shadowing due to the metallic struts (Videos 7 and 8). Continued withdrawal reveals the stent body as a continuous ring of regularly spaced echogenic reflections corresponding to the stent architecture. Thus the proximal stent edge was identified and deployed with the IVUS catheter in place (Videos 9 and 10). Postdilation was performed with a 3.0 × 8 mm NC balloon with good angiographic result (Video 11).

Preprocedural IVUS demonstrated a minimum lumen area of 2.67 mm2 (Figure 3). Final IVUS demonstrated a minimum stent area of 6.72 mm2 (Figure 4), representing approximately 95% expansion relative to the distal LAD reference segment (diameter: 2.97 mm; reference area: 7.12 mm2) (Figure 5, Video 12). The proximal reference segment in the distal LM measured approximately 4.7 mm in diameter (Figure 6). Stent expansion was satisfactory, with a symmetry index of 0.77 and adequate proximal optimization. There was no significant protrusion into the distal LM, and no edge dissection, malapposition, tissue prolapse, or geographic miss was observed. Side-branch flow remained preserved.

Outcome and Follow-Up

The procedure was uncomplicated. At the 3-month follow-up, the patient remained asymptomatic with no recurrence of angina or adverse cardiovascular events. No additional ischemia testing or repeat imaging was performed given the absence of symptoms.

Discussion

The real-time IVUS technique offers several advantages over conventional angiographic or landmark-based ostial stenting. Angiography alone may misidentify the true ostium in up to 54% of cases.1 IVUS provides precise visualization of the carina, ostial plane, LM-LAD transition, and proximal plaque edge, ensuring accurate identification of the true landing zone. Harding et al2 demonstrated that real-time IVUS guidance enables millimetric precision, with median stent protrusion limited to just 0.2 mm, and showed its feasibility across a wide variety of ostial lesions with excellent procedural success and no major complications. Fairley et al3 recently described real-time IVUS–guided ostial right coronary artery stenting in which the IVUS catheter was positioned on a guidewire within the coronary cusp to enable direct visualization of the aorto-ostial interface during deployment.

Alternative techniques for ostial LAD stent positioning include angiographic estimation of the LAD origin using multiple projections, alignment using radio-opaque stent balloon markers, side-branch wire landmarking, intentional minimal left main protrusion to ensure ostial coverage, side-branch balloon anchoring to stabilize the guiding catheter,4 stent tail wire (Szabo technique),5 and postdeployment intravascular imaging confirmation; however, these approaches rely primarily on indirect angiographic cues and may be influenced by vessel overlap, foreshortening, or guide catheter movement, potentially resulting in suboptimal ostial alignment.

Beyond procedural precision, the superiority of IVUS-guided PCI is well established: Both the ULTIMATE trial6 and the IVUS-XPL randomized trial7 demonstrated significantly better stent expansion, lower restenosis rates, and reduced target lesion revascularization compared with angiography-guided PCI. Collectively, these data support real-time IVUS–guided ostial stenting as a reproducible, highly accurate strategy that enhances both procedural control and long-term clinical outcomes.

Conclusions

Real-time IVUS guidance from a side branch can facilitate accurate ostial LAD stent placement by enabling direct visualization of the true ostial plane during deployment.
