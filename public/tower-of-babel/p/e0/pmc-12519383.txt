Exploring potential target drugs for vision-threatening diabetic retinopathy using the human plasma proteome: a Mendelian randomisation study
Authors: Yanhua Liang, Yu Huang, Xiayin Zhang, Zijing Du, Yaxin Wang, Honghua Yu, Yijun Hu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractBackgroundDiabetic retinopathy (DR) is a common microvascular complication of diabetes that has the potential to progress to vision-threatening DR (VTDR) even in the absence of symptoms. Plasma proteins in response to physiological and pathological conditions in the body may be intricately connected to the initiation of VTDR.ObjectiveTo determine the causality between 4489 plasma proteins and the risk of VTDR to explore potential therapeutic targets for VTDR.DesignA Mendelian randomisation (MR) study.SettingA two-sample MR study was performed to investigate the causality between plasma proteins and VTDR.ParticipantsGenetic information on plasma proteins and VTDR in European populations from IEU OpenGWAS.Primary and secondary outcome measuresMediation analysis was performed to evaluate the indirect impacts of plasma proteins on VTDR via related risk variables. Introduction

According to the International Diabetes Federation, the prevalence of diabetes in 2021 was 10.5% (536.6 million people) worldwide in individuals aged 20–79 years and is expected to increase to 12.2% (783.2 million people) by 2045.1 Diabetic retinopathy (DR) is a heterogeneous microvascular complication of diabetes, encompassing mild non-proliferative DR (PDR) and sight-threatening phenotypes.2 Among these, vision-threatening DR (VTDR), defined by severe non-PDR (NPDR), PDR and/or significant diabetic macular oedema (DME), is the most clinically urgent subtype. It directly threatens visual function and imposes a disproportionate global burden. Unlike milder forms of DR, which may remain asymptomatic and stable for years, VTDR is characterised by advanced pathological processes, including retinal neovascularisation, fibrous membrane formation and macular fluid accumulation. These mechanisms are driven by distinct molecular pathways, such as vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF)-mediated angiogenesis, inflammation and extracellular matrix (ECM) degradation, which differ from the early microvascular dysfunction observed in non-VTDR. A meta-analysis has reported a global prevalence of VTDR of 6.17% in diabetics,3 imposing growing burdens on a global scale. Thereby, it is pressingly urgent to find novel intervention strategies for VTDR. Although multiple VTDR risk factors, such as body mass index (BMI), hypertension and glycated haemoglobin (HbA1c),4,6 have been explored, new risk factors or effective biomarkers for VTDR warrant further exploration in order to identify new intervention strategies or new therapeutic targets. Plasma proteins hold significance in numerous biological processes,7 8 making them therapeutic targets for diverse diseases.9 10 In 2017, about 75% of drugs approved by the FDA were designed to target human proteins.11 The link between plasma proteins and diabetes12 or retinal disease13 has been well-established, indicating that the alterations in plasma proteins may also influence the development and progression of VTDR. While prior studies have identified plasma proteins associated with diabetic microvascular complications, including DR,12 our study extends this work by explicitly focusing on VTDR. Unlike broader analyses of DR, we explored causality across VTDR phenotypes (severe NPDR, PDR, DME) and integrated mediation analysis via established risk factors, revealing that pathways influence VTDR through proteins. Additionally, our focus on druggable targets provides a translational framework absent in prior studies, bridging proteomic discoveries with potential therapeutic applications for vision preservation.

Accessing the relationship between plasma proteins and VTDR may encounter challenges as plasma protein level varies in different internal or external environmental conditions. Consequently, inferring a causal relationship can be difficult. Mendelian randomisation (MR) is increasingly used to infer a causal link between an exposure and an outcome14 15 by using genetic variants as instrumental variables (IVs). MR studies can draw more causal inferences than traditional research. Since genetic variants are defined at birth, this reduces bias in reverse causality, thus raising the likelihood of the causality found in MR studies.16 Recent genome-wide association analyses (GWAS) on plasma proteins have identified protein quantitative trait loci (pQTLs), which reflect the inherited levels of plasma proteins and are not susceptible to environmental conditions.17 18 Incorporating pQTLs into MR analysis, the causal effect of blood plasma and diseases can be estimated.17 19 In the current work, two-sample MR analysis was performed using genetic IVs obtained from 4489 circulating plasma proteins and genetic associations of VTDR to investigate the causal effects of plasma proteins on VTDR. After the evaluation of the causal links between VTDR risk factors and plasma proteins, a mediation analysis was conducted to evaluate the indirect impacts of plasma proteins on VTDR via these risk factors. Finally, we summarise the current research on these proteins and explore potential therapeutic targets for VTDR.

Methods

Study design

Given the two-sample MR design, GWAS summary statistics were used to estimate the causal effect of exposures on outcomes (figure 1). MR design is grounded on three fundamental presumptions.20 First and foremost, genetic variants are robustly associated with the exposure. Second, the genetic variants can only show a relationship with the exposure, independent of confounders. Third, genetic variants influence the outcomes exclusively via exposure.

VTDR and risk factors

Applicable GWAS information was searched in the IEU OpenGWAS (https://gwas.mrcieu.ac.uk/) using the phrase ‘diabetic retinopathy’. Three datasets from the FinnGen Round 5 release were included: finn-b-DM_BCKGRND_RETINA_NONPROLIF (severe non-proliferative DR), finn-b-DM_RETINA_PROLIF (proliferative DR) and finn-b-DM_MACULOPATHY (DME). The control groups for VTDR phenotypes (severe NPDR, PDR, DME) in FinnGen datasets were healthy individuals without diabetes, based on self-reported medical history and electronic health records, excluding diabetes diagnoses (International Classification of Diseases (ICD-10 codes E10–E14). This design identified protein associations specific to VTDR pathogenesis by comparing case groups (patients with diabetes with VTDR). The FinnGen study is a large-scale genomics initiative that has analysed over 500 000 Finnish biobank samples and correlated genetic variation with health data to understand disease mechanisms and predispositions. The project is a collaboration between research organisations and biobanks within Finland and international industry partners. To control for population stratification, associations from European ancestry individuals were used for protein IVs, VTDR and risk factors.

For risk factors, a literature review in PubMed with keywords “risk factors” and “vision-threatening diabetic retinopathy” identified five relevant factors.221,31 GWAS summary data were obtained from the IEU OpenGWAS, including BMI (GWAS ID ukb-a-248), sourced from Neale Lab’s UK Biobank Round 1 results (September 2017 release), covering 336 107 European participants32; HbA1c (GWAS ID ebi-a-GCST90014006), derived from a study (PMID: 34017140), including 389 889 European individuals33; Estimated glomerular filtration rate (GWAS ID ebi-a-GCST90103634), based on a dataset (PMID: 34272381) of 1 004 040 European participants34; Microalbuminuria (GWAS ID ieu-a-1097), sourced from a study (PMID: 26631737), involving 54 116 European individuals35; and age at diabetes diagnosis (GWAS ID ukb-b-7435), obtained from the OpenGWAS database API with 20 911 European participants.36

GWAS summary data of proteins

GWAS summary data of plasma proteins were obtained from the IEU OpenGWAS website (table 1). The study about Prot-a was approved by the National Research Ethics Service (11/EE/0538).37 The individuals in the study about Prot-b were recruited in seven different centres in Finland, France, Italy, the Netherlands and Sweden. The relevant permits were given by ethical committees for each of the seven different centres as follows: Kuopio Research Institute of Exercise Medicine, Finland; Kuopio University Hospital, Finland; Karolinska Institute, Stockholm; University Medical Center Groningen, Groningen, the Netherlands; Groupe Hospitalier Pitié-Salpêtrière, Unité de Prévention Cardiovasculaire, Paris, France; Dipartimento di Scienze Farmacologiche e Biomolecolari, Milan; University of Perugia, Italy.38 GWAS summary statistics for each plasma protein were investigated. The included datasets must contain genetic variants that have been confirmed statistically significant across the genome to meet the first assumption.39 In the end, proteins with at least one Single Nucleotide Polymorphism (SNP) that reached a genome-wide significant threshold (p<5.0×10−8) in the corresponding GWAS were selected. Consequently, 4489 proteins that had at least one IV remained for MR analysis.

MR analysis

MR analysis was grounded in three key assumptions. First, genetic variants are robustly associated with the exposure (plasma protein levels). Second, genetic variants are independent of confounders and do not influence the outcome (VTDR) via pathways unrelated to the exposure (no horizontal pleiotropy). Third, genetic variants affect the outcome exclusively through the exposure (no reverse causality). MR analyses were implemented using the R package TwoSampleMR V.0.5.6.16 To ensure the stability of the exposure association (Assumption 1), MR analysis was conducted only on SNPs with p<5×10–8. Furthermore, variants that were in high linkage disequilibrium with the lead SNPs were excluded to ensure that only independent genetic variants were kept for MR analysis (using a clumping r2=0.001). The included SNPs were then standardised, indicating that an allele was responsible for the impact of an SNP on exposure and result. Two-sample MR analysis was implemented using four methods: MR Egger, weight median, inverse variance weighted (IVW) and weighted mode. The IVW method was the major analytical method to identify causal associations, p values <0.05 were considered significant. A consistent trend in the results of the four methods and the significant result in the IVW method suggested a causality between exposure and outcome. In cases where GWAS datasets contained significant missing information or lacked sufficient quality control measures, datasets with extensive missing data or inadequate SNP coverage may have been excluded from the analysis to ensure data integrity.

Heterogeneity and sensitivity analysis

MR-Egger and MR-PRESSO were used to assess horizontal pleiotropy. A p>0.05 indicated no significant indicators of gene pleiotropy, namely no horizontal pleiotropy. Furthermore, Cochran’s Q test was performed, and p<0.05 implied heterogeneity, where point random effects were used for further analyses.

Mediation analysis

Mediation analysis was conducted on proteins that were found to be causally related to VTDR, as well as on those identified as risk factors, in order to ascertain the complete causal pathways of proteins influencing VTDR. The mix of direct and indirect effects via one or more mediators is represented as the total effect when one is exposed to the outcome. The total effect was captured by the standard univariable MR analysis. Using two-step MR results, we identified both total and indirect effects. The product technique was then used to estimate the beta of the indirect impact. The total effect was between proteins and VTDR (β3). The coefficient product method was used as the main method for indirect effects (β1×β2). Therefore, the proportion mediated by plasma proteins via risk factors on VTDR was calculated: ((β1×β2)/β3). Furthermore, we estimated the indirect effect’s SE and CIs using the Delta technique.40

Druggability and potential roles in VTDR

We explored protein interactions through the string website (https://cn.string-db.org/), with a medium confidence interaction score threshold of >0.7 (score >0.7 indicates medium confidence) as screening criteria. This threshold was set to balance capturing biologically relevant interactions and minimising false positives, in line with common practices in similar proteomic studies. The possible target drugs corresponding to proteins associated with VTDR risk factors were searched through the DrugBank website (https://go.drugbank.com/) and DGIdb website (https://www.dgidb.org/).

Patient and public involvement

Patients and/or the public were not involved in the design, or conduct, or reporting, or dissemination plans of this research.

Results

Identification of potential VTDR-related proteins

In our analysis, 4489 plasma proteins were searched to assess if they were causally associated with VTDR outcomes (figure 1 and online supplemental table S1). 92 plasma proteins had causal links with VTDR, with 28 causally related to severe NPDR, 42 to PDR and 33 to DME. HLA-DQA2 (HLA class II histocompatibility antigen, DQ alpha 2 chain; prot-a-1347) and MICB (MHC class I polypeptide-related sequence B; prot-c-5102_55_3) were causally correlated with all three VTDR phenotypes. CD38 (ADP-ribosyl cyclase/cyclic ADP-ribose hydrolase 2; prot-a-276), C4 (Complement C4; prot-c-4481_34_2), CA10 (Carbonic anhydrase-related protein 10; prot-a-326) and CPA4 (Carboxypeptidase A4; prot-a-643) had causal effects on both PDR and DME. PSD (PH and SEC7 domain-containing protein 1; prot-a-2398) and TBCA (Tubulin-specific chaperone A; prot-a-2930) had causal effects on both severe NPDR and DME. PTGR1 (Prostaglandin reductase 1; prot-a-2428) had a causal impact on both severe NPDR and PDR.

Identification of potential VTDR-related risk factors

The causal links between VTDR risk factors and VTDR outcomes were analysed. IVs were extracted from GWAS summary data in European populations for five VTDR risk factors (figure 1). MR analysis noted two risk factors (BMI, HbA1C) associated with an elevated risk of VTDR (online supplemental table S2).

Identification of proteins associated with VTDR risk factors

MR analysis then assessed the causal links between 92 potential VTDR-related proteins and two VTDR-related risk factors. Ultimately, 22 proteins were linked to one or more VTDR risk factors (online supplemental table S3). Elevated BMI was associated with genetically dictated higher levels of ARL1 (OR (95% CI)=1.0689 (1.0350 to 1.1039), p=0.0001). However, genetically determined higher levels of MRPL33, MMP8 and BST1 were related to decreased BMI (MRPL33: OR (95% CI)=0.9878 (0.9795 to 0.9962), p=0.0046; MMP8: OR (95% CI)=0.9865 (0.9783 to 0.9947), p=0.0013; BST1: OR (95% CI)=0.9942 (0.9894 to 0.9991), p=0.0191). Genetically higher levels of SDF2L1 were substantially correlated with higher levels of HbA1c (OR (95% CI)=1.086 (1.0626 to 1.1099), p=0.0000). Besides, genetically higher levels of ARL1 and MSMP were correlated with a higher risk of metformin medication (ARL1: OR (95% CI)=1.0053 (1.0029 to 1.0076), p=0.0000; MSMP: OR (95% CI)=1.0033 (1.0009 to 1.0058), p=0.0081). Overall, these risk factors potentially mediate the protein-VTDR relationships because of the consistent direction of the protein-risk factor link and the protein-VTDR link.

Mediation analysis of protein effects on VTDR through risk factors

To find the impacts of proteins on VTDR outcomes through risk factors, we performed mediation analysis using effect estimates from two-step MR analysis combined with the total effect estimated in primary MR analysis. As a result, five proteins were associated with both VTDR and its risk factors. Specifically, 20.3% of the ARL1 effect and 9.3% of the MRPL33 effect on DME were mediated by BMI; 4.8% of the MMP8 effect and 4.5% of the BST1 effect on PDR were mediated by BMI (figure 2). Additionally, 16.4% of the SDF2L1 effect on DME was mediated by HbA1c (online supplemental figure S1).

Druggability of VTDR-associated proteins and their potential roles in VTDR

With an interaction score >0.7 as the threshold, PPI analysis was performed on five proteins with mediating relationships, and the PPI network was mapped for each protein (online supplemental figure S2).

Based on PPI analysis, MMP8 and BST1 were screened, which are intricately related to the pathogenesis of PDR. Matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs) are extensively involved in inflammation, tumour invasion and metastasis41 and can react with diverse substrates, including ECM, cell surface receptors, cytokines and chemokines.42 MMP8, also known as collagenase-2, was initially found in neutrophils.43 Various tumour studies44,46 have found that MMP8 essentially interacts with cell adhesion molecules to initiate the cleavage of cell signalling ligands and receptors. Bone marrow stromal cell antigen 1 (BST-1) belongs to the CD38 family. Nonetheless, different from CD38 in tissue distribution, BST-1 can be expressed in various types of cells, such as bone marrow stromal cells, mesothelial cells and umbilical vein endothelial cells, but not in the brain tissue.47 BST1 is indispensable in the adhesion, migration and invasion into the peritoneum of ovarian cancer cells,48 as well as the regulation of humoral immune response,49 white blood cell aggregation,50 adhesion and trans-endothelial cell migration of neutrophils.51 52

In addition, we searched for possible target drugs of these five proteins (MMP8, BST1, ARL1, MRPL33 and SDF2L1) associated with VTDR risk factors through the DrugBank website and DGldb website. Only seven kinds of drugs that interact with MMP8 were found on the DGldb website (table 2), three of which were regulatory approved (DOXYCYCLINE ANHYDROUS; DOXYCYCLINE CALCIUM; COLLAGENASE CLOSTRIDIUM HISTOLYTICUM-AAES), and the other four drugs were unapproved (REBIMASTAT; ILOMASTAT; CTS-1027; CIPEMASTAT). Rebimastat has been clarified as an underlying inhibitor of the SARS-CoV-2 replication by interacting with the Mpro enzyme.53 Doxycycline is an MMP13 inhibitor and is useful for treating periodontitis and diabetic foot.54 Ilomastat-Cyclodextrin (CD) may be formulated as an eye drop against fibrositis, which may benefit scar prevention in various conditions, like glaucoma filtration surgery.55 Animal studies showed that CTS-1027 treatment relieved liver injury and liver fibrosis in BDL mice.56 Cipemastat could lower mortality and brain injury in experimental pneumococcal meningitis.57 Collagenase clostridium histolyticum-aaes may be useful for treating thigh cellulite in women.58

Discussion

RCTs are widely recognised as the benchmark for establishing causal links in epidemiological research owing to their special advantages in reducing confounding bias.59 However, performing numerous RCTs in clinical research often poses challenges due to stringent inclusion and exclusion criteria, ethical limitations and high costs. MR can use genetic differences related to the exposure to assess possible causal links between modifiable risk factors and outcomes, thus reducing biases from confounding variables in epidemiological studies, such as reverse causality. By integrating pQTLs with MR, protein biomarkers for VTDR can be identified, and possible VTDR treatment targets can be identified by following drug target analysis.

Two-sample MR analysis was adopted to affirm the causal link between 4489 plasma proteins and VTDR. 92 proteins were identified to be associated with VTDR, with 45 showing protection and 47 associated with higher risk. Furthermore, two-sample MR analysis was used to determine the causal links between VTDR risk factors and VTDR, with six factors identified. Then we delved into the causal connections of the 92 potential VTDR-related proteins with two VTDR risk factors. As a result, 22 proteins were associated with one or more VTDR risk factors. Mediation analysis illustrated the indirect effects of plasma proteins (ARL1, MRPL33, MMP8, BST1, MSMP, SDF2L1) on VTDR via risk factors, which were linked to VTDR outcomes and risk factors. At last, we explored protein interactions and found seven possible target drugs for VTDR.

MMPs, also known as matrixins for their roles in degrading and clearing ECM, are zinc and calcium-dependent proteases of the metzincin family.60 Our genetic evidence unveiled that upregulation of MMP8 protein expression in plasma is protective against PDR, and higher MMP8 levels are associated with lower BMI. However, mRNA levels of MMP-9, MMP-2 and MMP-14 were elevated in retinal tissues of diabetic rats after 12 weeks of modelling, and MMP-9 levels in retinal endothelial cells were increased on high-glucose stimulation.61 Abu El-Asrar et al examined MMP levels in the vitreous fluid of PDR patients and controls and found that MMP-1, MMP-7, MMP-9 and VEGF were markedly elevated in vitreous samples of PDR patients.62 The observed outcomes can be attributed to two plausible reasons. First, MMPs may have different effects on DR, and such effects are intricately linked across different members of MMPs. A study of diabetic foot ulcers revealed that MMP-9 hindered diabetic wound healing, whereas MMP-8 exhibited a beneficial impact.63 Another explanation is the rescue mechanism. One study found opposite miR-1228 expression in plasma and tissue samples from colorectal patients, which may be since exocytosis miR-1228 was excreted from the circulation to further rescue tumour cells from tumour-suppressing miR-1228.64 MMP8 upregulation in the vitreous fluid of DR patients may be the result of the body’s reparative effect on local tissue repair. DR is one of the serious microvascular complications of diabetes mellitus, and PDR significantly jeopardises the vision of diabetic patients. Therefore, early diagnosis and treatment assume utmost importance. At present, the primary treatment modalities for DR are retinal laser and intravitreal injection of anti-VEGF drugs. However, these therapeutic options have certain limitations. Hence, it is imperative to explore novel alternative or complementary therapies. Currently, the primary therapeutic agents for DME and PDR are anti-VEGF drugs, and the most studied targets are MMP-2, MMP-9 and VEGF due to their crucial roles in the pathogenesis of retinopathy. These results suggest that MMP8 could potentially serve as a promising therapeutic target for PDR.

BST-1, also called CD157, a single-chain cell-surface glycoprotein with a 318-amino-acid signalling sequence, possesses various enzymatic activities and downstream intracellular pathways that regulate the immune response.65 Under physiological contexts, CD157 is crucial in regulating leucocyte adhesion, migration and haemocyte exudation based on its ability to bind to high-affinity-selected ECM components in the heparin-binding domain. This binding makes CD157 interact with β1 and β2 integrins and drives the organisation of multimolecular complexes, which promote the transmission of external-internal synergistic signals for optimal cellular adhesion and migration under physiological and pathological conditions.66

CD157-positive vascular endothelial stem cells (VESCs) drive vascular regeneration, and different populations of CD157-positive VESCs have recently been identified in the rat retina and choroid.67 Our analysis disclosed that a reduced risk of PDR was linked to higher levels of BST1. RPE cell migration is an initiation stage in proliferative vitreoretinopathy (PVR). When RPE cells encounter reticular vitreous fibres or reach the retinal surface, they begin to lose their original morphological characteristics and transition from a quiescent state to an active state, thus proliferating and synthesising ECM. Integrin-mediated adhesion of RPE cells to ECM proteins is implicated in the pathogenesis of PVR.68 CD157 can recruit integrins and other transmembrane molecules to form macromolecular complexes, which play significant roles as molecular partners in signal transduction, migration and adhesion.69 Current findings could pave the way for further exploration and management of ocular vascular disease.

DrugBank database and the DGldb database were investigated to assess the druggability of the candidate protein targets. Only seven drugs were found to interact with the MMP8 gene on the DGldb website, of which Ilomastat-CD and CTS-1027 act as antifibrotic agents. The pathogenesis of PDR involves neovascularisation and fibrous membrane formation. Thereby, further molecular experiments or animal experiments can be conducted to explore the preventive or therapeutic drugs for PDR.

Our findings provide a rationale for targeting MMP8 in VTDR. Existing drugs that are known to inhibit MMP8 (eg, doxycycline) offer a framework for translational studies, particularly given their favourable safety profiles in systemic administration. For instance, doxycycline can reduce retinal inflammation and ECM degradation in preclinical models, which aligns with its potential to hinder VTDR progression. Additionally, investigational agents like ilomastat (formulated as eye drops for fibrosis) highlight the promise of localised MMP8 inhibition to minimise systemic side effects, a critical consideration for ocular therapies.

Furthermore, the mediation of VTDR risk by BMI and HbA1c underscores the importance of integrating protein-targeted therapies with lifestyle or glycaemic interventions. Proteins that act through these risk factors, such as ARL1 and SDF2L1, may serve as adjunct targets for precision medicine approaches that combine pharmacologic modulation with metabolic control.

There are several limitations. First, the study sample was restricted to individuals of European ancestry (eg, FinnGen and UK Biobank datasets), which may limit the generalisability of the results to other ethnic groups. Second, control groups for VTDR were healthy individuals without diabetes rather than patients with diabetes without VTDR, potentially introducing confounding effects from diabetes-related factors (eg, proteins associated with diabetes severity could be misattributed to VTDR). However, the MR design mitigated this bias by using genetic variants (pQTLs) independent of diabetes-related confounders (clumped at r²<0.001, Section 2.4). Notably, 45 of 92 VTDR-related proteins showed protective effects (lower VTDR risk with higher protein levels), a direction inconsistent with typical diabetes-VTDR associations, suggesting potential specificity to VTDR pathogenesis. Larger, ethnically diverse studies with diabetic control groups are needed to validate these findings.

Our conclusions suggest possible therapeutic targets for VTDR, highlighting the significance of proteomics in identifying drug targets. More research is needed to evaluate the feasibility of the six proteins that have been identified as possible targets for therapeutic intervention of VTDR. Furthermore, due to the expanding scope of proteomics platforms and more studies on non-European populations, more pharmacological targets for VTDR therapy may surface.
