Safety evaluation of a fungal beta-glucanase
Authors: Dnyaneshwar Jondhale, Corrine Harris, Gregory S. Ladics
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Pig and poultry feed are often included with beta-glucanase to alleviate the anti-nutritional impacts of beta-glucans found in many cereal grains. Safety studies were conducted on beta-glucanase, including skin and eye irritation, dermal sensitization, a 90-day rat oral subchronic study, and genotoxicity, to evaluate the safety of a beta-glucanase for use as an animal feed additive. Beta-glucanase is produced by fermentation with a fungal (Trichoderma reesei) production strain expressing a wild-type fungal endoglucanase (egl2) gene to overexpress the endoglucanase (referred to as beta-glucanase throughout the paper) enzyme. Beta-glucanase was predicted to be non-irritant (No Category) to the skin or eyes in reconstructed human epidermis tissues (RhE) and reconstructed human cornea-like epithelium (RhCE), respectively. Beta-glucanase was tested in vitro in the direct peptide reactivity assay (DPRA), the KeratinoSens™ assay and the human cell line activation test (h-CLAT). Using the 2 out of 3 approach, beta-glucanase was not a dermal sensitizer based on the negative KeratinoSens™ and h-CLAT assays. In vitro genotoxicity testing confirmed beta-glucanase to be non-genotoxic. In the 90-day subchronic study, rats were administered beta-glucanase daily via oral gavage at dose-levels of 0 (Milli-Q® water), 250, 500 or 1000 mg total organic solids (TOS)/kg bodyweight (bw)/day (equivalent to 0; 42,102; 84,205 and 168,410 units (U)/kg bw/day, respectively). No test item-related adverse effects were observed. A no-observed-adverse-effect level (NOAEL) for beta-glucanase was established at 1000 mg TOS/kg body weight/day, the highest test concentration. Based on this NOAEL and an estimate of consumption determined from the proposed inclusion of the beta-glucanase in feed at the maximum recommended level (426 U/kg), a margin of safety value of 5681 was calculated based on highest expected daily feed intake in broilers, the target species with highest feed intake relative to body weight. The findings support the safe use of beta-glucanase as an animal feed additive.

Introduction

Commercial diets containing wheat, barley, and other cereal grains for monogastric animals (pigs, poultry) can present challenges with anti-nutritional factors, like beta-glucans [1], [2]. Beta-glucans are a soluble non-starch polysaccharide, consisting of monomers in a linear chain with mixed 1,4 and 1,3 linkages. The non-consecutive 1,3 linkages contribute to the solubility and the high water-binding capacity of beta-glucan that interferes with nutrient digestion and absorption in the small intestine. Endogenous enzymes cannot hydrolyze beta-glucans, which reduces feed efficiency and weight gain in production animals through increased intestinal viscosity, slowed peristalsis and reduced nutrient availability. Pig and poultry feed often include beta-glucanase to hydrolyze beta-glucans, reducing viscosity of the digesta, restoring normal peristalsis and helping improve nutrient availability of the diet [1], [2]. Additionally, beta-glucanase and other non-starch polysaccharide enzymes added to monogastric diets alter the gastrointestinal microbiota, supporting the proliferation of beneficial microorganisms and suppressing the growth of pathogenic bacteria in the large intestine [3], [4], [5], [6], [7], [8]. Xylanase and beta-glucanase supplementation increases villi height [5], [8] and crypt depth in the intestine, while also increasing cecal short-chain fatty acids and cecal microbiota concentration [7], [8]. Weaned pigs see similar changes in villus height and decreased fecal coliforms and intestinal mucosal microphages [6], [8]. Multiple studies also demonstrated reduction of pathogenic E. coli resulting from enzyme supplementation, expanding the possible uses of enzymes beyond digestibility enhancers to zootechnical feed additives [5], [6].

Beta-glucanase bio efficacy in pig and poultry feed is well recognized [1], [2], [3], [4], [5], [6], [7], [8], [9]. Enzymes focused on removing the anti-nutritive effects of non-starch polysaccharides, like beta-glucans, represent the first wave of commercial enzyme application to animal feed. Over time, enzyme applications have expanded to consider other nutrients and even altering the gut microflora to influence better health outcomes in animal production [8], [10]. The beta-glucanase enzyme described in this paper is an endoglucanase (EC 3.2.1.4) that performs endohydrolysis of 1,4-β-D glucosidic linkages in cellulose, lichenin and cereal β-D-glucans.

This study aimed to evaluate the safety of a fungal beta-glucanase as a feed additive using a battery of toxicological assessments in compliance with OECD guidelines. The battery of GLP compliant studies included a 90-day oral gavage toxicity study using rats [11], in vitro genotoxic studies [12], [13], skin sensitization [DPRA [14], KeratinoSens™ [15] and h-CLAT [16]] and irritation studies [Skin Irritation [17] and Eye Irritation [18]].

Materials and methods

Test item / Beta-glucanase preparation

The experimental beta-glucanase (referred to as beta-glucanase throughout the paper), was developed and manufactured by International Flavors & Fragrances Inc. (New York, NY, USA) as described below. The T. reesei production strain was developed using recombinant DNA techniques, by inactivating genes encoding cellobiohydrases 1 and 2 (cbh1 and cbh2) and endoglucanase 1 and 2 (egl1 and egl2) in the host T. reesei strain. DNA encoding endoglucanase 2 was then placed under the expression signals of an endogenous T. reesei cbh1 gene to produce an expression cassette that was integrated by polyethylene glycol (PEG)-mediated transformation into the host genome. This resulted in overproduction of the T. reesei endoglucanase enzyme, using a known safe T. reesei host strain. Following fermentation and removal of the cell debris and production strain, ultrafiltration with a MWCO of 10,000 Daltons was utilized to concentrate and desalt liquid beta-glucanase to produce the test item. The test item was stored at −20ºC. The beta-glucanase was analyzed and found to be free from heavy metal and microbial contamination. The beta-glucanase UFC (ultra-filtered concentrate) contained 253.94 mg/ml total protein, with a specific gravity of 1.089 g/ml and total organic solids (TOS) comprising 26.80 %, [Silliker Laboratories (Texas, USA)]. Activity of the test item was 45,133 units (U) per gram, where 1 mg TOS contained activity of 168.41 U (1 mg TOS = 0.870 mg total protein). One unit (U) was equivalent to the amount of enzyme releasing 3 µmol of reducing sugar equivalents expressed as glucose from barley glucan per minute at pH 5.0 and 50ºC. This was determined by utilizing an internal assay method, the DNS (Dinitrosalicylic Acid) reducing sugar method. The beta-glucanase was stable throughout the study period as confirmed analytically.

90-day rat oral gavage toxicity study

The study facility had accreditation of the Association for Assessment and Accreditation of Laboratory Animal Care (AAALAC). Animal care activities were according to guidelines of Committee for Control and Supervision of Experiments on Animals (CCSEA), India and reviewed and approved by test facility’s Institutional Animal Ethics Committee (IAEC) vide proposal number 074/November-2023 dated 03rd November.

Animal diets

Throughout the experimental period including the acclimatization period, animals were fed ad libitum with Altromin Rat/Mice Maintenance diet (Lot No. 202402150606) manufactured by Altromin Spezialfutter GmbH & Co. KG, Germany. Polycarbonate bottles with stainless steel sipper tubes were used for drinking water. Drinking water was exposed to ultraviolet rays in Eureka Forbes, India, manufactured Aquaguard® on-line water filter-cum-purifier and provided ad libitum. The water was sourced from deep bore-well and passed through activated charcoal filter before exposure to ultraviolet rays.

Dosing formulations

Test item dose levels were prepared by diluting the thawed stock solution in 0 %, 25 % or 50 % ultrapure grade of water (distilled water) and tested against the control vehicle (100 % distilled water). The rats received test item at 0 (distilled water), 250, 500 and 1000 mg TOS/kg body weight/day dose levels based on the analyzed TOS of 26.80 %. Equivalent activity units of dose levels were 0; 42,102; 84,205; and 168,410 units (U)/kg bw/day, respectively. OECD limit dose of 1000 mg/kg/day and subsequent dilutions were considered to select the dose-levels. The highest dose of 1000 mgTOS/kg/day was selected as enzymes are not expected to be toxic by the oral gavage route [19], [20]. Before use, dosing formulation preparations were kept at + 2 ºC to + 8 ºC and were used within the analytically established stability period. Dose formulations were collected and analysed for target dose levels on three occasions, beginning, middle and end of the experiment. Test item in the dose formulation was digested with Turbotherm digestion unit (Gerhardt Digestion Unit: Make-Gerhardt, Model-Turbotherm S) and the samples were auto titrated with Vapodest 500 distillation system (Vapodest-500 Distillation Unit: Make-Gerhardt, Model-Vap 500).

Test animals and dosing

Test animals used in the study were five to six-week-old CD Sprague-Dawley rats and were received from Hylasco Biotechnology Pvt. Ltd. (Medchal, Telangana, India). Total 90 rats (45 of each sex) were kept under acclimatization for 7 days. Post acclimatization, 80 animals (40 male and 40 female) were randomized to one of four dose levels (10 animals/sex/group) as per OECD Test Guideline 408. It was ensured that all the animals were of sound health and approximately of equal body weight (Males: 158.09–200.06 g, Females: 140.24 132–172.15 g). Provantis™ software system (Version 10.1.0.1, Instem LSS, Staffordshire ST150SD, UK) was used for randomization. Same sex rats (2 per cage) were housed in standard polysulfone cages with stainless-steel top grill and solid floor. These cages had feeders to offer the pelleted feed and provisions to keep drinking water bottles (polycarbonate water bottle with stainless-steel sippers). Experimental room was maintained at 20.1–24.9 °C temperature and 55–69 % relative humidity. A 12 h light-dark cycle with ventilation at 13.4–13.8 air changes/h was maintained throughout the study period. For bedding steam sterilized clean corn cob (Rowan Agro Nature Pvt. Ltd., Vijayapura, Karnataka, India) was used. Post acclimatization, rats received vehicle or beta-glucanase (3.43 ml/kg body weight) daily by oral gavage for 90 days. Each day, animals were dosed approximately at the same time (±2 h) and did not affect the validity of the study.

Animal observations and ophthalmology, animal body weights and feed consumptions

Daily animal observation performed post dosing and included changes in appearance, animal behavior, clinical signs and neurological changes whereas observations for morbidity and mortality were made two times a day. A thorough clinical examination was done on day 1 prior to the beta-glucanase administration and thereafter at weekly intervals (±2 days) during the study period. Individual animal weights were recorded at the initiation of the treatment and per week thereafter (±2 days). Prior to necropsy, fasting body weights were recorded. Feed consumption was measured per week (±2 days) during the study period and used to derive average feed consumption (g/rat/day). A direct ophthalmoscope was used for ophthalmological examinations which were conducted before start of the dosing and at the end of the dosing period (Day 89). 1 % Tropicamide solution (Sunways (India) Pvt. Ltd.) was used to induce the mydriasis.

Neurobehavioral test battery

A battery of neurobehavioral tests was carried out. The observer was not blinded to the group during the evaluations. It consisted of predefined assessment forming a functional observation battery (FOB) and motor activity (MA) assessment. All the animals were subjected to these evaluations on day 81 (males) and day 82 (females) of the dosing. The FOB parameters were observations in standard home cage, outside the home cage, in open field arena, during the removal of animal from cage. Further, FOB covered observations during handling, grip strength, body temperatures and landing hind limb foot splay. An automated animal activity measuring system (Make: Columbus Instruments) was used to evaluate the amount of spontaneous movement for 30 min. In the open field arena rats were assessed for sensory reactivity.

Hematological evaluation and blood coagulation

Blood samples were collected from animals following an overnight fasting. Under isoflurane anesthesia, approximately 4.0 ml blood was collected by puncturing retro-orbital sinus using a fine capillary tub. Approximately, 0.7 ml blood sample was collected and mixed with potassium salt of EDTA as an anticoagulant. Hematological parameters were analyzed using the ADVIA 2120i hematology analyzer (Siemens, USA). For coagulation profiling, around 0.5 ml blood was combined with 3.2 % trisodium citrate solution, followed by centrifugation at approximately 2500 times gravity (xg) for 10 min at 15 °C. The resulting plasma was collected and assessed for prothrombin time (PT) and activated partial thromboplastin time (APTT) using Ceveron Alpha automated coagulation analyser, Technoclone GmbH, Austria.

Clinical biochemistry

As outlined in Section 2.2.6, approximately 1.8 ml of blood was collected, and plasma was isolated by centrifuging the whole blood at 5000 rpm for 5 min at 4 °C. The plasma samples were subsequently analyzed using the AU480 automated clinical chemistry analyzer (Beckman Coulter Inc., USA)

Urine analysis

Urine samples were collected over an overnight period (∼ 16 h) prior to terminal sacrifice, with animals individually housed in metabolic cages. Animals had free access to water but were fasted during this urine collection period. The following morning, urine volume and appearance were recorded. Urinalysis was performed using a Multistix 10 SG strips, read with Clinitek Advantus® analyzer (Siemens Healthcare diagnostics Inc., USA) and Digital Handheld “pocket” Urine Specific Gravity Refractometer by PAL-10S, ATAGO Co., Ltd 32–10 Honcho, Itabashi, Tokyo, Japan. The parameters assessed included specific gravity, pH, nitrite, glucose, protein, ketone bodies, bilirubin, urobilinogen, leucocytes and erythrocytes. Additionally, microscopic examination of urine sediments was conducted to identify the presence of crystals, casts and epithelial cells.

TSH, T3 and T4 evaluations

Approximately 1.0 ml blood was collected from each animal as described in Section 2.2.6. Samples were left undisturbed at room temperature for 90 min to allow complete clot formation, followed by centrifugation at 5000 rpm for 5 min at 4 °C to separate the serum. The serum was transferred into pre-labelled plastic tubes and stored at approximately −70°C until analysis. Hormonal assays for rodent thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH), thyroxine (T4), and triiodothyronine (T3) were performed using enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kits procured from Calbiotech Inc., USA and/or Bioelsa Inc., USA. The assays were conducted using a BIO-RAD microplate washer (Model PW40) and microplate reader (Model 680). The thyroid parameters, TSH, T3, T4 were validated for accuracy, intra, inter assay precision and the variability was within ±15 %.

Organ weight measurement, macroscopic and histopathological evaluations

On day 91, all animals were euthanized under isoflurane anesthesia by exsanguination following blood collection. A comprehensive necropsy was performed, including examination of the abdominal, cranial and thoracic cavities for gross abnormalities. Organs and tissues were excised, inspected, and weighed. Terminal fasting body weights were recorded immediately prior to sacrifice and used to calculate relative organ weights. Organ-to-body weight (fasting body weight) and organ-to-brain weight ratios were expressed as percentages. Paired organs were weighed together and combined weights were reported. All the tissues/organs were preserved in 10 % Neutral Buffered Formalin (NBF), while testes and eyes with optic nerve were fixed in Modified Davidson’s fluid and Davidson’s fluid, respectively. Tissues were processed using standard paraffin embedding techniques, and 4–5micron sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) for histological evaluation. Histopathological examination was conducted on all the preserved organs and tissues from the control and high-dose groups. As no test item-related microscopic findings were observed in the high-dose group, tissues from the low- and mid-dose groups were not examined. Bone marrow smears were prepared from femoral marrow of all animals and stained using Giemsa; however, cellularity was not assessed due to the absence of any significant histological findings in the femur and sternum marrow.

Genotoxicity studies

Bacterial mutagenicity assay

The mutagenicity potential of beta-glucanase was assessed using a GLP compliant bacterial reverse mutation assay in accordance with OECD Test Guideline No. 471 [12]. The assay was conducted using Salmonella typhimurium strains TA98, TA100, TA1535 and TA1537, as well as Escherichia coli strain WP2uvrA. A two-phase approach was employed – an initial dose-range finding assay to evaluate cytotoxicity and establish appropriate dose-range concentrations, followed by a definitive mutagenicity assay. The treat-and-plate method, a modified version of the preincubation technique [21], was used for all treatments except for the positive control in E. coli with metabolic activation, where the plate incorporation method was applied [22]. Metabolic activation was provided by an S9 mix prepared from rat liver induced with Sodium phenobarbitone and β-naphthoflavone.

Positive controls included 2-aminoanthracene (with S9), and 2-Nitrofluorene, Sodium azide, 9-Aminoacridine, and 4-Nitroquinoline-1-oxide (without S9), all sourced from Sigma Aldrich and diluted in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO). For each treatment condition, the mean and standard deviation of revertant colony counts per plate were calculated. A valid positive response was defined as a ≥ 3-fold increase in revertant colonies compared to the corresponding vehicle control.

In vitro micronucleus assay

An in vitro micronucleus assay was conducted in accordance with OECD Test Guideline No. 487 [13] and GLP compliance, both in the presence and absence of metabolic activation (S9 mix). The assay was conducted using human peripheral blood lymphocytes (HPBL) obtained from two healthy female donors who were non-smokers, not pregnant, free from ongoing medication, and had no recent exposure to ionizing radiation, hazardous chemicals, or viral infections. The test system has been shown to be sensitive to the clastogenic effects of a wide range of chemical agents [23]. In a preliminary cytotoxicity test carried out to select the test concentrations for the micronucleus assay, human peripheral blood lymphocytes cultures were exposed to beta-glucanase at concentrations up to 5000 μg TOS/ml for 3 h with (Set 1) and without (Set 2) metabolic activation, and for 24 h without metabolic activation (Set 3).

The metabolic activation system consisted of S9 fraction prepared from rat liver induced with Sodium phenobarbitone and β-naphthoflavone. Positive controls included Mitomycin C (Sigma-Aldrich, USA)-clastogenic control (0.5 µg/ml) and Colchicine (Sigma-Aldrich, USA)-aneugenic control (0.01 µg/ml) for the non-activated system and Cyclophosphamide (Med Chem Express, USA)-clastogenic control (3 µg/ml) for the S9-activated system.

The micronucleus assay comprised of three independent experiments: Experiments 1 and 2 which involved 3-hour exposures with and without metabolic activation, respectively, while Experiment 3 involved a 24-hour exposure without metabolic activation. Based on cytotoxicity findings, test concentrations of 312.5, 1250 and 5000 μg TOS/ml were selected and tested in duplicate for all three experiments. Similarly, concurrent vehicle control (sterile water) and the positive controls; cyclophosphamide with metabolic activation (3-hour exposure) and Mitomycin C and Colchicine without metabolic activation (24-hour exposure), were tested in duplicate. Following treatment, interphase cells were harvested, stained and evaluated for micronuclei. The frequency of micronuclei in 2000 bi-nucleated cells per test concentration (1000 per culture) were analyzed. Approximately 500 cells from at least two slides per replicate were assessed for cytotoxicity. From this information Cytokinesis-block proliferation index (CBPI) was determined using the formula CBPI= {(No. mononucleate cells) + (2 x No. binucleate cells) + (3 x No. multinucleate cells)}/ (Total number of cells). Replicative index (RI) was determined using the formula RI= [(No. binucleate cells) + (2 x No. multinucleate cells)} / (Total number of cells of Treated culture)] / [(No. binucleate cells) + (2 x No. multinucleate cells)}/ (Total number of cells of control culture)] x 100. Percent cytostasis is determined by subtracting RI from 100.

Skin sensitization studies

Direct peptide reactivity assay (DPRA)

The skin sensitization potential of beta-glucanase was evaluated using an in chemico peptide reactivity assay in accordance with OECD TG 442 C [14]. Beta-glucanase was observed to be soluble in Milli-Q water at 20 mg TOS/ml, which was therefore selected as the vehicle. Synthetic heptapeptides consisting of either Cysteine (Ac-RFAACAACOOH) or Lysine (Ac-RFAAKAA-COOH), were used as the test system in this assay. Each peptide was incubated separately with the test item and a positive control (Cinnamaldehyde at 100 mM in acetonitrile) for 24 ± 2 h at 22.5–30 °C (in the dark). Depletion of the peptides was quantified using the high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) with gradient elution and UV detection at 220 nm. Percent depletion of Cysteine and Lysine peptides was calculated and interpreted using a prediction model to classify the test item into one of four reactivity categories, supporting the identification of sensitisers versus non-sensitisers [14]. Precipitation was not observed with the test item in case of both the peptides. Average peptide depletion values were determined for both Cysteine and Lysine peptides.

Keratinocyte-based ARE-NRF2 Luciferase assay

The skin sensitisation potential of beta-glucanase was evaluated in vitro using the Keratinocyte-Based ARE-Nrf2 Luciferase Reporter Gene method according to OECD TG 442D [15]. This assay examines the second key event (keratinocyte activation) in the skin sensitisation adverse outcome pathway. Following a solubility assessment, dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) was selected as the vehicle. Precipitation was not observed when 10 μL of the 40 mg TOS/ml stock solution was mixed with exposure medium containing 240 μL of dulbecco's modified eagle medium (D-MEM) with 1 % foetal bovine serum (FBS) medium without geneticin (25-fold dilution). Keratinosens™ (HaCaT) cells were exposed to twelve concentrations of beta-glucanase (0.20 – 400 μg TOS /ml), the positive control (trans-cinnamaldehyde, 4 – 64 μM), or the negative control (DMSO), and incubated for 48 ± 2 h in 5 ± 1 % CO2 at 37 ± 1°C. After incubation, induction of the luciferase gene was assessed by measuring the luminescence in the cells. Cell viability of the concurrently treated cells was also evaluated using a thiazolyl blue tetrazolium bromide (MTT) test with a separate set of plates. The values of Imax (maximal fold gene-induction value observed at any concentration of the test item and positive control) and EC1.5 (value representing the concentration for which a gene induction above the 1.5-fold threshold i.e., 50 % enhanced gene activity) were calculated based on luciferase activity, i.e., luminescence measured (reading of three plates), while the values of IC50 (50 % reduction of cellular viability) and IC30 (30 % reduction of cellular viability) were determined based on the cytotoxicity results (OD values, reading of one plate). The assay met the below conditions and was considered acceptable: i. Positive control (trans-cinnamaldehyde) induced statistically significant luciferase activity above the threshold of 1.5 (e.g., using a t-test) in at least one of the tested concentrations (4 – 64 μM). ii. EC1.5 value for trans-cinnamaldehyde was within two standard deviations of the testing facility historical mean (7 – 30 μM based on the validation dataset). iii. Although, where the average gene induction in three replicates for trans-cinnamaldehyde at 64 μM was above 2 and 8, there was a clear dose-response. Average coefficient of variation in the 3 × 6 negative control wells (DMSO) for each repetition was below 20 %. A positive KeratinoSens™ prediction was defined if all the following criteria were met in two of two repetitions: i. Imax > 1.5- fold with statistical significance versus negative control. ii. Cellular viability > 70 % at the EC1.5 concentration. iii. The EC1.5 < 1000 μM (or < 200 μg TOS/ml for substances without a defined Molecular Weight). iv. An observable dose-response or a biphasic pattern in luciferase induction.

h-CLAT assay for skin sensitization

The potential of beta-glucanase to cause skin sensitization was evaluated using the human cell line activation test (h-CLAT), in accordance with OECD Test Guideline 442E [16]. It is an in vitro assay which models the third key event in the skin sensitization adverse outcome pathway - dendritic cell activation – by measuring the expression of cell surface markers CD86 and CD54 in THP-1 cells following 24 h exposure to the test substance. Beta-glucanase was soluble in saline at 100 mg TOS/ml and 292 mg TOS/ml. Hence, saline was selected as the vehicle for this study. As the top concentration 1000 μg TOS/ml was non-toxic in the first set of Dose Range Finding i.e. DRF (Experiment 1 and 2), a second set of DRF (Experiment 3 and 4) was performed at the maximum testable concentration of 2920 μg TOS/ml. The CV75 for beta-glucanase was found to be > 2920 μg TOS/ml and concentrations of beta-glucanase selected for CD86/CD54 expression measurement were 2920, 2433, 2028, 1690, 1408, 1173, 978, 815 μg TOS/ml. Two independent experiments were conducted to assess CD86 and CD54 expressions at non-cytotoxic concentrations of beta-glucanase. For the assay, THP-1 cells were exposed to beta-glucanase at eight concentrations from 815 μg TOS/ml to 2920 μg TOS/ml and incubated for 24 ± 0.5 h at 37 ± 1℃ under 5 ± 1 % CO2. Expression study results were used to determine the relative fluorescence intensity (RFI) values of the beta-glucanase which were further used in a prediction model that differentiates the sensitizer versus non-sensitizer. Both the studies of CD86/CD54 expression measurement met the assay acceptance criteria. So, the results of both experiments were considered valid for the evaluation. 2, 4-Dinitrochlorobenzene (DNCB, 4.0 μg/ml in DMSO) served as the positive control. Each assay was included with concurrent negative controls, comprising of solvents and complete medium alone, with no beta-glucanase. For Saline Control, 2 % saline in complete medium and for DMSO control, 0.4 % DMSO in complete medium were tested at final concentrations of 1 % and 0.2 % in the plate, respectively. THP-1 cells, a human monocytic leukaemia cell line, was obtained from American Type Culture Collection (ATCC), as specified in OECD TG 442E. The cell line batch meeting the acceptance criteria (i.e. the viability of non-treated cells > 90 % and for both CD86 and CD54 markers, a positive response with DNCB (2, 4-dinitrochlorobenzene) and NiSO4 (nickel sulfate), and negative response with lactic acid) was used in the study.

Eight different concentrations i.e., 2920, 2433, 2028, 1690, 1408, 1173, 978, 815 µg TOS/ml were selected as the final concentrations to be tested in CD86/CD54 expression measurement. Flow cytometry (FACSLyric™ – Becton Dickinson) equipped with FACSuite software was used to analyse the CD86 and CD54 markers expression levels as well as cell viability. Geometric mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) was used to determine the RFI of CD86 and CD54 for negative control cells, positive control cells and chemically treated cells according to the below equation: RFI = {(MFI of chemically treated cells – MFI of chemically treated isotype control cells) / (MFI of solvent treated cells – MFI of solvent treated isotype control cells)} x 100. For each sample, the cell viability was recorded from the isotype control cells (stained with FITC labelled mouse IgG1). In both the experiments, RFI values for CD86 and CD54 did not meet the conditions of a positive response (CD86 ≥ 150 and CD54 ≥ 200). Therefore, the EC150 and EC200 values (i.e., the concentration at which beta-glucanase induced an RFI of >150 and 200) were not determined. An h-CLAT prediction is positive if at least one of the following conditions is met in two of two or at least two of three independent runs, otherwise, the h-CLAT prediction is termed as negative: i. The RFI of CD86 ≥ 150 % in at least one test concentration (with ≥50 % cell viability); ii. The RFI of CD54 ≥ 200 % in at least one test concentration (with ≥50 % cell viability).

Irritation studies

In vitro skin irritation assay using RhE

Potential of beta-glucanase to cause skin irritation was assessed employing the reconstructed human epidermis (RhE) tissue (Lot: 24-RHE-187_S; Validity: October 28, 2024), in accordance with OECD TG 439 [17]. The SkinEthic™ RHE model, a three-dimensional tissue construct composed of non-transformed human epidermal keratinocytes cultured to form a stratified and highly differentiated human epidermis model, was used in the study. Tissues were procured from SkinEthic Laboratories (Episkin 4, Lyon Cedex 07, France). The test procedure predicts the potential of the test chemical to induce skin irritation in humans and distinguishes between irritant test chemicals (UN GHS Category 1 or 2) and non-irritant test chemicals (UN GHS No Category) based on tissue viability following topical exposure. Triplicate tissues were exposed to the beta-glucanase, Dulbecco’s Phosphate Buffered Saline (DPBS; negative control), or 5 % aqueous sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS; positive control) for 42 min, at room temperature. After exposure, tissues were rinsed, dried, and incubated for 42 h in a growth medium. Tissues were incubated with MTT (3-(4, 5-Dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-2, 5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide) solution for 180 min, and then blue formazan salt was extracted using isopropanol. Optical density (OD) was measured at 570 nm, and mean percentage tissue viability was calculated relative to the negative control using the OD values obtained with beta-glucanase to provide the in vitro prediction. A cell viability of ≤ 50 % was interpreted as indicative of skin irritation, while values > 50 % were considered non-irritant, in line with UN GHS classification criteria. OD values and corrected OD values obtained from the software for individual tissue replicates were used and processed further to calculate percentage cell viability for the test item. The assay met all acceptance criteria, including appropriate responses from the positive and negative controls, confirming the validity of the results.

In vitro eye irritation assay using RhCE

Potential of beta-glucanase to cause eye irritation was assessed by evaluating its cytotoxic effects on Reconstructed Human Cornea-like Epithelium (RhCE) tissue, following the OECD Test Guidelines 492 [18] and in compliance with GLP. The study was conducted in alignment with UN GHS (United Nations Globally Harmonized System of Classification and Labelling of Chemicals). RhCE tissues (two tissues per set) were treated with beta-glucanase whereas 30 μL of Dulbecco’s Phosphate Buffered Saline (DPBS) and undiluted methyl acetate served as negative control and positive control, respectively. Epithelium was incubated for at least 30 min at 37 ± 2 °C with 5 ± 1 % CO2 and saturated with humidity on the day of treatment. Test items which directly reduce 3-(4,5-Dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-2,5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide (MTT) or which are strongly coloured may cause false estimate of tissue viability by interfering measurement of Formazan dye. Hence, prior to the main experiment direct MTT reduction and Colour interference tests were performed as pre-tests to determine if beta-glucanase causes non-specific reduction of MTT or colour interference, respectively. Tissue surface was moistened with DPBS before application of beta-glucanase mimicking the human eye wet conditions. Tissues were exposed to beta-glucanase, negative and positive control for 30 min. Post incubation, the MTT test was performed. The test was considered acceptable if negative control mean OD value was between 1.0 and 2.5 and the variation between the two negative control tissues was < 20 %, and mean viability of the positive control tissue replicates was below 30 % of control viability. Percent tissue viability was calculated using the formula as below. % Tissue viability = (Mean corrected OD of beta-glucanase or positive control)/(Mean corrected OD of mean negative control) X 100. Beta-glucanase was not considered as an eye irritant if the average tissue viability was > 60 %.

Statistical analysis

90-day rat oral gavage toxicity study: Data were reported per individual animal as the experimental unit, except for feed consumption, which was measured per cage and used to determine the mean weekly consumption (g) per animal. Statistical analyses were performed with Provantis™ (Version 10.1.0.1, Instem LSS, Staffordshire ST15OSD, UK) built-in statistical tool. Statistical analysis was performed for some parameters on raw data (ex. body weight gain-male: days 8–15; 21–28); for some parameters on rank transformed data (ex. body weight gain-male: days 1–8; 28–36), and for some parameters on log transformed data (ex. body weight gain-male: days 15–21). For comparative statistics, data were first assessed for homogeneity of variances using the Levene Test and for normality of distributions using the Shapiro-Wilks Test. Data which were homogenous and of normal distribution was analysed by analysis of variance (ANOVA) and the data which were non-homogenous or non-normal, was subjected for transformation and ANOVA was done on transformed data. When ANOVA indicated statistical significance, pairwise comparisons between treated groups and the control group were conducted using Dunnett’s test. All hypothesis testing was conducted at a 5 % (two-sided) significance level, unless otherwise specified.

In vitro micronucleus assay: Statistical analysis of the assay data was performed using the validated SYSTAT Statistical package (version12.0). Statistical significance was confirmed by Chi square test. All analysis and comparisons were conducted at a 5 % significance level (p < 0.05), unless otherwise specified.

Keratinocyte-based ARE-NRF2 Luciferase assay: For each concentration demonstrating greater than 1.5-fold gene induction, statistical significance (p < 0.05) was assessed- typically using a two-tailed Student’s t-test – by comparing luminescence values from three replicate samples to those of the solvent (negative) control wells. The lowest concentration with > 1.5-fold gene induction (i.e., a 50 % increase in luciferase activity) is termed as the EC1.5 value. For each case, it was verified that the EC1.5value was below the IC50/IC30 value, confirming that cell viability was not reduced by more than 30 % at the concentration used to determine EC1.5.

Genotoxicity studies

Bacterial mutagenicity assay

Beta-glucanase was neither toxic to the tester strain nor precipitated at any of the tested doses. Spontaneous revertant colonies were within the frequency ranges of the historical control data. In both the initial toxicity-mutation assay and the confirmatory mutagenicity assay, there were no positive mutagenic responses in any of the tester strains, either in the presence or absence of S9 metabolic activation, under the employed test conditions (Supplementary Material Tables 13 – 16).

In vitro micronucleus assay

Beta-glucanase was soluble in sterile water at 50 mg TOS/ml and no precipitation was observed up to the highest test concentration of 5000 µg TOS/ml, in both with and without metabolic activation. Beta-glucanase did not cause any appreciable changes in the pH or osmolality of the treatment medium at any tested concentration compared to the sterile water control (Supplementary Material Table 17). Beta-glucanase was non-cytotoxic up to the highest test concentration of 5000 µg TOS/ml, in both with and without metabolic activation. Furthermore, it did not induce any statistically significant increase in bi-nucleated cells containing micronuclei, compared to the vehicle control either with or without metabolic activation system up to the highest test concentration of 5000 μg TOS/ml (Supplementary Material Tables 18, 19, 20, 21). All the positive and vehicle control values were within acceptable historical ranges.

These RFI values met the assay acceptance criteria: RFI CD86 ≥ 150 %; and RFI CD54 ≥ 200 %. Additionally, the positive control cell viability was > 50 % in both experiments (Supplementary Material Table 24). All negative (solvent) control values were within the acceptable range defined by the test guideline, confirming the validity of experiments. Beta-glucanase produced a negative response for CD86 and CD54 marker in 8/8 tested concentrations, in both, experiment 1 and 2. Beta-glucanase cell viability was observed to be greater than 50 % at the all the tested concentrations up to 2920 μg TOS/ml, satisfying the assay’s acceptance criteria. CD86/CD54 expression measurement results indicated that beta-glucanase produced a negative response in h-CLAT assay.

Irritation studies

In vitro skin irritation assay using RhE

The mean percent viability of beta-glucanase treated tissues, expressed relative to the negative control, was 100 %. The standard deviation of each intra-batch mean was 1.557 and met the acceptance criteria of not more than 18 %. Beta-glucanase was predicted to be non-irritant (No Category) to skin.

In vitro eye irritation assay using RhCE

The mean percent viability of the beta-glucanase treated tissues, expressed relative to the negative control, was 91.993 %, which is above the established tissue viability threshold value of 60 %. Therefore, according to the United Nations Globally Harmonized System of Classification and Labelling of Chemicals (UN-GHS), beta-glucanase is classified as “No Category”, indicating it is not considered a skin irritant under the employed test conditions.

Discussion

Beta-glucanase used in the present study was obtained from the microbial production strain Trichoderma reesei (T. reesei) which has a long-standing history of safe use in the biotechnology industry [24]. The production strain T. reesei is widely employed for manufacture of industrial enzymes including those used as feed additives due to its ability to rapidly produce large quantities of extracellular enzymes [25]. It is a well-recognized principle that non-toxigenic and non-pathogenic microbial strains having a history of safe use in food manufacture establishes both, the safety of production strain and safe strain lineage [19], [26], [27]. T. reesei has been extensively used in enzyme production industry and its enzyme preparations are commonly used in several enzyme applications including human food and animal feed. T. reesei sourced beta-glucanase has a long history of safe use in food processing [26]. Technological improvements in gene isolation and subsequent expression of desired enzyme activities in host microorganism, and long-standing history of safe use in the biotechnology industry establishes the safe use of T. reesei in production of enzyme preparations that meet safety and stability standards for commercial usage [27]. T. reesei fungal strain is non-toxigenic, non-pathogenic, and does not produce antibiotics or mycotoxins under industrial enzyme production set up, establishes a safe strain lineage, and biotechnological techniques used in modification of the organism are well established and suitable for food applications [28]. T. reesei has been recognized by the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) for its safety, and the QM6a strain and its derivatives are exempt from full notification and reporting requirements as required according to provisions of the Toxic Substances Control Act (TSCA), as their use is not expected to pose an unreasonable risk to human health or the environment [29]. Furthermore, T. reesei is classified as a Biosafety Level 1 (BSL-1) organism by the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC) indicating that T. reesei is not known to cause disease in healthy adult humans. It has also been listed as a safe production organism in multiple authoritative reviews on the microbial enzyme safety [19], [26], [27].

A Safe Strain Lineage (SSL) is well established when no adverse effects are observed in the repeated toxicological evaluations of same microbial strain lineage derived enzymes, such as Trichoderma reesei derived enzymes [20], [26], [27], [28]. The SSL concept is supported by the Pariza and Johnson decision tree [26], which outlines a systematic approach for evaluating the pathogenic potential, antimicrobial activity, and toxic metabolite production of microbial production strains. This framework also includes conducting a 90-day repeated-dose oral toxicity study in laboratory animals to determine a No-Observed-Adverse-Effect-Level (NOAEL) and to establish a margin of safety (MOS) for the intended uses. The decision tree approach permits the extension of safety conclusions to other strains within the same lineage, including newly developed strains, to be used as safe production hosts, provided they share a common ancestry and meet the same safety criteria. However, for new branches derived through random mutagenesis, it is recommended to re-establish safety through whole genome sequencing, toxin analysis, and preferably, toxicological testing. This approach is accepted on a case-by-case basis by the US FDA and other international bodies, including JECFA, as a part of recent proposals to update its food enzyme evaluation guideline [30]. The specific T. reesei beta-glucanase production strain used in this study is from a broader lineage developed by International Flavors & Fragrances through classical mutagenesis, selection and genetic engineering from the original wild-type parent strain QM6a. Safety assessments of other strains within this lineage – including in vivo oral toxicity and in vitro genotoxicity studies – have consistently shown no safety concerns, as documented in multiple GRAS Notices submitted to FDA (e.g., GRN 230, 315, 333, 372, 567, 703, 727, and 808). The current toxicological data further emphasizes the weight-of-evidence supporting the safety of this Trichoderma reesei lineage, in line with the recommendations of Ladics and Sewalt [20] and Ladics et al. [31].

Enzymes are generally considered safe for oral exposure due to their natural presence in human and animal diets, their digestibility in gastrointestinal tract, and their endogenous production in gastric secretions [19], [20]. A substantial body of evidence indicates that microbial enzymes do not exhibit acute or subchronic toxicity, or genotoxicity [31]. The outcome from the mutagenicity and micronucleus assays, performed using beta-glucanase produced in a safe production microorganism T. reesei, are consistent with this understanding and confirms that this beta-glucanase is not genotoxic.

Beta-glucanase was evaluated for its potential to induce skin sensitization using a battery of in vitro assays aligned with the key molecular events for skin sensitization Adverse Outcome Pathway (AOP). In the direct peptide reactivity assay (DPRA), which models the initial molecular interaction involving covalent binding to skin proteins (Key Event 1), beta-glucanase predicted moderate reactivity (positive) by showing mean percent depletion of 28.5 %. However, subsequent testing in the KeratinoSens™ assay, which assesses the activation of keratinocytes (Key Event 2), and the human Cell Line Activation Test (h-CLAT) assay, which evaluates dendritic cell activation (Key Event 3), both yielded negative results. According to the “2 out of 3” defined approach (2o3 DA) for skin sensitization identification, a substance is not classified as a sensitizer if two of the three assays produce concordant negative outcomes [32]. Based on this criterion, beta-glucanase is not considered a skin sensitizer. Mean percent viability of beta-glucanase treated tissues in the in vitro irritation studies in reconstructed human epidermis tissues (RhE) and reconstructed human cornea-like epithelium (RhCE) supported the classification of beta-glucanase as non-irritant to both skin and eyes.

Beta-glucanase used in the 90-day repeated dose oral gavage toxicity study in rats was produced via a manufacturing process representative of commercial production. No beta-glucanase related adverse effects were observed in either sex at any dose level when administered to male and female rats at doses up to 1000 mg total organic solids (TOS)/kg body weight/day corresponding to 168,410 U/kg body weight/day. Minor reductions in feed intake noted sporadically in both male and female rats were considered toxicologically insignificant as the findings were inconsistent, without any dose correlation and without corresponding effect on mean body weights. Similarly, the lower body weight gain during Days 21–28 and 57–64 in female rats dosed with 250 mg TOS/kg body weight/day was considered incidental as no such significant findings were observed during other intervals, and the body weights at the highest dose were unaffected. No beta-glucanase related effects were identified in terminal fasting body weights, absolute or relative organ weights, macroscopic pathology, and histopathological evaluations in both males and females.

The No-Observed-Adverse-Effect-Level of 1000 mg TOS/kg body weight/day, established from the 90-day rat oral toxicity study, was used to evaluate the beta-glucanase safety when incorporated into animal feed. A margin of safety was determined considering targeted beta-glucanase use levels and daily feed intake in poultry and swine species. To estimate dietary beta-glucanase exposure, daily feed intake values for various categories of poultry and swine – broilers, laying hen, pig for fattening, sow for lactating, piglets – were considered. Among these, broilers exhibit the highest feed intake relative to body weight, with a default average of 79 g dry matter (DM)/kg bw/day, as reported by EFSA [33]. Similarly, National Research Council (NRC) has determined the maximum daily feed intake in poultry to 65 g dry matter (DM)/kg bw/day and in pigs to 45 g dry matter (DM)/kg bw/day [34]. For a conservative risk assessment, default average daily feed intake of 79 g dry matter (DM)/kg bw/day in broilers (being the highest feed intake relative to body weight) was selected for the exposure calculation. The proposed maximum inclusion level of beta-glucanase in animal feed is 426 U/kg. Assuming 88 % dry matter content in feed [33], maximum beta-glucanase exposure in broiler could be 30 U/kg body weight/day. Based on the specific beta-glucanase activity of 168.41 U/mg TOS, this equates to an estimated exposure of 0.176 mg TOS/kg body weight/day. The margin of safety (MOS) was calculated by dividing the NOAEL by the estimated exposure level, yielding a MOS of 5681. Similarly, a consensus bacterial phytase variant 6-phytase (PhyG), evaluated under comparable study conditions, exhibited a NOAEL of 1000 mg TOS/kg body weight/day, with a MOS of 1613 derived from estimated broiler consumption [31]. The MOS determined in this study far exceeds the generally accepted safety margin threshold, such as the 100-fold margin commonly applied by regulatory authorities like the US FDA and the 200-fold margin recommended by EFSA [35]. Although the 90-day oral toxicity study in rats demonstrated no adverse effects of beta-glucanase and established a wide margin of safety in target species such as pigs and poultry, certain limitations need to be acknowledged. The study in rats may not fully replicate the physiological responses of target species, specifically the gastrointestinal enzyme interactions and nutrient metabolism. Applicability of in vitro sensitization assays to non-human species remains uncertain. Future beta-glucanase tolerance trials in pigs and poultry will substantiate its safety under practical feeding conditions and identifying any species-specific responses. The estimated MOS supports the conclusion that beta-glucanase presents no safety concern, when used at the proposed maximum inclusion rate in poultry and swine feed.

Conclusion

The findings from the 90-day oral toxicity study in Crl:CD(SD) rats demonstrated that beta-glucanase, produced via a representative commercial process, did not induce any adverse effects at doses up to 1000 mg TOS/kg body weight/day. Complimentary in vitro genotoxicity assays confirmed the absence of mutagenic or clastogenic potential of beta-glucanase. Additionally, beta-glucanase was classified as non-irritant in validated in-vitro models for skin and eye irritation, and it did not exhibit skin sensitization potential based on the “2 out of 3” defined approach integrating DPRA, KeratinoSens™, and h-CLAT assays. The margin of safety of 5681 substantially exceeds the conventional and regulatory safety thresholds, supporting the conclusion that beta-glucanase is safe for its intended use as a feed additive in poultry and swine nutrition. Beta-glucanase is considered safe at proposed use levels based on cumulative toxicological evidence derived from repeated dose toxicity, genotoxicity, sensitization and irritation studies.
