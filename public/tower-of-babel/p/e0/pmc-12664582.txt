Preferences for harm reduction vending machine design among rural people who use drugs
Authors: April M Young, Tasfia Jahangir, Susan Westneat, Chelsi Cheatom, Amanda Fallin-Bennett, Laura C Fanucchi, Patricia R Freeman, Jennifer R Havens, Hannah K Knudsen, Kathryn E McCollister, Susannah Stitzer, Jack Stone, Peter Vickerman, Melvin D Livingston
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractGlobally, harm reduction vending machines (HRVMs) have been used for decades to dispense safe injection equipment and other supplies to people who use drugs (PWUD). However, HRVMs have only recently scaled up in the U.S. and few operate in rural settings. This study describes preferences for HRVM design among rural PWUD in two Appalachian Kentucky communities. Peer-referral and outreach were used to recruit participants (n = 731). Eligibility criteria were ≥ 18 years, residing in the target counties, and having used any drug (excluding alcohol, cannabis, and tobacco) to get high within 6 months. Interviewer-administered questionnaires elicited data on behavioral/demographic characteristics and HRVM design preferences. Sample characteristics and site-level differences were estimated accounting for peer-referral chain clustering. Naloxone was the most desired supply (95.6 %), followed by syringes (94.5 %) and fentanyl test strips (83.7 %). Supplies for basic needs were popular, including water (82.6 %), food (72.8 %), and personal care kits (71.3 %). The most endorsed location was the health department (71.1 %). More than twice the number of participants wanted HRVMs installed outdoors (52.3 %) rather than indoors (19.6 %), but for 25.4 %, indoor/outdoor installation preference varies depending on the venue. While over three-quarters reported that a surveillance camera would not affect their HRVM use, a substantial minority indicated it would be a deterrent, highlighting a key consideration for implementation. There were significant differences in supply and location preferences across the two communities. Introduction

Harm reduction services, such as syringe service programs (SSPs), effectively reduce drug-related harms among people who use drugs (PWUD) (Aspinall et al., 2014, Hagan et al., 2000, Ksobiech, 2003), yet these programs are often lacking or underutilized in rural settings (Jones, 2019, Kim and Harley, 2019, Lancaster et al., 2020, Thakarar et al., 2021, Welch-Lazoritz et al., 2017). Public health practitioners seeking to expand the reach of harm reduction services increasingly utilize innovative outreach strategies, including harm reduction vending machines (HRVMs) (Russell et al., 2023, Zhang et al., 2025).

HRVMs have operated globally for more than three decades (Stark et al., 1994), but have only recently proliferated in the U.S. HRVMs vary in size and design, their suitability for outdoor or indoor installation, and the supplies that they provide, such as naloxone, needles and syringes, other sterile injection equipment (e.g., cookers, filters), drug checking strips, informational resources, and provisions addressing basic needs (e.g., food, water). Programs can operate HRVMs to allow supplies to be free to the public or can control access through tokens, cards, codes, or fees.

HRVMs can increase harm reduction engagement by expanding hours of operation, providing increased client confidentiality, and increasing geographic coverage (Islam et al., 2008b). Recent studies have provided indications of effectiveness, including reductions in opioid-involved overdose deaths (Allen et al., 2022) and increased harm reduction supply distribution (Arendt, 2023, Herrera, 2025, Shaw et al., 2025). However, additional studies are needed to rigorously evaluate impacts on drug-related harms and risk behaviors.

As with all public health interventions, HRVM effectiveness will be impacted by whether their design responds to the needs and preferences of those it will serve. Unfortunately, there are few studies on preferences for HRVM design among PWUD. Most studies describe the post-implementation period rather than the design process or primarily focus on attitudes of the general public and program implementers (Bardwell et al., 2024, Islam et al., 2008a, Islam et al., 2009, Otiashvili et al., 2022, Otiashvili et al., 2019, Walker et al., 2024, White et al., 2016).

Only three pre-implementation studies on design preferences of PWUD in the U.S. have been published (Marschke et al., 2025, Stewart et al., 2023, Wagner et al., 2022). The studies were qualitative and involved small numbers of people with lived experience with substance use (n = 8) recruited from a federally qualified health center in Philadelphia (Stewart et al., 2023), young adults (n = 17) in substance use treatment in Denver (Wagner et al., 2022), and community-recruited PWUD in rural Kentucky (n = 20) (Marschke et al., 2025). The studies in Philadelphia and Kentucky elicited feedback on potential supplies and locations of HRVMs, while the Denver study focused on attitudes toward naloxone vending. In all studies, PWUD expressed enthusiasm for HRVMs because of their convenience, privacy, and potential for 24/7 access. However, PWUD noted that uptake could be negatively affected by fear of law enforcement and access requirements (e.g., registration with an agency), and that convenience and visibility of the installation location must be balanced with privacy and client safety (Marschke et al., 2025, Stewart et al., 2023, Wagner et al., 2022).

While these studies have provided valuable insights, reliance on small samples of PWUD limited their ability to understand which supplies and locations are most preferred, the extent to which those preferences vary by setting, and the proportion of potential clients who may be deterred by concerns around surveillance. This study addresses this gap by presenting preferences for specific features of HRVM design among a large sample (n = 731) of PWUD living in two rural regions of Appalachian Kentucky, a region that has experienced a disproportionate prevalence of drug-related harms (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, 2022; Van Handel et al., 2016; Zibbell et al., 2015).

Methods

The study area encompassed two rural counties in mountainous Appalachian Kentucky. The counties are approximately two hours apart by car, with one located in Northeastern Kentucky and the other in Southeastern Kentucky. The two counties have identical levels of rurality based on several indices (Health Resources and Services Administration, 2025), though the Northeastern Kentucky county, which lies along an interstate system, has slightly fewer residents designated as living in a rural community. The counties have similar population sizes with both falling in the range of 15,000–30,000 (U.S. Census Bureau, 2020) and age-adjusted drug overdose mortality rates with both falling between 70 and 100 deaths per 100,000 persons (Kentucky Injury Prevention and Research Center, 2023).

As described elsewhere (Knudsen et al., 2026), eligible participants were county residents aged ≥ 18 years who reported using drugs to get high in the past 6 months (excluding alcohol, cannabis, and tobacco). Participants were enrolled from March 2023 to April 2024. Staff invited individuals they met through outreach and participants from prior cohort studies who consented to being contacted about future research. Enrolled participants were invited to refer peers and receive compensation (up to five each, $10/peer), following standard respondent-driven sampling protocols (Heckathorn, 1997). Participants were paid $35 for survey completion.

Interviewer-administered surveys collected data on demographic characteristics, houselessness (e.g., living place-to-place, couch-surfing, on the street, or in a car, park, abandoned building, tent, campsite, squat, or shelter), transportation access (i.e., unable to go somewhere because they did not have a way to get there), experience of hunger (e.g. hungry but did not eat, ate less, or skipped meals because there was not enough money for food), drug use and injection behaviors, syringe sharing (inclusive of syringe and needle sharing), and engagement in harm reduction practices and services.

At the outset of the sequence of design preference questions, participants were read a description of an HRVM reflecting that the machine would “dispense harm reduction and health supplies (naloxone, clean injection supplies, etc.) to people who have been given a swipe card or code to use in the machine”. The survey then asked what supplies would be important to include in an HRVM, where they would want an HRVM to be located in their community, and preference for indoor or outdoor placement. Check-all-that-apply response options were developed based on advisory board feedback and qualitative research (Marschke et al., 2025) and included a write-in “other” option. Write-in responses are provided in footnotes on tables and figures. In response to community concerns around potential for vandalism and unauthorized access, questions were added shortly after data collection began on the potential impact of video surveillance on uptake and attitudes toward fingerprint access. The latter was asked as follows: “If the vending machine required a fingerprint to be used solely for the purpose of preventing fraud (the same person registering for multiple machine swipe cards, sharing of machine swipe cards, etc.) and the data were stored on a secure server and protected from subpoena, would the fingerprint requirement make you decide NOT to use the machine?” The caveat about protection from subpoena was included because the HRVM to be implemented in the community would initially be in the context of a research study protected by a Federal Certificate of Confidentiality. This was the only preference question that included this research-specific caveat. Descriptive statistics, including percentages and 95 % confidence intervals (CIs), were computed accounting for clustering by respondent-driven sampling recruitment chain. Generalized linear mixed models with a random intercept for recruitment chain were used to compute p-values for site-level comparisons while accounting for chain-level clustering. Analyses were performed using SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute, n.d.).

Results

Table 1 provides the characteristics of this sample (n = 731). The average age of people in the study was 42.6 (range: 20–76) years and approximately half identified as male (47.5 %). More than one-third (39.5 %) experienced houselessness and 77.4 % had difficulty accessing transportation in the past 6 months. Most participants (83.7 %) injected in their lifetime, with 59.5 % having injected in the past 6 months. Among those who injected in the past 6 months, 42.8 % shared syringes with others and less than half (48.5 %) received services at an SSP although nearly all participants (92.5 %) lived within 30 min of a program. Most participants had been given naloxone to carry in the past 6 months (59.9 %), but few (21.6 %) had used fentanyl test strips.

Site-level differences in sample characteristics are described in Appendix Table A1. Study sites were similar in demographic characteristics with the exception of average age (Northeastern Kentucky: 40.5 vs. Southeastern Kentucky: 44.2, p < 0.001) and proportion experiencing houselessness (Northeastern Kentucky: 46.4 % vs. Southeastern Kentucky: 34.7 %, p = 0.003). Compared to participants in Southeastern Kentucky, more participants in Northeastern Kentucky had injected in the past 6 months (72.0 % vs. 50.6 %, p < 0.001), but fewer in Northeastern Kentucky had shared syringes during that period (35.2 % vs. 50.5 %, p = 0.001). The study sites did not differ in proportion who had used the SSP, residential proximity to the SSP, or having received naloxone to carry, but more participants in Northeastern Kentucky had used fentanyl test strips (31.6 % vs. 14.5 %, p < 0.001). More participants in Northeastern Kentucky reported a past 6-month overdose than those in Southeastern Kentucky (24.3 % vs. 11.5 %, p < 0.001).

Preferences for HRVM supplies are described in Fig. 1. The most desired supplies were naloxone (95.6 %), syringes (94.5 %), and fentanyl test strips (83.7 %). Supplies for basic needs, including water (82.6 %), food (72.8 %), personal care kits (71.3 %), menstruation supplies (66.9 %), and supplies for warmth (e.g., blankets, socks, gloves; 66.9 %) were also popular. Nearly two-thirds wanted HIV and HCV self-tests (62.3 % and 60.9 %, respectively). Fig. 2 displays site-level differences in supply preference ranked by percentage who endorsed each item. PWUD in Southeastern Kentucky were more likely to desire food and those in Northeastern Kentucky were more likely to want menstruation supplies, small sharps containers, HIV and HCV self-tests, resource guides listing treatment centers, naloxone vouchers, pregnancy tests, and bus vouchers (data provided in Appendix Table A2).

HRVM location preferences are described in Fig. 3 (data provided in Appendix Table A3). The most popular location was the health department (71.1 %). Venues that provide healthcare, pharmacy services, and substance use disorder (SUD) treatment were also endorsed, ranging from 41 % for centers that provide SUD counseling to 51 % for hospital/medical clinics. Other community venue types (i.e., churches, libraries) and businesses (i.e., grocery stores, laundromats) were endorsed as a desired venue by less than 25 % of the sample. More PWUD in Northeastern Kentucky than in Southeastern Kentucky preferred healthcare-related sites for HRVM installation, including the health department, hospital, pharmacies, and substance use disorder treatment facilities. More participants in Northeastern Kentucky also endorsed libraries and churches as preferred locations, but in both communities, these settings were the least desired.

Discussion

Consideration of client preferences is critical when designing public health interventions. This study demonstrates the potential importance of input from local PWUD on HRVM implementation. PWUD from two demographically similar communities within the same general region (e.g., Appalachian Kentucky) differed in many of their HRVM design preferences. Although PWUD from both sites generally shared endorsement of the importance of HRVMs stocking harm reduction supplies and preferred outdoor installation, they differed in their preferences for certain supplies and locations. For example, the degree to which they wanted HRVMs placed at the health department and healthcare venues (e.g., hospitals, pharmacies, substance use disorder treatment facilities) differed. This may reflect known community characteristics (e.g., increased proximity of criminal legal system venues to the health department in the Southeastern KY site, more centrally located healthcare facilities accessible to those without transportation in the Northeastern KY site), but may also reflect differences in trust, policing patterns, or other features of the environment of which one would be unaware without qualitative PWUD input. Agencies considering HRVMs should involve advisory boards of people with living experience of substance use and/or conduct surveys or focus groups with PWUD to gauge interest and explore design preferences ahead of implementation. Agencies should also continue involving PWUD post-implementation to guide program rollout, troubleshoot challenges, and identify emerging needs.

Rather than solely focusing on harm reduction supplies, PWUD in this study expressed a desire for HRVMs to stock a variety of supplies, including not only those that address drug-related harms but also those that meet their basic needs (e.g., food, water, self-care kits, menstruation). This is corroborated by qualitative research in which PWUD discussed the importance of HRVMs holistically addressing client needs, particularly those who are unhoused (Marschke et al., 2025). Participants in qualitative research also noted that including items such as water and food could help mask the machine’s harm reduction focus from the general public who might oppose harm reduction (Marschke et al., 2025). Despite the absence of a site-level difference in experience of hunger, endorsement of food as an important supply was more common in Southeastern KY. This might reflect that preferences may not always be driven by personal need but rather reflect community context (e.g., differences in the extent to which participants perceived that the presence of food would reduce stigma around HRVM use, differences in accessibility or acceptability of existing food assistance programs relative to those available in a HRVM). Still, other site-level differences observed do likely reflect participant need and service availability. For example, bus vouchers, HIV and HCV self-tests, and lists of treatment centers were more frequently endorsed in Northeastern KY, where there is a bus system and fewer testing and treatment resources relative to the Southeastern KY site.

Most participants preferred installation outdoors near venues already providing services to PWUD. The most popular location was the health department, which currently operates the SSP in each of the counties in the study area. Participants did express interest in non-traditional venues, with more than one-third selecting gas stations and some writing in creative ideas (e.g., liquor/tobacco stores, fire departments, post offices). Previous studies have highlighted the importance of strategically locating HRVMs in areas that are walkable (Marschke et al., 2025), where passersby cannot identify or harass the clients (Dodding and Gaughwin, 1995, Islam and Conigrave, 2007) and travel time is minimized (Islam et al., 2008a, Islam and Conigrave, 2007, Russell et al., 2023) as being key to effective implementation. This may be especially important in rural settings where public transportation is limited and where lack of transportation impedes SSP access (Batty et al., 2023; Ibragimov et al., 2021).

Many program planners may assume that outdoor installation of HRVMs is best for increasing utilization through 24/7 access, but more than a quarter of participants expressed that whether the machine should be installed indoors or outdoors is venue-dependent. This finding highlights the need for PWUD to be involved in not just high-level discussions, but in the nuances of implementation. In our recent focus groups in Appalachian Kentucky, facilitators found it helpful to integrate handouts of aerial photos of the community and street view images of buildings where machines may be installed. Focus group participants circled areas on the photos to indicate preferred locations as they discussed advantages and disadvantages. Considerations that emerged included visibility to the general public and the need to deter use by children (Marschke et al., 2025), some of which were similar to concerns raised among people with lived experience with substance use in Philadelphia (Stewart et al., 2023).

Supplies related to wound care and overdose risk reduction (e.g., naloxone, fentanyl test strips) were among the most preferred supplies, which is not surprising given the prevalence of injection-related wounds (Fanucchi et al., 2025) and exceptionally high overdose rate (Kentucky Injury Prevention and Research Center, 2023) in the region. In a previous study, participants with more lifetime overdoses were more likely to want an HRVM in their community (Young et al., 2025). A study in Nevada found that HRVM dispensation of naloxone was associated with reductions in county-level overdose deaths (Allen et al., 2022). An Ohio study also reported a decrease in overdose deaths following HRVM installation, but called for further research to assess causality (Arendt, 2023). Although temporal trends complicate interpretation of findings, these two studies provide promising preliminary indications that HRVMs may be an effective tool to address the opioid overdose crisis.

Nearly all participants, regardless of injection drug use, expressed a desire for syringes to be included in the HRVM and most felt that it was important to include cookers, cottons, and sharps containers. This differs from the Philadelphia study where the inclusion of syringes was considered more controversial due to fears around syringe litter and enabling drug use (Stewart et al., 2023). These differences may reflect recruitment differences (i. e., community-recruited PWUD versus a clinic-recruited sample). Previous research in rural Appalachia found that PWUD who engaged in receptive and distributive syringe sharing were more likely to want HRVMs than their counterparts who did not share syringes (Young et al., 2025). International studies have demonstrated HRVM’s ability to increase syringe distribution (Duplessy and Reynaud, 2014, McDonald, 2009, Obadia et al., 1999), but HRVM dispensation of syringes remains rare in the U.S., a missed opportunity for public health impact. Many jurisdictions within the U.S. prohibit syringe services and/or classify harm reduction supplies (e.g., test strips) as illegal drug paraphernalia, or operate under funding constraints that limit program scope. These policies and constraints can impact programs’ ability to accommodate some community preferences and should be discussed in formative planning conversations with PWUD to level set expectations. However, constraints should not discourage program planners from eliciting data on PWUD preferences for prohibited supplies as the data may be valuable in demonstrating need and demand and could be instrumental to implementation decisions should policies and/or funding change.

Previous studies have cited that the anonymity and privacy afforded by HRVMs might increase reach to those who may be more secretive about their use, or have experienced or anticipate more stigma (Cama et al., 2014, Islam et al., 2008a, Islam et al., 2009, Islam and Conigrave, 2007). One would assume, therefore, that features that could infringe on privacy, such as surveillance and biometric access, could discourage use. Although most participants reported that they would be undeterred by the presence of a surveillance camera, nearly one in four reported that it would impact their HRVM use. If agencies are compelled to install one due to insurance requirements and to deter theft or vandalism installing the camera in a position where clients’ faces are not recorded may reduce its negative impact on utilization.

Most participants were similarly open to HRVM use of fingerprint technology in the context of research protections (e.g., secure data storage and protections from subpoena through a Federal Certificate of Confidentiality). This finding differs from those of qualitative research in the region that revealed concerns around use of fingerprint technology, a practice that may be reminiscent of police encounters or prompt concerns about data use by the criminal legal system (Marschke et al., 2025). This difference is likely due to question framing; the qualitative probes did not include caveats about secure data storage and protection from subpoena. Together these findings indicate that PWUD may be comfortable with fingerprint technology if used within the context of a protected research study, but not when used by community agencies whose data are subject to subpoena. Disadvantages of using biometric technology may include cost, reliance on outside vendors for database cross-referencing, and additional data security requirements necessitated by collecting such highly identifiable information. Fingerprint technology may also present a challenge for people who are unhoused and lack access to adequate hand washing. Currently, with the exception of machines that dispense controlled substances (Bardwell et al., 2024), we are unaware of any HRVMs that use biometric verification. However, conversations around biometric technology on access-controlled machines have arisen during deliberations around how to prevent code-sharing and use by minors. From a public health perspective, code-sharing and secondary distribution of supplies to those in need regardless of program registration is important, but for agencies navigating complex political and fiscal realities, these concerns are valid. More research on code-sharing and access among minors is needed to guide decision-making.

While the study had many strengths, the findings are important to consider in light of limitations. First, data were collected using interviewer-administered surveys; some participants may have over-reported openness to HRVMs and their features. Ranking data would have yielded valuable insight into preferences, as community differences in frequency of endorsement may be influenced by differences in overall acceptability of HRVMs (Knudsen et al., 2025), but ranking questions were not included in an effort to reduce participant burden. Second, the question about fingerprint technology included research-specific caveats, complicating interpretation of the findings for general community HRVM implementation. Finally, while the two-site design increases the representativeness of the study, findings may not be generalizable to other rural communities. Nevertheless, given the scarcity of research on HRVM preferences among PWUD in general and in rural settings in particular, this research is an important first step to quantifying design preferences around HRVMs.

Conclusions

HRVMs may expand access to harm reduction services in rural settings, but PWUD input is needed to ensure that their design responds to the community’s needs. In this study, PWUD from similar, nearby communities differed in many of their HRVM design preferences. The nuances and site-level differences observed in this study demonstrate that a one-size-fits-all approach, even within the same general region, could ignore important community-specific needs. Some differences may arise from community features that are evident to program planners (e.g., the presence and location of certain facilities and resources), while others may be more nuanced reflections of PWUD experiences (e.g., trust of certain institutions) that would not be known to program planners without PWUD input. When designing HRVM programs, planners should engage local PWUD through coalitions, focus groups, and/or surveys to maximize implementation effectiveness.
