Perianal Abscess as a Rare Manifestation of Hydatid Disease: A Case Report
Authors: Seyed Mohammad Ali Raisolsadat, Mohammad Javad Ghamari, Tooraj Zandbaf
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPrimary pelvic involvement has been reported in < 2% of cases of hydatid cysts, and perianal involvement is infrequent. We report a case of hydatid cyst primary involvement in the perianal region, which is unique in its presentation as a perianal abscess.Keywords: case report, hydatid cyst, hydatid disease, perianal abscess

Introduction

Hydatid cyst, a disease commonly found in humans and animals, is caused by the parasitic invasion of the Echinococcus species of tapeworms. The most prevalent species of this worm is Echinococcus granulosus, with the dog as its primary host and sheep, cattle, horses, and occasionally humans as its intermediate hosts [1]. The liver and lungs are the most common sites for hydatid cysts. Locations other than the liver, lung, and spleen are found in 6% of patients; most are secondary to hydatid disease [2]. Primary pelvic involvement has been reported in < 2% of cases of hydatid cysts, and perianal involvement is infrequent [3]. This report presents a case of a hydatid cyst primarily located in the perianal region, which uniquely presents as a perianal abscess. This case is significant for the study of hydatid cysts.

Case Presentation

Presentation

A 41‐year‐old male refugee presented to our emergency department with a complaint of a painful mass around his anus, diagnosed as an anorectal abscess. He had noticed the painful mass for 5 days before his visit, with a gradual increase in size over the last 2 days. The mass was causing him discomfort while sitting, prompting a referral to our emergency department.

The patient reported no history of systemic diseases, medications, or prior surgeries. During the clinical examination, he exhibited a low‐grade fever of 38°C; however, his vital signs remained stable. Examination of the affected area revealed a relatively round mass on the left side, located 3 cm from the anal verge, covered by inflamed skin. Tenderness was noted upon rectal examination. A preoperative chest X‐ray showed no pathological findings, despite the patient being a smoker. A complete blood count (CBC) indicated a raised white blood cell count of 11,000/mm3, with eosinophils at 2.9% and segmented neutrophils (SEG) at 78%.

Surgical Procedure

Based on these findings, the patient underwent surgical drainage using local anesthesia (spinal). An incision was made over the red and tender perianal lesion. Upon applying pressure, a semi‐purulent fluid was expelled that did not resemble the typical discharge seen in abscesses. During finger exploration to ensure complete drainage, a germinal layer (endocyst) of a hydatid cyst was released (see Figure 1). Given the appearance of the lesion, the surgical site was thoroughly washed with hypertonic saline.

Postoperative Follow Up

We prescribed Albendazole at a dosage of 400 mg, to be taken twice daily for four consecutive days. This was followed by 3 weeks of treatment, with doses administered at 2‐week intervals. Postoperative follow‐up, which included a complete abdominal and pelvic ultrasound, showed no evidence of hydatid cysts immediately after the operation and 1 year later.

Histopathology

Histopathologic examination showed and confirmed hydatid disease.

Discussion and Conclusion

The liver and lungs are common sites of involvement in hydatid cyst infection. Rarely have brain, heart, bone, or other organs been reported [4]. Although it has been suggested that lactic acid in muscle tissue prevents the muscles from transforming into cyst larvae, there have been rare reports of muscle involvement [5]. Pelvic Hydatidosis is a rare condition, accounting for 0.2% of all cases reported [1]. Very few cases of primary extra‐pelvic hydatid cysts of a large dimension have been reported [6]. Our knowledge shows this is a less‐than‐hand‐fingers case of perianal hydatid cysts.

Aljohani et al. have reported a complaint of a progressively enlarging painless deep perianal swelling for 1 year with a history of intermittent chronic constipation. Their patient was referred to a tertiary healthcare center as a possible case of retroperitoneal sarcoma. Routine contrast‐enhanced CT scan showed a large (7.2 × 7.9 × 15.5 cm) multilocular, hypoattenuating, retrorectal cystic lesion. It displaced the rectum and rectosigmoid anteriorly, with a hydatid cyst in the left ischio‐anal fossa and the subcutaneous left perineal [7]. Additionally, Bhatnagar et al. reported a hip hydatid cyst extending into the gluteal region, along with involvement of the ischiorectal fossa and iliopsoas muscle [8]. Another case of hydatid cyst was observed to cause a 10‐year shift of the rectal wall to the left side by Charalambous et al. [9]. It is essential to consider this in the differential diagnosis of any perianal conditions. Involvement of the perianal region in the presence of an abscess is rarely reported. Although it is not exactly clear how the scolex reaches the pelvic area, it seems likely that this may occur via systemic dissemination, the portal system, the retroperitoneal lymphatic system, or directly through the gastrointestinal tract [10]. Preoperative diagnosis of a hydatid cyst abscess can be challenging and may be confused with other differential diagnoses [11]. Some were diagnosed after re‐operation and Histopathological examination [12].

In light of the significant prevalence of Echinococcus infection in endemic areas, it becomes imperative for physicians practicing in these regions to possess a thorough understanding of the diverse and often severe manifestations of hydatid cysts, particularly those occurring outside the liver and within the lungs. Such knowledge is vital, as these cysts can lead to a range of complications that may impact patient health and treatment outcomes.
