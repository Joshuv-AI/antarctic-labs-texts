The affective impact of mixed reality on medical students’ learning outcomes in clinical skills: A preliminary study
Authors: Shuang-Shuang Chi, Wei-Chun Cheng, Shin-Yuan Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectives:This study aimed to investigate the relationship between affective responses to mixed reality (MR)-assisted learning and clinical skill performance among medical students in a preclinical training setting.Materials and Methods:A preliminary study was conducted among 33 fourth-year medical students who participated in an MR-assisted clinical skills curriculum. After initial traditional lectures, students engaged in MR-assisted sessions using Microsoft HoloLens 2® for themes of oropharyngeal management. Learning outcomes were evaluated via written tests, Objective Structured Clinical Examinations (OSCEs), and a validated Mixed-Reality Learning Environment (MRLE) questionnaire assessing satisfaction and affective attitudes.Results:A total of 33 students were analyzed. Checklist scores for Group A and Group B differed significantly (88.61 ± 4.79 vs. 75.45 ± 11.11, P < 0.001), while written test scores were comparable (P = 0.094). Regression showed a positive correlation between written and OSCE scores (P = 0.003). Female students reported higher affective responses (P = 0.022), while males achieved slightly higher, although nonsignificant, OSCE scores. MRLE satisfaction was not significantly associated with academic performance.Conclusion:MR-assisted learning potentially supported clinical skill acquisition and learner engagement, although affective attitudes and satisfaction might not directly predict performance. These findings highlighted MR’s value in bridging theoretical instruction and hands-on practice, with implications for curriculum design that account for individual learning differences.

INTRODUCTION

Medical education relied heavily on developing clinical skills and professional attitudes, which were traditionally cultivated through didactic lectures, hands-on practice, and patient interactions. Various instructional methodologies have been employed to enhance these essential competencies, with a growing focus on experiential learning. Kolb’s experiential learning theory emphasized the cyclical process of concrete experience, reflective observation, abstract conceptualization, and active experimentation, which aligned closely with the practical nature of medical training [1]. This theory underscored the importance of engaging learners in realistic scenarios where they reflected on their experiences and applied knowledge to future clinical encounters [2]. Research indicated that experimental learning methods yield higher Objective Structured Clinical Examination (OSCE) scores than traditional clinical skills training, and improvements across generic communication, clinical communication, physical examination, and procedural skills were noticed [34].

Digital technologies further expanded the possibilities for experiential learning by offering interactive and immersive environments that simulated real-life clinical situations while maintaining a safe and controlled setting. Mixed reality (MR) combines the physical and virtual resources, allowing learners to interact with three-dimensional holographic content while maintaining the awareness of their physical surroundings. Emerging literature supported using MR for its pedagogical value. While knowledge gained may be comparable to traditional methods, MR demonstrated superior learner satisfaction, engagement, and self-efficacy outcomes, particularly in procedural and communication skill acquisition [5678]. Moreover, MR-enhanced experiences allowed for repetitive and emotionally engaging learning, which might influence affective attitudes and motivation, key elements in sustained performance improvement [67]. Students immersed in virtual scenarios through MR enhanced learners’ motivation, engagement, and self-efficacy while improving clinical skills like anatomy understanding and needle insertion [5910]. However, the relationship between emotional engagement and objective learning outcomes remained unclear, especially in early-stage MR implementation. Above considerations, this preliminary study examined the correlation between medical students’ affective responses to MR-assisted learning and their clinical performance. The study contributed to the evidence base for integrating MR technologies into medical curricula aligned with experiential learning theory by investigating these associations.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study participants

This study involved informed medical students during a scheduled clinical skills training program at Tzu Chi University in November 2024. Ethical approval was granted by the Hualien Tzu Chi Hospital IRB (IRB113-213-B), including an explicit waiver of written informed consent. The study was classified as minimal-risk educational research and conducted using fully de-identified data. Instructors verbally informed students about the study objectives and procedures before the training, and students were assured that participation was voluntary and unrelated to their academic grading. All performance data were analyzed only after final grades were submitted and delinked from any personal identifiers. The study adhered to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Research workflow

A total of 40 fourth-year medical students (20 males and 20 females) were initially enrolled in the clinical skills training program [Figure 1]. All participants first received a traditional lecture on clinical procedures focusing on themes of the oral cavity: oropharyngeal examination (Group A) and oral intubation (Group B). Following the briefing, participants engaged in immersive learning sessions utilizing the MR learning resources. They practiced the skills on the simulated module with the faculty’s instructions. Upon utilization of MR learning, participants responded to questionnaires after both sessions. Two weeks later, those students participated in a summative assessment, including a written examination and an OSCE, for further inquiry into the correspondence of the learning outcomes in both procedures. After that, different domains of knowledge, skills, and affective attitude were measured. Seven samples were excluded due to a patterned response that indicated low-quality or nongenuine engagement. Among 33 participants, 18 were randomized to the oropharyngeal check station, and 15 had oropharyngeal intubation during the OSCE.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using the SPSS software (version 22.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the data. Independent-samples t-tests and Pearson correlation analyses were applied to explore between-group differences and variable relationships. Multiple linear regression analysis was used to identify the predictors of learning outcomes. Power analysis for regression and t-tests was performed using G*Power (Version 3.1; Heinrich Heine University, Düsseldorf, Germany) with α = 0.05 and power = 0.85.

Learning materials

The instructional materials comprised a series of prerecorded videos, each ranging from 3 to 5 min long, produced by clinical physicians. These videos demonstrated critical procedures for oropharynx care, such as oropharynx check physically and oral intubation within a realistic clinical setting featuring doctor–patient interactions. Utilizing the Microsoft HoloLens 2®, a cutting-edge MR device, the videos facilitated an interactive learning experience by allowing students to manipulate viewing angles and replay sequences, thus enhancing their understanding. This MR-assisted learning aimed to provide an immersive learning environment emphasizing the observation of clinical skills and adherence to competencies comprehensively and individually.

Curriculum

This preclinic clinical skills training program was conducted at Tzu Chi University in 2024. We developed the curriculum structured with authorized lectures and technology-assisted learning sessions: the first involved simulated lectures designed to teach students about oropharyngeal examinations and oral interventions, which covered clinical procedures such as swabbing and intubation. The second unit utilized MR technology to provide learning resources on topics related to the oropharynx, especially facilitating active participation in an immersive manner. Based on observational reflection on actual scenes during the procedures, students’ hands-on practice with modules under guidance from MR resources, as well as the faculty’s tips. The learning objectives for the MR learning emphasized the ability of clinicians to independently manage patient care and perform procedures with comprehensive precision and expertise.

Assessment tools

After engaging in hands-on practice in simulation sessions with training modules, students underwent a two-week interval before being randomized into either oropharyngeal examinations or oral intubation settings in the summative OSCE. The checklist items were checked for validity and reliability, which were derived from Taiwan’s National Board of Physicians OSCE. Each station of skill was operated for 8 min, and one qualified and pretrained rater observed and assessed students face-to-face. Single-choice questions with 40 items were tested to assess the acquisition level of knowledge relevant to anatomical and procedural techniques based on learning objectives.

Mixed reality learning environment questionnaire

A self-assessed survey was adapted from the validated virtual reality learning environment (VRLE) questionnaire [11]. The original VRLE questionnaire consisted of 12 items rated on a 5-point Likert scale, designed to capture students’ perceptions of learning experiences in VR. The researcher developed an additional item to assess students’ satisfaction with mixed-reality applications in clinical skills training. This resulted in a preliminary 13-item version, with a psychometric analysis conducted in 2024.

Following reliability testing and validity checks, one item was removed, leading to the finalized mixed-reality learning environment (MRLE) questionnaire. The Cronbach’s alpha for the domain assessing perspectives on practicing with mixed-reality tools and easy-to-use learning materials was 0.803. Similarly, the domain evaluating learner satisfaction with both basic medical sciences and clinical competencies yielded a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.964. Item analysis of the final 12-item MRLE questionnaire revealed significant differences across items. Exploratory factor analysis identified three underlying constructs: (1) learning satisfaction (six items), (2) positive attitudes toward mixed-reality-assisted learning (three items), and (3) negative attitudes (three items). The final version of the MRLE questionnaire was completed.

RESULTS

A total of 40 fourth-year medical students (20 females and 20 males) participated in the MR-assisted learning curriculum in 2024. Seven participants were excluded from the analysis due to invalidated responses, which were identified as inconsistent or invalid based on reverse-coded items and attention-check criteria on the MRLE questionnaire, yielding a final sample of 33 students (18 females and 15 males). The mean checklist scores for Group A and Group B were 88.61 ± 4.79 and 75.45 ± 11.11, respectively, with a significant difference between the two groups [P < 0.001; Table 1], while written test scores were comparable (P = 0.094). Based on the difficulty index (P) analysis, the items were classified as “very easy” (P = 0.94 for Group A and P = 0.92 for Group B), according to Kelley’s criteria [12]. Written and OSCE scores were comparable between the two procedure groups. Regression analysis revealed a significant positive association between written examination performance and OSCE scores [B = 0.347, P = 0.003; Table 2], suggesting that cognitive understanding may reinforce clinical execution. While OSCE scores significantly differed between high- and low-performing groups (P = 0.003, d ≈ 0.74), no significant differences were found in MRLE satisfaction, affective scores, or gender distribution [Table 3]. Female students reported significantly higher affective responses to MRLE than males (42.94 ± 7.59 vs. 38.73 ± 14.32, P = 0.022), despite similar satisfaction levels (44.16 ± 10.36 vs. 44.26 ± 16.55) and written test scores (85.41 ± 7.03 vs. 85.83 ± 6.31). Male students achieved slightly higher OSCE scores (84.73 ± 9.50 vs. 80.88 ± 11.24), although this difference was not statistically significant [Table 4]. These findings suggested that gender-based affective differences did not translate into performance outcomes. The regression model demonstrated a large effect (f2 = 0.7349), with five predictors explaining 42.35% of the variance (R2 = 0.4235). Overall, MRLE satisfaction did not exhibit a significant association with cognitive or psychomotor outcomes, highlighting the complexity of translating immersive engagement into objective learning gains in a short-term educational intervention. No statistically significant associations were observed between MRLE scores and academic outcomes, although this may be due to limited sample size or other confounding variables.

DISCUSSION

Our preliminary findings demonstrated that students who received MR-assisted instruction achieved satisfactory outcomes in knowledge retention and clinical performance assessments, with mean scores of 85.6 in the written test and 82.6 in the OSCE. Further analysis revealed a significant positive correlation between written examination and OSCE scores, suggesting that MR-assisted learning may be considered the integration of conceptual understanding and procedural application. This result aligned with Kolb’s experiential learning theory, which emphasized transforming concrete experiences into meaningful knowledge through reflective observation and active experimentation. In our MR-based design, each procedural step was captured from a physician’s first-person perspective, allowing learners to engage in visual demonstration and real-time clinical dialog. The physician verbalized key considerations while performing the procedures, simulating dynamic interactions with patients and interprofessional team members. Such immersive exposure contextualized clinical tasks within authentic care settings, fostering the development of both technical competencies and nontechnical skills, including communication, teamwork, and clinical reasoning. These findings commented on the pedagogical value of MR in bridging theoretical learning with practical execution in a safe and structured environment.

Compared to traditional simulations that focused on step repetition and procedural standardization, high-fidelity learning environments – such as those involving standardized patients, MR, or supervised clinical practice – provided a more authentic and emotionally engaging experience. These immersive settings enabled students not only to perform clinical skills but also to think critically during the procedure, to adapt in real time, and to reflect afterward on areas for improvement. In our preliminary study, students demonstrated solid performance following MR-assisted instruction. The average checklist scores for oropharyngeal inspection and oral intubation were 88.61 and 75.45, respectively. Although both OSCE stations were categorized as “very easy” according to Kelley’s index, the significant difference in checklist scores suggested that the effectiveness of MR-assisted learning may have varied across the clinical skills of different complexity. Written test scores remained comparable between the two procedure groups, suggesting that MR-supported instruction may have facilitated cognitive and procedural learning outcomes.

Prior research supported this notion. In a study on urinary catheter placement, students trained with Microsoft HoloLens achieved significantly higher scores than those who received traditional instruction, indicating the added value of realism in procedural learning [13]. These immersive experiences appeared to promote deeper reflection, a critical stage in Kolb’s learning cycle. Through this reflection, students often reassessed prior assumptions and constructed more clinically grounded mental models. Many likely developed greater intrinsic motivation to apply what they learned – transitioning naturally into the active experimentation phase, where they refined clinical strategies and built confidence through deliberate practice [14]. Unlike traditional models, which offered limited feedback and abstract anatomical references, MR enabled learners to visualize the internal human anatomy during procedures. This real-time visual-spatial connection between action and consequence may have heightened their appreciation for clinical relevance and reduced the gap between simulation and real-life application. As a result, learners might have engaged more seriously with the material and viewed technology-assisted instruction as directly tied to patient care. Taken together, a blended instructional approach, integrating traditional simulations with high-fidelity immersive experiences and guided by Kolb’s theory, could offer a structured yet flexible pathway to developing reflective, motivated, and clinically competent practitioners.

In our subgroup analysis based on written test performance, students with higher knowledge scores also tended to achieve better OSCE outcomes. Interestingly, this high-performing group expressed greater satisfaction with MR-assisted learning (mean satisfaction: 45.18 ± 9.54) and reported stronger preferences for using MR-based learning resources (43.00 ± 8.00), compared to the lower-performing group (41.44 ± 16.77 and 38.19 ± 13.64, respectively). This pattern suggested that students with stronger prior knowledge were more inclined to engage with immersive technology as a learning tool. One possible explanation was that these students were also more self-directed and disciplined in their learning strategies – characteristics previously linked to better academic performance in medical education [15]. Such learners might approach MR not just as a novelty, but as a meaningful extension of their study routine, using it to visualize complex procedures and integrate theory with practice. Our findings were further supported by a study showing that MR-enhanced anatomy learning improved knowledge retention in students who already had a strong foundational understanding [16].

Although previous studies had reported improvements in satisfaction and self-efficacy following MR interventions [5678910], our data did not reveal significant differences in these domains between high- and low-performing groups. This discrepancy highlighted that learner satisfaction might not consistently translate into improved outcomes in short-term MR learning contexts. It also suggested that individual factors – such as prior knowledge, learning preferences, or engagement strategies – could have mediated the relationship between immersive experiences and measurable performance, warranting further investigation. While our study did not replicate the reported gains in satisfaction and affect, the MR environment might still have fostered cognitive integration through immersive experience and contextualized reflection – core components of experiential learning theory. However, these potential benefits appeared to have depended on learner readiness and task design, rather than technological fidelity alone. This aligned with findings from a recent second-order meta-analysis, which showed that technology-enhanced learning only significantly improves the outcomes when it actively transforms or enhances learning activities – rather than merely substituting traditional methods. In our study, students with lower baseline knowledge might have lacked the cognitive or strategic resources to fully benefit from the added fidelity of MR, despite the tool’s immersive qualities. Therefore, the relationship between MR satisfaction, learning strategies, and academic achievement appeared to be more complex than previously assumed, warranting further investigation into learner characteristics, instructional alignment, and task design [17].

Furthermore, female students reported significantly positive affective responses toward MR-assisted learning (female vs. male: 42.94 ± 7.59 vs. 38.73 ± 14.32, P = 0.022). In contrast, male students performed better in the OSCE (female vs. male: 80.88 ± 11.24 vs. 84.73 ± 9.50, P = 0.604). These findings demonstrated that gender might influence learners’ attitudes, preferences, and performance in response to instructional methods, particularly when innovative technologies were introduced. Previous studies had shown that female learners often exhibited stronger engagement in MR-assisted environments when aligned with specific learning styles, such as concrete-sequential and abstract-random approaches, while males more favored concrete-random styles [18]. Regarding learning styles, females preferred concrete experiences, whereas males often demonstrated greater proficiency in abstract conceptualization [1920]. Regarding technology acceptance, models such as the Technology Acceptance Model and the Unified Theory of Acceptance and Use of Technology indicated that male learners generally reported higher self-efficacy and perceived greater utility in using educational technologies. Conversely, female learners were more influenced by subjective norms and emotional factors [212223]. These distinctions accounted for how male and female students perceive and benefit from MR-assisted instruction. These findings highlighted the importance of designing MR learning experiences that accommodated diverse learner preferences, including gender-related differences. Our study showed that while female students demonstrated greater emotional engagement with MR, male students emphasized the technology’s instrumental effectiveness in achieving learning outcomes. This absence of significance might reflect insufficient statistical power or underlying learner variability not captured by the current design.

CONCLUSION

This preliminary study explored the associations between medical students’ affective responses to MR-assisted learning and their clinical performance. MR learning was positively associated with student engagement and skill development. While emotional engagement differed by gender, it showed no significant link to academic outcomes. These findings supported MR’s potential as a complementary tool in preclinical education, warranting further validation through larger-scale comparative studies.

Limitation

Due to curricular constraints and ethical considerations safeguarding students’ rights, all participants in this preliminary study had been enrolled in MR-assisted clinical skills training. As a result, no control group receiving only traditional instruction was established, which limited direct comparisons across teaching modalities. Nevertheless, the study aimed to explore learner experiences within an authentic curricular setting. In addition, student performance was assessed through a summative OSCE rather than a direct workplace-based evaluation. Longitudinal tracking or follow-up assessments would be necessary to understand the sustained impact of MR-assisted training. Moreover, potential variation in the complexity of the two OSCE stations may have introduced a confounding effect, despite both stations being classified as “very easy” based on the difficulty index. This issue was acknowledged as a study limitation. Future research may incorporate a broader range of clinical skill complexity or include station type as a covariate to better control for this effect. Furthermore, the relatively small sample size, predetermined by the structure of the medical school cohort, reduced statistical power for subgroup analyses and may have contributed to the absence of statistical significance in some comparisons. Researchers suggested that interpretations involving gender differences or performance comparisons should be cautiously approached. Future studies should adopt randomized controlled designs and larger sample sizes to improve generalizability and methodological rigor.
