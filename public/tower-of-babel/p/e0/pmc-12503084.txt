Does e‐cigarette nicotine strength influence substitution for combustible cigarettes?
Authors: Jeffrey S Stein, Allison N Tegge, Jeremiah M Brown, Kelsey M Curran, Warren K Bickel
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractUsing the Experimental Tobacco Marketplace, we examined the effects of e‐liquid freebase nicotine strength (3–24 mg/mL) and e‐liquid price (US$0.25–$1.00/mL) on behavioral economic substitution for cigarettes in dual cigarette/e‐cigarette (n = 41) and exclusive cigarette (n = 40) users. Subjective effects of nicotine strength were also examined. All nicotine strengths served as a substitute for combustible cigarettes, with greater substitution observed in dual users. When analyzing e‐liquid volume purchased (mL), nicotine strength did not influence substitution; however, when analyzing e‐liquid nicotine purchased (mg), degree of substitution increased as a function of nicotine strength. Additionally, higher nicotine strengths reduced the volume and probability of e‐liquid purchasing, increased cigarette demand, and produced less favorable subjective effects than lower strengths. Increasing e‐liquid price reduced e‐liquid purchasing and did not influence substitution. We conclude that nicotine strength does not robustly affect the degree to which e‐liquid substitutes for cigarettes, as no effect was observed in analysis of the unit of purchase most relevant to real‐world sales (volume). Instead, high‐strength nicotine potentially reduces the appeal and probability of purchasing e‐liquid. Future work should examine these effects in nicotine salt‐based e‐liquids compared with freebase nicotine (used here) and in choice contexts with concurrent access to different nicotine strengths.Keywords: demand, cigarettes, e‐cigarettes, nicotine, substitution

INTRODUCTION

Cigarette smoking is the leading cause of preventable death in the United States, claiming 530,000 lives annually (GBD 2019 Tobacco Collaborators, 2021). Although e‐cigarettes pose distinct health risks that require continued monitoring (Seiler‐Ramadas et al., 2021), they are currently regarded as less harmful than combustible cigarettes (Hartmann‐Boyce et al., 2022; Marques et al., 2021; Qureshi et al., 2023) and may therefore help reduce exposure to tobacco‐related harms in adults who transition from exclusive combustible to exclusive e‐cigarette use (e.g., Hajek et al., 2019). However, of the nearly 12 million adults in the United States who use e‐cigarettes, more than 3 million report dual use of combustible cigarettes (CDC, 2023). Dual use of these products may also reduce harm exposure compared to combustible cigarette use alone, but less effectively than exclusive e‐cigarette use (Hartmann‐Boyce et al., 2023). Thus, maximizing e‐cigarettes' capacity for harm reduction requires an understanding of the policy and other environmental factors that drive purchasing and use.

E‐cigarette nicotine strength is one factor that potentially influences substitution. Nicotine content is federally unrestricted in the United States, with products commonly available in strengths ranging from 0 (nicotine‐free) to approximately 60 mg/mL. Understanding whether higher nicotine strengths facilitate the transition from combustible to e‐cigarettes or instead fosters dual use may inform tobacco control policy to improve public health.

In a recent systematic review of 34 observational and experimental studies on e‐cigarette nicotine strength, Gades et al. (2022) concluded that higher e‐cigarette nicotine strengths was associated with greater appeal and abuse liability. Mid‐ to high‐range strengths (12–18 mg/mL) were most commonly preferred, and higher nicotine strength was associated with increased plasma nicotine levels, increased satisfaction, and decreased craving and withdrawal following use, among other outcomes. Gades et al. also concluded that higher strength e‐cigarettes potentially facilitate substitution for combustible cigarettes, as some research shows that adults who have transitioned from combustible to e‐cigarette use show preference for higher nicotine strengths than do those who exclusively smoke cigarettes or have never smoked (Farsalinos et al., 2014; O'Connor et al., 2019; Sussan et al., 2018). However, this association has not been universally observed (Farsalinos et al., 2014; O'Connor et al., 2019; Sussan et al., 2018) and more work is needed to understand factors that may influence the role of e‐cigarette nicotine strength in substitution for combustible cigarettes.

Operant behavioral economics in tobacco regulatory science

Toward this end, operant behavioral economics provides a set of experimental methods to examine tobacco products' reinforcing value and factors that might influence the likelihood of product switching (Bickel et al., 2017). Assessment of own‐price demand measures the effects of a product's own price (Product A; e.g., combustible cigarettes) on purchasing or consumption of that product. Although a variety of outcome measures have been used, degree of own‐price demand is generally measured using indices that describe the amplitude (consumption at minimal price) and persistence (price sensitivity) of the demand curve (Bidwell et al., 2012).

Obtaining simultaneous estimates of cross‐price demand allows assessment of whether an alternative, price‐constant product (Product B; e.g., e‐cigarettes) can be a substitute for another product (Bickel et al., 2017; Hursh & Roma, 2013). Here, substitution is defined as increases in purchasing of Product B as the price of Product A increases (and Product A purchasing declines) (Hursh, 1984). Again, although outcome measures differ across studies, cross‐price demand is generally measured using indices that describe the direction and magnitude of the change in purchasing of the price‐constant product (Bickel et al., 2017; Hursh & Roma, 2013).

In this context, any tobacco product that is incrementally less harmful than combustible cigarettes and that also serves as a substitute potentially has the capacity to reduce tobacco‐related harms. However, additional demand measures that describe the absolute level of purchasing are needed to further support this interpretation. Estimating a product's potential for harm reduction based solely on the extent to which it interacts with another product's price fails to consider whether the product may engender dual use and increase total exposure to carcinogens and other toxicants. Toward this end, some prior studies have assessed the level of cross‐price demand independent of substitution using measures of cross‐price intensity (Stein, Koffarnus, et al., 2018), which describes Product B purchasing when Product A is available for free or at minimal price. We contend that measures of cross‐price intensity reflects dual product purchasing in the Experimental Tobacco Marketplace (ETM) and similar tasks (Stein, Koffarnus, et al., 2018). Higher cross‐price intensity for e‐cigarettes when combustible cigarette purchasing is high and minimally constrained by price suggests a tendency to use both products in combination. Consistent with this interpretation, dual cigarette/e‐cigarette use is associated with higher cross‐price intensity for e‐cigarettes, assessed using hypothetical purchase tasks (Snider et al., 2017). Whether e‐cigarette nicotine strength influences these estimates of dual purchasing requires further study.

Effects of nicotine strength on cross‐price demand in the Experimental Tobacco Marketplace

The extent to which any single product substitutes for another often depends on the presence or absence of other products (Johnson et al., 2004; Quisenberry et al., 2015). However, most hypothetical purchase tasks used to assess own‐ and cross‐price demand, despite their strengths, do not model this complexity and typically examine purchasing of two products at a time. In contrast, the ETM is a virtual storefront that allows participants to browse from a larger number of products while using an experimentally provided budget to make purchases (Bickel et al., 2018). Product prices, design characteristics (e.g., nicotine content), and product availability can be manipulated across conditions to examine their influence on purchasing of all products in the marketplace. In this way, the ETM provides important tests of generalizability in the assessment of own‐ and cross‐price tobacco product demand (for a review, see Bickel et al., 2018; Quisenberry et al., 2015).

Most relevant to the present study, Pope, Poe, Stein, Kaplan, Heckman, and colleagues (2019) used the ETM to investigate the effects of e‐liquid nicotine strength on substitution. After sampling each nicotine strength (0, 6, 12, and 24 mg/mL), participants completed four ETM purchasing conditions in which only one of these four strengths was available at a time. At each condition, the price of combustible cigarettes was increased across trials while the price of e‐liquid and other products remained constant. These authors' primary finding, where the amount of e‐liquid nicotine purchased (mg) at each trial served as the unit of demand curve analysis, revealed that substitution increased as a function of nicotine strength, with the greatest substitution observed for the highest 24‐mg/mL strength. These findings suggest that high‐nicotine e‐cigarettes may be more effective than lower strengths for promoting the transition from combustible to e‐cigarettes. However, at least three important gaps in knowledge remain to be explored.

First, Pope, Poe, Stein, Kaplan, Heckman, and colleagues' (2019) conclusions were specific to analyses using nicotine (mg) as the unit of purchase, where the volume of e‐liquid purchased (mL) was multiplied by the number of mg/mL. Use of this measure, which is common in ETM studies, aligns heterogeneous products that differ in unit size and other characteristics on a common scale. However, a secondary analysis of e‐liquid volume purchased revealed an inverted U‐shaped function. The intermediate 6‐mg/mL strength showed a significantly greater substitution effect than the 0‐, 12‐, and 24‐mg/mL strengths. Understanding these discrepant findings requires more research. Nicotine is the primary reinforcing constituent that drives tobacco consumption, but consumers purchase e‐cigarette products by volume (e.g., 1 mL of e‐liquid) or other units that combine e‐liquid volume and its delivery system (e.g., one Juul pod, containing 0.70 mL of e‐liquid).

Whether analysis of nicotine purchased or volume purchased is more germane to assess the effects of nicotine strength is unclear. Consideration of additional measures not examined by Pope, Poe, Stein, Kaplan, Heckman, and colleagues (2019) could aid in interpretation. Namely, the reinforcing and subjective effects of nicotine are often biphasic and not monotonically related to strength (e.g., Barrett & Odum, 2011; MacLean et al., 2021). All else being equal, consumers in the natural environment are likely to use the tobacco products or other drugs that elicit the most positive subjective effects (e.g., de Wit & Phillips, 2012; Gades et al., 2020), not necessarily the varieties that maximize nicotine intake. Thus, interpretation of the divergent effects of e‐liquid nicotine strength on substitution that emerge from analyses of e‐liquid volume versus nicotine purchased could benefit from additional investigation to assess subjective drug effects and the probability of e‐liquid purchasing. These data may elucidate how consumers are likely to allocate choice among the various e‐liquid nicotine strengths available in the tobacco marketplace.

Second, whether the price of e‐liquid moderates the effects of nicotine strength on substitution is unknown. Most prior ETM studies have examined only a single price for e‐liquid or other e‐cigarette products. However, the prices of e‐cigarette products in the real world vary substantially due to local taxes and other factors (Ali et al., 2024; Hoffer & Macumber‐Rosin, 2024). Thus, examining whether the price of e‐cigarettes influences the degree of substitution may inform estimates of generalizability and likelihood of substitution across regulatory contexts. One previous study in the ETM, using a single e‐liquid nicotine strength (24 mg/mL), investigated the effects of e‐liquid subsidies (Pope, Poe, Stein, Kaplan, DeHart, et al., 2019), reporting that reductions in price (relative to market price) increased cross‐price intensity but did not affect substitution itself. This suggests that e‐cigarette subsidies or discounts would likely not facilitate switching from combustible to e‐cigarettes and instead may promote dual use. Whether increasing e‐liquid price would, in turn, reduce cross‐price intensity remains to be examined.

Third, and finally, the study by Pope, Poe, Stein, Kaplan, Heckman, and colleagues (2019) was not able to systematically compare the effects of nicotine strength on substitution between adults reporting dual use of cigarettes and e‐cigarettes (n = 7 of 25) and exclusive cigarette use (n = 18 of 25). Thus, the extent to which the effects of nicotine strength or e‐liquid price on cross‐price demand differs between these subgroups is unknown. In observational research, dual use of these products is associated with a preference for higher nicotine strengths (Lee et al., 2018). This preexisting preference could drive greater substitution for high versus low‐nicotine e‐cigarettes or could simply increase the quantity of high‐nicotine e‐cigarette purchasing without influencing substitution. Comparisons between adults reporting dual use and exclusive cigarette use of the effects of nicotine strength on cross‐price demand for e‐cigarettes are needed.

To address these scientific gaps and extend prior research, the present study had three primary objectives:

Each of these primary objectives included a secondary objective of comparing the observed effects between adults reporting dual cigarette/e‐cigarette and exclusive cigarette use. Accordingly, in a 4 (e‐liquid nicotine strength) × 3 (e‐liquid price) × 2 (group) mixed factorial design, we recruited adults reporting dual use of both products (DU group) and exclusive cigarette use (CU group) to sample four e‐liquid nicotine strengths and complete multiple ETM conditions in which the available nicotine strength and price of e‐liquid was manipulated (within‐subjects).

METHODS

Participants

Eighty‐five participants (42 DU and 43 CU) were recruited from Roanoke, VA, and surrounding areas using flyers, the internet, and word‐of‐mouth referrals. Four participants were lost to follow‐up before the ETM assessment, leaving a final analytic sample of N = 81 (see Figure 1). In the original experimental design, planned sample sizes (N = 104; 52 each group) were calculated using a priori power analyses in which the DU and CU groups were recruited and assessed in two separate studies. However, the design was consolidated as a single study for three reasons: (1) data collection was interrupted by the COVID‐19 pandemic, (2) both groups were recruited concurrently and completed identical procedures (except self‐report measures of e‐cigarette use, noted below), and (3) combining data sets allowed direct comparisons of the DU and CU groups. In this updated design, a sensitivity power analysis conducted in G*Power 3.1 indicated that 81 participants provided 95% power to detect a small–medium effect size or greater (f ≥ .13) in mixed within‐/between‐subjects ANOVA, assuming 12 repeated measures, two groups, a correlation among repeated measures of r = .50, and alpha = .01 to account for multiple testing.

Participant flow diagram.

Eligibility was assessed online or by phone with the use of a generalized prescreening questionnaire for numerous studies, followed by in‐person screening for the present study using a timeline follow‐back interview (Brown et al., 1998), urine cotinine testing, and confirmation of self‐reported criteria. Inclusion criteria were (a) daily or almost daily cigarette smoking (≥28 of the last 30 days), (b) smoking ≥ 5 cigarettes per day, on average, and (c) regular e‐cigarette use for the DU group (≥10 of the last 30 days) and no history of regular e‐cigarette use for the CU group (≤4 lifetime uses). Additional inclusion criteria included (d) ≥ 200 ng/mL urine cotinine to confirm nicotine use (NicAlert; Nymox Pharmaceutical Corp, Hasbrouck Heights, NJ), (e) ages 18–65, (f) stable self‐reported mental and physical health, and (g) willingness to try study e‐cigarettes. Exclusion criteria precluded (a) plans to quit using cigarettes or e‐cigarettes during the study (b) plans to move away from the area during the study, (c) current use of prescription medicine that may affect smoking or nicotine metabolism (e.g., varenicline, buproprion), and (d) pregnancy or breastfeeding (females).

All study procedures were approved by the Biomedical Research Alliance of New York (BRANY) Institutional Review Board, contracted by Virginia Tech. All participants provided informed consent before completing study procedures. This study was preregistered on ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT03843047).

Study cigarettes, e‐cigarettes, and other products

The study e‐cigarette was the EXCEED D19 (118 × 19 mm; 1500 mAH battery) with 1.20‐ohm atomizer head, a tank‐style e‐cigarette purchased from www.joyetech.com (Shenzhen Joyetech Co., Ltd; Shenzhen, China). Study e‐liquid was purchased from www.VaporHQ.com in three flavors: tobacco (“American Red”), menthol (“32 Degrees”), and blueberry (“Blueberry Harvest”). E‐liquid was formulated with freebase nicotine in four strengths per flavor: 3, 6, 12, and 24 mg/mL. All e‐liquid contained a 50/50 ratio of propylene glycol to vegetable glycerine.

The specific e‐liquid flavors were those used in prior studies (Pope, Poe, Stein, Kaplan, DeHart, et al., 2019; Pope, Poe, Stein, Kaplan, Heckman, et al., 2019; Stein, Koffarnus, et al., 2018). These flavors were matched for pH across solutions, as pH can influence nicotine absorption (Burch et al., 1993). Before the study, e‐liquid pH was tested at the 6, 12, and 24 mg/mL strengths (HI98107 pHep pH tester; Hanna Instruments, Ltd., Germany). The 3‐mg/mL strength was unavailable at the time of pH testing. Four replicate samples were tested for each Flavor × Nicotine strength combination. The values for mean pH of the tobacco, blueberry, and menthol flavors were 8.23 ± 0.24, 8.24 ± 0.11, and 8.39 ± 0.21, respectively (see Supplementary Material for more detail; Supplementary Table 3).

Study cigarettes were participants' usual brands. Other study products included two varieties of smokeless tobacco (Camel snus pouches, Winterchill flavor and Grizzly chewing tobacco pouches, Wintergreen flavor) and two varieties of NRT (Nicorette 4‐mg nicotine polacrilex gum, White Ice Mint flavor and Nicorette 4‐mg nicotine lozenges, Mint flavor).

Procedures

Unless otherwise noted, all outcome measures reported below were assessed using Qualtrics survey software (Qualtrics, LLC, Provo, UT). Additional details regarding these procedures, including task instructions, can be found in Supplementary Material.

Session 1 (Intake)

Participants provided a urine sample to test for cotinine level and, if applicable, pregnancy. Participants also provided breath samples to measure CO and breath alcohol level. Positive breath alcohol samples resulted in the session being rescheduled for another day, although this occurred only once. Participants reported cigarette and e‐cigarette consumption over the last 30 days (Brown et al., 1998) using a timeline follow‐back interview. Participants in the DU group also reported usual e‐cigarette device type (e.g., tank‐style, Juul, disposable) by showing research personnel their personal e‐cigarette or identifying their device from an internet search.

Next, participants completed a survey comprising (a) demographic and tobacco‐use questionnaires; (b) the Fagerström Test for Cigarette Dependence (FTCD; Heatherton et al., 1991); (c) the Fagerström Test for E‐Cigarette Dependence (FTED; Etter & Eissenberg, 2015; Stein, Heckman, et al., 2018), with references to cigarettes replaced with e‐cigarettes (DU group only); (d) the Alcohol Use Disorders Identification Test (Saunders et al., 1993); (e) the Drug Use Disorders Identification Test (Berman et al., 2005); and (f) the Drug Effects/Liking Scale (DELS; Hatsukami et al., 1997) for their usual brand cigarettes and usual brand e‐cigarette (DU group only). In the DELS, participants used a 10‐point visual analog scale (VAS) to answer the following questions about each tobacco product: “Do you feel any effects? Do you feel any good effects? Do you feel any bad effects? How much do you like it? How much do you desire it? How much would you like to use it again?”

Product sampling

Participants were shown how to use the study e‐cigarette and briefly sampled (5–6 puffs) three flavors of nicotine‐free e‐liquid in the laboratory. Participants chose their favorite e‐liquid flavor for the remainder of the study. Only this flavor was available in subsequent ETM assessments to minimize the possibility of observing differences in purchasing due solely to varying flavor preferences. Participants received the e‐cigarette battery to take home along with four separate tanks, each containing 2 mL of a different nicotine strength. Participants were not blinded to nicotine strength in this and all subsequent procedures to model purchasing in the natural environment, in which nicotine strength is clearly labeled in advertising and at points of sale. Participants also received the manufacturer‐provided USB charger and instructional literature. At the end of this session, participants were instructed to (a) take at least 10 puffs of each e‐liquid over the next 7 days, (b) not mix any e‐liquid nicotine strengths or use nonstudy e‐liquid in the study device, (c) not use study e‐liquid in a nonstudy device, and (d) not sample more than one e‐liquid nicotine strength per day.

Session 2 (ETM purchasing)

Participants returned to the laboratory approximately 7 days later and completed the DELS for each e‐liquid nicotine strength (order counterbalanced by Latin Square), an ETM demonstration task, and the full ETM assessment. Purchases in this demonstration were hypothetical, whereas participants in the full ETM assessment received a randomly selected subset of purchases. Completing the ETM demonstration and the full ETM assessment required a median (IQR) time of 52.95 (38.58, 94.55) min. Participants also completed several assessments for a separate study that are not reported here, including hypothetical purchase tasks and a delay‐discounting task.

ETM demonstration

Research staff read instructions for the demonstration task aloud to participants while these instructions were displayed on a computer screen. Comprehension was assessed using seven multiple‐choice questions. Research staff confirmed correct answers and remediated incorrect answers by restating the relevant instruction, providing generic examples, and asking participants to restate the instruction in their own words. Task instructions and comprehension check questions may be found in Supplementary Material.

Next, participants completed four demonstration trials in which the price of cigarettes was manipulated in ascending order (USD $0.25, $0.50, $1.00, and $2.00 per cigarette) while the prices of all other products remained constant at their local market prices ($0.20/pouch of snus and chewing tobacco; $0.80/piece of gum; $0.60/lozenge). E‐liquid was not available for purchase in this demonstration. At each trial, all products appeared on the same page and were accompanied by a product image and brief description (see Supplementary Material; Supplementary Table 1). Nicotine content was included in the description only for products that are typically labeled with this information (e‐liquid and NRT). Participants were instructed that they may purchase as many individual units of each product as they would like to use over the next 7 days. However, expenditures could not exceed their budget. The total budget and the amount spent in total and on each product were displayed and updated in real time within each trial. Budgets were proportional to participants' real‐world tobacco product expenditures (Koffarnus, Wilson, et al., 2015) and were calculated by multiplying participants' average daily intake of each product available in the ETM (from the timeline follow‐back interview) by the average local market price of these products. These values were then summed across products and multiplied by 7 days.

ETM assessment

Next, participants completed 12 purchasing conditions in the ETM reflecting all possible combinations of e‐liquid nicotine strength (3, 6, 12, and 24 mg/mL) and e‐liquid price ($0.25, $0.50, and $1.00 per mL). Each of these 12 conditions comprised four cigarette prices, as described for the demonstration task, yielding 48 total trials. Across conditions, a different e‐liquid nicotine strength was available at each of three e‐liquid prices (order of conditions counterbalanced by Latin Square). Brief breaks (2–3 min) were scheduled after each condition to minimize the possibility of carryover effects and fatigue. After completion of all 12 conditions, one trial was drawn randomly and participants received all tobacco products purchased at that trial as well as any unspent budget.

Data analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.3.1 (R Core Team, 2013) using the lme4 package for mixed models (Bates et al., 2015). Results are reported using Type III sum of squares and pairwise contrasts, with p values adjusted for multiple comparisons using Tukey's method.

Participant characteristics

Demographic and other participant characteristics are summarized where appropriate using mean (standard deviation) and frequency (percentage). Demographics were compared between DU and CU groups using t tests for continuous variables and Fisher's exact tests for categorical variables.

ETM data quality

Accuracy in the comprehension check was expressed as the percentage of correct answers (of seven total items). Purchasing data quality was examined by applying standardized diagnostic criteria to all own‐price cigarette demand data to describe the extent to which purchasing was systematically affected by price (Stein et al., 2015). These criteria assume that systematic demand data show (a) from the lowest to highest prices, a minimum 0.025 log‐unit reduction in purchased amounts per log‐unit change in price (trend criterion); (b) no increases1 in purchasing between contiguous prices that are greater than 25% of purchasing at the lowest price (bounce criterion); and (c) no responses in which zero purchasing is followed by nonzero purchasing at higher prices (reversal criterion).

Probability of purchasing

The effects of e‐liquid nicotine strength, group, and e‐liquid price on the probability of product purchasing were modeled using logistic mixed‐effects models that included participant‐level random effects to account for the repeated measures across 12 conditions (e‐liquid nicotine strengths and prices). Participants were determined to have purchased the product(s) in each condition if they purchased at least one unit across any of the four ETM trials (i.e., cigarette prices). Product purchasing was represented as a binary variable. Due to the infrequent purchasing of smokeless tobacco and nicotine replacement products, these products were combined into a single oral products category in this and all subsequent analyses.

We performed model selection to identify the variables that were supported by the data. The optimal model was determined as the set of predictive variables with the lowest value for the Bayesian information criterion (BIC). The variables that were considered include e‐liquid nicotine strength, group, and e‐liquid price, and the pairwise interactions among these three variables. The analyses for ETM purchasing were performed independently for cigarettes, e‐liquid, and oral products.

Own‐price demand for cigarettes

Dependent measures of own‐price cigarette demand included observed values of the quantity purchased at $0.25 (Q $0.25), reflecting the amplitude of demand, as well as peak expenditure across prices (OMax ), reflecting both amplitude and persistence (Acuff et al., 2020; Aston et al., 2017; Bidwell et al., 2012). We did not calculate additional observed measures that describe demand via reference to price (PMax and breakpoint) because the use of four cigarette prices in the ETM limits the granularity of these values. Similarly, we did not derive measures using the exponential (Hursh & Silberberg, 2008) or exponentiated (Koffarnus, Franck, et al., 2015) models of demand because the use of four cigarette prices did not support the application of these three‐parameter models to all participants.

The effects of e‐liquid nicotine strength, group, and e‐liquid price on Q $0.25 and OMax were examined using linear mixed‐effects models (again including participant‐level random effects). Model selection was performed as previously described when considering the variables e‐liquid nicotine strength, e‐liquid price, and group, and the pairwise interactions among these three variables. E‐liquid nicotine strength and e‐liquid price were log transformed to adjust for nonlinear relationships.

Cross‐price demand for e‐liquid and oral tobacco/nicotine products

The effects of e‐liquid nicotine strength, group, and e‐liquid price on e‐liquid and oral product cross‐price demand were examined using separate linear mixed‐effects models (again including participant‐level random effects). Model selection was performed as previously described when considering cigarette price (log transformed), e‐liquid nicotine strength, e‐liquid price, group, and the pairwise interactions among these four variables. The mixed‐effects model for e‐liquid demand was repeated when using volume (mL) and nicotine (mg) as the unit of purchase.

Drug effects/liking scale

Ten‐point VAS ratings for individual items in the DELS were summed to produce an overall score (Stein, Koffarnus, et al., 2018). The “bad effects” item was reverse scored, and the “any effects” item was omitted because it reflects the combination of good and bad effects. The resulting score could range from 0 to 50, where higher scores reflect a more positive subjective drug response. These scores were analyzed using linear mixed‐effects models (again including participant‐level random effects). Model selection was performed as previously described when considering e‐liquid nicotine strength, group, and their interaction. E‐liquid nicotine strength was treated as a categorical variable in this analysis because visual inspection of the data indicated a nonlinear relationship. Descriptive statistics were also calculated for individual item ratings.

On average, participants in the DU group used an e‐cigarette approximately 13 times per day, where a single use was defined as any instance of picking the device up, using it for any period, and putting it back down again (Stein, Heckman, et al., 2018). A plurality of these participants (46.30%) reported a tank‐style e‐cigarette as their usual device. Usual e‐cigarette nicotine strengths were approximately evenly split across four levels: low, medium, high, and “unknown.” Fourteen of these participants (34.15%) reported using e‐cigarettes to facilitate cigarette smoking cessation.

E‐liquid sampling

Both groups most frequently selected blueberry as their preferred study e‐liquid flavor (DU: 63.41%; CU: 40%), followed by menthol (DU: 19.51%; CU: 35%) and tobacco (DU: 17.07%; CU: 25%). Adherence to at‐home sampling procedures was generally high. Over the sampling period, 95.06, 98.77, 96.30, and 91.36% of participants reported taking at least 10 puffs of the 3‐, 6‐, 12‐, and 24‐mg/mL strengths, respectively. Most participants reported refraining from mixing e‐liquid nicotine strengths or using nonstudy e‐liquid in the study‐provided device (96.3%), using study e‐liquid in nonstudy devices (98.8%), and sampling more than one e‐liquid nicotine strength per day (69.14%). Although estimates of actual e‐liquid use during the sampling period were collected (by weight), they are not reported here due to inaccurate (i.e. partial leakage) and missing data.

DISCUSSION

The present study examined the effects of e‐liquid nicotine strength and e‐liquid price in the ETM on purchasing of cigarettes, e‐liquid, and oral tobacco/nicotine products in DU and CU groups. In general, both e‐liquid and oral products were purchased as substitutes for combustible cigarettes. Moreover, own‐price demand for combustible cigarettes (Q $0.25 and OMax ) increased as a function of e‐liquid nicotine strength in all conditions and both groups. Several additional important findings, however, depended on the unit of e‐liquid analysis and group (DU and CU). Below, we summarize and discuss these and other findings in the context of the present study's three primary objectives.

Objective 1: Reexamine effects of e‐liquid strength on cross‐price demand in analyses of volume vs. nicotine purchased

Our first objective was to examine the effects of e‐liquid nicotine strength on cross‐price demand between analyses of volume and nicotine purchased. Here, in analysis of volume purchased, e‐liquid nicotine strength did not significantly influence the degree to which e‐liquid served as a substitute for combustible cigarettes. In contrast, in analysis of nicotine purchased, e‐liquid substitution increased as a function of nicotine strength, with the largest effect at 24 mg/mL. In both analyses (volume and nicotine purchased), the DU group showed significantly greater substitution than the CU group.

These divergent effects of e‐liquid nicotine strength in analyses of volume versus nicotine purchased are generally similar to those reported by Pope, Poe, Stein, Kaplan, Heckman, and colleagues (2019), with at least one critical difference. Pope et al. reported a biphasic effect of nicotine strength on substitution when analyzing volume purchased (largest effect at 6 mg/mL) and a monotonically increasing effect when analyzing nicotine purchased (largest effect at 24 mg/mL). Why a similar biphasic effect in volumetric analyses was not observed in the present study is unclear. Nonetheless, the present findings demonstrate that a statistically significant, strength‐related substitution effect on nicotine purchased emerges from undifferentiated volumetric slopes. This arises because incremental, linear variation in volume purchased corresponds to exponential variation in nicotine purchased (i.e., nicotine purchased = volume × 3, 6, 12, or 24 mg/mL nicotine), which magnifies the slope of the substitution curve as nicotine strength increases.

Objective 2: Examine effects of e‐liquid nicotine strength on subjective drug effects and probability of purchasing

Our second objective was to examine subjective drug effects and the probability of e‐liquid purchasing to facilitate interpretation of divergent effects of nicotine strength in analysis of volume versus nicotine purchased. Here, the 24‐mg/mL strength produced subjectively less favorable subjective effects than the 3‐, 6‐, and 12‐mg/mL strengths in both groups. In prior research, the reinforcing value and subjective effects of nicotine are not linearly related to nicotine strength (e.g., Barrett & Odum, 2011; MacLean et al., 2021). Thus, more nicotine is not necessarily more reinforcing. Indeed, for e‐cigarettes specifically, aversive effects of high nicotine strengths have been documented previously (Pullicin et al., 2020; Yingst et al., 2024). Although nicotine is the primary reinforcing constituent of tobacco products, sensory and other nonpharmacological factors remain important drivers of self‐administration (Johnson et al., 2004; Leventhal et al., 2021; K. Perkins, 1996; Rose et al., 2000). Deriving primary outcome measures from nicotine purchased in the ETM risks ignoring these subjective drivers in estimates of substitution, as e‐liquid is purchased by volume and not in units of nicotine.

If high nicotine strengths produce subjectively less favorable or aversive effects, as the present and prior data suggest, consumers in an open marketplace who can choose from a wide range of nicotine strengths would be less likely to purchase these varieties over others. Consistent with this consideration, participants in the present study were less likely to purchase any e‐liquid as nicotine strength increased. In contrast, analysis of substitution using nicotine purchased showed no evidence of this apparently aversive effect of high nicotine strength.

Objective 3: Examine effects of e‐liquid price on cross‐price demand

Our third objective was to examine the effects of e‐liquid price on cross‐price demand. Here, e‐liquid price did not significantly influence the degree of substitution for combustible cigarettes; that is, e‐liquid was a substitute at all e‐liquid prices. More generally, however, measures of overall e‐liquid purchasing and cross‐price intensity decreased as a function of e‐liquid price. This is consistent with a prior ETM study (Pope, Poe, Stein, Kaplan, DeHart, et al., 2019), which showed that e‐liquid subsidies (reductions in price) did not influence the strength of substitution but did increase cross‐price intensity for e‐liquid. The present study extends these findings by showing that the effect of e‐liquid price on overall volume purchased and cross‐price intensity was greater in the DU than in the CU groups (i.e., consistent with the former group's dual‐use status).

In additional the analyses in the present study, e‐liquid nicotine strength moderated the effects of e‐liquid price in opposing directions, depending on the unit of e‐liquid purchased. That is, increasing e‐liquid price reduced e‐liquid purchasing more at lower strengths in the analysis of volume purchased and at higher strengths in the analysis of nicotine purchased. Similar to the substitution effects discussed earlier, this discrepancy is likely because linear variation in volume purchased corresponds to exponential variation in nicotine purchased and reflects another case in which an effect on nicotine purchased was not evident in participants' direct purchasing behavior of e‐liquid by volume.

Interpretation of divergent effects and implications for tobacco control

Taking all findings into account (ETM and subjective ratings), the results of the present study suggest that higher e‐liquid nicotine strengths are no more effective, and may be less so, than lower strengths for facilitating transitions from combustible to e‐cigarettes. That is, e‐liquid substitution did not vary across nicotine strengths, and the highest nicotine strength appeared to have aversive properties that limited purchasing.

As in real‐world tobacco sales, participants in the ETM purchase e‐liquid by volume and not by units of nicotine. Thus, analysis of nicotine purchased describes an emergent effect of e‐liquid nicotine strength on substitution. This emergent effect should be interpreted with caution, as the extent to which it influences consumers' whole‐product purchasing decisions is unclear. At minimum, we contend that disproportionate or exclusive attention to nicotine purchased as a measure of substitution in the ETM does not adequately describe the direct effects of nicotine strength on purchasing behavior.

We note, however, that nicotine purchased may in some cases be an important marker relevant to total nicotine exposure and subsequent addiction risk. Nicotine is the primary driver of tobacco purchasing, and the present and prior studies show that varying levels of nicotine in e‐liquid will modulate the degree of substitution and corresponding nicotine intake, which may increase potential for addiction and drive further use and dependence (Kundu et al., 2025). Thus, effects of any experimental manipulation in the ETM that affects patterns of nicotine purchasing could have important implications for reducing the addictive potential of e‐cigarettes and total nicotine intake.

With these considerations in mind, the utility of the present study's divergent effects for informing tobacco control policy depend on future regulatory goals in an evolving tobacco marketplace. If the goal of policy is to facilitate substitution to lower risk products while limiting nicotine exposure, the present study's analyses of volume purchased suggest that e‐cigarette nicotine could be restricted to lower and less addictive levels (e.g., 3–6 mg/mL) without compromising e‐cigarettes' capacity to facilitate product switching. Conversely, if the goal of policy is to reduce exposure to the aerosol vehicle and potential pulmonary harms (Cobb et al., 2021; Szafran et al., 2020), then results of the analysis of nicotine purchased would be more germane to consider. That is, high nicotine strengths decreased the total volume of e‐liquid purchasing. This suggests that high nicotine strengths could remain unrestricted to reduce total aerosol exposure while still supporting some degree of substitution between e‐liquid and combustible cigarettes. In either case, we note that more research is required to examine the generality of these effects across different e‐cigarette products and populations, including subgroups with repeated exposure.

Freebase nicotine vs. nicotine salts

The conclusion that high‐nicotine e‐liquid is no more effective than lower strengths for facilitating substitution for combustible cigarettes may seem at odds with a recent systematic review of studies on the role of nicotine strength on appeal and abuse potential. Specifically, in their review, Gades et al. (2022) concluded that high nicotine strengths increase e‐cigarette appeal and may more effectively facilitate cigarette smoking cessation than lower strengths. However, we note two relevant considerations. First, these authors' conclusions were restricted to the available literature from 2013–2020 and, as a result, were primarily informed by studies assessing use of products formulated with freebase nicotine (as in the present study). Although these studies provide critical insights, the preferred freebase nicotine strengths that were most commonly associated with substitution or smoking cessation (12–18 mg/mL; Farsalinos et al., 2014; Sussan et al., 2018; cf. O'Connor et al., 2019) were lower than the strengths identified as having aversive or less favorable subjective effects in the present and prior studies (Yingst et al., 2024). Thus, although adults who switch to e‐cigarettes may prefer higher nicotine strengths than other users, this observation may not extend when considering strengths that produce subjective effects on the descending limb of the dose–response curve.

Second, from 2013 to 2020, the nicotine strengths described as “high” evolved relative to market changes due to the introduction (in 2015) and rising popularity of products formulated with nicotine salts (most prominently, Juul). Due to this market shift, average nicotine strength in sales data increased by more than twofold in the United States in subsequent years (Ali et al., 2023; Romberg et al., 2019). Unlike products formulated with freebase nicotine which are typically available in strengths up to 24 mg/mL (and less commonly, 36 mg/mL), salt‐based formulations are commonly sold at strengths ≥50 mg/mL. Salt‐based products can support higher nicotine content and deliver nicotine more effectively (Christen et al., 2024; Leventhal et al., 2021) due to formulations that minimize harshness and improve sensory experience (Leventhal et al., 2021).

For these reasons, we restrict our conclusions to examinations of e‐liquid formulated with freebase nicotine. The aversive effects that were evident for the highest 24‐mg/mL freebase nicotine formulations in the present study may not generalize to examinations of salt‐based formulations with even higher nicotine content. Future studies should examine the effects of nicotine strength on cross‐price demand using salt‐based formulations.

Limitations and additional areas for future research

Three limitations not already discussed deserve note and require additional study. First, although we compared DU and CU groups in the present study, reasons for dual use are varied (Coleman et al., 2022). The effects of nicotine strength and e‐liquid price on cross‐price e‐liquid demand may differ in subgroups who are using e‐cigarettes to facilitate smoking cessation versus other reasons (e.g., to subvert smoking bans). However, the sample size of participants in the present study who reported using e‐cigarettes for smoking cessation (n = 14) did not support analyses to specifically examine this subgroup. In light of observational data demonstrating preference for higher strength e‐cigarettes in those who have successfully transitioned between products (e.g., Farsalinos et al., 2014), future work should address this limitation by using larger sample sizes and stratifying based on participants' reasons for e‐cigarette use.

Second, we did not investigate the effects of e‐liquid nicotine strength and price using a unit‐price model of behavioral economic demand (i.e., expressing price relative to the most granular unit of drug dose). Prior work shows that unit‐price analyses explain much of the observed variance in demand and align data along a single, approximately continuous curve (e.g., Bickel et al., 1991; DeGrandpre et al., 1993; Madden et al., 2000; Pope, Poe, Stein, Kaplan, DeHart, et al., 2019). Importantly, unit price can be manipulated by changing a tobacco product's labeled price (e.g., price per mL of e‐liquid), its nicotine content (e.g., price per mg of e‐liquid nicotine), or both, where the unit price of higher strengths would be lower (per mg) than lower strengths (Smith et al., 2014). Because the present study involved both of these manipulations, a novel unit‐price model adapted for cross‐price demand may be particularly helpful for understanding observed effects. However, such a model for substitution has not been developed and it must simultaneously account for the unit price of e‐liquid, cigarettes, and the relative difference between them. Development of this more complex model was therefore outside the scope of this report. Future research should explore unit‐price reanalyses of this and other published data sets to advance our understanding of behavioral economics in tobacco regulatory science.

Third, and finally, the present study's findings are specific to short‐term e‐liquid exposure. Participants sampled e‐liquid in the week prior to the ETM assessment, although whether the observed effects may generalize over time and with continued exposure is unknown. Continued use may produce drug tolerance and habituation to the aversive effects of high nicotine strengths (Fattinger et al., 1997; K. A. Perkins, 2002). Thus, future work should examine longitudinal changes in own‐ and cross‐price demand measures with chronic exposure.

CONCLUSIONS

When restricting interpretation to short‐term exposure to e‐liquid formulated with freebase nicotine, the present study's results suggest that e‐liquid nicotine strength does not influence the degree to which e‐liquid serves as a behavioral economic substitute for combustible cigarettes. Together, these findings suggest that high e‐liquid nicotine strengths are no more effective than lower strengths to facilitate transition from combustible to e‐cigarettes. Although analyses of nicotine purchased in the present and prior research (Pope, Poe, Stein, Kaplan, DeHart, et al., 2019) revealed that nicotine strength increased substitution in a strength‐dependent manner, this emergent effect may not reflect consumer purchasing patterns in the real‐world tobacco marketplace. Moreover, the present study's results suggest that increasing e‐liquid price (i.e., via taxes) may enhance e‐cigarettes' capacity for tobacco harm reduction by reducing overall e‐cigarette use (by volume or nicotine) without compromising e‐cigarettes' role as a viable substitute for combustible cigarettes. Future research should examine the generality of the present study's findings with concurrent availability of multiple e‐liquid nicotine strengths, in salt and freebase nicotine formulations, with longer‐term exposure to e‐liquid, and in DU subgroups using e‐cigarettes specifically for smoking cessation.
