Hereditary transthyretin amyloidosis mimicking ALS: First genetically proven case report from Saudi Arabia
Authors: Hosna Elshony, Rakan Almuhanna, Khaled O Albazli, Rabia Muddassir
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT

Introduction

Amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) is a progressive neurodegenerative disorder characterized predominantly by degeneration of upper and lower motor neurons, leading to limb and bulbar weakness, fasciculations, and respiratory failure [1]. Although ALS is primarily a motor disorder, subtle extramotor features may occur in some patients; however, clinically significant sensory, autonomic, or systemic involvement is atypical and should prompt consideration of alternative diagnoses [2]. The identification of potentially treatable ALS mimics remains a critical component of neuromuscular evaluation.

Hereditary transthyretin amyloidosis (ATTRv) is an autosomal dominant multisystem disorder caused by pathogenic variants in the transthyretin (TTR) gene, resulting in misfolded protein deposition as amyloid fibrils in peripheral nerves, myocardium, and other tissues [3]. The classical phenotype consists of a length-dependent sensorimotor axonal polyneuropathy frequently accompanied by autonomic dysfunction and restrictive cardiomyopathy [4]. However, motor-predominant presentations mimicking motor neuron disease have increasingly been recognized and represent an important diagnostic pitfall [5], [6]. In such cases, patients may even fulfill the revised El Escorial diagnostic criteria for ALS [7].

Distinguishing ATTRv from ALS has major therapeutic implications. While ALS remains a relentlessly progressive disease with limited disease-modifying options, ATTRv is now a treatable condition, with TTR stabilizers and gene-silencing therapies shown to slow neuropathy progression and improve clinical outcomes [8], [9].

Here, we report the first genetically confirmed case of hereditary ATTRv mimicking ALS in Saudi Arabia, highlighting key diagnostic pitfalls and emphasizing the importance of considering systemic and potentially treatable etiologies in patients presenting with atypical motor neuron syndromes.

Case description

A 47-year-old man with no prior neurological illness presented with an 18-month history of progressive right-sided limb weakness and dysarthria. Symptoms began insidiously with impaired fine motor function of the right hand, including difficulty buttoning clothes and handling small objects. Over the subsequent year, weakness extended to the right lower limb, resulting in difficulty rising from a seated position. Three months before presentation, he developed slurred speech. He denied dysphagia, respiratory symptoms, bladder or bowel dysfunction, orthostatic dizziness, erectile dysfunction, gastrointestinal disturbance, or visual complaints.

He had been diagnosed with type 2 diabetes mellitus six months earlier and was receiving metformin and insulin glargine. There was no family history of neuromuscular or cardiac disease.

On neurological examination, he was alert and cognitively intact. Fasciculations were observed in the right upper limb, with distal muscle wasting in both upper limbs. Muscle tone was normal throughout. Strength was reduced in the right upper limb (Medical Research Council [MRC] grade 4/5 proximally and distally) and right lower limb (4 to 4+/5), with mild weakness in left ankle dorsiflexion (4/5). Deep tendon reflexes were brisk (3+) and symmetric, without clonus or extensor plantar responses. No spasticity was observed. Sensory examination demonstrated mild distal reduction in pinprick sensation in a glove-and-stocking distribution. Coordination and gait were preserved.

Routine laboratory investigations, including complete blood count, metabolic panel, thyroid function, vitamin levels, autoimmune screening (ANA, ENA), paraneoplastic antibodies, and serum and urine protein electrophoresis, were unremarkable. Cerebrospinal fluid analysis showed normal protein and cell counts. Brain and whole-spine magnetic resonance imaging revealed no structural lesion, corticospinal tract abnormality, or compressive myelopathy. Contrast-enhanced computed tomography of the chest, abdomen, and pelvis showed no evidence of malignancy.

Nerve conduction studies demonstrated reduced sensory nerve action potential (SNAP) amplitudes in the sural and ulnar nerves, along with reduced compound muscle action potential (CMAP) amplitudes, consistent with a length-dependent axonal sensorimotor neuropathy. There was no electrophysiological evidence of demyelination or conduction block. Needle electromyography revealed active and chronic denervation changes in bulbar (hyoglossus), cervical, and lumbosacral myotomes. These findings were widespread; however, the presence of abnormal sensory nerve conduction suggested a generalized axonal neuropathic process rather than a pure motor neuron disorder.

Given the asymmetric motor-predominant presentation, multifocal motor neuropathy was considered, and the patient received intravenous immunoglobulin (2 g/kg over 5 days). Although mild subjective improvement was reported during hospitalization, no objective functional gains were observed, and symptoms continued to progress over the following six weeks.

Due to the presence of sensory involvement, absence of definitive upper motor neuron signs, and atypical clinical course, genetic testing was pursued. Whole-exome sequencing identified a heterozygous pathogenic variant in the transthyretin gene: NM_000371.4:c.424G>A (p.Val142Ile). No pathogenic variants were detected in SOD1, TARDBP, FUS, or C9orf72.

Transthoracic echocardiography demonstrated mild concentric left ventricular hypertrophy (interventricular septal thickness 12 mm) with preserved systolic function (ejection fraction >55%) and no significant diastolic dysfunction. Cardiac magnetic resonance imaging and bone-avid tracer scintigraphy (e.g., 99mTc-DPD/PYP) were not performed due to local availability constraints. No formal autonomic testing (e.g., tilt-table testing or sudomotor assessment) was performed, though clinical history was negative for autonomic symptoms. There was no clinical evidence of renal or ocular involvement.

A diagnosis of hereditary transthyretin amyloidosis (ATTRv) with motor-predominant neuropathy was established. The patient received genetic counseling and was referred for evaluation of disease-modifying therapy.

Discussion

This case highlights the diagnostic challenge of hereditary transthyretin amyloidosis (ATTRv) presenting as a motor neuron disease mimic. The patient initially fulfilled clinical and electrophysiological criteria suggestive of probable ALS under the revised El Escorial framework [7]. However, several atypical features—including clinically significant sensory abnormalities, absence of definitive upper motor neuron (UMN) signs (spasticity, Babinski), mild left ventricular hypertrophy, and lack of sustained response to intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIg)—prompted further investigation leading to the diagnosis of ATTRv.

While ATTRv classically presents with length-dependent sensorimotor polyneuropathy and autonomic dysfunction, motor-predominant variants mimicking ALS are increasingly recognized [4], [5]. In this patient, nerve conduction studies revealed reduced sensory nerve action potentials, which is incompatible with pure ALS and strongly suggests a systemic neuropathy [10]. Although subtle sensory changes may occasionally occur in ALS, clinically significant sensory loss should always prompt evaluation for alternative diagnoses [2]. The absence of true UMN signs was another key differentiator; despite brisk reflexes, there was no spasticity or pathological reflexes, and MRI ruled out structural mimics. Brisk reflexes are not typical of ATTRv neuropathy and may reflect preserved corticospinal tract function rather than true upper motor neuron involvement [3].

Genetic testing identified a heterozygous pathogenic variant in the TTR gene: NM_000371.4:c.424G>A (p.Val142Ile). Although traditionally linked to late-onset cardiac-predominant disease, this variant has been increasingly reported in patients with early-onset, motor-predominant phenotypes [5], [6]. This phenotypic variability highlights the need for genetic testing even in patients without classic autonomic or severe cardiac manifestations. Notably, the patient had no known family history. While ATTRv is autosomal dominant, incomplete penetrance or lack of diagnosis in relatives may result in an apparently negative family history, particularly in regions with high consanguinity [11].

Cardiac involvement was limited to mild concentric left ventricular hypertrophy on echocardiography. We acknowledge the limitation that cardiac magnetic resonance imaging and bone-avid tracer scintigraphy were not performed due to availability constraints. Similarly, formal autonomic testing was not undertaken. However, unexplained left ventricular hypertrophy in the setting of progressive neuropathy is a recognized red flag for ATTRv [12]. The transient subjective improvement following IVIg was likely non-specific, as ATTRv is non-inflammatory; the lack of sustained benefit helped exclude immune-mediated mimics such as multifocal motor neuropathy [13], [14].

The distinction between ALS and ATTRv has profound therapeutic consequences. Unlike ALS, which has limited treatment options, ATTRv is treatable with TTR stabilizers (e.g., tafamidis) and gene-silencing therapies (e.g., patisiran, inotersen) that can stabilize neurological function and improve survival [8], [9]. Early diagnosis, as in this case, is critical to access these disease-modifying therapies.

This represents, to our knowledge, the first genetically confirmed report of ATTRv mimicking ALS in Saudi Arabia. The NM_000371.4:c.424G>A variant is the most frequent TTR variant in the Saudi population (allele frequency ∼0.001) [15]. The high rate of consanguinity in the region may increase the risk of autosomal dominant disorders with variable penetrance, potentially leading to underestimation of familial clustering [11]. Moreover, the neurological-predominant phenotype observed here—without overt autonomic or cardiac symptoms—may further contribute to misdiagnosis. Expanding genetic testing infrastructure, establishing multidisciplinary clinics, and fostering collaborative efforts across Gulf Cooperation Council (GCC) countries are essential to enhance regional capacity for diagnosis and access to disease-modifying therapies. Given the availability of targeted treatments, increasing awareness of ATTRv as an ALS mimic is not only clinically relevant but a public health imperative.

Conclusion

This case underscores the critical need to evaluate ALS mimics thoroughly. Clinicians should be vigilant for systemic signs, subtle sensory symptoms, cardiac abnormalities, and atypical progression patterns—all of which may point to a diagnosis other than ALS. As the first genetically confirmed case of ATTRv mimicking ALS in Saudi Arabia, this report highlights the importance of considering hereditary amyloidosis in the differential diagnosis of motor neuron syndromes, especially in populations with high consanguinity and emerging genetic data. Early diagnosis enables access to disease-modifying therapies and transforms prognosis. ATTRv remains an essential and increasingly treatable consideration in neuromuscular medicine.
