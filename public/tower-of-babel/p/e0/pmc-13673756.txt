Beyond Gilbert syndrome: exploring the heterozygous UGT1A1 c.41_40dup variant as a potential modifier of bile composition associated with a cholangiopathic phenotype in adults with unexplained chronic cholestasis
Authors: Alessandro Federico, Mario Romeo, Fiammetta Di Nardo, Carmine Napolitano, Alessia De Gregorio, Claudio Basile, Giusy Senese, Marcello Dallio
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundUnexplained chronic cholestasis (UCC) in adults is traditionally interpreted as a hepatocellular or canalicular disorder; however, a subset of patients may instead exhibit a cholangiopathy-driven phenotype. Uridine diphosphate glucuronosyltransferase 1A1 (UGT1A1) plays a key role in bilirubin conjugation and bile composition. The heterozygous UGT1A1 c.41_40dup variant, classically associated with Gilbert Syndrome (GS), is frequently observed in individuals without overt GS. In this setting, reduced UGT1A1 activity has been associated with the production of a bile characterized by an increased proportion of bilirubin monoglucuronide (BMG) and a reduced proportion of bilirubin diglucuronide (BDG). The present study investigates whether this variant contributes to a bile composition–driven cholangiopathic phenotype characterized by chronic biliary epithelial injury.MethodsSeventeen UDCA-naïve, GS-free adults with UCC were enrolled and stratified according to genetic profile: Group A (UGT1A1 c.41_40dup), Group B (UGT1A1 c.41_40dup plus additional variants), and Group C (non-carriers). Biochemical, histological, and clinical features were assessed. UGT1A1 activity and bilirubin conjugate composition (BMG/BDG ratio) were evaluated using high-performance liquid chromatography. At 6 months, biochemical evolution was assessed using ALP levels (> 1.9 × ULN) as a pragmatic benchmark.ResultsPatients carrying the UGT1A1 c.41_40dup variant (Group A and B) exhibited significantly reduced enzymatic activity and an increased intrahepatic BMG/BDG ratio compared with non-carriers (both p < 0.0001), along with a higher prevalence of gallstone-related manifestations. Despite normal serum bilirubin, they showed a biochemical profile characterized by persistent ALP and GGT elevation, along with a descriptive pattern of more frequent advanced fibrosis among variant carriers. The overall clinical and biochemical pattern was consistent with a cholangiopathy-associated, ascending cholestasis phenotype. At 6 months, ALP levels > 1.9 × ULN were observed in 70% of variant carriers and 28% of non-carriers; however, this difference did not reach statistical significance after re-analysis using Fisher’s exact test and should be interpreted as exploratory.ConclusionsThe heterozygous UGT1A1 c.41_40dup variant may contribute to a bile composition–driven cholangiopathic phenotype, promoting secondary biliary injury, consistent with a hypothesis-generating model. These findings support its role as a non-canonical modifier of cholestatic disease and underscore the importance of bile composition in shaping disease expression.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12876-026-05132-6.

Introduction

Unexplained chronic cholestasis (UCC) in adults represents a heterogeneous and clinically challenging condition, characterized by persistent or recurrent impairment of bile flow in the absence of an identifiable cause despite comprehensive diagnostic evaluation [1–3]. Traditionally, cholestatic disorders have been broadly categorized into “secondary” forms—related to biliary obstruction, neoplastic infiltration, or immune-mediated diseases such as Primary Biliary Cholangitis (PBC) and Primary Sclerosing Cholangitis (PSC)—and “primary” forms, typically attributed to intrinsic defects of hepatocellular bile secretion, frequently involving canalicular transporters [1–3]. However, this paradigm may be overly simplistic. Emerging evidence suggests that a subset of adult cholestatic phenotypes may instead reflect cholangiopathy-driven processes, in which alterations in bile composition, lithogenicity, and ductal epithelial vulnerability play a central role in initiating and sustaining biliary injury [3, 4]. In this context, the qualitative properties of bile—rather than bile flow alone—are increasingly recognized as key determinants of disease expression. While research has historically focused on canalicular transporters such as bile salt export pump (BSEP) and multidrug resistance protein 3 (MDR3) [5], it is now evident that proteins not directly localized at the canalicular membrane may still significantly influence bile composition and physicochemical stability [4, 6].

Uridine diphosphate glucuronosyltransferase 1A1 (UGT1A1) plays a central role in bilirubin metabolism by catalysing the conjugation of bilirubin into more water-soluble forms. While UGT1A1 has been extensively studied in the context of Gilbert syndrome (GS) [7]—a benign condition characterized by mild unconjugated hyperbilirubinemia due to reduced enzymatic activity—its broader implications in cholestatic disorders remain insufficiently explored.

The c.41_40dup variant, typically associated with GS in the homozygous state, is frequently observed in heterozygosity in individuals without overt hyperbilirubinemia [8]. Notably, even in this setting, reduced UGT1A1 activity has been associated with a relative increase in bilirubin monoglucuronide (BMG) and a decrease in bilirubin diglucuronide (BDG), resulting in a more lithogenic and unstable bile milieu [9, 10].

These alterations may promote biliary injury by enhancing lithogenesis and epithelial stress, supporting a bile composition–driven cholangiopathic model rather than a primary hepatocellular defect [9, 10]. This concept is also consistent with both classical experimental evidence and more recent imaging–genetic studies demonstrating that defects in bile composition may primarily drive biliary injury and cholangiopathy [11]. Within this framework, UGT1A1 variants may act as modifiers of bile composition, contributing to the development of cholestatic phenotypes.

Based on this rationale, the present study aimed to assess the prevalence and phenotypic impact of the heterozygous UGT1A1 c.41_40dup variant in adults with UCC, and to explore its potential role—alone or in combination with other genetic alterations—in shaping a cholangiopathy-associated phenotype. In addition, we sought to explore whether alterations in bile composition associated with this variant could be associated with a distinct early biochemical trajectory during ursodeoxycholic acid (UDCA) treatment, the standard therapeutic approach for cholestatic disorders [3].

Materials and methods

Experimental design

Between November 2023 and January 2025, after signing informed consent, seventeen UDCA-naïve GS-free adults (aged 18–80 years) inpatients with UCC were consecutively enrolled.

At baseline, for each patient, biochemical variables [gamma-glutamyl transferase (GGT), alkaline phosphatase (ALP), total bilirubin, alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), and biliary acids (BAs)] and anamnestic clinical data [including drug administration, smoking habits, neonatal jaundice, intrahepatic cholestasis of pregnancy (ICP), familial history of cryptogenic cirrhosis, and previous episodes of biliary colic (i.e., typical biliary pain due to symptomatic cholelithiasis) were collected. Moreover, pruritus severity was assessed through the Itch Numerical Rating Scale (Itch-NRS 0 = no pruritus; < 3 = mild; ≥ 3 < 7 = moderate; Itch-NRS ≥ 7 = severe) [12].

All patients underwent an abdominal ultrasound (US), cholangial magnetic resonance imaging (c-MRI), and US-guided percutaneous liver biopsy (pLB). C-MRI was systematically evaluated to exclude major biliary abnormalities; no PSC-like features were identified in the study population. For each patient, three histological samples were collected to estimate hepatic fibrosis severity and perform the following basic science analyses (i.e., BMG/BDG separation and UGT1A1 activity assessment). Moreover, venous peripheral blood samples (3 ml whole blood WB-EDTA) were collected. A Next-Generation Sequencing (NGS) analysis-based dedicated panel tested for genes “hot for cholestasis” [13]. Furthermore, individuals showing imaging evidence of gallbladder bile stones with an anamnestic history of biliary colic were recommended to receive a cholecystectomy, during which a 3 ml bile sample was collected. High-performance liquid chromatographic (HPLC) separation of bilirubin conjugates was performed on bile and liver samples to discriminate the BMG and BDG proportion [14–16], and the activity of hepatic UGT1A1 was properly assayed [9].

Based on the relative genetic profiles and current evidence on the clinical relevance and significance of NGS-identified polymorphism (Additional Table 1), patients were stratified as follows: individuals presenting exclusively the UGT1A1 c.41_40dup heterozygous variant (Group A); individuals presenting the UGT1A1 c.41_40dup heterozygous variant simultaneously with other variants of uncertain significance (VUS) in genes encoding canalicular transporters contributing to bile flow and metabolism (Group B); individuals presenting exclusively VUS in genes encoding for canalicular transporters contributing to biliary metabolism and composition (Group C).

After hospital discharge, UDCA administration (dosage 15 mg/kg/day) was started, and patients were followed up every two months for 6 months. After this period, UDCA response was assessed using an ALP-based threshold (> 1.9 × upper limit of normal—ULN), adapted from criteria proposed in PBC [17]. Given the different disease context, this threshold was used as a pragmatic biochemical benchmark rather than a validated definition of treatment failure. The primary objective was to assess the frequency of the heterozygous UGT1A1 c.41_40dup variant in adults with UCC and to evaluate its impact—alone (Group A) or in combination with other variants (Group B)—on clinical and histological phenotype. Accordingly, the distinction between Groups A and B was introduced as an exploratory stratification to investigate whether the coexistence of additional VUS modified the phenotype among UGT1A1 c.41_40dup carriers. A secondary objective was to explore its association with the early biochemical trajectory during UDCA treatment, while an ancillary aim was to investigate the underlying pathogenetic mechanisms.

Patients

This research complies with the ethical guidelines of the Declaration of Helsinki (1975). The study received approval by the ethical committee of the University of Campania Luigi Vanvitelli in Naples (Ethical Committee approval—study protocol n.0034563/i-2023).

Patients were enrolled at the Hepato-Gastroenterology Division of the University of Campania Luigi Vanvitelli between November 2023 and January 2025. Inclusion criteria were age ≥ 18 years and clinical evidence of UCC. According to the clinical practice guidelines [2], UCC was configured by the increase of biochemical cholestatic indexes [raised GGT and/or alkaline phosphatase ALP levels for ≥ 6 months] in the absence of other causes [i.e., no evidence of drug-induced liver injury (DILI) and major hepatotropic-agent infections; serological negativity of autoimmune-liver blot; cholangial magnetic resonance imaging (c-MRI) evidencing no pathological features, except for lithiasis]. Exclusion criteria were: (a) GS and/or chronic liver disease (CLD) of other etiology [hepatitis B and/or C virus, alcohol-related liver disorder (ALD) with evidence of alcohol abuse history assessed by using the Alcohol Use Disorders Identification Test questionnaire and autoimmune-based chronic hepatic disorders, including cholestasis-CLD]; (b) evidence of cancer, including hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC); (c) pregnancy; (d) psychiatric problems interfering with the informed consent. Following discharge, all patients received UDCA therapy at a dose of 15 mg/kg/day with follow-up evaluations every two months for a total duration of six months [17].

Ultrasound-guided percutaneous liver biopsy and histological evaluation

All liver biopsies were performed under real-time ultrasound guidance using a convex transducer (3.5–5 MHz). Biopsy specimens were immediately fixed in 10% neutral-buffered formalin, processed according to standard histopathological procedures, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned at 3–4 μm for subsequent staining. Routine histological evaluation was performed using hematoxylin and eosin (H&E), while additional stains—such as Masson’s trichrome for collagen fibres and reticulin for architectural integrity—were applied to assess fibrosis severity and the grading of necroinflammatory activity [Focal lytic necrosis, apoptosis, inflammation (FAI) (score 0–4)] by using the Ishak scoring system [18]. All slides were reviewed independently by two experienced hepatopathologists.

Next-generation sequencing of cholestasis-associated genes

Genomic DNA was extracted from peripheral blood samples and subjected to targeted NGS using the Prevention Genetics Cholestasis Panel, which includes over 70 genes implicated in hereditary cholestatic liver disorders (https://www.preventiongenetics.com/testInfo?val=Cholestasis-Panel; accessed on 24th November 2025) [13]. The panel covers all coding exons and at least 10 base pairs of flanking intronic regions, with a minimum coverage depth of ≥ 20 × for > 99.5% of targeted regions. Sequencing was performed on a high-throughput Illumina platform, and variant calling was conducted using a validated bioinformatics pipeline aligned to the GRCh37/hg19 reference genome. Detected variants were annotated using databases such as ClinVar, OMIM (Online Mendelian Inheritance in Man), and Human Gene Mutation Database (HGMD), and interpreted according to American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG)/American College of Pathologists (AMP) guidelines. VUS were not considered functionally equivalent and were interpreted as potential modifiers of hepatobiliary physiology based on gene-specific biological plausibility (Additional Table 1). VUS, likely pathogenic, and pathogenic variants were confirmed by Sanger sequencing when necessary. Copy number variants (CNVs) spanning two or more contiguous exons were assessed using read-depth analysis and confirmed by orthogonal methods (e.g., qPCR or MLPA).

Separation and quantification of bilirubin conjugates in bile and liver samples

Bile and liver samples (collected intraoperatively and from biopsy, respectively) were snap-frozen and stored at –80 °C until analysis. Bilirubin monoglucuronide (BMG) and diglucuronide (BDG) were quantified using reverse-phase HPLC with UV detection, following established protocols [15, 16]. In detail, bile specimens were collected intraoperatively (i.e., during cholecystectomy) and stored at –80 °C until analysis.

Because bile samples were available only from patients undergoing clinically indicated cholecystectomy for symptomatic gallstone disease, bile analyses were considered descriptive. No formal between-group statistical comparisons were performed, as bile samples were not systematically available across all study groups.

Before quantification, samples were thawed on ice, centrifuged at 10,000 × g for 10 min to remove particulate matter, and diluted 1:5 in mobile phase. The resulting solution was filtered through 0.22 μm nylon membrane filters. Chromatographic separation of BMG and diglucuronide BDG was performed using a reverse-phase C18 analytical column (250 × 4.6 mm, 5 μm particle size) maintained at 40 °C [15, 16]. The mobile phase consisted of 50 mM ammonium acetate buffer (pH 5.2) and methanol, applied in a linear gradient from 30 to 70% methanol over 15 min at a flow rate of 1.0 mL/min. UV detection was conducted at 450 nm. Calibration curves were generated using serial dilutions of purified BMG and BDG standards ranging from 0.5 μg/mL to 50 μg/mL, with each concentration run in triplicate. Peak areas showed excellent linear correlation (R2 > 0.998) with respective analyte concentrations. The limit of detection (LOD) and limit of quantification (LOQ) were determined according to signal-to-noise ratios of 3:1 and 10:1, respectively. The LOD was 0.2 μg/mL for BMG and 0.3 μg/mL for BDG; LOQ values were 0.6 μg/mL and 0.9 μg/mL, respectively. Retention times for BMG and BDG were approximately 7.5 and 9.2 min, respectively. All experiments were performed in technical duplicates, and intra-assay variability was < 5%.

Assay of hepatic UGT1A1 activity

The activity [bilirubin (mg) conjugated/hour/gram of tissue] of hepatic UGT1A1 was assayed in digitonin-activated liver homogenates, according to the method reported by Duvaldestin et al.[9], subsequently quantifying conjugated bilirubin using HPLC [16]. Briefly, liver tissue was homogenized in a digitonin-containing buffer, and UGT1A1 activity was assessed by measuring the rate of bilirubin conjugation in the presence of UDP-glucuronic acid. After incubation at 37 °C, conjugated bilirubin was extracted and quantified by HPLC with UV detection at 450 nm [16]. Enzymatic activity was expressed as mg of conjugated bilirubin formed per hour per gram of tissue (mg/h/g). The assay was standardized according to the method described by Duvaldestin et al. [9], and activity values < 0.7 mg/h/g indicated an abnormally low enzymatic functioning.

Statistical analysis and sample size

Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation, and categorical variables as counts and percentages. Normality was assessed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. Continuous variables were compared using Student’s t-test or the Mann–Whitney U test for two-group comparisons, and one-way ANOVA or the Kruskal–Wallis test, with appropriate post hoc corrections, for comparisons across three groups. Categorical variables were analysed using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate, with Fisher’s exact test being preferred when expected cell counts were low. Given the limited sample size, analyses involving categorical histological outcomes, including fibrosis stage, were considered exploratory and should therefore be interpreted with caution. As well, comparisons involving the proportion of patients exceeding the ALP threshold at 6 months were analysed using Fisher’s exact test and interpreted as exploratory because of the small sample size.

Given that the outcome (ALP levels > 1.9 × ULN at 6 months) was assessed at a fixed time point (6 months), time-to-event analyses were not applicable. Therefore, associations between clinical variables and outcome were explored using univariate analyses and logistic regression models. Due to the limited sample size (n = 17), multivariable modelling was not performed to avoid overfitting. Logistic regression analyses were therefore considered exploratory, and results are presented as odds ratios (OR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI). Statistical significance was defined as a two-tailed p-value < 0.05 with 95% CI. Analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism version 10.1.1.

Sample size was estimated using a chi-square test for two independent proportions, assuming a 70% difference in the prevalence of the heterozygous UGT1A1 c.41_40dup variant between groups (α = 0.05, power = 0.9), yielding a minimum of 7 subjects per group (STATA14, MacOS).

The sample size calculation was based on the primary comparison between UGT1A1 c.41_40dup carriers and non-carriers, whereas three-group analyses were considered exploratory. The subdivision of carriers into Group A (isolated UGT1A1 c.41_40dup variant) and Group B (UGT1A1 c.41_40dup plus additional VUS) was performed to explore potential genotype–phenotype heterogeneity among variant carriers and was not part of the primary study hypothesis or sample size calculation.

Results

Baseline characteristics of the study population

Of the 17 enrolled UCC patients, 10 (58.8%) carried the heterozygous UGT1A1 c.41_40dup variant.

For exploratory purposes, variant carriers were further stratified according to the presence or absence of additional VUS, whereas non-carriers constituted the comparator group (Group C). Of these, 5 harboured the UGT1A1 c.41_40dup variant alone (Group A), whereas the remaining 5 also carried additional VUS in genes encoding canalicular transporters involved in biliary metabolism and composition (Group B). The remaining 7 patients carried exclusively VUS affecting genes involved in biliary transport, ductal function, or cholangiocyte integrity (Group C). Discussion

Unexplained cholestasis remains a diagnostic and therapeutic challenge, often interpreted within a hepatocellular or canalicular framework based on classical models of bile secretion impairment [1, 2, 5]. However, the present findings suggest a different pathophysiological perspective. The biochemical and clinical profile of our cohort—characterized by persistent ALP and GGT elevation, normal serum bilirubin levels, frequent gallstone-related manifestations, and descriptive histological evidence of more advanced fibrosis in a subset of variant carriers—appears more consistent with a cholangiopathy-associated phenotype rather than primary hepatocellular cholestasis [3].

UGT1A1 plays a pivotal role in bilirubin conjugation, and reduced enzymatic activity—such as that observed in carriers of the heterozygous c.41_40dup variant—has been shown to alter the balance between BDG and BMG, favouring the accumulation of the latter [9, 19, 20]. Due to its lower solubility, BMG contributes to bile instability and lithogenesis [9, 19, 20]. Within this biological framework, the present findings are consistent with the hypothesis that the UGT1A1 c.41_40dup variant may function as a modifier of bile composition, potentially contributing to a more lithogenic bile milieu. Consistently, all variant carriers exhibited reduced UGT1A1 activity together with a marked shift toward BMG predominance in liver tissue. Moreover, among the three patients undergoing clinically indicated cholecystectomy, all available bile samples demonstrated the same BMG-predominant profile, providing further biological plausibility for the proposed bile composition–driven mechanism, although these observations should be interpreted descriptively given the limited availability of bile samples. Reduced UGT1A1 activity observed in variant carriers should be interpreted with caution, as similar reductions may also be present in the general population. Therefore, this finding alone cannot be considered specific for the cholestatic phenotype, but may represent a predisposing factor that, in combination with other genetic and environmental determinants, contributes to disease expression. Notably, in our cohort, these features occurred in the absence of overt hyperbilirubinemia, supporting the concept that the pathogenic signature of this variant lies not in bilirubin accumulation per se, but in qualitative alterations of bile composition. Although bile analyses were limited to the subset of patients undergoing clinically indicated cholecystectomy, the concordance between bile and liver bilirubin conjugate profiles supports the biological plausibility of this proposed mechanism. Importantly, the overall biochemical pattern—dominated by elevated ALP and GGT—further supports a biliary rather than hepatocellular origin of the cholestatic process.

The concept that alterations in bilirubin conjugation may promote a lithogenic bile milieu and drive biliary injury is not novel. As early as 1980, Duvaldestin et al. demonstrated that impaired bilirubin glucuronidation results in increased biliary lithogenicity [9], thereby contributing to gallstone formation and suggesting a primary role of bile composition in initiating biliary damage rather than hepatocellular dysfunction. More recently, this paradigm has been further reinforced by imaging–genetic studies in low phospholipid-associated cholelithiasis (LPAC) syndrome. In particular, Biyoukar et al. showed that variants in ABCB4 are associated with hepatobiliary MRI abnormalities reflecting biliary epithelial injury and explicitly framed these conditions as cholangiopathies driven by bile composition defects rather than primary hepatocellular cholestasis [11]. In this context, our findings appear to fit within the same pathobiological framework, with the UGT1A1 c.41_40dup variant acting as a modifier of bile composition, analogous—although mechanistically distinct—to ABCB4-related disease in LPAC, ultimately promoting a bile composition–driven cholangiopathic phenotype. In this scenario, the UGT1A1 c.41_40dup variant may contribute to a bile composition–driven cholangiopathy, where increased BMG proportion promotes lithogenesis and biliary epithelial stress. In this picture, injury is likely initiated at the level of the bile ducts and may propagate “upstream”, thus leading to secondary hepatocellular inflammation and fibrogenesis, ultimately configuring an ascending cholestasis phenotype. Therefore, the observed hepatic damage—including elevated transaminases and the descriptive occurrence of advanced fibrosis in some patients—may represent a downstream consequence of chronic biliary injury [7, 9, 10, 21] rather than a primary defect in hepatocellular bile secretion [1, 2, 5].

From a histopathological perspective, our analysis was based on standardized scoring systems, which primarily capture fibrosis and necroinflammatory activity. However, within a cholangiopathic framework, additional features such as ductular reaction, portal/peribiliary fibrosis, and biliary-type interface activity may provide a more comprehensive characterization of biliary injury. Although these parameters were not systematically evaluated in the present study, the overall histopathological findings were compatible with a biliary-type pattern of injury.

Importantly, while advanced fibrosis was numerically more frequent among UGT1A1 c.41_40dup carriers, this observation did not reach statistical significance after re-analysis using Fisher’s exact test and should therefore be regarded as hypothesis-generating.

From an imaging standpoint, MRCP did not reveal PSC-like abnormalities in our cohort. However, it is important to note that PSC-like imaging patterns are not disease-specific and may also be observed in secondary and genetic cholangiopathies, as highlighted by Venkatesh et al. [22]. Therefore, the absence of characteristic MRCP features does not exclude a cholangiopathic process, particularly in early or genetically driven disease.

Importantly, the genetic background of our cohort further supports a cholangiopathic framework. From a genotype–phenotype perspective, patient-level analysis suggests potential additive effects when the UGT1A1 variant coexists with polymorphisms affecting canalicular transporters. The variants identified across Groups B and C were not assumed to be functionally equivalent; rather, they affect distinct biological pathways—including bile acid transport (ABCB11), canalicular membrane stability (ATP8B1), epithelial secretion (CFTR), and ciliary signaling (PKD1L1)—that may converge in increasing susceptibility to biliary injury [3]. In this context, variants in genes such as ABCB11, ATP8B1, or SLCO1B3 may further impair bile flow (composition or tolerance), thereby amplifying biliary stress in the presence of an already lithogenic bile environment [3]. Accordingly, Groups B and C should not be interpreted as genetically equivalent, but rather as sharing a background of cholangiopathy-predisposing factors.

Consistent with this interpretation, patients in Group C—initially considered as a comparator—harbored variants in genes known to predispose to biliary disease, including CFTR, PKD1L1-related ciliopathy genes, and heterozygous variants in ABCB11 and ATP8B1. These genes have been implicated in adult-onset cholangiopathies and are increasingly recognized as disease modifiers rather than fully penetrant monogenic causes [3]. In this context, our cohort may be more appropriately interpreted as a cholangiopathy-prone population, in which multiple genetic factors affecting bile transport, ductal integrity, or ciliary function coexist. Within this framework, the UGT1A1 c.41_40dup variant may act as an additional “hit” by altering bile composition and increasing lithogenicity, thereby amplifying biliary epithelial stress and injury.

A secondary objective of this study was to explore the relationship between the UGT1A1 c.41_40dup variant and early response to UDCA [17]. In our cohort, 70% of variant carriers and 28.6% of non-carriers had ALP levels above 1.9 × ULN after 6 months of UDCA therapy. However, this difference did not reach statistical significance after Fisher’s exact test re-analysis and should therefore be interpreted as a descriptive biochemical trajectory rather than evidence of poor UDCA response. While UDCA exerts well-known choleretic and cytoprotective effects, its efficacy may be limited in conditions primarily driven by bile composition abnormalities [6]. In particular, a bile milieu enriched in poorly soluble BMG may counteract UDCA’s capacity to improve bile fluidity and reduce lithogenicity. Therefore, given the use of a pragmatic biochemical benchmark and the absence of validated response criteria in this setting, this observation should be interpreted cautiously and better viewed as reflecting differential biochemical trajectories rather than definitive evidence of treatment failure. Consistently, both the presence of the variant and reduced UGT1A1 activity were associated with biochemical outcome in exploratory logistic analyses, although estimates were imprecise and should be interpreted with caution. Finally, in line with the other findings, the more attenuated ALP and GGT reductions observed in Group B compared with Group A may support the potential additive or amplifying effect when the UGT1A1 variant coexists with transporter gene alterations. Comprehensively, this evidence may support a multi-hit model of cholangiopathy, in which bile composition and genetic susceptibility converge to shape disease expression.

In this context, the UGT1A1 c.41_40dup variant should not be considered a primary determinant of disease, but rather a modifier of bile composition that may contribute to phenotype expression in susceptible individuals.

Our study has several limitations. First, the relatively small sample size and monocentric design limit the generalizability of the findings, although they reflect the rarity and stringent selection of this clinical condition. From a statistical perspective, the limited sample size represents an important constraint. In particular, the numerically higher proportion of F3 fibrosis among UGT1A1 c.41_40dup carriers did not reach statistical significance using Fisher’s exact test and should therefore be considered exploratory rather than evidence of a definite association. Similarly, the higher proportion of variant carriers remaining above the ALP threshold at 6 months did not reach statistical significance using Fisher’s exact test; therefore, this observation should be considered exploratory and not interpreted as evidence of impaired UDCA response. Furthermore, although carriers were stratified into two subgroups according to the presence of additional VUS to explore potential genotype–phenotype heterogeneity, the study was not powered to compare the three groups individually. Accordingly, all subgroup analyses should be regarded as exploratory and hypothesis-generating. In line with this, logistic regression analyses were performed in an exploratory framework and should be interpreted with caution due to the risk of overfitting and imprecision of effect estimates. Accordingly, the strength of our findings lies primarily in the consistent biochemical and functional gradients observed across groups, rather than in adjusted statistical associations. As well, the relatively high frequency of the UGT1A1 c.41_40dup heterozygous variant observed in our cohort should be interpreted cautiously. Given that similar variants are present in approximately 30–40% of the general Caucasian population, and in the absence of a matched control group, we cannot exclude the possibility that this finding reflects random variation rather than true enrichment. Second, dietary and environmental factors influencing bile composition were not systematically controlled.

In addition, variability in transaminase levels across groups should be interpreted with caution, given the small sample size and the multiple factors influencing serum enzyme levels. Accordingly, these parameters were not considered central to the interpretation of the cholangiopathic phenotype.

Finally, we focused on bilirubin conjugates without assessing other key determinants of bile physicochemical properties, such as phosphatidylcholine and bile salt. Future studies integrating these parameters could help further elucidate the mechanisms underlying this phenotype.

Despite these limitations, this study provides novel insights into the role of UGT1A1 beyond GS, highlighting its contribution as a non-canonical modifier of cholangiopathy. This conceptual shift—from a hepatocellular to a bile composition–driven model—may refine the interpretation of UCC and inform more targeted therapeutic strategies. While UGT1A1 testing is not supported as a standalone diagnostic tool, its assessment within a precision medicine framework may aid phenotypic stratification and help identify patients in whom bile composition–related mechanisms are particularly relevant.

Conclusions

The heterozygous UGT1A1 c.41_40dup variant may contribute to a bile composition–driven cholangiopathic phenotype in adults with UCC in a hypothesis-generating framework, through altered bilirubin conjugation, increased lithogenicity, and possible secondary biliary injury.

Rather than acting as a primary determinant of hepatocellular cholestasis, it appears to function as a non-canonical modifier of bile physicochemical properties, shaping disease expression. These findings, although hypothesis-generating, highlight the role of bile composition in cholestatic disorders and warrant further investigation in larger, prospective studies.
