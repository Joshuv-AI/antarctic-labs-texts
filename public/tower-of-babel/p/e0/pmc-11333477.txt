Sexuality attitudes and beliefs survey (SABS): validation of the instrument for the polish nursing and midwifery students
Authors: Ludmila Marcinowicz, Barbara Ślusarska, Marta Zahor
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe aim of the study is to create a Polish version of the sexuality attitudes and beliefs survey (SABS), to assess its cultural adaptation and psychometric properties and to utilize it to enable a cross-sectional study of the sexual attitudes and beliefs among Poland’s nursing and midwifery students. Non-random network sampling and the adapted SABS v. PL scale were employed for the latter purpose. A total of 570 nursing and midwifery students from two Polish universities completed the questionnaire. Cronbach's alpha coefficient was utilized to measure the internal consistency of the SABS scale, while exploratory factor analysis (EFA) was employed to determine its construct validity. The factors that most affect respondents' sexual attitudes and beliefs were identified through a stepwise multiple regression method and through an econometric model. An exploratory factor analysis of the SABS v. PL scale revealed an adequate fit and confirmed the 4-factor model. The Cronbach's alpha coefficient for SABS v. PL amounted to: α = 0.66. The study's student population had a mean SABS score of 41.65 ± 5.63, while the mean SABS item score varied between 2.55 ± 1.27 and 4.35 ± 1.07. The study's results show that the SABS v. PL questionnaire is a valid and reliable instrument. The work revealed that Polish nursing and midwifery students have attitudes and beliefs which negatively impact their ability to evaluate a person's sexual health concerns and provide relevant health counselling.Keywords: Sexual attitudes and beliefs, Nursing students, Midwifery students, SABS scale, Psychometric evaluationSubject terms: Health care, Medical research

Introduction

In clinical practice, modern nursing encourages and supports a comprehensive person-centred approach that considers the patient's biological, social and spiritual needs. Providing the patient with holistic care is crucial in order to address their multidimensional needs and take into account their cultural backgrounds1. It is of key importance to educate nurses so that they can provide comprehensive care, which includes sexuality-related issues. Enhancing an individual's sexual health can lead to a better quality of life, as sexuality is a multifaceted and individual aspect of overall health.

Earlier studies reveal that nurses frequently overlook or fail to address the sexual health of their patients and feel helpless to incorporate sexual health into a comprehensive approach to patient care2. One of the contributing factors is that nurses are unprepared to handle patients' sexual health issues3. The systematic review's findings also indicate that one of the factors preventing nurses from providing patients with sexual health education is their lack of knowledge in this field of study4. Nurses must, therefore, be educated to deal with their patients' sexuality5.

Since nurses frequently have the first and most frequent interaction with patients, they must be properly qualified to provide care that takes into account all aspects of the patient's health, including sexual health. They frequently have intimate knowledge of the patient's health issues, functional status, and treatment applied6. In addition, nurses play an important role in promoting sexual health. Taking this into consideration, nursing students should be educated in sexual matters and develop positive attitudes toward sexuality7. In order to enhance nurses' ability to provide patients with holistic care, more research is required to better understand their behaviour. As a result, it is necessary to assess nursing students’ social and professional readiness and attitudes toward engaging in educational activities in their future profession in order to ensure the effectiveness of education and sexual health support for future patients8.

The researchers of this paper have noted that no instrument has been created to assess sexual attitudes and beliefs in professional education and clinical nursing in Poland. Moreover, no studies have been conducted in Poland to validate the internationally available Self-Assessment Behaviour Scale (SABS) for Polish-speaking nursing and midwifery students. The aim of this study is, hence to adjust the Polish version of the Sexual Attitudes and Beliefs Scale (SABS) linguistically and culturally, evaluate the psychometric properties of the scale, and examine the initial findings of sexual attitudes and beliefs among nursing and midwifery students.

Materials and methods

Study design and participants

This cross-sectional study was carried out in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology guidelines (STROBE)9. Nursing and midwifery students from two Polish universities, namely, Medical University of Białystok and Medical University of Lublin, participated in this non-random study, with convenience sampling applied. A total of 1540 students, that is 680 from the former and 860 from the latter who were studying and could be reached in 2023, were invited to take part in the study. The survey involved 570 students (37.1%), of these, 409 (72%) were nursing students and 161 (28%) were midwifery students. The inclusion criteria were as follows: nursing and midwifery students, native speakers of Polish, respondents’ informed consent. The exclusion criteria included: no consent to participate in the study and participation in a national or student exchange program. The minimum sample size for validation studies is consistent with recommendations for sample coverage of at least two subjects per item, with an absolute minimum of 100 to 250 subjects10.

Data collection and sample group

The data was collected using the PAPI method (Paper and Pencil Interviewing) between April and June 2023. Information about the study was provided to participants both orally and in writing, and participation was completely voluntary and anonymous. Respondents could ask the interviewer questions when completing the questionnaires. After the questionnaires were completed, respondents dropped the forms into ballot boxes, which were then opened. A total of 586 questionnaires were collected, and 570 of them were fully completed and accepted.

Measurements

The study participants were asked to fill out a form consisting of three sections:

Demographic characteristics (gender, age, year of study),

Demographic characteristics (gender, age, year of study),

Sexual orientation and religious denomination, and

Sexual orientation and religious denomination, and

Attitudes and sexual beliefs (The Sexual Attitude and Beliefs Scale, SABS).

Attitudes and sexual beliefs (The Sexual Attitude and Beliefs Scale, SABS).

Evaluation of sexual attitudes and beliefs according to SABS

The research tool was the Polish version of the Sexual Attitudes and Beliefs Scale (SABS). The Sexual Attitudes and Beliefs Scale (SABS) was first developed in English in collaboration between researchers and clinicians. A sample of nurses was used to test the psychometric properties of the scale, and reliability was confirmed with a Cronbach's alpha coefficient of 0.8511. Further studies confirmed the validity of SABS in Turkish12, Portuguese13, Spanish14 and Italian15.

The SABS scale consists of 12 statements rated on a Likert scale ranging from 1 to 6, where 1 = strongly disagree and 6 = strongly agree. In order to prevent response bias, seven items were reverse rated11. The total possible score ranges between 12 and 72, where higher scores indicate more negative sexual attitudes and beliefs, as well as a lower likelihood of nurses and nursing students engaging with patients' sexual health care and counselling. In addition, a higher score indicates the existence of more barriers to patient sexuality assessment in nursing practice.

Translation and cultural adaptation of the SABS v. PL scale

Translation and cultural adaptation of the scale was prepared in accordance with the World Health Organization (WHO) protocol for scale translation16 in the following manner:

Forward translation

Two independent speakers of Polish and English who were aware of the questionnaire's contents and the study's objectives were involved in the translation stage. The SABS scale was translated by academics in health sciences. In order to maintain conceptual and idiomatic equivalence, a semantic translation was used.

Expert panel

Discrepancies between the two versions were found by a bilingual panel of experts. The panel's goal was to identify and discuss discrepancies in terms, sentences, or meanings to obtain a consistent translation. In order to reach an agreement on the identified differences between the SABS translation versions, a reviewer was requested to collaborate with the translators involved to agree on the conceptual equivalence of the two translations into a single version (Synthesis 1). When addressing the discrepancies found, it was presumed that if only part of the meaning is present in the “target culture” or if the term in the “target culture” expands the meaning of the term in the “source culture”, the meaning of the original term can be modified in the translation process17.

Back-translation

The result of this work was then back-translated by two English language specialists working in the health sciences field who were unfamiliar with the original scale. The authors of this paper subsequently examined and evaluated the three versions: the translation, the back translation, and the original scale. Herein, the semantic, idiomatic and conceptual equivalence of the items was analysed. During the debriefing, the reporting person took note of whether or not each item was reported as difficult to understand in terms of the concept, question phrasing or response options. In addition, the selected response, as well as any suggestions for improvement made by the translators and reviewer (such as more appropriate vocabulary), were recorded. Following the completion of the debriefing, the data collected was reviewed, any concerns raised were addressed, and potential solutions were proposed. After having reached a consensus, the agreed-upon changes were implemented and utilized in the subsequent debriefing. Following some minor adjustments, the released version was agreed upon. After a sexuality specialist reviewed the final draft and made some minor changes to address use of informal language, the final draft was accepted (Synthesis 2).

Pretesting

In order to identify any inappropriate words or expressions, the developed version of the scale was tested on the target participants, i.e., 15 students. No objections were raised by the study participants at this stage.

Ethical consideration

The study was conducted in accordance with relevant guidelines and legislation. The study was approved by the Bioethics Committee of the Medical University of Bialystok (Approval Number: APK.002.181.2023) and was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Before completing the survey, participants received the following information: "The survey is anonymous and participation in the study is voluntary. The information collected will only be used for collective scientific research. It is possible to resign from participation at any stage. Completing the survey is tantamount to consenting to participate in the study.” The decision to obtain informed verbal consent to participate in the study was made to protect the anonymity and privacy of respondents by avoiding additional documents requiring their signature.

Statistical analysis

The statistical analyses were performed using Statistica 13.3. Descriptive statistics are presented in numbers (n) and percentages (%) for nominal variables and as mean ± standard deviation (M ± SD) for variables obtained by measurement. The reliability assessment was performed by measuring internal consistency reliability through Cronbach’s alpha. The Kolmogorov–Smirnov test was applied to determine the adequacy of the scale to a normal distribution. Construct validity was assessed via explorative factor analysis (EFA), with principal component as the extraction method. For factor rotations, Varimax with Kaiser normalization was employed. Items with high factor loadings defined each dimension. To be a clinically meaningful item in one of these factors, each item had to have a loading over 0.5018. Each item was referred to the factor in which it had the highest loading.

A scree plot was used to determine the optimal number of factors. Both the Kaiser–Meyer–Olkin measure of sampling adequacy and Bartlett test of sphericity were utilized so as to ensure the usefulness of factor analysis for the collected data. A value close to 1.0 on the Kaiser–Meyer–Olkin measure of sampling adequacy indicated the proportion of variance of variables that might be caused by underlying factors, and a value below 0.05 on the Bartlett test of sphericity was used as a level to indicate that factor analysis was suitable. The correlation between the factors studied and the dependent variable was analysed via Spearman's rank correlation coefficient, and the impact of the factors studied on sexual attitudes and beliefs was investigated by way of forward stepwise regression. The limit of statistical significance was set at α = 0.05.

Discussion

The aim of this cross-sectional study, which involved non-randomised network sampling and included Polish nursing and midwifery students, was to adapt the SABS v. PL scale linguistically and culturally for use in Poland, as well as to conduct a preliminary analysis of sexual attitudes and beliefs among Poland’s students in patient sexual health care. The main findings of the study are described below.

Psychometric evaluation of the questionnaire

The process of language adaptation and tool validation resulted in a Polish questionnaire measuring nursing and midwifery students' attitudes and beliefs about sexual health. The results of this study, which assessed the reliability and validity of the SABS v. PL show that this instrument has appropriate psychometric properties and is useful in measuring students' attitudes towards sexual health care.

The SABS Cronbach alpha reliability coefficient in our study was α = 0.66, which is lower than the Cronbach's alpha coefficient in the original studies of α = 0.75 and α = 0.82 in two separate evaluations11, in the Portuguese study α = 0.7213, in the Italian study α = 0.7615, and in the Indonesian study α = 0.7019. In the Turkish studies, the Cronbach’s alpha reliability coefficient of the survey and re-test text correlation value was found as 0.73 and 0.9012. Studies conducted in Spain found comparable values of the Cronbach's alpha coefficient (α = 0.65)14. While a Cronbach's alpha value of more than 0.60 can ensure internal consistency of measurements20,21, a value of more than 0.70 is recommended22. However, it should be noted that this coefficient is dependent on the sample size and limitations in the number of items within the scale.

Additionally, in our study, we measured construct validity using exploratory factor analysis (EFA), where the factors explain 62% of the variances in the factor analysis, which is a reasonable result. Four sub-scales were created using the extracted factors: Subscale 1—“Readiness to address sexual health”; Subscale 2—“Assurance”; Subscale 3—“Understanding”; Subscale 4—“Beliefs about patients' expectations”, which allowed us to more clearly identify obstacles to students' attitudes and beliefs about addressing future patients' sexual health. The results of the construct validity of the SABS were also tested using Exploratory Factor Analysis (EFA) in the Italian study15, where the authors did not extract SABS factors after scoring Kaiser–Meyer–Olkin (KMO) measures of sampling adequacy considered acceptable for EFA.

Based upon an analysis of scores from each of the 12 items, the original design of the SABS scale was developed to evaluate attitudes and beliefs that might hinder the assessment of human sexuality and counselling in nursing practice11. We developed an econometric model to analyse the SABS v. PL scale construct obtained during the adaptation process, and we were able to determine which individual subscales have the greatest impact on sexual attitudes and beliefs. The derived results are interesting from a cognitive standpoint, since the outcome is satisfactory with an upward effect for just one subscale. Here, it should be noted that if subscale 1—“Readiness to address sexual health” increases by one point, then the total SABS score rises by 1.136 points. The remaining subscales have a downward effect for the total SABS score. A review of other research using the SABS scale in Portuguese13, Italian15, Turkish12 or Spanish14 did not show similar references and observations.

Nursing and midwifery students’ attitudes and beliefs towards addressing sexual health according to SABS v.PL

We examined the students’ attitudes and beliefs towards addressing sexual health of their future patients as part of the analysis of the collected results. The mean total SABS score for Polish students was M = 41.65 (SD = 5.63), which is comparable to the results of an Ethiopian study conducted on nursing students (M = 42.3 ± 2.1)23, ranking these results slightly higher than previous research in terms of identified barriers. In practical terms, this means that a high SABS score indicates negative attitudes and beliefs regarding the patients’ sexuality. An increase in negative attitudes and beliefs has a negative impact on nurses' ability to assess a patient's sexual-related issues and to offer appropriate counselling11. The mean SABS score in other research was lower. In Spanish studies, it was M = 32.98 (SD = 6.42)14, in Portuguese studies, M = 28.49 (SD = 5.24)13, while in Turkish studies, M = 34.13 (SD = 7.34)12, M = 38.8 (SD = 8.3)24, indicating that students have less intense negative attitudes and beliefs. In addition, our study result was higher than the total SABS score of Swedish nurses (M = 40.7, SD = 7.8)5, but lower than Chinese nurses (M = 45.83, SD = 8.14)25.

After a thorough analysis of the questionnaire items, our study showed that statements in items 3, 6, and 12 were the most significant barriers to nursing students providing sexual health care, while item 11 was the least significant barrier. The statements with the highest values were as follows: item 3 “I feel uncomfortable talking about sexual issues” (M = 4.35; SD = 1.07), item 12 “Patients expect nurses to ask about their sexual concerns” (M = 4.34; SD = 1.05) and item 6 “I dedicate time to discuss sexual concerns with my patients” (M = 4.01; SD = 1.15). The statement with the lowest value was item 11 “Sexuality should be discussed only if initiated by the patient” (M = 2.55; SD = 1.27). In the Spanish study, item 6 (M = 3.68; SD = 1.14) was the most significant barrier, while item 2 (M = 2.45; SD = 1.08) was the least significant barrier14. In turn, in the Portuguese study, item 3 (M = 4.08; SD = 0.98) had the highest value and item 9 (M = 1.7; SD = 0.74) had the lowest value13. In surveys of Ethiopian students, the major obstacles to sexual health care were item 6 (mean 4.19), item 4 (mean 4.37), and item 2, (mean 4.27), while item 1 (mean 1.68) was the least significant barrier23. The most significant barrier among students in both our study and the Portuguese study was the statement, "I feel uncomfortable talking about sexual issues”. The above may be explained by the cultural understanding of sexuality within the two nations, which includes hiding and shame, making it difficult to disclose sexuality-related issues and to seek appropriate help and advice when a problem is identified.

According to our research, there are major gaps in the educational strategies to meeting nursing students' professional needs in Poland and to giving them the comfort and confidence to provide sexual health care and counselling26. Thus, sexual health should be integrated into Poland’s nursing study curriculum, and, therefore, research findings support the need for introducing changes in sexual health education27.

Sexuality is regarded as an important aspect of holistic care, but research has demonstrated that it is frequently overlooked in health-care settings. Dealing with patients’ sexuality requires a multidisciplinary approach and cannot be handled only by one healthcare practitioner. According to current literature, medical university students, healthcare professionals, and healthcare workers in hospitals and other health-related facilities in Poland are not properly prepared to handle patients' needs regarding their sexuality28.

Sexuality can be measured through behaviours, attitudes and opinions, however, there are few reliable and valid research instruments for making such observations. One of these tools is the SABS scale, which has been tested in many countries and may be used in multi-centre research in the future. A study using the SABS scale with a sample of 100 Swedish nurses showed that more than 90% understood how patients' diseases and treatment might affect their sexuality. Approximately two-thirds of nurses felt comfortable discussing sexual-related matters. However, 80% of all nurses polled did not make the time to talk about sexual issues, and 60% expressed lack of confidence in their capacity to handle patients' sexual concerns. Higher-educated nurses were more open to discussing sexuality with patients5.

Other studies reveal that nurses and midwives view sexuality as a multifaceted phenomenon and recognize its importance to people's overall health. However, nurses and midwives’ opinions regarding their role in providing sexual health care differ29.

When analysing sexuality issues, it is also important to consider religious and cultural beliefs, as noted by Iranian researchers30. Indeed, in previous work, researchers found that Ethiopian nursing students had negative attitudes and beliefs regarding sexual health care. Over all, research has indicated that nursing students cannot be receiving an adequate education, as comprehensive sex education is severely lacking in nursing schools. Due to the lack of proper sexual health subject matter, it is of key importance to standardize sexual health education in nursing schools, which would enable nursing students to gain the skills necessary to provide patients with comprehensive care23.

Practical implications for nursing education and practice

The study results support the idea that more comprehensive training is required to help Poland’s nurses better meet patients' holistic needs, including those related to their sexuality.

Additionally, the study results of Bal and Sahiner31 and Senturk Erenel and Cicek Ozdemir32 show that attitudes toward sexual health improve as a result of the students’ progress through their education. For this reason, it is critical to support more extensive education in this area and foster a positive learning environment. Furthermore, more research is needed to determine why barriers to discussing sexuality-related issues in nursing education and practice persist.

Study limitations

The non-probability sample group makes it difficult to draw conclusions and interpret the data, even though the study's sample size is suitable for this kind of validation research. Convenience sampling may have caused potential biases such as: non-representative sample, self-selection bias, lack of control, sampling error or potential response biases. Therefore, when using this technique to analyse research results, generalizing beyond the study sample is not possible33. Furthermore, because the SABS's subject area requires respondents to provide personal information about their attitudes towards sexual health issues, we cannot completely rule out the possibility of cultural barriers that may cause a respondent to feel pressured to fit in and provide an answer that "should" be given in light of the survey's purpose and the questions posed. Therefore, multi-centre and random sampling (as opposed to convenience sampling) could add greater value to the results. In addition, answering questions on sensitive sexuality-related topics could constitute a limitation of the study. With regard to the students' decision to complete or not complete the survey, it would be beneficial to ensure the anonymity of their results. This could be achieved through the use of an online tool that provides a higher degree of anonymity.

Conclusions

The psychometric evaluation of the sexuality attitudes and beliefs survey scale among Poland’s nursing and midwifery students demonstrated its validity and reliability for future research. The derived SABS v. PL scale is a valid and reliable tool for measuring nursing students' attitudes and beliefs about providing sexual counselling to patients and for identifying barriers related to sexuality. The scale has suitable psychometric properties and is helpful in professional education and clinical nursing practice.

It should be noted that in Poland, nursing and midwifery students have negative attitudes and beliefs about sexual health care. Nursing students cannot, therefore, be receiving an adequate education, as comprehensive sex education is severely lacking in nursing schools. Understanding sexuality-related beliefs and attitudes is critical for nursing and midwifery students to develop the skills and positive attitudes necessary to address patients' sexuality and sexual issues holistically.
