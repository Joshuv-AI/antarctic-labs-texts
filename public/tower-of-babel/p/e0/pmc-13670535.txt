Scoping review on alternative care options for patients calling the ambulance
Authors: Gayathri Devi Nadarajan, Brian Fu, Jemima Teo, Yean Lyi Lim, Benjamin Leong, Marcus Eng Hock Ong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe modern-day population with more chronic, complex medical and social comorbidities is increasing ambulance utilisation and conveyance to emergency departments (ED). This contributes to ambulance ramping, ED overcrowding and increased hospital bed utilisation. This scoping review aims to identify alternative care paths for patients calling for ambulance services.MethodsA total of 4902 articles were identified through four databases and the gray literature. The terms ‘paramedic’, ‘ambulance’ and ‘non-conveyance’, ‘alternative’ and ‘community care’ were combined to yield the search terms. The population studied includes individuals calling the national emergency number while the intervention focuses on prehospital services designed to avoid ED conveyance. Independent researchers screened the titles, abstracts and full texts, yielding 59 relevant articles which were then meta-synthesised.ResultsFive themes of care were identified - hear and treat, hear and refer, see and treat, see and refer and see and convey to a non-ED facility. Low-acuity patients, older patients, palliative and psychiatric patients, as well as those facing exacerbation of chronic conditions, alcoholic and frequent attenders appear to benefit from these alternative care service pathways (ACSPs). Allied health professionals such as nurses, social workers, occupational therapists, and physiotherapists collaborate with paramedics to frontload the care of these patients in the prehospital setting. Alternative conveyance destinations include general practices, geriatric wards, toxicology units, sobering centres and community hospitals.ConclusionsImplementing ACSPs and integrating healthcare approaches to enhance service efficiency and patient outcomes can alleviate pressure on emergency services by providing timely care and directing patients to appropriate services.

Background

Around the world, many emergency and prehospital services are strained by increasing patient loads. In Australia [1] and the United States of America (USA) [2], for example, there has been an increase in the number of calls received by ambulance services and patients being conveyed to the emergency department (ED). This has led to a downstream effect of overcrowding in the ED and overutilisation of hospital beds. As a result, there is also an increase in ambulance diversions, increased ambulance waiting time in the ED [3] and an increase in the number of patients on the ambulance waiting to be handed over to the ED, i.e., ramping [4]. Ramping has also shown to be associated with increased adverse events, mortality and poor morale and attrition of paramedics [3]. This phenomenon is further exacerbated by an increasing number of non-emergency calls to ambulance services which affects the timeliness and availability of ambulances for ‘real’ emergencies [2]. Overall, this is taking a toll on health systems, increasing demand for ambulances, ED space and hospital beds. This contributes to rising healthcare costs. This impacts the quality of care being delivered to patients [3].

The reasons for this increase in the use of emergency services are multifactorial, such as an ageing population [5–7] where patients are more complex, with multiple comorbidities or socioeconomic disparities manifesting as the utilisation of emergency services [1].

Alternative care service pathways (ACSPs) provide a possible solution to address the problems associated with increased ED utilisation. It allows for intervention at the stage of calling an ambulance to better right-site patients to a more appropriate disposition, while at the same time ensuring patient safety.

There is a need to re-think and re-design prehospital services to provide alternative care pathways for the populations utilising their services. However, there are challenges in identifying these groups of patients such that the right care is delivered safely. For each patient calling the ambulance, there are also challenges in identifying the appropriate alternative care services specific to their population needs without compromising the quality or safety of care.

Prehospital triage systems are crucial in enabling these alternative care pathways by identifying patients suitable for non-conveyance and referral to alternative destinations. The process of triaging occurs at various stages, such as primary triage at dispatch, secondary telephone triage and on scene clinical assessment using structured triage tools. However, triage systems are not perfectly accurate and can result in incorrect diversion from the emergency department. Studies of non-conveyed patients have reported re-contact rates of 6.3%, emergency department attendance of 4.2% and mortality of up to 1.3% [8].

Hence the main goal of this scoping review was to identify ACSPs implemented for patients utilising the ambulance services and its safety by addressing 3 questions: (1) “What type of alternative services are being provided?” (2) “Which group of patients are suitable and safe for these alternative services?” and (3) “Who provides these alternative services?”

It is hoped that this review can provide a broad understanding of alternative services that can be provided by the prehospital emergency care services, which will enable the right siting of patient care without compromising on patient safety.

Methods

This review followed the Systematic reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) checklist.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The Population, Intervention, Comparison, Outcome and Time (PICOT) framework was used. The population was defined as anyone calling the national emergency number for an ambulance while the intervention was defined as the service provided at the prehospital or emergency setting to avoid conveyance to the ED or admission to the hospital. The comparison is the current method of conveying patients to the ED. Papers describing qualitative or quantitative outcomes or narratives of the services provided over the past 5 years were included. Commentaries, non-English articles and articles describing non-conveyance of patients with respiratory symptoms during the COVID-19 pandemic were excluded.

Search strategy

The Cochrane Collaboration database, the Campbell Collaboration and PubMed were searched for existing complete systematic reviews addressing this topic. Although a similar paper exists [9], the review excluded articles targeting specific clinical conditions and looked at articles up to February 2020. Our hypothesis was that newer models of care and challenges have emerged during and after the COVID-19 pandemic and hence the relevance of an updated search from February 2020 to February 2024. Furthermore, we found it important to expand the search to include alternative care for specific conditions as this is in line with our objectives of searching for groups of patients whom these alternative care paths would benefit. Hence this scoping review includes these articles.

An initial pilot search was conducted in two databases by the senior reviewer (GN) and the librarian to define effective search terms. This iterative process ensured that the search strategy captured all relevant studies.

The search was eventually conducted across PubMed, Scopus, CINAHL and Embase together with a snowball search of systematic reviews and the gray literature. Keywords and MeSH terms were generated with the PICOT framework. The ‘who’ (paramedic, ambulance) and ‘what’ (non-conveyance, alternative, community care) terms were then combined using Boolean operators ‘OR’ and ‘AND’ across the PICOT elements. Three reviewers (BF, YL and J) screened the abstracts independently and conflicts were resolved by consensus with a fourth reviewer GN, who is a senior emergency doctor. Three reviewers (BF, LYL and JT) then independently performed the full-text review, conflicts were resolved by GN.

Data extraction and result synthesis

Three reviewers (BF, LYL and JT) completed the extraction of a smaller sample of 59 studies. The extraction methods were then refined through consensus of 2 senior reviewers (GN and MO). Data extraction and analysis were performed via Excel.

Results

A total of 4902 articles were found. After a title and abstract screen, 417 articles appeared relevant and the full texts were extracted. Among these, 59 articles were found to be relevant. (Fig. 1).

Five major themes of alternative care pathways/processes were derived from the literature: [1] hear and treat [2, 10–16] hear and refer [4, 17–20] see and treat [3, 8, 21–36] see and refer [37–45] and [5] see and convey to non-ED facilities [33, 46, 47].

Alternative care paths / processes

Part A: Hear (telephone triage)

Hear and treat

This refers to advice given over the phone to the public. This can include general first aid advice, guidance on where to seek appropriate and timely treatment or specific advice for specific groups of patients such as poisoning cases [10] or nursing home patients [11, 12]. ‘Hear and treat’ services usually exist with ‘hear and refer’ services where the service provider refers the callers to an appropriate service. This model is found in a few settings such as in Melbourne, Copenhagen, Osaka and the United Kingdom (UK) [13–16].

Hear and refer

This refers to the service provider assessing the patient over the phone and referring them to the appropriate services, as seen in the Victorian ambulance service or the UK National Health Service (NHS) 111 service. The service provider taking the call is usually a health professional who performs secondary triage after primary triage is performed by a layperson dispatcher [17]. The services described in the model include crisis management teams, community mental health teams, community pharmacies [18], and dental services. In some instances, the telehealth service redirects the caller to alternative transport services if ambulance transport is deemed inappropriate [19].

Both ‘hear and treat’ and ‘hear and refer’ fall under a broader umbrella of telephone triage services [20].

Part B: See (on-site assessment)

An entry point into the system is when the paramedic reviews the patients at scene and decides that alternative care is more appropriate.

See and treat / non-conveyance

This refers to the prehospital team assessing and treating the patient in the community itself, without having to transport them to a healthcare facility. The prehospital team may consist of paramedics with advanced training [21] or ambulance nurses with prehospital care training [22]. The team can also consist of a nurse and general practitioner [23] or an emergency medical technician and emergency physician [24]. Non-conveyance was one of the most common alternative paths [25] described in the literature, with many studies focused on the safety and acceptability [8, 26–28] of non-conveyance. Overall, it was a safe and efficient [29] option, except when applied to older patients. Studies show that non-conveyance can be a risky option for older patients, who have a higher risk of death and hospitalisation following non-conveyance in the absence of appropriate follow-up care [30, 31]. In some qualitative studies, patients felt that communication [32] and mode of follow-up for non-conveyed patients is crucial [22, 33, 34]. Adherence to the guidelines by the paramedics helps ensure the safety of this alternative path [35]. Without a safety net or clear guidelines on the patients’ disposition, paramedics felt that it was challenging to only treat and discharge patients in the community [36].

See and refer

This refers to paramedics or a team with emergency doctors reviewing patients in the community, providing treatment as needed, and referring them to appropriate medical services, social services [37–39], or alternative transport services when ambulance transport was deemed inappropriate. The literature describes a range of services that paramedics can refer to, such as primary healthcare [40], physiotherapy, occupational therapy, nursing, care co-ordination, social services, urgent care centres [41] and total home services [42]. A common point of referral is to primary healthcare services [43], but this appears to have certain limitations including reduced operating hours and increasing patient loads faced by primary healthcare services. Standardised clinical decision tools assist paramedics with this alternative path [40, 44]. A unique model is Pathfinder in Ireland, where a team of Advanced Paramedics, Physiotherapists, and Occupational therapists attend to older patients deemed to have a low-acuity condition after an initial assessment either over the phone or at the scene by a first responder (either paramedic or firefighter) [45].

See and convey to non-ED facilities

This refers to the prehospital team reviewing the patients in the community and sending them to an alternative appropriate healthcare facility instead of the ED. In Stockholm, a successful randomised trial was described where patients aged 65 years and above who met the inclusion criteria were sent to a geriatric ward in the community hospital after the ambulance nurse discussed the case with the on-call geriatric consultant [46, 47]. In some settings, patients are directly conveyed to a primary healthcare facility [23]. Some other types of facilities that patients get transferred to include poisoning centres, mental health centres and sobering centres.

Groups of patients suitable for alternative care paths

Various triaging tools have been used in different settings to help direct callers to alternative care paths. The Manchester Triage System (MTS), the Emergency Severity Index (ESI), the Adaptive Process Triage (ADAPT), the National Early Warning Score (NEWS), the Taiwan Prehospital Triage System (TPTS) and the Rapid Emergency Triage and Treatment System (RETTS) were some of the systems described in the literature. The RETTS and NEWS appear to be commonly used in the prehospital setting.

Low-acuity patients

Firstly, there is heterogeneity in the terms used to describe the cases deemed inappropriate for prehospital services. Non-life-threatening emergency, non-emergency, non-urgent, low-acuity are some of the terms that have been used. For the purpose of this paper, the term ‘low-acuity’ will be used. Next is the stage at which ‘low-acuity’ is determined - at the dispatch level versus the paramedic level. With non-medical professional-driven dispatch, ‘low-acuity’ is established when paramedics arrive at the scene [48].

This term describes a varied group of patients, from young patients seeking treatment for anxiety or minor injuries, to older patients seeking assistance after a fall with no physical injuries. What is seen across the literature is a rise in the number of low-acuity calls to prehospital services and hence the need for these alternative paths for such patients.

Several studies have explored alternative care for low-acuity patients with minor injuries and minor symptoms, deemed to be more suited for primary care. Many of these are qualitative studies exploring the paramedics’ and/or patients’ perspectives [49]. Interestingly, when multiple factors are taken into account, especially patients’ perceptions of their symptoms, the health system and accessibility of the setting, patients appear to have a valid reason for utilising prehospital services [50]. Those with chronic illness perceive their acute attacks to be severe and hence utilise emergency services. Those with chest pain and shortness of breath perceive their symptoms as severe enough to call an ambulance. In addition, limited accessibility to primary healthcare after office hours or to other medical services appears to be a driver for their utilisation of emergency medical services. Some of these patients also have difficulty accessing acute medical care due to mobility issues. As a result, these patients would utilise the emergency number. The potential benefit of pursuing non-conveyance for patients labelled low-acuity appears limited, as there was a high rate of reattendance observed [34].

Six significant groups of people appear suitable for a range of alternative care path services as described below.

Older patients

Utilisation of prehospital services by older patients is increasing due to the ageing population in these countries. A common finding is a lack of centralised referral points for the paramedics to refer this group of patients. The ED may not be the ideal referral point for older patients, as the ED and subsequent hospital admission puts them at risk of hospital-acquired infections, delirium, deconditioning and disruption to their existing social infrastructure. Hence, many studies have described the impact of the alternative services on this group of patients. It was found that solely adopting the method of ‘see, treat and discharge’ was unsafe. Secondary triage and dispatch, along with appropriate referrals or options for follow-up care, were useful for these patients [14, 51]. Additionally, it was found that older patients can be appropriately and safely conveyed directly to geriatric wards in community hospitals, while minimising the risks mentioned above that may result from an ED admission. Other models include sending a team of specialists to assess and treat them within the community itself [45, 52]. However, there was little intervention by the paramedics at scene itself as there was a lack of specialised knowledge and skills among the paramedics when they were required to manage older patients.

Older individuals in residential aged care

Telemedicine support is commonly described as a modality used to enable nurses at residential aged care facilities to provide appropriate treatment to older patients on-site without having to convey them to the ED to avoid risks associated with hospitalisation such as nosocomial infection, deconditioning and delirium. The impact of this is mixed [11], where only some of these interventions significantly reduced ED visits [12]. Programs that did not reduce ED transfers showed that the long-term facility staff felt that utilising telemedicine support was too time-consuming and undermined their judgment [53].

Hospice patients

An initiative in California describes a mobile integrated hospice unit where a paramedic with special palliative care training can manage palliative patients known to the hospice program under the direction of the hospice nurse [54]. This model prevented the palliative patients from being brought to the ED.

Patients with acute alcohol intoxication or acute poisoning

Sobering centres have been described in the USA for patients with acute alcohol intoxication. These are short-term monitoring facilities staffed with nonphysician providers, such as registered nurses or emergency medical technicians who triage and monitor intoxicated individuals transported by the ambulance or the police, until sobriety is achieved [55]. In New Zealand, triage advice is provided by the New Zealand National Poisons Centre (NZNPC). NZNPC would offer the caller appropriate advice based on assessment over the phone [10]. Both of these services displayed reduction in costs, measured based on the utilisation of emergency medical services.

Patients with mental health conditions, drug addiction or chronic alcoholism

Studies have shown a reduction in the utilisation of emergency services by direct conveyance of patients by ambulances to dedicated centres that manage mental health conditions [56, 57]. This pathway appears feasible and safe [58]. However, its cost-effectiveness has yet to be evaluated [59].

Frequent attenders with psychiatric and social issues

Many of the alternative services described enable the prehospital team (either an advanced paramedic, prehospital nurse practitioner or secondary triage via telemedicine) to connect frequent utilisers of prehospital services to appropriate social services [13, 60, 61].

Service providers for alternative care

Providers of alternative services can be organised at 2 levels: those who assess patients in the prehospital setting, and those who provide alternative care or efferent services to patients.

Most of the on-scene assessments are performed by an advanced paramedic [62], even for low-acuity cases. In the USA, these providers come from the pool of community paramedics or Mobile Integrated Health (MIH) [63]. Clinical decision tools are widely used to help paramedics decide alternative care paths for their patients. In contexts where advanced paramedics were not deployed, there was either a nurse or a doctor in the prehospital setting to assess and manage the patients.

The scope of the paramedics described in these services has broadened to include the ability to perform point-of-care assessments and clinical decision-making skills to enable them to offer appropriate alternative care pathways to patients. There was also significant discussion and job scope variation around the term ‘community paramedicine’ [64]. The role of a community paramedic in various systems ranges from home visits for chronic disease management to on-site intervention for acute medical conditions. A common goal for community paramedicine appears to be the provision of equitable emergency care to their population.

There are unique models of services where allied healthcare is involved in providing care to patients within the prehospital setting itself [45]. The efferent services described are usually provided by primary healthcare physicians, geriatric teams, allied healthcare teams, community allied healthcare teams, community pharmacists or social health support services. Mistriage risk in alternative care pathways

One of the biggest concerns in alternative prehospital care pathways is mistriage. Some of the articles described the adverse outcome rates when these alternative pathways were used. For example, in the paper by Paulin et al. [8], 6.3% called for an ambulance again, 4.2% attended the Emergency Department, 1.6% was eventually hospitalised and 0.3% needed hospitalisation. The mortality following non-conveyance was reported as 0.1% within 24 h by Paulin et al. [8], 0.7% within 7 days by Hoglund et al. [31] and 1.3% within 30 days by Heinonen et al. [28]

Discussion

Based on the findings, a ‘chain of care for unscheduled needs’ was developed (Fig. 3) to illustrate the relationship between patient access, triage, prehospital assessment, and downstream care pathways. Patients may enter the system through emergency calls for either true emergencies or perceived urgent needs. Initial dispatch triage is followed by secondary triage, either via telephone or on-site clinical assessment, to further determine patient acuity and care needs. Based on this assessment, patients may be conveyed to the emergency department, managed at scene, referred to community or primary care services, or conveyed to alternative healthcare facilities. This chain highlights the central role of secondary triage in directing patients to appropriate care providers and enabling alternative care pathways. It also demonstrates the need for integration between emergency services, community care, and alternative providers to ensure safe disposition, appropriate follow-up and continuity of care. Such an integrated system supports a ‘no wrong door’ approach, where patients can be safely up-triaged or down-triaged based on evolving clinical needs. This framework also highlights the dependency of alternative care pathways on accurate triage and the importance of clear referral pathways and safety-net mechanisms to minimise mistriage and adverse outcomes.

This scoping review showed non-conveyance being practiced in various systems in different forms. However, its effectiveness is limited by the lack of alternative care paths and the resultant emotions of fear of unsafe non-conveyance, uncertainty of the accuracy of their assessment and frustration in the lack of alternative care for patients [36]. Paramedics feel safer when there are services to which they can refer patients. They also describe their patients needing social support and hence express the need to work more closely with social services for these non-conveyance patients [26]. At the same time, patients have a sense of abandonment and feel unsafe without a safety net or support system [22]. These findings support the need for the provision of alternative care pathways for these patients who are not conveyed and the need to widen the scope of training and practice for paramedics to include understanding the social context of the population they are serving. In addition, there is a need for effective communication to reduce this feeling of abandonment and helplessness in patients [22, 32, 33, 65].

Furthermore, there is an increased risk of reattendance and complications for non-conveyance of older patients and those with respiratory symptoms [8, 28, 31]. Mistriage and adverse outcomes were not described in all the articles but found mainly in articles solely describing non-conveyance. The scoping review highlights the need for a common framework to ensure quality and consistency in the measures of success and failure. Strengthening triage systems is essential to ensure patient safety when implementing these models of care. Mistriage may occur due to the limitations of prehospital assessment, diagnostic uncertainty, lack of access to investigations, time pressures, and the complexity of patients with multiple medical and social needs. Strategies such as structured triage tools, clinical decision support, secondary triage systems, and access to alternative care pathways with appropriate safety-netting may help reduce mistriage and improve patient safety.

This review highlights the heterogeneity and complexity of low-acuity patients. The low-acuity patients appear to benefit from entering the alternative care pathways via the ‘Hear’ route. However, some of these low-acuity patients have complex medical and social co-morbidities that are not reflected in the triage systems, and they may benefit from the ‘see’ route or a more robust secondary triage system. Different forms of alternative care paths have been designed for these patients, the most common being a secondary triage system via a telephone helpline. More studies need to be performed to better understand the low-acuity patients and triage them more appropriately such that their needs can be captured at triage itself. In some of the systems described in these reviews, more advanced paramedics are sent to manage these low-acuity patients. This differs from the traditional approach of sending more advanced paramedics to attend to high acuity cases. Understanding the health and social needs of these low-acuity patients can also help reinforce the additional skills that paramedics need to redirect them to appropriate alternative care paths.

This scoping review shows that in addition to low-acuity patients, other groups of patients can benefit from alternative care paths in the prehospital setting rather than being conveyed to the ED (such as older patients, palliative patients and those with chronic or mental illness). These patients should be seen to have a better assessment of their needs prior to directing them to the alternative care paths. These findings on patient segmentation can help prehospital systems prioritise and plan alternative care paths for different groups of patients who are utilisers of prehospital services. Knowledge of this patient segmentation can be used together with the Bridges to Health Model [66] to guide evaluation of the ACSPs through evaluation of patient outcomes.

Multiple alternative care paths have been described for older patients, reflecting the need to address alternative care models for the older population in the prehospital setting. Interestingly, there is a paucity of alternative care models for palliative patients in the prehospital setting, which is a potential area for future studies.

This review describes paramedics with extended skills to facilitate the provision of ACSPs. Paramedics are required to do more at the scene and develop skill sets similar to that of a nurse or a medical doctor. There is a need to broaden paramedic education and the scope of paramedic practice. For older patients, it was recognised that there were fewer interventions by paramedics [14]. This could be due to the complexity of the conditions presented in older patients. This could also indicate a lack of familiarity and comfort level of the paramedics in managing these groups of patients. Traditional paramedic training focuses on medical emergencies with little focus on geriatric or social emergencies. This presents a potential area for further development in paramedic education [67].

This review describes ACSPs involving other allied healthcare providers in the prehospital setting. The models described implies frontloading care in the prehospital setting and may be a potential future model of prehospital care. However, more studies need to be performed to evaluate its cost-effectiveness in such a labour-intensive model of care.

This review showed alternative care paths for frequent attenders. Broadly, there are 2 groups: those with a complex psychiatric history and those with frequent acute exacerbations of chronic diseases. While the community paramedic approach is being adopted by some countries to help manage acute-on-chronic exacerbation of diseases, alternative models can be considered where community nurses can work alongside paramedics to treat acute events and prevent ED conveyances.

The review also uncovers a potential model of a virtual ED where emergency doctors can provide telemedicine services as support to other healthcare providers (such as paramedics, GPs or community nurses) who are by the patient’s side and provide real-time assessment and management.

Finally, an underlying theme was the need for seamless integration between the emergency and community services [68], the need for policy to support the prehospital system to divert patients to more appropriate care services besides the ED, as well as the need to support the financing system to ensure that the system is sustainable without incurring additional costs to the patients.

Conclusion

In this review, we found that there are multiple alternative care pathways available for different groups of patients in the prehospital setting. To implement these pathways, there is a need for effective triaging of these patients, widening the scope of paramedic education and practice, involving other allied healthcare professionals in provision of prehospital care and providing medical oversight through a virtual ED model. Implementing ACSPs and integrating healthcare approaches to enhance service efficiency and patient outcomes can alleviate pressure on emergency services by providing timely care and directing patients to appropriate services.
