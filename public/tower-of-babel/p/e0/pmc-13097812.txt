Epidemiological characteristics of six respiratory pathogens in respiratory infections in Fuzhou, China
Authors: Shaoting Chen, LIli Jiang, Huan Zhou, Danzhen Lunzhu, Lili Yu, Xiuqing Shen, Pengju Cao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo characterize the burden and features of respiratory pathogens infections among patients with Acute Respiratory Tract Infections (ARTIs) in Fuzhou, China. Providing evidence-based guidance for outbreak prevention.MethodsFrom December 2023 to February 2025, 10,355 ARTI patients at Fuzhou University Affiliated Provincial Hospital were tested for six pathogens (Influenza A virus (FLUA), Influenza B virus (FLUB), respiratory syncytial virus (RSV), Human rhinovirus (HRV), adenovirus (ADV), and Mycoplasma pneumoniae (MP)) using multiplex PCR. Detection rates and epidemiological characteristics were analyzed.ResultsAmong enrolled patients, 4,699 (45.37%) tested positive. Overall pathogen detection showed no significant gender difference. However, RSV positivity was higher in males (P =0.046), while MP infection predominated in females (P <0.0001). MP predominated among single infections (14.72%), followed by HRV (13.54%), ADV (9.13%), FLUA (8.93%), RSV (3.43%), and FLUB (2.57%). Major coinfections (>1%) included HRV+MP (2.98%), ADV+MP (2.87%), HRV+FLUA (2.06%), and ADV+HRV (1.64%). These pathogens constituted the primary etiology of pediatric ARTIs. Distinct seasonal patterns emerged: FLUA peaked in spring/winter; FLUB and MP surged in winter; RSV circulated in spring/autumn; ADV prevailed in autumn/winter; HRV was endemic in summer/autumn/winter. HRV positivity correlated inversely with improved air quality (daily index <60). RSV outbreaks exhibited a lagged correlation with precipitation and demonstrated a "compensatory peak" relative to FLUA. Compared to individuals >60 years（y）: Children <7 y had significantly elevated RSV risk; Minors <18 y showed reduced FLUA susceptibility; FLUB risk increased in ages 1-60 y; Those <60 y exhibited higher ADV, HRV, and MP infection risks. Relative to winter: Spring decreased FLUA/B/MP risk but increased RSV/HRV; Summer lowered FLUB/MP risk while elevating RSV; Autumn reduced FLUA/B/ADV/MP risk but heightened RSV/HRV. Multivariate analysis identified age >60 y and RSV/HRV/MP infection as independent risk factors for lower respiratory tract involvement.ConclusionThe epidemiology of these six pathogens exhibits significant age-, season-, and climate-dependent variations, necessitating tailored preventive strategies. Enhanced vigilance for lower respiratory complications is warranted in individuals ≥60 y and those infected with RSV, HRV, or MP. Introduction

Pediatric acute respiratory tract infections (ARTIs) were classified as either upper respiratory tract infections (URTIs) or lower respiratory tract infections (LRTIs) based on the anatomical demarcation at the inferior border of the cricoid cartilage [1]. URTIs encompassed pharyngitis, rhinitis, tonsillitis, and influenza cases without lower respiratory tract involvement, while LRTIs comprised bronchitis, bronchiolitis, and pneumonia. Viral pathogens represent the predominant etiology of pediatric ARTIs [2]. Although bacteria, mycoplasma, chlamydia, fungi, and parasites contribute to disease burden, influenza virus (FluV), respiratory syncytial virus (RSV), Human rhinovirus (HRV), adenovirus (ADV), and Mycoplasma pneumoniae (MP) constitute the most clinically significant agents.The immunological and respiratory immaturity in children potentiates pulmonary developmental impairment from early-life infections, elevating lifelong asthma susceptibility [3]. Globally, respiratory complications account for millions of preventable pediatric deaths annually, with disproportionate mortality in resource-limited settings [4]. Consequently, precise viral identification in pediatric cases is critical for targeted interventions and antimicrobial stewardship. Post-pandemic respiratory pathogen epidemiology in China has undergone substantial transformation. Following adjustments to national COVID-19 policies in early 2023, regional outbreaks of multiple respiratory pathogens emerged, revealing distinct geographic heterogeneity in transmission dynamics [5]. This evolving landscape remains inadequately documented in current literature.

Multiplex real-time quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR) permits high-throughput simultaneous detection of diverse viral pathogens from single clinical specimens, overcoming limitations of conventional diagnostics (e.g., viral culture, serology) which frequently demonstrate suboptimal sensitivity and specificity. This study employs multiplex PCR to evaluate the clinical utility and epidemiological dynamics of prevalent respiratory pathogens in the post-pandemic era. While previous research has predominantly focused on pediatric populations, the substantial burden of these pathogens in adults, particularly older adults, remains inadequately characterized. We therefore analyzed the epidemiological features of six target pathogens across all age groups, concurrently incorporating environmental variables to investigate their influence on transmission patterns. These findings will inform diagnostic optimization, evidence-based clinical management, and targeted prevention strategies for region-specific interventions.

Materials and methods

Study population

A total of 10,355 clinical specimens were collected from patients presenting with at least two of respiratory infection symptoms (including cough, rapid breathing, nasal congestion/purulent pharyngeal exudate, runny nose, sore throat, headache, fever, cough, wet rales, and dyspnea) at Fujian Provincial Hospital Affiliated to Fuzhou University, between December 2023 and February 2025. Exclusion criteria comprised autoimmune diseases, known immunodeficiency, repeated testing within 2 weeks, or a primary diagnosis unrelated to respiratory diseases. The cohort comprised 5,914 males and 4,441 females. This observational study adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Ethics Committee of Fujian Provincial Hospital affiliated Fuzhou University. Patient privacy was protected by encrypting all identifiable information within the electronic database. Informed consent was waived due to the non-interventional nature of the study and the absence of identifiable patient information. Patients were stratified by sex and age group: age < 1 y, 1 y ≤ age<3 y, 3 y ≤ age<7 y, 7 y ≤ age<18 y, 18 y ≤ age<60 y, age ≥ 60 y.

Specimen collection

Throat swab specimens from all patients were collected by trained healthcare professionals following standardized protocols. Sterile flocked swabs were used to obtain secretions from the posterior or lateral pharyngeal walls. Swabs were immediately placed in transport tubes containing viral preservation medium, with excess handles aseptically removed before sealing. Samples were delivered to the laboratory within 1 h of collection. When immediate transport was unfeasible, specimens were stored at 4 °C for 2–24 h or at -20 °C for prolonged storage (> 24 h).

Nucleic acid extraction and detection

Nucleic Acid Extraction: Total nucleic acids were isolated using magnetic bead-based extraction kits (DAAN GENE, Guangzhou, China). Detection was performed using the Six-Plex Respiratory Pathogen Nucleic Acid Detection Kit (Sansure Biotech, Changsha, China). This kit includes all necessary primers and probes for amplification, enabling the simultaneous detection of respiratory pathogen nucleic acids in samples through multiplex RT-qPCR.

Data collection

Clinical data was collected from the Laboratory Information System (LIS) of the Provincial Hospital Affiliated to Fuzhou University, comprising age, sex, nasopharyngeal swab collection dates, clinical diagnoses, and multiplex RT-qPCR results for six respiratory viruses. Concurrently, environmental monitoring parameters for the Fuzhou region were obtained from the Fuzhou Meteorological Station during the corresponding period. ARTIs were categorized into four seasonal groups according to the climate patterns of Fuzhou: spring (March–May), summer (June–August), autumn (September–November), and winter (December–February).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS software (version 22.0; IBM SPSS ). Categorical variables are presented as frequency (percentage). The 95% confidence interval (CI) for detection rates was calculated via the normal approximation method. Chi-square tests assessed positivity rate differences for six common respiratory pathogens across sex, age groups, seasons, and clinical diagnoses, with chi-square trend tests applied for multi-group comparisons. Multivariable analysis utilized multinomial logistic regression, and statistical significance was defined as P < 0.05 (two-sided).

Results

Gender and age distribution of patients infected with six respiratory pathogens

Between December 2023 and February 2025, among 10,355 enrolled patients, 4,699 tested positive (45.37%). Positivity rates differed significantly between males and females in RSV (P = 0.046) and MP (P < 0.001). MP demonstrated the highest overall positivity rate (14.72%) (Table 1; Fig. 1). Age-stratified analysis revealed significant variations across groups(Table 1):Flu-A peaked in 18 y ≤ age < 60 y cohort (20.32%); RSV predominated in < 1 y group (19.12%); HRV showed highest prevalence in 1 y ≤ age < 3 y children (29.94%); Flu-B reached maximal positivity in 18 y ≤ age < 60 y group (4.60%); ADV and MP exhibited peak rates in 3 y ≤ age < 7 y children (22.02% and 34.70%, respectively). Based on overall pathogen detection rates, six respiratory agents—Flu-A, Flu-B, RSV, ADV, HRV, and MP—constituted the predominant etiological pathogens of ARTIs in patients under 18 y, with particularly high prevalence among those aged between 1 and 18 y (Table 1).

Discussion

This large retrospective cohort study (N = 10,355) detected six respiratory pathogens at an overall prevalence rate of 45.37%. Prevalence was comparable between males (46.11%) and females (44.42%; P > 0.05). Gender disparities emerged for specific pathogens. RSV infections were significantly more prevalent in males (P = 0.046), consistent with other report [6]. Conversely, MP prevalence was higher in females (16.93%; P < 0.0001). Among the respiratory pathogens, MP exhibited the highest prevalence (14.72%), followed by HRV (13.54%), ADV (9.13%), Flu-A (8.93%),, RSV (3.43%), and Flu-B (2.57%). The peak MP prevalence potentially reflects a large winter 2023 outbreak. Increased susceptibility was observed in individuals with immature immunity and frequent contact exposure [7], potentially explaining the significantly prevalence in 3 ≤ age < 7. This age group corresponds to the stage of entering preschool or early schooling, where increased contact with peers in group settings may lead to higher exposure to potential asymptomatic or pre-symptomatic carriers of respiratory pathogens. HRV prevalence was significantly higher in minors versus adults, suggesting immune immaturity as a primary risk factor. Positivity peaked in children aged 1–7 y, exceeding rates in infants < 1 year, indicating frequent contact exposure as an additional risk factor. Flu-A predominated in adults, possibly indicating receptor-specific susceptibility within mature immune systems. ADV infections were most common in individuals aged 1–18 y, suggesting mature immunity and reduced contact activity lower transmission risk. RSV incidence peaked in children < 3 y, declining sharply thereafter, highlighting immature immunity as a key susceptibility factor. Flu-B prevalence was highest in individuals aged 3–60 y, implicating frequent contact exposure.

The combined prevalence of these pathogens exceeded 50% in minors, confirming MP, HRV, Flu-A, ADV, RSV, and Flu-B as major etiological agents of ARTIs in this group. The co-infection rate was 14.32%. Reflecting geographical variation in co-infection patterns [8], the predominant dual combinations locally (each > 1%) were MP/HRV, MP/ADV, HRV/Flu-A, and HRV/ADV in descending order. ADV/HRV/MP emerged as the most frequent triple infection, while Guangzhou reported Flu A /Flu B/MP as predominant [9]. This discrepancy may reflect differences in cohort demographics (our study included all ages, whereas Guangzhou and Shanghai studies focused on children) and regional epidemiology. MP predominated in co-infections, consistent with prior reports [10–12]. Integrating single-pathogen data, MP emerged as the dominant pathogen in both mono-infections and co-infections in Fuzhou during the 2023–2024 winter seasons. This finding aligns with reports from Wuhan [13], Shijiazhuang [6], and Guangzhou [8].

Monthly prevalence analysis indicated pronounced seasonal clustering patterns among the six pathogens. Flu-A and B exhibited peak prevalence during winter and spring. RSV and HRV demonstrated heightened activity in spring and autumn. ADV circulation was most pronounced in winter. MP experienced an abrupt outbreak in winter 2023, with subsequent gradual decline in positivity, suggesting a propensity for winter epidemics. The transmission dynamics of pathogenic viral diseases are affected by complex factors and their interactions, such as climate, virus traits, host life-history traits, and environmental conditions [14].Given this pronounced seasonality, we investigated potential correlations between pathogen prevalence and environmental indices. Notably, HRV positivity displayed an inverse relationship with daily Air Quality Index (AQI) values. Primarily spread via aerosols and contact, HRV viability is likely compromised under low AQI (< 60) conditions: heightened ultraviolet radiation inactivates environmental virions, while drier air destabilizes the non-enveloped virus. Furthermore, minimal PM2.5/PM10 concentrations reduce airborne particulate carriers, shortening aerosol persistence and shifting transmission dominance towards contact routes. This environmental shift is compounded by behavioral changes: studies indicate a 37% increase in children’s daily outdoor duration and a 52% rise in playground/park time under low AQI [15]. Each additional outdoor hour correlates with an increase in contact frequency with high-risk surfaces, and children neglecting hand hygiene before touching their face exhibit a 2.9-fold elevated HRV infection risk [16]. Consequently, the observed inverse HRV-AQI association under good air quality (AQI < 60) fundamentally reflects the interplay between contact-mediated transmission dominance and concomitant behavioral patterns, highlighting a unique transmission ecology distinct from aerosol-dependent respiratory viruses.

Multivariable logistic regression was subsequently performed, with female sex, age > 60 y, and winter season as reference groups, to assess associations between sex, age, season, and susceptibility to each pathogen:

Flu-A: Minors showed reduced susceptibility compared to individuals > 60 year. Spring and autumn were protective relative to winter. Potential explanations include: (1) Vaccination: Higher influenza vaccine coverage and more robust antibody responses in minors compared to adults, particularly the elderly who exhibit diminished immunogenicity and shorter-lived protection; (2) Exposure: Enhanced infection control in schools/childcare facilities mitigates transmission risks for minors, whereas frequent healthcare visits for comorbidities (e.g., COPD, diabetes) increase exposure risk in the elderly; (3) Seasonality: Reduced ventilation during temperature extremes (winter/summer) heightens aerosol transmission risk, while increased outdoor activity and airflow in spring/autumn lower infection probability. Consequently, improving indoor ventilation seasonally and prioritizing high-dose adjuvanted vaccines for the elderly may reduce Flu-A burden.

Flu-B: The risk was significantly higher in individuals aged 1 to < 60 years compared to those aged ≥ 60 years, while summer/autumn were protective relative to winter. Studies indicate heightened Flu-B susceptibility in individuals aged < 60 y, likely due to increased social mixing (school/work) and immature immune memory in children [17, 18]. Summer/autumn conditions (high heat, humidity, UV) suppress viral viability. Reducing gatherings and enhancing indoor ventilation in winter are key mitigation strategies.

ADV: Risk was significantly higher in individuals < 60 y, peaking in the 1–18 y group, autumn conferred lower risk than winter. ADV infection demonstrates a distinct age gradient, with highest susceptibility in young children and lowest risk in older adults [19]. Low temperature, low humidity, and high NO2 levels (characteristic of winter) correlate positively with ADV transmission risk, milder temperatures, higher UV, and increased outdoor activity in autumn likely suppress environmental persistence [20]. Therefore, promoting winter outdoor activity, enhancing indoor ventilation, and implementing classroom UV disinfection may reduce ADV transmission among school-aged children.

RSV: Surveillance in Guangdong Province (2013–2023) [21]revealed significantly elevated RSV activity during summer (June–August) and autumn (September–November), overlapping with the rainy season. Our analysis further indicates that RSV exhibited elevated infection risk in spring, summer, and autumn relative to winter. Children under 7 y demonstrated heightened susceptibility compared to individuals over 60 y, with peak incidence occurring among those under 3 y. Outbreak timing exhibited a time-lagged correlation with total precipitation. A moderate to strong mutually inhibitory relationship was observed between RSV and influenza viruses, infection with one virus conferred 40–100% cross-protection against the other [22]. However, an alternative perspective posits that viral interference may lead to a ‘pathogen suppression’ phenomenon, wherein the surge of one pathogen could subsequently inhibit the prevalence of another [23]. Consistent with the report, we identified a compensatory surge phenomenon specifically between RSV and FLU-A. The viruses likely compete for overlapping ecological niches within the susceptible host population, while immune-mediated interference (e.g., interferon responses) may reciprocally suppress viral replication. Consequently, within surveillance and early warning frameworks, sustained sub-baseline RSV activity should trigger preemptive resource mobilization for potential FLU-A outbreaks. Furthermore, enhancing vaccination coverage in high-transmission groups upon detecting compensatory surge risk can mitigate FLU-A dissemination. Certainly, the observed phenomenon cannot exclude the possibility of mere seasonal overlap in viral circulation. Consequently, substantiation of this hypothesis necessitates extended longitudinal studies in subsequent research.

HRV: Prevalence peaked in spring/autumn, with highest susceptibility in children < 7 y [24]. Surveillance in Fuzhou revealed higher HRV detection rates among males than females. Infection risk was significantly elevated during spring and autumn compared to winter. Individuals under 60 y exhibited greater susceptibility than those aged 60 and above. Among older adults, respiratory infections were predominantly bacterial or influenza-related. HRV infections in this demographic typically presented as mild upper respiratory symptoms with low healthcare-seeking behavior, likely contributing to reduced detection rates.

MP: MP can occur at any age but is most common among preschool and school-aged children [25]. Studies have shown that MP detection rates are significantly higher in females aged 23–38 years compared to males, with a distinct seasonal peak in winter [26, 27]. These established epidemiological characteristics align precisely with our findings. The higher infection rate observed in females may stem from sex-based differences in immune responses. Alternatively, social-behavioral factors likely contribute, as enhanced susceptibility in children places women of childbearing age, often in primary caregiving roles, at elevated exposure risk.

We conducted multivariate logistic regression analysis to identify factors (sex, age, season, pathogen) associated with LRTI development. The analysis revealed significantly increased odds of LRTI among individuals aged over 60 y. This elevated risk is attributable to age-related pulmonary senescence, immunosenescence (e.g., impaired alveolar macrophage clearance), comorbidities (including COPD and diabetes), and aspiration risk, collectively contributing to heightened LRTI susceptibility [28]. ADV infections in adults are typically self-limiting URTI, with lower respiratory tract involvement observed in only 5%-10% of cases [29]. Consistent with these patterns, our study identified FLU-B and ADV infections as protective factors against LRTI. Conversely, RSV, HRV, and MP infections emerged as significant risk factors for LRTI [30]. Immunosenescence impairs viral clearance, resulting in progression to LRTI in 30%-40% of elderly individuals infected with RSV, frequently leading to bronchiolitis or pneumonia [31]. HRV represents the second most prevalent pathogen in pediatric LRTI (detection rate: 20%-30%), increasing pneumonia risk through disruption of the respiratory epithelial barrier. MP infection significantly elevates pneumonia risk among elderly patients [32]. This study addresses a critical gap in respiratory pathogen research by encompassing all age groups, whereas most prior investigations focused exclusively on pediatric populations, lacking comprehensive adult data. The identified epidemiological patterns of the six respiratory pathogens align closely with established characteristics in southern China, though regional variations likely exist in northern areas. This study has several limitations. First, as a single-center study, it only has regional representativeness. Second, it did not investigate the primary pathogens causing acute respiratory infections in adults. Finally, the survey period was limited. In subsequent investigations, we will collaborate with multiple centers, extend the survey duration, and supplement the study of the primary pathogens responsible for acute respiratory infections in adults.

Conclusion

The collective analysis of six major respiratory pathogens causing acute respiratory infections has elucidated key epidemiological characteristics. These findings provide valuable insights for developing targeted etiological surveillance and evidence-based prevention strategies against respiratory infections in Fuzhou.
