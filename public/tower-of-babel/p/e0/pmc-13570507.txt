Negative psychological and behavioral effects associated with wearable devices in cardiovascular patients: a scoping review with implications for nursing assessment and patient education
Authors: Fei Liao, Yu Wang, Youjiao Zheng, Jingrui Yang, Yinyue Li, Xueru Huang
Published: 2026

Background

Wearable devices have become increasingly integrated into cardiovascular care, particularly in rhythm monitoring, cardiac rehabilitation, long-term risk management, and patient self-management after acute cardiovascular events. Smartwatches, wristbands, wearable electrocardiographic devices, activity trackers, mobile health applications, and remote cardiac rehabilitation systems can provide continuous or near-continuous information on heart rate, rhythm, physical activity, sleep, and rehabilitation progress. These technologies may help patients participate more actively in self-management and may support clinicians in follow-up, education, and risk communication [1–6].

Most existing research on wearable devices in cardiovascular populations has focused on clinical utility, feasibility, diagnostic accuracy, adherence, and potential benefits for rehabilitation and disease management [2, 3, 7–12]. In post-percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) care, wearable devices may appear particularly attractive because patients need to maintain long-term antiplatelet therapy, control cardiovascular risk factors, resume physical activity, participate in cardiac rehabilitation, and monitor recurrent or new symptoms [13]. However, revascularization does not end disease management. After discharge, many patients still need to interpret symptoms such as chest discomfort, palpitations, fatigue, sleep disturbance, or heart rate changes during exercise [14–17].

Continuous access to physiological data does not necessarily lead to better interpretation. Some patients may misinterpret normal fluctuations in heart rate, rhythm, sleep scores, step counts, or exercise indicators as signs of clinical deterioration. Device alerts, especially abnormal rhythm notifications or false alerts, may trigger worry, repeated checking, repeated reassurance seeking, unnecessary health information searching, or repeated medical visits [18–28]. These potential psychological and behavioral burdens have received less systematic attention than the benefits of wearable technologies.

Nurses are often responsible for discharge education, rehabilitation guidance, telephone follow-up, symptom triage, and patient education across cardiovascular care settings. If patients rely heavily on device data, misunderstand alerts, avoid rehabilitation exercise because of heart rate changes, or repeatedly seek reassurance after abnormal notifications, nurses need practical assessment clues and education strategies. Therefore, this scoping review aimed to map the evidence on negative psychological and behavioral effects associated with wearable devices in cardiovascular patients and to summarize implications for nursing assessment and patient education.

Methods

Study design

This study was conducted as a scoping review to map the available evidence on negative psychological and behavioral effects associated with wearable devices in cardiovascular patients. A scoping review design was selected because the topic is emerging, the evidence is heterogeneous, and the relevant literature includes different populations, device types, care contexts, and study designs. The review was guided by the Joanna Briggs Institute methodology for scoping reviews and reported with reference to the PRISMA Extension for Scoping Reviews [29–31].

Negative psychological and behavioral effects associated with wearable devices were defined as psychological or behavioral responses triggered or exacerbated by device-generated data, alerts, continuous feedback, insufficient interpretation of data, privacy concerns, or device-related burden. These effects included health anxiety, worry, repeated checking, repeated reassurance seeking, excessive self-monitoring, device dependence, reduced self-management confidence, and avoidance behaviors.

Eligibility criteria

Eligibility criteria were developed according to the population, concept, and context framework. The population included patients with cardiovascular diseases or studies conducted in cardiovascular care contexts, including coronary heart disease, post-PCI care, acute coronary syndrome, myocardial infarction, atrial fibrillation, arrhythmia, heart failure, cardiac rehabilitation, and general cardiovascular disease management. The concept was the use of wearable devices or related digital health technologies, including smartwatches, wristbands, wearable electrocardiographic devices, activity trackers, mobile health applications, telemonitoring systems, remote monitoring systems, and remote cardiac rehabilitation platforms.

Studies were eligible if they reported psychological responses, behavioral responses, patient experience, adherence, quality of life, health anxiety, technology anxiety, device dependence, repeated checking, false alerts, privacy concerns, data interpretation issues, or device-related burden. Original studies, qualitative studies, cross-sectional studies, cohort studies, randomized or nonrandomized intervention studies, diagnostic accuracy studies, systematic reviews, meta-analyses, scoping reviews, expert consensus papers, commentaries, and perspective articles published in English or Chinese from January 2014 to April 2026 were considered. To reduce scope drift, broader digital health publications were retained only when the technology, data feedback, monitoring process, or patient response was relevant to wearable-device use, mobile or remote monitoring, rehabilitation feedback, alerts, privacy concerns, or patient interpretation of health data. Studies focused only on device engineering, sensor materials, signal processing, algorithm development, or system architecture without patient-related psychological or behavioral content were excluded.

Information sources and search strategy

A comprehensive literature search was performed in PubMed, Web of Science Core Collection, Cochrane Library, China National Knowledge Infrastructure, Wanfang, VIP, and SinoMed. The search strategy combined three groups of terms: cardiovascular diseases and related populations, wearable devices and related digital health technologies, and negative psychological or behavioral effects. The search period began in January 2014 to capture the contemporary expansion of consumer-facing wearable and mobile health technologies in cardiovascular monitoring, rehabilitation, and self-management. The detailed search strategies and initial records retrieved from each database are provided in Supplementary Table 2.

Study selection and data charting

All retrieved records were imported into Zotero for reference management, deduplication, and screening. Two reviewers independently screened titles and abstracts according to the eligibility criteria. Potentially relevant records were assessed through full-text review when full texts were available. Full texts or complete available publication records were retrieved and reviewed for all publications included in the final synthesis. Where a conference abstract, short report, or database record was the only available publication format, the record was charted only if it contained sufficient information for standardized extraction. Records for which full texts could not be retrieved, or for which the available record contained insufficient information for standardized data charting, were excluded and documented with reasons. Disagreements were resolved through discussion, and a third reviewer was consulted when necessary.

A standardized data charting form was developed before data extraction. Extracted information included title, first author, year of publication, country or region, disease population, device or technology type, care context, study design, sample size, psychological or behavioral effects, main findings, implications for coronary heart disease or post-PCI care, evidence category, and language. One reviewer completed the initial extraction, and another reviewer checked the extracted data for accuracy and consistency. During revision, the final evidence matrix was rechecked against the retrieved full texts or complete available publication records to improve consistency between eligibility decisions, evidence categorization, and extracted findings.

Evidence categorization and data synthesis

To clarify relevance to post-PCI nursing care, included publications were classified as direct evidence, indirect evidence, or mechanism-supporting evidence. Direct evidence referred to studies in which the population or care context was coronary heart disease, post-PCI care, acute coronary syndrome, or cardiac rehabilitation. Indirect evidence referred to studies involving atrial fibrillation, arrhythmia, heart failure, or general cardiovascular diseases in which wearable-device or digital-monitoring findings were clinically relevant but not specific to post-PCI care. Mechanism-supporting evidence referred to studies not directly focused on post-PCI patients but useful for explaining possible mechanisms underlying device-related psychological or behavioral responses, such as health anxiety, reassurance seeking, privacy concerns, or quantified-self behaviors. When a publication could fit more than one category, it was assigned to the most directly relevant category according to population and care context.

Because of heterogeneity in populations, device types, study designs, and outcome measures, no quantitative meta-analysis was performed. Descriptive synthesis was used to summarize publication characteristics. Thematic synthesis was conducted by extracting reported or implied psychological, behavioral, and patient-experience findings, grouping similar findings into preliminary codes, and then iteratively organizing these codes into the four categories reported in this review. Formal methodological quality appraisal was not conducted because the purpose of this scoping review was to map the scope, characteristics, and gaps of the evidence rather than evaluate intervention effectiveness or generate pooled effect estimates. Therefore, the findings are interpreted as evidence-informed patterns and assessment prompts, not as estimates of effect size or evidence certainty.

Results

Study selection and characteristics

The database search identified 17,416 records, including 5,752 from PubMed, 9,498 from Web of Science Core Collection, 161 from Cochrane Library, 363 from CNKI, 1,359 from Wanfang, 120 from VIP, and 163 from SinoMed. After deduplication and merging, 12,257 records remained. Following title and abstract screening, 489 records were retained for full-text eligibility assessment. Of these, 349 records were excluded at the full-text eligibility stage. A total of 140 records proceeded to final relevance and data-charting assessment. After data charting, 30 records were excluded, including six records for which full texts could not be retrieved or the available records were insufficient for standardized extraction. Finally, 110 publications were included in this scoping review (Fig. 1).

The 110 included publications covered coronary heart disease, post-PCI care, acute coronary syndrome, cardiac rehabilitation, atrial fibrillation, arrhythmia, heart failure, and general cardiovascular disease management. Slightly more than half were published between 2024 and 2026 (n = 56, 50.9%), and most were in English (n = 90, 81.8%). In terms of relevance to post-PCI care, 57 publications (51.8%) were classified as direct evidence, 9 (8.2%) as indirect evidence, and 44 (40.0%) as mechanism-supporting evidence. The evidence categories were used to distinguish direct cardiovascular or rehabilitation evidence from broader mechanism-supporting literature and to avoid presenting indirect findings as direct post-PCI evidence. Health anxiety and heightened symptom perception were related to increased attention to heart rate, rhythm, sleep, activity, or bodily sensations. In some patients, normal fluctuations in physiological indicators may be interpreted as signs of deterioration or recurrent cardiovascular events [18, 27, 28]. Alert-related concerns were most evident in rhythm-monitoring contexts, in which abnormal notifications or false alerts may lead to worry, repeated checking, repeated reassurance seeking, or additional medical visits [18, 19, 21–26].

Excessive self-monitoring referred to frequent or difficult-to-control checking of device data, including heart rate, rhythm, sleep score, step count, or exercise targets. Device dependence occurred when patients relied heavily on device-generated data or scores to judge health status or make decisions about activity, medication, follow-up, or medical consultation. Several studies also suggested that alerts, data inaccuracies, technical burden, privacy concerns, and unclear feedback may reduce confidence in symptom management or rehabilitation participation [32, 33] [10, 34–36].

Preliminary nursing assessment clues for cardiovascular and post-PCI care

Direct evidence specifically focused on post-PCI patients was limited. Therefore, evidence from cardiac rehabilitation, rhythm monitoring, digital health psychology, coronary heart disease care, and mechanism-supporting literature was integrated to develop preliminary nursing assessment clues for post-PCI care. These clues should not be interpreted as validated predictive models or screening tools, but rather as evidence-informed prompts that may help nurses identify when wearable-device use is affecting symptom interpretation, rehabilitation behavior, reassurance seeking, or self-management confidence (Table 3).

First, fear of rehabilitation exercise may occur when post-PCI patients misinterpret exercise-related heart rate increases, missed activity targets, or occasional rhythm fluctuations as danger signs. Second, catastrophic interpretation of symptoms may occur when non-specific symptoms such as chest discomfort, palpitations, fatigue, sleep disturbance, or shortness of breath are followed by repeated checking of device data. Third, device dependence with weakened adherence to medical advice may occur when patients give greater weight to wearable-device data than to medical advice, symptoms, or objective clinical indicators. Fourth, excessive reassurance seeking may occur when abnormal alerts, previous disease-related fear, insufficient explanation, and easy access to online health information lead patients to repeatedly seek confirmation.

Discussion

Principal findings

This scoping review found that wearable devices have been widely examined in rhythm monitoring, cardiac rehabilitation, post-PCI care, secondary prevention, and long-term cardiovascular self-management. However, most studies focused on feasibility, accuracy, adherence, or clinical benefits, whereas potential psychological and behavioral burdens were less systematically addressed.

The findings suggest that wearable devices may not be psychologically neutral tools for all patients. By making physiological signals visible and continuously available, these technologies may alter how patients perceive, interpret, and respond to bodily sensations. This is particularly important after cardiovascular events, when patients may already be highly alert to symptoms and fearful of recurrence. However, the available evidence does not support a simple causal conclusion that wearable devices directly produce anxiety or maladaptive behavior [37]. The more cautious interpretation is that device data, alerts, usability problems, privacy concerns, and insufficient interpretation support may interact with pre-existing concerns, disease experience, and care context.

Implications for cardiovascular nursing practice

For cardiovascular nurses, the key issue is not only whether patients use wearable devices but how they interpret and act on device data. Nursing assessment should explore checking frequency, alert experiences, reassurance-seeking behavior, avoidance of rehabilitation activity, and whether device information changes exercise, medication, follow-up, or care-seeking behavior.

Nursing education should emphasize that wearable devices are supportive tools rather than diagnostic authorities. Patients should be informed that device data may fluctuate, alerts may require confirmation, and consumer-grade devices cannot replace medical advice, medication adherence, symptom assessment, or scheduled clinical review. Nurses can help patients distinguish between expected physiological responses, symptoms requiring observation, symptoms requiring consultation, and warning signs requiring urgent care.

In digital cardiac rehabilitation, technical training should be paired with data interpretation education. Patients need to understand expected physiological responses during exercise, such as moderate heart rate increases, and should be taught how to combine device feedback with symptoms, exercise tolerance, and clinical instructions. Patients with repeated checking, sleep disturbance, avoidance of activity, or self-directed treatment changes may require closer follow-up or multidisciplinary support.

Recent work on immersive virtual reality and cardiac telerehabilitation further supports this patient-centred interpretation. These studies emphasize that adherence, motivation, experience, relationship-centred support, psychological burden, and implementation context should be considered alongside feasibility and clinical utility when digital rehabilitation technologies are introduced [38–40].

Research implications

The current evidence base remains fragmented. Many studies examined device feasibility, accuracy, or rehabilitation benefits, but fewer directly measured negative psychological or behavioral outcomes. Future studies should develop clearer operational definitions of wearable device-related psychological and behavioral effects and should directly examine post-PCI populations.

Qualitative studies are needed to explore how post-PCI patients understand device data, respond to alerts, and integrate wearable feedback into symptom interpretation and care-seeking decisions. Cross-sectional studies could examine associations between device-use patterns and health anxiety, cardiac anxiety, sleep quality, self-management confidence, device dependence, and quality of life. Prospective studies are also needed to evaluate changes at 1, 3, and 6 months after PCI.

Strengths and limitations

This review synthesized evidence across multiple cardiovascular contexts and digital health technologies, included both Chinese and English literature, and used a broad scoping approach to identify psychological and behavioral effects that may be overlooked in traditional effectiveness-focused reviews. It also translated findings into practical nursing assessment clues for post-PCI care.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, direct studies on wearable device-related negative psychological and behavioral effects in post-PCI patients were limited. Therefore, the post-PCI nursing assessment clues were derived from direct evidence in coronary heart disease and cardiac rehabilitation, indirect evidence from rhythm monitoring and other cardiovascular populations, and mechanism-supporting evidence from digital health psychology and device-related studies. Second, included publications varied in population, device type, study design, outcome measurement, and publication format; some complete publication records, such as conference abstracts or short reports, contained less methodological detail than full articles. Third, formal methodological quality appraisal was not conducted, consistent with the scoping purpose but limiting conclusions about evidence strength. Fourth, although records without retrievable full texts or sufficient information for standardized extraction were excluded during revision, the broad and rapidly evolving nature of wearable-device research means that some relevant evidence may still have been missed.

Conclusions

Wearable devices can support cardiovascular rehabilitation, rhythm monitoring, and long-term self-management, but they may also contribute to worry, repeated checking, reassurance seeking, device dependence, or avoidance of rehabilitation activity in some patients. The available evidence is heterogeneous, and direct post-PCI evidence remains limited. Therefore, the findings should be interpreted cautiously. Nurses should use wearable-device information as part of broader clinical assessment rather than as a stand-alone indicator, and patient education should emphasize appropriate interpretation of device data, alert-response pathways, medication adherence, rehabilitation participation, and timely professional consultation when warning signs occur. Future studies should directly examine post-PCI populations and validate practical nursing assessment tools for wearable device-related psychological and behavioral responses.
