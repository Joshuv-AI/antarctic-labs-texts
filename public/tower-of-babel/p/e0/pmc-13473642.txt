Robust in-situ stress inversion in an underground powerhouse using tensor synthesis and surrogate-assisted differential evolution
Authors: Zhihong Dong, Shijie Hu, Yuan Qian, Xinhui Zhang, Ping Fu, Kaicheng Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Accurate characterization of the initial in-situ stress field is essential for stability assessment, support design, and surrounding rock control in deep underground engineering, yet field measurements are often highly scattered and three-dimensional inversion is computationally expensive. This study develops a robust and efficient inversion framework for a deeply buried underground powerhouse in the southeastern Tibetan Plateau. First, a three-dimensional borehole stress synthesis method is established by combining particle swarm optimization, the Huber loss, Levenberg–Marquardt iteration, and regularization to denoise multi-source measurements, suppress local outliers, and alleviate ill-conditioning in stress-tensor reconstruction. Second, a surrogate-assisted differential evolution workflow is constructed using a radial basis function network within a prediction–verification–correction active-learning loop to reduce the cost of repeated forward simulations while preserving global optimization capability. Application to the powerhouse shows that the mean relative error decreases from 14.12 to 9.34%, and the deviation variance decreases from 2.98 to 1.33 after optimization. The proposed framework improves both the reliability of inversion input data and the efficiency of field-scale stress reconstruction, providing practical support for stability evaluation and surrounding rock control in deep, geologically complex underground caverns.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-59862-4.

Introduction

In recent years, driven by expanded national infrastructure programs and the large-scale deployment of modern power systems, the construction of deep-buried underground projects-such as pumped-storage power stations-has grown significantly1. Their underground powerhouse cavern groups are typically sited in complex, deep geological settings and are subject to extensive excavation disturbance fields2. High in-situ stress now constitutes the primary driver of instability phenomena in these settings, including surrounding-rock deformation, failure, and rockbursting3–5. Topography and regional tectonics further impose strong spatial nonuniformity on the deep initial in-situ stress field. Failure to characterize this heterogeneity accurately can mislead stability analyses and complex structural predictions in deep formations6,7. Accordingly, high-precision in-situ stress measurement and inverse reconstruction are fundamental to safe construction of deep underground works8. Yet, complex measurement environments and sparse data make it challenging to derive accurate single-point stress states and to reconstruct a continuous in-situ stress field for the entire site; solving this problem remains a central task in rock mechanics.

Accurately determining the in situ stress at a single point is the essential first step in characterizing an engineering site’s stress field9. Common in situ testing methods include hydraulic fracturing, stress-relief techniques, and borehole-wall breakout analyses10,11. Hydraulic fracturing is widely used to determine the two-dimensional planar stress state of rock masses because it is simple to operate and can reach considerable depths12. By contrast, borehole-deformation methods-such as hollow inclusion strain gauges-record borehole-wall deformation during stress relief and, when combined with the rock’s elastic properties, can in principle yield the full six-component, three-dimensional stress tensor at the measurement location. This capability gives borehole-deformation approaches a clear advantage for three-dimensional in situ stress measurement in deep, hard-rock engineering13. However, the measurement accuracy of this method depends critically on the data-processing algorithm. Conventional procedures typically employ the least squares (LS) estimator, which assumes Gaussian errors and is therefore highly sensitive to gross outliers. In field settings, measurements often include non-Gaussian errors from sources such as microfractures in the rock and deviations during instrument installation. Furthermore, complex borehole-wall deformation can produce observation equations with very large condition numbers, rendering the problem ill-posed and yielding oscillatory or distorted solutions. Consequently, effective outlier removal and mitigation of ill-posedness remain the primary constraints on improving the accuracy of single-point, three-dimensional in situ stress measurements.

After acquiring high-precision single-point stress measurements, constructing a continuous three-dimensional initial in situ stress field is essential for engineering design. In situ stress inversion aims to identify the optimal set of boundary-condition parameters that make a numerical model reproduce measured field values. Traditional multiple regression models perform poorly in the complex, nonlinear geomechanical environments of deep formations. Recently, neural networks and other machine learning-driven inversion methods have been increasingly adopted to improve mapping capabilities in complex geological regions14. However, they require repeated forward simulations during iterative optimization, which imposes a heavy computational burden. Moreover, when the inversion objective is high-dimensional and multimodal, standard intelligent algorithms often converge to local optima, producing results that deviate substantially from geological reality. This nonconvex optimization issue constrains both computational efficiency and the physical fidelity of inversion outcomes. Consequently, reducing computational cost without sacrificing accuracy and enhancing global optimization capability remain urgent challenges in current in situ stress inversion research.

Despite these advances, a focused gap remains: scattered and outlier-contaminated 3D borehole constraints can destabilize single-point tensor reconstruction, while site-scale inversion remains computationally prohibitive when repeated 3D forward simulations are required. Existing studies typically address either robust single-point estimation or efficient inversion, but an integrated workflow that jointly improves the reliability of inversion inputs and the efficiency of global inversion is still limited. This study aims to fill this gap by combining robust tensor synthesis with surrogate-assisted global inversion in a unified framework.

To fill this gap, the proposed framework is integrated in a principled manner to address three coupled bottlenecks in deep in-situ stress reconstruction. First, measurement constraints are often contaminated by non-Gaussian gross errors, which motivates a Huber-robust objective. Second, the tensor-assembly equations can be ill-conditioned, which motivates LM refinement under Tikhonov regularization to stabilize the solution. Third, site-scale inversion requires repeated 3D forward simulations, which motivates surrogate-assisted global optimization. Accordingly, PSO is adopted to robustly optimize the Huber objective at the synthesis stage, while the SADE workflow combines DE-based population search with an RBF surrogate in a prediction–verification–correction loop to accelerate the expensive inversion stage.

Based on the above rationale, this study presents a robust and efficient framework for reconstructing the initial in-situ stress field in a deeply buried underground powerhouse. At the measurement-analysis level, a three-dimensional borehole stress synthesis strategy is developed by combining particle swarm optimization, Huber robust estimation, Levenberg–Marquardt iteration, and regularization. This strategy suppresses the influence of gross outliers and mitigates ill-conditioning in the observation equations, thereby improving the reliability of single-point stress-tensor estimates. At the field-inversion level, a surrogate-assisted differential evolution (SADE) method is introduced to reduce the computational burden of repeated three-dimensional numerical simulations. The method employs a radial basis function surrogate within a prediction-verification-correction active-learning loop to achieve efficient global optimization of regression parameters. By integrating robust data reconstruction with surrogate-assisted inversion, the proposed framework provides a practical route for refined stress-field characterization in deep underground engineering and offers a more reliable basis for stability assessment, support design, and surrounding rock control. Compared with conventional neural-network-based inversion approaches that are often coupled with gradient-type local search and require numerous full forward-model evaluations, the proposed SADE framework maintains population-based global exploration to alleviate entrapment in local minima, while substantially reducing forward-simulation calls through surrogate evaluation.

Situation of the relying project

Engineering background

This study examines an underground component of a large pumped-storage power station located in western Sichuan Province, China, within the rapidly changing topographic transition zone between the Qinghai–Tibet Plateau and the Sichuan Basin. Geographically, the site lies on the eastern margin of the Tibetan Plateau, where high-relief alpine valleys and active faulting dominate the regional geomorphology. Project elevations range from 3000 to 5000 m, with higher terrain in the northwest and lower terrain in the southeast. Intense river incision and both internal and external geodynamic processes have produced widespread, deeply incised V-shaped valleys and large local relief between ridges and valley floors. The study area lies adjacent to major, deep active faults, including the Xianshuihe Fault. Continuous plate compression combined with multi-stage faulting has driven persistent neotectonic activity here, and the region has long been within the influence of multiple surrounding seismic zones. The pronounced topographic incision, which creates large elevation differences, combines with strong modern tectonic activity to produce a complex, highly heterogeneous in-situ stress field in the deep rock mass. This complex stress environment complicates accurate determination of the initial stress state and hinders reliable assessment of the stability of surrounding rock for deeply buried underground powerhouse cavern groups.

The water conveyance and power generation hubs of the station are housed within a thick mountain on the river’s left bank. The overlying surface elevation generally exceeds 4000 m, characteristic of a high-mountain, deep-cut gorge. The underground powerhouse cavern group lies in the central section of the water conveyance system and follows an overall “three tunnels and six units” layout. The vertical overburden depth of the main caverns ranges from 565 to 691 m. As shown in the engineering geological and general layout plan (Fig. 1), the powerhouse system’s longitudinal axis extends more than 250 m, the sidewall height approaches 60 m, and the transverse clear span is roughly 27 m, making this a prototypical super-large, deeply buried underground project. The combination of great burial depth and extra-large spans creates severe challenges for surrounding-rock stability under complex regional geological conditions. Therefore, before construction, accurately characterizing the spatial distribution of the initial in situ stress field in the target area is a prerequisite for scientifically assessing deep surrounding-rock stability and for guiding engineering design.Fig. 1Engineering geological map. In-situ stress measurement methods

To address the complex stress environment of deep rock masses in the engineering area, this paper combines two in situ testing methods-hydraulic fracturing and borehole diameter deformation-to achieve multi-scale stress measurement, ranging from two-dimensional plane estimates to full three-dimensional stress tensors15.

For the two-dimensional stress measurement mainly targeting vertical holes, the hydraulic fracturing method is employed. Based on the Kirsch equation of elasticity, when the high-pressure fluid in the hole causes tensile failure of the borehole wall, the relationship between the fracture pressure (Pb) and the horizontal principal stresses is as follows:1\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$P_{b} = 3\sigma {}_{h} - \sigma_{H} + T - P_{0}$$\end{document}

In the formula, σH and σh denote the maximum and minimum horizontal principal stresses, respectively; T denotes the rock’s tensile strength; and P0 denotes the pore water pressure.

For three-dimensional full-tensor measurements, the aperture deformation hollow inclusion strain method is employed. This approach determines the three-dimensional stress state by monitoring borehole-wall deformation during overcoring and then performing a back-calculation13. The theoretical relation between the borehole-wall radial displacement u and the far-field stress components (σx,σy,τxy) is:2\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$u\left( \theta \right) = \frac{d}{E}[(\sigma_{x} + \sigma_{y} )\left( {1 + v} \right) + 2(1 - v^{2} )(\sigma_{x} - \sigma_{y} )\cos 2\theta + 4(1 - v^{2} )\tau_{xy} \sin 2\theta ]$$\end{document}

In the formula, u(θ) represents the radial displacement of the borehole wall at a specific azimuth angle θ. The parameters d, E, and v correspond to the reference borehole diameter, the in-situ elastic modulus, and Poisson’s ratio of the tested rock section, respectively. Relying on the combined responses of multi-azimuth boreholes or multi-dip measurement sections in a single borehole, the three-dimensional in-situ stress tensor with all six components at the measurement point can be solved in a closed form by constructing a simultaneous equation set of strain and stress. To achieve a unified spatial representation of the complex stress state in the field area, the analytical results of local measurement points need to be projected onto the global reference frame. Three-dimensional in-situ stress calculation based on the porous stress synthesis and optimization

Theoretical methods for 3D borehole stress synthesis and optimization

Traditional weighted least squares (WLS) is prone to outlier sensitivity and ill-conditioned equation systems when applied to three-dimensional in situ stress measurements. To address these limitations, this study proposes a Huber robust estimator tuned by particle swarm optimization (PSO) and a Levenberg–Marquardt (LM) regularization scheme, and integrates them into an optimized theoretical framework for three-dimensional borehole stress synthesis. PSO with Huber robust estimation

Classical three-dimensional stress tensor inversion typically uses the Least Squares (LS) criterion to obtain closed-form solutions for the overdetermined systems that arise from field observations. Because LS constructs its objective from the sum of squared residuals, it is highly sensitive to local outliers. Deep rock masses are inherently heterogeneous, and on-site tests can introduce errors such as instrument coupling deviations; consequently, measured samples are likely to contain non-Gaussian gross errors. To reduce the influence of such outliers on parameter estimation, this study adopts M-estimation from robust statistics. The conventional squared-loss measure is replaced by the Huber norm to rebuild the error metric and mitigate distortion. Under this formulation, the residual vector r of the system’s linear observation equation can be explicitly defined as:3r = Aσ - b

In this linear mapping, A denotes the coefficient matrix that encodes the geometric orientations of the measurement holes, b is the observation column vector of on-site measurements, and σ is the spatial in situ stress tensor to be determined. As a common piecewise robust error metric, the Huber function is defined mathematically as:4\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\rho \left( r \right) = \left\{ {_{{\delta \left| r \right| - \frac{1}{2}\delta^{2} , \, \left| r \right| > \delta }}^{{\frac{1}{2}r^{2} , \, \left| r \right| \le \delta }} } \right.$$\end{document}

In the given relationship, δ is set as the robust truncation threshold. In engineering, it is often taken as 1.345 times the standard deviation of the measured residuals to balance statistical efficiency. In the interval where the residuals are small, this function retains the quadratic form feature, thus inheriting the asymptotic approximation efficiency of the conventional least squares method. For gross errors with large deviations, it automatically degenerates into linear growth, thereby fundamentally weakening the leverage effect of extreme abnormal samples on the overall inversion16. Further, the comprehensive optimization objective function with this robust criterion as the core is set as:5\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$J\left( \sigma \right) = \sum\nolimits_{i} {\rho \left( {r_{i} } \right)} + \lambda \left\| \sigma \right\|^{2}$$\end{document}

The objective function includes a modest L2 regularization term, weighted by λ, to suppress numerical divergence when the normal equations have a high condition number. Because the Huber function has a second-derivative singularity at its truncation boundary and the inversion target space is often multi-peaked and nonconvex, deterministic gradient-based algorithms are prone to become trapped in local minima. To this end, this paper employs Particle Swarm Optimization (PSO), a swarm-intelligence heuristic, for global parameter optimization17,18. PSO iteratively updates multidimensional position vectors and exchanges information among agents to locate optimal solutions, thereby improving the global robustness of the regression outcome. For the three-dimensional in situ stress tensor problem considered here (mapping dimension D = 6), a parallel swarm of size N is initialized in the solution space. Let the current position and velocity of the i-th particle be xi = (xi1,xi2,…,xiD) and vi = (vi1,vi2,…,viD), respectively. During iteration step t, the swarm adapts parameters through dual feedback from each particle’s personal best vector pbest and the swarm’s global best vector gbest. The velocity and position update equations are as follows:6\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$v_{id}^{t + 1} = wv_{id}^{t} + c_{1} r_{1} \left( {p_{best,id} - x_{id}^{t} } \right) + c_{2} r_{2} \left( {g_{best,d} - x_{id}^{t} } \right)$$\end{document}7\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$x_{id}^{t + 1} = x_{id}^{t} + v_{id}^{t + 1}$$\end{document}

In the formula, w is the weight coefficient that controls search inertia and thus governs the algorithm’s dynamic trade-off between global exploration and local exploitation. The parameters c1 and c2 are acceleration constants that adjust individual experience feedback and group coevolution, respectively. The terms r1 and r2 are pseudo-random perturbations sampled from the uniform distribution U(0,1). To reduce numerical oscillation in later optimization stages and to improve convergence efficiency, an adaptive control strategy is introduced, which linearly decays w over the iteration sequence:8\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$w = w_{\max } - \frac{{w_{\max } - w_{\min } }}{{T_{\max } }}t$$\end{document}

In these expressions, wmax and wmin denote the initial upper bound and the evolutionary lower bound of the inertia coefficient, respectively, and Tmax denotes the preset global limit approximation algebra. To preserve the physical meaning of the solution, boundary constraints are also imposed on particle positions:9\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$x_{\min } \le x_{id} \le x_{\max }$$\end{document}

In the specific engineering case presented in this study, the initial parameters for the PSO algorithm were configured to balance computational efficiency and global search capability. Specifically, the swarm size was set to N = 60, and the acceleration constants governing personal and global learning were both initialized symmetrically as c1 = 2.0 and c2 = 2.0.

By combining the global search ability of the PSO algorithm with the robustness of the Huber loss function, this method can effectively resist the interference of gross errors in measurement data and obtain a more reliable in-situ stress solution.

Levenberg–Marquardt algorithm with regularization

Full-component analysis of the deep in-situ stress tensor depends critically on the completeness of the spatial observation topology. When on-site boreholes are sparsely distributed or their orientations are highly concentrated, the coefficient matrix A of the observation equations tends to become ill-conditioned, which makes the inverse problem ill-posed. Under this ill-posed mapping, even small observation errors are amplified, producing large oscillations in the inverted stress field and, in extreme cases, rendering the solution physically meaningless. To mitigate this numerical bottleneck, the Levenberg–Marquardt (LM) damping scheme is integrated into the parameter-optimization framework, and the evolution objective is reformulated using Tikhonov regularization. This core strategy suppresses the ill-posed behavior by adding a prior penalty operator to the residual space, thereby exchanging a modest reduction in fit for higher-order stability of the global analytical solution from a mathematical viewpoint19,20. The modified objective function that incorporates this penalty mechanism is expanded as:10\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$J\left( \sigma \right) = \left\| {A\sigma - b} \right\|^{2} + \alpha \left\| {L\sigma } \right\|^{2}$$\end{document}

In the above extended formula, || ||2 denotes the weighted Euclidean distance. The scalar α is a smoothing coefficient that dynamically adjusts the prior constraint’s contribution to the overall objective. L is the structural regularization operator matrix, typically chosen as the identity matrix I or a first-order diagonal matrix under the continuity assumption for geotechnical media21. Drawing on rock-mechanics priors, this paper assigns different penalty weights to normal and shear stress components and accordingly reconstructs the diagonal regularization operator L:11\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$L = diag\left( {l1,l2,...,l6} \right)$$\end{document}

Because coupling the physical penalty term makes the objective equation highly nonlinear, this paper adopts the Levenberg–Marquardt (LM) damped iteration to obtain efficient solutions. Mathematically, this mechanism adaptively combines the second-order accuracy of Gauss–Newton with the global robustness of steepest descent22. During iteration, the gradient-oriented vector of the reconstruction objective function is derived as follows:12\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\nabla J = A^{T} \left( {A\sigma - b} \right) + \alpha L^{T} L\sigma$$\end{document}

The approximate form H of the Hessian matrix is:13\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$H \approx A^{T} A + \alpha L^{T} L$$\end{document}

The iterative update step size △σ of the LM algorithm is determined by the following equation:14\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\left( {H + \mu I} \right)\Delta \sigma = - \nabla J$$\end{document}

In the formula, μ is the Damping Factor. During the iteration process, the value of μ is dynamically adjusted according to the decline degree of the objective function.15\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\rho = \frac{{J\left( \sigma \right) - J\left( {\sigma + \Delta \sigma } \right)}}{{L\left( 0 \right) - L\left( {\Delta \sigma } \right)}}$$\end{document}

If the scale factor ρ > 0, the algorithm accepts the update and reduces the damping parameter μ, which steers the optimization toward the Gauss–Newton direction and thereby improves the convergence rate. Conversely, when ρ ≤ 0, the iteration is rejected and μ is increased adaptively, which pushes the update back toward the steepest-descent direction to preserve global descent. To map the real mechanical response of rock masses, a prior physical penalty operator was specifically incorporated into the evolution objective23:16\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$J_{phy} = \beta \sum {\max \left( {0, - \sigma_{i} } \right)}$$\end{document}

This operator enforces the ordering of the principal stresses (σ1 ≥ σ2 ≥ σ3), thereby preventing nonobjective tensile stress artifacts. By integrating damping optimization, structural regularization, and mechanical boundary constraints, it suppresses the ill-conditioned divergence that can arise in the normal equations. As a result, even with sparse measurements or strong survey noise, the method robustly produces three-dimensional stress-field solutions that retain rigorous mechanical meaning and high-order numerical fidelity.

Software for 3D in-situ stress synthesis and optimization

To facilitate the engineering application of the analytical theory described above, a three-dimensional in-situ stress synthesis and optimization software system was developed (main interface shown in Fig. 5). The system uses a modular architecture to combine multi-source data management, core algorithm execution, and spatial visualization. On the front-end input side, it supports batch import of multi-format text files and automatically extracts each measuring hole’s spatial pose (azimuth, dip angle), occurrence depth, and strain-response components for multiple measuring sections; it also performs data integrity checks and rejects or flags abnormal formats. As the platform’s computational engine, the underlying layer implements three algorithm modules: weighted least squares (WLS), particle swarm optimization with Huber robust estimation (PSO + Huber), and Levenberg–Marquardt with Tikhonov regularization (LM + Tikhonov). The system uses measured sample quality and the normal-equation condition number to trigger adaptive algorithm strategies and to run multi-criterion parallel analyses. It then outputs key calculation indicators simultaneously, including the full six-component tensor at each measurement point, principal-stress deflection characteristics, and the mean squared error (MSE). For post-processing and characterization, the system incorporates a multidimensional graphic-rendering engine that automatically produces stereographic projections of principal stresses, spatial tensor distributions, optimization convergence trajectories, and histograms for algorithm-performance comparison. All calculation parameters and mechanical results are recorded to enable traceability and standardized report archiving, thereby providing efficient digital support for refined evaluations of in-situ stress fields at deep engineering sites.Fig. 5Main interface of the three-dimensional in-situ stress synthesis and optimization software.

3D in-situ stress results and optimization analysis

To characterize the three-dimensional stress state at the field site, detailed processing of the in situ test sequence was performed using the self-developed system described above. Using the measurements at the three-borehole intersection in the Point C machine pit as an example, the solution mechanism of the multi-algorithm collaborative optimization is analyzed. At this measurement location, a representative spatially intersecting borehole group was installed, which comprises one vertical borehole (inclination 90°, azimuth 90°) and two near-horizontal boreholes (inclination − 4°, azimuths 150° and 215°). From these three boreholes, strain responses from 6 effective measuring sections were obtained, and the solution system automatically assembled an overdetermined set of 18 observation equations. Because the raw observations include potential measurement errors and outliers, an initial weighted least-squares trial produced a mean square error of 13.64 and an equation condition number of 3.80. Although the condition numbers fall within the acceptable state interval, the residual-sequence dispersion statistics reveal significant outlier contamination in local measurement segments. This contamination makes conventional unbiased estimators highly vulnerable to bias from extreme outliers. To mitigate this problem, the system activates a dual-path optimization scheme that combines PSO with Huber robust estimation and LM with Tikhonov regularization. Figure 6 shows the convergence trajectories for both algorithms. Guided by second-order gradients, the LM algorithm reaches a steady state in about 8 iterations. By contrast, PSO requires roughly 76 optimization cycles but, thanks to its global search strategy, handles the non-smooth Huber loss more robustly and avoids entrapment in local extrema. Figure 7 compares the results of the three methods. The PSO + Huber method yields a Huber loss of 5.98, which is 2.39% lower than the conventional weighted least squares (WLS) reference value of 6.13. Although its mean square error increases slightly to 14.11, this rise does not imply a degraded solution; instead, it confirms the algorithm’s intent from a physical standpoint: to actively reduce overfitting to gross errors and thus enhance robustness of the global stress field. In-situ stress field inversion based on surrogate-assisted differential evolution

Traditional inversion methods

The deep in-situ stress state records the combined mechanical effects of gravitational consolidation, plate-tectonic forces, and later denudation and unloading accumulated over long geological times. Under the linear-elastic superposition assumption, the regional macroscopic stress field can be represented as a linear sum of a gravity background field and successive tectonic perturbation fields. To support inversion and reconstruction, this paper identifies the primary controlling variables-self-weight response, lateral tectonic compression, and deep shear dislocation-based on the geological context of hazard development in the study area, and defines the basic loading modes. It then establishes the following multivariate linear approximation mapping relationship24:17\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\sigma^{ \wedge }_{jk} = \sum\limits_{i = 1}^{n} {L_{i} \sigma^{i}_{jk} }$$\end{document}

In this mapping, k and j index the discrete measurement points and the independent tensor components, respectively; j = 1 ~ 6 corresponds to xx, yy, zz, xy, yz, zx. The target sequence σ^jk denotes the multivariable analytical approximation of component j at position k. The scalar Li is the optimization weight for the i-th boundary loading mode. σijk denotes the forward-simulated response of the specified stress component at measurement point k driven by a single base working condition i. The index n denotes the number of loading combinations, including gravity and tectonic disturbances.

To determine the optimal approximation coefficient vector L = (L1,L2,…,Ln)T, the normal form typically uses a least-squares criterion. This criterion minimizes the residual sum of squares (RSS) of each stress component across all global observation nodes, driving their RSS values toward a global minimum.18\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$S = \sum\limits_{k = 1}^{m} {\sum\limits_{j = 1}^{6} {\left( {\sigma^{*}_{jk} - \sum\limits_{i = 1}^{n} {L_{i} \sigma^{i}_{jk} } } \right)} }^{2}$$\end{document}

In the formula, σ*jk denotes the in situ measurement of component j at the k-th spatial sampling point, and m denotes the total number of validation samples. By minimizing the evolution process constraint, the procedure adaptively yields weight coefficients that maximize fit, which are then used to reconstruct the regression model of the initial in situ stress field in the target engineering area.

Classical multiple regression models often fail to capture complex, nonlinear geological boundaries at depth. Recent advances-such as neural-network-based inversion for cavern groups in pumped-storage power stations and a horizontal-stress prediction model that integrates borehole-wall collapse data25,26 -have improved nonlinear mapping capacity, and conventional heuristic algorithms have also extended this ability. However, these approaches commonly face numerical limitations18; they are prone to becoming trapped in local minima, suffer from slow convergence, and demand excessive forward-calculation power. For example, during early exploration and computations for this project, the standard PSO algorithm exhibits an inherent conflict between global exploratory search and local fine-tuning in high-dimensional inversion target spaces, which impedes retrieval efficiency and prevents it from meeting the demands of deep, large-scale computations.

Surrogate-assisted differential evolution algorithm and software

Algorithm principle and workflow

To overcome two major computational bottlenecks of conventional swarm intelligence methods-premature convergence on high-dimensional nonlinear inverse problems and the large computational cost of full-domain forward modeling-this paper proposes the Surrogate-Assisted Differential Evolution (SADE) framework27,28. The framework begins by ensuring that the initial training set generalizes well over the feasible solution domain; to that end, it uses Latin Hypercube Sampling (LHS) for spatial initialization. Unlike blind random exploration in standard Monte Carlo approaches, LHS achieves superior space-filling and projection uniformity through stratified sampling. Denote the parameter mapping dimension by D and the global initial sample size by N. LHS partitions each parameter’s domain into N mutually exclusive subintervals of equal probability and draws one independent random sample from each subinterval. In this scheme, the coordinate value xi,j of sample i in the j-th parameter dimension is defined as:19\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$x_{i,j} = \frac{{\pi_{j} \left( i \right) - U_{{\left\{ {i,j} \right\}}} }}{N}\left( {x_{j,\max } - x_{j,\min } } \right) + x_{j,\min }$$\end{document}

In the formula, πj(i) denotes a random permutation without replacement of the integer sequence {1,…,N}, and U{i,j} denotes a pseudorandom sequence drawn from the standard uniform distribution U(0,1). By using this orthogonal hierarchical projection mechanism, the initial samples both substantially reduce spurious collinearity among variables and provide a globally representative dataset for building a high-fidelity global surrogate model29.

Using the initial parameter set generated by LHS and the mechanical response sequence (fitness) obtained from forward modeling with FLAC3D, this study employs a radial basis function (RBF) neural network as a high-fidelity surrogate model. RBF networks, as feedforward architectures, perform well at reducing the dimensionality of nonlinear systems because their universal approximation properties and global basis functions reduce the risk of trapping in local extrema. For a given input vector x, the network’s predicted response f(x) is represented as a linear superposition of hidden-layer basis functions:20\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$f\left( x \right) = \sum\limits_{i = 1}^{N} {w_{i} \cdot \varphi \left( {\left\| {x - x_{i} } \right\|} \right)}$$\end{document}

In the formula, xi denotes the center of the i-th training sample, wi is the weight connecting the hidden layer to the output layer, || || indicates the Euclidean norm, and φ(r) is the radial basis kernel function. In this study, the Gaussian kernel is used, and its expression is given by:21\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\varphi \left( r \right) = \exp \left( { - \varepsilon r^{2} } \right)$$\end{document}

In the kernel function above, ε denotes the shape parameter that sets the radial smoothing scale. To recover the weight column vector w that maps the hidden layer to the output layer, impose exact interpolation of the training outputs to obtain the collocation system φw = y. Here φ is the symmetric kernel matrix whose entries satisfy φ{ij} = φ(||xi-xj||). The corresponding closed-form analytical solution for the network weights is:22\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$w = \phi^{ - 1} y$$\end{document}

Relying on the exact inverse of the above linear system, a fast response surface with high-dimensional fitting efficiency can be constructed. Thus, on the premise of ensuring the accuracy of inverse mechanics, the heavy computational burden of expensive numerical simulations can be substantially relieved30,31.

This study employs a high-fidelity RBF surrogate to replace part of the physical forward modeling when evaluating population fitness and applies the Differential Evolution (DE) algorithm for global parameter optimization32,33. DE is a population-based, parallel search method that exploits differences among individuals; its high-order memory and noise-resistant properties aid robust global exploration. Offspring are generated through the standard operators of mutation, crossover, and selection, applied iteratively. At generation G, for any target vector xi,G in the population, the algorithm selects three distinct individuals (r1,r2,r3) at random, computes their relative differences, and uses them to form the perturbed mutation vector vi,G.23\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$v_{i,G} = x_{r1,G} + F \cdot \left( {x_{r2,G} - x_{r3,G} } \right)$$\end{document}

In the above difference formula, the scaling factor F ∈ [0,2] adaptively calibrates the amplitude of differential perturbations among individuals, thereby giving the population the ability to escape local extrema at a fundamental mechanistic level.

To broaden the population’s distribution within the feasible solution domain, the system applies a binomial crossover between the original carrier xi,G and the synthetic mutant vi,G to produce the trial vector ui,G. Focusing on spatial dimension j, the crossover and recombination proceed according to these evolutionary criteria:24\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$u_{{i,j,G}} = \left\{ {\begin{array}{*{20}c} {v_{{i,j,G}} ,} & {{\text{ }}if{\text{ }}rand_{j} \le CRorj = j_{{rand}} } \\ {x_{{i,j,G}} ,} & {{\text{ }}otherwise} \\ \end{array} } \right.$$\end{document}

In the above criteria, randi denotes a pseudo-random scalar drawn from a uniform distribution, and CR ∈ [0,1] is the prescribed crossover recombination threshold. The random dimension index jrand is chosen to ensure that the resulting trial vector contains at least one component contributed by the mutation perturbation.

After mutation and crossover, offspring evolution follows a greedy selection rule. The trial vector replaces the target individual only if its fitness is strictly better; otherwise, the original individual is retained.25\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$x_{{i,G + 1}} = \left\{ {\begin{array}{*{20}c} {u_{{i,G}} ,} & {{\text{ }}if{\text{ }}f\left( {u_{{i,G}} } \right) \le f\left( {x_{{i,G}} } \right)} \\ {x_{{i,G}} ,} & {{\text{ }}otherwise} \\ \end{array} } \right.$$\end{document}

Because the global response surface often shows local evaluation errors near nonlinear extrema, this study implements an active learning loop of "prediction-verification-correction." At each generation, all candidate individuals are first evaluated by the RBF surrogate to obtain predicted fitness values. The algorithm then selects one infill candidate for high-fidelity verification (nexploit = 1), which is taken as the best individual under surrogate prediction after a short surrogate-driven DE search. Specifically, a DE population of size Npop = max(10, N) is evolved for Ts = 50 surrogate generations, and the candidate with the best predicted fitness is verified using the FLAC3D solver. The verified response is compared with measurements to compute the coefficient of determination R2, and the calibrated objective is defined as f(x) = 1-R2 (equivalently maximizing R2). The surrogate is updated every generation by appending the newly verified sample and retraining the RBF model. The loop terminates when either (i) the high-fidelity evaluation budget Neval (“Max Evaluations”) is exhausted, or (ii) the verified fit reaches the optimum for Gs = 3 consecutive verifications, i.e., |R2—1|< 10–4 for the latest three verified samples.26\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R^{2} = 1 - \frac{{\sum\limits_{i = 1}^{n} {\left( {y_{i} - \mathop {y_{i} }\limits^{ \wedge } } \right)^{2} } }}{{\sum\limits_{i = 1}^{n} {\left( {y_{i} - \overline{y} } \right)^{2} } }}$$\end{document}

The increment samples verified by physical checks are fed back to the basic training set in real-time to drive the dynamic reconstruction of the RBF surrogate network. This closed-loop mechanism not only accurately reduces the topological distortion of the response surface in the potential extreme value regions but also drives the adaptive improvement of the mapping accuracy until the output solution strictly reaches the preset convergence threshold30. Platform for 3D in-situ stress inversion based on surrogate-assisted differential evolution

Building on the methods described above, an autonomous 3D in-situ stress inversion platform based on surrogate-assisted differential evolution (3D In-situ Stress Inversion System via Surrogate-Assisted DE) was developed. As illustrated in the interactive window of Fig. 10, the platform integrates multi-source data management and control, boundary-optimization configuration, RBF network training, and evolutionary computation into a unified visual interface. In the front-end parameter panel, users can specify the extreme bounds of inversion variables, set the trust-region radius for the radial-basis-function surrogate, and define the number of global evolutionary generations. At the platform core, cross-program collaborative logic between MATLAB and FLAC3D was implemented, and a dual-mode computing engine that couples rapid response-surface evaluation with physical forward-model checking was embedded. This mechanism uses a surrogate model to perform inexpensive preliminary fitness screening across a large population of variants. It invokes the FLAC3D kernel only for individuals with high predicted potential, where full mechanical calculations are required; this approach substantially mitigates the computational burden imposed by high-dimensional iterations. To monitor evolutionary progress, the system includes a dynamic mapping window that concurrently displays the trajectory of the best approximation accuracy (R2) and the temporal parameters of the iterative sequence. For high-fidelity verification samples, a built-in data traceability mechanism enables extraction and restoration of complete evolutionary nodes, which supports closed-loop review of algorithm convergence and validation of surrogate mapping fidelity. Analysis of stress regression results

Because regional tectonic evolution and rock-mass heterogeneity are objectively coupled, in-situ stress measurements are inevitably contaminated by discrete background noise. To ensure reliable inverse modeling, strict data-screening criteria were therefore applied before parameter optimization. Samples that showed strong disturbance by fault fracture zones or mechanical distortion in borehole-wall test curves were removed, producing a measurement set with balanced spatial coverage and a clear response background. Based on that filtered dataset, the SADE algorithm was applied to drive global optimization of the regression coefficients. The algorithm employs a Gaussian Process Surrogate to approximate the target functional surface dynamically; this approach speeds population convergence toward high-potential solution domains in early iterations and avoids common numerical bottlenecks of gradient methods, such as strong sensitivity to initial values and entrapment in local extrema.

Figure 13 records the dynamic convergence trajectory of multiple regression. In the early stage of optimization, the coefficient of determination (R2) is driven by the global generalization exploration mechanism of the algorithm, showing a large-span oscillatory climbing response. As the iteration sequence advances, the coefficient of determination (R2) curve rapidly approaches the theoretical extreme value of 1.0 after the 15th iteration and enters a convergent steady state, and finally accurately anchors the global optimal solution (Optimal Point) three times in a row. This dynamic evolution characteristic intuitively confirms the high efficiency of the SADE strategy in crossing local traps and achieving robust convergence when dealing with high-dimensional non-convex inversion. The four core regression weights output by the algorithm are as follows: the self-weight component L1 = 1.211, the X-direction tectonic extrusion component L2 = 0.288, the Y-direction tectonic extrusion component L3 = 0.583, and the horizontal shear component L4 = 0.556. The above numerical solutions directly quantify the occurrence mechanism of the stress state in the field area: the current in-situ stress is based on self-weight consolidation, while the horizontal tectonic additional field shows strong anisotropy. Specifically, the tectonic lateral pressure in the Y-direction (nearly north–south) is significantly stronger than that in the X-direction (nearly east–west), and a non-negligible horizontal shear effect is superimposed. In-situ stress field in the powerhouse area based on optimization algorithms

Substituting the optimized regression coefficients into the forward model yields a three-dimensional reconstruction of the initial in-situ stress field for the study area. Focusing on the underground powerhouse-the project’s core hub-and extracting its spatial stress characteristics shows that the maximum principal stress (σ1) at the powerhouse elevation ranges from 17.3 to 18.1 MPa, the maximum horizontal principal stress (σH) ranges from 15.6 to 16.5 MPa, and the vertical stress (σZ) is approximately 14.4 to 16.4 MPa. The lateral pressure coefficient (K) in this zone varies between 1.0 and 1.1, indicating an overall medium–to–high in-situ stress regime dominated by horizontal tectonic compression. Spatially, the maximum principal stress in the powerhouse area trends northwest–north–south with a gentle dip, and the azimuth of σH remains stable between 153° and 163°. In underground engineering, aligning a deep-buried cavern so its axis crosses the maximum horizontal principal stress trajectory at a small angle reduces tangential stress concentration at the sidewall. The long axis of the main powerhouse in this project is oriented N13°W (i.e., 347°/167°), which makes a relatively small angle with the dominant σH orientation obtained from inversion. This spatial relationship matches the regional principal stress trajectory, supporting the geomechanical soundness of the current powerhouse alignment and enhancing the surrounding rock’s self-stability during excavation.

To characterize how the macroscopic stress field evolves with terrain, numerical solutions were extracted along longitudinal sections of the tunnel and the main powerhouse, and contour maps of each principal stress together with the self-weight stress were plotted (Fig. 17). The contour shapes indicate that surface terrain undulation imposes a boundary effect that produces a markedly nonuniform stress distribution along the tunnel. Across the full cross section, the maximum horizontal principal stress within the buried depth range generally exceeds the background self-weight stress at the same elevation, confirming the dominance of horizontal tectonic compression. Moreover, the gradient patterns in Fig. 17 show that at the bottoms of deeply incised gullies and in terrain-mutation unloading zones the contour lines become closely spaced, with local stress redistribution and pronounced deflection. Discussion

Accurate reconstruction of the initial in-situ stress field in deep underground caverns depends on both the reliability of measurement inputs and the efficiency of inversion. In conventional three-dimensional borehole stress analysis, least-squares estimation is highly sensitive to local anomalies and non-Gaussian measurement errors, which can bias the recovered stress tensor and amplify instability when the governing equations are ill-conditioned. The present study addresses this issue through a robust stress-synthesis strategy that combines particle swarm optimization, the Huber loss, Levenberg–Marquardt iteration, and regularization. Instead of directly discarding suspicious observations, the method adopts an adaptive down-weighting scheme to suppress the influence of gross errors while retaining useful information from the original measurements. At the same time, regularization stabilizes the inversion of ill-conditioned equation systems and reduces spurious oscillations in the solution. This combination improves the fidelity of single-point stress estimates and provides a more reliable data basis for subsequent field-scale stress inversion under complex geological conditions.

A second challenge in in-situ stress inversion is the high computational cost of repeated three-dimensional numerical simulations, especially when the objective function is nonlinear, multimodal, and sensitive to boundary-condition parameters. To improve efficiency without sacrificing inversion quality, this study introduces a surrogate-assisted differential evolution (SADE) framework based on a radial basis function network and an active-learning loop of prediction, verification, and correction. The surrogate model replaces most expensive forward evaluations during the search process, while periodic updates using physically computed samples help control local prediction bias and maintain consistency with the numerical model. The resulting framework preserves the global search capability of differential evolution and performs well for the present deep-underground case. After integrating the optimized measurement data and the SADE inversion workflow, the mean relative error decreases from 14.12 to 9.34%, the deviation variance decreases from 2.98 to 1.33, and the inversion accuracy remains satisfactory with an RMSE of 1.10 MPa. These results indicate that the proposed strategy can balance robustness, computational efficiency, and physical reliability more effectively than conventional inversion workflows.

The engineering implications of this framework extend beyond parameter fitting. A more reliable initial stress field provides a stronger basis for evaluating cavern stability, identifying local stress concentration zones, and guiding support design and risk control in deep underground powerhouse projects. In this sense, robust stress reconstruction is not only a numerical improvement, but also a practical contribution to surrounding rock control in high-stress and geologically complex environments. Nevertheless, the present analysis is still mainly based on linear-elastic assumptions and cannot fully represent strong lithological heterogeneity, progressive damage, or multi-field coupling. Future work should therefore incorporate more advanced constitutive descriptions (e.g., a strain-softening Mohr–Coulomb model and an elastoplastic damage model) and multi-source field feedback, such as microseismic, deformation, and excavation-response monitoring, to support dynamic updating of the in-situ stress field during construction and operation.

Despite the satisfactory performance of the proposed framework, several limitations and sensitivity issues should be acknowledged. In addition, the framework’s overall performance still depends on the representativeness and spatial coverage of the available in-situ measurements, and the present validation is primarily based on a single large-scale case study. First, the forward model adopts a linear-elastic constitutive assumption to enable stress superposition and regression-based inversion. This assumption is appropriate for capturing the macroscopic, far-field elastic response used in boundary-load inversion, but it cannot represent progressive damage, post-peak softening, or strong anisotropy at the local scale. In highly heterogeneous rock masses, these neglected nonlinearities may bias the inverted stress magnitudes locally, particularly near faults and fractured zones. Second, the efficiency and accuracy of SADE depend on several hyperparameters, including population size, mutation/crossover settings, surrogate-update frequency, and the number of verified infill samples per generation. To reduce sensitivity, repeated runs with different random seeds are recommended, and the surrogate update can be triggered adaptively when the prediction error exceeds a threshold. Nevertheless, the current linear-elastic forward model is adopted to target the macroscopic far-field stress reconstruction required for engineering decision-making, and it provides a consistent baseline for boundary-load regression at the project scale. Moreover, the surrogate-assisted search is continuously anchored by high-fidelity FLAC3D verification in the prediction–verification–correction loop, which helps control surrogate bias and improves the practical reliability of the inverted stress field.

Conclusions

To address the engineering challenges associated with complex initial stress environments, highly scattered measurements, and the high computational cost of three-dimensional inversion in a large, deeply buried underground powerhouse, this study integrates three-dimensional in-situ stress testing, robust optimization of measurement data, and surrogate-assisted intelligent inversion to establish a refined framework for initial stress-field reconstruction and assessment. This approach adaptively suppresses anomalous strain components associated with local fracturing or operational disturbance while retaining useful information from the original observations. The reconstructed stress tensors better reflect the regional tectonic stress regime in both magnitude and orientation, and they reduce the risk of numerical divergence caused by ill-conditioned inverse equations.
