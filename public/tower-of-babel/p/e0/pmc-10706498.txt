Anemia burden in pregnancy and birth outcomes among women receiving antenatal care services from a secondary level hospital in South India: A record review
Authors: Bhoomika Biradar, Sakthi Arasu, Naveen Ramesh
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroduction:Anaemia in pregnant women is a major public health problem and is associated with adverse outcomes both in pregnant mothers and new-borns. According to NFHS-5, 45.7% of women in urban India were affected by anaemia during their pregnancy. The objectives of this study were to estimate the proportion of pregnant women who were anaemic and its effect on maternal and birth outcomes, and additionally, to assess the various socio-economic factors contributing to anaemia during pregnancy.Methodology:Data was collected by reviewing records between December 2018 and December 2021 of 302 pregnant women who had received antenatal care at a secondary level health facility in Krishnagiri, Tamil Nadu. The data included details of socio-economic parameters, parity, haemoglobin levels, mode of delivery, pregnancy related complications during the antenatal period and during delivery, including need for blood transfusions in mothers. Birth-related outcomes like miscarriages, intra-uterine death, low birth weight (LBW), need for NICU admission and congenital defects were also analysed. Variables in the study are described as frequencies and proportions. Appropriate tests were used to check for association and a P- value of less than 0.05 was considered as statistically significant.Results:The mean age of the women was 24 ± 3.72 years and the mean Haemoglobin level during the first trimester was 10.5 ± 1.12 gm/dl. The proportion of women with anaemia in the first trimester was 174 (57.6%). Among the anaemics, 15 (8.6%) required treatment in the form of injectable iron and 29 (16.7%) required blood transfusion. Gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) (31.6%) (P < 0.05), preeclampsia (38.9%) (P < 0.02) less among anaemic. Hyperemesis (66.7%) (P = 0.58), risk of miscarriage (57.8%) (P = 0.94), postpartum haemorrhage (PPH) (80.0%) (P = 0.15) was higher in the anaemic group. Adverse Neonatal-related outcomes such as low birth weight babies (52.0%) (P = 0.54), meconium aspiration (72.7%) (P = 0.25) and need for NICU admission (75.0%) (P = 0.25) was higher in babies born to anaemic pregnant women.Conclusions:The proportion of anaemic women was 57.6%. There was no significant difference in maternal demographic characteristics or obstetric factors between anaemic and non-anaemic pregnant women. GDM, pre-eclampsia was less prevalent among anaemic women. Hyperemesis, risk of abortion, postpartum haemorrhage and low birth weight (LBW) infants was higher in anaemic pregnancies. The need for providing education regarding iron supplementation and early detection of anaemia during pregnancy should be emphasised to avoid complications.

Introduction

Anaemia is a condition in which the number of red blood cells (and consequently their oxygen-carrying capacity) is insufficient to meet the body’s physiological needs. In healthy, iron-sufficient pregnant women, haemoglobin concentrations change during pregnancy to accommodate the increasing maternal blood volume and increasing needs of the foetus.[1]

Anaemia among pregnant women is a major public health problem in India. This is reflected in the NFHS-5 report (2019-2020), where the prevalence of anaemia during pregnancy in India ranges from 45.7% in urban areas and 54.3% in rural areas in India. According to the report, 42.6% of pregnant women residing in urban areas of Tamil Nadu are diagnosed with anaemia.[2] This number is considerably higher than the overall prevalence of anaemia in pregnant women worldwide - 36.5%, according to WHO Global Anaemia Estimates, 2021 Edition.

Anaemia during pregnancy can have serious adverse outcomes both on the mother and the newborn. Anaemia has been found to be a significant contributor to adverse pregnancy outcomes such as preterm birth, low birth weight (LBW), small for gestational age, preterm delivery, instrumental delivery and increased foetal mortality.[3-7]

Maternal factors responsible for anaemia during pregnancy include factors such as gestational age, parity, consecutive pregnancy interval, history of excess bleeding during menstruation, intestinal parasitic infection, malaria, chronic illness, and blood loss during pregnancy.[8] In rural areas, this issue is exacerbated due to factors including illiteracy and inadequate dissemination of knowledge combined with limited access to healthcare.[9]

This study was therefore conducted with the aim of estimating the proportion of anaemic women among those who received antenatal care in our hospital and its effect on maternal and foetal outcomes. Our secondary objective aimed to assess the various socio-economic factors contributing to anaemia during pregnancy.

Methodology

This study was based on the review of the records of women availing antenatal care at a secondary level healthcare private facility located in Krishnagiri, Tamil Nadu between December 2018 and December 2021. This number accounted for 302 following exclusions of 22 participants who did not meet the inclusion criteria.

The inclusion criteria - pregnant women (18-49 years of age) who followed up regularly with a minimum of 6 antenatal check-ups at the above-mentioned health care facility.

Exclusion criteria - women who were at high risk of pre-eclampsia, foetal growth restriction or spontaneous preterm birth due to underlying medical conditions- chronic hypertension, diabetes, renal disease, systemic lupus erythematosus, anti-phospholipid syndrome, sickle cell disease, retroviral infection, women with a major uterine anomaly, a known major foetal anomaly or abnormal karyotype and women with twin pregnancies.

Prior research indicated that the best time to investigate risk factors associated with anaemia may be up until 20 weeks of gestation.[10] Thus, in this study, we took the haemoglobin level estimated during the 1st trimester (prior to 16 weeks of gestation) to determine factors associated with anaemia in pregnant women. We also analysed the 3rd trimester’s (post 36 weeks of gestation) haemoglobin level to assess anaemia. Additionally, post-delivery haemoglobin was observed to account for factors, such as post-partum haemorrhage contributing to maternal outcomes. Anaemia was further classified according to severity- mild anaemia (Hb 10-10.9 g/dl), moderate anaemia (Hb 7.0-9.9 g/dl) and severe anaemia (Hb <7.0 g/dl), and post-partum anaemia is defined as Haemoglobin concentration <12 gm/dl.[11]

Additionally, associated factors, including age, parity and birth spacing were analysed to further help to evaluate maternal and foetal outcomes among anaemics. Outcomes such as the presence of maternal complications (Pre-eclampsia, GDM, Hypothyroidism, maternal bleeding, Hyperemesis requiring hospitalisation, need for instrumental delivery and PPH) and foetal complications (Miscarriages, LBW, NICU admission and congenital anomalies) in the anaemic and non-anaemic group were studied.

The data were entered in Microsoft Excel and analysed using IBM Statistical Package for Social Sciences version (SPSS) V20.0. The outcome variables were described as frequencies and proportions. Chi-Square test and Fisher’s exact test of significance were used to look for an association between each of the outcome variables and anaemia and a P- value of < 0.05 was considered as statistically significant.

Results

A total of 302 participants who met the inclusion criteria were included in this study. The mean age of the study population was 24 ± 3.72 years. Among the study participants, 174 (57.6%) were anaemic and 128 (42.4%) were non-anaemic, according to Haemoglobin levels during the first trimester.

Sociodemographic factors

Most participants belonged to the 20-30 years age group (84.1%), followed by the under 20 age group and above 30 age group (7.9% each). Nearly half (45.7%) of the participants had attained at least an undergraduate level of education. Regarding the factors, we did not find any significant association between maternal characteristics (age, educational status) or other obstetric factors (gravida, parity, abortions, birth spacing practices) and anaemic women [Table 1].

Trends in Haemoglobin level concentration

Among the total number of participants, 174 (57.6%) of the antenatal women were anaemic and 128 (42.3%) were non-anaemic. The mean haemoglobin in first trimester in the anaemic group was 10.5 ± 1.12 gm/dl, which slightly increased to 10.9 ± 0.86 gm/dl by the third trimester. In the non-anaemic group, the mean haemoglobin in the first and third trimesters was 11.44 ± 0.39 gm/dl and 11.3 ± 0.77 gm/dl respectively. Post-delivery, the Haemoglobin level fell to 9.9 ± 0.82 gm/dl in the anaemic group, and to 10.17 ± 0.76 gm/dl in the non-anaemic group. Maternal complications

Table 4 shows the complications that developed during pregnancy among the anaemic and non-anaemic groups. We found the prevalence of Gestational Diabetes Mellitus (GDM) was lower in anaemic antenatal women 6 (31.6%) when compared to the non-anaemic antenatal women 13 (68.4%) and this association was found to be significant (P < 0.05). Pre-eclampsia in pregnant women with anaemia 14 (38.9%) was significantly lower than the non-anaemic participants 22 (61.1%), (P < 0.02). Hyperemesis (66.7% in the anaemic group compared to 33.3% in the non-anaemic group) and postpartum haemorrhage (80.0% in the anaemic group compared to 20.0% in the non-anaemic group) was higher in antenatal women who were anaemic, however, these associations were not statistically significant [Tables 4 and 5].

Among the 174 anaemic women, nine developed bleeding complications during pregnancy. Discussion

Anaemia during pregnancy is an important health concern for health authorities in India. The causes of anaemia include iron deficiency, malnutrition, multiple pregnancies, increased blood loss during menstruation, parasitic infections, malaria, chronic infections and misconceptions regarding iron supplements provided during pregnancy.[9]

According to the National Family Health Survery-4 (NFHS-4), the prevalence of anaemia among pregnant women stands at 50%. The Ministry of Health and Family Welfare has recognised this is a public health concern and has launched health programmes such as the Intensified National Iron Plus Initiative (I-NIPI) and Janani-Shishu Suraksha Karyakram (JSSK) to strengthen the service delivery to antenatal women with anaemia in India. I-NIPI adopts a multi-pronged approach to reduce the burden of anaemia including the identification of anaemic mothers, provision of iron supplementation with regular follow-ups and early escalation to specialised centres when needed. The initiative also pays attention to non-nutritional causes of anaemia with a focused effort to identify malarial infections and haemoglobinopathies. The JSSK initiative mitigates the financial burden on pregnant women by providing high-quality antenatal and postnatal healthcare services free of cost. Beneficiaries are provided diagnostics, drugs, delivery including caesarean section, and transport to health institutions at zero cost. However, despite years of medical intervention by these health programmes, the prevalence of anaemia among Indian women, especially pregnant women continues to be high.[12,13]

In this present study, we reviewed 302 antenatal records and found 57.6% of the women receiving care at the secondary level health care facility located in Tamil Nadu had anaemia. This value was higher than the NFHS 5 data of Tamil Nadu, with an average of 42.6%.[3]

Looking at the severity of anaemia, we found that the majority of the women had mild anaemia (55.7%), followed by moderate anaemia (43.7%) and severe anaemia (0.6%). More than half (58.3%) of the anaemic women belonged to the <20 years age group. This is comparable to the study conducted in China which found a high prevalence among women less than 20 years and in India where anaemia was more prevalent among those less than 25 years.[14,15] In this study, socio-demographic characteristics were not significantly associated with anaemia in pregnancy. This was similar to a study conducted by Vindhya J et al. which found that there was no significant association between educational status and anaemia.[16] However, studies conducted in northeast India, Madhya Pradesh and Aurangabad found a significant association between lower literacy and the prevalence of anaemia. Although the educational and socioeconomic status of women in south Indian states is better than in other parts of India, anaemia during pregnancy continues to pose a threat in these states.[15,17,18]

Also, in this study, we did not find a significant association between obstetric factors such as gravida, parity, and abortion status with maternal anaemia. However, in other studies, it was seen that lower socio-economic status, unplanned pregnancy, abortions, higher gravidity and higher parity were significantly associated with anaemia in pregnancy.[18-21]

Regarding the Haemoglobin level, the mean value in the first trimester was 10.5 ± 1.12 gm/dl which increased in the third trimester to 10.9 ± 0.86 gm/dl. This is reflected in the fact that, of the 277 participants who delivered live birth, the proportion of anaemia decreased from 57.6% in the 1st trimester to 47.0% in the third trimester. This could be due to routine antenatal care alongside counselling on dietary changes and oral iron supplementation as per National Programmes initiated by the National Health Mission, Government of India.[22,23] Further education and counselling of pregnant women on the importance of the consumption of iron supplements during antenatal check-ups will help increase compliance.

The mean Haemoglobin decreased post-delivery to 9.9 ± 0.86 gm/dl, and in the 277 participants who delivered live births, the proportion of anaemic women drastically increased to 90.6% in comparison to 57.8% in the 1st trimester. Immediate postpartum haemoglobin values in this study are similar to the findings of a study conducted by Selvaraj et al. and Rakesh et al.[24,25] Thus considerable attention has to be paid to women during labour and following delivery, with an emphasis on regular haemoglobin checks along with the prescription of iron supplements and dietary diversification to prevent further deterioration of the degree of anaemia.

We also studied the need for iron injections in anaemic mothers and found that the frequency was highest in moderate anaemia (86.7%), followed by mild and severe anaemia (6.7% each). With regards to the need for blood transfusions the frequency was highest in moderate anaemia (70.0%), mild anaemia (27.6%) and severe anaemia (3.4%). This could explain the fact, that despite receiving oral iron prophylaxis, a proportion of antenatal women who were anaemic required further interventions to prevent complications such as an obstetric emergency. A reason for this could be due to poor compliance to oral iron supplements, such as gastrointestinal side effects, inadequate counselling regarding the need for iron tablets, patient’s fears and forgetfulness. Hence it is crucial to educate women regarding the importance of iron tablets during pregnancy and to regularly monitor haemoglobin levels, especially among anaemic women.[26]

In our study, we found that the proportion of women with GDM were lower in anaemic group compared to non-anaemic one and this was statistically significant. This was also consistent with other studies conducted by Lao et al.[27] This could be due to a nutritional deficit and decreased weight gain in mothers who are anaemic.[28] A study by Suryanarayana found an association between maternal anaemia and pre-eclampsia.[19] Conversely, in this study, we found that anaemic mothers were at a significantly lesser risk of developing pre-eclampsia as compared to non-anaemic mothers. These findings were similar to a study conducted in Karnataka.[29] A possible explanation for this could be due to the mechanism of vasoconstriction caused by the release of reactive oxygen species in women with increased haemoglobin concentrations.[30] However further studies are required to understand this phenomenon and provide clinical correlation. Apart from this, we did not find significant associations between anaemia and other maternal complications developed during pregnancy which was similar to a study done by Kant et al. (2022).[31]

While adverse neonatal outcomes such as miscarriages, low birth weight and meconium aspiration were higher in newborns of anaemic maternal women compared to newborns of non-anaemic mothers, this association was not statistically significant. These findings were similar to a previous study conducted by Rahman et al. (2016).[4] Although few studies found that anaemia contributes to adverse pregnancy outcomes such as preterm birth, and LBW, our study did not find any such conclusive evidence.[7]

Thus, considerable attention has to be paid to Maternal and child health care (MCH) services, which significantly help in reducing adverse outcomes related to pregnancy and childbirth. The Indian Government has established services for MCH under the Reproductive, Maternal, New-born, Child and Adolescent Health (RMNCH+A), a framework which aims to provide comprehensive care from preconception to the postnatal period, as well as new-born and child healthcare. In order to reduce maternal mortality, the state of Tamil Nadu has developed various strategies to deliver high impact interventions to improve health seeking behaviour. One such strategy is the employment and training of various frontline workers (ASHAs, ANMs) to provide support and increased accessibility to public health services which is oftentimes delivered to the mother’s doorstep. Services include increased accessibility to healthcare services by way of timely antenatal care, free diagnostics, provision of medications, alongside monetary incentives to promote delivery at public health institutions. Other services include nutritional benefits such as providing free food during antenatal clinics during ANC clinics and during the postnatal stay at the hospital.[23,32] In the district where the study was conducted, a notable Health care scheme includes the Dr Muthulakshmi Maternity Benefit Scheme, which provides financial assistance and nutritional kits to pregnant and lactating mothers belonging to economically weaker sections.[33]

Limitations

Our study faced a few limitations. As this was a retrospective case record- based study, there was a lack of information related to factors that could contribute to the haemoglobin status of the woman prior to pregnancy. This included determinants such as nutritional habits, pre-pregnancy menstrual blood loss and parasitic infections. Additionally, we were unable to follow up on women discharged after delivery, as it is a cultural trend in India for mothers who have given birth to return to their maternal homes during their period of recovery.

Conclusion

More than half of the registered pregnant women were anaemic in their first trimester. Anaemic pregnancies are associated with a lower risk of developing GDM and Pre-eclampsia. There were no other significant associations between maternal and neonatal complications with anaemia in pregnancy found in this study. In addition to iron prophylaxis, further intervention in the form of blood transfusion or injectable iron supplements is often required in pregnant women with anaemia detected during the first trimester. Our study highlights the importance of regular Antenatal care and provision of iron supplementation with health education, as well as the timely detection of maternal anaemia and additional interventions to correct deficiencies in anaemic mothers, in order to prevent both maternal and neonatal complications.
