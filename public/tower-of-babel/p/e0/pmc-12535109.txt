Lactate dehydrogenase a is a crucial biomarker that affects the prognosis, chemotherapy effect, and immune infiltration of breast cancer
Authors: Jing Qi Xia, Yi Lin Wang, Xue Lian Li, Jia Nan Wang, Fu Cheng Li, Si Yuan Jia, Yu Ling Ba, Dan Li Luo, Tian Gao, Zhao Ting Li, Min Xiao, He Dou
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
PurposeLactate dehydrogenase A (LDHA) is a key node in tumor growth, metabolism, and invasion and is upregulated across multiple cancers. However, the molecular mechanisms by which LDHA influences breast cancer (BC) remain unclear. We analyzed public datasets and an institutional cohort to clarify the relationship between LDHA and BC, with the aim of informing future therapeutic strategies. Patients and methods Using The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA), we assessed LDHA expression in BC and examined its associations with tumor mutational burden (TMB), immune cell infiltration, immune checkpoint molecules, and drug sensitivity. We integrated multiple databases and used Kaplan–Meier analyses to evaluate prognostic value. To explore biological functions of LDHA, we performed Gene Ontology (GO), Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG), and Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA). We then analyzed BC patients receiving neoadjuvant chemotherapy (NAC) at Harbin Medical University Cancer Hospital to test the relationship between serum lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) and pathological complete response (pCR). Finally, we used National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) data to examine the association between serum LDH and mortality. Results LDHA was upregulated in BC tissues, and higher expression was significantly associated with worse overall survival (OS), recurrence-free survival (RFS), and distant metastasis-free survival (DMFS). Functional analyses indicated enrichment of metabolic pathways, such as glycolysis. LDHA expression correlated with multiple immune cell populations, suggesting involvement in the tumor immune microenvironment. Lower LDHA expression was associated with greater sensitivity to several chemotherapeutic agents. In our institutional cohort, patients with lower serum LDH were more likely to achieve pCR, and LDH was an independent predictor of pCR. In NHANES, elevated serum LDH was linked to increased mortality risk. Conclusion Our findings suggest that LDHA expression and serum LDH levels are promising prognostic biomarkers for survival and may predict chemotherapy response in BC patients. These results highlight the clinical relevance of LDHA-mediated metabolic pathways. However, as a correlational study, our findings warrant further validation through functional experiments to confirm LDHA's role as a potential therapeutic target.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12905-025-04039-w.

Introduction

It is estimated that the new breast cancer (BC) cases in 2022 will account for 11.6% of all female patients with malignant tumors. BC remains one of the leading causes of mortality in females, representing 6.9% of global female cancer deaths [1, 2]. In China, BC also ranks as a high-incidence disease among malignant tumors affecting women. Compared to Western countries, the situation regarding BC in Chinese women is deteriorating at an accelerated pace, resulting in increased susceptibility to metastasis and recurrence while heightening the likelihood of various adverse survival outcomes [3]. There is still a significant gap in the overall survival (OS) rate of BC in China compared to developed countries. Currently, treatment options for BC are becoming increasingly diverse; however, each treatment modality must be standardized and integrated [4]. To effectively address this pressing health issue, we advocate for enhancing early screening rates and improving treatment standards to mitigate the impact of BC on women’s health [5].

BC is a heterogeneous disease with diverse biological and clinical features. It arises from abnormal proliferation of ductal or lobular epithelial cells, leading to uncontrolled growth and potential metastasis [6]. As disease advances, tissue and circulating biomarkers change; identifying robust biomarkers helps assess disease burden and metastatic risk and can inform treatment [7].

Lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) has been utilized as a predictive biomarker for treatment efficacy in various cancers, patient recurrence, and survival outcomes [8, 9]. LDH is a nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NAD)-dependent enzyme that is a tetrameric molecule with a molecular weight of approximately 140 kDa. It comprises three subunits: A, B, and C, resulting in six tetrapolymorphic isoforms in the human body. While predominantly located in the cytoplasm, LDH has also been identified in the mitochondria and nucleus [10].

The Lactate dehydrogenase A (LDHA) isoform comprises 332 amino acids and exhibits a homotetrameric structure formed by four M subunits; each subunit contains a catalytic active site and a coenzyme binding site [11, 12]. As an essential enzyme for energy production across various tissues and cell types, the metabolic processes of LDHA are markedly distinct from those in healthy cells, with this divergence closely associated with cancer metastasis and patient survival rates [13]. In normal human tissues, most adenosine triphosphate (ATP) is generated through mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation, while only a tiny fraction is produced via glycolysis. Under hypoxic conditions, aerobic metabolism temporarily shifts to anaerobic metabolism, converting pyruvate into lactic acid. One notable characteristic of tumor cells compared to normal cells is the alteration in carbohydrate metabolism. Tumor cells often rely on glycolysis to generate ATP even in the presence of oxygen (the Warburg effect) [14, 15]. LDHA serves as a critical enzyme in this process by converting pyruvate into lactic acid with the assistance of NAD [16–18].

Furthermore, within hypoxic environments, metabolic activity among surrounding normal cells diminishes, and their metabolites may change, indirectly allowing tumor cells to grow. Hypoxia also represents a significant factor influencing the efficacy of tumor treatments [19]. Previous studies have demonstrated that LDHA is upregulated in cancer cells, whereas the expression level of lactate dehydrogenase B (LDHB) remains relatively unchanged in both cancerous and normal tissues. Given the unique characteristics of LDHA, it has emerged as a significant target for pharmacological intervention. Experts have identified that targeting LDHA can impede tumor progression and metastasis; however, the underlying molecular mechanisms remain highly contentious [20].

In this study, we analyzed The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) data to characterize LDHA expression in BC and its associations with clinicopathologic features and prognosis. We identified LDHA-related biological processes and pathways using Gene Ontology (GO), Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG), and Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA). Recognizing the growing roles of immunotherapy and neoadjuvant chemotherapy (NAC) in BC, we integrated public datasets with an institutional NAC cohort from Harbin Medical University Cancer Hospital to evaluate the relationship between baseline serum LDH and pathological complete response (pCR), and to explore LDHA’s links with the tumor immune microenvironment and drug sensitivity—aiming to inform future BC treatment strategies.

Material and methods

Collection of genetic data

Clinical-pathological annotations and gene-expression data were obtained from TCGA (https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov/). We analyzed 1,053 BC tumors and 112 normal breast samples, focusing on LDHA as the primary gene of interest.

Collection of clinical samples

We compiled clinicopathological data for 529 BC patients who received NAC at Harbin Medical University Cancer Hospital between January 1, 2013 and December 31, 2019. All patients completed the planned NAC cycles and subsequently underwent surgery at our institution. Before treatment, study procedures and use of clinical information and biospecimens were explained to patients and, when appropriate, their families. Written informed consent for secondary use of de-identified records and biospecimens was obtained from each participant. The study adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki (2013 revision).

Treatment regimens

Based on immunohistochemistry (IHC) profiles and patient preference, all patients received taxane- and/or anthracycline-based NAC. Regimens included: AC-T (pirarubicin 60 mg/m², cyclophosphamide 600 mg/m², docetaxel 90 mg/m²); AC-TH (pirarubicin 60 mg/m², cyclophosphamide 600 mg/m², docetaxel 75 mg/m², plus trastuzumab with a loading dose of 8 mg/kg followed by 6 mg/kg every 3 weeks); AC-THP (pirarubicin 60 mg/m², cyclophosphamide 600 mg/m², docetaxel 75 mg/m², plus trastuzumab [8 mg/kg loading, then 6 mg/kg] and pertuzumab [840 mg loading, then 420 mg]); TAC (docetaxel 75 mg/m², pirarubicin 50 mg/m², cyclophosphamide 500 mg/m²); EC (epirubicin 100 mg/m², cyclophosphamide 600 mg/m²); and TEC (docetaxel 80–100 mg/m², epirubicin 90–100 mg/m², cyclophosphamide 600 mg/m²). The number of cycles (4, 6, or 8) was determined according to international treatment standards and individual pathological features.

Follow-up and study endpoints

Postoperative follow-up occurred every 3 months for the first 2 years, every 6 months for the next 3 years, and annually thereafter, until June 2024 or death from any cause, pCR was defined as ypT0/is ypN0 (residual ductal carcinoma in situ permitted). OS was measured from surgery to death from any cause or last contact. Recurrence-free survival (RFS) was measured from surgery to first recurrence (local, regional, or distant) or last follow-up in recurrence-free patients.

Expression of LDHA

We assessed pan-cancer LDHA expression using the Tumor Immune Estimation Resource (TIMER) 2.0 (http://timer.comp-genomics.org/timer/) and Gene Expression Profiling Interactive Analysis (GEPIA). LDHA expression in BC tumors versus normal tissues was compared using TCGA and Genotype-Tissue Expression (GTEx) (https://www.gtexportal.org/home/), including paired tumor–normal analyses where available.

Association of LDHA with clinicopathological features and prognosis

Obtain the clinicopathological features of patients from the TCGA database, including age, race, estrogen receptor (ER), progesterone receptor (PR), human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2) status, T stage, N stage, M stage, and pathological stage. Employ the chi-square test to assess the correlation between LDHA expression and these features. To mitigate selection bias, three databases—namely the European Genome Phenome Archive (EGA), Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO), and TCGA—were utilized to compare the relationship between varying levels of LDHA expression and prognosis in BC patients. Additionally, survival curves were generated for further analysis.

Co expression analysis of LDHA

LDHA-associated genes were identified in TCGA by correlation analysis. We displayed the top five positively and top five negatively correlated genes as circle plots. Protein-protein interaction (PPI) networks were constructed using Search Tool for the Retrieval of Interacting Genes STRING (https://cn.string-db.org/). We queried the top 50 LDHA-interacting genes in STRING, prioritized the top 10, and visualized relationships in Cytoscape (v3.10).

Differential genes and enrichment analysis between high and low expression groups of LDHA

Differential genes and enrichment analysis between high and low expression groups of LDHA

GO enrichment analysis focuses on the functional annotation of genes across three distinct levels: molecular function, cellular components, and biological processes. This methodology aids in elucidating the specific roles and biological functions of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) within cellular contexts. The KEGG database provides comprehensive information regarding metabolic pathways; thus, KEGG enrichment analysis can identify which metabolic pathways are associated with DEGs, enhancing our understanding of their regulatory mechanisms and metabolic pathways within organisms. Although GSEA does not directly perform functional annotation or pathway analysis, it can indirectly infer alterations in biological processes or pathways represented by these gene sets under experimental conditions through enrichment analysis. This study stratified BC patients from the TCGA database into high and low-expression groups. The Limma package in R was employed to assess genetic differences between these two groups, followed by heatmaps to evaluate the top 50 genes. Simultaneously, functional and pathway analyses were performed on DEGs utilizing the ClusterProfiler package for GO enrichment and KEGG enrichment analyses. Additionally, GSEA was conducted based on the results obtained from these analyses.

TMB analysis of LDHA

Tumor mutational burden (TMB) was derived from TCGA mutation data and analyzed using maftools to assess its correlation with LDHA expression.

Immune infiltration analysis of LDHA

We used the Cibersort package in R to estimate proportions of tumor-infiltrating immune cells and compared immune-cell fractions between high- and low-LDHA groups. We further analyzed LDHA correlations with immune-checkpoint genes. Immunophenoscores for BC patients were downloaded from The Cancer Immunome Atlas (TCIA) (https://tcia.at/home) and compared between LDHA groups using t-tests.

Drug sensitivity analysis of LDHA

We applied the OncoPredict package in R to estimate chemotherapy sensitivity and compared half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) values between high- and low-LDHA expression groups.

Retrospective analysis of clinical samples in our center

The data collected from BC patients at our hospital were stratified according to serum LDH levels to investigate the relationship between these levels and pCR. Categorical data are presented as counts and percentages, with Chi-square tests employed for comparison and analysis. The correlation between clinical pathological features and pCR within each subgroup was assessed using Chi-square tests and univariate logistic regression analysis. Significant variables identified through univariate analysis were subsequently included in multivariate analysis to determine which factors influence pCR in BC patients.

Retrospective analysis of clinical samples in NHANES

The National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES), conducted by the National Center for Health Statistics under the auspices of the United States Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, represents a pivotal epidemiological study. Utilizing patient data sourced from NHANES, we rigorously validated the correlation between serum LDH levels and survival outcomes. Mortality data for our analysis were obtained from the NHANES-National Death Index. Survival curves were generated using the Kaplan-Meier method, while log-rank tests assessed survival differences among groups. The Cox proportional hazards model calculated multivariate-adjusted hazard ratios along with 95% confidence intervals (CI). P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Statistical analysis

All analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism (version 9.0), R software (version 4.1.2), and SPSS (version 27.0). A t-test was employed to evaluate differences between groups. The Chi-square test was utilized to compare clinical case parameters, while the Kaplan-Meier curve and Log-rank test were applied for survival analysis. A significance level of P < 0.05 indicated a statistically significant difference for all statistical tests.

The correlation between LDHA expression and clinicopathological features

To further explore the relationship between LDHA expression and clinicopathological characteristics in BC patients, we conducted an analysis using the TCGA database. Our results revealed that LDHA expression was significantly associated with ER status, PR status, HER2 status, and T stage (all P < 0.05) (Fig. 3A-I). Conversely, LDHA expression did not show a significant correlation with age, race, N stage, or M stage (all P > 0.05). Specifically, LDHA levels were elevated in patients with negative ER or PR status, or positive HER2 status. Furthermore, patients classified as T4 stage exhibited significantly higher LDHA expression compared to those in T stages T1-T3. Regarding racial groups, LDHA expression was observed to be highest in Asian populations, followed by White individuals, and lowest in Black individuals. Additionally, LDHA expression was slightly higher in patients with distant metastasis (M1) compared to those without metastasis (M0). High expression of LDHA leads to poor prognosis

To assess the impact of LDHA on the prognosis of BC patients, Kaplan-Meier survival curves were generated. To enhance the robustness of our findings, we integrated data from the GEO, EGA, and TCGA databases, encompassing gene expression and clinical information from over 30,000 samples across 21 tumor types. In our comprehensive analysis of BC patients, high LDHA expression was significantly associated with poorer OS, RFS, and distant metastasis free survival (DMFS) (all P < 0.05) (Fig. 4A-C). Specifically, patients in the high LDHA expression group exhibited significantly lower OS, RFS, and DMFS compared to those with low LDHA levels. Further stratification revealed that in HER2-positive patients, higher LDHA expression was strongly linked to diminished RFS (P < 0.05) (Fig. 4E). Although significant differences were not observed for OS or DMFS in this subgroup (P = 0.086 and P = 0.16, respectively), a clear trend of poorer prognosis was evident in patients with high LDHA expression compared to their low-expression counterparts (Fig. 4D, F). Similar prognostic trends were also recapitulated in TNBC patients (Fig. 4G-I). Enrichment analysis of LDHA

To elucidate the role of LDHA, we stratified patients into high and low-expression groups based on the median level of LDHA expression and identified the DEGs between these two cohorts. The results of the DEGs are visualized in a heatmap (Supplementary Fig. 2). A total of 856 DEGs were subjected to GO and KEGG enrichment analyses. The GO enrichment analysis revealed that these DEGs were significantly associated with humoral immune response, extracellular matrix structural constituents, and endopeptidase activity (Fig. 6A). Furthermore, KEGG enrichment analysis indicated their involvement in pathways such as neuroactive ligand-receptor interaction, cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction, and the phosphatidylinositol 3-kinases (PI3K)/Akt signaling pathway (Fig. 6B). GSEA demonstrated that gene sets related to olfactory receptor activity were significantly enriched in the high-LDHA expression group. These findings imply that LDHA may play a pivotal role in anaerobic glycolysis, tumor energy production, and metabolism, and it is likely to contribute significantly to chemotherapy resistance. TMB, which refers to the total number of somatic mutations per megabase of DNA, reflects genomic instability and is an important biomarker for predicting response to immunotherapy. LDHA affects the efficacy of immunotherapy

We initially investigated the correlation between LDHA and immune checkpoints (Supplementary Fig. 3 A). Subsequently, we utilized the TCIA database to assess whether LDHA expression influences the efficacy of immunotherapy in BC patients, focusing on Programmed death-1 (PD-1) and Cytotoxic T lymphocyte-Associated Protein-4 (CTLA-4) as two classic immune checkpoints for our analysis. Our findings indicated that the immune phenotypic scores for PD-1 positivity and CTLA-4 positivity were significantly higher in the low-expression group of LDHA. This suggests that patients with low LDHA expression may exhibit increased sensitivity to PD-1 and CTLA-4 immunotherapy (P < 0.05) (Supplementary Fig. 3B-E).

Correlation between LDHA expression and drug sensitivity

We subsequently analyzed the relationship between LDHA expression and drug sensitivity, discovering that the sensitivity to various drugs differed significantly between high and low LDHA expression levels (P < 0.05). We then selected several classic drugs for BC treatment; notably, a lower half maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) value indicated greater sensitivity to the agent.

Afuresertib is an ATP-competitive inhibitor with considerable significance in treating TNBC (Fig. 8A). Alpelisib functions as a PI3K inhibitor, effectively inhibiting the activity of Phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase catalytic subunit alpha (PIK3CA), making it suitable for treating HR (+)/HER2 (-) and PIK3CA-mutated advanced BC patients (Fig. 8B). Nilotinib is a novel oral tyrosine kinase inhibitor with selective efficacy (Fig. 8C). Ribociclib is a CDK4/6 inhibitor indicated for HR+/HER2- advanced BC; adjuvant use in selected stage II-III HR+/HER2- settings has also been supported (Fig. 8D). Additionally, postoperative BC patients may benefit from zoledronic acid as adjuvant therapy, which can help mitigate the risk of recurrence and metastasis following surgery for BC (Fig. 8E). Status of BC patients included in this cohort

From January 1, 2013, to December 31, 2019, 529 patients diagnosed with BC were studied at Harbin Medical University Cancer Hospital; 134 patients were excluded (66 patients without blood tests, 36 patients without IHC, 15 patients without completed treatment, 6 patients with occult BC, 11 patients with other malignant tumors). A total of 395 were included in the study (Supplementary Fig. S4).

The cut-off value of LDH in this cohort

This study measured serum LDH levels in BC patients before NAC, mid-NAC, and before surgery. The cut-off value of serum LDH level was analyzed using the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve. The area under the curve (AUC) and the 95% CI for serum LDH levels prior to NAC were found to be 0.578 (95% CI: 0.512–0.644, P = 0.032), with an optimal cut-off value of 215.5 U/L. In the mid-NAC group, the AUC and corresponding 95% CI for serum LDH levels were recorded at 0.576 (95% CI: 0.502–0.650, P = 0.036), with an optimal cut-off value of 187.5 U/L. For the preoperative group, the AUC and its associated 95% CI for serum LDH levels were determined to be 0.581 (95% CI: 0.512–0.650, P = 0.025), with an optimal cut-off value of 273.5 U/L (Supplementary Fig. S5).

Characteristics of the research sample in our center

Before receiving NAC, 340 patients (84.0%) exhibited serum LDH levels below the threshold of 215.5 U/L. Following the completion of half the chemotherapy cycle, there was a gradual increase in patients presenting with elevated serum LDH levels. During routine preoperative examinations after completing the entire course of chemotherapy, it was observed that 52 BC patients (13.1%) had serum LDH levels exceeding the cutoff value of 273.5 U/L. Significant differences in serum LDH levels across various ages, menopausal statuses, BMIs, and clinical N stages were observed, with statistical significance (P < 0.05). In this cohort study, elderly patients demonstrated higher serum LDH levels; however, this trend did not reach statistical significance before chemotherapy initiation. Regardless of NAC timing, menopausal women consistently exhibited higher serum LDH levels compared to their non-menopausal counterparts. Furthermore, when BMI values for BC patients ranged between 24 and 30 kg/m², their corresponding serum LDH levels were elevated relative to those observed in other female subjects.

Association of clinical factors with pCR in the whole group

In the entire cohort, 79 (20.0%) BC patients achieved pCR, while 316 (80.0%) did not. Univariate analysis identified several factors influencing BC patients’ pCR rate following NAC. Notable correlations were found between ER status, PR status, HER2 status, KI67 expression, molecular subtype classification, and serum LDH levels—measured pre-NAC, mid-NAC, and preoperatively—with the pCR rate (P < 0.05) (Table 1). Conversely, no significant associations were observed between age, menopausal status, BMI, clinical T stage, clinical N stage, lymphatic infiltration, or staging concerning pCR rates.

Regardless of the treatment phase, lower serum LDH levels correlated with a higher likelihood of achieving pCR: pre-NAC showed an achievement rate of 22.6% versus 3.6%, mid-NAC demonstrated 37.6% versus 16.2%, and preoperative results indicated 21.8% versus 7.6% for low vs. high LDH levels respectively (P < 0.05). In addition, BC patients with negative ER expression, negative PR expression, positive HER2 expression, and elevated KI67 levels are more likely to achieve pCR. Based on molecular typing classifications among patients revealed that those categorized as HR (-)/HER2 (+) exhibited the highest sensitivity to chemotherapy at a rate of 32.3% (n = 31), whereas HR (+)/HER2 (-) patients displayed only an 8.5% response.

Characteristics of the research sample in NHANES

This study employed ROC curve analysis to identify optimal diagnostic cutoff values for biomarkers including LDH, albumin, globulin, total protein, and uric acid (Supplementary Fig. 6). The optimal diagnostic threshold for LDH was determined as 131.5 U/L. The investigation encompassed 13,167 female participants aged 20–80 years from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) database (2009–2018), with 6,806 participants (51.7%) exhibiting serum LDH levels below 131.5 U/L and 6,361 (48.3%) above this cutoff. Comparative analysis revealed that the LDH-elevated group demonstrated significantly higher proportions of individuals with BMI ≥ 24 kg/m² (80.8% vs. 71.5%), globulin ≥ 35.5 g/L (16.7% vs. 11.2%), and hyperuricemia (24.2% vs. 15.3%), alongside a lower proportion of albumin ≥ 40.5 g/L (57.7% vs. 67.2%) (P < 0.001). Moreover, the LDH-elevated cohort exhibited a significantly increased prevalence of widowed/divorced/separated marital status (33.0% vs. 22.7%, P < 0.001) (Supplementary Table 2).

Higher expression of serum LDH levels elevates the risk of BC among participants

In the NHANES cohort study, 366 BC patients were included (Table 5). Multivariable logistic regression analysis identified multiple independent risk factors encompassing multidimensional characteristics of anthropometric indices and lifestyle status. The analysis demonstrated that divorced/separated marital status was associated with a significantly elevated BC risk (OR = 1.587, 95% CI: 1.274–1.978, P < 0.001), whereas unmarried status exhibited a reduced risk (OR = 0.327, 95% CI: 0.205–0.522, P < 0.001), suggesting potential involvement of psychosocial factors or reproductive behaviors in disease pathogenesis. Furthermore, serum LDH ≥ 131.5 U/L (OR = 1.496, 95% CI: 1.204–1.859, P < 0.001), uric acid ≥ 348 µmol/L (OR = 1.525, 95% CI: 1.200-1.938, P < 0.001), and globulin < 35.5 g/L (OR = 1.907, 95% CI: 1.310–2.778, P < 0.001) were independently associated with BC risk, implicating metabolic dysregulation and immune microenvironment disturbances as potential tumor-promoting mechanisms (Table 6). High expression of serum LDH levels increases the risk of mortality in participants

In the cohort of 13,167 participants (median follow-up: 70 months), 792 all-cause mortality events were documented. Kaplan-Meier survival analysis revealed significantly worse OS in individuals with LDH ≥ 131.5 U/L compared to the normal-range group (P < 0.001) (Fig. 11A). Although a comparable trend was observed in the BC subgroup, the difference failed to attain statistical significance (P = 0.207), likely attributable to reduced statistical power from limited sample size and event frequency (Fig. 11B). Discussion

In this study, we identified that LDHA plays a crucial role in BC, with its expression closely associated with tumor growth, proliferation, malignant progression, and poor prognosis. As a key glycolytic enzyme catalyzing the conversion of pyruvate to lactate, LDHA facilitates anaerobic glycolysis in tumor cells while reducing their reliance on oxygen [21]. Notably, LDHA levels are generally upregulated in BC patients. Vats et al. reported that the LDHA/Hypoxia-Inducible Factor 1-Alpha (HIF-1α)/TMPO-AS1 axis is overexpressed in invasive BC, whereas miR-383-5p is downregulated. This overexpression correlates with unfavorable prognostic outcomes and may contribute to activated dendritic cell infiltration within the tumor microenvironment [22]. Inhibition or silencing of LDHA initiates a cascade that enhances cytochrome c release by upregulating B-cell lymphoma 2 related X protein and downregulating inhibitors such as B-cell super large lymphoma and X-linked apoptotic protein.

Furthermore, this process promotes increased reactive oxygen species (ROS) production, collectively leading to heightened cell apoptosis [23, 24]. Experts have observed that the expression of LDHA in tumors responds to metabolic levels and shows a positive correlation with tumor size. A significant correlation exists between its expression level and factors such as the KI67 index, clinical stage, histological grade [25, 26]. In summary, LDHA emerges as a potentially important target for therapeutic intervention in BC treatment.

In addition, our enrichment analysis indicates that LDHA may play a pivotal role in anaerobic glycolysis and the production and metabolism of tumor energy. The glycolytic rate in tumor cells is markedly higher than in normal tissues. Even under conditions characterized by optimal mitochondrial function and sufficient oxygen concentration, cancer cells preferentially utilize the glycolytic pathway for ATP production [27]. This unique metabolic route results in the release of substantial amounts of lactic acid. Lactic acid serves as an energy source for tumor cells and can also be absorbed by other cell types within the tumor microenvironment, including tumor-associated fibroblasts and immune cells. Extracellular acidification contributes to chronic acidity within the tumor microenvironment, which subsequently drives metastasis of tumor cells [28–30]. Experts have established a close correlation between lactate levels and the motility of tumors. In an acidic microenvironment, proteases such as matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs) are activated, enhancing cellular migration. Furthermore, this acidic environment can modify macrophage functionality, promote tumor growth, inhibit killer T cell activity, and create a milieu wherein tumor cells evade immune system attacks. Some studies have also demonstrated that direct administration of lactate to tumor cells can augment their migratory behavior while being associated with increased phosphorylation of ERK1/2 [31].

Lactic acid typically exists as two enantiomers: L-lactic acid and D-lactic acid. In humans, L-lactic acid is produced through pyruvate fermentation during normal metabolic processes; conversely, D-lactic acid is primarily generated by microorganisms [32]. LDH plays a crucial role in converting pyruvate into lactate. Dysfunctional LDH leads to abnormal lactate accumulation, consequently elevating BC risk [33]. As a significant isoform of LDH, LDHA exhibits specific catalytic activity essential for these processes. This is due to the M subunit’s high affinity for pyruvate and its potent reducing capability, enabling LDHA to exhibit exceptional efficiency in lactate production [34, 35].

Another mechanism by which aerobic glycolysis confers a proliferative advantage to BC cells involves the incomplete utilization of glucose. This process halts the oxidation of glucose at the pyruvate stage, preventing its further conversion into carbon dioxide and water, thereby failing to release all the energy that would be generated through complete oxidation. Consequently, some intermediate products accumulate, which can be subsequently utilized in other biosynthesis pathways, including nucleotide synthesis, fatty acid synthesis, and angiogenesis. The activation and regulation of these pathways are crucial for the growth, proliferation, and metabolic adaptation of BC cells [36, 37].

In this study, we conducted bioinformatics analyses revealing that low expression levels of LDHA significantly enhance the sensitivity of certain anticancer drugs. Experts suggest inhibiting LDHA expression can increase drug-resistant cancer cells’ susceptibility to chemotherapy. The primary action mechanism of most anticancer agents is to induce apoptosis and related cell death pathways aimed at eliminating cancer cells. However, due to the dysregulation of apoptotic signals in cancer, some tumor cells manage to evade this programmed cell death and proliferate uncontrollably, leading to enhanced survival and metastasis [38]. This phenomenon of drug resistance is complex and arises from interactions among various molecules and signaling pathways [39]. Das et al. demonstrated that inhibiting LDHA through pharmacological or genetic approaches can diminish pro-survival autophagy, restore apoptotic processes, and reverse epithelial-mesenchymal transition-like characteristics observed in tamoxifen-resistant BC cases [40]. Trastuzumab exhibits significant efficacy in treating HER2 positive BC; however, targeted therapies often require extended treatment durations during which many patients may develop acquired resistance. Zhao et al. found that trastuzumab inhibits tumor growth by downregulating heat shock factor 1 (HSF1) and LDHA in HER2 positive BC cells, suppressing glycolysis [41]. Furthermore, researchers have identified that when LDHA is silenced or inhibited, alternative isoforms of LDH within BC cells can still produce lactate independently. Thus, silencing LDHA alone may not effectively prevent alterations in lactate production levels. Future research should consider dual knockdown strategies targeting LDHA and LDHB to enhance chemotherapy drug sensitivity [42].

LDHA, a significant subtype of LDH, exhibits a close correlation with LDH. While serum LDH cannot be fully equated with LDHA, its study remains paramount. Our study enrolled 395 patients undergoing NAC to investigate the impact of serum LDH levels at various treatment stages on the pCR rate among BC patients. Our findings revealed that patients with lower serum LDH levels are more inclined to achieve pCR (P < 0.05), aligning with previous study results. Notably, before and during NAC, serum LDH levels, and HER2 expression are independent predictors of pCR attainment in BC patients. By assessing these factors, healthcare providers can forecast the prognosis of BC patients and tailor treatment decisions accordingly, ensuring appropriate individualized care.

In the entire cohort, we found that the serum LDH levels of some patients gradually increased with chemotherapy. The increase in serum LDH levels after chemotherapy is often related to some biological and metabolic changes during treatment. Its clinical significance depends on several factors, including the type of cancer, treatment plan, overall patient condition, and other laboratory parameters [43]. The increase in serum LDH levels may indicate the effectiveness of treatment, as chemotherapy drugs can kill cancer cells, causing them to release LDH into the bloodstream, indicating that cancer cells have been damaged or have died [44]. However, in other cases, an increase in serum LDH levels may indicate disease progression or the presence of other complications. Firstly, some BC patients may have higher serum LDH levels before treatment, which may be due to high tumor burden and a large number of cancer cells releasing LDH. Secondly, chemotherapy drugs themselves may lead to an increase in serum LDH levels, as they can damage the liver and heart, affecting the metabolism and clearance of LDH. Hemolysis is the red blood cell rupture and release of LDH, which may also lead to increased LDH after chemotherapy [45]. Therefore, when the serum LDH level of BC patients increases, it is necessary to conduct sufficient examinations to avoid missing other diseases.

Serum LDH is a nonspecific marker that can be elevated in malignant and non-malignant conditions; higher levels may correlate with tumor burden but are not diagnostic [46]. Koukourakis discovered that patients with benign breast conditions, such as fibroadenomas and cystadenomas, exhibit serum LDH levels that are comparable to those of cancer patients [47]. Hence, paying attention to blood test results during routine physical examinations is crucial.

In the NHANES cohort, elevated serum LDH and hyperuricemia were independently associated with higher all-cause mortality, whereas higher albumin was protective; globulin and total protein were not significant after multivariable adjustment. Experts have observed that BC patients with stable and normal serum LDH levels tend to have longer overall survival rates. An earlier study revealed that serum LDH levels in BC patients are significantly higher than in the general female population. During the first week post-surgery for BC patients, serum LDH levels experience a notable surge, followed by a gradual decline over time, potentially due to the release of LDH into the circulatory system following tumor cell destruction [48, 49]. Liu’s research indicated that the prognostic impact of LDH is more pronounced in stage II and III BC patients, with a lesser effect in stage I patients. This may be attributed to the fact that higher serum LDH levels signify heightened tumor activity and occult metastasis, which are less evident in stage I patients [50]. Paclitaxel stands as one of the most potent drugs for treating BC. Zhou discovered that the expression and activity of serum LDH increase in paclitaxel-resistant cells, and using LDH inhibitors restored the sensitivity of these resistant cells to paclitaxel, offering valuable insights for paclitaxel-resistant BC patients [51].

The prognostic significance of serum LDH in various types of tumors has been extensively researched. Tas reported a significant correlation between serum LDH levels and the prognosis of small cell lung cancer, with patients having normal LDH levels exhibiting a 1-year OS rate of 60.2%. In comparison, those with elevated LDH levels had an OS rate of 33.1% (P = 0.0017) [52]. Renal failure is a prevalent complication in multiple myeloma patients, and studies have shown that individuals with high serum LDH levels have a higher incidence of renal failure (P < 0.002) [53]. Currently, there is no universally accepted prognostic indicator for mantle cell lymphoma. However, Hoster analyzed lymphoma patients across three clinical trials and identified age, performance status, LDH, and white blood cell count as independent risk factors influencing patient survival. These factors may be crucial for making risk-adaptive treatment decisions in patients with advanced mantle cell lymphoma [54]. LDH has been found to regulate MMR protein, promoting the proliferation of both dMMR and pMMR colorectal tumor cells. Combining pharmacologic or genetic LDH inhibition with immune checkpoint blockade has shown potential to improve patient outcomes [55]. Tomita compared the prognosis of non-Hodgkin lymphoma patients with differing serum LDH levels and discovered that patients with high LDH expression were more prone to central nervous system involvement when LDH levels doubled. Therefore, prevention of central nervous system diseases should be considered in such cases [56]. In summary, clinical practitioners must assess patients’ serum LDH levels, which may be a pivotal factor in future cancer research endeavors.

A notable limitation of our study is the use of total serum LDH levels as a surrogate for LDHA activity. We acknowledge that total serum LDH is a composite measure of multiple isoforms and may not exclusively reflect LDHA-driven metabolic reprogramming. Therefore, elevations in serum LDH could be attributed to a combination of factors, such as increased tumor burden or cell turnover, rather than specific LDHA activity. It is important to note, however, that the measurement of total serum LDH remains a widely adopted and clinically practical approach, as isoform-specific assays are not yet standardized for routine clinical use.​​.

Our study, while yielding promising results, has several limitations that warrant careful consideration. Firstly, and most importantly, we must acknowledge that serum LDH demonstrated only modest predictive accuracy as a standalone marker, with AUC values consistently below 0.6. This indicates that while statistically significant, the clinical utility of LDH alone for predicting NAC outcomes is limited. Consequently, we emphasize that LDH should be interpreted as part of a broader clinical picture rather than as a definitive predictive tool. Secondly, our study is a single-center retrospective analysis, and the treatment methods for each patient varied, which may introduce selection bias and influence patient prognosis. Despite these limitations, our findings provide valuable hypothesis-generating insights that merit further investigation. Thirdly, the management of BC patients is a long-term process. It is essential to consider that non-neoplastic diseases can arise during treatment and may impact serum LDH levels, potentially confounding the results. Therefore, future large-scale, prospective, multicenter studies are necessary not only to validate our findings and increase their reliability and applicability but also to explore the value of LDH within multivariable predictive models. Specifically, we recommend that such studies incorporate serial LDH measurements alongside detailed clinical covariates to better elucidate its dynamic prognostic role. Nonetheless, this cohort study possesses significant advantages. Although the effect of serum LDH levels on survival among BC patients has been extensively investigated, its implications for NAC patients remain unclear. Furthermore, we have identified a dynamic, cost-effective biomarker that can predict NAC efficacy in BC patients, which is an advancement with positive implications for their treatment. ​Most importantly, our study highlights the potential of LDH as an accessible and serial monitoring tool for assessing therapeutic response, offering a practical advantage for real-time clinical decision-making.​.

Conclusion

In this study, we investigated the expression of LDHA and its clinical relevance in BC using bioinformatics and retrospective cohort analyses. Our results indicate that higher LDHA expression is correlated with poorer prognosis and is associated with the infiltration of various immune cells. Furthermore, lower LDHA expression levels appear to be associated with greater sensitivity to chemotherapeutic agents. Clinically, our analyses of both the NHANES and our institutional cohorts showed that elevated serum LDH levels are significant predictors of adverse survival outcomes and lower pCR rates in BC patients. In summary, these findings suggest that LDHA may serve as a potential therapeutic target and a promising prognostic biomarker in BC. However, further mechanistic and functional studies are indispensable to validate its causal role in tumor progression and chemoresistance and to fully explore its therapeutic potential.
