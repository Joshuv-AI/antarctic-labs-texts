AI in primary care - a general practitioner’s bucket list
Authors: Veronica Milos Nymberg
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractWhile the development and use of Artificial Intelligence (AI) in health care have literally exploded in recent years, general practitioners (GPs) continue to struggle with a fragmented health care system and complex patients with multiple conditions and increasing care needs. An ageing population, task shifting from secondary care to ambulatory services without adequate resource allocation, and policy makers pushing for more accessible primary care are examples of factors driving the demand for AI-tools designed to triage patient complaints, improve workflows, ease clinicians’ burden and support clinical decision-making. The paradigm shift towards digital solutions may offer answers, yet evidence often trails behind their implementation. The paper will address current challenges in European primary care today, highlight areas where AI can improve administrative tasks and patient outcomes, and discuss the potential benefits and drawbacks of adopting AI. Will AI tools enhance decision-making or simply create new care demands? Will their implementation truly add value to the holistic patient care model in general practice?Keywords: Artificial intelligence, general practice, large language models, implementation, evidence

Introduction

It is impossible to deny that Artificial Intelligence (AI) is the twenty first century’s big scoop. Generative AI, powered by large language models (LLMs), can support general practice by automating documentation, enhancing education, and aiding clinical decision-making through intelligent data synthesis. Its integration promises improved efficiency, personalised learning, and earlier disease detection, yet rigorous evaluation is essential to ensure safe and effective use [1]. While it holds tremendous promises for enhancing patient care and boosting clinical decision-making, it also brings about new vulnerabilities and fuels the capabilities of malicious actors. Nevertheless, the pace at which AI-solutions are being integrated into general practices is outstripping the available evidence supporting their efficacy and safety. Even if one puts aside concerns over patient data ownership, the risk of data poisoning or leakage and the environmental damage, we must weigh the gains and pitfalls of giving clinicians vast access to patient information. The potential of creating new knowledge is enormous, perhaps hard to grasp. Yet, before getting too enthusiastic, researchers should ask: can we tailor AI tools to our needs, or will we end up adapting ourselves to the growing flood of information, as described by the Normalisation Process Theory [2]? Also, which consequences will this new work model have on patient safety, working environment and competence level of health care professionals?

This paper is based on the keynote lecture at the 100th conference for EGPRN in Gothenburg, Sweden, on May 8-10th 2025, with the theme ‘Telemedicine: its Benefits, Quality and Safety’. The aim is to highlight the importance of implementing evidence-based tools, and to reflect upon of medical staff’s challenges and needs for decision support while maintaining the holistic and person-centered approach in decision making.

Why is AI particularly challenging for primary care?

Compared to secondary care, which deals with selected populations and standardised care flows, general practice manages complex patients with multiple conditions and an overwhelming flow of data. Medical staff are suddenly faced with managing several channels of patient inflows within an already pressured daily clinical practice [3]. The impact of digitalisation on their working environment depends greatly on organisational aspects of the implementation process, with more training and early involvement of the professionals reported as essential [4]. A lack of integration between digital triage platforms and the electronic medical record, resulting in double documentation, is an example of increased administrative burden following digitalisation.

The decision to implement digital tools in general practice is multifaceted, involving various stakeholders, including healthcare providers, patients, policymakers, and technology developers. Each group has distinct priorities and concerns, ranging from improving patient outcomes and reducing administrative workload to ensuring cost-effectiveness and scalability. This complexity can sometimes lead to the premature adoption of digital tools without thorough validation.

The evidence gap

The heterogeneity of digital health solutions further complicates the evidence landscape. Different tools and platforms may have varying levels of effectiveness, usability, and integration capabilities. This variability makes it difficult to generalise findings from one study to another and underscores the need for context-specific research [5].

The implementation of e-health solutions often outpaces the generation of high-quality evidence due to the time and resource-intensive nature of clinical research [5]. Randomised controlled trials (RCTs), regarded as the gold standard for evaluating medical interventions, are particularly challenging to conduct in the rapidly evolving digital health landscape. For example, despite the widespread adoption of digital triage systems, no RCTs have compared their effectiveness with traditional telephone triage in terms of adherence to recommended care levels [6].

At the same time, AI-powered ambient scribe tools (voice-to notes transcription) have shown strong evidence of reducing administrative burdens and are already being partially implemented in primary care. These tools serve as an excellent example of how AI can enhance the working environment for health care professionals [7,8].

Is AI better at clinical reasoning, then? A recent Swedish study showed that GPT-4 performed worse than doctors in assessing simulated cases [9]. Another study showed that, even in a real setting and with actual patient data, there were no significant differences between the intervention and control groups. Interestingly, the large language model (LLM) alone outperformed the doctors. The authors suggest this may be due to the LLMs greater proficiency in prompt formulation [10]. A reasonable conclusion is that the practical value of such tools in clinical practice may depend on clinicians’ skills in prompt engineering.

Another promising area in the use of AI to develop models for predicting disease using big data. Several validation studies are published; for example, studies have validated incident cases of hypertension or heart failure using machine learning tools [11,12]. Some researchers argue that stratifying risk and predicting new cases of disease could revolutionise preventive medicine, although motivating patients to change their lifestyles or adhere to medication regimens remain a challenge [13]. Other GPs believe that precision medicine can improve patient outcomes but may also create tension with personalised care [14]. However, one important limitation in AI research is the absence of well-established methodological frameworks for evaluating large language models (LLMs) in primary care [15,16]. Beyond acquiring a technical understanding of the topic, research on LLMs requires thorough documentation of model configuration and prompt design, consistent version control, management of output variability, and clear definitions for the role of human oversight. At present, these critical aspects are not sufficiently standardised in LLM research [15,16].

A paradigm-shift in the definition of evidence-based healthcare?

If thoughtfully integrated, AI has the potential to strengthen general practice by supporting clinicians in managing complexity, enhancing patient outcomes, and alleviating administrative burdens. By reinforcing the foundations of primary care, it could help ensure that the holistic patient-centered approach remains at the heart of healthcare even as technology evolves.

The three pillars of evidence-based health care in decision making are: 1. considering the best available evidence; 2. placing it within the clinical context; and 3. taking into account the patient’s individual characteristics and preferences [17]. Exploring patients’ choices and preferences is arguably one of the most important aspects of the decision-making process, and aligns with the holistic approach at the core of general practice.

With the integration of AI in healthcare, it is becoming clear that a fourth pillar, ‘AI decision support,’ is emerging alongside the traditional triad of evidence-based decision-making. This new dimension leverages advanced algorithms and predictive models to complement the best available evidence, clinical context, and individual preferences. It is possible that, in time, AI will not merely serve as a tool, but as an integrated partner in clinical decision-making, supporting rather than hindering the clinician’s holistic approach.

Potential solutions of integrating AI in general practice

There is a crucial need to balance the rapid implementation of e-health solutions with rigorous validation, in order to ensure patient safety and optimal outcomes. Policymakers and healthcare organisations must prioritise funding and resources for high-quality research to close the evidence gap. Collaborative efforts among academia, industry, and clinical practice can facilitate the generation of relevant evidence and support the sustainable integration of digital tools. To enhance the validity and applicability of research findings, it is essential to develop standardised protocols and ensure interoperability among different digital health systems. This approach can facilitate data sharing, improve the quality of research, and enable more comprehensive evaluations of digital tools. Sweden’s efforts to build robust research databases and promote interoperability among electronic medical records (EMRs) serve as a model in this regard.

Involving healthcare professionals, patients, and policymakers in the research process can provide valuable insights into the practical challenges and benefits of digital health solutions. This participatory approach can help align research objectives with real-world needs and improve the relevance and implementation of study findings. Implementation of AI in healthcare necessitates a strategic approach that embraces innovation while recognising and mitigating potential cyber-security risks such as data leakage or data poisoning. By prioritising patient safety, data security, and ethical considerations, healthcare organisations can harness the transformative power of AI to benefit all stakeholders.

Recommendations for practice, policy and research

The rapid implementation of e-health solutions in general practice presents both opportunities and challenges. While these innovations hold great promises for transforming healthcare delivery, their adoption must be guided by robust evidence to ensure they meet the needs of patients and healthcare providers while acknowledging and mitigating potential risks. Therefore, research collaboration between experts in computer science, AI, and primary care research is essential in this field. Without combining these areas of expertise and establishing clearer guidance for study design and reporting, research may result in findings that are inconsistent or difficult to reproduce and interpret. To build a strong evidence base, the field needs coordinated infrastructure, such as dedicated guidelines and protocols, to address the specific challenges of implementing LLMs in real-world clinical research [15,16].

In the race towards digitalisation, let us not forget the general practitioner striving to make sound decisions for patients within a complicated and expansive landscape of information. On the GPs’ bucket list are the following:
