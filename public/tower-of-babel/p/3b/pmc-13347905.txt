Evaluation of pregnancy outcomes in patients with rheumatoid arthritis compared with the general population: results from a French national prospective and matched study
Authors: Sabrina Hamroun, Grégoire Martin de Frémont, Nathalie Costedoat-Chalumeau, Marion Couderc, René-Marc Flipo, Laure Gossec, Christophe Richez, Rakiba Belkhir, Aline Frazier-Mironer, Valérie Devauchelle-Pensec, Hubert Marotte, Jérémie Sellam, Elisabeth Gervais, Alban Deroux, Cédric Lukas, Emmanuelle Dernis, Véronique Le Guern, Gaëlle Guettrot-Imbert, Nathalie Lelong, Emmanuelle Pannier, Loïc Sentilhes, Camille Le Ray, Raphaèle Seror, Anna Moltó, on behalf of the GR2 and the ENP2021 Study Groups
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesThe aim of the study was to assess the contemporary frequency of adverse pregnancy outcomes in women with rheumatoid arthritis (RA) and to identify associated factors.MethodsThis French prospective multicentre cohort included pregnant women with RA between 2015 and 2021. Maternal characteristics, disease activity, treatments and pregnancy outcomes were analysed. Outcomes of pregnancies in women with RA were compared in univariable analysis with those of matched general population women from the 2016 and 2021 French perinatal surveys assessing maternal and neonatal health. Multivariable logistic regressions were performed to identify factors associated with adverse outcomes.ResultsA total of 100 pregnancies in 90 women with RA were analysed. Mean maternal age was 33.7±5.0 years and 57.6% were nulliparous. During pregnancy, 46% received glucocorticoids and 39% biologic disease-modifying antirheumatic drugs. Compared with the 359 controls from the general population, preterm birth (OR 2.08, 95% CI 1.04 to 4.09) and small for gestational age (SGA) (ORSGA 10th percentile 2.11, 95% CI 1.12 to 3.94; ORSGA 3rd percentile 3.21, 95% CI 1.13 to 9.44) rates were higher in RA pregnancies. SGA was associated with nulliparity (adjusted OR (aOR) 4.38, 95% CI 1.12 to 9.78), whereas preterm birth was associated with maternal age (aOR 1.14, 95% CI 1.01 to 1.30) and exposure to glucocorticoids at doses ≥10 mg/day during pregnancy (aOR 4.91, 95% CI 1.30 to 10.59).ConclusionIn this contemporary cohort mostly followed in tertiary care centres, pregnancies in women with RA remained at increased risk of preterm birth and SGA. Exposure to systemic glucocorticoids at doses ≥10 mg/day emerged as a risk factor for preterm birth, although a contribution of maternal disease activity cannot be entirely excluded.Keywords: Arthritis, Rheumatoid; Epidemiology; Autoimmunity; Biological Therapy

Introduction

Rheumatoid arthritis (RA) is a chronic inflammatory disease that predominantly affects women, often during their reproductive years.1 With the rising maternal age at first pregnancy in many high-income countries, including France,2 pregnancy in women with RA has become an increasingly common clinical situation, underscoring the need for dedicated reproductive and obstetric care in this population.3

Beyond its articular manifestations, RA is associated with multiple comorbidities, including cardiovascular, metabolic and obstetric complications. Several large registry-based and population studies have reported increased risks of adverse pregnancy outcomes among women with RA or other chronic inflammatory rheumatic diseases. Meta-analyses have shown higher ORs of caesarean delivery, pre-eclampsia, preterm birth, small-for-gestational-age (SGA) infants and low birth weight compared with the general population.4 5 For example, the recent 2024 French nationwide study by Pina Vegas et al from the healthcare claims database demonstrated higher rates of preterm delivery (adjusted OR (aOR) 1.84), very preterm birth (aOR 1.43), low birth weight (aOR 1.65) and caesarean delivery (aOR 1.46) in women with RA compared with the general population.6 However, most of these data derive from administrative registries, with limited information on disease activity or specific longitudinal follow-up from conception through the postpartum period. Therefore, complementary prospective cohort studies with granular clinical data are necessary to better understand the determinants of adverse pregnancy outcomes in RA pregnancies in the era of modern therapies.

Over the past decade, therapeutic strategies for RA have profoundly evolved. Early diagnosis, tight disease control and widespread use of biologic (b) disease-modifying antirheumatic drugs (DMARDs), particularly tumour necrosis factor inhibitors (TNFi), have transformed outcomes.7 Importantly, the approach to rheumatic disease management during pregnancy, including RA, has become increasingly permissive, a change endorsed by the 2025 update of the European Alliance of Associations for Rheumatology (EULAR) recommendations and supported by accumulating evidence of the efficacy and safety of several bDMARDs in this context.8 9 For instance, RA activity during the third trimester was much lower in the Pre-conceptional Counseling in Active RA (PreCARA) cohort (2011–2022, 90.4% in low disease activity) than in the Pregnancy-induced Amelioration of RA (PARA) cohort (2002–2010, 47.3% in low disease activity).9 This improvement coincided with a shift in treatment strategies in the more recent cohort, characterised by a greater use of TNFi and reduced use of prednisone doses >7.5 mg/day (0% and 70.6%, respectively, in PARA and 47.3% and 19%, respectively, in PreCARA). These findings highlight the need to also reassess pregnancy outcomes in contemporary RA cohorts, as historical data may no longer reflect current clinical practice.

To address this gap, we used data from pregnant women with RA from the prospective multicentre French Groupe de Recherche sur la Grossesse et les maladies Rares (GR2) cohort, with systematic assessment of disease activity and treatment exposure, and compared pregnancy outcomes with those of a matched representative sample of pregnancies from the French general population. This design provides a unique opportunity to evaluate the current obstetric risks in RA and to explore the clinical and therapeutic determinants of adverse pregnancy outcomes in the era of modern targeted therapies.

Methods

Study design and populations

Patients with RA: GR2 study

The GR2 study is a French multicentre prospective observational study of women planning to conceive or already pregnant with rare and/or chronic rheumatic diseases. It has been conducted since October 2014 across 76 centres and since December 2015 for rheumatic diseases. Women are included by their treating specialists (rheumatologists or internal medicine doctors) and are followed for up to 12 months postpartum.10 11 Treatment decisions are left to the discretion of the treating physicians. The GR2 study is part of the European Network of Pregnancy Registers in Rheumatology12 and follows EULAR recommendations regarding core datasets for pregnancy registers in rheumatology.13

In the present study, patients were eligible if they were included in the GR2 study before 28 June 2021 and were diagnosed with RA. The RA diagnosis was defined by a rheumatologist, and all items necessary for the 2010 American College of Rheumatology/EULAR classification criteria were collected.14 If a patient had several pregnancies prospectively included in the GR2 study during follow-up, all pregnancies were analysed. To avoid reporting bias, only women with pregnancies included before 12 weeks of gestation (WG) were considered in the present analysis.

Control population: ENP study

Starting in 1995, the French Enquêtes Nationales Périnatales (ENP) is a series of repeated cross-sectional national surveys designed to assess the health status of mothers and children in France and to monitor perinatal care practices at the population level.15 16 Approximately every 5 years, data on all births ≥22 WG of newborns weighing ≥500 g are collected for 1 week in all public and private maternity units, including midwife units. There is a core set of information common to all ENPs since inception, to which specific demands are added (or retrieved) for each survey. The data sources include medical records and face-to-face interviews performed by trained midwives 2–3 days after birth to collect information. Women are orally informed and consent to participate. Full details can be found in the reports.15 16 Since births before 22 WG and/or weighing <500 g are excluded from the ENP, only GR2 women with RA giving birth at a minimum of 22 WG with newborns weighing ≥500 g were included in the matched controlled study. Thus, we distinguished the total RA population (all pregnancies in the GR2 cohort) from the ENP-matched RA subpopulation (after excluding pregnancies that did not meet ENP inclusion criteria).

For the current comparison of the GR2 study with the general population, we used the ENPs 2016 (for GR2 women who delivered before 2019) and 2021 (for GR2 women who delivered 2019 onwards).15 17

Statistical analyses

Continuous variables were expressed by their means±SD and qualitative variables as numbers and percentages (%).

First, we described the characteristics of the GR2-RA population and the frequency of each pregnancy outcome.

Second, we compared the pregnancy outcome frequency between RA-GR2 patients and the general population (ENPs). This analysis was restricted to GR2 pregnancies meeting the ENP inclusion criteria (ie, birth at ≥22 WG and birth weight ≥500 g). Each GR2 pregnancy was matched to four ENP pregnancies by age group (≤29, 30–34, 35–39 or ≥40 years), parity (nulliparity vs multiparity), residential area (French region) and singleton versus multiple pregnancies. These matching variables were chosen because they are associated with adverse pregnancy outcomes and are therefore potential confounders. Univariate ORs and their 95% CIs were calculated for each adverse pregnancy outcome.

Finally, for adverse pregnancy outcomes identified as significantly more frequent in RA compared with women from the general population, a multilevel logistic regression was performed to identify factors independently associated with each adverse pregnancy outcome only in the RA pregnancies. We applied mixed-effects logistic regression. A random intercept at the centre level accounted for heterogeneity in clinical practice across sites. A patient-level random intercept accounted for several pregnancies within the same woman. This multilevel structure appropriately modelled within-centre and within-patient correlation.19 Univariate analyses included clinically relevant maternal, disease-related and treatment-related variables potentially associated with adverse pregnancy outcomes. Variables associated with the outcome with a p value <0.20 in univariate analyses were entered into the multivariable models. Multivariable modelling was not based solely on statistical selection and variables considered essential for clinical interpretation of pregnancy outcomes were systematically forced into the models, including disease activity (DAS28-CRP), BMI and all therapeutic exposure classes (csDMARDs, TNFi, NSAIDs and systemic glucocorticoids), even when not significant in univariate analyses. This was done to ensure that key potential confounders and mechanistically relevant exposures were retained in the final models. Missing data were limited for most maternal and treatment-related variables included in the multivariable analyses (<10% missing values for most variables), but were more substantial for trimester-specific disease activity assessments. Multiple imputation using chained equations20 was used to address missing data among the explanatory variables, using 20 imputed datasets and 20 iterations. The imputation model included maternal characteristics, disease-related variables, treatment exposures and pregnancy outcomes. Outcome variables used in regression analyses were not imputed.

All analyses were conducted with RStudio (R V.4.0.3; 10 October 2020). The MatchIt package was used to match participants between the GR2 and the ENP.

Results

Patient characteristics

From December 2015 to June 2021, 124 pregnancies in 111 women with RA were enrolled in the GR2 study. Among them, 100 pregnancies in 90 women, including one twin pregnancy, were included in this study (figure 1). The mean age at pregnancy was 33.7 (±5.0) years, the mean BMI was 23.1 (±4.4) kg/m2 and 12/100 (12.0%) women were active smokers during pregnancy.

Pregnancy outcomes in the RA population

Of the 100 pregnancies (total RA population), 90/100 (90.0%) resulted in a live birth, including 74/88 (84.1%) full-term births. Fetal losses before 22 WG were reported in 9/100 (9.0%) pregnancies (table 2). The most frequent adverse pregnancy outcomes were SGA, affecting 17/83 (20.5%) pregnancies, followed by preterm birth in 14/88 (15.9%) pregnancies, with only 1/88 (1.1%) being a severe preterm birth (<32 WG). Among preterm births, nine were spontaneous and five were iatrogenic. Gestational diabetes was reported in 12/100 pregnancies (12.0%).

Three (3.0%) pregnancies were complicated by four congenital malformations (table 2): one offspring had ventricular septal defect, one had an omphalocoele and pulmonary hypoplasia and one had a trisomy 21 with duodenal atresia.

Comparison of pregnancy outcomes between patients with RA and women from the general population

Of the 100 RA pregnancies (total RA population), 90 met ENP inclusion criteria (matched RA subpopulation) and were matched to 359 control pregnancies (ie, 195 from the ENP 2016 and 164 from the ENP 2021; the GR2 twin pregnancy only fully matched three control pregnancies). Comparison between women with RA and general population women revealed an increased rate of preterm birth (15.9% vs 8.4%, OR 2.08, 95% CI 1.04 to 4.09) and SGA, both at the 10th percentile (20.5% vs 10.9%, OR 2.11, 95% CI 1.12 to 3.94) and the third percentile (6.0% vs 2.0%, OR 3.21, 95% CI 1.13 to 9.44) in univariate analysis. In contrast, no significant differences were observed for other adverse pregnancy outcomes, such as gestational diabetes (12.2% vs 15.0%, OR 0.79, 95% CI 0.39 to 1.57), macrosomia (3.6% vs 8.1%, OR 0.42, 95% CI 0.13 to 1.30) or caesarean delivery (26.8% vs 21.7%, OR 1.33, 95% CI 0.75 to 2.31) (table 3).

Factors associated with adverse pregnancy outcomes in RA pregnancies

As SGA and preterm birth were significantly more frequent among patients with RA compared with the general population, a logistic regression analysis was performed among women with RA to identify factors potentially contributing to these increased poor pregnancy outcomes rates. Variables tested in the model as potential predictors of SGA were maternal age, disease duration, parity, disease activity during pregnancy, smoking during pregnancy, BMI, erosive RA, ACPA positivity and exposure to glucocorticoids, NSAIDs, csDMARDs and TNFi during pregnancy. In multivariate analyses, nulliparity was the only factor associated with an increased risk of SGA (aOR 3.61, 95% CI 1.08 to 8.54) (table 4), whereas preterm birth was independently associated with maternal age (aOR 1.14, 95% CI 1.02 to 1.27) and exposure to glucocorticoids at doses ≥10 mg/day during pregnancy (aOR 4.91, 95% CI 1.30 to 10.59) (table 4). In univariable analyses, glucocorticoid exposure at doses >7.5 mg/day was associated with an increased risk of preterm birth (online supplemental table 1), whereas in multivariable analyses this association persisted only for doses ≥10 mg/day (table 4). Disease activity as measured by the DAS 28-CRP at each trimester of pregnancy, particularly in the third trimester, was also not associated with an increased risk of SGA (aOR 1.06, 95% CI 0.52 to 2.14) nor preterm birth (aOR 1.43, 95% CI 0.58 to 3.56) (online supplemental tables 2 and 3).

Discussion

In this national multicentre cohort of pregnancies in women with RA, we found a twofold increased odds of preterm birth and up to a threefold increased odds of SGA compared with a matched sample of the general French population (on age, parity, residential area and singleton/multifetal). Among the RA pregnancies, SGA was independently associated with nulliparity, whereas preterm birth was associated with maternal age and exposure to glucocorticoids at doses ≥10 mg/day during pregnancy. In contrast, neither RA disease activity nor markers of disease severity or other treatment exposure, including bDMARDs, were found to be associated with adverse pregnancy outcomes.

These results are consistent with the increased rates of adverse pregnancy outcomes reported in the literature, including studies conducted on the same time period as this one.6 21 A particularly interesting comparison can be made with the French nationwide administrative study by Pina Vegas et al,6 which analysed data from 11 792 pregnancies in women with RA between 2010 and 2020. The large sample size of this nationwide registry-based study provides highly robust population-level estimates and constitutes an important reference for contemporary RA pregnancy outcomes in France. Their national analysis reported preterm birth in 10% of RA pregnancies and SGA in 9.8%, whereas in our tertiary-centre cohort the corresponding frequencies were higher (preterm birth 15.9%, SGA 20.5%). Pina Vegas et al also reported a higher rate of pregnancy-related hospitalisation and caesarean delivery at the population level, findings we did not replicate in our patients mostly followed in tertiary care centres. Therapeutic patterns differed substantially: in the nationwide study, glucocorticoids exposure (including intra-articular forms) was approximately 20% at each trimester and TNFi use 9.1% during pregnancy, compared with 46% (only systemic glucocorticoids) and 40% in our cohort, respectively.

Importantly, the strengths and limitations of the two studies are complementary. While the nationwide administrative study offers excellent statistical power and representativeness, it lacks detailed prospective information on disease phenotype and severity, disease activity, treatment details such as daily dosing and in-hospital administration and detailed obstetrical information. Conversely, although our cohort is smaller and includes patients managed in tertiary centres, its prospective design with granular clinical and therapeutic data allowed us to explore potential explanatory mechanisms underlying adverse pregnancy outcomes, particularly the association between glucocorticoid exposure ≥10 mg/day and preterm birth. The fact that both studies were conducted during a similar period and within the same healthcare system strengthens the validity and comparability of these findings.

The comparison between these two studies is particularly informative, as it highlights complementary aspects of RA pregnancies in France. First, the lower rates of obstetric interventions and pregnancy-related morbidity observed in our cohort compared with the general population may reflect management in tertiary expert centres, where obstetricians and rheumatologists are familiar with RA pregnancies and may provide closer surveillance and more individualised obstetric care. This may contribute to fewer intervention-driven outcomes such as caesarean delivery or hospitalisation, despite a potentially higher baseline disease severity. Second, the independent association observed between preterm birth and exposure to systemic glucocorticoids at doses ≥10 mg/day, but not at lower doses, is consistent with the accumulating evidence linking systemic glucocorticoid exposure (especially higher doses) to preterm delivery.22,24 Although cumulative glucocorticoid exposure and treatment duration were not available in our cohort, the observed dose-dependent association, together with the persistence of the association in multivariable analyses, strengthens the plausibility of a specific effect of glucocorticoids on prematurity risk. The predominance of spontaneous rather than iatrogenic preterm births in our cohort further supports the hypothesis that glucocorticoid exposure may contribute directly to the mechanisms leading to prematurity. However, although this association remained independent after adjustment, residual confounding by indication cannot be excluded. Indeed, glucocorticoid exposure is likely to reflect patients with more severe or refractory disease, including those with insufficient response to or temporary interruption of bDMARDs, which may not be fully captured by available disease activity measures. Finally, the higher rate of TNFi exposure in our cohort is unlikely to explain these findings, as our data and several cohort studies showed no increase in adverse pregnancy outcomes with continued TNFi use during pregnancy.9 25 26 The net balance in our sample was a substantial glucocorticoid burden that may have attenuated potential benefits associated with bDMARDs.

In our cohort, 21% of women had active disease during pregnancy, despite being followed in centres where physicians are accustomed to prescribing bDMARDs during pregnancy and practising treat-to-target management. Consequently, these women had a higher risk of being exposed to glucocorticoids to manage disease exacerbations during pregnancy. This low prescription of bDMARDs comparatively to the high use of glucocorticoids in RA pregnancies may reflect the expectation of a spontaneous improvement of RA with pregnancy,27 leading the clinician not to pursue strict disease control before pregnancy onset with the initiation of a bDMARD. However, recent prospective data show that remission during pregnancy is neither universal nor stable and that withdrawal of bDMARDs increases the risk of pregnancy and postpartum flares.28

Our findings suggest that patients followed in tertiary centres remain at higher risk of adverse fetal outcomes compared with the general population,6 with preterm birth being independently associated with exposure to systemic glucocorticoids at doses ≥10 mg/day, rather than with RA activity or severity. These results underscore the need for multidisciplinary care and strict adherence to EULAR recommendations, including minimal glucocorticoids exposure (≤5 mg/day) and continuation of recommended pregnancy-compatible bDMARDs during pregnancy.8 24

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the relatively small sample size limits statistical power to detect associations or interactions particularly for rare outcomes such as severe preterm birth or major congenital malformations, resulting in wide CIs and potentially unstable multivariable estimates. Second, given that all women were managed in tertiary centres, selection bias towards more complex or severe cases cannot be excluded. Third, DAS28-CRP data were missing for a substantial proportion of pregnancies, particularly for trimester-specific assessments, limiting interpretation of the respective contributions of disease activity and glucocorticoid exposure to adverse pregnancy outcomes. Missing disease activity data may not necessarily reflect underestimation of disease activity, as some patients may have had fewer consultations or no DAS28-CRP assessments because they were clinically stable. Although multiple imputation was used to limit potential bias related to missing variables, residual misclassification of disease activity cannot be excluded. Finally, despite multiple imputation to address missing data, residual confounding by unmeasured variables remains possible.

Nonetheless, the study has notable strengths. It combines prospective, longitudinal data collection from conception to postpartum with validated clinical instruments and detailed therapeutic follow-up including precise information on glucocorticoid dosing, allowing the identification of a dose-response relationship with the risk of preterm birth, an advantage over registry-based studies relying on treatment and hospitalisation proxies. Moreover, comparison with contemporary national perinatal data allows a direct and contextually relevant comparison to the general French population. These methodological features enhance the robustness and clinical applicability of our results.

In summary, our findings highlight that despite advances in RA management, pregnancies in women with RA remain at higher risk for preterm birth and SGA in a cohort with more severe forms of RA followed in tertiary care centres. Exposure to systemic glucocorticoids at doses ≥10 mg/day emerged as an independent factor associated with preterm birth. These results reinforce the need to prioritise glucocorticoid-sparing strategies and optimise the use of pregnancy-compatible bDMARDs, in line with recent EULAR recommendations, to further improve pregnancy outcomes in women with RA.
