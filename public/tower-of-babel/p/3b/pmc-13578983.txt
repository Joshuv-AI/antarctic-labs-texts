Dietary supplementation with raw potato starch enhanced tibia mass of meat ducks potentially linked to intestine-derived inflammation
Authors: Jixue Yin, Xin Yue, Simeng Qin, Yuying Huang, Minh Tu Nguyen, Herinda Pertiwi, Yanqun Huang, Rui Zheng, Wen Chen, Huaiyong Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Gait problems in poultry is a welfare issue with a prevalence of about 21% in commercial meat ducks, which compromises growth performance by restricting access to feed and water. This study evaluated the effects of raw potato starch (RPS) on intestinal barrier function and bone metabolism in both healthy and enteritis-affected meat ducks. Initial characterization revealed that RPS consists of round or oval granules with a polygonal B-type crystalline structure, exhibiting a nitrogen-corrected apparent metabolizable energy of 2.67 kcal/g for 21-d-old meat ducks. A total of 288 one-day-old male ducklings (initial body weight was 51.4 ± 1.7 g) were assigned to four groups (six replicates per group with 12 birds each) and fed diets containing 0, 6%, 12%, or 18% RPS until d 21. Dietary RPS supplementation dose-dependently (PLinear and PQuadratic < 0.05) increased tibia ash content and strength while reducing serum D-lactate content and systemic inflammation in 21-d-old ducks. Broken-line analysis identified 12% RPS as the optimal inclusion level for improving bone properties and minimizing D-lactate content. To further investigate the relationship between RPS, intestinal inflammation, and bone metabolism, an enteritis model was established using dextran sodium sulphate (DSS). A total of 288 ducklings (47.8 ± 0.8 g) were allocated to three groups until d 21: a basal diet (Ctrl), and two groups orally administered DSS at 1.2 g/(bird·d) from d 14 and fed either the basal diet (DSS) or the 12% RPS diet (DSS + RPS). Each group had 8 replicates with 12 birds each. The administration of DSS induced higher interleukin (IL)- 6 and tumour necrosis factor alpha (TNF-α) levels in both serum and bone marrow, which were accompanied by increased serum C-terminal cross-linked telopeptide of type I collagen and osteoclast frequencies in tibia, resulting in decreased tibia density (P = 0.002) and mechanical properties. Dietary 12% RPS intervention increased the content of fluorescein isothiocyanate dextran in serum (P = 0.002), a biomarker of gut leak, and reduced the TNF-α and IL-6 levels in ileum of DSS-treated ducks. Additionally, RPS supplementation altered the gut microbiota by increasing Firmicutes (P = 0.026), decreasing Bacteroidetes (P = 0.017), and enhancing the production of short-chain fatty acids (acetic acid and butyric acid, P < 0.05). Consequently, RPS improved tibia microstructure and strength in DSS-exposed birds. These benefits were associated with reduced osteoclast activity, lower osteoclast numbers, and elevated serum phosphorus contents.

Introduction

Leg problems in meat ducks not only compromise animal welfare but also likely reduce growth performance, as affected birds struggle to access feed and water. Recent reports indicate that approximately 21% of commercial meat ducks suffer from leg abnormalities (Yin et al., 2025) with intestinal barrier dysfunction emerging as a potential underlying cause. Meat ducks are highly sensitive to environmental stress, pathogens, and foodborne toxins, which often induce low-grade intestinal inflammation (Bi et al., 2018). This chronic inflammatory state negatively impacts overall growth and may contribute to infections, metabolic disorders, and gait impairments. Indeed, a well-established connection between intestinal health and bone metabolism has been observed in human inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) (Targownik et al., 2013), and similar mechanisms may apply to poultry. Pro-inflammatory cytokines, including tumour necrosis factor α (TNF-α), interleukin-6 (IL-6), and IL-1β, activated via the nuclear factor kappa B (NF-κB) pathway, have been shown to suppress bone formation and/or promote bone resorption (Irwin et al., 2013; Targownik et al., 2013). Furthermore, inflammatory cytokines modulate bone homeostasis by altering the receptor activator of nuclear factor-κ B ligand (RANKL)/osteoprotegerin (OPG) axis. Nuclear factor-κ B ligand binds to receptor activator of NF-κB (RANK) on osteoclast precursors, driving osteoclast differentiation, while OPG acts as a decoy receptor, inhibiting this interaction (Moschen et al., 2005). Notably, TNF-α upregulates RANKL expression and enhances its binding affinity, exacerbating osteoclastogenesis (Yamamoto et al., 2006). Studies in murine colitis models demonstrate that the suppression of NF-κB-mediated gut inflammation could alleviate bone loss (Ke et al., 2019; Metzger et al., 2019), supporting the existence of a “gut-bone” axis. Similarly, in broiler chickens, intestinal barrier dysfunction and elevated ileal inflammatory cytokines correlate with poor tibia quality and reduced bone mass (Zhang et al., 2021). These findings highlight the critical role of intestinal barrier integrity and inflammation control in maintaining skeletal health in poultry.

Multiple therapeutic strategies exist for treating intestinal dysfunction including antibiotics, which historically effectively controlled dysbiosis and enteropathogenesis (Dibner and Richards, 2005). The recent ban on antibiotic growth promoters has increased the incidence of intestinal injury and related diseases (Karavolias et al., 2018). This shift highlights the importance of early intervention, where preventive measures (e.g., dietary modifications) during preclinical or initial stages of intestinal disorders may help halt the progression of enteritis and subsequent bone loss. Among alternative strategies, resistant starch (RS) has emerged as a promising candidate. Resistant starch serves as a fermentable substrate for hindgut microbiota, fostering a mutualistic host-microbiome relationship that enhances intestinal health. A meta-analysis including nine randomized controlled trials administering RS at doses varying from 22 to 45 g/d to healthy subjects revealed significant improvements in fecal weight, butyrate content, and fecal pH following RS supplementation (Shen et al., 2017). A study in broilers showed that diets containing 10% RS increased duodenal weight, cecal weight, and ileal length, along with the increased fermentative production of short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) (Oluseyifunmi et al., 2024). Dietary supplementation of 12% raw potato starch (RPS, a type Ⅱ RS) improved gut barrier function, induced favorable microbiota shifts, and alleviated inflammation in commercial duck production (Qin et al., 2019, 2020). The benefits of RS extend beyond intestinal health to bone metabolism. Rodent models have shown that RS attenuates bone loss in ovariectomized mice and reduces arthritis-induced bone erosion through microbiota-mediated modulation of bone-marrow inflammation (Bai et al., 2021). Regarding studies focusing on bone metabolism in meat ducks, recent research revealed that dietary 12% RPS supplementation improves tibia mass and microstructure, reduces osteoclast-mediated bone resorption (Zhang et al., 2022a), and mitigates Escherichia coli-induced bone loss by inhibiting NF-κB inflammasome activation (Zhang et al., 2022b), strongly supporting the existence of a functional “gut-bone” axis in meat ducks.

Despite the importance of revealing the relationship between chronic gut inflammation and bone metabolism, as well as further evaluating the effectiveness of dietary modulation of metabolism, there is a lack of chronic enteritis models in meat ducks. In domestic birds, several experimental models have been established using feed deprivation, pathogenic infections, and stress-inducing agents to induce intestinal barrier injury and dysfunction, as summarized previously (Gilani et al., 2021). Among these, dextran sulfate sodium (DSS), a heparin-like polysaccharide, has emerged as a particularly valuable tool. It is widely recognized as an effective inducer of experimental colitis in rodents due to its direct toxicity to colonic epithelial cells, which triggers inflammatory cell infiltration and microbial dysbiosis (Solomon et al., 2010). Similarly, the administration of DSS through drinking water (Chen and Yu, 2022; Chen et al., 2023) or oral gavage (Liu et al., 2022) has also been shown to successfully induce small intestinal barrier damage in broilers due to its controllability, as evidenced by the disrupted intestinal structure, diffuse enteric inflammation, and increased gut permeability (Dal Pont et al., 2021; Chen and Yu, 2022; Liu et al., 2022; Chen et al., 2023). Considering inconsistent water consumption among individuals, administration of DSS via drinking water may be difficult to obtain exact results in an enteritis model of the waterfowl. Alternatively, oral gavage of DSS may be an appropriate option for inducing enteric inflammation in meat ducks.

Previous studies have demonstrated that enteritis promotes bone loss and that RS improves both intestinal barrier function and bone health. However, the specific effects of dietary RPS on bone metabolism in growing meat ducks (1 to 21 d) remain unclear. It was reported that this developmental period is particularly critical due to rapid skeletal changes (Zhang et al., 2019). Therefore, in this study, the characterized RPS properties were first evaluated using electron microscopy and metabolic trials. Subsequently, a comprehensive evaluation of dietary RPS supplementation was conducted to assess its effects on intestinal barrier integrity and bone metabolism parameters in both healthy ducks and an established enteritis model. This study hypothesized that optimal dietary RPS supplementation would enhance intestinal barrier function in 21-d-old ducks and consequently improve tibial biomechanical properties and microstructure in DSS-treated ducks, potentially via the “gut-bone” axis. The resulting data provide novel mechanistic insights that could inform evidence-based applications of resistant starch in commercial poultry.

Materials and methods

Animal ethics statement

Care, handling, and sampling procedures were approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee of Henan Agricultural University before initiation of the trial (approval No.: HNND-2022-112).

Structure of RPS and digestion in vitro

The structural characteristics of RPS, which were procured from AVEVE Co., Ltd (Leuven, Flemish Brabant, Belgium), were scanned using an electron microscope (HITACHI Model S-3000H, Hitachi High-Technologies Co, Tokyo, Japan) following the previous description (Polesi and Sarmento, 2011). The crystallinity patterns of RPS were analyzed by an X-ray diffractometer (Shimadzu XRD 7000, Shimadzu Scientific Instruments, Inc., Beijing, China) with Cu Kα radiation over a 2θ range of 5° to 40°. The in vitro digestion properties of RPS were measured using a standardized in vitro gastro-intestinal experimental protocol including the oral, gastric, and intestinal phases (Minekus et al., 2014). In this process, approximal 0.5 g of RPS was thoroughly mixed with 5 mL salivary fluid and then added to 7.5 mL of simulated gastric fluids consisting of 1.6 mL pepsin and 5 μL CaCl2 (pH = 3) for 2 h at 37 °C with horizontal shaking at 20 r/min. For the intestinal phase, 11 mL intestinal fluid, 2.5 mL bile salt solution, 40 μL CaCl2, and 5 mL pancreatin (pH = 7) were added to the reaction system and incubated for 2 h at 37 °C with horizontal shaking (20 r/min). A sample (100 μL) was taken out from this system and mixed with 0.4 mL of ethanol to stop the reaction at various time points. After centrifugation, the supernatant was used to quantify the amount of digested starch following the previous method (Shen et al., 2022).

Animals, diets, and experimentaldesign

A total of 576 Cherry Valley M3 ducklings obtained from a local hatchery were used in the current study. All birds were housed in a 23-h light and 1-h dark cycle room from 1 to 21 d and were allowed to drink and feed ad libitum. Ambient temperature was maintained at 32 ± 2 °C from d 1 to 3 post-hatch, then gradually reduced following a linear thermal profile to reach 22 °C by d 21. Relative humidity and air movement were adjusted to bird age, following normal management practices.

A metabolism test was performed from 16 Cherry Valley M3 male medium meat ducks at 21-d-old to analyze the crude protein (CP) content and nitrogen-corrected apparent metabolizable energy (AMEn) of RPS. Sixteen ducks were randomly allocated to one of 16 metabolism cages (0.68 m × 0.68 m × 0.36 m). Following a 3-d adaptation period to the environment and diets, ducks were subjected to a 48-h feed withdrawal. Subsequently, they received either the basal diet or the RPS diet via orogastric administration at a rate of 2% of their live body weight (n = 8 per diet). To examine the effect of RPS on the bone mass of meat ducks and whether it was associated with the intestinal inflammation, the enteritis model of meat ducks was established using oral DSS administration and ducklings were randomly allocated to three groups consisting of 1) the basal diet (control group, Ctrl), 2) the basal diet with DSS treatment group (DSS), and 3) the 12% RPS diet with DSS treatment group (DSS + RPS). Each group had eight replicates with 12 birds each. The DSS at 1.2 g/bird per day was given consecutively by oral gavage starting at 14 d of age for 21 d. Body weight, feed intake, and the number of dead birds on a pen basis were recorded during the trial period. At 21 d, after fasting for 12 h, one bird with a body weight close to the pen average was sampled. Blood was collected from the jugular vein and centrifuged to obtain serum. The mucosa of mid-ileum was collected for the analysis of intestinal barrier function and inflammatory markers. Cecal contents were collected for microbiota analysis. The bone marrow and proximal bone of the left tibia, as well as the whole right tibia, were harvested from two of the remaining ducks for bone characteristics and microstructure analysis.

Tibia characteristics

The length and average width of the tibia at mid-diaphysis (at 50% of length) were measured after the removal of soft tissues. The mechanical properties of tibiae were measured using a three-point bending test on a texture analyzer (TA.XT. Plus, Stable Micro Systems Ltd., Godalming, Surrey, United Kingdom) equipped with a 50-kg load cell. Force-displacement data were recorded to measure bone stiffness, yield load, fracture load, and area under the curve (AUC). After mechanical testing, the fractured tibiae were collected for subsequent composition analysis. Fat-free weight, ash content, and density were measured according to the method described by Zhang et al. (2019), with minor modifications. Briefly, bone volume was measured by the water displacement method. Fat-free weight was obtained after extracting with refluxing ethyl ether in a Soxhlet apparatus for 16 h and then oven-dried at 108 °C for 24 h. Bone density was calculated as fat-free weight (g) divided by volume. The defatted bones were then ashed in a muffle furnace at 550 °C for 24 h to analyze ash content.

Morphology of cortical and trabecular bone

The microstructure of the tibia was determined using micro-CT as previously described (Zhang et al., 2021). The left tibia was dried at 65 °C prior to micro-CT scanning to avoid an influence of the water content within the bone. Images of the cortical bone from the mid-diaphysis and trabecular bone from metaphysis, starting at the surface of the condyles were obtained by micro-CT with a voxel size of 25 mm (GE Healthcare, Piscataway, NJ, USA; 90 kV). To remove noise, images were first processed with a Gaussian filter. Bone morphology (both cortical and trabecular) was then quantified using ImageJ (version 1.54p) with the Bone J plugin.

He volume of interested cortical bone was chosen from the tibial mid-diaphysis and extended proximal-distally for a total length of 37 mm. The center of the diaphysis of the tibia was automatically classified into trabeculae and cortex. The thickness of cortical bone was quantified in diaphysis. To measure the trabecular bone in the proximal end of the tibia (metaphysis), a 4-mm-long region was defined starting 9 mm distal to the condylar surface. To exclude denser cortical regions at the bone surface, the outer 0.5 mm of the bone surface was removed from the region of interest. The total volume and the volume of trabecular bone were quantified, and bone volume to total volume ratio (BV/TV) was calculated.

Histological analysis of tibia

The fixed proximal tibia samples were decalcified, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned frontally into 10-μm slices. These sections were stained for tartrate-resistant acid phosphatase (TRAP) using a commercial kit (Cat. No. 387A, Sigma–Aldrich Corporation, St. Louis, MO, USA). Additionally, to assess osteoblast frequency, tibial sections were stained with an alkaline phosphatase (ALP) detection kit (Cat. No. EEA002, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cleveland, OH, USA). Histopathological images were captured and analyzed using a microscope (Nikon Corporation, Tokyo, Japan).

Intestinal permeability

On 21 d, ducks were randomly selected to gavage 4.16 mg/kg body weight fluorescein isothiocyanate dextran (FITC-d, Sigma–Aldrich Corporation, St. Louis, MO, USA), and blood from the jugular vein was collected after 2.5-h administration. The content of FITC-d in serum was quantified by measuring fluorescence intensity at 485 nm excitation and 528 nm emission.

Intestinal inflammation analysis

The supernatants of ileal mucosa were obtained after being homogenized in an ice-cold saline solution (1:9, wt/vol) followed by centrifugation. The contents of IL-1β (Cat. No. 15124), IL-6 (Cat. No. 5415), and TNF-α (Cat. No. 8267) were analyzed with the specific enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kits, according to the manufacturer's instructions strictly. Total protein content was quantified with a bicinchoninic acid protein assay (Cat. No. A045-2-2). All the kits were provided by Jiangsu Meimian Industrial Co., Ltd. (Yancheng, Jiangsu, China). All samples were tested in triplicate within each essay.

16S rRNA gene sequencing

The isolation of DNA from cecal content was performed, and the content and purity of DNA were then evaluated using a Nanodrop spectrophotometer (Nanodrop Technologies, LLC, Rockland, Wilmington, USA). Subsequently, the region V4 of 16S rRNA genes (Primer, F: 5′-GTGCCAGCMGCCGCGGTAA-3′, R: 5′-GGACTACHVGGGTWTCTAAT-3′) was amplified using the specific primers. Replicate amplicons were pooled, purified, and sequenced using an Illumina MiSeq platform (Illumina Inc., San Diego, CA, USA). Reads from the original DNA fragments were quality-filtered, and sequences were clustered into operational taxonomic units (OTUs) at a 97% identity threshold. Alpha- and beta-diversity indices were calculated using Vegan and GUniFrac 1.9 package in R software (version 4.3.3), respectively.

SCFA analysis

The contents of SCFAs in cecal contents were measured by gas chromatography (GC) (Varian CP-3800, Varian, Inc., Palo Alto, CA, USA). Briefly, approximately 0.5 g of the sample was diluted with 2 mL ultrapure water and centrifuged at 3000 × g for 15 min, the supernatant was harvested, and then 1 mL of supernatant was mixed with 0.2 mL ice-cold 25% metaphosphoric acid solution at 4 °C for 30 min. After centrifuging at 3000 × g for 15 min, the contents of acetate, propionate, and butyrate were qualified by the GC system.

Serum biochemistry

Serum total protein, albumin, globulin, alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), Ca, and P were quantified with Biochemistry Analyzer (Yellow Springs Instrument Co. Inc., Yellow Springs, OH, USA) using detection kits (Cat. No. C004-2-1 and C006-1-1 for Ca and P, respectively, Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, Nanjing, Jiangsu China). Serum D-lactate (Cat. No. S0204S), IL-1β (Cat. No. 15124), IL-6 (Cat. No. 5415), and TNF-α (Cat. No. 8267) contents were measured using ELISA commercial kits (Jiangsu Meimian Industrial Co., Ltd., Yancheng, Jiangsu, China). The content of serum endotoxin was measured by an ELISA kit (Mosak Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Wuhan, Hubei, China). As the markers of bone resorption, serum contents of C-terminal cross-linked telopeptide of type Ⅰ collagen (CTx; Cat. No. H287-1-2) and TRAP (Cat. No. D023-1-1) were assessed using commercial kits. Serum contents of procollagen type Ⅰ N-terminal propeptide (P1NP; Cat. No. H285-1-2) and ALP (Cat. No. A059-2-2), the markers of bone formation, were analyzed with corresponding assay kits. These related kits for serum bone turnover were obtained from Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute (Nanjing, Jiangshu, China).

Gene expression analysis

The RNA was reverse transcribed into cDNA using a cDNA Reverse Transcription Kit (Cat. No. RR037A, TaKaRa, Dalian, Liaoning, China). Statistical analysis

Data were checked for normality and equal variance using the Shapiro–Wilk and Levene's tests, respectively. Of note, a n+1 was applied to transfer mortality data to fit normal distribution. When the data were recognized as normally distributed and exhibited homogeneity of variance, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey's post hoc test was performed to evaluate the effects of RPS and DSS on intestinal barrier function and bone parameters. Linear and quadratic regression was conducted to examine the alterations of interested parameters response to dietary RPS levels. In addition, Pearson's correlation analysis was used to identify the relationship between tibia quality and serum biochemical parameters. Broken-line regression analysis was used to evaluate the recommended levels of dietary RPS inclusion in diets based on tibia ash content and strength, as well as serum content of D-lactate for 21-d-old meat ducks using the nonlinear regression (NLIN) procedure as the following:y=L+U×(R–x),where y is the response variable, x is the dietary RPS level, R is the breakpoint (optimal level), L is the estimated response at the breakpoint, U is the slope of the line for x < R, and (R – x) denotes. All the statistical results were presented as mean and standard deviation. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05, and a tendency or trend was considered when 0.05 ≤ P < 0.10.

Results

Molecular structure and digestive characteristics of RPS

The micrographs of the RPS studied showed an irregular and angular polygonal structure with a rough surface (Fig. 1A). The crystallinity pattern for RPS exhibited that characteristic diffraction peaks appear prominently at 2θ values of 17.6°, 22.5°, and 24.2°, indicating RS in raw potato took on a B-type crystal structure (Fig. 1B). In addition, the digestibility of RPS was analyzed through releasing sugars in the digestive juice, which show that the digestibility of RPS was not more than 20% in this study, indicating that RPS has good resistance to enzymatic hydrolysis in the digestive system (Fig. 1C). Dietary RPS prevented DSS-induced barrier leak and inflammation

To further define the link between RPS, intestinal inflammation, and bone metabolism, an enteritis model of meat ducks was established using DSS and found that DSS administration decreased body weight at 21 d when compared to the Ctrl group (P > 0.05), which was significantly improved by dietary RPS treatment in DSS-treated ducks (P = 0.039; Table 7). There was no significant difference in feed intake, feed to weight gain ratio, and mortality among treatment groups from 1 to 21 d (P > 0.05). Moreover, when compared to Ctrl ducks, DSS manipulation resulted in an increase in circulating FITC-d content (P = 0.002), which was normalized by supplementing 12% RPS diet (Table 8). As a direct result, the increased permeability in DSS birds was accompanied by an increase in TNF-α and IL-1β levels in ileal mucosa (P < 0.05), while IL-6 and IL-10 contents remained unchanged (P > 0.05). This led to a significant increase in the TNF-α-to-IL-10 ratio (P = 0.002). Dietary RPS altered gut microbiome and SCFAs content

There was a clear difference in the microbiota community structure among the Ctrl, DSS, and DSS + RPS birds (Fig. 3A). The species richness of gut microbiota was similar in response to DSS and RPS treatment, as indicated by the similar Chao1 indices (P = 0.616), whereas the evenness of gut microbiota in DSS + RPS ducks was significantly increased as compared to the DSS group (P = 0.032; Fig. 3B and C). The gut microbiota in meat ducks was dominated by the Firmicutes, Bacteroidetes, and Proteobacteria at the phylum level (Fig. 3D). Dextran sodium sulphate administration notably increased the abundance of Proteobacteria (P = 0.048), along with similar population of Firmicutes and Bacteroidetes as compared to the Ctrl group (P > 0.05). Thereafter, the diet containing 12% RPS increased the prevalence of Firmicutes (P = 0.026), decreasing Bacteroidetes (P = 0.041) and Proteobacteria (P = 0.088) in DSS-treated birds, which was accompanied by an increased ratio of Firmicutes to Bacteroidetes (Fig. 3E to H). Reflecting on SCFAs content, DSS treatment failed to reduce the content of SCFAs in canal thyme (P > 0.05), when compared with their normal counterparts. Discussion

Emerging evidence suggests that dietary RS may protect against gastrointestinal disorders, bone loss, and rheumatoid arthritis (Qin et al., 2019; Bai et al., 2021; Zhang et al., 2022a). This suggests that dietary supplementation with RPS, a type Ⅱ RS, may serve as a beneficial nutritional strategy for mitigating intestinal dysfunction and its complications, including osteoporosis. Herein, the roles of RPS in intestinal barrier function and bone metabolism were evaluated in both healthy ducks (fed a basal diet without DSS challenge) and ducks with enteritis. Dietary RPS inclusion enhanced intestinal integrity and reduced serum inflammatory status in a dose-dependent manner. Employing a DSS-induced enteritis model of meat ducks, the present study indicated that the 12% RPS diet reduced the intestinal disorders induced by DSS administration, including intestinal barrier leakage, dysbiosis, and intestinal inflammation. These positive effects were closely correlated with the improvement in tibia mass and mechanical properties associated with suppressing bone resorption in DSS meat ducks.

Recent studies have further confirmed the beneficial effects of RS on intestinal health (Wen et al., 2023) and its potential role in arthritis therapy (Bai et al., 2021). Notably, research in meat ducks has demonstrated that a 12% RPS diet serving as a source of RS enhances intestinal integrity and mitigates enteritis, likely due to modulations in gut microbiota composition (Qin et al., 2023). A significant protective effect of dietary 12% RS against bone loss by regulating the gut microbiota composition and inflammation was also noticed in ovariectomized mice (Tousen et al., 2011, 2016, 2019). In the present study, dietary RPS supplementation contributed to linear and quadratic increases in tibia ash and Ca contents, and bone strength. Importantly, these improvements in tibia parameters were negatively correlated with serum D-lactate, a biomarker of gut permeability, TNF-α, and TRAP. Given the established connection between intestinal health and bone metabolism in certain intestinal disorders (Targownik et al., 2013), it is possible that RPS-enhanced tibia mass in meat ducks may be mediated, at least in part, by the improved intestinal barrier function.

To test this hypothesis, an enteritis model of meat ducks was established using DSS and dietary 12% RPS. According to the results of Broken-line regression analysis, the recommended level of dietary RPS during 1 to 21 d meat ducks was 12.0%, 12.6%, and 11.8% based on the ash content, tibia strength, and serum D-lactate content, respectively. It was pointed out that intestinal injury is a typical pathological feature of the DSS-induced enteritis model in broilers, manifested by reduced intestinal weight (Chen et al., 2023) and infiltration of pro-inflammatory cytokines (Dal Pont et al., 2021). Oral DSS administration impaired intestine morphological changes, induced generalized mild and non-necrotic enteritis, and thus resulted in body weight loss, diarrhea, and intestinal bleeding in broilers (Menconi et al., 2015; Chen et al., 2023). In the present study, the harmful consequences of DSS challenge in meat ducks were associated with impaired gut integrity and enteritis, as evidenced by elevated serum FITC-d content and increased pro-inflammatory cytokine contents. The decreased body weight observed in this study can be partially explained by the injured intestinal barrier integrity and enteric inflammation, as previously reported in both broilers and laying hens (Simon et al., 2016; Chen et al., 2023). As a subgroup of dietary fiber, dietary RS could be consumed by microbiota-anchored hindgut and producing beneficial effects on intestinal health (Maier et al., 2017; Warren et al., 2018). Emerging research on meat ducks showed that a diet with 12% RPS has been correlated with an enhanced gut barrier, inhibited inflammatory response, and improved gut microbiota composition (Qin et al., 2019, 2020; Zhang et al., 2022b). Moreover, RS could be also used as substrates by bacterial species to generate important metabolites including SCFAs (Qin et al., 2020). In the current study, unweighted Principal coordinate analyses (PCoA) demonstrated distinct clusters separating the RS + DSS group from the DSS and Ctrl groups, suggesting that dietary 12% RPS supplementation can significantly modify the cecal microbiota of meat ducks. Specifically, DSS-treated birds exhibited a lower abundance of Firmicutes, and higher proportions of Bacteroidetes and Proteobacteria, which is consistent with previous research in broilers (Chen and Yu, 2022). The Firmicutes phylum is predominant in the gut of poultry and is associated with the efficiency of energy harvesting (Hou et al., 2016). A reduction in the proportion of Firmicutes with an increase in the abundance of both Proteobacteria and Bacteroidetes was reported in IBD subjects (Nishida et al., 2018). The phylum Proteobacteria contains many opportunistic pathogens, and an increase in its abundance is correlated to gut dysbiosis (Shin et al., 2015). Therefore, increasing the proportion of Firmicutes phylum together with decreasing the abundance of Proteobacteria and Bacteroidetes lends support to RPS inclusion helping to reverse the dysbiosis induced by DSS administration in meat ducks. As a main consequence, the alteration of cecal microbiota composition also mirrored the increase in acetic acid and butyric acid, which, in turn, facilitated the intestinal barrier function and depressed the intestinal inflammation in ducks (Feng et al., 2018).

It is well established that the inflammation motivated by the gut leak and dysbiosis is closely linked to bone loss (Targownik et al., 2013; Zhang et al., 2021). The impaired intestinal barrier and microbiota balance of broilers were noticed to upregulate the expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines in both ileum and bone marrow, resulting in inferior tibial material properties and bone mass in broilers (Zhang et al., 2021). Poor bone health and a higher risk of fractures were observed in both children and adults who were suffering from IBD (Targownik et al., 2013). Studies on the colitis murine model have also shown that the toxic DSS treatment reduced bone mass and altered the microarchitecture of mice (Dobie et al., 2018; Hamdani et al., 2008; Metzger et al., 2019). Similarly, in the current study, the DSS birds exhibited decreased BV/TV in the metaphysis and cortical thickness in the diaphysis, although there was no appreciable difference in tibia ash content and dimension. It might be on account of the relatively short duration of DSS. What is worse, oral DSS administration caused lower yield load, ultimate load, and stiffness of the tibia, indicating DSS induced a decrease in mechanical properties of the tibia, which was reported in previous studies (Ke et al., 2019; Metzger et al., 2019). Area under the curve is a measure of the amount of energy required to cause a fracture, and a larger amount of energy for failure is required in ductile bones than in brittle bones. The numerical decrease of AUC in the DSS group compared with the Ctrl group implied that the bones of the Ctrl ducks were stiffer and stronger than those of the DSS group. In this case, dietary 12% RPS supplementation restored the bone loss and compromised the tibia strength of meat ducks. Certainly, the improved roles of RS have been confirmed in ovariectomized mice (Tousen et al., 2019) and meat ducks (Zhang et al., 2022a,b).

The inhibitory effects of dietary RPS on inflammatory response and bone resorption are a likely explanation for the improved bone quality of DSS-treated ducks in this study. Increased bone resorption was found in DSS-treated birds, as supported by higher circulating bone resorption marker CTx content. The effect of colitis on bone resorption is consistent with previous reports in mouse models of colitis-associated osteopenia (Hamdani et al., 2008) and IBD sufferers (Targownik et al., 2013; Thearle et al., 2000). Some pro-inflammatory cytokines associated with the inflammatory response elicited in IBD and animal models of colitis such as TNF-α, IL-1, and IL-6 were shown to enhance osteoclastogenesis and osteoclast activity (Irwin et al., 2013; Targownik et al., 2013). Anti-TNF-α therapy for arthritis was accompanied by osteoclast precursors in the serum of mice (Li et al., 2004). In the present study, enhanced inflammatory response in bone marrow induced the increase of TRAP-positive cells (osteoclasts) and the upregulated mRNA abundance of cathepsin K, a gene encoding the cysteine protease that degrades bone matrix proteins (Novinec and Lenarčič, 2013). This might be attributed to the higher expression of RANKL induced by DSS manipulation, which binds to RANK expressed on the surface of osteoclasts to induce osteoclast differentiation (Yamamoto et al., 2006). Accordingly, attenuation of NF-κB-mediated inflammatory response in intestinal epithelial cells or remission of the inflammatory state in both gut and bone was noticed to mitigate bone loss by inhibiting osteoclastogenesis and osteoclast activity (Ke et al., 2019; Metzger et al., 2019). In collagen-induced arthritis, less bone erosion and increased bone mass in mice fed the RS diet were found to be correlated with IL-10 production and splenic Treg cell expansion (Bai et al., 2021). The outcomes of the recent trials on meat ducks indicated that dietary 12% RPS supplementation improved tibia quality by decreasing the bone resorption mediated by the secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines (Zhang et al., 2022a). Furthermore, RPS alleviated E. coli induced bone loss by inhibiting NF-κB inflammasome activation (Zhang et al., 2022b). Following previous research, the ducks fed 12% RPS diets under the DSS-challenge condition displayed reduced numbers of TRAP-positive cells, lower serum CTx content, and decreased cathepsin K transcription, along with downregulated expressions of pro-inflammatory cytokines in the tibia. These data indicate that dietary 12% RPS supplementation probably reduced bone resorption induced by DSS administration by inhibiting the release of inflammatory factors that can induce osteoclastogenesis because of impaired intestinal integrity and/or dysbiosis in the present study.

Decreased bone formation induced by DSS challenge was observed in this study is in agreement with previous studies in experimental models of colitis and importantly in IBD sufferers (Croucher et al., 1993; Hamdani et al., 2008). The DSS-induced colitis model in mice hindered bone formation by reducing trabecular double-labeling surface, mineralizing surface, and bone formation rate (Hamdani et al., 2008). Moreover, reduced bone formation was also reported in IBD sufferers (Croucher et al., 1993). Although the mechanisms linking DSS exposure to suppressed bone formation are not completely elaborated, TNF-α might be one of the most plausible contributors. Apart from increasing osteoclast activation, TNF-α was also shown to decrease osteoblast differentiation (Gilbert et al., 2000; Harada and Rodan, 2003). Anti-TNF-α therapy for infected animals was accompanied by a decline in bone formation markers in serum (Bernstein et al., 2005), further supporting a role for TNF-α in suppressing bone formation in the current study, where oral DSS treatment led to a decrease in circulating bone resorption markers including P1NP and ALP. As a key mediator of osteoblast differentiation and chondrocyte maturation, RUNX2 expression is upregulated in early bone formation (Komori, 2019). The diet containing 12% RPS induced an increase in RUNX2 mRNA expression in the tibia, and this could partly explain how RS could mitigate the effects of DSS in the DSS-RS treated ducks. Further studies are required to illustrate the possibility. In addition, increased P absorption might be another contributing factor for RS supplementation promoting bone mineralization, which was related to the production of SCFAs in the process of RS fermentation by bacterial species (Zhang et al., 2022a). It was pointed out that decreased intestinal tract pH via supplementation of an acidifier could produce better nutrient digestibility and increase mineral retention such as Ca and total P in broilers (Nourmohammadi et al., 2012; Zhang et al., 2021). Taken together, the above data demonstrated the diet supplemented with a 12% RPS diet increases serum P content to promote bone mineralization and tend to protect DSS-treated ducks from the inhibition of bone formation.

There are three limitations in this study. The first is the scope of the enteritis model. While DSS is well-documented to induce enteritis in mammalian models, its effects vary with dose, duration and species-specific responses (Yang and Merlin, 2024). In this study, the DSS enteritis model was designed to replicate intestinal dysfunction, but the absence of segment-specific biomechanical data (duodenum, jejunum, cecum) limits the ability to conclusively determine whether inflammation was localized to the cecum or extended to the small intestine. The second is the duration of this study. The 21-d experimental period may not reflect long-term RPS and DSS effects on intestinal barrier function and bone metabolism. Third, statistical assessment was limited due to unequal variances and non-normal distribution in the raw data, likely stemming from an insufficient sample size. Therefore, this study admit the possibility that some of the conclusions may include overestimation or underestimation of the roles of RPS or DSS in tibia mass and intestine-derived inflammation of meat ducks. Additional research will be essential to address these limitations in domesticated birds, including refining enteritis models, exploring different sources and levels of RS supplementation, and evaluating their long-term effects on intestinal homeostasis and bone metabolism.

Conclusion

This study characterized RPS as a polygonal B-type crystalline carbohydrate with round/oval granules, exhibiting a AMEn value of 2.67 kcal/g for 21-d-old meat ducks. Dietary supplementation with 12% RPS significantly enhanced gut barrier function through increasing the Firmicutes-to-Bacteroidetes ratio and boosting production of SCFAs, particularly acetic acid and butyric acid. Furthermore, this study identified that DSS-induced colitis caused osteopenia and reduced bone mechanical strength of growing meat ducks. The dietary 12% RPS intervention effectively restored tibia properties, which was strongly associated with beneficial modifications in gut microbiota composition, barrier function, and osteoclast regulation. These findings contribute to mounting evidence that dietary RS supplementation may serve as a therapeutic intervention or complementary strategy for managing bone loss, inflammatory conditions, and microbial dysbiosis in both clinical and agricultural settings.
