Demographic skew and market dynamics reveal unsustainable trade and conservation risks of the West African dwarf crocodile
Authors: Francis O Arimoro, Matthew P Dore, Robert B Ikomi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe West African dwarf crocodile (Osteolaemus tetraspis), classified as Vulnerable by the IUCN and listed under CITES Appendix I, is increasingly threatened by unregulated trade and habitat degradation. This study investigated the trade dynamics of the species across Delta and Edo States, Nigeria, focusing on market structure, trade routes, seasonal trends, and size-class composition. Market surveys, semi-structured interviews, and ecological observations were employed to examine trade dynamics of the West African dwarf crocodile from 2019 to 2021 identified nine key markets, with Ugbenu emerging as the primary hub, recording 1,818 individuals over 105 market days. Trade was dominated by mid-sized, sexually mature crocodiles (101–120 cm), while juveniles (0–20 cm and 21–40 cm) were scarcely represented, suggesting depletion of older cohorts. The trade is economically motivated and gendered, with middlewomen controlling over 80% of market flow, sourcing from remote riverine communities. Hunting methods included wire traps, baited hooks, and burrow excavation, raise ecological and welfare concerns. Introduction

The West African Dwarf Crocodile (Osteolaemus tetraspis), (WADC) the smallest of Africa’s three crocodilian species, is a cryptic, nocturnal reptile endemic to the swamp forests and wetlands of West and Central Africa. It plays a vital ecological role in nutrient cycling and prey population regulation within its habitat1,2. Despite its ecological importance, O. tetraspis faces increasing threats from habitat degradation, hunting, and unregulated trade. The species is currently listed as Vulnerable by the International Union for Conservation of Nature (IUCN) The species is listed on the IUCN Red List and included in Appendix I of the Convention on International Trade in Endangered Species of Wild Fauna and Flora (CITES), which confers the highest level of protection by prohibiting international trade3,4.

In Nigeria, particularly within the biodiverse yet ecologically fragile Western Niger Delta, the trade in O. tetraspis has intensified. This region, characterized by extensive swamp forests and riverine systems, serves as both a habitat and a hunting ground for the species. Local communities, driven by economic necessity and cultural practices, engage in the capture and sale of dwarf crocodiles, often with little regard for conservation laws or ecological sustainability5,6. Bushmeat markets such as Ugbenu, Uwa, and Ogbe–Ijaw have emerged as focal points for this trade, where crocodiles are sold openly despite national and international legal protections7. The Ogbe–Ijaw market in Delta State exemplifies how wildlife-based business activities are deeply embedded in local economies, with crocodile trade contributing significantly to livelihoods despite its ecological cost. The saltwater crocodile (Crocodylus porosus) and O. tetraspis are both seriously threatened in this region due to overexploitation and habitat pressures7. These findings align with broader patterns observed across West Africa, where crocodile populations are declining due to trade-driven pressures and habitat fragmentation8.

While previous studies have examined general bushmeat consumption patterns in Nigeria8,9, there remains a critical gap in understanding the specific dynamics of trade in O. tetraspis. These include the market structures, trade routes, hunting techniques, and demographic characteristics of traded individuals. Moreover, the role of middlewomen often overlooked in wildlife trade literature is particularly significant in West African bushmeat markets, where they act as gatekeepers and powerbrokers, controlling the flow of wildlife products and influencing pricing and availability10. Recent research has also emphasized the importance of understanding crocodile population structure and demography to inform conservation strategies. Eaton11 highlighted the need for multiscale approaches to monitor population dynamics and trade impacts, noting that Osteolaemus spp. exhibit complex spatial patterns that are easily disrupted by overharvesting. Similarly, Smolensky12 underscored the urgency of integrating biodiversity conservation with socio-economic realities in Sub-Saharan Africa, particularly for reptile species like O. tetraspis that are heavily targeted in the bushmeat trade. In Guinea-Bissau, Pizzigalli et al.13 documented how trade and habitat disturbance disrupt crocodile activity patterns and population viability, findings that resonate with the situation in the Niger Delta. Furthermore, hunting methods such as wire traps, baited hooks, and burrow excavation are commonly employed, often resulting in injury or death to the animals14. These studies collectively reinforce the need for localized, data-driven conservation interventions that address both ecological and socio-economic dimensions of wildlife trade.

This study aims to fill these gaps by providing a comprehensive appraisal of the trade dynamics surrounding O. tetraspis in the Western Niger Delta. Through systematic market surveys conducted over a two-year period (October 2019–September 2021), we document the volume and demographic composition of crocodiles offered for sale, analyze hunting techniques and trade routes, and examine the socio-economic context underpinning this trade. By integrating ecological data with ethnographic insights, this study examines the interplay between conservation challenges and local livelihoods. We employed market surveys, semi-structured interviews, and ecological observations to capture both quantitative trade dynamics and qualitative perspectives on cultural practices and gendered roles. Importantly, socioeconomic data provided trade volumes and demographics, while ethnographic insights came from interviews and participant observations, revealing cultural norms and livelihood strategies. The findings are intended to inform policy and conservation strategies, emphasizing community engagement, enforcement of wildlife laws, and sustainable use models that balance ecological integrity with socio-economic realities (Fig. 1).

Results

Hierarchical market networks and trade pathways of West African dwarf crocodiles

The trade in West African dwarf crocodiles followed a structured commodity chain linking rural harvest sites to urban markets (Table 1). Local hunters supplied live animals, which were aggregated by middlewomen and transported via canoe and road networks to Ugbenu Market, where intermediaries accounted for nearly 80% of trade volume. Weekly surveys recorded 50–120 individuals per market, with occasional sales of eggs from gravid females. Actor mapping and semi-structured interviews confirmed a hierarchical organization of hunters, middlewomen, transporters, and vendors, while participant observation documented informal price negotiations, reliance on personal networks, and concealment during transit. Route verification through GPS tracking and subsequent field visits revealed recurrent transport corridors from riverine hunting zones along the Benin and Orhionmwon Rivers and Ughelli South to downstream markets (Fig. 2), with GIS analysis highlighting key transit nodes and strong spatial connectivity. Market surveys across Delta and Edo States showed primary aggregation at rural markets such as Koko and Pessu Waterside, redistribution through Ugbenu as the principal hub, and final retail in Benin City, which emerged as the dominant terminal market. Secondary outlets, including roadside stalls and bushmeat sections in Warri and Jesse, handled smaller but consistent volumes, reinforcing the hierarchical structure of the trade (Fig. 3).

Hunting techniques

Field visits to hunters’ camps in Ughelli South Local Government Area and along the Orhionmwon River corridor provided direct documentation of capture practices, including trap placement, burrow excavation, and on-site storage methods. Hunters consistently reported declining capture success, with several indicating a recent shift from manual capture and burrow excavation to wire traps as traditional methods became less productive. Socio-economic data collected during these engagements highlighted strong income dependence on crocodile trade, particularly among middlewomen involved in aggregation and resale.

Five harvesting techniques targeting West African dwarf crocodiles were recorded through direct observation: wire traps, hooked baits, burrow excavation, manual capture, and occasional firearm use (Table 2; Figs. 4 and 5). Among these, wire traps equipped with spring mechanisms and positioned at burrow entrances were the most effective, accounting for the majority of recorded captures. Hooked baits and underwater trapping were less frequently employed but remained locally effective in specific habitats. Hunting intensity varied spatially, though evidence from field observations and hunter interviews indicates increasing reliance on wire traps in recent years.

Market survey results

Nine markets were identified as key points in the trade of WADC across Delta and Edo States (Table 3). Ugbenu Market was the dominant hub, recording 1,818 specimens over 105 market days in two years, with annual totals of 923 (2019–2020) and 895 (2020–2021). Monthly averages ranged from 74 to 78 specimens, confirming its role as the primary terminal market.

Trade volumes at Ugbenu market

Size-based variation in trade volumes

Analysis of trade volumes of WADC landed at Ugbenu Market revealed pronounced size-dependent variation across the two study periods, 2019–2020 and 2020–2021 (Table 4). Frequency distribution data demonstrated that trade was heavily skewed toward intermediate and large size classes. The 101–120 cm category consistently recorded the highest volumes, with 369 individuals in 2019–2020 and 360 individuals in 2020–2021. The next most traded categories were 121–140 cm, with 239 and 196 individuals respectively, and 81–100 cm, with 163 and 181 individuals across the two years. In contrast, the smallest size class (0–20 cm) was virtually absent, with no individuals recorded in 2019–2020 and only two in 2020–2021.

Comparative analysis of year-to-year trade volumes

A paired t-test was conducted to compare trade volumes of WADC across two consecutive periods (2019–2020 and 2020–2021) for nine size categories. The analysis revealed that most size classes exhibited no statistically significant differences between years (p > 0.05). However, three categories showed significant year-to-year variation. Trade volumes for the 61–80 cm category increased significantly in 2020–2021 compared to 2019–2020 (t = -2.7331, p = 0.0195). The 121–140 cm category displayed a significant difference (t = 2.1853, p ≈ 0.0500). The largest size category, 161–180 cm, exhibited a dramatic decline in 2020–2021 (t = 6.9663, p < 0.0001).

Temporal and seasonal variation in trade volume

Monthly trade volumes of WADC exhibited clear temporal and seasonal patterns across the two study periods (Fig. 8). Trade activity was consistently higher during the dry season (November–April) compared to the wet season (May–October). Seasonal totals confirmed this trend, with dry-season volumes reaching 541 individuals in 2019–2020 and 546 in 2020–2021, compared to 382 and 349 individuals in the wet season, respectively. Mean monthly volumes followed a similar pattern, averaging 90.17 and 91.00 during the dry season versus 63.67 and 58.17 in the wet season.

Temporal variation in monthly trade volumes during the 2019–2020 and 2020–2021 trading years.

Seasonal breakdown by size category revealed that larger individuals (101–120 cm and 121–140 cm) dominated trade in both seasons (Fig. 9), although their abundance was markedly higher in the dry season. For 2019–2020, the dry season recorded 209 individuals in the 101–120 cm class and 137 in the 121–140 cm class, compared to 160 and 102 in the wet season. A similar pattern was observed in 2020–2021, with 101–120 cm and 81–100 cm classes showing the highest dry-season volumes (194 and 123, respectively), while wet-season contributions remained lower (166 and 58). Notably, the largest size category (161–180 cm) nearly disappeared in the 2020–2021 wet season, dropping from six individuals in the previous year to zero. Discussion

The trade in West African dwarf crocodiles within the western Niger Delta is organized along a multi-tiered, gendered, and spatially distributed chain that reflects a complex interplay of ecological, economic, and socio-cultural factors. This structure, from primary collection to urban market distribution, mirrors broader patterns observed in bushmeat commodity chains across West Africa29,51. Primary collection is conducted by local hunters and farmers using traditional techniques such as wire traps, baiting, and burrow excavation. These methods are typically employed on foot or by canoe and are closely tied to seasonal cycles, particularly the dry season when access to wetland habitats is improved52. The integration of crocodile hunting with fishing activities suggests a subsistence-based strategy that maximizes resource extraction while minimizing effort, a pattern consistent with rural livelihoods in forest-savannah mosaics27,60.

The intermediary stage, dominated by middlewomen, is a defining feature of the trade chain. These women operate from river camps and village outposts, aggregating specimens from hunters and coordinating logistics to urban markets. Their control of approximately 80% of trade volume at Ugbenu market underscores the gendered nature of bushmeat commerce, where women often serve as key brokers and price negotiators53,54,58. This gendered control reflects broader socio-economic dynamics in West African bushmeat systems, where women’s roles are both economically central and culturally embedded. The trade chain therefore illustrates a fine balance between local cultural practices, women’s livelihoods, and market demand. Hunting and consumption practices are deeply rooted in cultural traditions, while women’s economic participation in market organization sustains household livelihoods. At the same time, rising urban demand intensifies trade pressure, situating crocodile exploitation within a broader regional context of bushmeat commodification. This balance highlights the complexity of conservation challenges, where ecological sustainability must be weighed against socio-cultural continuity and economic necessity.

Transport logistics involve a multimodal system, beginning with canoe-based movement from collection sites and transitioning to road vehicles for delivery to urban centers. Crocodiles are typically restrained alive to preserve market value, with weekly flows ranging from 50 to 120 individuals per market day. This live transport strategy, while economically advantageous, raises concerns about animal welfare and mortality during transit59.

A niche segment of the trade involves the sale of crocodile eggs, particularly from gravid females. These eggs are hand-carried by market women and sporadically appear in urban bushmeat sections, where they are valued for their purported medicinal properties. Although hatching is rare, the inclusion of eggs in the trade chain highlights the intersection of wildlife commerce with traditional medicine markets a phenomenon documented across West Africa55.

Urban market distribution represents the final stage of the trade chain, with cities such as Benin City, Warri, and Sapele serving as key consumption hubs. Benin City receives the bulk of its supply from Ugbenu, while Koko functions as a feeder market. In some cases, crocodiles are skinned and diverted into the leather trade, with Kano noted as a destination for such products. This integration of subsistence hunting with urban consumer demand and commercial processing reflects the commodification of wildlife in response to expanding urban markets30,56.

The capture of West African dwarf crocodiles employs five principal methods namely wire traps, hooked baits, burrow excavation, manual capture, and firearms with each reflecting cultural knowledge, economic necessity, and ecological consequences. Wire traps, the most common due to affordability, are placed at burrow entrances but often cause injury and stress, particularly when misapplied40; similar cable-based devices have harmed non-target species and juveniles when unregulated41. Hooked baits of snails, rats, or fish frequently result in fatal internal injuries, compromising welfare and meat quality42, with comparable cases in Queensland prompting legal scrutiny after baited hooks caused prolonged suffering43. Burrow excavation, though effective, is labour-intensive and risky for both hunters and animals, echoing concerns in gopher tortoise conservation where excavation can cause collapse and injury44. Manual capture, using spears or forked sticks, is typically reserved for smaller individuals but raises ethical concerns regarding safety and stress, consistent with reptile handling guidelines45. Firearms, though occasionally employed, damage carcasses and reduce market value, yet remain widespread in Nigerian bushmeat hunting, contributing to overharvesting and ecological degradation46–48. Collectively, these techniques illustrate indigenous knowledge adapted to ecological and market conditions but underscore urgent welfare and sustainability concerns. The reliance on wire traps and hooked baits highlights the need for regulatory oversight and community education, with management programs in Mozambique and Australia showing the value of integrating traditional practices with conservation science and regulated harvests49,50.

Trade dynamics revealed Ugbenu as the dominant aggregation hub, channelling crocodiles from multiple riverine communities and secondary markets. This hierarchical structure mirrors bushmeat trade systems across West Africa, where rural hunting zones supply intermediate hubs that funnel wildlife to urban centers8,27. Secondary markets such as Koko and Sapele played transitional roles, while Benin City emerged as the sole major urban terminal, consistently offering large volumes sourced from Ugbenu and surrounding rural markets. Such urban concentration reflects consumer demand patterns observed in other Nigerian bushmeat markets28,29. Field observations confirmed a well-integrated trade hierarchy that sustains high-volume exploitation but simultaneously amplifies conservation risks. The dominance of Ugbenu, coupled with consistently high volumes, indicates intense and potentially unsustainable harvesting pressure on crocodile populations in the Western Niger Delta. Comparable findings in other bushmeat networks show that structured trade chains accelerate wildlife depletion and complicate enforcement29,31. These results highlight the urgent need for policy interventions that combine community participation, regulated harvests, and spatial monitoring to mitigate exploitation risks and safeguard dwarf crocodile populations.

Our findings show a strongly size-biased structure in the WADC trade at Ugbenu Market, dominated by intermediate size classes (81–140 cm) and consistently peaking at 101–120 cm. This demand for mid-sized individuals reflects market optimisation for meat yield and handling efficiency, patterns widely documented in reptile and broader wildlife trade systems32–34. Juveniles (< 40 cm) were virtually absent, consistent with cultural and economic disincentives for low-yield specimens35,36. In contrast, the marked reduction in the largest size class (161–180 cm) suggests limited availability of large adults or shifting consumer preference toward more manageable carcass sizes. Comparable declines in large-bodied crocodilians under sustained hunting pressure are associated with demographic truncation and loss of high-value reproductive cohorts37–39. These patterns collectively demonstrate that exploitation is strongly selective rather than random, aligning with global wildlife trade trends driven by market-based extraction pressures32,33. Selective removal of large adults poses significant conservation risks by destabilizing population structure, reducing genetic diversity, and undermining long-term viability35,39.

Seasonal variation further shapes trade dynamics. Dry-season volumes (November–April) exceeded wet-season levels by over 40% across both periods, mirroring broader wildlife markets where ecological accessibility and socio-economic rhythms influence supply27,28. At Ugbenu, increased dry-season hunting efficiency was linked to easier access to burrows and greater detectability that coincided with heightened demand during festive periods. This alignment of ecological opportunity with cultural and economic drivers highlights how seasonal conditions structure both harvesting intensity and market behaviour. Size-class composition also varied seasonally. Intermediate size classes (101–120 cm; 121–140 cm) dominated year-round but were more prevalent during the dry season, reflecting increased capture efficiency and transport practicality. The absence of the largest size class (161–180 cm) during the 2020–2021 wet season suggests continued scarcity of large individuals or intensified selective harvesting, consistent with trends in other reptile markets29,30. Although hunters occasionally released very small juveniles or exceptionally large adults typically due to handling difficulties or low market interest. These sporadic events did not alter the overall demographic bias toward mid-sized crocodiles. Trade activity also showed clear temporal clustering, especially during March–April, corresponding with the late dry season when favourable ecological conditions intersect with peak consumer demand. Similar dry-season intensification of wildlife trade has been reported in West African bushmeat markets, where accessibility plays a central role in exploitation patterns29,31. The consistency of these seasonal peaks signifies the tight coupling between environmental cycles and market dynamics and highlights the need for conservation and regulatory strategies that explicitly account for seasonal pressures.

Insights from semi-structured conversations with local merchants further contextualize these patterns. Merchants consistently noted that crocodile availability tends to increase during the dry season when burrows are easier to access, while declines in hunter participation in certain areas were attributed to reduced catch success. They also described price fluctuations around festive periods, reflecting demand-driven market behaviour similar to broader bushmeat trade dynamics in West Africa, where cultural events strongly shape supply and consumption63,64. Respondents frequently referenced declining crocodile abundance and rising hunting effort, observations that align with regional assessments of unsustainable exploitation of O. tetraspis65.

Data integration highlighted the interplay between socio-economic and ecological drivers. Pricing trends and gender roles influenced market participation, while ecological data on trap sites and hunting intensity confirmed localized declines in crocodile populations. Geo-referenced trap sites provided spatial evidence of concentrated hunting pressure, consistent with earlier studies documenting exploitation clustered around accessible riverine and swamp forest habitats11,12. Together, these results underscore the dual vulnerability of dwarf crocodiles to ecological constraints and socio-economic pressures, supporting calls for integrated conservation strategies that address both livelihood dependencies and ecological resilience3.

Conservation and management implications

The structured trade network documented in this study poses significant challenges for enforcement and monitoring. Terminal markets such as Ugbenu act as critical nodes where interventions could be most effective, including trade surveillance, size-based restrictions, and community engagement programs. The persistence of dry-season dominance and selective removal of mid-sized, sexually mature individuals raises urgent conservation concerns. Seasonal concentration of harvesting, coupled with demographic skew, can accelerate population decline, reduce genetic diversity, and compromise resilience. Without targeted regulation, these pressures may exacerbate extinction risk for WADC, particularly given its slow growth and low reproductive output57(Pauwels, et al., 2007). Effective management should prioritize seasonal harvest restrictions, size-based trade regulations, and community-based monitoring systems. Strengthening local governance and integrating conservation awareness into market systems are essential steps toward mitigating these risks.

On the Global Conservation Context, the West African dwarf crocodile is currently listed as Vulnerable on the IUCN Red List and is included under Appendix I of CITES, which prohibits international commercial trade of wild specimens. The unsustainable practices documented here particularly the removal of breeding adults and dry-season concentration directly conflict with these global conservation mandates. Aligning local interventions with international frameworks is critical. This includes enforcing CITES compliance at regional trade hubs, integrating IUCN guidelines into national wildlife policies, and promoting transboundary collaboration to curb illegal trade. Such measures would not only safeguard WADC populations but also strengthen Nigeria’s role in meeting global biodiversity targets under the Convention on Biological Diversity (CBD).

Conclusion

Evidence from market observations indicates that trade in the West African Dwarf Crocodile (WADC) in the western Niger Delta poses a conservation concern. Reported harvesting practices and volumes suggest potential recruitment erosion and pressure on long-term viability, particularly given the species’ slow life history, reliance on specialized wetlands, and concentrated urban demand. Traditional capture methods (wire traps, baited hooks and burrow excavation) likely disturb habitats and raise animal-welfare issues during live transport. The market chain appears strongly gendered: at Ugbenu Market, middlewomen reportedly handle ~ 80% of volume, underscoring household economic dependence and the central role of female aggregators. Cultural demand for meat, medicinal products, and leather may further elevate extraction pressure. Within these bounds, near-term priorities include (i) strengthened enforcement of wildlife trade regulations, (ii) culturally sensitive, community-based awareness that recognizes livelihoods while promoting sustainable alternatives, (iii) routine market monitoring at hubs such as Ugbenu, and (iv) integration of crocodile conservation into broader bushmeat management frameworks across the Niger Delta. A key limitation is the absence of a systematic consumer profile, which constrains inference about demand drivers and socio-cultural motivations. Future work should characterize consumers and value chains more rigorously, quantify off take and supply dynamics, and co-design interventions with hunters, traders, middlewomen, and buyers. Such evidence will be essential to calibrate enforcement, target outreach, and ultimately reduce exploitation while safeguarding the ecological integrity of Niger Delta wetlands.

Methods

The study was conducted in the Western Niger Delta region of Nigeria, covering parts of Delta and Edo States (Figs. 1 and 2). This region is characterized by extensive swamp forests, mangrove ecosystems, and riverine communities, which provide critical habitats for the West African dwarf crocodile WADC37,61. The species is widely distributed across lowland swamp forests and riparian zones, where it occupies burrows along riverbanks and floodplains21. Within these ecosystems, dwarf crocodiles play an important ecological role as mesopredators, occupying a niche that links aquatic and terrestrial food webs.

The Western Niger Delta is also recognized as a biodiversity hotspot and a major center for bushmeat trade. Crocodiles are among the most targeted reptiles, with hunting intensity shaped by both ecological accessibility (e.g., dry-season burrow exposure) and socio-economic demand (e.g., festive market peaks). This dual pressure underscores the vulnerability of local populations and situates the study area as a critical landscape for understanding conservation risks to the species7,62.

Hunting and trapping techniques

Field visits were made to hunters’ camps in Ughelli South LGA and along the Orhionmwon River corridor. Hunting and trapping methods were documented through direct observation and interviews, including wire traps (land and underwater variants), hooked baits, spearing, and firearms. Techniques adapted from Thorbjarnason & Eaton21 and de Merode & Cowlishaw22 were used to record trap placement, burrow entrances, and storage methods. All trap sites were geo-referenced using handheld GPS units, and photographic documentation was undertaken for verification. Data on capture effort and success rates were analyzed to infer pressure on wild populations.

Transport and commodity chain dynamics

The commodity chain was analyzed using value-chain and risk-based frameworks adapted from Ribot17, FAO guidelines18, and recent wildlife trade protocols19,20. GPS tracking was employed to map transport routes from rural collection points (Benin and Orhionmwon Rivers, Ughelli South LGA) to urban markets. Researchers also engaged in participant observation, accompanying vendors during transport of purchased crocodiles from Ugbenu to Benin City. This immersive approach validated interview data and revealed logistical challenges and informal negotiation practices. Route verification data were later integrated into GIS for visualization of trade flows and identification of key transit hubs.

Market population survey

A comprehensive market survey was undertaken to quantify the trade in WADC across major bushmeat markets in Delta and Edo States. This survey aimed to establish baseline data on trade volume, demographic structure, and sourcing patterns of the species within the Western Niger Delta. Prior to the main survey, a three-month pilot phase (July–September 2019) was conducted to identify major and minor outlets, roadside sales points, and informal trade networks. During this phase, reconnaissance visits were made to open markets, and preliminary mapping of supply chains was completed. The pilot also involved negotiating pre-entry agreements with market women and vendors to build trust and ensure unhindered data collection; a critical step given the sensitivity of bushmeat trade. Following the pilot, systematic surveys were conducted at Ugbenu Market, a key terminal hub located at the junction of the Benin–Warri Highway and Koko Port Motor Road (GPS: N8°05′737″, E6°08′8852″). Ugbenu Market operates weekly on Wednesdays and serves as the primary aggregation point for crocodiles sourced from riverine communities and swamp forests in the Western Niger Delta. Intensive monitoring was conducted exclusively at Ugbenu, with researchers present on every market day for two consecutive years (October 2019–September 2021), resulting in a total of 105 survey days. This systematic effort provided a robust dataset on trade volumes, size classes, and seasonal fluctuations. Other markets in Edo and Delta States were visited opportunistically to document presence/absence and relative activity, but daily monitoring was not feasible across all sites. As such, the dataset captures a substantial proportion of the trade through Ugbenu, which functions as the largest and most consistent hub, while supplementary observations at secondary and urban markets provided comparative context.

Researchers arrived early on each market day to observe vehicles transporting crocodiles and to record specimens before they were sold or processed. Specimen enumeration

All crocodiles displayed in the designated section of the market were systematically counted immediately upon arrival to ensure accurate representation of daily trade volume. Enumeration was conducted before any transactions or processing occurred to avoid underestimation caused by rapid sales or specimen removal. Researchers positioned themselves strategically near the unloading area to observe vehicles transporting crocodiles and record specimens as they were offloaded. Both live and freshly killed individuals were included in the count, while smoked or processed specimens were noted separately to distinguish between primary and secondary trade forms. Each count was cross-verified by two observers to minimize error, and data were logged on standardized datasheets and later digitized for analysis. This approach provided a reliable baseline for assessing market throughput and temporal trends in crocodile trade.

Data analysis

Morphometric data (total length and snout–vent length) were analyzed using allometric regression models to assess size-class distributions and potential demographic shifts24,25. Individuals were grouped into nine size categories (0–20 cm to 161–180 cm) for comparative analysis across two study periods (2019–2020 and 2020–2021). Descriptive statistics were computed for each category, and frequency distributions were summarized in tables and visualized using histograms and boxplots. Differences in trade volumes among size classes were tested using one-way ANOVA for each year, followed by Tukey HSD post-hoc comparisons to identify significant pairwise differences (p < 0.05). Year-to-year variation in trade volumes was assessed using paired t-tests for all size categories. Seasonal effects were evaluated by aggregating monthly trade volumes into dry (November–April) and wet (May–October) seasons, with paired t-tests applied to compare seasonal totals. Time-series plots were generated to illustrate monthly trends. Commodity chain data were analyzed qualitatively and visualized using flow diagrams constructed in Graphviz and R to depict market hierarchy and trade routes. Node importance was inferred from cumulative trade volumes and connectivity metrics. All statistical analyses were performed in R packages, following protocols for wildlife trade trend estimation26.

Ethical considerations

All research activities were conducted in strict accordance with established ethical guidelines for wildlife handling and trade monitoring, with a strong emphasis on non-invasive methodologies wherever feasible. Community engagement was undertaken based on informed consent and with full respect for local cultural practices. The experimental protocols were reviewed and approved by the Faculty of Science Ethics Committee at Delta State University, Abraka, Nigeria. Ethical clearance was granted in 2018 under reference number Delsu/FOS/EC/2018-002. In addition, research permissions were obtained from the Ministries of Environment in both Edo and Delta States, Nigeria, prior to fieldwork implementation.
