Combining federated learning and travelling model boosts performance and opens opportunities for digital health equity
Authors: Raissa Souza, Emma A. M. Stanley, Erik Y. Ohara, Richard Camicioli, Oury Monchi, Zahinoor Ismail, Matthias Wilms, Nils D. Forkert
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Federated learning (FL) and travelling model (TM) allow privacy-preserving model training across sites without sharing patient-sensitive data. While both approaches have shown success, they face unique challenges related to distribution shifts between sites. To address this, we propose FedTM, a hybrid framework combining the strengths of FL and TM. FedTM begins with FL warmup training at sites with larger datasets, followed by sequential refinement through TM across all sites. We evaluated FedTM for Parkinson’s disease classification using 1817 brain scans from 83 international sites. Model performance, misclassification disparities, and communication costs were computed and compared to standard FL and TM approaches. Our results reveal that FedTM improves AUROC from 77 ± 0.01% to 82 ± 0.01%, reduces misclassification disparities from 34 ± 0.01% to 26 ± 0.01%, and decreases training load for smaller sites from 22 to 12 cycles. These advancements mark an important step toward promoting global healthcare equity and advancing responsible AI development.

Introduction

In recent years, distributed learning has emerged as an innovative paradigm for deep learning in healthcare, allowing models to be trained collaboratively while avoiding centralized data aggregation1. The core idea of distributed learning is that data does not leave the site where it was acquired, being used exclusively to train models locally. By avoiding sharing patient-sensitive data across sites and with developers, distributed learning complies with many relevant data regulations2 while enabling access to large and diverse datasets from distinct cohorts. This decentralized local training can be implemented through parallel training at all sites at the same time or in sequence, training at one site at a time. The parallel training approach, also known as federated learning (FL)3, is currently more commonly employed than the sequential approach, known as the travelling model (TM)4. From a technical perspective, the most important difference between the two distributed learning methods is that FL requires an additional training step called model aggregation, which is responsible for combining the knowledge of multiple models trained in parallel to form a final global model while TM essentially trains a single model. Nevertheless, each approach has strengths and limitations.

Most challenges associated with FL are related to dataset size and representativity across participating sites (i.e., data distribution shifts). For instance, when all sites have access to similar but very small quantities of local training data, local models may not perform better than a random decision. Thus, aggregating such underperforming models may create a suboptimal final global model5. Moreover, in scenarios where local data quantity is unevenly distributed, the aggregation process required in FL may disproportionately favor larger and likely better-resourced sites, which may lead to diminishing the value of data from smaller sites for the final model, despite their potential to contribute important data from unique or marginalized populations6. This imbalance may be particularly pronounced when including sites from remote, underserved, and low- and middle-income countries in FL setups, where limited data availability may reduce the relevance and utility of the final global model when applied to their local populations7. With respect to data representativity, FL methods often assumes that every participating site has sufficient data representing all the classes of interest for the deep learning model development. While this may be easy to enforce in simulated scenarios that are often used during development and in proof-of-concept evaluations, this assumption commonly falls short in real-world applications, where class distributions can be highly imbalanced or incomplete. In this context, class imbalance means that sites have different quantities of data per class, while class incompleteness means that some classes may not be represented at all at some sites. In such cases, locally trained FL models may struggle to learn reliable decision boundaries, compromising the performance of the final global FL model after the aggregation step5.

To address these data distribution shift issues in the FL approach, a range of aggregation and optimization strategies have been proposed to date. These include weighting each site differently based on the amount of data available8, incorporating an L2 regularization term during training to constrain the divergence between local and global models9, employing knowledge distillation10, applying model calibration11, utilizing reinforcement learning12, and fine-tuning a pre-trained model on selected sites13. While these strategies and technical solutions have shown promising improvements across specific tasks and datasets, their effectiveness heavily depends on the nature of the data shift. Thus, techniques that alleviate class imbalance may be less effective when discrepancies arise from variations in data quantity or in case of very small local data sample sizes14.

Although less explored, TM offers promising potential for overcoming some of the data distribution shift issues. More precisely, previous research has demonstrated that dataset size and class imbalance have a lower impact on TM performance compared to FL approaches across various tasks, including age prediction15,16, disease classification4, and tumor segmentation17. This improved robustness stems from the fact that TM does not require an aggregation step to combine contributions from participating sites. Notably, two of these aforementioned studies have shown that TM performs particularly well under the extreme condition of each site contributing only a single training sample5,15. This characteristic is especially relevant in medical settings, where data scarcity can be very common (e.g., rare diseases18 or under-resourced facilities19) and where the use of smartphone applications and wearable devices for clinical data collection and deep learning training is rapidly increasing20,21.

Another challenge faced by both the FL and TM approaches is the performance disparity observed when evaluating the final model at site-specific settings. More precisely, there is emerging evidence that models trained using these two approaches may exhibit bias when participating sites' training data are affected by data distribution shifts22,23. These potential model biases arise when site-specific features are inadvertently used as shortcuts for the task of interest, leading to the encoding of spurious attributes within the models24. As a result, the final model may overly rely on these confounding features, impairing generalizability and fairness across sites during the testing or deployment phase. Thus, researchers have recently begun to incorporate fairness metrics into the optimization of distributed learning models25–28. However, this research avenue remains in its infancy, and, as with previous optimization methods, the effectiveness of fairness-based methods is strongly dependent on the underlying data distribution characteristics and fairness metrics employed.

The duration of training time is commonly seen as a key strength of the inherently parallel FL approach when compared to TM. Intuitively, FL can be assumed to be faster than TM because it parallelizes training across sites, while in the TM approach, the model has to be trained at one site before transferring it to the next. However, this assumption is only valid under the very specific condition where all sites have identical amounts of data, require the same number of local training epochs, and utilize equivalent hardware, which is rarely the case in real-world applications. Although this aspect has not been widely explored, previous studies have shown that TM may require as little as one single local training epoch per site4,5, suggesting that TM may be more computational-efficient than FL despite its sequential nature.

Given that the strengths of the two distributed learning approaches are largely complementary, we propose a novel hybrid framework, FedTM, which is theoretically better because it combines the parallel training efficiency of FL for sites that have enough representative datasets with the flexibility of TM to include and use data from sites regardless of their dataset quantity or class coverage. Thus, FedTM employs a two-phase training strategy. First, sites are divided into larger and smaller based on dataset size, determining their participation in the FL and TM phases. Then, FedTM begins with a FL warmup phase where larger sites perform initial training rounds using well established FL solutions, which serves as a model initialization for the next phase. Next, the FL-initialized model is sequentially refined using the TM approach across all participating sites, including the smaller sites. We test and evaluate the effectiveness of FedTM using a real-world neuroimaging scenario: the classification of Parkinson’s disease (PD) based on 1817 T1-weighted brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) scans acquired at 83 sites around the world. This experimental setting presents considerable data distribution shifts, including considerable variations in dataset size and class imbalance or incompleteness. The main contributions of this work are: (1) the introduction of a novel hybrid distributed learning framework, and (2) a comprehensive evaluation focusing on model performance, fairness between larger (likely better-resourced) and smaller (likely less-resourced) sites, communication efficiency, and clinical applicability.

Results

Overview

We proposed FedTM, a novel hybrid method that combines the FL and TM distributed learning approaches. FedTM training was divided into two phases: FL warmup and TM refinement (see Fig. 1). In the warmup phase, only sites with larger local datasets (s ≥ 40, 30, or 20 samples) participated in the training process, which was conducted in parallel following a FL implementation (f = FedAvg [assumed equal contribution from all sites during the aggregation step] or f = FedProx [introduced an L2 regularization term to limit the divergence of local models from the global model]) for a predefined number of rounds (R = 2, 5, or 10). The goal of the warmup phase was to provide a strong initialization for the TM phase, which included all sites regardless of the local dataset size. The TM phase ran for a fixed number of cycles (C = 28, 25, or 20), with the exact number depending on the number of rounds completed during the warmup phase to ensure a combined total of 30 cycles across both phases to match the baselines.Fig. 1FedTM framework.Phase 1 (blue rectangle) corresponds to the federated warmup phase where the larger sites are selected to train the model, which serves as the initialization for phase 2 (pink rectangle). We evaluated FedTM in the context of PD classification using a large database comprising 1817 T1-weighted brain MRI scans collected from 83 real-world sites. Over half of these sites contributed fewer than ten scans, reflecting the typical challenges faced by sites in under-resourced, remote, rural, and low- and middle-income areas with limited data availability. Moreover, only 26 of the 83 sites contained data from patients with PD and healthy individuals, albeit with varying class proportions. As a result, the data network also exhibited considerable class imbalance and class incompleteness. Thus, this multi-site database was the ideal setting to investigate our main questions: (1) Can FL and TM approaches boost performance when combined?, (2) Does FedTM achieve fair outcomes for larger (likely better-resourced) and smaller (likely less-resourced) sites? And (3) Does FedTM alleviate the training burden on smaller sites? We quantitatively evaluated FedTM with respect to three key perspectives: model performance (accuracy and area under the receiver operating characteristic curve [AUROC]), fairness (misclassification rate differences between larger and smaller sites), and communication cost (number of travelling cycles needed to converge, total training time, and number of times that files were transferred). All experiments were compared to two FL and a TM baselines where all sites participated in the training process without any warmup phase. The best-performing FedTM setup was then evaluated for its clinical applicability using metrics such as balanced accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, precision, F1-score, and calibration scores. Moreover, all baselines and FedTM experiments were repeated three times with different random seeds determining the first model initialization for FL baselines and FedTM warmup phase, and the traveling sequence for the TM baseline and FedTM refinement phase. Sites lacking one of the classes were excluded from the FedTM warmup phase, even if they had ≥40, 30, or 20 samples, to avoid aggregating models with unreliable decision boundaries.

Baseline performance

Teamwork improves model performance

Overall, combining FL and TM improved accuracy and AUROC across all experiments, as shown in Fig. 2. However, our results indicated that using FedAvg as the aggregation function in the FedTM warmup phase produced better outcomes than FedProx. This is likely because the regularization term in the FedProx approach did not allow the final global model to move too far away from the random initialization, even when employing a very small proximal term (μ = 0.001). In terms of the number of sites included in the warmup phase, our findings suggested that including the seven largest sites (i.e., each site had ≥40 samples) yielded better results than the other two setups (largest 10 [≥30 samples] and 21 sites [≥20 samples]). This may be related to increased data variability across the sites, making it harder to learn a good starting point for the FedTM refinement phase. Teamwork yields fairer outcomes

Surprisingly, our findings revealed higher performance disparities for sites with larger datasets when compared to smaller ones. Figure 3 shows that the results for the TM baseline exhibited misclassification rates of 34 ± 0.01% and 26 ± 0.01% for larger and smaller sites, respectively, resulting in an 8% disparity. Utilizing FedProx as an aggregation function for the FedTM warmup did not reduce performance disparities and often resulted in equal or even worse misclassification rates compared to the TM baseline. On the other hand, employing FedAvg as an aggregation function for the FedTM warmup improved fairness, even though fairness-specific objectives were not included in the optimization process. Our results suggested that fairness is influenced more notably by the number of sites involved in the warmup phase than by the number of warmup rounds. Interestingly, warming up the model with data from the seven largest sites (Fig. 3A) for ten rounds led to more balanced misclassification rates of 26 ± 0.01% across both larger and smaller sites. Furthermore, a detailed analysis of the data distribution for the sites that participated in the warmup phase revealed that the seven largest sites had a more balanced ratio of patients with PD and healthy individuals compared to the other two warmup setups (largest 10 and 21 sites— see Fig. 4). Teamwork reduces computational demands on smaller sites

Increasing the number of warmup rounds in the FedTM experiments effectively reduced the number of travelling cycles and file transfer required to FedTM convergence (Fig. 5A, C), thereby alleviating the training burden on smaller sites. However, this did not directly translate into a substantial reduction in the total training time, as the computational burden is shifted to better-resourced sites during the FL warmup phase (Fig. 5B). As shown in Fig. 5, ten warmup rounds reduced the number of travelling cycles and file transfer needed by half, yet the total training time remained nearly unchanged. This is because a single FL warmup round took ~234 s to complete while a TM refinement cycle took ~204 s to complete. Each FL warmup round took longer because it required sites to train their local model for ten epochs whereas the TM needed only a single epoch per cycle. While this may seem disadvantageous, it is important to note that only the largest and likely better-resourced sites participated in the warmup phase. Thus, FedTM promoted a more equitable distribution of the computational load by allowing less-resourced sites to participate less often than better-resourced sites in the training process. Teamwork enhances clinical applicability

Our findings demonstrated that the best FedTM setup, consisting of ten warmup rounds performed by the seven largest sites, enhances clinical applicability. As shown in Table 2, FedTM achieved a consistent improvement of 3–4% across all performance metrics, including balanced accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, precision, and F1-score. These metrics are particularly relevant in clinical settings, as they reflect the model’s ability to correctly identify both affected and unaffected patients, minimizing diagnostic errors and ensuring reliable and equitable decision-making. In terms of fairness, Table 3 shows that when stratifying the evaluation by larger and smaller sites, performance differences across metrics remain within a 1–5% range. The largest disparity is observed for specificity (~5%), where the model performed better on the smaller sites. These results suggest that the warmup performed on larger sites does introduce bias toward either larger or smaller sites, despite their site-specific characteristics. Figure 6 further supports these findings by comparing the calibration performance of both models. Overall, FedTM demonstrated better calibration quality (Brier score = 0.175) than the TM baseline (0.204), indicating that FedTM provides more reliable and better-calibrated probability estimates. In terms of variability and robustness, FedTM showed a smaller standard deviation (0.002 vs. 0.007), reflecting more consistent performance across repeated experiments. In contrast, the TM baseline exhibited higher variability, suggesting greater sensitivity to model initialization or data partitioning. Moreover, the non-overlapping confidence intervals (FedTM = [0.171, 0.179]; TM baseline = [0.190, 0.218]) confirm that the observed difference is statistically meaningful rather than due to random variation. Discussion

This work introduced FedTM, the first hybrid distributed learning framework that efficiently combines and integrates the technical capabilities of FL and TM. Technically, FedTM enables a reduction of the training burden for less-resourced sites while effectively addressing data distribution shifts, including disparities in dataset size, class imbalance, and class incompleteness. This feature is particularly appealing in real-world healthcare settings, where limited data quantity and representativity often stem from the underdiagnosis of certain diseases, underrepresentation of some demographic groups, and the limited resources available at small, remote, rural, or underserved hospitals18,19. Moreover, our quantitative results show that FedTM demonstrates particularly strong scalability by enabling model training, even when a site contributes only data from a single individual. This makes it well-suited for the growing field of mobile and wearable health technologies, where clinical data are collected and used to train or fine-tune deep learning models directly on an edge device.

Our in-depth evaluation of the PD classification task as a real-world case example demonstrated that FedTM improves classification accuracy and AUROC, reduces misclassification disparities between larger (potentially better-resourced) and smaller (potentially less-resourced) sites, reduces the training burden on smaller sites, and enhances clinical applicability. Reducing misclassification disparities, i.e., achieving fairer outcomes, is especially crucial to minimize the risk of developing models that perpetuate existing healthcare inequities and to ensure that all sites benefit from the final model7. Notably, our results suggest that the proposed FedTM enhanced model fairness despite not being explicitly optimized for any fairness metric. Furthermore, the results for FedTM exemplify how the potentially better computational infrastructure of better-resourced sites can be strategically leveraged to reduce computational demands on less-resourced sites, which directly supports more inclusive and equitable collaboration in distributed learning and, in its current implementation, helps relieve environmental impact in resource-constrained settings20,21.

This work also offers insights beyond the technical implementation of distributed learning frameworks, by shedding additional light on how challenges related to responsible AI could be tacked. A key pillar of responsible AI is data sovereignty, which states that digital data should be governed by the laws and regulatory frameworks of the country where it is located29. Although distributed learning aligns well with this principle by keeping data locally protected and using it exclusively for on-site, decentralized model training, challenges remain in ensuring broad and equitable participation. To date, most advances in AI have emerged from better-resourced institutions and countries, which have the capacity for large-scale data collection, curation, annotation, storage, and model training. Expecting less-resourced institutions or countries to meet the same standards is, to some extent, unrealistic, not only taking into account their limited technical infrastructure but also shortages in skilled personnel and digital literacy30. In 2023, North America, Asia-Pacific, and Europe accounted for 88.8% of global digital data creation in general, while the Middle East, Africa, Latin America, and the Caribbean created only 11.2%31. Unfortunately, these disparities position less-resourced institutions and countries primarily as consumers of AI-based solutions rather than producers. As a result, there is a high risk that the AI tools trained using samples from the main data producers are biased, given that their populations, contexts, and technical clinical systems are unlikely to be adequately represented in the training data32. Within this context, if less-resourced sites have some data available and are willing to participate in deep learning development, FedTM can help integrate their unique datasets, increasing the likelihood that the final model will be able to provide meaningful and relevant outcomes and predictions for their populations.

It is important to highlight some limitations of this work. First, our analysis focused on a single deep learning architecture and a real-world clinical task, PD classification. Thus, the generalizability of the results to other models and clinical applications remains to be shown in future work. Nonetheless, the PD dataset employed here is notably large and includes significantly more sites than most prior studies in distributed learning, suggesting strong potential for broader applicability and generalizability of the overall results. However, hyperparameter tuning, such as adjusting the number of training rounds or site participation during the warmup phase, will likely be required to optimize the performance of this hybrid method in other contexts, as is also the case with any other distributed learning approach. It is important to acknowledge that despite our best efforts to engage collaborators and incorporate publicly available data to increase heterogeneity, our current dataset has not yet achieved full global coverage. For instance, we currently lack data from Africa and Latin America. Nevertheless, we recognize the importance of including underrepresented regions and view FedTM as an initial step toward enabling broader participation in distributed learning, particularly by lowering the barriers for collaboration in less-resourced areas. Second, our evaluation was limited to a single neuroimaging modality (T1-weighted MRI). Thus, exploring different data modalities and multimodal architectures is another relevant avenue for future research. Third, we evaluated only two widely used FL implementations, FedAvg and FedProx, while other variants, such as FedAvgM33 or SCAFFOLD34, were beyond the scope of this work. Although integrating additional FL variants into FedTM is a potential avenue for future research, our results show that even FL methods specifically designed to overcome problems related to data heterogeneity can perform poorly in cases of extreme data scarcity, as can be observed with the results of FedProx used as one of the baselines. As a result, integrating such methods into the FedTM may introduce additional hyperparameters and trade-offs, and their effectiveness may depend on assumptions or prior knowledge of the data distribution across participating sites during the FL warmup stage. Fourth, our distributed learning setup was simulated on a single machine equipped with an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 3090 GPU, a computational resource that may be unavailable in hospitals situated in resource-limited settings. Future studies should investigate the feasibility of real-world deployments, including infrastructure requirements and communication protocols. Such investigations may reveal a need for additional strategies to alleviate computational demands at less-resourced sites. Nevertheless, we believe our findings are reproducible by institutions with comparable hardware and software resources. Finally, although distributed learning systems can enhance data privacy by eliminating the need for data sharing, they do not fully remove all privacy risks. Such systems remain vulnerable to various attacks, including gradient inversion, backdoor, and membership inference attacks, among others. Therefore, future work should focus on incorporating additional protection mechanisms to further strengthen data security and safeguard patient privacy.

Methods

Distributed learning approaches

This work combines FL and TM approaches into a novel hybrid distributed learning framework. Briefly described, in the FL approach, a global deep learning model is initialized at a server and distributed to each site. Local training occurs independently at each site for a set number of epochs. After local training, the learned parameters (e.g., weights or gradients) are transmitted back to the server. The server then performs the aggregation step that combines these parameters using a predefined aggregation function to update the global model, which is subsequently redistributed to the sites for further training. This iterative process can be repeated over multiple rounds to improve the final global model performance.

The gold standard implementation of FL is FedAvg3, which, in this work, aggregates all locally trained models equally, as showing in Eq. (1). Unlike FL, TM4 sequentially trains a single model, visiting one site at a time. Therefore, an initial travelling sequence is established to determine the order in which sites within the distributed learning network are visited. The actual training process then begins with a server or the first site initializing a model. Following training at the first site, the updated model is transferred to the next site in the sequence, either directly or via the server. This sequential transfer and training process continues until the model reaches the last site, completing one training cycle. Practically, this approach eliminates the need for an aggregation function by iteratively updating the same single model. However, it also comes with some challenges. One significant challenge is selecting the appropriate travelling sequence to avoid catastrophic forgetting35. For example, let's assume that the travelling sequence begins with sites that have small amounts of datasets and ends with those that have large quantities of datasets. In that case, the model may overwrite patterns learned from the first sites with those from later ones (i.e., forget knowledge of earlier samples). To address this risk, this work employs the TM with two effective strategies: multiple training cycles and cycle-to-cycle variability (i.e., a new random travelling sequence is generated for each cycle)4.

Data collaborators, pre-processing, and splits

This work utilizes T1-weighted MRI scans from 83 sites worldwide. In total, 1817 brain scans, 867 (325 females and 542 males) from patients with PD and 950 (430 females and 620 males) from healthy individuals, were available for the PD classification task. The data available at each site vary in size, demographics, imaging protocols, and scanner types. A detailed characterization is provided in Supplementary Table 1. Ethical approval for this work was granted by the Conjoint Health Research Ethics Board at the University of Calgary (REB21-0454), and all participating sites obtained informed consent in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Clinical trial number: not applicable.

All datasets underwent to the same pre-processing pipeline36, consisting of skull-stripping37, affine registration to the PD21-T1-MPRAGE-1mm atlas38, and background cropping. Sites with more than 25 scans assigned 80% of their data for training and 20% for testing. For sites with fewer than 25 scans, their dataset was split to ensure balanced representation of sex and age across both sets. In the extreme case where sites only have two samples, one is selected for training and the other for testing. As a result, 1410 datasets were used for training and 407 for testing.

Classification model architecture and optimization

The deep learning model used for PD classification follows the architecture described in Souza et al.4. It comprises eight sequential blocks. The first five blocks apply 3 × 3 × 3 3D convolutional filters (32,64,128,256,256,64), batch normalization, 2 × 2 × 2 max pooling, and ReLU activations. The sixth block uses a 1 × 1 × 1 convolutional filter, batch normalization, and ReLU. The seventh block includes a 3D average pooling layer, dropout with a rate of 0.2, and a flattening layer. The final block is a fully connected dense layer with a sigmoid activation function for binary classification.

Model training employed the Adam optimizer with an initial learning rate of 0.0001 and exponential decay applied after each warmup training round and travelling cycle. Binary cross-entropy was used as the loss function. Following Souza et al.4, the batch size was adjusted based on the number of scans available at each site: sites with at least five scans used a batch size of five, while sites with fewer than five scans utilized their entire dataset in a single batch. TM was trained for a single local epoch at each site according to the same study4. Based on the findings of our previous work5 showing that the FL approach requires more local epochs for effective training compared to the TM, we ran a grid search (epoch = 1, 2, 5, and 10) for a single setup of FedAvg and FedProx and confirmed that 10 epochs lead to the best results. Another grid search was performed for FedProx to determine the best proximal term (μ = 0.001, 0.025, and 0.05), revealing that μ = 0.001 achieved the best performance (see Supplementary Table 2). The entire training was performed on a single NVIDIA GeForce RTX 3090 GPU, following the distributed learning concept very strictly by retrieving data from one site at a time.

Experimental setup

A total of 21 experiments were performed in this work. Three of those experiments served as baselines, where all sites participated in training using FedAvg, FedProx, and TM for 30 rounds/cycles. An additional 18 scenarios were explored, nine for each FL strategy, to assess the impact of the number of selected sites (7,10, or 21), their local dataset sizes (s ≥ 40, 30, or 20 samples), and the number of rounds (2, 5, or 10) in the FedTM warmup phase. It is important to note that sites with incomplete class representation (i.e., lacking data for one of the classes) were excluded from the FedTM warmup phase, even if they had larger local datasets (s ≥ 40, 30, or 20 samples). This design choice was made to prevent aggregating local models with unreliable decision boundaries. The 21 experiments were repeated three times with different seeds determining the first model initialization and the travelling sequence. Evaluation metrics

We evaluated our approach from three key relevant perspectives: model performance, fairness, and communication cost. Model performance assesses how accurately the models distinguish between patients with PD and healthy individuals, using accuracy and AUROC as metrics. Accuracy reflects the proportion of correctly classified cases using a threshold of 0.5, while AUROC captures the model’s performance across varying thresholds. Fairness is evaluated by comparing the misclassification rates between larger and smaller sites. The misclassification rate represents the proportion of incorrectly classified cases (false positives and false negatives) out of the total. In this work, we focused on comparing larger and smaller sites to evaluate whether FedTM can deliver fairer outcomes for both groups. Smaller sites participated only in the refinement phase, whereas larger sites took part in both the warmup and refinement phases. Communication cost evaluates the computational efficiency of the model training process and is measured in terms of three key metrics. (1) Total number of training cycles, computed as the sum of FL warmup and TM refinement rounds. (2) Total training time, obtained by adding the training times of both phases. For the FL warmup, the time required by the site with the largest local dataset is used, as aggregation must wait until all sites complete training. (3) Number of file transfers, where the FL warmup involves 2 × number of participating sites × number of warmup rounds (each round includes a model broadcast from the server and an update from each site), and the TM refinement involves number of sites × number of refinement rounds (each round includes one update per site).

We further assessed the best-performing FedTM setup in terms of clinical applicability using several key metrics. Balanced accuracy evaluates overall classification performance while accounting for class imbalance. Sensitivity measures the model’s ability to correctly identify patients with PD, whereas specificity determines how well it recognizes healthy individuals. Precision captures the proportion of positive predictions that are truly correct, and the F1-score provides a balanced summary of precision and sensitivity. Finally, the calibration score evaluates how well the model’s predicted probabilities correspond to actual outcomes.
