Adherence to infection mitigation strategies for facial dog bite wounds: a retrospective analysis of a level 1 paediatric trauma centre
Authors: Elizabeth M. Waltman, David M. Mills, Isabella G. Steidley, Michael C. Monuteaux, Eric W. Fleegler, Amir A. Kimia
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractDog bites are a frequent cause of paediatric facial injuries and present a significant risk of infection. This retrospective study evaluated adherence to antibiotic and repair guidelines in facial dog bite cases at a level 1 paediatric trauma centre. Among 356 cases, 99.2% received antibiotics; however, only 90.4% received the recommended first-line agent and 12.6% missed the initial dose in the emergency department. Many prescriptions had an incorrect dose or prolonged duration. Paediatric emergency medicine clinicians used fewer layered repairs compared to plastic surgeons. These results highlight variation in both antibiotic prescribing and laceration repairs, suggesting the need for quality improvement in both areas.

Dog bites are a significant cause of non-fatal injuries in paediatric patients and represent 0.33% of visits to paediatric emergency departments (PED).1 Previous research suggests both facial wounds and closed wounds are at increased risk of infection, with an estimated incidence of 6%–16%.2 3 Antibiotic prophylaxis can reduce infection rates.2 For facial dog bite wounds, the recommended antibiotic for prophylaxis is amoxicillin-clavulanate potassium in non-penicillin allergic patients, with a recommended duration of antibiotics 3–5 days, with the first antibiotic given as soon as possible. For children allergic to penicillin, trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole plus clindamycin may be used instead.4 Repair guidelines recommend against primary closure in areas other than the face. For certain high-risk facial bite cases, loose approximation is advised, and subcutaneous sutures should be avoided. This study sought to evaluate the adherence to standard antibiotic management of facial dog bite lacerations with regards to antibiotic administration timing, dosages and duration of antibiotic course and adherence to repair guideline recommendations in the PED of a level 1 paediatric trauma centre.

A cross-sectional chart review was performed on patients presenting for facial laceration repair between 2016 and 2020. We reviewed notes of patients with facial laceration repair, including pre-arrival notes, emergency department (ED) clinician notes, plastic surgery consult notes and follow-up notes. Data were abstracted via a natural language processing-assisted manual review (www.documentreviewtools.com). Patients and the public were not involved in the research. Data abstracted included demographics, wound characteristics including etiology, length, depth and complexity, single versus layered repair and paediatric emergency medicine (PEM) versus plastic surgery repair. We extracted antibiotic usage, including type, timing, dosage and duration given. To evaluate use of single versus layered repair, we estimated multivariable logistic regression models stratified by clinician with layered repairs as the dependent variable and dog bite versus non-dog bite laceration as the independent variable and controlled for age, sex, race and ethnicity, insurance status, day of week and time of day of ED presentation, laceration characteristics and plastic surgery repair of injury, with robust SEs clustered on patient.

5289 patient encounters were reviewed. We identified facial dog bite lacerations in 356/5289 (6.7%) of these encounters. 99.2% of dog bites received antibiotic prescriptions. Table 1 outlines the antibiotic characteristics of those dog bite patients who received antibiotics. 90.4% (322/356) of patients received amoxicillin-clavulanate potassium. 12.6% (45/356) did not receive the first dose of antibiotics in the PED. 25% of amoxicillin-clavulanate potassium prescriptions were <65% of the recommended 45 mg/kg/day dosing. 52.4% of prescriptions exceeded recommended duration (table 1). Emergency medicine clinicians used fewer layered repairs for dog bites compared with non-dog bite laceration repairs after controlling for laceration length, depth and complexity (OR 0.34; 95% CI 0.19 to 0.59). Among plastic surgery clinicians, no difference in layered laceration repair was detected by wound aetiology (OR 0.93; 95% CI 0.48 to 1.78) (table 2). Length and wound depth were associated with layered repair.

This study was limited to a single centre study, and the retrospective nature did not allow for assessment of wound infections. Given the risk of wound infection of facial dog bite lacerations, as well as the public health crisis of antibiotic resistance, it is paramount that prescribers adhere to guidelines for antibiotic prescriptions. Inappropriate prescribing is a leading cause of antimicrobial resistance. Efforts of antibiotic stewardship are focused on lower doses and shorter courses when appropriate.5 Similarly, in a study in Australia, only 62% of antibiotic prescriptions for patients with mammalian bites adhered to current guidelines.6 While our data show 99.2% adherence to prescribing prophylactic antibiotics as per recommended guidelines, it also suggests significant variation in the administration timing of first dose, dosage and overall duration of the course. Given that amoxicillin-clavulanate potassium is preferred in non-penicillin allergic patients and trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole plus clindamycin is preferred in penicillin allergic patients, only 94.6% were given the appropriate antibiotic choice. These data suggest opportunities for quality improvement of antibiotic prescribing practices to standardize patient care and reduce the burden of antimicrobial resistance.
