A comparative study of AI and human programming on environmental sustainability
Authors: Nolan H. Woo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Despite rising concerns over AI’s environmental impact, a recent article claimed human writers emit 130 to 1,500 times more greenhouse gases than AI. While the study utilized life cycle assessment methodology, it overlooked a critical factor: output quality. Unlike AI, which instantly generates content, human writers work for months, producing superior results. To provide a more objective comparison, we analyzed the environmental impacts of human and AI programmers generating functionally equivalent code. Using the USA Computing Olympiad database, we developed infrastructure to evaluate multiple GPT-based models. To our knowledge, this is the first study to correctness-control and quantitatively assess AI’s environmental impact in code generation. To address AI’s inaccuracies, we built a multi-round correction process to iteratively fix responses. We calculated AI emissions from usage and embodied impacts, while human emissions were estimated using average computing power consumption. Our case study results show that smaller models can match the environmental impact of human programmers when they succeed, though they often fail. However, standard, widely-used models are far more environmentally strenuous. For example, GPT-4 emitted between 5 and 19 times more \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\text {CO}_2$$\end{document}eq than humans, underscoring a much greater trade-off between efficiency and environmental cost than previously understood.

Introduction

As AI models continue to advance, there is a growing belief that increasing a model’s scale leads to higher performance1. However, this improvement comes at a cost: larger models require significantly more computing resources. As a result, the community has begun raising concerns about the environmental impact of both training and serving these large models2,3.

In contrast, a recent article reported that AI systems produce less carbon dioxide emissions than humans when performing tasks like writing and illustrating4. Although AI services consume significantly more power per user request, fulfilling these requests only requires a short duration of time. On the other hand, humans take longer to finish the same tasks, which leads to increased energy consumption, such as through prolonged use of a laptop. As a result, the article claims that an AI writer would emit 130 to 1,500 times less carbon dioxide than a human writer.

Unfortunately, the article did not compare the quality of the output delivered by AI and human creators, making a fair, apples-to-apples comparison impossible. To avoid subjectivity in evaluating writing or illustration quality, this study objectively quantifies and compares the environmental impacts of AI and human work by measuring their performance on programming tasks, where quality is precisely quantifiable.

Large language model (LLM)-based programming, in particular, is a rapidly evolving field, and its environmental impact is likely to change just as quickly. In addition to recent coding assistant tools such as GitHub Copilot5 and Cursor6, new concepts such as vibe coding7 or self-correcting multi-step agent8 have emerged. In this development practice, individuals without technical backgrounds can write code using natural language, often just their voice, and interact with LLM tools repeatedly until functionally satisfactory code is produced. This approach relies on LLMs even more heavily than traditional AI-assisted coding, where a human programmer reviews, understands, and debugs the generated code when it produces incorrect outputs. If this practice becomes mainstream, people will likely rely on LLMs even more aggressively for programming tasks. More importantly, the productivity gains could significantly increase the total amount of software being produced, making the environmental impact of LLMs an even more critical concern. This growing usage, combined with the lack of objective comparisons between humans and AI, highlights the need for research like ours to better understand the true environmental cost of LLM-based programming.

Research gap and contribution While recent studies have estimated the environmental impact of LLMs in general-purpose or other tasks2–4,9–11, none have quantitatively compared AI and human programmers on the same functionally equivalent coding problems with correctness explicitly measured. Prior work has also not examined how iterative error correction influences emissions. Our study addresses these gaps by (i) selecting programming problems with clear correctness criteria, (ii) using a multi-round correction process to simulate AI debugging behavior, and (iii) applying life cycle assessment methods to both AI and human programming scenarios. To our knowledge, this is the first ever correctness-controlled study to conduct an environmental comparison between AI and human programmers.

Design and methods

USACO problems

To objectively compare the quality of programming tasks done by AI and humans, we first needed to find a suitable set of programming tasks that both could tackle under comparable conditions. One approach would have been to gather a group of human programmers, assigning them a set of problems within a fixed time frame and measuring their energy consumption and environmental impact. However, conducting such a large-scale experiment would be costly and prone to sampling bias. Instead, we decided to leverage the historic database of the USA Computing Olympiad (USACO)12. USACO is a timed algorithmic programming contest held four times annually in the December, January, February, and US Open competitions. The USACO contest served as a strong evaluation benchmark because it maintains a historical database of problems and test suites, human participants are primarily focused on solving the problems without distractions, and the fixed time limits allow us to estimate energy consumption and its corresponding environmental impact. The competition is divided into four levels: Bronze, which assesses basic concepts like sorting and binary search; Silver, which tests foundational problem-solving strategies and data structures; Gold, which introduces more advanced algorithms such as shortest-path and dynamic programming; and Platinum, designed for top participants tackling sophisticated and open-ended challenges. Although USACO problems are competitive rather than real-world software tasks, they offer objective scoring that allows for reproducible comparisons.

Multi-round correction process

To measure the environmental impact of AI systems solving USACO problems, we built an infrastructure to ask the OpenAI API service13 to solve a given problem with the Python API. Out of the six different language choices that USACO allows, we selected Python because generative pre-trained transformer (GPT) models are known to perform better for high-level dynamically typed languages14. In fact, we initially conducted our experiments using C++, but the quality of LLM-generated code was insufficient to support meaningful comparisons with human programmers. By switching to Python, we were able to identify at least one problem set suitable for reliable evaluation, as detailed in a later section.

Figure 1 shows the infrastructure used to evaluate the environmental impact of AI-generated code. USACO problems and corresponding test cases are first selected and fed into a pre-processing stage that formats the prompts for GPT-based models. In this study, we used four different models available at the time of evaluation, GPT-4o-mini, GPT-4o, GPT-4-turbo, and GPT-4. The GPT service is then called through the OpenAI API. The returned code is executed and validated against USACO test cases.

Overall Workflows.

Please note that an open-source model would have been more transparent from the perspective of environmental impact modeling, but, as this study found, the accuracy of functional output turns out to be extremely important to reduce the environmental impact, and, as of today, commercial models significantly outperform open source models in terms of functional accuracy for coding tasks. Thus, we decided to use OpenAI models as OpenAI offers the wide spectrum of models according to the SWE-bench leaderboard15, allowing us to perform trade-off studies detailed later.

Unfortunately, because the outputted Python code is often incorrect, we built a multi-round correction process to iteratively correct erroneous responses16. Specifically, we categorized incorrect behaviors into three different issues and provided issue-specific feedback to the AI as shown in Table 1, followed by the prompt, “Can you review your code thoroughly and fix the code?” While USACO typically requires a Python program to execute within four seconds for each test case, achieving functional output from AI-generated code often proved difficult, as will be discussed later. Therefore, we extended the execution time for AI-generated problems to 100 seconds. Additionally, each USACO problem includes multiple test cases (usually between 10 and 20), and we allowed up to 100 rounds of iteration for a GPT model to produce correct outputs across all test cases. The number of LLM calls per task significantly depends on a given task and consequently affects energy consumption. For example, a recent article suggests a sophisticated decision tree to solve software engineering tasks, often calling LLMs 300 to 2,000 times17. Given that the number of calls needed will heavily depend on a target task, in this study, we decided to use 100 rounds following the observation made by Chen et al. (e.g., pass@100 metric)18. While the original proposal was more about statistical observations for the same prompt, we decided to follow their approach—which is heavily referenced by our research community—because a multi-step agentic execution flow is still an actively researched area and there is no single, well-established answer for the optimal number of LLM calls needed, as shown later from our experiments. Lastly, to account for the context length limitation of the models, we retained only the last ten rounds of conversations for each prompt. Environmental impact modeling

Once the program passes all test cases or reaches the iteration limit, the run is recorded. To estimate the energy consumption and environmental impact, we leveraged an open-source project, Ecologits 0.8.1, that employs life cycle assessment (LCA) methodology as defined by ISO 1404419. Ecologits accounts for the embodied ecological impacts and the usage impact of an AI inference service, but it excludes the impact of training: a limitation discussed later in this article. Specifically, the functional unit is one LLM inference request, while the system boundary adheres to the ‘Cradle-to-Gate’ scope20 from the perspective of an LLM inference request. This includes both usage impacts and embodied impacts of the computing and cooling facilities of a datacenter. End-of-life impacts are excluded, and the allocation method is based on the time-weighted resource consumption of each request.

Usage impacts account for the operational energy required to perform a single inference. At the top level, this is scaled by the power usage effectiveness (PUE), a metric that reflects overall data center efficiency by incorporating both computing and overhead energy use (e.g., cooling and power delivery). Total impact is calculated by summing the usage emissions and the amortized embodied emissions. To translate energy use into CO\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\phantom{0}_2$$\end{document}-equivalent emissions, we apply an emission factor \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$F_{\text {em}}$$\end{document}, which by default reflects the global electricity mix. While many modern data centers use renewable energy or low-carbon grid mixes, we use the global average to maintain comparability with prior work21; future work could improve the analysis with carbon intensities specific to a center.

Ecologits reports a range of environmental impact estimates—minimum and maximum values—instead of a single point value to reflect the uncertainty and variability in key input factors. These include differences in data center efficiency (PUE), hardware configurations, model sparsity, inference optimizations, and the carbon intensity of electricity used during inference. By capturing this spread, the framework allows for a more transparent comparison across systems and deployment contexts, since real-world conditions often deviate from standardized benchmarks.

To better understand the environmental cost of machine inference, we compared it to the energy used by a human solving the same task. Here, we model the environmental impact of human programmers following the functional unit, the system boundary, and the allocation methods of Ecologits. Specifically, we model embodied impacts and usage impacts of a 13-inch laptop connected to a 27-inch monitor, excluding only their end-of-life impact. If a programmer spends four hours working through the problem set, they would consume roughly 0.24 kWh on the laptop (60 W)22 and 0.06 kWh on the monitor (15.8 W during its ‘on’ mode)23. To use the same method as Ecologits for embodied impacts, we account for the reported manufacturing and transport impact of these devices, 88.1% of 265 kgCO2eq for the laptop24 and 55.8% of 195 kgCO2eq for the monitor25. We then scale these numbers to the four-hour USACO time limit, assuming 3-year lifecycle for the devices, 200 working days per year, and an 8-hour working day, resulting in 0.19 kgCO2eq and 0.09 kgCO2eq, respectively. Finally, we added the usage impact of 0.12 kgCO2eq derived from 0.30 kWh26.

Evaluation results

With the infrastructure in place, we evaluated GPT models using four years’ worth of data from the USACO database. Since human programmers tackle three problems over a span of four to five hours in each USACO contest (four hours for December, January, and February tests and five hours for US Open tests), our first step was to identify a contest where a GPT model could successfully solve all three problems. This allowed us to estimate the energy consumption of human programmers based on the contest’s time constraints. We acknowledge that USACO problems represent competitive programming challenges rather than the full spectrum of software engineering tasks; their primary advantage here is the objective scoring and time-bounded nature, which allows controlled comparisons.

Another noteworthy observation that we can make is the environmental impact of successful and failed runs. For example, out of 10 experiments that we did with GPT-4o to solve the 21_2_bronze_1 question, failed runs (Exp 2, 4, and 9) consumed 8.16 times more energy on average than successful runs (Exp 0, 1, 3, 5, 6, 7, and 8). If we exclude Exp 7, which solved the problem after 77 rounds, the ratio becomes 53 times. This again shows that failed runs are very expensive, and human programmers need good judgment on the capability of LLM services.

One interesting question is whether the energy estimation from Ecologits can accurately represent diverse service scenarios in which service operators perform various optimizations for large-scale serving through dynamic batching of user requests. Batching helps reduce energy consumption by amortizing the weight loading overhead of LLMs for the feedforward network of a transformer block, but it does not help to reduce the overhead of self-attention computation because context is unique to each user’s request29. Furthermore, self-attention computation overhead scales quadratically with the sequence length. Figure 6 shows the input and output sequence length of our experiments. As shown in the figure, due to the long failure logs used for debugging for multi-round correction, the input context length is very long. A recent study30 shows that, when the input sequence length is long, the energy consumption is dominated by prefill computation, and the energy consumption of the prefill computation is not sensitive to the size of batch, when the input sequence length is long. Thus, we believe that per-query estimates from Ecologits should be a reasonable order-of-magnitude approximation for the multi-round correction process that we evaluated, even for a large-scale service. Also, as previously mentioned, we had to restrict the failure log to the last ten rounds of conversations due to the current context length limitation of today’s LLM services. Given that LLM service providers are increasingly supporting longer context lengths, we believe that the energy consumption of future coding agents will not be significantly amortized through batching.Fig. 6Input / output sequence length distribution.

Consequently, its environmental impact tends to be far greater than that of its human counterpart, often failing to produce correct results, as illustrated in the figure.

As shown in the chart, the number of inference calls needed to finish a given task has a higher correlation to the amount of greenhouse gas emission than the API price itself. This suggests that using neither the cheapest model nor the most expensive model is an environmentally better choice. Rather, we need to find the appropriate model for a given task so that the number of trials needed to achieve the task can be minimized. It also suggests that failed experiments, even after 100 trials, are environmentally unfriendly. Limitations

While we attempt to objectively compare the environmental impact of AI against that of humans, there exist several caveats that readers should be aware of. First, we have performed our study using competitive programming tasks, which may not reflect typical day-to-day programming challenges. Such tasks are narrowly focused on algorithmic correctness and do not capture aspects like collaboration with others, which could affect real-world energy use. Correctness is not the only important factor in software development; readability, extensibility, and modularity are also crucial to ensure that code can be understood, reused, and extended by others. Second, the success rate of LLM-based programming was not high enough for us to conduct a study across a wide set of test suites. Instead, we had to assume that, for unsuccessful executions, LLMs would waste significantly more energy. Third, due to the nature of decoding strategies in LLMs33, outputs are not always deterministic, making experiments difficult to reproduce consistently. Running a sufficiently large number of trials to stabilize results statistically is both costly and environmentally unfriendly. Note that our multi-round correction process already runs an LLM up to 100 times for a given programming task. Fourth, the environmental impact reported by Ecologits is an estimation rather than a direct measurement. Ideally, each provider would publish the environmental impact of their LLM services. Fifth, training cost was not included in our estimates because it is unclear how to quantify it—training often involves many failed trials, and it is difficult to determine how those costs are amortized without knowing the exact usage volume of the deployed models. Also, our estimates assume a future scenario where LLM inference is widespread and training costs can be amortized. Sixth, estimating the environmental impact of proprietary AI models is not straightforward. As our study suggests, the quality of an AI model strongly affects the number of inferences required in the multi-round correction process. For this reason, using production-quality models was unavoidable in our evaluation. However, this raises questions about the validity of estimating the impact of proprietary systems. Ecologits estimates model size indirectly, using observed execution performance such as output token rates and comparing them against open-source models. While useful, this approach may not fully capture real usage, particularly at large scales where many requests are batched together. A more recent study34 attempts to account for such scenarios, but it also faces limitations because it relies on restricted information from an AI workload analysis service35, such as fixed token counts. We also tried to estimate energy consumption using the method suggested in that study, building an interpolation function across dynamically changing token counts. However, due to the limited number of data points, we found that this approach could not reliably estimate the energy consumption of GPT-4, like in the original work. As a community, we still lack a complete understanding of how to measure these impacts accurately. We exclude office energy, commuting, daily necessities like food, and personal device embodied impacts for humans, just as we exclude provider offices for AI. These are organizational overheads, not task-marginal impacts. In vibe coding, humans are still present in the work environment, so their normal lifestyle emissions continue regardless of whether AI writes the code, and should not be allocated to the programming task.

Suggestion

Given that our study found model selection to be critical in minimizing environmental impact, we propose that API providers leverage their operational data to develop routing mechanisms that optimize for both performance and sustainability. Since these services handle requests from a wide range of users and already monitor quality metrics, we believe they are well-positioned to analyze this data and build algorithms that can select the most appropriate LLM for a given task. Such routing could minimize the number of iterations needed to correct the code, which our results show is the largest driver of emissions. This change would prioritize both efficiency and environmental sustainability.

Conclusion

This study is, to our knowledge, the first-ever correctness-controlled analysis to quantitatively compare the environmental impacts of a human programmer and an AI programming service. Our findings reveal that AI models emit up to 19 times more CO\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\phantom{0}_2$$\end{document}eq than human programmers, demonstrating a far more severe trade-off between computational efficiency and environmental impact than previously understood. By focusing on competitive programming tasks with objective correctness metrics, we address the research gap left by prior work, which lacked direct, apples-to-apples comparisons of AI and human coding emissions. This disparity underscores the urgent need for strategic regulation and optimization of hybrid intelligence.

Additionally, we found that the imperfect output of AI services often requires numerous iterations of queries, significantly increasing emissions compared to those generated by a human programmer. This supports our hypothesis that the output quality must be considered when comparing the environmental footprint of AI versus humans.

Furthermore, while model size influences emissions, our findings indicate that greenhouse gas output is more closely tied to the number of LLM inference calls. With smaller models lacking accuracy and larger ones being carbon-intensive, selecting the appropriate model for each task is crucial to minimizing environmental impact. Future work should expand this comparison to more diverse programming contexts and include real-world software development workflows. Ultimately, these findings signal an urgent need for industry-wide accountability in ensuring sustainable AI deployment.
