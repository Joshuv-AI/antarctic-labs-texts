Stroke risk of COPD patients and death risk of COPD patients following a stroke: A systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Chaowei Ding, Runlu Wang, Xiaowei Gong, Yadong Yuan
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
Objective:Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) is closely related to the development and progression of cardiovascular disease. The purpose of this study is to clarify the answers to the following questions through systematic evaluation: the risk of stroke in COPD patients; the risk of stroke in acute exacerbations of COPD (AECOPD) patients; and the risk of death after stroke in COPD patients.Methods:Two reviewers independently searched EMbase, PubMed, and the Cochrane Library for relevant literature from the date of creation to February 17, 2023, for studies relating COPD to stroke patients. Of the 8039 publications retrieved, we identified 27 articles that met our selection criteria. Fixed-effects or random-effects models were used to calculate ORs and 95% confidence intervals for the combined risk.Results:combining studies on stroke risk in COPD patients by random-effects model suggested that COPD was an independent risk factor for stroke-associated pneumonia (OR 1.40, 95% CI: 1.24–1.59, I2 = 98.4%, P = .000), with significant heterogeneity in the results, and subgroup analysis did not find a source of heterogeneity. In the combined 7 AECOPD studies, a significantly higher risk of stroke was found (OR 1.53, 95% CI: 1.44–1.63, I2 = 49.2%, P = .066). In the combined 6 short- term prognostic studies, the relationship between COPD and risk of death was not highly significant (OR 1.12, 95% CI: 1.08–1.16, I2 = 37.4%, P = .131). In 10 long-term observational prognosis studies, COPD was suggested to be associated with death after stroke by combining data using a random-effects model (OR 1.20, 95% CI: 1.13–1.27, I2 = 56.8%, P = .014), and there was moderate heterogeneity in the combination, with subgroup analysis showing that stroke type may be a source of heterogeneity and the risk of death from ischemic stroke: OR 1.23, 95% CI: 1.17–1.29, I2 = 45.0%, P = .191 and the risk of death from both types of stroke: OR 1.12, 95% CI: 1.07–1.18, I2 =18.9%, P = .291.Conclusion:COPD is an independent risk factor for stroke. The risk of stroke is significantly increased, especially during AECOPD. In addition, the association between COPD and short-term death in stroke patients is insignificant, while it is more associated with fatal events in the long-term prognosis.

1. Introduction

Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) is the world’s third leading cause of death. A large-scale population survey shows that the prevalence of COPD among adults over 20 is 8.6%, and the prevalence rate of people over 60 is 27.0% as the age increases. A large number of people with COPD places a massive burden on individuals and society.[1]

COPD is characterized by a chronic airway inflammatory response and incomplete reversible airflow limitation that progresses progressively and often occurs in conjunction with multiple cardiovascular diseases, such as hypertension, heart failure, and acute myocardial infarction.[2] Some studies have shown that patients with COPD have a higher risk of stroke than those without COPD.[3–5] In a 40-year cohort study of 5617 COPD patients, it was observed that impaired FEV1 was strongly associated with stroke, and after controlling for confounding factors such as smoking, hypertension, and diabetes, the risk model still suggested that impaired lung function was strongly associated with stroke. Not only the increased incidence of stroke found in long-term follow-ups of COPD but also in short-term observation of acute exacerbations of COPD (AECOPD) showed the phenomenon. AECOPD is a very common event in the progression of COPD, accompanied by dyspnea, hypoxia, and hypercapnia. Most AECOPD is caused by an acute viral/bacterial infection in the airways, usually combined with a systemic inflammatory response and an oxidative stress state of the body, which is closely associated with thrombosis.[6,7] Therefore, barring other confounding factors, AECOPD may be a plausible explanation for the increased risk of stroke in patients with COPD.

Although meta-analyses were conducted several years ago to explore the risk of stroke in patients with COPD, the prognosis of patients with stroke has not been explained. Some studies have shown that patients with COPD have an increased risk of death after stroke.[8,9] However, other studies possess a different view.[10] Therefore, we systematically evaluated the literature on the risk of stroke in COPD patients, the correlation between AECOPD and stroke risk, short-term mortality risk after stroke in COPD patients, and long-term mortality risk after stroke in COPD patients; to comprehensively explore the relationship between COPD and stroke.

2. Method

Our study followed the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses.[11] A protocol was registered with PROSPERO, registration number CRD42022369938.

2.1. Literature retrieval

A systematic retrieval of the literature published from PubMed, EMBASE, and Cochrane Library from the date of creation to February 17, 2023, was performed to find studies on the association between COPD and stroke (see Table 1 for the search strategy). Also, citations to the included literature were reviewed for missing relevant studies.

2.2. Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria: Cohort studies or case-control studies. Data related to COPD and stroke (including COPD-stroke risk, AECOPD-stroke risk, short-term stroke mortality risk, and long-term stroke mortality risk) were reported. Clear COPD diagnosis based on pulmonary function or COPD diagnosis codes extracted from medical records. Stroke defined by a clear stroke medical record or diagnosis code (hemorrhagic stroke, ischemic stroke).

Exclusion criteria: Articles were duplicates, nonhuman, case reports, reviews, Meta-analyses, or conference papers. If there were multiple publications in the same cohort, the publication with the most recent statistical data was used. Literature for which full text was not available. The control cohort also had other conditions that contributed to the poor prognosis of stroke (cohorts with atrial fibrillation [AF], acute myocardial infarction, etc.).

2.3. Data extraction and quality assessment

Two authors (C.D. and R.W.) independently screened abstracts and performed a full-text review to check inclusion eligibility, and disputes were resolved by obtaining consensus from a third independent author (R.W.). The following data were extracted from the included studies: authors, year, country, sample size, study design (prospective cohort study, retrospective cohort study, nested case-control study, case-control study), baseline characteristics of patients, type of stroke, risk data extracted (HR, RR, OR, which were then uniformly quantified as OR combined). Newcastle-Ottawa score (NOS) was used to evaluate the quality of studies, and studies with NOS score ≥ 7 were considered high quality.[12]

2.4. Data pooling and analysis

We extracted HR, RR, and OR from the included articles and quantified them uniformly with OR.[13] and pooled them by random-effects or fixed-effects meta-analysis to measure the effect of COPD on stroke, defining events within 1 month after stroke as short-term risk and events above 1 year after stroke as long-term risk. To visually assess the pooled analysis across studies, we employed forest plots. Statistical heterogeneity of the included studies was evaluated using the I2 statistic. When heterogeneity was significant (I2 > 50%), a random-effects model was used; otherwise, a fixed-effects model was employed.[14] Sensitivity tests were performed to assess whether a single study would significantly affect the pooled results. Publication bias was assessed using funnel plots and Begg and Egger tests, and if significant publication bias existed, a trim-and-fill method was used to assess differences in the results.[15] All the above analyses were performed in STATA statistical software, version 15.1.

3. Results

3.1. Literature retrieval

We retrieved 8038 articles, and 1 study was supplemented from citations, making a total of 8039 articles. One thousand eight hundred sixty-seven duplicate articles were excluded, the remaining 4667 articles were roughly screened based on title and abstract, and 6045 irrelevant studies were excluded. For the remaining 127 articles, 27 were included after reviewing the full text for detailed evaluation. The screening process is shown in Figure 1.

Flow chart of study design.

3.2. Basic characteristics of included studies

Of the 27 included papers, 14 reported the risk of stroke in combination with COPD,[3,5,16–25] 6 reported the risk of stroke in combination with AECOPD.[19,26–30] The risk of death after stroke was reported in 12 articles.[8–10,16,26,31–37] Most studies of stroke risk in COPD adjusted for risk factors such as age, sex, and comorbidities, and only 2 studies had single-factor regression analysis data.[17,38] Four studies provided separate data on the risk of ischemic stroke,[17–20] and 4 studies provided separate risk data for hemorrhagic stroke.[16,18–20] All but two of these studies[16,36] provided all the data on the risk of stroke. Most stroke risk studies in AECOPD were self-control studies of COPD cohorts, so there were fewer confounding factors to consider.

Of the stroke prognosis studies, a total of 8 cohorts in 6 publications reported short-term patient prognosis outcomes.[9,10,16,33,36,37] A total of 10 cohorts in 8 publications provided data on the risk of death at more than 1 year of follow-up after stroke.[8,9,26,31–35] Among the prognostic studies, 1 study provided data on the risk of stroke prognosis for COPD with different diagnostic modalities.[8] Therefore, the conclusions of the included studies were adjusted for risk factors such as age, sex, and comorbidities. 3.3. Quality assessment

NOS scores were assigned to the included studies, of which the vast majority were of high quality (score ≥ 7.0), and 2 study were considered to be of moderate quality (NOS score of 6).

3.4. Data analysis

3.4.1. Data on the effect of COPD on the risk of stroke.

In the 14 papers dealing with the risk of stroke in COPD, COPD patients were compared with the normal population, and the stroke risk outcomes were pooled using a random-effects model: OR 1.40 (95% CI: 1.24–1.59, I2 = 98.4%, P = .000) (Fig. 2). Sensitivity analysis of the included studies, omitting 1 study at a time and recalculating the pooled OR of the other studies, found that Miguel-Diez, 2021(I)[36] had a significant effect on the results (Fig. 3A). Considering that this study provides the risk of stroke in male patients with COPD and has a small confidence interval, it may have a large effect on the results when pooling them by the inverse variance method. Excluding this study, the pooled outcome was OR 1.44 (95% CI: 1.27–1.64, I2 = 96.8%, P = .000), which did not qualitatively affect the results or reduce the heterogeneity of the combined outcome. No source of heterogeneity was found by subgroup analysis for the type of stroke diagnosis, the different regions to which the study was carried out, or the mode of analysis used (Table 4). Funnel plots were further drawn to observe for symmetry, and the results did not suggest significant publication bias (Fig. 4A).

3.4.2. Data on the effect of AECOPD on the risk of stroke.

A total of 7 studies from 6 publications reported the association between AECOPD and stroke, as seen after pooling risk data using a fixed-effects model: OR 1.53 (95% CI: 1.44–1.63, I2 = 49.2%, P = .066) (Fig. 5). Sensitivity analysis of the included studies showed that none significantly affected the results (Fig. 3B), suggesting that the pooled results were relatively robust. The funnel plot showed symmetry and did not suggest significant publication bias (Fig. 4B).

Forest plot of stroke risk in COPD. COPD = chronic obstructive pulmonary disease.

3.4.3. Short-term mortality risk outcomes in patients with COPD combined with stroke.

In the 8 cohorts of 6 papers involving short-term mortality risk, we pooled the relevant mortality risk outcomes using a Fixed-effects model: OR 1.12 (95% CI: 1.08–1.16, I2 = 37.4% P = .131) (Fig. 6). Sensitivity analysis of the included studies showed that no study had a large effect on the results (Fig. 3C), suggesting that the pooled results were relatively robust. Funnel plots displayed slightly asymmetric (Fig. 4C), suggesting that there may be a potential publication bias that may have affected the results. Further Begg and Egger tests were performed, and the results did not suggest significant publication bias (Begg test and Egger test = 0.386 and 0.130, respectively).

Forest plot of short-term mortality risk in COPD with stroke. COPD = chronic obstructive pulmonary disease.

3.4.4. Long-term mortality risk outcomes in patients with COPD combined with stroke.

In the 10 cohorts of 8 papers involving long-term mortality risk, we pooled the relevant mortality risk outcomes using a random-effects model: OR 1.20 (95% CI: 1.13–1.27, I2 = 56.8%, P = .014) (Fig. 7), and there was moderate heterogeneity in the combination, with subgroup analysis showing that stroke type may be a source of heterogeneity and the risk of death from ischemic stroke: OR 1.23, 95% CI: 1.17–1.29, I2 = 45.0%, P = .191 and the risk of death from both types of stroke: OR 1.12, 95% CI: 1.07–1.18, I2 = 18.9%, P = .291 (Table 5). Sensitivity analysis showed that no study greatly affected the outcome (Fig. 3D). The funnel plot was also slightly asymmetric (Fig. 4D), and further Begg and Egger tests were performed, and the results did not support significant publication bias (Begg test and Egger test = 0.152 and 0.056, respectively). Further trim-and-fill method was performed, and after supplementing the 4 potential studies, the pooled final result OR 1.162 (95% CI: 1.093–1.237) (Fig. 8) still suggested an increased risk and no qualitative change in outcome.

4. Discussion

This paper is the first meta-analysis to comprehensively explore the relationship between COPD and stroke, summarizing the risk of stroke in COPD and AECOPD and the risk of short-term and long-term death after COPD combined with stroke.

Our main findings are that in most studies investigating stroke risk in COPD patients, stroke risk was higher in COPD patients, and AECOPD was more closely associated with stroke risk than in COPD patients. One interesting observation in the pooled results of prognostic studies is that in the short term, COPD is not significantly associated with the risk of death from stroke, whereas in the long-term follow-up, there is good evidence that COPD can be an independent risk factor for death after stroke.

It is acknowledged that the mechanisms and outcomes of COPD and increased stroke risk are complex and uncertain. Risk factors for COPD and stroke often overlap, such as smoking history, advanced age, obesity, and comorbidities such as AF, but a growing number of studies have concluded that patients with COPD have a causative risk of stroke independent of common risk factors. Smoking is a critical risk factor for COPD, not only reducing lung function in COPD patients and is associated with worse FEV1/FVC but also causing damage to the vascular endothelium, leading to thrombosis and thus increasing the risk of stroke.[9] In this regard, we further reviewed all included studies, in which the results adjusted for confounding factors, including smoking, which were suggestive of an increased risk of stroke.[5,26] In addition to the effect of smoking, there is reason to believe that COPD is associated with stroke. Atrial fibrillation is an absolute risk factor for ischemic stroke, and in a recent meta-analysis, patients with COPD were shown to have an increased risk of AF, which may be related to the cardiovascular pathological changes caused by COPD, which in turn increases the risk of stroke.[39] Most of the studies also adjusted for the effects of AF, effectively reducing the impact of these comorbidities on the outcome.

It is now believed that, in addition to common causative factors, COPD is independently associated with stroke primarily by systemic inflammation and oxidative stress.[7] Firstly, in addition to pulmonary inflammation, COPD is usually associated with chronic systemic inflammation, especially in AECOPD, where inflammatory markers such as C-reactive protein, TNFα, IL-6, and IL-8 are significantly elevated, and systemic inflammation is also strongly associated with stroke.[40,41] Secondly, the relationship between oxidative stress and COPD is relatively straightforward, as oxidized low-density lipoprotein, advanced oxidation protein products, and malondialdehyde, among the most significant markers of the oxidative stress system, were elevated in COPD patients.[42] In contrast, antioxidants glutathione, vitamin C, uric acid, vitamin E, albumin, SOD, catalase, and glutathione were found to be downregulated in COPD patients.[43,44] The combined effect of systemic inflammation and oxidative stress impairs vascular endothelial cell regulation, repair function, and abnormal platelet aggregation, ultimately leading to stroke events.

Many interrelated factors in the prognostic outcome can explain the increased risk of death in COPD patients. Firstly, the airway structure of COPD patients is significantly different from the normal population, and the incidence of post-stroke pneumonia is significantly higher due to airway spasms, destruction and collapse of the airway wall, poor cilia function, and sputum aggregation.[9,45] Secondly, some studies have also reported that COPD patients’ mortality may be associated with post-stroke complications such as urinary tract infection, acute heart attack, and congestive heart failure.[26,36] Therefore, these comorbidities in COPD patients may increase the risk of post-stroke death. Finally, patients with COPD may also experience acute exacerbations. AECOPD is associated with hypoxemia, hypercapnia, systemic inflammatory response, and oxidative stress, which can exacerbate brain injury and lead to stroke-related death due to impaired blood and oxygen supply to the brain.

When the COPD-stroke mortality correlation was divided into short-term and long-term, we found that the evidence for the correlation of COPD effects on the short-term risk of death in stroke was not very strong, Studies by Lekoubou and Ovbiagele,[10] Szylińska et al,[9] and de Miguel-Diez et al[16] did not support this correlation, and they concluded that COPD has a limited effect on early stroke mortality. In contrast, COPD appears to have a progressive effect on stroke mortality as time prolongs.[9] This may be because COPD is a chronic progressive disease that causes damage to pulmonary function and the cardiovascular system during repeated acute exacerbations, leading to a cumulative risk of death. A few studies have also suggested that the association of COPD with the risk of death from stroke peaks at 3 months post-stroke and then gradually declines.[33] However, there are no other cohorts to validate this conclusion and more high-quality studies are needed to elucidate the temporal relationship between COPD and stroke prognosis so that precise therapeutic interventions can be arranged for patients with COPD combined with stroke.

Our study still has some limitations. Firstly, most of the included studies were from retrospective cohorts, in which the medical records involved may not be comprehensive and accurate, and there were some differences in the adjustment for confounding factors such as comorbidities. Some studies did not even adjust for important confounding factors such as smoking, which may lead to deviation in the results. Secondly, most of the diagnostic evidence for COPD patients came from diagnosis codes in medical databases, which do not reflect the severity of COPD. GOLD classification of COPD patients significantly impacts stroke risk and prognosis,[27] and most of the studies included did not adjust for GOLD classification of COPD in multifactorial regression models, which may have biased the final results. In addition, COPD-related medications such as inhaled corticosteroids and long-acting β2-agonists may significantly reduce the risk of stroke and improve prognosis.[3] However, most of the included studies did not specify the use of COPD-related medications during follow-up or adjust for the impact of medications at the time of statistical analysis. Extra clinical studies are needed to refine our conclusions.

5. Conclusion

In conclusion, this meta-analysis based on a literature review concluded that COPD is an independent risk factor for stroke, and the risk of stroke is significantly increased, especially during AECOPD. In addition, COPD is also associated with death in the long-term prognosis of stroke patients. Therefore, when treating patients with AECOPD, close coagulation monitoring is often required, and when combined with other risk factors for stroke (e.g., AF), further prophylactic treatment must be considered.
