Dual carrier status for HUPRA (hyperuricemia, pulmonary hypertension, renal failure, and alkalosis) syndrome and Wilson disease in a young dialysis patient: clinical and genetic counseling implications
Authors: Vishal Batheja, Bhavin Modasia, Saurabh Puri, Hari Shankar Meshram, Chandani Bhagat, Rajendra Prasad Mathur
Published: 2026

To the Editor:

The emergence of genetic testing has transformed our ability to detect rare pathogenic variants, not only in affected individuals but also in asymptomatic carriers [1,2]. Here, we report the identification of dual heterozygous carrier status for HUPRA (hyperuricemia, pulmonary hypertension, renal failure, and alkalosis) syndrome (SARS2 gene) and Wilson disease (ATP7B gene) in a young patient with chronic kidney disease (CKD). Although this finding is not directly causative of his renal failure, it carries significant implications for genetic counseling, reproductive risk, and clinical follow-up.

A 26-year-old male presented with stage 5 CKD. Abdominal ultrasonography revealed bilaterally shrunken kidneys with loss of corticomedullary differentiation, precluding biopsy. He is on maintenance hemodialysis at our tertiary hepato-renal center. Whole exome sequencing was performed to evaluate potential genetic contributors to CKD, revealing the following: (1) A heterozygous splice-site donor variant in intron 9 of the SARS2 gene (c.916+2T>C) affecting the invariant Guanine-Thymine donor splice site of exon 9 was identified. The observed variant has a minor allele frequency of 0.00001 in gnomAD exomes and is novel in genomes. The reference base is conserved across species, and in silico predictions (MutationTaster, GeneSplicer, MaxEntScan, NNSplice) indicate a damaging effect, with a spliceAI score of 0.87 and a CADD (Combined Annotation Dependent Depletion) score of 33. Based on this evidence, the variant has been classified as likely pathogenic and is associated with HUPRA syndrome [3], according to the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG) guidelines. (2) A heterozygous missense variant, c.3007G>A in exon 13 of the ATP7B gene, resulting in an amino acid substitution p.Ala1003Thr, was identified. The observed variant has a minor allele frequency of 0.00005 in gnomAD exomes and is novel in genomes. The severity of the impact on the protein is high, supported by a REVEL (Rare Exome Variant Ensemble Learner) score of 0.86 and a CADD score of 25.4. ClinVar has classified this variant as pathogenic/likely pathogenic (variation ID: 188802). This variant has been observed in individuals affected by Wilson disease, and experimental studies have shown that this missense change impairs protein function [4]. Based on this evidence, the variant has been classified as pathogenic according to ACMG guidelines [5,6].

No second pathogenic variant was identified in either gene, and no clinically relevant copy number variations were detected. Thus, the patient is a carrier of two rare autosomal recessive conditions. A meticulous family history was obtained, but none of the other family members showed abnormalities related to the SARS2 gene (Fig. 1).

HUPRA syndrome typically presents in infancy with severe renal failure, metabolic alkalosis, pulmonary hypertension, hypotonia, and developmental delay. The prognosis is poor, with death often occurring in early childhood [3]. Our patient had no features suggestive of HUPRA, and the heterozygous status is consistent with asymptomatic carriage. Similarly, Wilson disease requires biallelic pathogenic ATP7B variants. Carriers are usually asymptomatic, although subtle biochemical abnormalities (e.g., borderline ceruloplasmin or copper excretion) have been reported [5,6]. In our case, serum ceruloplasmin, 24-hour urinary copper, and liver enzyme levels were normal. If the partner carries a pathogenic SARS2 variant, there is a 25% chance of offspring with HUPRA syndrome—a typically lethal neonatal-onset condition. If the partner carries a pathogenic ATP7B variant, there is a 25% chance of offspring with Wilson disease, which is treatable if diagnosed early but can be devastating if unrecognized [5,6].

Accordingly, partner testing and family cascade screening are strongly recommended. These findings also carry implications in the transplant context. Related donors sharing these variants may not be ideal kidney donors, and awareness of carrier status can guide long-term monitoring of relatives.

This case underscores the dual utility of genomic testing in nephrology: (1) phenotypic correlation: the patient’s CKD does not appear to be due to an unrecognized monogenic disorder. However, our literature search identified only one case report describing a heterozygous mutation associated with renal failure [7]. (2) Incidental findings: detection of clinically silent but reproductively significant carrier states [1,2]. Given the rarity of HUPRA syndrome and the low population carrier frequency of ATP7B variants, the coexistence of these two carrier states in a single patient is noteworthy.
