Carbonyl stress markers are associated with binge‐eating/purging behavior of anorexia nervosa: A pilot study
Authors: Kana Morimoto, Masanori Isobe, Tomomi Noda, Keima Tose, Momo Sunada, Koichi Tabata, Momoka Taniguchi, Makoto Arai, Toshiya Murai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimAnorexia nervosa (AN) is a severe psychiatric disorder causing life‐threatening physical conditions. Management strategies differ between the restricting subtype (ANR) and the binge‐eating/purging subtype (ANBP), with underreported binge‐eating/purging behaviors. Objective biomarkers are needed to detect these behaviors for improved treatment. This study explored fingertip autofluorescence (FAF) Advanced Glycation End Products (AGEs), reflecting carbonyl stress, to assess their potential in reflecting binge‐eating/purging behavior in AN.MethodsThis cross‐sectional study involved 26 female patients with AN and 27 healthy control (HC) participants. Participants completed the Eating Disorder Examination Questionnaire 6.0 (EDE‐Q 6.0) and underwent percutaneous fluorescence measurement of AGE levels. Statistical analysis included one‐way anova with Tukey's Honestly Significant Difference (HSD) test, Pearson correlation, and multiple linear regression to determine if EDE‐Q items predicted fingertip AGE levels in patients with ANBP.ResultsFingertip AGE levels and all EDE‐Q 6.0 subscale scores significantly differed among groups (p < 0.05). Post hoc analyses indicated that participants with ANBP had significantly higher fingertip AGE levels than those with ANR. Correlation analysis showed that the frequency of binge‐eating and vomiting significantly positively correlated with fingertip AGE levels. Stepwise regression analysis suggested that the frequency of vomiting significantly predicted fingertip AGE levels.ConclusionFingertip AGE levels were higher in patients with ANBP than in those with ANR. Purging behavior, which was significantly correlated with binge‐eating behavior in the ANBP group, was suggested to be a primary determinant of elevated AGE levels. Fingertip AGE levels may serve as objective biomarkers for detecting these behaviors and facilitating improved treatment strategies.

INTRODUCTION

Anorexia nervosa (AN) is a severe psychiatric disorder characterized by an intense fear of weight gain, distorted body image, and significant weight loss. 1 AN is classified into two subtypes: the restricting subtype (ANR) and the binge‐eating/purging subtype (ANBP). Severe malnutrition resulting from AN can lead to physical complications and mortality, with a mortality rate of 51 per 10,000. 2 However, the impact of malnutrition on the metabolic system and how it varies across AN conditions and subtypes remain unclear. 1 Recent studies suggest a connection between systemic metabolic stress and the pathophysiology of various psychiatric conditions; carbonyl or glycation stress has been linked to several disorders, such as schizophrenia and Alzheimer's disease. 3 , 4

Advanced Glycation End Products (AGEs), which are indicative of carbonyl stress and generated via non‐enzymatic glycation and oxidation reactions, have been shown to be higher in adolescents with ANR than in healthy controls (HCs). This elevation in AGEs is thought to result from the clearance of lipophilic AGEs due to decreased adipocytes and low antioxidant levels caused by poor nutrition in patients with ANR. 5 It has also been reported that patients with AN have lower levels of antioxidants such as apolipoprotein B (Apo B), which is involved in lipid metabolism, compared with HC. 6 AGE levels have not been shown to be elevated in ANBP. However, overeating has been reported to increase malondialdehyde and affect lipid metabolism, and repeated vomiting may lead to chronic inflammation of the gastrointestinal tract and increased oxidative stress. 7 , 8 These may lead to elevated AGE levels in ANBP, reflecting an excessive metabolic load. Based on these previous studies, we preliminarily hypothesized that AGE levels in ANBP are higher than those in HC, as well as in ANR, and that patients with ANBP exhibit higher AGE levels than those with ANR due to the effects of overeating and vomiting.

Skin autofluorescence (SAF) enables the noninvasive assessment of AGEs. 9 A fingertip SAF reader has been developed, and its correlation with serum AGE levels has been confirmed. 10 The relationship between fingertip AGEs and psychotic symptoms among adolescents has also been reported. 3 This method offers a precise, noninvasive tool for measuring AGEs, thus advancing research and clinical applications.

This pilot study aimed to compare fingertip AGE levels among patients with ANBP, ANR, and HC using a fingertip autofluorescence (FAF) reader. We focused on exploring whether carbonyl stress markers are primarily associated with chronic malnutrition (as estimated by body mass index [BMI]) or with the binge‐eating/purging behaviors that are characteristic of AN.

METHODS

The study involved a small clinical sample of 26 female patients with AN and 27 age‐ and sex‐matched HC participants determined through an a priori power analysis. Twenty‐nine outpatients with AN were recruited from Kyoto University, and three patients were recruited from other hospitals or clinics in Kyoto. Three patients were excluded due to missing self‐reported measure data or measured AGE values. The final sample consisted of 26 female patients with AN (11 restricting type/15 binge‐eating/purging type; mean age: 31.0 years, standard deviation: 7.44 years; age range: 22–59 years; average duration of illness: 13.6 years) and 27 age‐ and sex‐matched HC. All patients were recruited between 2017 and 2019.

Diagnosis and sub‐classification of AN were confirmed by a psychiatrist who conducted structured interviews using the Japanese version of the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition (DSM‐5). 11 The exclusion criteria were as follows: patients under 17 and over 60 years old, patients with intellectual disability, schizophrenia, bipolar I disorder, neurocognitive disorder, neurological diseases, diseases affecting the metabolism of the central nervous system, and individuals with a history of significant head injury.

The number of planned study subjects was 25 in each HC and AN group. The basis for this calculation was Kouidrat et al. 12 and Yang et al. 13 And a comparison of the mean values of AGEs of two independent samples (t‐test), setting α = 0.05 and 1 − β = 0.80, required nine cases in a single group. Assuming dropout, we set a target of 25 patients each.

All participants completed the Eating Disorder Examination Questionnaire 6.0 (EDE‐Q 6.0). 14 This questionnaire includes subscales for restraint, eating concerns, shape concerns, and weight concerns, as well as a global score. Additionally, percutaneous fluorescence measurements of AGE levels were obtained from the left middle finger using an FAF reader (Sharp Life Science Corporation, Osaka, Japan). The RQ‐AG01J autofluorescence reader can noninvasively measure AGEs accumulation in the body within 30–60 s by simply inserting a fingertip. The details of the measurement are described in a previous study. 10 The FAF was assessed at an excitation wavelength of 340 nm and an emission wavelength of 440 nm. For statistical analysis, one‐way analysis of variance was used to compare age, BMI, fingertip AGE levels, and EDE‐Q 6.0 subscale and global scores among groups. Multiple post hoc comparisons were conducted using Tukey's test when equal variances and normality were confirmed, and the Games–Howell test in cases of confirmed normality but unequal variances. Effect sizes for age, BMI, AGE levels, and EDE‐Q subscales were calculated using η 2 from the anova. Illness duration, based on time since symptom onset, was compared between the ANR and ANBP groups using Student's t‐test, with Cohen's d used to estimate the effect size. An ROC (Receiver Operating Characteristic) analysis was performed to differentiate AGE values between ANR and ANBP. The Youden index was used as the optimization criterion. Pearson correlation analyses were conducted between fingertip AGE levels and age, BMI, number of episodes of overeating, vomiting, laxative use, and excessive exercise per 28 days (as measured by the EDE‐Q 6.0). Multiple linear regression analysis was performed on a clinical sample of 26 patients in the disease group and 27 HC participants to determine whether any of the above EDE‐Q 6.0 items were predictors of fingertip AGE levels. Stepwise regression analysis was utilized to avoid multicollinearity.

The study and its procedures complied with ethical standards and the Helsinki Declaration of 1975, as revised in 2008. All procedures were approved by the ethics committee of Kyoto University Graduate School and the Faculty of Medicine (R0992), and written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

RESULTS

There was no significant difference in age among the ANR, ANBP, and HC groups (p > 0.05). However, BMI (p < 0.001), fingertip AGE levels (p = 0.006), and all subscale scores and the global score of the EDE‐Q 6.0 differed significantly among groups (all p < 0.001). Illness duration differed significantly between the ANR and ANBP groups (p = 0.015, d = 1.05). Post hoc analyses of fingertip AGE levels revealed that participants with ANBP had significantly higher AGE levels than those with ANR (p = 0.006, η 2 = 0.19) (Table 1). The box plot of fingertip AGE levels among the three groups is shown in Figure 1. The fingertip AGE levels in the HC were comparable to those reported in previous studies. 3 ROC analysis was performed, and the cut‐off value to discriminate between ANR and ANBP was 0.46 on the Youden index (Figure S1).

DISCUSSION

This pilot study found that fingertip AGE levels were higher in participants with ANBP than in participants with ANR. Although we found no significant differences in fingertip AGE levels between the two AN groups and HC within this small sample, the frequency of vomiting in the ANBP group correlated positively with fingertip AGE levels. These preliminary results suggest that the purging behavior of participants with ANBP might be associated with binge‐eating behaviors rather than chronic malnutrition alone, potentially causing the elevation of fingertip AGEs.

The combination of excessive nutritional intake and inflammation, which typically occurs in patients with ANBP, is considered a significant factor for glycation stress. Overeating in those with ANBP may increase glycation stress through excessive carbohydrate intake. Additionally, malnutrition and chronic stress in AN persistently activate the HPA axis, leading to a significant increase in resting cortisol levels. Elevated cortisol enhances gluconeogenesis, intermittently creating a state of relative hyperglycemia, which supplies substrates for glycation reactions. Previous studies suggest that individuals with ANBP exhibit a higher cortisol awakening response. 15 Compared to those with ANR, individuals with ANBP may be more prone to blood glucose fluctuations and sudden spikes in hyperglycemia, making them more susceptible to glycation. 15 Furthermore, frequent vomiting may induce local inflammation in the upper gastrointestinal tract, such as persistent gastric acid reflux disease, potentially exacerbating glycation stress by impairing antioxidant defenses. 7 , 16 Such acceleration of glycation, activation of the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis, and chronic inflammation may lead to impaired neurogenesis and synaptic dysfunction in the brain. 17 In addition, elevated corticotropin‐releasing hormone (CRH) levels may suppress appetite, resulting in a sustained state of starvation. 18 These processes might contribute to cognitive rigidity and food avoidance in AN, thereby perpetuating a vicious cycle.

Furthermore, the higher levels of fingertip AGEs in ANBP may reflect the accelerated aging of body tissues, such as osteoporosis, which is a serious complication in patients with AN. 19 , 20 Similarly, AGEs are involved in telomere shortening and contribute to the aging process. 21 Therefore, the binge‐eating/purging‐associated pathophysiology of ANBP might accelerate the aging process, potentially contributing to the poor prognosis and high mortality rate of patients with AN.

The differences observed between the ANR and ANBP groups in this preliminary study suggest that fingertip AGE levels could objectively reflect the frequency of binge‐eating and purging, potentially making them clinically useful for assessing severity. This assessment is often difficult due to underreporting; thus, percutaneous FAF measurement of AGE levels may provide an objective, albeit crude, physical risk indicator of binge‐eating/purging behavior in patients with AN and may promote precision medicine.

A previous study demonstrated that AGEs are elevated in ANR shortly after the onset of puberty, proposing that an excessive oxidative stress environment promotes protein glycation mediated by α‐dicarbonyl compounds, which are byproducts of lipid peroxidation, thereby accelerating AGE formation. Additionally, in the case of anorexia‐associated low body fat content, its trapping capacity was exceeded with a consequent increase in plasma concentrations of AGEs. 5 However, in the present study, fingertip AGE levels were found to be comparable between participants with ANR and HC. This discrepancy may be attributed to the severely lower BMI (<13 kg/m2) of our ANR participants. This extreme starvation may have resulted in prolonged hypoglycemia, impairing the Maillard reaction. In addition, dietary‐derived AGEs may have been minimized. Alternatively, starvation might have induced heightened hepatic autophagy, and it is possible that autophagy contributed to the suppression of oxidative stress and the downregulation of AGE production. 22 , 23 As a result of these factors working in combination, it is possible that the fingertip AGE levels in ANR participants in this study did not show a significant difference compared with HC.

This study has several limitations. First, most notably, the small sample size limits the generalizability of the findings. Second, the extreme underweight status of the ANR group may have influenced the results. Third, blood parameters such as glucose levels and detailed dietary data were not collected. Fourth, the time of fingertip AGE measurement was not controlled. In addition, we used transdermal fluorescence estimation of AGE levels rather than direct testing using actual samples such as blood and urine. Given the pilot nature of this work, replication studies with larger cohorts are essential to confirm these results. Additionally, AGE levels should be compared between patients with bulimia nervosa, patients with binge‐eating disorder, and HC with similar BMIs to control for body weight and confirm that binge‐eating/purging behavior elevates AGE levels.

In conclusion, this study provides preliminary evidence that binge‐eating/purging behavior may be a primary determinant of elevated AGE levels in AN, and fingertip AGE levels may serve as objective biomarkers for detecting these behaviors and facilitating improved treatment strategies.

A PUBLIC SIGNIFICANCE STATEMENT

This study investigated Advanced Glycation End Products (AGEs), reflecting carbonyl stress from glycation and oxidation reactions. Finding revealed higher fingertip AGE levels in the binge‐eating/purging subtype (ANBP) compared to the restricting subtype (ANR), with purging behavior being a primary determinant. Fingertip AGEs may indicate elevated oxidative stress in ANBP and may serve as objective biomarkers for detecting these behaviors and facilitating improved treatment strategies.
