Persistent phenological synchrony in a coral-bivalve symbiosis across five decades
Authors: Noga Gavrieli, Tal Amit, Maya Gross, Netanel Kramer, Yossi Loya
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryLong-term species interactions are often sensitive to environmental change, yet some symbioses maintain coordinated phenological patterns over extended timescales. We examined a five-decade record of reproductive timing in the Red Sea coral Stylophora pistillata and its boring bivalve symbiont Leiosolenus lessepsianus at 30 m depth in the Gulf of Eilat/Aqaba. Historical data (1970s–80s) were compared with monthly observations from 2021–22 of coral and bivalve reproductive development. Both species exhibited clear shifts in seasonal phenology, yet their temporal synchrony remained intact. S. pistillata displayed a 3-month extension of its planula-release season, whereas L. lessepsianus now begins and ends reproduction approximately 1 month later than historically observed. Despite these shifts, larval settlement continues to coincide with the coral’s reproductive period. These findings demonstrate sustained phenological coordination within a tightly integrated symbiosis and underscore the importance of multi-decadal datasets in resolving ecological stability under environmental change.Subject areas: Zoology, Aquatic biology

Introduction

Coral reefs are among the most diverse and productive marine ecosystems, sustained by intricate symbiotic networks.1,2,3 Scleractinian corals, the principal framework builders of tropical reefs, engage in a spectrum of symbioses ranging from microbial to invertebrate associations, which collectively constitute the coral holobiont4,5 and underpin reef health and resilience. In addition to their well-known partnership with symbiotic algae6 and bacteria,5,7 corals host a diverse array of invertebrates, including mollusks, polychaetes, and crustaceans.8 These associations range from facultative relationships to highly specific and obligate partnerships, often reflecting deep evolutionary integration.9 At least 860 invertebrate species have been identified as coral-associated, with over half exhibiting obligate dependence on live corals.8 These complex associations enhance coral reef resilience and ecological stability,2 thereby supporting marine biodiversity.3

Among the less studied yet ecologically widespread coral symbioses is the association between hermatypic corals and their boring bivalves. Traditionally, boring bivalves have been regarded as parasites10,11 primarily because of their bioerosive activity, which causes internal structural damage to coral skeletons.12,13 Nevertheless, several studies14,15,16 suggest that this relationship may offer mutual benefits. Accordingly, this interaction may be better described along a mutualism-parasitism continuum, where outcomes are context-dependent and influenced by host health, resource availability, and environmental stressors.17

An example of a potential benefit from a boring bivalve can be found in Leiosolenus simplex (formerly Lithophaga simplex; Iredale, 1939), which inhabits Astreopora myriophthalma (Lamarck, 1816) and releases ammonium as a nitrogenous byproduct, potentially enhancing nutrient availability for the coral’s symbiotic algae.14 This localized ammonium enrichment may stimulate photosynthetic efficiency in Symbiodiniaceae, particularly under nutrient-limited reef conditions.18 By analyzing oxygen flux measurements, Amit et al. further showed that L. simplex relies on the coral host as a complementary food source.16 Given the pronounced diel fluctuations in oxygen levels within coral diffusive boundary layers (DBLs),19 such metabolic coupling could be particularly beneficial under conditions of elevated temperatures or eutrophication, which intensify nighttime hypoxia in coral microenvironments.20 In addition to physiological exchange, species-specific interactions and recognition mechanisms are fundamental to coral-bivalve associations.21,22,23,24 Leiosolenus purpureus exhibits host-specific reproductive patterns, with populations inhabiting different coral species displaying distinct seasonal traits.23 Leiosolenus larvae also display strong host specificity and select coral hosts during the larval stage,21,22 indicating a well-established evolutionary relationship between these organisms. Such host specificity may reflect coevolutionary dynamics or partner fidelity feedback, wherein long-term mutual benefits reinforce specificity and behavioral synchronization.25

Despite increasing evidence that environmental stress can decouple long-standing symbioses,2,26,27 some associations demonstrate remarkable temporal and functional resilience. In the northern Gulf of Eilat/Aqaba (GoE/A), the boring bivalve L. lessepsianus shows an exclusive association with the coral S. pistillata.13,28 L. lessepsianus larvae strongly prefer S. pistillata as a settlement substrate, rarely completing metamorphosis in the absence of a coral host; metamorphosis occurs within 1 month when coral extracts are present, compared with up to 4 months in their absence.28 Arazi further showed that although larvae can settle between or within coral polyps in laboratory settings, they primarily settle within polyp mouths in nature, likely gaining protection from predation by Trapezia crabs.29 Coral polyps neither consume nor reject bivalve larvae upon settlement, suggesting refined interspecific tolerance, similar to that observed in coral-goby and coral-crab associations mediated by chemical or tactile cues.30 Together, these observations suggest a well-established evolutionary relationship characterized by host-recognition mechanisms that extend beyond larval settlement to later developmental stages. The exclusive association and high specificity between S. pistillata and L. lessepsianus thus raise compelling questions regarding the long-term stability and adaptive resilience of this symbiosis.25 Several studies have documented shifts in the reproductive cycle of S. pistillata in the GoE/A.31,32,33 Historically (mid-1970s to the early 1980s), in shallow waters, the reproductive season spanned from December to July, with a peak in planula release during April-May, whereas deeper colonies exhibited a shorter season of 2 to 3 months.31 More recent findings indicate a prolonged reproductive season in shallow-water colonies, extending by up to 2 months and featuring a longer peak.33 These findings illustrate the phenological plasticity of reef-building corals, which may be driven by altered thermal regimes and photoperiod cues. Although coral reproductive timing has shifted, the present-day reproductive cycle of L. lessepsianus remains poorly documented, leaving open the question of whether the bivalve’s reproductive cycle has shifted in synchrony with that of its host.

Here, we revisit and expand upon a unique long-term coral-bivalve association first documented by Loya34 (Figure 1), evaluating whether reproductive synchrony between S. pistillata and L. lessepsianus has persisted in the face of five decades of environmental change. By comparing historical datasets (1970s–80s) with newly collected reproductive data (2021–22), we tested the hypothesis that both species have shifted their reproductive cycles in parallel, preserving phenological synchrony that may enhance bivalve larval settlement and stabilize long-term host-symbiont dynamics.

Results

Coral-bivalve association

Following decalcification, L. lessepsianus individuals were clearly visible within the coral hosts (Figure 2). Multiple individuals were found, with up to 83 individuals per ∼5 cm fragment. Their boring holes (BH) were visible in the exposed coral skeleton, forming apertures larger than adjacent coral polyps (Figure 2A). Dissection of coral tissue enabled direct extraction of bivalves embedded within the skeleton. During reproduction periods, males and females were distinguishable by visual inspection: males exhibited white sperm ducts, whereas females displayed pink to purple gonads (Figure 2B), consistent with previous descriptions.35

L. lessepsianus reproduction

Histological sections revealed the anatomy of L. lessepsianus, with gonads embedded within the visceral mass and a visible mantle (Figure 3). In females, the ovary occupied the central visceral mass and consisted of numerous developing oocytes at various stages (Figure 3A). The paired gills flank the body laterally and are composed of parallel lamellae. The digestive ceca, located posterior to the gonads, exhibited extensive lobulation. A specialized posterior boring gland was observed, consistent with its role in skeletal bioerosion. The mantle and adductor musculature were clearly visible. In males, the gonad (Figure 3B) also occupied a large portion of the visceral mass and consisted of spermatogenic acini, small sac-like units where sperm develop, progressing from spermatogonia to mature spermatozoa36 (Figures 4A–4C). In females, the acini contained developing oocytes undergoing growth and vitellogenesis prior to spawning (Figure 4D).

S. pistillata reproduction

The reproductive cycle of S. pistillata was monitored to document the progression from oocyte development to mature planulae. Gonad size and histological characteristics were used to define developmental stages. As a hermaphroditic species, S. pistillata contains both ova and testes within each polyp, attached to mesenteries by stalks. Male gonads (Figures 5A and 5B) appeared as sacs of various shapes: spherical, oval, elongated, or strip-like. Because of their high morphological variability, male gonads were excluded from quantitative analyses. Female gametes developed within the mesenteries, where mature oocytes displayed a large eccentric nucleus and ooplasm densely packed with deeply acidophilic, pink-stained granules.37 Fertilized eggs were brooded internally until reaching the planula stage. As planulae developed, they progressively expanded within the polyp cavity, eventually filling the entire gastrovascular space at maturity. Mature planulae were characterized by a well-defined ectoderm enclosed by the maternal polyp ectoderm (Figure 5C).

Temporal shifts and synchrony in reproduction

The monthly distribution of oocyte and planula sizes for S. pistillata is presented in Figure 6 and Table S4. These monthly patterns reflect the histological observations described above and highlight the seasonal progression of gametogenesis. Oocytes and planulae of S. pistillata were observed during most months, with a notable absence in October, when the gonads appeared empty. Significant seasonal variation in gamete size was detected across months (p < 0.001, permutational ANOVA). Coral gamete sizes peaked between spring and summer (April–August), exceeding 250 μm, followed by a decline after summer. The winter-spring transition (January–March) demonstrated a steady increase in gamete size, with significant differences between the early months and the spring peak period (p < 0.01, permutational t test). Compared with historical deep-water observations (1979–81),31 in which planula release peaked in April–May, the 2021–22 dataset reveals a prolonged reproductive peak extending from April to August (Figure 6, Tables S4 and S5), during which planula size remained consistently high.

The reproductive biology of L. lessepsianus was compared based on two datasets: historical (1975–76, reproductive index estimated based on oocyte size and abundance; Table S2) and current (2021–22; Table S3), as presented in Figure 7. Bivalve oocyte size also showed pronounced seasonal variation (p < 0.001, permutational ANOVA), with a clear pattern of presence during the winter months and complete absence during the spring-summer period. During winter months, mean bivalve oocyte size ranged from 46.9 to 48.3 μm, with no significant differences among these winter months (p > 0.05, permutational t tests). A temporal shift was also evident in the reproductive cycle of the symbiotic bivalve, with both onset and gamete release occurring approximately 1 month later in the current dataset.

Historically (1975–76), L. lessepsianus reproduction occurred from September to February, with gamete release around February, as indicated by the sharp decline in oocyte size observed in March, whereas in 2021–22, it extended from October to March, with gamete release occurring roughly 1 month later, in March, followed by a decline in April (Figure 7). This 1-month shift coincided with the prolonged reproductive season observed in the coral host, S. pistillata. Despite the temporal shifts, the reproductive synchrony between species was preserved, with the bivalves releasing gametes approximately 1 month before the coral host.

Overall, reproductive synchrony between S. pistillata and L. lessepsianus remained consistent with historical observations across decades.

Discussion

The sustained synchrony between S. pistillata and L. lessepsianus over five decades demonstrates a high degree of phenological resilience in this tightly integrated coral-bivalve association, despite documented environmental change. Together, our findings show that while coral reproductive timing has expanded, its symbiotic bivalve has shifted in parallel, thereby maintaining temporal synchrony.

Shifts in S. Pistillata reproductive seasonality

The reproductive pattern of S. pistillata at shallow depths in the GoE/A (0–5 m) has been extensively documented,33,37,38 revealing a progressive extension of the reproductive season over the past several decades. From 1974 to 1983, reproduction extended from December–January through June–July, with peak planula release in April–May.31 Peak reproductive activity was later recorded to show a modest extension from April to June,32 while a subsequent study documented a 2-month expansion of the reproductive window, with planulae observed as late as mid-September (2015) and August (2016), and a broadening of the peak from March–May (2–3 months) to February–July (5–6 months).33 Our findings from the deeper reef (30 m) in 2021–22 are consistent with this long-term trajectory (Figure 6), revealing oocytes and planulae during most months of the year, with only a temporary gap in October. Mature planulae were present from April to August, indicating a sustained 5-month peak reproductive period and aligning with the broader extension of reproductive timing documented in recent decades. The relatively large standard error in oocyte and planula presence (Figures 6 and 7) reflects substantial intraspecific variability, with colonies at different developmental stages occurring simultaneously.

Although deep-water coral reproduction at this site has been underreported, records of planula release from deep-water colonies observed in April–May between 1978 and 198131 provide a basis for comparison. Despite differences in methodological approach, the datasets provide independent yet complementary measures of reproductive timing and therefore remain contextually comparable. The earlier study quantified the percentages of colonies shedding planulae,31 while the present study quantified oocyte and planula sizes (Figure 6). Our histological findings indicate that the planulae-release period extended from the historical peak in April–May to a broader window spanning April to August (Figure 6). In addition to the data collected during 1979–81, 3155 deep-water colonies sampled between July and November were examined histologically and were found to be devoid of oocytes. Furthermore, these authors reported that no planulae were shed during a parallel study in which branches were carefully broken in situ from an additional 80 colonies, further confirming the assertion of the shorter reproductive period of S. pistillata at that time. These observations were described in the text of their study but were not presented in figures or tables, and thus the specific year of this subset could not be determined or graphically reproduced in the present work. Both datasets were obtained from a depth of 30 m at the coral reefs of Eilat, GoE/A, ensuring spatial consistency in sampling location. However, the historical records lack continuous monthly resolution, and several months were not sampled, limiting the precision with which the exact historical reproductive window can be reconstructed. Accordingly, these findings should be interpreted cautiously, given the considerable interannual variability previously documented in S. pistillata reproduction.31

Possible environmental drivers of reproductive temporal changes

Multiple non-exclusive factors may underlie the temporal changes observed in S. pistillata reproductive phenology. In addition to environmental forcing, genetic factors or local adaptation within the species may contribute to long-term shifts, although this possibility remains to be tested. Previous studies32,33 have suggested that increased seawater temperatures and chronic environmental stressors are likely drivers of altered phenology. Similarly, elevated temperature and ultraviolet radiation have been shown to modify spawning timing in other corals.39

These observations are consistent with environmental changes documented in the northern GoE/A, including enhanced nighttime light exposure,40,41 historical eutrophication,42,43 and long-term warming.40,44,45,46 Historically, S. pistillata planula release peaked in April–May, prior to the July–August sea-surface temperature (SST) maximum,31,47 whereas contemporary colonies now continue releasing planulae after the seasonal SST peak, a pattern consistent with phenological adjustment under changing environmental conditions.33

Although the seasonal timing of maximum SST has remained stable, mean SST and the duration of warm anomalies have increased in recent decades. However, high-resolution SST records from the 1970s–1980s are not temporally aligned with historical reproductive surveys, and the contemporary dataset spans only a single year. For these reasons, incorporating SST as a statistical covariate would introduce substantial temporal bias; therefore, SST is interpreted as a contextual background rather than a modeled driver. Temperature is a well-established regulator of coral gametogenesis and spawning,48,49 and elevated temperatures can disrupt reproductive timing and reduce fertilization efficiency.26,50,51 Such reductions in reproductive performance, together with increasing artificial nighttime lighting, which also impairs key fitness-related processes in S. pistillata, including calcification, skeletal growth, photosynthesis, and planula settlement,52,53 may collectively contribute to the altered reproductive patterns observed today.

Reproductive synchronization between L. lessepsianus and S. Pistillata

Historical evidence indicates that L. lessepsianus releases its gametes to the water column subsequent to January (Figure 7, Table S2), approximately 2 months before the reproductive peak of S. pistillata. Given that L. lessepsianus larvae remain in the water column for approximately 1 month during their pelagic phase before settlement,35 this would have facilitated settlement during a period of reduced skeletal accretion in the coral host, potentially minimizing the risk of skeletal overgrowth and increasing the likelihood of post-settlement survival. Currently, S. pistillata exhibits a reproductive peak that coincides with reduced calcification rates, likely a result of energetic trade-offs during gametogenesis,54 consistent with broader trade-offs between growth and reproduction documented in corals.55 In 2021–22, the bivalve’s reproductive period was delayed by about 1 month, with gamete release occurring subsequent to February. Consequently, larval settlement would have been expected during March, still overlapping with the coral’s reproductive period (Figure 7). Thus, despite measurable temporal shifts, reproductive synchrony between the species has been maintained across decades. The persistence of this reproductive synchrony is consistent with a tightly integrated host-symbiont relationship shaped by long-term selective alignment, whereby natural selection may favor bivalve lineages whose reproductive timing remains aligned with the host’s physiological cycles. These parallel trends are shown clearly in Figure 7, which illustrates the temporal overlap between the coral’s reproductive phase and the bivalve’s gametogenic cycle. Such alignment may enhance larval recruitment success and reduce post-settlement mortality, potentially stabilizing long-term host-symbiont association dynamics. Additional support for coral-bivalve reproductive synchronization comes from studies on L. purpureus (formerly Lithophaga purpurea), which exhibits distinct reproductive seasons depending on its coral host species.23 Individuals associated with Montipora erythraea exhibit synchronous spawning with their host’s reproductive peak, whereas those inhabiting Cyphastrea chalcidicum lack this synchrony.23 Beyond reproductive synchronization, the close biological relationship between S. pistillata and L. lessepsianus also includes species-specific recognition mechanisms. It has been demonstrated that coral tissue triggers metamorphosis in the bivalve, providing strong evidence of biochemical signaling between host and symbiont.35 This host specificity begins as early as the larval stage, with L. lessepsianus larvae demonstrating selective settlement and successful metamorphosis in S. pistillata.21,22

Although reproductive synchrony between the coral host and its symbiotic bivalves likely confers a biological advantage by enhancing larval settlement success, the precise mechanisms driving this synchronized shift remain unknown. Possible drivers of this synchrony may include shared exposure to environmental stressors, since both partners inhabit and respond to similar ecological conditions.40,44,45

Chemical signaling underpins a wide range of marine ecological interactions, including mate recognition, predator avoidance, and habitat selection.56,57 During coral spawning events, studies have shown that species such as Euphyllia ancora release elevated levels of conjugated estrogens, particularly estradiol-17β glucuronide, into the surrounding water, supporting the hypothesis that this conjugated estrogen may function as a waterborne pheromone that synchronizes spawning among conspecifics.58 These chemical signals may coordinate not only the reproductive timing of corals but also influence the gametogenic cycles of associated symbionts, such as the boring bivalve L. lessepsianus. It is therefore plausible that bivalves integrate both environmental and coral-derived chemical cues to regulate gametogenesis and spawning. Supporting this hypothesis, neuroendocrinological studies on bivalves have demonstrated that signaling molecules such as serotonin are central to gametogenesis and spawning regulation.59 Symbiotic bivalves derive nutritional benefits from their coral hosts, mainly by consuming coral mucus,16,60, which contains not only metabolic waste but also a rich mixture of organic compounds and bioactive substances.61 These compounds may serve dual functions, providing nutritional resources while also acting as biochemical modulators of bivalve reproductive cycles.

Together, these findings indicate that reproductive synchrony between S. pistillata and L. lessepsianus has remained stable for over five decades, despite environmental perturbations and an extended reproductive season in corals. Such synchrony may be mediated by shared environmental cues and coral-derived chemical signals, although direct experimental validation remains to be established. Moreover, species-specific recognition mechanisms evident in early life stages emphasize a tightly coupled biological partnership that enables long-term ecological integration. While the persistence of synchrony is clear, our study was not designed to experimentally identify its mechanistic basis. These findings suggest that tightly integrated host-symbiont systems can exhibit coordinated phenological flexibility under sustained environmental change. The multi-decadal patterns documented here provide a robust empirical framework for future experimental studies aimed at resolving the environmental and biochemical mechanisms that maintain long-term host-symbiont phenological alignment.

Limitations of the study

This study provides a rare multi-decadal comparison of reproductive phenology in a coral-bivalve association; however, its scope is temporally bounded. Contemporary data are derived from a single annual cycle, and historical records lack continuous monthly resolution. Although the datasets were generated using different methodological approaches, both resolve seasonal reproductive dynamics at comparable temporal scales. While histological analyses define reproductive timing robustly, larval release success, settlement dynamics, and the mechanistic basis of host-symbiont synchrony were not experimentally tested. These constraints do not alter the documented persistence of phenological synchrony but highlight the need for multi-year and experimental approaches to resolve the environmental and biochemical drivers of long-term coupling.

Experimental model and study participant details

Coral and associated bivalve samples were collected monthly from October 2021 to September 2022 at 30 m depth in the northern Gulf of Eilat/Aqaba (GoE/A), Red Sea, using SCUBA. To minimize spatial confounding, both the historical (1970s–80s) and contemporary (2021–22) datasets were obtained from the same reef site and depth (30 m). Environmental differences between periods (e.g., SST regime, artificial nighttime lighting, and eutrophication) were therefore treated as contextual background rather than modeled covariates. Colonies selected for sampling were spaced at least 5 m apart to reduce the likelihood of repeatedly sampling clonally related or genetically similar individuals. Sampling was conducted at Dekel Beach (20-30 m depth), a reef characterized by a moderate slope (10-20°), carbonate substrate, and terrace-like geomorphological features.62 This depth was selected based on preliminary surveys and long-term monitoring conducted in 1976–77 (>300 S. pistillata colonies hosting >8,500 L. lessepsianus individuals; Loya, unpublished data; Table S1), which indicated a positive association between coral depth and bivalve abundance (Figure 7). Colonies were categorized as high or low-inhabitation, based on the initial visual assessment. Field-based detection of L. lessepsianus is limited because of its small size and cryptic morphology. Colonies without visible bivalves were provisionally classified as low-inhabitation, whereas colonies with >100 visible individuals were classified as high-inhabitation colonies. The absence of bivalves in low-inhabitation colonies was subsequently confirmed through laboratory inspection. Each month, for 12 consecutive months, coral fragments (∼5 cm in length; median volume = 2.55 cm3, range = 2-11 cm3) were collected using a hammer and chisel from five high-inhabitation colonies and five low-inhabitation colonies. The presence or absence of bivalves was re-verified in the laboratory, where low-inhabitation fragments consistently contained no bivalves. To avoid repeated sampling of the same colony, a new set of colonies was selected every month. Fragments were individually placed into labeled organza bags, transported to the laboratory, photographed, and weighed to the nearest 0.01 g.

Method details

Histological procedures

After collection, coral fragments were fixed in 4% formaldehyde prepared in filtered seawater for 24 h, rinsed in running tap water, and preserved in 70% ethanol.63 The samples were decalcified in sodium citrate buffered with 25% formic acid until fully decalcified. Following decalcification, the bivalves were gently removed and counted. Tissues from corals and bivalves were dehydrated in graded ethanol, embedded in paraffin, sectioned at 7 μm, mounted on glass slides, and stained with Mayer’s hematoxylin and Putt’s eosin (H&E).

Quantification and analysis of the reproductive cycle

The sections were visualized using a Nikon Eclipse 90i microscope with a DS-5M camera and NIS Elements D 3.2 software. These images were used to determine the sex of the bivalves and to measure precise gonadal metrics. The reproductive status was assessed by measuring the longest axis of all oocytes and planulae in each coral sample and 30 randomly chosen oocytes per female. Individual averages were used to calculate the monthly mean for both coral and bivalve oocytes. Male gonads appeared as sacs with variable morphologies. Owing to the complex morphology of male gonads, their development was not categorized, and their dimensions were excluded from quantitative analyses. However, seasonal changes in the developmental stages were recorded descriptively.

Historical data

In addition to unpublished historical data recorded in the northern GoE/A during a broad-ranging project (1975–77) examining the abundance of L. lessepsianus inhabiting S. pistillata colonies of various sizes at 3 m and 30 m depths (Figure 1; Table S1), this study also incorporated previously unpublished historical reproductive data for L. lessepsianus (1975–76; Table S2). The latter dataset comprised 916 bivalves collected at the same reef location at depths of 3 m and 30 m and was used to evaluate the reproductive status of L. lessepsianus. Specimens from both depths were pooled for analysis after permutation-based ANOVA indicated no significant differences in female reproductive measurements between depths (P = 0.99). A reproductive index (RI) based on oocyte developmental stage was used to quantify gonadal development in L. lessepsianus. The RI ranged from 0 to 3 and provided a standardized monthly estimate of reproductive maturity (0 = empty gonads; 1 = early oogenesis with small previtellogenic oocytes; 2 = advanced vitellogenic oocytes filling the acini without evidence of spawning; 3 = ripe gonads with mature oocytes and/or post-spawning individuals). These historical data were compared with the 2021–22 dataset (Table S3) to assess potential shifts in reproductive timing and long-term synchrony between L. lessepsianus and its coral host, S. pistillata.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using the R software (R Development Team, 2025). We modeled the effects of depth, coral size class (in cm), and their interaction on the total number of L. lessepsianus individuals per coral colony. Additionally, we examined the seasonal variations in coral and bivalve oocyte sizes across months. Model assumptions were verified using residual diagnostics for normality and homogeneity of variance. To account for the unbalanced design and test the robustness of the model, we applied permutation-based ANOVA using the {RVAideMemoire} package. Post-hoc pairwise comparisons among depth-size groups were conducted using permutation t-tests.
