Baicalin alleviates H2O2-induced oxidative stress injury in human umbilical vein endothelial cells by regulating the TNF--mediated MAPK/ERK1/2/MPO pathway
Authors: Jieqiong Cao, Jialong Ling, Jialei Cui, Yulin Zhao, Zhenhua Xu, Yonghong Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPreeclampsia (PE) is a leading cause of morbidity and mortality among pregnant women, postpartum mothers, and perinatal infants worldwide. Currently, there are no effective pharmacological interventions for the prevention or treatment of PE. Oxidative stress in vascular endothelial cells represents a key pathophysiological mechanism underlying PE. Baicalin, a flavonoid compound derived from the traditional Chinese medicine Scutellaria baicalensis, exhibits anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, antiapoptotic, and immunoregulatory properties. However, its potential application in PE remains insufficiently investigated. In vitro experiments were conducted to establish an oxidative stress model using human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs) exposed to hydrogen peroxide. Transcriptomic analysis was employed to identify genes significantly expressed differently. The effects of baicalin on HUVEC viability were assessed using the CCK-8 assay. Myeloperoxidase (MPO) levels were quantified via ELISA and colorimetric assays; while, the expression of MAPK/ERK1/2 signaling pathway components was analyzed using RT-qPCR and Western blotting. Cell apoptosis was evaluated by flow cytometry. Clinical studies have shown elevated MPO levels in the peripheral blood of patients with PE. Transcriptomic analysis identified the MAPK/ERK1/2 signaling pathway as a central pathway associated with oxidative stress model. Exposure to H2O2 (800 M) for 30 min reduced cell viability to 66.36%, confirming the successful establishment of the oxidative stress model in HUVECs. Treatment with baicalin at concentrations of 100/200/400 M significantly downregulated MAPK/ERK1/2 expression (P < 0.05). Moreover, baicalin reduced apoptosis at all tested concentrations (P < 0.01). Notably, while 200 and 400 M baicalin de-creased MPO secretion, 100 M baicalin increased MPO release (P < 0.05). Co-administration of 200 M baicalin with TNF- antagonists did not produce additive effects on these parameters (P > 0.05). Baicalin effectively restored MPO secretion levels, inhibited MAPK/ERK1/2 activation, and reduced apoptosis, thereby alleviating oxidative stress-induced endothelial injury under conditions mimicking preeclamptic pathology. Importantly, treatment with 100 M baicalin induced adaptive changes in MPO secretion dynamics. Furthermore, the lack of additive effects between baicalin and TNF- antagonists suggests overlapping mechanisms of action. These findings enhance our our understanding of baicalin’s molecular mechanisms and highlight its therapeutic potential for PE-related vascular endothelial dysfunction.Keywords: Baicalin, Pre-eclampsia, Endothelial cells, Oxidative stress, Mitogen-activated protein kinase kinases, PeroxidaseSubject terms: Cell biology, Medical research, Cardiovascular diseases

Introduction

Preeclampsia (PE) accounts for 2–8% of pregnancy-specific disorders and continues to pose a major clinical challenge in obstetrics, primarily characterized by vascular endothelial dysfunction1–3. The mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) signaling cascades, especially the extracellular signal-regulated kinase 1/2 (ERK1/2) pathway, play pivotal roles in the pathogenesis of PE4–6. Our previous studies7 revealed that aberrant activation of the MAPK/ERK1/2 pathway in polymorphonuclear neutrophils (PMNs) isolated from the peripheral blood of PE patients significantly promotes the formation of neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs). Additional studies have indicated that TNF- and MPO are associated with endothelial injury in preeclampsia.

Baicalin, a bioactive flavonoid derived from Scutellaria baicalensis, exhibits strong antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties8. Although previous studies have documented its protective effects in various disease models9–17, its specific regulatory mechanism on the MAPK/ERK1/2 pathway in endothelial protection remains poorly understood. This knowledge gap is particularly relevant in the context of PE treatment, where maintaining endothelial cell integrity is essential1,2.

In this study, we explored the potential of baicalin to modulate the MAPK/ERK1/2 pathway by establishing an oxidative stress model using human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs). Our systematic investigation demonstrated that baicalin confers a concentration-dependent protective effect against oxidative stress-induced injury, with the optimal dosage effectively restoring endothelial function. Mechanistically, our findings indicate that baicalin’s protective effects are comparable to those of TNF- antagonists, mediated through selective modulation of the MAPK/ERK1/2 signaling cascade. This is supported by similar regulation of key pathway components and comparable antiapoptotic efficacy.

These research findings provide valuable insights into the mechanism underlying baicalin’s role in vascular endothelial dysfunction and underscore its potential as a targeted therapeutic agent for PE. The exposure of HUVECs to hydrogen peroxide constitutes a widely accepted in vitro experimental model for inducing endothelial dysfunction and investigating oxidative stress. This in vitro model facilitates high-throughput dose-response analyses, which are essential for preliminary screening, hypothesis generation, and preparatory work prior to more resource-demanding in vivo studies. Our study has preliminarily identified the potential molecular targets and therapeutic window of baicalin through in vitro cell experiments, thereby laying the groundwork for subsequent animal studies.A schematic diagram of the proposed mechanismin this study is shown in Fig. 1.

Methods

Study sample collection

This study was reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee of the Second Hospital of Shanxi Medical University. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. Peripheral blood samples were collected from pregnant patients at the Department of Obstetrics and Gynecology of the Second Hospital of Shanxi Medical University between October 2018 and December 2019. A total of 60 peripheral blood samples were collected, with 30 obtained from patients diagnosed with preeclampsia (PE) and 30 from healthy normal controls (NCs). The PE group was defined by a diagnosis of preeclampsia accompanied by severe clinical manifestations, including the following : systolic blood pressure 160 mmHg or diastolic blood pressure 110 mmHg (bed rest, with at least 4 hours between two blood pressure measurements); thrombocytopenia (platelet count < 100 109/L); impaired liver function (serum transaminase levels more than twice the upper limit of normal), with severe and persistent right upper quadrant or epigastric pain unexplained by other conditions; impaired kidney function (serum creatinine level greater than 1.1 mg/dL or creatinine concentration greater than twice the normal value in the absence of other renal disease); pulmonary edema; and new visual impairment or central nervous system abnormalities. Exclusion criteria included: severe comorbidities and complications during pregnancy; pregnancy complicated with rheumatic immune diseases; gestational diabetes or preconception diabetes; pregnancy complicated with upper respiratory tract infection; and pregnant women with intrahepatic cholestasis during pregnancy and/or mental disorders such as irritability and depression during pregnancy. Reagents and chemicals

Baicalin (purity > 98%) was purchased from Solarbio, Beijing, China.

Hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) was purchased from Hengjian, Guangdong, China.

TNF- Antagonist III (R-7050, purity=99.14%, EC50=0.63 M) was purchased from Sparkjade, Shandong, China.

Antibodies against phosphorylated ERK1/2 (p-ERK1/2), total ERK1/2, p-MAPK1/2, MAPK1/2, and GAPDH were obtained from Abcam, Cambridge, UK.

RNA primers for MEK1/2 and ERK1/2 were purchased from Sangon, Shanghai, China.

MPO ELISA kit, Annexin V-FITC/PI Apoptosis Kit, Enhanced Cell Counting Kit 8(WST-8/CCK-8), Lactate Dehydrogenase (LDH) Activity Assay Kit was purchased from Elabscience, HuBei, China.

NADP+/NADPH ELISA kit(WST-8) was purchased from Beyotime, Shanghai, China.

Cell culture

HUVECs were purchased from ServiceBio, HuBei, China.

The cells were cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS, 100 U/mL penicillin, and 100 g/mL streptomycin at 37 C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2.

Induction of oxidative stress

The sub-lethal oxidative stress induced by H2O2 can serve as a model for endothelial dysfunction18,19, HUVECs were exposed to H2O2 at concentrations of 200, 400, 600, 800, or 1000 M for 30 min. A concentration that yielded approximately 50% cell viability was selected for subsequent experiments. Treatment with 800 M H2O2 for 30 minutes resulted in a 33.64% decrease in cell viability (CCK-8 assay, Fig. 2A), which represented an optimal balance between inducing measurable cellular injury and maintaining the potential for recovery. For the evaluation of baicalin’s protective effects, cells were first exposed to 800 M H2O2 for 30 min, followed by treatment with baicalin at concentrations of 100, 200, or 400 M for an additional 24 h.

Baicalin was dissolved in DMSO. A DMSO concentration of % has been shown to effectively reduce cytotoxic effects associated with DMSO exposure20. To prepare a 50% DMSO solution, mix equal volumes of 100% DMSO and PBS. Subsequently, prepare a 200 mM stock solution of baicalin (chemical formula: C21H18O11; molecular weight: approximately 446.36 g/mol) as follows: dissolve 89.3 mg of baicalin in 1 mL of the prepared 50% DMSO solution to obtain a 200 mM stock. Vortex the mixture for 5 min to ensure homogeneity, followed by incubation in a water bath for 5 minutes to facilitate complete dissolution. Allow the solution to equilibrate to room temperature before use. For preparation of working solutions: For a 100 M solution: dilute 1 L of the stock solution in 1999 L of DMEM, resulting in a final DMSO concentration of 0.025%. For a 200 M solution: dilute 1 L of the stock solution in 999 L of DMEM, resulting in a final DMSO concentration of 0.05%. For a 400 M solution: dilute 2 L of the stock solution in 998 L of DMEM, resulting in a final DMSO concentration of 0.1%.

RT–qPCR

Total RNA was extracted via the Total RNA Extraction Kit (Solarbio, Beijing, China) and reverse-transcribed into cDNA via the M5 Super Plus qPCR RT Kit with gDNA Remover (Mei5bio, Beijing, China). qPCR was performed via SYBR Green Master Mix (Mei5bio, Beijing, China) on a QuantStudio 6 Flex Real-Time PCR System (Thermo Fisher Scientific). -actin served as the internal control. All the antibodies were diluted 1:1000. The primers and corresponding sequences utilized in the experiment are summarized in Table 2.

Western blot analysis

Total protein was extracted from the cells via RIPA buffer containing protease and phosphatase inhibitors. Protein concentrations were measured via a BCA protein assay kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Equal amounts of protein (30 g) were separated by SDS-PAGE and subsequently transferred onto PVDF membranes. The membranes were blocked with 5% nonfat milk and incubated overnight at 4 C with primary antibodies against p-ERK1/2, total ERK1/2, p-MAPK1/2, MAPK1/2, and GAPDH. Following incubation with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies, the protein bands were visualized via an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) detection system (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA). The antibody concentrations used were all 1:1000.

Measurement of MPO activity

MPO activity in the cell culture supernatants was measured via a commercial MPO enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kit. The content of MPO in human peripheral blood was determined via colorimetry. The absorbance was measured at 450 nm via a microplate reader (BioTek, Winooski, VT, USA).

Flow cytometry

Approximately cells are resuspended in 500 L of 1 x Annexin V binding buffer. 5 L of Annexin V-FITC and 2.5 L of PI staining solution are added successively and gently vortexed to mix. After incubation at room temperature in the dark for 20 min, the cells are immediately detected using a flow cytometer, and FlowJo software (version 10.8.1) is used for quantitative analysis of the cell cycle. Quadrants: Q1 (necrosis, Annexin V-/PI+), Q2 (late apoptosis, Annexin V+/PI+), Q3 (viable cells, Annexin V-/PI-), and Q4 (early apoptosis, Annexin V+/PI-).

Statistical analysis

The data are presented as the means ± SDs from at least three independent experiments. Statistical analysis was performed via GraphPad Prism 8.0 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). Differences between groups were by one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post hoc test. A P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

The MPO level (S) was 105.99 ± 14.32 U/L in the PE group and 37.78 ± 10.81 U/L in the control group (P < 0.01) (Fig.2).

Dose–response relationships of hydrogen peroxide-induced oxidative stress in HUVECs

After exposure to H2O2 solutions of 100, 200, 400, 600, and 800 M for 30 min, the viability of HUVECs was assessed via the CCK-8 assay. The resulting survival rates were 100%, 94.32%, 119.7%, 75.75%, 57.74%, and 66.36%, respectively (Fig. 3A). Concentration- and time-dependent effects of baicalin on HUVEC viability

To explore the effective concentration of baicalin, HUVECs were treated with baicalin solutions at concentrations of 50, 100, 200, 400, and 600 M for durations of 6, 12, and 24 h. Cell viability was assessed via the CCK-8 assay. As the duration of treatment with baicalin increased, a corresponding decrease in cell viability was observed. After a treatment period of 24 h, all concentrations of baicalin had inhibitory effects on cell survival. Notably, a solution containing 600 M baicalin significantly reduced the survival rate of HUVECs. The detailed results are presented in Fig. 4A. To assess the potential cytotoxic effects of DMSO on HUVECs, we conducted a CCK-8 viability assay (Fig. 4B). Our results indicated that exposure to 0.1% DMSO did not significantly affect HUVEC viability compared to untreated controls (P > 0.05).

Lactate Dehydrogenase(LDH) release was quantitatively assessed using a LDH Activity Assay Kit (Fig. 4C). The results showed that even at the highest concentration of baicalin (400 M), there was no statistically significant increase in LDH specific activity.

On the basis of the above experimental results, baicalin at concentrations of 100 M, 200 M, and 400 M was selected for further analysis.

The MAPK pathway is significantly enriched in the transcriptomic profiling of oxidative stress-related genes

To investigate the changes in gene expression in the oxidative stress model of HUVECs, transcriptomic analysis was performed on four samples from both the control group (Ctrl) and the model group (Treat). The threshold for differential expression was set at a fold change (FC) 2 or FC 0.5 (i.e., |log2FC| 1) with a false discovery rate (q value) < 0.05. For multigroup comparisons without fold change criteria, genes with q < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. Compared with the control group, the oxidative stress model group presented a total of 317 differentially expressed genes (DEGs), including 155 upregulated and 162 downregulated genes (Fig. 5A). A heatmap was generated to visualize the expression patterns of 100 DEGs across 8 sample groups (rows = genes, columns = samples). After the expression levels were converted to log2(FPKM+1) values, the data were normalized via score transformation (mean = 0, standard deviation = 1), with the color scale ranging from blue (low expression) to red (high expression) (Fig. 5B).

KEGG23–25 enrichment analysis revealed significant enrichment of several pathways, such as the oxytocin pathway (hsa04921), the IL-17 pathway (hsa04657), and the MAPK pathway (hsa04010), in the oxidative stress model in HUVECs (Fig. 5C). Within the MAPK pathway, 3 genes were downregulated, and 2 genes were upregulated, primarily influencing environmental information processing (P < 0.01) (Fig. 5D,E).

GO analysis indicated that DEGs were significantly enriched in functional categories related to membrane structure (GO:0016020), cytoplasm (GO:0005737), protein binding (GO:0005515), the plasma membrane (GO:0005886), and the cytoskeleton (GO:0005856) (FDR < 0.01). These findings suggest that the oxidative stress model of HUVECs may alter the cellular phenotype by interfering with processes such as membrane integrity, signal transduction, and the regulation of cell morphology (Fig. 5F–H).

Effects of baicalin on MEK1/2 and ERK1/2 mRNA expression in HUVECs under oxidative stress

ERK is the most classical member of the MAPK family, and the Raf-MEK-ERK signaling pathway is the most well-defined pathway within the MAPK signal transduction cascade, primarily involved in the regulation of cellular processes such as proliferation and differentiation. The MAPK/ERK signaling pathway activated by TNF- is also a critical component of the broader MAPK signaling network26. To identify the molecular target of baicalin, molecular docking was employed to assess its binding affinity to the TNF- receptor (TNFRSF1A) and RAF1. The results demonstrated that, compared with RAF1, baicalin exhibited higher binding stability, affinity, and specificity toward TNFRSF1A (Fig. 6A,B).

(B) baicalin interacts with RAF1 through four hydrogen bonds, with a docking energy of − 7.0 kilocalories per mole.

The mRNA levels of MEK1/2 and ERK1/2 were significantly elevated in the oxidative stress model (P < 0.05). In the oxidative stress model of HUVECs, treatment with baicalin (at concentrations of 100, 200, and 400 M) significantly reduced the mRNA expression levels of MEK1/2 and ERK1/2 (P < 0.05) in a dose-dependent manner (Fig. 7A–D).

Specifically, compared with the model group: 100 M baicalin significantly decreased the mRNA expression levels of MEK2 and ERK1/2; 200 M baicalin significantly reduced the mRNA expression levels of MEK1/2 and ERK2; 400 M baicalin significantly decreased the mRNA expression levels of MEK1/2 and ERK1/2.

Compared with the oxidative stress model of HUVECs, treatment with 0.63 M TNF- antagonist significantly reduced the mRNA expression levels of MEK1/2 and ERK1/2 (P < 0.05), indicating the effectiveness of the antagonist in reducing MEK1/2 and ERK1/2 mRNA expression levels. Pretreatment with 0.63 M TNF- antagonist for 30 minutes followed by the addition of 200 M baicalin resulted in no significant difference in MEK1/2 and ERK1/2 mRNA expression compared with that in the TNF- antagonist group, indicating that the inhibitory effect of baicalin strongly depends on the functional TNF- pathway.

However, this combined treatment led to a greater reduction in mRNA expression than did the group treated with 200 M baicalin alone, indicating that the basic activation of TNF- is a “necessary condition” for baicalin to exert its effect.

Baicalin reduces the oxidative stress-induced expression of MAPK/ERK pathway-related proteins in HUVECs

The oxidative stress model of HUVECs presented significantly increased protein expression levels of MEK, p-MAPK1/2, and p-MEK (P < 0.05). Treatment with baicalin (100, 200, and 400 M) dose-dependently reduced the expression and phosphorylation levels of the MAPK and ERK proteins: 100 M baicalin significantly decreased p-MEK expression, restoring it to levels comparable to those in the control group. 200 M baicalin markedly reduced MAPK1/2, MEK, and p-MEK expression. 400 M baicalin significantly suppressed MAPK1/2, MEK, p-MAPK1/2, and p-MEK expression.

Compared with the model group, the 0.63 M TNF- antagonist group presented significantly lower protein expression levels of MAPK1/2, MEK, p-MAPK1/2, and p-MEK (P < 0.05). Pretreatment with 0.63 M TNF- antagonist for 30 minutes, followed by 200 M baicalin, resulted in protein expression levels similar to those of the TNF- antagonist group, with no additional significant changes (Fig. 8A–E). However, this combined treatment resulted in stronger inhibition than baicalin monotherapy. The absence of additive effects between the TNF- antagonist and baicalin implies that (i) baicalin targets molecules downstream of or dependent on TNF- (e.g., the RAF-MEK-ERK cascade) and that (ii) 0.63 M TNF- antagonist achieves maximal pathway suppression, leaving no room for the additional contribution of baicalin.

Baicalin dose-dependently regulates MPO secretion in an oxidative stress model

The MPO levels in the supernatants of the HUVEC cultures significantly varied across the treatment groups (Fig. 9A). Compared with the control, exposure to H2O2-induced oxidative stress resulted in a marked increase in the MPO concentration. 100 M baicalin further increased MPO levels (P < 0.01 vs Model group). 200 M baicalin partially mitigated the increase in MPO levels (P < 0.05 vs Model group) but remained significantly greater than that in the control group. 400 M baicalin fully restored the MPO levels to those observed in the control group (P > 0.05). The mechanistic study revealed that the levels of MPO in cells treated with TNF- antagonists and in cells treated with a combination of TNF- antagonists and baicalin were both significantly reduced. The difference between these two groups did not reach statistical significance (P > 0.05). These results indicate that TNF- blockade plays a dominant role in MPO regulation, while baicalin exerts a regulatory effect on MPO only in the presence of functional TNF--mediated signal transduction.

To determine whether low-dose baicalin activates other pro-oxidative pathways, such as the nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate oxidase (NOX) pathway, we measured NADPH levels in HUVECs across all experimental groups (Fig. 9B). H2O2 treatment led to a significant decrease in NADPH levels (P < 0.01), indicating substantial oxidative stress-induced NADPH depletion. This reduction was partially reversed by treatment with 100 M baicalin (P < 0.05), and further increases in baicalin concentration (up to 400 M) were associated with dose-dependent elevation of NADPH levels, suggesting that baicalin contributes to the maintenance of redox homeostasis.

To investigate the association between MPO and the MAPK/ERK signaling pathway, a Pearson correlation analysis was performed to assess the relationship between p-MAPK1/2 and MPO, as well as between p-MEK and MPO (Fig. 10A,B). The results revealed that the association between p-MAPK1/2 and MPO was the most significant, suggesting that ERK phosphorylation may serve as a key regulatory mechanism in modulating MPO levels. While the correlation between p-MEK and MPO was slightly weaker, it still supports the involvement of the MAPK signaling pathway in this process.

Discussion

PE is a complex, idiopathic disorder of pregnancy and represents one of the leading causes of maternal and perinatal morbidity and mortality globally27. Data from 2022 revealed that the incidence of eclampsia is (1.6–10) per 10,000 in developed countries and (50–151) per 10,000 in developing countries, largely attributed to limited resources for prenatal care28. The increasing incidence of PE in recent years can be attributed to multiple factors, including declining birth rates, an increase in pregnancies among women of advanced maternal age ( 35 years), and comorbidities such as diabetes, obesity, recurrent pregnancy loss, and the use of assisted reproductive technology (ART)29–31. Advances in polygenic risk scoring and cell-free RNA analysis32,33 have improved the clinical prediction of PE, leading to modest improvements in pregnancy outcomes34.However, affected women remain vulnerable to severe complications,including placental abruption, eclampsia, intracerebral hemorrhage, and heart failure35. Moreover, preterm infants born following PE-associated deliveries face not only increased mortality but also long-term sequelae such as neurodevelopmental disorders and metabolic syndrome36. Pathophysiological changes in PE (Fig. 12) begin with the initiation of placental formation at approximately 12 weeks of gestation and persist throughout mid-to-late pregnancy, affecting multiple organ systems across the body, as widely documented in current studies1–3,37. During early pregnancy, abnormal remodeling of spiral arteries in the placental bed leads to placental ischemia, while widespread vascular endothelial cell injury in later stages represents the primary pathological hallmark of PE3. Currently, no definitive pharmacological therapy exists to prevent or halt the progression of PE. Therefore, developing targeted interventions for its pathogenesis is critical to reducing maternal and perinatal mortality and improving long-term public health outcomes.

Pathophysiological changes in PE.

Baicalin, a bioactive flavonoid compound predominantly isolated from the dried roots of “Scutellaria baicalensis” Georgi (Lamiaceae family) with the molecular formula C21H18O11, has been extensively utilized as a medicinal plant in China for thousands of years8,38,39. Baicalin exhibits a broad spectrum of biological activities, including regulation of metabolism regulation9, antiviral effects10, anti-inflammatory properties11,15, neuroprotection12, apoptosis regulation13, antidepressant effects14, and antitumour activity16. In traditional Chinese medicine (TCM), Scutellaria baicalensis is widely employed for its heat-clearing and detoxifying properties. Modern pharmacological studies indicate that baicalin exerts its anti-inflammatory effects by modulating critical pathways, such as the NF-B40,41 and MAPK9,15 pathways. Additionally, its potent free radical-scavenging capacity helps alleviate oxidative stress-related damage38. Owing to its excellent safety profile, it has been extensively incorporated into pharmaceutical formulations42, nutraceuticals43, and cosmeceuticals44, positioning it as a central compound in the advancement and modernization of TCM.

MPO is primarily derived from maternal or fetal neutrophils and functions as a key biomarker of oxidative stress. With a molecular weight of approximately 150 kDa and a positively charged structure, MPO has limited capacity to cross the placental barrier45. MPO is a crucial component of neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs), and NET formation is closely associated with vascular endothelial cell injury46. Given the pathological role of endothelial dysfunction in PE2, research on NETs and MPO has garnered significant attention in recent years47–49. Liu50 analysed 933 postpartum placental tissues and reported that MPO expression in the placental tissues of PE patients was markedly higher than that in normal pregnancies. Alan51 collected 173 serum and placental samples at delivery and revealed that serum MPO levels were significantly elevated in PE patients with preterm birth compared with controls, whereas no significant difference in placental MPO expression was detected. Fragoso52 examined umbilical cord samples from 100 PE patients and 50 normal pregnant individuals and reported that MPO expression in umbilical cord tissues did not increase significantly in PE patients. Our study, which included 60 pregnant women, revealed that the expression level of MPO in the peripheral blood of patients with PE was significantly elevated.

This study systematically investigated the protective effects of baicalin against H2O2-induced oxidative stress in HUVECs through comprehensive transcriptomic, molecular, and cellular analyses. It is noteworthy that cell viability in the 200 M H2O2 treatment group exhibited a transient increase (Fig. 3A), which may be attributed to the activation of antioxidant defense mechanisms by low-dose H2O2 exposure, consistent with a hormetic response. Similar observations have been documented in previous studies53,54, further supporting the biphasic nature of oxidative stress regulation. In contrast, higher concentrations of H2O2 (400 M) induced significant cytotoxicity, likely due to overwhelming the cellular repair and detoxification capacity. Our findings demonstrate that baicalin exerts dose-dependent cytoprotective effects by modulating the TNF--mediated MAPK/ERK1/2/MPO cascade and mitigating oxidative stress-associated cellular injury.

Baicalin solutions at concentrations below 400 M had no significant effect on the viability of HUVECs and did not induce substantial cell death. These findings are consistent with those reported by Yi-Ping You13. The biphasic response observed in MPO regulation represents a particularly intriguing finding. Specifically, low-dose baicalin (100 M) unexpectedly exacerbated MPO release, whereas higher concentrations (200–400 M) effectively normalized MPO levels, which contradicts the well-established antioxidant properties of baicalin38. The discrepancy suggests that the cellular context and the degree of oxidative stress may be key determinants. Factors such as cell type, the nature of the stimulus, the concentration range of baicalin, and the duration of treatment could all modulate the oxidative stress response. This phenomenon may reflect the concentration-dependent dual roles of baicalin in oxidative stress responses, wherein lower doses potentially prime cellular defense mechanisms before exerting antioxidant effects at higher concentrations. Different concentrations of baicalin can elicit distinct biological effects on cellular processes, including apoptosis, mitochondrial dysfunction, and inflammatory responses13. Specifically, low-dose exposure (e.g., 100 M baicalin) may mildly activate the antioxidant defense system, leading to a transient pro-oxidative state that enhances cellular adaptability. Studies55 have demonstrated that low-dose baicalin can transiently upregulate the expression of antioxidant enzymes such as superoxide dismutase (SOD) and MPO, which may represent a compensatory protective mechanism. In this study, 100 M baicalin may promote endothelial cell secretion of MPO through activation of the Nrf2 signaling pathway, thereby alleviating initial oxidative damage38. These underlying mechanisms warrant further investigation in future studies. Additionally, a concentration of 100 M baicalin may only partially inhibit TNF- , allowing for residual activation of ERK1/2, which leads to predominant activation of the MAPK/ERK1/2 signaling pathway and subsequent promotion of MPO release. In contrast, concentrations of baicalin at or above 200 M completely block the TNF- signaling cascade and suppress MPO production. Moreover, the transient increase in MPO levels may facilitate the removal of damaged proteins or pathogens via hypochlorous acid (HOCl) production, thereby promoting a microenvironment conducive to vascular regeneration56. The complete restoration of MPO levels at 400 M suggests that this concentration achieves optimal antioxidant efficacy.The paradoxical dose-dependent effects of baicalin on NADPH levels (an increase at 100 M and a decrease at higher concentrations) are consistent with previous findings57,58. A concentration of 100 M baicalin may trigger the early oxidative burst in neutrophils/macrophages by transiently activating NOX, thereby promoting MPO secretion. In contrast, high-dose baicalin counteracts this effect through direct inhibition of NOX assembly or activation of the Nrf2 pathway38,59.

The transcriptomic analysis of HUVECs under oxidative stress revealed significant alterations in gene expression profiles, with 317 differentially expressed genes (DEGs; 155 upregulated and 162 downregulated) involving key pathways such as the IL-17, oxytocin, and MAPK signalling pathways. Notably, the MAPK pathway (hsa04010) exhibited the dysregulation of five genes (three downregulated and two upregulated), suggesting its pivotal role in oxidative stress-induced endothelial dysfunction. This finding aligns with our study’ s focus on the TNF--mediated MAPK/ERK1/2/MPO axis, as MAPK activation is known to amplify inflammatory cascades and oxidative damage. Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment further highlighted disruptions in membrane integrity (GO:0016020), cytoskeletal dynamics (GO:0005856), and protein binding (GO:0005515), collectively implying compromised endothelial barrier function and signal transduction. These findings support the hypothesis that oxidative stress perturbs vascular homeostasis by targeting structural and signalling networks.

Our RT-qPCR and Western blot analyses confirmed that baicalin dose-dependently suppressed both the mRNA expression and protein phosphorylation of key MAPK/ERK components. Notably, 400 M baicalin completely normalized p-ERK levels, mirroring the pattern observed in MPO regulation. Endogenous TNF- signalling is activated in cells in the basal state. This persistent low-level activation partially counteracts the inhibitory effect of baicalin alone. Compared with the 200 M baicalin group, the TNF- antagonist + 200 M baicalin combined group exhibited a stronger inhibitory effect on MEK/ERK mRNA, with no significant difference between the combined group and the antagonist group. This may suggest that when endogenous TNF- is blocked with an antagonist, the inhibitory effect of baicalin is fully released or that the target site of baicalin is located downstream of the TNF- signal. These parallel findings strongly suggest that the antioxidant effects of baicalin are mediated by the MAPK/ERK pathway.

Apoptosis studies revealed that the protective effects of baicalin extend to enhancing cellular survival, with treatments ranging from 200 to 400 M significantly reducing H2O2-induced apoptosis. The concentration threshold for antiapoptotic effects (200 M) being lower than that required for complete MPO normalization (400 M) may indicate distinct sensitivities of different cellular processes to baicalin. This observation aligns with previous reports showing differential dose requirements for various pharmacological effects of flavonoids60. The cytotoxic effects of baicalin and its antagonists were excluded by measuring the early apoptosis rate.

Numerous studies based on baicalin have progressed to the clinical application stage, with the aim of enhancing its bioavailability or overcoming the blood-brain barrier. In a mouse model of osteoarthritis, it was demonstrated that baicalin (Bai) and 2-hydroxyethyl acrylate (HEA) could form a polymerizable monomer, HEA-Bai, which was subsequently polymerized to yield a baicalin-based polymer (Pm). This polymer was then incorporated into a Schiff base hydrogel, resulting in a multifunctional material with high mechanical strength and self-healing properties42. Ibrahim43 developed lipid nanocapsules (LNCs) loaded with baicalin and demonstrated their efficient penetration across the blood-brain barrier in a mouse model of glioma. Both intravenous injection and nasal administration were shown to enhance brain-targeting capability, thereby addressing the challenge posed by baicalin’s poor water solubility. These results suggest that optimizing the formulation may overcome the limitations of baicalin in placental targeting while improving its biocompatibility.

The study has several limitations. First, the long-term biological effects of low-dose baicalin-induced MPO elevation remain to be elucidated. Further investigations are warranted to evaluate potential systemic toxicity. The clinical relevance of the MPO levels observed in this study warrants further validation using endothelial cells derived from PE patients or relevant animal models. Second, while we focused on the MAPK/ERK pathway, other signalling cascades may contribute to the effects of baicalin. Third, the concentration of the TNF- antagonist was determined on the basis of the EC50. However, the “ceiling effect” of the TNF- antagonist cannot be ruled out; that is, the antagonist may have reduced the MAPK/ERK signal to the basal minimum level, and baicalin cannot further exert its effect in the completely inhibited system. Our study was conducted in vitro, and therefore, future animal studies are necessary to validate these findings in vivo. Future studies should further investigate the clinical relevance of combining low-dose antagonists with baicalin, particularly in drug-resistant models. Fourth, the antioxidant effects observed in HUVECs may not fully recapitulate human vascular pathophysiology. For example, efficacy of baicalin could be influenced by gut microbiota-derived metabolites in vivo61. Furthermore, although the H2O2-induced HUVECs model provides a tractable system for oxidative stress screening, it does not fully reflect the multifactorial pathophysiology of PE, which necessitates high-throughput dose-response testing as a prerequisite for in vivo studies.

Conclusions

This study demonstrated that baicalin can effectively protect HUVECs from oxidative stress damage induced by H2O2 through dose-dependent regulation of the TNF-/MAPK/ERK1/2 pathway. The main findings are summarized as follows:

Therapeutic window: Baicalin exhibits the most pronounced antioxidant effect on the oxidative stress model of HUVECs within the concentration range of 200–400 micromoles. This is evidenced by the normalization of MPO secretion, inhibition of MAPK/ERK activation, and restoration of apoptosis levels to baseline.

These results indicate that baicalin can mitigate the oxidative stress response in vascular endothelial cells. The identified concentration range (200–400 M) provides critical guidance for subsequent animal studies. Future research will aim to validate these findings in vivo and explore potential synergistic effects with other therapeutic strategies.
