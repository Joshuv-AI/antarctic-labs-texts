Next generation sequencing identifies novel gene alterations with potential prognostic relevance in pediatric cancer: a single-center study
Authors: Yuhan Ma, Mingwei Su, Xiaoshan Liu, Wenxia Wang, Yu Wu, Zhixuan Wang, Xiaomin Peng, Xilin Xiong, Jiaying Lei, Yang Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundNext generation sequencing (NGS) has driven the development of precision oncology. While adult cancer genomics has been extensively studied, pediatric cancer, particularly among Asian populations, has received less attention.MethodsWe screened gene alteration profiles of 99 pediatric cancer patients using the targeted NGS panel and compared the results with those from Western cohorts. The relationship between genes and clinical characteristics was also analyzed.ResultsThe most frequently mutated gene was KMT2D (18.2%), followed by TP53 (11.1%) and DICER1 (9.1%). The frequency of KMT2D mutations in our cohort is significantly higher than in Western cohorts, whereas TP53 mutations showed no significant difference. Pathogenic or likely pathogenic (P/LP) germline mutations were identified in only 3.5% of the patients. Notably, 69%-75% of patients had at least one genomic alteration with potential clinical significance. In neuroblastoma (NB), the most commonly altered genes were MYCN (7/48), DICER1 (6/48), ARID1B, EGFR, KMT2D, and TCF3 (5/48). In a subset of intermediate-/high-risk NB patients (n = 33), MYCN amplification was associated with disease progression during induction chemotherapy, and gene alterations in the MAPK pathway were associated with poor survival. We also identified novel alterations in specific tumor types, including SFPQ-TACC1 fusion in NB.ConclusionsOur findings enhance understanding of the genomic landscape of Chinese pediatric cancer populations. Further research is required to validate these findings and fully explore the potential of genomic data to improve outcomes for pediatric cancer patients.

Pediatric cancer genomics remains underexplored, particularly in Asian populations. This study retrospectively analyzed genomic alterations in Chinese pediatric cancer patients, compared the results with those of Western cohorts, and explored the relationship between mutations and neuroblastoma prognosis. These findings may inform the development of precision medicine strategies, enhancing treatment decisions, prognostic predictions, and personalized therapies for pediatric cancer patients.

Introduction

Cancer is one of the leading causes of death in children.1 Pediatric cancer mainly includes leukemia, central nervous system tumors, lymphoma, neuroblastoma, sarcoma, etc.2 Approximately 13,000 children die from pediatric cancer annually in the USA.3 A large majority of survivors endure long-term health complications related to chemotherapy and radiotherapy, with shorter life expectancies.4,5 So there is an urgent need for a novel treatment with improved efficacy and fewer side effects.

Next generation sequencing (NGS), developed in the early 2000s, enables simultaneous sequencing of large numbers of DNA/RNA molecules, reducing time and costs and paving the way for large-scale genomic research.6,7 Cancer genomics has reshaped our understanding of cancer by highlighting the roles of specific genomic alterations in tumorigenesis and cancer progression, leading to the discovery of novel therapeutic targets and biomarkers for tumor classification, therapy response, and prognosis.8 Despite the active research in adult cancer genomics, pediatric cancer genomics remains underexplored. Although much is known about the differences in histopathology, treatment response, and prognosis between pediatric and adult cancer, it was not until 2018 that two landmark pediatric pan-cancer genomic studies revealed that the genomic landscapes of pediatric and adult cancers show little overlap.9,10 Pediatric cancer presents lower tumor mutation burdens, more germline mutations and unique driver gene alterations.9–11 Only 30% of driver gene mutations identified in pediatric cancer had been previously reported in adult cancers.9 In 2020, the International Agency for Research on Cancer recognized pediatric cancer as distinct from adult cancer in the fifth edition of the WHO Classification of Tumors, based on molecular features, morphology, and immunohistochemistry.12 The genomics of pediatric cancer still needs further investigation. Further, racial disparities in genomic research shouldn’t be ignored. The current large-scale pediatric precision oncology projects include INFORM (Germany), MAPPYACTS (France), ITHER (The Netherlands), ZERO (Australia), the Pediatric Cancer Genome Project (USA), and PROFYLE (Canada).13,14 Despite extensive research in Western populations, studies on pediatric cancer genomics in Asian children, particularly Chinese, are sparse.

This study retrospectively analyzed genomic alterations in 99 Chinese pediatric cancer patients using targeted NGS panel sequencing and compared these findings with Western cohorts. Additionally, we explored the relationship between the genomic background of NB patients and poor prognosis. Our findings aim to enhance the understanding of the genomic landscape of pediatric cancer.

Methods

Patients and materials

We retrospectively collected NGS reports and clinical information from pediatric cancer patients treated in the pediatric oncology (solid tumor) unit of the Children’s Medical Center of Sun Yat-Sen Memorial Hospital from February 2019 to August 2022. Patients were included if they had a confirmed pathological diagnosis, supported by laboratory and imaging findings, and an available NGS report. Patients with NGS samples containing <10% tumor content were excluded due to low-confidence sequencing results, two patients were excluded accordingly. One patient, initially diagnosed with an unknown breast mass and later confirmed as leukemia, was included because NGS testing and initial management were performed in our pediatric oncology unit. NB patients were staged according to the International Staging System and the International Neuroblastoma Risk Group staging system.15,16 The study was conducted according to the Declaration of Helsinki. The study was approved by the institutional review board of Sun Yat-Sen Memorial Hospital, exempting informed consent due to retrospective design.

NGS procedure

Tumor samples, including formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tissues, blood, or bone marrow, were analyzed by MyGene Diagnostics Co., Ltd. (Guangzhou, China) using the Oncomine™ Childhood Cancer Research Assay (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) on the Ion Torrent S5 platform. This panel could simultaneously detect single nucleotide variants (SNV), insertions/deletions (Indel), copy number variations (CNV), and gene fusions across 203 genes relevant to pediatric cancers (Table S1, available as supplementary data at The Oncologist online). Variant nomenclature followed the Human Genome Variation Society (HGVS) guidelines.17 In this study, complex insertions and deletions involving both the loss and gain of nucleotides at the same genomic position were described as delins (deletion–insertion) variants according to HGVS recommendations. The detailed information can be found in the Supplementary Material_Methods, available as supplementary data at The Oncologist online.

Since May 2021, Sanger sequencing has been performed on blood samples from patients with “suspicious” germline mutations reported by NGS. Variants were considered “suspicious” if their variant allele fraction (VAF) was close to or exceeded 50%, or if they were recorded in the ClinVar database as germline variants. Somatic variants were classified into four tiers according to their clinical relevance for cancer diagnosis, prognosis, and therapy following AMP/CAP/ASCO guidelines,18 while germline variants were interpreted according to ACMG guidelines.19 After bioinformatics analysis, the final NGS result was sent to the doctor and patients’ families as the written report.

Publicly available data

Genomic alteration data from Western cohorts were downloaded from public databases, including the Pediatric Pan-Cancer database (DKFZ cohort) from cBioPortal (http://www.cbioportal.org) and TARGET-neuroblastoma from UCSC Xena Public Hub (https://xenabrowser.net/). Only the genes common to both the Chinese and Western cohorts were analyzed. Tumor mutation burden (TMB) data from the DKFZ cohort are available in the original paper.9 In the TMB analysis, cases with both primary and relapsed samples were represented by the relapsed sample to avoid duplicate inclusion.

Pathway enrichment analysis

Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analysis was performed using KOBAS 3.0(20) Pathways were ranked by adjusted P value (padj < 0.05 as the significance threshold), and the top ten enriched signaling pathways were presented as representative results.

Downsampling analysis

All downsampling analyses were performed in R (v4.3.1). Tumor types were categorized into five groups: Embryonal/Pediatric Tumors, CNS Tumors, Bone and Soft Tissue Tumors, Hematologic and Lymphoid Tumors, and Other Tumors. Mutation matrices and sample grouping data were organized accordingly. To balance sample sizes, 1:1 random downsampling was conducted within each disease group.

For mutation frequencies, a 2 × 2 contingency table was generated for each gene in every iteration, and Fisher’s exact test was applied to compare mutation frequencies between cohorts. The difference in mutation frequency was expressed as ΔProportion = Freq_ourcohort − Freq_DKFZ. For TMB, the Mann-Whitney U test was used to compare cohorts, with differences expressed as ΔTMB = TMB_KMT2Daltered − TMB_KMT2Dwildtype. To account for sample imbalance, 10 iterations for mutation frequency analysis and 20 iterations for TMB analysis were performed. The harmonic mean p-value method21 was used to summarize all p-values across the iterations using the R package 'harmonicmeanp’, and the median effect size across iterations was reported as a robust estimate.

Statistics

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 26.0 and R version 4.3.1. Qualitative data comparisons were made using the Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, and quantitative variables were compared using the Mann-Whitney U test. The 95% confidence intervals for prevalence estimates were calculated with the Clopper–Pearson method. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05, with adjusted p-values (padj) using the Benjamini-Hochberg method when necessary.

Genomic alterations

All 101 samples from 99 pediatric patients, including 99 FFPE tissue tumor samples, one blood sample, and one bone marrow sample, were analyzed using the targeted NGS panel. The median tumor content was 59.7%.

Ninety-one patients (91.9%) had at least one gene alteration. The median number of genomic alterations per patient was 2 (range 0–11). Eight patients, including six with neuroblastoma (NB), one with nephroblastoma, and one with a yolk sac tumor, had no detectable genomic alterations. We detected 270 genomic alterations, including 173 SNVs, four insertions, six deletions, nine delins, two duplications, 59 CNVs, and 17 gene fusions. The genomic alteration landscape is illustrated in Figure 1A. Previous studies of western pediatric patients have shown that most cancer types predominantly harbored C > T transitions (≥ 30% of SNVs in two-thirds of cancer types).9 In our cohort, the SNV was also dominated by the “C > T” form (49.1%) (Figure 1B).

The most prevalent altered gene was KMT2D (18.2%), followed by TP53 (11.1%) and DICER1 (9.1%). Variant allele frequency (VAF) may serve as a biomarker for monitoring treatment response, detecting disease relapse, and stratifying risk in several cancers.22,23 VAF of the top three most altered genes is shown in Figure 1C. 49 gene amplifications and 10 gene deletions were identified in this cohort (Figure 1D). The most common focal amplification region was at 2p24.2 (8.1%), which contains MYCN. The most frequent focal deletion region was at 11q14.2 (2.0%), which contains EED. Additionally, details of the fusions are presented in Table S2, available as supplementary data at The Oncologist online. After enrichment analysis of KEGG pathways for altered genes, we summarized the percentage of patients with at least one gene alteration involving PI3K-Akt (43.4%), MAPK (38.4%), Cellular senescence (31.3%), etc (Figure 1E).

We investigated the clinical significance of genomic variants. Overall, 69%-75% of patients had at least one type of variation with clinical impact (Tier I or Tier II somatic mutation, or germline variants) (Figure 2). A total of 30 germline mutations were identified in 38.6% (22/57) of pediatric patients. However, only 3.5% (2/57) cases were found to harbor pathogenic or likely pathogenic germline variants, which was much lower than previously reported in previous cohorts (8.5%-18%).24,25 All relevant information is listed in Table S3, available as supplementary data at The Oncologist online.

For KMT2D variants, 16 missense mutations, 3 nonframeshift deletions, and 2 CNVs were identified in 18 patients. Except for CNV, the KMT2D mutant sites of 15.2% (16/99) of patients are shown in Figure 3A. The most common KMT2D mutation form was KMT2D Q3910_Q3911del (n = 3), identified in a case of hepatoblastoma, dysgerminoma and Ewing sarcoma. KMT2D amplifications were detected in a colon cancer patient and an RMS patient. All types of cancer are depicted in Figure 3B. Furthermore, previous studies have reported that the frequency of KMT2D mutations was associated with sex, being significantly higher in female patients.26 We observed the same trend in our data (24.4% prevalence in females [10/41], 13.8% prevalence in males [8/58]), but the result was not statistically significant (p = 0.18). There was no significant co-occurrence or mutual exclusivity observed between KMT2D mutations and genes with mutation frequencies exceeding 5% (TP53, DICER1, ARID1B, MYOD1, ARID1A, CHD7, and TCF3). In adult cancer, KMT2D mutation is associated with increased TMB.27,28 To account for potential confounding by cancer types, downsampling was performed to match the KMT2D altered (n = 17) and KMT2D wildtype (n = 897) subgroups. In our analysis, KMT2D-altered samples showed slightly higher TMB in the DKFZ cohort(medianΔTMB = 0.064, harmonic mean p-value (HMP) =0.02) (Figure 3C).

Differences in genomic alterations between Chinese and Western pediatric cancer patients

To elucidate the genomic differences between pediatric patients in China and Western countries, we analyzed SNV/Indel variants in 68 genes across both cohorts. The disease distribution differed substantially between our and the DKFZ cohort (Table S4, available as supplementary data at The Oncologist online). To address this imbalance, downsampling was performed within each disease group. Across iterations, 91% (62/68) of genes showed directional consistency ≥ 0.9, indicating stable estimates. Figure 4A highlights four consistently significant genes: KMT2D (median ΔFreq = 0.162, HMP = 2.16E-05), DICER1 (median ΔFreq = 0.091, HMP = 0.01), ARID1B (medianΔFreq = 0.081, HMP = 0.01), and MYOD1 (median ΔFreq = 0.061, HMP = 0.03) (Table S5, available as supplementary data at The Oncologist online). All four genes maintained directional consistency across iterations, supporting the robustness of the observed mutation frequency differences between cohorts. We also compared mutations in 47 genes among NB patients across our cohort (n = 48), the DKFZ cohort (n = 59), and the TARGET-NB cohort (n = 238). Mutations in ARID1B, DICER1, KMT2D, EGFR, and TCF3 were modestly more frequent in our cohort than in the two Western cohorts (p < 0.05). No significant difference was observed in ALK mutation frequency (Figure 4B and 4C). Nevertheless, these findings should be interpreted cautiously, as potential biases may arise from differences in tumor composition, sample size, or sequencing platforms.

Neuroblastoma

We included 48 NB patients. Forty-two of them had 120 genomic alterations across 64 genes, whereas 6 had no detectable genomic alterations. The median number of alterations per patient was 2 (range 0–11). The landscape of NB is presented in Figure 5A. SNV was also mostly C > T substitutions (44.2%). The most frequently mutated gene was DICER1 (12.5%, including p. R1607W, p. S1076N, p. A74L, p. P603T, p. E1425del and p. K1396E), followed by ARID1B (10.4%, including p. M935L, p. D1503N, p. D1758N, p. M973T and p. Q158dup) and KMT2D (10.4%, including p. A1775V, p. P4048L, p. F1952L, p. T199P and p. P2349L). We detected 28 gene amplification regions, with MYCN (n = 7) being the most common, followed by PPM1D (n = 3) and ALK (n = 2). Seven gene deletion regions were identified, including EED (n = 2) and single occurrences in CBL, SMARCA4, TCF3, CDKN2A, and CDKN2B. We identified a novel fusion, SFPQ-TACC1, in a 3-year-old NB patient, previously unreported in NB.

(A) Landscape of gene alteration in neuroblastoma. (B) Venn diagram: altered genes in different prognosis group.

Next, we analyzed the correlations between genomic mutations and both induction chemotherapy efficacy and cancer recurrence. Chemotherapy response, evaluated by RECIST 1.129 approximately one month post-induction, categorized patients into an effective group (CR, PR) or an ineffective group (SD, PD).19 After excluding those with insufficient data or in the low-risk group, 33 high-/intermediate-risk NB patients were included in this analysis. MYCN amplification was associated with ineffective induction chemotherapy (ineffective vs effective induction chemotherapy: 3/4 vs 3/29, p = 0.014) (Table S6, available as supplementary data at The Oncologist online). However, other genes like ARID1B, KMT2D, and DICER1 did not reach statistical significance.

Previous studies suggested that poor response to induction chemotherapy predicts worse NB prognosis.30 Consequently, children with SD/PD post-chemotherapy or tumor recurrence were categorized as the poor prognosis group (n = 13), with others forming the good prognosis group (n = 20). We compared the genomic variant counts between the two groups and found that patients with poor prognosis demonstrated a higher number of variants (U = 69.5, p < 0.05). Additionally, we analyzed gene variants in both groups. Notably, we observed a possible predisposition of 27 non-overlapping genes in the poor prognosis group, which may be associated with unfavorable outcomes in NB patients (Figure 5B). The type and site of mutations, which are crucial for clinical interpretation, should also be considered. For example, children with poor prognosis showed ALK amplifications, whereas those with good prognosis exhibited ALK missense mutations. Conversely, GATA3 and NCOR2 were missense mutations in the poor-prognosis group but amplifications in the good-prognosis group. Details were provided in Table S7, available as supplementary data at The Oncologist online.

We conducted KEGG pathway enrichment analysis on mutations across both patient groups and selected representative signaling pathways. We observed that genes associated with the MAPK and Hedgehog (HH) signaling pathways were enriched in the poor prognosis group. Consequently, we focused on the relationship between the MAPK and HH pathways and poor prognosis. NB patients with ≥ 1 gene mutation in the specific signaling pathway were defined as the signaling pathway (+) group, while others were categorized in the signaling pathway (−) group. Alterations in the MAPK signaling pathway showed a statistically significant association with poor prognosis (p = 0.005) (Table S8, available as supplementary data at The Oncologist online).

Rhabdomyosarcoma

The histological subtypes of RMS patients included 4 embryonal, 3 alveolar, 1 sclerosing, and 2 unclassified patients due to insufficient clinical information. 29 gene alterations of 17 genes were detected in 10 patients. KMT2D (4 missense mutations) was the most frequently mutated gene, found in all histologic subtypes. KMT2D amplification was also identified in the patient with alveolar RMS. In our cohort, the MYOD1 p. L122R was found in three patients across sclerosing, unclassified, and embryonal subtypes, as previously reported.31 SMARCB1 R377H was detected in two patients, including an embryonal and a sclerosing. PAX7-FOXO1 was identified in 3 patients, and 2 belonged to the alveolar subtype, consistent with previous guidelines.32

Lymphoma

The histologic subtypes of the 6 lymphoma patients were 3 Burkitt lymphoma, 1 mixed cellularity Hodgkin lymphoma, 1 diffuse large B-cell lymphoma and 1 B-cell non-Hodgkin lymphoma. The most frequently altered gene was ID3 (n = 4), followed by CCDN3 (n = 3), TP53, MYC, and ARID1A (n = 2). A Burkitt lymphoma patient N62 was identified with CCND3 p. I290R and ID3 p. L64F (other mutations included CEBPA p. His195_Pro196dup, CEBPA p. Pro189_Ser190delinsHisPro, CHD7 p. S2199N). CCND3-ID3 double-hit mutations are linked to poor prognosis, 4/9 patients with this mutation died within 8 months, while none of the 9 patients without it did.33 Based on NGS and treatment response, his chemotherapy was adjusted from moderate-risk to high-risk. He received hematopoietic stem cell transplantation 5 months after diagnosis and had a follow-up 17 months after diagnosis.

Other tumors

DICER1 syndrome is caused by pathogenic DICER1 germline variants.34 Pleuropulmonary blastoma is a classical phenotype along with other diseases like cystic nephroma, embryonal RMS, and thyroid cancer.34 Its pathogenesis followed Knudson’s two-hit hypothesis: Individuals with a pathogenic germline DICER1 variant acquired somatic DICER1 missense mutation, which was usually identified in “hot spot” (E1705, D1709, G1809, D1810, E1813) of the DICER1 RNaseIIIb domain.35 In our cohort, a 3-year-old patient with type II pleuropulmonary blastoma had a germline DICER1 W1528* mutation, DAXX p. R256C mutation, and somatic DICER1 D1709N and PIK3CA p. H1047R mutations. Cascade screening revealed her father, also carrying the DICER1 p. W1528* variant, exhibited no related phenotype at age 39. The patient achieved CR after chemotherapy. Long-term clinical surveillance, as SIOPE HGWG and CanGene-CanVar guidelines, may be beneficial for such patients.36

Hasselblatt M et al. suggested that in children, SMARCB1-deficient malignant brain tumors featuring complex copy number alterations and somatic TP53 mutations might represent the initial clinical manifestation of Li-Fraumeni syndrome, emphasizing the need for prompt TP53 germline status assessment.37 In our cohort, a patient with an extracranial malignant rhabdoid tumor, a classical SMARCB1-deficient tumor, harbored deletions of SMARCB1, MYOD1, SUFU, EGFR amplification, and a TP53 p. Y220C mutation, which was eventually confirmed as a germline mutation. The primary tumor site of this patient was the neck. So this may extend the relevance of the aforementioned findings from SMARCB1-deficient brain tumors to extracranial tumors.

Patient N75, aged 3, was initially diagnosed with recurrent nephroblastoma, with MET and MYC amplification. Six months later, the patient resumed crizotinib (10 mg/kg/d) orally, but radiological examinations after 40 days suggested an increase in tumor size.

Discussion

While adult cancer genomic studies have rapidly expanded, pediatric cancer genomic research has relatively lagged behind, especially in Asian. To contribute to this field, our study collected and analyzed data from 99 Chinese pediatric cancer patients.

KMT2D, located on chromosome 12q13, encodes histone lysine methyltransferase 2D.38 In adults, KMT2D is among the top 10 most commonly mutated genes, with mutation rates of 9.2% in American and 9.7% in Chinese cancer cohorts.39 Our study showed that KMT2D was the most frequently mutated gene in Chinese pediatric cancer patients, occurring more frequently than in Western cohorts.

KMT2D has been associated with acute lymphoblastic leukemia, medulloblastoma, lymphoma, and Kabuki syndrome in pediatric patients, but reports in other pediatric cancers are rare. In this study, KMT2D variants were identified in 18 patients across various pediatric cancers. KMT2D germline variants were reported in Kabuki Syndrome patients with nephroblastoma, giant cell fibroblastoma, and spinal ependymoma.40–42 Our study reported three germline KMT2D mutations in patients with NB (p.T199P), RMS (p.Q3908L), and Langerhans cell histiocytosis (p.T54S), but no symptoms of Kabuki Syndrome were observed. KMT2D mutation causes genome instability.43 In lung cancer, KMT2D loss upregulated glycolysis, a protumorigenic program.44 The relationship between KMT2D mutation and cancer is complex,44 with some studies suggesting poor prognosis in cancers like T-cell lymphoblastic lymphoma, non-small-cell lung cancer,45,46 while others reported better prognosis in small-cell lung carcinoma, esophageal squamous cell carcinoma.26,47,48 In our study, no significant correlation was found between KMT2D mutations and treatment response post-induction chemotherapy in NB. Further validation of these findings is necessary.

Immune checkpoint blockade(ICB) have revolutionized cancer treatment, in which PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors show promise but are effective in only a limited number of patients.49 Trials suggested that these inhibitors are less effective in most common pediatric cancers.50–52 However, these results are not definitive. For example, one study showed that PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors exhibited exciting treatment responses and significantly improved prognosis in pediatric and young adult cancer patients with germline DNA replication repair deficiency.53 So finding a reliable predictive biomarker for treatment response is urgently needed. Multiple clinical studies indicated that patients with high TMB experience better response rates from immunotherapy.54,55 KMT2D mutation was associated to high TMB in many adult cancers and Wang G et al. demonstrated that Kmt2d loss increases DNA damage and mutation burden, enhances IFNγ-stimulated antigen presentation, and promotes immune cell infiltration, suggesting that KMT2D deficiency may sensitize tumors to ICB therapy.27 Our study suggested that KMT2D-altered tumors tended to be associated with higher TMB in pediatric cancers, KMT2D alterations may represent potential predictive biomarkers for ICB response; however, the difference was modest, and further validation is warranted.

Previous studies showed that ARID1B was associated with poor prognosis in NB patients.56,57 A study involving 72 patients revealed that ARID1B mutations and MYCN amplification did not coexist in NB cases, suggesting potential mutual exclusivity.56 In our cohort, MYCN amplification and ARID1B mutation showed a non-significant trend toward co-occurrence (2/5 vs. 5/43; OR = 5.07; 95% CI = 0.67–38.10; p = 0.148), contrary to the reported mutual exclusivity. Additionally, consistent with previous literature,30 we also confirmed the association between MYCN amplification and poor prognosis in this Chinese NB cohort.

Located on chromosome 14q32.13, DICER1 encodes RNA endoribonuclease essential for microRNA maturation.58 Previous studies have linked DICER1 expression to various cancers,59 with some reporting the association between DICER1 and NB.60 In mice, DICER knockout resulted in failure of neural crest cell differentiation.61 One study revealed that low DICER1 expression was significantly associated with stage 4 NB and unfavorable histology, predicting poor prognosis in non-MYCN-amplified NB patients.62 However, only a few studies described DICER1 syndrome with the NB phenotype.63–65 Previous NGS studies did not report a high frequency of DICER1 mutation in NB patients.56,57,66–69 Our study found DICER1 mutations in five Chinese NB patients (10.6%), with an average VAF of 52.7% (ranging from 48% to 60%). Our cohort exhibited a higher frequency of DICER1 mutations compared to Western cohorts, suggesting that ethnic differences may play a role.

We identified SFPQ-TACC1 in an NB patient, a previously unreported gene fusion. SFPQ plays a prominent role in neuronal development in zebrafish models.70 The nucleocytoplasmic distribution of SFPQ is crucial for maintaining neuronal integrity with nuclear depletion and cytoplasmic aggregation linked to some neurodegenerative diseases, such as amyotrophic lateral sclerosis, frontotemporal lobar degeneration, and Alzheimer’s disease.71–74 NB originated from blocked neural crest cell differentiation.75 Previous literature reported the SFPQ-TFE3 fusion in a girl with adrenal NB and perivascular epithelioid cell tumor,76 suggesting that further exploration of SFPQ fusions in NB is warranted.

Although clinical observations suggest that mutations in genes like ARID1B and TP53 are associated to poor prognosis, no significant association was found in this study, likely due to the small sample size and variations in chemotherapy regimens. Our study indicated that mutations in the MAPK pathway were associated with poor prognosis, consistent with previous studies.77,78 Further investigation is needed to confirm whether specific genes in this pathway are major prognostic factors.

This study has limitations, including its retrospective, single-center design and small sample size. Limited follow-up data and the use of commercial NGS panels also restricted the analysis. Despite these limitations, our findings offer preliminary insights into the genomic landscape and prognostic relevance of gene alterations in Chinese pediatric cancers, warranting validation in larger, multi-center studies with longer follow-up.

In conclusion, our study revealed distinct characteristic of the genomic alterations in Chinese pediatric cancer patients. The findings would facilitate the identification of potential biomarkers and provide the reference for targeted clinical trials in the future.
