Inhibitory control training and unhealthy behaviours: a meta-analysis testing short and long- term effects in clinical and at-risk populations
Authors: Elisa Di Rosa, Lucia Ronconi, Beatrice Del Carlo, Giulia Giordano, Eleonora Macchia, Valentina Cardi, Claudio Gentili, Fabio Masina, Claudia Guarrera, Daniela Mapelli, Nicky Edelstyn
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The consumption of tobacco, alcohol, and unhealthy foods is considered the three key factors contributing to preventable morbidity and early mortality worldwide. Inhibitory Control Training (ICT) is suggested as an intervention to reduce behaviours linked to deficient inhibitory control. However, evidence supporting its efficacy on clinical and at-risk populations is lacking. Here, we provide new meta-analytic evidence about the efficacy of ICT in reducing overeating, alcohol consumption, and cigarette smoking in clinical and at-risk populations. Our meta-analysis encompassed fifteen articles, comprising sixteen studies and data from one thousand two hundred and sixteen participants. Nine studies focused on eating behaviour, four on alcohol consumption, and three on cigarette smoking, and they focused on the following clinical and at-risk conditions: overweight, obesity and eating disorders; alcohol use disorder and heavy drinking habits; tobacco use disorders or nicotine dependence. In all the studies, ICT was employed as a stand-alone intervention. We performed meta-analyses to evaluate the effects of ICT on behavioural outcomes at post-training (short-term) and at follow-up (long-term), and meta-regressions to test the role of the following moderators: target behaviour; training paradigm, setting; training duration; and number of sessions. Results indicated no significant differences between the experimental and control conditions, both in the short and long-term. Furthermore, none of the considered moderators had a significant influence on ICT effects. These results suggest caution in the use of ICT as a stand-alone psychological intervention for excessive alcohol and food consumption, and cigarette smoking in clinical and at-risk populations, and suggest further investigation on the effectiveness of ICT when delivered alongside other treatments.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-43063-0.

Introduction

Health-related risk behaviours encompass actions or habits that increase the likelihood of an individual developing health issues or chronic diseases87; National Center for Chronic Disease Prevention and Health Promotion, 2014). Notably, the consumption of tobacco, alcohol, and unhealthy foods are recognized as the three key factors contributing to preventable morbidity and early mortality worldwide48,71; Global Burden of Disease Collaborative Network, 2021).

Tobacco use among adults is associated with over 50 serious health conditions, including various cancers and chronic diseases such as eye disorders, cardiovascular diseases, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, and diabetes. It is estimated that tobacco use causes more than 8 million deaths worldwide each year and leads to the loss of 56.4 million disability-adjusted life years (DALYs). A DALY represents the cumulative loss of one year of full health, which includes both years of life lost due to premature mortality and years of healthy life lost due to disability from prevalent diseases and health conditions in a population. Of these deaths, more than 7 million are directly attributable to smoking, while around 1.3 million results from nonsmokers’ exposure to second-hand smoke. Additionally, smoking during pregnancy increases the risks of ectopic pregnancy, low birth weight, and preterm birth. Babies and children exposed to second-hand smoke are at a higher risk for sudden infant death syndrome, respiratory illnesses, and cognitive impairments3,41,43,67. In 2016, alcohol consumption was associated with approximately 3.0 million deaths worldwide, as well as 131.4 million DALYs (Shields et al., 2020). A substantial majority of these fatalities were linked to noncommunicable diseases, including liver disease, heart disease, and various forms of cancer, as well as mental health and behavioural conditions such as depression and anxiety.

A smaller proportion of deaths is attributed to both intentional and unintentional injuries, which includes falls, drowning, burns, sexual assault, intimate partner violence, and suicide, as well as communicable diseases like tuberculosis and HIV. Notably, individuals aged 20 to 39 years were disproportionately affected, with this age group experiencing the highest percentage (13%) of alcohol-attributable deaths (WHO, 2024).

Unhealthy diets rich in sodium, sugar, trans fats, and saturated fats are linked to an estimated 5 million deaths and 187.7 million DALYs64. The major health implications related to these unhealthy dietary patterns include type 2 diabetes and several types of cancer, including breast, stomach, and colorectal cancers, along with digestive disorders. Each year, approximately 1.89 million deaths are connected to excessive sodium consumption, which significantly contributes to the development of high blood pressure and increases the risk of cerebrovascular disease, kidney disease, and cardiovascular disease54. Additionally, the consumption of foods high in sugars poses additional health risks, particularly concerning dental health, as it contributes to tooth decay6.

Health risk behaviours frequently co-occur14,20,49,62. For instance, a strong correlation has been found between alcohol consumption and smoking30,38,62. Additionally, studies indicate that smokers are less likely to choose foods classified as healthy or prepared in a healthy manner39. Similarly, individuals who consume alcohol tend to make less healthy food choices compared to those who abstain from alcohol60.

Despite extensive evidence of the physical harms associated with tobacco use, alcohol consumption, and unhealthy diets, these behaviours remain common.

Their persistence, even among those who understand the risks, suggests that these issues are not merely a matter of knowledge or choice. Instead, they reflect deeper psychological and neurocognitive processes that influence how individuals experience reward, manage their emotions, and regulate impulses. The strong tendency for these behaviours to occur together further indicates shared vulnerability mechanisms that predispose some individuals to engage in multiple health-risk behaviours. Identifying these mechanisms is crucial because interventions focused solely on educating about specific behaviours rarely lead to lasting change. Effective prevention and treatment require a better understanding of the fundamental psychological processes that drive vulnerability across various risky behaviours.

Understanding the psychological mechanisms that increase an individual’s vulnerability to developing one or more of these health risk behaviours is crucial for creating psychological interventions. Such interventions aim to reduce the negative impacts of these behaviours on both individuals and society as a whole.

The I-PACE model10 has proved to be particularly influential. The Interaction of Person-Affect-Cognition-Execution model draws on recent empirical findings from various problematic behaviours, including pathological gambling, excessive internet or gaming use, compulsive buying, overeating, problematic sexual behaviour, and substance use. The model suggests that risky behaviours, including unhealthy lifestyle choices, share several traits. A defining attribute is the self-reported difficulty in resisting the impulse to engage in one or more of these approach behaviours, actions aimed at immediate gratification, pleasure, or relief from negative feelings. This difficulty closely relates to reduced inhibitory control (IC), the mental process for controlling impulses or suppressing inappropriate actions.

IC denotes the cognitive system’s capacity to ignore and suppress irrelevant or maladaptive stimuli, thoughts, and actions24,44.

Within the realms of neuroscience and cognitive science, IC is recognized as a multi-domain executive function that plays a vital role in adapting to shifting task demands, making it an essential element of behavioural regulation15,83,85. Given its pivotal role in regulating impulses across a range of maladaptive behaviours, IC represents a promising target for intervention. However, despite its theoretical importance, evidence-based therapeutic approaches aimed at improving IC or reducing impulsivity remain limited.

Evidence-based psychological interventions to enhance control over impulsivity are scarce, mainly due to the small number of studies and the methodological heterogeneity across existing research. A recent systematic review by Aguilar-Yamuza and colleagues4 identified only seven studies, out of an initial pool of 764, that examined interventions for six distinct impulse control disorders: gambling, pornography use, shopping, internet use, social media use, and computer gaming. Across these studies, six different psychological approaches were used: motivational interviewing combined with cognitive-behavioural therapy, bias modification treatment, simultaneous addiction substitution or recovery, cognitive-behavioural therapy, acceptance and commitment therapy, and dialectical behaviour therapy. This limited and disparate evidence base underscores the lack of consolidated, empirically supported methods for enhancing inhibitory control. An important exception to the limited evidence is the more substantial body of research supporting Inhibitory Control Training (ICT).

ICT is a psychological intervention designed to reduce health-related risk behaviours. It has primarily been used to address excessive food consumption27,31,50, high levels of alcohol intake19,33,45,53, and cigarette smoking1,47,69,82 a;8.

ICT is viewed as a “bottom-up” intervention that helps individuals manage their automatic impulses toward specific stimuli79. This approach is grounded in classical paradigms of IC and involves tasks such as the Stop Signal Task44, the Go/No-Go Task57, and the Antisaccade Task28.

Each of these tasks assesses different facets of IC. The Stop Signal Task (SST) and the Go/No-Go task focus on the ability to manage pre-existing or habitual motor responses. In the SST, participants respond quickly to a ‘go’ trial, such as pressing a button when they see a left or right-pointing arrow. They must then inhibit their response on a stop trial. Conversely, in the Go/No-Go task, participants must press a button when they encounter a target stimulus (Go trials) and refrain from responding to non-target stimuli (No-Go trials)18. The SST interrupts an already initiated response or behaviour, while the Go/No-Go task evaluates response initiation61. The Antisaccade task assesses oculomotor inhibition rather than general motor inhibition. In this task, participants must avoid making an eye movement (saccade) toward a stimulus that appears after a cue and quickly redirect their gaze in the opposite direction22.

Typically, ICT uses stimuli directly related to the problematic behaviour, such as images of palatable foods, alcohol, and cigarettes. These protocols are often referred to as “behaviour-specific ICT” in contrast to “neutral ICT”, which employs stimuli unrelated to any behaviour. The rationale for using behaviour-specific ICT to reduce unhealthy or harmful behaviours is that repeatedly inhibiting responses to target stimuli diminishes their motivational salience1 b), ultimately leading to their devaluation21,40,56,79,80,84.

Although the theoretical rationale for ICT is well established, empirical findings regarding its effectiveness are far less consistent.

Recent comprehensive meta-analyses examining the efficacy of ICT in addressing excessive consumption behaviours have produced varied results. While Allom and colleagues5 and Jones and colleagues (2016) noted minor to moderate effects of ICT on excessive food and alcohol consumption, more recent findings by Iannazzo and colleagues (2024) suggest that significant effects of ICT on behavioural outcomes are observed mainly in food-specific studies. These contrasting outcomes may stem not only from the different numbers of studies analysed but also from variations in methodology, particularly regarding inclusion and exclusion criteria. For instance, Jones and colleagues focused exclusively on individuals aged 18 and older, while Iannazzo and colleagues (2024) encompassed studies involving children and adolescents, and Allom and colleagues5 did not specify the age ranges of participants in their analyses. Moreover, none of their meta-analyses required the presence of a clinical condition as a criterion for inclusion. Instead, they combined individuals with clinical diagnoses (e.g., obesity, eating disorders, and alcohol or tobacco use disorders), at-risk groups (e.g., those with overweight issues and heavy drinking habits), and healthy individuals.

As a result, there remains limited, specific evidence concerning the efficacy of ICT in addressing excessive food and alcohol consumption, or cigarette smoking, among clinically diagnosed and those in at-risk populations. This shortcoming represents a significant gap in the literature that our meta-analysis intends to address.

In this study, we focused exclusively on research involving clinical and at-risk populations, where unhealthy habits pose considerable challenges to health and well-being. We further limited our scope to adults aged 18 and older, as this is a commonly used threshold in research and reflects legal adulthood. Indeed, children and adolescents may exhibit different behavioural patterns and responsiveness to ICT interventions compared to adults, in part due to ongoing cognitive development and maturation, which occurs along a continuum.

Our objective is to contribute meaningfully to this area of research by evaluating the effects of ICT training in these populations. Specifically, we aimed to address the following questions: (a) Is ICT effective in clinical and at-risk populations, in both the short and long term? (b) Do the effects of ICT vary among the different behaviours examined? (c) Do specific training features influence the efficacy of ICT interventions?

Those questions were motivated by several considerations. First, when working with clinical and at-risk populations, it is essential to ascertain whether the effects of an intervention not only manifest immediately after training (short-term effects) but also whether they endure over time (long-term effects). Second, although excessive food consumption, alcohol use and cigarette smoking, are linked to similar cognitive and motivational processes (and by implication, overlapping neural substrate also), prior research suggests that ICT may affect these behaviours differently34. Third, from both theoretical and clinical perspectives, it is critical to identify which training parameters yield the strongest outcomes. Relevant features include the training paradigm employed (e.g., Go/No-Go, Stop-Signal Task, antisaccade), the number and duration of training sessions, and the setting in which training occurs (laboratory vs. home-based).

To address these questions comprehensively, we conducted a series of meta-analyses and meta-regression analyses in our study.

Methods

The study is reported following the PRISMA guidelines (Liberati et al., 2009;59; See Table S1 of the Supplementary materials for PRISMA checklist). The study was pre-registered (https://www.crd.york.ac.uk/PROSPERO/view/CRD42021232571).

Literature search

The search was conducted on articles published up to October 2025, and in the following databases: PubMed, APA PsychINFO and Web of Science. The Boolean strings are reported in the supplementary materials.

Eligibility criteria

The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) primary studies published in peer-reviewed journals; (2) Randomised Controlled Trials (RCTs) with an intervention group (ICT, either based on Go/No-Go, Stop-Signal or Antisaccade task paradigms) and a control group (comprising either no training, or neutral ICT or other cognitive training); (3) presence/availability of behavioural outcomes measures (means and standard deviations); (4) involving participants over 18 years of age; (5) samples consisting exclusively of individuals exhibiting unhealthy behaviours associated with clinical or at-risk conditions, including binge eating disorder, bulimia nervosa, overweight, obesity, alcohol use disorder, heavy drinking, nicotine dependence, or tobacco use disorder.

The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) review articles (scoping, narrative or systematic), although relevant systematic reviews were retained during screening for identifying potentially relevant primary studies; (2) studies not published in English; (3) studies involving non-human populations; (4) studies reporting participants under 18 years of age; (5) studies combining ICT with other structured interventions, such as cognitive-behavioural therapy or non-invasive brain stimulation.

Quality assessment

The included studies were evaluated for quality using the Appraisal tool for Cross-Sectional Studies (AXIS)16. Items 7, 13, 14, and 19 were excluded from the assessment because they did not apply to the present study (e.g., item 7: “Were measures undertaken to address and categorise non-responders?”).

Two authors independently performed the critical appraisal of the included studies (GG, FM). In cases of discrepancies between the two reviewers, a third reviewer resolved the conflicts (EM).

Data extraction

Two authors (BDC and EM) conducted data extraction. In case of disagreement, a third author (EDR) evaluated the discrepancies and solved them by consensus. Authors extracted the following information from each article: (a) study characteristics: authors, year of publication; (b) participants characteristics (sample size, age, sex, presence of binge eating disorder, bulimia nervosa, overweight and obesity, alcohol use disorder/heavy drinking, nicotine dependence/ tobacco use disorder); (c) training characteristics: paradigm (SST, Go/No-Go or antisaccade), training duration and number of sessions; (d) study design: type of control condition; laboratory vs. real world setting; presence of follow-up; (e) mean and standard deviations of behavioural outcomes and direction of change in the outcome (increase or decrease). Importantly, to allow a comparison, some of the signs of the ICT behavioural outcomes effect sizes have been reversed. For example, a decrease in daily cigarette consumption indicates a reduction in unhealthy behaviours. Thus, when comparing post-ICT training in experimental and control conditions, a negative effect size reflects the effectiveness of the training. Conversely, a decrease of the percentage of days abstinent from alcohol consumption typically signals a worsening of unhealthy behaviours.

In this case, the effect size was reversed to ensure consistent interpretation. This adjustment ensures that a negative effect size always means improved health-related behaviours, while a positive effect size denotes a worsening of unhealthy behaviours in experimental versus control conditions.

Statistical analysis

The analysis was conducted using R (version 4.3.0, R Core Team, 2023) and the metafor package (version 4.2.0,81).

To evaluate the difference between experimental and control conditions we have employed a random-effects meta-analysis model. Specifically, we have conducted three meta-analyses. In the the first we have compared the effect sizes between the two post training measurements (T1-experimental vs. T1-control condition) on the selected behavioural outcome; the second and the third were then conducted comparing the effect sizes between the two follow-up measurements (T2-experimental vs. T2-control condition) on the selected behavioural outcome, one with and one without outliers. For all the models, we have employed “SMD” (standardised mean difference) measure in the escalc function from the metafor package that computed the Hedges’ g29. We estimated the between-studies heterogeneity (τ²) and the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${I}^{2}$$\end{document} statistics representing the percentage of total variability due to real heterogeneity and not sampling variability. We explored the presence of publication bias using the funnel plot74 and tested the funnel plot asymmetry with the Egger’s regression test75. Crucially, in the presence of heterogeneity and a limited number of studies, results need to be interpreted with caution (e.g., Sterne,76).

Finally, to investigate the presence of differences between the three behaviours considered and to assess if the training features could represent ICT effect’s moderators, two random-effects meta-regression models were performed, one with and one without outliers. In details, we have explored the impact of the following moderators: behaviour targeted (food consumption vs. alcohol consumption vs. cigarette smoking), training paradigm (SST, Go/No-Go or antisaccade, setting (lab vs. home), training duration (number of weeks), and number of sessions on the effect sizes. For all the analyses, the significance level considered for the p-value was 0.05. All relevant data and R scripts are available at the following OSF link (https://osf.io/7sdvj/).

Results

Selection and inclusion of studies

A total of 5,411 items were found: 3145 on Pubmed, 265 on APA PsychINFO, and 2001 on Web of Science. After removing duplicates, 3895 articles remained; after title and abstract screening, 3842 were excluded, and the remaining 53 were assessed for eligibility. Of these, 38 articles were subsequently excluded after the full-text screening, leaving 15 articles, and 16 studies, included in the analyses.

The reasons for study exclusion are reported in the PRISMA flow chart (Fig. 1). Screening was conducted independently by at least two reviewers using the web application “Rayyan”58.

The selection of included articles, as well as the process of data extraction, was double-checked. For article inclusion, two reviewers (BDC and EM) independently and blindly screened the full set of articles Any discrepancies were resolved by a third reviewer (EDR) through consensus.

Quality assessment

The 15 included articles were of high quality (see Table S2 in the supplementary materials), meeting on average 90.44% (SD = 12.72) of the required criteria. Inter-rater consistency between the two reviewers (GG, FM) was high, with an 87% agreement across all criteria. The main limitations identified were the absence of power analysis and justification of sample sizes.

Nine studies examined the effects of ICT on food consumption, four on alcohol consumption, and three on cigarette smoking. Eleven studies used the Go/No-Go paradigm, two utilised the Stop Signal Test (SST), one study implemented the antisaccade task, and two combined SST and Go/No-Go paradigms. Notably, Jones et al., [36] reported two separate experiments using different ICT paradigms (Go/No-Go and SST), highlighting the diversity of training approaches within the literature. The number of training sessions varied between 1 and 28.

All experimental training used behaviour-specific ICT, i.e. food for eating behaviour, cigarettes for smoking, and alcohol-related stimuli for alcohol consumption.

Outcome measures varied by behaviour. For eating behaviour, measures included frequency of binge eating episodes, overall rate of binge eating, the Binge Eating Scale (BES, [25]), energy intake expressed in kilocalories, The Eating Disorder Examination (EDE-Q, global score) [46], and body fat. For cigarette smoking, measures comprised daily cigarette consumption and total cigarettes smoked per week. For alcohol consumption, measures focused on mean number of heavy drinking days over a 4-week period, weekly alcohol intake, and the percentage of days abstinent (calculated using an adjusted formula from the The Alcohol Timeline Followback- TLFB; [70]; controlling for days in a protected environment). For studies reporting percentage of abstinent days, the direction of the standardised effect size was inverted to enable comparisons with other studies.

Control conditions across studies included studies were the following: neutral ICT; with stimuli unrelated to the problematic behaviour (N = 9); behaviour-specific ICT “Beer Go” (beer-related pictures as go cues, empty glasses as no-go cues; N = 1); computerised tasks presenting behaviour-specific stimuli without inhibitory response associations (N = 2); treatment as usual (N = 2); informational brochures (N = 1); and waiting list (N = 1).

In one study [52], included after a search update in October 2025, they compared behaviour specific ICT with the general Go/No-Go training (sometimes referred to as “neutral ICT”), despite the study having two control conditions (sham training and waiting list), to maintain consistency with the majority of studies in the meta-analysis.

Moderators of ICT effects: differences between the three behaviours and role of training features

Meta-regression analyses revealed a significant moderator for post-training comparison between the experimental and the control conditions (See Tables S3 of the supplementary materials). Specifically, the setting emerged as a significant moderator (p = 0.0472; 95% CI -0.7589 to -0.0047), indicating that ICT delivered at home produced larger effect sizes than ICT delivered in the laboratory.

Discussion

We conducted a meta-analysis and meta regression to address several knowledge gaps related to health-related risk behaviours, specifically focusing on the consumption of tobacco, and on excessive alcohol and unhealthy foods, three of the principal contributors to preventable morbidity and early mortality globally.

The objectives of our study were to: (a) evaluate the efficacy of ICT in clinical and at-risk populations, considering both short and long-term outcomes; (b) evaluate if the effect of ICT varies across the three health related risk behaviours; and (c) identify specific training features that may function as potential moderators of ICT effects. To this end, we analysed data from 17 studies reported in 15 articles, involving a total of 1398 participants. Participants were classified with one or more of the following clinical or at-risk conditions, including obesity, binge eating disorder, bulimia, overweight, alcohol use disorder, heavy drinking habits, tobacco use disorders and nicotine dependence.

ICT was implemented using several paradigms, including the SST, the Go/No-Go task, and the antisaccade paradigm. Each study applied behaviour-specific ICT, with stimuli directly related to the target problematic behaviour. Control conditions mostly involved computerised tasks, including either neutral ICT or tasks using behaviour-specific stimuli without requiring inhibition. Other control conditions, such brochures, waitlist groups, and treatment as usual, were also present, but used less frequently. This meta-analysis and meta regression extend previous work assessing ICT efficacy on health-related behaviours [5,34,35] in two key regards: first, by focusing exclusively on clinical and at-risk populations; and second, by including only studies with adult populations (≥ 18 years). To the best of our knowledge, no previous meta-analysis has examined ICT efficacy specifically in these populations.

Our results indicate that behaviour-specific ICT did not significantly differ control conditions, at either post-training and at long-term follow-up. No differential effects were observed across the three behaviours considered, but the training setting emerged as a significant moderator: ICT delivered at home, produced larger effects than ICT delivered in the laboratory.

Two potential explanations may account for the lack of significant differences between the experimental and control conditions. They overlap in that both could lead to similar behavioural improvements across groups, particularly because most controls were active tasks. However, they differ in their underlying mechanisms.

First, a placebo and/or a general time effect may have produced comparable behavioural changes across groups, particularly since most control conditions were “active” cognitive interventions. These active tasks, although not targeting the specific problematic behaviour, may enhance general attentional control, inhibitory capacity, or self-monitoring, producing improvements that partially overlap with the mechanisms targeted by behaviour-specific ICT. While this hypothesis seems plausible, the limited number of studies comparing ICT with truly passive controls, such as wait list or brochure conditions, prevents testing this hypothesis, leaving this question unresolved.

Second, behaviour-specific and neutral ICT may have comparable effects. Notably, nine of the sixteen studies included, control groups received neutral ICT, in which stimuli were unrelated to the problematic behaviour, representing the majority of controls. The lack of significant differences between experimental and control conditions may therefore reflect that both approaches can reduce problematic behaviours, albeit through different cognitive mechanisms. Neutral ICT likely strengthens general inhibitory control and attentional processes, whereas behaviour-specific ICT additionally targets stimulus devaluation and attractiveness reduction, alongside general improvements in inhibitory control and attention.

In summary, both explanations could lead to similar behavioural improvements, but they differ in mechanism: the first reflects non-specific placebo or time effects, while the second reflects active cognitive processes, with neutral ICT enhancing general control and behaviour-specific ICT additionally reducing the salience and attractiveness of problematic stimuli. This distinction provides important context for interpreting our findings.

Previous research offers a somewhat contrasting picture. Allom and colleagues [5] reported significant effects for behaviour-specific ICT, whereas neutral ICT showed no significant impact. Similarly, Iannazzo and colleagues (2024) reported significant ICT effects only in studies using stimulus-specific training, not neutral ICT. Several key differences between these studies and ours may explain these discrepancies. First, we focused exclusively on clinical and at-risk populations, whereas the previous studies also included healthy individuals, whose responses to ICT may differ and dilute effects observed in high-risk. Second, we restricted our analysis to adults aged 18 and over, while the other studies included children and adolescents, whose developing cognitive control systems may influence responsiveness to ICT. Third, we achieved a more balanced comparison between behaviour-specific (N = 16) and the neutral ICT (N = 9), whereas the earlier studies were highly skewed (Allom et al.,: 18 behaviour-specific vs. 1 neutral; Iannazzo et al.,: 39 behaviour-specific vs. 4 neutral), which may have biased conclusions about the relative efficacy of the two approaches.

As a result, our hypothesis regarding the comparable effects of behaviour-specific and neutral ICT remains plausible, though speculative, given the lack of studies directly comparing both ICT types against passive control conditions, such as waitlists.

Importantly, the present findings should be interpreted also in light of the deliberate decision to include only studies in which ICT was delivered as a stand-alone intervention. While this approach allowed us to isolate the effects of ICT per se, it limits the conclusions that can be drawn regarding ICT training when delivered alongside other treatments.

Recent evidence suggests that specific ICT, when integrated into ongoing treatment, can lead to a significant reduction of problematic behaviours. In a large clinical trial, Stein et al., [72] randomly assigned 242 recently detoxified adults with severe alcohol use disorder to receive standard alcohol-specific ICT (Go/NoGo-ratio: 50/50), an improved high-demand alcohol-specific ICT (Go/NoGo-ratio: 75/25), or an active control training (neutral ICT, with Go/NoGo-ratio: 50/50) alongside inpatient treatment. Results show that the high-demand training significantly increased the percentage of days abstinent at 3-month follow-up relative to standard alcohol-specific ICT, indicating that inhibition training may yield clinically meaningful benefits when delivered as an adjunct to treatment and with optimized inhibitory contingency. Accordingly, the predominantly null effects observed here should not be interpreted as evidence against ICT training per se, but rather as reflecting a potential limited efficacy of ICT when is delivered alone under the contingencies examined.

Regarding the three behaviours examined, our findings partially align with previous meta-analytic studies. Allom and colleagues [5] reported homogeneous effects of ICT on both food and alcohol consumption, while Iannazzo and colleagues (2024) found significant effects only for problematic eating behaviours, not for alcohol consumption, cigarette smoking, or gambling. Differences in the number of studies included may partly explain these discrepancies: Allom et al., included a sample similar to ours (N = 19), whereas Iannazzo et al., considered 46 articles encompassing 57 studies, 29 of which focused on food-related behaviours. Population differences between studies, as noted earlier, likely also contributed.

Finally, the moderation analysis revealed that the only significant training feature influencing ICT effects was the setting, with smaller effect sizes observed when training occurred in a laboratory compared to at home. This finding suggests that conducting ICT in familiar, low-pressure environments may enhance participant engagement, attention, and motivation, thereby strengthening inhibitory control more effectively.

Home-based training also allows for self-paced, repeated practice, which may increase adherence and ecological validity compared with structured laboratory sessions (e.g., Belleville, et al., 2023; [17]). Although previous meta-analyses [5,34] did not report setting effects, our results highlight the potential importance of the intervention context in optimizing ICT outcomes. Given the limited number of studies directly comparing home and lab delivery, future research should systematically investigate how setting interacts with participant characteristics and training features to influence ICT efficacy in clinical and at-risk populations.

Clinical implications

Despite the relatively small number of studies included and the lack of significant differences between experimental and control conditions, our results have important clinical implications to consider.

Indeed, the absence of significant differences between experimental and control conditions raises important considerations for the use of behaviour-specific ICT, rather than the neutral one, as a recommended intervention for problematic behaviours, as well as for the use of ICT as an add-on to structured treatment, rather than a stand-alone intervention.

Specifically, if the lack of significant differences between experimental and control conditions is due to a general placebo/time effect, the fact that we did focus on clinical and at-risk populations only warns about the real effectiveness of ICT on those individuals where unhealthy behaviours are the core symptom of a pathological condition, as well as on those who are considered at risk of developing one.

Indeed, it is possible to think that ICT can have limited efficacy on those individuals where the target behaviour has not become problematic and addictive. Indeed, it is plausible to think that when the appetitive behaviour has reached a pathological or a high-risk level, ICT could have limited efficacy and might be not effective in reducing those behaviours.

On the other hand, if the proposed hypothesis of a comparable efficacy of behaviour-specific and neutral ICT is supported, then neutral ICT could be ‘safer’ intervention considering the implications of exposing individuals to potentially triggering stimuli linked to their problematic behaviour.

Moreover, while our meta-analysis is on excessive food and alcohol consumption, and cigarette smoking, evidence suggests that abnormal IC might contribute to the development of other harmful behaviours such hypersexuality and problematic pornography (for a recent review, see [51]).

Hence, if our second hypothesis is supported, with both neutral and behaviour-specific ICT being equally effective in reducing unhealthy behaviour linked to IC abnormalities, adopting neutral ICT over behaviour-specific ICT might be also a more ethically acceptable option in cases of those target behaviours, which might result problematic not only for the patient, but also for the clinicians and researchers administering the ICT.

Therefore, a careful assessment of the effects of behaviour-specific vs. neutral ICT vs. passive control conditions is crucial to determine the actual effectiveness of ICT, and the most optimal ICT approach for reducing harmful behaviour while minimising discomfort and possible ethical concerns.

Finally, as our meta-analysis included studies where ICT was employed as standalone intervention, the present results speak to the effects of ICT in isolation. They do not inform the efficacy of ICT used in combination with other treatments, such as psychotherapy or non-invasive brain stimulation. Notably, recent randomized clinical trials indicate that ICT may yield clinically meaningful benefits when delivered as an adjunct to ongoing treatment, particularly under stronger inhibitory contingencies (e.g., [72]). Thus, ICT may be better conceptualized as a complementary intervention rather than a replacement for established therapeutic approaches.

Strengths and Limitations

The main strengths of this study lie in its focus on adult individuals in clinical and at-risk populations and on the three major contributors to preventable morbidity and early mortality worldwide: tobacco, alcohol, and unhealthy food consumption.

Also, we included only studies where ICT was implemented as a stand-alone intervention, rather than as an add-on to other treatments such as psychotherapy or non-invasive brain stimulation. This focus allowed us to address a significant gap in the literature and provide new, clinically relevant evidence. However, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the small number of included studies reflects both the relative novelty of ICT interventions and the stringent inclusion criteria applied.

Second, there was considerable heterogeneity in control conditions, particularly a scarcity of passive controls, which limited our ability to directly compare behaviour-specific ICT, neutral ICT, and passive conditions. Indeed, one clear limitation emerging from this meta-analysis is the scarcity of studies using true neutral control conditions in clinical and at-risk populations. This gap, likely influenced by ethical concerns surrounding passive controls, restricts a full understanding of ICT’s efficacy in these populations. Heterogeneity also extended to ICT paradigms and their specific designs; for example, Go/No-Go tasks varied in the proportion of Go versus No-Go stimuli across studies. While paradigm type was addressed in moderation analyses, more fine-grained differences in task design were not. Notably, recent evidence suggests that domain-specific inhibition training may be effective primarily when inhibitory demands are high, such that most target stimuli are consistently paired with inhibition (Go/No-Go-ratio: 75/25), whereas paradigms using a 50/50 Go/No-Go contingency may yield weaker or null effects [72]. The inability to account for such design characteristics constrains interpretation of the mechanisms underlying ICT effects and may partly explain the absence of robust effects observed in the present meta-analysis. Other limitations include the restricted generalizability of our findings to other problematic behaviours associated with inhibitory control deficits. Our results specifically pertain to excessive food and alcohol consumption and cigarette smoking; extrapolation to behaviours such as gambling, hypersexuality, or problematic pornography remains speculative. Finally, limited follow-up data further reduced sample sizes, and long-term effects of ICT should therefore be interpreted with caution.

Future directions

Despite the limitations noted above, our findings highlight several important avenues for future research. First, studies should systematically compare ICT with passive control conditions, such as waitlist or brochure groups, to more rigorously test the potential contributions of placebo or general time effects. Second, research should directly compare behaviour-specific ICT with neutral ICT in adult clinical and at-risk populations, in order to clarify whether both approaches are equally effective and to identify potential differences in underlying cognitive mechanisms.

Third, future studies should examine the effectiveness of ICT when employed as add-on to other treatments, rather than stand-alone intervention.

Finally, examining moderators such as training setting, task paradigm, and design features will further help optimize ICT interventions.

Addressing these questions would also enable future meta-analyses with larger sample sizes, improving statistical power and precision in evaluating ICT’s effectiveness.

Additionally, investigating moderators such as training setting, paradigm type, and task design could provide further insight into optimizing ICT interventions. Ultimately, these efforts would help determine the most effective, safe, and ethically appropriate ICT protocols for reducing unhealthy behaviours in at-risk populations.

Conclusion

In adult clinical and at-risk populations, behaviour-specific ICT did not outperform control conditions, suggesting improvements may reflect general cognitive processes or placebo effects rather than targeted intervention. However, larger effects for home-based training highlight the importance of context in ICT delivery. These findings caution against relying on behaviour-specific ICT as a stand-alone treatment and underscore the need for future studies comparing neutral and behaviour-specific ICT, as well as comparing both ICT with passive controls, and examining optimal delivery strategies. Addressing these questions is essential to develop safe, effective, and evidence-based ICT interventions for high-risk populations.

Importantly, these conclusions apply specifically to ICT delivered as a stand-alone intervention; converging evidence suggests that selective inhibition training may show greater promise when implemented using stronger contingencies and when integrated into broader treatment frameworks.
