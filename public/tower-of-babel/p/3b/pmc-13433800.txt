Large-sample PCA eigenvectors stabilize cortical thickness components and improve small sample brain behavior prediction
Authors: Zhang Yun Feng, Kenchi Hosokawa, Chihiro Hosoda
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractReproducible brain-wide association studies remain challenging in structural MRI, in part because high-dimensional cortical measures yield unstable eigenspaces in small samples. Here, using cortical thickness data from the Human Connectome Project Young Adult cohort (N = 1,113), we examined how sample size influences the stability of principal component analysis (PCA) and whether eigenvectors derived from larger samples can improve brain-behavior prediction in independent small samples. PCA stability was quantified across overlapping and non-overlapping resampling schemes using cosine similarity and one-to-one Hungarian matching of components. PCA stability increased systematically with sample size: Subsamples under 100 participants yielded few stable components, whereas larger subsamples produced dozens. Thus, reproducibility hinges not only on statistical power but on the stability of the representational basis: components learned in small subsamples are fragile, while eigenvectors from larger samples converge to stable, transferable axes. We then compared three prediction settings (500 vs. 500, 500 vs. 100, and 100 vs. 100) across 65 cognitive and personality traits using linear regression and machine-learning models. Transferring eigenvectors derived from larger samples to another smaller samples consistently improved prediction relative to deriving PCA components within the same small sample, although absolute effect sizes remained modest. Prediction performance was highest at an intermediate dimensionality of approximately 30 principal components, indicating that increasing the number of retained components does not necessarily improve generalization. These findings identify PCA eigenspace stability as a key determinant of reproducible brain-behavior inference and suggest that reusing larger-sample PCA eigenvectors is a practical strategy for stabilizing feature extraction in resource-limited neuroimaging studies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-52800-4.Subject terms: Computational biology and bioinformatics, Neuroscience

Introduction

Numerous studies have linked MRI data to psychological and behavioral traits using multivariate methods to identify brain–behavior associations1. Reproducible brain-wide associations remain elusive in structural MRI because analyses often occur in a high-dimension, low-sample-size (HDLSS) regime in which the estimated eigenspace is highly variable2–4,12,15. In this regime, sampling noise distorts principal directions and inflates apparent brain–behavior correlations, undermining generalization across datasets2. A key contributor is sample dependence: effects estimated in one dataset frequently fail to generalize to independent datasets3–5. This issue is pronounced in neuroimaging, where small samples often yield fragile, non-generalizable findings, and the high dimensionality of the data further raises the risk of spurious correlations and overfitting2–7.

Recent work underscores that larger samples are essential for stable and reproducible brain-wide association results8. For example, functional MRI (fMRI) studies with ~ 20–30 participants rarely yield reproducible brain–behavior correlations across analytic methods5,7. Small samples limit statistical power, inflate sampling variability, and produce noisy estimates of brain–behavior relationships3,5. Although many studies have examined sample-size requirements, reported thresholds vary with analysis type and imaging measures, so the optimal sample size for robust neuroimaging remains uncertain6,8,9.

Despite technological progress, most neuroimaging studies still enroll fewer than 100 participants6,7, whereas some analyses suggest that stable brain-wide association studies may require on the order of 1,000 participants8. Because collecting large datasets is costly, several strategies have been proposed to improve reliability in small-sample studies11,12. These include designing rigorous control conditions10,13. However, these approaches have trade-offs (e.g., reduced flexibility, participant fatigue, longer protocols), so practical analytical methods that enhance stability and reproducibility remain needed.

Given the vertex-wise dimensionality of cortical measures, we adopt principal component analysis (PCA) as a simple, interpretable baseline for feature extraction in structural MRI14–16. Nevertheless, PCA instability becomes a critical issue when the sample size is small: noise can dominate the decomposition and compromise downstream analyses17–19. When the number of samples is far smaller than the number of features, PCA may capture noise rather than meaningful structure, making results highly sensitive to minor perturbations17,18,30. We therefore examine PCA stability under small-sample conditions and its implications for brain–behavior analyses, particularly how sample size affects the stability of PCA components and, in turn, neuroimaging reproducibility.

Large open datasets such as the Human Connectome Project (HCP)21,22 and the UK Biobank23,24 provide extensive imaging and behavioral data, enabling robust investigations of brain structure, function, and behavior25. These datasets thus offer an opportunity to improve the robustness of neuroimaging studies.

Here we assess how sample size affects the reliability of PCA components in structural MRI. We quantify how sample size shapes eigenspace stability and test whether pre-computed eigenvectors from large datasets improve the stability and out-of-sample performance of small-sample brain–behavior analyses. Using the HCP Young Adult dataset (N = 1,113)21, we: (1) apply PCA to subsamples of varying sizes; (2) compare the stability and consistency of component structure across sample sizes; and (3) evaluate whether eigenvectors computed from large samples improve the robustness of brain–behavior analyses in smaller, independent datasets using linear regression and machine-learning models (support vector regression, decision tree, random forest). Unlike strategies focused on experimental constraints 11,12, our approach leverages large-scale resources to mitigate small-sample PCA instability by porting stable features.

Results

Larger samples yield more stable PCA components across resampling schemes

To examine how sample size influences the stability of PCA-derived components, we analyzed surface-corrected cortical thickness data from the HCP Young Adult dataset (N = 1,113). We assessed PCA stability under two complementary resampling schemes: (i) overlapping subsamples of varying sizes, each compared with a large reference sample (n = 525), and (ii) non-overlapping subsamples of equal size compared within each sample-size level (Fig. 1). PCA correspondence across subsamples was quantified using cosine similarity, and the overall analysis workflow is summarized in Fig. 1.

Cortical thickness data from the Human Connectome Project (HCP) Young Adult cohort were resampled into smaller subsamples to examine the stability of PCA-derived Cortical thickness brain components across sample sizes. We then tested whether eigenvectors derived from larger subsamples could be transferred to independent smaller subsamples to improve brain-behavior prediction using linear regression and machine-learning models.

For each subsample pair, we calculated the cosine similarity matrix between PCs and used the Hungarian algorithm to obtain a one-to-one component matching that maximized total similarity. We then used mean Hungarian-matched cosine similarity as the primary threshold-free measure of PCA stability (Fig. 2). Overlapping comparisons were included to visualize convergence toward the large reference sample, whereas non-overlapping comparisons provided a stricter test of cross-sample stability.

Across both resampling schemes, PC similarity increased with sample size. In the hierarchical (overlapping) sampling condition, this pattern is consistent with the expected convergence of PCA structure as larger subsamples include more shared observations with the reference dataset. Importantly, a similar hierarchical pattern was also observed in the non-overlapping subsamples, where no shared participants were present, indicating that the increase in similarity cannot be attributed solely to overlapping variance. Larger subsamples exhibited a greater number of components with higher similarity values, whereas smaller subsamples—particularly those with n < 100—showed lower similarity, indicating reduced component stability (Fig. 2a, b).

Mean cosine similarity across the top 30 PCs increased monotonically with sample size under both resampling schemes (Fig. 2a–c). To quantify this trend, we performed three complementary statistical analyses of the pair-level mean cosine similarity. First, a one-way ANOVA with sample size as the grouping factor confirmed that stability differed significantly across sample-size conditions (non-overlapping: F(8, 956) = 4950.48, p < 0.001, η2 = 0.976; overlapping: F(7, 36) = 89.24, p < 0.001, η2 = 0.946), indicating that sample size alone accounts for over 94% of the variance in PC stability. Second, permutation-based regression (N = 5,000 permutations) confirmed a robust linear relationship between sample size and mean stability (non-overlapping: β = 0.00051, R2 = 0.952, p_perm < 0.001; overlapping: β = 0.00060, R2 = 0.906, p_perm < 0.001). Third, separate ANOVAs for each of the 30 PCs showed that the sample-size effect was pervasive across components, with η2 ranging from 0.81 (PC 1) to 0.22 (PC 30) in the non-overlapping condition (mean η2 = 0.46 and 0.64 for non-overlapping and overlapping, respectively), indicating that earlier, high-variance components are most sensitive to sample-size variation.

To further characterize the convergence of PC’s structure across subsample sizes, we evaluated the similarity score using both continuous and threshold-based summaries. Cosine similarity was analyzed as a continuous function across the full similarity range (0–1). Across all thresholds, larger sample sizes consistently exhibited higher proportions of stable components than smaller sample sizes (Supplementary Fig. 4). The relative ordering of stability across sample sizes remained unchanged throughout the similarity spectrum, indicating that the hierarchical pattern of stability was not dependent on any single cut-off value. The several previous studies that adopted 0.4 as a benchmark for moderate similarity26 in general statistics and entity matching tasks27, to observe the convergence of PCA structures, we additionally summarized the number of matched components exceeding provisional similarity thresholds (0.2, 0.4, and 0.6). As sample size increased, the number of components exceeding each threshold also increased under both overlapping and non-overlapping resampling schemes (Fig. 2; Supplementary Fig. 4). For example, as shown in Fig. 2a, b, in overlapping subsamples, the count of components exceeding 0.4 increased from one at n = 50 to forty at n = 375. Similar monotonic patterns were observed when thresholds were set at 0.2 or 0.6.

Beyond threshold similarity counts, to further characterize similarity patterns, we examined the full distribution of PC-level similarities. Row-wise similarity profiles without Hungarian matching indicated that smaller subsamples rarely exhibited strong one-to-one overlaps (Fig. 2c, Supplementary Fig. 2, 3), and showed that smaller subsamples rarely exhibited strong exclusive overlaps. Visualization of Hungarian matching trajectories on raw similarity matrices demonstrated progressively clearer diagonal alignment as sample size increased (Supplementary Fig. 5–7). Violin plots (Supplementary Fig. 8) of Hungarian-matched similarities per PC showed that larger subsamples yielded narrower, higher-median distributions, whereas smaller subsamples showed broader distributions, reflecting more diffuse correspondence across resamples. Prediction performance was evaluated using linear regression (LR) and machine learning (ML) approaches. For ML, three candidate models (SVR, Random Forest, and Decision Tree) were implemented under an identical preprocessing and hyperparameter optimization framework, and the best-performing model was selected exclusively within the training data using cross-validation, and its performance was then evaluated on the held-out test set. As shown in Fig. 4a,b, increasing the number of retained PCs (K = 10–99) did not result in monotonic improvements in prediction performance for either LR or ML. The optimal number of PCs depended on sample size and condition: peak R values occurred at 30 PCs for the 500 vs. 500 condition, 40 PCs for 500 vs. 100, and 20 PCs for 100 vs. 100. Thus, these findings indicate that simply adding components does not guarantee better performance; careful component selection is essential to maintain robust associations.

Stable PCA components from large samples improve prediction across cognitive and personality traits

To evaluate the reproducibility of PCA-derived brain components and downstream prediction performance, we conducted 50 resampling iterations per condition. Behavioral variables were grouped into two domains—cognition (37 traits) and personality (28 traits)—to improve interpretability (Supplementary Tables 1–2). Prediction performance was assessed using linear regression (LR) and machine learning (ML) models. Mean R values across resampling iterations are shown in Fig. 4c–f.

Across both behavioral domains and modeling approaches, small samples projected using eigenvectors derived from larger datasets (500 vs. 100) consistently outperformed small samples using their own eigenvectors (100 vs. 100) (Fig. 4c–f). For cognition, mean R values under LR were 0.10 (500 vs. 500), 0.10 (500 vs. 100), and 0.02 (100 vs. 100); under ML, they were 0.14, 0.14, and 0.09, respectively. For the personality domain, mean R values under LR were 0.06 (500 vs. 500), 0.06 (500 vs. 100), and 0.01 (100 vs. 100); under ML, they were 0.11, 0.10, and 0.08, respectively. ANOVA across the 50 resampling iterations indicated significant differences among conditions (F(2,98) = 137.2, p < 0.01). Across all three conditions, ML models yielded higher R values than LR (Fig. 4c–f). Although the 500 vs. 100 condition showed improvement relative to 100 vs. 100 and in some cases approached the 500 vs. 500 baseline, absolute prediction performance remained modest across domains (Supplementary Tables 3–4). This magnitude is consistent with prior large-scale brain-wide association studies reporting small cross-validated effect sizes even in large cohorts8,28. To determine whether performance differences across PCA conditions were driven by a specific regression algorithm, we also examined and reported model selection frequencies (Supplementary Table 5).Across bootstrap resamples and behavioral outcomes, SVR was selected as the best-performing model in 56.12% of cases, followed by Random Forest (36.57%) and Decision Tree (7.31%). These proportions were highly similar across PCA conditions, indicating that observed performance differences were not attributable to systematic variation in model selection. This pattern aligns with prior reports suggesting that, in neuroimaging-based prediction, feature representation and sample characteristics often exert greater influence on performance than the specific regression algorithm. Discussion

This study quantifies how sample size influences the stability of the PCA eigenspace for surface‑based cortical thickness and examined whether pre‑computed eigenvectors from large datasets can stabilize small‑sample brain–behavior analyses. Across analyses, as sample size increases, both component‑wise correspondence and subspace‑level overlap improve. A similar pattern in non-overlapping subsamples suggests that the effect is not solely driven by shared participants. These stability gains are observable across resampling schemes and have measurable consequences for downstream inference. PCs estimated in small samples remain comparatively variable, whereas eigenvectors derived from larger samples provide a transferable reference that reduces variance across resamples and mitigates overfitting under identical modeling conditions.

By comparing the stability curves of overlapping and non-overlapping subsamples across the full similarity spectrum (0–1) (Supplementary Fig. 4a vs. 4b), we observed a clear hierarchical shift: as sample size increases, the curves shift upward and to the right regardless of the sampling condition. In overlapping subsamples, higher absolute similarity values are expected because of shared data structure. Importantly, the same ordering persists in non-overlapping subsamples, which constitute a more stringent test of out-of-sample replicability. The dominance of larger-N curves in independent comparisons indicates that stability gains are not solely driven by shared variance but reflect improved estimation precision with increasing sample size. This pattern aligns with theoretical work showing that PCA estimates become more reliable as sample size increases relative to dimensionality17,30.

Although our original analyses reported 0.4 as a provisional benchmark for counting “stable” components, additional similarity analyses demonstrate that our conclusions are not dependent on this specific cut-off. Larger samples consistently exhibit higher stability across thresholds (0.2, 0.4, 0.6). The relative ordering of sample sizes remains unchanged, supporting threshold-independent inference. Thus, the value of 0.4 is retained as a descriptive reference point—consistent with conventional interpretations of moderate agreement26—but it does not determine the study’s conclusions.These observations position representational stability—rather than nominal effect of magnitude—as a primary lever for reproducible inference in cortical thickness. In the HCP Young Adult data, subsamples with N < 100 yielded few stable components, while larger subsamples produced dozens. Absolute prediction correlations were modest (mean r ≈ 0.1) across models and targets; crucially, this magnitude is consistent with large-scale BWAS benchmarks reporting modest cross-validated effects even in very large cohorts8. Low absolute r should therefore not be interpreted as anomalous in neuroimaging prediction settings, but as characteristic of the domain’s signal-to-noise regime. The precomputed, more stable PCA eigenspaces may provide a practical, low-overhead scaffold for resource-limited studies. A related practical consideration is operating dimensionality. In the large-sample and transfer settings, prediction performance generally peaked at around 30 PCs, suggesting that retaining approximately 30 stable components may provide a pragmatic operating point that captures reproducible variance while limiting noise without extensive hyperparameter tuning. This pattern is broadly consistent with PCA theory, in which leading components tend to capture more reproducible high-variance structure, whereas higher-order components are increasingly sensitive to sampling variability, particularly in high-dimensional settings17,30. This interpretation is also in line with standard treatments of principal component regression, where predictive performance may reach an interior optimum as the number of retained components increases32. Prior strategies to mitigate small-sample fragility include preregistration and rigorous designs11,12, as well as analytic choices such as variance thresholds, scree-plot heuristics, and sparse PCA15,18,34. Our approach complements these practices by providing a data-driven reference eigenspace learned from large samples that could be reused in independent, smaller datasets. In our experiments, the principal benefit stemmed from stabilizing the representation; by contrast, increasing model complexity had modest effects. Machine-learning models typically outperformed linear regression33,35, but improvements were incremental, and our ML models were not exhaustively tuned. This pattern suggests that representation stability, rather than model sophistication per se, contributed more substantially to variance reduction across resamples. One possible interpretation is that unstable feature spaces propagate estimation noise into downstream predictors, whereas stabilizing the eigenspace reduces variance in projected features—an effect conceptually related to variance control in statistical learning32. We therefore interpret large-sample eigenvectors primarily as providing a more stable basis rather than conferring intrinsic semantic superiority. Accordingly, our proposed framework does not replace existing model-based regularization strategies but operates at the representational level. By utilizing stable eigenvectors extracted from larger datasets, it offers a reference structure that may enhance generalizability in small-sample analyses17,19. Furthermore, additional analysis using Ridge regression (Supplementary Fig. 16) suggested that this effect was attributable not to regularization alone, but rather to improved eigenspace stability. Future work should explicitly examine model-specific interactions with PCA feature stability and compare this approach against alternative regularization baselines applied directly to high-dimensional vertex-wise data. Transfer efficacy depends on distributional similarity between the large-sample reference and the target dataset; demographics, acquisition parameters, preprocessing choices, and surface models can all influence alignment. Substantial domain shifts may reduce transfer performance. Our analysis focused on cortical thickness in HCP Young Adults; extension to other modalities and independent cohorts requires empirical validation21–25.

Against this backdrop, several limitations of the present work should be acknowledged, which also point to concrete directions for future research. First, although we added resampling-based statistical comparisons, future work could further strengthen inference by incorporating permutation-based significance testing at the level of individual behavioral outcomes. Second, although ML typically exceeded LR, gains were modest, and our models were not heavily tuned; regularization, ensembles, and feature-selection pipelines may yield further improvements33,35. Third, while PCA is interpretable and widely used13–16, the framework can be extended to supervised and non-linear dimensionality-reduction methods that may capture complementary structure20. Fourth, generalization beyond HCP Young Adults requires external replication under domain shift (scanner, site, population), ideally in additional large-scale datasets21–25. Finally, it remains open whether PCA stability saturates beyond N ≈ 1,000; probing such regimes and formalizing subspace-level diagnostics as routine outputs are natural next steps.Taken together, eigenspace stability emerges as a central determinant of reproducible brain‑wide association analysis. Reusing ‘eigenvectors’ from large samples offers a transparent and computationally efficient strategy to stabilize feature extraction when data collection is constrained. By prioritizing stable components over maximal dimensionality and by leveraging large‑sample ‘reference eigenspaces’, investigators may obtain more consistent results with reduced variance across resamples2,8,17,19. This perspective integrates with existing rigor practices and provides a practical framework for studies operating under realistic sample-size limitations.

Methods

In the present study, we emulated smaller datasets by resampling a large, open-access dataset into subsamples of various sizes. We then applied Principal Component Analysis (PCA) to each subsample and assessed the stability of the principal components by comparing their similarity across these subsamples. Finally, we tested an approach wherein components derived from a larger subsample were applied to a non-overlapping smaller subsample to determine whether this improved its correlation with behavioral data (Fig. 1).

Human connectome project (HCP) dataset

This study utilized the Human Connectome Project (HCP) Young Adult dataset, a comprehensive, open-access resource of high-resolution neuroimaging and behavioral data from 1,206 healthy adults aged 22–35 years, publicly available through www.humanconnectome.org21. Among the 1,206 participants, 1,113 individuals provided full 3 T structural MRI data, forming the foundation for precise analyses of cortical and subcortical brain structures. We used T1-weighted cortical thickness images from all 1,113 participants. Each participant’s behavioral data was collected using a comprehensive battery of standardized assessments covering cognitive, emotional, and social functions, among other domains. The T1-weighted images were acquired using a 3D MPRAGE sequence with the following parameters: repetition time (TR) of 2,400 ms, echo time (TE) of 2.14 ms, inversion time (TI) of 1,000 ms, flip angle of 8 degrees, field of view (FOV) of 224 × 224 mm, and voxel size of 0.7 mm isotropic.

Structural MRI (sMRI) data were processed using the HCP minimal preprocessing pipelines22. Preprocessing included distortion correction with reverse-phase encoding spin-echo images, intensity normalization to a whole-brain mode value of 1,000, and alignment to individual T1-weighted images via boundary-based registration (BBR). Data were transformed into MNI volumetric space, and cortical data were interpolated onto mid-thickness cortical surfaces mapped to the 164 k fs_LR surface mesh, equalizing left and right hemisphere sizes. Surface and volumetric data were integrated into CIFTI format to reduce high-frequency noise, and cortical thickness maps were smoothed on the surface with a 2-mm geodesic kernel. These steps ensured standardized, high-quality data for cross-subject analyses.

Previous research has demonstrated that demographic and developmental factors such as age and sex are associated with variation in brain structure and organization36–39. Large-scale population analyses likewise show joint covariation of brain measures with demographics and behavior40. To account for these potential confounding effects, we examined whether these relations were present in the Human Connectome Project (HCP) dataset by calculating the correlations among age, gender, and the subsample’s brain-image PCA components. The analysis revealed significant positive correlations between age and gender within the first 10 components (p < 0.05; Supplementary Fig. 1). Given our objective to investigate the relationship between sample size and the stability of principal component analysis (PCA) components, as well as the application of PCA eigenvectors derived from large samples, it was essential to minimize the influence of age and gender. To address these factors, we adjusted the brain imaging data using linear regression and used the residualized surface-brain data in all experiments.

Non-repeated resample procedure

Multiple independent samples of predetermined sizes were randomly drawn from a dataset comprising 1,113 individuals. Each group contained non-repeating participants within a given subsample, although participants could be included in subsamples of different sizes. The resampling process consisted of two distinct procedures: one involving hierarchical resampling into large and small subsamples, and the other focusing on entirely non-overlapping subsamples. In the hierarchical resampling procedure, a large subsample consisting of 525 individuals was drawn randomly from the total dataset of 1,113 participants. From this large subsample of 525 individuals, smaller subsamples of various sizes were subsequently selected. These smaller subsamples included 21 subsamples of size 25, 15 subsamples of size 33, 10 subsamples of size 50, 7 subsamples of size 70, 5 subsamples of size 100, 3 subsamples of size 135, 2 subsamples of size 200, and 1 subsample each of sizes 265 and 375. In the non-overlapping resampling procedure, independent subsamples of specific sizes were selected directly from the total dataset of 1,113 participants. In this case, no participant was included in more than one subsample of the same size. The sample sizes and corresponding numbers of subsamples were as follows: 44 subsamples of size 25, 33 subsamples of size 33, 22 subsamples of size 50, 15 subsamples of size 70, 11 subsamples of size 100, 8 subsamples of size 135, 5 subsamples of size 200, 4 subsamples of size 265, 2 subsamples of size 375, and 2 subsamples of size 525.

PCA computation and component similarity analyses across sample sizes

All data analytical procedures were carried out using Python 3.11.9 with the necessary libraries. Randomized Principal component analysis (PCA) in Scikit-learn is an algorithm designed for the efficient handling of high-dimensional data, which is based on the randomized truncated singular value decomposition (SVD) proposed by Halko et al. in 200941 and is optimized for low-rank matrix approximation. Thus, we applied PCA on processing brain imaging data from all randomly resampled groups, which consisted of gray matter thickness values measured across 164,000 vertices. For each resampled group, the number of principal components was set to the sample size minus one, ensuring that the dimensionality of the PCA corresponded to the number of subjects in that group. The seeds of pseudo-random sequences to initialize the PCA calculation were fixed for keeping consistency across runs. For each group, we stored key outputs including the PCA-transformed data (principal component scores and their corresponding eigenvectors), along with the sample IDs to link back to the original images. Additionally, sample IDs were recorded to link the transformed data to their corresponding original image files.

Cosine similarity metrics, focusing on the relative angles between feature vectors, are particularly suitable for assessing similarity across high-dimensional data43. Thus, cosine similarity was employed here to assess the stability of PCA eigenvectors across varying sample sizes by calculating the matrices of each subsample’s PCA component metrics. Firstly, the PC variability analysis aims to investigate how smaller sample sizes, composed of the same individuals as a larger dataset, influence the variability of PCs relative to the larger dataset. Among large and small subsamples across varying sample sizes from Hierarchical Resampling procedure, PCA components from a large reference group (n = 525) were compared to those from smaller, overlapping groups (n < 525). Cosine similarity was calculated for each smaller group’s component metrics against those from the large reference group, quantifying the degree of stability. For each smaller group, a similarity matrix was computed by taking the dot product of the normalized transformation matrices and was stored for each subsample. Each individual subsample is compared exactly once against the full reference set (the 525-sample baseline). Consequently, the number of pairs compared is identical to the number of subsamples available at each level. For a subsample size of 25, there are 21 pairs. This follows a strict linear correlation down the hierarchy: size 33 provides 15 pairs, size 50 provides 10 pairs, size 70 provides 7 pairs, and size 100 provides 5 pairs. For the remaining tiers, size 135 has 3 pairs, size 200 has 2 pairs, and sizes 265 and 375 each result in only 1 pair as they represent single-instance subsets being compared to the master reference.

Secondly, we further evaluate the stability of PCA-derived brain image components across varying sample sizes. Component similarity was calculated between the normalized transformation matrices of all different non-overlapping but the same subsample size. For each Non-overlapping Resampling group, a similarity matrix was computed by calculating the dot product of the normalized transformation matrices. Optimal pairings and average similarity pairings are also calculated. The number of pairs is derived from a combinatorial approach where every subsample within a specific size category is compared against every other subsample in that same category. This is calculated using the formula m(m-1)/2, where m represents the number of subsamples.

As a result, when the subsample size is 25 (with 44 subsamples), there are 946 compared pairs. At a subsample size of 33 (33 subsamples), the pairs decrease to 528. This trend continues downward: a size of 50 yields 231 pairs, a size of 70 results in 105 pairs, and a size of 100 produces 55 pairs. For the larger sizes, a size of 135 results in 28 pairs, while sizes of 200, 265, and the largest groups of 375 and 525 yield 10, 6, 1, and 1 pair, respectively. Although similarity was initially computed at the pair level, the unit of observation for subsequent statistical analyses was defined at the subsample level. For each subsample, its mean cosine similarity was averaged across all pairwise comparisons with other non-overlapping subsamples of the same size, yielding a single stability value per subsample. This aggregation avoids pseudo-replication arising from the non-independence of pairs that share a common subsample. To evaluate and visualize the similarity scores and component pairings and to find the most matched similarity scores for each component, a simple Mean similarity across all entries of the similarity matrix can be inflated by many-to-many moderate overlaps. To explicitly enforce one-to-one correspondence between components across pairs, we applied the Hungarian (Munkres) algorithm to the absolute similarity matrix between principal components derived from two subsamples. All similarity values were constrained to the range 0 to 1, and PC indices were aligned across pairs to ensure comparability. Alongside the optimal Hungarian matched unique matched index pairs and their corresponding matched similarities, for each subsample pair, we recorded:

(i) matched component indices; (ii) matched similarity values, and; (iii) total matched similarity.

To assess the stability of maximizing total absolute similarity under a one-to-one constraint correspondence between principal components across subsamples, we computed mean Hungarian matched similarity across all subsample pairs.

To analyze the similarity threshold for “stable component”, we analyzed component stability by treating cosine similarity as a continuous function rather than a dichotomous criterion. Specifically, for each subsample size, we calculated the mean stable ratio across the full similarity range (0.0–1.0). In addition, we arranged the number of principal components exceeding similarity thresholds of 0.2, 0.4, and 0.6 (Supplementary Fig. 4).

Each PC was visualized using Connectome Workbench (version 1.5.0, Human Connectome Project). When multiple components were displayed simultaneously, a visualization threshold was applied to improve readability. The threshold was set to either one-half of the maximum value or one-half of the number of nonzero vertices.

Statistical analysis of component similarity analyses across sample sizes

To formally quantify the effect of sample size on PCA stability, we computed the mean cosine similarity across the first 30 principal components for each split-half pair as a summary measure of PCA reproducibility. For the following statistical analyses, results were based on the first k = 30 PCs extracted in the similarity analysis, and only subsamples of N ≥ 33 were included, for ensuring consistent dimensionality across all sample-size conditions (K < N) and avoids rank-deficiency. Three complementary analyses were then applied to both the non-overlapping (k = 9 groups, N = 965 pairs) and overlapping (k = 8 groups, N = 44 pairs) subgroups. First, a one-way ANOVA tested whether mean cosine similarity differed across sample size groups, with eta-squared (η2) quantifying effect size following Cohen’s (1988) benchmarks (small = 0.01, medium = 0.06, large = 0.14). Second, a simple linear regression with sample size as the predictor was assessed via permutation testing (5,000 iterations) to evaluate the linear trend between sample size and PCA stability; in each iteration, sample size labels were shuffled and the p-value was defined as the proportion of permuted R2 values exceeding the observed R2. Third, separate one-way ANOVAs were conducted for each of the 30 PCs individually, with Bonferroni correction controlling the family-wise error rate (adjusted α = 0.05/30 = 0.0017). Additionally, the coefficient of variation (CV = SD/Mean × 100%) was computed for each sample size × PC combination to characterize within-group measurement precision.

To mitigate the effects of sampling variability, we employed a non-parametric bootstrap resampling approach with 50 iterations. In each iteration, the total sample was partitioned into two independent, non-overlapping subsets: a larger sample (n = 500) and a smaller sample (n = 100). Specifically, to assess whether a “stable component vector space” derived from a larger population could enhance the predictive reliability of a smaller one, we tract larger sample’s PCA eigenvector and apply it to the smaller sample to get the ‘new smaller sample’s Brain components. The predictive framework was implemented in two complementary stages to assess the effect of eigenvector source and the stability of prediction performance across the three conditions. First, we varied the number of retained PCs (K = 10–99) to evaluate how dimensionality influenced predictive accuracy under each condition. Second, to examine reproducibility, we repeated the full brain–behavior regression analysis across 50 independent bootstrap iterations. For this resampling-based comparison, dimensionality was fixed at K = 30 to enable direct evaluation of PCA-basis effects across conditions without confounding from varying component numbers.

Statistical comparison of brain–behavior regression across eigenvector-source conditions and robustness assessment

Predictive performance was evaluated separately for each modeling approach and eigenvector condition. As for resampled larger and smaller subgroups, the first K principal components (K ∈ [10, 99]) were used as predictors in both linear regression (LR) and machine learning (ML) models. Here, for each condition, we computed the average Pearson correlation coefficient (R) across the 65 behavioral variables. These values summarize overall prediction accuracy per bootstrap iteration.

To determine whether performance differences across PCA conditions were attributable to a specific regression algorithm, we quantified the frequency with which each model was selected as the best-performing estimator. An evaluation event was defined as one bootstrap iteration × one behavioral variable × one cross-validation split. Within each event, the model achieving the highest test-set Pearson correlation coefficient was designated as the selected model. For each condition, model frequency was calculated as the proportion of evaluation events in which a given model achieved the highest predictive performance. These percentages, therefore, represent how often each regression algorithm outperformed competing models under identical data splits and hyperparameter tuning procedures.

To assess regression robustness, we examined performance variability across 50 bootstrap resamples. For each behavioral variable, we computed the distribution of R values (LR and ML) and R2 values (ML only) across bootstrap iterations. Behavioral variables were additionally grouped into cognitive and personality domains for domain-level inference. The following statistical summaries were derived:

Mean and 95% Confidence Intervals (CI): Mean performance and 95% CIs for R (LR and ML) and R2 (ML only) were estimated from the bootstrap distributions using 3,000 resamples. This procedure ensured that observed improvements were not driven by a single favorable data split.

Statistical comparison of eigenvector-source conditions

To compare predictive performance across eigenvector-source conditions, we conducted a one-way repeated-measures analysis of variance (RM-ANOVA). For each bootstrap iteration (N = 50), we calculated the mean Pearson correlation coefficient (R) across the 65 behavioral variables under three conditions: (1) 500 vs. 500 (PCA derived and applied within the large sample), (2) 500 vs. 100 (large-sample eigenvectors applied to the small sample), and (3) 100 vs. 100 (PCA derived and applied within the small sample). Each bootstrap iteration therefore yielded three paired performance values.

A one-way RM-ANOVA with three within-subject levels (eigenvector condition) was performed to test overall differences in predictive performance. Sphericity was assessed using Mauchly’s test, and Greenhouse–Geisser corrections were applied when necessary. We report F statistics, p-values, and effect sizes as partial eta squared (η2ₚ). Post hoc pairwise comparisons were conducted using paired t-tests with Holm–Bonferroni correction. Statistical significance was defined as α = 0.05.

All analyses were implemented in Python using the statsmodels and pingouin packages. Results are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) of R values across bootstrap iterations, together with ANOVA and adjusted pairwise comparison outcomes.
