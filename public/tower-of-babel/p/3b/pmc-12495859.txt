“Unveiling the burden: mental health challenges and coping strategies among moroccan medical students”
Authors: Mounir Jaafari, Amal Ouraghen, Ferdaouss Qassimi, Amine Bout, Chadia Aarab, Rachid Aalouane, Ismail Rammouz
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMedical students face significant psychological distress due to academic, financial, and social pressures. While global studies highlight the mental health challenges of medical students, limited data is available for Moroccan medical students.ObjectivesThis study aims to assess the prevalence of psychological distress, identify associated factors, and explore coping strategies among Moroccan medical students.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted at the Faculty of Medicine and Pharmacy of Fez, involving 632 clinical-year students. Data were collected using the validated GHQ-12 questionnaire to assess psychological distress and self-reported measures of functional and dysfunctional coping strategies. Statistical analysis included chi-square tests and logistic regression to identify significant associations.ResultsThe mean GHQ-12 score was 5.00 (SD: 3.61), with 50.6% of students scoring above the threshold of 4, indicating psychological distress. The most frequently reported stressors were academic challenges (34.1%), emotional problems (12.3%), and financial difficulties (9.6%). Female students experienced significantly higher levels of distress compared to males (P < 0.001). Among the participants, 52.6% reported using coping strategies; of these, 26.5% relied exclusively on functional strategies, such as prayer, relaxation, and seeking social support, while 26.1% used at least one dysfunctional strategy. Dysfunctional coping strategies, such as dietary changes and smoking, were significantly associated with distress (P < 0.001).ConclusionThis study underscores the substantial mental health burden among Moroccan medical students, with significant associations between distress, gender, stressors, and dysfunctional coping strategies. These findings highlight the need for targeted mental health interventions and the development of supportive programs within medical education settings to promote well-being and resilience.

Background

Medical students (MS) are the healthcare professionals of tomorrow, and their role in delivering quality care is crucial. Studies have found that MS experience higher levels of stress than their peers in non-medical fields [1]. It is well-documented that MS face significant psychological challenges, including depression, anxiety, and burnout [2–6]. Additionally, they often suffer from sleep deprivation, particularly during their clinical years [7, 8]. Stress is conceptualized as a dynamic process resulting from an individual’s appraisal of external demands and the available coping resources [9]. The high prevalence of stress among MS is attributed to long working and study hours, heavy workloads, intense competition, social pressures, and financial concerns [10]. Medical students often encounter violations of professional and safety standards during their clinical rotations and, at times, may unintentionally participate in or perpetuate these inappropriate behaviors [11, 12]. Emerging research highlights that conflicts in professional values can lead to moral distress among MS [13]. High stress levels are known to negatively impact cognition, comprehension, and academic performance in medical students [14–16]. A meta-analysis [17], involving 122,356 MS across 43 countries, revealed that 27% had depressive symptoms—a rate significantly higher than in the general population—and 11.1% reported suicidal thoughts. The COVID-19 pandemic has further exacerbated the mental health challenges faced by medical students [18]. Students’ responses to stress vary widely, depending on their individual levels of vulnerability and their coping mechanisms [19]. Coping is defined as a conscious, deliberate effort to manage emotions, thoughts, and behaviors in response to stress [20]. Coping strategies can be broadly categorized into social support, positive thinking, active resolution, and avoidance [21]. Coping strategies can be classified in various ways. Some are considered adaptive, as they enhance functioning, while others are regarded as maladaptive, potentially maintaining or exacerbating levels of stress and distress [22]. According to the transactional model of stress and coping, stress arises not from events themselves but from an individual’s appraisal of environmental demands relative to their coping resources. Stress is experienced when a situation is judged to exceed these resources and threaten well-being. Coping is seen as a dynamic, ongoing process, with strategies typically classified as problem-focused (addressing the source of stress) or emotion-focused (managing emotional responses). This model highlights the evolving interaction between the person and their environment during stress management [23]. Research has shown that MS employ various coping processes, which can be either adaptive or maladaptive [19–21, 24–27]. Adaptive coping involves constructive approaches like problem-solving and emotional regulation, while maladaptive coping often includes avoidant behaviors such as rumination and social withdrawal [28]. Despite the growing body of research on medical student mental health globally, there is a significant gap in the literature regarding the prevalence of mental health problems among medical students in Morocco. This study seeks to explore the prevalence of psychological distress among Moroccan medical students and the key sociodemographic, academic, and behavioral factors associated with mental health challenges. It examines the coping strategies employed by medical students and their impact on psychological well-being, distinguishing between functional and dysfunctional coping mechanisms. Additionally, the study investigates whether dysfunctional coping strategies are significantly associated with higher psychological distress levels. Given the documented gender disparities in mental health outcomes among medical students, the research also explores how gender influences the prevalence and severity of mental health issues.

Methods

Study design

This was a cross-sectional study with both descriptive and analytical aims, conducted among medical students at the Faculty of Medicine and Pharmacy of Fez (FMPF) in Morocco.

Study setting

The study was conducted at the Faculty of Medicine and Pharmacy in Fez. The FMPF is a public university located in Fez, a city in northern inland of Morocco and it is the second largest city in Morocco. Students at the FMPF are divided into pre-clinical (1–2 years) and clinical (3–6 years) groups. The medium of teaching at FMPF is French.

Study population and sampling strategy

The study employed a convenience sampling method, targeting clinical medical students (third- to sixth-year students) at the Faculty of Medicine and Pharmacy of Fez (FMPF). Paper-based, self-administered questionnaires were distributed to 645 students who were present in class or at the university hospital center of Fez, and 632 students voluntarily participated, resulting in a response rate of 98%.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Students were eligible for participation if they were actively enrolled in their third to sixth year of medical studies, present at the time of data collection, and provided informed written consent. Students in preclinical years (first and second year), those absent during data collection, and those who declined to participate were excluded.

Bias prevention mechanisms

To minimize selection bias, data collection was conducted across multiple class sessions and clinical rotations to ensure a diverse representation of students at different academic stages. Additionally, participation was entirely voluntary, and students were informed that declining participation would not impact their academic standing. Confidentiality was strictly maintained, and no identifying information was collected to prevent response bias.

The questionnaire included sociodemographic questions, mental health assessments, and items designed to evaluate behavior-based coping styles, developed from qualitative material.

Measures

Short general health questionnaire (GHQ-12)

The General Health Questionnaire (GHQ) was developed as a screening tool to identify psychological distress in primary care settings [29]. For this study, we used the validated French version of the GHQ-12 [30]. which has demonstrated good psychometric properties, including high internal consistency and construct validity in various populations. Previous research has reported Cronbach’s α values ranging from 0.80 to 0.89, indicating strong reliability [30]. In our sample, the GHQ-12 exhibited excellent internal consistency, with a Cronbach’s α of 0.86. We selected the French version in accordance with the university’s language of instruction. Although no formal cultural revalidation was performed, it was considered appropriate for the Moroccan university context. Responses were recorded on a Likert scale (1–4), with each symptom scored as either absent or present based on the binary method (0-0-1-1). The total score ranged from 0 to 12, with a score above 4 indicating significant psychological distress [31].

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was conducted using SPSS version 27. Psychological distress (GHQ-12 score > 4) was the dependent variable. Independent variables included age, gender, study level, scholarship status, residence, housing conditions, perceived need for psychological support, and coping strategy type.

Normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Effect sizes were calculated using Cohen’s d for continuous variables and Cramér’s V for categorical variables, providing a complementary interpretation alongside p-values.

Ethical considerations

The purpose of the study was explained to the students, and informed consent was obtained from all participants. Anonymity and confidentiality of the participants were ensured throughout the study.

Results

A total of 632 students participated in the study, with 62.5% (395 students) being female. Participants were distributed across different years of study: 25.8% in Year 3 (163 students), 26% in Year 4 (183 students), 26% in Year 5 (183 students), and 16.2% in Year 6 (103 students). Most students (78.63%) lived with their parents. Regarding financial support, 78.63% of students were funded by their parents, 21.46% received scholarships, and none financed their studies through work. In terms of admission preference, 76.30% of students selected medical studies as their first-choice admission. When asked whether they would recommend medical studies to future high school graduates, 52.50% did not recommend it. All participant characteristics are listed in Table 2.

Mental health status

The mean score for psychological distress (GHQ-12) was 5.00 (SD: 3.61), with scores ranging from 0 to 12. Approximately 50.60% of the students scored above the threshold of 4, indicating notable mental health problems. A total of 259 students (40.9%) reported needing psychological help. The primary reasons cited for the need for psychological support, ranked by percentage, included: study and career-related challenges such as discouragement, disappointment, overload, stagnation, frustration, and loss of confidence (34.1%, n = 216); emotional issues involving family, friends, separation, or violence (12.3%, n = 78); financial difficulties, including self-financed studies or loss of scholarships (9.6%, n = 61); personal challenges such as accidents or illnesses (7.4%, n = 47); and family-related problems such as death, divorce, separation, or violence (7.1%, n = 45).

Association between mental health problems and sample characteristics: regression analysis

Table 5 presents the results of a logistic regression analysis examining the factors associated with mental health problems among medical students. The analysis identified gender, recommendation of medical studies, need for psychological support, stress management, and coping strategies as significant predictors of mental health problems. Female students had a significantly higher likelihood of experiencing mental health issues (Exp(B) = 1.873, 95% CI [1.306–2.686], P = 0.001). Students who did not recommend medical studies were found to have a lower likelihood of mental health problems (Exp(B) = 0.498, 95% CI [0.352–0.704], P < 0.001); however, this finding contrasts with the chi-square analysis, which suggested a higher prevalence of mental health problems among this group. This discrepancy highlights the potential influence of confounding factors, such as gender, coping strategies, and stress management practices. Additionally, students who expressed a need for psychological help were significantly more likely to have mental health problems (Exp(B) = 3.542, 95% CI [2.454–5.111], P < 0.001). The use of stress management techniques was associated with a decreased likelihood of experiencing mental health problems (Exp(B) = 0.438, 95% CI [0.209–0.911], P = 0.028). Finally, reliance on dysfunctional coping strategies significantly increased the risk of mental health issues (Exp(B) = 1.926, 95% CI [1.229–3.016], P = 0.004).

Discussion

In our study, the prevalence of psychological distress among medical students was 50.6%, based on GHQ-12 scores above the threshold of 4. This finding is consistent with studies from culturally and regionally similar countries, such as Tunisia, Saudi Arabia, and Egypt, where reported rates of depression and stress among medical students generally ranged from 55 to 65% Although slight differences exist, they may be attributed to variations in assessment tools and methodologies across studies [5, 6, 32]. Compared to global estimates, such as the 27% depression rate reported in a meta-analysis [33]. The higher prevalence observed in Arab, African, and Muslim contexts appears to be associated with heightened academic pressures, cultural stigma surrounding mental health, and limited institutional support. These contextual stressors, as outlined by the transactional model of stress and coping, may exacerbate students’ perceived stress levels and overwhelm their available coping resources, thereby contributing to increased psychological distress.

A total of 259 students (40.9%) reported a need for psychological help, with academic-related challenges—such as workload and examinations—being the most cited cause (34.1%), followed by emotional problems (12.3%), financial difficulties (9.6%), personal issues (7.4%), and family-related concerns (7.1%). Consistent with our results, previous studies have emphasized that academic pressures are significant predictors of depression, anxiety, and sleep disturbances among medical students [8, 16, 27, 34]. From a transactional stress-coping perspective, academic overload and competitive environments can act as persistent external stressors, leading to heightened emotional exhaustion when students perceive their coping resources as insufficient. These findings highlight the central role of academic stress in the mental health burden faced by medical students, especially in settings with limited psychological support services.

In our findings, 333 (52.6%) of the medical students reported utilizing coping strategies. Among these, 168 (26.5%) exclusively employed functional strategies. The most commonly reported methods included participating in sports (31.6%), seeking support from parents, family members, or friends (27.5%), and reading books (13.4%). Studies have demonstrated that regular exercise benefits not only physical health but also mental well-being. It enhances mood, boosts self-esteem, and improves cognitive function while reducing the risk of depression, anxiety, and burnout [35]. Among medical students, seeking support from family and peers is linked to improved mental health, reduced isolation, and better academic performance. Overall, strong social networks enhance resilience, lower depression and anxiety, and boost life satisfaction [36, 37]. Less frequently mentioned strategies included prayer or religious practices, art activities, and relaxation techniques. For example, prayer was the most frequently reported coping strategy among Nigerian and Saudi Arabian medical students during the COVID-19 pandemic [38, 39]. The lower percentage of students in our study who reported using prayer or a relationship with God as a coping strategy (5.7%) may reflect differences in how spirituality is integrated into daily life or perceived by medical students, even within a Muslim-majority country. Cultural or institutional factors may influence the importance attributed to prayer or a relationship with God as a coping mechanism. The use of relaxation techniques in our study was less frequent (1.1%) compared to findings from studies in other regions [26, 40]. These examples emphasize how cultural, institutional, and regional differences influence the adoption of functional coping strategies among medical students. A significant portion of students in our study (26.1%) reported using at least one dysfunctional coping strategy, including changes in diet (18%), smoking cigarettes (7%), consuming cannabis (3.8%), using alcohol (3.5%), and abusing stimulants (2.1%) or tranquilizers (4.3%). The results of our survey on dysfunctional coping styles among medical students in Morocco align with findings from various other studies. Research has shown that medical students often adopt concerning coping mechanisms, including increased alcohol consumption and the use of stimulants and/or sedatives. Additionally, passive coping strategies such as withdrawal, rumination, and increased screen time or computer use are also prevalent among this group [26]. The statistical analysis in our study revealed a significant association between gender and psychological distress, with a chi-square value of 24.308 and a p-value of < 0.001. These results align with studies from the Middle East, Africa, and some developing countries, which have consistently shown a higher prevalence of depression, anxiety, stress, and psychological distress among female medical students compared to their male counterparts [5, 6, 32, 34]41– [43]. The disparity in psychological distress between male and female medical students may be linked to societal and cultural expectations, academic pressures, and gender-specific stressors. Women often face additional burdens, such as familial roles and stigma around mental health, alongside challenges like perceived competition and occasional gender discrimination. In contrast, numerous studies conducted in Western and developed countries suggest that gender is no longer a significant risk factor for psychological distress [4, 17, 44, 45]. In our study, variables such as age, study level, and matching with the first-choice admission (reflecting career satisfaction or career indecision) were not significantly associated with psychological distress, contrasting with findings from other research [5, 46–48].

In our study, dysfunctional coping strategies showed a significant association with psychological distress, as indicated by a chi-square value of 22.974 and a p-value of < 0.001. Our findings are consistent with previous research [49–52]. A cross-sectional study conducted in Ireland demonstrated that students who utilized avoidance and emotion-focused coping strategies, including substance use and withdrawal, had a significantly higher likelihood of experiencing psychological distress [20]. Similarly, a study from Pakistan reported that dysfunctional strategies, including excessive smoking, substance use, and procrastination, were prevalent among medical students with high levels of stress and were associated with poorer mental health outcomes [51]. These behaviors often exacerbate feelings of helplessness and overwhelm, creating a negative cycle of stress and maladaptive responses. In China, research on medical students identified addiction to unhealthy eating habits, lack of sleep, and overconsumption of caffeine as common dysfunctional coping behaviors among students with high levels of distress [53].

Recommendations

To improve mental health among medical students, several strategies can be implemented based on current research. Mindfulness-based interventions, such as mindfulness-based stress reduction (MBSR) programs, have been shown to significantly reduce anxiety and depression and should be integrated into the medical curriculum [54, 55]. Establishing peer support groups and mentorship programs can foster resilience and a sense of community, as emphasized in studies highlighting the importance of social support [56]. Additionally, ensuring access to professional mental health services on campus, reducing stigma, and providing confidential counseling can address barriers to seeking help [57, 58]. Flexible academic policies, such as alternative assessment methods and allowances for academic leave, can help mitigate stress associated with heavy workloads [59]. Resilience training programs focusing on adaptive coping mechanisms and emotional regulation have proven effective in reducing burnout and improving mental health [60]. Promoting physical activity through access to fitness facilities and group sports, along with educating students on sleep hygiene, has also been shown to enhance overall well-being [55]. Finally, fostering a collaborative and supportive learning environment, rather than a competitive one, and training faculty to recognize and address stress in students, are critical steps toward improving their mental health [57]. Given the cross-sectional nature of this study, the findings do not establish causal relationships but rather associations between psychological distress and various factors. Longitudinal studies are needed to better understand the directionality of these relationships.

Clinical implications

The findings of this study have several clinical implications for mental health management among medical students. Given the high prevalence of psychological distress and the significant impact of dysfunctional coping strategies, early identification and intervention are essential. Mental health screening programs should be integrated into medical curricula to detect students at risk and provide timely psychological support. Universities should also develop structured mental health services, including access to counseling, stress management workshops, and peer support groups. Additionally, promoting adaptive coping strategies, such as problem-solving techniques and social support networks, can help mitigate stress and reduce the risk of long-term mental health complications. Finally, institutional policies should address workload management, academic pressures, and stigma surrounding mental health care to create a supportive environment for medical students.

Limitations

Our study has several limitations that should be considered. The cross-sectional design restricts the ability to determine causal relationships between psychological distress and factors such as gender, coping strategies, and academic stress. Using self-reported tools like the GHQ-12 may introduce biases, including social desirability and recall bias, which could impact the accuracy of the results. Furthermore, the study was conducted at a single institution, limiting the generalizability of findings to medical schools with varying cultural and academic contexts. Additionally, crucial factors such as sleep quality, extracurricular involvement, and family dynamics were not assessed. Sampling bias is another limitation, as students experiencing significant distress might have opted not to participate. Cultural norms and mental health stigma may also have influenced participants’ willingness to report sensitive behaviors, such as substance use. These limitations underscore the need for future multi-institutional, longitudinal research incorporating qualitative approaches to provide a more nuanced understanding of medical students’ mental health issues.

Conclusions

In conclusion, our study highlights the high prevalence of psychological distress among Moroccan medical students, with significant associations observed with factors such as gender and dysfunctional coping strategies. These results emphasize the need to integrate mental health support systems, resilience training, and awareness programs into Moroccan medical education. The results of this study can be used for screening and guiding university counseling services in Morocco, enabling tailored interventions to support students’ mental health. Implementing structured psychological support, promoting adaptive coping strategies, and reducing stigma around seeking mental health care can contribute to improving students’ well-being. Future longitudinal research is needed to assess the long-term effects of these interventions and further refine strategies to address mental health challenges in this population.
