Integrating morphology, nuclear SSR, and chloroplast genomes to investigate hybrid origin and species delimitation in the Lycium barbarum L. and its varieties in China
Authors: Lei Zhang, Jinju Zhao, Chaopan Zhang, Xiulian Liao, Guoqi Zheng, Yuqing Wei
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The genus Lycium L. has a considerable economic and ecological value, yet species delimitation has long posed taxonomic challenges. The two varieties, L. barbarum var. auranticarpum K. F. Ching and L. barbarum var. implicatum T. Y. Chen & Xu L. Jiang, exhibit intermediate or overlapping morphological characteristics and are therefore speculated to be of hybrid origin involving L. barbarum var. barbarum L. and L. ruthenicum Murray. To test this hypothesis, we assessed their phylogenetic relationships using morphological traits, nuclear simple sequence repeat (SSR) markers, and chloroplast genomic data. Morphological examination confirmed that the individuals of both varieties exhibited intermediate traits between L. barbarum var. barbarum and L. ruthenicum or overlapping L. barbarum var. barbarum. Analysis of nuclear SSR data further indicated that all assessed individuals possessed a mixed genetic composition derived from the two putative parental species, supporting their hybrid status. Chloroplast phylogeny reconstructed using both the maximum likelihood (ML) and Bayesian inference (BI) methods yielded highly consistent topologies. These analyses strongly support (BS = 100%; PP = 1) a monophyletic clade containing L. barbarum var. auranticarpum, L. barbarum var. implicatum, and L. ruthenicum. Collectively, evidence from morphology, SSR markers, and chloroplast genome data indicate that L. barbarum var. auranticarpum and L. barbarum var. implicatum were hybrids derived from crosses between L. ruthenicum and L. barbarum var. barbarum. This study highlighted the value of integrating multiple data types to resolve complex taxonomic issues and provides a practical framework for future species delimitation studies. It also offers molecular tools and genetic insights that may facilitate the breeding of improved Lycium varieties with desirable agronomic traits.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12862-026-02533-9.

Background

Accurate delimitation and description of species are fundamental to biodiversity conservation and are critical for research in taxonomy and conservation biology [1–3]. Clear species boundaries allow communication across disciplines, better assessment of biodiversity, and reduced economic costs in conservation planning [4, 5]. However, species delimitation remains a topic of great interest and is actively debated [6]. Several criteria have been proposed for species delimitation based on phenotypic variation and frequent hybridization [7, 8]. Despite the hybridization of plants being common in nature [9–11]. However, a few published species may be established at the species level hybrids rather than independently evolving lineages and statistical morphological clustering [12–14]. Hybrid morphological variations may lead to incorrect species delimitation. However, systematic statistical analysis usually shows that parental individuals usually form tight, coherent morphological clusters, whereas hybrids fall between them [3, 15, 16]. In addition to traditional morphological data, considerable efforts have been directed toward using simple sequence repeat (SSR) markers and chloroplast genomes (CPGs) to delineate species [17–21]. These genetic tools are consistent with the biological species concept (reproductive isolation) and help detect interspecific hybridization [22, 23]. Moreover, a comprehensive approach, based on the consensus that different species represent different evolving lineages, morphological statistics, and population genotyping, has been proposed as the method of choice for detecting putative species and inferring their boundaries [24–27].

Species of the genus Lycium L. (Solanaceae) are widely distributed worldwide [28, 29]. The number of recognized Lycium species in China ranges from seven to 12, with numerous additional hybrids, varieties, and cultivars also documented [13, 14, 30–32]. Due to high levels of morphological variation and extensive interspecific hybridization, controversy remains over the species delimitation of Lycium [33, 34]. Several described species are suspected to be hybrids of other independently evolving lineages (good species) or merely reflect intraspecific variation within polymorphic taxa [13, 20, 30]. Despite their taxonomic ambiguity, these species have not been thoroughly investigated. The L. barbarum includes L. barbarum var. barbarum L., L. barbarum var. auranticarpum K. F. Ching [13], and L. barbarum var. implicatum T. Y. Chen and X. L. Jiang [20]. The latter two varieties are found in Ningxia, and exhibit leaf and fruit morphologies that are intermediate or blended relative to L. barbarum var. barbarum and L. ruthenicum Murray (Fig. 1).

To clarify the relationships between these taxa and assess the taxonomic status of L. barbarum var. auranticarpum and L. barbarum var. implicatum, we conducted a comprehensive investigation using the nSSRs, CPGs, and morphological data. The current study aimed to address the following questions: (1) Based on integrated evidence, are L. barbarum var. auranticarpum and L. barbarum var. implicatum distinct from L. barbarum var. barbarum, or do they represent hybrids of L. barbarum var. barbarum and L. ruthenicum, respectively? (2) How is the parental genetic composition in the resulting hybrids.

Results

Morphological data analysis and clustering

We conducted normality tests on 14 measured morphological traits of four taxa (Table S1), and only a small portion followed a normal distribution, while the majority did not (Fig. S1). We further performed homogeneity of variances tests on all traits. For traits where variances were homogeneous (p > 0.05), we conducted standard ANOVA. For traits with heterogeneous variances (p < 0.05), we used the Welch test instead (Table S1). Our results revealed that L. barbarum var. barbarum is distinguished by its large leaf area, lanceolate shape, and long petioles. In contrast, L. barbarum var. auranticarpum, L. barbarum var. implicatum, and L. ruthenicum share a very small leaf area but differ in leaf form: L. barbarum var. auranticarpum has linear leaves with long petioles, L. barbarum var. implicatum has linear leaves with short petioles, and L. ruthenicum has strip-shaped leaves and lacks a petiole. In terms of flower structure, L. barbarum var. barbarum and L. barbarum var. implicatum have short flowers, whereas L. barbarum var. auranticarpum has long flowers. L. barbarum var. barbarum and L. barbarum var. auranticarpum possess corollas with a large radius and prominent lobes, whereas L. barbarum var. implicatum and L. ruthenicum have corollas with a small radius and reduced lobes. The tube length also varied: L. barbarum var. barbarum and L. barbarum var. implicatum have short tubes, L. barbarum var. auranticarpum has a long tube, whereas L. ruthenicum has a long tube despite its short flower length. The angle between the corolla tube was consistently obtuse across all taxa (Table 1, S3). Each taxon exhibited a unique fruit color and shape. L. barbarum var. barbarum has red, oblong fruit; L. barbarum var. auranticarpum has orange-yellow, ovate fruit; L. barbarum var. implicatum has reddish-brown, ovate fruit, and L. ruthenicum has black, globose fruit. In conclusion, leaf architecture, floral dimensions, and fruit appearance serve as diagnostic characteristics for differentiating these taxa. Among the four taxa, L. barbarum var. barbarum and L. barbarum var. auranticarpum share some similarities in floral traits but are clearly distinct in leaf and fruit morphology (Table 1, S3).

Morphological characteristics of four Lycium taxa

Among the 14 morphological traits, only three shared no significant differences (Fig. S2; Table S3). Using Gower distance, we conducted principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) on the 14 traits measured from 38 specimens (Fig. 2). PC1–PC4 were the only PCs that individually represented more than 5% of the variance (PC1 = 37.63%, PC2 = 15.45%, PC3 = 7.42%, PC4 = 6.52%, and PC5 = 5.28%), which together represented 72.3% of the total variance. All remaining PCs accounted for less than 5% of the variance individually. The greatest shape variance was captured by PC1 and PC2 (Fig. 2). Scatterplots of PC1 and PC2 showed clear separation between L. barbarum var. barbarum and L. ruthenicum, whereas most individuals of L. barbarum var. auranticarpum and L. barbarum var. implicatum were positioned between L. barbarum var. barbarum and L. ruthenicum. Scatterplots of PC1 and PC2 revealed a clear separation between L. barbarum var. barbarum and L. ruthenicum, whereas most individuals of L. barbarum var. barbarum and L. barbarum var. implicatum were positioned between these two taxa. The PCoA produced consistent results regardless of whether Euclidean distance, Chord distance, or Manhattan distance was used, and all analyses showed similar relationships (Fig. S3). Notably, the results based on unweighted pair group method with arithmetic mean (UPGMA) and detrended correspondence analysis (DCA) indicate that the L. ruthenicum forms a separate cluster on its own, whereas L. barbarum var. barbarum, L. barbarum var. auranticarpum and L. barbarum var. implicatum form another cluster together (Fig. S4).

Clustering analyses of nuclear SSR data

Structural analysis of the 10 nSSR loci for 67 individuals from the four taxa showed that the data were best explained by two genetic clusters (K = 2), based on both the Pritchard and Evanno methods (Fig. 3A, B). At K = 2, the first cluster was primarily composed of L. barbarum var. barbarum, whereas the second cluster included all individuals of L. ruthenicum (Fig. 3C). The genetic composition of L. barbarum var. auranticarpum and L. barbarum var. implicatum represents a mixture of the two clusters. The genetic composition of L. barbarum var. implicatum contained more from L. barbarum var. barbarum than from L. ruthenicum. In contrast, L. barbarum var. auranticarpum was dominated by L. ruthenicum, with a minor contribution from L. barbarum var. barbarum. The Principal Coordinates Analysis (PCoA) of the SSR data showed that L. barbarum var. barbarum and L. ruthenicum formed distinct clusters, whereas all sampled individuals of L. barbarum var. auranticarpum and L. barbarum var. implicatum were positioned between them, indicating mixed origin (Fig. 2D).

Chloroplast genome structure and comparative analysis

The 24 chloroplast genomes from the four Lycium taxa exhibited a typical quadripartite structure and were highly conserved in overall size (154,830–155,148 bp) and GC content (37.90%) (Figs. 4, S5A; Table S4). Primary variation was observed in the structures of the Inverted Repeat (IR) regions. The IR of L. barbarum var. barbarum (25,449–25,458 bp) was larger than that of L. barbarum var. auranticarpum, L. barbarum var. implicatum, and L. ruthenicum (25,395–25,396 bp) (Fig. S5B; Table S4). Gene content was identical across all genomes, each harboring 132 genes (86 protein-coding genes, 37 tRNA genes, and 8 rRNA genes) (Fig. S5C; Table S4). Correspondingly, the GC content of IRs was slightly lower in L. barbarum var. barbarum (43.14–43.15%) compared with the other three taxa (43.18–43.19%) (Fig. S5D; Table S4). A minor difference was also noted in the GC content of the Large Single-Copy (LSC) region, which was slightly higher in the latter three taxa. The Small Single-Copy (SSC) region showed no significant interspecific variations. In summary, although the CPGs are structurally and functionally conserved, the size and GC content of the IR regions clearly distinguish L. barbarum var. barbarum from the three other closely related Lycium taxa. Phylogenetic analysis of four Lycium taxa

Lycium schweinfurthii Dammer (PP952499) was designated as the outgroup to infer the phylogenetic relationships among the four Lycium taxa. Our analysis was based on a concatenated dataset of 79 PCGs encompassing 42,960 sites (Table S5). ML and BI analyses yielded largely congruent tree topologies, with most nodes receiving high statistical support (bootstrap support, BS > 90%; posterior probability, PP = 1) (Figs. 5, S6-S9). The 24 sampled individuals, representing the four taxa (ten L. barbarum var. barbarum, seven L. ruthenicum, five L. barbarum var. auranticarpum, and two L. barbarum var. implicatum), were resolved into two distinct clades. Clade 1 was exclusively composed of L. barbarum var. barbarum (BS = 100%; PP = 1). Clade 2 included L. barbarum var. auranticarpum, L. barbarum var. implicatum, and L. ruthenicum, along with strong support (BS = 100%; PP = 1).

Discussion

Hybridization among species is a widespread phenomenon that provides an important driving force for biological evolution [10, 11]. The accurate identification of hybrid offspring has always been a challenge in taxonomy and evolutionary biology [35–37]. Recently, scholars have conducted extensive studies on the origin of hybridization in groups such as Populus L [38]., Rhododendron L [18, 39]., and Lycium [20], thereby providing valuable insights into interspecific gene flow and hybridization fitness [40, 41]. This study integrated morphological analysis, nuclear genomic SSR markers, and chloroplast data to systematically identify the origins of L. barbarum var. auranticarpum and L. barbarum var. implicatum. Our results confirmed the effectiveness and reliability of the integration method in clarifying the origin of Lycium hybridization and providing strong evidence supporting the hybridization events of the genus Lycium.

Morphological analysis is an intuitive and simple method for species delimitation [15, 16, 41, 42]. Based on 11 significantly different morphological traits (including leaf, flower, and fruit characteristics), our analyses revealed both consistencies and interesting discrepancies. Specifically, PCoA (Fig. 2A) positioned L. barbarum var. auranticarpum and L. barbarum var. implicatum as intermediate between L. barbarum var. barbarum and L. ruthenicum, whereas UPGMA and DCA (Fig. S4) grouped these two varieties together with L. barbarum var. barbarum rather than as a distinct intermediate cluster. Overall, hybrid individuals exhibited typical intermediate or overlapping distribution patterns between the supposed parental species (Figs. 2A, S4). Morphologically, L. barbarum var. auranticarpum and L. barbarum var. implicatum have nearly linear leaves and ovate fruits that are orange-yellow or reddish-brown, making their fruit shape somewhat similar to that of L. barbarum var. barbarum. By contrast, L. barbarum var. barbarum has lanceolate leaves and red fruits, while L. ruthenicum possesses strip-shaped leaves and black globose fruits (Table 1). This combination of continuity and discontinuity in phenotypic traits aligns well with classic expectations for hybrid taxa and provides clues for understanding their hybrid origin [3, 15, 16, 20]. The discrepancies among the results obtained from PCoA, UPGMA, and DCA are likely to be caused by frequent natural hybridization and gene exchange, which tend to blur species boundaries. Consequently, ordination methods such as PCoA, which capture continuous variation along major axes, tend to reveal gradual transitions among taxa. In contrast, clustering methods (UPGMA) and correspondence analysis (DCA) emphasize overall similarity and thus place hybrid‑derived varieties closer to their dominant parent (L. barbarum var. barbarum). The complementarity of these analytical approaches provides a more comprehensive understanding of the complex morphological relationships. Nevertheless, morphological characteristics are susceptible to environmental factors, which may interfere with phenotypic plasticity. Furthermore, our geographic sampling is restricted to Ningxia Province of China, yet both putative parent species have much broader distributions. A broader sampling would be more representative and help clarify the hybrid origins more conclusively.

SSR markers are powerful tools for nuclear genomes and provide strong genetic evidence for verifying hybridization. Through population genetic structure analysis, this study clearly reveals that genetic composition ratios of L. barbarum var. auranticarpum mostly originates from L. barbarum var. barbarum, while a small part comes from L. ruthenicum; the situation for L. barbarum var. implicatum is the opposite. This quantitative analysis not only confirms the existence of hybridization events but also infers the specific contribution of parental lineages, providing in-depth insights into hybridization history. Chloroplast genomic data, owing to their maternal genetic characteristics, provide unique value in determining the direction of hybridization [19, 21, 43, 44]. In the present study, all hybrid individuals (L. barbarum var. auranticarpum and L. barbarum var. implicatum) formed highly supported monophyletic populations with L. ruthenicum (Figs. 5, S6-S9), indicating that L. ruthenicum was the maternal donor for the hybridization event.

In conclusion, our findings indicate that L. barbarum var. auranticarpum and L. barbarum var. implicatum likely originated from the hybridization between L. barbarum var. barbarum and L. ruthenicum. This study highlights the importance of integrating multiple lines of evidence, including morphology, SSR molecular markers, and phylogenetic analyses, to resolve complex taxonomic issues. Our study offers a valuable framework for future species delimitation studies and provides essential molecular tools and genetic insights for breeding superior Lycium varieties with improved agronomic traits. Future research should aim to precisely delineate gene flow among these four taxonomic groups and elucidate the underlying genomic mechanisms driving their morphological variations. In particular, further investigations using whole-genome data, population genomics, and coalescent-based approaches are needed to distinguish between hybrid origin, chloroplast capture, and ancient introgression, thereby clarifying the evolutionary processes underlying the observed phylogenetic patterns.

Materials and methods

Field survey and sample collection

Before conducting field investigations, we systematically examined specimens of the four target taxa from major herbaria (PE!, KUN!, WUK!, and IBSC!), along with digital images of type specimens accessed through the Chinese Virtual Herbarium (http://www.cvh.ac.cn/). Additionally, the type specimens of L. barbarum var. auranticarpum and L. barbarum var. implicatum were preserved in the PE herbarium (Table S6). Samples were collected from the field in accordance with both the specimen records and all applicable national/international legislation and institutional guidelines. To ensure accurate identification, we verified key morphological traits of the leaves, flowers, and fruits during collection. Healthy leaves were collected from mature plants spaced at least 50 m apart during the field sampling of all accessible populations. The leaves were promptly dried on silica gel for subsequent DNA extraction. Simultaneously, branches with flowers and fruits were collected to prepare voucher specimens, which were identified by Dr. Lei Zhang (zhangsanshi-0319@163.com) and deposited in the Herbarium of North Minzu University (NMU). We recorded the latitude, longitude, and elevation of each population sampled. In total, our study included 67 individuals and 38 specimens from 10 representative populations across four taxa (Fig. S10; Table 2, S7), which were all used for subsequent population genetics and morphological analyses.

Morphological analyses

To obtain comprehensive information on the specimens assigned to the four taxa, 38 specimens from different individuals were analyzed (Table S7). We measured 14 morphological traits related to leaves, flowers, and fruits, focusing on traits that previous taxonomic studies have used to distinguish species [12, 13, 20, 30, 45]. An average of two or three mature leaves, flowers, and fruits was chosen to conduct measurements for each of the 38 specimens using ImageJ version v1.53t [46]. Final morphometric analyses were conducted on four leaf traits (petiole length, leaf length, leaf width, and leaf area), five flower traits (flower length, corolla radius, tube length, corolla lobe length, and the angle between the corolla and tube), and three fruit traits (color, fruit length, and fruit width). Prior to parametric analyses, the normality of each quantitative trait within each taxon was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test (α = 0.05). Most traits conformed to a normal distribution after log-transformation (see Supplementary Table S3 for details). Homogeneity of variances among taxa for each quantitative trait was then examined using Levene’s test. When variances were homogeneous (P > 0.05), one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed; otherwise, the Welch test was applied. The variability in each quantitative trait is shown in violin plots generated using R software [47]. ANOVA and Welch test was performed for each quantitative trait using SPSS version 19 [48]. Subsequently, Exclude the traits that do not show significant differences, A phenogram was constructed using the UPGMA based on a Gower distance matrix. PCoA was performed using Euclidean, Chord, Manhattan, and Gower distance matrices separately, DCA was performed on the same raw data matrix. All analyses were performed using the R package ggrepel version 0.9.5 (https://ggrepel.slowkow.com) and visualized using the ggplot2 package [49].

Genetic analyses

Total genomic DNA was extracted from the dried leaves using the hexadecyltrimethylammonium bromide (CTAB) method [50]. We used 10 SSR primers (Table S8 [51, 52]) to genotype the samples. Polymerase chain reactions (PCRs) were performed using a 25-µL reaction mix comprising 2.5 µL of 10× Taq buffer, 0.5 mM of each dNTP, 50–100 ng diluted genomic DNA, 0.5 µL of each primer, and 0.5 units of Taq polymerase (Vazyme Biotech, Nanjing, China). The cycling conditions were as follows: a single cycle at 95 °C for 5 min, followed by 36 cycles of 95 °C for 45 s, 55 °C for 40 s, and 72 °C for 80 s, and a final extension cycle at 72 °C for 10 min. The amplified products were analyzed using an ABI 3830xl DNA analyzer (Applied Biosystems Inc., Foster City, CA, USA) at Nuohe Biological Technology (Tianjin, China).

We analyzed the genetic composition and structure of the sampled populations using STRUCTURE v2.3.4 [53] with Bayesian hybrid mixture computation. Ten replicates were performed for each genetic cluster with K values ranging from 1 to 10 [54]. All the runs included 1,000,000 Markov chain Monte Carlo repetitions after a burn-in period of 500,000 iterations. We determined the most likely number of clusters using a combination of the Evanno [55] and Pritchard [56] methods in the STRUCTURE HARVESTER Python program [57]. For the nSSR data, we conducted PCoA using the ape package in R [58].

Chloroplast genome assembly and annotation

Library construction and sequencing were performed according to the standard protocol for the NEBNext DNA Library Kit (New England Biolabs, Ipswich, MA, USA). Genomic DNA was randomly fragmented into sequences that were 350-bp long on average, and the resulting libraries were sequenced on an NovaSeq 6000 platform (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA), yielding at least 2 Gb of 2 × 150 bp paired-end data per sample. After filtering out low-quality reads (average quality score < 7 or > 10% ambiguous nucleotides), the high-quality data were assembled de novo using NOVOPlasty v2.7.2 [59]. The resulting contigs were mapped to the reference CPG of L. chinense Miller (PV013647) in Geneious v9.1.8 [60] for sequence completion. The assembled CPGs were annotated using Plann v1.1 [61]. Circular graphical maps of the CPGs were generated with OGDRAW v1.2 [62], and all annotated sequences were deposited into GenBank [63].

Phylogenetic analysis

Two independently constructed datasets underwent phylogenetic analyses: one comprising protein-coding genes (PCGs) and the other consisting of whole plastomes (WP). The PCG dataset was derived from 25 CPGs. Using customized Perl scripts, PCGs were extracted by removing the start and stop codons. Following the exclusion of suspected pseudogenes, 79 PCGs universally present across all taxa were retained. Individual gene alignments were generated using PRANK version 130,410 software [64] and translated into amino acid sequences. Any genes missing entirely from any taxon were excluded, and the final aligned sequences were concatenated into a supermatrix. The maximum likelihood (ML) and Bayesian inference (BI) methods were applied to each dataset. The ML analysis, implemented in RAxML v8.1.24 [65], employed the GTR + Γ model and assessed branch support with 1,000 bootstrap replicates. The BI analysis was executed in MrBayes v3.2.6 software [66], and the jModelTest function determined GTR + I + G as the optimal substitution model. For BI, two independent Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) runs were conducted, each with four chains (one cold and three heated). The runs lasted for 10 million generations, with sampling every 1,000 generations. The first 25% of sampled trees were discarded as burn-in. Convergence was evaluated by checking that the average standard deviation of split frequencies fell below 0.01 and by ensuring that the potential scale reduction factor (PSRF) approached 1.0 for all parameters. Additionally, effective sample sizes (ESS) were confirmed to exceed 200 for all estimated parameters using Tracer v1.7 [67]. All the resulting phylogenetic trees were visualized using FigTree v1.4.2 software [68].
