Persistent use of broad-spectrum antibiotics for suspected community acquired urinary tract infections in children in Italian primary care settings
Authors: Emelyne Gres, Piero Veronese, Giulia Brigadoi, Maria Vego Scocco, Giangiacomo Nicolini, Daniele Dona’, Elisa Barbieri
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundUrinary tract infections (UTIs) are common in children, and diagnosis is often difficult due to nonspecific symptoms, urine collection challenges, and increasing antimicrobial resistance. The emergence of ESBL-producing organisms highlights the need for locally informed guidelines supported by antibiotic stewardship activities. This study aimed to describe antibiotic prescribing patterns for suspected community-acquired UTIs (CA-UTIs) in the Italian primary care setting among children aged 0–14 years and to assess the impact of the 2019 Italian Society of Paediatric Nephrology (SINePe) guidelines update on prescribing behaviour in children aged 2 months to 3 years.MethodsWe conducted a database analysis including Italian children aged 0–14 years between 2015 and 2024 using data from the Pedianet database, which contains anonymized electronic health records of over 275,000 children. CA-UTI episodes were identified through ICD-9-CM codes and free-text searches, then manually validated. All antibiotic prescriptions (classified by ATC codes and AWaRe classification) issued during each episode and associated to the CA-UTI were included. An interrupted time-series (ITS) analysis using a Poisson model was conducted to evaluate the impact of the 2019 national consensus guidelines on yearly antibiotic prescribing patterns in children aged 2 months to 3 years with a first episode of CA-UTI.ResultsFrom 2015 to 2024, 32,519 suspected CA-UTI episodes in 26,650 children were included. The majority of children experienced a single episode (79.9%). Co-amoxiclav was the most frequently prescribed antibiotic, followed by third-generation cephalosporins, which remained common across the study period. Fosfomycin use increased with age and was more frequent among older children. Over 2550 first CA-UTI episodes were included in the ITS analysis showing no significant changes in prescribing patterns following the 2019 guideline update. Similar trends were confirmed when restricting the analysis to empiric antibiotic treatments.ConclusionCo-amoxiclav remains the first-line empiric therapy for paediatric CA-UTIs in Italian primary care, although third-generation cephalosporins are still frequently prescribed despite revised recommendations. These findings highlight the need for continued antimicrobial stewardship efforts and monitoring to optimize paediatric UTI management in the community setting.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13052-026-02260-3.

Introduction

Febrile urinary tract infection (UTI) is one of the most common causes of fever in infants and children [1]. Escherichia coli is the predominant uropathogen, responsible for approximately 80–90% of paediatric cases, followed by Klebsiella spp., Proteus mirabilis, Enterococcus spp., and Enterobacter spp.[1, 2, 3].

Although the diagnosis and management of paediatric UTIs may appear straightforward, several controversial and complex aspects can complicate both diagnostic accuracy and clinical decision-making. Especially in infants, the clinical presentation is highly heterogeneous, ranging from nonspecific symptoms such as fever and irritability to more localized urinary signs, making the assessment particularly challenging. Furthermore, obtaining reliable urine samples in young children represents an additional obstacle in achieving an accurate diagnosis [4, 5, 6].

Prompt initiation of antibiotic therapy is essential to improve clinical outcomes, eradicate infection, prevent bacteraemia, particularly in the first months of life, and reduce the risk of bacterial dissemination to other body sites [7, 8]. However, given the growing concern of antimicrobial resistance, antibiotic selection should be guided by local antimicrobial susceptibility patterns, in line with national and regional guidelines [3, 9, 10].

Antimicrobial resistance in UTIs, particularly when caused by multidrug-resistant E. coli, represents a growing public health concern and has contributed to increased use of broad-spectrum antibiotics [9, 10]. In Italy, resistance to key classes such as third-generation cephalosporins and fluoroquinolones remains substantial, with 2024 data showing cephalosporin resistance reaching 27.7% overall and up to 26.6% in older children. Fluoroquinolone resistance among paediatric isolates also remains high, ranging from 19 to 23% [11].

In response to these challenges, the Italian recommendations for the diagnosis and management of paediatric febrile CA-UTIs in were revised in 2019, following a comprehensive review of the literature published between 2012 and October 2018 [12]. These guidelines, endorsed by the Italian Society of Paediatric Nephrology (SINePe) and the Italian Society for Paediatric Infectious Diseases (SITIP), introduced several major updates. Key changes included updated recommendations for antimicrobial therapy, advocating prompt initiation of treatment in febrile children with a positive urine dipstick or microscopy result, as well as guidance on specific antibiotic choices. For CA-UTIs, oral therapy is preferred, with co-amoxiclav recommended as the first-line treatment. To address the increasing resistance of E. coli to third-generation cephalosporins, largely driven by their widespread and often inappropriate use, this class of antibiotics should be reserved as a second-line option. In this context, cefixime or ceftibuten may be considered for oral therapy, while intravenous cefotaxime or ceftriaxone should be limited to selected cases of severe infection, given their higher efficacy and rapid onset of action. For children with confirmed beta-lactam allergy, aminoglycosides such as amikacin or gentamicin are recommended as the preferred intravenous alternatives. The guidelines also redefined urine collection and culture interpretation methods and revised the algorithm guiding clinical decisions regarding voiding cystourethrography.

The aim of this study is to describe antibiotic prescribing patterns for suspected CA-UTIs in the Italian primary care setting among children aged 0–14 years, and to evaluate the impact of the 2019 updated Italian guidelines on prescribing practices in children aged 2 months to 3 years.

Materials and methods

Data sources

Data for this study were obtained from the Pedianet database, a national population-based resource that includes anonymized patient-level information for more than 500,000 children since 2004 [13]. This cohort represents approximately 4% of the annual Italian paediatric population. Pedianet integrates data from family paediatricians (FPs) distributed across several Italian regions designated by the National Health Service (NHS), including Friuli Venezia Giulia, Liguria, Lombardia, Piemonte, Veneto, Lazio, Marche, Toscana, Abruzzo, Campania, Sardegna, and Sicilia. All participating FPs use the same electronic health record software, Junior Bit® (Padova, Italy), in their daily clinical practice. For the present study, data from 193 FPs who actively contributed to the database between 1 January 2015 and 31 December 2024 were included. Data extraction was performed on 23 February 2025. Family paediatricians participating in active intervention studies designed to improve antibiotic prescribing behaviour through a clinical decision support tool were excluded to minimize bias, as their prescribing patterns may have been influenced by targeted training. In the Italian NHS framework, each child is assigned to a FP who serves as the primary point of contact for all healthcare needs. Italy’s tax-funded universal healthcare system ensures that families do not incur direct costs for primary care visits. The Pedianet database captures a wide range of patient-level information, including reasons for healthcare encounters, demographic characteristics, health status, diagnoses and clinical symptoms (recorded as free text or ICD-9-CM codes), prescribed drugs (classified by Anatomical Therapeutic Chemical [ATC] codes), specialist consultations, diagnostic procedures, hospital or emergency department admissions, growth parameters, and clinical outcomes. All data are anonymized and updated monthly in a centralized repository managed by Società Servizi Telematici srl, the legal owner of Pedianet, located in Padova, Italy. Informed consent is obtained from parents or guardians for the inclusion of children’s data in the database and for linkage with external datasets—such as vaccine registries or hospitalization databases—using unique patient identifiers. Data quality is ensured through manual validation for study-specific variables, and diagnostic accuracy is routinely verified.

Study design and population

This is a database analysis including data from January 2015 to December 2024. National guidelines consensus for the diagnosis and treatment of paediatric CA-UTI were published in October 2019, thus the study period was divided in pre-consensus publication (January 2015 and December 2019) and post-consensus publication (January 2020 and December 2024) [12]. All children aged 0 to 13 years enrolled in the Pedianet database during the study period were eligible for inclusion. Children aged over 14 years or children with less than 14 days of follow-up from their first prescription date were excluded.

Suspected community-acquired urinary tract infection (CA-UTI) episode definition

The suspected CA-UTI diagnosis is defined as any reported medical diagnosis of urinary tract infection identified with International Classification of Diseases, 9th Revision, Clinical Modification codes (ICD-9-CM 519.11, 786.07), or free text in medical charts, retrieved by a string (containing Italian terms “urin”, “ivu”, cistit”, “pielonefr”) and validated by an investigator with a clinical background. For reporting purposes, we considered all diagnoses retrieved, including UTI, cystitis, pyelonephritis, or as suspected CA-UTI, because in most cases the diagnosis is only suspected and not microbiologically confirmed. All consecutive CA-UTI visits for the same patient were grouped to form an episode, and a gap of 14 days from the last visit was required to identify a new episode. Only episodes with no concurrent diagnosis of an infectious disease requiring antibiotic treatment (i.e. in the previous 14 days and during the episode) were considered. Further, episodes with an antibiotic prescribed in the previous 30 days and episodes with an hospitalization or ER visit in the previous 14 days were excluded.

Outcomes

All antibiotic prescriptions issued during a CA-UTI episode (as previously defined) were considered part of the treatment for that episode. Antibiotics were classified according to the Anatomical Therapeutic Chemical (ATC) system as follows: amoxicillin (J01CA04), co-amoxiclav (J01CR02), third-generation cephalosporins (J01DDx), second-generation cephalosporins (J01DCx), fosfomycin (J01XX01), and others (J01*) [14]. When multiple prescriptions with the same 7-digit ATC code were recorded during the same visit for the same diagnosis, only one was included in the analysis. Antibiotics were also grouped according to the WHO AWaRe classification, which promotes appropriate antibiotic use by prioritizing agents with lower resistance potential (Access), restricting those with higher resistance risk (Watch), and reserving critical antibiotics (Reserve) for infections caused by multidrug-resistant pathogens [15]. An antibiotic treatment switch was defined according to previously published methodologies [16, 17]: [1] treatment prolongation was defined as a subsequent prescription with the same 7-digit ATC code; and [2] switch was defined as a subsequent prescription belonging to a different antibiotic class. Switches were further categorized based on timing as early (1–3 days after the first prescription), late (4–14 days), or very late (15–21 days). The day of switching was calculated as the number of days between the first and second prescriptions within the same treatment episode.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize demographic characteristics and antibiotic prescribing patterns overall, as well as by calendar year and age group (< 2 months, 2 months–3 years, 4–6 years, 7–11 years, and 12–14 years). Antibiotic class prevalence and Access/Watch antibiotic prevalence were illustrated by year and age group by calculating the number of antibiotics prescribed divided by the number of episodes with an antibiotic prescription.

We evaluated the impact of the 2019 consensus guidelines on antibiotic prescriptions issued by family paediatricians actively participating in the study from 2015 to 2024. This subgroup analysis included children who met al.l of the following criteria: [1] aged between 2 months and 3 years at the time of their first suspected CA-UTI episode; [2] continuously followed since birth, defined as having a follow-up start date within ± 1 month of the 15th day of their birth month; and [3] presenting with a first documented CA-UTI episode in the primary care setting, without any previous emergency room visits or hospitalizations for CA-UTI. Interrupted time series analyses with an immediate step change and a change in trend over time, were evaluated using Poisson regression models. The models included: the total number of prescriptions for the antibiotic classes of interest (co-amoxiclav, third-generation cephalosporins, and Access-class antibiotics), the log-transformed total number of antibiotic prescriptions, a dummy variable for the pre- versus post-publication periods, a variable representing time in years since the publication, and a variable representing time in years since the start of the analysis. Autocorrelation was assessed through residual plots and the partial autocorrelation function. Relative risks with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (95% CI) were estimated using normal approximation.

Two-sided p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. The analysis was performed using R statistical software—v. 4.0.3 (Vienna, Austria) [18].

Sensitivity analysis

A sensitivity analysis was performed including only empiric treatments, defined as those prescribed on the same day of the first suspected CA-UTI consultation or the prescription for urine culture.

Results

Between 2015 and 2024, a total of 275,789 children under 15 years of age were included and followed in the Pedianet database. We identified 50,334 UTI events among 33,072 children, representing 46,213 episodes of suspected CA-UTI. After applying the exclusion criteria (i.e., episodes with antibiotic prescriptions in the previous 30 days, concomitant bacterial infections within 14 days before or during the episode, hospitalization or emergency visits, insufficient follow-up, or urine dipstick analysis without a corresponding diagnosis or urine culture), 32,519 CA-UTI episodes in 25,650 children were retained for analysis (Fig. 1).

Figure 2 illustrates temporal trends in antibiotic prescriptions for suspected CA-UTI among children from 2015 to 2024, stratified by age group, and categorized by antibiotic class and WHO AWaRe classification. Across most age groups, co-amoxiclav remained the most frequently prescribed antibiotic, accounting for more than 40% of prescriptions in all age classes (supplementary file 1), followed by fosfomycin accounting more than 30% of prescriptions before 2020 in those aged > 3 years and third-generation cephalosporins increasingly used after 2020 from around 20% to around 30%, particularly among children aged 4–13 years. In 2019, fosfomycin use increased with age from 4.9% for children aged from 2 months to 3 years to 29.3% for those aged from 7 to 11 years (Supplementary file 1). Amoxicillin alone was prescribed in around 10% of cases in children under 3 years, while prescribing patterns in infants under 2 months were irregular, likely reflecting the small sample size and cautious clinical management. According to the WHO AWaRe classification, Access antibiotics were prescribed more frequently than Watch antibiotics among children under 3 years of age (73.1% vs. 37.2% of prescriptions in children aged 2 months to 3 years; Supplementary File 1). However, fluctuations between Access and Watch use were notable among children older than 4 years, with a decreasing trend in Watch prescriptions followed by an increase in Access prescriptions from 2019 to 2020, after the reduction in fosfomycin prescriptions, which is classified as Watch. In children aged 12–13 years, approximately two-thirds of antibiotic prescriptions were from the Watch category, mostly fosfomycin and third-generation cephalosporins (64.3%, Supplementary file 1).

Multiple prescriptions at the first consultation were uncommon (1.2%), while treatment prolongations and switches occurred in 4.3% and 4.1% of episodes, respectively (Supplementary file 2). Among switched treatments, the mean interval between prescriptions was 7 days (SD 5), with early (1–3 days), late (4–14 days), and very late (> 14 days) switches accounting for 33.4%, 60.7%, and 5.9%, respectively. Figure 3 illustrates antibiotic class switches, showing that most transitions occurred from co-amoxiclav to third-generation cephalosporins, reflecting a tendency toward broader-spectrum therapy during ongoing CA-UTI episodes.

The interrupted time series analysis evaluates temporal trends in third-generation cephalosporin, co-amoxiclav, and Access antibiotic prescriptions for suspected CA-UTI in relation to the 2019 update of national guidelines. The analysis was restricted to 2,550 first CA-UTI episodes treated with antibiotics in children aged 2 months to 3 years who had continuous follow-up since birth. Before 2019, third-generation cephalosporin prescription rates varied from 23.4% to 17.8%. Following the guideline update, an immediate but non-significant increase of approximately 35% was observed (p = 0.075), with no change in the trend afterwards. Co-amoxiclav prescription rates did not vary after the consensus update, even though before 2019, a non-significant increase from 49.2% to 58.1% was noted. Similarly, Access antibiotic prescription rates did not increase after 2019 (Fig. 4).

Sensitivity analysis

Considering only antibiotics prescribed on the day of the consultation or microbiological test request, defined as empiric treatment, we observed similar overall prescription patterns and including trends following the 2019 update of national consensus guidelines (Supplementary files 3–6). However, for children aged 12–14 years, the co-amoxiclav peak observed in 2023 disappears when specifically examining empiric treatment. This difference likely reflects the fact that the original peak in 2023 may have been driven by antibiotics prescribed after test results were available, rather than at the time of consultation. By restricting the analysis to empiric prescriptions, which only include antibiotics given on the day of consultation or test request, delayed prescriptions are excluded.

Discussion

In this large, population-based study conducted in Pedianet between 2015 and 2024, we analysed 32,519 episodes of suspected CA-UTI in 25,650 Italian children. Co-amoxiclav emerged as the most frequently prescribed antibiotic, followed by third-generation cephalosporins, which maintained a consistent use throughout the study period. Fosfomycin prescriptions increased with age, particularly among older children before 2020. The interrupted time-series analysis revealed no statistically significant changes in antibiotic prescribing patterns among younger children following the 2019 update of national consensus guidelines, with similar trends observed when the analysis was restricted to empiric treatments.

Overall, 31% of suspected CA-UTI episodes were treated with antibiotics, while 69% were not prescribed antibiotics. This likely reflects cases of suspected but unconfirmed infections, as our definition included both clinically suspected and microbiologically unconfirmed episodes. Many of these untreated cases probably involved children with mild or nonspecific symptoms, for whom paediatricians adopted a “wait-and-see” approach while awaiting urine test results. Among infants less than 2 months, only 16.3% of episodes were prescribed antibiotics, likely due to diagnostic challenges in this age group and the common referral of suspected cases to hospital care for parenteral management rather than treatment in primary care [5].

When antibiotics were prescribed, co-amoxiclav was the most frequently used treatment for suspected CA-UTI across all paediatric age groups in primary care, and its use has generally increased over time despite some fluctuations. These findings align with international guidelines, including those from the American Academy of Paediatrics, the European Society for Paediatric Urology, and the Italian Society of Paediatric Nephrology (SINePe), which recommend oral Access antibiotics such as co-amoxiclav as first-line therapy for uncomplicated CA-UTIs, reserving third-generation cephalosporins for complicated cases or treatment failure [7, 12, 19, 20]. Third-generation cephalosporins ranked second in use since 2020 and remain widely prescribed in primary care, raising concerns about antibiotic resistance [3]. The 2025 WHO GLASS report highlights that inappropriate use of broad-spectrum antibiotics is a key driver of emerging resistance [21]. Fosfomycin prescriptions, previously common in children aged 4–14 years between 2016 and 2019, have declined across all age groups following the 2020 EMA recommendation removing its paediatric indication due to insufficient evidence of benefit [22]. Nonetheless, some family paediatricians may continue to prescribe fosfomycin because of its single-dose oral formulation, ease of use, and role as an alternative to co-amoxiclav and second-generation cephalosporins.

Treatment switches occurred in a small proportion of episodes (4.1%). Early switches may reflect practical or non-clinical factors, such as issues related to tolerability, adherence, or temporary unavailability of the prescribed antibiotic [23]. However, these determinants could not be directly assessed in our dataset. In contrast, late switches (60.7%) occurred more frequently several days after treatment initiation and may plausibly be associated with clinical reassessment, lack of improvement, or the availability of microbiological results prompting therapy adjustment [24]. The Sankey diagram illustrates the main transitions, showing a trend toward broader-spectrum therapy, particularly increased use of third-generation cephalosporins, with potential implications for antimicrobial resistance.

Through the analysis of prescribing practices, our study shows a general shift toward greater use of Access antibiotics for the treatment of suspected CA-UTIs in children older than 4 years. This trend aligns with WHO recommendations promoting rational and targeted antibiotic use [15]. Among children aged 4–14 years, the proportion of Access antibiotics increased from 40% in 2015 to 60% in 2024, while the use of Watch antibiotics declined over the same period. These findings likely reflect not only changes in clinical prescribing behaviour but also the influence of updated treatment guidelines and educational programs promoting rational antibiotic use. Another contributing factor may be the 2020 EMA restriction on oral fosfomycin for paediatric use; as a Watch antibiotic, its removal could have shifted prescriptions toward Access antibiotics [22]. In the oldest age group (12–14 years), Watch antibiotics remained more prevalent, although this subgroup had a smaller sample size compared to younger children.

Our analysis of the impact of the 2019 update of the Italian guidelines showed no significant changes in prescribing patterns for third-generation cephalosporins, co-amoxiclav, or Access antibiotics for paediatric suspected CA-UTIs. Although the updated recommendations positioned co-amoxiclav as the first-line therapy for uncomplicated CA-UTIs, we did not observe a clear increase in its use, and prescriptions of third-generation cephalosporins slightly increased, albeit without statistical significance. This finding indicates that third-generation cephalosporins continue to be frequently prescribed in Italy for first episodes of community-acquired urinary tract infections (CA-UTIs), despite updated recommendations. In contrast, their use is more restricted in other European countries, suggesting that prescribing behaviour is driven more by local habits and individual paediatricians’ practices than by national guidelines [25]. The sensitivity analysis, restricted to empiric treatments defined as antibiotics prescribed on the day of consultation or microbiological testing, revealed similar overall patterns and trends after 2019. Notably, among first-episode CA-UTIs in children aged 2 months to 3 years, only about 75% received empiric antibiotics, lower than guideline recommendations, which advocate prompt empirical therapy for suspected infections. These findings underscore that guideline publication alone may be insufficient to change clinical practice. Consistent with previous research in paediatric infectious diseases, educational interventions and active implementation strategies are critical to improving guideline adherence. For example, Barbieri et al. demonstrated that centres with structured educational programs had significantly higher adherence to evidence-based pathways for upper respiratory tract infections, including acute otitis media and pharyngitis [26, 27]. Similarly, updated bronchiolitis guidelines in 2014–2015 had minimal impact on antibiotic overprescription in primary care, highlighting the persistent reliance on non-evidence-based treatments despite guideline dissemination [28].

Our study has several limitations. First, its retrospective design and the heterogeneity in how family paediatricians recorded data in electronic medical charts may have introduced reporting bias. In most of the cases, no information regarding nitrates and leucocyte esterases was recorded at the episode index date, as we are able to identify cases only based on the diagnosis and clinical symptoms reported. Further, we are not able to discern between a really strong clinical suspicion for UTI or a suspected case based on very general signs and symptoms. This might be one of the reasons why we found an amoxicillin prescription associated with the suspected CA-UTI episodes. Second, we were not able to discern between febrile and non-febrile CA-UTI, which was the target of the 2019 consensus update. Still, we expect no variation in the epidemiology of febrile and non-febrile CA-UTI in children aged 2 months − 3 years of age over time, confirming the generalizability of our results. Third, although we analysed prescriptions issued by family paediatricians, we cannot ascertain whether all prescribed antibiotics were actually administered. Some paediatricians may have advised a “wait-and-see” approach, recommending treatment only if symptoms persisted or laboratory results confirmed infection, even if not recommended by guidelines. This is further supported by the fact that around 70% of CA-UTI diagnoses were not associated with an antibiotic prescription. Fourth, prescribing behaviours varied among family paediatricians, influenced by local practices, personal knowledge, available diagnostics at the ambulatory level, and turnaround times for results. However, this variability should not be viewed solely as a limitation; rather, it reflects the real-world diversity of outpatient antibiotic management in Italian paediatric primary care, which should be taken into account in guidelines.

Conclusion

This study revealed that co-amoxiclav remains the most commonly prescribed antibiotic for suspected CA-UTIs in Italian children, in line with current recommendations, while third-generation cephalosporins continue to be inappropriately used in some cases. Encouragingly, the use of Access-class antibiotics increased over time, exceeding WHO targets, and empirical treatments were generally adequate. However, the 2019 SINePe guideline update did not significantly influence prescribing behaviours, suggesting that clinical practice is still guided by individual habits rather than evidence-based recommendations. To optimize antibiotic use and mitigate antimicrobial resistance, coordinated antimicrobial stewardship initiatives—such as education, clinical decision support tools, and standardized care pathways—are essential in paediatric primary care.
