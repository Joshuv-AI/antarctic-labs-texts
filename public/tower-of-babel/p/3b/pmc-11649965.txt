KDF1 promotes ameloblast differentiation by inhibiting the IKK/IκB/NF‐κB axis
Authors: Hangbo Liu, Miao Yu, Kai Sun, Jinglei Zheng, Jiayu Wang, Haochen Liu, Hailan Feng, Yang Liu, Dong Han
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractEnamel protects teeth from external irritation and its formation involves sequential differentiation of ameloblasts, a dental epithelial cell. Keratinocyte differentiation factor 1 (KDF1) is important in the development of epithelial tissues and organs. However, the specific role of KDF1 in enamel formation and corresponding regulatory mechanisms are unclear. This study demonstrated that KDF1 was persistently expressed in all stages of ameloblast differentiation, through RNAscope in situ hybridization. KDF1 expression in the mouse ameloblast cell line LS8 was demonstrated via immunofluorescence assay. KDF1 was knocked out in LS8 cells using the CRISPR/Cas‐9 system or overexpressed in LS8 cells through lentiviral infection. In vitro ameloblast differentiation induction, quantitative reverse transcription PCR, western blot analysis, and alkaline phosphatase (ALP) assay indicated that knockout or overexpression of KDF1 in LS8 cells decreased or increased the mRNA and protein levels of several key amelogenesis markers, as well as ALP activity. Furthermore, liquid chromatography‐mass spectrometry and co‐immunoprecipitation analyses revealed that KDF1 can interact with the IKK complex, thereby inhibiting the NF‐κB pathway. Suppressing NF‐κB activity partially recovered the decreased ameloblast differentiation in LS8 cells induced by KDF1‐knockout. This study demonstrated that KDF1 can promote ameloblast differentiation of LS8 cells by inhibiting the IKK/IκB/NF‐κB axis, and is a potential target for functional enamel regeneration.Keywords: ameloblast differentiation, enamel formation, KDF1, NF‐κB signaling pathway

INTRODUCTION

Enamel is the most mineralized tissue in the human body, and not only guarantees masticatory and aesthetic functions but also protects the dentin and dental pulp from external stimuli (Lacruz et al., 2017). Enamel formation is a dynamic process mediated by a variety of reciprocal signal molecules from the dental epithelium and mesenchyme, in which the epithelium occupies a dominant role (Jiang et al., 2017; Stephanopoulos et al., 2005). Ameloblasts, derived from the dental epithelium, play a core role in enamel development and biomineralization by synthesizing and secreting matrix protein, degrading and resorbing the matrix, and transferring minerals (Yu & Klein, 2020). Different morphological and functional behaviors of ameloblasts correspond to diverse differentiation stages that can be divided into three sequential periods: secretion stage, transition stage, and maturation stage (Smith et al., 2017; Varga et al., 2015). Any signal interference or protein defect during the abovementioned periods may lead to enamel dysplasia, which can affect general health (Dong et al., 2023; Wright et al., 2015). Evidence shows that the differentiation of ameloblasts could be regulated by multiple signaling pathways including the NF‐κB, BMP, and WNT pathways (Guo et al., 2015; Huang et al., 2021; Yamada et al., 2020). However, the precise gene network regulating ameloblast differentiation is still unclear, and the corresponding molecular mechanisms require further investigation (Smith et al., 2017; Wright et al., 2015).

Keratinocyte differentiation factor 1 (KDF1) was recently identified as a rare candidate gene of human congenital tooth agenesis, which is located on chromosome 1p36.11, encoding the KDF1 protein that shares 90% identity with the mouse Kdf1 homolog (Lee et al., 2013). Secondary structure predictions have indicated that KDF1 mainly contains a few isolated helical structures, which suggests that KDF1 may function as a protein‐binding ligand or adaptor, scaffold, or cofactor (Shamseldin et al., 2017). KDF1 contains a domain of unknown 4656 (DUF4656), which is a hotspot region for germline variations (Yu et al., 2022) and locates the KDF1 binding motif (Li et al., 2020). Studies have demonstrated that KDF1 variations are associated with the pathogenesis of non‐syndromic and syndromic tooth agenesis (ectodermal dysplasia) (Manaspon et al., 2019; Pan et al., 2022; Shamseldin et al., 2017; Yu et al., 2022; Zeng et al., 2019). Similarly, our latest study segregated a novel pathogenic heterozygous KDF1 missense variation c.920 G > C (p.R307P) from a nonsyndromic tooth agenesis family. Interestingly, we found that patients carrying this variant exhibited abnormal tooth morphology and defects in the proliferation and differentiation of gingival epithelial cells (Yu et al., 2022). These findings from human genetic studies confirm the correlation between KDF1 and tooth development.

Extensive studies using mouse models have revealed the role of KDF1 in the development of epithelium‐derived organs. A study reported that KDF1 mutant mice Kdf1 GT/GT exhibited enhanced proliferation and restricted differentiation of epidermal keratinocytes (Lee et al., 2013). Based on biological studies on skin development, KDF1 can regulate the differentiation of epidermal stem cells by promoting de‐ubiquitination and stabilization of IKKα (Lee et al., 2013; Li et al., 2020). Preliminary findings reported that Kdf1 was mainly expressed in the inner enamel epithelium, which is the predifferentiated form of ameloblasts, during tooth development (Zeng et al., 2019). Notably, a KDF1 missense mutation knock‐in mouse model constructed by Li et al. exhibited anomalous enamel structure and decreased enamel mineralization, further suggesting that KDF1 may be involved in enamel formation (Li et al., 2023). However, the specific role of KDF1 in the regulation of ameloblast differentiation has not been clarified, and its corresponding molecular mechanisms including the KDF1‐mediated protein interaction network must be explored in‐depth.

In this study, KDF1 was investigated for its role in ameloblast differentiation. Our data indicate that KDF1 promotes ameloblast differentiation through IKK/IκB/NF‐κB signaling axis. Collectively, our results broaden the regulatory network of KDF1 and provide an innovative target for enamel regeneration.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Tooth germ preparation and RNAscope in situ hybridization

ICR male mice were euthanized on postnatal Day 14. The mandibles of the mice were dissected and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA; Solarbio) overnight, demineralized with EDTA calcifying solution (Solarbio), dehydrated in graded ethanol series, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned in the sagittal direction (5 μm). The RNAscope Probe‐Mm‐Kdf1 (ACD‐1029511; Advanced Cell Diagnostics) was used to detect KDF1 mRNA using RNAscope 2.5 HD Reagent Kit‐Red (ACD‐322350; Advanced Cell Diagnostics), following the manufacturer's protocols (Wang et al., 2012). All animal experiments were approved by the Ethics Committee of Peking University Health Science Center (LA2020191).

Cell culture and differentiation induction

The mouse‐derived ameloblast cell line LS8 was cultured in a growth medium containing high‐glucose Dulbecco's modified Eagle's medium, 10% (v/v) fetal bovine serum, and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (Invitrogen) at 37°C in 5% CO2:95% humidified air. As LS8 has the differentiation characteristics of secretory ameloblasts, which can undergo both amelogenic induction and mineralization induction (Chen et al., 1992; Jiang et al., 2018; Sarkar et al., 2014; Yang et al., 2018) we established two kinds of differentiation induction systems to assess the effect of KDF1 on LS8 differentiation. The amelogenic induction medium included the growth medium supplemented with 20 μg/mL retinoic acid and 10−7 M dexamethasone (DEX; Sigma). The mineralization induction medium included the growth medium containing 50 mg/L ascorbic acid, 10 mM β‐sodium glycerophosphate, and 10−8 M DEX (Sigma). At 60%–70% confluence, the growth medium was changed with the differentiation medium. Day 0 was defined as the time the induction medium was added.

Generation of KDF1‐overexpressing cells

The KDF1‐overexpressing lentivirus plasmids (HBLV‐m‐Kdf1‐3xflag‐ZsGreen‐PURO) and a negative control (HBLV‐ZsGreen‐PURO) were constructed by Hanbio (Hanbio Biotechnology Co., Ltd.). LS8 cells were infected with the KDF1‐overexpressing (LS8‐Over) and control (LS8‐Control) lentiviruses, respectively. The stable transfected cells were selected using 7 μg/mL puromycin. The extent of overexpression was detected via western blot analysis using an anti‐KDF1 antibody (1:1000; ab224760, Abcam).

Generation of KDF1 knockout cells using CRISPR/Cas‐9

The KDF1 gene was knocked out in LS8 cells using the CRISPR/Cas‐9 system. Briefly, single guide RNA (ATCATCTGGGCCTCCACGCT) targeting the second exon of KDF1 was synthesized and subcloned into the lentiCRISPR v2‐puro vector. After transfection with this plasmid, LS8 cells were selected using 5 μg/mL puromycin and reseeded into 96‐well plates for single‐colony formation. After extended culture, genomic DNA was extracted from individual clones. The candidate KDF1 knockout cells were verified using Sanger sequencing of PCR fragments (primers: 5′‐TCGACGTTTGCAAGGGTTTC‐3′ and 5′‐CTTTGAGCTTAACATCAGGG‐3′) and western blotting. The KDF1 knockout and the control cells were named LS8‐KO and LS8‐Scramble, respectively.

Cell counting kit 8 (CCK‐8) assay

Four groups of the LS8 cells (LS8‐KO, LS8‐Scramble, LS8‐Over, LS8‐Contorl) constructed were seeded into 96‐well plates at a density of 2 × 103 per well (n = 5). Next, the CCK‐8 reagent (C0037; Beyotime) was added to each plate, and the cells were incubated for 4 h. The absorbance of the supernatant was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader (Synergy H1; BioTek).

Immunofluorescence staining

After deparaffinization and rehydration, the paraffin slices of molar germ of E18.5 and sections of mandibular incisors were antigen retrieved using sodium citrate solution (C1031; Solarbio), blocked with normal goat serum (SL038; Solarbio), and incubated with an anti‐IKKα antibody (1:50; A2062, ABclonal) or anti‐KDF1 antibody (1:50; ab224760, Abcam), followed by FITC‐conjugated anti‐rabbit IgG secondary antibody (1:200; ZF‐0311, ZSGB‐BIO) or TRITC‐conjugated anti‐rabbit IgG secondary antibody (1:200; ZF‐0316, ZSGB‐BIO).

For cellular immunofluorescence staining, LS8 cells grown on coverslips were fixed with 4% PFA and treated with 0.5% Triton X‐100 (Solarbio) for 15 min to permeate cytomembranes. The cells were blocked and incubated with an anti‐KDF1 antibody (1:50; Abcam) or anti‐Ki67 antibody (1:50; ab15580, Abcam), followed by a FITC‐conjugated anti‐rabbit IgG secondary antibody (1:200; ZSGB‐BIO) or TRITC‐conjugated anti‐rabbit IgG secondary antibody (1:200; ZSGB‐BIO). Next, the cells were mounted using a mounting medium with 4′,6′‐diamidino‐2‐phenylindole (DAPI; ZSGB‐BIO) to stain the nucleus. A Leica TCS‐SP8 confocal microscope was used to photograph the immunofluorescence cells.

Quantitative RT‐PCR (qRT‐PCR) analysis

Total RNA from cultured cells was extracted using TRIzol (Carlsbad) following standard protocol. The TaKaRa reverse transcription kit (RR0036A) was used to synthesize cDNA using 1 μg RNA. An ABI QuantStudio 6 system (Applied Biosystem) was used to perform qRT‐PCR analysis using 2x Universal SYBR Green qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme). The thermal cycling conditions included: 5 min at 95°C, followed by 40 cycles at 95°C for 10 s, and 60°C for 30 s lastly. Relative gene expression was calculated using the value of 2‐ΔΔT, which was normalized to β‐actin. The primers are shown in Table S1.

Western blot analysis

Cells were lysed with RIPA buffer (P0013; Beyotime) supplemented with a protease inhibitor cocktail (Solarbio) and phosphatase inhibitor (CWBIO). The concentration of protein samples was quantified using the BCA kit (Invitrogen). Equal quantities of proteins were separated on 10% polyacrylamide gel, and transferred to polyvinylidene fluoride membranes and incubated with different primary antibodies, including anti‐KDF1, anti‐β‐actin (1:1000; KM9006T, Sungene), anti‐Amelx (1:1000; ab153915), anti‐Mmp20 (1:1000; 55467, Proteintech), anti‐Alp (1:500; ab229126), anti‐Runx2 (1:300; 20700), anti‐p65 (1:1000; 8242, CST), anti‐p‐p65 (1:1000; 3033, CST), anti‐IκBα (1:2000; 10268, Proteintech), anti‐p‐IκBα (1:1000; 2859, CST), anti‐IKKβ (1:500; 15649, Proteintech), and anti‐p‐IKKβ (1:1000; 2697, CST), followed by incubation with 1:10000 goat anti‐mouse (bs‐0296G, Bioss) or goat anti‐rabbit secondary antibodies (bs‐0295G). Protein bands were visualized using an electrochemiluminescent reagent (NCM Biotech), and the integrated density was semi‐quantified using the ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health). β‐actin was set as the normalized control.

Co‐immunoprecipitation (Co‐ip)

Recombinant plasmids pCMV‐Flag‐KDF1, pCMV‐HA‐IKKα, and pCMV‐HA‐IKKβ containing the complete cDNA of mouse KDF1, IKKα, and IKKβ between 5’‐EcoRI and 3’‐XholI were acquired from Tsingke Biotechnology Co. Ltd. LS8 cells were transfected with plasmids using lipofectamineTM 3000 (Invitrogen), and the proteins were extracted. Co‐ip was conducted using the DYKDDDDK Fab‐Trap™ Agarose Kit (ffak; Proteintech), following the manufacturer's instructions. Lastly, western blot analysis was performed to detect the expression of Flag or HA by incubating with anti‐Flag (1:1000; ab205606, Abcam) or anti‐HA (1:1000; 3724, CST) antibodies.

Isolation of KDF1‐binding proteins and liquid chromatography‐mass spectrometry (LC‐MS) analysis

Total proteins were extracted from LS8‐Over cells and immunoprecipitation was performed to achieve protein enrichment of KDF1‐binding proteins using anti‐Flag and IgG antibodies for the experimental and control groups, respectively. After the proteins of the two groups were separated using polyacrylamide gel, the lanes of the gel corresponding to different protein samples were divided into tubes, followed by sequence desiccation, trypsin hydrolysis, and peptide extraction. The final samples were subjected to LC‐MS analysis by Sangon Biotech Co., Ltd. Subsequently, the obtained data was initially screened with the condition of reliability score of differential proteins (−10logP) ≥20 and the number of unique peptides ≥2. All data are provided in Table S2.

Alkaline phosphatase (ALP) activity

After 14 days of mineralization induction, cells were fixed and stained using the BCIP/NBT ALP Color Development Kit (C3206; Beyotime) to detect ALP expression. For ALP quantification, cells were solubilized in 1% Triton X‐100. After sonication and centrifugation, protein concentration was determined using the BCA kit. ALP activity in the supernatant was measured using an ALP assay kit (A059‐2, Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute), and optical density was assessed at 520 nm using a microplate reader, and further normalized to the total cellular protein.

Statistical analysis

Quantitative data were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical calculation and visualization of data were conducted using GraphPad Prism 8.0. Student's t‐test and one‐way ANOVA with Bonferroni's test were used to analyze the comparisons. P values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

RESULTS

Expression pattern of KDF1 during ameloblast differentiation

As growing mouse incisors exhibit all stages of ameloblast differentiation after eruption, we conducted an RNAscope using the sagittal section of mandibular incisors (Figure 1a) and observed that KDF1 was expressed throughout ameloblast differentiation, including secretion, transition, and maturation stages (Figure 1c–e). Moreover, KDF1 was also observed in the epithelial cells of the cervical loop (Figure 1b).

KDF1 is upregulated during ameloblast differentiation

Immunofluorescence staining demonstrated that KDF1 was localized in the cytoplasm of LS8 cells (Figure 1f–h). Next, in LS8 cells subjected to amelogenic and mineralization induction, respectively, KDF1 expression was significantly upregulated at both the mRNA (Figure 1i,j) and protein levels (Figure 1k–n). Therefore, KDF1 may be involved in the regulation of ameloblast differentiation.

Knockout or overexpression of endogenous KDF1 increases or decreases LS8 cell proliferation, respectively

KDF1 knockout in LS8 cells was achieved using the CRISPR/Cas9 system (Figure 2a,b). Sanger sequencing of LS8‐KO cells showed that the deletion of 14 bases in exon 2 of KDF1 resulted in a frameshift mutation of the allele (Figure 2b), and the termination codon appeared in advance at position 43‐46 after the mutation site, resulting in the deletion of most of the KDF1 functional domain. KDF1 knockout efficiency was determined via western blot analysis. Results showed that KDF1 levels in LS8‐KO cells decreased significantly, by ~75%, compared with LS8‐Scramble cells (Figure 2c,d). Moreover, a lentiviral system was employed to transduce overexpression lentiviruses of KDF1 into LS8 cells (Figure 2e–h). Overexpression efficiency, assessed using western blot analysis, revealed that the KDF1 levels were threefold higher in LS8‐Over cells, compared with LS8‐Control cells (Figure 2i,J).

Next, we performed the Ki67 cellular immunofluorescence staining and CCK‐8 assay on four groups of cells to test the effect of KDF1 on the proliferation of LS8. The results exhibited a significant increase in the proportion of Ki67‐positive cells in LS8‐KO cells, while the proportion was significantly decreased in LS8‐Over cells (Figure 2k–x). CCK‐8 activity of LS8‐KO cells increased significantly 96 h after cell seeding (Figure 2y), unlike LS8‐Over cells, which exhibited suppressed proliferation (Figure 2z).

Knockout or overexpression of endogenous KDF1 inhibits or promotes ameloblast differentiation, respectively

Next, we explored the effect of KDF1 on the differentiation of LS8 cells. After amelogenic induction for 2 days, qRT‐PCR and western blot analysis revealed that mRNA and protein levels of Amelx and Mmp20 had decreased significantly in LS8‐KO cells (Figure 3a–e) and increased in LS8‐Over cells (Figure 3f–j). Consistently, for mineralization‐induced differentiation, the mRNA and protein levels of Alp and Runx2 were significantly lesser in LS8‐KO cells (Figure 3k–o) and greater in LS8‐Over cells in the continuous culture on Days 7 and 14 (Figure 3p–t). These findings were further supported by the results of ALP staining (Figure 3u,w) and ALP activity (Figure 3v,x). Collectively, KDF1 is crucial for ameloblast differentiation and knockout of KDF1 inhibits LS8 cell differentiation, whereas KDF1 overexpression promotes it.

Knockout or overexpression of endogenous KDF1 promotes or inhibits the NF‐κB signaling pathway through the IKK complex/IκB/NF‐κB, respectively

To further explore the mechanism through which KDF1 regulates ameloblast differentiation, LS‐MS was employed to identify the possible proteins that bind to KDF1. Based on the differential proteins shown in Supporting Information S1: Table 2, the proteins affiliated with the amelogenesis‐related signaling pathway were enriched and ranked by their reliability score (−10logP) (Figure 4a–b). Next, co‐ip analysis determined the relationships between KDF1 and IKKα (Figure 4c) and KDF1 and IKKγ (Figure 4d).

Since IKKα is a leading kinase that combines with the regulatory subunit IKKγ to form the IKK complex, we detected the expression pattern of IKKα in the late bell stage (E18.5) of the first mandibular molar. Immunofluorescence showed that IKKα was specifically expressed in the dental epithelium, with high expression in the inner enamel epithelium (Figure 4e–g). Furthermore, we used the sagittal section of mandibular incisors to assess the expression pattern of IKKα in different stages of ameloblast differentiation. Immunofluorescence showed that IKKα was expressed in the secretory and mature stages of ameloblast differentiation, which overlapped with the expression pattern of KDF1 (Figure 4h–s).

qRT‐PCR and western blot analysis assays showed that there was no significant difference in the expression level of KDF1 in LS8 cells before and after TNF‐α stimulation (Supporting Information S1: Figures 1a–c). Immunofluorescence staining demonstrated that KDF1 was localized in the cytoplasm of LS8 cells with TNF‐α stimulation, which was consistent with the results of LS8 cells without TNF‐α stimulation (Supporting Information S1: Figures 1d–f). Subsequently, to assess whether KDF1 regulates NF‐κB signaling activity, the activation level of key proteins (IKKβ, IκBα, and p65) in the IKK complex/IκB/NF‐κB axis was determined via western blot analysis of different groups. After 15 min of TNF‐α stimulation, the ratio of the phosphorylation levels of IKKβ, IκBα, and p65 to the total IKKβ, IκBα, and p65 were significantly increased in LS8‐KO cells, but were significantly decreased in LS8‐Over cells (Figure 4t–z). Furthermore, western blot analysis was conducted to investigate the NF‐κB signaling activity of LS8‐KO, LS8‐Over and corresponding control cells under conditions of amelogenic induction and mineralization induction (Supporting Information S1: Figure 2a). The phosphorylation level of p65 were significantly increased in LS8‐KO cells, but significantly decreased in LS8‐Over cells (Supporting Information S1: Figures 2b–c), which was consistent with the results using TNF‐α stimulated cells.

Inhibition of NF‐κB signaling partially recovered the reduced amelogenic ability in LS8‐KO cells

To further clarify whether KDF1 affects ameloblast differentiation by regulating the NF‐κB signaling pathway, we performed phenotypic rescue experiments in the presence of BAY11‐7082 (1 μM), an NF‐κB pathway inhibitor, in LS8‐KO cells. Western blot analysis showed that in the amelogenic induction group, Amelx expression increased significantly in LS8‐KO cells treated with BAY11‐7082, compared to LS8‐KO cells with DMSO treatment (Figure 5a,b). Meanwhile, for mineralization‐induced differentiation, compared with LS8‐KO cells without BAY11‐7082 treatment, LS8‐KO cells treated with BAY11‐7082 displayed elevated Alp expression (Figure 5a,c). The results of ALP staining and ALP activity were consistent with the results of western blot analysis (Figure 5d,e). Therefore, inhibition of NF‐κB signaling partially recovered the reduced amelogenic ability of LS8‐KO cells, further confirming that KDF1 knockout suppressed ameloblast differentiation by stimulating the NF‐κB signaling pathway.

DISSCUSSION

The sequential differentiation of enamel epithelial cells provides a foundation for enamel formation, unveiling cascade signal molecules that regulate differentiation, and ameloblasts are vital to the process (Smith et al., 2017). KDF1, a newly identified cell differentiation regulatory factor, participates in the proliferation and differentiation of epithelial progenitor cells (Lee et al., 2013; Li et al., 2020). Although enamel demineralization was reported in KDF1‐mutated mice, (Li et al., 2023) the molecular mechanisms through which KDF1 regulates ameloblast differentiation are unclear.

As LS8 cells exhibit protein expression patterns (Amelx, Mmp20, Alp, and Runx2) similar to sequentially differentiated ameloblasts with different modes of induction (Wang et al., 2018; Yang et al., 2018) they were used in our study. Immunofluorescence confirmed the nuclear and cytoplasmic localization of KDF1 in LS8 cells. As the differentiation progressed, the endogenous expression of KDF1 increased significantly, indicating that KDF1 may play a positive regulatory role in ameloblast differentiation. To further verify this hypothesis, we introduced a lentivirus system to knockout or overexpress KDF1 in LS8 cells separately, using CRISPR/Cas9. During amelogenic induction, the expression of secretion stage markers (Amelx, Mmp20) decreased in KDF1‐knockout cells, but increased in the KDF1‐overexpressing cells. After mineralization induction, KDF1 knockout suppressed the expression of mineralization‐related markers (Alp, Runx2) and ALP activity, while KDF1 overexpression promoted them. Based on these results, we concluded that KDF1 promotes ameloblast differentiation and enamel mineralization.

NF‐κB signaling is an inhibitory pathway for enamel formation (Blackburn et al., 2015; Liang et al., 2019; Yamada et al., 2020). Activation of the canonical NF‐κB signaling pathway can inhibit the differentiation of rat ameloblasts (Liang et al., 2019). Likewise, an in vivo model evidenced that epithelium‐specific‐IKKβ‐overexpressing mice (K5‐Ikkβ) exhibit reduced enamel mineralization and significantly downregulated amelogenin expression (Yamada et al., 2020). In the canonical NF‐κB signaling pathway, IKKα and IKKγ can combine with IKKβ to constitute the IKK complex (Karin & Ben‐Neriah, 2000). After being stimulated by the upstream signal, IKKβ is phosphorylated and acts as a marker of IKK complex activation, and thus, catalyzes the phosphorylation and ubiquitination of IκB (Karin & Ben‐Neriah, 2000). Then, IκB is degraded through the proteasome pathway, which mediates the entry of NF‐κB/Rel from the cytoplasm into the nucleus, regulating the expression of downstream target genes (Antonia et al., 2021). KDF1 regulates the epidermal progenitor cell differentiation by deubiquitinating and stabilizing IKKα (Li et al., 2020). Combining previous findings and the results of LC‐MS in this study, we focused on exploring the relationship of IKKα, IKKγ and KDF1. Co‐ip analysis in this study revealed that IKKα and IKKγ may interact with KDF1. Western blot analysis confirmed that KDF1 knockout can increase IKK/IκB/NF‐κB signaling activity in LS8 cells, while KDF1 overexpression decreases it. BAY11‐7082 inhibits canonical NF‐κB activity by suppressing the phosphorylation of IκB and disrupting the transference of NF‐κB/Rel (Kfoury et al., 2005). After treatment with BAY11‐7082, the decreased differentiation in LS8 cells induced by KDF1 knockout was partially recovered. Overall, this study demonstrated that KDF1 positively regulates LS8 cell differentiation by inhibiting NF‐κB signaling pathway activation (Figure 6). This study is the first to reveal the KDF1‐mediated protein interaction network in ameloblast differentiation.

KDF1 interacts with IKK complex to inhibit the activation of NF‐κB signaling by IKK/IκB/NF‐κB axis, thereby exerting a positive regulatory role during ameloblast differentiation.

KDF1 is also known to promote the differentiation of keratinocytes (Lee et al., 2013; Li et al., 2020). Interestingly, it has been reported that human keratinocytes can differentiate into enamel‐secreting ameloblasts, when combined with the embryonic dental mesenchyme, providing a strategy for cell‐based enamel regeneration (Hu et al., 2018; Wang et al., 2010). In this study, we highlighted the function of KDF1 in regulating ameloblast differentiation, which may serve as an important target in genetic approaches toward enamel regeneration. However, tissue‐ and time‐specific KDF1 knockout or overexpression mouse models should investigate the intrinsic mechanism through which KDF1 regulates enamel development.

CONCLUSION

In this study, we revealed the specific epithelial expression pattern of KDF1 during ameloblast differentiation. Furthermore, our results elucidate the functional role of the interaction between KDF1 and IKK complex in inhibiting the activation of NF‐κB signaling, which promotes ameloblast differentiation. Our results not only broaden our understanding of the functions of KDF1 but also enhance the understanding of the gene regulatory network involved in enamel development, and provide an innovative target for enamel regeneration.
