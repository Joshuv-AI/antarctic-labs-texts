High retear rates despite clinical improvement after arthroscopic partial repair for large to massive rotator cuff tears
Authors: Chang Hee Baek, Bo Taek Kim, Jung Gon Kim, Chaemoon Lim, Truong Nguyen Khanh Hung, Hai Le Dinh, Phuoc Hau Huynh, Phuoc Tho Pham
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundManagement of large to massive rotator cuff tears (RCTs) remains challenging, particularly when complete anatomic repair is not feasible. The current study aims to evaluate short-term clinical outcomes and complications following arthroscopic partial repair of large to massive RCT with incomplete footprint coverage and to compare outcomes according to concomitant subscapularis repair and post-operative rotator cuff integrity.MethodsPatients who underwent arthroscopic partial repair of the supraspinatus tendon, with or without associated infraspinatus tears, and incomplete footprint coverage between July 2022 and April 2023 were retrospectively identified. Inclusion criteria included intraoperative confirmation of incomplete anatomic footprint coverage, an anteroposterior tear size ≥ 3 cm, and the presence of either an intact subscapularis tendon or a reparable concomitant subscapularis tear (Lafosse type ≤3). Patients were excluded if they were lost to follow-up, had a history of prior shoulder instability surgery, or had insufficient clinical or radiologic data. Clinical outcomes were evaluated pre-operatively and at final follow-up using the visual analog scale for pain, Constant score, American Shoulder and Elbow Surgeons score, Single Assessment Numeric Evaluation score, active range of motion (ROM), and shoulder strength. Radiologic assessment included evaluation of repair integrity, acromiohumeral distance, and progression of glenohumeral arthritis.ResultsAfter excluding 15 patients, 142 patients were included in the final analysis. At a mean follow-up of 26.2 months, significant improvements were observed in all pain, patient-reported outcome measures, ROM, and strength (all P < .001). Post-operative retear was identified in 43 patients (30.3%). Clinical outcomes did not differ significantly between patients with intact subscapularis tendons and those who underwent concomitant subscapularis repair. However, patients with intact post-operative repair integrity demonstrated significantly superior pain relief, functional scores, ROM, and strength compared with those who experienced retears (all P < .001).ConclusionArthroscopic partial repair with incomplete footprint coverage was associated with significant short-term improvements in pain, function, ROM, and strength in patients with large to massive RCT. Outcomes were similar between patients with intact subscapularis tendons and those undergoing concomitant repair of reparable subscapularis tears. However, post-operative structural failure occurred in 30.3% of patients and was associated with inferior outcomes and frequent revision surgery. These findings should be interpreted cautiously given the retrospective design, absence of a comparator group, and short-term follow-up.Keywords: Rotator cuff tear, Arthroscopic partial repair, Incomplete footprint coverage, Large to massive rotator cuff tear, Retear, Structural integrity

Management of large to massive rotator cuff tears (RCTs) remains challenging and is associated with high structural failure rates, and there is currently no consensus regarding the optimal surgical approach.8,10,16,24 Consequently, a variety of arthroscopic treatment options have been described, including débridement, partial repair, marginal convergence, biceps augmentation, and patch augmentation.3,4,22 Selection of the most appropriate treatment should be individualized, taking into account patient age, functional demands, tissue quality, and the ability to comply with post-operative rehabilitation.2,4,22,23 Although restoration of complete anatomic footprint coverage represents the ideal goal of rotator cuff repair, it is frequently unattainable in large to massive RCTs because of tendon retraction, compromised tissue quality, and advanced muscle degeneration.10,20 In such cases, arthroscopic partial repair with incomplete footprint coverage may be performed as a nonanatomic yet biomechanically meaningful alternative.14 This technique aims to reestablish balanced force couples of the shoulder and reduce pain, even when full coverage of the footprint cannot be achieved.14,19 Previous clinical studies have reported favorable short- to midterm outcomes following arthroscopic partial repair for large to massive RCTs.10,12,14,19,20,27 Nevertheless, partial repair has been associated with a substantial risk of post-operative retear.12,27 Also, reported outcomes remain heterogeneous, and the clinical impact of concomitant subscapularis tears and post-operative structural integrity has not been fully clarified.10,16,20

The purpose of the present study was to evaluate the short-term clinical outcomes and complications of arthroscopic partial repair of large to massive RCTs with incomplete footprint coverage and to compare outcomes between patients with and without concomitant subscapularis repair, as well as between patients with intact repair integrity and those with post-operative retears.

Methods and materials

Study design and patient selection

The current study was a retrospective cohort analysis. Institutional review board approval was obtained prior to study initiation, and the requirement for informed consent was waived due to the retrospective nature of the study.

Between July 2022 and April 2023, patients who underwent arthroscopic partial repair of full-thickness RCTs (supraspinatus tears, with or without associated infraspinatus tears) with incomplete footprint coverage were identified. Inclusion criteria included intraoperative confirmation of incomplete anatomic repair, an anteroposterior tear size ≥ 3 cm, and the presence of either an intact subscapularis tendon or a reparable concomitant subscapularis tear classified as Lafosse17 type ≤3. In the current study, large to massive RCTs were defined based on an anteroposterior tear size of ≥3 cm measured intraoperatively. Exclusion criteria included loss to follow-up, incomplete clinical or radiologic data, and a prior history of shoulder instability surgery, such as Bankart repair or Latarjet procedures (Fig. 1).

Flowchart illustrating patient selection and inclusion process.

Surgical technique

All procedures were performed arthroscopically by a single senior shoulder surgeon (C.H.B.) with the patient in the lateral decubitus position under brachial plexus block with intravenous sedation. Diagnostic arthroscopy was initially performed to evaluate the glenohumeral joint, including the articular surfaces, long head of the biceps tendon, and RCTs (Fig. 2A). Any concomitant subscapularis tears were repaired when present using a double-row suture bridge technique in subacromial space (n = 39). The long head of the biceps tendon was managed with tenotomy in cases where more than 50% of its structural integrity was compromised (n = 68).

Arthroscopic partial repair of a large-to-massive rotator cuff tear in the right shoulder. View from the lateral portal demonstrating (A) a retracted supraspinatus tendon tear (red asterisk) displaced to the level of the glenoid; (B) partial repair of the supraspinatus tendon (red asterisk) performed only to the extent that the tendon could be reduced without excessive tension; (C) insertion of 2 triple-loaded medial-row anchors (white arrows) at the humeral footprint; and (D) final arthroscopic appearance showing incomplete footprint coverage (blue asterisk) following partial rotator cuff repair (red asterisk).

Before partial repair, mobilization of the torn rotator cuff tendon was achieved through an anterior interval release combined with coracohumeral ligament release. Repair was attempted only to the extent that the tendon could be reduced without excessive tension (Fig. 2B). Nonviable rotator cuff tissue was débrided, and the footprint was prepared by débridement and decortication to promote bone-to-tendon healing. For partial repair, 2 triple-loaded medial-row suture anchors were placed at the footprint near the articular margin, 1 anteriorly and 1 posteriorly (Fig. 2C). All suture limbs were passed through the torn rotator cuff tendon at evenly spaced intervals to achieve uniform load distribution. The sutures were sequentially tied to secure the tendon medially. Lateral fixation was then completed using 3 knotless lateral-row anchors positioned along the lateral aspect of the greater tuberosity, creating a double-row suture-bridge construct [Fig. 2D].

Post-operative rehabilitation

Post-operatively, the operated arm was immobilized in a 30° abduction brace with the shoulder maintained in neutral rotation for six weeks. Patients were instructed to use continuous passive motion machine to preserve joint mobility and reduce the risk of post-operative stiffness. From post-operative week 6 to week 12, rehabilitation progressed to active-assisted, and then active range of motion (ROM) exercises. After three months, patients began a structured strengthening program designed to restore dynamic stability and improve overall shoulder function.

Clinical and radiologic assessment

Clinical outcomes were evaluated pre-operatively and at the final follow-up using validated outcome measures, including the visual analog scale (VAS) for pain and patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs), such as the Constant score, the American Shoulder and Elbow Surgeons (ASES) score, and the Single Assessment Numeric Evaluation (SANE) score. Active ROM, including forward elevation, abduction, and external rotation at the side, was measured using a goniometer. Internal rotation was documented according to the highest vertebral level reached by the thumb. Active shoulder strength in forward elevation was assessed using a handheld dynamometer and recorded in Newtons. All post-operative complications, including retear and infection, were systematically documented.

Standardized radiographic evaluations, including true anteroposterior, lateral scapular, and axillary lateral views, were obtained pre-operatively and at the final follow-up for all patients. The acromiohumeral distance (AHD) was measured on the true anteroposterior view as the shortest distance between the inferior surface of the acromion and the apex of the humeral head. Progression of glenohumeral arthritis was assessed using the Hamada9 grading system, with a Hamada grade of 3 or higher at final follow-up considered indicative of advanced progression of arthritis. Fatty infiltration of the rotator cuff muscles was evaluated on sagittal oblique (Y-view) images using the Goutallier6 classification, ranging from stage 0 (normal muscle) to stage 4 (more fat than muscle). All patients underwent post-operative magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) at the final follow-up regardless of clinical symptoms, and MRI was uniformly used as the post-operative imaging modality for all patients. Structural integrity of the repaired rotator cuff tendon was assessed according to the Sugaya classification system, with Sugaya25 classification IV and V, defined by discontinuity of the repaired tendon, considered indicative of retear (Fig. 3). Because incomplete footprint coverage was intentionally accepted in the present study, residual uncovered footprint without tendon discontinuity was not classified as structural failure. Formal MRI interpretation was performed by a musculoskeletal radiologist with more than 20 years of experience who was blinded to the study design and clinical outcomes.

Post-operative magnetic resonance imaging of the left shoulder. (A) Coronal image demonstrating an intact repaired rotator cuff tendon (white asterisk) with incomplete footprint coverage (white arrow).(B) Coronal image demonstrating complete retear of the repaired tendon (red arrow).

For subgroup analysis, patients were stratified into subgroups according to subscapularis status and post-operative rotator cuff integrity. Subscapularis status was categorized as an intact subscapularis or a concomitantly repaired subscapularis at the time of surgery. Post-operative rotator cuff integrity was classified as an intact repair or a retear based on post-operative imaging. Pre-operative and post-operative clinical outcomes, ROM, and shoulder strength were compared between these subgroups.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS for Windows (version 21.0; IBM, Armonk, NY, USA). Continuous variables were expressed as means with standard deviations, and categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages. Comparisons between pre-operative and post-operative outcomes within the same group were conducted using paired t-tests or Wilcoxon signed-rank tests, depending on data distribution. Between-group comparisons were performed using independent t-tests or Mann–Whitney U tests for continuous variables and chi-square tests or Fisher exact tests for categorical variables, as appropriate. To adjust for multiple comparisons in subgroup analyses, Bonferroni correction was applied.

Results

Patient demographics

After exclusion of 15 patients due to loss to follow-up (n = 5), incomplete clinical outcome data (n = 8), or a history of prior shoulder instability surgery (n = 2), a total of 142 patients were included in the final analysis. The cohort comprised 76 men (53.5%) and 66 women (46.5%), with a mean age of 66.9 ± 7.1 years (range, 44-84 years). The mean follow-up duration was 26.2 ± 1.9 months (range, 24-31 months). Pre-operatively, the average size of RCT was 37.0 ± 5.6 mm (range, 30.1-62.9). Concomitant reparable subscapularis tears were identified and repaired in 39 patients (27.5%). Clinical outcomes of the entire cohort

All clinical outcome measures demonstrated significant improvement at final follow-up compared with pre-operative values (Table II). The mean VAS pain score improved from 4.8 ± 1.1 pre-operatively to 1.7 ± 1.1 at final follow-up (P < .001). PROM also improved significantly, including the Constant score (53.7 ± 9.5 to 70.3 ± 12.0), ASES score (57.4 ± 9.1 to 74.4 ± 12.1), and SANE score (55.3 ± 9.0 to 72.5 ± 11.9) (all P < .001). Active ROM increased significantly in forward elevation, abduction, external rotation at the side, and internal rotation behind the back (all P ≤ .001). Active shoulder strength during forward elevation improved from 46.3 ± 7.8 N pre-operatively to 70.0 ± 16.5 N at final follow-up (P < .001).

Retear of the repaired rotator cuff was identified in 43 patients (30.3%) at final follow-up. Among these patients, 29 (67.4%) subsequently underwent additional surgical intervention because of persistent pain and functional limitation, including revision rotator cuff repair with patch augmentation (n = 18) and tendon transfer procedures (n = 11) (Table III). The remaining patients did not pursue additional surgical intervention because their symptoms remained tolerable at the time of final evaluation. Revision rotator cuff repair with patch augmentation was selectively performed in patients in whom the torn tendon remained sufficiently mobilizable and partial repair was considered feasible; in these cases, an acellular dermal matrix graft was used as an onlay augmentation to reinforce the repair. The mean time to revision rotator cuff repair was 5.1 ± 3.9 months (range, 2-14 months) after the initial repair. In contrast, tendon transfer procedures were performed in patients with irreparable tears in whom the tendon could not be adequately mobilized or reduced to the footprint. The mean time to tendon transfer was 5.6 ± 3.8 months (range, 2-12 months) after the initial repair. Other post-operative complications included shoulder stiffness in 12 patients (8.4%) and post-operative infection in 5 patients (3.5%). Among the patients with infection, 3 had superficial infections and were successfully treated with arthroscopic irrigation and débridement combined with antibiotic therapy. The remaining 2 patients developed deep infection requiring multiple arthroscopic irrigation and débridement procedures, removal of suture anchors, and excision of infected residual tissue in addition to prolonged antibiotic treatment. Progression of glenohumeral arthritis by one Hamada grade was observed in 4 patients (2.8%), whereas progression to advanced arthritis (Hamada grade ≥3) occurred in 2 patients (1.4%).

Subgroup analysis

When patients with an intact subscapularis tendon were compared with those who underwent concomitant subscapularis repair, no significant differences were observed in post-operative VAS pain scores, PROM, or ROM. Patients who required subscapularis repair demonstrated lower pre-operative internal rotation than those with an intact subscapularis tendon (P = .008); however, this difference was no longer present post-operatively, with comparable internal rotation between groups (Table IV). Post-operative imaging demonstrated an intact rotator cuff repair in 99 patients (69.7%) and a retear in 43 patients (30.3%) (Table V). At final follow-up, patients with intact repairs exhibited significantly superior VAS pain scores and PROM compared with those who experienced retears. In addition, active ROM and forward elevation strength showed greater post-operative improvement in patients with preserved rotator cuff integrity than in those with retear.

Discussion

The principal finding of the present study was that arthroscopic partial repair of large to massive RCTs with incomplete footprint coverage resulted in significant short-term improvements in pain, PROM, ROM, and shoulder strength. Compared with patients with an intact subscapularis tendon, those who underwent concomitant repair of a reparable subscapularis tear did not demonstrate significantly different post-operative clinical outcomes, and results were comparable between the 2 groups. However, post-operative retear occurred in 30.3% of patients, and those in the retear group demonstrated inferior post-operative clinical outcomes. Patients with preserved post-operative rotator cuff integrity demonstrated superior pain relief, PROM, ROM, and strength compared with those who experienced retear.

The clinical improvements observed in this study are consistent with an expanding body of literature supporting arthroscopic partial repair as one treatment option for large to massive RCTs. Several systematic reviews have demonstrated significant post-operative improvements in pain and functional outcomes following partial repair despite incomplete restoration of the native footprint. Malahias et al20 reported meaningful improvements in validated outcome scores and shoulder strength after arthroscopic partial repair, even though nearly half of the repairs demonstrated retear on post-operative imaging. Similarly, Hyde et al,10 in a systematic review, found that arthroscopic partial repair provided acceptable short- to midterm pain relief and patient satisfaction. In addition, Jeong et al12 demonstrated that an arthroscopic incomplete (hybrid) repair technique for large to massive RCTs resulted in favorable clinical outcomes at a minimum 2-year follow-up in 45 patients, supporting the concept that even limited increases in footprint coverage and load-sharing capacity can translate into meaningful functional benefits. Similar to the findings of the present study, these results suggest that restoration of shoulder biomechanics and force couple balance through partial repair may contribute to early clinical improvement. Collectively, these studies reinforce the role of partial or incomplete repair as a viable surgical option in appropriately selected patients.

Despite these encouraging short-term clinical outcomes, the relatively high retear rate observed in the present study and the inferior clinical outcomes in patients who experienced retear warrants careful consideration. Similar to the findings of the current study, a high rate of retear following partial repair has been consistently reported in the literature. In a systematic review, Baumann et al1 demonstrated that retear rates after partial repair of large to massive RCTs can reach up to 72.5% at short- to midterm follow-up, underscoring the structural vulnerability of nonanatomic repairs. Likewise, Laprus et al18 reported a retear rate of 73.3% following partial repair of large to massive RCTs at a mean follow-up of 34.5 months. Also, Yoon et al26 found that partial repair with incomplete footprint coverage resulted in a lower retear rate of 28.9% in medium-sized RCTs at a 2-year follow-up. Several factors likely contribute to the high failure rate, including advanced fatty infiltration, poor tendon quality, large tear size, residual medial defects, and increased mechanical stress across a nonanatomic repair construct.5,7,19,21 In theory, incomplete footprint coverage inherently reduces the tendon–bone contact area and may compromise biologic healing potential, particularly at the medial repair site. Nevertheless, comparative studies have suggested that while complete anatomic repair provides superior structural integrity, short- to midterm functional outcomes may remain similar between complete and incomplete repair strategies.11,13,15 In particular, Malahias et al19 conducted a prospective study comparing 34 patients who underwent complete anatomic repair with 34 patients treated with incomplete partial repair for massive RCTs. The authors reported inferior post-operative structural integrity in the incomplete partial repair group compared with the complete anatomic repair group; however, this difference did not consistently translate into clinically meaningful differences in PROMs. These observations help contextualize the current results, indicating that although incomplete partial repair is associated with high retear, restoration of balanced shoulder biomechanics may still provide durable symptom relief and functional improvement in selected patients.

Several limitations should be acknowledged in the present study. First, the retrospective design introduces inherent selection and information bias. Second, the absence of a control group treated with alternative surgical strategies, such as débridement alone, patch augmentation, superior capsular reconstruction, tendon transfer, or complete anatomic repair, limits the ability to draw comparative conclusions or determine the relative superiority of arthroscopic partial repair over other treatment options. Third, the relatively short follow-up period precludes a comprehensive assessment of long-term outcomes, including the durability of clinical improvement and the progression of glenohumeral arthritis. Nonetheless, Zhou et al27 demonstrated sustained clinical improvement at a mean follow-up exceeding 8 years after arthroscopic partial repair, despite radiologic progression of fatty infiltration and muscle atrophy. Fourth, the relatively higher post-operative infection rate observed in this cohort may have been influenced by the complexity of large to massive RCTs, prolonged operative time, and the performance of concomitant procedures such as subscapularis repair or biceps tenotomy; therefore, this complication rate should be interpreted with caution when generalizing the findings. Fifth, subgroup comparisons according to subscapularis status and post-operative structural integrity were based on unadjusted analyses and may have been influenced by baseline differences in tear severity, fatty infiltration, tendon quality, and other confounding factors. Therefore, these findings should be interpreted cautiously, and future studies incorporating multivariable regression analysis are warranted to better identify independent predictors of clinical outcomes and structural failure. Finally, although all procedures were performed by a single experienced surgeon—enhancing surgical consistency—this may limit the generalizability of the findings. Future prospective studies with longer follow-up durations and comparative cohorts are warranted to further define the role of arthroscopic partial repair in the management of large to massive RCTs.

Conclusion

Arthroscopic partial repair with incomplete footprint coverage was associated with significant short-term improvements in pain, function, ROM, and strength in patients with large to massive RCT. Outcomes were similar between patients with intact subscapularis tendons and those undergoing concomitant repair of reparable subscapularis tears. However, post-operative structural failure occurred in 30.3% of patients and was associated with inferior outcomes and frequent revision surgery. These findings should be interpreted cautiously given the retrospective design, absence of a comparator group, and short-term follow-up.
