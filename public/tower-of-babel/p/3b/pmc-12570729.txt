Interaction effect of age and axial length-to-corneal radius ratio on myopia in children and adolescents: a cross-sectional study in China
Authors: Jingfeng Mu, Haoxi Zhong, Huimin Zhu, Mingjie Jiang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMyopia has emerged as a significant public health concern in China. Despite age and axial length-to-corneal radius ratio (AL/CR) being widely recognized as independent factors influencing myopia in children and adolescents, very few studies have investigated the combined effects of these two factors or the extent of their interaction. This cross-sectional study aimed to examine the association and interaction between age and AL/CR and the prevalence of myopia in children and adolescents, providing evidence for early myopia prevention and control.MethodsIn 2022, three primary schools in Shenzhen were selected via random cluster sampling. Students who had lived in Shenzhen for over 6 months were administered surveys, and underwent myopia screenings (visual acuity and noncycloplegic autorefraction) and ocular biometric measurements. Logistic regression analysis was performed to assess the interaction between age and AL/CR using both multiplicative and additive models. A generalized additive model was used to evaluate the relationship among age, AL/CR, and myopia prevalence, with the results presented through visualization analysis.ResultsA total of 3,220 participants were included (mean age of 8.95 ± 1.77 years; mean AL/CR ratio of 3.01 ± 0.13). The overall prevalence of myopia was 47.67%. After adjusting for sex, parental myopia, reading and writing posture, per capita monthly income, and anterior chamber depth, a significant multiplicative interaction between age and AL/CR on myopia prevalence (OR = 2.147, 95% CI 1.553–2.968) was observed. Additive interaction analysis further revealed a relative excess risk due to interaction of 10.816 (95% CI 8.027–13.605), an attributable proportion of 0.700 (95% CI 0.636–0.763), and a synergy index of 3.971 (95% CI 3.065–5.143).ConclusionThe interaction between age and AL/CR was associated with a greater likelihood of myopia, with both positive multiplicative and additive effects observed. Older age and increased AL/CR ratios were associated with a greater likelihood of myopia in children and adolescents, emphasizing the importance of early prevention strategies.

Introduction

Myopia has emerged as a major global public health issue, with its prevalence projected to reach 50% of the world’s population by 2050 [1]. The burden is particularly pronounced among school-aged children in East Asia, where the prevalence rate exceeds 70% [2], and in China, where it is projected to reach 66.8% by 2050 [3, 4]. High myopia significantly increases the risk of vision-threatening complications, such as retinal detachment, glaucoma, and cataracts [5–8]; moreover, uncorrected or pathological myopia can substantially impair quality of life [9]. Therefore, a thorough understanding of the development, epidemiology, etiology, and risk factors of myopia is essential for controlling its growing prevalence and mitigating its impact on public health.

While myopia is not curable, it can be effectively managed with appropriate interventions. Accurate prediction of refractive status and its progression, combined with timely and effective measures, can significantly slow myopia progression, improve visual outcomes, enhance quality of life, and reduce the associated socioeconomic burden. Therefore, identifying factors influencing myopia and developing predictive models are crucial for its prevention and control. Previous studies have demonstrated strong associations between myopia and both environmental and lifestyle factors [9], including time spent outdoors [10, 11], near work [12, 13] and sleep patterns [14, 15], as well as biological factors, such as ocular biometric parameters [16, 17] and age [18, 19]. Among ocular biometric parameters, axial length (AL) and axial length-to-corneal radius (AL/CR) ratio show the strongest associations with myopia [20, 21]. Evidence also suggests that AL/CR has a stronger correlation with myopia than AL alone [22].

Age and AL/CR ratio are widely recognized as independent factors influencing myopia in children and adolescents [19, 22]. However, very few studies have investigated the combined effects of these two factors or the extent of their interactions. Most studies have focused on the role of individual factors in myopia prevalence, with minimal attention given to the impact of the interaction between age and AL/CR on the risk of myopia. Therefore, using data from myopia surveillance and questionnaire surveys among children and adolescents, we aimed to investigate both the independent and combined effects of age and AL/CR on myopia prevalence. We aim to provide scientific evidence to inform early prevention and control strategies for myopia in this population.

Methods

Research participants

In 2022, a random stratified cluster sampling method was used to select three districts in Shenzhen. From each district, one primary school was randomly chosen, and students in grades 1–6 were recruited. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Shenzhen Eye Hospital (Approval No.: 20201230–06), and written informed consent was obtained from all participants’ guardians. Eligible participants were students aged 6–12 years who had resided in Shenzhen for at least 6 months and were able to complete all study examinations. Participants with severe eye conditions, such as nystagmus, glaucoma, cataracts, photosensitivity, or retinal detachment, were excluded.

The minimum sample size was calculated based on a myopia prevalence rate of 34.1% among primary school students in Shenzhen [23]. Ultimately, this study included 3,220 primary school students, distributed across age groups as follows: 870 students from grade 1, 598 from grade 2, 555 from grade 3, 388 from grade 4, 475 from grade 5, and 334 from grade 6.

Data collection

Uncorrected visual acuity

Uncorrected visual acuity was assessed for all participants by two ophthalmologists in accordance with the Specifications for Screening Refractive Error in Primary and Secondary School Students (WS/T 663–2020) [24]. All measurements were obtained in well-lit classrooms to ensure optimal testing conditions.

Refraction testing

Refraction testing was performed by trained optometrists using noncycloplegic autorefraction, in accordance with the Specifications for Screening Refractive Error in Primary and Secondary School Students (WS/T 663-2020) [24]. Cycloplegia was not applied owing to ethical and logistical constraints associated with large-scale, school-based field studies, such as time limitations and concerns over pupil dilation in classroom settings.

Ocular biometric measurements

Ocular biometric parameters were measured using an optical biometer (SW-9000, China). Key parameters included AL, corneal radius (CR), anterior chamber depth (ACD), and central corneal thickness (CCT).

Questionnaire survey

A self-designed questionnaire was developed with reference to the National Handbook for Monitoring Common Diseases and Health Risk Factors in Students published by the National Health Commission of the People’s Republic of China and Peking University [25] and used for data collection.

The questionnaire was refined through expert consultation and finalized after a pilot survey conducted in a separate school not included in the final analysis. To assess reliability, a test–retest evaluation was conducted among 100 students with a 1-day interval; the correlation coefficients between the two rounds of responses for each item exceeded 0.95, indicating excellent stability and reliability. To minimize recall bias, most questions used clear reference periods (e.g., dietary intake over the past 7 days or 1 month) and were designed as simple, close-ended questions with consistent response formats.

The finalized online questionnaire was distributed to participants’ guardians by trained teachers. After excluding incomplete submissions, the overall response rate was approximately 90.4%. The questionnaire collected information on demographic characteristics, visual behaviors (both in and outside of school), parental myopia, dietary habits, weekend outdoor activity, age of first exposure to electronic products and other factors potentially associated with myopia.

Definition of myopia

Myopia was defined as an uncorrected visual acuity of less than 5.0 in either eye, combined with a noncycloplegic autorefraction spherical equivalent (SE) of less than − 0.50 diopter (D). Participants wearing orthokeratology lenses were classified as having myopia [26]. The SE of the right and left eyes of the study population were highly correlated (r = 0.90, p < 0.001) in the present study. Myopia was further categorized based on the SE value of the right eye: mild myopia as − 3.00 D to − 0.50 D, moderate myopia as − 6.00 D to − 3.00 D, and high myopia as less than − 6.00 D.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was conducted using R software (Version 4.4.2; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Categorical data were compared between groups using the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{\varvec{\chi}}}^{2}$$\end{document} test, while continuous data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\overline{{\varvec{x}}}\pm {\varvec{s} })$$\end{document} and compared using an independent samples t-test. A significance level of α = 0.05 was set for all statistical tests.

Logistic regression models were used to examine the interaction effect between age and AL/CR. For the primary interaction analyses, both age and AL/CR were dichotomized based on clinically and epidemiologically relevant thresholds. Age was categorized as < 9 vs. ≥ 9 years [27], and AL/CR as < 3 vs. ≥ 3 [28]. To evaluate the robustness of these cutoffs, sensitivity analyses were performed treating both age and AL/CR as continuous variables in logistic and generalized additive models (GAMs), These analyses yielded similar effect directions and statistical significance.

Additive interaction was assessed by calculating the relative excess risk owing to interaction (RERI), attributable proportion owing to interaction (API), and the synergy index (SI). A GAM was constructed to analyze myopia as the dependent variable and other variables as independent variables. Smooth terms were applied to variables with suspected nonlinearity, such as AL/CR. Model diagnostics including the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC), and effective degrees of freedom were used to assess model fit and confirm the presence of nonlinear effects. The collinearity diagnosis of independent variables was conducted in this study, and the tolerance and variance inflation factor were calculated.

Results

Basic characteristics of the research participants and the prevalence of myopia

This study included 3,220 participants, comprising 54.81% boys and 45.19% girls. The mean age was 8.95 ± 1.77 years, with 54.41% aged 9 years or older and 45.59% under 9 years. The mean AL/CR ratio was 3.01 ± 0.13, with 52.61% and 47.39% of participants having an AL/CR of 3 or higher and below 3, respectively. The overall prevalence of myopia was 47.67%. By grade level, the prevalence of myopia was 24.7% in grade 1, 36.3% in grade 2, 51.7% in grade 3, 60.6% in grade 4, 69.3% grade 5, and 75.4% in grade 6 (χ2 = 436.475, p < 0.001) (Fig. 1). These grade-specific trends are consistent with patterns observed in national and regional studies, including post-coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) data [23], which report sharp increases in myopia prevalence beginning in the lower grades of primary school. Association analysis among age, AL/CR, and myopia incidence

A multivariate logistic regression model was constructed to identify factors associated with myopia prevalence, with myopia status (yes/no) as the dependent variable. Independent variables included age, sex, paternal and maternal myopia, premature birth, breastfeeding, per capita monthly income, living area per person, consumption of sugared beverages, sweet food, and fried food, as well as vegetable intake, sleep duration, reading and writing posture, weekend outdoor activity, age of first exposure to electronic products, AL/CR, central corneal thickness, and anterior chamber depth.

After adjusting for potential confounders, the following variables remained significantly associated with the risk of myopia: age (OR = 2.817, p < 0.001), sex (OR = 1.717, p < 0.001), paternal myopia (OR = 1.249, p = 0.017), maternal myopia (OR = 1.522, p < 0.001), posture during reading and writing (OR = 1.302, p = 0.002), per capita monthly income (OR = 1.262, p = 0.007), anterior chamber depth (OR = 3.587, p < 0.001), and AL/CR (OR = 4.444, p < 0.001) (Table 2). Collinearity diagnosis of independent variables was conducted and collinearity was indicated with a tolerance of greater than 0.7 and a variance inflation factor of less than 1.4 (Table 3). Additive interaction analysis

To further evaluate this interaction, age and AL/CR were converted into binary variables and included in the regression model. The analysis indicated that the joint presence of age ≥ 9 years and AL/CR ≥ 3 was associated with a higher myopia prevalence than that expected from the sum of their independent effects (RERI = 10.816, 95% CI 8.027–13.605). The SI was 3.971 times greater than the combined effect of the two factors acting independently (SI = 3.971, 95% CI 3.065–5.143). Furthermore, 70.0% of the excess odds of myopia among participants with both older age and higher AL/CR could be explained by their interaction (API = 0.700, 95% CI 0.636–0.763) (Fig. 5).Fig. 5Additive interaction effect of age and AL/CR on myopia

Discussion

In recent years, myopia has emerged as a significant global public health concern, with a steadily increasing prevalence worldwide (1). China, in particular, has experienced one of the fastest growing myopia rates [30]. Among children and adolescents, the onset of myopia is occurring at younger ages, with an increasing detection rate in lower-grade primary school students. Without timely intervention, affected individuals face a higher risk of progressing to high myopia in adulthood, which may result in permanent vision loss. The primary school years represent a critical period for eye development and the establishment of healthy visual habits, underscoring the importance of this period for prevention and intervention strategies [31].

In the current study, the prevalence of myopia among primary school students in Shenzhen was 47.67%, which is higher than the national average in China (35.6%) [32] and the prevalence in Beijing (37.5%) [33]. This elevated rate may be partly explained by Shenzhen’s urban setting, where students are subjected to more intensive academic demands, reduced outdoor activity, and greater access to electronic devices—factors commonly linked to increased myopia risk. Grade-specific prevalence trends also align with recent national data, including post-COVID findings [23], which show a steep rise in myopia rates with increasing age, particularly after early primary school.

Although the precise mechanisms underlying the onset and progression of myopia remain unclear, it is widely recognized that myopia results from a combination of environmental, behavioral, and genetic factors [9]. Previous studies [16, 34] have described age-related changes in axial length and AL/CR; however, few have examined their combined effects on myopia risk. The current study addresses this gap by evaluating both additive and multiplicative interactions between age and AL/CR. To our knowledge, this is the first study to quantify these interactions in a large sample of Chinese schoolchildren. The results suggest that older age and elevated AL/CR jointly increase the likelihood of myopia, identifying a high-risk subgroup for early screening and targeted intervention.

At birth, the human eye is typically in a hyperopic state. With increasing age, refractive status gradually shifts toward emmetropia or myopia. This study found that individuals with myopia were significantly older than those without myopia, and the prevalence of myopia increased with age. Logistic regression analysis confirmed that age was independently associated with higher odds of myopia, even after adjusting for potential confounders. This finding is consistent with previous studies reporting a strong association between increasing age and myopia in children and adolescents [19, 35].

Previous studies have consistently demonstrated that the prevalence of myopia increases with age in children and adolescents. One study in eastern China reported a myopia prevalences of 32% and 69% in second-grade and fifth-grade students, respectively [36]. An observational cohort of 19 primary schools in Guangzhou revealed a myopia prevalence of 12.0% in first-grade students, which rose to 67.4% among seventh-grade students [37]. Similarly, a school-based cross-sectional study in Qinghai Province observed an increase in myopia prevalence from 2.0% at age 4 to 62.8% at age 12 [38].

Consistent with these findings, our study also revealed a higher prevalence of myopia among girls than boys, a pattern reported in other provinces in China [39] as well as in international studies [40]. This sex disparity may be explained by both behavioral and biological factors. Girls tend to more time indoors, engage in less outdoor activity, and perform more near work tasks compared with boys [41]. Moreover, earlier puberty onset in girls has been linked to earlier axial elongation and increased susceptibility to myopia [42]. Females are also more likely to progress to high myopia, possibly owing to a combination of hormonal, anatomical, and behavioral influences [43].

Logistic regression analysis indicated that students with myopic parents had a higher likelihood of myopia compared to those whose parents were not myopic. This finding is consistent with previous studies indicating that children with two parents with myopia are more likely to develop myopia than those with one or no parents with myopia[44, 45]. The association likely reflects both inherited genetic predispositions and shared environmental exposures [46]. Studies have shown that children of parents with myopia tend to exhibit longer axial length and more negative spherical equivalent values [45], supporting a potential biological mechanism for familial aggregation of myopia.

Incorrect reading and writing posture (e.g., the eyes being less than one foot from the book, the chest being less than one fist away from the edge of the table, or the distance between the pen tip and fingers exceeding 1 inch) was also associated with a greater likelihood of myopia, aligning with previous research findings [47]. Additionally, students from families with lower per capita income showed a higher myopia prevalence. This may reflect differences in health awareness, as lower-income families may pay less attention to changes in their children’s vision and being less likely to implement preventive measures to reduce the risk of myopia.

Numerous studies have demonstrated a strong association between ocular biometric parameters, such as AL and anterior chamber depth, and myopia prevalence [48, 49]. Research has established that the AL/CR ratio is a crucial indicator of myopia progression [50], with an AL/CR > 3 considered a high-risk factor for the transition from emmetropia to myopia [51]. Zadnik et al. [52] reported that factors such as AL, corneal refractive power, and intraocular lens power are effective predictors of myopia onset. Zhang et al. [18] developed a myopia prediction model incorporating AL, anterior chamber depth, lens thickness, vitreous chamber depth, and corneal curvature, which successfully predicted myopia development 3 years later. This model was validated in a cohort of Singaporean children of the same age group. In our study, the AL/CR demonstrated the strongest association with myopia prevalence, consistent with previous findings [51].

Myopia results from an interplay among genetic, environmental, and behavioral factors, often interacting with one another. However, no studies have specifically explored the interaction between age and AL/CR in relation to myopia prevalence among children and adolescents. In this study, we employed both multiplicative and additive models to analyze the interaction between age and AL/CR. The results revealed significant positive interactions, indicating a combined effect of these two factors on the risk of myopia. Additionally, the association between age and myopia prevalence varied with changes in AL/CR. Specifically, myopia prevalence increased with age, and higher AL/CR values amplified this prevalence, with the highest rates observed when both age and AL/CR were elevated.

This study had some limitations. First, as a cross-sectional study, causal relationships and temporal direction among age, AL/CR, and myopia prevalence could not be established. Longitudinal designs could clarify temporality in age-AL/CR-myopia sequences. Second, the diagnosis of myopia was based on noncycloplegic autorefraction. While this screening method has been shown to have high sensitivity and specificity in children and adolescents (53), it may slightly overestimate myopia prevalence owing to accommodative error, particularly among younger participants. This may have affected the accuracy of our estimates and potentially biased the observed interaction between age and AL/CR. Cycloplegic refraction was not used because of logistical constraints commonly encountered in large-scale, school-based screenings. Third, questionnaire data were self-reported by parents or guardians, which may introduce recall bias. However, efforts were made to reduce this bias with clear reference periods and standardized close-ended questions; moreover, the questionnaire demonstrated strong test–retest reliability in our previous validation work. Fourth, this study included only three primary schools in Shenzhen, an urban and economically developed area, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to the broader student population in China. Future studies should incorporate schools from diverse geographic and socioeconomic settings and incorporate larger sample sizes. Lastly, refractive status was classified based on right-eye measurements only to avoid inter-eye correlation issues. Given the high symmetry of refractive error between eyes in children, this approach is commonly used and provides a representative estimate for population-level analysis.

In conclusion, both age and AL/CR were significantly associated with myopia prevalence in this population, and the interactions between these factors were both multiplicative and additive, influencing myopia prevalence. Therefore, effectively prevention and control of myopia in children and adolescents requires regular monitoring and assessment, particularly for older children and those with elevated AL/CR ratios. Timely intervention may help mitigate the risk of myopia onset development and progression.
