Mid‐term survival in patients awaiting heart and kidney transplantation with Impella 5.5 support
Authors: Smruti Desai, Smit Paghdar, Jose Ruiz, Ji‐Min Jang, Sharan Malkani, Daniel S Yip, Juan Leoni, Jose Nativi, Basar Sareyyupoglu, Kevin Landolfo, Si Pham, Parag Patel, Rohan Goswami
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPatients with end‐stage heart failure and chronic kidney disease requiring dual‐organ transplantation (DOT) face significant challenges in utilizing durable mechanical circulatory support due to the risks associated with renal replacement therapies (RRTs) and multi‐organ failure. Given the limited options available for long‐term support in this patient population, there remains a critical need for alternative strategies to optimize end‐organ function and bridge patients safely to transplant. With prolonged waitlist times for DOT, we present our experience with the Impella 5.5 as temporary mechanical circulatory support, demonstrating its potential to provide hemodynamic stability and support as a bridge to transplantation (BTT) in this complex cohort.MethodsA single‐centre retrospective review was completed of all patients listed for single‐organ transplantation or DOT between December 2019 and November 2022 at Mayo Clinic in Florida, supported by the Impella 5.5 intended as BTT. The focus of this analysis was patients requiring RRT or listed for heart/kidney transplantation. Data were extracted from the electronic health record at baseline and during their transplant episode of care after institutional review board approval as exempt status for retrospective data collection.ResultsA total of 41 patients were supported with Impella 5.5, intended as BTT. All patients underwent successful transplantation. We focus on the 10 patients with Impella support who underwent DOT. In the DOT group, the median age at transplantation was 63 years (59–66), with nine males and one female. The baseline median ejection fraction was 19% (15–22), with 50% Caucasian and 50% African American and an even split between ischaemic and non‐ischaemic aetiology. Median body mass index was 30 kg/m2 (26–31), and 60% were in blood group O. The median time on the waitlist for DOT patients was 53 days (29–75). Perioperative management of DOT Impella patients demonstrated baseline haemodynamics of RA 11 mmHg (7–16), mean PA 36 mmHg (32–47), PCWP 29 mmHg (21–35), mixed venous saturation (SVO2%) 51 (46–61) and Fick CI 2.03 L/min/m2 (1.66–2.5). Post‐Impella placement haemodynamics demonstrated significant improvements in RA pressure to 5 mmHg (4–8), P = 0.02, SVO2 to 70% (65–72), P = 0.002, and Fick CI to 5.5 (5.2–8), P = 0.03. The average duration of support was 44 days (range 10–94). The median glomerular filtration rate at baseline was 27 mmol/L (16–29). Twenty‐four hour urine protein averaged 168 mg/24 h (range 87–328), with the 24 h creatinine clearance of 29 mg/24 h (range 24–35). Eight of the 10 patients required continuous or intermittent RRT before DOT. The median total duration of RRT (including Impella support) was 36 days (9–72). DOT recipients had a 1 year survival of 90%, with an average follow‐up of 432 days.ConclusionsOur findings demonstrate that prolonged use of the Impella 5.5 provides safe and effective haemodynamic support for patients with end‐stage heart and kidney failure awaiting dual‐organ transplantation. With a 1 year survival rate of 90%, our data suggest that Impella 5.5 can be a viable alternative to traditional support strategies, particularly in patients who are otherwise limited by RRTs. As dual‐organ transplantation becomes more prevalent, the Impella 5.5 offers a promising bridge to transplant, improving both short‐term and long‐term outcomes in this complex patient population.Keywords: heart failure, heart transplant, Impella, kidney transplant, mechanical support, transplant

Introduction

Patients with end‐stage heart failure and chronic kidney disease requiring dual‐organ transplant (DOT) are often limited in their ability to utilize dischargeable mechanical circulatory support [e.g., durable left ventricular assist device (LVAD)] due to the risk of infection or ineligibility attributed to renal replacement therapy (RRT) needs. 1 Survival in patients with end‐stage renal disease requiring RRT prior to durable LVAD placement is extremely limited, with reported survival of fewer than 3 weeks. 2 Survival based on the bridge‐to‐transplant strategy has been comprehensively evaluated after the 2018 UNOS allocation change for heart transplant listing. Improved survival has been reported after transplant in patients that did not utilize LVAD support as their BTT method compared with durable LVAD at one and 5 years. 3 This highlights an unmet need in end‐stage heart and kidney failure patients. Most patients awaiting DOT have an increased waitlist time, well beyond 14 days, prior to organ transplantation in the United States. The approved duration of support in patients with percutaneously placed Impella 5.5 with Smart Assist is less than 14 days by the FDA and 30 days by the EU CE mark. 4 We describe our single‐centre experience with the use of Impella 5.5 as temporary mechanical circulatory support (tMCS) in patients awaiting DOT. Our institution has supported 41 patients with Impella 5.5, intended as a bridge to transplant (BTT), of which all were transplanted. We focus our analysis and data presentation on the 10 patients who successfully underwent DOT after utilizing Impella 5.5 as BTT.

Methods

A single‐centre retrospective review was completed of all patients listed for single‐ or dual‐organ transplantation between December 2019 and November 2022 at Mayo Clinic in Florida, supported by the Impella 5.5 intended as BTT. Patients requiring RRT (CVVH or intermittent dialysis) or listed for heart/kidney transplantation are the focus of this analysis. Data were extracted from the electronic health record at baseline and during their transplant episode of care after institutional review board approval as exempt status for retrospective data collection. Variables are reported as median and interquartile range or as mean and range where appropriate. IBM SPSS version 27 was utilized for statistical analysis with a P value cutoff of 0.05 utilizing either χ 2 or Mann–Whitney U tests for significance.

Patient selection

Individuals with New York Heart Association class 3 or greater, AHA Stage D in need of or already on inotrope therapy with progression to dual inotropes or with evidence of refractoriness to optimizing guideline‐directed medical therapy, and those with clinical testing (e.g., CPET, RHC and 6 min walk) consistent with the need for initiating a transplant evaluation fit the spectrum of patients selected for the utilization of surgically placed Impella support as a BTT. Patients with RV failure (tricuspid annular plane systolic excursion < 1.8 mm, pulmonary artery (PA) pulsatility index ≤ 1.5, renal failure requiring RRT) not considered LVAD candidates may undergo Impella 5.5 placement if they meet transplant criteria. The potential for optimizing patients and their end‐organ function with tMCS before declaring them unsuitable for OHT should be considered. This may increase candidacy for OHT or provide options to recovery of native cardiac function.

Institutional standard of care

Our institutional practice includes patients' medical, physical and nutritional optimization as best tolerated in outpatient, with or without continuous home inotrope therapy. Subsequently, those patients with clinical or laboratory evidence of decline (e.g., increasing serum creatinine, progressive deterioration in functional status, inability to maintain a healthy weight or intolerant of cardiopulmonary rehabilitation) are considered for admission and optimization. The standard of care for optimization at our institution for advanced heart failure patients includes the placement of a peripherally inserted central catheter (PICC) to monitor central venous pressures and record mixed venous saturation, thereby allowing us to calculate Fick cardiac output and index. We then devise a care plan based on the patient's Stevenson profile. Finally, appropriate care, as determined by the treating physician, is undertaken. For those individuals that remain refractory to therapy, considerations for advanced treatments follow suit.

Multidisciplinary Impella management

Monitoring after Impella support at our institution is standardized and consists of weekly trans‐thoracic echocardiogram and daily chest radiograph with daily securement device assessment for patient comfort and serum lactate dehydrogenase, renal profile and complete blood counts. Additionally, patients have baseline PA catheterization during their assessment for requiring advanced mechanical support if not already present before inotrope initiation. Most patients are monitored with an indwelling PA catheter for up to 96 h after Impella placement. Lastly, daily multidisciplinary rounds comprising—cardiovascular intensive care, transplant cardiology, cardiothoracic surgery, physical therapy and critical care nursing teams review patient maintenance of support and potential needs for escalating care.

VIS is the summation of maximally dosed inotrope or vasopressor requirements and accounts for differential measurement units—normalized to the patient by BSA inclusion. We utilized the VIS to standardize the nonmechanical support‐based needs of patients within the perioperative period and further demonstrate the potential benefit of Impella support during this period. VIS has been traditionally used in post‐cardiac surgery manuscripts to describe mortality risk and estimate the severity of critical illness. 7

The kidney donor profile index (KDPI) was determined by the Organ Procurement and Transplantation Network (OPTN) methods based on standardized criteria. This measurement numerically quantifies an index based on donor factors, clinical parameters and demographics to objectively assess the quality of the deceased donor kidney relative to other recovered kidneys. Lower KDPI values are associated with better donor quality, longevity and survival in clinical practice. 8 KDPI is a universally accepted method of assessing donor kidney graft function, which may provide insight into the potential for post‐transplant graft failure or delayed graft function.

Results

Of the 41 patients listed for heart transplantation and supported with Impella 5.5, intended as BTT, 10 patients during the review period necessitated heart and kidney transplantation and are the focus of the analysis outlined below. All 41 patients reached heart or heart–kidney transplantation without device‐related complications.

In the DOT group, the median age at transplantation was 63 years (59–66), with nine males and one female patient. The baseline median ejection fraction was 19% (15–22), with 50% Caucasian and 50% African American. There was an even split between ischaemic and non‐ischaemic cardiomyopathy. At transplantation, the median body mass index (BMI) was 30 kg/m2 (26–31). Most patients were in blood group O (60%). The average duration of support with Impella 5.5 was 44 days (range 10–94 days) (Figure 1).

Individualized duration of support. DOT, dual‐organ transplant.

Before support, all patients were managed for heart failure cardiogenic shock based on standardized care plans at our institution, and when deemed appropriate by the treating provider, they were considered for escalation to Impella 5.5, as outlined in our methods. The surgical placement of the device was performed by cardiothoracic surgery in conjunction with cardiothoracic anaesthesia who monitored these patients under general anaesthesia. The standard right or left axillary cutdown approach was used. All patients were extubated within 12 h of Impella placement and maintained active ambulation and participated in daily physical therapy until their transplant.

Pretransplant lab profile

The median serum creatinine before Impella placement was 2.67 mg/dL (2.17–4.28) and GFR of 27 mmol/L (16–29). Prior to being listed for DOT, a workup was undertaken to evaluate renal function in conjunction with our transplant nephrologists. The 24 h urine protein averaged 168 mg/24 h (range 87–328), with the 24 h creatinine clearance of 29 mg/24 h (range 24–35). Eight of the 10 patients required continuous or intermittent RRT before DOT. The median total duration of RRT (including Impella support duration) was 36 days (9–72). The median time on the waitlist for patients requiring DOTs was 53 days (29–75). Perioperative management of Impella patients demonstrated baseline haemodynamics of RA 11 mmHg (7–16), mean PA 36 mmHg (32–47), PCWP 29 mmHg (21–35), mixed venous saturation (SVO2%) 51 (46–61), Fick CO 4.15 L/min (3.4–5) and Fick CI 2.03 L/min/m2 (1.66–2.5). The intraoperative care of Impella 5.5 at the time of organ explantation is managed as follows: After cross‐clamp of the aorta and initiation of cardiopulmonary bypass, the Impella 5.5 is stopped. The device is cut at the level of the aortic valve below the cross‐clamp and explanted in a retrograde fashion from the axillary artery cutdown site at the time of arterial repair. If necessary, graft thrombectomy may be performed to remove stagnant clots.

Our post‐operative care was standardized with a multidisciplinary team approach, including transplant cardiologists, nephrologists, surgeons and critical care team members.

Discussion

The utilization of tMCS devices was greatly impacted by the changes made by UNOS in 2018. These changes aimed to improve organ allocation and transplant patients with the highest acuity of illness. The increase in tMCS use has favoured IABP. As reported by Liu et al., there was a noticeable increase of 15× (3% vs. 45%) in IABP use in the current era compared with before 2018. 9 This may be due to comfort with the IABP, preconceived notions based on experience from past Impella 5.0 complications (surgical or medical), or potentially from lack of Impella 5.5 outcomes data.

Complications of tMCS can be significant, potentially resulting in patients being removed from transplant candidacy or worse. Our institution and others have reported such complications, including IABP migration, stroke, bleeding and vascular occlusion. 10 , 11 Patients awaiting dual‐organ transplantation have an increased risk of adverse events in the setting of multi‐organ failure and shock. Given this, we must further expand our knowledge on Impella 5.5 in patients awaiting dual‐organ transplantation.

Our data look to provide first‐hand experience using Impella 5.5 for prolonged support (>14 days) in patients with end‐stage heart and kidney failure in cardiogenic shock. We address knowledge gaps within organ transplantation and multiorgan support. In the sections outlined below, we discuss the impact of changes in UNOS criteria, duration of support and pretransplant optimization related to the overall outcome in patients awaiting dual‐organ transplantation.

Duration of support and outcomes

In patients with chronic heart and kidney failure, it is imperative to provide a solution that can optimize filling pressures and end‐organ oxygen delivery while safely supporting patients awaiting organ transplantation—and, if possible, decrease the time to transplantation. The Impella 5.5 with Smart Assist provides this option while maintaining the ambulatory status of our patients with limited complications over an increased duration of support, as outlined above. We have previously described and presented renal improvement and recovery after Impella placement. It has also been seen in other institutions, published as a series of four patients by Zaky et al. 4 , 12 We provide further expansion on this data by including 10 patients presented here who received DOTs after successful BTT with Impella support. Our data highlight the feasibility and safety of long‐term support, beyond the standard indication of 14 days, in patients with cardiogenic shock and renal failure without increased bleeding, haemolysis, thrombosis or right heart failure.

Our cohort of DOT recipients has a 1 year survival of 90%, compared with the reported 1 year survival of patients transplanted after BTT with durable LVAD, 80.3% 13 (Figure 3). Of our DOT recipients, 80% have maintained normal cardiac and renal allograft function after transplantation, with an average follow‐up of 432 days. Two patients experienced renal graft dysfunction and eventual graft failure required post‐transplant dialysis. One is currently awaiting renal retransplantation and has required RRT for 812 days at follow‐up. The second patient expired due to occult primary sclerosing cholangitis after DOT, surviving 88 days until enrolling in hospice care.

Impact on tMCS use after 2018: Protocol implementation and team‐based approach

The utilization of percutaneously placed tMCS increased after the UNOS change in 2018. 14 Most increases have occurred in tertiary transplant centres rather than all cardiac intensive care units. 15 IABP as tMCS is historically favoured compared with Impella 5.5 placement. 16 Although IABP placement via the axillary position is possible, published data have shown increased complication rates despite a limited support duration. Our experience in IABP support use for patients awaiting heart or heart–kidney transplantation has demonstrated increased complication rates of migration and arterial thrombosis. 10 The limited increase in Impella 5.5 use likely has to do with prior experience, individual centre practice patterns or surgical comfort in placing axillary devices via a tunnelled graft. 17

As outlined in the methods section of our manuscript, creating individualized protocols to optimize outcomes is critical. The utility of IABP support preferentially favours ischaemic cardiomyopathy patients based on its mode of action. 18 Our experience with 41 patients supported with the Impella 5.5 intended as BTT has confirmed the limited haemolysis, improved mobility and minimal vascular complication risk—which is also demonstrated in the literature. 19 This was unchanged in patients awaiting heart‐alone or dual‐organ transplantation.

General use of the Impella 5.5 remains limited—as such, we include further discussion based on our previously published data. 20 Areas that remain challenging due to the lack of multisite data are (1) risk of anaemia, (2) anticoagulation strategies and haemolysis, (3) infection risk and (4) RV failure.

Our centre 's understanding has grown significantly after implementing Impella 5.5 instead of Impella 5.0. When understanding the risk of anaemia, especially in patients with heart and kidney failure, it is critical to perform a baseline assessment of iron stores and erythropoiesis, avoiding transfusion to decrease human leukocyte antigen (HLA) sensitization at all costs. We have not observed a significant increase in the need for transfusions in our patients—this is partly due to aggressive repletion of iron with intravenous iron dextrose (2–500 mg daily for 3 days) and repeat iron panels at set 60 day intervals. Furthermore, in patients with RRT needs with Impella 5.5 support, our team's threshold for transfusion remains at <8 g of haemoglobin or if the patient has a profound decrease in mixed venous saturation from baseline, which other causes cannot explain.

Initially, we anticoagulated all patients with intravenous heparin and heparinized purge solution. However, we noticed an increased rate of false‐negative heparin‐induced thrombocytopenia (HIT) testing. 21 Our daily PTT goals range from 50 to 70 s, and for those with discordant PTT results and frequent titration, a thromboelastogram is used intermittently to confirm anticoagulant efficacy.

After new indications for use were released by Abiomed in April 2022, we converted to sodium‐bicarbonate purge 22 and have worked to identify risk factors for systemic anticoagulant needs, aside from Impella 5.5 support alone. Other anticoagulants, such as bivalirudin, are used when patients develop HIT. Despite the underlying platelet dysfunction in patients with end‐stage renal failure, we have not had an increased rate of bleeding or thrombosis—as monitored by daily lactate dehydrogenase, haemoglobin, Impella console and serum lactate (we do not use plasma free haemoglobin at our institution due to the increased turnaround time for this send‐out test of 1 week). Systemic anticoagulant‐free strategies are being considered, but more data need to be analysed.

With regard to infection after device placement, we report no infections in our entire cohort. We also do not have any infections in other support devices, such as our PICC or tunnelled venous dialysis catheters. Our protocol for optimizing patient care has centred around standardizing both positioning and dressing of all lines, completed once each week by a team focusing on central line management (outside of standard nursing care and assessment). It is not unexpected to experience initial post‐implantation swelling at the site of the axillary cutdown, and we have performed an ultrasound serially to assess the potential fluid collection—however, no such collections have been identified.

Lastly, right heart failure can be a concern in patients with issues managing intravascular volume (e.g., RRT needs) after Impella 5.5 placement. Data to outline right ventricular dysfunction after left ventricular optimization focus on the deformation of the right ventricle and the progression of underlying tricuspid or pulmonic valve regurgitation. 23 Large data do not exist regarding right heart failure after Impella 5.5. However, based on data presented here, we demonstrate that there is improvement in PA pressure, wedge pressure and stability of the PA pulsatility index—all pointing towards the stability of the right heart after Impella 5.5 placement—with or without vasoactive use as background therapy.

Peri‐transplant physical optimization

Utilizing the axillary approach, avoiding a full or partial sternotomy or thoracotomy, the Impella 5.5 minimizes the procedural risk associated with surgically placed devices—bleeding, anaesthesia time, right heart strain and postoperative recovery. 17 Physical therapy is a crucial benefit of all axillary devices, and functional status prior to organ transplant is known to impact short and long‐term outcomes. 20 Our institutional focus is on early ambulation and aggressive rehabilitation for patients awaiting organ transplantation. The physical and occupational therapy protocols are implemented to provide essential exercise guides for our patients and nursing staff. These can then be independently performed at the bedside or within our unit to improve conditioning. This includes using a bedside treadmill, stationary bicycle, supported quadricep exercise ramps and supervised time outside the hospital for sunshine privileges. Utilizing the manufacturer‐recommended three‐point anchoring system is followed by our nursing and physical therapy staff. We have only had one therapy‐related complication requiring pump exchange, and this occurred in our second Impella patient, with no complications in the following 40. 24 Along with nutritional optimization, this likely has a significant impact on outcomes after dual‐organ transplantation. 25 , 26

Limitations

As a retrospective evaluation of outcomes, our data are hypothesis‐generating and should be further confirmed prospectively. As a large academic centre utilizing Impella 5.5 intended for BTT, most patients are primarily heart‐only transplants. Dual‐organ outcomes are limited in the literature, and despite only 10 patients supported with Impella, this represents nearly 25% of our total yearly volumes—since our program inception in 2019, this volume has been maintained. Given the limited data on Impella and DOT, 10 should not be considered a small sample size. 27 We performed multiple steps to confirm data validation. Given the acuity of patient illness, necessitating expedited evaluations for organ transplantation, pretransplant renal data were unavailable in four patients, limited to the 24 h urine protein/creatinine clearance. Post‐implantation haemodynamic data were also limited in some patients due to the individual attending physician and the care team's decision to promptly remove PA catheters to reduce the risk of infection or deep venous thrombosis development.

Conclusions

Our data explore Impella 5.5's impact on the clinical management of heart failure cardiogenic shock patients necessitating dual‐organ transplantation. As dual‐organ transplantation becomes more common, prolonged support with axillary placed Impella 5.5 is demonstrably safe and feasible, potentially improving end‐organ function and patient mobility while limiting device‐associated complications prior to transplant.
