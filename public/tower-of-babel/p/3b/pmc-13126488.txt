Genome-Wide Meta-Analysis for High Myopia Provides Insights into Disease Mechanisms and Reveals a Causal Link to Primary Open-Angle Glaucoma
Authors: Kazuya Morino, Akira Meguro, Masao Nagasaki, Ikuko N. Motoike, Masato Akiyama, Takahisa Kawaguchi, Shogo Numa, Yuki Mori, Shota Yasukura, Masahiro Akada, Shin-ya Nakao, Takuro Kamei, Ai Nakata, Masaki Takeuchi, Norihiro Yamada, Yuki Mizuki, Takeshi Yoshida, Yukihide Momozawa, Yoichiro Kamatani, Hayato Anzawa, Yasuharu Tabara, Fumihiko Matsuda, Kengo Kinoshita, Kyoko Ohno-Matsui, Nobuo Fuse, Masayuki Yamamoto, Akitaka Tsujikawa, Nobuhisa Mizuki, Masahiro Miyake, Takeo Nakayama, Takeo Nakayama, Akihiro Sekine, Shinji Kosugi, Yasuharu Tabara, Fumihiko Matsuda
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeTo investigate the genetic architecture of high myopia.DesignA meta-analysis of genome-wide association studies (meta-GWASs).ParticipantsA total of 7867 individuals with high myopic eyes and 21 700 controls were enrolled.MethodsWe performed a meta-GWAS comprising 3910 cases with high myopic eyes and 5718 controls in the discovery stage. A replication study included an independent set of 3957 participants with high myopic eyes and 15 982 control participants. Functional enrichment analysis was conducted using FUMA and ToppFun. We assessed shared genetic architectures between high myopia and 4 phenotypes (2 ocular diseases and 2 biometric features) using the LDSC software, followed by the evaluation of causality utilizing a bidirectional inverse variance–weighted Mendelian randomization (IVW-MR) analysis with sensitivity analyses.Main Outcome MeasuresThe association between single-nucleotide polymorphisms and high myopia.ResultsWe identified 10 loci exceeding genome-wide significance (P < 5.0 × 10–8); 2 of those, rs2727196 in ADAMTS17 (odds ratio [OR] = 1.13, P = 2.91 × 10–8) and rs6564696 in MAFTRR (OR = 1.19, P = 1.78 × 10–10), were novel. Functional enrichment analysis revealed a significant role of 8 pathways in the pathogenesis of high myopia, including 2 pathways regarding acetylcholine-gated channels (gene ontology [GO]:0005892 and GO:0022848) and 4 pathways regarding Golgi body (GO:0033116, GO:0032580, GO:0030134, and GO:0031985). Genetic correlation analysis revealed shared polygenic architecture between high myopia and primary open-angle glaucoma (POAG) (rg [the measure of genetic correlation] = 0.41, P = 2.60 × 10–3). In the IVW-MR analysis, a causal relationship from high myopia to POAG was confirmed (OR = 1.14, P = 3.92 × 10–3), whereas an inverse causal relationship was not noticed (P = 0.97).ConclusionsOur study identified 2 novel high myopia–associated loci. Subsequent functional enrichment analysis offered important insights into the molecular biology of high myopia, demonstrating potential therapeutic targets. Previous studies could not definitively determine whether high myopia had a causal effect on POAG due to the potential influence of unmeasured confounders. By utilizing an MR method, which can control for even unmeasured confounders, this study provides data that support a causal association of high myopia on POAG.Financial Disclosure(s)Proprietary or commercial disclosure may be found in the Footnotes and Disclosures at the end of this article.

High myopia, a multifactorial disease influenced by both genetic and environmental factors,1 is a global cause of blindness with a high risk of pathological myopia, represented by posterior staphyloma, myopic maculopathy, myopic macular neovascularization, and high myopia-related optic neuropathy.2,3 By 2050, approximately 1 billion individuals are predicted to be diagnosed with high myopia (9.8% of the world population).4 The increase in the incidence of myopia and high myopia over the past few decades is likely related to environmental factors, such as reduced outdoor activities and increased near-work activities. However, a significant genetic predisposition also plays a crucial role.5, 6, 7 Therefore, genetic investigation of myopia and high myopia is becoming increasingly important.

Genome-wide association studies (GWASs) for myopia have been extensively performed, and more than 400 susceptibility genes have been identified.8, 9, 10, 11, 12, 13, 14 In general, high myopia is considered to be an extension of common myopia, and the Singapore Epidemiology of Eye Diseases study has shown that the polygenic risk scores for myopia increased in the order of low myopia, moderate myopia, and high myopia.15 In contrast, however, GWASs for high myopia, including several of our previous reports, have identified loci specific for high myopia (i.e., not reported as susceptibility loci for myopia).16, 17, 18 Furthermore, previous epidemiological and clinical studies have indicated that high myopia and pathological myopia might not be a simple extension of common myopia.19,20 Considering these facts, the triggers of developing high myopia may differ to some extent from those of common myopia, and extending the genome-wide association analysis for high myopia is crucial for evaluating the similarities and differences in genetic architecture between high myopia and common myopia.

In this study, we aimed to conduct a large genome-wide case-control meta-analysis for high myopia defined by axial length followed by replication in an independent cohort and a functional enrichment analysis to investigate the functional background of high myopia. Additionally, we aimed to evaluate the shared polygenic architecture with other ocular diseases or biometric features and conduct a two-sample Mendelian randomization (MR) analysis for examining the causality.

Methods

Ethics and Inclusion

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Boards of Kyoto University Graduate School of Medicine and the Nagahama Municipal Review Board (G278 and G566), the Institutional Review Board of Yokohama City University (A150122004), and the Research Ethical Committee of Tohoku Medical Megabank Organization (2023-1006). All procedures were conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. All participants provided written informed consent.

Participant Enrollment

In the discovery stage meta-analysis, we utilized 4 cohorts, namely, the Kyoto High Myopia Genome Cohort, healthy Japanese cohort from Aichi Cancer Center Research Institute, Nagahama Study cohort (phase 2), and Yokohama City University Genome Cohort. Although detailed information on these cohorts is provided elsewhere,9,16,17,20, 21, 22, 23 we have briefly described them in the Supplementary Methods (available at www.ophthalmologyscience.org). The data of participants were divided into 5 data sets (Asian Screening Array [ASA], 610K, whole-genome sequencing [WGS], 500K, and OmniExpress; summarized in Table S1, available at www.ophthalmologyscience.org) based on the affiliations and genotyping platforms. In cases where individuals were genotyped on more than one platform, we prioritized data sets in the following order: WGS > ASA > 610K > 500K > OmniExpress. Each participant was included in only 1 data set based on this hierarchy, ensuring no overlaps between cohorts.

Description of the 5 Genome-Wide Association Study Data Sets

The ASA data set comprised 3105 individuals from the Kyoto High Myopia Genome Cohort and Nagahama Study (phase 2), who were genotyped using the Infinium ASA-24 BeadChip (Illumina Inc) or Illumina Infinium Japanese Screening Array-24 BeadChip. The 610K data set comprised 1694 individuals from the Kyoto High Myopia Genome Cohort and healthy Japanese cohort from the Aichi Cancer Center Research Institute who were genotyped using either the Illumina Human610-Quad BeadChip, Illumina HumanHap550 BeadChip, or Illumina Human660W-Quad BeadChip. The WGS data set consisted of 952 individuals from the Kyoto High Myopia Genome Cohort and Nagahama Study (phase 2) who underwent whole-genome sequencing. For these 3 data sets, participants with axial length ≥26 mm in at least one eye were defined as cases, and those with axial length <24 mm in both eyes were defined as controls, except for the healthy Japanese cohort from the Aichi Cancer Center Research Institute, which served as the general population control.

In the 500K and OmniExpress data sets, we included 3110 individuals who were genotyped using the GeneChip Human Mapping 500K Array (Affymetrix) or Illumina Infinium OmniExpress BeadChip from the Yokohama City University Genome Cohort. For these 2 data sets, participants with axial length ≥26 mm in both eyes were defined as cases, and those with axial length <24 mm in both eyes were defined as controls.

The detailed genotyping and quality control methods are described in the Supplementary Methods.

Genome-Wide Meta-Analysis

For the analyses of ASA and WGS data sets, we applied the SAIGE software v.1.1.9 (https://saigegit.github.io/SAIGE-doc/), which enabled us to consider sample relatedness between individuals when analyzing binary traits.24 We performed genome-wide logistic regression analysis for high myopia based on the generalized mixed model with adjustment for age, sex, and the first principal component. For the 610K data set, genome-wide logistic regression analysis for high myopia was conducted, adjusting for age, sex, and the first principal component using the PLINK software (https://www.cog-genomics.org/plink2). For the 500K and Omni Express data sets, genome-wide logistic regression analysis for high myopia was performed with an additive model in HelixTree SVS software v.8.8.3 (Golden Helix, Inc), adjusting for age, sex, and the first principal component.

The results of 5 GWAS were meta-analyzed using the METAL software.25 Meta-analysis was performed with the inverse variance-based method and the fixed-effects model. Those single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) present in fewer than 2 of the 5 GWAS data sets and with a heterogeneity P value < 0.05 were excluded in the discovery stage. P values < 0.05, 1.0 × 10–5, and 5.0 × 10–8 indicated statistically significant threshold, genome-wide suggestive threshold, and genome-wide significant threshold, respectively. In this analysis, we first identified the lead SNPs with genome-wide significant or suggestive associations and extracted their proxy SNPs (linkage disequilibrium [LD] r2 = 1 in Japanese in Tokyo) within each region. Subsequently, we carried them forward to the replication stage. Pairwise measures of LD r2 in Japanese in Tokyo were obtained via LDlink v.5.1 (https://ldlink.nih.gov/?tab=home) using the LDlinkR package, v.1.1.2, for R software (The R Foundation for Statistical Computing).26,27 Unless otherwise specified, R v.4.3.0 was used for statistical analysis.

Replication Analysis

For the replication stage, we enrolled 3957 highly myopic participants with axial length ≥26 mm in at least one eye, and 15 982 control participants with axial length <24 mm in both eyes from the Tohoku Medical Megabank Organization Eye Study. The details of the Tohoku Medical Megabank Organization Eye Study have been previously described.28, 29, 30 The samples were genotyped using the Affymetrix Axiom Japonica Array (v2), and imputation was performed using a phased reference panel of 3552 Japanese individuals included in the cohort studies of the Tohoku Medical Megabank project.31,32 We conducted the genome-wide logistic regression analysis based on the generalized mixed linear model with adjustment for age and sex using the GCTA software,33 and looked up SNPs of interest from the output results. Finally, the results of discovery and replication stages were meta-analyzed using the fixed-effects model with the METAL, similar to that of the discovery stage. Because we evaluated 26 independent regions, statistical significance in the replication stage was set at a P value < 1.92 × 10–3 (0.05/26) for this look-up according to the Bonferroni correction method. The detailed genotyping and quality control methods are described in the Supplementary Methods.

Functional Enrichment Analysis

We conducted a functional enrichment analysis for investigating the functional background of high myopia. A positional gene mapping of the SNPs was performed using the SNP2GENE function of FUMA. FUMA is an online software for genetic research, which integrates 18 biological databases and tools for processing GWAS summary statistics.34 Detailed settings and criteria for this analysis are described in Table S2 (available at www.ophthalmologyscience.org). Subsequently, we performed a gene ontology (GO) analysis using genes mapped by the ToppFun function of ToppGene. ToppGene is an online software that evaluates functional enrichment from a list of genes based on functional annotations and protein interaction networks.35 We focused on 3 categories of pathways, namely, biological process, cellular component, and molecular function. A Bonferroni-corrected q value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Single-Tissue Expression Quantitative Trait Locus and Splicing Quantitative Trait Locus Queries

We conducted single-tissue expression quantitative trait locus (eQTL) and splicing QTL (sQTL) queries for elucidating the association between the high myopia susceptibility SNPs and expression of transcripts. For single-tissue eQTL queries, we examined genes whose mRNA levels differed by the genotypes of high myopia susceptibility SNPs in the Genotype-Tissue Expression Portal v.8 online database (http://www.gtexportal.org/home/) accessed on October 6, 2022.36 For single-tissue sQTL queries, we also examined genes whose selective splicing was affected by genotypes of high myopia susceptibility SNPs in the Genotype-Tissue Expression Portal.

Genetic Correlation Analysis

To assess shared genetic components between high myopia and other ocular diseases or biometric features, we analyzed genetic correlations using summary statistics obtained from the genome-wide meta-analysis for high myopia of this study in the discovery stage and those of previously reported GWASs conducted with the Japanese population, which were either in-house or publicly available from the Biobank Japan project. For ocular diseases, we targeted 2 diseases, namely, primary open-angle glaucoma (POAG)37 and age-related macular degeneration (AMD).38 For biometric features, we targeted height39 and body mass index.40 Genetic correlations were evaluated by LD score regression analysis using the LDSC software.41,42

Mendelian Randomization Analysis

Mendelian randomization analysis is a novel method that allows for causal inference from results of observational studies using genomic information as an instrumental variable. Given the observed genetic correlation between high myopia and glaucoma, we conducted a two-sample MR analysis for evaluating their causal relationship (Fig S1, available at www.ophthalmologyscience.org). In the main MR analysis of high myopia to POAG, we selected independent genome-wide significant lead SNPs from the genome-wide meta-analysis of this study. We excluded SNPs that were weakly correlated with high myopia (F-statistic ≤ 10) in the Japanese population to avoid weak instrument bias43 and SNPs mapped to the POAG susceptibility genes. We matched the remaining SNPs with the GWAS summary statistics for POAG in the Japanese population.37 In the inverse MR analysis of POAG to high myopia, we selected POAG susceptibility SNPs identified through the largest Japanese GWAS for POAG and the largest multiethnic GWAS for POAG.37,44 We excluded (1) SNPs that had the potential to cause weak instrument bias in the Japanese (F-statistic ≤ 10), (2) SNPs mapped to myopia or high myopia susceptibility genes, and (3) SNPs in LD (with r2 ≥ 0.01 in Japanese in Tokyo or within 250 kb) with the most strongly associated SNP in each region. We matched the remaining SNPs with the GWAS summary statistics for high myopia in the Japanese population of this study. In the primary analysis, we used an inverse variance–weighted model to assess the associations between high myopia and POAG. In addition, we conducted 3 sensitivity analyses using other MR methods, that is, MR-Egger, weighted-median, and MR Pleiotropy Residual Sum and Outlier (PRESSO) models. All analyses except for the MR-PRESSO model were conducted using TwoSampleMR v.0.6.2 package in R, and the MR-PRESSO model was used with MRPRESSO package v1.0 in R.

A meta-analysis of discovery and replication data sets identified 10 loci showing a genome-wide significant association with high myopia, including 4 additionally identified loci (Table 3 and Table S3, available at www.ophthalmologyscience.org). All of 10 loci were also statistically significant (P < 1.92 × 10–3) in the replication stage after the Bonferroni correction (Table 3). Two-Sample MR Analysis between High Myopia and POAG

In the primary inverse variance–weighted MR analysis, genetic predisposition to high myopia was significantly associated with the risk of POAG (OR = 1.14 [95% CI, 1.04–1.25], P = 3.92 × 10–3; Table 7). Of the 3 sensitivity analyses for the effects of high myopia on POAG, high myopia was associated with POAG in the weighted-median model (OR = 1.15 [95% CI, 1.01–1.29], P = 2.81 × 10–2) and in the MR-PRESSO model (OR = 1.14 [95% CI, 1.05–1.24], P = 1.42 × 10–2), whereas in the MR-Egger approach, no significant association was noted from high myopia to POAG (OR = 1.29 [95% CI, 0.65–2.54], P = 0.25). The P value for the MR-Egger intercept was not statistically significant (P = 0.74), indicating limited evidence for horizontal pleiotropy. In contrast, genetic predisposition to POAG was not associated with the risk of high myopia in all 4 MR analyses (Table 7). Discussion

In this study, a relatively large genome-wide meta-analysis conducted to date, involving 9628 Japanese individuals and followed by replication in an independent cohort of 19 939 Japanese individuals, we successfully identified 10 susceptibility loci for high myopia, including 2 novel loci, rs2727196 in ADAMTS17 and rs6564696 in MAFTRR. This result enabled a functional enrichment analysis that revealed associations of acetylcholine-gated channel-related pathways and Golgi body–related pathways with the pathogenesis of high myopia. Furthermore, a genetic correlation analysis highlighted the significantly positive correlation between high myopia and POAG, and a bidirectional MR analysis demonstrated that high myopia causally contributed to the development of POAG.

Of the 10 high myopia susceptibility genes identified in this study, many of them have been previously identified as susceptibility genes for intermediate phenotypes such as refractive error and axial length. GJD2, RASGRF1, PRSS56, BMP4, ZC3H11B, and LRRC4C are susceptibility genes for both refractive error and axial length. KCNMA1 is a susceptibility gene for refractive error, and SNTB1 and MAFTRR are susceptibility genes for axial length.8,13,29,45,46 Among 2 genes newly identified as susceptibility genes for high myopia, ADAMTS17 has not been reported to be associated with refractive error or axial length in previous large-scale GWASs; therefore, this gene can be considered a high myopia-specific susceptibility gene. This finding supports the idea that high myopia possesses a distinct genetic component. From a clinical perspective, individuals with an AL of 26–28 mm likely represent a “borderline zone” in which the extension of common myopia and a distinct pathological entity coexist. The proportion of the latter is expected to increase as the axial length lengthens beyond 26 mm, and extreme myopia is hypothesized to be driven by unique genetic and biological mechanisms.20,47 Because our study population (AL ≧ 26 mm) represented a spectrum of these conditions, it is reasonable that our analysis identified substantial overlap with common myopia loci while also successfully detecting the high myopia–specific locus, ADAMTS17. In this context, a potential explanation for this observation that ADAMTS17 is specific to high myopia is that the effect of ADAMTS17 is limited in the early stages of myopia and becomes pronounced with the progression of myopia beyond a certain threshold.

ADAMTS17 encodes a metalloprotease and is a member of the ADAMTS family that plays diverse roles in the formation, remodeling, and destruction of extracellular matrix. ADAMTS17 is considered to interact with fibrillin-1 and regulate its deposition in microfibrils in the extracellular matrix.48 ADAMTS17 is expressed in human fibroblast cells in the sclera and human horizontal, rod photoreceptor, Muller glia, and bipolar cells in the retina (Fig S6, available at www.ophthalmologyscience.org).49,50 Loss of ADAMTS17 function impairs the secretion and extracellular activity of ADAMTS17 protease, leading to Weill–Marchesani syndrome. It is characterized by severe myopia secondary to a small spherical lens, lens dislocation/abnormal positioning, and glaucoma,51 despite presenting with a short axial length.52 In contrast, the results of the current study suggest that a gain of ADAMTS17 function might be associated with axial high myopia because the T allele of rs2727196 in ADAMTS17 is associated with higher intron excision of ADAMTS17 in human tissues (Fig 2), and the allele is also associated with the development of high myopia. This intriguing correlation between axial length and ADAMTS17 function highlights the necessity of further molecular biological studies for investigating the specific role of ADAMTS17, particularly its splicing variation, in the pathogenesis of high myopia.

The current study identified novel pathways for high myopia. Importantly, these pathways were not previously reported, even in the pathway analyses of 3 large-scale GWASs for refractive error.12, 13, 14 Among the identified pathways, involvement of the acetylcholine channel–related pathways is the most important finding because the acetylcholine receptor blocker atropine is the most effective agent for suppressing myopia progression, though the molecular mechanism is not yet clear.53 The finding that the acetylcholine channel–related pathway is associated with high myopia is important because it indicates that high myopia shares, at least in part, the same molecular biological background as that of childhood myopia. In contrast, involvement of the Golgi body–related pathways is an unexpected but important discovery. Golgi body is an essential cellular organelle for transporting, modifying, and packaging proteins and lipids into vesicles for delivery to targeted destinations and is known to be involved in neurodegenerative diseases such as Alzheimer disease.54,55 Although, how Golgi body is associated with high myopia is unknown, disorders in the structure, function, and delivery of secretory and membrane proteins caused by abnormalities in Golgi body may modify childhood myopia, leading to the development of high myopia.

Multiple epidemiological studies have shown that myopia and high myopia are risk factors for the development of POAG.56, 57, 58, 59, 60 In our genetic correlation analysis, POAG was the only phenotype among 4 that showed a significant correlation. While previous reports on European populations have also indicated a significant genetic correlation between high myopia and POAG, our observed genetic correlation (rg = 0.41 [95% CI, 0.15–0.67]) was higher than that in Europeans (rg = 0.23 [95% CI, 0.05–0.41]).61 This suggests that individuals with high myopia in Japanese or East Asian populations may have a higher susceptibility to POAG than that of Europeans. To evaluate this causal relationship, we conducted an MR analysis. Although MR analyses between myopia and POAG have been previously conducted, indicating bidirectional causal effects,62 we successfully reproduced a causal relationship from high myopia to POAG in the East Asian population. Given that the MR analysis is a robust causal inference method that is capable of controlling unmeasured confounders, our findings provide definitive evidence that high myopia is a risk factor of glaucoma.

In this study, we conducted a relatively large GWAS conducted to date on high myopia, identifying 2 novel genome-wide significant loci. However, there are some limitations to this research. First, the sample size presents certain limitations. Increasing the sample size and focusing on more extreme phenotypic cases via GWAS might enhance our understanding of the molecular mechanisms underpinning high myopia. Second, this study solely focused on a Japanese ethnic cohort. Subsequent studies are essential to ascertain if the genomic regions pinpointed in our study are relevant to high myopia in diverse ethnic populations. Replication in external cohorts, particularly those of different ancestries, remains a key component of validating newly identified susceptibility loci. Third, in the Kyoto High Myopia Genome Cohort, healthy Japanese cohort from the Aichi Cancer Center Research Institute and the Yokohama City University Genome Cohort, height information was not available; therefore, the impact of height on high myopia could not be analyzed. Fourth, this study relied on eQTL and sQTL data for other available human tissues rather than ocular-specific tissues. Further studies are necessary to confirm the functional impact of the identified susceptibility SNPs on the expression or splicing levels of genes, including rs2727196 on ADAMTS17 splicing, in human retinal or scleral tissues. Fifth, the observed enrichment of acetylcholine-gated channel pathways was largely driven by CHRND and CHRNG, which were assigned to the PRSS56 locus by positional mapping by FUMA. Because these genes reside within the same LD block as the lead SNP at this locus, our current data cannot clearly determine whether PRSS56 itself or nearby genes of CHRND and CHRNG. Therefore, the implication of acetylcholine-gated channel pathways should be interpreted with caution rather than as definitive evidence regarding specific genes at this locus. Last, the OR for the effect size from high myopia to POAG observed in this MR study was smaller than the OR reported in previous epidemiological studies.60 Although the effect sizes derived from MR analyses and observational studies may often differ,63 we cannot ascertain which measure more accurately reflects the true effect size and need to be cautious when interpreting the difference between the effect size in this MR study and that reported in epidemiological studies.

In conclusion, we identified rs2727196 in ADAMTS17 and rs6564696 in MAFTRR as novel susceptibility SNPs for high myopia via a relatively large genome-wide meta-analysis. A functional enrichment analysis revealed that Golgi body and acetylcholine-gated channels were suggested to be involved in the pathways of high myopia. Additionally, we identified a significantly positive genetic correlation between high myopia and POAG and revealed the causal relationship from high myopia to POAG using a bidirectional MR analysis. Further genetic studies will elucidate the pathogenesis of high myopia.
