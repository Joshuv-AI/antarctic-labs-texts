Development of electrochemical sensor for vanillin determination using carbon spheres–LaFe0.9Ni0.1O3 nanohybrid
Authors: Ali Ramzannezhad, Shahram Ghasemi, Behrooz Eftekharinia, Shayan Safari
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Vanillin is widely used in the confectionery and pharmaceutical industries due to its unique aroma, flavor, antioxidant, and antimicrobial properties. However, excessive consumption leads to adverse important effects such as headaches, nausea, vomiting, and impaired liverier and kidney function. Here, an electrochemical sensor based on LaFe0.9Ni0.1O3 nanoparticles functionalized with carbon spheres (C-LFNO) is designed for the determination of vanillin in food samples. The structural characteristics of C-LFNO nanohybrid were investigated using X-ray diffraction, Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy, energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy, and transmission electron microscopy. The objective of this study was to develop a sensor with a low limit of detection (LOD) and high sensitivity for rapid and accurate determination of vanillin using amperometry. The sensor exhibited a linear detection range from 0.008 to 0.36 μM as well as LOD and limit of quantification of 0.011 and 0.035 μM, respectively. Additionally, the recovery of vanillin in real food samples, including ice cream and chocolate at different matrix ratios (25, 50, and 70%), ranged from approximately 87 to 104%, with relative standard deviation values between 2.17 and 4.72%, demonstrating the sensor’s good accuracy and precision in complex sample matrices.

Introduction

Vanillin (4-hydroxy-3-methoxybenzaldehyde) is the principal component responsible for the characteristic aroma of vanilla. Since its first isolation from vanilla pods by Nikolay Nikolaevich Zinin in 18601, vanillin has found wide applications in food, pharmaceutical, and fine-chemical industries2,3. However, excessive intake of vanillin leads to adverse health effects, including headaches, skin sensitization, fungal infections, and gastrointestinal disorders4,5. Conventional analytical methods for vanillin determination often involve laborious sample preparation, long analysis times, and limited sensitivity6, highlighting the need for fast, accurate, and sensitive sensing platforms.

Several electrochemical sensing platforms have been reported for vanillin determination, employing diverse electrode modifiers such as metal–organic frameworks, hydroxide composites, carbon-based nanomaterials, and perovskites7–9. These sensors have achieved detection limits in the nanomolar range and broad linear dynamic ranges, but often face challenges related to stability, reproducibility, or fabrication complexity. Despite these advancements, there is still a need for sensors that combine high sensitivity with excellent selectivity and reproducibility while maintaining a simple fabrication process. An overview of previously reported electrochemical sensors and their performance metrics is provided6,8,10,11, highlighting the necessity for novel sensing materials. Among the promising electrode modifiers, double perovskites have gained considerable attention owing to their tunable electronic structure, chemical stability, and high catalytic activity12–21. In this work, LaFe0.9Ni0.1O3 (LFNO) was selected as the base material because Ni substitution at the Fe site enhances electrical conductivity by modulating the electronic structure and generating additional oxygen vacancies, thereby improving charge-transfer and surface reactivity. Moreover, coating LFNO with conductive carbon spheres (C-LFNO) further increases electron mobility, enlarges the electroactive surface area and creates synergistic catalytic effects, leading to improved electrocatalytic oxidation of vanillin22–27.

This study reports the synthesis and application of C-LFNO as a novel electrode modifier for vanillin detection. The unique combination of perovskite catalytic properties and conductivity-enhancing carbon coating results in exceptional sensitivity, selectivity, and reproducibility, highlighting the sensor’s potential for routine quality control in food products.

Materials and methods

Chemicals and instruments

Lanthanum nitrate hexahydrate (La(NO3)3.6H2O, 99.99%), citric acid monohydrate (C6H8O7.H2O, 99.5%), nickel (II) nitrate hexahydrate (Ni (NO3)2.6H2O, ≥ 99.99%), iron (Ш) nitrate nonahydrate (Fe(NO3)3.9H2O, ≥ 99.95%), glucose monohydrate (C6H12O6.H2O, ≥ 99.0%), ethanol (C2H5OH, ≥ 99.5%), phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, pH = 7.4), and vanillin (C8H8O3, 99%)—were obtained from Sigma-Aldrich.

Synthesis of LaFe0.9Ni0.1O3 (LFNO) nanoparticles

LFNO nanoparticles were synthesized as follows:

A mixture of 1.34 g of La(NO3)3.6H2O, 0.12 g of Ni(NO3)2.6H2O, 1.50 g of Fe(NO3)3.9H2O, and 1.80 g of C6H8O7.H2O was dissolved in 10 mL of deionized water. The solution was sonicated for 15 min, followed by stirring at 80 °C for 1 h. The resulting precursor was calcined at 650 °C for 2 h (Fig. 1a).Fig. 1Schematic illustration of synthesis steps of (a) LFNO nanoparticles, (b) carbon spheres, and (c) C-LFNO nanohybrid.

Schematic illustration of synthesis steps of (a) LFNO nanoparticles, (b) carbon spheres, and (c) C-LFNO nanohybrid.

Synthesis of carbon (C) spheres

Carbon spheres were fabricated hydrothermally using glucose (C6H12O6.H2O) precursor. Glucose was dissolved in deionized water, stirred to full dissolution, and transferred into Teflon-lined stainless steel autoclave (100 mL) and heated at 180 °C for 6 h. After cooling naturally, the product was separated using centrifuge and dried at 60 °C for 5 h (Fig. 1b).

Preparation of C-LFNO nanohybrid

Equal stoichiometric amounts of LFNO and carbon spheres were dispersed in deionized water separately for 1 h. The suspensions were mixed and sonicated for 1 h, then 20 mL of ethanol (C2H5OH) was added, and the mixture was irradiated under stirring for another 1 h. The nanohybrid was recovered by drying at 80 °C (Fig. 1c).

Fabrication of the modified electrode

Glassy carbon electrode (GCE) surface was polished using Al2O3 powder (0.05 μm) on an emery pad, rinsed with deionized water and ethanol, and dried at 25 ºC. The C-LFNO nanohybrid was dispersed in deionized water, and an optimized volume of suspension (3 μL) was drop-cast onto the electrode surface. The electrode was dried at 60 °C prior to use in electrochemical experiments.

Material characterization

X-ray diffraction of C-LFNO nanohybrid

XRD patterns were recorded using a Rigaku Ultima diffractometer using Cu Kα radiation with λ = 1.54 Å over 2θ = 10–80 °, with 0.02 ° steps and 10 ° per minute scan rate.

FTIR spectroscopy of C-LFNO nanohybrid

Spectra were acquired using a Perkin-Elmer 1730 spectrometer across 500–4000 cm−1. Samples pellets were prepared through mixing the sample with potassium bromide (KBr) using a hydraulic press.

Morphology and composition studies

The morphology of the C-LFNO nanohybrid was studied using a TESCAN Mira4 field emission scanning electron microscope (FE-SEM). Furthermore, energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDX) was used to determine the elemental composition, including both the weight and atomic percentages of the constituent elements.

Electrochemical investigations

Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS), cyclic voltammetry (CV) and amperometric measurements were performed using an Autolab 302N potentiostat/galvanostat (Metrohm, Netherlands).

Figure 2(b) displays the FTIR spectra of LFO, LFNO, and C-LFNO nanoparticles. The spectra reveal several vibrational features associated with metal–oxygen bonds and surface-adsorbed species29. A characteristic absorption band appears near 687 cm−1 in all samples, which can be ascribed to M–O stretching vibrations within the perovskite lattice30. In the case of C-LFNO, the band appears slightly more intense, suggesting the formation of more robust or ordered lattice possibly influenced by carbon matrix31. The band observed around 1638 cm−1 corresponds to the bending vibration of molecular water or surface hydroxyl groups (−OH), typically originating from ambient moisture adsorption. This feature becomes more intense in the C-LFNO sample, indicative of a greater surface area or enhanced hydrophobicity following carbon modification32. The relatively higher response in the carbon-modified sample suggests an increased surface interaction capability, which may reflect greater porosity or active surface sites33. Furthermore, the broad absorption in the region of 3480 cm−1 is associated with O–H stretching vibrations, again related to physisorbed water or hydroxylated surfaces34. This band is notably enhanced in C-LFNO, consistent with a higher surface polarity or improved water affinity after carbon integration. Collectively, these findings indicate that while all samples retain the core perovskite structure, the incorporation of Ni and subsequent surface modification with carbon significantly influence the surface properties. Such alterations may be beneficial for applications requiring elevated surface activity, such as gas sensing, catalysis, or electrochemical processes35.

In contrast, Fig. 4d–f displays the TEM images of C-LFNO, in which the particles also demonstrate polyhedral shapes but are embedded within a thin and relatively amorphous carbon matrix. The presence of the carbon layer results in a noticeable contrast difference. The core of the particles appears darker and the edges being slightly brighter, confirming successful surface coating. The carbon layer is estimated to be a few nanometers thick and serves as a protective shell around the perovskite particles. This coating not only reduces interparticle agglomeration but also facilitates improved electrical conductivity, which is crucial for applications such as electrocatalysis or energy storage. These morphological differences clearly highlight the influence of carbon coating on the structural dispersion, particle connectivity, and potential surface reactivity of the nanostructures. Such modifications are expected to significantly impact the physicochemical performance of the material in practical applications.

Figure 4g and h shows the particle size distribution of LFNO and C-LFNO, respectively. As shown, the histograms of LFNO sample exhibits a relatively wide distribution, ranging from approximately 20 to 60 nm, with a mean particle size of 30.96 nm. This broad distribution reflects some degree of non-uniformity and possible aggregation among the particles, which is consistent with the microstructural observations in the corresponding TEM images. In contrast, the histogram in Fig. 4h reveals that the C-LFNO nanohybrid has a slightly broader size range (10 to 70 nm), but with a more symmetric and Gaussian-like distribution. The average particle size increases to 39.33 nm upon carbon incorporation. Despite the increase in mean size, the improved symmetry of the distribution indicates better control over particle growth, potentially due to the templating or stabilizing effect of the carbonaceous phase during synthesis. The comparative analysis suggests that carbon modification influences the nucleation and growth dynamics of the perovskite nanoparticles. Although it may leads to a slight increase in average particle size, the uniform morphology of C-LFNO is beneficial for enhancing structural stability and facilitating charge transfer in catalytic or electrochemical applications.

Electrochemical studies

EIS was conducted to investigate the reaction kinetics, elucidate the reaction mechanisms, and measure electrical resistance and capacitance. Figure 5a shows the Nyquist plots for bare GCE, LFNO/GCE, C-LFNO/GCE in a 5 mM solution of [Fe(CN)6]4−/3− containing 0.1 M KCl, over a frequency range of 100–1 Hz. All plots exhibit a semicircular arc, indicative of charge transfer resistance (Rct) and the capacitive behavior of the electrode surface. The absence of a linear region at low frequencies suggests that Warburg diffusion is not dominant under these conditions. LFNO, as a perovskite material, shows notable electrocatalytic activity and conductivity, enhancing performance relative to the bare GCE. The addition of the carbon phase in C-LFNO nanohybrid further improves electronic conductivity and increases the effective surface area in contact with the electrolyte, thus reducing charge transfer resistance and facilitating electron exchange. To quantify the experimental data, an equivalent circuit model was used, including elements of solution resistance (RS), charge transfer resistance (Rct), Warburg impedance (W), and a constant phase element (CPE). The Warburg element represents the diffusion of ions in the electrolyte or within the electrode, with its impedance being frequency-dependent; at low frequencies, diffusion limitations become more pronounced. In some cases, a capacitor (C) can be used instead of a CPE. The capacitor corresponds to the double-layer capacitance at the electrode–electrolyte interface and exhibits more ideal capacitive behavior, whereas the CPE is introduced to account for deviations from ideality in real systems. The Rct of the C-LFNO electrode (55 kΩ) is significantly lower compared to LFNO/GCE (85 kΩ) and bare GCE (110 kΩ). This notable reduction can be attributed to the enhanced electrical conductivity provided by the carbon phase and the increased effective surface area. Cyclic voltammetry (CV) was employed to characterize the electrochemical behavior of the C-LFNO/GCE modified electrode and to evaluate its catalytic activity at various stages of electrode modification. The cyclic voltammograms recorded in 5 mM [Fe (CN)6]4−/3− containing 0.1 M KCl at a scan rate of 50 mV/s reveal the redox peaks of Fe2+/Fe3+ for all modified electrodes. As shown in Fig. 5b, the currents of redox peaks in voltammogram of C-LFNO/GCE are higher than that for the unmodified electrode. This enhancement confirms the significant improvement in conductivity due to the presence of perovskite structure, along with good activity.

In this study, a scan rate of 100 mV/s was selected as a representative condition to evaluate the response of bare GCE, LFO/GCE, LFNO/GCE and the modified C-LFNO/GCE toward vanillin detection (Fig. 5c). At this scan rate, the modified C-LFNO/GCE exhibited a markedly higher anodic peak current and a sharper voltammetric profile compared to bare GCE, LFO/GCE and LFNO/GCE. This pronounced enhancement at a moderate scan rate reflects the improved charge transfer kinetics and lower resistance to electron flow at the surface of the C-LFNO/GCE.

Figure 5d shows CV curves of C-LFNO/GCE recorded at different scan rates (v). The anodic peak current increases with increasing scan rate from 10 to 300 mV/s. The scan rate plays a critical role in determining the electrochemical behavior of redox-active analytes and the performance of modified electrodes. The increase in anodic peak current (Ipa) is attributed to the presence of conductive carbon and catalytically active LFNO nanoparticles, which facilitates faster electron exchange between vanillin and electrode surface. Moreover, the selection of an intermediate scan rate of 100 mV/s ensures a balance between capacitive and faradaic currents, allowing more clear observation of redox features. It also minimizes distortions that originate from diffusion limitations at lower scan rates or capacitive artifacts at excessively high ones. The performance at this scan rate indicates that the C-LFNO/GCE is more kinetically favorable and better suited for sensitive electrochemical sensing of vanillin.

Figure 6c illustrates the influence of pH on the electro-oxidation of 10 mM vanillin at a scan rate of 100 mV/s. As the pH increases from 2 to 10, the vanillin oxidation peak generally shifts to lower potentials, revealing the direct involvement of H+ ions in the redox process. According to the Nernst equation39, decreasing the proton concentration (higher pH) drives the equilibrium toward lower oxidation potentials. It is worth noting that at pH = 2 and pH = 5 the potential shift is less pronounced, which can be attributed to proton saturation effects in strongly acidic media (pH = 2) and buffering interactions around pH = 5. At acidic pH, the reaction requires a higher potential, whereas in mildly alkaline media (pH = 6–8) the oxidation proceeds more readily. Extremely alkaline conditions, however, can disturb the stability of the electro-active species; therefore, a pH near neutrality is preferred for a reproducible vanillin assay.

Figure 6d shows the effect of pH on the oxidation peak current of vanillin at the C-LFNO/GCE. The current exhibits a maximum value in strongly acidic medium (pH = 2), decreases progressively up to neutral conditions, and then increases slightly in the alkaline range. This trend suggests that proton availability strongly influences the electro-oxidation kinetics, with proton-assisted electron transfer being more favorable in acidic solutions. The reduced current response around neutral pH may be attributed to limited proton participation, whereas the partial recovery at higher pH reflects changes in the electroactive species stability.

Figure 6e presents the variation of the oxidation peak potential as a function of pH. A nearly linear negative shift is observed with increasing pH, with a slope of –40.7 mV per pH unit (R2 = 0.93). This slope, lower than the theoretical Nernstian value (− 59 mV/pH), indicates that the oxidation of vanillin involves an unequal number of electrons and protons. The shift confirms the direct role of protons in the redox mechanism. This effect is likely due to proton saturation in highly acidic conditions (pH = 2) and buffering interactions around pH = 5, which may moderate the electrochemical response. Overall, the results suggest that mildly acidic to near-neutral pH provides a balance between current intensity and potential reproducibility, making it favorable for analytical applications.

Proposed mechanism of vanillin detection

The sensing mechanism of vanillin at the modified GCE can be explained based on the electrochemical interaction between vanillin molecules and the electrode surface. Upon introduction of vanillin, an electrochemical oxidation occurs at the active sites of the electrode, involving electron transfer from vanillin to the electrode. This process generates a measurable current proportional to the vanillin concentration. The key functional groups of vanillin, particularly the phenolic hydroxyl group, participate in this oxidation reaction, enabling selective detection.

Modification of the GCE with C-LFNO significantly enhances the sensor performance. The carbon component improves the overall conductivity of the electrode and increases the effective surface area, facilitating faster electron transfer and higher current response. The LFNO component in nanohybrid provides specific active sites that interact with vanillin molecules, promoting adsorption and stabilizing the oxidation process. The synergistic effect of carbon sphere and LFNO results in enhanced sensitivity, improved signal-to-noise ratio, and greater reproducibility of the sensor response.

Overall, the combination of improved electron transfer through carbon and selective molecular interaction via LFNO enables highly efficient and reliable electrochemical detection of vanillin. Optimization of active material loading

To achieve the best electrochemical response, the effect of the amount of C-LFNO nanohybrid drop-cast on the GCE surface was systematically studied in PBS solution (pH = 7) containing 1 mM vanillin. Different volumes of the nanohybrid suspension were applied to the electrode, dried under ambient conditions, and their corresponding peak currents were recorded (Fig. 8a). Each measurement was repeated three times (n = 3), and the average values of standard deviation are reported. The results revealed that the peak current increased with the amount of active material up to 3 μL (10.9 μA), which provided the highest response. At higher loadings (4 μL), the current response decreased slightly due to the formation of a thicker deposited layer, which hinders electron transfer and mass transport at the electrode–electrolyte interface. Sensitive determination of vanillin

To evaluate the sensitivity of the C-LFNO electrode, amperometric measurements were carried out at 0.50 V (vs. Ag/AgCl) in 0.1 M PBS while successive aliquots of vanillin were injected (Fig. 8b). The modified electrode produced rapid, well-defined current steps; each addition reached a steady-state value within seconds, confirming its fast response.

The resulting calibration plot (Fig. 8c) shows a linear increase in current with vanillin concentration from 0.008 to 0.36 μM (R2 = 0.99). From the slope of the plot and the standard deviation of the blank, LOD and LOQ were calculated as 0.011 and 0.035 μM, respectively.

Once the LOD and linear range are clarified, the C-LFNO modified electrode can be confidently reported as a high-performance sensor for trace-level vanillin determination.

Selectivity and reproducibility

Figure 8d demonstrates the selectivity of the C-LFNO sensor for vanillin in the presence of potentially interfering electroactive species. Amperometric measurements were recorded at an applied potential of 0.5 V in 0.1 M PBS (pH = 7). For this test, each interfering species was introduced at ten times the concentration of vanillin (10 mM), including common compounds such as phenol, hydroquinone, acetylsalicylic acid, ascorbic acid, catechol, uric acid, aniline, 4-nitrophenol, and gallic acid.

Despite the structural and electrochemical similarities of some of these species to vanillin, the sensor maintained a highly selective response with no significant current overlap or false signals, confirming that the sensor possesses excellent specificity for vanillin. This high selectivity is attributed to the synergistic effect of the C-LFNO nanohybrid, which provides active catalytic sites for vanillin determination. In addition, the lack of interference peaks in the amperometric profiles reinforces the sensor’s robustness and reliability for real sample applications, even in the presence of common phenolic and biological interferents.

As summarized in Table 1 and illustrated in Fig. 9a, the recovery rates of vanillin in ice cream samples ranged from approximately 90.4 to 104%, with low RSD values demonstrating excellent accuracy and precision. For chocolate samples, recovery varied between about 87.0 and 94.5%, with RSD values ranging from 2.17 to 4.72%, indicating good repeatability despite the more complex matrix. It is noteworthy that the vanillin concentration used in these real sample analyses (5 μM) exceeds the established linear range of the sensor (0.008–0.36 μM). Despite operating outside the linear calibration range, the sensor exhibited satisfactory recovery and reproducibility, highlighting its robustness and practical applicability. Nevertheless, future work could focus on extending the linear dynamic range or implementing sample dilution strategies to further enhance accuracy for higher analyte concentrations. These findings confirm that the C-LFNO sensor maintains high sensitivity, reproducibility, and reliability for the quantitative detection of vanillin, even in complex food matrices with varying compositions. Thus, this sensor is suitable for practical applications in food quality control and analysis.

Reproducibility and stability studies

To evaluate the repeatability and stability of the C-LFNO/GCE sensor, intra-day and inter-day measurements were conducted using a standard vanillin solution (0.1 μM) in 0.1 M PBS. For intra-day precision, three replicate measurements were performed within a single day. For inter-day reproducibility, the same procedure was repeated over three consecutive days. The relative standard deviations (%RSD) for intra-day and inter-day measurements were found to be 2.1 and 3.4%, respectively. These low RSD values confirm the high precision and long-term stability of the C-LFNO modified electrode, reinforcing its suitability for reliable vanillin detection in real-world applications.

Furthermore, long-term working stability of the C-LFNO/GCE sensor was investigated using the amperometry technique. As shown in Fig. 9b, the current response was recorded continuously at two different vanillin concentrations (10 and 30 mM). The sensor exhibited a stable and consistent current signal with minimal drift over the extended measurement period, confirming its robust operational stability. These results further demonstrate that the C-LFNO/GCE maintains reliable electrochemical performance during prolonged use, making it suitable for practical applications.

Conclusions

In this study, a C-LFNO nanohybrid was successfully prepared through hydrothermal method. The structural features and morphology of the nanohybrid were characterized through various experimental techniques. The electrocatalytic activity of the C-LFNO/GCE was evaluated for vanillin determination using cyclic voltammetry. From amperometry studies, the sensor presents a linear detection range of 0.008 to 0.36 μM, with a low LOD of 0.011 μM. In addition, recovery percentages in real food sample analyses ranged from approximately 86.9 to 104.1%, with relative standard deviations (%RSD) below 5%, confirming excellent accuracy, precision, and repeatability. These results indicate that the C-LFNO electrode offers high sensitivity, reliability, and robustness. The sensor is a promising candidate for the reliable determination of vanillin in complex matrices and practical applications.
