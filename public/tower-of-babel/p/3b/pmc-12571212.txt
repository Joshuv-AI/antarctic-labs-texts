Visible‐Light‐Driven TiO2/Pt Nanozyme‐Embedded Dressings: Integrating PDT, Oxygenation, and Biomechanical Support for Pressure Ulcer Repair
Authors: Jie Yang, Qingyu Han, Liping Li, Ruiping Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPressure ulcer (PU) cause metabolic disorders and ischemia via prolonged pressure, leading to secondary infection, inflammation, and vascular neuropathy. However, existing therapies rely on microenvironment, H2O2, low repair efficiency, and lack efficient collaborative therapy. Herein, a confined multifunctional TiO2/Pt nanozyme is developed via atomic layer deposition for PUs repair. Pt doping narrowed the TiO2 bandgap (3.30→3.05 eV), enhance visible‐light‐driven •OH generation, and achieves 100% and 97.38% antibacterial efficacy against E. coli and S. aureus by visible photodynamic therapy (PDT). Demonstrating robust CAT/SOD‐like activities, it neutralizes ROS in wound microenvironments. For clinical translation, the nanozyme is embedded into a thermosensitive chitosan/β‐glycerophosphate hydrogel (TPCG) and combined with a compressible chitosan/nanofibrillated cellulose aerogel. In vitro and in vivo therapeutic experiments, the TPCG hydrogel have proved that in TPCG hydrogel can sterilize and neutralize ROS by visible light while producing O2 to promote cell migration, angiogenesis and reduce inflammation, thereby promoting PU healing, while the aerogel mitigated mechanical stress. Biosafety assays confirmed negligible systemic toxicity. This work provides a synergistic strategy combining nanocatalytic therapy, PDT, and biomechanical support for advanced PU management.

Introduction

Pressure ulcers (PUs) are a significant clinical challenge due to long‐term pressure on the skin of the body that affects blood circulation, resulting in excessive local production of reactive oxygen species (ROS), impaired tissue regeneration and bacterial infection, leading to prolonged inflammation and delayed healing.[ 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 , 9 ] Conventional wound dressings, including hydrogels and foams, primarily focus on moisture retention and barrier protection but fail to address the complex biochemical microenvironment of PUs, such as hypoxia, biofilm formation, and oxidative stress.[ 5 , 10 , 11 , 12 ] Therefore, while reducing pressure, eliminating bacterial infections, clearing ROS, inhibiting inflammatory reactions, and inducing blood vessel and tissue formation are the keys to treating refractory wounds in PU.

Recent advances in nanotechnology have introduced catalytic nanomaterials(nanozymes) as promising candidates for wound management, owing to their enzyme‐like activities (e.g., catalase (CAT), superoxide dismutase (SOD), and peroxidase (POD)) to regulate ROS and enhance tissue repair.[ 13 , 14 , 15 , 16 , 17 , 18 , 19 , 20 ] Among these, TiO2‐based photocatalysts have garnered attention for their antimicrobial properties under UV light; however, their wide bandgap (≈3.2 eV) limits practical applications under visible light, which constitutes ≈43% of solar energy.[ 21 , 22 , 23 ] Moreover, conventional TiO2 lacks the ability to simultaneously scavenge multiple ROS species (e.g., H2O2, ·O2 −) and generate therapeutic reactive oxygen species (e.g., •OH) in a controlled manner, hindering synergistic antibacterial and pro‐healing effects.[ 24 , 25 , 26 ]

To overcome these limitations, noble metal doping (e.g., Pt, Au) has been explored to enhance visible‐light absorption and charge separation in TiO2.[ 27 , 28 , 29 ] For instance, Pt nanoparticles (NPs) act as electron sinks, reducing recombination of photogenerated carriers and enabling ROS modulation.[ 30 , 31 , 32 ] However, existing Pt/TiO2 systems often suffer from insufficient catalytic specificity, poor stability in physiological environments, and a lack of integration with biomaterial platforms for sustained therapeutic delivery.[ 33 , 34 ] Furthermore, while hydrogels and aerogels have been widely used as wound dressings, their combination with multifunctional nanozymes to simultaneously address biochemical imbalances (e.g., ROS, hypoxia) and biomechanical stress (e.g., pressure, shear) remains underexplored.[ 35 , 36 , 37 , 38 , 39 , 40 , 41 ]

In this work, we designed a TiO2/Pt nanozyme with spatially confined Pt NPs within anatase nanotubes, synthesized via template‐assisted atomic layer deposition (ALD), to achieve triple catalytic activities (Scheme 1): (1) visible‐light‐driven water splitting produce •OH for PDT antibacterial, independent of the wound microenvironment and without the addition of H2O2. (2) CAT‐like activity, i.e., H2O2 decomposition to produce O2 to alleviate hypoxia, and (3) SOD‐like activity, i.e., ·O2 − scavenging to mitigate oxidative damage. The nanozyme was further integrated into a chitosan‐based thermosensitive hydrogel (TPCG) for localized PDT and oxygen supply, combined with a compressible chitosan/nanofibrillated cellulose (CS/NFC) aerogel to reduce mechanical stress at wound sites. This dual‐functional system synergistically addresses the multifaceted challenges of PUs by coupling nanocatalytic therapy with biomechanical support. Our study not only advances the rational design of nanozymes for ROS modulation but also provides a clinically translatable strategy for PU management through material innovation and mechanistic integration.

Results and Discussion

Preparation and Characterization of TiO2/Pt nanozyme

For the synthesis of TiO2/Pt nanozyme, Pt nanoparticles were first deposited on the carbon nanofibers (CNFs) by Pt ALD,[ 42 ] obtaining Pt/CNFs, and then an amorphous TiO2 film was deposited on Pt/CNFs by the template‐assisted ALD method using CNFs as templates, obtaining TiO2/Pt/CNFs. Subsequently, the CNFs were removed by calcination under an air atmosphere, producing TiO2/Pt nanozyme with anatase nanotubes (the sem‐sectional view is shown in Figure S1a, Supporting Information). As shown in Figure S1b (Supporting Information), the transmission electron microscope (TEM) image reflects that the prepared TiO2/Pt nanozyme was a micron‐sized tube, and many nanopores can be seen on the surface. The high‐resolution TEM (HRTEM) image shows that the thickness of the outermost TiO2 layer is 11 nm (Figure 1a), and the Pt nanoparticles with an average size of 8.51 nm are confined to the inside of the TiO2 and dispersed homogeneously (Figure 1a–c). The loading amount of Pt nanoparticles for the TiO2/Pt nanozyme determined by the inductively coupled plasma‐atomic emission spectrometry (ICP‐AES) is 17.63 wt%. From the energy‐dispersive X‐ray (EDX) spectroscopy mapping (Figure 1d), Pt elements were uniformly distributed in the inner layer of the TiO2 tube, which also proved the successful preparation of the confined TiO2/Pt nanozyme. As shown in Figure 1e, the X‐ray powder diffraction (XRD) peaks of the porous TiO2 nanotubes are consistent with the standard PDF card (No. 21–1272) of the anatase phase of TiO2.[ 43 , 44 , 45 ] And, due to the doping of Pt nanoparticles, the intensity of the TiO2 peaks in the confined TiO2/Pt nanozyme is weakened, while the peaks of the Pt nanoparticles are consistent with the standard PDF card (No. 04–0802), which is attributed to the Pt (111) plane.[ 46 , 47 ] As depicted from X‐ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) spectra, after deposition by Pt ALD, a Pt 4f peak appeared in the confined TiO2/Pt nanozyme. All data demonstrate that confined TiO2/Pt nanozyme was successfully synthesized.

Catalytic Properties of TiO2/Pt Nanozyme

To explore the photocatalytic activity under visible light (660 nm, 200 mW cm−2), generation of •OH is evaluated by degradation of MV.[ 48 , 49 ] As shown in Figure 2a, the intensity of the absorption peaks of TiO2 does not change in the presence or absence of visible light, and the color of the solution is almost unchanged after the reaction (Figure 2a inset), indicating that TiO2 cannot be triggered to produce •OH during visible light exposure. This can be attributed to the wide band gap of TiO2, which is photocatalytically active only under UV irradiation.[ 50 , 51 ] However, the TiO2/Pt nanozyme doped with Pt nanoparticles showed a significant decrease in the intensity of the absorption peak under visible light and the color of the solution was changed from dark purple to colorless (Figure 2a inset), implying that Pt doping significantly reduced the band gap of TiO2 and significantly increased the visible light photocatalytic activity. Then the MV degradation activity was investigated for different light times and nanozyme concentrations (Figure 2b; Figure S2, Supporting Information). In order to investigate the effect of acidity and alkalinity on the photocatalytic performance of TiO2/Pt nanozyme, we conducted experiments at different pH values. As shown in Figure 2c, the absorption peak intensities were almost unchanged between pH 4.6 and 9.0, and the solution colors were all colorless, which indicated that the acid‐base had almost no effect on the visible photocatalytic performance of the materials. The above results show that TiO2/Pt nanozyme can exert antibacterial effects in the complex microenvironment of PUs only by irradiation of visible light without adding H2O2.

The efficiency of TiO2/Pt photocatalytic decomposition of water to ·OH is evaluated by the reaction rate (r) and rate constant (k). According to the experimental data in Figure 2b, it can be concluded that the reaction is approximately linear within the first ten minutes, so we used the initial rate method to calculate the reaction rate and rate constant. As shown in Figure 2d, the r of TiO2/Pt photocatalytic water decomposition to produce ·OH is 1.78 µM/min. The k is calculated according to Equation 1: (1) r=kcatalystmIn where, m is the order of catalytic reaction, and n is the order of light intensity.

Through calculations, we determined that the reaction order (m) of the reaction is 1 and the light intensity order (n) is 0.5. Therefore, the k of this reaction calculated by the above formula is 0.002518.

Since ROS (e.g., H2O2, ·O2 −, etc.) in PUs lead to local inflammatory response and poor wound healing,[ 52 , 53 ] it is crucial to utilize these endogenous ROS. CAT‐like enzymes can decompose H2O2 into O2 and H2O, while SOD‐like enzymes can convert ·O2 −into O2 and H2O2, thus promoting tissue oxygenation and chronic wound repair. Therefore, the CAT‐like activity of TiO2/Pt nanozyme was first tested. As shown in Figure 2e, TiO2/Pt nanozyme could produce O2 rapidly in the presence of a very small amount of H2O2 (5 mM), while TiO2 could not catalyze the O2 production from H2O2, which indicated that Pt was the main catalytic activity center. Meanwhile, we conducted experiments on CAT‐like activity at different pH values (4.6‐7.4). As shown in Figure 2f, the CAT‐like activity of TiO2/Pt nanozyme showed better performance with the increase of pH value. In the weak alkaline environment (pH = 7.4), more oxygen is generated, but the nanozyme also has good CAT‐like activity in the weak acidic environment (pH = 5.6 or 6.2). Then, we tested the SOD‐like activity of TiO2/Pt nanozyme using a SOD activity assay kit. Figure 2g showed that, compared with TiO2 (16.56%), TiO2/Pt nanozyme had a higher ·O2 − scavenging capacity (88.90%). Furthermore, the generation of •OH and the scavenging of·O2 − was confirmed by ESR spectroscopy using 5,5‐Dimethyl‐1‐pyrroline N‐oxide (DMPO) as a spin trapping agent.[ 54 , 55 ] As depicted in Figure 2h and i, under the irradiation of visible light, it can effectively produce •OH, and the nanozyme can effectively remove ·O2 −, which is consistent with our above experimental results.

To further study the efficiency of TiO2/Pt CAT‐like enzymes, its kinetics were evaluated by the Michaelis‐Menten equation at room temperature (25 °C).[ 56 ] The time‐dependent O2 production using H2O2 (0.625, 1.25, 2.5, 5, 10 mM) as substrate were measured. In the first step, the data were fitted based on the Michaelis‐Menten curve (Figure 2j), and the Michaelis constant (Km ) were determined with the typical double‐reciprocal Lineweaver‐Burk plots (Figure 2k). Thus, according to the calculation of the x‐intercept, the Km and Vmax of TiO2/Pt nanozyme were 3.77 mM and 69.86 µM min−1 at 25 °C, respectively. Then, we compared the kinetic parameters of CAT‐like enzymes with those reported in the literature (Table S1, Supporting Information) and found that the Km value of TiO2/Pt nanozymes was lower than that of many previously reported nanomaterials, and the Vmax value was higher than that of many previously reported nanomaterials, indicating that TiO2/Pt nanozymes had excellent substrate affinity and catalytic efficiency. For the efficiency of SOD‐like enzyme, the SOD‐like enzyme activity of TiO2/Pt quantified by using a commercial SOD assay kit (WST‐1) was approximately 4002 U/mg, which is even 1.3 times better than the native SOD tested in the literature[ 57 ] and higher than many previously reported nanomaterials (Table S1, Supporting Information).

Taken together, with three synergistically coupled nanocatalytic activities (the generation of •OH, CAT‐like, and SOD‐like), TiO2/Pt nanozyme was able to sustained and effective generation of •OH for antimicrobial and utilization of endogenous ROS in PUs to promote tissue oxygenation and wound repair (Figure 2l).

Catalytic Mechanism of Nanozymes

To explore the catalytic mechanism of nanozymes, we performed XPS characterization of the surface properties of nanozymes. As shown in Figure 3a, the high‐resolution XPS Ti 2p peak of TiO2/Pt nanozyme shifted to higher binding energies than TiO2, which demonstrated the electron transfer from TiO2 to Pt nanoparticles, and confirmed the strong interaction between Pt and TiO2.[ 58 , 59 ] Consequently, it is likely that electrons can readily accumulate on the surface of Pt to contribute to the reduction of ·O2 − and the production of O2, which falls under the category of chemical kinetics. The high‐resolution XPS spectra of Pt 4f exhibits three predominant peaks at 70.9, 72.4, and 74.3 eV, which can be attributed to the Pt0, Pt2+ and Pt4+ (Figure 3b and Table S2, Supporting Information). As shown in Figure 3c, the high‐resolution XPS spectra of O 1s were deconvoluted into three peaks, the lattice oxygen (OL) peak was at 529.72 eV, the oxygen vacancy (OV) peak was at 531.95 eV and the adsorbed oxygen (OA) peak was at 533.5 eV. Semi‐quantitative analysis of three oxygen species (Table S3, Supporting Information) revealed a significant increase in OV content in TiO2/Pt nanozyme, which is attributed to the excessive O3 etching effect during the deposition of Pt ALD. This effect precisely favors the catalytic reaction of nanozyme.[ 60 ]

In order to better explain the mechanism of visible photocatalytic reaction, we first performed UV–vis absorption spectra. As shown in Figure 3d, TiO2 has weak visible light absorption. When Pt nanoparticles are loaded, the absorption band of TiO2/Pt is red‐shifted, and its absorption at long wavelengths is significantly enhanced. Strong visible light absorption means that TiO2/Pt has stronger photon absorption capabilities. In addition, the forbidden band width of the material is calculated by plotting Tauc plots.[ 61 ] The band gaps of TiO2 and TiO2/Pt are estimated to be 3.30 and 3.05 eV, respectively (Figure 3d inset). The small band gap of TiO2/Pt indicates that it is more easily excited by visible light than TiO2. The separation efficiency of photogenerated electron‐hole pairs will also affect the photocatalytic properties of materials. The valence‐band (VB) XPS spectra of TiO2 and TiO2/Pt were conducted. As shown in Figure 3e, the VB edge for TiO2/Pt was determined to be at 1.32 eV, below the VB edge for TiO2 (1.84 eV), suggesting that the electrons in TiO2/Pt are more likely to transition from the valence band to the conduction band, thereby accelerating the separation efficiency of photogenerated electrons and holes, and thus exhibiting higher photocatalytic activity. Photoluminescence (PL) spectroscopy is used to evaluate the separation efficiency of photogenerated electron‐hole pairs in materials.[ 62 ] Compared with TiO2, TiO2/Pt has the lowest PL intensity, which means that doping of Pt nanoparticles effectively suppresses recombination of high charges (Figure 3f). In addition, the time‐resolved fluorescence spectroscopy suggests that the fluorescent lifetime of TiO2/Pt is estimated to be ≈3.39 ns (Figure 3g), which is larger than that of TiO2 (1.65 ns), further confirming the high charge separation efficiency. Therefore, TiO2/Pt has the highest photocatalytic efficiency. In addition, transient photocurrent response and electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) further confirmed the improvement of charge separation efficiency at the TiO2/Pt nanozyme interface. The photocurrent response value shows a trend of TiO2/Pt > TiO2 (Figure 3h). The larger the photocurrent, the higher the separation efficiency of photogenerated electron‐hole pairs in the sample.[ 63 ] Therefore, photocurrent data shows that TiO2/Pt has the best photocatalytic effect. EIS data showed that TiO2/Pt nanozymes showed rapid electron transfer (Figure 3i). The above results show that the doping of Pt improves the photocatalytic efficiency of the heterojunction.

Based on all the above experimental results and characterization analysis, we have proposed the mechanism for coupled muti‐nanocatalytic activities of TiO2/Pt nanozyme. As shown in Figure 3j, under the excitation of visible light, the band gap of TiO2 narrows due to the doping of Pt, accelerating the separation of photogenerated electron‐hole pairs. H2O molecules adsorbed on the surface of TiO2/Pt undergo an oxidation reaction to generate highly toxic •OH, which plays an antibacterial role. Under the strong interaction between Pt and TiO2, excited electrons transfer and accumulate from TiO2 to the surface of Pt nanoparticles, which contributes to the reduction of ·O2 − to produce O2. Impressively, under the synergistic effect of a large amount of OV on the surface of the nanozyme, the entire reaction reaches a new balance,[ 60 ] and it is more conducive to the oxidation of excess H2O2 to produce more O2, thereby promoting tissue oxygenation and wound repair in pressure ulcers.

Evaluation of Antibacterial Activity in Vitro

The antibacterial efficiency of intrinsic TiO2/Pt nanozyme toward Gram‐negative E. coli and Gram‐positive S. aureus were evaluated in the absence and presence of visible light. As shown in Figure 4a, most E. coli and S. aureus in all treated groups without visible light were alive. Similarly, the control and TiO2 treated groups with visible light irradiation (control (+) and TiO2 (+)) still showed negligible antibacterial ability. In contrast, the TiO2/Pt nanozyme treated groups with visible light irradiation (TiO2/Pt (+)) exhibits a much higher antibacterial activity than other groups. The antibacterial efficacy of TiO2/Pt (+) against E. coli and S. aureus was more than 90%, or even completely killed (Figure S3, Supporting Information). This is attributed to the fact that TiO2/Pt produces large amounts of highly toxic ∙OH under visible light irradiation, thereby destroying bacterial membrane and induce apoptosis.[ 64 , 65 ]

Characterization of antibacterial properties and mechanisms. a) Photos of bacterial colonies formed by E. coli and S. aureus after different groups of treatment in the presence or absence of visible light (control, TiO2, TiO2/Pt). b) Live/dead bacterial assay of E. coli and S. aureus. Bacteria were co‐stained with SYTO 9 and PI. Scale bar: 100 µm. c) SEM pictures of E. coli and S. aureus after various treatments. Scale bar: 1 µm.

Moreover, the antibacterial effects of different samples were also explored through the bacterial live/dead staining method. As shown in Figure 4b, the surfaces of the control and TiO2 treated groups were mainly covered by evenly distributed live bacteria (green fluorescent) even after visible light irradiation. On the contrary, a large number of dead bacteria (red fluorescent) were found on TiO2/Pt under visible light irradiation, which could be attributed to the highly efficient PDT sterilization effect. However, all treated groups without visible light exhibited weak antibacterial efficacy due to the absence of the PDT effect.

To clarify the antibacterial mechanisms of the samples, bacterial morphology after various treatments was observed through SEM measurement. Figure 4c showed that the E. coli and S. aureus cultured with the control and TiO2 displayed typical and intact rod‐shaped (E. coli) and spherical (S. aureus) morphologies regardless of visible light irradiation or not. Similar intact morphology was also observed for the bacteria treated by TiO2/Pt without visible light irradiation. On the contrary, the bacterial membranes of E. coli and S. aureus in TiO2/Pt groups became partial wrinkling and disruption of the bacterial membrane after irradiation. The above phenomenon shows that visible light produces a large amount of ∙OH, which can stimulate materials to kill bacteria by destroying bacterial membranes in a short period of time, causing proteins to leak out, and ultimately causing bacterial apoptosis.

Preparation and Characterization of Hydrogels and Aerogels

As mentioned above, the TiO2/Pt nanozyme acting as therapeutic drugs were embedded in the hydrogel to construct the wound dressing for promoting PUs healing. To achieve this, the TPCG hydrogel was prepared by a simple and rapid polymerization process utilizing TiO2/Pt nanozyme as an additive agent (Figure 5a). Figure 5b,c illustrated SEM images of CG hydrogel and TPCG hydrogel. It could be found that they had typical porous structure, the porous structure of the hydrogels did not change and no obvious two‐phase structure appeared after the addition of nanozymes,[ 66 ] revealing the good interactions between nanozymes and polymer matrix. Magnified SEM images (Figure 5d,e) further provided visual evidence of the nanozymes, verifying the successful fabrication of the nanozyme composite hydrogel. In addition, the rheological behavior was investigated to assess the dynamic mechanical properties of TPCG hydrogels. When the storage modulus (G′) is higher than the loss modulus (G″), the hydrogel exhibits elasticity (more solid‐like capacity). In contrast, when G″ is higher than G′, the hydrogel exhibits fluid‐like properties. As can be seen from Figure 5f and Figure S4 (Supporting Information), the hydrogel exhibits both solid and fluid characteristics, demonstrating the fluidity of the hydrogel and its ability to adapt to different depths and irregular wound shapes. FTIR spectra of the different samples were shown in Figure 5g. The stretching vibration absorption peak of ‐OPO3 2− stretch of β‐GP (around 1082 cm−1) appeared in both hydrogel samples. The peaks corresponding to ‐OPO3 2− stretching vibrations were red‐shifted from 1082 cm−1 to 1074 cm−1 in both hydrogel samples, indicating the formation of the physical crosslinking network within TPCG hydrogel. Compared with CS and β‐GP, the FTIR spectra of both hydrogel samples showed strong tensile vibration peaks corresponding to O─H (3420 cm−1 and 3419 cm−1). This suggests that the CS molecular chain and β‐GP interact to form hydrogen bonds. At the same time, compared with CS, the‐C═O stretching vibration (1655 cm−1) and the‐N─H deformation vibration (1597 cm−1) have red‐shifted, indicating that the amino groups on the CS molecular chain form hydrogen bonds and complexes with O─H and‐PO4 2− on β‐GP. On the other hand, the deformation vibration absorption peak of C─CH3 (1380 cm−1) becomes weaker, indicating that hydrogen bonds may have been formed. It could also be found that there was almost no difference between the FTIR spectra of CS/β‐GP and TPCG hydrogel, which was perhaps derived from the very small amount of TiO2/Pt within nanozyme composite hydrogel. The above analysis proves that we have successfully synthesized TiO2/Pt nanozyme composite hydrogels.

It is very important to reduce the pressure of pressure ulcers. For this goal, CS/NFC aerogels with excellent elasticity and stability were synthesized using CS and NFC with good biocompatibility as raw materials. The internal morphology of the CS/NFC aerogel was characterized by SEM. As shown in Figure 5h,i, it is clear that CS/NFC aerogel has a highly porous and macroscopically ordered network‐like structure. This type of network structure can give aerogels significant stress transfer and energy dissipation capabilities, thereby preventing the aerogels from being destroyed under external pressure.[ 67 , 68 ] To further explore the interaction between CS and NFC, CS/NFC aerogel was characterized using FTIR. It can be seen from Figure 5j that the peaks around 3400 cm−1 and 1560 cm−1 are assigned to the stretching vibration absorption peak of the N─H bond, and the peaks around 1644 and 1316 cm−1 are assigned to the C─N stretching vibration absorption peak, indicating that an amide condensation reaction occurred between CS and NFC, forming an amide bond. The compressive properties of TPCG hydrogel and CS/NFC aerogels were compared through stress–strain test. As shown in Figure 5k, with the increase of stress, the maximum stress of the CS/NFC aerogel is 1.16 MPa when ɛ = 80%, which may be due to the formation of a porous and orderly network‐like skeleton formed by hydrogen bond cross‐linking between CS and NFC. However, with the increase of stress, the maximum stress of the TPCG hydrogel is 1.79 MPa when ɛ = 80%, which may be due to the higher water content and porous density of the hydrogel, which significantly improve the mechanical properties of the comprehensive system. It can be seen from Figure 5l,m that CS/NFC aerogel quickly returns to its original shape and size without collapsing after undergoing cyclic deformation in air and water, showing excellent elasticity and structural stability, which gives the aerogel a longer life cycle to cope with pressure shocks. The above analysis showed that CS/NFC aerogel was successfully prepared.

The sterilization efficiency of hydrogels toward Gram‐negative E. coli and Gram‐positive S. aureus were evaluated in the presence of visible light. As shown in Figure S5 (Supporting Information), for CG hydrogel alone, it has certain antibacterial effect, and its antibacterial efficiency against E. coli and S. aureus is 36.7% and 13.3%, respectively. When TiO2/Pt nanozyme was added, the antibacterial efficiency of TPCG hydrogel was significantly improved under the irradiation of visible light, and the antibacterial efficiency against E. coli and S. aureus reached 98.9% and 85.6%, respectively. This is consistent with the antibacterial efficiency of TiO2/Pt nanozyme in Section 2.4, which strongly proves that TiO2/Pt nanozyme produces many highly toxic ∙OH for sterilization under the action of visible light.

Assessment of Pressure Ulcer Repair in Vitro

In order to explore the mechanism of PU repair at the cellular and molecular levels, we first conducted experiments to promote cell migration and angiogenesis in vitro. Angiogenesis and cell migration are critical to wound healing. Next, we performed cell scratching experiments on TPCG hydrogel to determine its ability to promote cell migration. As shown as Figure 6a, after 24 hours, the TPCG Light (+) group showed excellent scratch healing ability and achieved the highest migration rate of HUVECs, which was 91.60% (Figure 6b), indicating that TPCG hydrogel has a stronger ability to promote cell migration under visible light irradiation. Then, the ability to promote angiogenesis is further assessed in vitro. HUVECs were co‐cultured with TPCG hydrogel on Matrigel for 12 hours and tube formation was examined under the microscope. As shown in Figure 6c, the TPCG Light (+) group exhibited significant tubular formation in most areas, while only a few short cell junctions were observed in the control group and other hydrogel groups. In addition, we quantified the number of junctions points (Figure 6d). Consistent with our observations, the TPCG Light (+) group had a significant increase in the number of junction points compared to other groups, indicating that TPCG hydrogel effectively promotes angiogenesis under visible light irradiation. The mechanism by which TPCG hydrogel promotes cell migration and angiogenesis is due to TiO2/Pt nanozyme promoting the sustained release of O2, which involves the upregulation of αvβ3 integrin on endothelial cells, which is an important adhesive integrin endothelial cell survival, migration and angiogenesis.[ 69 ] Taken together, these results demonstrate that TPCG hydrogel has a satisfactory ability to promote cell migration and angiogenesis.

Macrophages are crucial for innate immunity and have multiple functions, including host defense, immune regulation and tissue repair aid. These versatile cells have impressive adaptability and can present different phenotypes, traditionally classified as M1 macrophages, which cause inflammation, and M2 macrophages, which focus on immune regulation and tissue repair. In the PU area, the transition from M1 to M2 is often hindered, leading to prolonged inflammation and slow tissue healing. High ROS levels in the PU environment exacerbate oxidative stress, which can adversely affect wound healing. Our research aims to improve the wound environment by fighting bacterial infections and neutralizing ROS, promoting more effective transfer from M1 to M2 macrophages. To induce M1 signatures, lipopolysaccharide (LPS) and interferon γ (IFN‐γ) were used as positive controls, while interleukin‐4 (IL‐4) was used as negative control to induce M2 signatures. The expression of CD206 and CD86 markers, which represent the M2 and M1 phenotypes, was measured by immunofluorescent staining under inflammatory conditions triggered by LPS and IFN‐γ. Compared with the control group, the TPCG Light (+) group showed increased CD206 expression and decreased CD86 levels, indicating increased M2 macrophage activity (Figure 6e). This is well demonstrated by the average fluorescence intensity (Figure 6f,g) of CD86/CD206. These experimental results demonstrate that TPCG hydrogel can accelerate wound healing by effectively fighting bacterial infections and neutralizing ROS under visible light irradiation by reducing inflammation in the PU environment.

Therefore, the above experiments have proved that in TPCG hydrogel can sterilize and neutralize ROS by visible light while producing O2 to promote cell migration, angiogenesis and reduce inflammation, thereby promoting PU healing.

Assessment of Pressure Ulcer Repair in Vivo

In order to study the wound healing properties of TPCG hydrogel and CS/NFC aerogel, an infected wound model of PU in mice was further constructed. The entire treatment process is shown in Figure 7a. Initially, the infected mice were randomly divided into six groups to receive different treatments (each group with or without light): control light (‐), control light (+), TCG/CN (‐), TCG/CN (+), TPCG/CN (‐), TPCG/CN (+). For better fixation, cover the wound with a 3 M sterile dressing. Wound healing maps at different time points showed that wounds in the TPCG/CN light (+) group basically healed after 16 days of treatment, while wounds in the other groups (with or without light) showed delayed wound healing (Figure 7b–d). At the same time, the weight of mice in each group was monitored. The TPCG hydrogel combined with CS/NFC aerogel had no obvious toxic effect on mice, which was proved by the fact that there was no significant weight change in mice in each group. These results show that due to the PDT effect of TiO2/Pt nanozyme, natural light irradiation of the inner composite hydrogel effectively kills bacterial diseases and prevents the formation of bacterial biofilms. Meanwhile, the continuous generation of oxygen in the wound microenvironment due to the CDT effect of nanozymes will also promote the healing of infected wounds. In addition, CS/NFC aerogel reduces the pressure on the wound site throughout the treatment process, plays a certain supporting role in the wound, and promotes the therapeutic effect of TPCG hydrogel.

To further clarify the wound repair process for different group treatments, mouse wound tissues were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) on day 16.[ 70 , 71 ] As shown in Figure 8a, a complete epidermal layer and regular collagen fibers with regenerated hair follicles and new blood vessel formation were observed in the TPCG/CN light (+) group, showing better wound healing relative to the other groups. In contrast, the other groups showed significant scab and inflammatory cell infiltration. Masson's trichrome staining method is used to verify the formation of collagen fibers during wound healing (blue). Compared with other groups, the TPCG/CN light (+) group showed orderly arranged collagen deposits and denser blood vessel structures in skin tissue at the wound site. Therefore, faster wound healing may be due to the synergistic effect of rapid sterilization and inflammation relief.

During the healing process, the establishment of new blood vessels also plays an important role. Wound tissue from each group of mice was stained with immunohistochemistry to determine the distribution of CD31 and VEGF.[ 72 , 73 ] As shown in Figure 8b, the CD31 and VEGF contents of TPCG/CN light (+) group were higher than those of other groups, indicating that during the process of covering the TiO2/Pt nanozyme composite hydrogel to the skin wound, more TiO2/Pt can be retained and continuously released into the wound tissue under natural light irradiation, which reduces the accumulation of inflammatory factors and the increase of oxygen content in the wound, promotes the formation of blood vessels and accelerates wound healing.

The biological safety of TPCG hydrogel and CS/NFC aerogel was assessed by H&E staining of major organs (i.e., heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney). Compared with the control group (Figure S6, Supporting Information), all organs showed normal conditions with no significant abnormalities, indicating that TPCG hydrogel and CS/NFC aerogel has excellent biological safety and has negligible toxicity and side effects on other organs, making it a potential treatment for pressure ulcers.

Conclusion

In conclusion, we developed a TiO2/Pt nanozyme with triple catalytic activities using ALD and integrated it into a bifunctional hydrogel/aerogel dressing for the treatment of PU. Pt‐based nanozymes can achieve effective bacterial eradication and tissue oxygenation without relying on the wound microenvironment or adding H2O2. In vitro and in vivo treatment experiments have demonstrated that TPCG hydrogel can disinfect and neutralize ROS through visible light, while simultaneously producing O2 to promote cell migration, angiogenesis and reduce inflammation, thereby promoting PU healing, while aerogel reduces mechanical stress. This study highlights the potential of nanocatalytic materials in designing next‐generation wound dressings for clinical applications, especially in managing infected and ischemic wounds.

Experimental Section

Materials

All the chemical reagents were analytical grade without further purification. Cu nanoparticles, HNO3, methyl violet (MV) and hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) were purchased from Shanghai Macklin Biochemical Co., Ltd. (China). Trimethyl(methylcyclopentadienyl)platinum (IV) (MeCpPtMe3), tetraisopropanol titanium and the other chemicals were all obtained from Sigma‐Aldrich. The superoxide anion assay kit was obtained from Beijing Solarbio Science & Technology Co., Ltd. ICR mice (6 weeks) were acquired from Beijing Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd.

Synthesis of TiO2/Pt Nanozyme

First, carbon nanofibers (CNFs) were synthesized by chemical vapor deposition at 260 °C using acetylene as a carbon source and copper nanoparticles as a catalyst. It was then heat treated in an inert atmosphere at 900 °C for 2 hours, treated with concentrated HNO3 in an oil bath at 110 °C for 2 hours, and finally filtered and washed to obtain the finished product. CNFs were firstly decorated with Pt nanoparticles by Pt ALD and then coated with a TiO2 layer by TiO2 ALD, producing TiO2/Pt/CNFs. After the ALD processes, TiO2/Pt/CNFs were calcinated at 500 °C for 2 h in air to remove the CNF templates, obtaining TiO2/Pt nanozymes, in which Pt nanoparticles were confined in TiO2 nanotubes.

Measurement of Visible Light Catalytic Activity – ·OH Generation

The methyl violet (MV) method was used to measure the generation of ·OH. Take 10 mg of sample and add it to 10 mL MV (0.01 g L−1), and let it stand in the dark for 10 minutes to achieve adsorption/desorption equilibrium. Then, after exposure to visible light (660 nm, 200 mW cm−2) for 40 minutes, the absorption value of the supernatant at 580 nm was measured on an UV–vis spectrometer, and the color change of the solution was observed and recorded.

Measurement of CAT‐Like Activity

A certain amount of sample (1.67 µg mL−1) and H2O2 (5 mM) were added to PBS solution at pH 7.4, and the amount of O2 at different time points was measured using a portable dissolved oxygen meter (JPBJ‐608), and the data was observed and recorded.

Measurement of SOD‐Like Activity

The SOD‐like activity of the samples was measured using a SOD activity detection kit (Beijing Solarbio Science & Technology Co., Ltd). First, the sample was prepared into a solution with a certain concentration, and then each reagent was added to the sample solution according to the instructions. The absorption value of the supernatant at 560 nm was measured with an ultraviolet‐visible spectrophotometer, the value was recorded, and the SOD activity of the sample was calculated.

Antibacterial Effect of TiO2/Pt Nanozyme

As previously reported,[ 42 ] the plate counting method was used to determine the antibacterial effect of TiO2/Pt nanozymes by the number of colony forming units (CFU). The experimental procedures were as follows: Three sets of samples (control, TiO2, TiO2/Pt) were tested with or without 660 nm light irradiation (200 mW cm−2, 10 min), and then incubated at 37 °C for 2 h. After dilution with bacterial solution (1.0×107 CFU mL−1), take 100 µL of the suspension to coat the plate, and incubate in an incubator at 37 °C for 24 h. Take photos and calculate the antibacterial rate.

Synthesis of TPCG Hydrogel and CS/NFC Aerogel

A certain concentration of chitosan‐acetic acid solution and β‐GP solution were arranged. Under an ice bath, the nanozymes were added to the above two solutions and stirred for 15 min, and then placed in a water bath at 37 °C until the hydrogel polymerized to obtain TPCG hydrogel. A certain concentration of chitosan‐acetic acid solution and cellulose solution were arranged, and then the two were mixed and stirred at room temperature for 6 h, and the freeze‐dried aerogel was lyophilized for 36 h, and finally the freeze‐dried aerogel was dried in a vacuum drying chamber of 110 °C for 1 h to obtain CS/NFC aerogel.

In Vivo Wound Healing Assay

All animal experiments were approved by the Experimental Animal Ethics Committee of Shanxi Bethune Hospital (Shanxi Academy of Medical Sciences) (No. SBQKL‐2021‐040, Taiyuan, China). First, hair removal cream (Veet) was used to remove hair from the back of ICR mice. Gently pull the dorsal skin up and sandwich between two round neodymium magnets (diameter 12 mm, height 5 mm). Mice were maintained in a pressure ulcer cycle (12 h ischemia cycle and 12 h perfusion cycle) for a total of 3 cycles, and then inoculated with S. aureus (1.0×108 CFU mL−1) for infection. All mice had two round purpura or ulcers, and the infected wound model of PU formed. The hydrogel and aerogel were then applied sequentially to the infected wounds, and named it TPCG/CN group. Before evaluating the wound healing status of each group, some groups of wounds were irradiated with 660 nm laser (200 mW cm−2, 10 min), bacteria were photodynamic sterilized, wounds were photographed, and the weight of mice in each group were recorded. After 16 days, mice were euthanized, and major organs (heart, lung, liver, spleen, and kidney) and wound tissue were collected for staining for further histological analysis.

Statistical Analysis

Each experiment was repeated at least three times. The experimental data were quantitatively expressed as mean ± standard error of the mean using Student's t‐test. The differences were considered statistically significant at p<0.01 (**), p<0.001 (*) and p>0.05 (no significance, NS). Use Origin 2024 software to draw graphics.
