Aryl hydrocarbon receptor in club cells drives Th17-mediated lung injury following inhalation exposure to environmentally persistent free radicals
Authors: Rashmi Pathak, Avinash Kumar, Ashley Ferrier, David Burk, Stephania A. Cormier
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Environmentally persistent free radicals (EPFRs), generated during thermal combustion processes including hazardous waste remediation, are emerging redox-active pollutants linked to adverse respiratory outcomes. EPFR inhalation induces neutrophilic asthma characterized by substantial lung injury. Neutrophilic asthma is driven by T helper 17 (Th17) cells which secrete IL-17. We identified aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AHR) as a key mediator of EPFR-induced pulmonary Th17 responses. Building on this, we discovered that club cells, specialized airway epithelial cells, are a primary site of AHR activation following EPFR exposure. To dissect the specific role of AHR in club cells, we generated mice with a conditional deletion of Ahr in club cells (AhrΔCC) by crossing Ahrfx mice [Ahrtm3.1Bra/J strain] with Scgb1a1-CreERTM mice [B6N.129S6(Cg)-Scgb1a1tm1(cre/ERT)Blh/J strain]. Littermate (LM; Cre-negative Ahrfl/fl) control and AhrΔCC mice were exposed to air or EPFRs via inhalation. Flow cytometry and immunofluorescence confirmed loss of AHR in club cells of AhrΔCC mice. As expected, EPFR-exposed LM mice exhibited a pronounced Th17-biased pulmonary immune response accompanied by increased neutrophils in the bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) compared to air-controls. In contrast, EPFR-exposed AhrΔCC mice failed to induce Th17 responses or neutrophilia. Supporting these findings, BALF analysis showed increased cytokines associated with Th17 signaling and neutrophil recruitment (IL-17, IL-6, IL-1β, KC/GRO) in EPFR-exposed LM mice but not in AhrΔCC mice. Histopathology revealed that EPFR exposure induced airspace enlargement, fibrosis, and mucus hyperproduction in LM mice, whereas AhrΔCC mice were protected. Pulmonary function testing further demonstrated increased airway hyperresponsiveness in EPFR-exposed LM mice which was attenuated in AhrΔCC mice. Collectively, these findings highlight a key epithelial-immune crosstalk in the lung triggered by EPFRs and suggest that AHR signaling in club cells is critical for mediating lung inflammation and functional decline following EPFR exposure. Targeting this pathway may offer a promising therapeutic strategy to prevent or reverse EPFR-induced lung injury.

Introduction

Exposure to environmental pollutants is a significant risk factor for the development and exacerbation of chronic respiratory diseases, including asthma [1]. One emerging class of pollutants with potent pro-inflammatory properties is environmentally persistent free radicals (EPFRs) [2,3]. These redox-active species are formed during the incomplete combustion of organic materials, often through interactions between transition metals and organic compounds on particulate matter surfaces [4,5]. These are commonly associated with fine and ultrafine particulate matter (PM) and generate reactive oxygen species (ROS) upon interacting with biological tissues. Importantly, it has been observed that EPFRs can persist in the environment upwards of five years [6]; because EPFRs can undergo redox-cycling and catalyze Fenton-like reactions, this means that they have a significant capacity to produce hydroxyl radicals and other ROS in a sustained manner [2,4,5,7]. Inhalation of EPFRs has been shown to induce oxidative stress, skew immune responses toward a T helper 17 (Th17) phenotype, and promote neutrophilic inflammation, which has been observed in steroid-resistant asthma [7,8]. Th17 cells are a subset of CD4+ T helper cells that produce interleukin-17 (IL-17) family cytokines, primarily IL-17A and IL-17F [9]. These cytokines are key drivers of neutrophilic inflammation, as they stimulate airway epithelial cells, endothelial cells, fibroblasts, and airway smooth muscle cells to release pro-inflammatory cytokines (such as IL-6, IL-1β, TNF-α) and potent neutrophil chemoattractants (CXCL1/GRO, CXCL5, CXCL8/IL-8) that recruit and activate neutrophils [9,10].

The aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AHR) is a ligand-activated transcription factor that regulates genes involved in xenobiotic metabolism, oxidative stress, and immune modulation [[11], [12], [13], [14], [15], [16], [17], [18]]. EPFRs and other environmental toxicants can activate AHR, which in turn influences immune cell differentiation and cytokine production [7,8]. In particular, AHR activation has been linked to enhanced Th17 polarization in various tissues, including the lung [8,19]. Wheeler et al. identified respiratory epithelial cells as critical AHR-dependent regulators of neutrophil recruitment during influenza infection, highlighting the importance of epithelial AHR in coordinating innate immune responses [20]. In addition, Traboulsi et al. reported that chlorine inhalation promotes macrophage and neutrophil dominated inflammation and increases airway hyperresponsiveness, effects that are dependent on AhR expression, establishing a direct link between epithelial AHR activation and lung function outcomes [21]. Despite these insights, the specific cellular sources and compartmental control of AHR signaling in the context of pollutant-induced immune responses remain poorly defined. In our earlier published work, which primarily demonstrated that EPFR exposure induces AHR activation responsible for Th17 responses [8,22], in situ hybridization revealed increased expression of Cyp1a1 mRNA in both parenchymal and bronchiolar regions of the lung in EPFR-exposed mice [8]. Cyp1a1 is a canonical downstream target of AHR and is widely used as a molecular marker for AHR activation. Though detailed identification of specific epithelial cell types was not the primary focus, these findings provided initial evidence of AHR activation in these lung compartments. Alveolar type II (AT2) and club cells represent the primary metabolically active epithelial populations in the parenchymal and bronchiolar regions, respectively. More recently, flow cytometric analysis confirmed that AT2 and club cells are the predominant epithelial populations exhibiting CYP1A1 induction following EPFR exposure, reinforcing their roles as key cellular sites of AHR signaling within the lung [23]. To further explore the roles of these epithelial subsets, we first investigated the effect of Ahr deletion in AT2 cells using a conditional knockout model. Following EPFR exposure, we noted enhanced eosinophilic inflammation and Th2-skewed responses in AT2-specific Ahr knockout mice, contrasting with the Th17-polarized neutrophilic inflammation typically observed with EPFR exposure (Fig. S1). While not central to the present work, these findings prompted further investigation into the role of AHR within club cells.

Club cells are non-ciliated secretory epithelial cells comprising approximately 20-30% of the bronchiolar epithelium [24]. They are characterized by high expression of AHR and an abundance of xenobiotic-metabolizing enzymes, positioning them as key responders to inhaled toxicants [24]. Given their robust AHR signaling response to EPFR exposure [8,23], club cells likely play a key role in mediating EPFR-induced airway responses. However, it remains unclear whether AHR signaling specifically within club cells is required for the development of EPFR-induced Th17-mediated lung injury. To address this critical knowledge gap, we employed a conditional knockout mouse model in which Ahr was selectively deleted in club cells. We hypothesized that club cell-specific AHR is critical for mediating EPFR-induced Th17-biased responses in the lung. Our findings demonstrate that loss of AHR in club cells markedly reduces EPFR-induced Th17 cell expansion, neutrophilic inflammation, lung remodeling and lung function impairment, highlighting a previously unrecognized, cell type- and context-specific role of AHR in regulating pollutant-driven immune responses. These results provide novel insights into the epithelial-immune interface and underscore the importance of club cell-intrinsic AHR signaling in shaping pulmonary inflammatory responses to environmental exposures.

Immunofluorescence imaging of lung sections confirmed the targeted deletion of Ahr in club cells. In AhrΔCC mice, there was a loss of AHR expression in the bronchiolar regions (primarily lined by club cells), where the club cell specific marker, CC10 was observed. In contrast, robust AHR expression was observed in the CC10-positive bronchiolar epithelium of littermate (LM; Scgb1a1-Cre−/−Ahrfl/fl) controls (Fig. 1b). Additionally, flow cytometric analysis revealed a significant reduction in the AHR-positive club cell population (CD45−CD31−CD326+CC10+) in AhrΔCC mice compared to LM controls (Fig. 1c and d). Importantly, flow cytometric analysis also confirmed that Ahr deletion was restricted to club cells and not observed in alveolar type II (AT2) cells, further supporting the specificity of the targeted deletion (Fig. S4; gating strategy is provided in Fig. S3). These findings collectively confirm the successful and specific deletion of Ahr in club cells.

This is consistent with our previous findings and further validates the experimental model. In contrast, AhrΔCC mice failed to show an increase in Th17 cells upon EPFR exposure. To further evaluate immune cell recruitment, we performed bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) cytology. EPFR exposure in LM mice led to a significant increase in total BALF cellularity, with macrophages (∼90%) remaining the predominant population, followed by neutrophils (∼10%). Macrophages increased approximately 3-fold, whereas neutrophils exhibited a marked ∼50-fold increase compared with air-exposed controls, consistent with Th17-associated neutrophilic airway inflammation. In contrast, AhrΔCC mice exhibited lower levels of macrophage and neutrophil recruitment following EPFR exposure compared to LM controls (Fig. 2d and e). This inflammatory profile is consistent with elevated levels of Th17-associated cytokines, including IL-17, IL-6, IL-1β and TNF-α which promote Th17 differentiation and pro-inflammatory signaling as well as increased KC/GRO, a potent neutrophil chemoattractant in the BALF of EPFR-exposed LM mice, but not in AhrΔCC mice (Fig. 2f–j). Together, these results highlight the critical role of AHR signaling in club cells in mediating EPFR-induced Th17-biased immune responses, pro-inflammatory cytokine release, and associated neutrophilic inflammation.

Club cell AHR is essential for EPFR-induced lung remodeling and structural damage

Having established that club cell-specific AHR is required for EPFR-induced Th17 responses and neutrophilic inflammation, we next investigated whether these immune changes translated into structural lung damage. We examined key histopathological features including airspace enlargement, fibrosis, and mucus production in lung tissues collected from LM and AhrΔCC mice exposed to air or EPFRs.

Quantitative morphometric analysis revealed a significant increase in mean linear intercept (MLI) in H&E-stained lungs of LM mice following EPFR exposure, indicating marked airspace enlargement consistent with emphysematous damage. This structural alteration was considerably absent in EPFR-exposed AhrΔCC mice (Fig. 3a; left and 3b). Picrosirius red (PSR) staining highlighted substantial collagen deposition and peribronchial fibrosis in the lungs of EPFR-exposed LM mice, which was also dramatically reduced in the absence of AHR in club cells (Fig. 3a; middle and 3c). Likewise, periodic acid-Schiff (PAS) staining demonstrated extensive mucus hyperproduction in EPFR-exposed LM mice, a pathological feature significantly alleviated in AhrΔCC counterparts (Fig. 3a; right and 3d). Club cell-specific AHR deficiency attenuates EPFR-induced airway hyperresponsiveness

To determine the functional consequence of club cell-specific AHR deficiency following EPFR exposure, airway resistance was measured using forced oscillometry in response to escalating methacholine concentrations to determine airway hyperresponsiveness. In air-exposed groups (Air-LM and Air-AhrΔCC), only modest increases in airway resistance were observed across methacholine concentrations, with no significant differences between genotypes (Fig. 4a). This indicates that under baseline exposure (air) conditions, Ahr gene deletion does not alter pulmonary responsiveness. In contrast, EPFR exposure induced a robust, dose-dependent increase in airway resistance compared to air controls, confirming the development of airway hyperresponsiveness (Fig. 4a). However, no significant differences were observed between EPFR-LM and EPFR-AhrΔCC groups, suggesting a limited overall impact of Ahr deletion. Interestingly, when analyzed by sex, females appeared to be resistant to EPFR-induced airway hyperresponsiveness whereas elevated airway resistance was observed primarily in EPFR-exposed males (Fig. S9a and S9b). Further, EPFR-exposed AhrΔCC males exhibited a trend towards lower airway resistance compared to EPFR-exposed LM males, indicating a protective effect of Ahr deletion specifically in males (Fig. 4b and S9c). A sex-stratified reanalysis of our flow cytometric data from Fig. 2b also revealed a robust Th17-biased immune response in EPFR-exposed male LM mice compared to EPFR-exposed female LM mice (Fig. S6). Collectively, these data indicate a possible sex-specific role for club cell AHR in mediating EPFR-induced airway hyperresponsiveness, with male LM mice displaying a trend towards heightened airway resistance that appears attenuated by Ahr deletion. Discussion

Environmentally persistent free radicals (EPFRs) are an emerging class of airborne pollutants generated primarily during the incomplete combustion of organic materials [25,26]. Increasing evidence implicates EPFR exposure in the pathogenesis of respiratory diseases characterized by oxidative stress and neutrophilic inflammation [2,7,8]. Notably, neutrophilic asthma, which is frequently resistant to corticosteroid therapy, is driven by T helper 17 (Th17) cells that secrete pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-17 [10]. Club cells are non-ciliated secretory epithelial cells abundant in the bronchioles that express high levels of the aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AHR), a ligand-activated transcription factor that mediates cellular responses to environmental toxicants and modulates immune function [24]. Although AHR signaling has been associated with pollutant-induced immune alterations [8,27], the specific contribution of AHR within club cells to Th17-mediated pulmonary inflammation has not been clearly defined. In this study, we demonstrate that AHR within club cells plays an essential role in mediating EPFR-induced pulmonary pathology. Using a conditional knockout mouse model (AhrΔCC), we show that loss of AHR specifically in club cells abolishes the EPFR-induced Th17-biased immune response, attenuates neutrophilic inflammation, prevents structural lung injury, and reduces airway hyperresponsiveness. These findings highlight a previously unrecognized epithelial-immune axis in which club cell-intrinsic AHR signaling acts as a critical driver of pollutant-induced lung inflammation and dysfunction.

Our findings establish a critical role for AHR signaling in club cells in orchestrating the Th17 immune response and neutrophilic inflammation following EPFR exposure. Prior studies have shown that AHR activation modulates immune responses in barrier tissues, including the lung, by influencing the balance of T helper subsets [8,27]. Specifically, AHR has been implicated in the differentiation and maintenance of Th17 cells [8], in part through its regulation of IL-6 and IL-1β [27]. Consistent with this, we observed an increase in pulmonary Th17 cells following EPFR exposure along with elevated levels of IL-17 (Th17-associated cytokine), IL-6, IL-1β and TNF-α (inflammatory cytokines) and KC/GRO (neutrophil chemoattractant) in the BALF of LM mice. Further, the loss of Th17 expansion in AhrΔCC mice indicates that epithelial (club cell-specific) AHR activity is a key driver of this response, highlighting a non-hematopoietic cellular source as a modulator of adaptive immunity. This is consistent with previous reports demonstrating that epithelial cells can shape T cell responses by sensing environmental cues and producing cytokines that direct immune polarization [28,29]. The observed reduction in neutrophilic inflammation in AhrΔCC mice further supports the link between Th17 responses and downstream effector recruitment. IL-17 promotes neutrophil chemotaxis primarily through induction of CXCL1 (KC/GRO in mice), and its expression was markedly blunted in EPFR-exposed AhrΔCC mice, suggesting functional impairment of the IL-17 axis. These findings align with the paradigm that IL-17-producing cells and IL-17-dependent cytokines contribute significantly to the pathogenesis of pollutant-induced airway inflammation [[30], [31], [32], [33]].

Notably, while prior work has demonstrated the necessity of AHR in T cells for Th17 differentiation, our study highlights a novel epithelial-immune interaction whereby AHR activity in airway epithelial cells, specifically club cells, is upstream of and necessary for Th17-mediated responses. Interestingly, we observed that neither Th1 nor Th2 cell populations were significantly altered by EPFR exposure (Fig. S7), and this was further supported by the absence of increased Th1 (IFN-γ) or Th2 (IL-5) cytokines in BALF (Fig. S8). This specificity underscores the selective role of AHR signaling in promoting Th17 responses following EPFR exposure, without broadly affecting other T helper subsets. Moreover, this AHR-dependent selective Th17 skewing appears to be spatially and functionally restricted to club cells. Although both club cells and alveolar type II (AT2) cells are the primary epithelial targets of inhaled EPFRs on ultrafine particulate matter and express high levels of AHR [8], our prior work using AT2-specific Ahr deletion revealed a distinct immune phenotype characterized by enhanced Th2/eosinophilic responses following EPFR exposure (Fig. S1). These findings contrast sharply with the pro-inflammatory role of AHR in club cells, where its function is to drive EPFR-induced Th17/neutrophilic responses. However, this divergence is consistent with emerging evidence that AHR activation in AT2 cells plays a protective, anti-inflammatory role by preserving epithelial integrity and limiting inflammatory signaling, as demonstrated by Zimmerman et al. [34]. Notably, our observations also align with those of Wang et al. [35], who reported that Ahr deletion in AT2 cells exacerbated allergen-induced airway inflammation, characterized by enhanced Th2 cytokines and eosinophilic infiltration. Extending these insights to our studies, we similarly found that AT2-specific AHR loss promoted eosinophilic inflammation and a Th2-skewed immune profile, diverging from the Th17/neutrophilic pattern typically seen in LM controls following EPFR exposure. In contrast, AHR activation in airway epithelial compartments promotes innate inflammatory responses dominated by neutrophils and macrophages. Wheeler et al. demonstrated that AHR expression in respiratory epithelial cells is required for enhanced neutrophil accumulation during influenza virus infection, identifying epithelial AHR as a key regulator of neutrophilic inflammation in response to inhaled stressors [20]. Similarly, Traboulsi et al. showed that chlorine inhalation induces robust macrophage and neutrophil recruitment and significantly increases airway resistance, effects that were attenuated in the absence of AHR, indicating that AHR actively promotes airway hyperresponsiveness following toxic inhalation exposure [21]. Together, these findings highlight the compartment-specific roles of epithelial AHR in modulating EPFR-induced immunity, promoting pro-inflammatory Th17/neutrophilic axis in club cells, while exerting a potentially regulatory, Th2-suppressive function in AT2 cells.

Consistent with the observed immune dysregulation, our histopathological analyses demonstrate that club cell-specific AHR is also essential for EPFR-induced structural lung damage, including airspace enlargement, fibrosis, and mucus hyperproduction. EPFR exposure in LM mice led to significant increases in MLI, collagen deposition, and PAS-positive mucus cells, hallmarks of lung remodeling and chronic injury, whereas these features were considerably absent in AhrΔCC mice. These findings suggest that AHR signaling in club cells serves as a critical upstream modulator of epithelial remodeling and fibroinflammatory responses to environmental insults. While AHR has been classically studied in the context of detoxification and xenobiotic metabolism, emerging evidence points to its role in maintaining epithelial integrity and regulating tissue remodeling. Airspace enlargement, for example, is a well-recognized consequence of sustained neutrophilic inflammation and protease activity, both of which are supported by IL-17 and its downstream effectors. Importantly, the development of emphysematous changes in our model is mechanistically relevant to human exposure, as inhaled particulate matter and combustion-derived pollutants are well known to induce oxidative stress, epithelial injury, and chronic inflammatory signaling in the lung. Epidemiologic evidence has also linked exposure to EPFRs with increased risk of emphysema [36]. This is consistent with our findings presented here and our previous work which demonstrated septal destruction, and peribronchial remodeling using the EPFR-containing particle DCB230 in neonatal rats [37]. The reduction of MLI in AhrΔCC mice therefore supports a model in which club cell AHR facilitates a permissive environment for IL-17-mediated epithelial damage and extracellular matrix remodeling. Moreover, the attenuation of fibrosis in AhrΔCC mice aligns with prior studies showing that AHR activation can induce TGF-β-dependent fibroblast activation under certain contexts [[38], [39], [40], [41]]. Given that EPFRs induce oxidative stress and epithelial injury, AHR may amplify these effects in club cells, promoting a pro-fibrotic milieu via cytokine and chemokine release. The reduced mucus hypersecretion in AhrΔCC lungs further highlights the multifaceted role of AHR in airway remodeling, potentially through regulation of mucin genes (e.g., Muc5ac) and goblet cell differentiation, processes known to be exacerbated by chronic inflammation and pollutant exposure. Collectively, our findings support a mechanistic framework in which club cell-intrinsic AHR functions as an upstream environmental sensor that translates EPFR exposure into coordinated inflammatory and structural responses. Activation of AHR in club cells likely promotes production of cytokines such as IL-6 and IL-1β, which are well-recognized drivers of Th17 differentiation and expansion. The resulting increase in IL-17 can subsequently amplify neutrophilic inflammation leading to the release of neutrophil-derived proteases, reactive oxygen species, and other mediators that contribute to epithelial injury and extracellular matrix degradation. In parallel, AHR activation in epithelial cells may directly influence epithelial repair pathways, mucin production, and fibroblast activation through cytokine-dependent and independent mechanisms. Consistent with this model, the coordinated attenuation of Th17 responses, neutrophilic inflammation, fibrosis, and airspace enlargement observed in AhrΔCC mice suggests that club cell AHR links pollutant sensing to downstream immune activation and lung remodeling.

In line with the observed inflammatory and structural alterations, our functional analysis demonstrates that EPFR exposure leads to a significant increase in airway resistance, confirming the development of airway hyperresponsiveness, a hallmark of asthma and chronic lung injury. Unexpectedly, our data indicated that females appeared largely resistant to EPFR-induced airway hyperresponsiveness whereas EPFR-exposed males tended to exhibit greater increases in airway resistance (Fig. S9). Consistent with our findings, previous studies have shown that male mice display greater airway hyperresponsiveness and LPS-induced inflammation than females, with androgen signaling contributing to this heightened response [42]. In the present study, although no genotype differences were observed under air-exposed conditions, and despite findings from Traboulsi et al. showing reduced chlorine-induced airway hyperresponsiveness in global AHR-deficient mice [21], we did not detect a significant genotype-dependent difference when male and female cohorts were analyzed together. However, when the data were stratified by sex, EPFR-exposed male LM mice exhibited a trend toward greater airway hyperresponsiveness compared with male AhrΔCC mice. Although the group sizes were small and these sex-specific trends should be interpreted with caution, this observation is consistent with emerging evidence that AHR-dependent physiological responses may differ by sex, as reported in other disease models [43]. Our results extend this concept to airway epithelial cells, where Ahr deletion in club cells may confer protection in males following EPFR exposure, highlighting potential sex-dependent mechanisms in AHR-mediated lung responses. The differences between our findings and those of Traboulsi et al. likely reflect important distinctions in exposure type and underlying pathology. The chlorine inhalation model produces injury primarily centered in the conducting airways without emphysematous changes, whereas EPFR exposure in our model induces a mixed distal lung pathology characterized by Th17-driven neutrophilic inflammation, epithelial injury, and airspace enlargement. These distinct pathological features suggest that the mechanisms driving airway hyperresponsiveness may differ substantially between models. Consistent with this concept, Traboulsi et al. also reported no effect of AHR on OVA-induced airway hyperresponsiveness, further supporting the idea that AHR's contribution to airway physiology is highly dependent on the nature of the exposure and the underlying inflammatory milieu. Together, our findings highlight that AHR-mediated regulation of airway physiology is context- and sex-dependent and may vary according to the toxicant and underlying immune response. Consistent with these functional outcomes, a sex-stratified reanalysis of our flow cytometric data also revealed a trend towards a Th17-biased immune response in EPFR-exposed male LM mice compared to EPFR-exposed female LM mice (Fig. S6). Although the mechanisms underlying the observed sex-associated differences in EPFR-induced Th17 responses remain unexplored, studies suggest a male bias toward Th17-mediated inflammation across various disease contexts [[44], [45], [46], [47], [48], [49]]. Airway hyperresponsiveness is often the downstream consequence of chronic inflammation, epithelial injury, and tissue remodeling, processes that are tightly linked to Th17 responses and neutrophilic inflammation. Our findings are congruent with previous reports showing that IL-17 can directly contribute to airway smooth muscle contraction and hyperresponsiveness [[50], [51], [52]]. The reduced airway hyperresponsiveness observed in EPFR-exposed AhrΔCC males may therefore reflect blunted Th17-associated inflammation and diminished lung remodeling, supporting a mechanistic connection between AHR-driven immune activation and airway dysfunction. While these data suggest possible compartment and sex-related differences in responses to EPFR exposure, we recognize that the relatively small sample sizes in some groups limit statistical power and warrant further investigation of sex as a biological variable in studies of environmental lung disease.

A major strength of this study lies in the use of a highly specific conditional knockout model, which allowed for dissection of cell type-specific mechanisms, coupled with comprehensive immunological, histological, and physiological analyses. This integrated approach enabled a detailed characterization of how AHR signaling in club cells governs EPFR-induced Th17 responses and neutrophilic inflammation. Importantly, these findings carry translational relevance given recent reports from the Colfax community, where residents exposed to by-products of the thermal remediation of hazardous wastes containing elevated levels of EPFRs exhibited increased urinary levels of tryptophan metabolites (endogenous AHR ligands) and glutathione sulphonamide, a biomarker of pulmonary neutrophilia [53]. This epidemiological evidence supports the biological plausibility that EPFR exposure activates AHR signaling in the airway epithelium and promotes pulmonary neutrophilic inflammation. Our findings in mice demonstrate that AHR activation, particularly in club cells, contributes mechanistically to EPFR-driven Th17 immunity and neutrophilic inflammation, offering a potential explanation for environmentally linked respiratory morbidity in exposed populations.

One limitation of our study is the lack of mechanistic insight into the elevated basal Th17 cell frequencies observed in AhrΔCC mice. While reproducible, this finding was not accompanied by inflammation or functional deficits, suggesting that these cells lack effector competency in the absence of epithelial AHR signaling. Further studies are needed to determine whether this represents a compensatory immune shift, altered epithelial-immune crosstalk, or a developmental consequence of Ahr deletion in club cells. Additionally, while IL-1β, IL-6, KC/GRO and TNF-α were elevated in BALF of EPFR-exposed LM mice, the cellular sources and molecular pathways linking epithelial AHR signaling to their production require further investigation. Moreover, this study focused on short-term exposure and future models incorporating chronic EPFR exposure will be necessary to evaluate long-term consequences, including airway remodeling and persistent immune dysregulation. Additionally, while we observed sex-specific differences such as a trend towards enhanced Th17 cell frequency and elevated airway hyperresponsiveness in EPFR-exposed male LM mice, the molecular basis for these sex-biased responses remains to be elucidated. Lastly, our supplementary data using AhrΔAT2 mice show that AT2-specific AHR deletion promotes a Th2-skewed immune profile with increased eosinophilic inflammation following EPFR exposure, a response distinct from that observed in AhrΔCC mice, the primary focus of the present study. As the AhrΔAT2 animals were obtained through a collaborative project, additional histopathological analyses could not be performed due to limited availability. Consequently, it remains unclear whether AT2-driven Th2/eosinophilic responses in the absence of AHR contribute to lung remodeling following EPFR exposure.

In conclusion, our findings demonstrate that AHR signaling in club cells is a key driver of EPFR-induced lung pathology. By establishing a direct link between environmental sensing at the epithelial barrier and downstream Th17 polarization, neutrophilic inflammation, airway remodeling and dysfunction, this study uncovers a central mechanism of pulmonary immunotoxicity with clear relevance to human environmental health. These insights not only enhance our understanding of EPFR-driven respiratory disease but also suggest that selectively targeting airway epithelial AHR may offer a novel therapeutic strategy to mitigate the adverse pulmonary effects of environmental exposures.

Materials and methods

Animal husbandry and breeding strategy

All animals were housed in ventilated cages in a temperature- and humidity-controlled facility with a 12-h light/dark cycle. Food and water were provided ad libitum. All procedures were conducted in accordance with the National Institutes of Health Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals and were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of the Pennington Biomedical Research Center, Baton Rouge, LA.

Male Ahrtm3.1Bra/J mice carrying a floxed exon 2 allele of the Ahr gene (JAX stock #006203) and female B6N.129S6(Cg)-Scgb1a1tm1(cre/ERT)Blh/J mice expressing tamoxifen-inducible Cre recombinase under the control of the club cell-specific Scgb1a1 promoter (JAX stock #016225) were obtained from Jackson Laboratory. These breeders were initially crossed to generate F1 heterozygous offsprings. Female F1 mice were then crossed with Ahrfl/fl males to produce F2 progeny. F2 mice were genotyped, and only Scgb1a1-CreERTM; Ahrfl/fl (AhrΔCC) females were selected and crossed again with Ahrfl/fl males to generate experimental cohorts consisting of approximately 50% AhrΔCC and 50% Cre-negative Ahrfl/fl littermate (LM) controls (Fig. S2). Exposure to combustion-derived particulate matter

Combustion-derived particulate matter was generated by our colleagues Dr. Lavrent Khachatryan and Dr. Slawo Lomnicki (LSU) as previously described [23,54]. EPFR-containing particles were produced using a two-stage combustion reactor through oxidative pyrolysis of a precursor mixture consisting of 1-methylnaphthalene (85.9%), anthracene (0.3%), and 1-monochlorophenol (13.8%).

The presence and concentration of persistent free radicals in the particulate material were verified by electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR) spectroscopy using a Bruker EMX EPR spectrometer, as described previously [23]. The EPFR-containing particles used in this study exhibited an average radical concentration of approximately 5.5 × 1017 ± 5.27 × 1016 radicals/g. In prior work, EPFR-containing particles that lacked polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAH) [8,22] were shown to elicit adverse pulmonary effects, supporting a functional role for EPFRs in driving biological responses independent of PAHs.

For animal exposures, PM containing EPFRs was dissolved in sterile saline containing 0.02% Tween-80 at 1 mg/mL concentration. To ensure uniform dispersion and prevent agglomeration, the resulting suspension was subjected to probe sonication at 50% amplitude in pulse mode (1 s on/1 s off; 10 s/cycle) for 1 min. Sonication was performed on ice to prevent overheating and following sonication, the suspension was vortexed vigorously to ensure thorough dispersion before use in nebulization exposure. Adult mice (8-10 weeks of age of both sexes) were placed in a whole-body exposure chamber and exposed to nebulized particles for 30 min per day for seven consecutive days. The average daily exposure concentration was 200 μg/m3 [55]. These exposure levels are environmentally relevant, as ambient particulate matter has been reported to contain EPFR concentrations ranging from 1.1 × 1017 to 3.7 × 1019 radicals/g PM [56].

Lung cell isolation and flow cytometry

Mice were euthanized on day 7 following the exposures and lungs were isolated to prepare single cell suspension for flow cytometric staining. The isolated lungs were placed in Hank's Balanced Salt solution (HBSS) containing collagenase I (Invitrogen) and DNase I (Sigma Aldrich, St. Louis, MO), and coarsely pulverized using an octodissociator (Miltenyi, Auburn, CA) and incubated for 20 min in a heater/shaker set at 37 °C and 250 RPM. Following incubation, the lungs were further subjected to dissociation to reduce the remaining cell clumps to single cell suspension. Cells were strained through a 40 μm cell strainer (BD Biosciences, Fraklin Lakes, NJ). The resulting cell suspension was treated with RBC lysis buffer (eBioscience, San Diego, CA) to remove any residual blood cells. For Th cell profiling, cells were incubated for 5 h at 37 °C with a cell activation cocktail with brefeldin A (BioLegend, San Diego, CA) made in complete RPMI media. Extracellular and intracellular (cytoplasmic and nuclear) staining was performed following the standard ThermoFisher staining protocols using the antibodies listed in Table 2. For T-cell analysis, the panel included CD45, CD3, CD4, IFN-γ, IL-4, IL-17A, and a FITC dump channel. For epithelial cell analysis, CD45, CD31, CD326, CC10, CD74, and AHR antibodies were used. Viability dye staining was performed prior to surface and intracellular staining. The samples were run on Cytek Aurora (Cytek, Fremont, CA) and 3x105 events per sample were collected. Data were analyzed using FlowJo Software v.10 (FlowJo LLC, Ashland, OR). Dead cells and doublets were excluded from analysis. Fluorescence minus one (FMO) and non-activated samples (lacking activation cocktail) were used to establish gating (Figs. Bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) cellularity

After the exposure, mice were humanely euthanized and BALF was collected. Using a canula, 1 mL of saline containing 0.5% BSA was injected into the lungs to obtain BALF. BALF was placed on ice and later processed for total and differential cell counts. BALF slides were prepared using a cytospin, air-dried and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) for morphological identification of immune cell types. At least 300 cells per slide were counted in an unbiased manner to determine the BALF differentials.

ELISA

IL-17A levels in BALF samples were measured using a commercially available Mouse IL-17A Sandwich ELISA Kit (Proteintech, Rosemont, IL; catalog# KE10020) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Standards and samples were added to antibody pre-coated 96-well microplate and incubated at 37 °C. After washing, detection antibody and Streptavidin-HRP were added sequentially with incubation steps in between. Plates were developed with TMB substrate and the reaction was stopped using stop solution. Absorbance was measured immediately at 450 nm using a microplate reader. A standard curve was generated using a four-parameter logistic (4-PL) regression model, and IL-17A concentrations (pg/mL) were determined accordingly.

Multiplex cytokine analysis

Cytokine concentrations in BALF were quantified using the V-PLEX Proinflammatory Panel 1 Mouse Kit (Meso Scale Discovery, Rockville, MD), a multiplex electrochemiluminescence-based immunoassay. Samples and calibrators were processed according to the manufacturer's instructions and added to pre-coated 96-well plates. Following incubation with detection antibodies, plates were washed, developed with read buffer, and analyzed using the MESO QuickPlex SQ 120 instrument. Cytokine concentrations (pg/mL) were determined using a four-parameter logistic (4-PL) standard curve in MSD Discovery Workbench analysis software (version 4.0).

Histology

The lung samples were fixed in 10% neutral buffered formalin and processed using a TissueTek VIP 6 Vacuum Infiltration Processor. Lung tissue was embedded in paraffin, sectioned at 5 μm thickness, and stained with H&E for microscopy and histopathological examination. H&E staining was performed using a Leica ST5020 Autostainer. Additional sections were stained with Picrosirius Red (PSR) to evaluate collagen deposition and fibrosis, and with Periodic Acid Schiff (PAS) to assess mucus production, using standard histological protocols. All stained slides were scanned at 20× magnification using a Hamamatsu NanoZoomer Digital Pathology system (Hamamatsu City, Japan). Quantitative analysis of stained sections was performed using ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD).

Quantification of PSR and PAS staining

Quantitative analysis of PSR and PAS-stained lung sections was performed using ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD). For each mouse, three non-overlapping regions were selected from scanned whole-slide images. Images were processed using color deconvolution to isolate the PSR or PAS signal. A fixed threshold was applied uniformly across all samples and the percentage of positively stained areas within each region of interest was calculated. For each region, four measurements were obtained, and values were averaged to generate a single value per animal for statistical comparison.

Mean linear intercept (MLI) analysis

MLI was determined using a standard morphometric technique on H&E-stained lung sections. Images were captured from three randomly selected, non-overlapping regions per mouse lung section, avoiding large airways and blood vessels. From each region, four readings were taken by overlaying a digital grid consisting of two sets of parallel lines using ImageJ software. The total length of the grid lines was divided by the number of intercepts with alveolar walls to calculate MLI, which represents the average distance between alveolar septa. MLI values were averaged for each animal, and group comparisons were made to assess structural changes in the lung parenchyma [57,58].

Immunofluorescence staining

Paraffin-embedded lung sections were deparaffinized and subjected to heat-induced epitope retrieval (pH 6.0). Slides were allowed to cool at room temperature for 30 min, then rinsed in Tris-buffered saline with Tween-20 (TBST). To reduce autofluorescence, slides were photobleached for 1 h and subsequently rinsed in TBST. Slides were incubated overnight at room temperature with primary antibodies against AHR (Proteintech, Rosemont, IL; catalog# 67785-1-Ig) followed by HRP-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG2b secondary antibody (Jackson ImmunoResearch, West Grove, PA; catalog#115-035-207) and CF594 tyramide (Biotium, Fremont, CA; catalog#92174) signal amplification. After washing, slides were incubated overnight at room temperature with a second primary antibody against CC10 (Proteintech, Rosemont, IL; catalog# 10490-1-AP), followed by an Alexa Fluor Plus 488-conjugated goat anti-rabbit secondary antibody (Invitrogen, Waltham, MA; catalog# A32731). Slides were then counterstained with Hoechst 33342 (1 μg/mL) for 10 min, rinsed with PBS and mounted in Aqua-Poly/Mount (Polysciences, Warrington, PA; catalog# 18606). Slides were scanned at 20X using a Zeiss Axioscan 7 slide scanner (Zeiss, Jena, Germany).

Lung function assessment

Lung function was assessed using the Buxco FinePointe RC system (DSI, St. Paul, MN) as described previously [59]. Mice were anesthetized via intraperitoneal injection of ketamine (180 mg/kg) and xylazine (10 mg/kg). Following confirmation of anesthetic depth by the absence of reflexes, animals were tracheostomized using an 18G cannula and connected to a small-animal ventilator. To suppress spontaneous respiration during measurements, pancuronium bromide (0.5 mg/kg, intraperitoneally) was administered as a neuromuscular blocking agent. Mice were then placed on a heated bed in plethysmography chamber and ventilated with a tidal volume of 10 mL/kg, a respiratory rate of 180 breaths per minute, and a positive end-expiratory pressure (PEEP) of 3 cmH2O. A stabilization period of 3-5 min was allowed prior to data collection. Airway hyperresponsiveness was evaluated by administering increasing concentrations of aerosolized methacholine (0, 3.125, 6.25, 12.5, 25, and 50 mg/mL) using an in-line nebulizer (Aeroneb Lab, DSI), with each dose delivered over 10 s. Following each dose, lung resistance (RI) was recorded continuously for 3 min and 30 s.

Statistical analysis: GraphPad Prism software (version 10.2.3, San Diego, CA) was used for data analysis. Data are presented as mean ± SEM. Statistical significance at p < 0.05 is denoted by (∗) or (a,b).
