Provision of gender-affirming care for trans and gender-diverse adults: a systematic review of health and quality of life outcomes, values and preferences, and costs
Authors: Erin E. Cooney, Luke Muschialli, Ping Teresa Yeh, Connor Luke Allen, Dean J. Connolly, Rose Pollard Kaptchuk, Katrina S. Kennedy, Brooke Wong, Caitlin E. Kennedy
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryBackgroundThere is a substantial evidence base documenting the impact of gender-affirming care on health outcomes among transgender (trans) and gender-diverse people globally, but this rapidly growing body of evidence has not been comprehensively synthesized.MethodsWe conducted a systematic review of studies examining outcomes, values and preferences, and costs of gender-affirming care. Protocols were registered in PROSPERO (CRD42024539078 and CRD42024569628). We searched CINAHL, Cochrane Central, Embase, LILACS, PsycINFO, PubMed, and grey literature sources for studies published between 1 January 2018 and 31 March 2024. The outcomes review included data from randomized controlled trials and longitudinal studies that compared health outcomes among adults (age 18+) who received gender-affirming care to those who did not. We synthesized data by subtype (e.g. psychosocial, hormone therapy, surgical) and by study design, using GRADE evidence profiles. We assessed risk of bias with the Cochrane ROB2 and ROBINS-I tools. We summarized values and preferences data qualitatively and provided confidence ratings using GRADE CERQual, and narratively summarized cost data.FindingsTwenty-eight studies, including four randomized controlled trials and 24 longitudinal studies, evaluated health outcomes following gender-affirming care. Moderate to very low certainty evidence from these studies suggests gender-affirming care may improve quality of life, stigma, utilization of health services, and mental health. No studies identified significant negative outcomes or harms. An additional 87 values and preferences studies, six of which also provided cost data, found that gender-affirming care is acceptable and affordable, although costs vary by type of care.InterpretationGender-affirming care may improve critical outcomes, including quality of life, among trans and gender-diverse adults who seek this care. Additional data from prospective controlled studies would enhance the evidence base to comprehensively document the impact of gender-affirming care on physical and mental health outcomes.Funding10.13039/100004423World Health Organization through a grant from the 10.13039/100014302Elton John AIDS Foundation and the 10.13039/100000865Bill and Melinda Gates Foundation.

Research in context

Evidence before this study

Prior systematic reviews have examined the association between some types of gender-affirming care and select health and well-being outcomes. For example, these have examined the associations between gender-affirming hormone therapy and quality of life, mental health, cardiovascular outcomes, and skeletal health, with additional reviews examining mental and physical health outcomes and quality of life following gender affirming surgeries. However, we did not identify any systematic reviews that have comprehensively synthesized evidence on outcomes, values and preferences, and cost for gender-affirming care globally. We searched six databases (PubMed, Embase, CINAHL, Cochrane Central, LILACS, and PsycINFO) for articles published between 1 January 2018 and 31 March 2024 without geographic or language restriction. Search terms included two primary concepts: Concept 1 (trans and gender-diverse people) + Concept 2 (gender-affirming care). We identified 28 articles reporting outcomes of longitudinal studies, and 87 reporting on values and preferences and cost. Findings were assessed to be moderate to very low certainty depending on the outcome.

Added value of this study

This systematic review comprehensively and rigorously identified and synthesized the best available evidence on the outcomes, values and preferences, and costs of gender affirming care provided by the health sector across global settings. We reviewed comparative data for fifteen key health outcomes comparing trans and gender-diverse adults who received gender-affirming care within the health sector to trans and gender-diverse adults who sought but did not receive this care.

Implications of all the available evidence

Our findings indicate that provision of gender-affirming care may improve quality of life, stigma, utilization of health services, and mental health for trans and gender-diverse adults who seek this care, but these findings are predominately informed by low certainty evidence. We also identified research gaps for the outcomes of: access to health services, discrimination related to gender identity, physical health conditions, violence related to gender identity, satisfaction with health services, substance use, adverse events, and mortality. Continued research, including prospective controlled study designs with rigorous measures for outcomes not previously reported, would further enhance the evidence base and inform clinical guidelines. However, available evidence supports the provision of gender-affirming care to trans and gender-diverse adults in alignment with evidence-based clinical practice guidance based on the outcomes, acceptability, and affordability data available.

Introduction

The term ‘transgender (trans) and gender-diverse’ refers to individuals whose gender identity (internal and individual experience of gender) differs from social expectations based on one's sex assigned at birth.1,2 Trans and gender-diverse people globally experience disproportionate burdens of stigma, violence, and discrimination. This is primarily attributable to institutionalized cisnormativity, hostile legislative and social environments, and individual prejudice,3,4 which manifest in an increased burden of both physical (e.g., sexually transmitted infections,5 cardiovascular conditions6) and mental (e.g., anxiety disorders,7 mood disorders8) health conditions.9,10 Trans and gender-diverse people of color and individuals who do not visually conform to cisnormative expectations of gender experience disproportionately more violence and discrimination, including within healthcare settings.11,12

Gender-affirming care—defined as health services specifically designed to support and affirm an individual's gender—is often sought to facilitate alignment of gender expression with gender identity. Gender-affirming care includes psychosocial interventions (e.g., voice therapy, gender-affirming psychotherapeutic interventions) as well as surgery (e.g., mastectomy/breast augmentation, vaginoplasty/phalloplasty) or medications (e.g., masculinizing hormones such as testosterone, feminizing hormones such as estrogen). Notably, gender-affirming care is distinct from inclusive health care for trans and gender-diverse individuals, which focuses on structural and/or health service-level interventions that increase trans and gender-diverse people's inclusion in, and access to, healthcare. Examples include health workers using respectful terminology and inclusive facilities (e.g. bathrooms/emergency intake).

Recent systematic reviews have examined the associations between gender-affirming hormone therapy13, 14, 15, 16, 17 and surgical care18, 19, 20, 21, 22, 23 and quality of life, mental health, cardiovascular outcomes, and skeletal health. Reviews of psychosocial interventions such as voice training have been associated with greater satisfaction with gender expression and identity.24 Previous systematic reviews have primarily focused on specific gender-affirming interventions or have not included data on the values, preferences, or costs.

We sought to systematically and comprehensively review the available evidence on gender-affirming care, including outcomes among trans and gender-diverse adults, the reported values and preferences of both trans and gender-diverse individuals receiving care and healthcare professionals providing this care, and the costs of such interventions.

Methods

Search strategy and selection criteria

We systematically reviewed available evidence on gender-affirming care among trans and gender-diverse adults globally, including data on fifteen key health outcomes, values and preferences, and cost, following PRISMA 2020 guidelines.25 The fifteen key health outcomes were selected by the WHO Guideline Development Group, which is comprised of external (i.e., non-WHO) international experts on trans and gender-diverse health, many of whom also bring lived experience as trans and gender-diverse individuals, based on community importance. Protocols for the outcomes review and values and preferences and cost review were registered in PROSPERO (CRD42024539078 and CRD42024569628, respectively).

All relevant studies from any geographic region and published in any language between 1 January 2018 and 31 March 2024 were included. These dates were selected to capture relatively recent data corresponding with the change in International Classification of Diseases (ICD) version 11 (ICD-11), which removed gender dysphoria and added gender incongruence. We excluded studies that did not report primary data. Our population of interest was trans and gender-diverse adults aged 18 years or older who sought gender-affirming care; therefore, we excluded studies that focused on children (<18 years). If a study included both adults and children, we requested disaggregated data for adult participants. For the outcomes review, we included studies that presented quantitative data on at least one of fifteen health outcomes selected a priori (Appendix A). We included randomized controlled trial (RCT) or uncontrolled/controlled longitudinal (before/after) study designs because we were interested in comparative data between adults who received gender-affirming care within the health sector (defined as health services specifically designed to support and affirm an individual's gender identity) to those who did not (but who expressed a desire to receive such care). We excluded studies that only reported outcomes data comparing two or more forms of gender-affirming care. For the values and preferences and cost review, we included studies that presented values and preferences of trans and gender-diverse individuals and/or health workers or data on intervention cost, with no study design restrictions.

We searched 6 databases (CINAHL, Cochrane Central, EMBASE, LILACS, PsycINFO, and PubMed) for relevant publications. Search terms were adapted from other recent reviews4,26,27 and included two primary concepts: 1) gender-affirming care and 2) trans and gender-diverse people (Appendix B). References of included articles and five relevant systematic reviews13,15, 16, 17,28 were secondarily searched to identify additional studies. For the outcomes review, we searched for grey literature by searching conference abstracts from health-related scientific consortia focused on trans and gender-diverse individuals (World Professional Association for Transgender Health (WPATH), US PATH, European PATH, Asia PATH) and websites of relevant organizations (Appendix C).

A study team member conducted initial screening of all titles/abstracts to remove clearly irrelevant articles (e.g., pre-clinical studies) and duplicates. Titles/abstracts were then screened by two study team members independently in duplicate. Full-text articles were assessed independently in duplicate to determine eligibility for inclusion. Differences were resolved through consensus. For studies in English, French, Spanish, Portuguese, Japanese, or Chinese, the article was assessed by study team members proficient in these languages. Studies in other languages were translated into English via Google Translate.

Data analysis

Data were extracted independently by two reviewers using standardized forms in Microsoft Excel. Differences in data extraction were resolved through consensus and referral to senior study team members. Extracted information for all studies included: citation information, study methods (sample size, study design) location (country, region, urban/rural, World Bank income classification), population description (e.g., age, gender identities), intervention description, and key findings. Extracted information for outcome studies additionally included: follow-up period, attrition, comparator description, study outcomes, limitations, conclusions, and ethical considerations. Extracted information for values and preferences/cost studies additionally included: qualitative/quantitative, cost/cost-effectiveness data (when available).

For RCTs, we assessed risk of bias (ROB) using the Cochrane Collaboration's ROB 2.0 tool.29 For controlled and uncontrolled before/after studies, we used the ROBINS-I tool.30 For the “bias due to missing data” domain, we judged loss to follow-up at and after 6 months as “low” if <5% missing, “moderate” if 5–10% missing, “serious” if 10–30% missing, and “critical” if >30% missing. If baseline characteristics of those lost to follow-up and those retained were compared by study investigators and found comparable, we moved the risk down a level (e.g. from serious to moderate). We used the robvis tool to create risk of bias traffic light and summary plots.31

Findings for the outcomes review were summarized in GRADE Evidence Profile tables using GRADEPro, prioritizing RCT data over observational data where available and where rigorously conducted according to appropriate ethical standards. The certainty for each outcome was judged as either high, moderate, low, or very low based on the risk of bias, consistency, directness, and precision of the estimate of effect per outcome. Imprecision was operationalized as a risk ratio with a confidence interval including both clinically relevant benefit and harm (i.e., greater than 1.25 or less than 0.80); we downgraded once for imprecision. Inconsistency was downgraded once if there was only a single study, since consistency could not be assessed. We downgraded once for indirectness if a study included a sample that may not represent the wider trans and gender-diverse population (e.g., trans women living with HIV). Statements of confidence throughout the manuscript reflect standard GRADE language.32 While our protocol planned for meta-analysis, the diversity of interventions, outcome measures, and study designs did not facilitate this.

Findings for the values and preferences review were summarized qualitatively and organized by study design and methodology, location, and population. Findings for the cost-effectiveness review were summarized descriptively. We used GRADE CERQual to synthesize evidence from studies and provide a confidence rating.33

Role of the funding source

This review was commissioned to inform a WHO guideline development process around the health of trans and gender-diverse people. Members of the review team were not involved in decision making regarding recommendations. Conversely, those involved in developing the recommendations had no engagement in the screening, data extraction, data synthesis, quality assessment, manuscript development and/or decision to submit for publication.

Summary and study-level risk of bias assessments are presented in Appendix F. RCTs were judged as having some concerns or high risk of bias overall: two RCTs had high risk of bias due to missing outcome data because of high attrition; three RCTs had some concerns due to outcome measurement; one RCT had potential bias arising from the randomization process; and one RCT had potential bias from selection of the reported results. All RCTs were judged low risk of bias in at least two domains. Uncontrolled before/after studies were judged to have serious to critical risk of bias, primarily due to lack of participant/assessor blinding potentially effecting self-reported outcome measures. Other domains effecting some studies included attrition of study participants over time and uncertainty around comprehensive reporting of outcomes data due to lack of study pre-registration/protocol publication. A few studies also had challenges with measurement reporting, due to lack of data on specific measures used and appropriateness or validity of measures.

Several pre-specified outcomes of interest were not measured in any study: access to health services, discrimination related to gender identity, physical health conditions, violence related to gender identity, satisfaction with health services, substance use, adverse events, or mortality. Data for the other outcomes are presented by intervention type, and organized by study design.

Three RCTs36,44,51 and three uncontrolled before/after studies38,56,60 reported on the impact of gender-affirming psychosocial interventions on quality of life, utilization of health services, stigma related to gender identity, and perception of wellbeing (Appendix Tables AG1, AG2 and Appendix H1). RCTs included an empowerment model-based educational program,36 a gender-affirming peer navigation program to support HIV care engagement following HIV diagnosis,44 and a gender-affirming, theory-based, peer-delivered, 7-session intervention to improve HIV care engagement.51 Two of the three uncontrolled before/after studies focused on voice training for trans women,56,60 and one examined two psychosocial strategies for gender-affirming care (transgender affirmative psychotherapy alone, and transgender affirmative psychotherapy with building awareness of minority stressors).38 One RCT provided moderate certainty evidence that psychosocial interventions likely improve quality of life (Appendix Table AG1).36 Comparing 3-month follow-up between groups, overall quality of life was 62.01 (SD: 6.10) in the intervention group and 58.03 (SD: 6.73) in the control group (P = 0.005), which represents a clinically important difference.62 One uncontrolled before/after study provided very low certainty evidence about the effect of psychosocial interventions on stigma related to gender identity (Appendix Table AG2). The study showed non-statistically significant improvements in internalized transphobia, both immediately and 6 months after transgender affirmative psychotherapy.38 Two RCTs provided very low certainty evidence suggesting psychosocial interventions may improve utilization of health services but the evidence is very uncertain (Appendix Table AG1).44,51 Both assessed HIV care engagement using multiple outcome measures and showed trends toward higher engagement among intervention participants, but neither were statistically significant. Two uncontrolled before/after studies of trans women in the US assessed satisfaction with body-image through the Trans Woman Voice Questionnaire, which measures voice-related quality of life and satisfaction, after gender-affirming voice training.56,60 Both reported statistically significant improvements in perception of well-being following voice training (Appendix Table AH1.12).56,60

One RCT48 and eight uncontrolled before/after studies39,41,45,50,53, 54, 55,59 assessed the impact of gender-affirming hormone therapy on quality of life, mental health, suicidal behaviors, perception of wellbeing, and gender incongruence (Appendix Tables AG3, AG4 and AH2). A single RCT with 62 trans men compared immediate initiation of gender-affirming testosterone treatment to a control group receiving standard care (initiation of testosterone after a 3-month waitlist; no testosterone therapy during the 3-month study period) in Australia.48 The eight uncontrolled before/after studies examined the impact of hormone therapy over 3 months to 3 years of follow-up. Low certainty evidence from the Australian RCT suggests gender-affirming hormone therapy may increase or have no effect on quality of life, depending on the assessment tool (Appendix Table AG3).48 Similarly, five uncontrolled before/after studies showed statistically significant improvements or no change in quality of life depending on the measures used and subpopulations assessed (Appendix Table AG4). Low certainty evidence from the Australian RCT suggests gender-affirming hormone therapy may improve mental health (Appendix Table AG3).48 One uncontrolled before/after study found a statistically significant improvement in global mental health symptom severity 12 months after starting hormone therapy.39 Of two studies assessing changes in depressive symptoms, one found an improvement in symptomology45 while the second found a non-significant minimal increase in symptoms.59 The Australian RCT found a significant reduction in suicidal ideation using two different measures (Appendix Table AH2.10).48 One uncontrolled before/after study showed improvements in perception of wellbeing/satisfaction with body image 12 months after hormone initiation (Appendix Table AH2.12).53 Four studies (one RCT and three uncontrolled before/after studies) examined changes in gender incongruence/dysphoria following hormone therapy (Appendix Table AH2.14): three, including the RCT, found improvements in these domains,39,41,48 while one found no difference.54

Thirteen uncontrolled before/after studies34,35,37,40,42,43,46,47,49,52,57,58,61 reported on the impact of gender-affirming surgical interventions on quality of life, utilization of health services, mental health, suicidal behaviors, perception of wellbeing, and gender incongruence (Appendix Tables AG5 and AH3). These studies examined a variety of gender-affirming surgeries across a wide range of follow-up periods (between 1-week and 40 years post-surgery). Four studies investigated changes in quality of life following surgical intervention, all observing statistically significant improvements.35,40,42,47 One uncontrolled longitudinal study assessed utilization of mental health treatment, comparing pre-operative outcomes and outcomes 40 years following surgery, but the authors did not assess for need for mental health treatment so the findings are difficult to interpret.49 Five studies evaluated changes in mental health outcomes following gender affirming surgery40,43,47,49,57: some found improvements in mental health while others found no change (Appendix Table AG5). Two studies reported on suicidal behaviors before and after gender affirming surgery40,49: neither reported significant changes in suicidal behaviors, but there were very few events in both studies. Six studies reported statistically significant improvements in various measures of satisfaction with body image after gender-affirming surgery.34,43,52,57,58,61 Five studies measured gender dysphoria before and after the start of gender-affirming surgery,34,37,46,49,57 with all finding improvements in gender congruence following surgical care.

Two uncontrolled before/after studies54,56 reported on the impact of combinations of gender-affirming interventions on quality of life, perception of wellbeing, and gender incongruence (Appendix Tables AG6 and AH4). One study combined voice training with gender-affirming glottoplasty; the other study included individuals who were started on hormone therapy and then received gender-affirming surgery at later time points. One study observed a decline in quality-of-life score following gonadectomy in combination with hormones.54 The same study observed a statistically significant decrease in gender dysphoria. The study of combined voice therapy with glottoplasty reported improved voice-related quality of life following the interventions.55

Values and preferences

Eighty-seven studies presented the values and preferences of trans and gender-diverse individuals and/or gender-affirming care providers (summary description in Appendix I). Thirty-eight studies were cross sectional surveys, 35 semi-structured or in-depth interviews, nine retrospective chart/database reviews, four focus groups, three case study/series and one cost and trend analysis. More than half of studies were conducted in the US. Ten studies included health providers.

Key findings and results from a confidence assessment using the GRADE-CERQual framework are reported in Appendix J (bibliography in Appendix K).

In summary, trans and gender-diverse individuals and providers were generally satisfied with their experiences of gender-affirming hormonal, surgical and psychosocial care. Providers and trans and gender-diverse individuals cited a range of benefits associated with gender-affirming care, including improved social (e.g., increased acceptance from society, increased feelings of safety/comfort in public space), health/wellbeing (e.g., improved quality of life, improved self-image) and gender outcomes (e.g., facilitating expression of gender identity, increasing alignment with internal body image). Although some individuals did report fears around engaging with gender-affirming care prior to the intervention, articulated fears were surrounding the implications of the intervention for employment (e.g., time off work, response from employers) and fears about the responses of their loved ones to the intervention.

Trans and gender-diverse individuals highlighted how access to gender-affirming care allowed them to exercise personal preference and autonomy, particularly within facilities that were practicing the informed consent model of care. However, trans and gender-diverse individuals also highlighted experiences when this autonomy had been compromised by numerous political (e.g., restrictive legislature, lack of protection against discrimination), financial (e.g., insufficient insurance coverage, high cost of procedures), social (e.g., anticipated societal exclusion, difficulties associated with travelling to care) and systemic healthcare barriers (e.g., gatekeeping of services, insufficient information about procedures, lack of provider competence). Participants with multiple intersecting marginalised identities, such as trans and gender-diverse individuals from minoritised racial and ethnic communities, articulated increased barriers in accessing gender-affirming care, and access inequity was also reported by participants who did not have a binary gender identity (i.e., non-binary individuals).

Trans and gender-diverse individuals and gender-affirming care providers identified several features of gender-affirming care services they valued, including having specialized care for trans and gender-diverse individuals, engaging with culturally competent and knowledgeable staff, incorporating wider support services within gender-affirming care settings, such as legal and social support for transitioning, and coordination between gender-affirming care and other healthcare (e.g., HIV) services.

Participants articulated multiple factors which determined whether they sought out any gender-affirming care, with the prioritised outcomes varying across individuals and intervention types. Broadly, trans and gender-diverse individuals described anticipated recovery time, perceived difficulty of intervention, reversibility and wider social determinants (e.g., legislative environments requiring gender-affirming care for changing legal gender, anticipated impact on acceptance in wider society) as factors influencing whether they engaged with gender-affirming care. Regarding choice of which type of gender-affirming care to access, individuals discussed certain factors which informed their choice, including a desire for specific anatomical/biological features to facilitate gender expression (e.g., facial hair), health considerations (e.g., medical history, potential mental, physical or sexual health complications) and implications for post-operative care (i.e., whether intervention required post-operative douching or dilation).

Across the eight key values and preferences findings we determined high confidence in four key findings, and moderate confidence in four key findings. Moderate confidence was generally due to small sample sizes, low response rates, non-representative samples and a lack of validated tools to assess outcomes.

Cost

Six studies with relevant cost data were identified. Four studies presented costs of gender-affirming surgery,63, 64, 65, 66 one for psychosocial interventions67 and one for hormone therapy.68 Interventions for which cost information was provided were vaginoplasty, phalloplasty, laser hair removal, electrolysis, hormonal therapies (estrogen and antiandrogens, testosterone), facial feminization surgery, and unspecified affirming surgeries. Five cost studies63,64,66, 67, 68 were based in the US. One study68 reported the average annual cost of gender-affirming hormone therapy at US dollars (USD) 157 (USD121 for testosterone and USD153 for estrogen and antiandrogen). Costs of gender-affirming surgery in the US ranged from USD10,055 (vaginoplasty) to USD89,176 (facial feminization surgery).63,64,66 An additional study from the US found the cost of laser hair removal was significantly cheaper than electrolysis post-vaginoplasty (USD981 versus USD5161).67 Only one study provided cost data outside the US.65 This study, conducted in Thailand, found the median cost of unspecified gender-affirming surgery to be 120,000 Thai Baht (∼USD4000).

Discussion

This systematic review identified 28 studies evaluating outcomes of gender-affirming care among trans and gender-diverse adults globally across fifteen outcomes of interest. These studies evaluated diverse interventions, including gender-affirming hormones, surgery, psychosocial interventions, and combination interventions. Four RCTs provided moderate to very low certainty evidence, while uncontrolled before/after studies all had serious to critical risks of bias. Across the seven reported outcomes (quality of life, mental health, suicidal behaviors, utilization of health services, stigma related to gender identity, gender incongruence, perception of well-being), studies generally found positive effects, and no studies reported evidence of substantive harms. Evidence from one RCT of a gender-affirming psychosocial intervention suggests that gender-affirming care likely improves quality of life based on moderate certainty evidence. Data for the remaining outcomes were based on low to very low certainty evidence and suggest that gender-affirming care may improve or have no effect on mental health, suicidal behaviors, utilization of health services, stigma related to gender identity, gender incongruence, and perception of well-being. Studies which showed no difference across these outcomes had important limitations in their study designs (for example, limited statistical power to detect differences or lack of control group). We also identified research gaps for the outcomes of: access to health services, discrimination related to gender identity, physical health conditions, violence related to gender identity, satisfaction with health services, substance use, adverse events, and mortality. Continued research, including prospective controlled study designs with rigorous measures for outcomes not previously reported, would further enhance the evidence base for evaluating the impact of gender-affirming care on these health outcomes.

We also identified 87 values and preferences studies, which found that trans and gender-diverse individuals and health workers reported personal and social benefits to gender-affirming care for individuals who desire this care. Gender-affirming care empowers trans and gender-diverse individuals to exercise bodily autonomy within the health system but social, legal, and health system barriers in many settings have limited individuals’ access to this care thereby impeding access to these perceived benefits, including health and quality of life impacts.69, 70, 71

We did not identify any economic evaluations or cost-effectiveness studies, but six studies, mostly from the US, reported estimated costs of specific procedures or hormone regimens. Costs are likely to vary across settings due to differences in health systems. Some types of gender-affirming care may require investments in infrastructure or other program costs beyond the direct costs of providing specific care to individuals (e.g., costs associated with health worker training). Cost estimates provided are therefore not necessarily generalizable across settings.

Strengths of this review include our inclusion of evidence from all regions published in all languages, adhering to a rigorous and comprehensive search and evidence synthesis processes, and coordinated evaluation of outcomes, values and preferences, and cost data. Our synthesis was limited by a lack of data for several outcomes and from some regions, and reliance on significance testing rather than established minimal clinically important difference thresholds in some of the included studies.72,73 Due to incompatibility in interventions, outcome measures, and study designs, we were unable to conduct meta-analyses. To facilitate meta-analyses in the future, standardization of outcome measures in the field is needed, which may by facilitated by wider adoption of patient-reported outcomes measures that are non-proprietary and specifically designed for trans and gender-diverse individuals.74, 75, 76 It is possible that we have excluded relevant data from methodologically rigorous studies published prior to 2018 or after March 2024, as these would not have been captured in our search; thus, these findings may be subject to publication bias. Future research from prospective controlled studies to enhance the evidence base would be informative, although such studies may not be feasible due to the associated costs and complexity, particularly given that this care is already integrated into evidence-based clinical care guidelines in many settings.1 Further, this review focused exclusively on adults receiving gender-affirming care. Future research to synthesize the evidence base for adolescents is needed given the unique context and considerations for trans and gender-diverse people under 18 years.69

In summary, this systematic review of outcomes, values and preferences, and costs of gender-affirming care for trans and gender-diverse adults demonstrates that such care may be associated with positive health outcomes and is acceptable and affordable across many settings.
