The impact of winter clean heating in northern China on air quality and its spatial spillover effect of the pilot cities
Authors: Wei Hong, Lu Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Air pollution is a serious environmental problem China has faced in recent years, with winter coal combustion being one of the main sources of air pollution in the northern region. Based on meteorological and economic statistical data from 285 Chinese cities, this study uses the spatial Durbin Difference-in-Difference model to evaluate the actual effect on air quality of the winter clean heating pilot implemented in the northern region since 2017. Our research found that: (1) Pilot policy significantly reduced the level of pollutants in cities, and the air quality index (AQI) score decreased by 3.798. Six air pollutants that make up AQI, including PM2.5, PM10, SO2, CO, and NO2, reduced by 5.637 μg/m3, 9.724 μg/m3, 7.386 μg/m3, 0.219 mg/m3, and 1.34 μg/m3 respectively. But O3 concentration increased instead. (2) The pilot policy has a spatial spillover effect on the emission reduction of several pollutants, but it has not significantly affected the AQI of other cities. (3) By comparing the effect between heating and non-heating periods, the clean heating pilot policy mainly improved the AQI in winter, due to the increased usage of clean energy such as electricity and natural gas. The above results indicate that, as an energy transition project, the clean heating policy in China had a significant effect on relieving air pollution. Developing countries are recommended to accelerate the promotion of clean energy substitution and update facilities to improve energy efficiency, in order to improve local environmental quality and protect the residents’ health.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-20674-7.

Introduction

Air pollution is a persistent hot topic in global environmental governance. With the continuous improvement of industrialization and urbanization, the modern lifestyle has brought high energy consumption and waste gas emission, thus the concentration of air pollutants is showing a surging trend. Air pollution, especially PM2.5 or haze, has a significant negative impact on human society. Severe haze disrupts people’s travel and production activities1,2. Air pollution can also cause acute and chronic damage to the human body. Exposure to air pollutants such as carbon monoxide (CO), sulfur dioxide (SO2), and nitrogen oxides (NOx) increases the likelihood of suffering from respiratory diseases, heart disease, lung cancer, and more3,4. Furthermore, long-term exposure to air pollution can lead to excess mortality rates and lower life expectancy5. Although it is widely recognized that air quality improvement is crucial for the sustainable development of human civilization, it remains a challenge to effectively mitigate air pollution.

Since the 1980s, China has undergone a rapid transformation from an agricultural society to an industrial society, with rapid growth of per capita gross domestic product (GDP) and consumption ability. However, this growth has been accompanied by a continuous deterioration in the air quality. Northern China, especially the Beijing-Tianjin-Hebei region, is the region with the most severe air pollution in China. The energy supply in northern China is mainly coal, and heating causes a huge demand for coal consumption in winter. For a long time in the past, small coal-fired boilers and residents in northern areas were accustomed to using lower combustion efficiency loose coal (including briquettes, honeycomb coal, etc.), and even burning biomass energy such as straw, to meet heating, cooking, and other needs. Traditional energy combustion is one of the most important sources of PM2.5 and significantly contributed to haze6, and the unreasonable energy structure has led to China facing serious air environment problems in the past two decades. 2013 was China’s “first year of smog”, with the most annual haze days in the last 52 years (https://www.gov.cn/govweb/jrzg/2013-11/01/content_2519820.htm). In the same year, China introduced the Action Plan for Air Pollution Prevention and Control, which focused on reducing inhalable particulate matter (PM10), fine particulate matter (PM2.5), and haze, as well as addressing regional air pollution issues.

To solve the air pollution problem caused by traditional energy overuse in the northern region, China has been implementing a prefecture-level policy named “Central Financial Support for Winter Clean Heating Pilot in the Northern Region” since 2017 (http://jjs.mof.gov.cn/zhengcefagui/201705/t20170519_2604217.htm). The policy selects pilot cities every year and implements clean heating renovation from both the “heat source side” and “user side” with the support of special central financial funds. For the specific renovation content, on the one hand, it is to follow the principle of “choosing the most suitable clean energy for heating”, and replace loose coal combustion with clean heating facilities. On the other hand, it is to promote the improvement of building energy efficiency at the user end. For pilot regions, the central government provided a fixed subsidy for three consecutive years (which can be extended). Subsidies are allocated according to different city levels. As of the end of 2020, a total of 63 prefecture-level cities have carried out pilot demonstration work in four batches. The pilot cities have gradually expanded from the Beijing-Tianjin-Hebei region to the Fenwei Plain. China announced that in 2023, 59.9% of the 339 prefecture-level and above cities met the air quality standards. Therefore, the contribution of a clean heating policy to this change is worth discussing. (https://www.gov.cn/lianbo/bumen/202402/content_6934935.htm).

Research related to air quality index (AQI) and air pollution is widely present in fields such as environmental science, ecology, medicine, and economics. The evaluation and trend analysis of regional air quality levels have always been a hot research topic. Li et al.7 evaluated the AQI and energy efficiency of 31 cities in China from 2013 to 2016, and the results showed that the related index of each city had no significant improvement during the study period. Tao et al.8 studied 19 cities from 2015 to 2019 and found that clean air days in China were gradually increasing, and there were seasonal differences in the concentrations of different types of pollutants. Much literature considers air pollution as an exogenous explanatory variable and finds that it has a wide range of impacts on various aspects of human life. According to existing research, the economic and social impacts of air pollution include leading to obesity9, reducing enterprise productivity10, enhancing a firm’s cost by spatial spillover11, and triggering population migration12. Hu et al.13 based on the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study, found that long-term exposure to air pollution can lead to a decline in cognitive abilities. Zhang et al.14 discovered that the alert for heavy air pollution can lead to an increase in the number of hospitalizations. The recent research trend has shifted from solely analyzing air pollutants to a more varied exploration, including methods, policies, and health effect15. Currently, more research is focusing on the impact of human activities, especially policies, on air quality. Lu et al.16 found that the expansion of urban scale has a direct impact on air pollution, and adopting green transition methods can alleviate air pollution. Li and Yang17 found that the development of the digital economy would increase public environmental awareness and promote industrial development, thereby reducing the AQI of the region.

Many studies have evaluated the specific effects of energy transformation on air quality improvement, as well as the economic and social impacts it generates. Li et al.18 combined the Cobb–Douglas production function with the spatial Durbin model, and they found that a higher proportion of clean energy can lead to reduced air pollutant emissions. Galimova et al.19 estimated that due to the use of clean energy, the total emissions of air pollutants in the global energy sector will reduce by nearly 92% by 2050 compared to 2015, and the associated deaths will decrease from 5.2 million in 2015 to 150 thousand by 2050. Chen et al.20 use a dynamic recursive computable general equilibrium (CGE) model to simulate the effect of the “coal-to-gas” policy; their results indicate that under high and complete substitution conditions, carbon emissions and air pollutant content will significantly decrease by 2030. Hong and Li21 found that a clean heating policy has an improving effect on energy and environmental efficiency.

Some studies focused on clean energy policies like “coal-to-gas” and “coal-to-electricity” as the exogenous shocks to energy transformation, and have empirically evaluated their impact on environmental and socio-economic indicators. Yu et al.22 used the Propensity Score Matching Difference-in-Difference method (PSM-DID) to evaluate the impact of “coal-to-gas” conversion on air pollution. Zeng et al.23 set the time for each city to implement the “coal-to-gas” policy by querying the municipal government website and legal documents at the prefecture level. Wang et al.24 considered six provinces and cities in North China as the “coal-to-gas” treatment group, and with 2017 as the policy time point, research has confirmed that energy transformation has a reducing effect on PM2.5. Wu et al.25 regard “2 + 26 cities” in the Beijing-Tianjin-Hebei air pollution transmission channel as a “coal to gas” region, to evaluate its impact on air quality. Leng et al.26 also considered “2 + 26 cities” as pilot cities, and adopted the DID method to study the impact of policy on the health of the elderly. However, these studies’ identification is inaccurate. Related studies have only focused on the impact of policies on the sample itself, failing to explore the spatial spillover effects of energy transition. Then, does the energy transition have a positive externality on AQI in nearby areas as well? This previously overlooked issue deserves further research.

This study uses monthly panel data from 285 cities from June 2015 to May 2020 and adopts the spatial Durbin Difference-in-Differences Model (SDMDID) to study the impact of the “Central Financial Support for Winter Clean Heating Pilot in the Northern Region” policy on the AQI and six major atmospheric pollutants. Based on exploring policies to improve air quality in pilot cities, this study analyzes the spatial spillover effects of clean energy transformation on other regions. The workflow of this study is carried out in the following order: Data selection and modeling, model applicability test (parallel trend tests, spatial correlation tests, Lagrange multiplier test, etc.), baseline regression (DID result, SDMDID result, direct and indirect effect, etc.), further discussion (robustness test, mechanism analysis), conclusion and discussion.

Compared to other existing studies, this study’s marginal contributions are as follows: (1) Firstly, this study evaluates the impact of clean energy transformation on air quality for the first time using the pilot project of “Central Financial Support for Winter Clean Heating in Northern Regions” as a quasi-natural experiment. The existing literature identified clean heating policies based on the first appearance of keywords on the website, or “2 + 26” cities as pilot areas, which is inaccurate. The pilot cities for each batch selected in this study are derived from government documents, and the policy goal is aimed at the renovation of clean heating facilities, which can better evaluate the effectiveness of the policy. (2) Secondly, this study, maybe for the first time, empirically evaluates the improvement effect of winter clean heating on air quality in northern regions, taking spatial spillover effects into account. Air pollutants have a strong spatial correlation, but most pieces of literature have not considered the impact of spatial spillover effects. We use monthly air quality data at the urban level and combine the DID model with panel spatial econometrics for empirical analysis to explore the spillover effects of policy in space.

Data selection and modeling

Variables selection

Dependent variable

We use the Air Quality Index (AQI) and the concentrations of six pollutants that make up AQI, including PM2.5, PM10, SO2, CO, NO2, and O3, as indicators to measure air conditions. The AQI released by China is calculated by weighting the six air pollutants mentioned above, and the calculation formula is derived from the “Technical Regulation on Ambient Air Quality Index (on trial)”. The higher the index, the worse the air quality.

Core independent variable

The core explanatory variable is the winter clean heating pilot policy in the northern region. The first batch of pilot cities was announced on June 5, 2017, with 12 prefecture-level cities, including Tianjin, Shijiazhuang, Tangshan, Baoding, Langfang, Hengshui, Taiyuan, Jinan, Zhengzhou, Kaifeng, Hebi, and Xinxiang. The second batch of pilot cities was announced on August 27, 2018, with 23 cities, including Handan, Xingtai, Zhangjiakou, Cangzhou, Yangquan, Changzhi, Jincheng, Jinzhong, Yuncheng, Linfen, Lvliang, Zibo, Jining, Binzhou, Dezhou, Liaocheng, Heze, Luoyang, Anyang, Jiaozuo, Puyang, Xi’an, and Xianyang. The third batch of pilot projects was launched on June 13, 2019, with 4 prefecture-level cities in Sanmenxia, Tongchuan, Weinan, and Baoji, as well as four county-level cities. There was no new list added in 2020, and other cities have been included in the pilot list since then. Due to the research scope of this study being from June 2015 to May 2020, only the first three batches of pilot cities were used as the treatment group, while excluding four county-level cities from the pilot cities. Therefore, a total of 39 cities participated in the pilot. Considering the policy time lag, we chose the first month after the policy was released as the time point for policy implementation. When the city is on the pilot list and the time is after policy implementation, take 1; otherwise, take 0.

Control variables

Control variables include some meteorology and macro data. We select the monthly average temperature, the highest temperature, the lowest temperature, and the number of rainy days and snowy days to characterize meteorological factors. Simultaneously, we select per capita GDP, population density, proportion of secondary industry, local general public budget revenue, and local general public budget expenditure at the urban level to control the impact of macroeconomic and social factors.

Data resource

AQI, six pollutants, and meteorological data are from 2345 tianqi (https://tianqi.2345.com/). The original data is the city’s daily data, and we retain the data from June 1, 2015, to May 31, 2022. Due to the requirement of using balanced panels to construct spatial weight matrices in spatial econometric models, and the presence of missing values in the meteorological data of some cities, we have carried out the following data processing. First, we remove cities with observation days less than 1800, then remove cities with missing dates, and finally summarize the daily data into monthly averages. Before cleaning, there were 534,449 samples from 295 cities, and after cleaning, there were 502,740 daily data points from 285 cities, resulting in a total of 17,100 monthly samples of temperature data for 60 months.

The macro data comes from the China Urban Statistical Yearbook, China Energy Statistical Yearbook, and statistical bulletins of various cities, and some missing values are filled in using the interpolation method. Per capita GDP, local general public budget revenue, and local general public budget expenditure are adjusted using the GDP deflator. Model setting

Difference-in-difference model

This study regards the winter clean heating policy in the northern region as a quasi-natural experiment. Firstly, a Difference-in-Difference model without spatial effects is established to examine the impact of the winter clean heating pilot on air quality, in order to compare with expanded spatial econometric models. Lagrange multiplier test

It is crucial to choose the most suitable model from the numerous spatial econometric models in empirical analysis. Under the linear spatial model setting, compared to SEM, SAR, and SAC models, the SDM model is unbiased regardless of the spatial effects involved in the real data generation process32 and is being adopted by more scholars. We refer to Elhorst’s33 introduction on model selection and use the Lagrange multiplier method for testing. Supplementary Table S1 presents the statistical results of the Lagrange multipliers and robust Lagrange multipliers for spatial error and spatial lag, respectively. Both significantly reject the null hypothesis of no spatial error effect and no spatial lag effect, indicating that the spatial Durbin model is most suitable.

The direct, indirect, and total effects

Much literature directly reports the results of point estimation in spatial regression models, but these parameter values do not represent the marginal effects of independent variable changes on dependent variable changes. LeSage and Pace32 provided expressions for the direct effects, indirect effects, and total effects of a model containing spatial lag terms based on partial differential decomposition calculations. The direct effect is the impact of changes in the unit i’s independent variable on its dependent variable, while the indirect effect is the mean impact of changes in all independent variables except for unit i on the unit i’s dependent variable. We calculated three effects based on the result of the SDMDID model, shown in Table 4. A city participating in the winter clean heating pilot program reduces its own AQI value by an average of 3.798, but neighboring cities participating in the pilot program did not significantly improve the city’s air quality level. From the perspective of various pollutants, the direct effect is that PM2.5, PM10, SO2, CO, and NO2 decreased by 5.637 μg/m3, 9.724 μg/m3, 7.386 μg/m3, 0.219 mg/m3, and 1.34 μg/m3, respectively. Meanwhile, the indirect effect shows that pilot policy in surrounding cities can also significantly reduce pollutant concentrations. It should be pointed out that the indirect effect calculates the sum of spatial spillover effects for each neighboring city if included in the pilot program. So the indirect effect is much greater than the direct effect. Placebo test

To verify the robustness of the treatment effect of pilot cities, all city samples were resampled to generate the pseudo-treatment cohort and re-perform the regression. There are two methods used for pseudo-treatment group generation: (1) unrestricted randomizing, which generates a pseudo-treatment time for each unit (including ∞, i.e. never treated cohort), and (2) restricted randomizing, first generates G cohorts based on the actual treatment time, randomly partition all units in the sample into G cohorts with keeping the number of units in each cohort unchanged, then generating a pseudo treatment time for each cohort. Taking AQI as an example, we used the above two randomizing methods to generate treatment time cohorts, repeated 200 times, and plotted the kernel density graph of the coefficients. As shown in Figs. 3 and 4, pseudo coefficients are distributed around 0, which is significantly different from the true coefficient of -3.33, indicating that it has passed the placebo test.Fig. 3Placebo test of AQI (unrestricted).Fig. 4Placebo test of AQI (restricted).

Replace spatial weight matrix

To avoid bias caused by the selection of the spatial weight matrix, we applied a contiguity spatial weight matrix (W2) to Formula (2) for regression and obtained the result shown in Supplementary Table S3. The impact of the clean heating pilot on AQI is no longer significant, but it can still reduce the concentrations of PM2.5, PM10, SO2, CO, and NO2, and increase the concentration of O3. Considering the first-order contiguity spatial matrix assumes that only contiguous elements produce spatial effects, which is not in line with the meteorological characteristics of pollutants. Therefore, inverse distance matrices may have more flexibility compared to using inverse distance matrices in air pollution research.

Narrowing the sample range

Due to the differences in energy structure between the northern and southern regions of China, as well as the meteorological characteristics, Supplementary Table S4 selects all cities in the northern region and the provinces where the pilot cities are located as sub-samples for regression analysis. The northern region includes 15 provinces or municipalities, including Beijing, Tianjin, Hebei, Shanxi, Inner Mongolia, Liaoning, Jilin, Heilongjiang, Shandong, Shaanxi, Gansu, Qinghai, Ningxia, Xinjiang, and Henan (https://www.gov.cn/xinwen/2017-12/20/content_5248855.htm). And the result remains significant. It means that even with coal-fired heating areas as the control group, the atmospheric improvement effect of the winter clean heating policy in northern regions is significant.

Consider other policies

In recent years, China has attached great importance to air quality issues and introduced a series of related policies. Since the implementation of the Air Pollution Prevention and Control Action Plan (also known as Ten Measures for Air Pollution), China’s air pollution situation has been effectively curbed. “The 2017 Air Pollution Prevention and Control Work Plan for the Beijing-Tianjin-Hebei and Surrounding Areas” first proposed the “2 + 26 cities” for air pollution transmission channels in February 2017, and proposed specific arrangements in subsequent documents focused on energy structure upgrading, industrial structure adjustment, and deep industrial governance to improve the air quality in the Beijing-Tianjin-Hebei region. The low-carbon city pilot policy aims to build low-carbon consumption and establish a resource-saving and environmentally friendly society by carrying out three batches of pilot projects in 2010, 2012, and 2017. Supplementary Table S5 incorporates these two policies that may cause interference as other explanatory variables in the model, and the result remains consistent.

Mechanism analysis

In the mechanism analysis section, we also explored the mechanism of clean energy transition to indicate the potential improvement effect on air quality. We mainly did two tasks to test how the policy works. Firstly, we conduct a regression analysis separately for heating and non-heating periods. If the policy has indeed reduced the use of loose coal in winter, we should see significant differences in policy effects across different seasons. Secondly, we analyzed the impact of policy on cities’ energy structure. According to expectations, clean heating will increase the use of clean energy sources such as electricity and natural gas, as well as an increase in energy utilization efficiency. These analyses can help us better understand the mechanism by which reducing traditional energy use improves air quality.

Considering the heating period

The statutory heating time in China is from November 15th to March 15th each year, and the local government will adjust the range of the days before and after based on the actual temperature. Therefore, we set five months, 11, 12, 1, 2, and 3, as heating periods, and performed group regression on the heating period group and non-heating period group separately in Table 6. Therefore, the consumption of electricity and natural gas as the main alternative energy sources should significantly increase. Table 7 uses annual data from prefecture-level cities for regression. We use the total amount of coal gas and natural gas supply, and the total amount of liquefied petroleum gas supply in the city’s jurisdiction to represent the corresponding energy consumption. Because there is no cross-city supply problem with natural gas, energy supply and demand are equivalent. Conclusion and discussion

The energy transition is an important prerequisite in China’s modernization, and winter clean heating could help to meet residents’ energy needs while breathing in better air. Based on AQI and pollutant concentrations from national air quality monitoring, this study constructed an SDM-DID model with monthly spatial panels to evaluate the impact of the winter clean heating pilot in northern China on air quality, while considering the spatial spillover effects, and evaluate the impact of the winter clean heating pilot in the northern region since 2017 on air quality. Our findings are as follows: (1) The clean heating policy has reduced AQI and PM2.5, PM10, SO2, CO, and NO2 in the city itself, but had no statistically significant impact on ozone concentration. Meanwhile, the policy also reduced the concentration of air pollutants in other regions. (2) This result is still significant after changing the spatial weight setting and limiting the research scope to the northern region, considering dynamic models, and the pilot policy has passed parallel trend tests and placebo tests. (3) The pilot policy mainly improved the air quality in November, December, and January, and indeed changed the energy structure, increasing the use of clean energy in the region and reducing total energy consumption.

The extensive use of loose coal was once a common phenomenon in China, especially in northern regions. Small boilers, kilns, household earthen beds, and some self-built commercial coal stoves all used loose coal in large quantities. In recent years, China has proposed that “green mountains and clear waters are like mountains of gold and silver”, placing the ecological environment in an equally important position as economic growth. China is also one of the contracting parties to the Paris Agreement and is striving to provide a Chinese solution for global environmental governance through the innovation of clean energy technology. The research results of this study are a verification of China’s efforts to improve air quality in recent years. There is still a great possibility of clean energy substitution projects in developing countries like China, where infrastructure construction is lagging. The use of clean energy can not only enhance the stability of energy access for residents and reduce the probability of energy poverty, but also provide immeasurable positive externalities for residents by improving air quality. In the future, the scope of clean heating should be expanded, taking into account the current energy use situation in rural areas, and promoting the transformation of clean heating more efficiently based on local characteristics.

There are still some shortcomings in this study. First, this study did not evaluate the difference in effect between various types of new energy transitions. The energy transformation dominated by “coal-to-gas” and “coal-to-electricity” is not universally applicable nationwide, as coal is used as the main primary energy source in northern China, while water conservancy is more commonly used for energy supply in southern China. The western region has abundant solar and wind energy, and each region has a unique path to achieve energy transformation. Second, this study did not eliminate potential interference policies. Since China began to attach great importance to air pollution in 2013, a series of policies have been introduced for the most severely polluted northern regions. This includes not only energy equipment renovation policies for enterprises and residents, but also environmental regulations policies such as “Ten Measures for the Air”, carbon emission rights pilot projects, and environmental protection negotiation systems. We cannot exclude the interference of all policies in the model. Third, this study applied econometric models but did not involve economic theory models. This makes it difficult for the study to explain in depth how residents’ energy consumption behavior leads to macro-level changes.

This study provides important evidence that energy transition can improve environmental quality. In fact, the impact of energy transition on regional development goes far beyond that. Clean energy has higher opportunity costs, and clean energy is not only more expensive but also requires an inevitable expense for energy equipment upgrades. In recent years, several cities in northern China have established coal ban zones in urban areas, gradually phasing out small tonnage coal-fired boilers, which has increased enterprise costs and reduced total output. Then, can clean heating balance economic growth and environmental protection? What is the overall impact on energy efficiency? Will energy transition policies change the decisions of businesses and households? These are unresolved issues. In the future, we will merge macro-data with micro-data from enterprises or households to further explore the relationship between clean heating renovation and regional economic development in the northern region. At last, spatial econometric techniques have been more and more popular in socioeconomic analysis39, while air pollution issues are examined in broader scopes of emerging threats40–42. Therefore, this study is just the beginning of a batch of future studies.
