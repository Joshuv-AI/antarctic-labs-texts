Prognostic value of heart rate variability for neurological outcomes in cardiac arrest patients: machine learning-based analysis of the International Cardiac Arrest Research (I-CARE) database
Authors: Aman Liu, Xiao Chen, Xiaomeng Xu, Chong Li, Boda Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo predict neurological outcome of cardiac arrest patients who are in a comatose state, incorporating Heart Rate Variability (HRV) from electrocardiogram (ECG) signals to enhance prognostic value.MethodsThe International Cardiac Arrest Research (I-CARE) database which includes clinical information and continuous ECG data for 1020 patients aged 16 or older who were comatose after return of spontaneous circulation following cardiac arrest. We collected 86 HRV indicators from patients’ ECG readings, in addition to clinical parameters, to predict neurological outcome [categorized into good (Cerebral Performance Category (CPC) score 1–2) and poor (CPC score 3–5)]. Six machine learning models were constructed to predict neurological outcome, including logistic regression (LR), Random Forest (RF), Adaptive Boosting (Adaboost), Gradient Boost (GB), eXtreme Gradient Boosting (XGB), and Light GBM (LGB). A rigorous 5 repetitions of 10-fold cross-validation was implemented as the internal validation strategy on the training set to mitigate overfitting, and an ensemble model integrating these six validated algorithms was further explored. Twenty most important variables were identified using the Shapley method. The correlation between neurological outcomes and different HRV metrics were analyzed. Recursive feature elimination was applied to develop a clinical scoring system modified by HRV indicators.ResultsA total of 406 individuals with complete ECG & HRV data were included. We used 70% data as training set, with the rest 30% as test set. 5 × 10-fold cross-validation confirmed the robustness of all models, with the XGB model exhibiting a mean cross-validated AUROC of 0.7815 (95% CI: 0.657–0.886). The study demonstrated that the ensembled model, utilizing both clinical and HRV features, outperformed the model based solely on clinical parameters in eXtreme Gradient Boosting, Light GBM and Random Forest algorithms. The ensemble model exhibited the best performance in Light GBM algorithm (AUROC = 0.831; 95% CI 0.813–0.850), while HRV along exhibited an AUROC 0.809 (95% CI 0.792–0.825) in predicting neurological outcome in Gradient Boost algorithm. As to parameter analysis, Shapley’s method identified Perinatal Index, Triangular Index, Mean Circular Variance of NN Intervals as key HRV features in predicting neurological outcomes.ConclusionsThe integration of HRV features into machine learning models, combined with a rigorous internal cross-validation strategy, significantly enhanced prediction of neurological outcome in cardiac arrest. HRV features, alone or in combination with other clinical parameters, offered high interpretability and significant prognostic value in cardiac arrest. Future external validation in multicenter independent cohorts will further confirm the generalizability of these findings for clinical translation.Keywords: Cardiac arrest, HRV, Machine learning, Neurological prognosis

Introduction

More than 80% of patients who are admitted to an intensive-care unit (ICU) after resuscitation from out-of-hospital cardiac arrest (OHCA) are comatose,1 with 2/3 die before hospital discharge.2, 3 The majority of these deaths result from withdrawal of life-sustaining treatment (WLST) because of a predicted poor neurological outcome.4 Accurate neuro-prognostication is, therefore, essential in these patients, to avoid both inappropriate withdrawal of life-sustaining treatment or prolonged treatment of patients with no chance of neurologically meaningful survival.5 Recent studies suggested that there is a strong association between heart rate variability (HRV) and neurological outcome.6, 7, 8

EEG, neuroimaging, and serum biomarkers provide direct or indirect assessments of cortical activity, structural injury, and biochemical damage, and are central to current multimodal prognostication strategies. However, these methods also have practical limitations, including intermittent availability of monitoring, inter-reader variability in assessment, delays in data collection, and dependence on professional technicians. In contrast, heart rate variability (HRV) can be continuously acquired from routinely monitored electrocardiogram (ECG) signals, which not only enables non-invasive acquisition of relevant information but also dynamically evaluates the integration status of the autonomic nervous system and the central nervous system.

HRV refers to beat-by-beat heart variations as a result of a physiological variation in the duration of the R-R intervals in electrocardiogram (ECG) recordings between sinus beats.9, 10 HRV is regulated by the autonomic nervous system (ANS), which reflects ANS function and also can be used as predictors of impending outcome.11, 12 Recent investigations have highlighted HRV as a promising indicator for evaluating the risk of mortality and prognosticating neurological outcome.13 While qualitative review suggested that HRV to be associated with functional outcomes including neurological functions and functional independence in patients with moderate-to-severe traumatic brain injury (TBI).14

The present study aims to systematically evaluate heart rate variability (HRV) metrics derived from the International Cardiac Arrest Research (I-CARE) database in order to assess their validity for predicting neurological outcomes following cardiac arrest. Specifically, we examine the prognostic performance of selected HRV parameters and determine their association with neurological recovery. Furthermore, we investigate whether the integration of optimally selected electroencephalography (EEG) features with HRV characteristics enhances predictive accuracy. We hypothesize that combining complementary physiological biomarkers may improve risk stratification and serve as an informative adjunct within ensemble machine learning frameworks for outcome prediction.

Materials and methods

I-CARE database

The dataset includes retrospectively gathered from four U.S. academic medical institutions. Furthermore, data gathered in advance is sourced from two Dutch hospitals and a Belgian hospital.15 Demographic information (such as return of spontaneous circulation, sex, age, out-of-hospital cardiac arrest, shockable rhythm occurrence), and evaluations of functional neurological results conducted between 3 and 6 months after cardiac arrest.

A good neurological outcome was defined as a CPC score of 1 or 2 (minimal to moderate neurological disability), while an poor outcome was characterized by a CPC score of 3–5 (severe neurological disability, persistent coma or vegetative state, or death) at the 3 to 6-month mark.16, 17

Data preprocessing

The Neurokit2 Python library, a comprehensive and integrated ECG signal processing package, was used to preprocess ECG waveforms and calculate HRV feature parameters.18 Lead II ECG data from the first hour and the 1 h before end of ECG signal was selected for each patient and segmented into 12 five-minute epochs. For data cleaning, a 0.5 Hz high-pass Butterworth filter (order = 5) was applied to each epoch, followed by powerline filtering as we previously described.19

Feature extraction

The HRV indices consist of 21 time-domain indices, 9 frequency-domain indices, and 56 nonlinear indices. Time-domain metrics were calculated based on variations in RR intervals, successive differences between adjacent beats, as well as geometric characteristics of the interval distribution. Frequency-domain parameters were obtained through spectral analysis, quantifying power within predefined bands: ultra-low frequency (ULF, ≤0.003 Hz), very low frequency (VLF, 0.0033–0.04 Hz), low frequency (LF, 0.04–0.15 Hz), and high frequency (HF, 0.15–0.4 Hz). Nonlinear measures were derived from Poincaré plot analysis and heart rate fragmentation methodologies. In addition, we incorporated indices reflecting HRV asymmetry, signal complexity, and fractal-related physiological properties. Asymmetry metrics describe the imbalance between heart rate accelerations and decelerations. Complexity-related measures characterize the degree of irregularity and unpredictability within cardiac rhythm dynamics. Fractal features, calculated using detrended fluctuation analysis (DFA), evaluate long-range correlations and multiscale behavior of heart rate fluctuations. A detailed summary of all HRV parameters included in the present study, together with their definitions, is presented in Table S2.

Machine learning framework

The patients were randomly divided into training and test sets in a 7:3 ratio. Predictors in the comparative analysis included clinical features only, HRV features only, or a combination of both to train the clinical-only-based (COB) model, HRV-only-based (HOB) model, and ensembled model, respectively. The study initially selected six algorithms for model development: logistic regression (LR), Random Forest (RF), Adaptive Boosting (AdaBoost), Gradient Boost (GB), eXtreme Gradient Boosting (XGB), and Light GBM (LGB). Each model was tested on the test sets to analyze the performance features AUROC (Area Under the ROC Curve) and AUPRC (Area Under the Precision-Recall Curve). For every algorithm, the threshold was determined to maximize the Youden index. Based on this threshold, model sensitivity, specificity, precision, accuracy, and F1-score were calculated. For all six algorithms, 5 repetitions of 10-fold cross-validation was performed on the 80% training set as the internal validation strategy to mitigate overfitting and verify model stability.

To address the high dimensionality of HRV features and mitigate the risk of overfitting, we implemented a multi-stage, pre-specified feature selection and model development pipeline; the primary outcome was encoded as a binary variable (Good vs. Poor neurological outcome), all HRV indices were cleaned to remove infinite values and missing data (NaN), which were imputed using either a global zero or the median value to ensure numerical stability, and clinical and HRV features were then aligned to ensure complete sample-wise matching; a two-step strategy was employed to reduce dimensionality, we first removed highly redundant features by eliminating any pair of HRV metrics with a Pearson correlation coefficient greater than 0.9, resulting in the removal of 32 features to reduce multicollinearity, then trained a Random Forest model on the remaining features, calculated feature importance using Mean Decrease in Impurity (MDI), constructed a cumulative importance curve to identify the point of diminishing returns, and selected the top 20 HRV features that collectively contributed the most to model performance for final modeling, this approach balanced predictive power with model parsimony, adhering to the events-per-variable (EPV) principle; the dataset was split into an 80% training set and a 20% held-out test set using stratified random sampling to preserve the outcome distribution, we developed a suite of predictive models, including ensemble learning algorithms (Random Forest, XGBoost, LightGBM, Adaboost) and a linear baseline model (Logistic Regression), hyperparameter tuning was performed via 5-fold cross-validation on the training set to optimize performance and prevent overfitting, and class weights were incorporated to account for the imbalanced nature of the outcome data; model performance was assessed on the independent test set using AUROC, accuracy, sensitivity, and specificity, the stability of these estimates was quantified using bootstrapping with 1000 iterations to generate 95% confidence intervals, Delong’s test was used to statistically compare the AUROC values of models incorporating HRV features (Ensemble, HOB) with those relying solely on clinical features (COB), and post-hoc SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) analysis was performed on the final models to interpret feature contributions and identify key prognostic drivers, rather than for feature selection, to avoid data leakage.

Feature importance and univariate analysis

A SHapley Additive explanation (SHAP) method, rooted in cooperative game theory, was utilized to evaluate the importance of prediction parameters in the model.20 The study identified the 20 most significant factors from models with superior predictive performance, including ensembled and HRV-only-based (HOB) models. Logistic regression modeling with recursive feature elimination (RFE) was employed to create a scoring system using the GPC score and specific HRV parameters.19

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables were compared using the Pearson chi-square test, normality of continuous variables was assessed by the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. P < 0.05 was considered to be statistically significant. All data analysis were conducted using Python 3.11.

Results

A total of 1020 cases were obtained from the iCare database. Initially, 421 cases without ECG data were excluded, leaving 599 cases. Among these remaining 599 cases, 406 had available HRV data. Meanwhile, there were 607 cases with clinical features. By taking the intersection of the above 406 cases (with HRV data) and the 607 cases (with clinical features), a total of 406 cases with complete HRV & clinical features were eligible for analysis (Fig. 1). There were 85 effective HRV features computed from each signal, including time domain, frequency, and nonlinear features (Table S1). Additional 5 clinical features such as sex, age, OHCA (out-of-hospital cardiac arrest) and ROSC (return of spontaneous circulation, in minutes) and shockable rhythm were also included. The prediction models were trained with only clinical information (COB), only HRV features (HOB), and both (ensembled model). Clinical and HRV traits were compared based on patient's neurological outcome in Table S2. There was significant difference in age, return of spontaneous circulation, and shockable rhythm between good and poor outcome groups. In addition, some important features of HRV showed significant difference between good and poor outcome groups.

To evaluate the time-dependent nature of HRV-based prognostic performance, we additionally analyzed ECG data from the first hour after ICU admission (Table S3). Across all machine learning algorithms, the AUROC values of both HRV-inclusive (Ensemble, HOB) and clinical-only (COB) models in the initial hour were consistently lower than those observed in the last hour before outcome assessment. For instance, in the Light GBM model, the Ensemble AUROC was 0.582 (95% CI: 0.564–0.600) in the first hour, compared with a higher value in the last-hour analysis. These early-stage results confirm that the prognostic value of HRV evolves over the course of critical illness, likely reflecting the progressive accumulation of physiological deterioration. While these early models demonstrate potential prognostic signal (AUROCs > 0.5), their performance is not yet sufficient to reliably inform high-stakes clinical decisions, supporting the need for further refinement and validation in prospective cohorts.

Next, we identified 20 most important variables in the ensembled model from the eXtreme Gradient Boosting algorithm (Fig. 3). In Table S1, several heart rate variability (HRV) features are presented using abbreviated labels. These variables represent time-domain, frequency-domain, and nonlinear metrics reflecting distinct aspects of autonomic regulation. Time-domain indices such as MinNN (minimum normal-to-normal interval), Prc20NN (20th percentile of NN intervals), MCVNN (mean consecutive variation of NN intervals), IQRNN (interquartile range of NN intervals), MadNN (median absolute deviation of NN intervals), TINN (triangular interpolation of NN interval histogram), and HTI (HRV triangular index) primarily quantify overall variability and short-term beat-to-beat fluctuations. Frequency-domain analysis includes LF (low-frequency power), reflecting combined sympathetic and parasympathetic modulation. Nonlinear and complexity-based measures include PI (Porta’s index, reflecting Poincaré plot asymmetry), SD1SD2 ratio (short- to long-term variability balance), CSI_Modified (modified cardiac sympathetic index), MSEn (multiscale entropy), KFD (Katz fractal dimension), and multifractal detrended fluctuation analysis (MFDFA)-derived parameters (α1_mean, α2_fluctuation, α2_delta, and α2_peak), which characterize multiscale correlation properties and signal complexity. Collectively, these metrics capture complementary physiological dimensions, including vagal withdrawal, sympathetic predominance, and loss of multiscale complexity following neurological injury. Shapley's method identified Perinatal Index, Triangular Index, Mean Circular Variance of NN Intervals as top 3 HRV features in predicting neurological outcomes, while Low Frequency Power (LF), Multifractal Detrended Fluctuation Analysis (MFDFA), Multiscale entropy (MSEn), features related to the Poincaré plot (the ratio of SD1 to SD2 – SD1SD2), distribution characteristics of NN interval (Minimum, 20th Percentile, Interquartile Range, and Median absolute deviation of NN Intervals), index based on geometric/graphical methods (Heuristic Triangular Index, Katz's Fractal Dimension), and modified Cardiac Sympathetic Index made significant contributions.

To verify the consistency of SHAP-based feature importance ranking with other widely used methods, we supplemented an additional experiment using Random Forest (a representative tree-based model with mature feature importance evaluation capability) to re-identify the 20 most important variables in the ensemble model. The results of this supplementary experiment are presented in the newly added Fig. S1, which shows the top 20 predictive features ranked by Random Forest Gini importance.

As shown in Fig. S1, the feature importance rankings obtained by Random Forest Gini importance are highly consistent with those from SHAP values (Fig. 3). Key HRV indicators (e.g., HRV_MinNN, HRV_LF, HRV_MFDFA_alpha2_Peak) and clinical parameters (e.g., Shockable Rhythm, ROSC, Age) that are biologically relevant to neurological outcomes are consistently ranked among the top features by both methods. This overlap confirms the reliability and robustness of our SHAP-based feature importance assessment, further supporting the validity of our identified key features. The minor differences between the two rankings are mainly attributed to the inherent differences in the two methods: SHAP values reflect the global importance of features across all samples and model types, while Random Forest Gini importance focuses on the contribution of features to node splitting in tree-based models. These differences do not affect the identification of core important features, and instead highlight the comprehensiveness of SHAP values in capturing feature importance.

Discussion

This study analyzed an extensive repository of clinical and HRV data derived from critically ill patients experiencing coma following cardiac arrest, which underscores the link between HRV features and the likelihood of neurological recuperation. Previous research focused on improvement on EEG readings to predict neurological outcomes, with limited effects.21, 22

The analysis of HRV provides a non-invasive window into autonomic nervous system (ANS) function, which may help in predicting neurological outcomes. Evidence from cardiac denervation and heart transplantation studies suggests that, in the absence of autonomic input, overall heart rate variability amplitude is markedly reduced.23, 24 After cardiac arrest, global cerebral ischemia severely damages the central autonomic network (especially the brainstem), leading to characteristic changes in HRV. These changes are powerful predictors of neurological prognosis. In summary, the prognostication power lies in the absence of healthy variability: (A) “Flatline” in Complexity: The most robust predictors are the nonlinear and geometric metrics (like multifractal detrended fluctuation analysis Spectrum Width, alpha1, alpha2, SD1/SD2, and mean consecutive variation of NN intervals). Their profound suppression indicates a complete breakdown of the complex, multi-scale control of the heart by the brain, which is consistent with impaired autonomic-central regulation and poor outcome. Their suppression may reflect disruption of multi-scale autonomic–central regulation, which has been associated with severe neurological injury in prior studies.11, 25 (B) Loss of Overall Fluctuation: Geometric indices (HRV triangular index, interquartile range of NN intervals, triangular interpolation of NN interval histogram) and the frequency-domain Low Frequency Power (LF) show a transition from a broad, healthy distribution of heart rates to a narrow, “rigid” one. This loss of overall variability reflects systemic autonomic failure. (C) Specific Failure of Rapid Regulation: Time-domain indices like MinNN (minimum normal-to-normal interval) and Prc20NN (20th percentile of NN intervals) show a loss of the fastest heart rates, indicating a specific failure of vagal withdrawal, which is a sensitive sign of brainstem impairment. The combination of these metrics provided a multi-faceted and highly accurate assessment of central autonomic integrity, making HRV an invaluable tool for predicting neurological outcome after cardiac arrest.

Importantly, the present study does not aim to establish HRV as a standalone prognostic instrument for clinical decision-making. Rather, our objective was to evaluate whether HRV-derived features provide additive physiological and prognostic information when integrated into ensemble machine-learning models alongside conventional clinical variables. Although the model demonstrated moderate discrimination, its performance does not support its use as a standalone tool for life-sustaining treatment decisions. Instead, it may serve as an adjunctive risk stratification instrument within a broader clinical context.

This study has certain limitations. The high patient exclusion rate was due to missing or insufficient ECG/HRV data, which may introduce potential selection bias. The intricacies of patient care for comatose cardiac arrest individuals introduce complexity, and specific details pertaining to the clinical context of ECG recording are unavailable. Factors such as temperature modulation, sedative administration, and vasopressor usage may exert an influence on HRV features. Secondly, ECG recordings analyzed in this study were procured during routine clinical care and underwent conversion using standard clinical software at individual sites, lacking a centralized method. Consequently, issues related to data quality stemming from the process of data conversion may exist.26 Meanwhile, institutional-level heterogeneity (e.g., differences in ECG acquisition protocols and patient management) may have potential impacts on model performance and data quality. Additionally, due to data constraints, both training and verification were conducted within the same database, thus it is necessary to introduce additional datasets for external validation.

Despite adjusting for six key clinical variables (age, sex, ROSC, OHCA, shockable rhythm, and TTM), residual confounding may still exist owing to data limitations, which prevented adjustment for other relevant factors including sedation, vasoactive drugs, and illness severity. While HRV can be derived from routine ECG data, its clinical utility for real-time decision-making was not established in the present study. Further prospective investigations are warranted to validate its incremental clinical benefit and external validity in independent cohorts.

Conclusion

In clinical practice, HRV has unique advantages, such as easier and faster to access in emergency situations. The integration of HRV features into machine learning models significantly enhanced prediction of neurological outcome in cardiac arrest. HRV features, along or in combination with other clinical parameters, offered high interpretability and significant prognostic value in cardiac arrest.
