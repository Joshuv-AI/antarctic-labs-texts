MLKL confers ferroptosis resistance in colorectal cancer cells via impaired ESCRT-III-mediated membrane repair
Authors: Sasiprapa Sonkaew, Nattaya Duangthim, Chutikarn Butkinaree, Yodying Yingchutrakul, Swati Choksi, Zheng-Gang Liu, Siriporn Jitkaew
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundFerroptosis resistance is a major challenge in cancer therapy, contributing to poor prognosis and unsatisfactory treatment outcomes. Thus, identifying key regulators of ferroptosis is essential for developing strategies to overcome this limitation. Mixed lineage kinase domain-like protein (MLKL) is well established as the executioner of necroptosis, but emerging evidence suggests broader functions beyond necroptosis. A previous study demonstrated that MLKL regulates ferroptosis in an acute kidney injury model. However, its role in ferroptosis regulation in cancer remains largely unexplored.ResultsBioinformatics analyses revealed that MLKL expression positively correlates with glutathione metabolism-related genes in colorectal cancer (CRC) patients and with reduced sensitivity to GPX4 inhibitors across pan-cancer cell lines. Functional studies demonstrated that MLKL depletion sensitizes not only CRC cells but also multiple cancer cell types to ferroptosis induced by GPX4 inhibitors. Mechanistically, MLKL-deficient CRC cells exhibited reduced intracellular glutathione levels and dysregulation of the NRF2/KEAP1/SLC7A11 antioxidant axis. However, KEAP1 knockdown in MLKL-depleted cells partially reduced ferroptosis sensitivity, indicating that additional mechanisms are involved. Proteomic analysis identified CHMP5, a component of the ESCRT-III membrane repair machinery, as a key downstream effector associated with MLKL. MLKL depletion suppressed CHMP5 expression and impaired its translocation to the plasma membrane during ferroptosis induction. Consistent with defective membrane repair, MLKL-knockdown CRC cells exhibited increased HMGB1 release following GPX4 inhibitor treatment, indicating enhanced membrane damage. Notably, CHMP5 overexpression reduced ferroptosis in MLKL-knockdown cells, highlighting that MLKL regulates ferroptosis susceptibility in part through CHMP5-dependent ESCRT-III signaling.ConclusionsThis study reveals a previously unrecognized role for MLKL in ferroptosis regulation through coordinating NRF2/KEAP1/SLC7A11-mediated glutathione metabolism and ESCRT-III–dependent membrane repair. These findings identify MLKL as a potential regulator of ferroptosis resistance and warrant further studies to evaluate the therapeutic relevance of MLKL-targeting strategies in colorectal cancer.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13062-026-00887-0.

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) is the second leading cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide, with incidence rates rising particularly among younger adults [1, 2]. Despite therapeutic advances, treatment outcomes remain unsatisfactory, with frequent recurrence and poor overall survival [3, 4]. Thus, novel therapeutic strategies are urgently needed to improve clinical outcomes. Resistance to cell death is a major driver of cancer progression and treatment failure [5]. Most conventional therapies exert anticancer effects by inducing apoptosis; however, cancer cells frequently acquire apoptosis resistance, resulting in poor therapeutic responses [6]. Consequently, alternative forms of regulated cell death have gained attention as promising therapeutic targets.

Ferroptosis is a regulated form of cell death characterized by iron-dependent accumulation of lipid peroxides [7]. Many cancer cell types are intrinsically susceptible to ferroptosis due to elevated iron uptake, that supports high metabolic demands and rapid proliferation [8]. To counteract iron-induced oxidative stress and prevent lipid peroxidation, cells rely on glutathione peroxidase 4–glutathione (GPX4–GSH) antioxidant defense system [9, 10]. Accordingly, inhibition of GPX4 or suppression of GSH biosynthesis has been widely used to induce ferroptosis in cancer. While targeting the GPX4-GSH axis can be effective, some cancers develop intrinsic or acquired resistance, thereby limiting the therapeutic potential of ferroptosis-based treatments. Therefore, elucidating the molecular mechanisms and key regulators underlying ferroptosis resistance is critical for developing novel strategy to enhance ferroptosis sensitivity and improve therapeutic outcomes.

A previous study reported that depletion of mixed lineage kinase domain-like protein (MLKL), a key regulator of necroptosis, was shown to enhance ferroptosis in an acute kidney injury model [11], suggesting a compensatory relationship between necroptosis and ferroptosis, and raising the possibility that MLKL may also regulate ferroptosis in cancer. Although MLKL is widely recognized as the executioner of necroptosis, emerging evidence indicates functions beyond necroptotic signaling [12]. MLKL has been reported to interact with the endosomal sorting complexes required for transport (ESCRT) machinery and co-localize with ESCRT-III components such as charged multivesicular body protein 4B (CHMP4B) to facilitate membrane shedding, thereby delaying membrane permeabilization and cell death [13, 14]. Notably, ESCRT-III–mediated membrane repair has also been implicated in ferroptosis regulation. Previous studies showed that Ca²+ influx prior to plasma membrane rupture recruits CHMP5 and CHMP6, promoting ESCRT-III–mediated membrane repair, thereby conferring ferroptosis resistance [15, 16]. Taken together, these observations suggest a potential interplay among MLKL, ESCRT-III, and ferroptosis regulation that warrants further investigation.

In this study, we investigated the role of MLKL in ferroptosis regulation, with a particular focus on CRC. First, we performed bioinformatics analyses to examine the correlation between MLKL expression and glutathione-related genes in CRC patients, as well as its association with sensitivity to GPX4 inhibitors, key ferroptosis-inducing agents, across cancer cell lines. We then evaluated the effect of MLKL depletion on GPX4 inhibitor–induced ferroptosis in CRC cells and extended the analysis to additional cancer types, including breast cancer, lung cancer, and cholangiocarcinoma. To gain mechanistic insights, we conducted LC-MS/MS–based proteomic profiling of MLKL-knockdown versus MLKL-expressing CRC cells. Collectively, these data provide initial evidence that MLKL contributes to ferroptosis regulation in cancer cells. Our findings provide a rationale for future studies evaluating whether targeting MLKL can enhance ferroptosis sensitivity in colorectal cancer.

Materials and methods

Bioinformatics analysis

Gene expression profiles of TCGA-COAD obtained from colorectal cancer tissues were downloaded from the University of California at Santa Cruz (UCSC) Xena platform (https://xena.ucsc.edu/). Patients (N = 380) were categorized based on the median expression level of MLKL into two groups (N = 190/group): high (≥ median) and low (< median). To identify signaling pathways associated with MLKL expressions, gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) was conducted using the GSEA software [17] with referencing gene sets from the MSigDB Hallmark collection. A gene set was considered statistically significant with a |normalized enrichment score (NES)| > 1, p-value < 0.05, and false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.25. Furthermore, the correlation between MLKL expressions and genes in the HALLMARK REACTIVE OXYGEN SPECIES PATHWAY were analyzed using Pearson’s correlation test. Genes with a p-value < 0.05 were used for subsequent pathway analysis in a STRING tool (https://www.string-db.org/) and results were visualized using a Sankey-dot diagram.

The expression of MLKL and GPX4 inhibitors sensitivity (RSL3, ML160, and ML210) for 734 cancer cell lines from the Cancer Cell Line Encyclopedia (CCLE) were retrieved from the DepMap portal (https://depmap.org/portal/download/, release version: public 24Q2) [18]. The correlation between MLKL expression and sensitivity to GPX4 inhibitors was assessed using Pearson’s correlation analysis.

Plasmids, shRNA, reagents and antibodies

Lentiviral packaging plasmids (pCMV-VSV-G and pCMV-dR8.2 dvpr) and lentiviral plasmids encoding shRNAs targeting MLKL (TRCN0000194846, shMLKL#1; TRCN0000003227, shMLKL#2) were obtained from Sigma (St. Louis, MO, USA). For CRISPR/Cas9, sgRNA specific to MLKL were generated by ligating hybridized oligonucleotides into the LentiCRISPRv2 vector (Addgene #52961, Watertown, MA, USA). Lentiviral plasmids encoding shRNAs targeting KEAP1 (TRCN0000154656, shKEAP1#1; TRCN0000156676, shKEAP1#2) were purchased from Dharmacon (Lafayette, CO, USA). The lentiviral vector for overexpressing mCherry-CHMP5 was designed and packaged by VectorBuilder. All plasmid constructs were validated by DNA sequencing.

Chloroquine, Deferoxamine, Ferrostatin-1, Liproxstatin-1, ML210, and RSL3 were purchased from APExBIO (Houston, TX, USA), while zVAD-fmk was obtained from Merck Millipore (Darmstadt, Germany).

Antibodies used for Western blot and immunofluorescence were purchased from the following sources: anti-GPX4 (ab125066), anti-HMGB1 (ab79823) and anti-MLKL (ab194699) from Abcam (Cambridge, UK); anti-β-actin (3700), anti-LC3B (2775) and anti-SLC7A11 (12691) from Cell Signaling Technology (Danvers, MA, USA); anti-KEAP1 (10503-2-AP) and anti-NRF2 (16396-1-AP) from Proteintech (Rosemont, IL, USA) and anti-CHMP5 (sc-374337) from Santa Cruz Biotechnology (Dallas, TX, USA).

Cell lines and cell culture

HT-29 (RRID: CVCL_0320), SW620 (RRID: CVCL_0547), A549 (RRID: CVCL_0023), and MDA-MB-231 (RRID: CVCL_0062) cell lines were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC). RMCCA-1 (RRID: CVCL_M257), a human cholangiocarcinoma (CCA) cell line, was established from a Thai patient [19]. HT-29, A549, and MDA-MB-231 cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM; HyClone, Logan, UT, USA), whereas SW620 and RMCCA-1 cells were maintained in Roswell Park Memorial Institute (RPMI) 1640 medium (HyClone). All media were supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Sigma, St. Louis, MO, USA) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (HyClone). Cells were maintained in a humidified incubator at 37 °C with 5% CO2. All cell lines were tested and confirmed to be free of mycoplasma contamination.

Lentiviral and plasmid transfection

Lentiviral particles were generated by co-transfecting HEK293T cells with a packaging plasmid (pCMV-VSV-G), an envelope plasmid (pCMV-dR8.2 dvpr), and one of the following plasmids: shRNA non-targeting control (shNT, pLKO.1-puro), shRNA targeting MLKL (shMLKL#1 or shMLKL#2), shRNA targeting KEAP1 (shKEAP1#1 or shKEAP1#2), LentiCRISPRv2, LentiCRISPRv2-MLKL, or an mCherry-tagged human CHMP5 plasmid. At 48 h post-transfection, culture supernatants containing viral particles were collected and filtered through a 0.45 μm sterile filter. The viral suspension was then used to transduce cancer cells in the presence of 8 µg/mL polybrene (Merck Millipore). After 24 h, transduced cells were selected with 1–2 µg/mL puromycin (Merck Millipore) for 48 h.

Western blot

Cells were lysed in RIPA buffer (Merck Millipore) supplemented with protease inhibitors (Roche, Mannheim, Germany). Protein concentrations were measured using the Bradford assay (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA). Equal amounts of protein (30 µg) were resolved by SDS-PAGE and transferred to PVDF membranes. Membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat milk in TBS-T for 1 h at room temperature, followed by incubation with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C. After washing, membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated anti-mouse (Cell Signaling, 7076) or anti-rabbit (Cell Signaling, 7074) secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. Protein bands were visualized using an ECL detection system (Merck Millipore) and captured with the Amersham ImageQuant 800 Western blot imaging system (Cytiva, Marlborough, MA, USA).

RNA extraction and RT-qPCR

Total RNA was extracted from cells using TRIzol™ Reagent (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Complementary DNA (cDNA) was synthesized from the isolated RNA using RevertAid Reverse Transcriptase (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) with oligo(dT)18 primers. Real-time PCR was performed using iTaq™ Universal SYBR® Green Supermix (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA) to quantify the expression levels of target genes. Gene-specific primers were designed based on published sequences (Table S1). ACTB was used as an internal control to normalize RNA input, and relative expression levels were calculated using the 2-ΔΔCt method.

MTT

Cell viability was assessed using the MTT assay (PanReac AppliChem, Darmstadt, Germany). After treatment, MTT reagent was added to each well, and incubated for 2 h. The formazan crystals were dissolved in DMSO, and absorbance was measured at 570 nm using an EnSpire 2300 microplate reader (PerkinElmer, Waltham, MA, USA).

Flow cytometry

After treatment, cells were harvested and washed with PBS. For cell death analysis, cells were resuspended in AnnexinV binding buffer containing AnnexinV-FITC (ImmunoTools, Friesoythe, Germany) and propidium iodide (PI; Invitrogen) for 15 min. For intracellular Fe²+ detection, cells were stained with 1 µM BioTracker™ FerroOrange dye (Merck) for 30 min prior to analysis. For lipid peroxidation detection, cells were stained with 1 µM BODIPY™ 581/591 C11 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) for 15 min. Following staining, a total of 10,000 events per sample were acquired on a BD Accuri™ C6 Plus Flow Cytometer (BD Biosciences, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA) and analyzed using FlowJo™ software version 10 (Becton Dickinson and Company).

GSH/GSSH detection

Cells were lysed in 1% NP-40 buffer, and intracellular reduced glutathione (GSH) levels were quantified using a GSH/GSSG Detection Kit (Abcam, ab138881) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Fluorescence intensity was measured using an EnSpire 2300 microplate reader (PerkinElmer) at an excitation wavelength of 490 nm and an emission wavelength of 520 nm. GSH concentrations were calculated from a standard curve and normalized to protein content (µM/mg protein). Relative concentrations were then calculated by comparison with shNT control cells.

Proteomic analysis

A total of 20 µg protein was reduced with 10 mM DTT at 62 °C for 20 min, alkylated with 12 mM iodoacetamide at room temperature for 25 min in the dark, and digested with trypsin (Thermo Fisher Scientific) at a 1:40 enzyme-to-protein ratio for 6 h at 37 °C. Peptides were reconstituted in 0.1% formic acid, and 1 µg of each digest was injected for LC–MS/MS analysis. Samples were analyzed on a Sciex TripleTOF 6600 + mass spectrometer (AB Sciex) coupled with an EASY-nLC 1000 system (Thermo Fisher Scientific) using an Acclaim PepMap™ C18 nano column. Mobile phase A was 0.1% formic acid in water, and mobile phase B was 80% acetonitrile with 0.1% formic acid. Peptides were eluted over 95 min at 300 nL/min, and MS data were acquired in high-sensitivity mode (MS1 range 400–1,500 m/z; up to 30 precursors per cycle; dynamic exclusion 15 s). SWATH-MS was performed in data-independent acquisition (DIA) mode with variable Q1 isolation windows optimized by the SWATH Acquisition Variable Window Calculator [20]. Collision energy was different for each window. Single injections were performed for each of the biological triplicates. Protein identification was performed using the Paragon™ Algorithm in ProteinPilot™ software [21] against the Homo sapiens UniProt database, with trypsin digestion, IAA alkylation, and a 1% false discovery rate (FDR). Proteins were filtered with ≥ 10 peptides/protein and unused ProtScore ≥ 0.05. Quantification was carried out in PeakView® software with a peptide confidence threshold of 99%, MS tolerance of 25 ppm, and MS/MS tolerance of 50 ppm. Shared peptides were excluded, with six transitions per peptide considered. All experiments were performed in triplicate. Data are presented as mean ± SD, and statistical significance was determined by one-way ANOVA with Duncan’s multiple range test and multiple testing correction (Bonferroni and Benjamini–Hochberg FDR). The mass spectrometry proteomics data have been deposited to the ProteomeXchange Consortium via the PRIDE partner repository with the dataset identifier PXD078766.

Immunofluorescence staining

Following treatment, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100. Afterward, cells were blocked with 2% BSA in PBS for 1 h at room temperature, incubated overnight at 4 °C with anti-CHMP5 primary antibodies (1:100 dilution). After washing, cells were incubated with Alexa Fluor 555-conjugated anti-mouse secondary antibodies (1:500 dilution). Hoechst 33342 and Annexin V-FITC were used to counterstain the nucleus and plasma membrane, respectively. Immunofluorescence images were captured using Leica DMi8 confocal microscope (Leica Microsystems, Wetzlar, Germany), and the intensity profiles of CHMP5 and Annexin V signals were analyzed using ImageJ software.

Statistical analysis

All data were analyzed and visualized using R statistical software (version 4.1.0), SPSS (version 22.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY), or GraphPad Prism (version 9.0). Experimental results are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD) from three independent experiments. Comparisons between two groups were performed using two-tailed Student’s t-tests. Statistically significant was considered when the p-value is less than 0.05.

Results

High MLKL is associated with enrichment of glutathione metabolism–related genes in CRC patients and with GPX4 inhibitor sensitivity across pan-cancer cell lines

Previous studies have shown that high MLKL expression is associated with poor prognosis in CRC patients [22, 23], suggesting an important role for MLKL in CRC development and progression. To further investigate the mechanisms by which MLKL contributes to CRC progression, we performed bioinformatics analyses to identify differentially enriched gene sets in CRC patients with high and low MLKL expressions. Through comprehensive bioinformatics analysis, we found that CRC patients with high MLKL expression exhibited significant enrichment of the HALLMARK REACTIVE OXYGEN SPECIES PATHWAY gene set (Fig. 1A, Table S2). We further performed correlation analysis between MLKL and individual genes within this pathway. Of the 45 genes analyzed, 22 were significantly correlated with MLKL expression in CRC patients (Fig. 1B, Table S3). These MLKL-associated genes were subsequently subjected to pathway enrichment analysis to identify the underlying biological processes. The results showed that the majority of these genes are involved in oxidative stress responses, glutathione (GSH) metabolism, and GSH-related antioxidant activity (Fig. 1C, Table S4). Notably, MLKL expression was positively associated with GSH metabolism genes including GLRX2, GLRX, and GSR, as well as with GSH-related antioxidant enzyme genes such as GPX4, GLRX2, MGST, and PRDX6. Collectively, these findings suggest that MLKL may play a regulatory role in modulating GSH metabolism and GSH-dependent antioxidant defense mechanisms in CRC.

In view of the crosstalk between necroptosis and ferroptosis, along with the ongoing clinical development of GPX4 inhibitors as anticancer agents, we examined the association between MLKL expression and sensitivity to GPX4 inhibitors (RSL3, ML162, and ML210) across various cancer cell lines. Notably, MLKL expression was positively correlated with reduced sensitivity to GPX4 inhibitors (Fig. 1D and Fig. S1), highlighting a potential role of MLKL in modulating cellular responses to GPX4 inhibitors in cancer cells.

MLKL knockdown enhances GPX4 inhibitor-induced ferroptosis across multiple cancer cell types

To validate the bioinformatics findings, we used two independent shRNAs to knockdown MLKL in CRC cell lines (HT-29 and SW620) and subsequently determined the sensitivity to the GPX4 inhibitor, RSL3. Although MLKL depletion did not affect GPX4 expression (Fig. S2A), cell death was markedly increased in MLKL-depleted CRC cells following RSL3 treatment (Fig. 2A-B, S2B-C). To broaden the relevance of our findings, we determined RSL3 sensitivity in other MLKL-knockdown cancer cell types including lung cancer (A549), breast cancer (MDA-MB-231) and cholangiocarcinoma (RMCCA-1) cell lines. Consistent with the finding in CRC cell model, loss of MLKL enhanced RSL3-induced cell death across all tested cancer cell lines. (Fig. 2C-E and Fig. S2A). To explore this further, we assessed the effect of MLKL depletion on sensitivity to an additional GPX4 inhibitor, ML210, using CRISPR-Cas9 as an alternative strategy to suppress MLKL. Notably, MLKL knockout markedly increased the sensitivity of HT-29 cells to both RSL3 and ML210 treatment. (Fig. S2D-F). Altogether, MLKL suppression sensitizes cancer cells to GPX4 inhibitors-induced cell death.

Inhibition of GPX4 is a well-established trigger of ferroptosis [9]. We therefore investigated whether MLKL suppression enhances ferroptosis induced by GPX4 inhibitors. The increased cell death observed in MLKL-knockdown cells was reversed by the ferroptosis inhibitors, ferrostatin-1 (Fer-1) and deferoxamine (DFO) (Fig. 3A-B, Fig. S3A-D and Fig. S4A-C), indicating ferroptosis-mediated cell death. Moreover, lipid peroxidation, a hallmark of ferroptosis, was also elevated in MLKL-knockdown cells (Fig. 3C-D and Fig. S4D-F), supporting increased ferroptosis. Altogether, these findings demonstrate that reduced MLKL expression sensitizes multiple cancer cell types to GPX4 inhibitor-induced ferroptosis.

MLKL-depleted CRC cells exhibit decreased intracellular GSH levels and downregulated SLC7A11 expression

Based on our bioinformatics analysis, which revealed enrichment of GSH metabolism-related genes in CRC patients with high MLKL expression, we further compared intracellular GSH levels between MLKL-knockdown and MLKL-expressing cells. Notably, MLKL-knockdown CRC cells exhibited significantly lower GSH levels than MLKL-expressing cells (Fig. 4A). GSH synthesis depends on cystine import via the transporter SLC7A11; therefore, we examined SLC7A11 expression following MLKL depletion. Interestingly, MLKL knockdown markedly reduced SLC7A11 expression at both mRNA and protein levels (Fig. 4B-C). Consistently, bioinformatics analysis of pan-cancer cell lines from the CCLE database showed a positive correlation between MLKL and SLC7A11 expression across multiple cancer types (Fig. 4D), reinforcing the potential association between MLKL and SLC7A11 in cancer cells. Given the role of SLC7A11 in GSH synthesis and ferroptosis resistance [24], these findings suggest that MLKL silencing enhances ferroptosis sensitivity and is associated with decreased SLC7A11 expression and reduced intracellular GSH levels.

Proteomic profiling identifies MLKL-associated ferroptosis-related proteins under basal and RSL3-treated conditions

To investigate the functional role of MLKL in ferroptosis, we compared the proteomic profiles of MLKL-knockdown and MLKL-expressing HT-29 cells. Principal component analysis (PCA) revealed distinct proteomic signatures between the two groups under both basal conditions and after 8 h of RSL3 treatment (Fig. S5A). A total of 99 and 467 differentially expressed proteins (DEPs) were identified under basal and RSL3-treated conditions, respectively (Fig. S5B-C). To identify MLKL-associated ferroptosis-related proteins, DEPs were overlapped with ferroptosis-related genes from the FerrDb2 database (Table S5). The analysis revealed 4 ferroptosis-related DEPs under basal level, whereas RSL3 treatment increased this number to 20. (Fig. 5A and Table S6-7).

To investigate the mechanism underlying SLC7A11 downregulation in MLKL-knockdown cells, we performed protein–protein interaction analysis between SLC7A11 and the basal level DEPs using the STRING. Among the identified DEPs, KEAP1 exhibited the strongest interaction score with SLC7A11 (Fig. 5B). Previous studies have established that NRF2/KEAP1 signaling is a key regulatory pathway controlling SLC7A11 expression [25–27]. Increased KEAP1 promotes NRF2 degradation, thereby reducing NRF2-dependent transcription of target genes, including SLC7A11. Consistent with this mechanism, Western blot analysis revealed elevated KEAP1 levels and reduced NRF2 expression in MLKL-knockdown CRC cells (Fig. 5C). Notably, KEAP1 mRNA levels remained unchanged in MLKL-knockdown cells (Fig. S6A), suggesting that MLKL regulates KEAP1 at the post-translational level.

Since KEAP1 turnover appears to be autophagy-dependent [28], and previous studies have demonstrated a role for MLKL in facilitating autophagy in CRC cells [22], we further examined autophagic flux in MLKL-expressing and MLKL-knockdown cells using chloroquine (CQ), a lysosomal acidification inhibitor that blocks autophagic cargo degradation and is widely used to assess autophagic flux. MLKL-knockdown cells exhibited markedly reduced LC3B-II levels relative to LC3B-I compared to MLKL-expressing cells following CQ treatment (Fig. S6B), suggesting reduced autophagic activity in the absence of MLKL. Important, CQ treatment led to increased accumulation of KEAP1 protein in MLKL-expressing cells, confirming that KEAP1 is actively degraded through the autophagic pathway in MLKL-expressing cells. In contrast, CQ treatment failed to increase KEAP1 levels in MLKL-knockdown cells, suggesting that lysosome-dependent regulation of KEAP1 may be compromised upon MLKL depletion. Taken together, these findings demonstrate that MLKL facilitates autophagic degradation of KEAP1, and that MLKL loss impairs this process, which may in turn contribute to reduced NRF2 activity and decreased SLC7A11 expression.

Furthermore, we found that KEAP1 knockdown partially restored SLC7A11 expression in MLKL-depleted cells (Fig. S6C), indicating that SLC7A11 downregulation is mediated, at least in part, through dysregulation of NRF2/KEAP1 signaling. However, KEAP1 knockdown only partially reduced RSL3-induced cell death in MLKL-knockdown cells (Fig. 5D), indicating that the NRF2/KEAP1/SLC7A11 axis contributes to ferroptosis regulation, while additional mechanisms are likely involved.

To elucidate the precise functional role of MLKL in ferroptosis, we performed functional interaction analysis using the STRING database to identify MLKL-associated proteins. CHMP5, HMGB1, and SQSTM1 were identified as MLKL-interacting candidates (Fig. 6A), with CHMP5 showing the strongest interaction score (Table S8). CHMP5 was prioritized not only because it exhibited the strongest predicted interaction with MLKL, but also because of its established role in ESCRT-III-mediated membrane repair, a process increasingly recognized as a critical determinant of ferroptosis resistance [15]. As the potential link between MLKL and ferroptosis-associated membrane repair has not previously been explored, CHMP5 represented the most mechanistically relevant candidate among the identified proteins for further investigation. Western blot analysis confirmed decreased CHMP5 expression in MLKL-knockdown HT-29 cells under both basal and RSL3-treated conditions (Fig. 6B). Reduced CHMP5 expressions were also observed in SW620 and A549 cells, reinforcing the functional link between MLKL and CHMP5 on ferroptosis regulation in cancer cells. However, CHMP5 mRNA levels did not change in MLKL-knockdown cells (Fig. S7), suggesting that MLKL might regulate CHMP5 expression at post-translational level. Moreover, we found that CHMP5 overexpression rescued RSL3-induced cell death in MLKL-knockdown cells (Fig. 6C and Fig. S8), suggesting that MLKL may regulate ferroptosis, at least in part, through CHMP5.

MLKL knockdown impairs CHMP5 translocation to the plasma membrane and increases membrane damage upon ferroptosis induction

Previous studies have shown that CHMP5 can translocate to the plasma membrane during ferroptosis activation, where it facilitates ESCRT-III–dependent membrane repair and promoting ferroptosis resistance in cancer cells [15]. MLKL has also been reported to regulate ESCRT-III–mediated repair by modulating the translocation of CHMP proteins to the plasma membrane [13]. Based on these findings, we examined CHMP5 localization in MLKL-knockdown and MLKL-expressing cells following RSL3 treatment using immunofluorescence staining. CHMP5 is reported to accumulate at the inner leaflet of the plasma membrane during the early stages of membrane repair, before damaged membrane is shed. To visualize membrane proximity, cells were co-stained with AnnexinV-FITC which labels phosphatidylserine and serves as a plasma membrane marker. Under basal conditions, CHMP5 was evenly distributed in the cytoplasm in both MLKL-expressing and MLKL-depleted cells. Upon RSL3 treatment, CHMP5 in MLKL-expressing cells relocalized and accumulated near the plasma membrane (Fig. 7A and Fig. S9), consistent with its role in membrane repair during ferroptosis. In contrast, CHMP5 localization did not change in MLKL-knockdown cells after RSL3 treatment, indicating that MLKL is required for CHMP5 redistribution in response to ferroptosis induction.

Discussion

Although ferroptosis-based therapies hold therapeutic potential for cancer treatment, many tumors develop intrinsic or acquired resistance, limiting treatment outcomes. In this study, we showed that MLKL modulates ferroptosis sensitivity in CRC and additional cancer models. Integrative bioinformatics analyses link high MLKL with glutathione metabolism-related genes and reduced sensitivity to GPX4 inhibitors. Consistent with this, MLKL depletion sensitized cancer cells to GPX4 inhibitor-induced ferroptosis in vitro. MLKL-deficient CRC cells exhibited reduced intracellular glutathione levels, accompanied by altered expression of KEAP1, NRF2, and SLC7A11 expressions, suggesting a potential association between MLKL and altered antioxidant defense. However, modulation of the KEAP1/NRF2/SLC7A11 axis only partially affected ferroptosis sensitivity, indicating that this pathway contributes to ferroptosis regulation, while additional mechanisms are also likely involved. In parallel, MLKL suppression decreased CHMP5 expression and disrupted ESCRT-III-mediated membrane repair, leading to increased membrane damage during ferroptosis (Fig. 8). Notably, restoration of CHMP5 expression rescued ferroptosis sensitivity in MLKL-knockdown cells, supporting an important contribution of CHMP5-dependent membrane repair to MLKL-mediated ferroptosis regulation. Collectively, these findings uncover a previously unrecognized role of MLKL in ferroptosis regulation through CHMP5-mediated membrane repair, with additional involvement of glutathione metabolism, and suggest MLKL as a potential therapeutic target to overcome ferroptosis resistance in cancer.

MLKL is widely recognized as a key effector of necroptosis. However, growing evidence indicates that MLKL also influences other forms of regulated cell death. For example, plasma membrane disruption by activated MLKL can promote NLRP3 inflammasome assembly, thereby inducing pyroptosis [32]. Conversely, MLKL depletion has been reported to enhance apoptosis triggered by TNF-α and TRAIL agonists [33, 34]. A previous study also demonstrated that loss of MLKL increased susceptibility to ferroptosis in an acute kidney injury model [11], suggesting a potential link between MLKL and ferroptosis. However, this observation was made in a non-cancer setting and did not provide mechanistic insight into how MLKL regulates ferroptosis. Furthermore, although MLKL deficiency has been associated with increased therapeutic vulnerability in CRC [22, 23], previous studies focused on mechanisms unrelated to ferroptosis. Therefore, prior to the present study, whether MLKL directly regulates ferroptosis in CRC and the molecular basis of this regulation remained unknown. Here, we provide the first evidence that MLKL functions as a suppressor of ferroptosis in CRC through coordinated regulation of the KEAP1/NRF2/SLC7A11 antioxidant pathway and CHMP5/ESCRT-III-mediated membrane repair. Notably, we identify the CHMP5/ESCRT-III pathway as a novel downstream effector linking MLKL to ferroptosis resistance. Collectively, these findings expand the biological functions of MLKL beyond necroptosis and provide new mechanistic insight into ferroptosis resistance in CRC. However, future studies incorporating long-term survival assays will be valuable for assessing the long-term consequences of MLKL-mediated ferroptosis sensitization on cancer cell persistence and therapeutic relevance.

GPX4 is a key suppressor of ferroptosis by reducing lipid hydroperoxides into non-toxic lipid alcohols using glutathione (GSH) as an essential cofactor [35]. Accordingly, inhibition of GPX4 or depletion of GSH sensitizes cancer cells to ferroptosis [7]. In this study, we show that MLKL-depleted CRC cells exhibit markedly reduced intracellular GSH levels compared with MLKL-expressing cells. This reduction coincided with downregulation of SLC7A11, the cystine transporter responsible for importing the rate-limiting substrate for GSH biosynthesis. Thus, decreased GSH levels in MLKL-knockdown cells may reflect impaired cystine uptake due to reduced SLC7A11 expression.

Moreover, our proteomic and Western blot analyses revealed that MLKL depletion is associated with increased KEAP1 and decreased NRF2 expression, and that KEAP1 depletion partially restores SLC7A11 levels in MLKL-knockdown cells. Given that the NRF2/KEAP1 axis is a well-established regulator of SLC7A11 expression [25–27], these results suggest that MLKL may modulate SLC7A11, at least in part, through regulation of NRF2/KEAP1 signaling. However, the mechanism by which MLKL influences KEAP1/NRF2/SLC7A11 pathway remains unclear. Notably, KEAP1 mRNA levels were unchanged in MLKL-depleted cells, indicating that MLKL likely regulates KEAP1 at the post-translational level. Previous studies have shown that KEAP1 undergoes autophagy-dependent degradation [28, 36] and MLKL has been implicated in promoting autophagic flux [22, 37]. Consistent with these reports, our findings demonstrated impaired autophagic flux in MLKL-knockdown cells and showed that lysosomal inhibition increased KEAP1 accumulation in MLKL-expressing cells, but not in MLKL-deficient cells. These findings suggest that MLKL contributes to autophagy-dependent KEAP1 turnover, and that MLKL depletion may disrupt this process, leading to KEAP1 accumulation. Nevertheless, the precise mechanism by which MLKL regulates KEAP1 turnover remains unclear. Whether MLKL directly interacts with KEAP1 or indirectly influences its autophagy-dependent degradation through intermediary proteins warrants further investigation.

Importantly, although KEAP1 knockdown partially restored SLC7A11 expression in MLKL-deficient cells, it only partially reduced ferroptosis sensitivity, indicating that NRF2/KEAP1-mediated regulation of SLC7A11 is not sufficient to confer ferroptosis resistance and that additional mechanisms are involved. The partial recovery of SLC7A11 further suggests that MLKL regulates SLC7A11 through both NRF2/KEAP1-dependent and -independent mechanisms, leaving residual defects in cystine uptake even after KEAP1 depletion. Moreover, glutathione metabolism is likely compromised beyond cystine import, as our bioinformatics analyses revealed that MLKL expression correlates with multiple glutathione metabolism-related genes, including GLRX2, GLRX, and GSR, which are essential for GSH synthesis and recycling [38, 39]. Thus, antioxidant capacity may remain insufficient despite recovery of SLC7A11. Importantly, MLKL depletion also impairs CHMP5-dependent ESCRT-III–mediated membrane repair, a critical downstream defense mechanism against ferroptosis damage. Consequently, even if lipid peroxidation is partially limited, defective membrane repair may still drive cell death. Collectively, these findings suggest that MLKL coordinates both antioxidant defense and membrane repair, and that disruption of these processes, particularly membrane repair, underlies the heightened ferroptosis sensitivity observed in MLKL- deficient cells.

The ESCRT machinery plays a critical role in membrane remodeling and is composed of three subcomplexes—ESCRT-I, ESCRT-II, and ESCRT-III—each contributing to distinct cellular processes in a context-dependent manner [40]. Upon plasma membrane disruption, calcium influx recruits charged multivesicular body proteins (CHMPs) to sites of damage, where ESCRT-III components mediate membrane repair and suppress cell death pathways such as necroptosis [13], pyroptosis [41], and ferroptosis [15]. Among these components, CHMP5 and CHMP6 are specifically implicated in preventing membrane damage during ferroptosis [42]. Previous studies have shown that CHMP5 knockdown sensitizes pancreatic cancer cells to ferroptosis [15], whereas its overexpression protects against ferroptosis cell death [30]. Importantly, our study indicates that disruption of CHMP5-dependent ESCRT-III–mediated membrane repair represents a dominant mechanism underlying ferroptosis sensitivity in MLKL-knockdown cells. MLKL depletion markedly reduced CHMP5 expression and impaired its membrane translocation, leading to increased membrane damage during ferroptosis. As maintenance of plasma membrane integrity is a critical determinant of cell survival, failure of this repair system likely overrides residual antioxidant defenses. Consistently, restoration of CHMP5 expression rescued ferroptosis sensitivity, highlighting its central role in this process. Collectively, these findings support a model in which MLKL regulates ferroptosis through multiple complementary mechanisms, including CHMP5-dependent membrane repair and KEAP1/NRF2/SLC7A11-mediated glutathione metabolism. While our data support an important contribution of CHMP5-dependent membrane repair, further studies are required to define the relative contributions and potential interplay between these pathways.

Our findings align with previous studies showing that MLKL has broader functions beyond its canonical role in necroptosis, including the regulation of ESCRT-III–mediated membrane shedding. Indeed, MLKL has been reported to co-localize with several ESCRT components, including CHMP4B, VPS37A, VPS28, TSG101, and MVB12B, and MLKL depletion reduces ESCRT protein translocation and impairs membrane repair during the early stages of cell death [13, 43]. However, the mechanism by which MLKL regulates CHMP5 expression remains unclear. Our findings indicated that MLKL may regulate CHMP5 through post-translational mechanisms. In MLKL-knockdown cells, CHMP5 protein levels were markedly reduced without a corresponding change in CHMP5 mRNA, suggesting that MLKL influences CHMP5 stability, turnover, or trafficking rather than transcription. Given prior evidence that MLKL contributes to endosomal trafficking [34, 43], MLKL may modulate CHMP5 homeostasis by altering endosomal pathways in which ESCRT proteins are sorted and recycled. Collectively, although the precise mechanism by which MLKL governs CHMP5 expression and activity remains unclear, the ability of CHMP5 overexpression to rescue ferroptosis sensitivity in MLKL-knockdown cells supports a role for CHMP5-mediated membrane repair in MLKL-dependent ferroptosis regulation.

Conclusions

This study identifies MLKL as a novel regulator of ferroptosis in cancer, extending its role beyond necroptosis. Therefore, targeting MLKL may sensitize cancer cells to ferroptosis, particularly in patients with high MLKL expression that warrants further investigation in preclinical models. Although MLKL high tumors might benefit from necroptosis-based therapy, expression of key necroptosis proteins, particularly RIPK3, may be limited. Further studies are needed to evaluate the therapeutic potential of MLKL-targeting strategies and their combination with ferroptosis-inducing agents in clinically relevant models.
