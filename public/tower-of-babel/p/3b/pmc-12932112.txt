A Rare Case of Anterior Papillary Muscle Rupture Following Acute Non‐ST Elevation Myocardial Infarction
Authors: Biyas Thapa, Somee Rauniyar, Pratiksha Paudel
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTSudden hemodynamic deterioration after myocardial infarction should prompt immediate evaluation for papillary muscle rupture, even in the absence of a murmur or large‐vessel occlusion. Acute severe mitral regurgitation from papillary muscle rupture may mimic recurrent ischemia, and early echocardiography with prompt surgical intervention can be lifesaving.Keywords: echocardiography, mitral regurgitation, NSTEMI, papillary muscle rupture

Introduction

Papillary muscle rupture (PMR) is an uncommon yet potentially life‐threatening complication of acute myocardial infarction (AMI), as it contributes to about 5% of post‐myocardial infarction mortality [1, 2]. The majority of PMR cases involve the rupture of the posterior papillary muscle owing to its single blood supply from the posterior descending artery (PDA). Ischemic rupture of the anterior papillary muscle is extremely rare, as it receives a dual blood supply [3]. PMR can lead to catastrophic clinical outcomes such as mitral regurgitation, pulmonary edema, and cardiogenic shock, which necessitate immediate medical intervention [4]. In patients with high clinical suspicion, transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) or transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) is used for the diagnosis of PMR. Immediate surgical management with mitral valve repair or replacement can be life‐saving. Here, we present a rare case of anterior PMR in a patient with acute non‐ST elevation myocardial infarction (NSTEMI), associated with distal occlusion of the obtuse marginal branch of the LCX, who underwent successful mitral valve replacement.

Case History/Examination

A 76‐year‐old man presented to the emergency department with an 8‐h history of substernal chest pain radiating to the neck and bilateral arms, associated with shortness of breath and a nonproductive cough. On arrival, he was tachycardic with a heart rate of 130 beats/min, normotensive (blood pressure 110/75 mmHg), and had an oxygen saturation of 94% on room air. Cardiovascular examination revealed an S4 gallop, and pulmonary auscultation demonstrated bibasilar crackles. The initial electrocardiogram revealed mild ST‐segment depression in the precordial leads, accompanied by biphasic T waves, without diagnostic ST‐segment elevation. Serum troponin I was markedly elevated at 22 ng/mL (Figure 1). Chest radiography demonstrated left basilar atelectasis and a mildly enlarged cardiac silhouette. A diagnosis of non–ST‐elevation myocardial infarction (NSTEMI) was made, and the patient was admitted to the cardiovascular intensive care unit (CVICU). Intravenous unfractionated heparin was initiated; however, dual antiplatelet therapy was deferred due to declining blood pressure and concern for a potential surgical indication. An initial transthoracic echocardiogram was attempted but was nondiagnostic because of poor acoustic windows.

Differential Diagnosis, Investigations, and Treatment

During his ICU course, the patient developed worsening chest pain with a rising troponin level to 36 ng/mL, persistent tachyarrhythmia (heart rate in the 150s, sinus versus atrial tachycardia), and progressive hypotension with systolic blood pressures in the 70s, consistent with evolving cardiogenic shock. He underwent two attempts at electrical cardioversion. Given the acute pulmonary edema and hemodynamic collapse disproportionate to the presumed ischemic burden, a mechanical complication, specifically papillary muscle rupture with acute severe mitral regurgitation, was strongly suspected.

The patient was emergently intubated and taken to the cardiac catheterization laboratory. A limited bedside echocardiogram demonstrated a hyperdynamic left ventricle, prompting administration of intravenous fluids. Although image quality was suboptimal, there was concern for a posterior pericardial effusion. In acute, torrential mitral regurgitation, rapid left ventricular–left atrial pressure equalization may result in a brief or absent systolic murmur, and color Doppler interrogation may underestimate the severity of regurgitation due to a short‐lived systolic jet, particularly in the presence of tachycardia and poor acoustic windows. This represents a recognized physiologic and technical limitation of clinical examination and transthoracic echocardiography rather than a diagnostic oversight, underscoring the importance of maintaining a high index of suspicion and early use of transesophageal echocardiography in unstable patients. Subsequent transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) confirmed severe (grade 4+) holosystolic mitral regurgitation due to rupture of the anterior papillary muscle (Figures 2 and 3).

Rupture of the anterior leaflet protruding into the left ventricle.

Coronary angiography revealed a normal left main artery and diffuse nonobstructive disease in the mid‐to‐distal left anterior descending artery with TIMI III flow. The left circumflex artery was small and otherwise normal; however, the obtuse marginal branch demonstrated a distal cutoff with TIMI I flow, consistent with the culprit lesion (Figure 4). The right coronary artery was dominant, ectatic, and exhibited mild luminal irregularities. No percutaneous coronary intervention was performed, as the obtuse marginal lesion was distal and not amenable to intervention. Given the distal location, small vessel caliber, and lack of suitability for percutaneous intervention, medical management was favored over stent placement.

Cath image showing OM occlusion branch of LCx.

Left ventriculography demonstrated hyperdynamic systolic function with an estimated ejection fraction of 65%–70% and severe mitral regurgitation with complete opacification of the left atrium (Figure 5). Right heart catheterization revealed a low cardiac index of 1.4 L/min/m2, elevated pulmonary artery pressures (52/19 mmHg; mean 32 mmHg), and a preserved right atrial pressure (Figure 6). Given profound hemodynamic instability from acute severe mitral regurgitation secondary to papillary muscle rupture, a percutaneous left ventricular assist device (Impella) was placed for circulatory support, and the patient was transferred emergently for surgical intervention.

Echocardiography showing severe mitral regurgitation.

Cath image showing patent RCA.

The patient underwent emergent median sternotomy with initiation of cardiopulmonary bypass. The Impella device was repositioned under echocardiographic guidance. Following cardioplegic arrest, a left atriotomy was performed, revealing rupture of the anterior papillary muscle. The anterior mitral leaflet was excised, and the posterior leaflet was preserved. A 31‐mm St. Jude Biocor bioprosthetic valve was implanted using pledgeted sutures. After adequate de‐airing, the patient was weaned from bypass with inotropic support. The Impella device was subsequently removed once ventricular function was deemed sufficient. Final intraoperative TEE demonstrated normal prosthetic valve function with moderately reduced biventricular systolic function.

The patient was transferred to the CVICU in critical but stable condition. His postoperative course was complicated by paroxysmal atrial fibrillation and a pulseless electrical activity (PEA) arrest requiring 3 min of cardiopulmonary resuscitation and administration of 1 mg epinephrine, with return of spontaneous circulation. Amiodarone was discontinued due to episodes of sinus pauses following resuscitation. He remained in rate‐controlled atrial fibrillation and, after a prolonged ICU stay, was discharged home on beta‐blocker therapy and warfarin with close outpatient cardiology follow‐up.

Conclusion and Results (Outcome and Follow‐Up)

This case highlights the rare but potentially catastrophic nature of papillary muscle rupture following myocardial infarction, even when associated with a small‐vessel culprit lesion. Sudden hemodynamic deterioration may occur without a classic murmur, and TTE can underestimate severity, underscoring the need for a high index of suspicion. Early recognition, often requiring TEE, and prompt surgical intervention with mitral valve replacement are critical for survival. Our patient not only survived anterior PMR complicated by pulseless electrical activity arrest but continues to do well on long‐term follow‐up with guideline‐directed medical therapy and anticoagulation, with no further revascularization indicated. This case emphasizes the importance of integrating clinical chronology, hemodynamics, and advanced imaging, alongside vigilant monitoring and multidisciplinary management, in high‐risk post‐MI patients.

Discussion

The incidence of papillary muscle rupture (PMR), a devastating mechanical complication of acute myocardial infarction (AMI), has significantly reduced due to improved revascularization techniques such as percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) [2]. However, despite its rarity (often cited as less than 0.5%–1% of AMI cases), it contributes to a disproportionate share of infarct‐related mortality, historically approximated at around 5% [4]. PMR generally occurs 2–7 days after an episode of AMI and is characterized by acute severe mitral regurgitation, pulmonary edema, and cardiogenic shock, with very high mortality in the absence of prompt surgical intervention [4, 5].

PMR is more prevalent in the posterior papillary muscle due to its singular blood supply from the right coronary artery (RCA) or a dominant left circumflex artery (LCX) following inferior wall myocardial infarction. In contrast, the dual blood supply from branches of the LCX and the left anterior descending artery (LAD) generally protects the anterior papillary muscle from rupture, making anterior PMR significantly less common [3].

Risk factors for PMR include advanced age, delayed treatment of AMI, chronic kidney disease, history of heart failure, etc. Here, we present a case of a 75‐year‐old man who suffered from anterior PMR, 7 days after an episode of non‐ST‐elevation myocardial infarction (NSTEMI), due to the blockage of the small obtuse marginal branch of the LCX artery. His past medical history included hypertension, hyperlipidemia, stage V chronic kidney disease (baseline creatinine 1.0–1.6), anemia, a prior stroke in June 2018 without residual deficits, early Alzheimer's disease, gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD), anxiety/depression, and a history of tobacco use.

PMR should be suspected in patients with sudden, decompensated congestive heart failure and a new‐onset, regurgitant murmur, primarily in the days following acute MI. PMR can be diagnosed with transthoracic echocardiography (TTE), although its diagnostic accuracy is limited, with a sensitivity at 65%–85% [6]. As the mitral valve apparatus is a posterior structure, transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) can provide better visibility with superior diagnostic accuracy, with a sensitivity of 95%–100% [7]. Although an alternative sequence of primary papillary muscle rupture with secondary myocardial injury is biologically plausible, the clinical chronology in our patient suggests that ischemia may have preceded the mechanical complication. The patient initially presented with prolonged substernal chest pain radiating to the neck and arms, with significant dyspnea and hemodynamic deterioration developing later during hospitalization. This temporal progression is consistent with NSTEMI complicated by subsequent papillary muscle rupture and acute severe mitral regurgitation. Nevertheless, given the severity of hemodynamic compromise at presentation and the limited size and distal location of the obtuse marginal lesion, definitive causality cannot be established. Accordingly, this case highlights the diagnostic challenge in distinguishing ischemia‐driven papillary muscle rupture from primary papillary muscle rupture leading to acute severe mitral regurgitation with secondary diffuse subendocardial ischemia (type 2 myocardial infarction).

The electrocardiographic findings, including diffuse ST‐segment depression and conduction abnormalities, are more consistent with global subendocardial ischemia than with a focal lateral wall infarction. Acute severe mitral regurgitation, regardless of the initiating event, can abruptly elevate left ventricular diastolic filling pressures, reduce coronary perfusion, and result in diffuse ischemia with marked troponin elevation.

This case highlights several important teaching points. Acute severe mitral regurgitation may lack a prominent systolic murmur and can be underestimated on transthoracic echocardiography due to rapid left ventricular–left atrial pressure equalization and technical limitations. Additionally, diffuse electrocardiographic changes and marked troponin elevation may reflect global subendocardial ischemia from elevated left ventricular diastolic pressures, underscoring the importance of integrating clinical chronology, hemodynamics, and advanced imaging when evaluating suspected mechanical complications of myocardial infarction.

Despite its usual protection by dual blood supply from the left anterior descending and left circumflex arteries, rupture of the anterolateral papillary muscle has been reported in the setting of isolated branch vessel occlusion, including diagonal and obtuse marginal arteries.10 In such cases, mechanisms such as distal branch infarction, microvascular dysfunction, or supply–demand mismatch may contribute to papillary muscle ischemia despite the absence of significant epicardial coronary obstruction, underscoring the rarity and diagnostic complexity of this presentation.

Immediate surgical intervention is critical for the management of PMR to prevent adverse outcomes [5, 8]. Despite the risk of increased operative mortality, both mitral valve repair and mitral valve replacement are practiced as definitive surgical methods in patients with PMR due to acceptable statistics of favorable outcomes in the long term [9, 10]. Our patient underwent emergency mitral valve replacement with a 31 mm Biocor prosthesis, followed by Impella device removal via right groin, as the bi‐ventricular function was deemed normal even without Impella. His postoperative course was uneventful. After a prolonged stay and recovery in the ICU, the patient was discharged home in a stable condition with appropriate follow‐ups scheduled with the Department of Cardiology.
