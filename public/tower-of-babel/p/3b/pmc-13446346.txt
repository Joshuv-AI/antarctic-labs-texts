Late neurologic and functional outcomes in patients with severe anoxic brain injury after cardiopulmonary arrest: a multicenter cohort study
Authors: Jayne Chu, Doanh Ly, Y Pham, Aileen Freer, Minetta Ralleca, Marlon Caballero, Baby Lyn Abadilla, Craig Hou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundNeurological prognostication after cardiac arrest remains challenging, and most patients with severe anoxic brain injury have an indeterminate prognosis despite multimodal assessment. Although delayed awakening has been reported in the era of targeted temperature management, long-term functional outcomes in this population remain poorly described.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective multicenter cohort study across 18 hospitals to assess the late neurologic and functional outcomes of patients with severe anoxic-ischemic brain injury after cardiopulmonary arrest. Patients who were hospitalized between Jan 1, 2018 and Jan 1, 2023 with anoxic brain injury due to cardiopulmonary arrest were included if they were functionally independent at baseline (CPC 1–2) but remained comatose or in an unresponsive wakefulness syndrome (UWS)/vegetative state at 2 weeks. To study the natural course of disease, we included only patients whose families wanted to continue life-sustaining treatment at the time of inclusion, and those who survived at least three weeks after arrest. Neurological and functional outcomes were assessed longitudinally for up to 12 months using the Cerebral Performance Category (CPC) scale.ResultsA total of 169 patients met inclusion criteria (mean age 52.9 years). Initial hospitalization was prolonged (mean length of stay 61.2 days). Twenty-seven patients died before hospital discharge. Of the 142 patients discharged, an additional 40 died within 12 months, for a total of 67 deaths (39.6%). Forty-four of these deaths were due to eventual withdrawal of life support, due to deteriorating medical status with superimposed medical complications. Among the 102 patients alive at 12 months, 99 remained severely disabled (CPC 3–4). Only three patients (1.8% of the cohort) regained CPC 1–2 at 12 months.ConclusionsIn this large multicenter cohort of patients with severe anoxic brain injury who remained comatose or in a UWS/vegetative state at two weeks, functional recovery with CPC 1–2 at 12 months was rare. Although delayed neurological improvement may occur, favorable functional recovery was rare and prolonged aggressive care was most often associated with death or severe long-term disability. These findings provide additional outcome data to inform neuroprognostication and goals-of-care discussions after cardiac arrest.Keywords: Cardiac arrest, Anoxic brain injury, Neuroprognostication, Late recovery, Delayed awakening, Cerebral performance category

Introduction

More than 600,000 people suffer from cardiac arrest each year in the United States, with most survivors suffering some degree of anoxic ischemic brain injury.1, 2 Most deaths following resuscitation from cardiac arrest result from the active withdrawal of life-sustaining treatment because a poor neurological outcome is predicted.3, 4, 5 Earlier guidelines recommended prognostication at 72 h after arrest.6, 7, 8 However, neurologic outcomes have improved with bystander training/awareness and targeted temperature management, and there are increasing reports of patients with late awakening and eventual functional recovery.9, 10, 11, 12, 13, 14 Due to these reports of late recovery, guidelines from the neurology, neurocritical care and rehabilitation medicine societies now recommend extended observation periods, even up to 28 days, for patients with indeterminate prognosis, as recovery may occur over weeks to months.7, 15, 16, 17, 18

In this multicenter study, we sought to characterize the late neurologic and functional outcome in patients with severe anoxic brain injury who remained comatose or in a UWS/vegetative state at two weeks after cardiopulmonary arrest. It is during this subacute period after cardiac arrest that clinicians and families usually have to consider whether to pursue additional invasive therapies (ie, tracheostomy, percutaneous gastrostomy, chronic vascular access) for ongoing life-sustaining care. Data describing long-term outcomes in this population remain limited, and additional information may help support families with goals-of-care decision-making.

Methods

From the electronic medical record, we identified patients with diagnoses of cardiopulmonary arrest and anoxic brain injury across 18 Kaiser Permanente Northern California hospitals between January 1, 2018, and January 1, 2023, using the diagnoses of “cardiac arrest,” “cardiopulmonary arrest,” “coma,” “vegetative” and “anoxic brain injury.” We used the Cerebral Performance Category (CPC) Scale to assess neurologic outcomes since it is a simple and widely used tool in research outcome assessment after cardiac arrest.

Both in-hospital and out-of-hospital cardiac arrest survivors were included in the study. Usual post–cardiac arrest care, including TTM (targeted temperature management), when indicated, was administered and patients were assessed with multimodal neuroprognostication, including daily neurologic exams by the medical team. Because this was a retrospective multicenter study, prognostic testing was performed according to local practice and was not standardized across participating hospitals. EEG, somatosensory evoked potential (SSEP), MRI, and biomarker data were therefore not consistently available for retrospective analysis.

Patients with baseline CPC 3–4 or incomplete follow-up data were excluded.

Patients included in the study were subsequently re-evaluated at 1-month, 1–3-month, 3–6-month, 6–9-month, and >12-month intervals to assess neurological and functional recovery using the CPC Scale. Follow-up intervals, rather than fixed assessment dates were used because evaluations at precise time points were often infeasible due to patients' functional limitations, recurrent hospitalizations, and variable ability for clinical follow-up.

All data were obtained through retrospective review of electronic medical records, including inpatient, outpatient, and emergency department encounters. Reviewed documentation included notes from physicians, nurses, speech therapists, occupational therapists, physical therapists and social workers.

Data on wakefulness, speech, mobility, independence with ADLs and iADLs, and neuropsychological assessments were extracted and analyzed. Activities of daily living (ADLs) included basic self-care tasks such as feeding, dressing, bathing, toileting, and transfers. Instrumental activities of daily living (iADLs) included more complex activities required for independent living, such as medication management, financial management, shopping, transportation, and meal preparation. Extracted data were re-reviewed for accuracy and independently spot-checked by additional reviewers.

The study was approved by the Kaiser Permanente Northern California Institutional Review Board, which serves as the central IRB for participating Kaiser Permanente Northern California hospitals. The requirement for informed consent was waived because of the retrospective design and minimal risk to participants (Fig. 1).

Results

We identified 169 patients across 18 hospitals in Northern California who met inclusion criteria. All patients had been functionally independent (CPC 1–2) prior to their cardiopulmonary arrest but remained in a comatose or a UWS/vegetative state two weeks after the event. These patients were included because their surrogates opted to continue aggressive life-sustaining care, and they were able to survive at least 3 weeks after cardiac arrest. At baseline, most of these patients were fully functional; five required partial assistance due to limited mobility.

The study cohort comprised 109 males and 60 females, ranging in age from 11 to 93 years (mean 52.9 years). There were 117 out-of-hospital cardiac arrests, and 52 in-hospital cardiac arrests. There were 44 cardiac causes, 104 noncardiac causes, and 21 were of unknown etiology. Of the 104 noncardiac causes, 16 were attributed to drug overdose (Table 1).

Most patients experienced a prolonged initial hospitalization (mean length of stay of 61.2 days; range 17–293 days) as they required stabilization and recovery from the initial critical illness precipitating cardiopulmonary arrest. Hospitalization was also prolonged by complications related to anoxic brain injury (e.g., myoclonus, seizures), and complications associated with critical illness (e.g., sepsis, acute kidney injury, gastrointestinal bleeding, ventilator-associated pneumonia, thromboembolic events, critical illness polyneuropathy and myopathy).

Twenty-seven patients survived at least 3 weeks but died before hospital discharge. Among the 142 patients who were discharged, many required feeding tubes, tracheostomies, and even ongoing ventilatory support. An additional 40 patients died after the initial hospitalization, resulting in a total of 67 deaths (39.6%) within the 12-month observation period following cardiopulmonary arrest. Of these 67 deaths, forty-four were due to eventual withdrawal of life support due to persistently poor neurologic status and/or subsequent medical complications.

Following the initial hospitalization, 97 of the 142 patients discharged alive experienced one or more rehospitalizations during the follow-up period, with some patients experiencing multiple readmissions. These hospitalizations were due to progression or exacerbation of pre-existing comorbidities, post-arrest organ dysfunction, complications of anoxic brain injury, or resultant medical frailty (ie sepsis, aspiration pneumonia, urosepsis, arrhythmias, thromboembolic events, tracheitis, pressure ulcers, osteomyelitis, seizures, gastrointestinal bleeding, dislodgement of indwelling urinary or gastrostomy catheters).

Of the 102 patients who were alive at 12 months following cardiopulmonary arrest, 99 remained significantly functionally impaired (CPC 3–4). Only three patients (1.8%) achieved good functional recovery (CPC 1–2). These patients are as described: Patient CJ suffered cardiac arrest at age 30 from drug overdose. At 12 months he was independent in ADLs despite contractures and spasticity and had persistent impairments in memory and executive function. Patient XA developed cardiopulmonary arrest at age 31 due to drug overdose. He regained independence in ADLs but had a residual foot drop and difficulty walking. Neither CJ or XA required caregiver assistance at 12 months. Patient EB was 26 years old at the time of cardiac arrest of unclear etiology. He also regained independence in ADLs but had residual speech and gait deficits that did not substantially limit independent function. Among available studies, none had MRI or EEG findings considered universally predictive of poor neurological outcome (Table 2).

Discussion

In this multicenter cohort of patients with severe anoxic brain injury who remained comatose or in a UWS/vegetative state two weeks after cardiopulmonary arrest, neurologic and functional recovery with CPC 1–2 at 12 months was uncommon. Although delayed neurological improvement occurred in a small number of patients, most either died due to eventual withdrawal of life support or organ failure. Most of those that survived had severe disability or CPC 3–4.

In our study, the two-week time point was selected as the critical inclusion threshold for several reasons. At around this time, decisions regarding long-term care are usually considered, including whether to proceed with invasive procedures such as tracheostomy, percutaneous gastrostomy and chronic vascular access for ongoing life-sustaining treatments. At two weeks, neurologic exam and neuroprognostication are more reliable since sedative and neuromuscular blockade effects from TTM would have resolved, and other confounding factors — such as hypothermia, hypoglycemia, delirium, seizures, strokes, and metabolic derangements — would have been identified and appropriately managed. By this time, underlying medical conditions precipitating cardiopulmonary arrest would typically have been stabilized or deemed non-survivable.

Although the average age of patients that suffer cardiopulmonary arrest in the U.S. is 64 years for out-of-hospital arrests and 66–70 years for in-hospital arrests,1, 2 the average age in our cohort was only 52.9 years old. This likely reflects both improved short-term survival among younger patients and a greater tendency for surrogates to pursue life-sustaining care in younger individuals. Despite this relatively young cohort, only 3 out of 169 patients (1.8%) achieved functional recovery (CPC 1–2) by 12 months. These three patients with favorable outcomes were young (ages 29, 30, and 31 years), and had been functionally independent prior to arrest.

Although age may confer advantages since younger patients have greater neuroplasticity, higher baseline neurologic and physiologic reserve, our study suggests that age alone is insufficient to predict outcomes. An additional 17 patients in the cohort were 31 years of age or younger, yet none of these patients regained favorable functional status. Other unmeasured factors—such as time to CPR, time to ROSC, etiology of cardiopulmonary arrest, pre-existing comorbidities, secondary medical complications, socioeconomic factors and caregiver support—may have also contributed to outcome variability.16, 19, 20, 21 These findings suggest that although younger age may be a characteristic among the rare patients who recover, age alone is not sufficient to overcome the prognostic implications associated with persistent coma or UWS/vegetative state two weeks after cardiopulmonary arrest.

Several important limitations should be considered when interpreting our findings. First, this was a retrospective study and neuroprognostication was not standardized across participating hospitals. Although all patients received usual post-cardiac arrest care and underwent daily neurologic exams, other multimodal prognostication data such as (EEG), somatosensory evoked potentials (SSEP), brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), and serum biomarkers—were not uniformly available for analysis. Consequently, we were unable to compare patient outcomes within guideline-based prognostic categories.

Second, we were unable to adjust for potentially important clinical variables such as arrest etiology, duration of cardiopulmonary resuscitation, time to return of spontaneous circulation (ROSC), targeted temperature management exposure, or other factors that may influence neurological outcome. These variables likely contributed to outcome variability.

Third, our study outcome was vulnerable to the self-fulfilling prophecy effect, since forty-four of the sixty-seven deaths were due to withdrawal of life-sustaining treatment (WLST). Although we had excluded patients whose families elected to withdraw life support at or before the two-week inclusion point, this only reduced the potential influence of early prognostic pessimism. Subsequent WLST decisions were made due to expectations of poor recovery. Therefore, the observed mortality rate does not reflect solely the consequence of severe anoxic brain injury, but rather, the combined result of neurological injury, medical complications, and decisions regarding ongoing life-sustaining treatment.

Lastly, the CPC scale has recognized limitations, including inter-rater variability and potential misclassification, particularly in retrospective studies. To enhance accuracy, all records underwent repeated reviews and independent verification by additional investigators, although formal inter-rater reliability testing was not performed. Importantly, the principal finding of this study—that favorable functional recovery (CPC 1–2) is rare—is unlikely to be substantially affected by minor differences in CPC classification.

Despite the limitations inherent to retrospective observational studies, our findings suggest that most patients with severe anoxic brain injury who remain comatose or in a UWS/vegetative state at 2 weeks rarely achieve good functional recovery. While some studies in the literature have reported late awakening with good long-term recovery, other studies, similar to ours, report this as a rare event.22, 23, 24 To our knowledge, few prior studies have specifically focused on patients who remain comatose or in a UWS/vegetative state at two weeks after cardiac arrest, making this a clinically important subgroup for prognostic counseling and goals-of-care discussions.

Studies of long-term neurologic and functional outcome with severe anoxic brain injury may help improve prognostication in patients with indeterminate prognosis while better prediction models are still being evaluated.25, 26, 27, 28, 29, 30 Inaccurate prognostication may lead either to the withdrawal of life-sustaining treatment in patients who might otherwise achieve meaningful function, or conversely prolonged intensive interventions when there is limited likelihood of recovery.

This information is also relevant to health-care systems because we need to allocate resources to support these patients who may recover, in variable degrees, over the subsequent weeks and months. These patients have complex needs after hospital discharge, including ventilator support, tracheostomy tubes, feeding tubes, chronic vascular access, urinary catheters, and access to multidisciplinary teams (neurology, physical medicine and rehabilitation, palliative care, physical/occupational/speech therapy, psychiatry, psychology and social services). There will be need for additional inpatient rehabilitation, subacute and chronic care facilities post-hospitalization, and caregiver support for patients who remain debilitated. Anticipating these needs is important for resource planning and delivery of appropriate longitudinal care.

Conclusion

In this multicenter cohort of 169 patients with severe anoxic brain injury who remained comatose or in a UWS/vegetative state two weeks after cardiopulmonary arrest, meaningful neurological and functional recovery at 12 months was uncommon. Only 1.8% of patients achieved CPC 1–2; 39.6% died within the 12-month period, and the rest survived with severe disability. These data may provide additional context for neuroprognostication and support values-based decision-making regarding long-term care for patients with severe anoxic brain injury after cardiac arrest. Future prospective studies incorporating standardized multimodal neuroprognostication, arrest-specific clinical variables, and longer-term follow-up are needed to better define which patients may still achieve meaningful recovery despite severe anoxic brain injury.
