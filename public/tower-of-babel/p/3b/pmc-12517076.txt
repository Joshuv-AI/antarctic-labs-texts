Examining the role of social environment on COVID-19 infections and vaccine uptake among migrants in Japan: Findings from nationwide surveys in 2021 and 2023
Authors: Yuanyuan Teng, Clive E Sabel, Tomoya Hanibuchi, Tomoki Nakaya
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractStudies have shown that migrants and ethnic minority groups were disproportionately affected by the COVID-19 pandemic, yet the role of the social environment in shaping their vulnerabilities remains underexplored in Japan. This study explored the experiences of migrants in Japan during the COVID-19 pandemic and examined the association between social environmental factors (i.e., population density, neighborhood deprivation, ethnic density, and social networks) and both COVID-19 infections and vaccination uptake. Two nationwide online surveys were conducted in 2021 and 2023 to capture migrants' experiences and analyze these associations during the middle and waning stages of the pandemic. Modified Poisson regressions with robust standard errors were applied for the analysis. The findings revealed the complex and evolving influence of social environmental factors on infections and vaccination uptake as the pandemic progressed. Larger neighborhood ties with co-nationals were associated with a higher risk of infection in 2021, while neighborhood population density, neighborhood deprivation, and ethnic density showed no significant association with infection in both surveys. Regarding COVID-19 vaccination, more social contacts with Japanese natives were negatively associated with vaccine hesitancy in 2021 and incomplete vaccination in 2023. Additionally, neighborhood deprivation was positively associated with vaccine hesitancy and incomplete vaccination in 2021 before adjusting for other variables. In anticipation of future pandemics, customized programs should be developed to address the unique healthcare needs of migrants and tailored to different stages of the pandemic.Keywords: Social capital, Social determinants of health, Social networks, Neighborhood ties, Density, Immigrants

Introduction

The COVID-19 pandemic has had a profound, yet uneven, impact across global populations, with migrants and ethnic minority groups often facing particular vulnerability. Previous studies indicate that ethnic minorities have higher rates of infection, hospitalization, and even COVID-19-related mortality (Duong et al., 2023, Mackey et al., 2021, Magesh et al., 2021, Sze et al., 2020), yet relatively lower vaccine acceptance (Abba-Aji et al., 2022; Khubchandani and Macias, 2021). The disproportionate vulnerabilities among migrants and ethnic minorities stem from socioeconomic disparities, unequal exposure to high-risk occupations, greater pre-existing health challenges, reduced access to healthcare, and lower-quality neighborhood and housing conditions (Duong et al., 2023, Kjøllesdal et al., 2021, Magesh et al., 2021, Parolin and Lee, 2022). However, most of the evidence comes from Western countries, leaving COVID-19 related health outcomes and risk factors among ethnic minorities in Asian contexts, such as Japan, largely under-explored. Unlike nations with a long history of immigration, Japan has a small but growing foreign population, and its integration policies are limited (Solano and Huddleston, 2020). Consequently, the situation faced by migrants and ethnic minorities in Japan may have been more challenging than migrants’ experiences elsewhere.

The first confirmed COVID-19 case in Japan was detected on January 15, 2020. The first Statement of Emergency (SoE) was made on April 7, 2020. Although the SoEs were no mandatory lockdowns, the daily lives of citizens in Japan had largely been restricted. COVID-19 vaccination in Japan began on February 17, 2021, with a staged roll-out to healthcare workers, the elderly, and chronically ill patients, followed by the general population in mid-June 2021 (Kayano et al., 2023). The vaccines were provided free of charge to all residents, including foreigners living in Japan (Fujita et al., 2023). The COVID-19 related health status of ethnic minorities in Japan remains unclear as there are no official data on COVID-19 infection or vaccination rates by nationality or ethnicity. Limited evidence suggests that racial and ethnic minorities in Japan were at a greater risk of COVID-19 exposure (Nomoto et al., 2022).

Due to the less severe Omicron variant and the high rate of vaccination nationwide, the death rate declined since 2021. In early 2022, a new concept entitled “Living with COVID-19” was introduced by the Japanese government, promoting a lifestyle adapted to coexisting with the disease (Kamegai and Hayakawa, 2022). On May 8, 2023, Japan downgraded the legal category of COVID-19 to the same level as common infectious diseases, such as seasonal influenza, marking the beginning of the post-pandemic era. Although the WHO formally declared that the COVID-19 pandemic was over in March 2023, it is still important to understand the health-related situation and its risk factors among migrants in Japan during the COVID-19 pandemic, as new novel threatening viruses could appear anywhere at any time (Biancolella et al., 2023). This study aimed to examine the complex interactions between social factors and both infection and vaccination among foreign residents in Japan, thereby providing valuable insights to better prepare vulnerable groups for possible future global health emergencies.

During the COVID-19 pandemic, social environmental factors have been identified as significant contributors to ethnic health disparities (Finucane et al., 2022, Medcalfe and Slade, 2023, Vahidy et al., 2020, Yang et al., 2021). Living in high population density areas was linked to ethnic differences in COVID-19 infection rates during the early stages of the pandemic (Vahidy et al., 2020). Individuals in deprived neighborhoods faced higher COVID-19 risk and had lower vaccination rates, while ethnic minorities are more likely to live in such neighborhoods due to factors such as financial limitations and discrimination (K.C. et al., 2020; Perry et al., 2021). Studies have also shown that areas with higher ethnic densities faced greater risks from COVID-19 and experienced more limited access to vaccinations (Yang et al., 2021; Wu, 2022).

Furthermore, larger social networks might increase COVID-19 risk due to more frequent interactions, but could also enhance vaccination acceptance through prevailing social norms and effective peer pressure. However, this relationship among migrants and ethnic minorities may be unclear as their social networks are relatively limited and greatly impacted by the pandemic. Additionally, as people were less mobile and spent more time in their neighborhoods during the COVID-19 pandemic, social networks within neighborhoods may have had a greater impact on health-related situations than broader social networks.

As social factors can influence health both positively and negatively (Villalonga-Olives and Kawachi, 2017), this study considers the impact of social environment on both infection and vaccination simultaneously. Furthermore, the factors affecting COVID-19 risks may change as pandemics develop due to societal responses (e.g., implementation or lifting disease prevention measures, changes in public compliance), shifting of geographic spread of the virus, the emergence of milder variants, and the introduction of vaccines (Foster et al., 2024, Hoebel et al., 2021, Lundberg et al., 2023, Meurisse et al., 2022, Wu, 2022). Using data from two nationwide online surveys conducted in Japan in 2021 and 2023, this study examined the association of social environment (i.e., population density, neighborhood deprivation, ethnic density, and social networks) with COVID-19 infections and vaccination uptake among migrants in Japan during the middle and the waning stages of the pandemic.

Materials and methods

Data collection

Since no official data on COVID-19 infection rates and vaccine uptake by nationality or ethnicity is available, we conducted two cross-sectional online surveys among foreign national residents in Japan in 2021 and 2023 to understand their situation during the COVID-19 pandemic. Both surveys were carried out through a survey company named GMO Research, which has access to over 20 million residents in Japan, including >20,000 foreign national residents. All foreign registered panelists living in Japan, aged 20 years or older were invited to the survey. Given that online survey panelists tend to have higher levels of education and socioeconomic status (Jang and Vorderstrasse, 2019), and that registering as a panelist requires some knowledge of the local language, it is likely that the participants in such surveys possess higher socioeconomic status and better language proficiency compared to the average migrant. This tendency was also confirmed in our previous comparison of the 2021 survey participants (including both first- and second-generation migrants) with data from the census, official statistics, and a random sampling survey (Teng et al., 2023a).

The 2021 survey was conducted from October 5 to October 14, 2021, with questionnaires available in English, Japanese, and plain Japanese (Yasashii Nihongo, which uses simpler phrases and fewer Chinese characters). This survey yielded 1986 valid responses after data cleaning (see Teng et al. (2022) for further details regarding data cleaning). The 2023 survey was conducted from March 24 to 30, 2023, and received a total of 1063 valid responses. Several modifications were made to the design and implementation of the 2023 survey. First, the questionnaire was only available in English and Japanese. The plain Japanese questionnaire was omitted due to the small number of participants who used it in the 2021 survey and the difficulty of ensuring consistency. Instead, efforts were made to use simpler phrases in the Japanese questionnaire. Second, we limited the number of Taiwanese participants in the 2023 survey because Taiwanese participants in the 2021 survey were over-represented. Third, due to budget constraints, the number of participants in the 2023 survey was capped at 1100.

Additionally, since the structure and behavior of second-generation migrants in Japan differ significantly from those of the first generation and more closely resemble those of Japanese nationals (Teng et al., 2023), this study focuses on first-generation migrants and excluded second-generation migrants from the analysis in both surveys. Furthermore, participants who did not provide valid postal codes in the questionnaire as well as those where the number of households in the residential area was <25 were also excluded to preserve confidentiality. Consequently, the responses used in the analysis are 1351 and 645 participants from 48 and 33 countries and regions in the 2021 and 2023 survey, respectively.

To contextualize our sample within the broader foreign resident population in Japan, we compared its demographic characteristics with data from the Statistics on Foreign Residents in Japan (2021) (see Supplementary Table 1). Since our survey included only first-generation (foreign-born) immigrants, while the official statistics do not distinguish between first- and second-generation immigrants, these comparisons should be interpreted with caution.

The study design was approved by the ethics review board of the Center for Northeast Asian Studies of Tohoku University (CNEAS-ER2021-04; CNEAS-ER2022-03). Informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Measures

COVID-19 infection and vaccination

The dependent variable, self-reported COVID-19 infection, was derived from a question in both surveys asking whether the participant had ever been infected with COVID-19.

Another dependent variable is vaccine uptake; those who received two or more doses were defined as the fully vaccinated group, while the rest were defined as the incomplete vaccination group. Moreover, given the different progress of vaccination rollout in 2021 and 2023, besides vaccination status, we also included attitude—vaccine hesitancy—as the dependent variable in the 2021 survey. This decision was based on the fact that, at the time of the 2021 survey, the vaccination rollout for young adults had only recently begun in some regions, and many individuals who intended to be vaccinated had not yet had the opportunity to receive the vaccine. Therefore, to more accurately capture vaccination status during this transitional phase, we included both intention and actual vaccination in the analysis. Vaccine hesitancy is defined as a delay in acceptance or refusal of vaccination despite the availability of vaccination services (MacDonald, 2015). By March 2023, it is reasonable to assume that those who had the intention to be vaccinated would have been. However, in October 2021, many who had not been fully vaccinated were scheduled to be fully vaccinated as the vaccination campaign was still ongoing. Therefore, it is difficult to directly compare vaccination status. In the 2021 survey, the fully vaccinated group and those who were planning to be vaccinated were defined as the intent group. The remaining participants who indicated that they did not want to be vaccinated or had not yet decided were defined as the hesitant group.

Social environment

Social environment was defined to include population density, neighborhood deprivation, ethnic density and social networks. Population density, neighborhood deprivation and ethnic density were calculated using the data from Japan’s 2020 Population Census based on the postal code provided by the participants, while social networks were measured through questionnaires.

Population density was calculated based on the population and area of the postal code area in which the participant resides. Neighborhood deprivation was measured using Area Deprivation Index (ADI), which consists of a weighted sum of eight poverty-related variables (i.e., proportions of old couple households, old single households, single-mother households, rent houses, sales and service workers, agricultural workers, blue collar workers, and unemployment rate). The detailed calculation of the ADI is described in Nakaya et al. (2014). Higher ADI scores indicate higher levels of deprivation. Ethnic density was calculated by dividing the foreign population by the total population of their residential areas at the Oaza-level, a scale in the Population Census that is closest to neighborhood or postal code areas.

Social networks

Regarding social networks, we examined social ties within the neighborhood as well as general social contacts with Japanese.

We distinguished between social ties with co-national neighbors and Japanese neighbors, recognizing that different forms of social capital can have varying impacts on health (Ferlander, 2007). Social capital refers to the resources individuals accessed through their social connections, including networks of relationships, norms of reciprocity and trust. In a migratory context, social ties with co-nationals and locals are usually regarded as bonding and bridging social capital, respectively, based on the similarity of the network (Putnam, 2000, Putnam, 2007). Bonding social capital refers to connections to people who are like oneself, such as co-nationals, while bridging social capital refers to connections with people who are different, such as native residents to a migrant. To capture these different types of ties, we asked the participants to what extent they agreed with the statement that they know many Japanese/co-nationals in their neighborhood. In the 2021 survey, we did not specify the definition of “neighborhood” in the questionnaire; however, in the 2023 survey, we defined “neighborhood” as an aera within a 10- to 15-minute walk from home. Options for the 2021 survey included “true,” “somewhat true,” “somewhat untrue,” “untrue,” and “I don’t know”, while options for the 2023 survey used a five-point Likert scale ranging from true to untrue. Although the original response items were ordinal, for the purpose of analysis, we dichotomized the responses: participants who selected “true” or “somewhat true” as having social ties with Japanese or co-national neighbors, whereas the other responses were coded as not having such ties.

Social contacts with Japanese were measured using two items from the social dimension of integration in the Immigration Policy Lab Integration Index (IPL-12) (Harder et al., 2018), which also reflects one’s bridging social capital. The items were “before the outbreak of the COVID-19 pandemic (2021 survey)/ in the last 12 months (2023 survey), how often did you eat dinner with Japanese who are not part of your family?” and “with how many Japanese did you have a conversation - either by phone, messenger chat, or text exchange - in the last 4 weeks?”. Because interpersonal interactions changed tremendously during the pandemic, and the amount of pre-pandemic in person interactions with locals may be more reflective of the reality of social networks, we modified the item in the 2021 survey. Answers to each question were assigned a value from 1 to 5 and summed to create a composite score. Higher scores indicate more social contacts with Japanese. The Cronbach’s alpha values for the two items were 0.47 in the 2021 survey and 0.51 in the 2023 survey. While modest, these values are considered reasonable given that the measure includes only two items addressing distinct facets of social integration.

Control variables

In addition to the primary variables of interest, we include several control variables in the models to account for their potential influence. These variables are sex, age, marital status, educational level, work status, household income, length of stay in Japan, home country or region, and residential region. Residential regions of participants were divided into metropolitan and non-metropolitan areas. Metropolitan areas consisted of 11 regions, which are Saitama, Chiba, Tokyo, Kanagawa, Gifu, Aichi, Mie, Kyoto, Osaka, Hyogo, and Nara Prefectures. The rest of the regions were coded as non-metropolitan areas. Home country or region were coded into China, Taiwan, Korea, Brazil, Philippines, Vietnam, Western countries, and other.

Statistical analysis

We first calculated descriptive statistics for all variables in the two surveys. Following this, we performed modified Poisson regressions with robust standard errors to examine the association of social environmental factors with COVID-19 infections and vaccination uptake. We chose modified Poisson regression instead of logistic regression because it allows for direct estimation of relative risk, which is more interpretable than odds ratios, particularly when the outcome is common (incidence >10 %) (Zou, 2004). Additionally, modified Poisson regression has been recommended when comparing binary outcomes across different datasets, as odds ratios from logistic regression can be sensitive to the underlying distribution of the outcome. For comparison, we also included the results of logistic regression models in the supplementary materials (Supplementary Tables 2 and 3). For these models, we also provided the area under the ROC curve (AUC), sensitivity, and specificity as an additional measure of model fit (Supplementary Table 4).

We further applied a multilevel model that included random effects at the municipal level, because there is a possibility that there are differences in vaccination policies by municipality, but the random effects were not significant.

COVID-19 infection, COVID-19 vaccination status, and vaccine hesitancy were used as the dependent variables, and all were treated as binary variables. The independent variables were social environmental factors. The ADI was standardized in each survey, and the population density was log-transformed. Crude rate ratios and adjusted rate ratios with 95 % confidence intervals were calculated for each coefficient of the model. Statistical analyses were performed using Stata/SE 17.0.

In 2022, Japan's foreign population was nearly 3.1 million, accounting for 2.5 % of the total population. Most of the foreign population were from Asian countries (84.4 %), with the largest groups coming from China (24.8 %), Vietnam (15.9 %), South Korea (13.4 %), and the Philippines (9.7 %) (Immigration Services Agency of Japan, 2023). In the 2023 survey, we received a disproportionately large number of responses from Chinese participants (47.3 %).

In the 2021 survey, 5.6 % of participants indicated that they had been infected with COVID-19, and this number increased to 37.5 % in the 2023 survey. These percentages were substantially higher than the national infection rates in Japan, which were 1.4 % as of October 4, 2021, and 26.7 % as of March 23, 2023 (Sapporo Medical University School of Medicine, 2023).

Furthermore, in the 2021 survey, 66.7 % of the participants were fully vaccinated, and 21.8 % had received a first dose and planned to receive a second dose. In the 2023 survey, 81.4 % of the participants were fully vaccinated. The proportions of the participants fully vaccinated in our surveys were comparable to those of population in Japan, which were 69.4 % as of October 4, 2021, and 78.1 % as of March 23, 2023 (Sapporo Medical University School of Medicine, 2023).

In terms of social networks, participants reported more social ties in their neighborhoods in 2023 than in 2021, likely indicating increasing in social interactions as the pandemic evolved. Furthermore, because Japanese were the dominant ethnicity in most neighborhoods, participants had more social ties with their Japanese neighbors than with co-national neighbors in both surveys. Furthermore, the 2021 survey results, reflecting pre-pandemic conditions, showed higher scores for social connections with Japanese than the 2023 survey.

Table 2 presents the results from modified Poisson regression analyses examining the relationship between self-reported COVID-19 infection experience and various social environmental factors. In the 2021 survey, a statistically significant positive association was found between COVID-19 infection experience and social ties with co-national neighbors. Discussion

This study investigated the COVID-19 infection and vaccination situation among migrants in Japan and examined relationship between various social environment factors and COVID-19 infection and vaccination, comparing the differences between the middle and waning stages of the pandemic. We found that migrants in Japan were more vulnerable to COVID-19 infection but were more receptive to vaccination compared to Japanese nationals. Although the mortality differentials between foreign nationals and Japanese did not change significantly (Ghaznavi et al., 2022), higher infection rates among migrants could pose a risk to broader society.

A significant and negative association was found between population density and incomplete vaccination before controlling for other variables in the 2021 survey. This may be due to the regional inequalities in promotion of vaccinations at the early stage, as densely populated and metropolitan areas had more vaccination sites (e.g., mass vaccination sites set up in large commercial spaces) and better access to transportation. However, no significant association was found between population density and COVID-19 infection experience in both surveys. This result aligns with previous studies (Alidadi et al., 2023; Kontokosta et al., 2024), which found that population density in smaller geographic scale, such as neighborhood is not necessarily a determinant of COVID-19 infection. One possible explanation is that residents of high-density neighborhoods may feel more threatened by the virus and therefore take more precautions (Hamidi et al., 2020, Kontokosta et al., 2024). Furthermore, unlike ethnic minorities in the U.S., who often live in densely populated areas with limited ability to modify their mobility behavior (Kontokosta et al., 2024), highly skilled migrants in Japan are more likely to be concentrated in densely populated areas, both regionally and intra-regionally (Kirimura, 2013; Tong, 2019). This allows them more likely to work from home more than migrants in other areas, thus offsetting the risks associated with density. That is, the geographic structure of minority populations varies in different host societies, so the relationship between population density and the risk of infection for minorities will also vary.

Regarding neighborhood deprivation, significant positive relationships were found between neighborhood deprivation and both incomplete vaccination and vaccine hesitancy in the 2021 survey, before accounting for other variables. While the ADI was not a determinant of vaccination acceptance, efforts should be made to prevent delays in vaccination among migrants living in deprived neighborhoods, as they were more likely to have lower vaccination acceptance during the early stages of the vaccination campaign. Additionally, although the relationship between the ADI and COVID-19 infection was not statistically significant, it reversed between 2021 and 2023. Migrants living in deprived neighborhoods were more likely to be infected with COVID-19 in 2021, but less likely to be infected in 2023. This shift may be because, during the middle of the pandemic, migrants in less deprived areas had better housing conditions and a greater ability to work from home, reducing their likelihood of infection. Conversely, as the pandemic waned and milder variants emerged, society returned to normal. Migrants in less deprived areas, more involved in productive activities, may become more susceptible to infection. These results suggest that determinants of infection and vaccination may differ between the mid-pandemic and receding periods.

Concerning ethnic density, we found that ethnic density was negatively associated with incomplete vaccination in 2021. Ethnic communities and religious facilities, such as mosques, played important roles in the early stages of the COVID-19 vaccination rollout in Japan (Kotani et al., 2022; Tamura et al., 2022), providing better access to COVID-19 vaccination for those living in more concentrated areas where ethnic communities or religious facilities are often located. Additionally, no significant association was found between ethnic density and COVID-19 infection, unlike in the case of ethnic minorities in the U.S. (Yang et al., 2021). Previous studies have indicated that the relationship between ethnic density and the number of infections is stronger in highly segregated areas (Yang et al., 2021). In Japan, due to the small proportion of the migrant population, significant residential segregation and uneven ethnic-related geographic distribution of healthcare resources are not as prevalent as in the U.S., which may explain the differing results.

In terms of social networks, social ties with co-national neighbors were positively related to COVID-19 infection in 2021, but social contacts with Japanese were negatively related to vaccination hesitancy in 2021 and incomplete vaccination in 2023. These results demonstrate that different forms of social capital can have varying impacts on health-related conditions. Geographically proximate bonding social capital may increase infection risk. However, migrants who have more social contacts with Japanese, reflecting greater bridging social capital not confined to the neighborhood, were more likely to be vaccinated, a finding also noted in our previous study (Teng et al., 2022); this relationship remained evident in 2023. Regarding social ties with Japanese neighbors, no associations were found with either COVID-19 infection or vaccination in both surveys. This may be because we only asked about the size of their neighborhood ties, not their intensity. Immigrants and their Japanese neighbors may have known each other, but their interactions were not intensive. This may be because we only inquired about the size of their neighborhood ties, rather than their intensity. Migrants and their Japanese neighbors may have known each other, but their interactions were not intensive. As a result, these ties neither posed a risk of virus transmission through communication, as neighborhood ties with co-nationals did, nor did they have the same effect in promoting vaccination as general social contacts with Japanese.

Although our study addresses important gaps in the knowledge on social determinants of COVID-19 infection and vaccination among migrants during the pandemic, a few limitations should be noted. First, due to the unavailability of a sampling frame for migrants, we did not use probability sampling and instead implemented our surveys through a large online survey panelist network in Japan. This approach may have led to a selection bias and limited generalizability. Compared to other migrants, our participants generally had a certain level of Japanese language proficiency and relatively high socioeconomic status (Teng et al., 2023a). In particular, migrant workers employed in low-skilled occupations, especially in the manufacturing sector, were underrepresented in our study. These migrant workers were more likely to live in less densely populated areas, have more ties with co-national neighbors but fewer social contacts with Japanese, which may have made them more vulnerable in the COVID-19 pandemic.

In addition, methodological differences between the 2021 and 2023 surveys—such as variations in sample size, language options, and country distribution—may limit the direct comparability of findings across the two time points, despite efforts to account for these differences in the analysis. Additionally, although we examined a range of social environmental factors, only a limited number of adjusted associations reached statistical significance. To explore whether multicollinearity may have contributed to this, we conducted variance inflation factor (VIF) analyses for all covariates in both the 2021 and 2023 surveys. All VIF values were below 3, suggesting that multicollinearity was not a substantial issue. It is possible, however, that a larger or more heterogeneous sample would yield more statistically robust results. Future research with broader and more diverse samples may better capture the variation in health-related experiences and risks among foreign residents in Japan.

Furthermore, we examined the social environment factors based on the postal codes of participants’ current residences. Despite our efforts to align the spatial units of the variables of interest, their boundaries and sizes still differ. Additionally, as migrants are a highly mobile population, they were more likely to have changed their residence during the pandemic, which could affect the accuracy of our social environment assessments. Moreover, while we aimed to investigate changes in the effect of social factors over time, we did not ask about the specific timing of infection or vaccination. This limitation may make the effects observed in 2023 less clear, as some participants in the 2023 survey may have been infected with COVID-19 before 2021. However, given that number of COVID-19 cases in 2021 was relatively small compared to 2023 in Japan, it is likely that most participants infected with the COVID-19 between the 2021 and 2023 survey.

Lastly, we must acknowledge that different social norms might have existed between host and home countries for migrants. For recent migrants, science knowledge about infection and vaccine efficacy might well have been obtained from the home country not Japan (Gorman et al., 2020). This would have impacted decisions about vaccination and compliance with suggested protective measures.

Conclusion

While most existing studies on the health-related situations of ethnic minorities during the COVID-19 pandemic come from Western countries, our paper adds understanding of the situation among migrants in an Asian context. The effects of social environment factors on infection and vaccination among migrants in Japan may differ from those in Western countries due to differences in the geographic distribution of migrant population and health resources. The impact of social environment on infection and vaccination was complex and could change as the pandemic progressed. Our findings suggest that social environment factors played a more significant role in determining COVID-19 vaccination uptake than infection experiences and were more influential during the middle stage of the pandemic than in the waning stage. Therefore, measures to prevent infection or promote vaccination should be tailored to different stages of the pandemic. Additionally, sharing knowledge on virus prevention and protection with migrants who have extensive networks of co-national neighbors may effectively reduce their risk of infection. Importantly, we underscore the importance of expanding general social networks among migrants. While COVID-19 is a transmissible disease, our results indicate that general social networks were not associated with infection experiences but consistently promoted vaccination uptake.
