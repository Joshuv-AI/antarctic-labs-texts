Post-operative infection treatment in cardiac surgery: current practices and future directions
Authors: Rishab Jain, Shikha Yadav, Sarad Pawar Naik Bukke, Ananda Kumar Chettupalli, Chandrashekar Thalluri
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Surgical site infections (SSIs) are a major complication in surgical patients, particularly after cardiac surgeries, where the risk of postoperative infection ranges from 3.5% to 26.8%. Mediastinitis, severe concerns associated with open cardiac surgery, is linked to extreme deaths, increased medical expenses during hospitalization. We investigated the incidence and features of mediastinitis over a twenty-nine-year period as patient demographics and surgical indications evolved. Escherichia coli bloodstream infections (BSIs) contribute to significant mortality (5%–30%), with factors contributing to death remaining unclear, particularly with the rise of ESBL-producing organisms. Infective endocarditis (IE) is an infection that affects the cardiac endocardial layer, may cause valve vegetation, abscesses, and myopericarditis. Postoperative management requires a clinician with a deep understanding of cardiopulmonary function to address complications promptly. Infections of cardiac implanted electronic devices (CIEDs) are catastrophic, causing significant morbidity and mortality. Among CIED complications, infections drastically affect survival rates, require re-intervention, and lengthen hospital stays. Research shows a 1% infection rate within 12 months after CIED surgery. Acute Kidney Injury (AKI) is a common complication following major cardiac surgery, particularly in procedures involving cardiopulmonary bypass (on-pump). AKI significantly increases the risk of chronic kidney disease, cardiovascular complications, and mortality. Advanced age and pre-existing chronic kidney disease are recognized as key risk factors. Sepsis-induced cardiomyopathy (SICM), though primarily a general complication of severe sepsis, can also occur in post-cardiac surgery patients who develop sepsis as a secondary complication. The absence of standardized diagnostic criteria highlights an important knowledge gap and underscores the need for further research to improve recognition and management in this high-risk group.Graphical Abstract

Introduction

Post-operative infections remain one of the most serious complications in cardiac surgery, as they can lead to devastating consequences for both patients and healthcare systems. These infections most commonly include surgical site infections, pneumonia, bloodstream infections, and infective endocarditis. Despite significant advances in surgical techniques and perioperative care, post-operative infections continue to pose substantial challenges, contributing to increased morbidity, prolonged hospital stays, greater healthcare costs, and higher mortality. Understanding both current approaches to prevention and management, as well as future strategies to address these complications, is therefore critical to improving patient outcomes and advancing the field of cardiac surgery (Pettersson and Hussain 2019; Alghamdi et al. 2022). Considering impressive advancements in surgical procedures and perioperative care, infection scraps is significant contributor to getting sick or dying in cardiac surgery. Cardiac surgery-complex operations, usually involving prolonged procedures, device interventions, like prosthetic cardiac artery orpacemaker-are willingly targeted by infections (Hermanns et al. 2023). Cardiac surgery places patients with an elevated risk of post-operative bacteria because of a variety of reasons. They are usually associated with pre-existing diseases, such as diabetes, obesity, and chronic obstructive lung disease. All these factors hamper their immune system and increase susceptibility toward infection. The very nature of cardiac surgery connected with the use of prosthetic devices such as valves, pacemakers, and CABGs signifies a higher risk of infection. These devices may become colonized with bacteria leading to long-lasting or resistant infections. Infections in this case can involve not only the surgical site, like the heart valves and mediastinum but also vital structures like the bloodstream, with life-threatening consequences as well (Sivamani et al. 2023).Cardiac surgery is linked to a variety in post-operative infections, all of which influence patient results. Simple, mildly superficial infections can dramatically complicate the recovery process by leading to delayed wound healing and prolonged antibiotic treatment courses. Mediastinitis or endocarditis was linked to increased illness and death following cardiac surgery (Beiner et al. 2003). Post-operative complications may require different kinds of interventions, including re-operation or long-term antibiotics antibiotics, both of which may carry the risk of other complications. Severe infections can manifest as multi-organ failure or sepsis, a condition that can threaten life and, in such cases, one needs intensive care management. Thus, post-operative infections underline poorer overall prognoses, higher readmission rates, and elevated risk of long-term disability in cardiac surgery patients (Gómez-López et al. 2020).

The effect in after surgery infections in cardiac surgery patient sequel, morbidity, and mortality is profound and multifaceted. These infections occur after interventions like coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG), valve replacements, device implantations, pacemakers and defibrillators, thereby posing serious threats to recovery and long-term health. Post-operative infections increases the morbidity of the patient taking longer to recover from the cascade of health problems following surgery thus leading to longer hospital stays and prolonged recovery in several instances where a second operative procedure would be indicated (Zukowska et al. 2023). This increased morbidity adds to the burden of the patient, who may come to terms with a number of relapses and with periods of prolonged immobilization, antibiotic therapy, and hospitalizations, and these factors affect the person's quality of life. Moreover, rise of antibiotic-resistant pathogens complicates the management of these infections, making them more difficult to treat effectively (Serwecińska 2020). These infections are becoming a cause for concern and, therefore, a conscious effort is being made to prevent them more effectively with better perioperative protocols, early detection, and newer therapeutic approaches (Blot et al. 2022).

These are usually very hard to treat, given that they entail prosthetic materials, like valve replacements or cardiac implanted devices. Most often, device removal and subsequent re-implantation is the treatment course of choice for these complications, which further increases the complexity and risk of complications. More severe consequences also occur with infections like endocarditis, a virus that infects inner lining pertaining to the cardiac, where permanent damage the cardiac pump could result are more invasive procedures and a greater chance of long-term cardiac dysfunction (Baddour et al. 2024). Post-operative infections in cardiac surgery patients result in a significant mortality rate, primarily from severe forms of infection such as mediastinitis, BSIs, or endocarditis. As an instance, the Mediastinitis is a profound infection following cardiac surgery within the chest cavity; this may even result in mortality up to 15–30%, relying upon the initial patient conditions and the time taken for the initial treatment (Alghamdi et al. 2022). Bloodstream infections, especially those attributable to antibiotic resistance organisms, include methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA), can rapidly become sepsis, a potentially fatal condition leading to multifocal failure and death. Infection can occur in the heart valves, and endocarditis leads to valve destruction, heart failure, systemic emboli, thereby increasing the mortality further (Wu et al. 2020).

Post-operative infections prolong morbidity and mortality risks and also have serious psychological and social impacts on patients, like stress due to repeated hospitalization, fear of recurrence, or extended time for recovery (Jindani et al. 1993). Anxiety, depression, and reduced sense of well-being are the typical results of such fears. Patients who have serious post-operative infections may never regain their former level of physical functioning or return to work. All this can bring about significant financial and social burdens. Economically, the post-operative infections in cardiac surgery patients are a heavy burden: lengthened hospital stays, treatment above and beyond that necessary to treat the underlying illness, and more intense care are the greatest cost factors (Lustberg et al. 2023). These infections need an extended duration for recovery, and that means the patient will be in the hospital for much longer periods than initially thought to, resulting in increased health care expenses. The more days that are spent in the hospital because of an infection, the more charges related to room, nursing care, diagnostic test, and medical supplies increase. This not only stretches healthcare resources but also puts attention to other patients'side lines, hence probably reducing the overall efficiencies of hospitals. Intensive after-treatment in case of a post-operative infection is one of the major cost drivers. Such treatment requires intensive antibiotic therapy, which may be costly, especially when there is a need to extend treatment for a prolonged period. Additionally, using a wide-range or combination antibiotics might be required time fighting contaminants, including methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus, significantly adding the cost of care. Moreover, with patients like those suffering from deep infections, such as mediastinitis or endocarditis, treatment usually involves long-term intravenous antibiotic therapy; this means either a longer hospital stay or use of home healthcare services, which increases the total cost (Vena et al. 2023).

Post-operative infection also leads to more extensive utilization of diagnostic and monitoring tools. Once an infection is suspected, patients are later exposed to a lot of tests, ranging from blood cultures to imaging studies like CT scans or MRIs, repeated laboratory work that would closely monitor the infection. These diagnostic procedures may be used to observe which pathogen is causing an infection, how severe the infection is, and what kind of treatment would be ideal for the patient (Andersen 2019). The cumulative costs of these tests can be substantial, especially if the patients have prosthetic device infections complicated by the location risk of disease transmission to internal composition like valves in heart. When the infections involve implanted devices, including pacemakers, ICDs, or prosthetic valves, there may be a requirement for secondary surgery to remove and replace infected devices and, accordingly, greatly increase the expenses of care. Furthermore, prolonged hospitalization due to infections may be a domino effect of additional medical requirements, such as increased utilization of ICU resources for sepsis or other severe systemic infections patients. Intensive care management necessitates specialized personnel, technologically advanced life support equipment, and increased pharmaceutical use-all very costly within the economy. Stays in the intensive care unit are especially expensive because monitoring goes on 24 h a day and the level of care is very high in intensity (Boriani et al. 2021). Besides the direct costs of care, infections also impose indirect economic burdens through their long-term complications: on-going medical management, frequent follow-up visits, and rehabilitation-all contribute to the overall cost of healthcare. Moreover, readmissions after post-operative infections further contribute to rising costs. The healthcare systems may also penalize hospitals economically for having high rates of hospital-acquired infections or readmissions associated with preventable complications, thereby adding another dimension to this economic burden (Gidey et al. 2023).

This paper will be a comprehensive review in light of the present methods for dealing with postoperative infections in cardiology. This will include the epidemiology, risk factors, established and emerging therapies in the light of multidisciplinary management in achieving optimal patient care. Only with a better understanding of the intricacies and complexity of postoperative infections in cardiac surgery patients can healthcare professionals appropriate the best strategies in reducing their occurrence and improving patient outcomes (Zukowska et al. 2023).

Pneumonia

Patients undergoing cardiac surgery often experience pulmonary problems, such as pneumonia, pulmonary embolism, prolonged mechanical ventilation (more than 24 h), and drainage of pleural fluid, all of which should be reported. Extended time spent in the hospital is a common consequence of pulmonary complications subsequent to cardiac surgery (Hubers et al. 2020). Infections of the urinary tract

Urinary tract infections (UTIs) represent a frequent post-operative complication in hospitalized patients, including those undergoing cardiac surgery (Johnson et al. 2005). They are often associated with indwelling urinary catheters, prolonged hospitalization, and pre-existing comorbidities. The most common pathogens are Enterobacteriaceae such as Escherichia coli. Clinical presentations can range from asymptomatic bacteriuria to symptomatic lower or upper urinary tract infections. Although generally less severe than bloodstream or mediastinal infections, UTIs contribute to increased morbidity, delayed recovery, and higher healthcare costs. Preventive strategies such as catheter care, early removal of urinary catheters, and appropriate antibiotic therapy remain central to reducing their burden (Rosner and Okusa 2006).

In addition to infectious complications, renal dysfunction is also common after cardiac surgery, with acute kidney injury (AKI) being one of the most clinically significant. AKI occurs in up to 30% of patients following cardiac surgery, particularly in procedures involving cardiopulmonary bypass (Thakar et al. 2007). Severe AKI requiring renal replacement therapy is less frequent (2–6%) but is associated with a hospital mortality rate as high as 50%. Diagnosis is based on KDIGO criteria, which use both serum creatinine and urine output (Hobson et al. 2009). However, perioperative hemodilution can mask rises in creatinine, delaying detection (Lassnigg et al. 2008). Urine output criteria improve sensitivity, although the prognostic significance of isolated oliguria in cardiac surgery patients remains controversial (Lagny et al. 2015). This underscores the complex challenges in accurately diagnosing and managing AKI in this population.

Sepsis

Sepsis is a life-threatening condition that results from the body’s dysregulated immune response to infection, leading to organ dysfunction. Despite advances in intensive care management, sepsis remains a leading cause of mortality in critically ill patients and is one of the most common non-cardiac diagnoses encountered in cardiac intensive care units (Singer et al. 2016). In the setting of cardiac surgery, sepsis may arise from post-operative infections such as pneumonia, bloodstream infections, mediastinitis, or surgical site infections. Patients undergoing cardiopulmonary bypass are particularly vulnerable, as bypass induces systemic inflammation, immune suppression, and tissue hypoperfusion, which can predispose to infectious complications. Other contributing factors include prolonged mechanical ventilation, invasive devices, and impaired wound healing in high-risk patients (Beesley et al. 2018).

An important cardiac complication of sepsis is sepsis-induced cardiomyopathy (SICM), a transient but significant form of myocardial dysfunction. SICM is increasingly recognized in the post-operative setting, where it may exacerbate hemodynamic instability in already vulnerable surgical patients (Vincent et al. 2014). While SICM lacks standardized diagnostic criteria, common features include reversible left ventricular dysfunction, reduced ejection fraction, and ventricular dilatation (Holland and Moss 2017). Its diagnosis is challenging, as it requires clinical suspicion and echocardiographic confirmation during an episode of sepsis. The increased mortality associated with sepsis introduces bias in recognition and may hinder clinical trials and outcome studies (Rao 2015).

Post-operative complications and emergency events

Post-operative cardiac surgery patients are at risk of acute complications that may necessitate urgent intervention. Arrhythmias, including atrial fibrillation and ventricular tachyarrhythmias, are among the most frequent causes of emergency management in the immediate postoperative period. Additionally, unforeseen events such as sudden hemodynamic collapse, septic shock, or acute respiratory failure can escalate into medical emergencies, requiring rapid multidisciplinary response to stabilize the patient and prevent mortality (Zakkar et al. 2015; Greenberg et al. 2017).

Epidemiology and risk factors

Surgical site infection

The incidence of post-cardiac surgery infections varies widely depending on procedure type, patient demographics, infection definitions, and institutional surveillance practices (Surat et al. 2021); Lepelletier et al. 2013); Robichet al. 2015). Reported rates of surgical site infections (SSIs) range from 0.5% to 7.8%, with superficial SSIs occurring in 0.5–8% and deep infections in 0.5–5.6% of cases. Large cardiac surgical centers in Europe and the United States report deep sternal wound infection (DSWI) incidence rates of 1–2% annually. Post-sternotomy mediastinitis continues to affect 1–5% of patients, with mortality ranging between 3 and 20%, and reaching up to 50% in severe cases (Rehman et al. 2014).

In a prospective observational study by Celik et al. (2014), 147 patients undergoing various cardiac procedures were evaluated, of whom 44 (29.9%) developed infections. Risk factors included smoking, renal failure, diabetes mellitus, hypertension, and prolonged ICU stay. Other reports note specific infection distributions: urinary tract infections (1.4%), deep wound infections (1.7%), bloodstream infections (2.8%), and surgical site infections (5.7%). Contaminated TEE probes have also been implicated in post-operative pneumonia, with infection rates ranging from 2.4% to 10.7%.

Hyperglycemia has also been identified as an important modifiable risk factor. Perioperative glucose control using intravenous insulin infusion is recommended, with major cardiac surgery societies (STS, AATS, EACTS) advocating glucose targets below 180 mg/dL, while others (SECTCV, SEICAV) recommend 110–180 mg/dL (Hysi et al. 2014; Talbot 2005). Several validated models, such as the BHIS, ACRI, IRIC, Friedman score, and the Alfred Hospital risk index, can stratify patients into low-, medium-, or high-risk categories for mediastinitis and other infections (Bustamante-Munguira et al. 2019).

Mediastinits

Post-sternotomy mediastinitis is most caused by Staphylococcus species, particularly Staphylococcus aureus (both methicillin-sensitive and methicillin-resistant) and coagulase-negative staphylococci (Prado–Calleros et al. 2016). Gram-negative organisms are also frequently implicated, including Enterobacteriaceae and non-fermenting Gram-negative bacilli such as Pseudomonas aeruginosa and Acinetobacter baumannii. Fungal pathogens are rare but have been reported in selected series (Charbonneau et al. 2014).

Mono-microbial infections predominate. In a large series of 309 patients admitted to intensive care with post-cardiac surgery mediastinitis, 262 cases (84.7%) were due to a single pathogen. The most frequent isolates were methicillin-susceptible S. aureus (31.9%), followed by Enterobacteriaceae, coagulase-negative staphylococci, streptococci, and methicillin-resistant S. aureus (Norder Grusell et al. 2013). In a Chinese cohort, Gram-negative bacteria accounted for 55.4% of isolates, while S. aureus comprised 20.4% (methicillin-susceptible) and 5.7% (methicillin-resistant), and coagulase-negative staphylococci 14%. Fungal pathogens accounted for 3.8% of cases.

Antimicrobial resistance is an increasing concern. High rates of resistance have been documented among Gram-negative isolates, including 100% resistance of A. baumannii to ceftazidime, 22.5% to piperacillin–tazobactam, and 17.5% to carbapenems. In Brazil, among 92 patients with post-operative mediastinitis, 35.8% of Enterobacteriaceae were carbapenem-resistant, and nearly half of those were also resistant to colistin (Laulajainen-Hongisto et al. 2017). Such findings highlight the influence of local microbiological ecology and institutional antibiotic stewardship policies on the spectrum of causative organisms and their resistance profiles (Sielaff et al. 1996).

Factors that determine the microbiology of oesophageal-perforation–related mediastinitis (a condition distinct from post-sternotomy mediastinitis after cardiac surgery) include perforation site, baseline health status, acid suppression, enteral feeding, degree of immunosuppression, and recent antibiotic exposure. In untreated adults, oesophageal flora mirror oropharyngeal communities (e.g., viridans group streptococci such as S. salivarius, S. mutans, S. mitis, S. sanguinis, and S. anginosus; Neisseria spp.; Haemophilus spp.; and anaerobes such as Prevotella and Fusobacterium). In critically ill, recently antibiotic-exposed patients, flora shift toward aerobic Gram-negative organisms and Candida spp. (Pastene et al. 2016; Gunaratne et al. 2018). We include EPM here for differential diagnosis; our primary focus remains post-sternotomy mediastinitis following cardiac surgery.

Blood stream infections

The incidence of bloodstream infections (BSIs) following cardiac surgery ranges between 1 and 5%, varying with patient populations and definitions applied (Sandner et al. 2022). Higher rates are reported in complex procedures such as valve replacement and coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG), and in patients with comorbidities including diabetes mellitus and renal insufficiency. Prolonged intensive care unit (ICU) stay and extended mechanical ventilation are also major contributors to risk.

The microbiological spectrum of BSIs reflects the nosocomial environment. Gram-positive organisms are most common, particularly Staphylococcus aureus (both methicillin-sensitive and methicillin-resistant) and coagulase-negative staphylococci, often linked to skin colonization or catheter-related infection (Hadaya et al. 2022). Gram-negative bacteria such as Klebsiella, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, and Escherichia coli are also implicated, especially in secondary BSIs and in patients with prolonged ICU stays (Nuru et al. 2019). Candida species are occasionally isolated, particularly in immunocompromised or broad-spectrum antibiotic–treated patients (Pasquina et al. 2004).

Several patient- and procedure-related risk factors increase BSI susceptibility. These include prolonged cardiopulmonary bypass duration, multiple transfusions, and immune modulation from transfused blood products (Johnson et al. 2006). Comorbid conditions such as diabetes, chronic kidney disease, and COPD contribute to impaired host defenses and delayed wound healing, thereby elevating infection risk (Brun-Buisson et al. 1987). Patients with pre-existing infections (e.g., pneumonia, urinary tract infection) or receiving corticosteroids/immunosuppressants—particularly in the transplant setting—are also highly vulnerable (Abodakpi et al. 2019). Advanced age and poor nutritional status further predispose patients to postoperative BSIs due to immune senescence and impaired recovery capacity (Clermont et al. 2017).

Endocarditis

The epidemiology of infective endocarditis (IE) has shifted over recent decades. The global incidence has increased from 5–7 cases per 100,000 person-years in the 1970s–2000 to approximately 15 cases per 100,000 by 2011 (Hryniewiecki et al. 2019). In typical Western populations, IE occurs more frequently in men than women, with a median age of 67 years. While predominantly affecting older individuals in developed countries, it also impacts younger patients, particularly those with intravenous drug use (IVDU) or from disadvantaged socioeconomic backgrounds. Hospitalisations for IVDU-associated IE rose significantly between 2000 and 2013, particularly among individuals aged 15–34 years. In developing regions, rheumatic heart disease remains a major risk factor, whereas its role in developed countries has continued to decline (Pérez-Vázquez et al. 2000); Tleyjeh et al. 2005).

The microbial spectrum demonstrates regional variation. Staphylococcus aureus remains the predominant pathogen globally, accounting for approximately 31% of cases, followed by viridans group streptococci (17%) and enterococci (Wurcel et al. 2016). Streptococcus gallolyticus (bovis) is more frequent in Europe, while HACEK organisms are relatively uncommon in North America. In South America, viridans streptococci represent 26% of cases compared with 9% in North America, reflecting the higher prevalence of rheumatic heart disease (Bevan et al. 2017).

Risk factors for IE in the cardiac surgery population include the presence of prosthetic valves, prior valve surgery, and intracardiac devices (pacemaker leads, intra-aortic balloon pumps, central venous catheters), which can act as entry points for pathogens (Vilacosta et al. 2002). Prolonged device use, suboptimal insertion technique, or inadequate aseptic handling increases this risk. Additional factors include inadequate perioperative antibiotic prophylaxis—such as incorrect dosing, timing, or antibiotic selection—which may permit colonization by resistant organisms like MRSA, complicating both prevention and treatment (Duval 2010). Comorbidities such as diabetes, renal insufficiency, and immunosuppression further predispose patients to postoperative IE.

Pneumonia

Postoperative pneumonia remains a significant complication following cardiac surgery, with incidence rates varying between 3 and 6% depending on population characteristics and diagnostic definitions. Risk factors consistently identified across large multicenter cohorts include advanced age, chronic lung disease, tobacco use, diabetes mellitus, reduced hematocrit, renal dysfunction, and impaired cardiac function (Canbaz et al. 2004; Ng et al. 2002). In a prospective study of 16,084 patients across 33 sites, Strobel et al. reported a pneumonia incidence of 3.3% following CABG and developed a predictive model incorporating 17 preoperative factors (C-statistic: 0.74). Similarly, Kilic et al. established and validated a 33-point risk score using three key predictors: age ≥ 65 years, chronic Lung disease, and peripheral vascular disease, reporting an overall pneumonia rate of 4.5%. In another study involving 17,143 patients across six states, Kinlin and colleagues identified smoking, COPD, and elevated serum creatinine as significant predictors of post-CABG pneumonia, achieving robust predictive accuracy (C-statistics 0.78 and 0.75). Hortal et al. demonstrated an overall pneumonia rate of 5.7% in 1,844 patients, rising dramatically to 45.9% in those requiring prolonged mechanical ventilation beyond 48 h (Vlaar et al. 2011).

These findings highlight that both patient-level factors (age, comorbidities, smoking) and procedural elements (ventilation duration, renal function, hematologic status) play central roles in shaping pneumonia risk after cardiac surgery. The identification and stratification of high-risk patients is critical for implementing targeted preventive strategies in the perioperative and ICU setting.

Device-related infections

Device-related infections (DRIs) constitute a major source of morbidity following cardiac interventions. In the context of cardiac surgery, prosthetic valve endocarditis and infections related to ventricular assist devices (VADs) are among the most clinically significant, often requiring complex surgical revision in addition to prolonged antimicrobial therapy. Distinct from these are infections associated with cardiac implantable electronic devices (CIEDs), such as pacemakers and implantable cardioverter-defibrillators (ICDs), which are generally implanted by electrophysiologists through interventional procedures rather than open-cardiac surgery. Urinary tract infections

Typical symptoms encompass a persistent and intense urge to urinate, even when the bladder is practically devoid of urine, coupled by a burning or painful sensation during micturition (dysuria). Patients may suffer discomfort in the lower abdomen or pelvis, and urine may present as hazy, malodorous, or occasionally blood-tinged (haematuria). In more severe instances, fever, chills, and malaise may manifest, suggesting that the infection has potentially disseminated from the bladder to the kidneys (pyelonephritis) (O’Neal et al. 2016).

Individuals which had recent cardiac surgery, particularly those with indwelling urinary catheters, face a heightened risk of CAUTIs. In some individual, symptoms may be less apparent due to the catheter; however, fever, flank pain, or an inexplicable elevation in heart rate may indicate infection. Sepsis

Sepsis is a serious complication after cardiac surgery, with reported incidence rates ranging from 5 to 15%, depending on patient demographics, procedure type, and institutional infection control practices (Huang et al. 2013; Sato et al. 2016). Among these cases, sepsis-induced cardiomyopathy (SICM) occurs in approximately 10–70% of septic patients, contributing to increased morbidity and mortality (Vallabhajosyula et al. 2017).

Current and future directions

Advancements in data science, particularly AI and machine learning, are beginning to reshape how we assess infection risk in cardiac surgery. These tools may help identify patterns and risk factors that traditional models overlook. However, their use in real-world clinical settings remains limited, and concerns around data privacy, algorithm bias, and regulatory oversight must be carefully addressed. Future developments should focus not just on prediction, but also on guiding interventions and improving bedside decision-making. Additionally, research into novel antimicrobial coatings, rapid diagnostics, and strategies to reduce device-related infections continues to grow. Despite this progress, many approaches remain in early development and require further validation through well-designed clinical trials. Moving forward, a balance between innovation and practical application will be necessary to ensure these technologies translate into improved patient care.

Limitations of the study

While this review offers a comprehensive overview of post-operative infections in cardiology, there are several limitations worth noting. Much of the data are drawn from retrospective studies, which may be prone to selection bias. In addition, variability in diagnostic definitions, local infection control practices, and patient populations across different studies may influence outcomes and limit generalizability. Many of the cited infection rates and management strategies also come from high-resource settings, which may not reflect conditions in lower-resource environments. Furthermore, while emerging technologies like AI and machine learning hold promise, their clinical utility remains largely theoretical at this stage, and evidence from real-world application is still limited. Finally, the literature on fungal and device-related infections is relatively sparse, leaving certain areas underexplored. These gaps underscore the need for more standardized, multicenter, and prospective studies to validate current findings and guide future practice.

Conclusion

Post-operative infections following cardiac surgery continue to represent a substantial clinical and public health concern, despite remarkable progress in surgical techniques, perioperative management, and critical care. These infections, which include surgical site infections (SSIs), mediastinitis, ventilator-associated pneumonia (VAP), bloodstream infections (BSIs), catheter-associated urinary tract infections (CAUTIs), and device-related infections (DRIs), significantly contribute to morbidity, mortality, and prolonged hospitalisation. The incidence of these infections varies across centres and patient populations, but their impact is universally profound, often leading to increased healthcare costs, delayed recovery, and long-term complications.

The pathogenesis of post-operative infections in cardiac surgery is multifactorial. Patient-related factors such as advanced age, diabetes mellitus, renal dysfunction, malnutrition, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), and immunosuppression predispose individuals to higher infection risk. Surgical factors—including prolonged operative time, use of cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB), extended mechanical ventilation, and need for indwelling catheters or devices—further compound this vulnerability. Importantly, the emergence of multidrug-resistant organisms (MDROs), including methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA), vancomycin-resistant enterococci (VRE), and resistant Gram-negative bacilli such as Acinetobacter baumannii and Pseudomonas aeruginosa, has created additional therapeutic challenges, limiting the efficacy of conventional prophylaxis and treatment regimens.

Clinically, the spectrum of post-operative infections ranges from superficial wound infections to fulminant sepsis and septic shock, with complications such as sepsis-induced cardiomyopathy (SICM), prosthetic valve endocarditis, and disseminated infections that carry exceptionally high mortality. Early recognition and prompt management are critical to improving outcomes. Conventional diagnostic modalities—clinical examination, microbiological cultures, and imaging—remain central; however, newer technologies such as positron emission tomography (PET), cardiac CT, and advanced biomarkers are increasingly being utilised to enhance diagnostic accuracy, especially in prosthetic valve and device-related infections where conventional criteria may be inadequate.

The pre- and post-operative treatment of patients undergoing cardiac procedures is best managed by specialised multidisciplinary teams due to the inherent risks of the surgery. Furthermore, given their tight anatomical and functional interrelationship, the pulmonary system requires particular attention and support during the acute stress of cardiac surgery, as compromised respiratory function often predisposes to postoperative pneumonia and ventilator-associated complications.

From a preventive perspective, stringent adherence to aseptic surgical techniques, perioperative glycaemic control, optimisation of nutritional status, and judicious use of invasive devices are vital. Perioperative antibiotic prophylaxis remains a cornerstone, but its effectiveness depends on correct selection, dosing, and timing. Current international guidelines advocate tailored prophylactic strategies based on institutional epidemiology and resistance patterns. Additionally, multidisciplinary infection prevention programs—including surveillance, antimicrobial stewardship, and implementation of care bundles in intensive care units—have demonstrated significant reductions in infection rates.

Looking forward, the future of infection prevention and treatment in cardiac surgery lies in precision and personalisation. Risk stratification models such as the Northern New England Cardiovascular Disease Study Group prediction criteria, Friedman score, and other validated indices enable clinicians to identify high-risk individuals and implement targeted interventions. Advances in immunomodulatory therapies, biofilm-disrupting agents, and next-generation antimicrobials hold promise for addressing resistant pathogens. Furthermore, the integration of machine learning and artificial intelligence (AI) into perioperative care may improve risk prediction, early detection, and real-time infection control strategies.
