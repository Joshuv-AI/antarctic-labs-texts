What might Confucius say? Digital doppelgängers for people who are newly dead, dying, or comatose
Authors: Nancy S Jecker, Jing-Bao Nie, Anita Ho
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis paper explores what Confucius might say about the use of generative AI-based digital doppelgängers in healthcare for patients who are newly dead, dying, or comatose. We focus on the use of these technologies to support patients’ family members, loved ones, and surrogate decision-makers. Since Confucian approaches are largely missing from bioethics discussions of AI-based digital doppelgängers and patient preference predictors, this paper helps to fill an important gap. Section I introduces three cases involving digital doppelgängers representing people who are newly dead, dying, or comatose. Section II considers a Confucian response to the three cases. Section III applies Confucian values to a further situation, where the digital doppelgänger for a comatose patient functions as a patient preference predictor helping with a surrogate decision for a comatose patient. Section IV sets forth ethical benchmarks for using digital doppelgängers in healthcare. Section V recommends proceeding with caution and highlights the importance of further understanding the benefits and risks of using these tools in diverse cultural contexts.Keywords: Confucian ethics, death and dying, digital doppelgängers, generative AI, surrogate decision-making, deathbots, artificial intelligence (AI), patient preference predictor (P3)

Introduction

With the advent of artificial intelligence (AI), discussions about death and dying have taken on a new twist: the possibility of digitally recreating deceased loved ones to enable continued interaction with a lifelike representation or “digital doppelgänger” (also known as “virtual deceased person”, “digital clone”, “AI avatar”, “digital replica”, “deathbot”, “thanabot”, “digital ghost,” and “deep fake” of the dead). AI-powered virtual representations of deceased people that utilise large multi-modal models (LMMs) can process and generate multiple modalities of data simultaneously, including text, images, audio, and video. Using content such as social media posts, video clips, texts, emails, and photos, they mimic a deceased person’s appearance, voice, personality, and behaviour. LMM-based models may serve multiple purposes: leaving a legacy, helping survivors grieve, sharing wisdom or values, continuing relationships with those we care about, or enabling a kind of “digital immortality” by extending an individual’s existence after death in ways that matter. LMM-based models rely on generative AI to hold conversations with and function as companions for those who are grieving their loved one’s incapacity, dying, or death. They can be accessed on mobile devices, or join virtual meetings via apps like Zoom, Teams, or Meet. After death, they can project a life narrative into the future to create “a simulacrum (a multimodal double) that replicates the form of a lived identity without its lived reality” (Sabre & Meads, 2025, p. 1).

A second possible application of digital doppelgängers is to function as patient preference predictors (P3s) while a patient is still alive but lacks decisional capacity. P3s that utilise LMMs can enable an incapacitated patient to be present through a life-like virtual representation that can engage in real-time conversations with family members, loved ones, and members of the healthcare team, potentially doing far more than large language model (LLM)-based P3s to help family members understand patient preferences, provide emotional support to loved ones, and alleviate moral distress. Given widespread use of surrogates among patients who are older, critically ill, or diagnosed with dementia or delirium, both more basic P3s and LMM-based digital doppelgängers could rapidly scale-up (Devnani et al., 2017).

Other types of P3s include text-based models that do not utilise generative AI (Rid & Wendler, 2010, 2014a, 2014b), and text-based models that utilise generative AI and are trained on different datasets, such as publicly available demographic data (basic model), electronic health records (EHRs) (clinical model), or unique information about patients’ personalities (personalised model) (Earp et al., 2024; Starke et al., 2025). While text-based models can enhance goal-concordant care by more accurately predicting patient preferences (Starke et al., 2025), they may provide less emotional support to surrogate decisionmakers and clinicians compared to LMM-based digital doppelgängers.

This paper applies a Confucian lens to LMM-based digital doppelgängers and considers their application in healthcare for patients who are newly dead, dying, or comatose. Confucianism offers uniquely valuable insights to discussions about digital doppelgängers by providing a vision of human beings as relational, and stressing the ethical significance of family relationships, including relationships with the deceased. As this paper will demonstrate, despite the brand-new nature of digital doppelgängers as a technology, the virtues and ethical values expressed and embedded in people’s choices and responses today are thousands of years old. With a few notable exceptions (Cheng, 2025; Elder, 2019; Huang & Cong, 2025; Sparrow & Zhang, 2025; Wang, 2025), Confucian approaches have been largely missing from bioethics debates about AI and digital doppelgängers. Confucian analyses are also not included in emerging discussions about P3s. This essay helps to fill these gaps. Section I introduces three cases involving digital doppelgängers representing individuals who are newly dead, dying, or comatose. Section II considers a Confucian response to the three cases. Section III applies Confucian values to a further situation, where the digital doppelgänger for the comatose patient functions as a P3, assisting a surrogate decision-maker with a decision about withdrawing a ventilator. Section IV sets forth ethical benchmarks for using digital doppelgängers in healthcare. Section V concludes that more research is needed to better understand the benefits and risks of these tools in different cultural settings for different types of patients over time.

I. Three cases: digital doppelgängers for people who are newly dead, dying, and comatose

For thousands of years Chinese people have travelled to the graves of ancestors to pay respect to the dead. Holidays such as the annual tomb-sweeping festival, attest to this. Digital doppelgängers offer a new way of not only remembering but also “resurrecting” the dead: for as little as 20 yuan, Chinese people can create a digital replica of their loved one (Hawkins, 2024). Well known cases like the Taiwanese singer, Bao Xiaobai, show what is possible.

Case 1: Bao Rong’s Digital Doppelgänger 1

Bao Rong died in December 2021 at the age of twenty-two due to a rare blood disease (aplastic anaemia). Her father, the well-known Taiwanese singer, Bao Xiaobai, reportedly struggled with the loss of his young daughter. He decided to pursue a Ph.D. in a technology-related field with the hope of using generative AI to recreate his daughter’s voice and image: “I want to give her the most perfect image, the most perfect voice, allowing her to continue existing in the beautiful digital world” (Long, 2024). In January 2024, Xiaobai created a digital doppelgänger of his daughter singing happy birthday to her mother on her mother’s birthday, and posted it online. He has stated that for him, “AI is a tool for expressing yearning, a way to express missing someone” (Long, 2024).

The online posting of Bao Rong’s digital doppelgänger was impactful across East Asia, prompting people to imagine that in the future, tombstones might display their owners’ image and voice so that “those who pay visits to the deceased can have a chat with the AI version, which will have the memories of the deceased and his or her personality” (Long, 2024). Digitisation is already impacting traditional ancestor veneration practices, which originally featured portraiture (Stuart and Rawski, 2001) and later, photographs. Today, some Chinese shrines feature digital photos of deceased loved ones that allow multiple images to be displayed in succession (Sparrow & Zhang, 2025). Digital doppelgängers have the potential to make ancestor veneration more interactive and engaging.

Two further cases, inspired by the 2024 South Korean film, Wonderland, illustrate additional potential uses of digital doppelgängers (Tae-Young, 2024). Given uncertainty about how this technology will unfold in the future, fictional cases provide a valuable resource for “testing” moral decisions and their consequences. A South Korean film was selected to illustrate the profound contemporary influence of Confucian values in South Korean society (and in East Asian society more broadly).

Case 2: Bai-li’s digital doppelgänger

Bai-li was diagnosed with a terminal illness and has begun to plan for the end of her life, not only documenting her treatment preferences, but also figuring out the best way to comfort her seven-year-old daughter, Jia. She decides that for now, the best thing for Jia is to spare her the painful reality of her deteriorating health and looming death. Bai-li works with the AI company, Wonderland, to create a digital doppelgänger of herself to video chat with Jia during the terminal stage of her illness and after her death. While in real life Bai-li was employed as a fund manager, she had dreamt of becoming an archaeologist, and decides to embellish her digital replica with a backstory that reflects this dream while also providing an alibi to explain her absence to Jia. The digital doppelgänger presents to Jia as an archaeologist away on an expedition in a remote desert. It begins to speak regularly with Jia via video chat. Jia’s grandmother, Hwa-ran, who has stepped-in to serve as Jia’s primary caregiver, initially goes along with Bai-li’s plan, but over time, she begins to feel agitated about the time Jia spends with Bai-li’s digital doppelgänger, and is unsure that the emerging relationship between Jia and the digital doppelgänger is good for Jia.

In Case 2, a terminal diagnosis is withheld from a patient’s young child, perhaps as a temporary measure until she matures and is better able to cope. It also spares Bai-li from having to engage in a difficult and emotionally distressing conversation where she discloses her impending death to her daughter. Although nondisclosure is widely practiced in Bai-li’s Korean culture, it is increasingly contested, and in some regions there is a shift toward greater disclosure (Fujimori et al., 2014; Mori et al., 2023). Jia’s grandmother’s reaction to not disclosing Bai-li’s diagnosis and impending death may reflect these broader societal tensions.

Case 3: Tae-joo’s digital doppelgänger

Jeong-in’s boyfriend, Tae-joo, sustained a serious injury. He now lies in a hospital bed in a coma with a poor prognosis. The physician caring for Tae-joo informs Jeong-in that Tae-joo is 'not coming back.' Since Tae-joo is a young man, formerly in good health, the news devastates Jeong-in. Eventually, the medical team gently encourages Jeong-in to try imagining a life without Tae-joo. The social worker refers Jeong-in to an AI company, Wonderland, that offers to create a digital replica of Tae-joo to ease her grief and potentially help her transition at her own pace. Since Tae-joo’s ambition was to become an astronaut, Jeong-in decides to create a digital doppelgänger of Tae-joo as an astronaut working at a space station. This both enhances her memory of him, and also furnishes Jeong-in with a comforting narrative that makes sense of Tae-joo’s absence. After sharing images and texts of her boyfriend with Wonderland, Jeong-in pays a monthly subscription fee to access a digital doppelgänger of Tae-joo, available to video chat 24-7. Simultaneously, she continues to visit the real Tae-joo, still lying in the hospital bed.

In Case 3, the digital replica of Tae-joo seems to help Jeong-in cope with loss, and her mood lightens. In the futuristic setting of the film, people are frequently shown chatting with digital doppelgängers of people who are deceased or unavailable. Thus, it appears normal for Jeong-in to do so.

II. What might confucius say about digital doppelgängers? 2

What might Confucius say about the use of digital doppelgängers for people who are newly dead (Bao Rong), dying (Bai-li), 3 and incapacitated (Tae-joo)? Confucius did not put forth a general ethical theory in the sense discussed in contemporary Western moral philosophy. But he did offer a potent ethical system by prescribing and articulating three core values (ren/humaneness or benevolence, yi/righteousness, and li/rites) and a large set of other moral norms. Moreover, he discussed how to apply these values to real-life scenarios and cases, often considering what an ideally morally virtuous persons (junzi) would do. In considering the three cases, we thus begin with Confucian virtues, before turning to the related topics of how virtues develop (family-centeredness); how families shape identity (relational personhood); and how other-regarding duties take priority (duties over rights).

1. Prosocial Virtues . Confucian ethics is frequently interpreted as a virtue ethic featuring five primary virtues and several secondary virtues. The five primary virtues include benevolence, empathy, or loving kindness (ren); righteousness or conduct befitting a social role (yi); propriety in rituals that convey moral attitudes (li); practical wisdom in making moral judgements (zhi); and trustworthiness (xin). Complementing these primary virtues are secondary virtues, including a sense of shame (chi); loyalty to superiors (zhong); filial piety or dutifulness to superiors (xìao); parental affection or loving benevolence for those in younger generations (ci, the counterpart to filial piety); and harmony (hé). Both the primary and secondary virtues are prosocial in the sense that they are oriented toward others as well as oneself, placing relationships at the centre of a flourishing human life.

According to Yu and Fan, the process of cultivating prosocial virtue is ongoing: people must “continuously prove that they are “worthy” of [moral] status by appropriately cultivating their character at each stage of their life” (Yu & Fan, 2007). Mencius (Mengzi) (2008, c. 372–389 B.C.E.) a prominent Confucian philosopher (and considered among Confucius’s most important interpreters), underscores the urgency of assiduously cultivating virtues, calling the difference between humans and animals, “slight” (p. 106).

Confucius’s emphasis on prosocial virtues shed lights on what Confucius might say about the cases of Bao Rong, Bai-li, and Tae-joo’s digital doppelgängers. Digital doppelgängers can play a potentially positive role if they foster Confucian virtues. For Bao Xiaobai (Case 1), his dedication to acquiring the technological skill to recreate Bao Rong’s voice and image conveyed an abiding devotion (ci) to his daughter. It not only allowed him to work through grief but transformed grief into a positive, creative force. To love as Bao Xiaobai did epitomises loving kindness (ren), understood not just as a passive feeling, but an active creative force, producing and reproducing life (sheng sheng) in a way that mimics the cosmic process of giving life (Lan, 2012). The gifting of the digital doppelgänger to his wife on her birthday also expresses ren, understood as loving kindness and empathy (ren). Bao (2024) told the press that he and his wife had not spoken to each other for six months following their daughter death, and that creating a “digital daughter” enabled them to reconnect: “AI is a tool we have used to express our love for her.”

However, we can imagine the situation between Bao Xiaobai and his wife unfolding differently. His wife might have been traumatised or upset by the birthday gift. Bringing Bao Rong forcefully “back to life” might have made it harder, not easier, for her to grieve and let go. From a Confucian perspective, it matters if the digital doppelgänger was principally self-serving or other-serving (i.e., based on Bao Xiaobai’s needs and preferences or those of his wife).

For Jia (in Case 2), the ritual of video chatting might help her express filiality (xìao), loving kindness (ren), devotion (zhong), and affection (ai) toward her mother, and show conduct befitting her role (yi) as Bai-li’s daughter. In these ways, it may foster Jia’s moral growth.

Yet, with her mother’s imminent passing, it is important that Jia continue to enact virtues toward others, especially the grandmother who has stepped-in to parent her. As Jia’s story unfolds, Jia becomes increasingly attached to her mother’s digital doppelgänger, resulting in tensions between Jia and her grandmother, disrupting family harmony (hé), and eventually leading Jia’s grandmother to terminate the Wonderland service that provided the digital doppelgänger. In a farewell scene, the digital doppelgänger tells Jia that her mother has died, giving her permission to break away, while simultaneously conveying to Jia’s grandmother, “in my next life, I’ll be your mom. I’ll take care of you.” In this way, the digital doppelgänger facilitates Jia’s moral growth, helping Jia face the finality of death yet also understand the endurance of a mother’s love. Bai-li’s digital doppelgänger was thus a transitional figure, helping Jia to mature and move on.

However, we can imagine a variation of the film in which Bai-li’s digital doppelgänger went rogue. The digital doppelgänger might have advised Jia to join her mother in the afterlife. It might have hallucinated and generated lies about family members that harm people or tarnish people’s beliefs about the living and the dead. While such risks can be minimised through fine-tuning the algorithm, they cannot be eliminated.

For Jeong-in (Case 3), the situation differs. Her boyfriend is still alive and his condition is not considered terminal. In the film, Tae-joo wakes up from a coma confused, different, and impaired, not fully understanding why Jeong-in seems unhappy. When Jeong-in confides that she has developed a relationship with a simulated version of him, and feels guilty about it, Tae-joo perceives that he is an obstacle for Jeong-in, and decides to leave her. Yet, in the end, Jeong-in chooses a life with Tae-joo over the digital doppelgänger and terminates Wonderland’s service. In this way, Tae-joo’s digital doppelgänger apparently helped Jeong-in to choose a human relationship over a human-digital doppelgänger relationship.

Yet the plot might have unfolded differently, veering off course in ways that harmed both Jeong-in and Tae-joo. For example, the digital doppelgänger might have encouraged Jeong-in to reject her disabled boyfriend as flawed and imperfect, and choose it instead. Some worry that digital doppelgängers might become an “artificial sweetener,” tricking people into thinking they have a valuable relationship with someone, when in reality that relationship has changed or ended (Elder, 2019). What might Confucius say? Would it be wrong for Jeong-in to prefer the more idealised digital doppelgänger over Tae-joo? What if Tae-joo remained in an unconscious state for many years?

Confucius might say that preferring a digital doppelgänger over a human is wrong because artifice should not be an end-in-itself, but a means to help us embrace humanity. Thus, the Confucianist, Xunzi (2014, c. 286-238 B.C.E.), insisted that ornamentation of the dead was essential to avoid feeing disgust toward corpses and enable mature grief. For Xunzi, “we learn, we innovate, we adopt and modify, and an account of human excellence should embrace this rather than reject it as unnatural” (Elder, 2019, p. 79). Yet the point of artifice for Xunzi was to allow people to face grief, fear, and other hard emotions, not to shut down these feelings. This suggests that Tae-joo’s digital doppelgänger should not be used to enable Jeong-in to run away from an emotionally painful situation, but should help her to face it. Whether Jeong-in accepts or rejects her boyfriend, she should not reject human companionship in order to pursue a relationship with a digital doppelgänger. To ensure this, Confucius might say that a digital doppelgänger of an unconscious patient should self-terminate if the patient it represents wakes up.

2. Family-Centred. While Confucius held that a virtuous person exhibits virtue to everyone, he gave pride of place to certain relationships: ruler-subject, parent-child, siblings, spouses, and friends. Confucius emphasises sincerity and love in the enactment of virtues and considered mere conformity to virtuous acts to be inadequate. In an oft-cited, but philosophically and hermeneutically controversial, passage, Confucius (Kǒngzǐ) (2016, c. 475-220 B.C.E.) accentuates this point by contrasting filial piety toward parents with the support provided to animals: “the filial piety of now-a-days means the support of one’s parents. But dogs and horses likewise are able to do something in the way of support;—without reverence, what is there to distinguish the one support given from the other?” (p. 2). The key feature of caring well for parents is the love and reverence a child’s acts express, which is not present in the same way in relationships with dogs or horses.

Family-centeredness might inform Confucius’s approach to digital doppelgängers. Consider first, the digital doppelgänger of Bao Rong (Case 1). Since it reportedly brought Bao Xiaobai and his wife closer, Confucius might say that it promoted family-centeredness. However, even if it comforted and helped Bao Xiaobai and his wife, the situation might have unfolded differently. For example, Bao Xiaobai’s wife might have opposed her husband’s creation, driving them apart. Even if Bao Rong’s parents agreed, other family members, such as Bao Rong’s aunts, uncles, grandparents, etc. might not, leading to division and disrupting family harmony (hé).

It might also be argued that the purpose of ancestral rites runs deeper. According to Wang (2025), unlike the earlier, Shang dynasty, when people sought to summon ancestors’ spirits through frequent and bloody sacrifices to gain favour and extract benefits, Confucianism inherited from the later, Zhou dynasty a “virtue-centric” view of ancestral rites: “even though the ancestors do not actually appear, participants perform … rituals with the same reverence and propriety as if ancestors’ spirits were truly present. Rather than attempt to summon…Confucian rituals focus on honouring and remembering” as the way to cultivate character through sustained ritual practice (p. 979). Wang (2025) maintains that “AI Shamanism” is not true piety; instead, it offers “passive, on-demand interactions that demand no spiritual effort” (p. 980). When creators of digital doppelgängers embellish not only a deceased person’s outward appearance, but their occupation and identity (as occurred in cases 2 and 3), they may betray the Confucian duty to authentically honour and remember the dead.

Consider next, the digital doppelgänger of Jia’s mother, Bai-li (Case 2). Given that the parent-child relationship is a central relationship in Confucian ethics, continuing this relationship via a digital doppelgänger might carry tremendous value. If Jia carries out the ritual (li) of speaking regularly with her mother with true sincerity and love, this might cultivate filial piety (xìao) and foster Jia’s moral growth. Just as Confucianists use technologies like telephones to support family relationships, digital doppelgängers might perform a similar function via videochat (Wheeler, 2022).

Yet Muyskens et al. (2024) worry that “the ability of technology to satisfy our desires… can have the unanticipated consequence of crowding out opportunities for genuine connection” (p. 1006). Unlike the telephone, which connects humans to humans, the digital doppelgänger of Bai-li connects Jia to an LMM, not a human being. Would she be better off connecting to her flesh and blood mother during the course of her terminal illness? Or to her grandmother, Hwa-ran, who began parenting her?

One reply is that Confucianism is a pragmatic philosophy. It flexes and bends as circumstances change. Since “every family is unique… [virtues like] filial piety…will be realised in different ways” (Wheeler, 2022, p. 283). Some argue that Confucian ethics values AI-human relationships if such relationships afford an opportunity for enacting Confucian virtues (Jecker & Fan, Forthcoming). On this interpretation, Bai-li’s digital doppelgänger is a good thing if it helps Jia develop filial piety (xìao) and other prosocial virtues.

Yet a different reply holds that Confucianism emphasises human relationships. Jia’s relationship with her grandmother (Hwa-ran) ought to take priority over Jia’s relationship with a digital doppelgänger. Hwa-ran’s growing unease about her granddaughter’s videochatting may create tensions in the relationship between them, or lead Jia to feel guilty about spending time with a digital doppelgänger.

In further reply, perhaps virtues shown toward digital doppelgängers are not genuine virtues. Sparrow (2021) reasons that “there is nothing virtuous in kindness towards a robot,” because robots lack consciousness and cannot know or appreciate kindness (p. 27). While Sparrow (2021) regards the aspiration to positively affect another as key to making kind acts virtuous, Sparrow also grants the possibility that “‘kindness’ to a robot might actually be … virtuous if a person genuinely mistakes the robot for something that is an appropriate object of kindness and when it was reasonable for them to do so” (p. 28). On this analysis, Jia can cultivate virtues, such as loving kindness (ren), by interacting with her mother’s digital doppelgänger if she sincerely believes that her mother is video chatting with her. Yet the possibility of expressing virtue would presumably be time-limited: as Jia grows older, persisting in this belief would become unreasonable.

However, Confucius might say that video chatting with Bai-li digital doppelgängers would be reasonable, even after Bai-li’s death, if Jia sincerely thought that this was what her mother wanted. According to this interpretation of Confucian teachings, Jia’s duties toward her mother do not cease abruptly at Bai-li’s death, but continue, albeit in a different way, after death. Confucius (Kǒngzǐ) (2015, c. 150-130 B.C.E.) frequently characterises filial piety as extending to ancestors, commenting, “He sacrificed to the dead, as if they were present. He sacrificed to the spirits as if the spirits were present” (p. 11). Thus, if Bai-li wanted her daughter to continue to show her respect by sustaining a connection with her digital doppelgänger as she grew up, Confucius might say it is righteous (yi) and befitting her role as Bai-li’s daughter to do so. At the same time, Confucius might insist that Jia uphold relationships with other family members, especially her grandmother.

Yet cultivating virtue by means of a digital doppelgänger might fail for another reason. If the digital doppelgänger is embellished and perfected, showing virtue toward it might be extremely easy. For example, if Bai-li’s digital doppelgänger were endlessly patient or excessively kindhearted, Jia might cultivate virtues that do not transfer readily to humans. In the film, this does not occur. For example, while Bai-li embellishes her digital doppelgänger, it still makes mistakes just like Bai-li, such as becoming preoccupied with work and ending videochats with Jia too abruptly.

Consider next the digital doppelgänger of Tae-joo (Case 3). While Jeong-in and Tae-joo are not married, Jeong-in treats Tae-joo in a family-like way and centres her relationship with him. Since Tae-joo is not terminally ill or imminently dying, Confucius might be concerned that over time, as Jeong-in devotes energies to a more “perfect” version of Tae-joo, she may prefer it over the human Tae-joo who is comatose and impaired. In the film, this does not occur; Jeong-in remains loyal to Tae-joo, regularly visiting him in the hospital even while she videochats with his digital doppelgänger.

Yet Sparrow’s analysis suggests that Jeong-in’s loyalty and devotion toward the unconscious Tae-joo and toward the digital doppelgänger are not genuinely virtuous, because there is no one on the receiving end who can appreciate a benefit. What might Confucius say? Perhaps he would say that despite this lack of reciprocity, Jeong-in’s actions are sincere and deeply felt. Perhaps, from a Confucian perspective, that is enough. Jeong-in might not expect reciprocity under the circumstances, and for her, showing love (ren) might be valuable for its own sake.

3. Relational Persons . Confucius regarded virtues as incipient moral capacities that must be deliberately developed over time through relationships with others. Mencius (Mengzi) (2015, c. 372-389 B.C.E.). refers specifically to the four beginnings of goodness as “moral sprouts” (duan), which can take root and flourish if a person exerts continuous effort in a favourable environment (p. 70). Specifically, Mencius held that compassion, shame, modesty, and a sense of right and wrong are sprouts that can mature into virtues: compassion becomes benevolence (ren), shame becomes righteousness (yi), modesty grows into propriety (li), and a sense of right and wrong is the sprout of wisdom (zhi). However, the Way is difficult and long, because “the distance from where we begin to where we become a junzi is considerable” (Wong, 2004, p. 423).

Confucius’s belief that we need others to develop morally is what Wong (2004) refers to as the developmental sense of relationality: “whatever we become, we become through the help and hindrance of others” (p. 421). The environment considered most conducive to developing virtue is a loving family. For Wong (2004), the family not only nurtures and develops us but constitutes us: “I am not warm and generous simpliciter but warm and generous to certain people, and other ways to other people. If warmth and generosity are part of who I am, then so are the people to whom I am warm and generous” (p. 422). Wong (2004) refers to this as the constitutive sense of relationality. Rosemont (1991) expresses this idea clearly: “there can be no me in isolation, to be considered abstractly: I am the totality of roles I live in relation to specific others…Taken collectively, they weave, for each of us, a unique pattern of personal identity, such that as some of my roles change, the others will of necessity change also, literally making me a different person” (p. 90).

Relationality is germane to what Confucius might say about all three digital doppelgängers. In the first two cases, a digital doppelgänger may keep intact important family roles that partly constitute a person’s identity (constitutive relationality). In Case, 1, the role of Xiaobai and his wife as parents to Bao Rong is kept intact through a “digital daughter.’ In Case 2, while the physical relationship is gone, the emotional relationship Jia formerly had with her mother, Bai-li, continues through a digital rendering, limiting the loss of identity that a mother’s death would ordinarily bring. During Jia’s formative development, Bai-li’s digital doppelgänger can also support Jia by cultivating the sprouts of virtue within her (developmental relationality).

Elder (2025) emphasises the point (mentioned earlier) that ancient funerary practices, such as ornamentation, makeup, and perfume, were a practical way to avoid feeling aversion toward human remains, and to protect people’s emotional capacities to stay connected to and be reverent toward deceased loved ones. Perhaps, the digital doppelgängers in cases 1 and 2 can likewise help the living stay meaningfully connected with their deceased family members. For example, in Case 1, video chatting may help Jia in much the same way that other rituals do, e.g., erecting a household alter with pictures of ancestors; making offerings of food; lighting incense; and during annual festivals, such as the Tomb-Sweeping Festival (Qingming), visiting and cleaning an ancestor’s grave. While digital doppelgängers are a new technology, the virtues they express are thousands of years old.

Yet it might be objected that digital doppelgängers hold people back. Perhaps, in Case 1, creating a digital doppelgänger of his newly dead daughter prevents Xiaobai (and his wife) from making efforts to connect with human beings. Similarly, in Case 2, by creating a digital doppelgänger of herself for her daughter, Bai-li may impede her daughter from forming loving relationships with others. Yet, Confucius might be sceptical of these concerns. He did not consider it inappropriate to keep psychological connections to those we have lost. By contrast, Mozi, the prototypical utilitarian, rejected lavish burials as wasteful expenditures of resources. Mohists prescribed, “quickly bury[ing] corpses in simple wooden coffins, …and encourage[ing] people to return to work and ordinary responsibilities as quickly as possible.” (Elder, 2019, p. 77).

Commenting on the contrast between Confucius and Mozi’s approaches to death, Elder (2019) defends a Confucianist stance, arguing that the Mohists’ “quick and efficient approach does not seem to do justice to the internal life of the bereaved” or allow people to address grieving in a morally mature way (p. 77). According to Elder (2019), for some Chinese people, digital doppelgängers “merely externalise a robust pre-existing phenomenon” in which people imagine themselves in conversation with loved ones, using their own extensive knowledge of that person’s patterns of response to generate answers like those the person would have given (p. 74).

In contrast to cases 1 and 2, Confucius might say that in Case 3, videochatting with a digital doppelgänger is harmful, since Tae-joo could live in a comatose state for many years. In the film, Tae-joo wakes up and undergoes rehabilitation, prompting Jeong-in to sever ties with the digital version of him. Yet what if Jeong-in had not been so willing? Or what if Tae-joo remained comatose for many years, and Jeong-in developed a close bond with his digital doppelgänger? Under such conditions, ending a relationship with the digital version of Tae-joo might be hard. Jeong-in might prefer the more perfect digital doppelgänger over a disabled human being.

4. Duties over rights. Confucius prioritised duties to others over rights, setting a high bar for the moral life. Contemporary commentators often interpret him as blurring the line between supererogatory and obligatory conduct, although Confucius did not draw conceptual distinctions of these kind. Instead, Confucius distinguished what a junzi would do from what a xiaoren (petty or mean person) would do while keeping the focus on the junzi. Confucius’s emphasis on the junzi meant the threshold for an ethical life was set high. It is fair to say that Confucian ethics is in this sense demanding or maximal.

Given Confucianism’s maximalist focus, digital doppelgängers might furnish a way for people to better realise positive relationships with the deceased. In Case 1, a digital doppelgänger allows Xiaobai (and his wife) to sustain a relationship with their newly dead daughter, Bao Rong, and continue showing her love (ren) and conduct befitting their role as parents (yi). For young people like Jia (in Case 2), speaking with a digital doppelgänger of her mother might be a way to continue confiding in her mother and anticipating what her mother might say, thereby demonstrating filial piety (xìao); sincerity (xin); and conduct fulfilling her role as Bai-li’s daughter (yi). Continuing to recall Bai-li’s counsel potentially helps Jia develop her own moral judgement (zhi) and facilitates virtues like love (ren) and affection (ai) (Lam, 2025). However, these remarks must be qualified, because moral excellences associated with Confucian virtues do not arise in isolation from their social and familial contexts. Thus, it matters not just how Jia relates to her deceased mother, but also how this impacts other family relationships, most notably Jia’s relationship with her maternal grandmother, who has taken over parenting her. For adults, like Jeong-in in Case 3, speaking with a digital doppelgänger could also foster moral growth and human-heartedness (ren). It could help Jeong-in embrace human imperfections and limitations, maturing in her love for Tae-joo. In Confucian teachings, the sage is morally fallible, suggesting that Confucius rejected moral perfection (Harris, 2023).

Yet the strong emphasis on duty in Confucianism can also pose risks for users. Voinea et al. (2025) worry about companies using digital doppelgängers to “sell products and services or to steer people towards goals not chosen by them” (p. 6). In Wonderland, this occurs when we are briefly introduced to a grandmother who creates a digital doppelgänger of her grandson. The virtual grandson demands the grandmother buy him (virtual) products, such as a new clothes and a fancier car, which the grandmother struggles to pay for, taking on a second job to afford them. The technology company depicted in the film has run amuck, becoming what one film critic calls, “a capitalist nightmare” (Yousuf, 2024). To minimise risks of exploitation, best practices for the use of digital doppelgängers in healthcare settings should be established.

III. Digital doppelgängers for surrogate decision making

As noted, emerging evidence suggests a further role for AI technologies as P3s to aid decision-making for incapacitated patients. To illustrate, imagine the case of Tae-joo unfolding differently.

Case 3 (revised). Tae-joo does not awaken from a ventilator-dependent comatose state and is diagnosed as being in a persistent vegetative state. Tae-joo designated Jeong-in to be his surrogate decision-maker. She must decide whether to remove the ventilator. This would most likely result in Tae-joo’s death since he is not breathing on his own. In the revised case, Jeong-in created the digital doppelgänger to accurately represent Tae-joo, rather than to embellish him.

In the amended Case 3, Tae-joo’s digital doppelgänger might help Jeong-in make difficult decisions that align with what Tae-joo would have wanted (the standard referred to as “substituted judgement”). Jeong-in might also use a text-based P3 that uses generative AI, or an algorithmic-based P3 that does not rely on generative AI. However, the more sophisticated digital doppelgänger has the visual appearance of Tae-joo, as well as his voice, non-verbal gestures, and demeanour. It can mimic Tae-joo better, and in ways that may establish more emotional rapport and support for Jeong-in. This function is significant given that making treatment decisions for loved ones has a substantial negative effect on many surrogates that persists for months (or years) (Wendler & Rid, 2011). A 2025 survey of surrogate decision-makers reported that the majority regard their experience as a surrogate as “one of the most stressful experiences of their lives” (Mishkin et al., 2025, p. 895), which is consistent with prior studies (Vig et al., 2007).

Beyond providing greater comfort to Jeong-in, a digital doppelgänger functioning as a P3 could alleviate moral distress providers might feel about the ventilator decision by increasing confidence that the decision aligns with what the patient wanted. While any form of P3 with accurate outputs can increase confidence about patient preferences, anthropomorphised digital doppelgängers can arguably do this more effectively than text-based models because they are “believable characters,” able to simulate emotions, such as empathy, warmth, and kindness, using subtle movements, facial expression, tone and intonation, and nonverbal cues that reinforce the patient preference prediction (Nah et al., 2022, p. 4).

However, the use of digital doppelgängers as P3s creates risk. First, Tae-joo’s digital doppelgänger was created by Jeong-in, not Tae-joo. Perhaps, Tae-joo did not want a digital doppelgänger to function in this way, or would have objected to this use of his personal data. Perhaps Tae-joo would have agreed to a digital doppelgänger that served as Tae-joo’s companion, but not one that functioned as a surrogate decision-maker. 4 A Confucian might argue that using a loved one’s digital content to create a digital doppelgänger without their knowledge shows a lack of trustworthiness (xin).

Deploying digital doppelgängers as P3s also raises privacy concerns. One approach to mitigating this concern is for patients to decide how their personal information will be used. For example, P3s might be considered during advance care planning, and incorporated into advance directives. Yet, even in Western societies where advance directives are more widely used, most people do not execute them (Yadave et al., 2017); over time, usage has remained low to moderate in North America and Europe (Valenti et al., 2025). In East Asian societies, advance directives are even more challenging, because patients may not know their rights or might prefer not to exercise them (Kawakami et al., 2020). In Korea, the setting for Wonderland, although there is more interest than before in autonomous decision making (Kim, 2015), “advance care planning is rare, and most do-not-resuscitate decisions are made between the family and physician at the very end of a patient’s life” (Shin et al., 2015, p. 407).

Since privacy concerns vary depending on the type of data used to train a model, an alternative way to minimise them is to utilise a basic LLM trained only on publicly available demographic information. However, this would offer less comfort and support to family members like Jeong-in than a more robust model trained on personalised information. According to Annoni (2025), a P3 “built solely on population-based data risks reducing a patient’s moral agency to a set of demographic variables, potentially conflicting with the core principle of respecting patient individual autonomy” (p. 450). A more robust clinical model that utilises the patient’s EHR provides additional data, but might have limited value for patients like Tae-joo, who are young and previously healthy. Equipping the P3 with personalised information about Tae-joo such as social media posts, emails, photos, and videos enhances its ability to console and comfort Jeong-in, which might be a priority for Tae-joo, who loved her. Earp et al. (2024) argue that utilising more personalised patient information can be ethically sound even absent patient consent, because patients care about “the process of decision-making” (p. 19). An ethically sound process on this analysis allows surrogates to decide about using a patient’s information to create a digital doppelgänger, since the patient chose them to make health-related decisions on their behalf.

Setting privacy concerns aside, a further challenge is that P3s might turn out to be inferior to, or no better than, the decisions made by human surrogates. Still more concerning, digital doppelgängers could go rogue, leading to decisions based on false information or hallucinations. To address this, humans should be kept in-the-loop. Rid and Wendler (2014a) envisioned P3s keeping clinicians in-the-loop by discussing treatment options with both them and surrogate decision-makers. Alternatively, the risk of hallucinations could be avoided by relying just on a P3 that does not use generative AI and is fully explainable. Optimal human oversight must be adapted to local contexts, as a configurable part of an intervention, rather than globally standardised, because resources, risk tolerance, trust in institutions, and expectations of medicine differ markedly across countries and cultures (Haug and Harrison, 2026; Jecker et al., 2026).

Finally, automation bias, the well-documented tendency to favour decisions generated by machines, creates ethical challenges when it results in regarding the outputs of AI predictors as determinative (Pearson et al., 2026). This concern can be exacerbated if family members feel weighed down by conflicted and emotionally charged decisions, and P3s appear to offer an “easy out” (Blumenthal-Barby et al., 2024). Offsetting automation bias requires training clinicians to avoid it and ensuring they remain in-the-loop and accountable at critical decision points. A distinct set of concerns, outside the scope of our enquiry but dealt with elsewhere, arises if patients designate digital doppelgängers to function as a “roboagents,” making decisions on their behalf if they become incapacitated (Appel, 2026).

Balancing benefits and risks of different applications of digital doppelgängers requires considering the different ways they can help and harm people in different social and cultural contexts. In a different setting, different moral themes might be centred. For example, Morris et al. point out that while digital doppelgängers can pose mental health risks for mourners, what qualifies as a “mental health risk” is partly context-dependent and grief paradigms vary across cultures. Thus, the Māori of New Zealand “see ancestors as kaitiaki (guardians) who offer spiritual support and ensure the well-being of their descendants” (Morris & Brubaker, 2025, p. 8). This belief blurs the distinction between life and death, which might be considered risky from a Western perspective, but beneficial from a Maōri perspective.

IV. Ethical benchmarks

We recommend proceeding with caution. While each society must evaluate for itself how digital doppelgängers and other AI tools impact its socio-cultural practices; religious rituals; grieving processes; and attitudes toward death, dying, and disability, we provisionally propose the following ethical benchmarks for deploying these technologies in healthcare settings.

Use digital doppelgängers as tools to strengthen relationships between human beings, not to replace or substitute for them. To this end, design them as transitional figures, facilitating either continuing a relationship with the AI’s human counterpart when their condition improves (for patients who are unconscious but expected to recover) or forming new relationships (for patients who are dying or newly dead).

Our benchmarks are most relevant to societies that emphasise prosocial virtues, centre the family, stress a relational view of persons, and prioritise duties over rights. We invite continued discussion and empirical studies to better understand the impacts of these emerging technologies in healthcare across diverse cultural settings and over time.

V. Conclusion

Highlighting a Confucian perspective, this essay addressed possible roles for digital doppelgängers and other emerging AI technologies in healthcare. In addition to comforting the loved ones of patients who are newly dead, dying, or comatose, digital doppelgängers may increase confidence that treatment decisions align with what the patient would have wanted and reduce moral distress for surrogate decision-makers and members of the healthcare team. Yet these same technologies pose substantial risks that bioethics has only recently begun to address. While our analysis emphasised what Confucius might say, our recommendations have potentially broader reach in other societies that stress similar values. Given AI’s global impact, we encourage more bioethics enquiry exploring applications of AI in diverse settings.
