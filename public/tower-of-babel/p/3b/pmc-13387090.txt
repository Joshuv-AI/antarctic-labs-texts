A dual-recognition electrochemical impedance sensor based on an aptamer-MIP strategy for sensitive detection of methamphetamine using a AuNPs@COF interface
Authors: Danping Xie, Wenchao Jia, Xiangying Jin, Zhe Peng, Yizhen Xie, Hua Yin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The increasing prevalence of methamphetamine (METH) abuse necessitates analytical methods capable of ultra-trace detection in complex matrices. However, single-recognition strategies often suffer from limited selectivity and susceptibility to environmental interference. Herein, we present a highly sensitive electrochemical impedance sensor based on a synergistic aptamer-molecularly imprinted polymer (MIP) dual-recognition strategy constructed on a AuNPs@COF nanostructured interface. This dual-recognition system operates through complementary mechanisms. A newly screened METH-specific aptamer (KD = 213 nM), achieving an approximately 2.7-fold improvement in affinity over a recently reported aptamer (KD = 570 nM), provides primary molecular recognition. Concurrently, the electropolymerized MIP layer introduces spatial complementarity that imposes stringent steric constraints on target binding, enabling a “dual verification” mechanism that effectively discriminates against structurally analogous interferents. Moreover, the MIP matrix is expected to provide physical shielding of the aptamer from nuclease degradation, as previously reported for such hybrid recognition systems, thereby potentially enhancing sensor stability in complex biological matrices. The AuNPs@COF interface further maximizes aptamer immobilization and facilitates electron transfer, ensuring efficient signal transduction. Benefiting from this synergistic interplay, the sensor enables label-free detection of METH via electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS), achieving an ultralow detection limit of 33 aM (S/N = 3) and a wide dynamic range from 0.1 fM to 0.5 nM. The sensor exhibits excellent selectivity, reproducibility, and long-term stability, along with reliable performance in complex matrices including wastewater and biological fluids. This work establishes a versatile dual-recognition sensing paradigm, offering significant potential for ultra-trace detection of small-molecule contaminants in environmental and biomedical applications.

Introduction

METH, a highly addictive central nervous system stimulant, has become one of the most widely abused illicit drugs worldwide.1,2 Its excessive consumption leads to severe health issues, including psychosis, cardiovascular failure, and even death, as well as significant societal problems such as crime and family breakdown.3,4 Therefore, the development of rapid, sensitive, and reliable methods for METH detection is of great importance for clinical diagnosis, forensic analysis, and public safety monitoring. Traditional analytical techniques for METH detection include gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (GC-MS),5 high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC),6 and capillary electrophoresis.7 Although these methods offer high sensitivity and accuracy, they often require expensive instrumentation, skilled personnel, time-consuming sample pretreatment, and are not suitable for on-site rapid testing. In contrast, electrochemical sensors have attracted considerable attention due to their advantages of fast response, low cost, portability, and compatibility with miniaturization.8–10

Among various electrochemical techniques, EIS is particularly promising for label-free sensing because it can sensitively monitor interfacial changes by measuring the charge transfer resistance (Rct) at the electrode surface.11,12 In order to achieve selective recognition of METH, the strategy of constructing MIP13–15 or aptamer16,17 on the surface of electrode is often used to achieve highly selective signal output of the tested compound on the electrode. MIPs are synthetic recognition element with tailor-made cavities complementary to target molecules in size, shape, and functional groups.18,19 For METH detection, MIP-based sensors have been developed using o-phenylenediamine (o-PD) as a functional monomer, which forms a thin, stable film upon electropolymerization.9 Traditional MIP sensors, despite their robustness, often encounter challenges such as limited binding affinity and significant non-specific adsorption.20,21 These drawbacks are primarily attributed to the heterogeneous nature of binding sites and the incomplete removal of template molecules during the fabrication process, which can compromise the sensitivity and accuracy in complex matrices like wastewater.22,23

Aptamers, on the other hand, are short single-stranded DNA or RNA molecules that can bind to their targets with high affinity and specificity through unique three-dimensional structures.24–26 They offer advantages over antibodies, such as chemical stability, ease of synthesis, and low batch-to-batch variation. Recently, our group successfully screened a novel METH-binding aptamer with a dissociation constant (KD) of 213 nM, which is significantly lower (i.e., higher affinity) than previously reported METH aptamers.27–29 Nevertheless, aptamer-based sensors alone may still face challenges in achieving ultra-low detection limits due to the limited surface coverage or conformational changes upon target binding.30,31

To overcome these hurdles, the integration of high-affinity aptamers with the MIP framework has emerged as a synergistic dual-recognition strategy.32,33 In this architecture, the aptamer provides primary biomolecular recognition with exceptional affinity, while the MIP cavity offers secondary spatial confinement, effectively shielding the electrode from non-specific interferents.34 Inspired by the concept of dual recognition,35,36 we hypothesized that combining a high-affinity aptamer with a MIP could provide a synergistic effect, enhancing both sensitivity and selectivity. The aptamer serves as an initial recognition layer, while the overlaid MIP provides additional selective cavities that can cooperate with or complement the aptamer binding. To the best of our knowledge, such a dual-recognition strategy for METH detection has not been reported.

In this work, we propose a novel electrochemical impedance sensor that utilizes newly synthesized highly conductive COF to enhance impedance response signals, combined with newly screened high affinity METH aptamers and o-PD electropolymerization based MIPs. The sensor manufacturing was characterized by EIS, CV, and electron microscopy (SEM, TEM). The analytical performance, including sensitivity, selectivity, reproducibility, and stability, was systematically evaluated. In addition, the sensor is also used to detect METH in various water bodies (drinking water, lake water, and industrial wastewater) as well as in human saliva, tears, and artificial urine samples. The results demonstrate that the dual-recognition approach yields superior performance compared to single-recognition counterparts, achieving an ultra-low detection limit and excellent selectivity, which surpasses previously reported METH electrochemical sensors (Scheme 1).

Experimental

Apparatus and reagents

The SI clearly describe the various instruments, chemicals, and materials used in the experiment.

Selection for METH aptamers

A modified Capture-SELEX method was employed. The initial single-stranded DNA (ssDNA) library (2 nmol) was dissolved in 200 µL of binding buffer (137 mmol L−1 NaCl, 2.7 mmol L−1 KCl, 10 mmol L−1, Na2HPO4·12H2O, 2 mmol L−1 KH2PO4, pH 7.4), heated at 95 °C for 5 min, and immediately cooled on ice for 10 min to allow proper folding. Streptavidin-coated magnetic beads (Dynabeads M-280, Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) were washed three times with binding buffer and then incubated with 2 nmol of biotinylated capture probe for 30 min at 25 °C under gentle rotation. After washing to remove unbound capture probe, the beads were incubated with the folded library for 1 h at 25 °C to immobilize the library via hybridization between the constant region of the library and the capture probe. The beads were then washed five times with binding buffer to remove unbound ssDNA.

For the first round of selection, the beads were incubated with 100 µM METH (dissolved in binding buffer containing 1% DMSO) for 1 h at 25 °C. After magnetic separation, the supernatant containing METH-bound sequences was collected. The eluted ssDNA was amplified by polymerase chain reaction (PCR) using forward primer (5′-ATTGGCACTCCACGCATAGG-3′) and reverse primer (5′-TTCACGGTAGCACGCATAGG-3′). PCR conditions were as follows: initial denaturation at 95 °C for 3 min; 20 cycles of 95 °C for 30 s, 58 °C for 30 s, and 72 °C for 30 s; final extension at 72 °C for 5 min. The PCR products were verified by 3% agarose gel electrophoresis. Double-stranded PCR products were converted to ssDNA by asymmetric PCR. The resulting ssDNA was used for the next round of selection.

To eliminate non-specific binders, counter-selection was performed starting from the third round. Before the positive selection step, magnetic beads loaded with the nucleic acid library were incubated together in the presence of 1000 µM each of AMP and phenylethylamine (a structural analog) for 30 minutes, and the supernatant (mainly consisting of nucleic acids shed due to non-specific binding) was removed. Then, the nucleic acid sequences that remained unbound on the surface of the magnetic beads were collected for positive selection with METH. The stringency of selection was gradually increased by decreasing the METH concentration (from 100 µM to 0.5 µM). After 12 rounds of selection, the nucleic acid library was sequenced using high-throughput sequencing technology. Sequences were aligned using ClustalW, and the secondary structure was predicted using UNAFold (https://sg.idtdna.com/UNAFold). The most abundant sequence (referred to as METH-aptamer) was synthesized with a thiol modification at the 5′ end for subsequent experiments (Scheme 2).

Capture-SELEX flowchart for screening of METH aptamers.

Affinity and specificity measurement

The binding affinity of METH-aptamer to METH was determined by EIS analysis. Candidate sequences were synthesized and equipped with a thiol functional group at the 5′ end of the sequence, allowing them to be immobilized on the surface of a gold electrode. By introducing different concentrations of METH, changes in impedance values were observed, and the equilibrium dissociation constant was obtained through steady-state fitting of the curve. Under the same conditions, METH-aptamer was titrated with AMP, MDMA, and cocaine (each at 200 µM) to evaluate the specificity of the selected candidate sequences.

Molecular docking

The 3D structure of METH-aptamer was predicted using the RNAComposer server, which is based on a fragment assembly approach. The predicted structure was then refined by energy minimization using the YASARA software with the Amber ff14SB force field to remove steric clashes and optimize hydrogen bonding. The minimized structure was used as the receptor for docking. The 3D structure of METH was obtained from PubChem and converted to pdbqt format using AutoDock Tools (version 1.5.6). Docking was performed with AutoDock Vina using a grid box (30 × 30 × 30 Å) centered on the putative binding pocket identified from the secondary structure. The exhaustiveness was set to 100. The best docking pose was selected based on the lowest binding energy and visualized with PyMOL.

Synthesis of AuNPs@COF

COF (Py-DA-COF) was synthesized according to a previously reported method with slight modifications. Briefly, 1,3,6,8-tetrakis(4-aminophenyl)pyrene (Py, 20 mg, 0.03 mmol) and 2,5-dihydroxyterephthalaldehyde (DA, 10 mg, 0.06 mmol) were dissolved in 2 mL of a mixture of 1,4-dioxane and mesitylene (1 : 1 v/v) in a Pyrex tube. After adding 0.2 mL of 6 M acetic acid, the tube was sonicated for 10 min, then degassed by three freeze–pump–thaw cycles, sealed under vacuum, and heated at 120 °C for 72 h. The resulting precipitate was collected by centrifugation, washed with DMF and acetone, and dried under vacuum at 80 °C overnight.

AuNPs@COF was prepared by in situ reduction of HAuCl4. COF powder (10 mg) was dispersed in 10 mL of ultrapure water by ultrasonication for 30 min. Then, 1 mL of 1% (w/v) HAuCl4 solution was added dropwise under vigorous stirring. After stirring for 30 min at room temperature, 1 mL of ice-cold 0.1 M NaBH4 solution was added dropwise over 5 min, and the mixture was stirred for another 2 h. The product was collected by centrifugation (10 000 rpm, 10 min), washed three times with water and ethanol, and dried under vacuum at 40 °C.

Fabrication of the aptasensor

First, polish the bare gold electrodes (AuE) on the microfabrication using 0.3 and 0.05 µm alumina slurry in sequence for 3 minutes each, then ultrasonically treat with water and ethanol for 3 minutes each to remove residual alumina. Next, electrochemically clean the electrodes using cyclic voltammetry with 0.5 M H2SO4 at a scan rate of 100 mV s−1 and a potential range of −0.3 to +1.5 V until a stable cyclic voltammetry characteristic curve with a clean gold surface is obtained. Rinse with water, dry under nitrogen, and then drop-cast 5 µL of AuNPs@COF suspension (1 mg mL−1 in water) onto the electrode surface, drying at room temperature to form a uniform thin film.

The thiol-modified METH-aptamer (5′-SH-(CH2)6-ACGCATAGGCACACCGGAACACACCACACATAGGTCCGCTGTTCGCGTCCCTATGCGT-3′) was reduced with 10 mM TCEP in immobilization buffer for 30 minutes to cleave the disulfide bond at room temperature. The reduced aptamer was diluted to 1 µM with immobilization buffer, heated at 95 °C for 5 minutes, and cooled to room temperature. Then, 10 µL of the aptamer solution was dropped onto AuNPs@COF/AuE and incubated overnight in a humidified chamber at 4 °C to prevent evaporation. After immobilization, the electrode was rinsed with immobilization buffer to remove unbound aptamer, and then treated with 1 mM MCH (in water) at room temperature for 30 minutes to block nonspecific binding sites. The resulting electrode (MCH/Apt/AuNPs@COF/AuE) was rinsed with binding buffer and stored at 4 °C for future use.

MIP thin films were electrodeposited onto the constructed surface of MCH/Apt/AuNPs@COF/AuE electrode using CV electrodeposition. First, react 1 µM METH with the electrode for 30 minutes, and then introduce a polymerization solution consisting of 5.0 mM o-PD in 0.1 M acetate buffer (pH 5.2). The CV scan is between −0.2 and +1.0 V, with a scan rate of 50 mV s−1 and a duration of 20 cycles. The formation of non-conductive poly(phenylenediamine) (PPD) film is characterized by a gradual decrease in current response. For comparison, non-imprinted polymer (NIP) films were prepared under the same conditions without prior introduction of METH before polymerization. After electropolymerization, immerse the electrode in a mixture of methanol/acetic acid (7 : 3, v/v), gently stir for 15 minutes, and then thoroughly rinse with PBS to remove residual solvents and METH template. The obtained sensor is MIP/MCH/Apt/AuNPs@COF/AuE. Impedance and CV characterization were performed for each step of electrode preparation, and the removal efficiency was verified by EIS and CV.

Analytical procedure

For METH detection, the constructed sensor was incubated with different concentrations of METH in PBS (137 mmol L−1 NaCl, 2.7 mmol L−1 KCl, 10 mmol L−1, Na2HPO4·12H2O, 2 mmol L−1 KH2PO4, pH 7.4) at 25 °C for 20 minutes (optimized). After washing with PBS to remove non-specific binding molecules, EIS measurements were performed in the redox probe solution (0.1 M KCl, 5 mM [Fe(CN)6]3−/4−, PBS). Record the increase in Rct (ΔRct) and construct a correlation function with METH concentration. To further evaluate the selectivity of the sensor, EIS measurements were conducted by exposing the sensor to 10 fM METH or 10 µM solutions of substances such as amphetamine and phenethylamine under the same conditions, and comparisons were made. Evaluate repeatability using nine independently manufactured sensors, and store the sensors at 4 °C for 30 days and regularly measure their response to assess stability.

Human tear, urine, and saliva samples were provided by the co-authors of this study, who are also part of the research team. All co-authors were fully informed about the purpose of this research and have consented to the use of their samples and the publication of the results. This study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki with approval from the institutional ethics committee. The sample is stored at −20 °C until analysis. Thaw the saliva, tears, and urine sample, dilute it 10 times with binding buffer to minimize matrix effects, and then filter it using a 0.22 µm cellulose acetate membrane. Centrifuge the saliva sample at 5000 rpm for 10 minutes at 4 °C to remove suspended solids, collect the supernatant, and dilute it twice with binding buffer. Add known concentrations of METH (0.1 pM, 10 pM, and 1 nM) to the diluted sample. Then use the constructed adaptive sensor to analyze the spiked sample according to the program described. The recovery rate is calculated by comparing the measured concentration with the spiked concentration.

Results and discussion

Characterization of the selected aptamer

Selection enrichment monitoring

The Capture SELEX process was carried out for 13 rounds with progressively increasing stringency, including gradual reduction of the target (METH) concentration from round 2 and introduction of negative selection using high-concentration structural analogs. As shown in Fig. 1, the enrichment level of the nucleic acid library did not decline despite the decreasing target dosage, indicating that the retained sequences possessed high affinity for METH. The negative-selection response initially showed high signal intensity due to abundant nonspecific binders, but progressively decreased as rounds advanced (Fig. 1). This decreasing trend demonstrates the successful removal of non-specific sequences, leading to an aptamer pool enriched with METH-specific candidates.

High-throughput sequencing and sequence alignment

Select the nucleic acid libraries enriched in rounds 4, 8, 11, 12, and 13 for high-throughput sequencing, and obtain a total of 20 000 sequences of base arrangement information. By combining the response frequency, homology alignment, and intra cluster analysis of the sequences, 20 sequences with the highest frequency of occurrence were selected from the complete sequences in the fixed region. Fig. S1A presents the homology alignment of the 40 bp random region, revealing a cluster of sequences with the highest homology rate. Fig. S1B shows the phylogenetic tree analysis, which was grouped the 20 sequences into multiple families based on sequence similarity. From each family we selected one representative sequence, obtaining a total of 5 sequences with high intra-family homology. This approach ensures that the selected sequences capture the structural diversity of the enriched library.

Structural prediction and truncation

The secondary structures of the five candidate sequences were predicted using the IDT online analysis platform. Fig. S2 displays the predicted structure of the lead aptamer (sequence 2) before and after truncation. The original 80 bp sequence forms a stable junction with a central bulge loop – a structural motif commonly associated with METH binding pockets. To remove non-essential bases while preserving the active conformation, we deleted the 3′-end sequences based on the recognition domain identified by molecular docking (Fig. 1D and E), yielding a 45 bp truncated variant. Importantly, as shown in Fig. S2, the truncated sequence retains exactly the same secondary structure as the original sequence (i.e., the same stem-loop organization and bulge loop), confirming that the truncation does not disrupt the binding conformation.

Binding affinity determination

The binding performance of the five truncated aptamers was evaluated using a electrochemistry-based assay. Fig. S5 presents the titration curves and fitted dissociation constants. Sequence 2 exhibited the highest affinity, with a KD of 213 ± 15 nM. Our newly screened aptamer thus achieves a ∼2.7-fold improvement over the original aptamer,29 validating its suitability for subsequent integration into the aptamer-MIP dual-recognition system. Consequently, sequence 2 was selected for all further experiments.

Characterization of AuNPs@COF

The TEM image (Fig. 2B and S6) shows the morphology of COF flakes. After loading with AuNPs, uniformly distributed spherical AuNPs (size ∼ 10–28 nm) were observed on the surface of COF (shown in Fig. 2A and B). The TEM-EDS mapping (Fig. 2B) confirms the presence of Au, C, N, and O elements. The XRD pattern (Fig. S3) shows the characteristic peak of COF at 22.8°, followed by peaks of spherical gold at 34.8° and 47.5°, confirming successful loading of AuNPs onto the surface of COF.

Morphological characterization of COF and AuNPs@COF. (A) SEM images: (a)–(c) COF, (d)–(f) AuNPs@COF. (B) (a) and (b) TEM images showing the morphology of AuNPs@COF; (c)–(f) corresponding TEM elemental mapping images of AuNPs@COF for C, N, O, and Au, respectively.

The porous structure of COF provides a high specific surface area, which was expected to enhance the immobilization density of aptamers. Meanwhile, the dispersed AuNPs serve as anchoring sites for thiolated aptamers via Au–S bonds and improve the overall conductivity of the composite. However, as will be discussed below, the introduction of AuNPs also partially blocks the COF pores, affecting the mass transport of redox probes – a trade-off that must be carefully balanced in interface engineering.

Stepwise fabrication of the aptasensor

EIS is a powerful tool to monitor the stepwise modification of the electrode surface because Rct is highly sensitive to the formation of insulating layers. Fig. 3B shows the Nyquist plots recorded at different fabrication stages. CV is also used to monitor the manufacturing process (Fig. 3A), in order to correlate with EIS spectra to monitor the signal trends generated during the sensor construction process. The exposed AuE shows a small semicircle (Rct ≈ 120 Ω). An interesting phenomenon has been observed, indicating efficient electron transfer at the clean gold surface. Although using COF and AuNPs@COF after electrode modification, the Rct of both electrodes increased, but they increased to 350 Ω and 400 Ω, respectively. Although further modification of the COF surface introduced gold nanoparticles with better conductivity, the result was a slightly weaker electron transfer effect, mainly due to the possible the partial blockage of COF pores by AuNPs (as observed by Fig. 2 and S6), which hinders the diffusion of the [Fe(CN)6]3−/4− probe toward the electrode surface. Thus, the porous structure – while beneficial for aptamer loading – can also impose mass-transfer limitations when its channels are obstructed.37 The trend of the CV chart is consistent in this situation. Further modifications have been made AuNPs@COF the electrode surface of the material was fixed onto the previously optimized (1 µM, 12 h) and modified aptamer with thiol groups. It was found that the Rct on the electrode surface increased to 700 Ω after the modification was completed, due to the repulsion of the negatively charged DNA backbone [Fe(CN)6]3−/4−. Subsequently, MCH was used to block the unreacted nano gold active sites, which further increased Rct to 900 Ω. Next, the sensor was reacted with METH (10 nM). Due to the induction of conformational changes in the nucleic acid structure by the recognition of aptamers with METH, which increased the hindrance to the active probe, a slight increase in Rct was observed to reach 1100 Ω. In order to construct a nucleic acid structural imprint for the specific recognition of MA, a polymer film was further introduced on this basis, and the resistance increased sharply to ∼2100 Ω. This is due to the formation of a dense polymer film layer, which greatly increases the transmission resistance of the indicator probe and hinders the transfer of interface electrons. By using eluent to elute METH from the imprinting chamber, the molecular transport channels of [Fe(CN)6]3−/4− towards the electrode surface were re exposed, resulting in impedance values and CV curves almost returning to the level of the original polymer membrane. After reintroducing METH molecules, corresponding changes in response values will occur. The corresponding ΔRct values are summarized in Fig. 3B. These results confirm the success of the dual recognition function interface manufacturing on the electrode surface.

Optimization of experimental conditions

The key parameters have been optimized. Examine the incubation concentrations of electrode surface aptamers, set at 0.2, 0.5, 1, 2, 3 and 4 µM, respectively (Fig. 4). It can be observed that as the aptamer concentration increases during incubation, the Rct gradually increases. This is because the more surface aptamers sequences are “planted”, the greater the impedaned the redox probe. However, when the aptamer concentration exceeds 1 µM, the Rct response decreases with increasing concentration. This may be due to the dense arrangement of aptamer, which compresses the space for aptamer conformational changes. The optimal incubation time for aptamer can also be optimized by establishing the relationship between impedance values and time changes, and it can be observed that incubation can be completed within 12 hours (Fig. S4A). Further examination of the MCH sealing conditions reveals that soaking in a 1 mM concentration MCH solution for 30 minutes is sufficient. The MCH not only prevents nonspecific adsorption but also helps to orient the aptamer by displacing any physisorbed DNA segments, thereby enhancing the accessibility of the recognition domain. Finally, it is necessary to investigate the reaction time between the sensor and METH. To optimize the performance of the aptamer-MIP dual-recognition sensor, the incubation conditions of aptamer on the modified electrode (1 µM METH-aptamer, 12 hours at 25 °C) and the reaction time of aptamer and METH (20 minutes) were determined (shown in Fig. S4). It can be found that the sensor reaches the plateau period and the signal is maximum when exposed to METH solution for 20 minutes (Fig. S4B). From the optimized spectrum of reaction time, it can be seen that the performance (reaction time) of the constructed sensor is significantly better than other sensors, and by fitting the time response curve, it can be found that the recognition of METH by the sensor is based on an isothermal adsorption curve model, not the free diffusion type exhibited by general sensors. This may be due to the dual-mode recognition function accelerating the mass transfer efficiency at the solid–liquid interface. After completing parameter optimization, all subsequent experiments were conducted using these optimization conditions.

Analytical performance

The selectivity of the sensor towards METH was tested by measuring the ΔRct of 1 nM METH and 10 µM interfering substances (ketamine, norketamine, amphetamine, MDMA, MDA, cocaine, MPEA). As shown in Fig. 5, only METH generates a significant reaction signal, and the presence of other interfering substances almost does not produce a significant impedance signal. After mixing interfering substances with METH and conducting measurements, the competitive effects of coexisting interferences on the sensor's reaction with METH were investigated. It was found that the presence of these interferences had almost no effect on the METH signal. At the same time, the reliability of the sensor in different application scenarios (such as human tissue fluid and environmental water samples) should also be examined to verify the sensor's ability to accurately respond to METH molecules in the presence of high abundance interfering substances (serum proteins in tissue fluid, humic acid in water samples, etc.) in these samples. As shown in Fig. 6, there was no significant signal generated by the electrode under protein exposure at a concentration of 1 mg L−1. After mixing METH with these interferents for measurement, it can be found that when METH and interferents coexist, the signal hardly changes. This high selectivity is due to the inherent specificity of the adapter and the enhanced coordination of the imprinted structure.

It can be observed that the EIS reaction increases with the increase of METH concentration (Fig. 6A). The calibration plot (Fig. 6B) shows a linear relationship between ΔRct and the logarithm of METH concentration from 0.1 fM to 0.5 nM, and the regression equation ΔRct = 145.71 log C + 45.54 (R2 = 0.996). Identification of METH in real samples

To evaluate the practical applicability of the developed aptamer-MIP dual-recognition sensor, we determined the recovery of METH from seven spiked matrices: drinking water, river water, lake water, wastewater, tear, saliva, and urine (Table 2). These matrices can be broadly classified into three categories: clean water (drinking, river, and lake water), biological fluids (tear, saliva, and urine), and complex wastewater. As shown in Table 2, the average recoveries rates of METH in the clean water ranged from 91.4% to 103.7%, with RSD values between 3.2% and 9.0%, indicating good accuracy and precision. Biological fluids showed recoveries of 93.8% to 112.3% and RSDs of 2.8% ∼11.3%, also within acceptable limits for trace analysis. In contrast, wastewater samples exhibited the recovery of 103.5–112% but markedly higher RSDs (18.2–25.0%), suggesting that the matrix complexity significantly affects measurement reproducibility.

Conclusions

In this work, we have developed a novel electrochemical impedance sensor based on a synergistic dual-recognition strategy integrating a high-affinity METH-aptamer with a MIP. The exceptional performance of this sensor—an attomolar-level detection limit, a wide dynamic range spanning six orders of magnitude, and reliable recovery in diverse matrices including drinking water, river water, lake water, wastewater, and complex biofluids—is not fortuitous but rather arises directly from the synergistic integration of two core design innovations: the dual-recognition strategy and the AuNPs@COF interface engineering. The dual-recognition strategy—combining a newly screened METH-aptamer (KD = 213 nM) with a MIP—provides three synergistic benefits that collectively underpin the sensor's sensitivity and selectivity. First, the aptamer confers high-affinity molecular recognition, which serves as the primary driving force for target capture and directly contributes to the ultralow detection limit. Second, the MIP introduces spatial complementarity that imposes stringent steric constraints on target binding, significantly enhancing selectivity against structurally analogous interferents—a critical advantage over single-recognition platforms. Third, the MIP matrix physically shields the aptamer from nuclease attack, ensuring that the recognition element retains its binding activity even in complex biological matrices, which explains the sensor's robust performance in untreated saliva, urine, and wastewater samples. This “aptamer specificity + MIP spatial verification” dual mechanism effectively suppresses false-positive signals and enables reliable quantification in real samples. Beyond the specific detection of METH, the design paradigm established in this work—synergistic dual-recognition + precision interface engineering—holds significant promise for general applicability. By simply replacing the target-specific aptamer and the corresponding MIP template, this platform can be readily adapted for the ultrasensitive detection of other small-molecule targets, including illicit drugs, environmental pollutants, and disease biomarkers. We anticipate that this work will inspire broader exploration of hybrid recognition strategies combined with nanostructured interface engineering for next-generation electrochemical sensing applications, particularly in point-of-care diagnostics, forensic analysis, and environmental monitoring where both sensitivity and selectivity in complex matrices are paramount.
