Effects of age and cognitive features on comprehension of healthcare symbols in hospitals in Guangzhou
Authors: Lujie Deng, Wanting Wang, Pingxiu Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This study aimed to identify the impact of age and cognitive features of healthcare symbols on users’ comprehension and contribute to the design of an age-friendly hospital signage system. In this study, 50 younger and 50 older participants were recruited to interpret the meanings of 42 healthcare symbols and rate them on five cognitive characteristics. Healthcare symbols were categorized by cluster analysis into “three categories: high-performing symbols,moderate-performing symbols, and low-performing symbols”.The results of the test showed that younger adults outperformed older adults (74% vs. 57% correct rate, respectively; 9.55 s vs.11.92 s reaction time, respectively), with the age difference being most pronounced for moderate and low-performing symbols. Correlation analyses showed that all five cognitive features positively predicted symbol comprehension accuracy, but their predictive strength increased with symbol difficulty. Older adults relied more on familiarity, concreteness, and semantic accuracy when decoding low-performance symbols. Multiple regression confirmed identification accuracy as the primary predictor of cognitive ratings, with age and education also contributing significantly. Based on these findings, we recommend designing healthcare symbols for familiarity and concreteness, optimizing semantic precision, appropriately disseminating healthcare signage knowledge and training, and advancing the design of a universal healthcare signage system. The findings of this study may contribute to future hospital signage improvements and help to further improve the wayfinding dilemma of elderly users in healthcare environments.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-20128-0.

Introduction

Hospitals, as large and complex environments, pose significant wayfinding challenges1–3. With the development of medical technology and society, patients and visitors can easily get lost in modern urban hospitals due to their large floor space and numerous departments4,5. Studies have shown that lost in a hospital may lead to delays in patient visits, wastage of healthcare resources, and increased patient anxiety levels6,7. One of the critical reason for this phenomenon is that the graphic symbols of hospital signage systems are difficult to understand8–10, a challenge particularly acute among older adults11–14.

Signage provides critical visual information to assist wayfinding. Its purpose is to guide, inform, and identify, thereby improving users’ navigation efficiency15–17. Essential elements of signage such as text, graphics, color, material, shape, or size, are used to provide precise and specific instructions during the wayfinding process18. Compared with a single text message, signage with graphic symbols as design elements is easier to recognize and understand, and graphic symbols are also superior to text in terms of recall and recognition accuracy19–22. A good hospital signage system has more positive aspects than just helping users’ wayfinding, such as improving healthcare service effectively, lessening patients’ anxiety, and improving healthcare and medical results1,23–27, especially for older adults3,15. Therefore, it is important to provide an efficient hospital signage system for urban residents28.

Despite these benefits, healthcare-related symbols are often more difficult to interpret than general public symbols. This is partly due to medical concept signage that uses abstract or unfamiliar graphic symbols14,29, which are more difficult for older adults to understand. Due to visual impairments, cognitive decline, and reduced physical mobility, wayfinding concerns pose significant challenges for older adults30–33. Studies indicate that aging is associated with cognitive decline, leading to reduced ability to comprehend and remember graphic symbols, as well as a rise in navigational errors34–36. In addition, older adults experience a deterioration in visual performance-largely attributable to diminished selective attention, which impairs their capacity to focus on relevant information while filtering out distractions36,37.

The effectiveness of graphic symbol design is primarily evaluated based on recognizability and comprehensibility38,39. In the cognitive process of graphic symbols, the visual features are the main factors that influence their recognition and understanding40,41, such as size, color, text, contrast, clarity, and layout12,22,42,43. Also, cognitive features that affect the recognizability and comprehensibility of graphic symbols include Familiarity, Concreteness, Simplicity, Meaningfulness, and Accuracy of Semantic Description12,22,44,45. These cognitive features are not independent; rather, they interact and jointly affect users’ perception and understanding of symbols36,46–49.

Familiarity refers to how often a graphic symbol is encountered; greater familiarity enhances users’ comprehension of signage or icons20,45,50. Concreteness denotes the degree to which a symbol depicts recognizable objects, actions, or scenes; more concrete symbols are interpreted more quickly and accurately by users51–53. Such symbols maintain a high degree of restoration of the original reference in the design transformation process and contain more details familiar to the user, thereby increasing visual salience and recognizability12,21,38,39,45. Conversely, complex symbols (those with numerous or intricate elements) are generally understood more slowly and with less accuracy than simpler ones. And importantly, simplicity should not be defined solely by minimal detail; it must be assessed alongside other cognitive factors such as familiarity and meaningfulness12,45. However, the “Familiarity” of a symbol can come from the user’s familiarity with the frequency or “Concreteness” of the graphic symbol itself, it can also come from the user’s familiarity with the concepts represented by the symbol (e.g., “Pharmacy”, “Ophthalmology”), etc.. For example, a patient may be familiar with the concept of “Stomatology” but not “Oncology”; the corresponding symbols may also have high frequency and specificity, but because the user is not familiar with relevant concepts, it will also affect the user’s understanding of the symbols.

In addition, this situation involves symbolic “Accuracy of the Semantic Description” and “Meaningfulness”. “Accuracy of the Semantic Description” is a measure of the closeness of the relationship between the symbol and what it is intended to convey45. When this relationship is direct and unambiguous—such as using an eye icon to denote ophthalmology—users can immediately infer function and meaning, for example, use the “eye symbol” for “Ophthalmology”45. However, many symbols carry multiple potential interpretations: for example, the “cross symbol” may represent Pharmacy, First Aid, or Red Cross in different backgrounds, depending on context, and such ambiguity can impede accurate comprehension54. Accordingly, for novel or unfamiliar symbols, both semantic description accuracy and inherent meaningfulness are crucial during initial learning; as exposure increases, growing familiarity further enhances recognition and recall22,38,45. Moreover, the effects of concreteness and visual complexity on the perception of pictorial symbols also decreased as the familiarity with pictorial symbols increased52,57. Thus, distinguishing between “symbol familiarity” (how well the form itself is known) and “conceptual familiarity” (how well the referent is understood), and clarifying the distinct roles of semantic accuracy and familiarity across learning versus memory phases, is essential for a comprehensive analysis of the cognitive features underpinning symbol comprehension in this study.

Therefore, understanding how user characteristics (e.g., age, gender, and education level) interact with these symbol attributes is essential for the design of effective signage. To provide a theoretical foundation for understanding this interaction, this study adopts the Communication-Human Information Processing (C-HIP) model proposed by Wogalter, DeJoy, and Laughery55,56. The C-HIP model breaks down symbol information processing into four stages: attention, encoding, comprehension, and response. Age-related differences can impact each of these stages57. The integration of the C-HIP model facilitates a systematic analysis of user characteristics-symbol attributes interactions across cognitive stages. This framework provides a structured foundation for examining symbol comprehension in this study.

Consequently, this study aims to investigate the effects of age and cognitive characteristics on the comprehension of healthcare signage symbols. The findings are expected to provide a theoretical basis and practical guidance for the design of age-friendly hospital signage systems.

Methods

Participants

A total of 100 participants were recruited for this study, including 50 older adults (65–80 years) and 50 younger adults (18–45 years).To ensure that the sample was broadly representative, we intentionally attempted to recruit participants of different ages, genders, and educational backgrounds. Regarding the test procedure and sample size, we followed the ISO 1984:2001 procedure to test graphic symbols, and the ISO protocol requires a minimum of 50 subjects29,58; according to Corbetta, sample size may be smaller if group homogeneity is high59. In this study, participants were screened for visual acuity and cognitive ability test (Mini-Mental State Examination, MMSE) before enrolment, and all subjects had normal corrected visual acuity and were excluded from the study if they were known to be cognitively impaired. Vision screening was conducted via the Super Vision Manager app, and participants had corrected near visual acuity (e.g., for presbyopia) of 1.0 (Snellen equivalent at 30 cm). The MMSE was developed by Folstein and Mc Huge in 1975. It assesses orientation, attention, memory, language, oral comprehension and behavior, and constructive skills60. These measures ensured that the participants were able to recognise the symbols presented on the screen and that the results of the experiment were reliable.

Written informed consent was obtained from all participants before testing. All participants were informed that they may withdraw from the study without any queries or negative consequences. Participants’ identities and personal details are kept confidential, and only the researcher and supervisor can access the original data and recorded information.

All procedures involving human participants were conducted in accordance with the ethical standards of the institutional or national research committee and with the 1964 Helsinki Declaration and its subsequent amendments or comparable ethical guidelines. Ethical approval for this study was obtained from Guangdong Provincial People’s Hospital, where the research was carried out.

Research sites

The healthcare symbols in this study were collected from three large general hospitals in the centre of Guangzhou: (A) Guangdong Provincial People’s Hospital (Yuexiu District); (B) Gzucm No.3 Affiliated Hospital(Liwan District); and (C) The Second Affiliated Hospital of Guangzhou Medical University (Haizhu District). Among them, Hospital A was established the earliest and has the largest number of departments (n = 75) and beds (n = 2852), and is also the hospital that serves the largest elderly population in the area (n = 314,300), followed by Hospitals C and B. Measures and procedures

42 representative healthcare symbols were selected for comprehensibility testing (Table 3). These symbols were initially photographed in situ from the signage systems of three hospitals; duplicates and incorrect, or damaged icons were excluded. The photographs were then vectorized in Adobe Illustrator to create standardized black-on-white graphics. In the experiment, participants saw only the symbol images, without any accompanying text, displayed as solid black figures on a white background, ensuring that text cues did not influence comprehension, as shown in Table 2.

The pre-test presentation consisted of (1) an introduction to the source of the 42 healthcare symbols, an informed consent form, and (2) a walkthrough of how to perform the test. Each participant completed the 42 healthcare symbol comprehension experiments in a randomized order, following the methodology of Liu& Ho12and Rousek Hallbeck62. Testing occurred in a quiet laboratory under standardized lighting. Symbols were presented on a 14-inch LCD monitor (1920 × 1080 resolution) positioned 60 cm from each participant’s eyes, all of which were displayed in a 20 cm × 20 cm square (without borders), as shown in Fig. 1. By calculation, each symbol occupied approximately 19° × 19°of the visual field horizontally and vertically, within which symbol details remained clearly discernible. Two experimenters were needed to control the experimental procedure. Experimenter A showed the symbols to the participants and asked them to observe the symbols and respond verbally. Reaction time (RT) was manually recorded by Experimenter B using a digital stopwatch (accuracy: 0.01 s). During each trial:(a) Experimenter A displayed the symbol on a 14-inch screen and signaled stimulus onset;(b) Experimenter B initiated timing upon this signal;(c) Timing ceased upon the participant’s verbal response. To enhance accuracy, experimenters received training on timing procedures, and all sessions were video-recorded. Post-test, each participant’s RT was verified and adjusted if necessary based on video review (Table 3).

They then rated each of the 42 healthcare symbols on a 10-point Likert scale (1 = does not exhibit the characteristic; 10 = exhibits the characteristic exceptionally well), as illustrated in Fig. 2.Fig. 2Example of assessment of the cognitive features of symbols.

Statistical analysis

The collected data were subjected to a series of analyses using SPSS 25.0. First, internal consistency of the five cognitive-dimension ratings was assessed via Cronbach’s α. Next, raw scores were coded and summarized with descriptive statistics (mean ± SD). For performance categorization, K-means clustering was applied to z-score–normalized measures of comprehension accuracy and reaction time. To evaluate the effects of age and symbol category, two-way ANOVA analyses were used to compare differences in symbol correctness and reaction time between the age groups. A two-way ANOVA examined age × cognitive dimensions interactions to confirm whether age-related comprehension differences aligned with changes in cognitive ratings. In addition, Pearson correlation analyses explored associations between each cognitive feature and symbol accuracy within and across age groups. Finally, multiple linear regression identified predictors of comprehension accuracy, including age, education level, and symbol category, with variance inflation factors checked to rule out multicollinearity.

Reliability and validity testing

SPSS 25 software was used to test the reliability of the results of the test of 42 healthcare symbols by the participants, and the chosen level of statistical significance was P < 0.05. The α coefficient of this experiment was 0.923, which reflects a high degree of internal consistency in the intelligibility ratings of each symbol, indicating that the data results are credible.

Statistical efficacy of sample size

To evaluate the adequacy of the sample size, this study compared the overall mean correct rate across 42 symbols between the two groups and conducted a post hoc sensitivity analysis in SPSSAU: with a significance level of α = 0.05, group sample sizes of n = 50 vs. 50, and a target power of 1–β = 0.80, the analysis for an independent-samples t-test yielded power > 0.95 (the software may round this to 1.00), suggests that the sample size is sufficient to examine the effect.

Categories of symbols

In this study, K-means clustering was applied to two behavioral indices—mean accuracy and mean reaction time to classify the 42 healthcare symbols into three performance categories (Table 7).Category I (High-performing symbols;n = 17) exhibited the highest comprehension rates and shortest response times; Category II (Moderate-performing symbols;n = 16) showed intermediate performance; and Category III (Low-performing symbols; n = 9) had the lowest accuracy and longest reaction times.This data-driven classification allows us to systematically analyze how age groups differ in interpreting symbols of varying difficulty levels. Specifically, high-performing symbols achieved mean accuracy rates of 84% (younger) and 74% (older) with mean reaction times of 3.29 s and 5.45 s, respectively. Moderate-performing symbols reached 70% and 51% accuracy with reaction times of 9.36 s and 11.69 s, while low-performing symbols recorded 60% and 37% accuracy and reaction times of 23.29 s and 26.14 s for younger and older groups. Participants’ ratings on familiarity, concreteness, simplicity, meaningfulness, and semantic depiction accuracy are summarized in Tables 8, 10, 11. Notably, as seen in the results for Moderate-performing and Low-performing symbols, both young and old participants still misunderstood or failed to fully understand many of symbols due to unknown or misunderstood meanings of the symbols. For example, for the symbols () “Internal Medicine Department” and() “Surgery Department”, although the stethoscope and scissors images in the two symbols were recognized, participants did not connect them to their medical functions. Similarly, the Oncology Department symbol() was too unfamiliar for accurate interpretation.

Discussion

Symbol classification analysis

The cluster analysis revealed that 16 of the 42 symbols are at the moderate-performance tier and 9 are at the low-performance tier, indicating that approximately 60% of the symbols have comprehension problems, especially for older adults. Older participants’ correct rates for moderate-and low-performing symbols were roughly 20% lower than those of younger participants (Table 6; Table 7). Such misunderstandings can affect older adults’ wayfinding efficiency in real healthcare settings: older patients who misinterpret departmental icons risk becoming disoriented, missing appointments7,66; this process can also increase their anxiety and lead to a worse experience of medical care7,67–69. While healthcare organisations incur higher administrative costs and reduced operational efficiency6,7,28.

One reason for the moderate-performing symbols is that they are easily misinterpreted, and the user may be able to guess partially correctly, but not accurately enough. This confusion may come from the visual similarity between the symbols. For instance, the icons for “Nephrology Department()” and “Urology Surgery Department()” share analogous elements: graphics in the upper left corner denote “Internal Medicine Department()” and “Surgery Department ()” respectively, while identical right-side elements induce misinterpretation. Similarly, the symbol B-Ultrasound Room of Gynecology()was easily confused with Gynecology Department () and Obstetrics Department (). Each of these symbols uses a graphic representing a woman or a pregnant woman; users would interpret it to mean Obstetrics and Gynaecology or Gynecology Department, not B-Ultrasound Room of Gynecology.

In the category of incomprehensible Low-performing symbols, the “Familiarity”, “Accuracy of semantic depiction”, and “Concreteness” of the symbol are the main factors that make it difficult for users to understand. Consistent with prior work20,45,50, figurative symbols with concrete referents (e.g., Ophthalmologyand Stomatology icons) elicited higher accuracy. Users have a higher rate of correctly comprehending graphic symbols that contain specific character images, actions, and scenes because these symbols are designed to be more restorative of the original referent and contain more details familiar to the user during the transformation process50,70, that is, the semantic description is more accurate. In contrast, the lower correctness rate for Moderate and Low-performing symbols is due to users’ lack of familiarity and semantic ambiguity with these graphical symbols. Consequently, in signage systems, the more familiar and specific the graphical symbols are, the closer their semantic distance is, which is conducive to enhancing the efficiency of users’ recognition of signage and further guiding their wayfinding behaviors12,43,70.

However, this correspondence is not absolute, and there is a difference between familiarity with the symbol itself and familiarity with the concept to which it refers. Symbol familiarity refers to the degree to which users are familiar with the frequency of occurrence of the graphic form in daily or other environments, while concept familiarity is the degree to which users understand the function or section concept itself represented by the symbol. Again, using the symbols representing “Internal Medicine Department”() and “Surgery Department”() as examples, () directly represents “stethoscope” and ()represents “scissors and knife.” Here we can see in healthcare settings, these two symbols are given a more precise and specialized meaning because they are related to medical terminology, which makes it difficult for users to associate the two symbols() ()with the concept of “Internal Medicine Department” and “Surgery Department” respectively. In other words, the stethoscope is more common and familiar in daily life, and users are familiar with its image, but it is difficult to relate it to a specific medical department, resulting in a vague semantic depiction11,43. In addition, the symbol may correspond to a variety of departments, such as internal medicine, cardiology, etc., in an actual hospital, and its polysemy may also interfere with the user’s accurate understanding. It is thus clear that the process of symbol comprehension depends not only on symbol familiarity in the visual recognition stage but also on the ability to map concepts in the subsequent interpretation stage. In order to mitigate ambiguous designs with multiple meanings or inaccurate semantic descriptions, the graphic should be kept visually intuitive and legible and enhance the semantic elements (e.g., figurative details, semantic cues, supporting text, or colour coding).

Prospective user factors

Consistent with previous findings, many healthcare symbols remain challenging to interpret8–10, especially for older adults11–14, as shown in Tables 6 and Table 7.

Although gender did not predict comprehension performance, age showed a robust association: older participants averaged 57% accuracy and 11.92 s response time versus 74% and 9.55 s for younger participants. We can see that both the younger and older groups misunderstood some of the symbols tested, but the reasons for the misunderstandings were different. The root causes of misunderstanding differed by age. For example, the symbol for “Operating Room” (), Many older people misinterpret it as “Medical Examination Room” or “Ward.” In addition, the “OPD Office” () icon was sometimes seen as a “reading room” because it depicts a person at a desk. These errors reflect older users’ reduced ability to map familiar forms onto specialized medical concepts, despite intact recognition of basic shapes.

Symbol familiarity and concreteness emerged as key determinants of successful interpretation. However, it is worth noting that during the actual visit, the symbol comprehension effect may be higher for older patients who visit the hospital frequently than for first-time visitors, as they may be more familiar with the hospital environment and healthcare symbols50,64. On the other hand, due to factors such as habit and learning pressure, older participants may prefer to keep themselves confined to familiar environments, and some of the health care symbols may be unfamiliar to them, which may affect their understanding of the health care symbols to some extent. This has been explored in several studies by comparing symbol comprehension efficiency before and after training. The findings from Ou& Liu43, Shen&zhang70, and Lesch Horrey et al.11 indicated that there is a significant positive aspect of symbol comprehension for the different user groups after the training program. This is because training enhances symbol familiarity, which helps users to recognize complex icons and recall relevant semantic information.

Previous studies show that physiological activity slows down with age in older participants and that visual processing speed and visual attention are also worse in older adults than in younger adults36,71–73. These findings align with symbol comprehensibility tests showing that older users require more time to recognize graphic symbols, spend longer searching for directional signage, and experience greater disorientation in unfamiliar environments than younger participants11,49,74. Consequently, diminished search efficiency, prolonged information processing intervals, and age-related motor delays collectively contribute to increased travel times and slower arrivals at destinations among older adults.

Overall, in terms of the effect of age on symbol comprehension, “High-performing symbols” improved users’ information processing and reaction time, especially for elderly participants. In the categories of “Moderate-performing and Low-performing symbols”, older participants perceived lower familiarity, concreteness, and lower accuracy of semantic descriptions as a more significant challenge than younger participants. This result suggests that designers should focus on the familiarity, concreteness, and accuracy of semantic descriptions of age-friendly symbols when applying ergonomic principles and practices12,75.

Additionally, the results of this study demonstrated that participants’ educational level also affects symbol comprehension performance, with those with higher levels of education having higher rates of correctness, faster response times, and being able to comprehend more of the 42 symbols, as shown in Table 18. Previous studies have shown that participants with higher levels of education have a more accurate understanding of graphic symbol information in signage systems6,29, perhaps since those with higher levels of education have a more significant knowledge base of the relevant fields and a greater familiarity with healthcare terminology and symbols. Similarly, research from Al-Madani and Al-Janahid has shown that Western drivers (from developed countries) have a significantly better understanding of traffic signs compared to drivers from other countries76, and the reason for this finding may be attributed to differences in economic levels and differences in the development of wayfinding systems.

However, the influence of educational background observed here may extend beyond general knowledge, potentially reflecting familiarity with domain-specific terminology, as suggested by research linking specialized knowledge to product comprehension77. It may be worthwhile to investigate the speculation of healthcare symbols by related professionals, such as healthcare professionals and designers. This suggests that cognitive differences and limited healthcare knowledge may lead to misunderstanding, highlighting the need for learning and familiarization64,75.

Cognitive features of the symbols

Previous studies have shown that familiarity, concreteness, simplicity, meaningfulness, and accuracy of semantic depiction affect comprehension and guessability of symbols10,18,22,41,45. Similarly, correlation and subgroup analyses (Section "Differential analysis of symbol categories and cognitive features") demonstrate that all five cognitive features: familiarity, concreteness, simplicity, meaningfulness, and semantic depiction accuracy are positively associated with symbol comprehension (Table 15). However, the strength of these associations varies by task difficulty and by age. For high-performing symbols (Category I), correlations with overall accuracy were modest, indicating that when symbols are well designed, deeper cognitive appraisals play a smaller role. In contrast, moderate-and low-performing symbols (Categories II-III)showed progressively stronger correlations, with familiarity, concreteness, meaningfulness, and semantic accuracy emerging as the best predictors in challenging tasks. Prior research has also shown that symbols with high familiarity and concreteness also have advantages in improving user meaningfulness and semantic description10,64,75. In addition, the age group comparisons further reveal that older adults rely even more heavily on these features under high-difficulty conditions.

Taken together, these results underscore a graded model of symbol comprehension: for simple, concrete icons, design quality alone suffices; as symbol complexity increases, user-centered factors, especially familiarity, concreteness, and semantic precision, become critical, and even more so for older users. Future work should therefore prioritize enhancing these cognitive features in symbol design, for example by embedding literal referents or contextual cues, and by supporting learning through consistent exposure.

Suggestions for healthcare symbol design improvement

The results of this study indicated that when the symbols are familiar or specific, users recall the symbols more accurately and quickly, which is because familiar graphical symbols and their associated information are more readily encoded and maintained in working memory70. Therefore, designers should use real-life familiar figurative elements, e.g., use the human figures (or partial human figures) to design healthcare symbols and enhance the semantic distance and meaningfulness of the symbols so that users can clearly understand the meaning of the symbols and respond quickly11,20,21,28,78.

However, the findings also suggest that even when a healthcare symbol appears familiar and concrete, users may still misinterpret its meaning due to a lack of relevant conceptual knowledge. Here, the user’s relevant knowledge base and visiting experience become particularly important. Studies revealed that learning experience affects the recognition of medical symbols, especially for novice and inexperienced users11,43. Accommodations such as appropriate learning and orientation materials can be effective in enhancing older adults’ understanding of healthcare symbols. In China, community outreach and promotion at senior universities may provide significant benefits to older adults. However, for densely populated metropolitan areas with large elderly populations with varying levels of education, comprehensive training can be costly and have limited impact, making it challenging to provide healthcare symbol recognition training for older adults in terms of practical roll-out. Therefore, a more realistic strategy would be a combination of improved symbol design and optimised signage systems, such as improving the intuitiveness and consistency of the symbols, together with the provision of the necessary guidance and promotional materials.

However, in a real healthcare setting, more environmental and individual factors will affect users’ understanding of signage, such as lighting, sign size, patient flow, and walking speed. Another effective way is to enhance standardization and promote universal design principles to maximize the universality of the hospital signage system4. As hospitals continue to upgrade and expand, existing hospital signage systems and healthcare symbols do not meet the needs of the general public, especially for older adults62,79,80. In the long term, standardizing healthcare symbols can accelerate the cognitive process of recognizing signage and interpreting its meaning. Moreover, employing consistent, universally understood graphic symbols benefits both users and providers by conveying shared concepts of people and behaviors64,81.

Conclusion

This study demonstrated significant effects of age and symbol cognitive features on the comprehension of healthcare symbols in Guangzhou hospitals. Our findings can offer empirical guidance for designing age-friendly healthcare symbols to improve wayfinding. The findings of this study show that younger adults approached the ISO-recommended comprehension standard, and older adults fell substantially below it, particularly for Moderate and Low-performing symbols, which suggests that differences in the speed of perceptual and cognitive processing brought about by age magnify the probability of error. Among the five cognitive features assessed, “Familiarity” and “Accuracy of semantic depiction” are the most relevant to the correct rate of comprehension, followed by “Concreteness,” suggesting that the fit of symbols to concepts and the familiarity of the audience are the key to improving comprehension. Therefore, designers should prioritize creating symbols that are both highly familiar to the target user group (especially older adults) and possess unambiguous semantic links to their intended meanings (high accuracy of semantic depiction), as these factors were the strongest predictors of comprehension in our study.

However, this study still has many limitations. One of the limitations of this study is the cultural specificity. All participants were from Guangzhou city, which is specific to the cultural environment and hospital signage design. Therefore, the results may not be generalizable to other populations or healthcare settings without further study. Future research could enhance the generalisability of the findings by repeating the experiment in different regions and cultural contexts, and incorporating a baseline assessment of participants’ prior knowledge of medical terminology and symbols24,34,51,77. Secondly, due to the limitations of the experimental environment and equipment, this study used manual recording of reaction times, which may introduce some error. Future studies may consider using automated software (e.g., E-Prime or PsychoPy) for millisecond-precision timing and improve data reliability and interpretation of results. In addition, there may be a difference between testing symbol comprehensibility (with text labels and colors removed) in an experimental setting and testing users’ comprehension of complete signage in an actual hospital setting, because more design factors such as text labels, colors, sizes, spatial distances, and lighting often need to be included. Future studies could consider testing in real hospital settings and categorising symbols according to their design quality or subjective ratings to validate the applicability of laboratory findings.
