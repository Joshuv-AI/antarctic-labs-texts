MlPET: a localized neural network approach for probabilistic post-reconstruction PET image analysis using informed priors
Authors: Thomas Mejer Hansen, Nana Louise Christensen, Mikkel Holm Vendelbo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposePET images often make small lesions difficult to identify because of noise and system blur. We address this by developing and evaluating MlPET , a fast localized machine-learning method that approximates a computationally expensive probabilistic sampling approach while reducing noise and increasing spatial resolution.MethodsBuilding on a probabilistic deconvolution framework with informed priors, MlPET replaces computationally demanding Markov chain Monte Carlo sampling with a localized neural network trained to directly estimate the posterior mean of voxel activity from small image neighborhoods. The method incorporates scanner-specific point spread functions (PSF), spatially correlated noise modeling, and flexible prior information. Performance was evaluated on NEMA phantom data acquired on three PET systems (GE Discovery MI, Siemens Biograph Vision 600, and Siemens Biograph Vision Quadra Edge) under varying reconstruction settings and acquisition times.ResultsOn NEMA IEC phantom data, MlPET produced contrast-recovery coefficients consistently higher than PET and frequently close to 1.0 (including the 10 mm sphere in several settings), while simultaneously reducing background noise and improving spatial definition. The effective point-spread function (PSF) full width at half maximum (FWHM) was on average reduced from about 2 mm in PET to below 1 mm with MlPET , corresponding to roughly a 2.5× decrease in effective blur. Comparable image quality was obtained at 40–80 s acquisition time using MlPET versus 900 s with conventional PET. A clinical example in a breast cancer patient illustrates potential clinical applicability.ConclusionsMlPET provides a computationally efficient approach for quantitative probabilistic post-reconstruction PET image analysis. By combining informed priors with the speed of a neural network, it achieves both noise suppression and resolution enhancement. The method shows promise for improved small-lesion detectability and quantitative reliability in clinical PET imaging. Background

Positron Emission Tomography (PET) provides quantitative images of tracer uptake reflecting tissue metabolism and has become indispensable in oncology and functional imaging [5, 11]. Modern PET systems combine high sensitivity, time-of-flight (TOF) capability, and advanced reconstruction algorithms, yet accurate identification of small regions of increased uptake remains challenging. Image noise and limited spatial resolution, due to finite count statistics and system blurring, constrain lesion detectability [36, 39, 42, 44, 53].

Efforts to address these limitations can be broadly divided into pre-reconstruction and post-reconstruction approaches.

Pre-reconstruction methods incorporate physical and statistical models into iterative algorithms such as maximum-likelihood expectation-maximization (MLEM) [49], ordered-subsets expectation-maximization (OSEM) [18], or penalized likelihood frameworks [55]. Resolution modeling, including PSF-based corrections and system-matrix approaches derived from point-source measurements [39], and TOF information [27], further improve image quality but do not fully eliminate the trade-off between noise and resolution.

Post-reconstruction approaches operate on already reconstructed images rather than raw sinogram data, which offers a key practical advantage. The relationship between the observed activity in a voxel and the underlying true activity can be treated as a local problem, depending primarily on a small surrounding neighbourhood rather than the full volume. Classical techniques include Gaussian and edge-preserving filtering [40], wavelet- and patch-based denoising, and nonlocal means filtering [9], which reduce noise but at the cost of reduced spatial resolution.

More recently, deep learning has emerged as a dominant data-driven alternative for post-reconstruction PET enhancement. U-Net and encoder-decoder architectures have demonstrated effective noise suppression while preserving structural detail. Commercial implementations such as SubtlePET have entered clinical PET workflows [1], and a growing body of academic work has trained CNN denoisers on paired low- and full-count data [12, 31, 48, 51]. GAN-based methods have extended this supervised paradigm to low-dose PET synthesis, using adversarial training to produce sharper images [37, 60, 62], and have also been applied to PET super-resolution [45]. Iterative CNN-regularized reconstruction methods, such as BCD-Net [28], integrate learned denoisers as implicit regularizers within the reconstruction loop, achieving improved noise-resolution trade-offs compared to purely post-reconstruction denoising approaches. Although powerful, these supervised models are typically trained end-to-end on scanner-specific paired data and do not provide a transparent probabilistic model of the imaging process. Moreover, methods that strongly suppress noise often reduce contrast, whereas those enhancing sharpness tend to amplify noise [12].

A separate line of work seeks to reduce dependence on paired training data. Self-supervised and deep image prior schemes learn denoising from the noisy images themselves [7, 21, 50]. Such methods can be further guided by anatomical information: Onishi et al. [38], for example, incorporate CT or MR priors as additional inputs, trading the need for clean-PET labels for the requirement of co-registered anatomical scans. Anatomical and structural priors have a longer history in iterative PET reconstruction, including Markov random field formulations using segmented MRI for partial-volume correction [4] and anatomically-aided kernel-method reconstruction [23]; in such methods the prior is encoded implicitly through the regularizer or kernel rather than as an explicit, sampleable probabilistic model. Diffusion-based generative models have been applied to 3D low-count PET denoising [61] and to PET image reconstruction using a deep diffusion image prior [16] or supervised diffusion training [57]. Score-based approaches have been proposed as a means of sampling from an implicit conditional distribution over PET images [46], and posterior-mean denoising diffusion models have recently been used to produce realistic PET reconstructions [47]. While such generative methods can in principle produce multiple plausible reconstructions by varying the noise initialization, this variability reflects the stochasticity of the generative process rather than a principled estimate of posterior uncertainty conditioned on an explicit forward model of the imaging physics. They also remain computationally demanding at inference. Comprehensive reviews of deep learning approaches to PET image denoising and reconstruction are provided by Hashimoto et al. [22], Bousse et al. [3] and, with a specific focus on post-reconstruction noise reduction and resolution enhancement, by Liu et al. [30].

Some work has specifically addressed uncertainty quantification in reconstructed PET images. An early example is Filipović et al. [10], who reconstructed a posterior probability of the PET image, including multimodal images, within a Bayesian sampling framework. Carrillo et al. [8] combined the Deep Image Prior with stochastic gradient Langevin dynamics to recover per-voxel uncertainty on NEMA phantom data. Vlašić et al. [56] proposed generating approximate posterior samples using a conditional generative adversarial network conditioned on the low-dose reconstruction and an MRI prior. More recently, Levital et al. [29] introduced a non-parametric Bayesian deep learning framework using stochastic gradient Langevin dynamics within a U-Net to jointly estimate the reconstruction and its uncertainty. These methods share the goal of quantifying reconstruction uncertainty but operate primarily within or alongside the reconstruction pipeline. Their priors are implicit, embedded in network weights, rather than explicit, user-controllable components of a probabilistic model.

Probabilistic post-reconstruction analysis

To address these limitations, Hansen et al. [20] introduced a probabilistic framework for post-reconstruction PET analysis that integrates prior information, the PSF, and a correlated noise model into a single posterior probability distribution. Sampling this distribution using the extended Metropolis algorithm [32] yields an ensemble of PET images, each consistent with all available information. From these samples, statistical quantities such as the posterior mean activity, standard deviation, and the probability of elevated tracer uptake can be derived, enabling quantitative uncertainty analysis and improved lesion detectability.

However, the sampling approach is computationally demanding and currently limited to small image volumes. To address this, we build on the concept of Hansen and Finlay [15], who demonstrated in a geoscientific context that neural networks can be trained to directly estimate posterior statistics, without the need for explicit sampling. By adopting this idea for PET image analysis, we introduce a localized neural network that directly estimates the posterior statistics, such as the posterior mean voxel activity, from reconstructed PET data and informed priors.

This MlPET framework substantially reduces the computational cost of probabilistic PET analysis compared to the Metropolis-based sampling approach of Hansen et al. [20], while preserving the statistical rigor of the probabilistic formulation. It enables fast, voxelwise analysis of post-reconstruction PET images and naturally produces mean activity maps with both reduced noise and improved spatial resolution. To our knowledge, existing deep learning methods for PET denoising and low-dose reconstruction do not construct their training data by explicit simulation from a user-specified prior, scanner PSF, and noise model. Instead, they are trained on paired images, typically low-count and full-count reconstructions, so that the prior is implicit, and cannot easily be inspected, modified, or replaced.

In MlPET, training examples are generated entirely by simulation: 3D activity volumes are sampled from the prior , convolved with the scanner PSF, and corrupted with a realization of the noise model, producing synthetic data that reflect the full probabilistic model. No real PET images enter the training procedure. The network is then trained to map these simulated noisy observations directly to posterior statistics, so that at prediction time it estimates a specific and well-defined quantity, such as the marginal posterior mean voxel activity, under a fully specified probabilistic model. The prior is an explicit, user-controllable component of the inversion, allowing different prior assumptions to be tested transparently and their effect on the result to be directly assessed. This is in contrast to methods where the prior is an implicit consequence of the training data, outside the user’s control. Finally, because MlPET operates on reconstructed images rather than raw sinograms, it benefits from a key locality property: the observed activity in a voxel arises predominantly from activity within a small surrounding neighbourhood, rendering the inversion tractable as a series of low-dimensional local problems and enabling the same trained network to be applied uniformly across the entire image volume. If the same methodology was applied to the raw sinogram data, the relationship between the observed data and the underlying activity would be non-local, depending on the entire image volume, and require a network with an infeasibly large input dimension. In this work, we demonstrate and validate MlPET primarily on phantom data to establish a best-case performance benchmark, and discuss its potential for future application to clinical datasets.

Training

The networks were trained on N=800 000 realizations drawn from the prior, sufficient for a robust representation of the training distribution, using the ADAM optimizer [25] with an initial learning rate of , reduced by a factor of 0.99 at each epoch. The dataset was partitioned into training, validation, and blind test sets using a 70/15/15 split. To mitigate overfitting, early stopping was implemented, terminating training if the validation error failed to decrease for 20 consecutive epochs. For each posterior statistic, the network was trained five times with different random initialisations, and the model with the lowest blind-test MSE was selected for all subsequent analysis.

Choice of neighborhood size

Smaller neighborhoods reduce computational cost but can limit achievable resolution, whereas excessively large ones increase training time without additional benefit. The neighborhood must be sufficiently large to encompass the effective PSF and the spatial correlation of the noise [53, 58]. Accordingly, the minimal neighborhood size that covers both the PSF and noise correlation length in all dimensions was selected.

PET scanners and phantom data

The MlPET method was tested on NEMA IEC body phantom (NEMA) data [35] acquired on three PET systems: GE Discovery MI, Siemens Biograph Vision 600, and Siemens Biograph Vision Quadra Edge. The phantom contains six spherical inserts (10–37 mm diameter) with known activity concentrations. For the GE Discovery MI, images were reconstructed both with and without PSF modeling; Vision 600 and Quadra data were reconstructed with OSEM (TOF + PSF modeling, 4 iterations, 5 subsets). For the Quadra system, acquisition times ranged from 1 s to 900 s (leading to 11 reconstructed phantom data sets). All reconstructions included standard corrections (attenuation, scatter, randoms, decay, and normalization) and Gaussian post-filtering (2–4 mm).

The prior model

The prior probability density function (pdf) expresses prior beliefs about the underlying activity distribution , independent of the PET scanner, reconstruction algorithm, or noise model. In the present probabilistic framework, the prior provides an explicit way to incorporate medical or physical knowledge into the inversion process. Unlike implicit regularization methods, this approach requires a deliberate and transparent choice of prior model, which can then be visualized, validated, and discussed with clinical experts.

It is essential that the prior is constructed from biological or physical knowledge, not from reconstructed PET images, as these are already affected by blurring and noise. For medical imaging, prior information may come from clinical experience, tracer physiology, or known uptake patterns in specific tissues. For example, uptake of 18F-FDG is typically elevated in malignant or inflamed tissues due to increased glucose metabolism [2]. Pathological regions such as malignant lymph nodes often exhibit sharp boundaries in the in vivo activity distribution, reflecting abrupt transitions between high- and low-uptake tissues. Such features should be captured in the prior model, even if they are blurred in the reconstructed PET images [17].

No analytical expression for is required; only an algorithm capable of generating realizations of the prior. This allows explicit, flexible construction of priors that reflect realistic spatial patterns and variability in tracer uptake. Below, two representative priors are defined: one for controlled phantom data, and one for clinical breast cancer imaging.

Activity levels are modeled as spatially heterogeneous but structured. This reflects the physiological contrast between malignant and healthy tissues observed in FDG-PET.

Realizations of are generated using a hierarchical stochastic model. First, a 3D multivariate Gaussian field describes the smooth background uptake, scaled to the patient’s liver activity distribution. Localized high-uptake regions are then introduced using truncated Gaussian fields that define discrete, spatially correlated lesions with variable shapes and intensities. The size of the high uptake regions is controlled by the choice of range of the truncated Gaussian field, . The combination produces tracer distributions consistent with expected clinical variability in FDG uptake.

Siemens biograph vision 600 - Sensitivity analysis

Throughout this manuscript, the main focus is on the posterior mean as the primary MlPET output, as it is directly comparable to conventional PET images. However, at the end of this subsection we include a single example demonstrating that MlPET can equally estimate other posterior statistics, such as the posterior standard deviation and the probability of activity exceeding a threshold.

It is essential for any probabilistic inversion method that the noise model accurately represents the actual noise in the data, and that the chosen prior model is consistent with the true forward process. Even when care has been taken to estimate the noise characteristics and the PSF kernel, as described in Sect. “Estimation of the properties of the noise”, some uncertainty in these estimates will always remain. For instance, it is well known that running the OSEM algorithm for many iterations can affect image quality [6, 53, 59]. In particular, the number of iterations influences the statistical properties of the noise and may introduce artifacts such as undershooting or overshooting near sharp transitions in activity.

To evaluate the robustness of MlPET with respect to these uncertainties, we investigate the sensitivity of the results to both the amplitude of the noise model and the width of the PSF kernel used in the forward model (Eq. 1). MlPET is applied using five different noise models, where the noise amplitude is scaled to of the estimated noise level. This allows assessment of both underestimation and overestimation of the noise variance. Similarly, five PSF kernel widths are considered, corresponding to of the estimated PSF range. In total, 25 combinations of noise level and PSF range are analyzed to examine the sensitivity and stability of the probabilistic inversion process.

As expected, assuming an excessively high noise amplitude blurs the spheres and reduces spatial resolution but does not introduce spurious structure, whereas underestimating the noise level causes the algorithm to fit random fluctuations as signal, producing artificially fine details absent in the true activity distribution. To avoid such overfitting, and acknowledging that the 3D correlated Gaussian noise model may not perfectly represent the true noise characteristics, it is generally preferable to slightly overestimate the noise level when applying probabilistic inversion.

Similarly, overestimating the PSF range increases apparent image noise and blurring. The combination of an underestimated noise level and an overestimated PSF range produces the poorest results, characterized by overfitting.

Overall, the sensitivity analysis demonstrates that MlPET achieves both noise reduction (Fig. 7) and improved resolution, as quantified by the CRC (Fig. 8), relative to PET reconstruction when realistic estimates of the effective PSF and noise model are used. The results also indicate that the noise amplitude should not be underestimated. Using a slightly higher noise level than the estimated value provides robustness to uncertainties in both the PSF and noise characterization. This choice results in a minor loss of resolution but effectively prevents overfitting, while still yielding markedly better noise-resolution performance than conventional reconstruction.

In contrast, simultaneous underestimation of the noise level and overestimation of the PSF width produces the poorest results, characterized by strong overfitting and loss of quantitative accuracy. The best overall performance is obtained when using the inferred PSF width together with a modest overestimation of the noise level.

Based on these observations, in all subsequent analyses we increase the noise level by 20% above the estimated value (NL = 120%) and reduce the PSF range to 80% of the estimated value ().

These observations reflect a general principle of probabilistic inversion: the forward and noise models are almost always approximations of the true physical system, and discrepancies between model and reality, referred to as modeling errors, are inevitable when characterizing any physical imaging system. Acknowledging modeling errors explicitly, rather than fitting them as data signal, is what motivates the practical recommendation above. This applies to the probabilistic inversion framework in general, not only to the machine learning component of MlPET .

Comparing MlPET to rejection sampling

The true posterior distribution cannot be computed analytically for the sampling-based prior used here. However, the rejection sampler can generate a finite sample from the posterior, providing a reference against which MlPET can be compared. As the rejection sampler becomes intractable for large neighborhoods, we restrict this comparison to a reduced 2D neighborhood of voxels, where it remains computationally feasible.

GE discovery MI – PSF modeling

In clinical practice, PET reconstruction algorithms often incorporate point-spread-function (PSF) modeling to enhance spatial resolution. While this generally improves contrast recovery, it may also introduce artifacts such as edge overshoot or ringing, which in some cases motivates the use of reconstructions without PSF modeling [33, 43, 52]. Here, we apply the MlPET method to NEMA phantom images reconstructed both with and without PSF modeling using data from the GE Discovery MI scanner, in order to evaluate the impact of PSF modeling on image quality and quantitative accuracy.

Figure 10a and b show transaxial slices through the centers of the six NEMA spheres obtained using PET reconstruction without (a) and with (b) PSF modeling. As expected, the reconstruction without PSF modeling (Fig. 10a) exhibits substantially higher noise compared to the reconstruction with PSF modeling (Fig. 10b). Figure 10c and d present the corresponding posterior mean activity concentrations estimated using MlPET , again applied to data reconstructed without (c) and with (d) PSF modeling. In both cases, MlPET markedly reduces noise relative to the standard reconstructions and provides visibly enhanced spatial resolution.

Two-dimensional x-slice through the centers of the six spheres in the NEMA phantom, showing PET reconstructions without PSF (a) and with (b) PSF modeling in the reconstruction, and the corresponding activity concentration estimated using the MlPET algorithm (c, d)

These visual observations are supported quantitatively by Fig. 11, which shows a clear reduction in the effective PSF width and a significant decrease in background noise, measured as the standard deviation within a uniform background region. Collectively, these results demonstrate that MlPET improves both spatial resolution and noise characteristics compared with conventional PET reconstructions, regardless of whether PSF modeling is included in the reconstruction process.

Effective PSF FWHM (black bars, left axis) and background noise standard deviation (gray bars, right axis) for the GE Discovery MI, comparing PET and MlPET with and without PSF modeling in the reconstruction

Figure 11 compares the estimated noise level in the low-activity region and the FWHM of the effective PSF obtained from PET and MlPET , both without and with PSF modeling. It is clear that MlPET achieves lower noise and higher spatial resolution (smaller PSF width) than PET reconstruction, irrespective of whether PSF modeling is used. The PSF width drops from around 2.5 mm using conventional PET to around 1 mm using MlPET , an effective blur reduction factor of around 2.5. As expected, for conventional PET reconstruction, incorporating PSF modeling reduces the estimated noise level. In contrast, when applying MlPET , the estimated noise level is nearly identical for reconstructions with and without PSF modeling. This finding suggests that when MlPET is used, one can choose to disregard PSF modeling during PET reconstruction, and obtain similar results with MlPET .

Siemens biograph vision quadra - reduced scan time

The Siemens Biograph Vision Quadra Edge is a long axial field-of-view (LAFOV) total-body PET/CT system that enables simultaneous imaging of large body regions with high sensitivity and spatial resolution [41]. Its extended axial field of view allows for substantially shorter scan times compared to conventional PET scanners while maintaining high image quality. Reducing scan duration is particularly advantageous for patients who may struggle to remain still, such as children or individuals in pain, since minimizing motion artifacts and the need for sedation improves diagnostic reliability and patient comfort. Reduced acquisition time without loss of diagnostic quality or quantitative accuracy has been demonstrated on conventional PET/CT [19], and the long-axial-FOV Quadra system has been shown to extend this gain considerably [24], increasing scanner throughput and reducing radiation exposure.

In this study, we evaluate the performance of MlPET applied to data from the NEMA phantom acquired on the Siemens Biograph Vision Quadra Edge. The same data set was reconstructed using a range of acquisition durations, t = [1, 2, 5, 10, 20, 40, 80, 160, 320, 640, 900] seconds, resulting in 11 reconstructed PET images used to assess MlPET performance for different scan times.

Importantly, MlPET attains high contrast recovery at substantially shorter acquisition durations, with 40–80 s scans yielding CRC values that exceed those of conventional PET even at 900 s.

The evaluation using phantom data from the Siemens Biograph Vision Quadra demonstrates that MlPET provides improved contrast recovery, reduced noise, and enhanced spatial resolution compared to conventional PET reconstruction when applied to the NEMA phantom. In this controlled setting, MlPET with 40-80 s acquisitions achieved image quality metrics comparable to or exceeding those of conventional PET at 900 s, indicating the potential for a substantial reduction in required scan time.

These results, however, are based solely on phantom data acquired under idealized conditions. The NEMA phantom is a simplified model with known geometry, uniform background, and spherical lesions of well-defined activity ratios. Clinical PET imaging presents additional challenges, including irregular lesion shapes, heterogeneous backgrounds, patient motion, variable tracer uptake, and anatomical variability, factors not represented in phantom experiments. Further validation using patient data is therefore essential to determine potential performance improvements in a clinical setting and to assess the robustness of the method across diverse patient populations and pathological conditions before any conclusions can be drawn about clinical applicability.

All three MlPET images demonstrate reduced variability in activity concentration in the region surrounding the lymph node compared to standard OSEM reconstruction. The choice of prior range parameter notably influences the image characteristics. Using a prior representing very small features ( mm, Fig. 15b) produces the sharpest image, revealing potential small high activity regions at multiple locations, as evidenced by the highest peak intensity in the profile (Fig. 15e). Assuming larger lesions ( mm, Fig. 15d) leads to overall smoothing, consistent with the prior assumption that no features much smaller than the prior range exist.

This behavior is a direct consequence of the prior: a smaller assigns higher prior probability to small high-activity features, which is reflected in the posterior mean as increased sensitivity to such features. Conversely, a larger assigns lower prior probability to features smaller than the range, and the posterior mean accordingly suppresses them. The comparison across different values of therefore represents an explicit investigation of different hypotheses about lesion size, where each result is the posterior mean under a specific, well-defined assumption.

For this particular case, r=14 mm (Fig. 15c) represents a well-balanced choice, as it clearly reduces noise while enhancing resolution and contrast at the lymph node location, without introducing excessive small high activity features. The improved contrast ratio suggests that the true activity concentration may be higher than indicated by the conventional PET image, implying enhanced detectability of the small lesion associated with the lymph node.

The ability to specify prior assumptions about lesion size represents a strength of the MlPET method, enabling clinical end users to tailor the MlPET analysis to search for lesions with specific properties, such as expected size characteristics. Overall, this single clinical example indicates improved sensitivity and reduced image noise when using MlPET compared to PET reconstruction, consistent with the findings from the phantom analysis. Nevertheless, a larger study including diverse patient data and appropriate quantitative validation is required before any conclusions can be drawn regarding the clinical applicability of the method.

Discussion

Relation to prior work

MlPET is a practical realization of the probabilistic post-reconstruction framework introduced by Hansen et al. [20], made tractable by adopting the neural-network based posterior-statistics approach of Hansen and Finlay [15]. The present results demonstrate that this combination delivers efficient voxelwise inference across full 3D PET volumes while preserving the statistical rigor of the original probabilistic formulation, transforming probabilistic PET analysis from a theoretical framework into a practical methodology for routine post-reconstruction evaluation. Unlike data-driven denoising methods in which the prior is implicit in the training data, the prior in MlPET is an explicit, user-controllable component of the inversion that can be inspected, modified, and tested transparently.

The freedom to specify a prior also carries responsibility: a prior that is inconsistent with the true activity distribution will bias the posterior mean accordingly, and may introduce spurious features in the output. This risk is not unique to MlPET . Any image analysis method applied to an underdetermined inverse problem implicitly relies on prior assumptions, but in MlPET the prior is transparent and explicit rather than hidden. The effect of the prior can be directly visualized by comparing results obtained under different prior assumptions, as demonstrated in Fig. 15 for three choices of prior range parameter. This transparency allows the end user, in consultation with clinical expertise, to assess whether the chosen prior is consistent with the available evidence, and to treat results obtained under a specific prior as a hypothesis to be tested rather than a definitive answer.

Computational performance and practicality

The MlPET workflow is computationally efficient, allowing practical post-reconstruction analysis of full 3D PET datasets. A typical training dataset consisted of N = 800 000 neighborhood-based training examples. Generating the training data required approximately 44 s in total: sampling prior realizations took 26 s, computing the PSF-blurred forward response took 9 s, and adding noise realizations took 9 s. Network training required approximately 90 s per run; since the network is trained five times with different random initialisations and the best model selected (Sect. “Estimation of the effective PSF”), the total training time was approximately 450 s. Application of the trained network to a full 3D PET volume took approximately 16 s on a standard workstation. The complete workflow (training data generation, network training, and prediction) therefore required approximately 510 s (about 8.5 min) in total.

The original probabilistic deconvolution framework of Hansen et al. [20] formulated PET image analysis as a Bayesian inverse problem and relied on explicit posterior sampling using the extended Metropolis algorithm. In that approach, sampling even a small 2D subset of approximately 90× 90 voxels required several hours, and extending the analysis to full 3D volumes was computationally prohibitive: the enormous memory demands and high dimensionality of the posterior space make such random walks infeasible, as obtaining statistically independent samples would require an unmanageable number of iterations. By replacing explicit sampling with a localized neural network trained to estimate posterior statistics directly, MlPET overcomes these limitations and provides a speed-up of several orders of magnitude, enabling efficient estimation of posterior statistics for large-scale 3D PET data.

Direct quantitative comparison of MlPET with established deep learning denoising methods (e.g., U-Net or GAN-based approaches) on clinical data is an important direction for future work. Such comparisons will need to account for the fundamental differences in inputs, training data, and the statistical interpretation of the outputs, since MlPET estimates a well-defined posterior quantity under an explicit probabilistic model, whereas conventional DL methods produce point estimates without an explicit forward model or prior.

Reduced scan time

Several commercial and research methods have been developed to enable reduced-count or short-duration PET imaging. Deep learning approaches such as SubtlePET and related CNN- or GAN-based techniques have demonstrated effective noise suppression at 30–50% of standard counts [1, 26, 51, 60]. However, these methods typically rely on large scanner-specific paired datasets, which limits their generalizability [3, 22]. In contrast, MlPET achieves comparable improvements in image quality with straightforward retraining for individual scanners or protocols and, unlike purely data-driven methods, simultaneously enhances both noise and resolution through explicit probabilistic and physical modeling. This capability suggests that substantial reductions in acquisition time may be achievable while maintaining diagnostic image quality.

Clinical applicability and future perspectives

The present evaluation was based primarily on controlled phantom experiments, enabling quantitative benchmarking of contrast recovery, noise, and resolution. A single clinical example was also included to demonstrate the potential applicability of MlPET to patient data. In this case, the method produced qualitatively similar improvements to those seen in the phantom studies, showing visibly reduced noise and enhanced lesion contrast. While this result is encouraging, it represents only an initial demonstration. For evaluation of the diagnostic potential further studies are required. In real-world imaging, factors such as heterogeneous tissue composition, irregular lesion morphology, tracer kinetics, and patient motion will influence both the data and the suitability of the chosen prior model. A key strength of MlPET is its ability to incorporate informed priors that reflect domain-specific physiological knowledge. In many applications, particularly brain imaging, such priors can be derived from anatomical atlases, regional uptake patterns, or kinetic models. Future work will focus on developing and validating such priors for clinical use, enabling robust probabilistic PET analysis across a wide range of imaging contexts.

Limitations and future work

The forward and noise models used in MlPET rely on explicit simplifying assumptions: a linear, spatially stationary isotropic Gaussian PSF, and a spatially stationary 3D correlated Gaussian noise field with a power-law amplitude-mean relation (Eq. 9). In practice, the effective PSF varies spatially and depends on local attenuation, and noise statistics may be non-stationary near activity boundaries. Both assumptions lead to modeling errors. As demonstrated in Sect. “Siemens biograph vision 600 - Sensitivity analysis” and Fig. 6, their practical consequence is a tendency towards overfitting when the assumed noise level is too low or the assumed PSF range too large. We address this directly by recommending a modest upward adjustment of the noise level and, if needed, a slight reduction of the PSF range relative to the estimated values.

Because the PSF and noise model parameters are scanner- and protocol-specific, they must be estimated from phantom data for each system and reconstruction setting (Sects. “Estimation of the properties of the noise” and “Estimation of the effective PSF”). In practice this is a modest setup cost: medical physicists routinely scan calibration phantoms with known geometry and activity for quality control, so suitable data are typically already available in clinical centres. Once the PSF and noise model have been estimated, the generation of training data, the training of the network, and its application to new images can be fully automated.

Future work will focus on extending the method to incorporate spatially varying PSF and noise models, on developing procedures for estimating model parameters directly from clinical data, and on a unified framework in which a single network accepts the PSF and noise model as inputs and can be applied across different scanners and protocols without retraining.

Conclusion

We have presented MlPET, a fast, localized machine learning framework for probabilistic post-reconstruction PET image analysis. By replacing computationally intensive sampling with an efficient neural network-based approximation, MlPET enables practical application of probabilistic analysis to full 3D PET volumes while explicitly incorporating prior information on tissue activity distributions, PSF characteristics, and correlated noise.

In controlled phantom studies across three PET systems (Siemens Biograph Vision Quadra Edge, Siemens Biograph Vision 600, and GE Discovery MI), MlPET yielded substantial improvements in key imaging metrics. Contrast recovery coefficients moved consistently toward unity across sphere sizes, with several conditions producing CRC values near 1.0 for the 10 mm sphere. The effective PSF FWHM decreased from above 2 mm in conventional PET to below 1 mm with MlPET , corresponding to roughly a 2.5× reduction in effective blur. Notably, MlPET images acquired at 40-80 s scan times exhibited image quality comparable to, or better than, conventional PET at 900 s.

These results were obtained under controlled phantom conditions, which provide well-defined geometry and known activity distributions but do not fully capture the complexity of clinical imaging, including heterogeneous tissue backgrounds, patient motion, and anatomical variability. The sensitivity analysis demonstrated that MlPET remains robust across a range of noise model parameters, with optimal performance when slightly overestimating the noise level. This is an encouraging indicator for clinical translation where precise noise characterization may be challenging.

Future work will focus on clinical validation using patient data, development of appropriate prior models for diverse clinical contexts, and assessment of whether the improvements demonstrated here translate into diagnostic benefit in routine PET imaging.
