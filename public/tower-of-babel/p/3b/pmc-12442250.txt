Nutritional Status and Outcomes Following Open Laryngeal Surgery for Laryngeal Cancer: A NSQIP Database Study
Authors: Emma R. Thompson, Nour Abdel‐Azim, Kenneth Yan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectivesMalnutrition and weight loss in patients with laryngeal cancer impair immune function and wound healing. Understanding how underweight status impacts outcomes is crucial to improving care. Accordingly, we evaluated perioperative outcomes of patients undergoing laryngectomy for laryngeal cancer, with a secondary focus on patients with recent unintentional weight loss.MethodsThis retrospective cohort study used the American College of Surgeons National Surgical Quality Improvement Program database. CPT codes were used to identify patients undergoing open total or partial laryngectomies for cancer. Only those with underweight (< 18.5) or normal weight (18.5–29.9) BMIs were included. A subgroup analysis assessed patients with recent unintentional weight loss. Univariate and multivariate analyses were performed, with significance set at p < 0.05.Results2012 patients were analyzed: 276 underweight (79 female, 197 male) and 1736 normal weight (306 female, 1430 male). Age distribution among underweight patients was 2.5% (ages 20–44), 76.1% (ages 45–69), and 21.4% (ages 70–90), compared to 2.9%, 66.7%, and 30.1%, respectively, in normal‐weight patients. Underweight patients were more likely to experience medical complications (p < 0.001), longer hospital stays (p < 0.001), wound disruptions (p = 0.002), and to have greater transfusion requirements (p < 0.001). In those with recent, unintentional weight loss, underweight individuals were at increased risk of experiencing medical complications (p = 0.046) and requiring a transfusion (p < 0.001), but were not more likely to experience wound disruptions (p = 0.119) or experience increased lengths of stay (p = 0.225).ConclusionUnderweight status and recent weight loss are associated with heightened risks of perioperative medical complications and transfusions. Preoperative optimization may enhance surgical recovery and outcomes.Level of EvidenceLevel 3.

Introduction

Patients with head and neck cancer experience high rates of malnutrition, which can impair immune function, delay wound healing, and increase their susceptibility to infections [1, 2]. This heightened risk is likely multifactorial, as tumor obstruction and dysphagia can hinder oral intake, while anatomical distortion and the side effects of both radiation and chemotherapy further contribute to a decline in nutritional status [3, 4, 5].

Among head and neck cancer patients, those with laryngeal or pharyngeal involvement routinely undergo open laryngeal surgery as part of their treatment [6, 7]. While surgery is a critical component of disease management, it also heightens the risk of weight loss and malnutrition, complicating recovery [8]. Perioperative malnutrition and nutritional deficiencies have been linked to increased incidence of post‐operative complications, including fistula formation, prolonged hospital stays, and greater costs of care [9, 10]. One study reported that laryngeal cancer patients with poor preoperative nutritional indices had worse overall prognosis than those with normal scores, while another found similar results for patients with BMIs < 18.5, underscoring the negative impacts of malnutrition [11, 12].

Despite this, the specific relationship between malnutrition and outcomes in open laryngeal surgery remains underexplored, with most studies focusing on the impact of chemotherapy and radiation on malnutrition [4, 13]. Furthermore, existing evidence is largely from single institutions with small sample sizes or from countries outside of the United States of America, where diet and other environmental factors likely significantly differ [1, 9, 10, 12, 14]. Other studies, including those discussed above, utilize measurements of malnutrition that are not endorsed by consensus groups, which recommend using a BMI < 18.5 as a proxy for malnutrition, or if available, the validated Malnutritional Universal Screening Tool (MUST) [12, 15, 16, 17].

To address these limitations, our study utilized the American College of Surgeons' National Surgical Quality Improvement Program (ACS NSQIP) database. This is a comprehensive, multicenter dataset that was chosen to provide a more detailed understanding of the impact of nutritional status on outcomes for patients undergoing open laryngeal surgery. This study aims to provide insights that can help guide perioperative management strategies, improve patient care, and ultimately enhance clinical outcomes for this vulnerable population.

Materials and Methods

The 2005–2020 ACS NSQIP database was queried for cases where the primary surgical procedure code, variable “CPT,” matched any of 10 concurrent procedure codes that describe either open total laryngectomies or open partial laryngectomies (Table 1). A total of 2606 cases were identified; 29 were initially excluded due to missing either height or weight data. The case identification and exclusion process is summarized in Figure 1.

Body mass index (BMI) was calculated using the formula BMI = weight (kg)/[height (m)]2. Patients were categorized as underweight (BMI < 18.5), normal weight (BMI 18.5–29.9), or overweight (BMI ≥ 30). The 429 patients classified as overweight were excluded, underweight patients comprised the study group, and normal‐weight patients served as the control group. Using diagnosis codes, 136 patients without cancer‐related diagnoses were also excluded. The NSQIP variable “discharge destination” was recoded to be a binary variable. Discharges to either the patient's home or a home facility were coded as “home,” while discharges to a facility—including hospice, rehabilitation, skilled or unskilled nursing facilities, or transitional care units—were coded as “non‐home.” The relationship between exposure, confounders, and outcomes in the analytic framework is outlined in Figure 2.

Relationship between potential confounders, exposures, and outcomes.

Statistical analyses, including Chi‐squared, Monte Carlo, and Mann–Whitney U tests, were performed using IBM SPSS software v29, with significance set at p < 0.05. Multivariable modeling was performed via binomial logistic regressions or negative binomial regressions for statistically significant findings, with covariates of weight category, race, functional health status, and smoking status. Using Python, post hoc power analyses were conducted for key outcomes where statistically significant differences between groups were observed. Effect sizes were calculated using Cohen's h for categorical outcomes and Cohen's d for continuous variables [18]. For variables that required it, pairwise analysis was performed in R software v4.4.1. Subgroup analysis was performed for patients in the “recent weight loss” group, defined by the NSQIP variable “> 10% loss of body weight in the 6 months prior to surgery.” A Non‐Human Research Exemption was received by the Institutional Review Board in New Brunswick, NJ (Pro2025000278).

Results

Baseline Characteristics

Among the 2577 laryngectomies performed, patients had a mean BMI of 24.88 (SD 6.11), a median of 24.4, and a maximum BMI of 92.84. Of the 2012 included cases, 276 (13.7%) patients had BMIs that classified them as underweight, and 1736 (86.3%) were within the normal weight BMI range.

Most patients were male, representing 71.4% of patients within the underweight group and 82.4% in the normal weight group (Table 2). The most common race was White (64.9% underweight group, 66.1% normal weight), followed by Black or African American (20.1% underweight group, 14.5% normal weight). Most patients were aged between 45 and 69 years, representing 76.1% of the underweight and 66.7% of the normal weight patients.

Preoperative Clinical Characteristics

The two BMI groups exhibited significant differences in several preoperative clinical characteristics (Table 3). Underweight patients had significantly higher rates of severe COPD (28.3% vs. 17.1%, p < 0.001) but were less likely to have diabetes mellitus (p < 0.001), hypertension requiring medication (p < 0.001), and more frequently reported smoking within the past year (70.7% vs. 46.1%, p < 0.001). Underweight patients were significantly less likely to have an independent health status, which represents their ability to perform activities of daily living without requiring any assistance (90.6% vs. 94.5%, p = 0.015). Underweight patients were also significantly more likely to be ventilator dependent before their surgery (2.2% vs. 0.7%, p = 0.036). No significant differences were found between BMI groups in rates of congestive heart failure, pneumonia at the time of surgery, or bleeding disorders (p = 0.545, p = 0.642, p = 0.868).

On multivariate analysis (Table S1), underweight BMI remained independently associated with higher odds of severe COPD (OR 1.68, 95% CI 1.24–2.28, p < 0.001), ventilator dependence (OR 2.96, 95% CI 1.04–8.39, p = 0.041), and weight loss of > 10% of a patient's body weight in the prior 6 months (OR 3.34, 95% CI 2.45–4.54, p < 0.001). By contrast, underweight status was protective against diabetes (OR 0.27, 95% CI 0.15–0.51, p < 0.001) and hypertension (OR 0.58, 95% CI 0.44–0.77, p < 0.001).

Postoperative Complications

Compared to patients in the normal weight category, underweight patients were significantly more likely to experience any medical complication (48.9% vs. 36.4%, p < 0.001, Table 3). On multivariate analysis, underweight BMI remained independently associated with increased odds of experiencing a medical complication (adjusted OR = 1.72; 95% CI 1.32–2.25, p < 0.001, Table S2). Having a partially or totally dependent functional status was also significantly associated with increased risk of medical complications (OR 1.83, 95% CI 1.21–2.76, p = 0.004).

Bleeding requiring transfusion was a major driver of medical complications in underweight patients (30.8% vs. 15.9%, p < 0.001). In multivariate regression, underweight BMI was still predictive of bleeding requiring transfusion (OR 2.43, 95% CI 1.79–3.29, p < 0.001), as were functional dependence (OR 1.62, 95% CI 1.01–2.62, p = 0.047) and current smoking (OR 1.32, 95% CI 1.03–1.68, p = 0.027). Underweight patients also experienced higher rates of wound disruptions (8.3% vs. 4.1%, p = 0.002, Table 4). In multivariate regression, underweight BMI (OR 1.93, 95% CI 1.16–3.23, p = 0.011) and male gender (OR 1.66, 95% CI 1.03–2.66, p = 0.037) were independent significant risk factors, while smoking, diabetes, and race were not associated. No significant differences were observed between BMI groups for cardiac arrest, pulmonary embolism, sepsis, stroke, or superficial/deep incisional SSIs (all p > 0.05, Table S2).

Hospital Course

Hospital course differed substantially by BMI status. Underweight patients had longer mean hospitalizations (14.6 vs. 11.9 days, p < 0.001, Table 3), and this remained significant in multivariate analyses (IRR 1.24, 95% CI 1.08–1.42, p = 0.003, Table S3). Functional dependence (IRR 1.30, p = 0.015) and unknown race (IRR 1.47, p = 0.001) were also predictive of prolonged stays. Discharge destinations varied significantly: underweight patients were less likely to be discharged home (77.3% vs. 84.0%, p = 0.012, Table 3). On multivariate analysis, underweight BMI was independently associated with non‐home discharge (OR 1.57, 95% CI 1.09–2.25, p = 0.015), along with functional dependence (OR 5.19, 95% CI 3.19–8.46, p < 0.001) and older age (OR 1.06 per year, p < 0.001).

Underweight BMI was also predictive of delayed surgery, with patients experiencing longer average times from admission to operation (mean 2.4 days in underweight patients, 0.99 days in the normal weight group, p < 0.001, Table 3). On multivariate analysis, being underweight was associated with having more than double the time from admission to operation (IRR 2.14, 95% CI 1.81–2.53, p < 0.001, Table S3). Functional dependence (IRR 2.74, p < 0.001), Black race (IRR 1.29, p = 0.003), American Indian/Alaska Native race (IRR 2.84, p < 0.001), and unknown race (IRR 1.31, p = 0.002) were also significant predictors, while non‐smokers had shorter admission‐to‐operation intervals (IRR 0.69, p < 0.001). Underweight status was not significantly associated with increased operative time (p = 0.103) or 30‐day mortality (2.2% vs. 1.2%, p = 0.156).

Post hoc power analyses were conducted for key outcomes (Table 5). Observed effect sizes of our findings, calculated using Cohen's h or d, ranged from small to medium in magnitude. Small effect sizes were found for the preoperative independent functional status (h = 0.15), postoperative wound disruption (h = 0.18), and discharge destination (h = 0.17). Medical complications (h = 0.25), bleeding requiring transfusion (h = 0.36), and length of hospital stay (d = 0.21) had medium effect sizes. All outcomes had post hoc statistical power ≥ 0.99.

Subgroup Analysis: Patients With Recent Weight Loss

Underweight patients were significantly more likely to have “recent weight loss,” defined as a loss of > 10% of their total body weight in the 6 months prior to the date of their surgery (31.5% vs. 11.2%, p < 0.001, Table 3). Of the 281 patients who were identified as the “recent weight loss” subgroup, 87 (31%) were underweight, while 194 (69%) were normal weight. Demographics for this subgroup were still mostly male and White (Table 2). Of patients with recent weight loss, underweight individuals were still significantly more likely to experience at least one medical complication (59.8% vs. 46.9%, p = 0.046) and to experience postoperative bleeding that required a transfusion (50.6% vs. 49.4%, p < 0.001, Table 6). However, unlike within the overall study group, underweight patients with recent weight loss were not significantly more likely to experience wound disruptions (p = 0.056) or longer total hospital stays compared to the normal weight patients within the subgroup (8.0% vs. 3.1%, p = 0.119).

Discussion

This study utilized a large, multicenter population to examine the risks associated with malnutrition in patients with head and neck cancer undergoing a laryngectomy. To our knowledge, this is the first large, retrospective study based in the United States to examine these associations.

Our primary goal was to evaluate how underweight status may be associated with surgical outcomes. We found that underweight patients were significantly more likely to experience postoperative complications, including higher rates and higher numbers of both wound dehiscence and postoperative bleeding that required transfusion. Functional dependence was also independently associated with an increased risk of overall complications and bleeding, suggesting an interplay between malnutrition and overall frailty. Both outcomes were highly powered, with post hoc powers of above 0.99, but the effect sizes were small (wound dehiscence) and medium (postoperative bleeding). These findings support the proposed physiological consequences of malnutrition, implying impaired immune function, delayed tissue repair, and increased infection risk [19].

Increased wound dehiscence and postoperative bleeding may have contributed to longer hospital stays observed in underweight patients, with an average stay of 14.95 days. Functional dependence and older age were also associated with prolonged hospitalization and non‐home discharge, suggesting that there may be a compounding effect of nutritional status with overall vulnerability that influences recovery trajectories. A National Cancer Database study found that stays longer than 13 days following total laryngectomies increased the risk of mortality within 90 days [20]. Underweight patients were also more likely to be discharged to hospice, a skilled care facility, or a rehabilitation facility, indicating greater postoperative care needs. This highlights the importance of optimizing malnourished patients preoperatively when possible.

Underweight patients were also more than twice as likely to experience surgical delays between admission and operation, raising concern that nutrition or medical optimization may prolong treatment timelines. Such optimization may be necessary, but prolonged time to surgery has been associated with worse oncologic outcomes and requires careful balancing within patients with head and neck cancers [21].

Interestingly, underweight patients did not have significantly higher rates of most preoperative comorbidities. They were significantly more likely to have smoked in the year prior to surgery but were less likely to have diabetes than patients with a normal BMI. The higher smoking rate is expected, given its well‐established role as a risk factor for laryngeal cancer [22]. The lower prevalence of diabetes within underweight patients may be due to cancer‐associated weight loss rather than a true protective effect. Both diabetes and smoking are linked to poor outcomes, including impaired wound healing [23].

We also assessed the role of acute weight loss, examining a subgroup of patients who lost more than 10% of their body weight in the 6 months leading up to their surgery. Our finding that underweight patients were significantly more likely to have experienced rapid weight loss suggests that acute weight loss is a large contributor to malnourishment in this patient population.

While underweight patients with acute or chronic weight loss were both significantly more likely to experience at least one medical complication and to require a transfusion for postoperative bleeding, only those with chronic malnutrition faced increased risks of wound disruptions or prolonged hospitalizations. This suggests that immune compromise related to long‐term malnutrition may impair wound healing, whereas short‐term malnutrition may have a lesser impact on recovery [24, 25].

Our study suggests a need for improved interventions to address malnutrition in laryngeal cancer patients. Laryngeal cancer and its treatment—including laryngectomy and adjuvant therapies like radiation—can negatively impact a patient's nutritional status by contributing to dysphagia and xerostomia, making early intervention crucial. Involvement of speech‐language pathologists and proactive management of oral intake may also improve recovery.

Another possibility is the implementation of an enhanced recovery after surgery (ERAS) pathway, an extensive evidence‐based protocol that aims to improve postsurgical recovery [26]. Despite its name, ERAS pathways encompass the entire preoperative to post‐discharge period and were first developed for colorectal cancer, where they have been found to reduce the length of hospital stays, decrease postoperative complications, and improve 5‐year survival rates [27, 28, 29].

ERAS pathways for laryngeal cancer are still being evaluated. Several pilot studies have demonstrated their feasibility and have found that ERAS can reduce length of stay in laryngectomy patients [30]. In addition, a retrospective study found that ERAS pathways improved the rates of providing proper preoperative nutritional intervention and reduced rates of refractory nausea and emesis following laryngectomy [31]. Together, these suggest that ERAS may be particularly effective at improving nutrition‐related outcomes in this population, but large, randomized trials are needed to better validate these findings.

There are several limitations to this study. We used BMI < 18.5 as a proxy for malnutrition, which may fail to identify patients as underweight if they require a more comprehensive screening tool. Future prospective studies could use validated tools, like the Malnutrition Universal Screening Tool, to confirm our findings. Additionally, we could not differentiate between initial treatment and salvage surgeries, which may impact nutritional risk [20, 32]. Many of our findings were statistically significant with high post hoc power, indicating that the size and makeup of our sample population were adequate to detect even minute differences. However, several of those differences were associated with small effect sizes, which may limit their clinical significance. As such, the importance of these findings should be interpreted within the context of overall clinical impact.

Conclusion

This study highlights the significant risks associated with malnutrition in laryngeal cancer patients undergoing laryngectomy, particularly for those who experienced acute weight loss prior to surgery. We found that being underweight was significantly associated with experiencing more postoperative complications, especially bleeding that requires transfusion and wound dehiscence. We also found that underweight patients more often had longer lengths of stay and were less likely to be discharged to their homes. The relationship established between chronic weight loss and challenges to surgical recovery underscores the importance of addressing long‐term malnutrition to improve wound healing and reduce complication rates.

While further research is needed to confirm these findings and evaluate the efficacy of interventions like ERAS protocols and preoperative protein supplementation, our study calls for a multifaceted approach to improve nutritional care and optimize outcomes for patients undergoing laryngectomy.
