Effectiveness of a near-peer teaching model in an otolaryngology bootcamp for medical students: a pilot study
Authors: Helia Mansouri Dana, Samuel Tholl, Alexander C Moise, Andrew Thamboo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground:Studies show that near-peer, simulation-based training can help develop technical skills. We sought to examine the effectiveness of near-peer instruction in otolaryngology emergencies by assessing student knowledge and procedural confidence before and after a 2-hour workshop.Methods:We designed a pilot study for an otolaryngology workshop led by a second-year resident and 2 third-year medical students. The workshop consisted of lectures for anatomy and procedural overview, followed by hands-on simulation stations. Participants completed pre- and post-workshop quizzes and surveys to assess confidence in epistaxis management, cricothyrotomy, and nasolaryngoscopy.Results:Sixteen medical students participated (mean age 25.8 yr). Median quiz scores increased significantly from 7 to 17 out of 21 after the workshop (p = 0.0005; median change 10, 95% confidence interval 6.0 to 11.5). Median self-reported confidence in epistaxis management, cricothyrotomy, and nasolaryngoscopy significantly improved from 2 to 3 (p = 0.003), 1 to 3 (p = 0.0009), and 1 to 3 (p = 0.001) out of 4, respectively. Among participants, the likelihood of attending this workshop again or recommending it to peers was high (median score 4/4).Conclusion:The workshop effectively improved knowledge and procedural confidence in epistaxis management, cricothyrotomy, and nasolaryngoscopy among medical students. It was well received, with high satisfaction scores, and feasible to coordinate.

Otolaryngology emergencies can be life-threatening situations that demand immediate intervention.1–3 However, many medical students and new medical graduates report feeling unprepared for critical scenarios, such as epistaxis and airway management, as they transition into their first year of residency.4 This may be, in part, because of limited exposure to the full spectrum of emergency situations during training, which can leave residents lacking confidence in their practical skills.5 Additionally, the time constraints of medical school reduce opportunities for extensive hands-on learning, potentially compromising preparedness. 4 Studies suggest that combining lectures with simulated emergency scenarios is substantially more effective than traditional lecture-only approaches in preparing students for otolaryngology emergencies.1,3,6,7 In these studies, students report feeling more confident and more knowledgeable about emergency procedures after completion of a hands-on training session.1–9 Further, 1 study reported that the introduction of simulation training in cricothyrotomy directly led to improved patient survival.10 It is thus important to provide such learning opportunities for medical students in a way that prioritizes overcoming discomfort and technical limitations, such that confidence can be fostered and maintained through future medical training.

Near-peer teaching, where junior doctors and medical students deliver training, has been shown to enhance accessibility and relevance, as these instructors can better identify areas where learners require focused support.4,11,12 This approach further helps reinforce concepts for student-teachers, as teaching requires in-depth understanding, reevaluation, and reconstruction of knowledge.13 In addition, peer teaching is reported to allow knowledge acquisition at the same level as, and improvement in hands-on technique at a greater degree than other teaching methods, including expert- or faculty-led teaching.13 This degree of effectiveness is accompanied by the auxiliary benefit of a comfortable learning environment that encourages collaboration and the exploration of errors.13,14 However, student teachers are limited in experience and depth of knowledge, and further research is needed to determine the effectiveness of near-peer teaching for otolaryngology workshops.11,15 We sought to explore the effects of near-peer teaching on the confidence of medical students in conducting otolaryngology emergency procedures, and to assess the feasibility of such a model as an alternative to traditional teaching by attending otolaryngologists.

Methods

Workshop design

We offered a 2-hour otolaryngology emergency bootcamp to medical students in Vancouver, Canada. We recruited participants via social media posts and used a secure online survey to collect participant contact information. We then emailed participants with information regarding the study.

We developed the workshop to include topics that were considered challenging or deemed to require additional hands-on training after consulting residents training at the Department of Otolaryngology, University of British Columbia. We selected 3 main areas: epistaxis management, cricothyrotomy and complex airway management, and laryngoscopy using a flexible endoscope (Figure 1).

Simulation stations that were completed during the workshop, including A) epistaxis management station using a task trainer, where participants practised anterior nasal packing and adjunctive airway protection techniques such as foley insertion for posterior epistaxis; B) cricothyrotomy station using a high-fidelity mannequin; and C) flexible nasolaryngoscopy station using an endoscopy station and participant volunteers.

All educational and teaching material was preapproved by otolaryngology faculty before delivery. Hands-on training was preceded by lectures encapsulating relevant anatomy, indications, complications, and step-by-step instructions. Participants were then divided into small groups for rotation through the 3 stations. Each topic’s lecture and station was guided by 2 third-year medical students and 1 second-year otolaryngology resident.

Study design

We conducted a pilot study with exploratory objectives, using a single-arm prospective design. We included participants (aged ≥ 18 yr) who currently attended the undergraduate medical program at the University of British Columbia. Exclusion criteria were previous participation in an otolaryngology educational or emergencies workshop or previous extensive training in otolaryngology or emergency procedure techniques.

All participants completed a pre-workshop survey with questions about demographic information and level of training (Appendix 1, available at www.canjsurg.ca/lookup/doi/10.1503/cjs.000126/tab-related-content). The preworkshop survey also included 4 questions to assess interest in otolaryngology using a 4-point Likert scale (1 = no interest; 4 = high interest), exposure to otolaryngology topics (1 = no exposure; 4 = extensive exposure), ability to describe the steps of otolaryngology procedures (1 = poorly; 4 = very well), and self-reported confidence in ability to complete the technique independently (1 = poor confidence; 4 = high confidence). Participants also completed a 21-question, multiple-choice quiz before the workshop, with questions targeting the topics covered during the teaching session (Appendix 2, available at www.canjsurg.ca/lookup/doi/10.1503/cjs.000126/tab-related-content). The quiz contained 21 questions, with 7 questions on each main topic (epistaxis, cricothyrotomy, nasolaryngoscopy). They completed the same survey after the workshop, with the addition of 2 questions using a 4-point Likert scale to assess each participant’s likelihood to do this workshop again and likelihood of recommending this workshop to others (1 = unlikely; 4 = highly likely). They also completed the same quiz after the workshop.

Statistical analysis

We compared results of the pre- and post-workshop surveys and quizzes using paired Wilcoxon signed-rank tests. We estimated 95% confidence intervals (CIs) using bootstrap resampling with 2000 replicates to represent the median paired differences (post- v. pre-workshop). We calculated descriptive statistics and performed statistical analyses using R version 4.4.2.16

Ethics approval

This study was approved by the University of British Columbia Research Ethics Boards (no. H24–03465).

Results

A total of 16 medical students completed the otolaryngology emergencies bootcamp, including 10 females (62%) and 6 males (38%) (Figure 2). The mean age of participants was 25.8 (standard deviation 2.2) years. Ten participants had recently completed their first year of medical school, 4 had completed their second year, 1 had completed their third year, and 1 had completed their fourth year of studies.

Distribution of participants by expected year of medical school graduation.

The median pre-workshop quiz score was 7/21, and this increased significantly to 17/21 (p = 0.0005; median change 10, 95% CI 6.0 to 11.5) after the workshop (Figure 3).

Pre-and post-workshop multiple-choice quiz scores assessing knowledge of otolaryngology emergencies. Boxplots represent median scores and interquartile ranges (IQRs), overlaid with individual participant scores.

From the survey, the median self-reported confidence score for epistaxis management increased from 2/4 to 3/4 (p = 0.003; median change 1, 95% CI 0 to 2) (Figure 4). Self-reported confidence for performing cricothyrotomy techniques increased from 1/4 to 3/4 (p = 0.0009; median change 2, 95% CI 1 to 2) (Figure 4). Self-reported confidence in performing nasolaryngoscopy increased from 1/4 to 3/4 (p = 0.001; median change 2, 95% CI 1 to 2) (Figure 4).

Pre-and post-workshop self-reported confidence scores for (A) epistaxis management, (B) cricothyrotomy, and (C) nasolaryngoscopy. Scores were measured on a 4-point Likert scale. Boxplots represent median scores and interquartile ranges (IQRs), overlaid by individual participant responses.

At the end of the workshop, participants were asked, “How likely are you to do this workshop again?” and “How likely are you to recommend this workshop to others?” The median score for both questions was 4/4.

Discussion

In this study, participation in an otolaryngology workshop led by residents and medical students using a near-peer teaching model was associated with large improvements in objective knowledge regarding common otolaryngology emergencies and in self-reported confidence in epistaxis management, cricothyrotomy, and nasolaryngoscopy techniques. The workshop was feasible to coordinate and had high satisfaction rates among participants.

Skill acquisition in surgical fields is challenging, with students and resident doctors often managing emergencies without prior training or experience.17 This can continue throughout residency, as exposure to emergencies may differ across different hospitals and learning sites.17,18 Furthermore, although medical students are well supported in their clinical years, the sudden transition into residency training can present with an unexpected surge in responsibilities. Often, medical students have not been exposed to life-threatening emergencies, and they have rarely had the opportunity to be guided through emergency procedures. Meanwhile, junior residents are often the first and, at times, the only available providers in the setting of life-threatening emergencies, which presents a dramatic transition in learner responsibilities. In smaller residency programs such as otolaryngology, this gap can feel amplified, and new graduates may find themselves faced with circumstances requiring urgent patient stabilization without prior experience.

In addition, there is little exposure to otolaryngology-related topics, patient presentations, and procedures in Canadian undergraduate medical school education; this can further present as a barrier for residents in other specialties in managing otolaryngology emergencies on the ward. For example, in the case of epistaxis in the anticoagulated patient, an otolaryngology resident may not be immediately available, requiring urgent care by resident doctors in other services. Additional skills, such as competence in airway visualization and management, are essential not just in otolaryngology, but in other specialties like emergency medicine and anesthesiology.19–21 Therefore, medical students interested in both surgical and nonsurgical specialties — such as critical care, anesthesiology, emergency medicine, and internal medicine — may further benefit from exposure to these skills. Expanding simulation training to include medical students can aid in building skills before an initial emergency encounter, not for immediate competence, but to allow repetition, increased confidence and, consequently, increased awareness of procedures when faced with these scenarios in future clinical settings.22,23

Previous studies of simulation training in surgical techniques and emergency management in otolaryngology have shown benefits for learner confidence; however, these reports typically focus on training for medical residents. 1–3,5–10 Additionally, in a great majority of these studies, training is provided by attending physicians and faculty members.1–3,5–10 One Australian study assessed the effectiveness of an otolaryngology emergency bootcamp presented by a junior doctor, for medical student education.4 An additional study involved using a junior resident as a near-peer teacher for other medical residents.15 Therefore, our bootcamp provides a unique Canadian perspective on otolaryngology simulation training by implementing senior medical students as near-peer teachers for the education of other medical students, specifically those in earlier years of training. Our study was further comparable to the existing literature in terms of curriculum and selected techniques, albeit shorter in duration than other workshops (Table 1).

Despite the difference in study design, our results aligned with those of other studies in otolaryngology simulation training, as we found a 10-point increase in knowledge of otolaryngology emergencies after the workshop, as well as significant improvements in self-reported confidence in all procedures taught and simulated. Our observational results further corroborate previous reports of near-peer teaching as an effective teaching modality,12,13,19,24 as the stations led by medical students in this workshop resulted in similar changes in participant confidence and knowledge as those led by a junior resident. Notably, our study extends existing literature by demonstrating that medical students can serve as near-peer teachers despite minimal differences in formal training relative to participants, although a larger sample would allow for better assessment of effectiveness and long-term retention in future studies.

We present this teaching method as a platform for early exposure to surgical specialties, which is an important factor in fostering medical student interest in surgical fields.25,26 This is particularly important, as fewer Canadian students are choosing surgical specialties because of concerns about lifestyle or misconceptions about what a career in surgery may involve.26,27 As such, we show that the benefit of this workshop model can be twofold: by improving confidence and competency in the implementation of these skills in future emergencies, and by sparking interest in surgical fields through earlier exposure to hands-on training.

Limitations

Our study is limited in its conclusions by our small sample, the selection of all participants from 1 medical school, and possible selection bias relating to our near-peer tutors, as teaching skills may differ across medical students. Additionally, using a 4-point Likert scale on our survey may have limited sensitivity to detect smaller changes in confidence and other factors, and could contribute to a ceiling effect in post-workshop responses. We did not measure retention of knowledge after the workshop; however, Chen and colleagues found that benefits persisted for 6 months post-workshop when remeasured. Future studies should consider using expanded Likert scales to allow for greater response granularity and improved differentiation among participants. The skills taught in our workshop could also be assessed using simulated patient scenarios, in addition to written exams, for further determination of how well participants can implement the knowledge from this workshop into a more emergent situation. Another potential limitation was the training of the near-peer teachers themselves, as they were trained by staff otolaryngologists and more senior learners, which can introduce variability as opposed to a more standardized approach. However, this reflects real-world educational practice and was unlikely to meaningfully detract from the overall findings.

Conclusion

This research presents near-peer teaching by senior medical students and a junior otolaryngology resident as an effective method in providing immediate post-workshop improvements in otolaryngology emergency procedures, for medical students. Although larger samples will allow for assessment of the true effectiveness of this teaching model, our pilot study points toward a role for near-peer teaching in aiding hands-on learning in these surgical procedures. These workshops may further benefit students interested in a variety of medical specialties, including but not limited to anesthesiology and emergency medicine. Additionally, this curricular structure can be used for simulation training in other surgical specialties to supplement training in the preclinical years of medical school, helping to improve exposure to underrepresented fields.
