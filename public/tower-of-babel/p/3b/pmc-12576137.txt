Morphological and Molecular Identification of Trichosporon asahii Caused Oral Thrush in Neonates
Authors: Youssuf A. Gherbawy, Daniela Puscasiu, Abeer Baddar, Asmaa S. Yassein, Eman G. A. M. El‐Dawy
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACT Trichosporon asahii, though rare in neonatal oral infections, can mimic Candida spp. in presentation and enzyme activity. In cases unresponsive to prophylactic antibiotics, herbal alternatives like Boswellia carterii and Rosmarinus officinalis may offer effective and safe alternative treatments.

Introduction

Neonatal thrush is termed a cursory infection that appears on the tongue and the oral mucosa of newborns as white flecks [1, 2]. It is an invasive infection that affects low‐weight newborns (< 1.5 kg) with a proportion of about 2%–20%. This proportion represents 10% of all sepsis‐infected preterm newborns with an increased high mortality rate [3, 4, 5, 6]. Trichosporon infections generally have a low rate; however, they represent the second most common fungal infections [7]. Trichosporon belongs to basidiomycetes, which are characterized as a member of the normal human microbiota, but under specific conditions, it turns into a pathogen. Trichosporon is associated with superficial infections (allergic pneumonitis and white Piedra) and invasive infections [8]. Invasive trichosporonosis generated by Trichosporon spp. emerged as an opportunistic infection affecting immunocompromised patients, with mortality rates ranging from 42% to 90% [9]. T. asahii is the major species isolated in cases of immunosuppression, persistent fever, and neutropenia [10]. The variation of clinical manifestations during infections of T. asahii depends on the organs affected, and the most common symptoms comprise fever, endophthalmitis, brain abscess, fungemia, liver abscess, and respiratory or urinary tract infections [11]. Treatment of trichosporonosis is based on antifungal agents in general, although its sensitivity to azole antifungals is limited [12]. Natural products obtained from plants exceed synthetic products due to lower side effects and foolproof biological activities [13].

Case History/Examination

A 5‐day‐old male neonate was admitted to the intensive care unit of Naqada Central Hospital. The consent was obtained from the baby's parents for medical intervention.

The primary complaint involved the appearance of creamy patches on the tongue and inner cheeks, which developed 3 days after premature delivery (Figure 1). Additionally, the infant exhibited fever, skin rashes, and excessive fussiness, particularly during feeding attempts. These symptoms led to difficulties in both breastfeeding and bottle feeding.

The appearance of thrush on the tongue of infected neonate.

A general examination revealed that the baby was underweight. Upon local examination of the oral cavity, a white plaque was observed, which was scrapable and exposed the erythematous mucosa upon scraping. This finding supported the clinical diagnosis of pseudomembranous candidiasis.

The prescribed treatment included Daktarin oral gel (miconazole 20 mg/g) for thrush. Initially, sulbactam/ampicillin (375 mg) and ceftazidime (1 g) were administered as first‐line antibiotics. Subsequently, the regimen was adjusted to vancomycin (500 mg) and meropenem (500 mg) as part of the hospital's protocol to address persistent fever and skin rashes.

A sample was taken from the mouth of the neonate with a sterile swab, then cultured on (SDA) at 37°C for 2 days. It was subjected to microscopic examination, which revealed yeast cells. The isolate was subcultured on Candida Chromogenic agar (Madrid, Spain) and HiChrom (Himedia, India) media and showed light blue and dark blue colonies, respectively. DNA was extracted for further identification via sequencing of the ITS region. ITS and IGS regions of DNA, which have been extensively sequenced in the fungi molecular ecology, represent a world fungal barcode sequence that the molecular identification of yeasts is based on [14]. The amplified sequence (accession number PP475385) was subjected to blasting into the GenBank database and compared with other yeast sequences introduced into the GenBank database, which showed nucleotide identity of 95% with Trichosporon asahii (accession number KC127676). The phylogeny tree was conducted by a neighbor‐joining algorithm using a maximum composite likelihood model by TREECON Windows (version 1.3b, 1998) (Figure 2). T. asahii was the only causal pathogen associated with the observed white plaque and isolated from the neonate.

Phylogeny of Trichosporon asahii based on ITS sequences data. The numbers above the branches indicate bootstrap values that were constructed after a run of 1000 replications. Our isolate was shown by a straight line.

The virulence has emerged through the study of some enzymatic activities. T. asahii did not express hemolysin activity after growing on blood agar. The egg yolk agar plate medium and casein hydrolysis medium were applied for the determination of phospholipase and proteinase activity, respectively. The opaque zone precipitated around colonies was determined by phospholipase activity, and in the case of proteinase, a clear zone around colonies. The studied enzymatic activity showed positive results in this case.

Treatment

Prophylaxis used for treatment failed to appear any improvement in this case; so in this study, we tested the effect of some plant extracts in vitro, in comparison with the antibiotics obtained. The MIC and the MFC of Boswellia carterii and Rosmarinus officinalis aqueous extracts and Nystatin medication were determined against the growing T. asahii using the microdouble dilution method according to Kebede & Shibeshi [15]. Concentrations used for B. carterii and R. officinalis aqueous extracts were 400–0.20 mg/mL. Nystatin medication concentrations were 100–0.008 mg/mL.

Conclusion and Results

The result showed great susceptibility to the three tested agents against T. asahii. The MIC of nystatin was 0.008 mg/mL, and the MIC of B. carterii and R. officinalis aqueous extracts was 0.20 mg/mL for both. The MFC value is only shown by nystatin medicine (0.008 mg/mL). B. carterii and R. officinalis aqueous extracts did not show MFC values, but R. officinalis aqueous extract revealed a low number of cells in some concentrations (0.78, 0.39, and 0.20).

These results verified the antifungal activity of B. carterii and R. officinalis aqueous extracts against the growth of T. asahii. A very low concentration of B. carterii and R. officinalis aqueous extract was used to inhibit the growth of T. asahii. Also, a very low concentration of nystatin medicine was applied to inhibit and kill the growth of T. asahii compared to the concentrations of antibiotics in the ICU.

Discussion

Oral thrush widely spreads among newborns, especially those who are immunocompromised and detained in neonatal intensive care units. According to Sharma and Chakrabarti [16], candidiasis or thrush is not only caused by species of Candida but also by other genera; however, they share many characteristics with Candida spp. T. asahii appeared on the tongue and mucosa of neonates as white spots. The pathogenesis of T. asahii isolated from ICU‐admitted patients is like that commonly exhibited during candidiasis [17].

Phospholipase enzymes promote the hydrolysis of phospholipids, which is the main part of the cell membrane in fungi and breaks down one or more ester bonds of glycerophospholipids [18]. Proteinase is an aspartyl acid protein enzyme; its role stands out in degrading surface protein and disrupting local immunity, leading finally to tissue invasion [19]. According to Alexandra et al. [20] most of the isolated T. asahii produce aspartyl proteinase, and few produce hemolysin enzymes, but phospholipase was not secreted by any of the tested isolates. In our study, T. asahii revealed both proteinase and phospholipase activity; on the other hand, hemolysin activity was not shown. The treatment with antibiotics failed in this case. Therefore, we test alternative medicine by medicinal plants in vitro and compare them with nystatin. In pediatric patients, ampicillin/sulbactam is recommended for the treatment of several serious infections, including acute epiglottitis, acute fulminant meningococcemia, periorbital cellulitis, and sepsis, due to its demonstrated clinical efficacy and broad‐spectrum activity against resistant pathogens [21].

Ceftazidime is both safe and effective in treating infections in neonates, achieving therapeutic levels in the blood, urine, cerebrospinal fluid, and bronchial secretions. According to Pollock et al. [22], no significant adverse effects were observed.

Several studies report on the use of vancomycin and meropenem to manage meningitis and ventriculitis in adults and pediatric patients [23]. Although nystatin is the most effective in our experiments, B. carterii and R. officinalis aqueous extracts showed promising antifungal efficiency, and we propose these aqueous extracts as potentially safer alternatives against oral thrush in neonates to reduce the side effects of artificial drugs. The B. carterii aqueous extract had a constructive impact on the phagocytic process by increasing the rate of phagocytosis of the treated blood samples [24]. Nikolic et al. [25] confirmed that Jordanian B. carterii extracts have a potential anticandidal effect on some species of Candida. Both the formulated ethyl‐acetate extract (FEAE) and the formulated petroleum–ether extract (FPEE) of B. carterii displayed antifungal activity against some filamentous fungi at different concentrations [26]. Frankincense essential oil exhibits excellent antibacterial and antifungal properties against bacterial pathogens and fungal pathogens that cause an infection of the skin [27]. Boswellia exhibits diverse pharmacological properties, including antihyperglycemic, antioxidant, and anti‐inflammatory effects. When administered, it has the potential to restore disrupted metabolic pathways, as demonstrated in vivo animal studies [28]. For a long time in China, B. carterii resins have been utilized to treat inflammation‐related conditions, including traumatic injuries and inflammatory pain. Various components of the resin have demonstrated therapeutic potential in laboratory studies [29]. B. carterii shows a mild protective effect against kidney failure, causing a slight improvement in kidney function, as indicated by Mahmou [30]. Jones et al. [31] reported the selective cytotoxicity of B. carterii methanolic extract against 3 leukemia cell lines. Frankincense extracts were used to treat many clinical cases, which confirm their safety on human health, such as periodontitis, chronic low back pain, hepatic inflammation, and lipid metabolism [32, 33, 34]. B. carterii extract and its nano sponge complex effectively improved respiratory distress [35].

It was reported that R. officinalis has antifungal activity against dermatophytes, which was proven in vivo [36, 37]. In a mouse model, the regulatory effects of R. officinalis were investigated by assessing various parameters related to skin tumors, including mean latency period, incidence, burden, yield, weight, and diameter [38]. The safety evaluation confirms that rosemary is widely utilized in cosmetic formulations, serving purposes such as skin conditioning and fragrance enhancement [39, 40, 41, 42]. A previous study confirmed that milk supplementation with rosemary induced immunity and skin thickness in newborn goat kids [43]. Another study on rats reported that rosemary ethanolic extract had hepatoprotective and antimutagenic properties due to the high content of phenolic compounds with antioxidant potential [44]. The influence of rosemary aqueous and methanolic extract on normal cell lines, mouse embryonic fibroblast (MEF) was found to be very low compared to tumor cell lines [45].

Nystatin is a prominent polyene antifungal drug, and it is the best for treating oral candidiasis with a history of positive results; its effectiveness lies in the interaction with the cell membrane essential component (ergosterol), making it porous, susceptible to lysis, and emptying its cellular contents, causing fungal death [46, 47].
