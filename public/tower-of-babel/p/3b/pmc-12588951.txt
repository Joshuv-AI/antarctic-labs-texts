Lung Cancer‐Like Presentation of Pulmonary Vein Stenosis After Radiofrequency Catheter Ablation for Atrial Fibrillation
Authors: Onofre Moran‐Mendoza, Bronwyn Hersen, Sabrina Allarakhia, J Alberto Neder, Gurmohan Dhillon
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTA woman in her 50s presented with a two‐month history of haemoptysis, dyspnea, cough and weight loss. She had recently undergone radiofrequency ablation for atrial fibrillation. A computed tomography (CT) scan of her chest showed left upper lobe opacity, mediastinal lymphadenopathy, and left pleural effusion, concerning for malignancy. Bronchoscopy revealed a malignant‐appearing lesion that was negative for malignancy on biopsy. Despite improvement of the CT abnormalities and the lesion on a repeat bronchoscopy, she continued to experience recurrent episodes of dyspnea and haemoptysis. A ventilation/perfusion scan showed decreased perfusion to her left lung. A CT pulmonary angiogram with pulmonary vein phase established the diagnosis of pulmonary vein stenosis. This case highlights the necessity of considering pulmonary vein stenosis in patients post‐ablation presenting with features suggestive of lung cancer to avoid unnecessary delays in diagnosis and treatment.Keywords: atrial fibrillation, lung cancer, pulmonary vein stenosis, radiofrequency ablation

Introduction

Radiofrequency ablation (RFA) is becoming increasingly common as a treatment for atrial fibrillation, but it can result in pulmonary vein stenosis (PVS) [1]. This complication can often be misdiagnosed with other conditions due to its nonspecific symptoms [1]. Here we describe a rare case of a patient with post‐RFA PVS presenting with clinical, radiological and bronchoscopic features suggesting lung cancer. We report unexpected bronchoscopic findings which mimicked malignancy, and the diagnostic work‐up that led to the correct diagnosis of PVS. This case highlights the necessity of considering PVS in patients post‐ablation for atrial fibrillation presenting with features of lung cancer to avoid unnecessary procedures and delays in diagnosis and treatment.

Case Report

A woman in her 50s presented to the emergency department reporting two episodes of small‐volume haemoptysis that were preceded by 2 months of worsening cough, dysphonia and an 18‐kg weight loss. She reported dyspnea (Medical Research Council dyspnea score 4/5) and left pleuritic chest pain. She denied fevers, chills or sweats. She reported a 20‐pack‐year smoking history. Her past medical history was significant for atrial fibrillation, for which she had received RFA 7 months prior to her presentation. A computed tomography (CT) scan of her chest showed mediastinal lymphadenopathy, ground‐glass opacity in the lingula, and a left‐sided pleural effusion (Figure 1a,b). She was referred to pulmonology due to concerns of underlying malignancy.

On exam, her blood pressure was 141/77 mmHg, heart rate 76 beats/min, and oxygen saturation 96% on room air. She had no cervical or axillary lymphadenopathy, had normal breath sounds on chest auscultation, with no crackles or wheezes and had no signs of heart failure.

A flexible bronchoscopy with endobronchial ultrasound was performed under conscious sedation. Bronchoscopy showed a malignant‐appearing, protruding lesion distally in the left main bronchus at the junction with the left upper lobe bronchus. The lesion seemed to be ‘infiltrating’ the left main bronchus (Figure 2a). The rest of the tracheobronchial tree appeared normal. The lesion bled profusely after biopsy, requiring cold saline for bleeding control. There were no other endobronchial lesions or extrinsic compression identified. Bronchoalveolar lavage was performed, and a four‐centimetre sonographically normal station seven lymph node was biopsied.

(a) Photograph taken during initial bronchoscopy, showing a ‘malignant appearing lesion’ that seemed to be ‘infiltrating’ the left main bronchus mucosa. (b) Photograph taken during repeat bronchoscopy showing ‘resolution’ of the malignant appearing lesion in the left main bronchus. (c) Photograph taken during repeat bronchoscopy showing diffuse hyperaemia, oedema and engorgement of the submucosal vessels in the bronchus intermedius.

Pathology of the bronchial lesion showed benign respiratory mucosa with focal squamous metaplasia and minimal chronic inflammation, with no granulomas, dysplasia or malignancy. Cytology of the station seven lymph node showed no malignant cells. Microbiology for bacteria, fungi and mycobacteria was all negative. A positron emission tomography scan, typically done to stage lung cancer, was not done because the endobronchial biopsies to investigate the lesion were negative for malignancy.

When pathology from the bronchoscopy was negative for malignancy, it was suspected that she may have a bacterial pneumonia, so she was prescribed a seven‐day course of oral levofloxacin. Her symptoms initially improved with antibiotics. However, over the following 5 months, she experienced recurrent episodes of worsening dyspnea, and one episode of small‐volume haemoptysis. A follow‐up CT chest showed resolution of the lingular ground‐glass opacity with a new focal subpleural opacity in the left lower lobe (Figure 1c,d).

Due to the waxing and waning nature of the ground‐glass opacities, either organising pneumonia or an atypical infection was suspected. She underwent another flexible bronchoscopy under conscious sedation. At this time, bronchoscopy showed diffuse hyperaemia, oedema and significant dilatation of the submucosal vessels, both proximally in the left mainstem bronchus and distally in the right bronchus intermedius. However, the previously observed malignant‐appearing lesion in the left main bronchus was no longer present (Figure 2b,c).

Following the two bronchoscopies, a ventilation/perfusion (V/Q) scan was completed to investigate chronic thromboembolism as a possible cause of her recurrent haemoptysis and dyspnea. This scan showed decreased perfusion to her entire left lung and right lower lobe (Figure 1e–h). A CT pulmonary venogram showed stenosis of the left upper, left lower, and right lower pulmonary veins and the diagnosis of PVS was made (Figure 1i).

The patient then underwent stenting of the left lower pulmonary vein with a 9 × 25 mm2 biliary stent (Figure 1j). Stenting initially improved her symptoms, but this was later complicated by in‐stent restenosis requiring anticoagulation.

The patient unfortunately experienced recurrent episodes of dyspnea and chest pain so she underwent further pulmonary vein dilatations and balloon angioplasties. Her symptoms resolved for a few months following each of the procedures, but then unfortunately continued to recur. At present, she continues to experience significant chest pain and dyspnea, limiting her functional ability.

Discussion

Here we report a rare case of a patient with post‐RFA PVS presenting with clinical, radiological and bronchoscopic features suggesting lung cancer. PVS is a well‐known complication of RFA [1]. Patients often present with dyspnea, chest pain, haemoptysis, or cough [1], leading to extensive investigations for various pulmonary diseases with frequent misdiagnosis [2, 3]. Severe PVS is a serious complication of RFA with an incidence of 0.5%–4.0% [1]. Due to the low incidence and non‐specific symptoms at presentation, patients with PVS might be misdiagnosed, commonly with pneumonia, pulmonary embolism or tuberculosis [2], and undergo unnecessary procedures including bronchoscopy and lung resection [2].

The patient described in this report presented with multiple findings that were highly concerning for malignancy in a heavy smoker, including weight loss, haemoptysis, lung opacities and mediastinal lymphadenopathy on chest CT, and a protruding, malignant‐looking lesion on bronchoscopy. The bronchoscopic findings resembling lung cancer can be attributed to severe submucosal oedema and varices secondary to pulmonary venous hypertension since the bronchial veins drain into the pulmonary veins [4]. Interestingly, this lesion disappeared on a subsequent bronchoscopy, likely due to compensatory mechanisms that decreased the venous pressures, such as pulmonary blood flow redistribution and increased venous drainage into the azygous vein [5]. Similarly, the fluctuating ground‐glass changes seen on chest CT could be explained by resolving areas of haemorrhage [3].

This case emphasises the importance of considering PVS in patients presenting with clinical, radiological and/or bronchoscopic features of lung cancer after RFA.
