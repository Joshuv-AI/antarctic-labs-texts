Pediatric Orthopaedic Workforce in 2022: Current Workforce, Projections, and Trends in the United States
Authors: Robert H. Cho, Benjamin W. Sheffer, Apurva S. Shah, Michael P. Glotzbecker, Matthew R. Schmitz, Michael J. Heffernan, Marcella Woiczik, Emily R. Dodwell, Pooya Hosseinzadeh, Benjamin J. Shore, Jeffrey R. Sawyer, Wudbhav N. Sankar
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAccurate workforce projections are critical for any market seeking to appropriately match supply with demand. Recently, the Pediatric Orthopaedic Society of North America (POSNA) convened a workforce taskforce to analyze the supply of, demand for, and distribution of pediatric orthopaedic surgeons (POSs) within the United States. The purpose of this analysis is to determine if we are training the appropriate number of POSs, if the distribution of POS meets current and future demands, to compare our data to previous analyses, and to report and disseminate our findings.MethodsDatabases and surveys (POSNA, SF Match, KID, MGMA) were utilized to determine current pediatric orthopaedic workforce supply, demand, and distribution. Projections based on pediatric population growth and POS in practice were performed.ResultsThe membership (Active + Candidate) of POSNA has increased from 904 in 2014 to 1125 in 2022. The mean number of recent pediatric orthopaedic fellows trained per year is 57.5, with 73 positions offered per year through the San Francisco Match as of 2022. In addition, a significant volume of pediatric orthopaedic surgery is performed by adult orthopaedic surgeons. Growth of the pediatric population (children <18 years) has slowed over time and is estimated to slightly decrease each year moving forward. Accounting for retirement projections, it appears that we are currently overtraining new POSs by 8.5 new fellows per year. Although pediatric orthopaedic surgeon demand is met in many areas of the US, there continue to exist “surgical deserts” within the country where the population remains underserved.DiscussionWhile difficult to determine the exact number of POS that are necessary for the service demand in the United States, we have made estimates as to the supply and demand for POS in the near future based on demographic factors, increasing surgical subspecialization, and expected training and attrition rates of POS. Based on a slightly negative expected population growth rate of children in the United States, increasing subspecialization of POS, and expected POS attrition, we estimate that current and future demand will be met by training approximately 49 new POS fellows each year.ConclusionsThe current training rate of 57.5 pediatric orthopaedic fellows per year may eventually lead to a slight surplus of POS, especially in urban areas. It is important to follow these trends over time, especially regarding subspecialization within pediatric orthopaedics. The modeling described in this manuscript is designed to facilitate future comparisons.Key Concepts(1)The supply of POS and physician extenders continues to increase. Growth of the number of POS in the United States is expected to occur with the current rate of 57.5 fellows trained per year.(2)Based on our model, a rate of 49 fellows trained per year shows neither an increase nor decrease in POS supply in the United States. Thus, there is projected to be a slight surplus of POS supply over the next decade.(3)Because of the age–gender distribution of the POS workforce, as well as the increasing proportion of females entering the specialty, gender parity is expected by 2050.(4)The population of children under 18 years of age will likely continue to decline because of declining birth and immigration rates. This decrease in demand may possibly be offset by the increasing amount of subspecialization among nonpediatric orthopaedic surgeons and the subsequent decrease in pediatric cases performed by them, leading to more surgical cases being available for POS over time.(5)There continues to be an expansion in the scope of practice of POS, most commonly in sports, hand, and spine surgery. There is also a trend toward treatment of traditionally pediatric orthopaedic conditions through early adulthood (transitional care), which can lead to an expansion in the number of patients available for the pediatric orthopaedist.Level of EvidenceLevel II, (economic and decision analysis)

Introduction

The need for pediatric orthopaedic surgeons (POSs) in the United States is determined by multiple factors such as population growth, evolving healthcare needs, advancements in medical technology, and changes in healthcare policy. Given this, it is challenging to project the demand for POS. However, some projections can be made based on demographic trends and regional population growth. A best-effort analysis of the pediatric orthopaedic workforce is critical for the Pediatric Orthopaedic Society of North America (POSNA) in order to train an appropriate number of specialists and meet the needs of children. Ideally, workforce analyses should be performed regularly to capture trends in both supply and demand. This report explores the current and future supply of POS based on this analysis.

The balance between the supply of physicians and the need for their expertise and services has been discussed and debated in the United States for more than a century. The Association of American Medical Colleges (AAMC) began in 2008 to issue broadly focused “Reports on the state of the physician workforce” [1]. In subsequent iterations of this work, they highlighted the challenges of accurately forecasting the balance of supply and demand but consistently anticipated the likelihood of a shortage of physicians in the United States. The AAMC and its contractors based their conclusions on standards set as part of the federal government's process for identifying physician shortage areas and a “physician requirements model” that forecast supply and demand for practitioners [2]. In a recent report, the overall supply of orthopaedic surgeons was compared to the demand for orthopaedic surgeons in general, but this did not evaluate orthopaedic subspecialties, POS supply, or demand for pediatric orthopaedic services [3].

POSNA last investigated the workforce of POS in 2014. Sawyer et al. [4] showed that there was an overall increase of 38 POS in the workforce over the previous decade (2004-2014), with the increase in newly trained fellows offsetting the number of POS lost through attrition, primarily due to retirement. In the decade since that study, there have been significant changes in the number of fellows trained, demographic factors including slower pediatric population growth, population migration during and post-COVID pandemic, macroeconomic factors, and increased subspecialization within pediatric orthopaedics.

The purpose of this analysis is to determine if the appropriate number of POS is being trained, if the distribution of POS meets current and future demands, to compare our data to previous analyses, and to report and disseminate our findings.

Methods

Current pediatric orthopaedic workforce (Supply)

In order to estimate the current number of POS, the taskforce queried several different sources. Two estimates were drawn from the American Medical Association (AMA) Masterfile® survey, and the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services (CMS) National Plan Provider Enumeration System (NPPES). Finally, a recent POSNA database (2022) was used to identify the number of active + candidate (A + C) members. Active + candidate members were chosen to most accurately reflect the actual number of POS in the workforce. Senior (emeritus) members were excluded, as their status requires retirement from active surgical practice. Corresponding members were also excluded, as they represent surgeons practicing outside of the United States. Age and gender data were also obtained from membership records. This information was combined with data from the POSNA 2022 Member Survey to understand the rate and likelihood of attrition based on age and sex.

Non-POS workforce contributions

In addition to POS, both advanced practice providers (APPs) and adult orthopaedic surgeons deliver a significant amount of orthopaedic care to children in the United States. It is challenging to determine the precise amount of surgical care performed on children by adult orthopaedic surgeons. To make our best estimate, we used the American Board of Orthopaedic Surgery (ABOS) database to identify the proportion of ABOS exam cases involving pediatric patients that were submitted by nonPOS between 2005 and 2022. We theorized that this would yield a percentage that was a reasonable estimate of the amount of work on children performed by adult orthopaedists.

Estimating the effect of APPs on the pediatric orthopaedic surgery workforce is even more challenging, as outpatient care is difficult to track as is care delivered in conjunction with a practicing surgeon. To estimate the impact of APPs on the workforce, we used the 2022 POSNA Member Survey, which asked participants about the nature and extent of APP usage in their clinical practice. Lastly, it is nearly impossible to determine which POS are nonoperative, thereby skewing some likely supply assumptions.

Attrition data

Projection of the rate of attrition of POSs was estimated by utilizing the results of the 2022 POSNA Member Survey and the estimated age of exit from practice from the 2022 AMA Masterfile® survey.

Pediatric orthopaedic fellows (Workforce additions)

Both the POSNA database and SF Match were used to identify the number of available fellowship positions and how many fellowship positions were filled from 2014 to 2022. This gives a reliable estimate of the number of new POS entering the workforce each year. The POSNA 2022 Member Survey was also utilized to determine where fellows were trained and their perceptions of level of satisfaction as well as challenges obtaining employment.

Workforce distribution

To determine the geographic distribution of supply vs demand, the US pediatric population was obtained from the 2022 US census data. Active and candidate POSNA members were stratified similarly. Distribution of POS with respect to hospital referral regions was determined using the Dartmouth Atlas of Health Care project to create denominators for analysis of regional distributions of clinicians and demand for their services [5]. Estimated driving distance to visit a POS was plotted over county boundaries using the POSNA database cross-referenced with US census data in the National Bureau of Economic Research (NBER) Zip Code Tabulation Area (ZCTA) database [6].

Pediatric orthopaedic need (Demand)

The Healthcare Cost and Utilization Project (HCUP), Kid's Inpatient Database (KID), and the American College of Surgeons National Surgical Quality Inpatient Program Pediatric (NSQIP) files were used to create calculations on demand for inpatient procedures. Outpatient procedures were not evaluated as there is no standard benchmark available for pediatric-related procedures. ABOS exam data, however, did partially account for outpatient surgical procedures performed.

Population data from the US Census Population Projections and analyses from the Brookings Institute were utilized to identify trends in birth rates and population projections by age group.

Projections

Utilizing the number of POS trained per year while factoring in expected attrition, the number of expected POS over time can be calculated. A model was created to identify projected supply of POS with respect to expected demand based on the expected pediatric population.

Of these, 52% listed “Spine” as their subspecialty, 30% reported “Sports”, and 18% reported “Hip”—these were not exclusive in some instances, e.g. respondents indicted combinations such as: “Hip, deformity, clubfoot,” or “Hip preservation, sports medicine.”

Perceived oversupply from POSNA members

In the 2022 POSNA Member Survey, respondents were asked: “Do you think there will be an oversupply of POS in your region in the next 10 years?” Among respondents, 215 (49.4%) replied “Yes” and 220 (50.6%) replied “No.” Neither age, gender, nor the “urban” character of the practitioner's location was statistically correlated with perceived oversupply. The perception of oversupply correlates strongly (P = .000) with responses to the question: “Do you think that the United States & Canada are training the “right” amount of pediatric orthopaedic fellows?” Of those who felt there was an oversupply, 80% answered “too many,” whereas of those who felt there was no oversupply, only 10% felt there were “too many” fellows being trained. The POSNA survey also asked: “How satisfied are you with your current surgical volume in pediatric orthopaedics?” 57.4% chose the response: “Perfect volume,” while 35.4% chose “Too little volume, would like to do more,” and only 7.3% chose “Too much volume.” This response aligned, roughly, with the respondents' perceptions of an “oversupply.”

The fundamental factor that affects “demand” for physicians, as well as pediatric subspecialists, is the relevant population. The US Census Bureau anticipates that the total US population will grow by 9.3% between 2022 and 2060, but the <18 population is projected to shrink by 12.6%, from 72.5 to 64.3 million. This underlying factor is important in any projection of demand or use of pediatric subspecialty services by children (Fig. 10).Figure 10Projected population growth, 2022 to 2060 based on US census data.Figure 10

Projected population growth, 2022 to 2060 based on US census data.

Analysts of population trends in the US anticipate a slowing rate of growth in the US under 24-population. The Brookings Institute has, for example, published an analysis of existing Census data and projections based on the National Demographic Analysis from the US Census Bureau. Their findings showed that although there is significant total population growth is predicted in the United States, the pediatric population is projected to slowly decrease over time (Fig. 11).Figure 11Brookings projection of population growth rates by age group [9].Figure 11

Brookings projection of population growth rates by age group [9].

The Brookings Report anticipates a slowing of the birth rate in the United States as well, and further suggests that growth in younger age cohorts in the overall population will come primarily from immigration. With an under-24 US national growth rate at nearly zero, relocation—either in or out of a state—will determine if a region registers a gain or loss of young people. This is reflected in US Census Bureau estimates for 2010-to-2020 of state-level changes in under-18 populations: Fully 31 states registered losses in their youth populations, including large swaths of the Northeast, Midwest, and interior South [10]. In contrast, 19 states and Washington, D.C. showed population gains among children, largely due to in-migration (from outside the country and from other states) of younger residents and families with children.

Under those conditions, the overall supply of POS will decline to just below 1,000 by 2034. The final step in this analysis is combining or comparing the growth in anticipated demand or need with anticipated supply. Given the recent trend of a decline in hospital admissions in the pediatric population and an assumed decline in morbidity for that population due to testing, preventive measures, medical interventions, and alternative treatment options in the pediatric population—as well as a very slow rate of overall population growth with a shrinking pediatric population, a comparison of the proportional growth of the number of trained pediatric orthopaedists to the pediatric population shows an increase among practitioners that is faster than growth in the pediatric population.

Discussion

Since POSNA last investigated the pediatric orthopaedic workforce in 2014, there have been several national trends which that affect both the supply of and demand for POS including increased subspecialization in pediatric orthopaedics, shifting percentages in pediatric care provided by general orthopaedic surgeons, increasing roles of APPs, and declining growth of the pediatric population [12]. This workforce analysis was performed to assess the status of the current pediatric orthopaedic workforce and make projections on supply and demand for POS in the future. Variability will continue to exist regarding these projections based on external factors that influence both the supply and demand curves at the local and national level. However, best-effort projections are critical for our society to appropriately assess the need for services provided and the appropriate number of new fellows to train. Given the shortcomings of any model developed to assess future workforce needs, we feel it is important to review these projections with updated data at regular intervals.

In almost all contexts, discussions over supply and demand or requirements or needs for practitioners are hotly contested among the professions themselves. In the twenty-first century in the US, the Association of American Medical Colleges (AAMC) has taken a lead in analyzing and assessing the balance of physician supply and demand. The latest AAMC Report was issued in 2021 as “The Complexities of Physician Supply and Demand: Projections from 2019 to 2034” and was prepared for the AAMC by IHS Markit, Ltd., a successor to the earlier consulting firms that prepared these reports [13]. That report stated that they and the AAMC continue to project that physician demand will grow faster than supply and that will mean shortages in primary care, surgical, and medical specialties. However, they further report that the population under age 18 is projected to grow by only 5.6% during the same period, which portends low growth in demand for pediatric specialties. In a summary statement, the AAMC foresaw a “reduced demand for 3,600 physicians in internal medicine and pediatric subspecialties”.

The AAMC and most policy analysts in the field of health workforce analysis generally use economic theory to guide their analysis of physician demand and supply. This approach can be summarized with the assistance of a “conceptual guide” that considers the nuances of “needs,” “wants,” and “demand” as they relate to “shortages” [14]. That explication of demand considers other factors that might affect policy decisions beyond the simple calculation of supply and demand for medical care. This analysis proceeds under the assumption that any calculation of the balance of supply and demand for medical and surgical care will be complex and only approximate the sum of all the factors that go into decision-making for the training of clinicians.

For surgeons overall, in the US, an analysis of 21 years of AMA Masterfile® data predicted a “surgeon shortage across nine specialties: cardiothoracic, otolaryngology, general surgery, obstetrics—gynecology, ophthalmology, orthopaedics, plastics, urology, and vascular” with workloads “estimated to increase by 10–50%” [15]. That study applied predicted relative value unit (RVU) changes from the Medical Group Management Association (MGMA) along with US Census population projections as proxies for demand to estimate a shortage of 13,542 orthopaedic surgeons by 2050 [16]. Pediatric specialties and subspecialties were not considered in that analysis. The general field of medical workforce analysis presents a picture of emerging shortages--with some exceptions.

For example, an analysis of the future supply and demand for pediatric general surgeons using a subspecialty-specific modeling approach found that the then-current (2016) rapid growth in the subspecialty was likely to exceed demand by 2035 [17]. Another example shows the intersecting effects of changing population and training trends in the orthopaedic care of children, a study by Muffly et al. concluded that the increase in pediatric anesthesiologists was also likely to exceed the growth in both the pediatric population and inpatient procedures between 2015 and 2035 [18]. That conclusion was based on a survey of fellowship program directors that found that graduates in the period 2008–2010 outnumbered the retiring academic faculty by a ratio of 12 to one.

The use of physician assistants (PAs) and nurse practitioners (NPs) has expanded across almost all specialties (which mirror physician specialties), and their continued expanding deployment is anticipated to affect the balance of supply and need for physicians and surgeons, but the specific effect on any given subspecialty is not well understood [19,20].

With those factors in mind, there continues to be an increase in POS supply since 2014. Both the growth in POSNA membership numbers and the number of POS identified by public data files (National Provider Identifier—NPI, NPPES) have shown significant increases since 2014. The POSNA membership and the number of POS identified by NPI and NPPES closely align and can be used as a proxy for the other. In 2014, there were 911 POSNA members (653 active and 258 candidate). This number grew to 1078 POSNA members (active and candidate) in 2022, for an increase of 20.9 members per year. This indicates that previous projections of increased POS have remained largely correct. However, the “flattening” of the growth trend in 2019–2021 may suggest caution in using the overall growth rate to estimate future supply. An earlier analysis of POSNA membership trends that anticipated a steady increase in the number of pediatric orthopaedists was based on the rate of growth through 2014, when the rate was close to its highest over the 20-year period for which data are available.

The trend of increasing POS into the workforce should continue over the next decade. The number of fellowship positions offered through the SF Match has not grown appreciably since 2014 and has remained stable at 73, and the number of filled fellowship positions is approximately 55–60 per year [21]. At this rate, there is projected to be continued growth of POS supply to 1320 by 2034. If the rate of fellows trained per year is at 49, then supply of POS should remain flat over time given expected attrition rates. This growth is contingent on multiple factors, including, but not limited to number of fellowship spots available, subspecialization trends, perception of jobs available, anticipated salary, and satisfaction at work.

When looking at POS supply, the rate of males entering the workforce is projected to stay relatively flat, but there continues to be an increase in the percentage of female POS entering the workforce over time. In 2014, 23% of the 653 POSNA (active+candidate) members were female, compared to 4.4% of all American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons (AAOS) members. In 2022, the percentage of female POSNA members increased to 29.2%, compared to 7.4% of AAOS members. Thus, the trend of increasing female-to-male POS parity is projected to continue to rise and outpace the ratio seen in all orthopaedic surgeons. Based on this increase in rate of entry of female POS into the workforce, we anticipate that female-to-male POS parity likely will be achieved by 2050.

It is concerning that the pediatric population has continued to shrink from 2014 to 2022. The US census data project that there will be a continued decrease in the number of pediatric patients over time, based on current birth and immigration rates. Since demand for pediatric orthopaedic services is primarily dependent on the number of pediatric patients available, this will likely affect the need for POS to some degree. However, the trend for decreasing pediatric patients being treated by adult surgeons will likely increase demand for POS to an extent as well. Thus, it is difficult to determine actual demand for POS services in the future with conflicting data sets.

There are still no surgeon–population ratio benchmark standards that exist for POSs, as opposed to other specialties. Previous studies have identified anywhere between 6.2 and 8.4 surgeons per 100,000 people as the ideal benchmark for surgeon demand [22,23]. Other studies have shown that any less than 6.0 per 100,000 is considered a “surgical desert,” meaning there is limited access to necessary surgical care [24]. What is known already is that there is a maldistribution of POSs, and they are underrepresented in rural and underserved regions in the US [25]. A study from Farivar et al. separated patients into “surgeon deserts” and “surgeon clusters” based on whether the distance to the nearest surgeon was less than or greater than the national average and the average of all neighboring counties, respectively [26]. The mean distance to the nearest pediatric orthopaedic surgeon for a patient residing in a surgeon desert or a surgeon cluster was 141.9 ± 53.8 miles and 30.9 ± 16.0 miles, respectively. Surgeon deserts were found to have lower median household incomes (P < .001) and greater rates of children without health insurance (P < .001). Thus, clearly there are areas that are severely underserved, even if overall national demand appears to have been met. Indeed, our study finds that the geographical distribution of POS continues to remain uneven, with higher concentrations in the northeast, Florida, Texas, Southern California, and the Pacific Northwest. This closely follows the urban areas in which they have completed fellowships. This may be a result of fellows attempting to stay near the areas where they completed fellowships or a result of fellowships usually being located in larger urban areas. To decrease the number of surgical deserts, some degree of private or governmental incentivization may be necessary to attract surgeons to these areas. This may also naturally stabilize if the number of jobs available in overserved regions decreases secondary to falling demand, thereby increasing the likelihood of filling positions in underserved areas.

Lastly, this analysis shows the steady decrease in the number of overall pediatric inpatient discharges over time, but this likely does not completely represent demand for pediatric orthopaedic services, as increasing outpatient case mix and changing practice patterns for POS have led to fewer children being admitted for inpatient procedures. However, certain procedures (like pediatric spinal fusions) have seen an increase over time, indicating that calculating pediatric demand is complex and cannot be explained by hospital inpatient utilization alone.

As with any study looking at workforce projections, there are limitations that should be identified to better understand the context of the analysis. The input data could possibly be inaccurate, both in supply and demand. The data from multiple sources for the supply of pediatric orthopaedists generally converge, however, and the overlap in populations and procedures across the subspecialties in orthopaedics creates problems for assigning a condition or procedure to any particular subspecialty. This creates a need to understand the scope of overlaps, and that will require extensive data describing the content of practice of the subspecialties in orthopaedics, pediatrics, and other overlapping fields. The analysis could leave out variables important to supply and demand. The fundamental data that allow for a calculation of the supply of practitioners are available, but long-term trend data are not available due to the cutoff of the AMA Masterfile series, the lack of physician identifiers in discharge data, and the costs of acquiring and analyzing those data, including the HCUP, discharge databases, and board certification data. It is also possible that incorrect weights were used for the various factors in the analysis. This is a particular problem when assigning cases to specific subspecialties. Again, the overlap of cases between pediatric and non-POSs is not completely understood. However, the trend seen in ABOS case submissions over time indicates that non-POSs are likely taking care of fewer pediatric patients in the future. It is also entirely possible that incorrect assumptions are made about the number of POS entering retirement, for example. Because of the large number of variables present, it is impossible to know if any assumptions made will continue to carry forward over time. Also, the “formula” for calculating supply excludes new policies (such as novel pathways into the specialty) or technologies (new ways to treat both identified and unidentified pediatric musculoskeletal conditions) that may arise. This study is also unable to separate foreign medical graduates undergoing multiyear fellowship training for licensing, which may skew the supply-side data. Lastly, it is impossible to know the effect of physician extenders (nurse practitioners, physician assistants) or nonoperative pediatric orthopaedists in the workforce with regard to POS demand. Physician extenders can provide high-quality and cost-effective pediatric orthopaedic care and may reduce the need for POS for particular conditions, especially nonsurgical ones. And although not addressed by this paper, there continues to be an expansion in the scope of practice of POS, most common in sports, hand, and spine surgery. There also is a trend toward treatment of traditionally pediatric orthopaedic conditions through early adulthood (transitional care), which can lead to the expansion in the number of patients available for the pediatric orthopaedists.

Summary

Based on our best projections, the current rate of training pediatric orthopaedists will result in a slight surplus in POS over the next decade and beyond, largely due to flat population growth projections for patients less than 18 years of age. There will likely continue to be disparity between overserved and underserved regions of the country, with locations near urban centers and pediatric orthopaedic fellowship training sites likely continuing to increase in supply of POS but not addressing the “surgical deserts” that are present throughout the United States. The increasing scope of practice of POS, combined with the decreasing pediatric patient load of non-POSsa, may lead to an increase in apparent demand for POS. This analysis builds on the 2014 work of Sawyer et al. and should be continued on a regular basis to identify new trends in pediatric orthopaedic demand to help POSNA guide fellowship accreditation and subsequent POS supply accordingly.
