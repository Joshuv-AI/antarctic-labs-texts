Veterinary antimicrobial monitoring systems in Brazil, the United Kingdom, and the Netherlands: a descriptive comparison to support system development in Brazil
Authors: Jessica Kayamori Lopes, Briana Gomes, Bruno Machuca Thon, Saulo Henrique Weber, Ruan Rolnei Daros, Fernando Bittencourt Luciano, Claudia Turra Pimpao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The use of antimicrobial agents in animals contributes to the emergence and spread of bacterial antimicrobial resistance, posing risks to human, animal, and environmental health. Monitoring antimicrobial consumption in food-producing animals is a critical component of antimicrobial stewardship. This study describes the current state of antimicrobial use and resistance monitoring in animals in Brazil and compares it to systems implemented in the United Kingdom (UK) and the Netherlands, countries known for having well-established surveillance frameworks. Data were collected between July 2019 and November 2025 from official technical reports on veterinary antimicrobial sales and use, as well as policy documents and legislation. Findings show that the UK and the Netherlands have developed integrated systems based on mandatory data reporting and sector-specific metrics. These systems are supported by coordinated policies and high stakeholder engagement. In contrast, Brazil lacks a national system for monitoring antimicrobial use in animals. Sales data are not publicly available, and there is no standardized mechanism to track antimicrobial consumption on farms. Although policy frameworks exist, enforcement is limited, and access to surveillance data is fragmented. The absence of a centralized, transparent system hinders Brazil’s capacity to evaluate antimicrobial use patterns or inform targeted interventions. Given Brazil’s leading role in global animal protein production, addressing these gaps is essential to advancing national stewardship efforts and contributing to global strategies to contain bacterial antimicrobial resistance.

Introduction

In 2020, the global antimicrobial use in food-producing animals was estimated at 99,502 tons, with projections suggesting an increase of 8.0 % by 2030 [1]. This sustained level of antimicrobial use creates selective pressure on pathogenic and commensal bacteria, accelerating the emergence and spread of bacterial antimicrobial resistance (AMR) [2]. Resistant bacteria and resistance genes can disseminate through the food chain, the environment, and direct contact, posing serious risks to human, animal, and environmental health [3]. Notably, in 2021, 4.71 million deaths were associated with bacterial AMR, including 1.14 million deaths directly attributable to it [4].

The international community has made important commitments to address bacterial AMR. The European Union (EU), for example, banned the use of antimicrobial growth promoters in animal feed in 2006 [5] and, in 2019, introduced new rules that restrict imports of animal products from countries that allow such practices [6]. Brazil has taken similar steps, including the ban on colistin in animal feed in 2016 [7]. Despite these regulatory advances, efforts to measure and monitor antimicrobial use and resistance in livestock remain uneven across countries [8].

One of the most widely used approaches for estimating antimicrobial consumption in animals is the analysis of sales data. These data serve as a proxy for use in different production sectors and animal species, and as a basis for evaluating the effectiveness of public policies aimed at controlling antimicrobial distribution [9]. However, there is considerable variation in how these data are collected, reported, and used. While countries like the United Kingdom (UK) and the Netherlands have established integrated, digitized systems that support surveillance and policy refinement [10,11], other nations, including Brazil, face structural, institutional, and logistical barriers to implementing similar systems [8,12]. In Brazil, livestock production is characterized by a dual structure. Family farming comprises 3.9 million establishments, representing 77 % of all agricultural properties in the country, yet occupying only 23 % of Brazil’s agricultural land [13]. Commercial livestock farms, although fewer in number, are responsible for the majority of Brazil’s animal production and supply most of the formal market. This structural context influences the feasibility and scalability of antimicrobial stewardship initiatives, particularly regarding veterinary oversight and data collection.

The goal of this article is to describe the current landscape of veterinary antimicrobial use monitoring in Brazil and compare it to the systems adopted by the UK and the Netherlands. By identifying key differences, strengths, and challenges, this comparative analysis helps inform potential strategies to improve Brazil’s capacity for antimicrobial stewardship in the animal health sector. Given Brazil’s status as one of the world’s leading producers and exporters of animal protein [14], advancing its surveillance and regulatory frameworks is essential to protecting food safety, aligning with international standards, and reducing the risk of AMR at the human–animal–environment interface.

Methods

Ethical aspects

This study relied exclusively on publicly available documents and did not involve human participants or animals directly. Therefore, no ethical approval was required.

Study design and data collection

A non-systematic review was conducted using data collected between July 2019 and July 2025 from official websites managed by government agencies, international organizations, and national institutes responsible for monitoring the veterinary use of antimicrobials in Brazil, the UK, and the Netherlands, with no date restrictions applied for the selection of documents. The UK and the Netherlands were selected as reference countries due to their long-standing, well-documented, and internationally recognized approaches to reducing antimicrobial use in animals and improving AMR monitoring [10,15]. Both countries have achieved measurable reductions in veterinary antimicrobial consumption and have transparent, publicly accessible data collection and reporting mechanisms [11,15,16].

The selection of the UK and the Netherlands was also informed by the feasibility of in-person data collection. Members of the research team were able to be physically present in these countries during the study period, enabling direct contact with relevant stakeholders and access to contextual information not always captured in official documents. This process included informal, non-structured discussions with professionals involved in antimicrobial stewardship and surveillance activities, which supported the identification, interpretation, and contextualization of publicly available data sources. These interactions were used solely to guide data collection and were not treated as primary data sources. Primary data sources included annual technical reports on veterinary antimicrobial sales and use, as well as policy documents and guidelines outlining strategies to reduce the use of antimicrobials in animals. We also reviewed legislation and regulations related to antimicrobial use in animals and documents describing the structure, governance, and operational details of veterinary antimicrobial monitoring systems.

For each country, the following information was extracted: (1) regulatory frameworks and policies targeting antimicrobial use in animals, (2) data collection systems for veterinary antimicrobial sales and use, and (3) implementation mechanisms, including enforcement strategies and stakeholder responsibilities in antimicrobial stewardship efforts.

The UK

Regulatory framework and governance

The UK formally implemented a national system for monitoring antimicrobial sales and resistance in 2014 [17], while still a member of the EU. This system was based on a harmonized surveillance framework established by the European Food Safety Authority [18] under Directive 2003/99/EC [19], which focused on AMR in major food-producing animals, including cattle, pigs, poultry, and turkeys. Following the UK’s departure from the EU in 2020 (Brexit), the UK’s Veterinary Medicines Directorate (VMD) assumed full responsibility for maintaining and further developing this surveillance system independently.

Monitoring of antimicrobial sales and use

Antimicrobial sales data are legally required and submitted annually to the VMD by all marketing authorization holders of veterinary antibiotics. The reported figures correspond to the total quantity (in tons) of active ingredient sold per product. These data are calculated using the product’s quantitative composition and the number of units sold [11]. Upon receipt, the VMD collates, validates, and cross-checks these data against periodic safety update reports (PSURs) submitted by marketing authorization holders for pharmacovigilance purposes [11]. Population data used to calculate the Population Correction Unit (PCU)—the denominator for mg/PCU estimates—are obtained from the Department for Environment, Food & Rural Affairs (for terrestrial animals), Centre for Environment, Fisheries and Aquaculture Science (for aquaculture), and TRAde Control and Expert System (for trade figures) [11]. From 2009 to 2022, the UK also contributed veterinary antimicrobial sales data to the European Surveillance of Veterinary Antimicrobial Consumption (ESVAC) project, coordinated by the European Medicines Agency (EMA). With nine participating countries in 2009, ESVAC expanded over time, with 25 countries voluntarily reporting sales data continuously between 2011 and 2022 [20]. The ESVAC project was discontinued in November 2023, with the publication of its final report. From 2024 onwards, data collection on veterinary antimicrobial sales and use in EU Member States transitioned to the mandatory system established under Regulation (EU) 2019/6 [6].

The Veterinary Medicines Regulations 2013 regulate medicated feedstuffs, including antibiotics, permitting their use in poultry and pig feed only under prescription by a licensed veterinarian and on authorized farms [21]. Each prescription must correspond to a single course of treatment and must be used within five days of issuance. The prophylactic use of antibiotics in feed is prohibited, except in exceptional clinical circumstances where the risk of infection is deemed high [21]. Although data on antimicrobial use can be collected through voluntary industry programs [22], there is no legal obligation for producers, veterinarians, or feed manufacturers to report these data directly to government authorities.

In companion animals, prescription-level data are collected through voluntary participation by veterinary clinics enrolled in surveillance initiatives [23]. These clinics use compatible practice management software that enables the extraction and electronic transmission of data in near real-time. Collected data include the species, sex, age, estimated weight, active substance prescribed, dosage, duration of treatment, and the clinical indication for treatment [24]. However, because participation is limited and not nationally representative, these data are used for exploratory analyses only. In the Veterinary Antimicrobial Resistance and Sales Surveillance (UK-VARSS) report, estimates of antimicrobial use in companion animals are based solely on sales data reported by marketing authorization holders, without linkage to actual prescription or treatment records [11].

AMR surveillance

AMR surveillance is carried out in healthy animals at slaughter, with laboratory testing focused on bacteria such as Escherichia coli, Salmonella spp., and, more recently, Campylobacter and Enterococcus spp. In 2023, the surveillance program included pigs, with poultry monitored on a rotating basis, and pilot studies initiated in cattle and sheep. In addition, clinical surveillance captures resistance data from pathogens isolated from diseased animals, using diagnostic samples submitted by veterinarians to public laboratories [11].

Currently, surveillance for AMR in companion animals in the UK only covers a limited number of clinically unwell animals [11] and there is no representative national surveillance system for AMR in healthy dogs and cats. Recognizing this gap, the UK’s VMD has launched a pilot study to assess antibiotic resistance levels in healthy household dogs and cats across the country. This initiative, conducted in collaboration with Scotland’s Rural College, aims over a four-year period to generate AMR surveillance data from fecal samples contributed by pet owners and veterinary practices, with the goal of identifying reliable methods for national companion animal AMR monitoring and understanding AMR transmission between pets and humans [25].

Trends in antimicrobial use

Between 2014 and 2022, total sales of veterinary antibiotics in the UK decreased by 59 % [11]. This decline was observed across all major livestock sectors, including a significant reduction in the use of critically important antibiotics. Notably, sales of polymyxins fell by 99.7 % and third- and fourth-generation cephalosporins by 80 % during the same period [11]. These trends reflect the effectiveness of the UK’s stewardship program.

Netherlands

Regulatory framework and governance

The Netherlands has adopted a proactive approach to AMR since 1998 [26], with increasing national priority given to the issue following public concern about methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus and extended-spectrum beta-lactamase-producing bacteria in farm animals [10]. Since 2002, national surveillance of veterinary antimicrobial use and resistance has been documented annually through the Monitoring of Antimicrobial Resistance and Antibiotic Usage in Animals (MARAN) report [27,28]. From 2002 until 2010, the MARAN reports were published through a collaboration led by the National Institute for Public Health and the Environment and the Central Veterinary Institute of Wageningen University & Research. From 2010 onwards, the Netherlands Veterinary Medicines Institute (Autoriteit Diergeneesmiddelen, SDa) became responsible for the coordination of antimicrobial use data and contributes these analyses to the MARAN reports.

In 2008, stakeholders across the livestock production chain (supported by government authorities and veterinary associations) developed species-specific action plans for poultry, swine, and cattle. These plans introduced sectoral monitoring systems focused on antimicrobial prescriptions at the farm level, the incidence of bacterial AMR, and clearly defined responsibilities for both veterinarians and producers [10]. Responsibilities include the mandatory registration of all prescribed and delivered antimicrobials on farms, the establishment of Farm Health and Farm Treatment Plans, and compliance with benchmarking targets [10]. In the Netherlands, veterinarians must adhere to prescribing guidelines and are subject to audits and sanctions if excessive use is detected [10]. Farmers are required to work with a designated veterinary practice and ensure the accurate submission of data for monitoring purposes [10].

In 2010, the Dutch government introduced a mandatory policy aimed at reducing antimicrobial use in animals by 50 % by 2013, using 2009 as the baseline year. In 2012, this target was revised to a 70 % reduction by 2015 for total livestock production [26]. To support the implementation of this policy, an independent organization—the SDa—was established in 2010 [29]. The SDa is responsible for analyzing and reporting data on antimicrobial prescriptions and use at the farm level. It also sets annual reduction targets by animal species and production sector, enabling benchmarking across farms and guiding stewardship interventions.

Monitoring of antimicrobial sales and use

The current surveillance system in the Netherlands integrates two complementary data streams to monitor veterinary antimicrobial sales and use. Sales data are collected annually from marketing authorization holders and submitted to the Dutch Organization for Producers and Importers of Veterinary Medicines, which compiles national sales volumes by antimicrobial class. The consolidated data are shared with the SDa for national monitoring and reported in the MARAN. From 2024 onwards, marketing authorization holders must also submit sales volumes to the EU Union Product Database (UPD) in compliance with Regulation (EU) 2019/6 [6]. Farm-level prescription and use data must be recorded by veterinarians through certified digital platforms, such as MediRund, VeeOnline, or VetCis. These data are transmitted to species-specific sectoral databases managed by producer organizations and subsequently consolidated by the SDa, which validates, analyzes, and reports more detailed results in the yearly SDa reports [30]. These SDa reports combine datasets to present information on antimicrobial consumption, including trends by species, farm type, and antibiotic category. It also provides comparisons between the volumes of antibiotics sold to veterinarians and those used on farms [30]. Similarly to the UK, the Netherlands has reported veterinary antimicrobial sales and use data to EMA under Regulation (EU) 2019/6 since 2024 [6].

Data on antimicrobial use in companion animals are primarily derived from veterinary antimicrobial sales data [28]. Under Regulation (EU) 2019/6, the scope of veterinary antimicrobial monitoring is expected to expand progressively, with mandatory use monitoring for cats and dogs planned from 2029. This future framework will incorporate prescription- and use-level data, including cascade use of antimicrobial medicinal products authorized for human use [28].

AMR surveillance

AMR surveillance in food-producing animals in the Netherlands focuses primarily on major livestock sectors and includes laboratory testing of bacterial isolates obtained from both healthy and diseased animals. Isolates from healthy animals are mainly collected through active monitoring at slaughterhouses, focusing on indicator bacteria like E. coli [28] while isolates from diseased animals originate from submissions to veterinary diagnostic laboratories [28]. AMR monitoring in companion animals remains more limited and largely relies on clinical isolates from a restricted number of clinically ill animals [28].

Trends in antimicrobial use

The establishment of clear targets to reduce antimicrobial use in livestock resulted in a progressive decline in veterinary antimicrobial consumption in the Netherlands. The government-defined objective (set in 2010) of a 50 % reduction by 2013 was achieved by 2012 [29]. By 2023, the total reduction had reached 77 % compared to 2009 levels [28].

Brazil

Regulatory framework and governance

Brazilian institutions, including the Ministry of Health, the Ministry of Agriculture, Livestock and Supply (MAPA), and the National Health Surveillance Agency (ANVISA), developed the National Action Plan for the Prevention and Control of Antimicrobial Resistance [31]. The plan was published in 2019 and outlines a series of strategic actions for the period from 2018 to 2022. In the animal health sector, this includes the implementation of surveillance programs for AMR in bacteria isolated from slaughterhouses and animal production chains, the development of systems to monitor antimicrobial use on farms, and the evaluation of the quality of veterinary antimicrobials. Additional measures include structuring a national laboratory network for antimicrobial quality assessment, promoting good veterinary practices, and encouraging the development of therapeutic protocols and rational use guidelines for antimicrobials in animals [31]. However, the surveillance system for AMR in animals proposed in the plan has not been implemented to date.

MAPA manages the Integrated System of Agricultural Products and Establishments (SIPEAGRO), a digital platform used to register establishments involved in the manufacture, storage, distribution, commercialization, manipulation, import, or export of veterinary products, including antimicrobials [32]. In addition, veterinarians who prescribe or administer controlled veterinary drugs in ambulatory settings must be registered with MAPA and are required to use standardized prescription and acquisition forms provided through the SIPEAGRO system [32]. This system is accessible to government inspectors and plays an important role in regulatory oversight by ensuring traceability, standardization, and compliance with legal requirements throughout the veterinary pharmaceutical supply chain.

The Department of Animal Products Inspection at MAPA is responsible for regulating and overseeing the manufacturing and commercial distribution of veterinary-use products. Federal public policies cover the registration and authorization for the production and sale of these products [32,33], the regulation of growth-promoting antimicrobials [34], oversight of veterinary drug quality [34,35], and enforcement of good veterinary practices. In Brazil, only veterinary medicinal products registered with the MAPA may be used in animals. Although the use of human medicinal products in animals is not prohibited, it must occur under veterinary prescription [36,37].

Between 2019 and 2021, MAPA published quarterly bulletins with SIPEAGRO data, containing information on the registration and administrative updates of agricultural establishments and veterinary products, such as requests for new registrations and modifications to existing records. However, these reports did not specify which products were antimicrobials or the quantities sold. In 2022, MAPA launched its business intelligence dashboards, a public data analysis tool that allows users to explore information on veterinary establishments and pharmaceutical products registered in Brazil. The platform enables the identification of the drug classes, active pharmaceutical ingredients, and main animal species targeted by veterinary products in the country. The business intelligence dashboards show the number of veterinary antimicrobials registered in Brazil, but do not provide data on the quantities sold or distributed [38].

Monitoring of antimicrobial sales and use

Brazil does not currently have a national system that reports quantitative data on veterinary antimicrobial sales or use.

AMR surveillance

Although surveillance initiatives have been proposed within national action plans, no publicly available data is currently generated to monitor AMR in either food-producing or companion animals at the national level.

Trends in antimicrobial use

Assessment of trends in veterinary antimicrobial use is not currently feasible in Brazil, as there is no publicly available national system reporting quantitative data on antimicrobial sales or use in animals. Moreover, there is no country-specific data available through global reporting initiatives such as those coordinated by the World Organisation for Animal Health [39]. Existing data sources are limited to product and establishment registries and do not support longitudinal analyses comparable to those conducted in the UK and the Netherlands.

Discussion

The experiences of the UK and the Netherlands illustrate how sustained institutional commitment, coordinated governance, and long-term investment in surveillance infrastructure can shape effective strategies to monitor and reduce antimicrobial use in animals. These efforts enabled the development of integrated surveillance systems that generate data across both human and veterinary sectors [11,28]. Data collection in these systems is digital, detailed, and continuous, allowing for monitoring, benchmarking, and policy refinement. Their strength is further supported by active engagement from relevant stakeholders, including veterinarians, producers, and regulatory agencies, as well as public awareness driven by mass media, consumer demand, and growing concerns over food safety and AMR [10,40,41]. Importantly, the UK and the Netherlands initiated their national action plans and surveillance strategies more than a decade before Brazil. This time gap partly explains the differences in system maturity and implementation progress across countries.

Despite the regulatory framework and strategic planning efforts outlined in the 2018–2022 National Action Plan [31], Brazil still lacks a harmonized system for the systematic monitoring of antimicrobial use and resistance in animals. No standardized metrics are currently in place to quantify antimicrobial sales or usage or to track resistance trends in bacterial isolates from animals. As of 2025, no updated action plan focused on the animal health sector has been released for the post-2022 period, which hinders continuity in stewardship efforts. Relevant laws, decrees, and regulations concerning veterinary antimicrobials are dispersed across multiple platforms and not centralized in a single official repository. This fragmentation compromises information accessibility and undermines efforts to promote informed and consistent use of antimicrobials across the sector.

Building a national system for monitoring antimicrobial use and resistance in animals is a complex and time-intensive process. It requires multisectoral coordination, sustained investment in infrastructure, and capacity building across multiple levels of the animal health system [42]. These challenges are particularly pronounced in low- and middle-income countries, where technical resources may be limited and competing public health priorities often reduce the attention given to veterinary surveillance [12,43,44]. In addition, national characteristics, such as production models, territorial size, availability of trained professionals, and governmental organization, directly influence the feasibility of implementing structured surveillance systems. For example, the Netherlands collects digital antimicrobial use data from around 42,000 farms through a centralized reporting platform [27]. In contrast, Brazil has over five million agricultural establishments, 77 % of which are classified as family farms [13]. Although these farms account for only 23 % of the country’s total agricultural production [13], their high number makes it more challenging to implement standardized prescription controls and electronic data collection. Strategies such as incentivizing voluntary data sharing through public-private partnerships, integrating reporting into existing animal health programs, and providing technical support or subsidies for digital adoption could help address these structural barriers and support the development of a scalable national surveillance system. Within this context, larger commercial livestock operations may represent a more feasible and strategic starting point for implementing antimicrobial stewardship and surveillance systems. In addition to having more structured production systems, greater access to veterinary services, and closer integration with formal supply chains, these farms contribute a substantial share of Brazil’s animal production. Prioritizing stewardship measures among industrial stakeholders could therefore yield earlier and broader impact, generating evidence, capacity, and infrastructure that can later be expanded to smaller family farms as implementation systems mature.

In Brazil, the human health sector already operates the National System for the Management of Controlled Products (SNGPC), a digital platform that tracks antimicrobial sales in pharmacies [45]. This experience shows that it is possible to integrate digital tools in public health policy. Strengthening regulatory mechanisms and aligning veterinary antimicrobial distribution with existing systems like the SNGPC could improve oversight and support the implementation of rational use policies in the veterinary sector. Notably, the SNGPC already captures data on human antimicrobials used in animals, suggesting opportunities for cross-sector data integration. Such efforts would reinforce multisectoral collaboration and enhance the impact of public policies on antimicrobial stewardship.

The selection of the UK and the Netherlands was partly guided by pragmatic considerations related to in-country access and stakeholder engagement, which may limit the generalizability of comparisons to other high-income countries or to low- and middle-income settings. Nevertheless, these countries illustrate how combining policy enforcement, professional accountability, and systematic data analysis and diffusion leads to measurable reductions in antimicrobial use and resistance. Both countries reported significant declines in overall sales of veterinary antimicrobials, especially critically important classes, following the implementation of structured stewardship programs and digital monitoring systems [11,28]. These achievements are underpinned by a strong regulatory framework. In the EU, only licensed veterinary antimicrobial products can be sold, exclusively to registered veterinarians, and prescriptions may only be issued by veterinarians in accordance with Regulation (EU) 2019/6 [6]. Over-the-counter sales of antimicrobials are strictly prohibited and subject to high penalties if identified. Moreover, prophylactic use is highly restricted and allowed only in exceptional and justified circumstances [6].

In Brazil, the sale of antimicrobials used in animals does require a veterinary prescription [32]. However, dispensing is not restricted to licensed veterinarians and antimicrobials are often sold over the counter by non-professional staff in veterinary pharmacies and agricultural supply stores. Strengthening the implementation and enforcement of existing legal requirements is therefore a critical step to ensure that antimicrobial use data are reliable and representative. Without consistent compliance by veterinarians, retailers, and farmers, monitoring systems risk capturing information only from those who follow the rules, resulting in biased datasets and undermining fair benchmarking efforts. The establishment of an effective surveillance system also depends on strengthening laboratory capacity for AMR testing and investing in the education and continuous training of veterinarians and other professionals involved in animal health. Given Brazil’s position as one of the world’s largest exporters of animal protein [14], there is also an opportunity to engage the livestock industry as a financial and strategic partner in the development and maintenance of antimicrobial use and resistance surveillance systems. Industry participation could support infrastructure, laboratory networks, and digital reporting systems, contributing to long-term system sustainability. In addition to public health considerations, there are also economic incentives for Brazil to strengthen antimicrobial stewardship, as the country may face trade and market access implications if importing countries adopt stricter requirements or restrictive measures related to antimicrobial use and stewardship standards. Anticipating these potential market pressures by investing in robust stewardship practices could therefore protect Brazil’s competitiveness in the global animal protein market while aligning the sector with emerging international expectations.

It is important to note that the current measures used to express antimicrobial use in the UK and the Netherlands are not directly comparable, although they remain effective for tracking national trends over time in both countries. At the European level, comparable metrics are still under development through initiatives such as the Joint Interagency Antimicrobial Consumption and Resistance Analysis (JIACRA) [46]. A One Health approach to antimicrobial use and resistance monitoring requires harmonization across human, animal, and food systems at national and international levels [47]. This includes aligning data sources (e.g., prescription vs. sales data) and standardizing key methodological elements, such as units of measurement for antimicrobial use, sample types and laboratory methods for AMR, and the criteria used to interpret results [47,48]. Standardizing these components would improve cross-country comparability and support the exchange of lessons learned and effective stewardship strategies. Moreover, antimicrobial use selects for AMR, and reductions in antimicrobial use are often followed by decreases in AMR [49]. Therefore, having data on both is essential to enable meaningful comparative analyses.

Finally, in addition to AMR surveillance in healthy animals, monitoring resistance in diseased animals is essential to inform clinical decision-making in veterinary practice and detect emerging resistance patterns of direct therapeutic relevance [50,51]. Several countries have implemented complementary systems that collect AMR data from clinical isolates. For example, national systems such as RESAPATH in France, GERM-Vet and Laboklin in Germany, and FINRES-Vet in Finland contribute to the EARS-Vet, a European network for surveillance of AMR in animal pathogens [52]. Integrating data from both healthy and diseased animal populations provides a more comprehensive understanding of resistance dynamics and enhances the effectiveness of antimicrobial stewardship policies.

Conclusion

To advance toward more effective antimicrobial stewardship, Brazil must prioritize the development of a harmonized framework for monitoring the sales and use of antimicrobials in animals. This system should include mandatory data reporting by manufacturers, veterinary professionals and other sellers (such as pharmacies, agroshops, veterinary clinics, and hospitals), centralized digital platforms, and clear metrics for tracking usage patterns and resistance trends. Strengthening the enforcement of veterinary prescription regulations, improving inspection capacity, and expanding access to education on best practices are also essential steps. Finally, it is also important to enhance laboratory capacity and train professionals to analyze, document, and communicate data on AMR. To be effective, these measures must align with a coordinated, One Health approach that addresses the interlinked contributions of human, animal, and environmental sectors to AMR.
