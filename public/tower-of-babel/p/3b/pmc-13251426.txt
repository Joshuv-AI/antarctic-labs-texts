Minimally Clinically Important Difference of the Clinical Assessment Scale in Autoimmune Encephalitis
Authors: Yihui Goh, Yoon Hee Shin, Soo Hyun Ahn, Su Yee Mon, Soo Jean Shin, Woo‐Jin Lee, Yoonhyuk Jang, Myoung‐Jin Jang, Soon‐Tae Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe Clinical Assessment Scale in Autoimmune Encephalitis (CASE) tracks disease severity in autoimmune encephalitis (AE), but no threshold for significant change exists. We aimed to determine the minimally clinically important difference (MCID) for CASE. Using our AE cohort, receiver operating characteristic analyses were anchored to ≥ 1‐point improvement in mRS over 3‐month intervals. Among 222 AE patients (65 NMDAR, 49 LGI1, and 108 seronegative AE), a 30% CASE reduction showed good discriminatory performance for mRS improvement in the first 6 months, particularly in NMDAR and seronegative subgroups. A 30% CASE reduction may standardize AE response definitions.Keywords: autoimmune encephalitis, clinical assessment scale in autoimmune encephalitis, outcomes

Introduction

The Clinical Assessment Scale in Autoimmune Encephalitis (CASE) score is an outcome scale for autoimmune encephalitis (AE) [1] with increasing clinical utility. Unlike the modified Rankin Scale (mRS), which primarily reflects motor disability, the CASE score captures a broader spectrum of AE manifestations, making it more sensitive and disease‐specific [2, 3]. Unlike other established neurology scales such as UPDRS and MMSE [4], a validated threshold for clinically meaningful change in the CASE score has not been defined, which is necessary for use as a primary endpoint in AE clinical trials.

Determining what constitutes a significant change in the CASE score can provide a framework to address this gap. Minimally clinically important difference (MCID) can be defined as the smallest change in physician or patient‐reported outcome measures that is perceived as meaningful by both patients and clinicians [5, 6, 7]. Here, we aim to define an operational MCID of the CASE score based on cohort data of key seropositive and seronegative AEs, enabling a consistent benchmark of clinical response in AE.

Methods

Study Population

Adult AE patients (age ≥ 18 years old) with available longitudinal CASE scores at Seoul National University Hospital from 1 January 2014 to 31 December 2024 were included. Antibody testing was performed by screening patients' serum and CSF with rat brain immunohistochemical staining (in‐house or Euroimmun Ag), cell‐based analyses for synaptic/cell surface antibodies (Euroimmun Ag), and immunoblotting for intracellular antibodies (Euroimmun Ag), as previously described [8]. All patients met criteria for autoimmune encephalitis (NMDAR subgroup: definite NMDA receptor‐antibody encephalitis [9]; LGI1 subgroup: definite LGI1‐antibody encephalitis [10]; seronegative subgroup: autoantibody‐negative but probable autoimmune encephalitis and definite autoimmune limbic encephalitis) [9]. An investigation panel for infectious encephalitis included cultures, polymerase chain reaction or antibody assays for infectious pathogens including Herpesviridae, Enterovirus, Japanese B virus, Mycobacteria, and fungi. Neoplasms, prion disease and demyelinating diseases were also excluded with aquaporin‐4 or myelin oligodendrocyte glycoprotein antibody testing, cytology, flow cytometry and other relevant diagnostic modalities [9]. The CASE score was evaluated by trained neurologists (G.Y., Y.S., A.S.H., W.‐J.L., Y.J., or S‐T.L.).

Given the often‐subacute disease progression in AE [9], the highest mRS/CASE score recorded within the first 2 weeks of symptom onset was designated as the baseline value. Patients with baseline CASE scores of ≤ 2 were excluded to mitigate floor effects. In follow‐up timepoint analyses, patients with missing CASE scores were excluded from that specific analysis.

This study was approved by the Seoul National University Hospital Institutional Review Board (IRB no. 1402‐047‐555, 1705‐130‐856, and 2308‐152‐1459) and written informed consent was obtained from all patients and/or legal guardians.

Derivation of MCID Threshold and Statistical Analysis

We derived an anchor‐based, ROC‐derived CASE change threshold (operational MCID) using ≥ 1‐point improvement in mRS over the same interval. To reduce the impact of varied baseline values, we calculated the percentage change in CASE for each interval (0–12 weeks, 12 weeks–6 months, 6–9 months, 9–12 months) and constructed receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves to determine optimal thresholds by maximizing Youden's index. Given differing disease trajectories within AE subgroups, this was performed separately for the pooled AE cohort and individual N‐methyl‐D‐aspartate receptor‐antibody (NMDAR), leucine‐rich glioma‐inactivated 1 (LGI1), and seronegative subgroups.

To derive a standardized threshold, the sensitivity and specificity of multiple candidate cutoffs were compared across subgroups. Additional sensitivity analyses were performed, including calculation of the mean change percentage of CASE score between patients with a 1‐point mRS improvement (ΔmRS = −1) and those with no change (ΔmRS = 0). In NMDAR and seronegative AE groups, further MCID threshold analyses were stratified by baseline CASE scores.

Statistical analysis was performed with R software v4.4.2 (2024; R Team, Vienna, Austria). Continuous variables were summarized as mean (standard deviation, SD) and categorical variables as counts and percentages.

Results

Demographics and Disease Trajectory

A total of 222 patients were included: 65 NMDAR, 49 LGI1 and 108 seronegative AE patients (Figure 1). The mean age of patients was 45.2 (18.5), 29.4 (10.1), 61.8 (9.8), and 47.2 (18.0) years for the pooled AE, NMDAR, LGI1 and seronegative subgroups respectively. Male patients accounted for 107 (48%) of the overall AE cohort: 15 (23%) in NMDAR, 36 (74%) in LGI1 and 56 (52%) in seronegative AE groups. Thirty‐one (48%) NMDAR encephalitis patients had coexisting teratomas.

Flowchart illustrating patient selection for the study cohort.

NMDAR and seronegative AE patients presented with higher CASE scores at onset and showed rapid improvement, whereas LGI1 patients had lower initial CASE scores and hence smaller detectable changes (Figure 2). In the overall AE cohort, mean CASE scores decreased from 13.6 at baseline to 6.1 at 1 year and 5.3 at 2 years; corresponding mRS scores improved from 4.3 to 2.4 and then 2.0. By subgroup, baseline CASE scores were 18.2 (NMDAR), 4.1 (LGI1), and 15.2 (seronegative AE), improving to 6.1, 2.5, and 7.4 at 1 year, and to 4.2, 1.8, and 6.5 at 2 years. Baseline mRS scores were 4.6 (NMDAR), 3.2 (LGI1), and 4.6 (seronegative AE), improving to 2.0, 2.2, and 2.7 at 1 year, and further to 1.4, 1.7, and 2.2 at 2 years (Table S1).

Calculation of Optimal MCID

From baseline to 3 months, the optimal MCID for predicting a ≥ 1‐point improvement in mRS corresponded to a CASE score reduction of 21.1% in the overall cohort (AUC 0.95, sensitivity 0.96, specificity 0.84). In contrast, the performance of MCID prediction using absolute CASE score differences was poor (data not shown). Subgroup thresholds were 29.2% in NMDAR (AUC 1.00, sensitivity 1.00, specificity 0.95), 22.5% in LGI1 (AUC 0.84, sensitivity 0.79, specificity 0.82) and 27.9% in seronegative AE (AUC 0.96, sensitivity 0.94, specificity 0.88) (Table 1A).

Using a fixed MCID of 30% reduction in CASE score showed good sensitivity and specificity both from baseline to 3 months and from 3 to 6 months, especially in the pooled AE cohort, NMDAR and seronegative subgroups (Table 1B). For the overall cohort, from baseline to 3 months, this threshold yielded an AUC of 0.95, sensitivity 0.90 and specificity of 0.88. Subgroup results were consistent: NMDAR (AUC 1.00, sensitivity 1.00, specificity 0.95), LGI1 (AUC 0.84, sensitivity 0.67, specificity 0.82), and seronegative AE (AUC 0.96, sensitivity 0.92, specificity 0.88). Performance at 3 to 6 months was similar.

Across most time points, patients with 1‐point improvement in mRS (ΔmRS = −1) showed mean percentage CASE reduction clustered around 30% when compared to those without change (ΔmRS = 0), both in the pooled AE cohort and within subgroups (Table S2).

Further sensitivity analyses confirmed the robustness of the 30% threshold in NMDAR and seronegative AE when stratified by baseline CASE scores (Table S3). This stratification was less informative in LGI1 encephalitis as few patients had high baseline CASE scores (data not shown).

Discussion

The anchor‐based threshold of 30% reduction in CASE score demonstrates good sensitivity and specificity predicting mRS improvement within the first 6 months, particularly in patients with greater baseline disease burden, such as the NMDAR and seronegative AE subgroups. However, the sensitivity of this threshold in the LGI1 group was lower, due to low baseline CASE scores [11, 12]. In subgroups with lower CASE scores, such as LGI1 and post‐acute (> 6 months from onset) AE patients, a 30% change may correlate with a lower absolute value of change and hence be less clinically significant and more susceptible to confounding by variables such as inter‐rater variability. Analysis of alternative anchors with complementary measures such as the Global Rating of Change and other patient‐reported outcomes (PROMs) may further enhance assessment, especially in LGI1 and post‐acute AE patients [5, 11, 12, 13]. Furthermore, the seronegative AE subgroup is clinically heterogenous [14] and hence this single threshold may not apply equally well across all phenotypes of seronegative AE.

The CASE score is well‐validated in AE and offers several advantages over the mRS for outcome assessment [3, 15, 16]. It captures disease‐specific AE manifestations, such as seizure, memory decline, psychiatric symptoms, and dyskinesia. Despite limitations such as floor effects, reduced sensitivity to long‐term mood symptoms [17], and transient fluctuations from comorbidities [18], it allows for a more comprehensive assessment of disease severity and greater sensitivity to clinical change [1, 2, 3]. When integrated with an operational, ROC‐derived MCID threshold, the CASE score shows further advantage in the acute setting as an objective physician‐administered outcome that remains applicable even in unconscious or uncooperative patients [16].

Our findings support the CASE score as a sensitive, multidimensional measure of AE disease severity and treatment response, and propose 30% reduction as an operational response threshold, especially in NMDAR and seronegative patients within the first 6 months. Adoption of the proposed threshold could provide a practical benchmark for defining clinical response, improving consistency of outcome reporting and enabling comparability across future therapeutic trials in autoimmune encephalitis. Further studies with alternative anchors may be helpful in the post‐acute phase of AE and LGI1 patients, where symptom burden is predominantly driven by cognitive and mood disturbances [11, 13].
