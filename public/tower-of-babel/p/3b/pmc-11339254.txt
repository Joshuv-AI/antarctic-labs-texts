Reply to: The effects of ENDOG on lipid metabolism may be tissue-dependent and may not require its translocation from mitochondria
Authors: Wenjun Wang, Qinghua Zhou
Published: 2024

replying to M. Llovera et al. Nature Communications 10.1038/s41467-024-51447-x (2024)

The authors welcome the opportunity to address Llovera and colleagues’ questions and concerns about our article.

About the concern that ENDOG-regulated lipid metabolism requires its translocation from mitochondria, Llovera et al. say that ENDOG knockout could reduce lipid accumulation in the control medium, so they think “without exiting the mitochondria, ENDOG can already regulate lipid synthesis and/or accumulation in the liver”. It has been well-known that ENDOG can be released from mitochondria and translocated into nuclear to promote apoptosis1,2. Furthermore, David used density gradients and sequential centrifugations to show that ENDOG is localized primarily in the inner membrane and matrix3. In addition, ENDOG is released from the inner membrane upon treatment with Na2CO33. Moreover, Llovera et al. also showed that ENDOG can be released into cytosol and nucleus under ischemia conditions by Mitochondria/Nuclei/Cytosol Extraction and immunofluorescence staining (Yang et al., Figure 54). There is no need to argue that ENDOG can be released from mitochondria. Naturally, when knocked out, ENDOG also loses its ability to be released from mitochondria.

Most importantly, the results of Dr. Llovera and colleagues also showed that under the control or normal conditions, ENDOG can be detected in cytosol and nucleus by Mitochondria/Nuclei/Cytosol Extraction (Shuai Yang et al., Figure 5A, B4). Consistently, Dr. Richard J. Youle (Arnoult et al., Figure 4 C2) and Dr. Yunliang Guo (Li et al., Figure 85) also showed that ENDOG can be detected in cytoplasm and nucleus under control or normal conditions. The immunofluorescence staining of Dr. Zee-Fen Chang’s (Chao et al., Figure 4B, C6) and Dr. Jeong Woo Park’s (Kim et al., Figure 5d7) work consistently showed that there is a small portion of ENDOG translocated into the nucleus and cytosol in control or normal conditions. What’s more, Dr. Llovera and colleagues’ previous work also found that ENDOG does exist out of the mitochondria by co-immunofluorescence staining of ENDOG (red) and Mitotracker (green) (Chris McDermott-Roe et al., Supplementary Fig. 38). All these publications demonstrated that a small portion of ENDOG is located in the cytosol or nucleus, which may translocate from the mitochondria, and have some functions we did not know in stress and even normal conditions.

For the mito/cyto extraction in our previous work9, we have overexposed Western blot images and showed that no TIM23 or SDHA was detected in the cytosol under our procedure (Fig. 1a), indicating that our procedure did not cause any mitochondrial damage. We also isolated primary hepatocytes and treated them with oleic acid and performed the mito/cyto extraction and found that the cytoplasmic ENDOG was increased after OA treatment consistently (Fig. 1b). For the mice concern, Dr. V. L. Dawson and his colleagues generated ENDOG null mice and found that“there is no discernable physical difference between ENDOG null mice and wild-type littermate control” (David et al., Figure 43). Additionally, Dr. Michael R. Lieber also generated ENDOG null mice and found that “physical examination and gross anatomic dissection of the endo G+/− and endo G−/− mice did not reveal any abnormalities relative to their wild-type littermates at any age from birth to 1 year of age. Detailed histological analysis of the liver, brain, heart, etc. failed to reveal any differences from the wild-type mice at any age” (Irvine et al.10).

To our surprise, Llovera et al. showed that their ENDOG null mice (which is a gift from Dr. Michael R. Lieber10) exhibit a significant decrease in the mass of major WAT depots, such as inguinal, gonadal, and retroperitoneal, as well as a reduction in the mass of interscapular BAT. This reduction in fat mass correlates with a significant reduction in body weight. Conversely, no differences were observed in the weight of other major organs, such as the liver, heart, or gastrocnemius/soleus skeletal muscles (Rosario Pardo et al., Figure 4C–E11). It’s possible that housing conditions could explain these dramatic differences between the mice from the same lab.

The BODIPY and oil red staining of ENDOG knockout mice shown in the Matters Arising manuscript by Llovera et al. (in their Fig. 1e, f), show dramatically increased lipid accumulation in the liver in normal conditions, compared to the wild-type mice. Thus, some changes in the liver weight or liver morphology in the ENDOG knockout mice are expected. However, there were no changes in the steatosis phenotype of liver histological and the body weight in the ENDOG null mice, which had been revealed by Dr. Michael R. Lieber’s group since they performed the physical examination and gross anatomic dissection of the ENDOG null mice from birth to 1 year of age.

In our study, consistent with Dr. V. L. Dawson’s and Dr. Michael R. Lieber’s observations, ENDOG knockout mice also did not show a dramatic change in the body weight and liver histological in normal conditions (Fig. 2a, b). Based on their findings and our results, we think that the effect of ENDOG on lipid metabolism needs to be boosted by HFD stress, thus we focus more on HFD diet conditions. For the ENDOG antibody specificity, we have tested several commercial antibodies and tested the specificity. For IF staining, the ENDOG antibody (NOVUS, NBP1-76657) is a very good one. The results showed that in the wild-type cell, ENDOG mainly co-located with Cytochrome c (mitochondria marker), but there is still a small portion of ENDOG located in the cytosol and nucleus. However, the ENDOG knockout cell didn’t show any signal. Furthermore, we overexpressed the wild-type ENDOG, Del 1-48-ENDOG, and NLS-ENDOG in the ENDOG knockout cells. We found that, compared with its adjacent ENDOG null cells (has no ENDOG staining signal), the wild-type ENDOG was mainly located in mitochondria as well as in cytosol and nucleus; Del 1-48- ENDOG could not locate in the mitochondria, and the NLS- ENDOG indeed located in the nucleus (Fig. 2c). These data suggested that the ENDOG staining antibody (NOVUS, NBP1-76657) we used in our study has good specificity.

In fact, Llovera et al.’s work supports our findings. They also showed that knockout and knockdown of ENDOG repressed the phosphorylation of AKT and GSK3β in hearts and rat cardiomyocytes12. In our study, we also found that knockout of ENDOG repressed the phosphorylation of AKT and its target ACLY, and ENDOG knockout reduced the HFD-induced body weight, fat weight, and NAFLD phenotype in female mice.

We agree that the function of ENDOG may be in a tissue-dependent manner, which may need further study. So far, we just focused on the liver.

Overall, although we appreciate the careful analysis of Dr. Llovera and colleagues, none of the issues presented therein had any substantive impact on the analysis and conclusions of our study.

Methods

Mouse primary hepatocyte isolation and subcellular fractionation isolation

All mice were handled according to the China Animal Welfare Legislation guidelines and as approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Jinan University. Primary hepatocytes were isolated from female mice at 10 weeks old. Briefly, after the portal vein of anesthetized mice was cannulated under aseptic conditions, the liver was perfused with 100 mL pre-perfusion buffer (NaCl 8 g; KCl 0.4 g; NaH2PO4•2H2O 0.078 g; Na2HPO4•12H2O 0.151 g; NaHCO3 0.35 g; EGTA 0.19 g; Glucose 0.9 g; dissolved in 1 L ddH2O and adjust pH to 7.4), followed by digestion with 50 mL of Enzyme-perfusion buffer (NaCl 8 g; KCl 0.4 g; NaH2PO4•2H2O 0.078 g; Na2HPO4•12H2O 0.151 g; NaHCO3 0.35 g; HEPES 2.38 g; dissolved in 1 L ddH2O and adjust pH to 7.4, then add 1 M CaCl2 and 10 mg/mL of collagenase (sigma C0130) before use) at 37 °C. The liver was then aseptically removed to a sterile 10-cm cell culture dish containing 20 mL cold DMEM medium. After dispersed by aspirating with a large-bore pipette, the isolated mouse hepatocytes were filtrated through a 70μm cell strainer (Fisher) into a 50-mL centrifuge tube and centrifuged at 500 rpm for 1 min at 4 °C. Cells were then re-suspended with 20 mL DMEM medium and centrifuged at 500 rpm for 1 min at 4 °C for three times. Then, re-suspend the cell pellet with 10 mL DMEM medium and mix with cold 10 mL Percoll solution adequately (0.96 mL 10×PBS + 8.64 mL Percoll (Life Technologies). After centrifugation at 50 × g for 10 min at 4 °C, the supernatant cells were removed and centrifugated cells were washed with DMEM medium and counted. Hepatocytes were plated at 5 × 105 cells per well in DMEM medium containing 10% FBS in collagen-coated six-well plates overnight before further assays. After 200 μM oleic acid treatment for 24 h, primary hepatocytes were digested and centrifuged at 400 × g at 4 °C for 5 min. The cell pellet was permeabilized with 10 mg/mL digitonin in PBS at room temperature for 10 min and centrifuged at 10,000 × g at 4 °C for 10 min. The supernatants were collected as the cytosolic fraction. The pellets were re-suspended in mitochondrial lysate buffer on ice for 30 min and then centrifuged at 10,000 × g at 4 °C for 20 min. The supernatants which contain mitochondrial fractions were collected.

Immunofluorescence staining

For immunofluorescence staining, the cells were fixed with 4% formaldehyde and blocked with 2% BSA (Amresco, E588), then incubated with anti-Cytochrome c (Santa Cruz, sc-13560, 1:500 dilution) and ENDOG (NOVUS, NBP1-76657, 1:100 dilution) overnight at 4 °C. The cells were then incubated with Alexa Fluor® 594-AffiniPure goat anti-rabbit (Jackson, 115-585-146) and Alexa Fluor® 488-AffiniPure goat anti-mouse IgG (Jackson, 115-545-146) and counterstained with DAPI. Images were taken with a Leica TCS SP8 confocal microscope.
