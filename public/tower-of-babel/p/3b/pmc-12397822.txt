Unraveling the radiological enigma: ``Para testicular adenomatoid tumor masquerading as a triorchidism” A case report and literature review
Authors: Khalid Hussain, Rao Nouman Ali, Obaid Adil, Maria Khalid, Sohaib Irfan, Wajiha Irfan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractParatestiular tumors are uncommon tumors in the vicinity of the testis with adenomatoid being the most common benign subtype. Adenomatoid tumors have a special predilection towards genital structures in both males and females. The epididymis is the most common site of occurrence of para-testicular adenomatoid tumors. These tumors show diversity in clinical presentation and most of the time remain inconclusive on ultrasonography and MRI. The radiological findings often mismatch with histopathological diagnosis. In our case, a young male of 28 years’ age presented with persistent pain in the scrotum, and on clinical examination a separate testis-like structure was observed. The radiological modalities were suggested as a case of triorchidism with the presence of a normal extra testis in the right scrotal sac. The surgical excision of the lump was done and sent to the histopathology department which revealed the occurrence of adenomatoid tumor hence clinicians should be aware of these clinical and radiological diversities of paratesticular adenomatoid masses and should be meticulous in managing patients with spurious scrotal masses.Keywords: Paratesticular tumor, Adenomatoid tumor, Triorchidism, Case report

Introduction

Paratesticular tumors (PT) are a group of tumors that originate from the structures around the testis. They usually arise from tunica albuginea, tunica vaginalis, epididymis, and spermatic cord structures in ascending order. The vas deference involvement by these tumors is rare [1]. These tumors constitute about 10 % of all intrascrotal tumors and are broadly classified into benign and malignant tumors, however 80% of these tumors are benign in nature. The most common benign para-testicular tumors (PT) are lipoma, fibroma, and adenomatoid tumors, however the malignant para-testicular tumors are usually sarcomas [2].

The para-testicular adenomatoid tumors (PTAT) are the most common benign category of paratesticular tumors and these constitute about 30% of all PTT masses [3]. These tumors are mesothelial in origin and have a special predilection towards the genital tract system with the epididymis being the most common site of occurrence [4]. They usually present as pain or as a gradually increasing mass in the scrotum. that pain is reported as a presenting symptom in approximately 15%-30% of adenomatoid tumor cases but most cases present as painless masses. The ultrasonography of the scrotum and MRI are utilized to evaluate the diverse pattern of their radiological presentation and surgical excision is the mainstay of treatment in the majority of cases. The definitive diagnosis of PTAT depends on histopathology and immunohistochemistry [5].

We have found a unique pattern of PTAT in our case that must be added to the existing literature the tumor initially presented as a third normal testis both on clinical examination and radiological investigations and we surgically excised for the persistent pain and the anticipated chance of malignancy in supernumerary testis but histopathology amazed us when we found it was a para-testicular adenomatoid tumor who camouflaged as a triorchidism.

Case presentation

A 28-year-old male presented with chronic pain in the right hemiscrotum for 5 months off and on with no associated history of trauma to the genitals. He has no medical or surgical part history. The cause of pain was presumed to be chronic irritation and localized inflammation secondary to the pressure effects from the mass within the confined scrotal compartment. The patient has no history of infertility. On examination of the inguinoscrotal area, a separate testicular lump caudal to normal testicular lump was felt in the right scrotal sac which was tender on palpation. The left scrotal sac contains single left testis. The rest of the general physical and systemic examination was unremarkable.

His baseline investigations were within normal range and hormonal profile serum FSH, serum LH, and serum testosterone were within normal limits. The tumor markers were also within normal limits. The patient was designated as genotypically and phenotypically male on karyotyping of the XY chromosome. On the Ultrasonography of the scrotum narrated two testes in the right scrotal sac and one in the left scrotal sac. All testicular lumps have a normal echogenic pattern with no suspicious area noted in any testis or around them which warranted further investigation. The right testis measures 4×2.1×2.4cm and the left testis measures 4×2.2×3.0 while the supernumerary testis size measured 2.6×2.3×2.4cm on the right side which was smaller in size than the principal testis.

The Doppler indices were normal in all testicular areas with the resistive indices (RIs) were within normal range (0.55-0.65) across all testicular structures, including the supernumerary one. This reinforces the absence of torsion or vascular compromise.

No evidence of hydrocele, varicocele, epididymal cyst, or inguinal hernia on either side was noted (Fig. 1).

The surgical decision was taken because of consistent pain and anticipated malignant transformation in supernumerary testis. The extra testicular lump is removed through a right-sided vertical incision on the right side of the scrotum. The per-operative findings were also consistent with triorchidism depicting a separate testis without any vas deferens and epididymis with no direct link to the primary right testis (Fig. 6). This per-operative finding suggested that this triorchidism was of type 2B according to bergholz classification (Table 3). The retrieved testis was sent to the histopathology department and the cut section of the testis grossly revealed grey tan surface with no testicular parenchyma identified and no evidence of any area of hemorrhagic necrosis identified. On microscopic examination columnar to cuboidal cells with intervening stroma in the background of inflammatory cells identified with the presence of cytoplasmic vacuolization suggestive of adenomatoid tumor (Fig. 7). The immunohistochemistry densely stained positive with cytokeratin, and calretinin further endorsed the presence of paratesticular adenomatoid tumor (Fig. 8, Fig. 9). Hence the radiological mystery of triorchidism on clinical examination and on radiological modalities is solved by histopathology as a unique existence of paratesticular adenomatoid tumors.

The patient's postoperative recovery was uneventful and discharged on the second day of surgery and followed 3 monthly for one year and annually for 3 years with the help of tumor markers and ultrasonography of the scrotum with no evidence of recurrence.

Discussion

The para testicular masses are a rare group of intrascrotal masses that are known for their diversity in clinical presentations, radiological appearances, and histopathological patterns. These tumors have both benign and malignant variants reported in the literature (Table 1). However we have emphasized an adenomatoid variant of para testicular tumors and these adenomatoid tumors (AT) are benign tumors of mesothelial origin and were first defined by Gold man and ash in 1945 as tumors consist of blends and sheets of cuboidal to columnar cells with cytoplasmic vacuolization [6]. These tumors are found in many extragenital sites like kidneys, adrenals, pericardium, heart, and mesocolon but they have a special affinity of occurrence in genital structures of both males and females [5]. The epididymal adenomatoid is most commonly reported in males however in females it can be found in the uterus, fallopian tubes, and ovaries [7].

The para-testicular adenomatoid (PTAT) is usually present between the 3rd to 5th decade of life and usually comes into notice due to persistent pain or gradually increasing mass in the scrotum. These tumors have immense variations on radiological investigations on ultrasonography they can present as isoechoic, hypoechoic, or hyperechoic but on histopathology, they are benign adenomatoid tumors similarly they can also surprise on MRI as well but in the majority of cases, they are usually hypointense on T 2 weighted images and remain isointense on contrast-enhanced T1 weighted images [8].

The surgery is the mainstay of treatment and excision is according to the site of occurrence however the diagnosis is mainly attributed to histopathology and immunohistochemistry. They share the same impression on histopathology irrespective of the site of occurrence and radiological findings. The most commonly utilized immune markers for para-testicular adenomatoid tumors are cytokeratin, calretenin, and vimentin which endorsed the origin from mesothelium [3].

There are many cases reported in the literature of (PTAT) Efared et al. examined an epididymal painful swelling in a middle-aged man and found a hyperechoic lesion of about 10×10 mm on ultrasound scrotum which was dealt with testis sparing surgery and diagnosed as an adenomatoid tumor on histopathology [6]. Similarly, Neham et al. reported another male with epididymal swelling on the left side which was hypoechoic on ultrasonography with negative tumors. The radical orchidectomy was performed and histopathology confirmed the occurrence of epididymal adenomatoid tumor [9]. In another study by Dighe et al. an isoechoic lesion of about 30×30mm in the left epididymis was found to be an epididymal adenomatoid on histopathology [10]. Just like the occurrence of (PTAT) in epididymis its presence in other para testicular sites is rare like abumohssin et al. reported an old man with left testicular swelling which was hypoechoic on ultrasonography explored and found to be an adenomatoid tumor of tunica albuginea which was densely positive for cytokeratin, calretinin, and WT-1 endorsing mesothelial origin of the tumor [11]. Chong Qing Cheng et al. reported a rare case of 32 years old male presented with an irregular solid mass in the left scrotum and on ultrasonography a hypoechoic mass which showed enhancement on the contrast computed tomography initially suspected malignancy was excised through radical orchidectomy and just like our case histopathology solved the mystery and endorsed the presence of an adenomatoid tumor of the tunica vaginalis [12]. After detailed scrutiny of the literature on the PTATs of the last 5 years we found epididymis is the most common site than tunica virginals and tunica albuginea. All the cases have negative tumor markers and follow the same histopathological pattern despite variations in clinical and radiological presentation. (Table 2).

The novelty of our case lies in the rare way of presentation of adenomatoid tumors mimicking as triorchidism on clinical examination and radiological investigations, moreover mostly triorchidism presents as an incidental painless mass but our case is also relatable with few reported cases of painful polyorchidism previously in literature. The limitation of our literature review is that less data is available in medical literature to directly compare with our study and the exact mechanism behind the mimicking of adenomatoid tumor as triorchidism is less well understood which can be further explored globally on a greater scale.

Conclusion

Paratesticular adenomatoid tumors are a rare benign group of tumors that are diverse in clinical and radiological presentations but follow a common distinguished pattern in histopathology. Radiologists and urologists should keep in mind the possibility of adenomatoid tumors even if the chances of their presence seem minimal on clinical examination and radiological modalities and should always wait for the histopathology report before disclosing the final diagnosis and future management strategies to the patient and the family members.
