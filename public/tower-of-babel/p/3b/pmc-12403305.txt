Comprehensive analysis of the sucrose nonfermenting 1-related protein kinase 2 gene family in broomcorn millet (Panicum miliaceum L.) under abiotic stress conditions
Authors: Wei Zhou, Yue Qiao, Rui Li, Chunyang Dai, Xiuyan Cui, Ling Chen, Haigang Wang, Junjie Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSucrose nonfermenting 1-related protein kinase 2 (SnRK2) proteins constitute a family of plant-specific serine/threonine kinases that play critical roles in mediating abscisic acid (ABA) signaling and responses to abiotic stresses, including drought and salinity. Nevertheless, systematic bioinformatics analysis and expression profiling of the SnRK2 gene family in broomcorn millet (Panicum miliaceum L.) have not yet been reported.ResultsA total of 16 PmSnRK2 genes were identified in the broomcorn millet genome, unevenly distributed across 12 chromosomes and phylogenetically classified into three subfamilies (I–III). Gene structure analysis revealed that the majority of PmSnRK2 genes harbor seven to eight introns. Promoter region mining uncovered abundant cis-elements responsive to hormones and abiotic stresses. Synteny analysis detected 12 PmSnRK2–PmSnRK2 paralogous pairs, with duplication events dated between ~ 1.28 and 219.36 million years ago; all Ka/Ks ratios were below 1, consistent with strong purifying selection. Multiple sequence alignment confirmed the presence of the conserved serine/threonine kinase active-site motif, the ATP-binding signature, and two characteristic C-terminal domains (I and II) across all family members. Structural superimposition of four representative PmSnRK2 proteins (PmSnRK2.6.2, PmSnRK2.11.1, PmSnRK2.13.1, PmSnRK2.15.1) onto Arabidopsis AtSnRK2.3 (PDB: 3UC3) yielded RMSD values of 0.922–1.134 Å within the kinase domain, underscoring three-dimensional conservation. Expression profiling using public RNA-seq datasets demonstrated that all 16 genes are transcribed in at least one of eight tissues, with individual members peaking in shoots, leaf blades, stems, inflorescences, roots, or seeds. Under exogenous ABA (50–100 µM), salt (100 mM NaCl), and PEG (20%) treatments, PmSnRK2 genes exhibited distinct induction and repression dynamics, revealing functional divergence in stress-response pathways.ConclusionOur study provides comprehensive insights into the genomic characteristics, evolutionary patterns, and potential functional roles of the PmSnRK2 gene family in broomcorn millet. These results enhance the current understanding of how PmSnRK2 genes may contribute to abiotic stress tolerance, offering a valuable foundation for further functional validation and targeted improvement in broomcorn millet breeding programs.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12864-025-11992-1.

Introduction

Plants are constantly exposed to a variety of environmental stresses, such as drought, high salinity, and extreme temperatures, all of which significantly impact both biomass production and grain yield. In response to these challenges, plants have evolved an array of molecular and physiological mechanisms to adapt to changing conditions [1, 2]. Plants efficiently regulate growth, development, and defense in response to environmental changes through complex signal transduction mechanisms involving protein phosphorylation and dephosphorylation by diverse kinases and phosphatases that modulate gene expression [3]. Among these signaling components, protein kinases—including mitogen-activated protein kinases (MAPKs) [4], glycogen synthase kinase 3 (GSK3) [5], calcium-dependent protein kinases (CDPKs) [6], and most SNF1-related kinases (SnRKs) [7] —play pivotal roles in detecting external stress signals and converting them into precise transcriptional and metabolic responses that ultimately bolster plant stress tolerance.

Sucrose nonfermenting 1-related protein kinases (SnRKs), which are homologous to yeast SNF1 genes and mammalian AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK), are ubiquitously distributed in plants [8]. They serve as critical regulatory switches in sugar signaling, as well as in mediating responses to both biotic and abiotic stresses, seed germination, and seedling growth [9]. SnRKs are serine/threonine protein kinases characterized by a highly conserved catalytic domain and are conventionally classified into three subfamilies: SnRK1, SnRK2, and SnRK3 [6]. The SnRK2 subfamily, which is unique to plants, plays a crucial role in mediating responses to abiotic stresses and regulating abscisic acid (ABA)-dependent developmental processes [7, 10, 11]. SnRK2s are further subdivided into three groups: group 1 kinases are not activated by ABA; group 2 kinases are either not activated or only very weakly activated by ABA; and group 3 kinases are strongly activated by ABA [9].

Abscisic acid (ABA) is one of the most critical phytohormones and plays a multifaceted role in regulating plant growth and development, including key processes such as seed dormancy, germination, and stomatal closure. In addition to its developmental functions, ABA is essential for modulating plant responses to a wide array of biotic and abiotic stresses, including drought, salinity, pathogen attack, and extreme temperatures. Through complex and highly coordinated signaling networks, ABA initiates a cascade of molecular and physiological responses that increase plant tolerance to adverse environmental conditions, thereby ensuring survival and sustained productivity [12, 13]. In response to environmental stresses, ABA binds to its PYR/PYL/RCAR receptors, leading to the inhibition of clade A PP2Cs and the subsequent activation of SnRK2 kinases. These kinases then phosphorylate downstream transcription factors, triggering the expression of stress-responsive genes and initiating adaptive responses [14, 15]. The ABA signaling cascade consists of three principal components: ABA receptors, which detect hormones and initiate the signaling process; type 2 C protein phosphatases (PP2Cs), which function as negative regulators by deactivating downstream kinases; and SNF1-related protein kinases (SnRK2s), which act as positive regulators by phosphorylating transcription factors and activating stress-responsive gene expression [16–18]. In the absence of ABA, PP2Cs interact with SnRK2s, suppressing their kinase activity and keeping the pathway inactive. Conversely, when ABA is present, it forms a complex with PYR/PYL/RCAR receptors that subsequently bind to PP2Cs, relieving their inhibition on SnRK2s and thereby activating the ABA signaling cascade. Consequently, SnRK2 kinases are critical positive regulators of ABA signaling, and their activity is essential for toggling the pathway in response to abiotic stresses [19, 20].

Salinity and drought impose critical constraints on plant growth, development, and crop productivity. Elevated soil salinity triggers adverse effects such as osmotic imbalance, ion toxicity, dehydration, wilting, and metabolic disruption [21]. To counter these stresses, plants have evolved intricate signal transduction pathways that integrate mechanisms for maintaining ionic and osmotic homeostasis, activating detoxification responses to control and repair damage, and modulating growth. These coordinated processes enable plants to effectively perceive environmental stress signals and initiate adaptive responses, ultimately increasing survival and sustaining productivity under adverse conditions [1]. Extensive research has shown that SnRK2 kinases play pivotal roles in plant responses to salt and drought stresses. For example, TaSnRK2.3—a novel SnRK2 gene isolated from common wheat—is highly expressed in leaves and is upregulated in response to osmotic, salt, and cold stresses. Its overexpression in Arabidopsis has been shown to significantly enhance tolerance to salt, drought, and freezing stresses, as demonstrated by improved root architecture, elevated expression of stress-responsive genes, and favorable physiological adjustments [22]. Similarly, the expression of ZmSnRK2.3 and ZmSnRK2.6 in maize is strongly induced by salt stress, highlighting their crucial functions in mediating adaptive responses to both salt and drought conditions and thereby contributing to increased stress tolerance [23]. Under salt stress, certain CsSnRK2 genes—particularly CsSnRK2.11 and CsSnRK2.12—are markedly upregulated, whereas others are downregulated, and under drought stress induced by PEG, a distinct subset of CsSnRK2 genes is significantly induced, collectively underscoring the diverse and stress-specific roles of these kinases in cucumber responses to environmental challenges [24]. Sucrose nonfermenting 1-related protein kinase SAPK4 localizes to both the cytoplasm and nucleus, and its overexpression in rice enhances salt tolerance by improving growth and photosynthesis, reducing Na⁺ and Cl⁻ accumulation, and regulating genes involved in ion homeostasis and the oxidative stress response [25]. SRK2C (SnRK2.8) overexpression has been shown to result in improved drought tolerance in transgenic Arabidopsis [17]. These results suggest that the SnRK2 family may play a crucial role in regulating the plant response to salt stress and drought stress.

Broomcorn millet is an ancient, gluten-free orphan crop that was domesticated in northern China approximately 10,000 years BP and was introduced to Europe between 3,600 and 4,000 BP [26, 27]. Its high protein content, rapid growth, and exceptional drought tolerance make it a promising alternative cereal [28, 29]. Recent years have yielded three chromosome-scale reference assemblies for broomcorn millet: (i) the Longmi4 genome (PacBio + Hi-C), covering ~ 95% of the genome with a scaffold N50 of 8.2 Mb [30]; (ii) a landrace assembly (accession 00000390) comprising 18 pseudomolecules, scaffold N50 46.7 Mb and 98% BUSCO completeness [31]; (iii) a 32-accession long-read pangenome that reveals > 50 000 structural variants [32].

To elucidate the roles of PmSnRK2 gene family members in abiotic stress responses, we conducted a comprehensive bioinformatics analysis to investigate their physicochemical properties, conserved motifs, gene structures, cis-acting elements, and phylogenetic relationships. In addition, qRT‒PCR was used to assess the expression patterns of the PmSnRK2 genes under ABA, salt, and drought stresses. These findings not only increase our understanding of the PmSnRK2 gene family but also provide a robust theoretical foundation for further exploration of the functions of PmSnRK2 in the abiotic stress response of broomcorn millet.

Results

Chromosome distribution and basic information of PmSnRK2 genes

To investigate the potential functions of SnRK2 genes, we systematically analyzed the physicochemical properties of SnRK2 proteins in broomcorn millet (Table S1). We identified 16 PmSnRK2 genes and named them sequentially from PmSnRK2.1.1 to PmSnRK2.18.1. The SnRK2 genes differed substantially in terms of the encoded protein size and biophysical properties (Table 1). The number of amino acids ranged from 187 to 501. The sequence of PmSnRK2.1.3 protein was the shortest, containing 187 amino acids, whereas the sequence of PmSnRK2.18.1 protein was the longest, containing 501 amino acids. The molecular weight of PmSnRK2s ranged from 21.06 to 54.29 kDa, and the theoretical isoelectric point (pI) ranged from 4.56 to 7.05. PmSnRK2.15.1 is classified as a basic protein, whereas the others are acidic proteins. Among the 16 PmSnRK2 proteins, seven have an instability index below 40 (overall range 29.56–54.56) and are therefore predicted to be stable, whereas the remaining nine (> 40) may be intrinsically unstable, potentially requiring molecular chaperones or oligomerization for structural stability. All proteins display high thermal resilience, with aliphatic index values ranging from 76.21 to 94.52. The total average hydrophobic index of the 16 PmSnRK2 gene family members was negative, indicating that they are hydrophilic proteins.

Secondary structure and subcellular localization of PmSnRK2 genes

Chromosomal localization analysis revealed that the 16 identified PmSnRK2 genes were unevenly distributed across 12 chromosomes of broomcorn millet (Fig. 1; Table 1). Among them, chromosome 1 harbored four genes (PmSnRK2.1.1, PmSnRK2.1.2, PmSnRK2.1.3, and PmSnRK2.1.4), chromosome 6 contained two genes (PmSnRK2.6.1 and PmSnRK2.6.2), whereas chromosomes 2, 3, 4, 5, 11, 12, 13, 15, 16, and 18 each contained a single gene (PmSnRK2.2.1, PmSnRK2.3.1, PmSnRK2.4.1, PmSnRK2.5.1, PmSnRK2.11.1, PmSnRK2.12.1, PmSnRK2.13.1, PmSnRK2.15.1, PmSnRK2.16.1, and PmSnRK2.18.1, respectively).

Multiple sequence alignment of SnRK2 proteins

Multiple sequence alignment of PmSnRK2 proteins revealed several highly conserved regions characteristic of the SnRK2 family (Fig. 2). The serine/threonine protein kinase active site signature and the protein kinase ATP-binding region signature are both present in all PmSnRK2 members, indicating evolutionary conservation of their kinase activity. In addition, two conserved domains at the C-terminus, Domain I and Domain II, were identified. Domain I, corresponding to the ABA-dependent regulatory region, is highly conserved among all PmSnRK2s, whereas Domain II, associated with the ABA-independent pathway, exhibits greater sequence variation. These results demonstrate that PmSnRK2 proteins possess the typical structural features of the SnRK2 family, which may underlie their conserved functions in signal transduction.

Collinearity analysis of the PmSnRK2 genes

Gene duplication events are essential drivers of gene family expansion and diversification. Here, collinearity analysis performed using the multiple collinearity scanning toolkit (MCScanX) identified 12 SnRK2 gene pairs in broomcorn millet (Fig. 3A; Table 3). Across the PmSnRK2 family, individual genes experienced between one and three duplication events. Ten genes each possess a single collinear partner—for example, PmSnRK2.1.2–PmSnRK2.2.1, PmSnRK2.3.1–PmSnRK2.4.1, PmSnRK2.5.1–PmSnRK2.6.2, PmSnRK2.6.1–PmSnRK2.13.1, and PmSnRK2.15.1–PmSnRK2.16.1. Four genes underwent two independent duplication events (PmSnRK2.1.1 pairing with PmSnRK2.3.1 and PmSnRK2.4.1; PmSnRK2.12.1 pairing with PmSnRK2.15.1 and PmSnRK2.16.1). Notably, PmSnRK2.11.1 participates in three distinct paralogous relationships, making it the most extensively duplicated member of the family.

Furthermore, interspecies synteny analysis revealed significant collinearity between SnRK2 genes in broomcorn millet and their homologs in Arabidopsis thaliana and Oryza sativa. Several SnRK2 genes in broomcorn millet were found to be located within highly conserved syntenic blocks with corresponding orthologs in these two model species, suggesting evolutionary conservation and close relationships of the SnRK2 gene family among grasses and dicots (Fig. 3B).

To assess the selective pressures acting on the duplicated genes, Ka/Ks ratios of the collinear gene pairs were calculated. Typically, the Ka/Ks values less than 1, equal to 1, and greater than 1 indicate purifying selection, neutral evolution, and positive selection, respectively. The Ka/Ks ratios of all collinear gene pairs in broomcorn millet were less than 1, indicating that these genes have likely experienced purifying selection during their evolutionary history. Additionally, the duplication events of the 12 gene pairs were estimated to have occurred between 1.2830 and 219.3643 million years ago (MYA) (Table 3).

Analysis of cis-regulatory elements in the promoters of PmSnRK2s

Cis-regulatory elements in promoters regulate gene expression by mediating transcriptional processes. In this study, the 2000 bp sequences upstream of the translation start sites of the PmSnRK2 genes were analyzed for the presence of these elements (Fig. 4 and Table S2). Promoter analysis of the PmSnRK2 gene family revealed an abundance of diverse cis-acting elements, including those responsive to light, jasmonic acid (MeJA), abscisic acid (ABA), and anoxia-specific inducible signals. Notably, PmSnRK2.3.1, PmSnRK2.13.1, and PmSnRK2.16.1 were particularly enriched in ABA-responsive elements, whereas PmSnRK2.1.3, PmSnRK2.6.1, PmSnRK2.6.2, PmSnRK2.11.1, and PmSnRK2.12.1 harbored a greater number of MeJA-responsive elements (Fig. 4 and Table S2).

Predicted cis-elements of the PmSnRK2 gene family

With the exception of PmSnRK2.1.2, the remaining PmSnRK2 genes predominantly contained a greater abundance of anoxia-specific inducible elements. Moreover, nearly all members of the PmSnRK2 family possessed light-responsive elements, and over half also contained meristem expression-related elements; in addition, some genes exhibited root specific, cell cycle regulatory, and endosperm expression-related elements.

Structure and conserved motif analysis of the PmSnRK2 genes

To investigate the structural diversity of the PmSnRK2 genes, the exon–intron organization of individual PmSnRK2 genes was analyzed by comparing their cDNA sequences with the corresponding genomic DNA sequences (Fig. 5A). Among the PmSnRK2 genes, the number of introns ranged from three (PmSnRK2.18.1) to nine (PmSnRK2.3.1), with most genes containing seven or eight introns. Six genes (PmSnRK2.1.2, PmSnRK2.1.3, PmSnRK2.1.4, PmSnRK2.2.1, PmSnRK2.13.1, and PmSnRK2.18.1) contained both 5′ and 3′ untranslated regions (UTRs). Further analysis of the conserved motifs revealed that the number of motifs in the PmSnRK2 genes varied from five (PmSnRK2.1.3, PmSnRK2.4.1) to nine (PmSnRK2.1.2, PmSnRK2.13.1, PmSnRK2.16.1), with most genes harboring eight motifs (Fig. 5B). Notably, motifs 1, 4, and 5 were present in 15 PmSnRK2 genes.

Phylogenetic analysis and classification of the PmSnRK2 gene family

To investigate the functional characteristics and evolutionary relationships of the PmSnRK2 genes, multiple sequence alignments were performed via SnRK2 protein sequences from 16 broomcorn millet genes, 10 Arabidopsis genes, 10 rice genes, and 11 maize genes, followed by the construction of a phylogenetic tree. The analysis revealed that the SnRK2 gene family clustered into three distinct groups: Group I, Group II, and Group III (Fig. 6). Notably, the distribution of broomcorn millet genes among these groups was uneven: Group I comprised 7 members, Group II contained 9 members, and no members were assigned to Group III.

Comparative analysis of SnRK2 protein structures

Based on the phylogenetic analysis, structural superimposition was performed between AtSnRK2.3 (PDB: 3UC3) and four broomcorn millet SnRK2 proteins (PmSnRK2.6.2, PmSnRK2.11.1, PmSnRK2.13.1, and PmSnRK2.15.1), all of which are classified within the same Group II clade. The results revealed a high degree of structural overlap between homologous SnRK2 proteins from both species, especially in the kinase domain, indicating strong evolutionary conservation of this domain (Fig. 7).

Structural superimposition of broomcorn millet and Arabidopsis SnRK2 proteins. A–D Superposed models of PmSnRK2.6.2, PmSnRK2.11.2, PmSnRK2.13.1 and PmSnRK2.15.1 from broomcorn millet (green) with AtSnRK2.3 from Arabidopsis thaliana (cyan; PDB ID 3UC3)

Specifically, superimposition of PmSnRK2.6.2 with AtSnRK2.3 using PyMOL yielded an RMSD of 0.922 Å (1593 atoms aligned), reflecting a highly conserved three-dimensional structure between the two proteins, particularly within the kinase domain. Similarly, the RMSD values for PmSnRK2.11.1, PmSnRK2.13.1, and PmSnRK2.15.1 were 0.922 Å (1125 atoms aligned), 0.930 Å (1598 atoms aligned), and 1.134 Å (1647 atoms aligned), respectively, all supporting the conclusion of a remarkable structural conservation, especially in the kinase domain, between foxtail millet and Arabidopsis SnRK2 proteins.

Prediction of the PmSnRK2 family protein interaction network

To explore potential functional relationships among the nine PmSnRK2 proteins detected in broomcorn millet, we constructed a protein–protein interaction (PPI) network (Fig. 8). The network contained 17 edges with a non-random topology characterised by two highly connected hubs. PmSnRK2.1.1 and PmSnRK2.1.2 each displayed five predicted interactions, accounting for almost 60% of all observed edges, and therefore represent the major network centres. A second-tier hub, PmSnRK2.5.1, was linked to four partners, whereas PmSnRK2.6.2, PmSnRK2.12.1, PmSnRK2.15.1 and PmSnRK2.18.1 each interacted with three proteins. By contrast, PmSnRK2.16.1 showed only two connections and thus formed the periphery of the network.

Expression patterns of PmSnRK2 genes in different tissues

To investigate the tissue-specific expression patterns of the PmSnRK2 gene family in broomcorn millet, we analyzed publicly available RNA-seq datasets comprising eight representative tissues at different developmental stages (Fig. 9). All sixteen PmSnRK2 genes were detected in at least one of the eight examined tissues. Two genes, PmSnRK2.3.1 and PmSnRK2.4.1, reached their highest transcript levels in three-week-old shoots, whereas PmSnRK2.18.1 peaked in leaf blades at eight weeks. Three members (PmSnRK2.6.1, PmSnRK2.15.1, and PmSnRK2.16.1) were most abundant in stems, and five genes (PmSnRK2.1.1, PmSnRK2.1.4, PmSnRK2.6.2, PmSnRK2.11.1, and PmSnRK2.13.1displayed maximum expression in inflorescences. PmSnRK2.1.3 and PmSnRK2.2.1 showed root-preferential expression, while PmSnRK2.1.2, PmSnRK2.5.1, and PmSnRK2.12.1 were predominantly expressed in mature seeds.

Abiotic stress response of SnRK2 genes in broomcorn millet

In this study, we systematically analyzed the expression profiles of 12 PmSnRK2 genes in broomcorn millet under various abiotic stress conditions, including ABA (50 µM and 100 µM), NaCl (100 mM), and PEG (20%) treatments (Fig. 10). The results revealed that most PmSnRK2 genes exhibited significant differences in expression compared with the water control.

Under ABA treatments, PmSnRK2.5.1, PmSnRK2.6.2, and PmSnRK2.16.1 exhibited significant induction at multiple time points. Specifically, PmSnRK2.5.1 reached peak expression at 12 h under 50 µM ABA, while PmSnRK2.6.2 showed highest induction at 6 h under 50 µM ABA. Notably, PmSnRK2.16.1 exhibited sustained and significant up-regulation at all tested time points (3 h, 6 h, 12 h, and 24 h) under both 50 µM and 100 µM ABA treatments. PEG treatment led to a sustained down-regulation of PmSnRK2.6.1 and PmSnRK2.1.3, whereas PmSnRK2.18.1 was significantly up-regulated across nearly all time points. Under NaCl stress, PmSnRK2.3.1 showed no significant change at 3 h but was significantly down-regulated thereafter; PmSnRK2.1.4 was already significantly lower at 3 h and remained suppressed at later time points. PmSnRK2.13.1 expression was significantly reduced at all time points, while PmSnRK2.18.1 was significantly elevated at 3 h and 6 h but returned to control levels at 12 h and 24 h. These results collectively indicate that the broomcorn millet PmSnRK2 gene family members exhibit distinct functional divergence and specific response patterns in response to various abiotic stresses, suggesting diverse regulatory roles in stress adaptation mechanisms.

Discussion

Detailed characterization and evolution of SnRK2 in broomcorn millet

SnRK2 genes are ubiquitously present in the plant kingdom and play pivotal roles in regulating plant growth and stress responses. They have been identified in various species, including Arabidopsis thaliana [17], rice [25], wheat [22], maize [23], and cucumber [24]; however, their characterization in broomcorn millet remains limited. In this study, 16 PmSnRK2 genes (designated PmSnRK2.1.1 to PmSnRK2.18.1) were identified using BLASTP searches by Arabidopsis SnRK2 proteins and HMM domain analyses, and their sequences were comprehensively examined via bioinformatics approaches. These 16 genes are unevenly distributed across 12 chromosomes (Fig. 1). Genes within the same subfamily typically exhibit similar exon–intron organizations and conserved motifs [34]. Our findings indicate that most PmSnRK2 genes harbor 7 to 8 introns with consistent motif arrangements (Fig. 5), which may imply a degree of functional redundancy among some family members. However, some genes show structural differences within the same subfamily; for example, PmSnRK2.1.3 contains only 4 introns, whereas PmSnRK2.4.1 has 5 introns, in contrast to the typical 7–8 introns observed in other members. These variations suggest that, during evolution, changes in intron number may have facilitated the neofunctionalization of certain PmSnRK2 genes, enabling them to better adapt to environmental challenges [35, 36].

Phylogenetic and gene structural analyses revealed high similarity between broomcorn millet SnRK2 genes and those from Arabidopsis, rice, and maize, suggesting that SnRK2 kinases originated before the divergence of monocots and dicots, with some key functions possibly conserved throughout plant evolution. Gene duplication events contribute significantly to genomic evolution, providing abundant genetic material for diversification, which facilitates rapid adaptation under selective environmental pressures [37]. In our study, we identified 12 collinear SnRK2 gene pairs in the broomcorn millet genome, indicating that segmental duplications have substantially contributed to the expansion of the PmSnRK2 family. Such duplication events could have contributed to both structural and functional diversification, potentially increasing the regulatory versatility of these genes in plant growth, development, and stress responses.

PmSnRK2 genes play important roles in the ABA pathway

Cis-acting elements are important regulatory sequences involved in gene transcription, enabling plants to perceive and respond to external signals. Analysis of the cis-elements in the promoters of the PmSnRK2 genes revealed that 81% of these genes contained ABA-responsive elements (Fig. 4), a finding that is consistent with reports in Brassica napus [38], maize [23], and cucumber [24], suggesting that ABA regulation may be a common feature among different crop species. Previous studies in Arabidopsis have shown that loss-of-function mutations in ABA-activated SNF1-related protein kinase 2 genes, such as SRK2D/SnRK2.2, SRK2E/SnRK2.6/OST1, and SRK2I/SnRK2.3, are associated with the upregulation of ABA repressor genes and the downregulation of ABA activator genes, resulting in developmental defects during seed development, such as reduced dormancy [39]. This phenomenon highlights the important role of the ABA signal transduction pathway in regulating plant growth and stress responses.

Our detailed analysis of the cis-acting elements in the PmSnRK2 promoters suggested that this gene family may play an important role in ABA signaling. This hypothesis is further supported by the observed changes in PmSnRK2 gene expression following exogenous ABA treatment. Under ABA treatment, members of the PmSnRK2 gene family in broomcorn millet exhibited diverse and dynamic expression profiles, suggesting that individual genes within the family could participate in ABA-mediated stress responses through potentially distinct and coordinated regulatory mechanisms (Fig. 10). Moreover, previous studies, such as Mahadi et al. [11], demonstrated that OsSnRK2 enhances plant growth under normal conditions but inhibits growth in the absence of ABA. Collectively, these findings indicate that PmSnRK2 genes may play roles in ABA signaling, providing a basis for future functional studies and potential genetic improvement aimed at enhancing ABA-mediated stress tolerance in broomcorn millet.

PmSnRK2 genes play important roles in salt and drought stress responses

Abiotic stresses such as drought and salinity significantly constrain plant growth, development, and productivity [1]. Plants have evolved sophisticated networks involving protein kinases to perceive and respond effectively to these stresses [40]. Among these kinases, the SnRK2 family, a group of plant-specific serine/threonine kinases, has been reported to regulate downstream targets involved in stress signaling pathways [7, 41, 42]. Analysis of promoter regions revealed a high abundance of stress-responsive cis-elements in PmSnRK2 genes, highlighting their likely central role in abiotic stress adaptation in broomcorn millet.

Previous research has consistently demonstrated the involvement of SnRK2 genes in diverse abiotic stress responses [10, 43]. For example, in Arabidopsis thaliana, the ABA-nonactivated SnRK2 members SnRK2.1.4 and SnRK2.6.2 effectively maintain reactive oxygen species (ROS) homeostasis under salt and other abiotic stresses by regulating the expression of ROS generation and scavenging-related genes, thus alleviating oxidative stress-induced cellular damage [44]. Comparable observations were made in cotton, where five GhSnRK2 genes were substantially upregulated under salt and drought stress conditions [45]. Additionally, in rice, OsSAPK7, a SnRK2 family member, significantly improved salt tolerance by modulating ion homeostasis, redox balance, and photosynthetic efficiency [46]. Consistent with these previous findings, our study revealed significant alterations in the expression of almost all PmSnRK2 family members under salt and drought stress conditions, albeit with distinct expression patterns among individual genes. These differential expression profiles may reflect specialized yet coordinated roles for PmSnRK2 genes, potentially operating through diverse regulatory mechanisms to facilitate broomcorn millet’s response to various abiotic stresses.

Materials and methods

Plant materials and treatments

The broomcorn millet cultivar AJ 08 used in this study is a high-quality line developed and selected by the Center for Agricultural Genetic Resources Research, Shanxi Agricultural University. Seeds of AJ 08 were first disinfected with 5% sodium hypochlorite solution and then germinated until the two true leaves were fully expanded. Seedlings were cultivated under controlled conditions in an artificial climate chamber located at the Center for Agricultural Genetic Resources Research, Shanxi Agricultural University, Taiyuan city (37°27′N, 111°30′E), maintained at 25 °C/18°C (day/night) and 70% relative humidity. The samples were collected at the three-true-leaf stage.

Four treatments were applied: 50 µM ABA, 100 µM ABA, 100 mM NaCl, and 20% PEG. Samples were collected at five time points (0, 3, 6, 12, and 24 h) after treatment. At each time point, leaves from three seedlings were excised and pooled to form one biological replicate. Three independent biological replicates were collected per treatment and time point. Total RNA was extracted from each pooled sample, and qRT‒PCR analysis was performed accordingly. All collected samples were immediately flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at − 80 °C until further use.

Identification of PmSnRK2 genes

The broomcorn millet reference genome (accession GCA_002895445.2) was downloaded from the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) Assembly database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/assembly/GCA_002895445.2) [31]. The maize reference genome (assembly Zm-B73-REFERENCE-NAM-5.0) was obtained from Phytozome v13 (https://phytozome-next.jgi.doe.gov/info/Zmays_Zm_B73_REFERENCE_NAM_5_0_55). The rice genome (IRGSP-1.0/RGAP Release 7) was retrieved from the Rice Genome Annotation Project (RGAP; https://rice.uga.edu/download_osa1r7.shtml). The Arabidopsis genome (TAIR10) was downloaded from The Arabidopsis Information Resource (TAIR; https://www.arabidopsis.org/download/list?dir=Genes%2FTAIR10_genome_release).

The conserved HMM domain (PF00069.25) for SnRK2 proteins was obtained from the Pfam database (version 37.2, http://pfam.xfam.org/) [47]. Using the downloaded HMM model, HMMER (version 3.4) [48] was employed on a Linux server to identify candidate SnRK2 genes in the broomcorn millet genome, with an E-value cutoff of < 0.001 applied. The awk command (awk ‘{print $1}’) was then used to extract a list of all identified SnRK2 genes. In parallel, a BLASTp (version 2.10.1+) search was conducted on the same server using Arabidopsis SnRK2 protein sequences as queries, applying an E-value cutoff of 1 × 10−30 and filtering for hits with at least 50% sequence identity; the awk command (awk ‘{print $1}’) was again used to extract the resulting gene list. The final set of PmSnRK2 genes was defined as the intersection of candidates identified by both the HMMER and BLASTp approaches. Gene names were standardized as “PmSnRK2.X.Y” (X = chromosome number, Y = physical order on that chromosome).

Multiple sequence alignment of PmSnRK2 proteins

Protein sequences of the 16 PmSnRK2 family members were aligned using the msa package (v1.24.0) with the ClustalOmega algorithm. Alignments were generated with default settings (BLOSUM62 substitution matrix, gap opening penalty = 10, gap extension penalty = 0.20) and exported in Clustal format. Post-alignment, we called msaPrettyPrint() with the TeXshade style for visualization. In the resulting figure, the serine/threonine protein kinase active‐site signature is manually highlighted with a green box and pentacle, the kinase ATP‐binding signature with an orange box and pentacle, and the C‐terminal Domain I and Domain II regions are manually outlined with black boxes.

Chromosome localization and basic information analysis

A chromosome map of the PmSnRK2 genes was generated via MapChart (version 2.32) [49]. Protein subcellular localization was predicted with WoLF PSORT (https://wolfpsort.hgc.jp/) [50], and the molecular weight (MW) and the isoelectric point (pI) were calculated via the R package Peptides (version 2.4.6) [51]. Additionally, the secondary structure of the broomcorn millet SnRK2 protein was predicted via the PRABI website (https://npsa-prabi.ibcp.fr/cgi-bin/npsa_automat.pl?page=npsa_sopma.html).

Collinearity analysis of PmSnRK2 genes

Collinearity analysis was conducted via the MCScanX toolkit [52] with default parameters to investigate the duplication events of PmSnRK2 genes in the broomcorn millet genome, and the distribution of collinear gene pairs was visualized via MCScanX on the Linux server. The Ka/Ks ratios for each gene were calculated via KaKs_Calculator [53] with the following parameters: genetic code selection 1 (standard code) and the YN method for calculation [54]. The divergence time (T) is calculated via the equation T = Ks/(2 × 9.1 × 10−9) × 10−6 million years ago (MYA) [55].

Phylogenetic, structural and conserved motif analyses

On the Linux server, MUSCLE (version 3.8.1551) [33] was first used to perform a multiple sequence alignment of SnRK2 protein sequences from Arabidopsis, rice, maize, and broomcorn millet. The alignment results were then used to construct a phylogenetic tree with IQ-TREE (version 2.3.6) [56] via the parameters: -m MFP -bb 1000 -nt 4 -pre. The phylogenetic tree was subsequently visualized via the online tool iTOL (https://itol.embl.de) [57]. Additionally, conserved motif analysis of the SnRK2 protein sequences was conducted on the Linux server via MEME software (version 5.5.5) with parameters set to detect up to 10 motifs, a minimum motif width of 6 amino acids, and a maximum motif width of 200 amino acids [58]. The gene structures and motifs of the PmSnRK2s were visualized using the online platform GSDS (https://gsds.gao-lab.org/) [59]. The Search Tool for the Retrieval of Interacting Genes/Proteins (STRING) (http://string-db.org/cgi) database was used to construct an interaction network among the PmSnRK2 proteins.

Structural modeling and superimposition of PmSnRK2 proteins

The three-dimensional (3D) structures of SnRK2 proteins from broomcorn millet were predicted using the SWISS-MODEL server (https://swissmodel.expasy.org/). The predicted structures were saved in PDB format for further analysis. The 3D structure of Arabidopsis SnRK2.3 (PDB ID: 3UC3) was downloaded from the RCSB PDB database (https://www.rcsb.org/). Structural superimposition was performed using PyMOL (Version 3.1.0) to compare and analyze the folding patterns and structural similarities between broomcorn millet SnRK2 proteins and Arabidopsis SnRK2.3.

Analysis of cis-acting elements

The 2 kb upstream sequences of the broomcorn millet SnRK2 genes were extracted as promoter regions and submitted to the PlantCARE database (http://bioinformatics.psb.ugent.be/webtools/plantcare/html/) [60] for cis-acting element analysis, with the results subsequently visualized via the online platform GSDS.

Expression patterns of PmSnRK2 genes in different tissues

To characterize the tissue-specific expression of PmSnRK2 genes in broomcorn millet, we analyzed public RNA-seq datasets [31] comprising seedlings (1 week old), shoots (3 weeks old), leaf blades, leaf sheaths, stems, inflorescences, and roots (all 8 weeks old), as well as mature seeds (12 weeks old). The expression levels (FPKM) were calculated and visualized as a heatmap using the pheatmap package.

RNA extraction and real-time PCR (qRT‒PCR)

Total RNA was extracted from the samples via the RNAprep Pure Kit (for Plants) (TIANGEN, Beijing, China). To eliminate genomic DNA contamination, the RNA was treated with DNase I, and its quality was subsequently assessed via gel electrophoresis and a Nanodrop2000 spectrophotometer. One microgram of DNase I–treated RNA was then reverse-transcribed into first-strand cDNA via the PrimeScript™ RT Reagent Kit (Takara, Dalian, China). PCR amplification was carried out with SYBR® Premix Ex Taq™ (Takara), and gene-specific primers were designed via Primer 5.0 (Table 4). Actin was used as the internal reference gene.

Primer sequences for qRT‒PCR

For the qRT‒PCR assay, the cDNA was diluted to 100 ng/µl with ddH₂O. The reactions were performed on an ABI 7900 HT Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems) in a total volume of 20 µl, which comprised 10 µl of 2× SYBR® Premix Ex Taq™, 0.4 µl of each primer (10 µM), 0.4 µl of ROX Reference Dye (50×), and 1 µl of template (approximately 100 ng/µl), with ddH₂O added to reach the final volume. The thermal cycling conditions were as follows: initial predenaturation at 95 °C for 30 s, followed by 40 cycles consisting of 95 °C for 5 s (denaturation) and 60 °C for 1 min (annealing/extension and fluorescence data collection). Three biological replicates were performed, each with three technical replicates. The relative expression levels of the SnRK2 genes were calculated via the 2–ΔΔCT method [61].

Conclusions

In this study, comprehensive bioinformatics analyses combined with qRT-PCR were employed to systematically characterize the SnRK2 gene family (PmSnRK2) in broomcorn millet. A total of 16 PmSnRK2 genes were identified, including 12 collinear gene pairs, with duplication events estimated to have occurred approximately between 1.28 and 219.36 million years ago. qRT-PCR analyses demonstrated diverse expression patterns of most PmSnRK2 genes under ABA, salt, and drought treatments, suggesting their potential involvement in broomcorn millet’s response to abiotic stresses. However, findings presented here are based primarily on transcript-level analyses and bioinformatics predictions; thus, further experimental validation at protein and functional levels is necessary to clarify the exact biological roles of the PmSnRK2 gene family. Nevertheless, this work provides fundamental insights into the physicochemical characteristics, evolutionary relationships, and potential functions of the PmSnRK2 family, laying an essential foundation for subsequent functional studies in broomcorn millet.
