Integration of locking screw fixation into 3D-printed patient-specific porous titanium implants enhances osseointegration: an in vivo study in a rabbit bone defect model
Authors: Kyu-Won Kang, Sook Yang, Sun Young Kim, Jihye Choi, Byung-Jae Kang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractImportanceThree-dimensional (3D) printing enables fabrication of patient-specific implants (PSIs) tailored to complex bone defects. Although locking screw fixation offers mechanical advantages in conventional plating, its integration into 3D-PSIs is technically challenging and has not been evaluated for biological outcomes.ObjectiveThis study aimed to assess whether locking screw fixation enhances osseointegration compared to nonlocking fixation when directly integrated into 3D-printed porous titanium implants.MethodsSegmental femoral defects (20 mm) were created in ten adult rabbits, and customized porous Ti6Al4V implants were designed based on preoperative computed tomography (CT) scans. The implants incorporated threaded holes replicating the ARIX locking system and were fabricated via selective laser melting. Animals were randomly assigned to locking (n = 5) or nonlocking (n = 5) screw fixation groups. After 12 weeks, implant osseointegration was evaluated using radiography, micro-CT, and histological analyses.ResultsAll implants remained stable without failure. Micro-CT revealed significantly higher bone volume fraction in the locking group (27.8% ± 3.7%) than the nonlocking group (23.8% ± 1.7%; p < 0.05). Histology showed more extensive and homogeneous bone ingrowth in the locking group, whereas fibrous tissue formation was observed in some nonlocking cases. The bone area percentage was significantly greater in the locking group (24.4% ± 9.9%) than in the nonlocking group (12.2% ± 7.1%; p < 0.05).Conclusions and RelevanceThis study demonstrates that integrating locking screw fixation into 3D-printed porous titanium PSIs enhances osseointegration by improving primary stability. These findings suggest that locking fixation in PSI designs may improve outcomes for load-bearing bone defect reconstructions in veterinary and potentially human applications.Keywords: Osseointegration, 3D-printed implant, patient-specific implant, locking plate system

INTRODUCTION

Advances in three-dimensional (3D) printing technology have significantly expanded its applications in orthopedic surgery [1]. Early applications included 3D-printed bone models for surgical planning and patient-specific cutting guides. More recently, 3D printing has enabled the fabrication of patient-specific implants (PSIs) using medical-grade metals such as the Ti6Al4V titanium alloy [2]. Owing to the customization capability, 3D-PSIs are effective for addressing bone defects; they can be tailored to the precise size and shape of the defects [3]. Titanium-based PSIs exhibit excellent biocompatibility and mechanical strength, and hence, they are well-suited for reconstructing bone defects that require both anatomical conformity and mechanical support [4,5].

In addition to the structural advantages of 3D-printed titanium implants, their porous architectures allow for the modulation of implant stiffness to reduce stress shielding, and promote direct biological integration with the host bone [6,7]. Implants designed with interconnected pores of controlled size and porosity allow the bone tissue to gradually infiltrate the structure, forming a biologically stable bone–implant interface [8]. Previous studies suggest the pore sizes between 500 μm and 800 μm and porosity levels of 70%–90% as optimal for promoting bone ingrowth and vascularization [9]. Porous titanium PSIs exhibit strong potential for long-term success in bone defect repair by supporting both structural reconstruction and biological integration.

Although osseointegration promisingly offers long-term implant stability, this biological process is effective only when adequate fixation is achieved during surgery. Implant stability is typically described in two phases: primary stability, which occurs immediately after fixation, and secondary stability, which develops gradually through osseointegration [10]. For a successful transition between these phases, micromotion at the bone–implant interface must be carefully regulated [11]. Controlled micromovements within 50 to 150 micrometers promote bone ingrowth, whereas excessive motion can lead to fibrous tissue formation rather than bone ingrowth. Conversely, overly rigid fixation may suppress the biological signals necessary for osseointegration [10,11].

Hence, selecting an appropriate initial fixation method for porous 3D-PSIs is important, because it directly influences the primary stability and subsequent osseointegration. Earlier designs typically combined a 3D-printed implant body with conventional fixation systems such as plate-screw constructs or intramedullary nails. Some recent approaches have incorporated fixation components directly into implants during the design and printing processes, which enables single-step implantation and reduces the involvement of additional hardware [10,12]. Despite the growing adoption of this integrated strategy, the choice of specific fixation methods within such systems remains underexplored, particularly regarding their in vivo effects on implant stability and osseointegration.

In conventional orthopedic plating, locking screw fixation is well known to provide superior mechanical stability compared to nonlocking fixation [13,14,15,16]. However, its integration into 3D-printed PSIs remains rare due to manufacturing challenges in creating precise threaded locking holes, limiting its clinical application despite potential advantages. Moreover, the biological impact of locking versus nonlocking fixation within 3D-printed porous implants has never been directly evaluated in vivo. Addressing this knowledge gap is crucial for developing PSI designs that maximize both mechanical stability and biological integration.

In the present study, we integrated a locking plate system into a 3D-printed porous titanium implant that was compatible with both locking and nonlocking screws. To identify the effect of the screw type, we analyzed various screw configuration under identical implant designs and surgical conditions. We directly compared the in vivo performance of locking and nonlocking fixations using a rabbit femoral segmental defect model. Considering the critical role of primary stability in promoting osseointegration, we hypothesized that the fixation method influences both clinical outcomes and osseointegration.

METHODS

Animal model

All experimental procedures involving animals were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Seoul National University (SNU-221024-2-1). Ten adult male New Zealand white rabbits weighing 3.7–4.2 kg were enrolled in this study and randomly divided into two groups: (1) locking (n = 5) and (2) nonlocking (n = 5). All rabbits were acclimated to the facility for two weeks before the operation. They were housed in separate cages in a temperature-controlled room with free access to antibiotic-free food and water.

Implant design and fabrication

Computed tomography (CT) scans of both pelvic limbs were generated and saved for designing customized osteotomy guides and implants (1-mm slice thickness; Aquilion 64; Toshiba Medical Systems, Japan). Rabbits were sedated with the intramuscularly (IM) administered medetomidine (0.25 mg/kg) and positioned in sternal recumbency with both pelvic limbs flexed cranially and symmetrically aligned. The DICOM images from the CT study were transferred to an image analysis software, 3D slicer (Slicer 4.10, http://slicer.org) to create a stereolithography (STL) model of the right femur. The resulting STL files were exported to a computer-aided design software (Rhinoceros 5.0; McNeel, USA; Magics, Materialize, Belgium).

A virtual osteotomy was performed in the mid-diaphyseal region of the right femur to induce a 20 mm bone defect (Fig. 1A). The contact surface of the proximal and distal osteotomy guides were the inverted representation of a specific anatomical contour of the proximal and distal femurs, respectively (Fig. 1B). Each osteotomy guide comprised two cylindrical sleeves required for inserting a fixation Kirschner wire. The virtual implant included two main components: a central shaft to replace the 20 mm bone defect and a fixation plate conformal to the craniolateral surface of the remaining bones (Fig. 1C and D). The size and shape of the shaft were the same as the original bone. The thickness of the plate was 2.0 mm; it covered ≥ 65% of the remaining femur. Ten screw holes were incorporated into the plate and designed to replicate the ARIX variable-angle locking system (Jeil Medical, Korea). Screw trajectories were simulated using cylindrical placeholders, positioned to maximize the bone purchase (Fig. 1E).

Design of patient-specific three-dimensional-printed titanium implant and osteotomy guides. (A) Virtual osteotomy was performed to create a 20 mm defect in the mid-diaphyseal region of the femur. (B) Proximal and distal osteotomy guides were designed to facilitate the precise placement of the implant. (C) Craniocaudal and (D) lateral views of the implant incorporated on to the femur. The magnified image shows threaded screw holes, replicating the ARIX locking system. (E) Screw trajectories were defined using cylinders of uniform diameter to optimize fixation.

Osteotomy guides were 3D-printed (Pixel One; Zerone, Korea) using a biocompatible urethane acrylate resin (SG-Clear; Dentis, Korea) (Fig. 2A). The implants were 3D-printed with Ti6A14V using a Selective Laser Melting printer (MetalSys150; Winforsys, Korea) (Fig. 2B); 10 threaded holes were directly printed on the plates to accommodate either 1.5/2.0 mm ARIX locking or cortical screws. The shaft included an internal mesh structure with 750 μm pores, strut thickness of 300 μm, a porosity of 75%, and a Dode design to enhance bone incorporation (Fig. 2C) [13]. Each implant was plasma sterilized before the surgery.

Fabricated osteotomy guides and porous titanium implants. (A) Three-dimensional-printed osteotomy guides fabricated with biocompatible urethane acrylate resin. (B) Patient-specific porous titanium implants manufactured using selective laser melting. (C) Magnified view of the porous shaft, illustrating internal lattice structure.

Surgical procedures

The rabbits were sedated with medetomidine (0.25 mg/kg, IM) and anesthetized with isoflurane used at 4 MAC for induction and 1.5–2 MAC for maintenance, administered through a 1–2 L/min flow of oxygen. Tramadol (5 mg/kg) was administered subcutaneously (SC) as an analgesic; cefazolin (50 mg/kg, IM), a prophylactic antibiotic, was administered. Surgery was performed under sterile conditions, placing the animal in the left decubitus position. The right femur was exposed via a lateral approach between the flexor and extensor muscles; the muscle attachments were separated using a periosteal elevator through the intramuscular spaces.

A 20 mm long mid-diaphyseal segmental defect was created using an oscillating saw, aligned with osteotomy guides to ensure optimal apposition between the implant and remaining femur (Fig. 3A and B). The implant was placed onto the craniolateral surface of the remaining proximal and distal bone fragments to reconstruct the femoral defects. In the nonlocking group, ten 1.5 mm ARIX cortical screws were used, while ten 1.5 mm ARIX locking screws were used in the locking group to secure the bone plate (Fig. 3C and D). The wound was cleaned and rinsed with saline. The subcutaneous tissue and skin were closed using continuous sutures.

Intraoperative procedures and implant fixation. (A) Osteotomy guides were placed on the femur. (B) The femoral osteotomy was performed to create a 20 mm bone defect. (C) A customized titanium implant was placed end-to-end in the defect (D) and secured with 10 locking and 10 cortical screws in the locking and nonlocking groups, respectively.

Cefazolin (50 mg/kg, IM) and meloxicam (0.3 mg/kg, SC) were administered as postoperative therapy for 7 days. Unprotected weight bearing was allowed immediately after the procedure. The subjects were housed individually in standard rabbit cages and provided ad libitum access to standard rabbit food and water. The rabbits were sacrificed 12 weeks after surgery. All interventions were performed by the same researcher.

X-ray and micro-CT analysis

Radiographs were obtained immediately after surgery, and subsequently, every 2 weeks to monitor implant failure and bone formation. Rabbits were euthanized 12 weeks after implantation, and their right femurs were retrieved for micro-CT analysis. The titanium implant, along with the surrounding tissue, was fixed by incubating them in 10% buffered neutral formalin for one week, followed by micro-CT scanning (SkyScan 1173 ver. 1.6.0; SkyScan, Belgium). The specimens were scanned using 130 kV voltage, 60 μA current, with a pixel size of 14.98 μm, an exposure time of 500 ms, and a 1.0 mm aluminum filter. Raw micro-CT data were reconstructed using NRecon (SkyScan), and the reconstructed images were analyzed using Data Viewer and CT-An software (SkyScan).

Two regions of interest (ROIs) were determined for each specimen to assess the intraporous bone formation in the implant, which were located at a 3 mm distance from the proximal and distal bone-implant junctions. Newly grown bone was distinguished from the metal implants and soft tissue by adjusting the upper and lower gray thresholds to 39 and 36, respectively. The newly formed bone volume fraction (BVF) was calculated for each specimen based on the sum of the BVF of the proximal and distal ROIs. The BVF for each ROI was determined by dividing the volume of new bone tissue by the total void volume of the porous titanium implant.

Histological and histomorphometric analysis

After the nondestructive micro-CT analysis, specimens were dehydrated in a graded series of alcohol. Undecalcified tissues were processed and embedded in methacrylate resin (Technovit 7200 VLC; Kulzer-Exakt, Germany). For each specimen, longitudinal section was cut in the midsagittal plane with a diamond coated saw (Exakt 300 CL; Exakt Apparatbau, Germany), ground to an appropriate thickness (70–90 μm) with a microgrinder machine (Exakt 400 CS; Exakt Technology, USA). Sections were stained with Goldner’s Trichrome method, which colored the implant black, mature mineralized bone blue, and osteoid orange-red. Red-stained areas with osteocytes were classified as premature bones, while those without osteocytes were classified as fibrous tissue.

The slides were digitally scanned (Pannoramic 250 Flash III; 3DHISTECH, Hungary) and analyzed using computer software (CaseViewer version 2.3; 3DHISTECH). The composition and distribution of the newly formed bone were evaluated. Bone ingrowth within the porous shaft of the implant was quantitatively assessed in two predefined ROIs, located 3 mm from the proximal and distal bone–implant interfaces. For each region, the bone area percentage was calculated by dividing the newly formed bone area by the total pore area. The sum of these two measurements was used as the final bone area percentage for each specimen.

Statistical analysis

Data were statistically analyzed using GraphPad Prism 10.4.0 for Windows (GraphPad Software, USA). The normality of the data distribution was evaluated using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation. As the data met the assumption of normality, intergroup comparisons of BVF (from micro-CT analysis) and bone area percentage (acquired from histomorphometric analysis) were conducted using independent sample t-tests with Welch’s correction. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05 for all tests.

RESULTS

General observation

The implants were placed into the femoral bone defects without difficulty. All rabbits tolerated the surgical procedure and resumed load bearing of the operated limb immediately after returning to consciousness. Postoperative radiographs indicated successful reconstruction and stabilization of the defect using the porous titanium implant. Intraoperative observation and immediate postoperative radiographs showed no apparent gap between the osteotomy site and the implant surface in either group. The operative time ranged from 65 to 89 min, with a mean of 77 min. Primary wound healing was achieved within the first week after surgery.

Radiographic and micro-CT findings

Radiographs confirmed a static position of the implants in both groups over the 12 weeks (Fig. 4A-D and 4F-I). In the nonlocking group, subtle callus formation was detected on the contralateral side of the plate at week 4 onward. By 12 weeks, the callus crawled out from the residual bone; gross inspection revealed tissues wrapping around the implant (Fig. 4E), which was not fully mineralized and was not detected on radiographs. The locking group exhibited more pronounced callus formation than the nonlocking counterparts, which started 4 weeks after surgery. Eight and 12 weeks postoperatively, mineralized callus began to bridge the defect in the caudal aspect of the femur, which was also confirmed by gross inspection after harvest (Fig. 4J).

Radiographic images and gross appearance of reconstructed femurs. Representative postoperative radiographs and harvested specimens of the nonlocking (A-E) and locking (F-J) groups at different post-operative time-points (shown in lateral view).

Micro-CT images of the specimens harvested at 12 weeks revealed bone ingrowth within the porous regions of all the implants, indicating osseointegration (Fig. 5A and B). The transverse sections at the screw level revealed bone resorption around the screw threads in four of the five specimens in the nonlocking group, whereas the screw remained well integrated with the surrounding cortical bone in the locking counterpart. At the bone-implant junction level, bone formation was more evenly distributed across the entire cross-sectional area in the locking group than in the nonlocking group. Quantitative analysis showed that the average BVF was significantly higher in the locking group (27.8% ± 3.7%) than in the nonlocking group (23.8% ± 1.7%) (p < 0.05) (Fig. 5C).

(C) Bone volume fraction of the two groups. Scale bars = 1 mm.

Histological findings

Histological images revealed new bone tissue infiltrating the interconnected pores of porous titanium implants in most specimens (Fig. 6A and B). In the nonlocking group, the mineralized bone was predominantly confined to the peripheral regions of the shaft. In two specimens, a fibrous tissue layer was observed in the distal ROI, which separated the bone tissue from the implant and prevented direct bone infiltration. Contrastingly, in the locking group, mineralized bone and osteoid formed homogeneously within the implant pores and were tightly interlocked with the titanium struts in all specimens. Furthermore, quantitative histomorphometric analysis supported these findings; the average bone area percentage was significantly higher in the locking group (24.43% ± 9.9%) than in the nonlocking group (12.2% ± 7.1%) (p < 0.05) (Fig. 6C).

DISCUSSION

The present study demonstrated that locking screw fixation significantly enhanced osseointegration in 3D-printed patient-specific porous titanium implants compared to that achieved using nonlocking fixation. Locking fixation systems are designed to provide angular and axial stability through mechanical interlocking of the screw heads with the plate body [13]. Several biomechanical studies have consistently shown that locked constructs offer superior mechanical stability over nonlocked constructs in fracture models, particularly under osteoporotic conditions [13,14,15,16], however the biological consequences of these mechanical differences, including their impact on osseointegration, have remained largely unexplored. Our findings present a direct experimental validation that the superior primary stability achieved by locking fixation can contribute to improved biological integration in the context of bone defect reconstruction using porous implants.

Beyond the fixation method, the design of all porous implants was standardized in this study; they featured 75% porosity, a 750 μm pore size, and fully interconnected pores. These parameters were selected based on previous studies demonstrating that such configurations optimize bone regeneration [8,9]. The implants also incorporate a dense outer shell surrounding their porous cores. Fully porous implants with open cellular structures may promote vascularization and supposedly improve healing. However, Popov et al. [17] reported that air entrapment within the lattice promotes aerobic bacterial growth, inducing severe inflammation and limb amputation in severe case. To mitigate this potential risk, a solid outer shell is incorporated into the design. This standardized implant configuration minimized confounding variables, allowing the biological effects of different fixation methods to be evaluated more clearly.

Throughout the 12-week observation period, no implant failures or screw pull-out events were recorded in either group, which suggests that both locking and nonlocking fixations provided sufficient primary clinical stability. These findings are consistent with previous clinical reports that involved various fractures, including those of the humerus [18,19], tibia [20,21], fibula [22,23], ankle [24,25], and mandible [26], which generally reported comparable outcomes in locking and nonlocking plate fixation in terms of union rates, infection, hardware failure, or reoperation rates. However, these studies primarily evaluated functional recovery and radiographic bone-to-bone healing without using porous implants, and fundamentally differ from our model. In addition to radiographic and functional evaluations, we have directly quantified osseointegration within a 3D-printed porous structure using micro-CT and histological analysis, providing deeper insights into the biological effects of the fixation strategy at the bone–implant interface.

The key finding of this study was that locking fixation enhanced osseointegration compared to nonlocking fixation. The micro-CT analysis of the locking group revealed a significantly higher BVF with the bone well integrated around the screws, whereas the nonlocking group exhibited localized bone resorption near the screw heads. Consistent with mechanical remodelling, this conical resorption pattern likely resulted from micromotion at the screw–bone interface under repetitive loading. Unlike locking screws stabilized via mechanical interlocking with the plate, nonlocking screw system relied on frictional compression, which made them more susceptible to micromotion-induced resorption [27]. In contrast, the locking group demonstrated bone on-growth around the screws, indicating a more stable fixation. These findings align with previously reported total hip arthroplasty results showing reduced resorption and improved osseointegration with locking mechanisms [28]. Although titanium-related artifacts may have influenced the absolute BVF values, both groups were evaluated under identical scanning conditions to validate the observed differences.

Furthermore, histological evaluation validated the effects of the fixation method on osseointegration. The locking group exhibited a significantly higher bone area percentage and more uniform bone infiltration throughout the porous structure, with the presence of osteoids compared to the nonlocking counterparts; these outcomes indicated active and ongoing bone formation. Contrastingly, the nonlocking group showed bone ingrowth considerably confined to the periphery of the implant, with fibrous tissue occasionally observed at the bone–implant interface. Excessive micromotion, which favors fibrous encapsulation over bone integration [29,30], potentially contributed to the impaired osseointegration in the nonlocking group. Meanwhile, the progressive bone formation observed in the locking group aligns with the model proposed by Zhang et al. [31], which suggests that under optimal mechanical stability, bone ingrowth proceeds within porous implants until mechanical stability is achieved through permanent bone–implant fusion, after which further infiltration naturally ceases.

Notably, we detected the formation of peri-implant callus. Similar outcomes were reported in previous studies using 3D-printed porous implants for load-bearing bone defect reconstruction, which revealed callus patterns to be either “crawling” along the implant surface [6,32,33] or forming a continuous “bridging” structure [5,31,34]. Some authors attributed this phenomenon to relative fracture healing under controlled micromotion [32], whereas others suggested that the bioinert nature of titanium potentially stimulates callus formation [5]. Importantly, studies by Zhang et al. [4] and Hou et al. [32] emphasized that mineralized calli enhance the implant–bone mechanical stability. In our study, nonlocking fixation was associated with crawling calli, whereas locking fixation promoted continuous bridging. Given that excessive interfragmentary movement, particularly in large-gap models, delays healing and results in an unmineralized callus [35], the observed callus patterns likely reflect the difference in mechanical stability between the groups. These findings suggest that in porous implant-based defect reconstruction, achieving sufficient primary stability may be more critical than promoting micromotion. Locking fixation appears to provide a more favorable mechanical environment for organized and mineralized callus formation.

Although this study demonstrated that locking fixation enhances osseointegration in 3D-printed porous implants, several considerations remain. First, while the ARIX plate system is well suited for direct 3D printing due to its simplified internal thread design, other thread designs may yield different outcomes; thus, further validation is necessary before broader clinical application. Second, periosteal elevation was required to position the osteotomy guides and implants accurately; therefore, the potential biological advantage of locking fixation in preserving periosteal integrity could not be assessed using this model. Third, the 12-week observation period may be insufficient to capture complete bone maturation, which is evidenced by the presence of osteoids in the locking group. Long-term studies or staged harvests can provide deeper insights into the progression of bone-implant interface fusion over time. Lastly, although this study compared purely locking and nonlocking fixations, a hybrid technique—using initial compression near the osteotomy followed by locking screws—may offer a clinically relevant alternative. This approach could reduce potential interface gaps while maintaining the stability of locking constructs. Future implant designs could incorporate compression capability, allowing for such strategies to be evaluated in porous 3D-printed constructs.

In conclusion, this study demonstrated that locking screw fixation significantly enhances osseointegration in 3D-printed patient-specific porous titanium implants for bone defect reconstruction. Achieving long-term implant stability through successful osseointegration is crucial for their durability over a patient’s lifetime. While locking fixation has been well established in conventional fracture repair, its application in 3D-PSIs has been limited by manufacturing challenges. This study provides both technical validation for integrating locking mechanisms via additive manufacturing and in vivo evidence supporting their biological benefits. This strategy could be particularly beneficial in load-bearing or osteoporotic bone conditions owing to the reduced the risk of screw loosening and preserved underlying bone biology; it offers a promising approach for optimizing the long-term success of porous 3D-printed implants even in clinically challenging scenarios.
