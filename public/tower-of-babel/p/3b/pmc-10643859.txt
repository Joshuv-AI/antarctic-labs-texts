Unmet Needs of Patients With Chronic Urticaria: A Survey in Korea
Authors: Joonhong Min, Young Her, Ki Won Moon, Ji In Park, Sunmi Kim, Eun-Hee Cho, Kyu-Hyoung Lim, Jae-Woo Kwon
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
Chronic urticaria (CU) is a common problem with a high disease burden that has a significant negative impact on quality of life. Many patients are undertreated, and awareness of management strategies is low among clinicians. The present study aimed to improve understanding of CU from the patients’ perspective, including the disease burden and current healthcare system use. Adult patients who presented to our referral hospital for CU treatment completed self-report questionnaires about demographics, clinical characteristics of CU, the impact of CU on daily life, unmet needs, and the history of medical service usage. This self-report survey included 127 participants (females, 57.0%; mean age, 42.0 ± 13.6 years; mean CU duration, 1.8 ± 3.4 years); 51.6% reported frequent discomfort with CU in daily life, including 44.1% of those who reported a good response to medication. More than half of the respondents reported a depressed mood and anxiety. Although 46.4% of the respondents reported that urticaria completely disappeared while on medication, only 10% were satisfied with the CU management provided by primary care hospitals. The principal cause of dissatisfaction was that they did not know the cause of CU (68.4% of patients). In total, 55% of the patients visited 2 or more hospitals before presenting to our referral hospital and 6.3% had tried folk remedies. In conclusion, most patients report that CU is not adequately controlled. Therefore, in addition to appropriate medication, information on the cause of CU, long-term treatment plan, medication safety, and expected prognosis is required to meet patients’ needs.

INTRODUCTION

Urticaria, which is one of the most common skin diseases,12 is characterized by pruritic wheals and flare skin reactions that usually persist for less than 24 hours and are caused by mast cell activation followed by the release of inflammatory mediators, including histamine.1 Urticaria is acute, usually lasting less than 6 weeks. However, when it lasts longer than 6 weeks, it is called chronic urticaria (CU). Chronic spontaneous urticaria (CSU) persists for at least 1–5 years,1 while chronic inducible urticaria has an even longer course. Most patients experience daily lesions and report a markedly reduced quality of life, significant emotional distress,34 sleep deprivation, and psychiatric comorbidities.156 CU is associated with large direct and indirect healthcare costs, and reduces patient performance both in the workplace and in private life.1

As CU is a common, chronic inflammatory disease that imposes a large burden, appropriate long-term management is essential to control this chronic disease. However, awareness of management strategies seems poor among clinicians.5 Many CU patients are undertreated5 despite high medical expenses.3 The epidemiology of CU as well as the associated issues of healthcare accessibility and medical problems vary among regions and populations. In Taiwan, 69% of CU patients were dissatisfied with their current CU management and switched physicians even if the symptoms were under control.6 In Germany, 60% of CU patients are currently untreated.5 Few studies on unmet needs about medication and medical service in Korean CU patients are available.

We assessed the impact of CU and the unmet needs of Korean patients seeking medical care to better understand the disease from the patients’ perspective in terms of disease burden and utilization of the healthcare system.5

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study population

Adult (aged ≥ 20 years) CU patients who first visited to Kangwon National University Hospital between October 2020 and February 2022 were enrolled. CU was defined as any type of urticaria, including CSU and physical urticaria, persisting for > 8 weeks and diagnosed by dermatologists or an allergist. Angioedema status was also evaluated by specialists. CU for > 8 weeks was used as an inclusion criterion in this study to avoid recall bias, and therefore only CU patients were enrolled, in accordance with a large survey of CU conducted in Germany.5 The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of Kangwon National University Hospital (IRB No. KNUH-B-2020-07-022-001).

Questionnaire surveys

The self-report questionnaire was designed based on several international studies on the unmet needs of CU patients5678 and a structured questionnaire recently used to survey the unmet needs of Korean patients with other diseases.9 The questionnaire was used to collect data on demographics, clinical characteristics, urticaria status (including the response to primary care treatments), the impact of CU on daily life, unmet needs in terms of CU control and care, and healthcare service utilization patterns. Responses to medication were determined by a question asking whether the urticaria completely disappeared with medication, improved but not eliminated, or showed no change. A good response to medication was defined as complete disappearance of urticaria with medication; such patients are called ‘responders.’ The impact of CU on quality of life was evaluated by the following question: “How much does urticaria affect your quality of life?”. A 5-point Likert scale response was used (not at all, very little, a little, moderately, or severely); the moderate and severe options reflected a “serious impact.” The subjective effects of over-the-counter medications were assessed by the following question: “How helpful were over-the-counter medications?” (not at all, poor, fair, good, or excellent).

Statistics

Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation and categorical variables as frequencies. Groups were compared using Student’s t-test or the χ2 test. SPSS for Windows software (ver. 26.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) was used for the analyses. P values < 0.05 were considered significant.

DISCUSSION

CU patients reported a major disease burden in daily life. Although 46.4% of the patients receiving primary care medication reported that the urticaria disappeared with medication, only 10% were satisfied with CU management by primary clinics. Such dissatisfaction is mainly attributable to ignorance of the cause of CU (68.4% of respondents); 31.5% were provided with almost no information and 41.7% were told that CU was ‘idiopathic.’

CU patients often report sleep disturbance, reduction in quality of life, and emotional distress,13456 with more than half of CU patients stating that they try to avoid stressful situations, reduce sporting or social activities, and modify their diet to prevent symptom flare-up.5 Similarly, more than half of all patients reported frequent discomfort in daily life and 80% sleep disturbances in this study. However, it was possible that many of the CU patients in this study did not take their medication regularly, since 44% reported that their urticaria disappeared with medication. If the effect of CU medication was good, appropriate regular medication may effectively control CU.10 Poor adherence to a medication regime has been frequently observed. In Taiwan, CU patients with severe symptoms preferred to visit clinics for medication, while those with milder symptoms preferred herbs or no treatment.6 Although about 40% of CU patients do not respond well to antihistamines,1 it appears that many patients take antihistamines irregularly.5 Therefore, clinicians may need to explain to CU patients that the prescribed medications should be taken even in the absence of symptoms.

Although about half of CU patients reported that medication led to a resolution of the urticaria, approximately 90% were not satisfied with their medical service. A high rate of distrust of medical services was reported in other studies. In Germany, 60.3% of CU patients stated that they were not currently receiving treatment and 52.8% of those who were on treatment stated that the medication did not help.5 In Taiwan, 69% of CSU patients under treatment were dissatisfied and often switched physicians, even if their symptoms were well controled.6 These findings suggest discrepancies between patient and physician perceptions of good CU management. The current study showed that a significant number of CU patients experience frustration due to the lack of a known underlying cause, resulting in a lack of curative treatment. Considering that symptoms and a high disease burden can be prevented with regular antihistamine treatment1 and that CU is usually resolved despite being long-lasting, clinicians must educate CU patients on the etiology of urticaria, the prognosis of CU, and the safety and importance of regular, long-term antihistamine use.

CU can persist for a long time, and cause serious problems in daily life. CU patients reported difficulties at work, social relationship and family relationships that were similar to those experienced by patients awaiting coronary artery bypass grafting.3 We found that older age may be a risk factor for isolation of CU patients from society and family, although most patients felt that family and friends understood their condition. It has also been reported that the psychological stress caused by CU was greater in an elderly group (aged 51 - 60 years) than in a younger group.6 Thus, feelings of isolation and emotional distress should be taken into account, especially when managing older patients with CU.

This study had several limitations. First, it was based on a small number of participants. However, to evaluate the subjective unmet needs of CU patients in the community and under primary care, we applied strict enrollment criteria, such as first-time visit to a referral hospital for CU; thus, the patients had previously been managed in a community or primary care environment, or were self-managed, with a CU diagnosis eventually confirmed by a CU specialist. Therefore, our study differed from large-scale studies based on telephone or email surveys conducted in other countries. As a first descriptive study of the unmet needs of CU patients in Korea, our results showed that the majority of CU patients identified unmet needs related to a lack of information from clinicians on the cause of disease. Secondly, evidence supporting the validity and reliability of the questionnaire used in this study was lacking. However, this reflects a lack of standardized tools, even in other languages, available to evaluate the unmet needs of CU patients. The questionnaire used in this study was based on similar studies of CU patients conducted in other countries, and on studies of the unmet needs of Korean patients with other diseases, because the unmet needs of people are closely related to the local community and the primary care environment, and may differ greatly among countries, regions, and cultures. This study is the first to examine the unmet needs of Korean patients with CU. Thirdly, data on medications prescribed in primary care settings (steroids or antihistamines) were lacking. We relied on patient self-reports; some patients may not have known all of the details of their medications. However, marked dissatisfaction with medical services was evident even when medication was effective. This study shows that a good CU patient-clinician relationship through patient education about CU is important to control this chronic inflammatory disease. In addition to the medication profiles, there may have been other variables related to subjective unmet needs not included in this study. The clinical characteristics of CU, including laboratory tests and autoimmune markers, were not included since this was self-reported survey of first-time visitors to a referral hospital. Comorbidities were also not included. Finally, in contrast to other studies with general CU patients, this study enrolled first-time visitors to a referral hospital.5678 Therefore, the cohort may have included patients with recent-onset CU and moderate-to-severe symptoms who were dissatisfied with their primary care.

The current study showed that most CU patients were dissatisfied with their medical service and experienced a high disease burden, although many of them subjectively reported good responses to medication. The main subjective unmet need of CU patients is a lack of explanation on CU etiology. Therefore, patients should be provided with information on the cause of CU, the long period of treatment, the safety of prescribed medications, and the expected prognosis.
